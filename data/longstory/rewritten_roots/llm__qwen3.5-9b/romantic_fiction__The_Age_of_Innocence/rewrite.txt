On a crisp January evening in the early 1870s, Christine Nilsson took the stage at New York's Academy of Music, bringing *Faust* to life for an audience that had traveled through snow-slicked streets in private carriages just to witness her. While whispers circulated of a grand new opera house rising in the distant uptown neighborhoods—promising to rival the splendor of Europe—the city's elite remained loyal to the Academy. They loved its cramped, outdated charm, which conveniently kept the nouveau riche at bay, and its legendary acoustics, a rarity in modern architecture.

When Newland Archer finally pushed open the heavy doors of the club box, the curtain had just risen on the famous Garden Scene. His tardiness wasn't due to neglect; he had dined promptly at seven with his mother and sister, lingering afterward in the family's Gothic library—a sanctuary where smoking was permitted—to savor a cigar. In New York, arriving late was simply "the thing" to do; punctuality suggested a lack of social awareness. Moreover, Newland was a man who believed anticipation offered a deeper pleasure than fulfillment itself. The prospect of seeing the opera's heroine perform was exquisite enough to justify the wait.

As the music swelled, Nilsson sang the iconic lines of the Daisy Song. Though the libretto was originally German, tradition dictated it be sung in Italian for the American ear. With notes as clear as morning dew, she sang *"M'amor! Non m'amor!"* before pressing a fallen daisy to her lips and lifting her gaze to the handsome, albeit vain, Count Capoul playing Faust.

Newland shifted his focus from the stage to the box directly across from him. Occupying it was the formidable Mrs. Manson Mingott, whose size prevented her attendance, represented today by her daughter-in-law, Mrs. Lovell Mingott, and her daughter, Mrs. Welland. Seated slightly behind them was a young girl in white, her eyes wide with rapturous adoration. As Nilsson's voice soared, a flush of pink warmed the girl's cheeks, coloring her brow and spreading down to her chest, where a single gardenia pinned her modest tucker. Her gloved fingers gently touched a massive bouquet of lilies-of-the-valley resting on her lap.

A surge of possessive pride washed over Newland. He watched the girl, recognizing her immediately as May Welland, the fiancée-to-be. "She has no idea what is happening," he thought, a thrill running through him. He imagined a future where he would guide her through the complexities of high society, transforming her innocence into worldly wisdom without ever compromising her purity. He envisioned reading *Faust* to her by the shores of Italian lakes, introducing her to the arts and the subtle games of courtship that defined their world. He did not wish for her to be naive, but rather socially adept, capable of charming men while maintaining her dignity.

Beside him, Lawrence Lefferts, the arbiter of all things proper in New York, suddenly gasped. "Good heavens!" he exclaimed, lowering his opera glass.

Newland followed his friend's gaze to see a striking new arrival in the Mingott box. A slim woman with dark brown hair curled tightly around her temples, secured by a diamond headband that gave her a fashionable "Josephine" look. Dressed in a dramatic dark blue velvet gown cinched at the waist, she moved with an air of unconscious confidence. After briefly consulting with Mrs. Welland regarding seating arrangements, she settled gracefully beside Mrs. Lovell Mingott.

Mr. Sillerton Jackson, the patriarch of the box and keeper of New York's deepest secrets, returned the opera glass to Lefferts. The entire group leaned in, knowing that whatever Jackson observed next would define the social landscape. With his silver hair and piercing blue eyes, Jackson was the living encyclopedia of the city's genealogy and gossip, remembering everything from the lineage of the Thorleys to the mysterious disappearance of Bob Spicer.

After a moment of silent scrutiny, Jackson twisted his mustache and delivered his verdict with characteristic gravity: "I didn't think the Mingotts would have tried it on."

Newland Archer found himself plunged into a peculiar and acute state of discomfort. It was irritating enough that the box currently commanding the undivided attention of the male spectators in New York was occupied by his fiancée, seated between her mother and her aunt. For a fleeting moment, he failed to recognize the lady in the striking empire-waist gown, unable to fathom why her presence should spark such animated speculation among those in the know. Then, clarity descended upon him, accompanied by a sudden surge of indignation.

No, certainly; no one would have presumed the Mingotts capable of such a maneuver! Yet, they had done exactly that. The hushed whispers drifting from the rear seats left no room for doubt in Archer's mind: the young woman was May Welland's cousin—the relative universally whispered about within the family as "poor Ellen Olenska." Archer knew she had arrived unexpectedly from Europe merely days prior; he had even received a note from Miss Welland (delivered without disapproval) confirming that she had visited her cousin, who was residing with the elderly Mrs. Mingott.

Archer held family unity in high regard. One of the traits he most revered in the Mingott clan was their steadfast defense of the few "black sheep" their otherwise impeccable lineage had produced. There was nothing petty or uncharitable in the young man's nature; indeed, he rejoiced that his future wife would not be stifled by artificial modesty from showing kindness—in private—to her unfortunate cousin. However, receiving Countess Olenska within the domestic sphere was one thing; parading her publicly at the opera, specifically in the very box containing the young woman whose engagement to him, Newland Archer, was scheduled for announcement within weeks, was quite another.

"No," he thought, echoing the sentiments of old Sillerton Jackson. "They surely wouldn't try something so brazen!" Of course, he acknowledged that whatever a man dared within the bounds of Fifth Avenue, old Mrs. Manson Mingott, the matriarch of the line, would dare. He had always admired the formidable elder lady. Despite having begun life merely as Catherine Spicer of Staten Island—with a father whose reputation had been mysteriously tarnished, and lacking either the wealth or status to erase that stain—she had allied herself with the head of the prosperous Mingott dynasty. She had married two of her daughters to "foreigners"—an Italian marquis and an English banker—and capped her audacity by constructing a vast mansion of pale cream-colored stone in an isolated stretch of wilderness near Central Park, a stark contrast to the ubiquitous brown sandstone that dominated the city's architecture.

Old Mrs. Mingott's foreign daughters had become local legends. They never returned to visit their mother, yet the matriarch, possessing a mind as active and dominant as her will, had remained sedentary and corpulent at home. Philosophically accepting her fate, she presided over her cream-colored estate—a structure rumored to be modeled after Parisian aristocratic hotels—as if living above Thirty-Fourth Street or installing French windows that swung open like doors rather than sliding up like sash windows were entirely ordinary matters. Everyone, including Mr. Sillerton Jackson, concurred that old Catherine lacked conventional beauty—a gift that, in New York society's eyes, justified every triumph and excused numerous shortcomings. The unkind remarked that, like her imperial namesake, she had secured her success through sheer force of will, a hard heart, and a haughty effrontery that was somehow redeemed by the extreme decency and dignity of her private conduct.

Mr. Manson Mingott had passed away when she was only twenty-eight, leaving his fortune tied up with the extra caution typical of the Spicer family's distrust. But his bold young widow moved fearlessly through the world, mingling freely in foreign circles, marrying her daughters into heavens-knows-what corrupt and fashionable realms, socializing with dukes and ambassadors, associating intimately with Catholics, entertaining opera singers, and maintaining close friendship with Mme. Taglioni. All the while, as Sillerton Jackson was quick to declare, there had never been a whisper against her reputation. The only distinction he noted was that, unlike the earlier Catherine, she had achieved everything without compromising her standing.

Mrs. Manson Mingott had successfully untied her husband's financial bonds and lived in opulence for fifty years. Yet, memories of her early hardships had instilled in her an excessive thriftiness. While she ensured that any dress or piece of furniture purchased was of the highest quality, she refused to lavish money on the transient pleasures of dining. Consequently, for entirely different reasons, her meals were as frugal as Mrs. Archer's, and her wines offered no redemption. Her relatives believed this austerity discredited the Mingott name, historically synonymous with fine living. Nevertheless, guests continued to flock to her despite the "made dishes" and flat champagne. In response to her son Lovell's protests—who attempted to restore the family's culinary prestige by hiring the finest chef in New York—she would laughingly retort, "What's the use of two excellent cooks in one family now that I've married off the girls and can't enjoy sauces myself?"

As Newland Archer reflected on these details, he cast his gaze once more toward the Mingott box. He observed that Mrs. Welland and her sister-in-law faced their semi-circle of critics with the Mingottian *aplomb* that old Catherine had instilled in all her descendants. Only May Welland betrayed, through a flushed complexion (perhaps exacerbated by the knowledge that he was observing her), a sense of the situation's gravity. As for the source of the commotion, she sat gracefully in her corner, eyes fixed on the stage. When she leaned forward, she revealed a glimpse of shoulder and bosom greater than New York society typically tolerated in ladies who wished to remain inconspicuous.

Few things appeared more abhorrent to Newland Archer than an offense against "Taste," that distant deity of whom "Form" served as the visible representative. Madame Olenska's pale, serious countenance appealed to his imagination as fitting for both the occasion and her tragic circumstances. However, the manner in which her dress—which possessed no tucker—slanted away from her slender shoulders shocked and disturbed him. He detested the thought of exposing May Welland to the influence of a young woman so indifferent to the dictates of Taste.

"After all," he heard a younger man murmur behind him amidst the intermission between the Mephistopheles and Martha scenes, "just *what* happened?"

"Well—she left him; nobody tries to deny that," came the reply.

"He's an awful brute, isn't he?" continued the young inquirer, a candid gentleman named Thorley, clearly preparing to champion the lady's cause.

"The very worst," declared Lawrence Lefferts with authority. "I knew him at Nice. A half-paralyzed, white-sneering fellow—rather handsome head, but eyes full of lashes. Let me tell you the type: when he wasn't with women, he was collecting china. Paying any price for both, I understand."

A general laugh rippled through the crowd, and the young champion pressed on, "Well, then—"

"Well, then; she bolted with his secretary."

"Oh, I see." The champion's face fell. "It didn't last long, though. I heard of her a few months later living alone in Venice. I believe Lovell Mingott went out to fetch her. He said she was desperately unhappy. That's acceptable—but parading her at the opera is another matter entirely."

"Perhaps," ventured young Thorley, "she's too unhappy to be left at home."

This remark was met with an irreverent laugh, causing the youth to blush deeply as he attempted to feign innocence regarding what the cognoscenti termed a "double entendre."

"Well—it's odd to have brought Miss Welland, anyhow," someone whispered in a low tone, casting a sidelong glance at Archer.

"Oh, that's part of the campaign: Granny's orders, no doubt," Lefferts chuckled. "When the old lady does something, she does it thoroughly."

The performance concluded, and a general stir erupted in the box. Suddenly, Newland Archer felt compelled to take decisive action. The urge to be the first to enter Mrs. Mingott's box, to proclaim to the waiting world his engagement to May Welland, and to stand by her through whatever difficulties her cousin's anomalous situation might entail, had abruptly overridden all scruples and hesitation. He hurried through the crimson corridors to the far side of the theater.

Upon entering the box, his eyes met Miss Welland's. He realized instantly that she had comprehended his motive, though the rigid family dignity both regarded as a supreme virtue prevented her from voicing it. The inhabitants of their world existed in an atmosphere of faint implications and delicate subtleties; the fact that he and she understood one another without a single word seemed to the young man to draw them closer than any explicit explanation ever could. Her eyes conveyed: *"You see why Mother brought me,"* and his replied silently: *"I would not for the world have had you stay away."*

"You know my niece, Countess Olenska?" Mrs. Welland asked as she extended her hand to her future son-in-law.

On the night of her annual ball, Mrs. Julius Beaufort never failed to make her appearance at the Opera House. Indeed, she strategically scheduled her grandest event on an evening of operatic performance, thereby signaling to the city her total immunity from domestic drudgery and her command of a staff capable of orchestrating every detail of her celebration in her absence. The Beaufort residence stood as one of the rare estates in New York boasting a dedicated ballroom—a luxury that predated even the mansions of Mrs. Manson Mingott or the Headly Chiverses. At a time when it began to seem provincial to cover a drawing-room floor with a crash mat and shift the furniture upstairs, the Beauforts maintained a space reserved solely for dancing, left to shuttered darkness for three hundred and sixty-four days of the year. With gilt chairs stacked in corners and chandeliers stored in bags, this architectural excess served as a potent compensation for any regrets regarding the Beaufort legacy.

Mrs. Archer, prone to distilling social observations into axioms, once remarked, "We all have our pet common people." While the phrase was bold, its truth was quietly acknowledged within many an exclusive circle. Yet, the Beauforts occupied a peculiar stratum; some whispered they were merely common, others claimed they were worse. Mrs. Beaufort hailed from one of America's most revered families; she was formerly the radiant Regina Dallas of the South Carolina branch, a penniless beauty introduced to New York society by her cousin, the perpetually imprudent Medora Manson, who consistently acted wrongly from noble motives. Possession of connections to the Mansons and Rushworths granted a *droit de cité*—a right of entry—as Mr. Sillerton Jackson, a frequent visitor to the Tuileries, termed it. However, did one forfeit this privilege upon marrying Julius Beaufort? Who, precisely, was Beaufort? He passed as English, possessing charm, handsomeness, wit, and hospitality, though plagued by an ill temper. Having arrived in America with recommendations from Old Mrs. Manson Mingott's English son-in-law, a banker, he quickly secured a prominent position in business. Yet, rumors persisted of dissipated habits, a bitter tongue, and mysterious origins. When Medora announced her cousin's engagement to him, it was viewed as another lapse in judgment in her long history of indiscretions.

Yet, folly often bears fruit as surely as wisdom. Two years after the wedding, it was conceded that Mrs. Beaufort presided over the most distinguished house in New York. The mechanics of this miracle remained obscure. Indolent, passive, and occasionally dismissed as dull by the caustic, she nonetheless grew younger, blonder, and more breathtaking annually. Dressed like an idol and draped in pearls, she reigned supreme in Mr. Beaufort's heavy brownstone palace, commanding the attention of the entire city without raising a jeweled finger. The cognoscenti speculated that Beaufort himself managed the household: training the servants, instructing the chef, directing the gardeners, selecting guests, brewing punch, and dictating the notes his wife sent to friends. If true, these duties were executed in private, allowing him to present himself publicly as a carefree millionaire strolling into his drawing room with the detached ease of a guest, remarking, "My wife's gloxinias are a marvel, aren't they? I believe she imports them from Kew."

New York agreed that Beaufort's secret lay in his ability to carry things off. Whispers that he had been aided in leaving England by an international banking house were shrugged off as lightly as the rest. Though New York's business conscience was as sharp as its moral standards, Beaufort swept everything before him, dragging the city into his drawing rooms. For over two decades, invitations to the Beauforts were received with the same sense of security as visits to the Mingotts, accompanied by the added assurance of hot canvas-back ducks and vintage wines, rather than tepid champagne and reheated croquettes from Philadelphia.

As usual, Mrs. Beaufort appeared in her box just prior to the Jewel Song. And as customary, when she rose at the conclusion of the third act, wrapped her opera cloak around her lovely shoulders, and vanished, New York understood that the ball would commence within the hour. The Beaufort house was a destination New Yorkers proudly displayed to foreigners, particularly on ball nights. They were pioneers in owning their own red velvet carpets, having them unrolled by footmen beneath their own awnings, rather than renting them with supper and chairs. They also initiated the custom of removing cloaks in the hall, sparing ladies the indignity of retreating to the hostess's bedroom to re-curl their hair with gas burners; Beaufort was rumored to have quipped that he presumed all his wife's friends had maids to ensure proper coiffure before departure. Furthermore, the house was boldly designed with a ballroom, eliminating the need to squeeze through narrow passages as at the Chiverses'. Instead, guests marched solemnly down a vista of enfiladed drawing rooms—the sea-green, the crimson, and the bouton d'or—seeing from afar the reflection of multi-candled chandeliers in polished parquet floors, leading eventually to a conservatory where camellias and tree-ferns arched their costly foliage over seats of black and gold bamboo.

Newland Archer, fitting his station as a young man of consequence, arrived somewhat late. He had left his overcoat with the silk-stockinged footmen—a vanity of Beaufort's—dawdled briefly in the library adorned with Spanish leather and furnished with Buhl and malachite tables, where a few men conversed while donning dancing gloves, before joining the line of guests awaiting reception on the threshold of the crimson drawing room. Archer felt distinctly nervous. Unlike the typical young bloods who retired to their clubs after the opera, he had walked several blocks up Fifth Avenue on the fine night before returning toward the Beauforts. He harbored a definite fear that the Mingotts might be pushing boundaries, perhaps acting on Granny Mingott's orders to escort the Countess Olenska to the ball. Recalling the grave tone of the conversation in the club box, he realized the potential magnitude of such a breach. Although more determined than ever to "see the thing through," his chivalric eagerness to champion his betrothed's cousin had waned since their brief exchange at the opera.

Wandering into the bouton d'or drawing room, where Beaufort had presumptuously hung "Love Victorious," the controversial nude by Bouguereau, Archer found Mrs. Welland and her daughter near the entrance to the ballroom. Couples were already gliding across the floor beyond; candlelight illuminated swirling tulle skirts, girlish heads crowned with modest blossoms, the dashing plumes and ornaments of married women's hairstyles, and the gleam of starched shirt fronts and fresh *glacé* gloves. Miss Welland, seemingly ready to join the dancers, lingered on the threshold holding her lilies-of-the-valley as her sole bouquet. Her face was faintly pale, her eyes alight with candid excitement. A cluster of young men and girls surrounded her, exchanging handshakes, laughter, and pleasantries, while Mrs. Welland, standing slightly apart, offered a beam of qualified approval. It was clear that Miss Welland was about to announce her engagement, while her mother adopted the air of parental reluctance deemed appropriate for the occasion.

Archer paused. This announcement had been his express wish, yet he would have preferred a different manner of revealing his happiness. Proclaiming it amidst the clamor of a crowded ballroom seemed to strip the event of the delicate bloom of privacy due to matters closest to the heart. His joy ran so deep that this superficial blurring left its core untouched, yet he desired the surface remain pure as well. He found solace in realizing that May Welland shared this sentiment. Her eyes fled to his with a beseeching look, communicating silently: *"Remember, we are doing this because it is right."* No appeal could have resonated more immediately within Archer's chest. Still, he wished the necessity of their action stemmed from some higher ideal rather than the unfortunate circumstances surrounding poor Ellen Olenska.

The group around Miss Welland parted to make way for him with significant smiles. After accepting his share of congratulations, he drew his betrothed into the center of the ballroom floor and placed his arm around her waist. "Now we shan't have to talk," he smiled into her open eyes as they drifted away on the soft waves of the Blue Danube. She offered no verbal reply. Her lips trembled into a smile, but her gaze remained distant and serious, fixed on some ineffable vision. "Dear," Archer whispered, pulling her closer. It struck him then that the initial hours of engagement, even spent in a ballroom, held something grave and sacramental. What a new life awaited, with this whiteness, radiance, and goodness at his side!

Once the dance concluded, the pair, becoming an affianced couple, wandered into the conservatory. Sitting behind a high screen of tree-ferns and camellias, Newland pressed her gloved hand to his lips. "You see, I did as you asked," she said. "Yes; I couldn't wait," he replied, smiling. After a pause, he added, "Only I wish it hadn't had to be at a ball." "Yes, I know," she responded, meeting his glance with understanding. "But after all—even here we are alone together, aren't we?" "Oh, dearest—always!" Archer exclaimed.

By the following day, the formalities of betrothal began in earnest. The social rituals of New York were a machine of exquisite precision, allowing for no deviation. True to tradition, Newland Archer first accompanied his mother and sister to pay their respects to Mrs. Welland. From there, the trio—Archer, his mother, and May—drove out to the ancestral home of Mrs. Manson Mingott, where the young couple awaited the blessing of the matriarch.

For a young man of Archer's generation, a visit to Mrs. Mingott was invariably a highlight, a delightful interlude in the rigid schedule of courtship. Her residence stood as a historical artifact in itself, though perhaps less ancient than the formidable estates lining University Place and lower Fifth Avenue. Those older homes were monuments to the pure style of the 1830s: grim yet harmonious, draped in cabbage-rose garlands, furnished with rosewood consoles, featuring round-arched fireplaces framed by black marble mantels, and lined with immense mahogany bookcases. In stark contrast, Mrs. Mingott, having constructed her own home later, had decisively discarded the heavy furniture of her youth. Instead, she had blended her family's cherished heirlooms with the frivolous, ornate upholstery of the Second Empire.

Her daily routine involved sitting in a window of her ground-floor sitting room, appearing to watch with calm detachment as life and fashion flowed northward toward her solitary entrance. She showed no urgency in welcoming guests; indeed, her patience matched her absolute confidence. She was convinced that eventually, the chaotic remnants of the old city—the construction hoardings, the quarries, the one-story saloons, the wooden greenhouses in ragged gardens, and the rocky outcrops where goats grazed—would vanish before the inevitable advance of residences as majestic as her own. Perhaps, she reasoned with her characteristic impartiality, even grander. Furthermore, she believed the cobblestones, currently battered by the clatter of old omnibuses, would soon yield to the smooth asphalt rumored to exist in Paris. Until then, she suffered no sense of isolation. Her rooms filled easily with visitors, and she managed her household with the same ease as the Beauforts, requiring no additions to her supper menu.

Time, however, had transformed Mrs. Mingott physically. What had once been a plump, active woman with a neatly turned foot and ankle had swollen into something vast and august, akin to a natural phenomenon. This mid-life expansion had descended upon her like a flood of lava on a doomed city. Yet, she accepted this metamorphosis with the same philosophical grace she applied to all her trials. Now, in extreme old age, she presented to her mirror an almost unwrinkled expanse of firm pink and white flesh. At its center, the traces of her original small face remained, waiting to be excavated. Two smooth flights of double chins led downward to the dizzying depths of a still-snowy bosom, veiled in snowy muslins secured by a miniature portrait of the late Mr. Mingott. Surrounding and beneath this central image, waves of black silk surged outward over the edges of a capacious armchair, topped by two tiny white hands poised like gulls upon the crest of the billows.

The sheer volume of Mrs. Mingott's flesh had rendered stair-climbing impossible. With characteristic independence—and in flagrant violation of New York propriety—she had relocated her reception rooms to the upper floors while establishing her permanent residence on the ground level. Consequently, when one sat in her sitting room beside her, one could peer through an always-open door and a looped-back yellow damask portière into an unexpected vista: a bedroom featuring a huge, low bed upholstered like a sofa, alongside a toilet table adorned with frivolous lace flounces and a gilt-framed mirror. Visitors found this arrangement both startling and fascinating, recalling scenes from French fiction and architectural temptations to immorality that the average American had never conceived. Such layouts were how women with lovers lived in those supposedly wicked old European societies, keeping all rooms on one floor to facilitate the indecent proximity described in their novels.

Newland Archer found this amusing. He secretly imagined the love scenes of *Monsieur de Camors* taking place within Mrs. Mingott's bedroom. While he admired the audacity of a woman leading a blameless life amidst such a stage-set for adultery, he also conceded with considerable respect that if a lover had truly desired her, the intrepid matriarch certainly would have taken him.

To the general relief of the party, the Countess Olenska was absent from the drawing room during the betrothed couple's visit. Mrs. Mingott claimed she had gone out—a statement that, given the glaring sunlight and the busy shopping hour, seemed itself an indiscretion for a woman of her compromised reputation. Nevertheless, her absence spared the group the embarrassment of her presence and the faint shadow her unhappy past might otherwise have cast upon their radiant future.

The visit proceeded successfully, as anticipated. Mrs. Mingott was delighted by the engagement, a union long foreseen by watchful relatives and carefully ratified in family councils. She examined the engagement ring—a large, thick sapphire set in invisible claws—with unqualified admiration.

"It's the new setting," Mrs. Welland explained, offering a conciliatory glance at her future son-in-law. "Of course, it shows the stone beautifully, but it looks a little bare to old-fashioned eyes."

"Old-fashioned eyes?" Mrs. Mingott asked, lifting the stone to her small, bright orbs, which required no glasses. "I hope you don't mean mine, my dear? I adore all novelties."

"Very handsome," she added, returning the jewel. "Very liberal. In my time, a cameo set in pearls was considered sufficient. But it is the hand that sets off the ring, isn't it, my dear Mr. Archer?" She waved one of her tiny hands, the small pointed nails and rolls of aged fat encircling her wrist like ivory bracelets. "Mine was modeled in Rome by the great Ferrigiani. "Oh—" Mrs. Welland murmured.

The young man, smiling at his betrothed, replied, "As soon as ever it can, if only you'll back me up, Mrs. Mingott."

"We must give them time to get to know each other a little better, mamma," Mrs. Welland interposed, adopting the proper affectation of reluctance.

Don't wait until the bubble pops off the wine. Marry them before Lent; I may catch pneumonia any winter now, and I want to give the wedding breakfast."

These declarations were received with the customary expressions of amusement, incredulity, and gratitude. As the visit was breaking up in a vein of mild pleasantry, the door opened to admit the Countess Olenska, entering in bonnet and mantle, followed unexpectedly by Julius Beaufort.

A cousinly murmur of pleasure rippled among the ladies. Mrs. Mingott extended Ferrigiani's model to the banker. "Ha! Beaufort, this is a rare favor!" She possessed an odd, foreign manner of addressing men by their surnames.

"Thanks. I wish it might happen oftener," the visitor replied in his easy, arrogant tone. "I am generally so tied down; but I met the Countess Ellen in Madison Square, and she was good enough to let me walk home with her."

Now that I have you, I require a good gossip. I heard your ball was magnificent, and I understand you invited Mrs. Lemuel Struthers? Well, I have a curiosity to see the woman myself."

She had momentarily forgotten her relatives, who were drifting out into the hall under Ellen Olenska's guidance. The following evening, Old Mr. Sillerton Jackson arrived to dine with the Archers. Mrs. Archer was a woman of quiet reserve, naturally shrinking from the clamor of society, yet she possessed an insatiable hunger to remain abreast of its movements. Mr. Sillerton Jackson approached the study of his neighbors' lives with the meticulous patience of a collector and the rigorous classification methods of a naturalist. His sister, Miss Sophy Jackson, who resided with him and was courted by everyone else deemed unworthy of securing her highly prized brother, supplemented his findings with fragments of minor gossip that neatly filled the gaps in his narrative. Consequently, whenever Mrs. Archer desired information on current events, she extended an invitation to Mr. Jackson. Since she honored very few with such requests, and since she and her daughter, Janey, constituted an ideal audience, Mr. Jackson invariably attended personally rather than dispatching his sister. Had he the power to dictate terms, he would have selected evenings when Newland was absent—not because the young man was disagreeable to him (they were fast friends at the club)—but because the old storyteller occasionally sensed in Newland a propensity to scrutinize his anecdotes with a critical weightiness that the ladies of the household rarely displayed. Furthermore, if perfection were achievable on Earth, Mr. Jackson would have requested that Mrs. Archer's culinary offerings be somewhat superior. However, New York, as far back as human history could trace, had bifurcated into two fundamental factions: the Mingotts and Mansons and their clans, obsessed with gastronomy, fashion, and finance; and the Archer-Newland-van der Luyden tribe, devoted to travel, horticulture, and high literature, who regarded coarse pleasures with disdain. One cannot have everything, after all. Dining with the Lovell Mingotts promised canvas-back ducks, terrapin, and vintage wines; conversing at Adeline Archer's offered discussions of Alpine vistas and *The Marble Faun*; fortunately, the Archer Madeira had circumnavigated the Cape. Thus, when summoned by Mrs. Archer, Mr. Jackson, a true eclectics, would typically remark to his sister, "I've suffered a bit of gout since my last repast at the Lovell Mingotts'; it will do me good to diet at Adeline's."

Mrs. Archer, a widow for many years, resided with her son and daughter in West Twenty-Eighth Street. An upper floor was exclusively dedicated to Newland, while the two women compressed themselves into narrower quarters below. In a harmonious alignment of tastes and interests, they cultivated ferns within Wardian cases, crafted macramé lace and wool embroidery on linen, amassed collections of American Revolutionary glazed ware, subscribed to *Good Words*, and devoured Ouida's novels for the atmospheric quality of their Italian settings. (They favored tales of peasant life for their scenic descriptions and wholesome sentiments, though generally preferring stories of society where motives and habits were transparent; they criticized Dickens harshly for failing to capture the essence of a gentleman, and viewed Thackeray as less comfortable in the grand world than Bulwer—though Bulwer himself was beginning to seem antiquated.) Both Mrs. and Miss Archer were ardent admirers of scenery, the primary pursuit of their occasional European excursions, regarding architecture and painting as pursuits reserved for men, particularly scholars versed in Ruskin.

Mrs. Archer was born a Newland, and mother and daughter, resembling each other as sisters, were universally acknowledged as "true Newlands": tall, pale, and slightly stooped, possessing long noses, sweet smiles, and a specific drooping dignity reminiscent of certain faded Reynolds portraits. Their physical similarity would have been absolute had not an elderly robustness strained Mrs. Archer's black brocade, whereas Miss Archer's brown and purple poplins grew increasingly loose upon her youthful frame as the years progressed. Mentally, however, the resemblance was less complete than their identical mannerisms suggested. Years of living in mutually dependent intimacy had gifted them with the same vocabulary and the shared habit of prefacing opinions with "Mother thinks" or "Janey thinks," depending on which party wished to assert dominance. Yet, in truth, while Mrs. Archer's serene unimaginativeness found comfort in the accepted and familiar, Janey was prone to sudden flights of fancy springing from wells of suppressed romance. Mother and daughter adored one another and revered their son and brother; Archer loved them with a tenderness rendered compunctious and uncritical by their exaggerated admiration, bolstered by his secret satisfaction in it. Ultimately, he believed it beneficial for a man to have his authority respected within his own home, even if his sense of humor occasionally led him to question the strength of his command.

That evening, once Mr. Jackson had departed and the ladies had retreated to their chintz-draped bedrooms, Newland Archer climbed the stairs to his private study. As always, a watchful hand had tended the fire and trimmed the lamp. The room, lined with endless rows of books, adorned with bronze and steel figurines of "The Fencers" on the mantel, and filled with reproductions of masterpieces, seemed uniquely warm and inviting.

As he sank into his leather armchair before the hearth, his gaze landed on a large portrait of May Welland—a gift from her in the early days of their courtship. It now dominated the table, pushing aside all other images. With a fresh wave of reverence, he studied the open brow, the earnest eyes, and the cheerful, innocent smile of the young woman destined to be the guardian of his soul. Yet, staring back at him through May's familiar face was something terrifying: the product of the rigid social system he both inhabited and revered. This was the girl who knew nothing and expected everything. Suddenly, marriage ceased to appear as the safe harbor he had been taught to believe in; instead, it revealed itself as a voyage across uncharted waters.

The scandal surrounding the Countess Olenska had agitated his settled beliefs, setting them adrift in dangerous currents. His own earlier declaration—"Women should be free, as free as we are"—struck deep roots in a problem his world conveniently ignored. In polite society, "nice" women, regardless of their suffering, never claimed the liberty he envisioned; consequently, generous men like himself were all too eager to grant it verbally, masking their true adherence to convention with chivalric rhetoric. In truth, these words were merely a disguise for the iron laws binding everyone to the old patterns.

Here, however, he found himself pledged to defend, on behalf of his fiancée's cousin, behavior that, if committed by his own wife, would invite the full wrath of Church and State. Of course, the dilemma was theoretical; since he was not a reckless Polish nobleman, speculating on his wife's rights in such a scenario was absurd. Yet, Newland Archer was too imaginative to ignore the possibility that the bond between him and May might become a source of pain for reasons far more subtle than mere infidelity. How much did they truly know of one another? It was his duty as a "gentleman" to hide his past from her, and hers, as a marriageable maiden, to possess no past worth hiding. What if, driven by quieter, deeper forces, they grew weary of each other, misunderstood, or irritated?

He surveyed the marriages of his friends—the ones deemed happily united—and found none that resembled the passionate, tender companionship he dreamed of sharing with May. He realized that such an ideal required experience, adaptability, and independent judgment—qualities she had been meticulously trained *not* to possess. With a chill of dread, he foresaw his union devolving into what surrounded him: a dull alliance of material and social interests, cemented by ignorance on one side and hypocrisy on the other. Lawrence Lefferts came to mind as the husband who had perfected this enviable ideal. True to his role as the high priest of form, he had molded his wife entirely to his convenience. Even during his notorious affairs with other men's wives, she wandered about in a blissful unawareness, claiming, "Lawrence is so frightfully strict." She had even blushed indignantly when anyone hinted that Julius Beaufort, a "foreigner" of dubious lineage, maintained "another establishment."

Archer tried to comfort himself with the notion that he was not quite as foolish as Larry Lefferts, nor May as simple as poor Gertrude Lefferts. But the distinction was merely one of intellect, not of moral standard. In reality, they all existed within a kind of hieroglyphic world where the truth was never spoken, acted, or even thought, but only represented by arbitrary symbols. Just as Mrs. Welland, knowing precisely why Archer had urged her to announce May's engagement at the Beaufort ball (and expecting nothing less), felt compelled to feign reluctance and pretend she had been forced into compliance, so too did the savage bride in primitive tales scream as she was dragged from her parents' tent.

The result was that the young girl at the center of this elaborate charade became even more enigmatic because of her sheer openness. She was frank, poor darling, because she had nothing to hide; she was assured because she knew of nothing to fear. Armed with nothing more substantial than this, she was about to be plunged overnight into what people euphemistically termed "the facts of life."

Newland loved her sincerely, though placidly. He adored her radiant beauty, her health, her horsemanship, her grace, and her growing interest in literature and ideas under his tutelage. (She had progressed far enough to mock the *Idylls of the King*, though not yet to appreciate the depth of *Ulysses* or the *Lotus Eaters*.) She was straightforward, loyal, and brave, possessing a sense of humor proven chiefly by her laughter at *his* jokes. He sensed, in the depths of her innocently gazing soul, a warmth waiting to be awakened.

Yet, after circling her briefly, he returned discouraged. All this frankness and innocence appeared to him as an artificial construct. Untamed human nature was rarely so open; it was usually bristling with the twists and defenses of instinctive guile. He felt oppressed by this manufactured purity, so cunningly crafted by a conspiracy of mothers, aunts, grandmothers, and long-dead ancestors, simply because it was what he desired, what he felt entitled to demand, so that he might enjoy the lordly pleasure of shattering it like a statue made of snow.

These reflections carried a certain cliché, common to young men approaching their wedding day. Usually, they were accompanied by a sense of guilt and humility, feelings absent in Newland Archer. He could not lament, as Thackeray's heroes often did, that he lacked a blank slate to offer his bride in exchange for her unblemished one. He could not escape the realization that if he had been raised as she had, they would have been equally lost, no more capable of navigating life than the Babes in the Wood. Nor could he, despite his anxious rumination, find any honest reason—aside from his own fleeting pleasure and masculine vanity—why his bride should not have been granted the same freedom of experience as himself.

Such questions drifted through his mind inevitably at such an hour, but he recognized that their uncomfortable persistence stemmed from the untimely arrival of the Countess Olenska. Here he was, at the precise moment of his betrothal—a time for pure thoughts and cloudless hopes—suddenly thrust into a vortex of scandal that raised every issue he had hoped to avoid.

"Hang Ellen Olenska!" he muttered, covering the fire and beginning to undress. He could not fully grasp why her fate should concern him, yet he dimly sensed he had only just begun to measure the risks inherent in the championship his engagement demanded.

In New York, there was a universal consensus: the Countess Olenska had lost her looks.

She had first arrived in the city during Newland Archer's boyhood, a dazzlingly pretty child of nine or ten, about whom everyone whispered that she "ought to be painted." Her parents were continental wanderers; after a nomadic infancy, she had lost them both and fallen into the care of her aunt, Medora Manson. Medora, too, was a wanderer, perpetually returning to New York to "settle down"—though each return saw her in a less expensive house, accompanied either by a new husband or an adopted child. Within a few months, however, she invariably parted ways with her spouse or quarreled with her ward, sold her residence at a loss, and set sail once more. Given that her mother was a Rushworth and her final, wretched marriage had bound her to one of the erratic Chiverses, New York tolerated her eccentricities with indulgence. Yet, when she brought back her little orphaned niece—whose parents had been beloved despite their unfortunate penchant for travel—the public sighed that such a lovely child was in such hands.

Everyone felt inclined to be kind to little Ellen Mingott, although her dusky red cheeks and tight curls bestowed upon her an air of gaiety ill-suited to a child who should still be dressed in black for her deceased parents. One of Aunt Medora's many misguided peculiarities was her defiance of the immutable laws governing American mourning. When she disembarked from the steamer, the family was scandalized to find that the crape veil she wore for her own brother was seven inches shorter than those of her sisters-in-law, while little Ellen appeared in crimson merino and amber beads, resembling a gypsy foundling. But New York had long since resigned itself to Medora's whims; only a handful of old ladies shook their heads over Ellen's garish attire, while her other relatives were charmed by her high color and high spirits. She was a fearless, familiar little creature who asked disconcerting questions, offered precocious commentary, and displayed exotic talents, such as dancing the Spanish shawl dance and singing Neapolitan love songs to a guitar.

Under the guidance of her aunt—who bore the real name of Mrs. Thorley Chivers but, having acquired a papal title, reverted to her first husband's surname to call herself the Marchioness Manson, noting that in Italy she could transform it into Manzoni—the girl received an expensive yet incoherent education. This curriculum included "drawing from the model," a concept previously unheard of, and playing the piano in quintets alongside professional musicians. Naturally, nothing good could result from such chaos. A few years later, when poor Chivers finally perished in a madhouse, his widow (clad in bizarre weeds) once again packed her bags and departed with Ellen, who had now matured into a tall, bony girl with strikingly conspicuous eyes.

For a time, silence reigned regarding them. Then word spread of Ellen's marriage to an immensely wealthy Polish nobleman of legendary renown, whom she had encountered at a ball in the Tuileries. He was rumored to possess princely estates in Paris, Nice, and Florence, a yacht at Cowes, and countless acres of hunting grounds in Transylvania. She vanished in a sort of sulfurous apotheosis. Years later, when Medora returned to New York, humbled, impoverished, mourning a third husband, and seeking yet another smaller dwelling, people marveled that her affluent niece had not managed to assist her. Finally came the news that Ellen's own marriage had ended in catastrophe, and she was returning home to seek solace and oblivion among her kin.

These events drifted through Newland Archer's mind a week later as he watched the Countess Olenska enter the van der Luyden drawing-room on the evening of the fateful dinner. The occasion was solemn, and he wondered anxiously how she would navigate it. She arrived somewhat late, one hand still bare, busy fastening a bracelet around her wrist. Yet, she entered the drawing-room, where New York's most select society was gathered with palpable awe, showing no sign of haste or embarrassment.

Pausing in the center of the room, she surveyed her surroundings with a grave mouth and smiling eyes. In that single instant, Newland Archer dismissed the general verdict concerning her appearance. It was undeniable that her early radiance had faded. The red cheeks had pale; she was thin, worn, and appeared slightly older than her actual age, which must have been nearly thirty. However, she possessed a mysterious authority of beauty—a certainty in the carriage of her head, a fluidity in the movement of her eyes—that, far from being theatrical, struck him as highly cultivated and imbued with conscious power. Simultaneously, she was simpler in manner than most of the women present, and many (as he learned later from Janey) were disappointed that her appearance lacked sufficient "style," for style was what New York prized above all. Perhaps, Archer mused, this was because her early vivacity had vanished; she was so quiet—in her movements, her voice, and the low, resonant tones of her speech. New York had anticipated something far more vocal from a young woman with such a dramatic history.

The dinner proved to be a formidable affair. Dining with the van der Luydens was, at best, no light undertaking, and doing so with a Duke who happened to be their cousin approached the status of a religious solemnity. Archer took pleasure in noting that only an old New Yorker could discern the subtle distinction (in New York society) between being merely a Duke and being *the* van der Luydens' Duke. New York accepted stray nobles calmly, and even (save for the Struthers set) with a certain distrustful hauteur; but when presented with credentials of this magnitude, they were welcomed with an old-fashioned cordiality that they would be greatly mistaken in attributing solely to their standing in Debrett's. It was precisely for such distinctions that the young man cherished his old New York, even as he smiled at its quirks.

The van der Luydens had done their utmost to emphasize the gravity of the event. The Du Lac Sevres porcelain and the Trevenna George II plate were laid out; so was the van der Luyden "Lowestoft" (from the East India Company) and the Dagonet Crown Derby. Mrs. van der Luyden looked more than ever like a painting by Cabanel, while Mrs. Archer, adorned in her grandmother's seed pearls and emeralds, resembled an Isabey miniature. All the ladies wore their finest jewels, yet it was characteristic of the household and the occasion that these were set in rather heavy, old-fashioned mounts. Even old Miss Lanning, who had been reluctantly persuaded to attend, wore her mother's cameos and a Spanish blonde shawl.

The Countess Olenska was the only young woman at the dinner. Yet, as Archer scanned the smooth, plump, elderly faces framed by diamond necklaces and towering ostrich feathers, they struck him as curiously immature compared to hers. The Duke of St. Austrey, seated to the hostess's right, was naturally the chief figure of the evening. However, if the Countess Olenska was less conspicuous than hoped, the Duke was almost invisible. Being a well-bred man, he had not (unlike another recent ducal visitor) arrived in a shooting jacket; yet his evening wear was so shabby and baggy, worn with such an air of rustic simplicity, that combined with his stooped posture and the vast beard spilling over his shirt front, he barely appeared to be in dinner attire. He was short, round-shouldered, sunburnt, with a thick nose, small eyes, and a sociable smile. Nevertheless, he rarely spoke, and when he did, his low tones were lost to everyone except his neighbors, despite the frequent silences of expectation at the table.

When the men joined the ladies after dinner, the Duke immediately approached the Countess Olenska. They retreated to a corner and plunged into animated conversation. Mrs. Archer and Janey had lived up to this principle with unflinching rigidity during their European tours, meeting the friendly advances of fellow travelers with an air of impenetrable reserve. Even their own countrymen, unless previously known or properly accredited, were treated with a pronounced disdain. Unless they ran across a Chivers, a Dagonet, or a Mingott, their months abroad were spent in an unbroken *tête-à-tête*.

Yet, the utmost precautions are sometimes unavailing. One night at Botzen, one of the two English ladies in the room across the passage—whose names, dress, and social situation were already intimately known to Janey—knocked on the door and asked if Mrs. Archer had a bottle of liniment. The other lady, the intruder's sister, Mrs. Carfry, had been seized with a sudden attack of bronchitis. Mrs. Archer, who never traveled without a complete family pharmacy, was fortunately able to produce the required remedy.

Mrs. Carfry was very ill. Since she and her sister, Miss Harle, were traveling alone, they were profoundly grateful to the Archer ladies, who supplied them with ingenious comforts and whose efficient maid helped nurse the invalid back to health. When the Archers left Botzen, they had no idea of ever seeing Mrs. Carfry and Miss Harle again. Nothing, to Mrs. Archer's mind, would have been more "undignified" than to force oneself upon the notice of a "foreigner" to whom one had happened to render an accidental service. But Mrs. Carfry and her sister, to whom this point of view was unknown and who would have found it utterly incomprehensible, felt themselves linked by an eternal gratitude to the "delightful Americans" who had been so kind at Botzen.

With touching fidelity, they seized every chance of meeting Mrs. Archer and Janey during their continental travels, displaying a supernatural acuteness in finding out when they were to pass through London on their way to or from the States. The intimacy became indissoluble. Whenever the Archers alighted at Brown's Hotel, they found themselves awaited by two affectionate friends who, like themselves, cultivated ferns in Wardian cases, made macramé lace, read the memoirs of the Baroness Bunsen, and held similar views about the occupants of the leading London pulpits. As Mrs. Archer said, it made "another thing of London" to know Mrs. Carfry and Miss Harle. By the time Newland became engaged, the tie between the families was so firmly established that it was thought "only right" to send a wedding invitation to the two English ladies, who sent, in return, a pretty bouquet of pressed Alpine flowers under glass.

And on the dock, when Newland and his wife sailed for England, Mrs. Archer's last word had been: "You must take May to see Mrs. Carfry." Newland and his wife had had no idea of obeying this injunction; but Mrs. Carfry, with her usual acuteness, had run them down and sent them an invitation to dine. They had not gone to the Italian Lakes; on reflection, Archer had not been able to picture his wife in that particular setting. Her own inclination (after a month with the Paris dressmakers) was for mountaineering in July and swimming in August. This plan they punctually fulfilled, spending July at Interlaken and Grindelwald, and August at a little place called Étretat, on the Normandy coast, which someone had recommended as quaint and quiet.

Once or twice, in the mountains, Archer had pointed southward and said: "There's Italy." May, her feet in a gentian-bed, had smiled cheerfully and replied: "It would be lovely to go there next winter, if only you didn't have to be in New York." And when they finally got back to London (where they were to spend a fortnight while he ordered *his* clothes), she no longer concealed the eagerness with which she looked forward to sailing.

In London, nothing interested her but the theatres and the shops. She found the theatres less exciting than the Paris *cafés chantants*, where, under the blossoming horse-chestnuts of the Champs Élysées, she had had the novel experience of looking down from the restaurant terrace on an audience of "cocottes," and having her husband interpret to her as much of the songs as he thought suitable for bridal ears.

Archer had reverted to all his old inherited ideas about marriage. It was less trouble to conform with the tradition and treat May exactly as all his friends treated their wives than to try to put into practice the theories with which his untrammeled bachelorhood had dallied. There was no use in trying to emancipate a wife who had not the dimmest notion that she was not free; and he had long since discovered that May's only use of the liberty she supposed herself to possess would be to lay it on the altar of her wifely adoration. Whatever happened, he knew, she would always be loyal, gallant, and unresentful; and that pledged him to the practice of the same virtues. By early November, this domestic ritual concluded, leaving society free to survey itself. By the fifteenth, the season roared into full force; opera houses unveiled new stars, theaters offered fresh attractions, dinner invitations piled up, and dance schedules were finalized. In Archer's youth, awaiting this annual verdict from his mother had been a favorite amusement. He would listen as she enumerated the minute signs of decay that his casual observation had missed. To Mrs. Archer, New York never evolved without deteriorating. Miss Sophy Jackson wholeheartedly shared this sentiment. It was easier, and less dastardly on the whole, for a wife to play such a part toward her husband. A woman's standard of truthfulness was tacitly held to be lower; she was the subject creature, versed in the arts of the enslaved. He had just returned from a grand official reception celebrating the opening of the new galleries at the Metropolitan Museum. The sight of those vast halls, teeming with the spoils of centuries, where fashionable crowds drifted through rows of scientifically cataloged treasures, had suddenly triggered a dormant spring in his memory.

"Why, this used to be one of the old Cesnola rooms," someone had remarked nearby. Instantly, the present dissolved. It was the sanctuary where the true events of his life unfolded. Here, nearly twenty-six years prior, his wife had broken the news of her pregnancy to him—a blush-inducing circumlocution that would have amused the women of the younger generation. Here, their eldest son, Dallas—too frail for winter church services—had been christened by their old friend, the Bishop of New York, the ample, magnificent, and irreplaceable clergyman who had long been the pride of his diocese. It was in this library that he and May had always debated the future of their children: Dallas's and his younger brother Bill's studies, Mary's incurable disdain for "accomplishments" and her fervor for sports and charity, and the vague inclination toward "art" that eventually guided the restless, curious Dallas into the office of a rising New York architect.

Young men today were liberating themselves from the rigid paths of law and commerce, embracing a myriad of new pursuits. Unless absorbed in state politics or municipal reform, they were likely venturing into Central American archaeology, architecture, or landscape engineering; developing a keen, scholarly interest in the pre-revolutionary structures of their own nation, studying and adapting Georgian styles, and protesting the meaningless application of the term "Colonial." Nowadays, only suburban millionaire grocers possessed "Colonial" homes.

"Men like you..." How Archer had burned with pride at those words! How eagerly he had risen to the call! It echoed Ned Winsett's ancient plea to roll up his sleeves and dive into the mud; but spoken by a man who set the example, whose summons to follow was irresistible.

Looking back, Archer was uncertain whether men like himself truly constituted what his country needed, particularly in the active service Theodore Roosevelt had championed. Indeed, there was cause for doubt; after a single term in the State Assembly, he had failed to secure re-election and retreated gratefully into obscure, albeit useful, municipal work, and thence to writing occasional articles for one of the reformist weeklies striving to shake the nation from its apathy. It was little to boast of; yet, remembering the narrow groove of money-making, sport, and society to which the young men of his generation and circle had looked forward, even his modest contribution to this new era seemed significant, like each brick in a well-constructed wall. He had achieved little in public life; by nature, he would remain a contemplator and a dilettante. Yet he had contemplated high things, delighted in great things, and possessed one great man's friendship as his strength and pride.

In short, he had become what people were beginning to term "a good citizen." In New York, for many years, every new movement—philanthropic, municipal, or artistic—had sought his counsel and desired his name attached. People said, "Ask Archer," when questions arose regarding the establishment of the first school for crippled children, the reorganization of the Museum of Art, the founding of the Grolier Club, the inauguration of the new Library, or the creation of a new chamber music society. His days were full, filled decently. He presumed this was all a man ought to aspire to.

Thus, having rescued little Bill from the grave and dedicated her life to the effort, she departed contentedly to her resting place in the Archer vault at St. Mark's, where Mrs. Archer already lay safely beyond the terrifying "trend" her daughter-in-law had never even perceived.

Opposite May's portrait stood one of her daughter. Mary Chivers was as tall and fair as her mother, yet large-waisted, flat-chested, and slightly slouching, as the altered fashions demanded. Mary Chivers's monumental athletic feats could not have been executed with the twenty-inch waist that May Archer's azure sash so effortlessly encircled. The disparity seemed symbolic; the mother's life had been as tightly cinched as her figure. Mary, though no less conventional and no more intelligent, lived a broader life and held more tolerant views. There was goodness in the new order, too.

The telephone clicked, and Archer, turning from the photographs, unhooked the receiver at his side. How distant they were from the days when the legs of the brass-buttoned messenger boy served as New York's sole means of rapid communication!

"Chicago wants you."

Ah—it must be a long-distance call from Dallas, who had been dispatched to Chicago by his firm to discuss plans for the Lakeside palace they intended to build for a young millionaire with ambitious ideas. The firm invariably sent Dallas on such missions.

"Hallo, Dad—Yes: Dallas. I say—how do you feel about sailing on Wednesday? Mauretania: Yes, next Wednesday as ever is. Our client wishes me to inspect some Italian gardens before finalizing anything, and has requested I nip over on the next boat. I must be back by the first of June—" the voice fractured into a joyful, self-conscious laugh— "so we must look lively. I say, Dad, I need your help: please come."
