"Festina lente"—speed tempered with delay.

Gwendolen had spent her time abroad in the electric haze of gambling, imagining herself an empress of fortune. She carried from these experiences a vague, modern conviction that in this chaotic world, actions held no inherent meaning; they were merely distractions for amusement. Alongside her, a mysterious entity known as Grapnell & Co., equally obsessed with ruling the realm of luck and seeking diversion, had orchestrated a painful shift in her family's circumstances. Consequently, she had returned home, dragging with her, against her will, a necklace she had pawned and which someone else had redeemed.

As she traveled back to England, Grandcourt was moving to intercept her. Yet, he approached this pursuit in his own peculiar fashion. He did not rush via express train from Diplow to Leubronn, where she was believed to be. Instead, he lingered at Baden-Baden, indulging in appointments with Russian acquaintances, only to break them later when his desire to reach Leubronn became urgent. Grandcourt's passions were intermittent and flickering; they rarely burned with intense flame. Yet, a vast amount of life proceeds without such fervor: countless cravats are tied with precision, dinners attended, and toasts offered to august figures, all devoid of the zest born of deep desire. A man can maintain a formidable appearance in high society—feigned erudition, scientific reserves, political opinions, and the sentiments of the English gentleman—all at a minimal cost of vital energy. He can remain obstinate or persistent at a low rate, displaying sudden impulses that falsely mimic demonic strength because they seem inexplicable. Often, however, their secret lies merely in a lack of regulated channels for the soul; without the ducts of habit, human nature turns to ooze and mud, yielding nothing but a spurt or a puddle under pressure.

Grandcourt was not entirely displeased by Gwendolen's flight from the splendid chance he had held out to her. The act possessed a certain piquancy. He liked to believe it stemmed from resentment regarding his careless behavior at Cardell Chase, which, upon reflection, did seem rather cool. To bring her so close to a tender admission, only to watch her walk headlong away from further opportunities of securing her consent, was enough to provoke a girl of spirit. And to be worth mastering, she ought to possess some spirit. Doubtless she expected him to follow, and he expected the same. Yet, for a full week, he took no measures to start, nor did he inquire where Miss Harleth had gone. Mr. Lush felt a triumph mixed with deep distrust; Grandcourt had said nothing to him about her, appearing as neutral as an alligator. One could never predict what might surface in the slowly churning depths of his mind. Still, delaying a decision allowed for the wasteful dissipation of Grandcourt's energy.

The guests at Diplow felt more curiosity than their host. How was it that nothing more was heard of Miss Harleth? Was it credible that she had refused Mr. Grandcourt? Lady Flora Hollis, a lively middle-aged woman well-endowed with curiosity, felt a sudden urge to make a round of calls with Mrs. Torrington, including the rectory, Offendene, and Quetcham. Through this tour, she not only doubled her efforts but also gleaned from the Arrowpoints that Miss Harleth had gone to Leubronn with old friends, the Baron and Baroness von Langen. The immediate agitation and disappointment of Mrs. Davilow and the Gascoignes had resolved itself into a collective wish that Gwendolen's disappearance should not be interpreted as eccentric or something requiring secrecy. The rector's mind, indeed, entertained the possibility that the marriage was merely deferred, for Mrs. Davilow had not dared to tell him of the bitter determination with which Gwendolen had spoken. Despite his practical ability, some of his experience had hardened into maxims and quotations. Amaryllis fleeing desired her hiding place to be known; and that love will find the way "over the mountain and over the wave" may be said without hyperbole in this age of steam. Gwendolen, he conceived, was an Amaryllis of excellent sense but coquettish daring; the question remained whether she had dared too much.

Lady Flora, returning charged with news about Miss Harleth, saw no reason why she should not attempt to electrify Mr. Grandcourt by mentioning it at the table. In doing so, she dropped a few hints of a rumor that he was a disappointed adorer. Grandcourt heard with quietude but with keen attention. The next day, he ordered Lush to arrange a decent pretext for breaking up the party at Diplow within another week, claiming he intended to go yachting to the Baltic or elsewhere. It was impossible to stay at Diplow as if he were a prisoner on parole, surrounded by a set of people he had never wanted. Lush needed no clearer announcement that Grandcourt was heading to Leubronn, though he might proceed with the slowness of a creeping billiard ball, sticking to the path. What Mr. Lush intended was to render himself indispensable so that he might accompany his patron; he succeeded. Gwendolen's repulsion for him was a fact that only amused his patron, making him none the less willing to keep Lush always at hand.

Thus, it came to pass that Grandcourt arrived at the *Czarina* on the fifth day after Gwendolen had left Leubronn, finding there his uncle, Sir Hugo Mallinger, with his family, including Deronda. It is not necessarily a pleasure, either to the reigning power or the heir presumptive, when their separate affairs—a touch of gout in the one, a touch of willfulness in the other—bring them to the same spot. Sir Hugo was an easy-tempered man, tolerant of differences and defects. However, a point of view differing from his own concerning the settlement of the family estates fretted him more than issues of Church discipline or the ballot. Faults were less venial when they belonged to a person whose existence was inconvenient to him. In no case could Grandcourt have been a nephew after his own heart; yet, as the presumptive heir to the Mallinger estates, he was the sign and embodiment of a chief grievance in the baronet's life: the want of a son to inherit the lands, in no portion of which had Sir Hugo more than a life-interest. For in the ill-advised settlement chosen by his father, Sir Francis, even Diplow with its modicum of land had been left under the same conditions as the ancient and wide inheritance of the two Toppings—Diplow, where Sir Hugo had lived and hunted through many seasons in his youth, and where his wife and daughters ought to have been able to retire after his death.

This grievance had naturally gathered emphasis as the years advanced. Lady Mallinger, after bearing three daughters in quick succession, had remained childless for eight years until now, when she was over forty, without producing another girl. Sir Hugo, almost twenty years older, was at a time of life when, notwithstanding the fashionable retardation of everything from dinners to marriages, a man's hopefulness shows signs of wear, until restored by second childhood.

In fact, he had begun to despair of a son. This confirmation of Grandcourt's interest in the estates certainly made his image and presence more unwelcome; yet, on the other hand, it carried circumstances that disposed Sir Hugo to ensure their relationship remained as friendly as possible. It led him to dwell on a plan that had grown alongside his disappointment: to try and secure Diplow as a future residence for Lady Mallinger and her daughters, keeping this pretty bit of family inheritance for his own offspring despite the lack of an heir. His knowledge of his nephew's disposition and affairs encouraged the belief that Grandcourt might consent to a transaction granting him a good sum of ready money as an equivalent for his prospective interest in the domain of Diplow and the moderate land attached to it. If, after all, the unhoped-for son should be born, the money would have been wasted, and Grandcourt would have been paid for giving up interests that turned out to be worthless. But Sir Hugo set this risk as *nil*, and of late years he had husbanded his fortune so well through mining and lease sales that he was prepared for the outlay.

Here was an object that made him careful to avoid any quarrel with Grandcourt. Some years prior, when making improvements at the Abbey and needing Grandcourt's concurrence to fell an obstructive mass of timber on the demesne, he had congratulated himself on finding no active spite in his nephew's peculiar mind. Nothing had since occurred to make them hate each other more than was compatible with perfect politeness or any accommodation that could be strictly mutual.

Grandcourt, on his side, thought his uncle a superfluity and a bore, feeling that the list of things in general would improve whenever Sir Hugo was expunged. But he had been made aware, through Lush, always a useful medium, of the baronet's inclinations concerning Diplow. He was gratified to have the alternative of the money in his mind: even if he did not think it likely he would accept it, his sense of power would be flattered by his ability to refuse what Sir Hugo desired. The hinted transaction had influenced his motives for asking for a year's tenancy of Diplow, which had rather annoyed Sir Hugo to grant, since the excellent hunting in the neighborhood might decide Grandcourt not to part with his chance of future possession—a man who has two places, in one of which the hunting is less good, naturally desiring a third where it is better. Also, Lush had thrown out to Sir Hugo the probability that Grandcourt would woo and win Miss Arrowpoint; in that case, ready money might be less of a temptation. Hence, on this unexpected meeting at Leubronn, the baronet felt much curiosity to know how things had been going on at Diplow, was bent on being as civil as possible to his nephew, and looked forward to some private chat with Lush.

Between Deronda and Grandcourt there existed a more faintly marked but peculiar relation, depending on circumstances yet to be revealed. But on no side was there any sign of suppressed chagrin at their first meeting at the *table d'hôte*, an hour after Grandcourt's arrival. When the quartette of gentlemen afterward met on the terrace, without Lady Mallinger, they moved off together to saunter through the rooms. As they entered the large *saal*, Sir Hugo said to them,

"Did you play much at Baden, Grandcourt?"

"No; I looked on and betted a little with some Russians there."

"Had you luck?"

"What did I win, Lush?"

"You brought away about two hundred," said Lush.

"You are not here for the sake of the play, then?" said Sir Hugo.

"No; I don't care about play now. It's a confounded strain," said Grandcourt, whose diamond ring and demeanor, as he moved along playing slightly with his whisker, were being stared at by rouged foreigners interested in a new milord.

"The fact is, somebody should invent a mill to do amusements for you, my dear fellow," said Sir Hugo, "as the Tartars get their praying done. But I agree with you; I never cared for play. It's monotonous—it knits the brain up into meshes. And it knocks me up to watch it now. I suppose one gets poisoned with the bad air. I never stay here more than ten minutes. But where's your gambling beauty, Deronda? Have you seen her lately?"

"She's gone," said Deronda, curtly.

"An uncommonly fine girl, a perfect Diana," said Sir Hugo, turning to Grandcourt again. "Really worth a little straining to look at her. I saw her winning, and she took it as coolly as if she had known it all beforehand. The same day Deronda happened to see her losing like wildfire, and she bore it with immense pluck. I suppose she was cleaned out, or was wise enough to stop in time. How do you know she's gone?"

"Oh, by the Visitor-list..." said Deronda, with a scarcely perceptible shrug. "Vandernoodt told me her name was Harleth, and she was with the Baron and Baroness von Langen. I saw by the list that Miss Harleth was no longer there."

This held no further information for Lush than that Gwendolen had been gambling. He had already looked at the list and ascertained that Gwendolen had gone, but he had no intention of thrusting this knowledge on Grandcourt before he asked for it; he had not asked, finding it enough to believe that the object of search would turn up somewhere or other.

But now Grandcourt had heard what was rather piquant, and not a word about Miss Harleth had been missed by him. After a moment's pause, he said to Deronda,

"Do you know those people—the Langens?"

"I have talked with them a little since Miss Harleth went away. I knew nothing of them before."

"Where is she gone—do you know?"

"She is gone home," said Deronda, coldly, as if he wished to say no more. But then, from a fresh impulse, he turned to look markedly at Grandcourt and added, "But it is possible you know her. Her home is not far from Diplow: Offendene, near Winchester."

Deronda, turning to look straight at Grandcourt, who was on his left hand, might have been a subject for those old painters who liked contrasts of temperament. There was a calm intensity of life and richness of tint in his face that, on a sudden gaze, was rather startling, often making him seem to have spoken, so that servants and officials asked him automatically, "What did you say, sir?" when he had been quite silent. Grandcourt himself felt an irritation, which he did not show except by a slight movement of the eyelids, at Deronda's turning round on him when he was not asked to do more than speak. But he answered, with his usual drawl, "Yes, I know her," and paused with his shoulder toward Deronda to look at the gambling.
