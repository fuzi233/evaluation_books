Captain Willoughby was a notorious bore at his club. He possessed a singular talent for recounting tedious tales of strangers to an audience composed entirely of his own kind, yet he was utterly immune to the social friction his stories generated. He spoke with a relentless, vacant amiability, crushing conversation beneath the heavy tread of his monologues as easily as a steamroller flattens a road. To the uninitiated, he was irresistible; to those who knew him, he was a plague. Usually, within half an hour of entering the smoking-room, Willoughby would secure a large, empty corner, having driven away every potential listener.

One crisp afternoon in early October, the waiter approached this solitary island of silence with a calling card. Willoughby, craving company despite his usual isolation, accepted it with alacrity. However, his expression darkened instantly upon reading the name: *Colonel Durrance.*

"Durrance!" Willoughby muttered, scratching his chin. "Has he somehow guessed I'm here?"

"He knows, sir," the waiter confirmed.

"Show him in," Willoughby decided, though his internal alarm bells rang. Diplomacy would be required. He had no desire to further entangle himself in Miss Ethne Eustace's affairs; Feversham and Durrance must settle their score alone. Yet, as Durrance entered, the blind man moved with a deliberate grace that unnerved Willoughby. Durrance offered no immediate questions, instead guiding Willoughby to his deserted corner.

"Smoke?" Willoughby asked, checking himself. "I beg your pardon."

"Oh, I'll smoke," Durrance replied, striking a match with deliberation. "A man shouldn't let his cigar go out, not unless he wants to know immediately that it has. I assure you, I shall not let mine out." He leaned back, the ember glowing steadily in the gloom.

"I am fortunate to find you, Willoughby," Durrance continued smoothly. "I am only in town today, visiting my oculist in Devonshire. Mather mentioned you were away in the country last time I was there. Remember Mather? We served together in Suakin."

"Mather?" Willoughby perked up, eager to pivot the conversation away from Durrance's intent gaze. "Yes, perfectly. Tell me about him."

"We are both curious, Willoughby," Durrance said, his tone shifting subtly. "About a certain Arab named Abou Fatma."

"Abou Fatma?" Willoughby hesitated. "One of the Hadendoas?"

"No, a Kabbabish," Durrance corrected gently. "Did you ever encounter him?"

Willoughby stammered, "No... I don't believe I did." It was a lie, born of panic. He had never met the man, though Feversham had spoken of him. The silence stretched, uncomfortable. Durrance pressed on.

"On our last reconnaissance in '84, Mather and I found Abou Fatma hiding near Sinkat Fort," Durrance explained. "He claimed to have hidden the Gordon letters in Berber. The *Evening Standard* reported they arrived in Suakin shortly after. I wonder if he ever retrieved them."

Willoughby exhaled a cloud of smoke, trying to sound casual. "Since Gordon was dead, there was little incentive for anyone to return to Berber. It seems unlikely."

"Precisely," Durrance nodded. "For no obvious reason."

Willoughby felt a wave of relief. The trap was sprung, but he had dodged it. Durrance seemed content, his curiosity sated regarding the letters. Yet, Willoughby sensed a deeper current running beneath the surface. Why was Durrance probing? Was he testing his loyalty? Willoughby decided to steer toward Feversham, hoping to deflect further scrutiny.

"I wanted to see you because of Harry Feversham," Willoughby blurted out, surprising himself. "He's back in the Sudan, isn't he?"

Durrance leaned forward, his blind eyes seemingly fixed on Willoughby. "Indeed. I met him at Wadi Halfa this spring. He has fallen on hard times, Willoughby. Disappeared as if dropped down an oubliette. I urge you to watch for him. Help him regain his footing. He was one of my few true friends."

Willoughby, relieved to have changed the subject, agreed cheerfully. "I'll look out for him. Give me a shout if you need funds."

As Willoughby lit his briar pipe, the flame flickered briefly before catching. "You and I dined with him recently," Durrance mused, recalling the memory vividly. "High up above St. James's Park, before we left England."

At these words, Willoughby's pipe froze mid-light. The ash, black and cold, sat atop the tobacco, untouched. A sudden, violent oath escaped Willoughby's lips, followed by a stamp of his foot on the rug. He stared at the blind man, his face flushed.

"There were you, me, and Feversham," Durrance continued, oblivious to the storm he had stirred. "Feversham announced his engagement to Miss Eustace. Then, a telegram arrived."

"A telegram?" Willoughby asked, his voice tight. "Who sent it?"

"Trench was there," Durrance said casually. "And Castleton was expected, though he didn't arrive. He sent the telegram."

Willoughby's mind raced. The pieces clicked together with terrifying clarity. That dinner, the telegram from Castleton, the resignation that followed—it all aligned. The specific act of cowardice, the one that merited the four white feathers, was revealed. Castleton, dining with a high official from the War Office, had likely telegraphed news of Feversham's impending transfer to active service. Feversham, terrified, had crumpled the message and thrown it into the fire, resigning his commission moments later, playing the coward when every fiber of his nature should have been rallying.

Durrance smiled to himself, the puzzle solved. The mystery of Feversham's panic was clear: fear of the unknown duty ahead. The atonement, however, would be immense.

"I shall astonish old Sutch," Durrance chuckled softly. "Now, I take the night mail for Devonshire."

As the colonel departed, Willoughby watched him leave, a mixture of shame and resolve settling over him. He returned to the smoking-room, ordering a whiskey and potass, the weight of the revelation heavy in his chest. The game was up, and the path to redemption, however difficult, was finally laid bare.
