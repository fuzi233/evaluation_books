The wintry days went over Romola as white-laden ships glide past a solitary watcher on the shore—each alike in its muffled pace, each freighted with some unseen change. After Tito’s half-spoken warning, dread had spoiled her curiosity; she began to choose ignorance as one chooses a darker room when the light shows too plainly what one fears to see. The German Emperor had withdrawn again; the sharpest peril to Florence seemed eased; yet the city’s hunger and cold did not withdraw with him. Distress still sat in doorways and under arches, and Romola’s hands were still needed—measuring out help, carrying messages, soothing the sick, arranging bread and broth with the quiet exactness of one who knows that nourishment is not an idea but a warmth and a smell and a strength returning to the limbs. In such tasks her mind found refuge from suspicion.

She dared not rejoice that the relief which had come at the last moment—relief that many declared a sign of Heaven’s favour on the Frate’s party—was making that party triumphant. Francesco Valori, fiery chief of the Piagnoni, had been chosen Gonfaloniere at the year’s beginning and was pushing, within his two months, to do as much as his ardour called “liberal” and his enemies called “tyrannical.” For a little while it looked like strength for the defenders of freedom and a shield for Savonarola; yet Romola had grown quick to every omen that darkened her foreboding. The present, whatever its brightness, felt to her like a pot set on embers: within it, mixed seeds of change were warming toward a boil that might scald.

By Carnival time—mid-February, when the air can smell at once of damp stone and the first faint promise of spring—her presentiment took shape. The Mediceans had ceased to be patient; they were openly labouring to secure Bernardo del Nero as the next Gonfaloniere.

On the last day of Carnival, between ten and eleven in the morning, Romola went out as she had promised, towards the Corso degli Albizzi to fetch her cousin Brigida. Both were to start early from the Via de’ Bardi and take their places at a window Tito had reserved in the Piazza della Signoria, where a spectacle was expected so strange, so new, that even Florentine eyes—long accustomed to pageant—burned to see it. The Piagnoni were shaping Carnival in their own image. Dolfo Spini and his companions had fought for the dear old masques, the rough tricks, the songs salted with indecency; but such things were not to live in a city where Christ had been proclaimed king.

Romola set out with the languor that comes upon anyone facing a long day of sight-seeing not from eagerness but from love—when one submits to noise and press and waiting because some child, or some friend with a child’s delight, longs for it. An epoch in Carnival it might be; it stirred no enthusiasm in her. She did not know it was an epoch in her own life too—when another destiny would no longer lie hidden, but would begin to be visibly twisted with hers.

She chose to pass through the great Piazza first, to take her own survey of what was preparing there while she was still alone. Entering from the south, she stopped short. Something monstrous and many-coloured rose before her—a pyramid, or rather a fir-tree made vast: sixty feet high, its “branches” widened into shelves, each tier spreading broader until the base ran to an eighty-yard circumference. The Piazza swarmed. Slender youths in white garments, olive wreaths on their heads, moved to and fro like pale flames tamed into human shapes, carrying baskets filled with bright objects. Men older and heavier—some in monastic frocks, some in the loose tunics and dark-red caps of artists—helped, judged, adjusted, then walked away to view the whole at a distance. On the marble steps of Orgagna’s Loggia stood a knot of watchers who held themselves aloof with discontent and scorn; among them Romola recognized Piero di Cosimo, his rough head set like a weathered stone.

She drew nearer. The tiers were crowded with things arranged as if for a market—only this market was for the fire. Tapestries and brocades with shameless figures; pictures and sculptures condemned as temptations; gaming-boards and tables; playing-cards with the very blocks for printing them; dice and the tools of gambling. Music-books of a worldly kind; instruments—lutes with smooth bellies, drums, cymbals, trumpets—whose very shapes seemed to promise riot and ringing excess. Masks and masquerade garments from old Carnivals. Fine copies of Ovid, Boccaccio, Petrarch, Pulci—books denounced as vain or impure. And among them the small, intimate paraphernalia that cling most obstinately to household life: rouge-pots; coils of false hair; mirrors that catch and flatter; perfumes; powders; veils so thin they were less concealment than invitation. At the summit stood an unflattering effigy of a Venetian merchant—perhaps a creature of rumour more than history—said to have offered a great sum for these marketable abominations; and above him, uglier still, the symbolic figure of old debauched Carnival itself, lifted up as if only to have farther to fall.

Within the pyramid, hidden behind the tiers of “vanities,” lay dry fuel and gunpowder. At evening, on this last day, it was to be lit. Trumpets would sound; the pile would blaze; the grim old Carnival would tumble down into the flames amid songs of reform.

Such a crowning act could scarcely have been brought about without the peculiar organisation Savonarola had begun two years before. Florence’s boyhood and youth were no longer left to their own genial impulse toward street mischief and crude dissoluteness. Under the training of Fra Domenico—Savonarola’s lieutenant—lads and striplings, the city’s hope, were to keep pure words on their lips, to burn with zeal for an Unseen Good that should shame the slackness of their elders, and to know pleasures only of an angelic sort—chanting divine praises, walking in white robes.

It was for them that seats had been raised high against the walls of the Duomo. It was to them Savonarola appealed as the future glory of a city chosen for God’s work. Those fresh-cheeked troops were the chief agents in this regenerated merriment, a sacred parody of the old. Had there been bonfires in former Carnivals? There would be a bonfire now—consuming impurity. Had there been symbolic processions? There would be processions now—but the symbols would be white robes and red crosses and olive wreaths: emblems of peace and innocent gladness; and banners and images held aloft would tell the triumphs of goodness. Had there been dancing in rings under the open sky to choruses of loose song? There would be dancing in rings now—monks and laity clasped in fraternal joy—and the music would be hymns. Even the street collections were to be greater than ever: not for gross and superfluous suppers, but for the hungry and needy. And besides alms, there was the collecting of the Anathema—the objects to be laid on the great bonfire.

Troops of young inquisitors went from house to house on this business, knocking at doors with an excitement that was half piety, half triumph. After the more obvious vanities were surrendered, perhaps the Madonna of a household still kept certain little reddened balls from the Levant—meant to coax a bloom onto a sallow cheek, and with it a false innocence. Let her bring them down, then, and drop them into the basket of doom. Or perhaps she had ringlets and coils of “dead hair.” Let her carry them to the street-door, not on her head but in her hands, and publicly renounce the Anathema that hid respectable age beneath a ghastly mockery of youth. In return she would hear young voices pronounce a blessing on her and her house.

The beardless inquisitors did their work willingly. To coerce by shame—or by that spiritual pelting which stings worse than stones—into surrendering things it will vex one to lose, is a form of “piety” readily learned by boyhood; and if some obstinate man grew furious and threatened whip or cudgel, that too had the spice of danger.

Savonarola himself felt—plainly enough—how difficult it is for noble yearning to see the right means, and how easily the mind, impatient for great ends, leans toward supernatural constraint as the only sure hope. The Florentine youth had foul tongues and evil habits. At first it seemed unmingled blessing that they now shouted “Viva Gesù!” Yet he was forced at last to warn from the pulpit: there was too much shouting; sacred words, repeated as if they were pebbles tossed for sport, would be brought into contempt. Let there be no more of that cry until the next Festa.

Still, the long stream of white-robed youth that went at dawn to the Duomo to receive the communion from Savonarola’s hands had been a sight of beauty; and doubtless many of those young souls stored memories of awe and hope that might one day save them from sinking into a vulgar sense of their work as men and citizens. There is no conscious obedience that does not mark an advance beyond lawlessness. These boys would become the men who fought greatly and endured greatly in the last struggle of their Republic.

Now, in the hours between early communion and dinner-time, they made their final rounds for alms and vanities. That was why Romola had seen the slim white figures moving about the base of the enormous pyramid, their steps soft on stone, their voices rising and falling like the calls of swallows—an aural net spread over the Piazza. In Florence the sound of a thing was never merely sound: it was governance. A chant could soothe a street; a trumpet could turn it into a weapon; and Romola, moving through that tide of voices, felt how the city’s spirit was being tuned—how certain words, shouted and repeated, could become the only permitted language, making other modes of feeling seem like sins because they had no sanctioned name.

“What think you of this folly, Madonna Romola?” said a brusque voice close to her ear.

She turned. Piero di Cosimo had come up beside her, his face set in its habitual grimness.

“Your Piagnoni will make l’inferno a pleasant prospect to us, if they carry things their own way on earth. It’s enough to fetch a cudgel over the mountains to see painters—Lorenzo di Credi, and young Baccio there—helping to burn colour out of life in this fashion.”

“My good Piero,” Romola said, looking up and smiling, “even you must be glad to see some of these things burnt. Look at the gewgaws and wigs and rouge-pots. I have heard you speak against them as angrily as Fra Girolamo himself.”

“What then?” Piero snapped, turning on her as if her mildness were an insult. “I never said a woman should make a black patch of herself against the background. Va! Madonna Antigone—it’s a shame for a woman with your hair and shoulders to run into such nonsense. Leave it to women who are not worth painting. What! the most holy Virgin herself has always been dressed well; that’s doctrine of the Church. Talk of heresy, indeed! And I should like to know what the excellent Messer Bardo would have said to the burning of the divine poets by these Frati, who are no better an imitation of men than if they were onions with the bulbs uppermost. Look—Petrarch sticking up beside a rouge-pot! Do the idiots pretend that the heavenly Laura was a painted harridan? And Boccaccio, now—do you mean to say, Madonna Romola—you who are fit to be a model for a wise Saint Catherine of Egypt—do you mean to say you have never read the stories of the immortal Messer Giovanni?”

“It is true I have read them, Piero,” Romola answered. “Some of them many times, when I was a little girl. I used to take the book down when my father was asleep, so that I could read to myself.”

“Ebbene?” Piero said, fierce as if he were challenging her to confess to pleasure.

“There are things in them I do not want ever to forget,” Romola said; and for a moment the Piazza’s mingled smells—damp wool, cold stone, the faint resin of stacked wood—seemed to sharpen into one clear memory, as if recollection too had an odour. “But you must confess, Piero, a great many of those stories are only about low deceit for the lowest ends. Men do not need books to make them think lightly of vice, as if life were a vulgar joke. And I cannot blame Fra Girolamo for teaching that we owe our time to something better.”

“Yes, yes—it’s very well to say so now you’ve read them,” Piero retorted bitterly. He turned on his heel and strode away, his steps loud on the stone, like a protest that would not be absorbed into the hymn-sounds.

Romola too moved on, smiling at Piero’s innuendo with a tenderness for his anger—because she knew her father would have felt something like it. For herself she felt no inward collision with the strict, sombre view of pleasure that tried to repress poetry while it repressed vice. Sorrow and joy each have their narrowness. A religious ardour like Savonarola’s—an ardour that in the end may bless mankind by thrusting the soul toward sympathy with pain, indignation against wrong, and the subduing of sensual desire—will always draw upon itself the reproach of negation.

Romola’s own life had given her an affinity with sadness that made her unjust toward merriment. That subtle refinement we call Taste was subdued in her by the need for a deeper motive—just as the palate’s niceties vanish before urgent hunger. Moving habitually among the city’s suffering, and carrying in her heart a woman’s heaviest disappointment, she found no dissonance in the severity that allied itself with self-renouncing strength. And yet, as she passed under the mingled murmur of the Piazza—hymns and admonitions, footfalls, the rustle of baskets, the thin calls of boys—she felt, without putting it into words, how much power lay in what was named and what was forbidden to be named; how a city could be ruled not only by laws and swords, but by the very sounds it allowed to live in its streets.
