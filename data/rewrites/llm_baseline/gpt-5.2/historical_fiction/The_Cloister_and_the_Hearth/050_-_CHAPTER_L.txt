Among strangers Margaret Brandt found a kind of peace, as if the very speech about her—strange vowels, new proverbs, different scolding—kept her own grief from being handled too roughly. In Rotterdam she could be merely “the leech’s daughter,” not the woman whose heart had been packed away with a traveller’s chest and sent beyond reach.

Then fortune, that capricious kitchen-maid, flung her a ladleful of comfort.

A civic dignitary, one of those men whose body is ruled by his imagination and whose imagination is ruled by his dignity, had been handed from physician to physician. Each had left a mark: this one had bled him to pallor, that had purged him to weakness; he lay at last like a linen bag that has been rinsed too often—empty of colour, empty of strength, yet still stuffed with complaints.

In desperation they sent for Peter Brandt.

Peter came in, smelt the room—stale sweat, sour possets, sharp vinegar of old cordials—and saw the battalion of bottles ranged like little glass soldiers, each promising salvation and delivering only thirst. His eyes flashed; with an anger that was half pity and half pride he swept the bottles up and sent them flying out of the window. He left the casement open, to let God’s air do what man’s drugs had failed to do.

Then he went to work like a cook who understands that a stomach is not a kettle to be scoured, but a hearth to be coaxed back into warmth.

He took yolks of eggs—fresh, thick as gold paint—beat them patiently, and laced them with neat Schiedam in small, careful measure, as one tempers a sauce so it warms without scorching. In little doses he fed it to the man, watching the swallowing, the blink of relief, the faint return of appetite. After that he gave meat stewed in red wine and water, and into the pot he shredded only mild herbs—febrifugal simples that would not fight the body, only help it. Nothing violent, nothing showy; just nourishment, warmth, and time.

The dignitary rose again. He looked, when first he came out, like something between a man and a pillow-case; but he was alive, and being voluble, he filled every street with Peter’s name. The old doctor, who had long deserved a reputation and been denied it, found it arriving at last—late, noisy, and public. The purse began to fill; the pride, too, which is a kind of hunger, was fed.

Yet Peter spent much of his gain on sovereign herbs and choice drugs, as if his very success made him fear scarcity. He would have poured all their money into jars and packets—roots, resins, powders—had not Margaret, with a wife’s thrift and a daughter’s clear sight, held him to earth and forced a portion into hoard.

The victory came too late. The excitement that should have cheered him killed him by degrees.

One evening, when she kissed him good-night, his voice stumbled; the words came thick, as if the tongue were suddenly a stranger in the mouth.

In the morning he was found speechless, and only just sensible.

Margaret, who had been her father’s attentive pupil for years—watching not only the prescriptions but the pauses, the listening, the laying on of fingers to a wrist—saw at once a paralytic stroke had struck him. Still, she would not trust herself alone; she ran for a doctor.

One of those men whom Peter had blocked from finishing off the civic dignitary came. He confirmed her judgement with a cheerful air, like a man pleased to find himself right. He was for bleeding the patient.

Margaret’s chin lifted. “He was ever against blooding,” said she, “and most of all the old.”

Peter lived; but he did not return. His memory faltered; his mind, once a cupboard of well-labelled simples, became a disorderly shelf where jars stood without names. He could not be trusted to prescribe. Yet patients came all the same—some waiting with stubborn faith for the “new doctor,” refusing any other hand.

Misery stared her in the face with an honest, hungry gaze. There were two men to lodge and feed, and she herself was with child; another mouth was already on its way into the world.

And that helpless mouth, still unbreathed, was her best friend.

Nature in Margaret was strong—the same nature that teaches birds to line nests before the eggs are laid, and beasts to hoard fat against winter. She sat and brooded as a hen broods: warming her fear with thought, thought with fear, till a plan hatched.

Though she had five gold pieces left, she could see starvation, steady as a clock, advancing foot by foot. If she was to be beaten, it would not be for want of craft.

She resolved first to seek counsel and comfort from her cousin William Johnson, whom pride and poverty had kept her from. She went to him, and found him in one room with his servant; and neither of them was the better for liquor. The air stank of spirits gone flat, and of talk that had turned sour.

She mastered her surprise, gave her greetings, and told him she came on a family matter; with that she glanced quietly at the servant—hinting for privacy.

The woman took the hint—and twisted it to a weapon.

“Oh, you can speak before me, can she not, my old man?”

At the familiarity Margaret reddened, and rose at once. “I cry you mercy, mistress. I knew not my cousin had fallen into the custom of this town. I must take fitter opportunity.”

“I wot not what ye mean by custom o’ the town,” the woman cried, bouncing up, “but this I know: ’tis the part of a faithful servant to keep her master from being preyed on by his beggarly kin.”

Margaret’s tongue was sharp when honour was touched. “Ye are too modest, mistress. Ye are no servant. Your speech betrays you. ’Tis not till the ape hath mounted the tree that she shows her tail so plain. Nay, there sits the servant; God help him! And while so it is, fear not thou his kin will ever be so poor in spirit as come where the likes of you can flout their dole.”

She cast one mute look of reproach at her cousin—sitting passive and silent—and went out haughtily. Not a tear fell until she reached home and thought on it. Then she wept, not for her own pride, but for the shame of seeing a man of her blood so tamed.

Now indeed she stood alone: two helpless men and a child-to-come depending on one girl.

She wiped her face, set her jaw, and went to work.

She bade Martin move Peter into another room. She made the front room very neat and clean, scrubbed till the boards gave up their old smells of damp and ash. She polished the glass globe till it shone like a pale moon. She suspended it from the ceiling. She dusted the crocodile—Peter’s odd trophy—and nailed it to the outside wall as a sign, as if a monster’s jaw could keep misfortune at bay.

Then she instructed Martin and set him to lounge like a sentinel by the street door.

When people stopped to stare at the crocodile, Martin was to say a great, aged, learned alchymist dwelt within—one so deep in secrets that curing mortal diseases was but his pastime.

The first patients came. They demanded to see the leech.

“That might not be,” said Margaret, in a voice she took care to keep steady. “He is deep in his studies, searching for the grand elixir. Not princes could have speech of him. Tell me your symptoms, and return in two hours.”

In two hours they returned. Oh, mystery of mysteries: the draught was ready, the powder measured, the potion labelled.

Sometimes, if the patient was worshipful, she studied his face, felt pulse and skin, listened closely, then went softly to Peter’s room. There she stood a while, thinking not only what he had taught her, but how he had chosen between remedies—the way he watched a man’s breathing, the way he distrusted a complaint that did not match the body’s smell. She asked herself, as she had asked a hundred times at his side, what her father’s system would do.

Then she wrote an illegible scrawl with a cabalistic letter, brought it down reverently, showed it the patient. “Can you read that?”

Most answered, “I am no reader,” or, “Nay, mistress, nought can I make on’t,” with admiration.

“Ay, but I can. ’Tis sovereign. Look on thyself as cured!”

If she had the materials, she compounded before them—economical as she was, favouring medicines already in her stock. She would consult the “prescription” anxiously, as if great Science trembled in her hands. And so she sent them away relieved of cash, but with pockets full of medicine, mouths tasting of bitters or honeyed cordials, and minds full of faith.

When they were gone, she took down two little boxes Gerard had made her, long ago—small, neat, beloved. On one she had written To-day, on the other To-morrow. She dropped the smaller coins into “To-day,” the larger into “To-morrow,” along with such gold pieces as had survived her journey and housekeeping. Thus she met current expenses and laid by against the rainy day she saw, sure as cloud.

She mixed drugs with simples, and vice with virtue.

Her conscience pricked her. Each evening, after the day’s comedy, she knelt and begged God’s pardon “for the sake of her child.” But then cures were reported. This neighbour’s fever broke; that burgher’s pain eased; some would have recovered without her, yet they credited her. Gradually her conscience hardened, as hands harden that must work in cold water.

Martin Wittenhaagen had become, of late, a dead weight. Like many men who have endured long hardship, he had stiffened suddenly. Yet he was as strong as ever, and at his own pace could have carried the doctor herself around Rotterdam. He carried instead her slops and errands.

In her new business he showed soldierly qualities: obedience without question, punctuality, accuracy, despatch—and drunkenness.

He fell among “good fellows.” Blackguards plied him with Schiedam. He babbled and bragged. Doctor Margaret rose high in his cups, not as Science but as something grander: Strategy. The crocodile as standard, the dotard hidden in back rooms, the money taken briskly—Martin admired it like a campaign.

And he boasted, before mixed company, how “me and my General we are a biting of the burghers.”

Words, once loosed, have their own legs. The tale walked through the city. Before long constables called.

They took Margaret—trembling, begging them to forgive her—before the burgomaster. By his side stood real physicians, a terrible row, in long robes and square caps, accusing her of practising unlawfully on the duke’s lieges.

At first she could not speak. The very word Law struck her like a cudgel; she felt again the helplessness of a poor girl in a town of strangers. But they questioned her—closely, not cruelly—and at last truth came out as breath comes out of a squeezed lung.

She told them she had long been her father’s pupil; she had followed his system; she had cured many. And then, forgetting caution, she pleaded, and the pleading betrayed her condition.

“It is not for myself in very deed, sirs,” she said, “but I have two poor helpless honest men at home upon my hands, and how else can I keep them? Ah, good sirs, let a poor girl make her bread honestly; ye hinder them not to make it idly and shamefully; and oh, sirs, ye are husbands, ye are fathers; ye cannot but see I have reason to work and provide as best I may—”

Even as the appeal left her lips she would have recalled it. She stood with one hand upon her heart and one before her face, hiding it—yet tears slipped out beneath.

But it went to the wrong address.

“What have we to do with that,” said the burgomaster, “save and except that if thou wilt pledge thyself to break the law no more, I will remit the imprisonment and exact but the fine?”

Margaret clasped her hands and vowed most penitently never, never, never to cure body or beast again. Dismissed with constables to pay the fine, she curtsied at the door and thanked the gentlemen for their forbearance—poor soul, grateful even for a whip laid lightly.

To pay, the To-morrow box must be opened that instant. With excess of caution she had nailed it up so temptation could not pry it. Now she could not draw the nails. The constables grew impatient, doubting its contents.

“Let us break it for you,” they said.

She would not let them. “Ye will break it worse than I shall.”

She took a hammer, struck too faintly, lost strength, and wept hysterically—breath catching, heart racing, belly heavy with child. At last she broke it. A little cry bubbled from her as the wood split. She paid the fine: it swallowed all her unlawful gains and two gold pieces besides.

When the men were gone, she drew the broken pieces together on the table with what little money remained. Her arms went round them as if she could hold the past intact by embrace. Her rich hair came loose and fell down like a curtain. She bowed her forehead on the wreck and sobbed, “My love’s box it is broken, and my heart withal.”

So Martin found her. She could not lift her head; she only sighed out what had befallen, ending again, “My love his box is broken, and so mine heart is broken.”

Martin’s grief turned to wrath. A traitor had betrayed them. Some stony heart had told. Whoever it was should feel his arrow’s point—he spoke as if vengeance were bread.

“Idle chat! idle chat!” moaned Margaret, not lifting her brow. “When you have slain all the gossips in this town, can we eat them? Tell me how to keep you all, or prithee hold thy peace, and let the saints get leave to whisper me.”

He held his tongue then, glancing uneasily at his defeated General.

Towards evening she rose, washed her face, did up her hair with a stubborn care, and bade Martin take down the crocodile and put out a basket instead.

“I can get up linen better than they seem to do it in this street,” said she, “and you must carry it in the basket.”

“That will I for thy sake,” said the soldier.

“Good Martin! forgive me that I spake shrewishly.”

Even while they spoke, a man came seeking advice. Margaret told him the mayor had forbidden her to sell drugs. “But I will gladly iron and starch your linen,” said she, “and come and fetch it from your house.”

“Are ye mad, young woman?” he cried. “I come for a leech, and ye proffer me a washerwoman.” He went out in dudgeon.

“There is a stupid creature,” Margaret said sadly—because he could not hear what a woman must hear in the city’s soundscape: not only voices, but the thin howl of need beneath them.

Soon after came a woman, frantic with the tale of her sick child. Margaret stopped her with the same words—no drugs, but she would wash, iron, and starch.

“Oh, ay,” said the woman. “I have smocks and ruffs foul. Come for them; and when you are there, you can look at the boy.” She told Margaret where she lived, and when her husband would be out; and yet she was fond enough of her husband to plan around him.

An introduction is an introduction. And so, out of those turned away from physic, two or three made Doctor Margaret their washerwoman.

“Now, Martin,” said she, “you must help. I’ll no more cats than can slay mice.”

“Mistress,” said he, “the stomach is not awanting for’t, but the headpiece, worst luck.”

“Oh, I mean not starching and ironing; that takes a woman and a handy one. But the bare washing—surely a man can contrive that. Why, a mule has wit enough to do’t with his hoofs, an’ ye could drive him into the tub. Come, off doublet, and try.”

“I am your man,” said he, stripping for the unwonted toil. “I’ll risk my arm in soapsuds, an you will risk your glory.”

“My what?”

“Your glory and honour as a—washerwoman.”

“Gramercy! if you are man enough to bring me half-washed linen t’ iron, I am woman enough to fling’t back i’ the suds.”

So the brave girl and the brave soldier worked with a will. The tub slopped; lye bit the hands; wet linen thumped on boards; steam rose with a homely smell of soap and sour cloth. Outside, Rotterdam clattered and shouted; inside, Margaret learned to measure time by the music of labour—water poured, cloth wrung, irons hissed—sounds that were honest and therefore soothing.

They kept the wolf from the door. More they could not do.

Margaret repaired the To-morrow box. As she leaned over the glue, her tears mixed with it; she cemented her exiled lover’s little chest with salt water from her own eyes. When mended it stood unreplenished. They paid the weekly rent and kept the pot boiling, but no more.

Then came a concatenation—one small link hooking another.

Recommended from house to house, Margaret washed for the mayor himself. Bringing home clean linen one day, she heard in his kitchen that his worship’s only daughter was stricken with disease and like not to live. Margaret could not help cross-questioning. A servant gave such symptoms as she had seen; too general at first—weakness, paleness, silence. But one gossip added one detail, another another, until a pattern formed in Margaret’s mind like a recipe glimpsed from scattered notes.

At last she met the mayor. He knew her at once.

“Why, you are the unlicensed doctor.”

“I was,” said she. “Now I’m your worship’s washerwoman.”

The dignitary coloured; it was a drop in state. Margaret met it without malice. “Your worship might have been harder. Rather would I do you a good turn. Sir, you have a sick daughter. Let me see her.”

He shook his head. “That cannot be. The law I do enforce on others I may not break myself.”

“Alack, sir,” she answered, “I seek no guerdon now for curing folk; why, I am a washerwoman. I trow one may heal all the world, an if one will but let the world starve one in return.”

“That is no more than just,” said he stiffly; then, as if reasoning with his own conscience, “an’ ye make no trade on’t, there is no offence.”

“Then let me see her.”

“What avails it? The learnedest leeches in Rotterdam have all seen her, and bettered her nought. Her ill is inscrutable. One saith spleen; another, liver; another, blood; another, stomach; and another, that she is possessed. In very truth she seems to have a demon. She shunneth all company; pineth alone; eateth no more victuals than might diet a sparrow. Speaketh seldom, nor hearkens them that speak, and weareth thinner and paler and nearer the grave.”

Margaret listened, taking in not only the words but the silences between them—the way the man’s voice cracked on “only daughter,” the harshness that grief gives to authority.

“Sir,” said she, “an if you take your velvet doublet to half-a-dozen shops in Rotterdam and speer is it fine or sorry velvet, and worth how much the ell, those traders will eye it and feel it and all be in one story to a letter, for they know their trade. But your leeches are all in different stories. Why? because they know not their trade. Each is enamoured of some one evil and seeth it with his bat’s eye in every patient. Had they stayed at home and never seen your daughter, they had answered all the same—spleen, blood, stomach, lungs, liver, lunacy, or as they call it possession. Let me see her. We are of a sex, and that is much.”

He still hesitated. The law was a bridle he feared to drop.

“Saints of heaven!” cried Margaret, the quick irritability of a breeding woman flaring. “Is this how men love their own flesh and blood? Her mother had ta’en me in her arms ere this and carried me to the sick room.”

Her violet eyes flashed fire.

“Come with me,” said the mayor hastily.

In the chamber, the mother sat pale, worn with watching. Near the fire, as if heat could fill what was empty, sat the girl: eighteen, thin as a fasting saint, her face drained of all summer.

“Mistress,” said the mayor, “I have brought thee a new doctor.”

The girl gave a contemptuous wrench of her shoulder and turned more decidedly to the fire.

Margaret came softly and sat beside her, as one sits beside a kettle that must not be jolted lest it boil over. “But ’tis one that will not torment you.”

“A woman!” said the young lady, surprised and scornful at once.

“Tell her your symptoms,” urged the father.

“What for? you will be no wiser.”

“You will be none the worse,” said Margaret.

“Well,” said the girl, “I have no stomach for food, and no heart for any thing. Now cure me, and go.”

“Patience awhile! Your food—doth it taste like nought in your mouth?”

“Ay. How knew you that?”

“Nay, I knew it not till you did tell me. I trow you would be better for a little good company.”

“I trow not. What is their silly chat to me?”

Margaret asked the father to leave them. When he was gone she put practical questions—about sleep, about mornings, about the body’s tremblings, about what sounds in the house were bearable and what were hateful. The girl tried to be sullen, but answered despite herself.

“When you wake i’ the morning you find yourself quiver, as one may say?” asked Margaret, lightly touching the wrist.

“Nay. Ay. How knew you that?”

Margaret smiled a little. “Shall I dose you, or shall I but tease you a bit with my silly chat?”

“Which you will.”

“Then I will tell you a story,” said Margaret, lowering her voice as if making a private music in the room. “’Tis about two true lovers.”

“I hate to hear of lovers,” said the girl. “Nevertheless canst tell me; ’twill be less nauseous than your physic—maybe.”

So Margaret told her, not as leeches tell symptoms but as women tell their own lives when they cannot speak them plainly. The maiden in the tale was Ursel, daughter to an old physician; the youth was Conrad, son of a hosier at Tergou. Margaret gave the story from the woman’s view, with that sweet, earnest voice that turns a room’s harsh noises soft. She spoke of trouble and waiting, of courage in small acts—like making a thin broth from bones when hunger is near; like keeping a hearth alive with a pinch of embers.

The sullen heart beside her began to listen. Breath steadied. The eye, dulled all day, sharpened. When Margaret stopped, the girl turned almost eagerly.

“Nay, nay, I must hear the end. I will hear it.”

“Ye cannot,” said Margaret gently, “for I know it not; none knoweth that but God.”

“Ah, your Ursel was a jewel of worth,” the girl said, earnest now. “Would she were here.”

“Instead of her that is here?”

“I say not that,” the girl murmured, and blushed.

“You do but think it.”

“Thought is free. Whether or no, an she were here, I’d give her a buss, poor thing.”

“Then give it me,” said Margaret, her voice suddenly thick with her own sorrow, “for I am she.”

“Nay, nay,” cried the girl, startled into life. “That I’ll be sworn y’ are not.”

“Say not so,” said Margaret. “In very truth I am she. And prithee, sweet mistress, go not from your word, but give me the buss ye promised me—and with a good heart, for oh, my own heart lies heavy: heavy as thine.”

The young gentlewoman rose, put her arms round Margaret’s neck, and kissed her.

“I am woe for you,” she sighed. “You are a good soul; you have done me good—a little.” Her throat gulped. “Come again! come again!”

Margaret came again, and again. She talked; she watched keenly under gentleness; she listened for what the girl’s voice quickened upon, what names made the pulse leap. There was but one.

At last Margaret said to the mayor, “I know your daughter’s trouble, and ’tis curable.”

“What is’t? the blood?”

“Nay.”

“The stomach?”

“Nay.”

“The liver?”

“Nay.”

“The foul fiend?”

“Nay.”

“What then?”

“Love.”

“Love? Stuff, impossible! She is but a child; she never stirs abroad unguarded. She never hath from a child.”

“All the better,” said Margaret. “Then we shall not have far to look for him.”

“I vow not. I shall command her to tell me the caitiff’s name that hath by magic arts ensnared her young affections.”

“Oh, how foolish be the wise!” cried Margaret. “What, would ye go put her on her guard? Nay, let us work by art first; and if that fails, then ’twill still be time for violence and folly.”

With difficulty she persuaded him to use the common ritual of Saturday wages. Let all his people be paid in his daughter’s presence, and Margaret’s.

It was done. Some fifteen came in, each receiving coin and a word. Margaret sat close by the girl, holding her hand as if for comfort, her finger lightly at the wrist like a listener at a wall.

Most entered and went as if dogs and cats had passed through the room—mere noise, no meaning. Then came a black-haired lad.

The girl’s pulse leaped beneath Margaret’s finger. Her eye shone; her mouth, which had forgotten taste, almost remembered sweetness. When he went out she sank back and sighed, as if the sun had gone out with him.

Margaret rose and slipped away. The mayor followed her.

“Sir,” she whispered, “how call you yon black-haired lad?”

“That is Ulrich, my clerk.”

“Well then—’tis he.”

The mayor’s face went startled, then hard. “Now Heaven forbid! A lad I took out of the streets.”

“Well,” said Margaret steadily, “you took him not up without some merit?”

“Merit? not a jot! I liked the looks of the brat, that was all.”

“Was that no merit? He pleased the father’s eye. And now he hath pleased the daughter’s. That has oft been seen since Adam.”

“How know ye ’tis he?”

“I held her hand,” said Margaret, “and touched her wrist. At others her pulse lay quiet. At Ulrich’s coming it did leap; at his going she did sink and sigh. ’Twas to be seen the sun had gone out of the room for her. Look not so scared. No witch am I, but a poor girl that hath been docile, and so bettered herself by a great neglected leech’s art and learning. Now bide thou there till I come, and prithee, prithee, spoil not good work wi’ meddling.”

She went back, asked the girl for a lock of hair.

“Take it,” said the girl, listless again, as if tenderness shamed her back into stone.

“Why, ’tis a lass of marble,” Margaret teased softly. “How long do you count to be like that, mistress?”

“Till I am in my grave, sweet Peggy.”

“Who knows?” said Margaret. “Maybe in ten minutes you will be altogether as hot.”

She ran down to the shop as if for some household errand. When she returned to the mayor her eyes were bright.

“Good news! He fancies her—and more than a little.”

“How is’t to be?” he demanded, torn between pride and fear. “Will you marry your child, or bury her? For there is no third way. Shame and love do rend her virgin heart to death.”

The dignitary chose the more cheerful rite, but not without struggle; the battle showed on his face as lines and colour. He went with Margaret to his daughter and stood silent, for men are seldom in haste to drink their wormwood.

So Doctor Margaret spoke for him, cheerfully, like one stirring a stubborn sauce so it will not catch.

“Mistress,” said she, “your lock is gone; I have sold it.”

“And who was so mad as to buy such a thing?” the young lady asked, scornful by habit.

“Oh, a black-haired laddie wi’ white teeth. They call him Ulrich.”

The pale face reddened at once—brow and all—like a flame had been put back into the room.

“Says he, ‘Oh, sweet mistress, give it me.’ I had told them all whose ’twas. ‘Nay,’ said I, ‘selling is my livelihood, not giving.’ So he offered me this, he offered me that, but nought less would I take than his next quarter’s wages.”

“Cruel,” murmured the girl, scarce audible.

“Why,” said Margaret, “you are in one tale with your father. Says he to me when I told him, ‘Oh, an he loves her hair so well, ’tis odd but he loves the rest of her. Well,’ quoth he, ‘’tis an honest lad, and a shall have her, gien she will but leave her sulks and consent.’ So what say ye, mistress—will you be married to Ulrich, or buried i’ the kirkyard?”

“Father! father!” cried the girl, and all her coldness cracked at once like thin ice.

“’Tis so,” said he hoarsely. “Speak thy mind.”
