The day appointed for Madam Esmond’s entertainment to the General came in with a Virginia softness that seemed made to flatter hospitality, and to mock grief. Castlewood wore its best face. The broad house-front, with its covered gallery and white-pillared shade, stood as if newly washed by light; the windows were thrown wide; and from within there issued, not the thin perfume of idle finery, but the substantial incense of a great kitchen in full labour.

Madam Esmond, who commonly dressed with a reserved neatness, had this day armed herself in splendour; her silk made a faint, continual whisper as she moved, like dry leaves stirred in a still wood. If her heart shrank from the occasion, she would not have her guests guess it. To do honour to a man of war, she would have the house speak, in its own language, of loyalty and plenty. The sideboard shone; the glasses caught the sun; and past the parlour door, as she walked, she saw the domestic procession—negro servants, steady-footed and silent, bearing covered dishes, trenchers, jugs, and baskets. The very air was thick with culinary promises: the sharp sweetness of apple-toddy newly mixed in the great china bowl; the iron-and-fat fragrance that comes when meat has been turned and basted; the peppery breath of greens; a ghost of nutmeg from some custard or syllabub; and underneath all, the honest smoke of wood, the smell of bread-crust, and the clean wet scent of rinsed pewter.

The General’s new aide-de-camp was the first to arrive. He reined in at the steps; his servant took the bridle; another led the horses away to the well-known quarters. No young gentleman in the colony sat a saddle with more ease than Mr. Washington, nor carried his tall frame with more soldierly economy. Madam Esmond received him in the covered gallery itself, where the boards gave out a sober creak under their measured pacing, and where the soundscape of the estate—distant axe-strokes, a far-off call from the quarters, the soft fanning of pigeons in the eaves—made a kind of rural music that soothed, even while it reminded her of all that must soon be broken by drums and marching.

For a while, before the Major retired to rid himself of his riding-boots, he and his hostess walked there in talk. She had much to ask and much to say; she must hear, from his own mouth, confirmation of his appointment; and she must speak of her son’s approaching departure, though the words, for all their practised composure, tasted bitter as burnt sugar.

They descended the steps to the rough lawn and paced a little in the shade, where the leaves made that light, incessant rustle which country-bred people take for granted, until they are robbed of it; and Madam Esmond, who would have hated to live where wheels and hammers never ceased, listened almost greedily to every natural sound, as if laying up a store against the day when George and Harry should be hearing only orders, oaths, and the clank of arms.

Mr. Washington announced his Excellency’s speedy approach, and that Mr. Franklin of Pennsylvania was coming in the General’s coach.

“This Mr. Franklin,” Madam Esmond said, with a little tightening of her mouth, “was a common printer’s boy, I have heard. A pretty pass things are coming to, when such persons ride in the coach of the Commander-in-Chief.”

Mr. Washington, whose handsome gravity seldom altered, yet whose mind was liberal beyond his years, answered that a more shrewd and sensible gentleman never rode in coach nor walked on foot. A useful and meritorious man, he maintained; ingenious, too.

Madam Esmond thought him far too charitable to tradesmen; but the Major held his ground, and would not allow that worth must be measured by the cut of a coat or the ancestry of a name.

“I am glad, at least,” the widow said at last, drawing herself into her stateliest manner, as if the very stiffness of her back could keep the future at bay, “that, as my boy is going to make the campaign, he will not be with tradesmen, but with gentlemen—with gentlemen of honour and fashion.”

Mr. Washington had seen gentlemen of honour and fashion over their cups, and had observed, too, how the noise of their mirth—forced, loud, and idle—could sour a room more certainly than the smoke of bad tobacco. He wisely chose not to tell tales; and he only said that Harry and George, now they were coming into the world, must take their share of good and bad, and hear what both sorts had to say.

“To be with a veteran officer of the finest army in the world,” faltered Madam Esmond; “with gentlemen bred in the midst of the Court; with friends of his Royal Highness, the Duke—” She checked herself, as though names and titles were charms that might keep bullets off a son.

Her friend only inclined his head. He did not choose to let his countenance depart from its usual composure; yet a colour rose in him at her next words, and she, too, betrayed herself by a blush which, for all her breeding, would come.

“And with you, dear Colonel Washington, by whom my father always set such store,” she said. “You do not know how much he trusted in you. You will take care of my boy, sir, will you not? You are but five years older, yet I trust to you more than to his seniors. My father always told the children—I always bade them—to look up to Mr. Washington.”

“You know I would have done anything to win Colonel Esmond’s favour,” he replied; and then, in a tone that made the simple words carry a weight beyond their plainness, “Madam, how much would I not venture to merit his daughter’s?”

He bowed—not too ill—and she dropped one of the lowest curtsies in Virginia; her curtsey had long been esteemed unrivalled, and to see it now, with the colour in her cheek, was to be reminded how ceremony may serve as veil to feeling.

“Mr. Washington,” she said, “will always be sure of a mother’s affection, whilst he gives so much of his to her children.”

She gave him her hand, which he kissed with profound politeness; and leaning upon his arm, she re-entered the house. The parlour received them with its cool shade and its waiting plenty. On a side-table the apple-toddy stood, tawny and fragrant; the sliced fruit bobbed like golden boats; the spice rose warm and sweet; and the ladle clinked softly against china when a servant, rehearsing his duty, stirred it and withdrew again.

Here they were joined by George, who came to them accurately powdered and richly attired, saluting his parent and his friend alike with low respectful bows. His coat fitted to perfection; his ruffles were fine; his sword sat on him as if it had belonged there always; and the scent of his hair-powder mingled strangely with the kitchen odours—flour and roasting fat and peel—so that one might have thought him a confection dressed for war.

Yet, for all the politeness of his smile, he was by no means in good-humour with either of them. The lower part of his face performed civility; the upper part watched and burned. Madam Esmond continued to blush; Mr. Washington, as he shook George’s hand, looked somewhat red, and held his gravity with an effort. Nothing had been uttered that might not have been spoken before the most decent company; and yet the air was as if some sharp spice had been overscattered—pleasant in itself, but too much, too sudden, pricking the tongue.

The Colonel, to ease the moment, asked Mr. George if he had had good sport.

“No,” George answered curtly. “Have you?” And then, with a glance that was like the point of a knife, he looked at the picture of his father hanging in the parlour—Colonel Esmond, calm in paint, remote in death, and yet present enough to be appealed to.

Mr. Washington, not ordinarily a talkative man, straightway entered into a long description of the morning’s sport with the king’s officers: what woods they had ridden, how many birds they had shot, what game they had brought down. He described, too, Mr. Braddock’s heavy person and great boots as the General floundered through Virginian undergrowth; hunting, as they called it, with a pack of dogs gathered from various houses, with a pack of negroes barking as loud as the dogs, and actually shooting the deer when they came in sight. Mr. Washington gave it in sober mimicry—never insolent, never loud; but his very restraint made the scene comic.

“Great God, sir!” puffed Mr. Braddock in the story, “what would Sir Robert have said in Norfolk, to see a man hunting with a fowling-piece in his hand, and a pack of dogs actually laid on to a turkey!”

“Indeed, Colonel, you are vastly comical this afternoon!” cried Madam Esmond with a neat little laugh; but George listened, looking more glum than ever, as if each description of field-sport were an affront to his present humour.

“What Sir Robert is there at Norfolk?” she asked. “Is he one of the newly arrived army-gentlemen?”

“The General meant Norfolk at home, madam, not Norfolk in Virginia,” said Mr. Washington. “He had been speaking of Sir Robert Walpole, who lived in that county, and of the great hunts the old Minister kept there, and his palace and his pictures at Houghton.” And then, like a man who, however he strides forward, still hears the home-birds in his mind, he sighed: “I should like to see a good field and a good fox-chase at home better than any sight in the world.”

“Nevertheless, there is good sport here, as I was saying,” George put in, with a sneer.

“What sport?” asked Mr. Washington, looking at him.

“Why, sure you know, without looking at me so fiercely, and stamping your foot, as if you were going to charge me with the foils,” George rattled on. “Are you not the best sportsman of the country-side? Are there not all the fish of the field, and the beasts of the trees, and the fowls of the sea—no—the fish of the trees, and the beasts of the sea—and the—bah! You know what I mean. I mean shad, and salmon, and rock-fish, and roe-deer, and hogs, and buffaloes, and bisons, and elephants, for what I know. I’m no sportsman.”

“No, indeed,” said Mr. Washington, with a look of scarcely repressed scorn.

“Yes, I understand you,” George pursued, and now his voice took that thin sweetness which is sharper than bitterness. “I am a milksop. I have been bred at my mamma’s knee. Look at these pretty apron-strings, Colonel! Who would not like to be tied to them? See of what a charming colour they are! I remember when they were black—that was for my grandfather.”

“And who would not mourn for such a gentleman?” Mr. Washington said; and Madam Esmond, astonished, looked from one face to the other as if she had heard a dish crash in her pantry.

“And, indeed, I wish my grandfather were here,” George continued, “and would resurge, as he promises to do on his tombstone; and would bring my father, the Ensign, with him.”

At this moment Harry entered—freshly dressed in just such another suit as George wore: gold-corded frock, braided waistcoat, silver-hilted sword, and solitaire. The sight of him, so like and unlike his brother, was too much for their mother’s strained composure.

“Ah, Harry!” she cried, and burst into tears, and flew to her younger son.

“Oh, Harry, Harry!”

“What is it, mother?” Harry asked, taking her in his arms. “What is the matter, Colonel?”

“Upon my life, it would puzzle me to say,” answered Mr. Washington, biting his lip.

“A mere question, Hal, about pink ribbons,” George said airily, “which I think vastly becoming to our mother—as no doubt the Colonel does.”

“Sir, will you please to speak for yourself?” cried Mr. Washington, stepping forward with a suddenness, then sinking his voice again, as a man must in a lady’s room.

“He speaks too much for himself,” wept the widow.

“I protest I don’t any more know the source of these tears than the source of the Nile,” George said, with an insolence that wore a courtier’s mask. “And if the picture of my father were to begin to cry, I should almost as much wonder at the paternal tears. What have I uttered? An allusion to ribbons! Is there some poisoned pin in them, which has been struck into my mother’s heart by a guilty fiend of a London mantua-maker? I professed to wish to be led in these lovely reins all my life long,” and he turned a pirouette on his scarlet heels, his shoe-buckles flashing as they struck the floor.

“George Warrington! what devil’s dance are you dancing now?” Harry demanded. He loved his mother; he loved Mr. Washington; but, of all creatures, he loved and admired his brother George, and would have followed him into fire.

“My dear child, you do not understand dancing—you care not for the politer arts—you can get no more music out of a spinet than by pulling a dead hog by the ear,” George said. “By nature you were made for a man—a man of war. I do not mean a seventy-four, Colonel George, like that hulk which brought the hulking Mr. Braddock into our river. His Excellency, too, is a man of warlike turn, a follower of the sports of the field. I am a milksop, as I have had the honour to say.”

“You never showed it yet,” Harry broke out. “You beat that great Maryland man was twice your size.”

“Under compulsion, Harry. ’Tis tuptu, my lad, or else ’tis tuptomai, as thy breech well knew when we followed school,” George said, letting scraps of scholarship fall like coin. “But I am of a quiet turn, and would never lift my hand to pull a trigger—no, nor a nose, nor anything but a rose.”

As he spoke he took one of Madam Esmond’s bright pink apron ribbons between finger and thumb, and handled it with a teasing delicacy that made his mother wince as if he had touched a nerve.

“I hate sporting, which you and the Colonel love, and I want to shoot nothing alive—not a turkey, nor a titmouse, nor an ox, nor an ass, nor anything that has ears. Those curls of Mr. Washington’s are prettily powdered.”

Mr. Washington, offended by the first part and perplexed by the last, had taken a modest draught from the great china bowl of apple-toddy, as was the Virginian custom, the liquor smelling of orchard-cellars and autumn afternoons; and then, to cool himself without making a scene, he paced the balcony outside with a stiff propriety, listening, perhaps against his will, to the house’s noises—the clatter of a dish-lid, the far-off crackle of firewood, the steady hum of a place that kept its order through the hands of the enslaved.

Again almost reconciled with George by the mere need of holding the household together, Madam Esmond stood giving a hand to each of her sons. George, suddenly gentle in gesture if not in spirit, placed his disengaged hand upon Harry’s shoulder.

“I say one thing, George,” Harry began, flushing.

“Say twenty things, Don Enrico,” George returned, calling him by a name half-mocking, half-affectionate.

“If you are not fond of sporting and that,” Harry persisted, “and don’t care for killing game and hunting, being cleverer than me—why shouldst thou not stop at home and be quiet, and let me go out with Colonel George and Mr. Braddock? That’s what I say.”

Madam Esmond looked eagerly from the dark-haired to the fair-haired boy. She knew not from which she would rather part; and the fact that she must part from one of them at all made her heart feel like dough overworked—stretched, then torn.

“One of our family must go because honneur oblige,” George said, and his voice grew suddenly cold with a kind of fatalism dressed in wit. “And my name being number one, number one must go first.”

“Told you so,” said poor Harry, unable to hide his disappointment.

“One must stay,” George went on, “or who is to look after mother at home? We cannot afford to be both scalped by Indians, or fricasseed by French.”

“Fricasseed by French!” cried Harry, indignant and eager at once. “The best troops of the world! Englishmen! I should like to see them fricasseed by the French! What a mortal thrashing you will give them!” And he sighed to think he should not be present at the battue, thinking of war as boys think of it, with appetite and ignorance, as of a dish described rather than tasted.

George, as if to show how little he cared for such masculine talk, sat down to the harpsichord and began to play and sing, in a voice trained for drawing-rooms, “Malbrouk s’en va-t-en guerre, Mironton, mironton, mirontaine.” The tune, half nursery, half satire, went tinkling through the house like a spoon striking glass, and for a moment overlaid even the deeper kitchen-sounds. At the sound the gentleman from the balcony entered again.

“I am playing ‘God save the King,’ Colonel,” George said, looking over his shoulder, his fingers running in trills, “in compliment to the new expedition.”

“I never know whether thou art laughing or in earnest,” said Mr. Washington simply; “but surely methinks that is not the air.”

George crashed and fluttered among the keys with ever so many quavers; and the Colonel watched him, perhaps wondering that a gentleman of George’s condition could set himself so eagerly to an occupation which soldiers call effeminate, yet which in that house was part of gentility’s very grammar.

Then Mr. Washington took out his watch, saying his Excellency’s coach would be there almost immediately, and asking leave to retire to his apartment and put himself in fit condition to appear before her ladyship’s company.

“Colonel Washington knows the way to his room pretty well,” George called from the harpsichord, never offering to stir.

“Let me show the Colonel to his chamber!” cried Madam Esmond, in great wrath; and she sailed from the room, followed by the enraged and bewildered young officer. Her silk hissed; his spurs rang faintly; and the sound of their steps, retreating down the passage, seemed to beat time with George’s jangling music, as if the house itself were a drum.

Mr. Washington felt himself insulted—he could hardly say how. He was outraged and could not speak. His anger sat in him like something choking; and Harry, who could read the weather of a friend’s face, noticed his condition with alarm.

“For heaven’s sake, George, what does this all mean?” he asked when the two had gone. “Why shouldn’t he kiss her hand? I tell you it is nothing but common kindness.”

“Nothing but common kindness!” George shrieked out, and now the mask dropped, and jealousy showed its raw skin. “Look at that, Hal! Is that common kindness?”

He thrust into his brother’s hands a paper over which he had been brooding; only a fragment, yet plain enough. Harry read, his brows drawing together, as if the ink itself had a sting.

The paper began mid-thought: “… is older than myself, but I, again, am older than my years; and you know, dear brother, have ever been considered a sober person. All children are better for a father’s superintendence, and her two, I trust, will find in me a tender friend and guardian.”

“Friend and guardian! Curse him!” cried George, clenching his fists.

Harry read on, the words falling like crumbs from a loaf one did not want to eat: “… The flattering offer which General Braddock hath made me, will, of course, oblige me to postpone this matter until after the campaign. When we have given the French a sufficient drubbing, I shall return to repose under my own vine and fig-tree.”

“He means Castlewood,” George burst out, shaking his fist toward the creepers sunning themselves upon the wall outside, as if the harmless leaves were accomplices. “These are his vines.”

Harry’s eyes went back to the fragment: “… Under my own vine and fig-tree; where I hope soon to present my dear brother to his new sister-in-law. She has a pretty Scripture name, which is…”—and there the document ended, the rest torn away like a word lost from a dying tongue, leaving only silence where certainty should have been.

“Which is Rachel,” George said bitterly, supplying what the broken paper implied. “Rachel is by no means weeping for her children, and has every desire to be comforted. Now, Harry! Let us upstairs at once, kneel down as becomes us, and say, ‘Dear papa, welcome to your house of Castlewood.’”
