On the day Gwendolen Harleth became Mrs. Grandcourt, the morning air was crisp and clear, the low sun casting a slight frost that curled the edges of the leaves. The bridal procession was a spectacle in itself; half the village of Pennicote lined the path to the church to witness it. An old friend of the rector officiated, while the rector himself acted as the bride's father—a role that added a certain dignified gravity to the parade. Yet, amidst the general rejoicing, two faces betrayed a distinct sadness: Mrs. Davilow's and Anna's. The mother's delicate eyelids were flushed pink, as though she had wept throughout the night, and it was easy to understand her grief at parting with a daughter who was the pride of her life. It was harder to comprehend why Anna, adorned in her bridesmaid's finery, appeared so troubled. Everyone else, including the radiant bride, reflected the brilliance of the occasion.

Gwendolen was universally acknowledged as possessing the figure and carriage of a "lady of title." While some might have wished her face were rosier, the groom's own pale complexion—remarkably similar to that of the miller's wife—made the match seem perfectly balanced in the public eye. They must be deeply in love, the villagers reasoned, hoping he would never hold it against her that she had once worked as a governess or that her mother lived modestly at Sawyer's Cottage. Even the miller's fourteen-year-old daughter couldn't conceive that high-born gentlemen treated their wives poorly, until her mother whispered, "Oh, child, men are men, whether gentle or simple. I've heard Squire Pelton carry his dogs and a long whip into his wife's room to frighten her."

"That's unlucky talk for a wedding, Mrs. Girdle," the tailor interjected. "A quarrel may end with the whip, but it starts with the tongue, and women usually get the worst of that."

"The Lord gave them tongues to use, I suppose," Mrs. Girdle replied. "He didn't intend for you to have it all your own way."

"From the gentleman who handles the grooming at Offendene," the tailor noted, "Mr. Grandcourt seems to have a wonderful little tongue. Everything must be done silently, without his ordering."

"Then he's the more whip, I suspect," Mrs. Girdle murmured. "She's got plenty of tongue herself. Look, there they come!"

"What wonderful long corners she has to her eyes!" the tailor observed. "They make you feel comical when she looks at you."

In truth, Gwendolen displayed more elasticity in her bearing and more luster in her long brown gaze than ever before. She possessed the brilliancy of intense excitement, a state that can sometimes arise even from pain. Yet, today, it was not pain she felt. She had driven herself into a condition akin to standing at a gambling table while Deronda watched her; she was beginning to lose. There was a perverse enjoyment in it. Whatever unease a growing conscience might have stirred was dismissed as a minor ailment, drowned out by the gratification of her ambitious vanity and craving for luxury.

She could not honestly claim regret for accepting Grandcourt, nor could she admit that vague fears clouded the glowing reality of the moment. She ignored the possibility that she was doing wrong, that a punishment awaited her, or that the woman she had promised to protect was suffering in bitter reproach. She pushed aside the image of Deronda despising her for this union, just as he had despised her for gambling. Above all, she silenced the terrifying thought that the bond uniting her to her lover was now being strangled and thrown over her neck. This chaotic mixture of dim facts, deep impressions, and half-real fantasies had disturbed her during the weeks of engagement. Was that turmoil nullified this morning? No; it was surmounted with an exultant defiance. Standing before many eyes, she felt herself playing a game of life, daring everything to win glory—or, if she lost, to lose with *éclat* and a profound sense of importance. For the first time, the prospect of a losing destiny did not terrify her; instead, she believed she was stepping into a realm of greater power, armed with the official authority of marriage.

The intoxicating fog of youthful egoism, previously shattered by trouble and shame, had returned, fed by a renewed strength of her old desires. She bore no resemblance to the tearful, trembling bride others imagined. Poor Gwendolen, deemed too worldly and forward by some, walked among illusions with an erect head and elastic step, though beneath it lay a subconscious realization that she was slightly drunk on the moment.

"Why, thank God you bear it so well, my darling!" Mrs. Davilow exclaimed, helping Gwendolen remove her white veil and slip into her traveling dress. The mother's trembling had been severe, urging Gwendolen to view the morning as a triumph.

"You might have said that if I were going to Mrs. Mompert's, you dear, sad, incorrigible mamma!" Gwendolen laughed tenderly, pressing her hands to her mother's cheeks before pulling back to spread her arms wide. "Here I am—Mrs. Grandcourt! What else would you have me?"

"Hush, my child, for heaven's sake!" Mrs. Davilow whispered, overcome. "How can I help feeling it when I part from you? But I can bear anything gladly if you are happy."

"Not gladly, mamma, no!" Gwendolen shook her head, smiling brightly. "You would bear it willingly, but always sorrowfully. Sorrowing is your sauce; you cannot take anything without it."

Clasping her mother's shoulders, she rained kisses upon her cheeks. "And you shall sorrow over my having everything at my command! Splendid houses, horses, diamonds—I shall have diamonds! Going to court, becoming Lady Certainly and Lady Perhaps, grand everywhere, and always loving you better than anyone else in the world."

"My sweet child! But I shall not be jealous if you love your husband better; he expects to be first."

Gwendolen thrust out her lips and chin in a pretty grimace. "Rather a ridiculous expectation. However, I don't mean to treat him ill, unless he deserves it."

The two embraced tightly, and Gwendolen could not stop a rising sob as she said, "I wish you were going with me, mamma." The slight moisture on her long eyelashes only enhanced her charm as she handed her arm to Grandcourt to lead her to the carriage. The rector paused to offer a final blessing—"God bless you; we shall see you again soon"—before turning to Mrs. Davilow with a mix of cheerfulness and solemnity. "Let us be thankful, Fanny. She is in a position well suited to her, far beyond what I dared hope. Few women have been chosen more entirely for their own sake. You should feel yourself a happy mother."

After a fifty-mile railway journey, the new couple arrived at the station near Ryelands. The sky had grown overcast, and twilight descended as they entered the park gates. Yet, Gwendolen, peering from the carriage window, drank in the grandeur of the scene: the winding drive bordered by evergreens and massive gray trees, opening into vast grassy plains dotted with dark clumps, finally revealing the white house framed by a hanging wood and a sweeping terrace.

During the ride, Gwendolen had been at her liveliest, chatting incessantly and ignoring the shift in their dynamic from the previous day. Grandcourt remained quietly ecstatic, allowing her to seize his hand with both of hers, her energy bubbling like a kitten refusing to be petted. She was genuinely feverish with excitement, and the changing light and scenery caused her heart to palpitate anew. Was it merely the novelty, or the fulfillment of her girlish dream to become "somebody"? Walking through corridors of impossible height, under ceilings where her own painted springs shed flowers and zephyrs blew trumpets, served by lackeys who revered her insolence? Was it the closeness of this dream realized, or a dim, insistent premonition mixing triumph with dread? In her nature, exultation inevitably carried an infusion of fear, ready to curdle.

As they approached the gates, she fell silent despite herself. When Grandcourt declared, "Here we are at home!" and kissed her on the lips for the first time, she barely registered it, offering only a passive acceptance amidst the overwhelming display. Wasn't her frantic life of the last three months merely a show, with her consciousness as a wondering spectator? Following the willful excitement of the wedding day, a numbness had settled over her.

Yet, the hall shone with brilliant light, warmth, matting, carpets, full-length portraits, and Olympian statues, attended by diligent servants. There were not many, only a few transferred from Diplow plus the permanent staff, and her new maid was being guided by the housekeeper. Led by Grandcourt down a subtly scented corridor, she entered an ante-room where an open doorway spilled rich light and color.

"These are our dens," Grandcourt said. "You will like to be quiet here until dinner. We shall dine early." He pressed her hand to his lips and withdrew, more in love than he had anticipated.
