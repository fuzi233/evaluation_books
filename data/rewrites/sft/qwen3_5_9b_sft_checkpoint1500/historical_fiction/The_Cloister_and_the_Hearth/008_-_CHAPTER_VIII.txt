While the burgomaster of Tergou exposed Gerard to the town's scorn, a quieter, yet deeper torment beset Margaret at Sevenbergen. Her sorrow was born of a housewife's despair, a hunger that gnawed harder than any physical want. She knelt before Martin Wittenhaagen, the aged soldier, her eyes wet with unshed tears.

"Martin," she whispered, "there is naught in the larder, yet Gerard returns tonight. His heart is kind, though his mind is scattered; he forgets to dine at home. When his labors cease, he rushes to me, often so weak with fatigue that he nearly faints. And what cruel irony—that I possess no bread to feed the servant who loves me so dearly!"

Martin scratched his chin, his brow furrowed. "Pray tell, what hope remains?"

"It is Thursday," she replied, her voice trembling. "By custom, it is your day to hunt. I had pinned my hopes upon your return today."

"Nay," the soldier grunted, shaking his head. "The Duke hunts today, and his friends. To venture forth would be madness. Read this." He produced a parchment sealed with a grand crest. It bore the King's command: a stipend of four merks annually, granted to Martin Wittenhaagen for service in the Duke's wars and a wound sustained at the Duke's side, alongside a license to shoot three arrows weekly—only on Thursdays—in the Duke's forests of Holland. The law was strict: he might only take a seven-year-old buck or a doe with a fawn, and never if the Duke himself hunted. To break this rule meant forfeiting his salary, facing a heavy fine, and risking his life.

Margaret let out a long, weary sigh and fell silent.

"Cheer up, mistress," Martin said gently, softening his tone. "For your sake, I will risk my carcass. I have done so for many who were not worthy of a forefinger. Is there not a hare or a young fawn within reach?"

"If I permit you to go," she insisted, her voice breaking, "promise me you will not stray far, nor be seen. Far better for Gerard to sleep without supper than for harm to come to you, faithful Martin."

Her plea moved him. With a solemn vow, Martin took his bow and three arrows, slipping quietly into the woods that bordered the estate. The air hummed with the distant, faint notes of horns; every beast was on the move. *Here,* Martin thought, *I shall fill the pot unseen.*

He positioned himself behind a massive oak overlooking a sunlit glade, drawing his formidable weapon. It was crafted from English yew, towering six feet two inches, thick and powerful. Martin, a man of iron arms and cord-like muscles, had drawn it since boyhood. He could pull a three-foot arrow to its limit, and when released, the string sang like a harp string plucked by a master, the flight swift enough to blur the eye. This bow had felled countless soldiers in the wars of the Hoeskens and Cabbel-jaws, where battles were fought not in clouds of smoke, but with stark, visible brutality.

First, a hare cantered into view, stopping with ears pricked forward in a perfect 'V'. Martin leveled his aim. The arrow flew, the string twanged, but in his haste, he missed by mere inches. The shaft struck the earth beside the fleeing animal, passing beneath her belly like a ghost before vanishing into the grass. The hare leaped three feet upward and vanished.

"Bungler!" Martin muttered to himself. A true proof of his skill was that he blamed the hare, not his hand. Before he could fit another arrow, a wood-pigeon landed softly upon the branch above him. *Small, but dainty,* he thought. This time, he took care. He drew slowly, loosed smoothly, and watched the arrow pierce the bird's breast. Yet, instead of falling, the bird fluttered feebly, rising with a great effort only to fall dead fifty yards away, hidden by the thick foliage.

"Fortune turns against me," he murmured, spirits sinking. He fitted another arrow and scanned the glade. Suddenly, a commotion erupted behind him. Turning, he saw a magnificent stag crossing the open space, but it was too late; the animal had already slipped past. Frustrated, Martin slammed his bow down in anger. Just then, a long-spotted beast glided swiftly across the clearing after the deer—a leopard. Its belly brushed the earth as it moved.

Martin snatched up his bow instantly. Recognizing the Duke's leopard, he knew the hunters would be close. "Gerard must go supperless tonight," he resolved grimly.

He plunged back into the woods, following the tracks of the stag and the leopard. Soon, the sounds grew louder: leaves rustled violently, and the ground trembled under hooves. Rushing forward, he found the scene of chaos: the leopard, mounted upon the stag's back, tearing flesh with teeth and claws. The stag ran in a convulsive circle, blood streaming down its hide.

Desperation sparked a new resolve. For Margaret, he would secure the meat. Drawing his arrow to full length, he buried it deep in the stag's flank. Despite the beast atop him, the stag bounded high into the air and collapsed, dead. The leopard continued to tear at the carcass, undeterred.

Martin hoped the predator would gorge itself until it slept, allowing him to claim the kill. Minutes passed. He stepped forward, placing a hand on the stag's leg. The leopard emitted a terrible growl, pausing her feeding. She regarded him with a sullen, wary gaze. How to proceed? He recalled the old lore: wild creatures fled before the steady human eye. Standing tall, he fixed his gaze upon the beast. She returned the stare, unblinking. Then, the leopard, ignorant of nature's laws, lunged at his head with a roar, jaws gaping wide, eyes aflame.

Time froze. Martin caught her by the throat just as her teeth sought to crush his face. One claw raked his shoulder, tearing through leather and skin; the other, aimed at his cheek, snagged on the loose leather of his scapulary, which he wore like a hood over his jerkin. The brute's claw caught, and the struggle began.

Martin held her teeth back with iron strength, squeezing her throat while she ripped at his shoulder. It felt like blunt reaping-hooks grinding together. The pain was excruciating, yet it fired his blood rather than cowed him. He gnashed his teeth in a rage nearly equal to hers, and with a grip of steel, he maintained the pressure. Their eyes flared in a deadly duel of wills. Realizing she was being strangled, she made a wild, frantic lunge, dragging his hood across his face and blinding him, tearing the claw free from his shoulder, flesh and bone. Yet, he did not falter; he throttled her with hand and arm.

Suddenly, her tail, raised high, dropped. "Aha!" Martin cried joyfully, gripping her like death itself. Next, her body lost all elasticity. He held a choked, powerless thing, maintaining his hold until all motion ceased. Only then did he dash her to the earth, panting heavily, and remove his hood.

At his feet lay the leopard, silent, tongue protruding, a bloody paw twitching. For the first time, terror seized Martin. "I am a dead man," he whispered. "I have slain the Duke's leopard."

Hastily, he gathered handfuls of dry leaves and threw them over the corpse, concealing the evidence. Shouldering the heavy carcass, he staggered away, leaving a trail of mixed blood—the stag's and his own.

He burst into Peter's house, a horrifying figure, bleeding and stained. "There!" he roared, casting the deer down. "Broil me a steak, and ask no questions. I am faint."

Margaret, seeing the blood, assumed it came solely from the deer. She busied herself at the fire while the sturdy soldier bound his wounds. Soon, Gerard joined them, bringing a flask of Schiedam wine. Under its warmth, Martin recovered, recounting the tale of the hunt. They ate royally on the broiled venison, their mirth ringing with the memory of the exploit.

Then, silence fell. Margaret's eyes widened, fixed and transfixed, her cheeks draining of color. She gasped, unable to speak, pointing a trembling finger toward the window.

All turned to look. There, in the deepening twilight, crouched a dark, hunched form. Two eyes burned with the cold, green fire of glowworms.

It was the leopard.

Petrified, they stared as the beast sat unmoving. From the woods outside, a single, deep bay echoed, followed immediately by the chorus of many more.

"They have lost her," Martin trembled, the realization chilling his blood. "Muzzled bloodhounds are on her scent. They will find her here, and the venison. Good-bye, friends. Martin Wittenhaagen ends here."

Gerard grabbed his bow and thrust it into the soldier's hands. "Be a man!" he cried. "Shoot her and cast her into the wood before they arrive. Who knows?"

Voices of hounds grew louder, closer, their bays a terrifying crescendo.
