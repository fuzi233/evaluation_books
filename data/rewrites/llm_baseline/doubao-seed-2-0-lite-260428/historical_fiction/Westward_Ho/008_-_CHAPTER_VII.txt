It was far too late and far too dark the night before to explore the old Grenville family seat at Stow, so we wander its grounds now under a bright October sun, as Sir Richard Grenville and young Amyas Leigh pace the south-facing terraced garden just past eleven o’clock in the forenoon. Amyas slept through until a late luncheon, waking only an hour prior, but Sir Richard, unfazed by the bustle of last night’s arrivals, was up long before dawn at six, tramping the valley with his two sturdy terriers to course badgers and breathe in the sharp, sweet West Country air.

Old Stow House – the original, not the later grand folly – stood some four miles past the Cornish border, on the northern slope of the largest, loveliest of the sheltered combes described earlier. Eighty years after Sir Richard’s time, a vast, gaudy Palladian pile would be raised there, crammed full of the most vulgar excesses of bad taste, built, local legend says, by Charles II for Sir Richard’s great-grandson, heir to the famed Sir Bevil Grenville who routed Parliament’s troops at Stratton and fell fighting valiantly soon after on Lansdowne Hill outside Bath. But like most works born of Stuart patronage, that great new mansion rose only to fall: an old man who had watched its foundations laid as a boy lived long enough to see it pulled down to the last brick, its timbers sold off on the spot. Now the old stables are a plain farmhouse, the former tennis court a sheep cote, the great central quadrangle a rick yard, and all the rush of outward-spreading civilization has ebbed away from this lonely western corner of the land – let us hope, only for a little while.

But we do not tell of that later Stow, nor of the gallant Sir Bevil, whom the poet Prince immortalized alongside his grandfather Sir Richard in the famous epigram:
> “Where next shall famous Grenvil's ashes stand?
> Thy grandsire fills the sea, and thou the land.”

Our story belongs to a simpler, sterner age, and the old house that had stood there in part since grey, mythic medieval days, when the first Sir Richard, son of Hamon Dentatus, Lord of Carboyle, grandson of Duke Rollo of Normandy, settled at Bideford after slaying the Prince of South Wales and the Lord of Glamorgan, and gifted all his conquered South Welsh lands to the Cistercian monks of Neath. It was a sprawling, rambling half-castle, half-manor, much like the surviving Compton Castle near Torquay – home of Humphrey Gilbert, Walter Raleigh’s half-brother and Sir Richard’s dearest friend, of whom we will hear more soon. To the north, west, and south, the lofty old curtain walls of the original Norman bailey still stood, lined with machicolated turrets, narrow arrow slits, and deep hidden murder holes designed to drop stones and boiling pitch down on the heads of besiegers, relics of a far more violent, unsettled era. But the southern bailey had long been converted to a flower garden, stepped with quaint terraces, dotted with stone statues, laid out with knots of scented blooms, clipped yews and hollies, all the careful, pedant art of the topiarian’s craft. To the east, where the valley view opened wide, the old fortifications were gone, and the frowning Norman keep ruined in the Wars of the Roses had been replaced by the rich, carved, bright architecture of the Tudors. The whole house, like the era it stood in, was in a state of transition, a perfect mirror of the old Middle Ages giving way to the new, bright, blooming life of exploration and discovery that was bursting across all of Europe.

From the house, the hill sloped steeply down on three sides, and the garden path where Sir Richard and Amyas walked unfolded a view that could only be called truly English. One turn of the path gave them a glimpse over the western wall of the blue Atlantic, dotted with far-off passing sails; the next revealed range after range of fertile deer park, stately elm avenues, golden autumn woodland, and purple heather moorland, rolling one over the other up the valley sides to the old pre-Roman British earthwork, black and overgrown with gorse, perched on its conical peak. And rising sharp against the sky on the highest ridge closing the valley to the east was the tall tower of Kilkhampton Church, lined with the monuments and memorial gifts of five centuries of Grenvilles. A soft yellow autumn haze hung low over park, wood, and moor; red cattle lowed to each other as they stood swishing flies away in the shallow brook far below; foals in the horse paddock close at hand whinnied as they chased each other, and their sires from the Queen’s Park on the opposite hill answered back in deeper, fainter calls. A rutting stag’s deep roar shook the quiet woodland, and a rival far up the valley called back a ringing note of challenge, echoed again from the mist-shrouded heather brows high overhead. And close by, on the terrace directly before the house, surrounded by romping spaniels and golden-haired Grenville children, sat Lady Grenville herself – the beautiful St. Leger of Annery, the central jewel of that whole glorious place – looking first down at her children, then up at her noble husband, then out over that broad, sunlit western paradise, until life felt almost too full of happiness for mortal souls to bear.

All the while Amyas and Sir Richard paced slowly, talking long, earnestly, and low, for both knew this was the great turning point of the boy’s young life.

“Aye,” Sir Richard said, after Amyas, in his plain, blunt way, had told him the whole tale of his foolish infatuation with Rose Salterne, and his brother Frank’s choice to renounce that fruitless love too, “aye, sweet lad, you have chosen the better path, you and your brother both, and no man shall take that gift from you. Only be strong, lad, and trust that God will make a true man of you.”

“I do trust Him,” Amyas said.

“Thank God for that,” Sir Richard replied. “You have lifted the great weight off my heart that I carried for you from the day your good father, who sleeps in peace, gave you into my care. For all the best things in the world, Amyas, when misused, turn to the very worst: the love of a woman, which can lift a man’s soul all the way to heaven, can drag him just as easily down to hell. But you have learned better. You know, as our old German forefathers did, as Tacitus wrote, that *late is the love of young men, and so their strength of manhood never runs dry*. And more than that: that silly, frivolous fashion of the French and Italians, where no boy counts himself a man unless he hangs at some woman’s apron strings, fawning over maids and married wives alike, that habit is no less poisonous to the soul than open sin. It breeds vanity and waste, envy and bitter resentment, even hatred and murder, all too often. Even if you escape that, you squander the rich treasure of your manly, full-hearted worship on a dozen trivial flings, so that when your own bride at last comes to you, all that is left for her is the dregs, the empty leftover of your heart, and she becomes no more than a pretty ornament for your table, a mere means to give you heirs. God, who saved me from that living death, save you too!” As he spoke, he glanced down at his wife on the terrace below, and she, as if guessing instinctively that he spoke of her, looked up with a smile so soft and sweet that Sir Richard’s stern, weathered warrior face melted into a glow of pure, bright happiness.

Amyas looked at them both and sighed, then turned the conversation sharply.

“Then I may leave for Ireland tomorrow?”

“You shall sail on the *Mary* for Milford Haven, carrying these dispatches to Captain Winter. If the wind holds, you may tell the master to drop down the river tonight and put to sea at once – we cannot waste a single day.”

“Winter?” Amyas frowned. “He is no friend of mine, the coward who abandoned Drake and the rest of us in the Straits of Magellan.”

“Duty does not wait for private quarrels, even just ones, lad. But he will not be your commanding officer. When you reach the Lord Deputy, give him this letter, and he will set you to work – hard work, I promise you.”

“I want nothing better.”

“Well said. The best reward for work well done is more work to do. The man who is faithful over a few small things will always be given charge over many greater ones. That is the only true, heroic rest worthy of gentlemen and sons of God. As for the men who look for idleness in this life or the next, who hope God will feed them nice things like a fussy nurse feeds a spoiled child, I count them cowards and base, even if they call themselves saints and the elect.”

“I wish you could make my poor cousin see that.”

“He has yet to learn what it means to lose your life to save it, Amyas. Bad men have taught him – and I fear these Anabaptists and strict Puritans at home teach little else – that the one great business of every man is to save his own soul after he dies, every man for himself alone, that this selfish preoccupation, not divine self-sacrifice, is the one thing needful, the better part Mary chose in the Gospel.”

“Men are selfish enough already, without being taught to make a virtue of it,” Amyas said.

“Aye, lad. For my part, if I could hang up such a teacher high for an enemy of mankind and a corrupter of youth, I would do it gladly. Is there not enough cowardice and self-seeking already in the hearts of us fallen sons of Adam, without these false prophets using the bait of heaven and the terror of hell to turn our dirtiest vices into holy virtues and the surest way to bliss? Farewell to chivalry and desperate valor, farewell to patriotism and loyalty, farewell to England and the manhood of England, once our preachers start bidding every man, Jesuit-like, to care first and foremost for what they call the safety of his own soul. Every man will be afraid to die at his post, because he will fear he is not fit to meet his end. Amyas, do your duty like a man, to your country, your queen, and your God, and count your own life a worthless thing, as all the holy men of old did. Do your work, lad, and leave your soul in the care of the One who is just and merciful, who rewards every man according to his deeds. God shows no favor to persons. Now come in, take your letters, and mount your horse. If I hear you died at Smerwick Fort, all your wounds in front, I shall weep for your mother, lad – but I shall never weep for you.”

If any reader is startled to find a fine gentleman and hardened warrior like Sir Richard quoting and thinking deeply on Scripture, let them turn to the writings of that age, and see for themselves how thoroughly the men of that world were steeped in the Gospel, free from any taint of superstitious fear or false, performative piety. The faith of those days was one no soldier need ever have been ashamed to confess. Sir Richard died as he lived, without a shudder, without a whine: these were his last words, fifteen years later, when he lay shot through and through, a captive among Spanish Catholics, surrounded by priests, crucifixes, and all the trappings of the Inquisition: “Here die I, Richard Grenville, with a joyful and quiet mind; for that I have ended my life as a true soldier ought, fighting for his country, queen, religion, and honor: my soul willingly departing from this body, leaving behind the lasting fame of having behaved as every valiant soldier is in his duty bound to do.” Those were the last words of the man the pulpits of Elizabeth’s day had taught to serve his God and his country fearlessly.

But back to our tale. The day’s events were far from over. When Sir Richard and Amyas stepped into the great hall, the old steward came hurrying up, flustered, in search of his master.

“There’s a roguish sort of masterless man at the gate, Sir Richard, a forward fellow who swears he won’t leave till he speaks to you.”

“A masterless man? He’d better not speak to me, unless he’s got a fancy for gaol and the gallows.”

“Well, your worship,” the steward said, “I reckon that’s just what he wants, for he’s sworn he’ll not budge an inch from the gate until he sees you.”

“Sees me? By Halidame, he shall see me, here and at Launceston Gaol too, if he likes. Bring him in.”

“Faith, Sir Richard, we’re half afraid – with your good leave –”

“Hey, Tony,” Amyas laughed. “Who ever heard of being afraid when Sir Richard gives you leave?”

“What, does the fellow have horns and a tail?”

“Mass no! But we fear it’s treason for your honor, for the man’s pricked all over with heathen tattoos, brown as a filbert nut, tall and raw-boned as a plough ox, a foreigner, and carrying a great heavy staff. We think he’s some sort of Jesuit, or wild Irish rebel. The grooms won’t lay a hand on him, and the dogs won’t go near him, else he’d have had a ducking under the pump by now – the men that saw him coming up the hill swear he breathed fire out of his mouth.”

“Fire out of his mouth?” Sir Richard huffed. “The men are drunk.”

“Pricked all over? That’s a sailor’s mark,” Amyas said. “Let me go out and see the fellow. If he needs throwing out –”

“He’s not so big you couldn’t tuck him in your coat pocket. Go on, lad, while I finish writing my dispatches.”

Amyas went out to the back gate, where the tall, ragged, raw-boned man leaned on his heavy staff, as covered in tattoos as the steward had said.

“Hey, mate!” Amyas called. “Before we talk, you’ll be so good as to lay down that Plymouth cloak of yours.” He nodded at the cudgel, the common West Country sailor’s nickname for the stout club all mariners carried.

“I’ll warrant,” the old steward muttered, “where he found that ‘cloak’ he found a purse he stole not long before.”

“But no hose or doublet to go with it, so the magic power of his staff hasn’t done him much good. Lay it down, man, and speak like a Christian, if you are one.”

“I am a Christian, even if I look like a heathen, and no rogue, though masterless, alas. I want nothing, deserve nothing, I only ask to speak to Sir Richard before I go on my way.”

There was a quiet, humble dignity to the man’s tone that caught Amyas’s attention. He asked more gently where he was bound, and where he had come from.

“From Padstow port, sir, bound for Clovelly town, to see my old mother, if she is yet alive, which God alone knows.”

“A Clovelly man! Why didn’t you say so in the first place?” all the grooms cried out at once, for any West Countryman was a brother to them. The old steward spoke up.

“What’s your mother’s name, then?”

“Susan Yeo.”

“What, the one who lived under the old archway by the quay?” a groom asked.

“Lived?” the man said.

“Aye, sure enough, we heard she died three days back, poor soul.”

The man stood perfectly silent, unmoving, for a full minute, then said quietly to himself, in fluent Spanish: “That which is, is best.”

“You speak Spanish?” Amyas asked, more and more curious.

“I had to, young sir. I was five years in the Spanish Main, and only set foot on English soil two days ago. If you let me speak to Sir Richard, I will tell him a tale that will make the ears of everyone that hears it tingle. If I cannot, I will go on to Mr. Cary of Clovelly, if he still lives, and unburden my soul to him. But I would far rather speak to a man who is a mariner, like myself.”

“And you shall,” Amyas said. “Steward, we will bring this man inside. Rags and all, he is a man of wit and honesty.” He led the sailor in.

“I only hope he is not one of those Popish murderers,” the old steward said, keeping a safe distance from him as they crossed the hall.

“Popish, old master? There’s little fear of that. Look here!” He pulled back the ragged edge of his shirt, to show a ghastly, silvery scar that circled his wrist and wound all the way up his forearm.

“I got that on the rack, in the Lima Inquisition,” he said, quiet as anything.

“O Lord, why didn’t you tell us you were a poor persecuted Christian?” the steward stammered, suddenly penitent.

“Because I have never asked for pity, and I was not going to turn myself into a beggar or a sideshow for that scar. The Lord delivered me, that is enough.”

“By heaven, you are a brave fellow!” Amyas cried. “Come straight to Sir Richard’s study at once.”

They walked in to find Sir Richard at his desk, surrounded by books, dispatches, state papers, and warrants. Though he was not yet, as he would be in later years, admiral, general, Member of Parliament, Privy Councillor, and justice of the peace all at once, there were few great men in the realm he did not correspond with, few great state matters he did not have a hand in.

“Hey there, Amyas! Have you tied up this wild man already, and brought him in to swear allegiance to the queen?”

Before Amyas could answer, the sailor looked at him, sharp. “Amyas? Is that your name, sir?”
