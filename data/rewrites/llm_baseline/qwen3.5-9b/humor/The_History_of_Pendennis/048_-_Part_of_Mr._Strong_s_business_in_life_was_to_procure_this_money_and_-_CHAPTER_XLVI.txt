Noble Henry Foker, whom we haven't seen for a few pages, has been busy, as expected of a man of such stubborn devotion, pursuing and indulging his all-consuming passion for love.

I'd like to suggest that some of my young readers, who seem fond of this sort of thing, take the trouble to calculate exactly how much time is wasted on it. They'll discover it's one of the most expensive hobbies a human being can afford. What sacrifices don't you make, you young gentlemen and ladies of unruly minds? First, you burn through hours of precious sleep, tossing and turning thinking about your beloved object, only to drag yourself down to breakfast late, while everyone else is already off to their daily duties. Once you finally join them, you pay no attention whatsoever; your mind is elsewhere, engaged in nothing with any real energy. You slough off the day's work, ignore your friends and family—the people naturally meant to accompany you in life—just so you can catch a glimpse of your darling, peek at her window, or spy her carriage in the park. At night, the simple charms of home bore you; your mother's chatter grows tiresome; the delicious meals she prepares for her favorite are sent away untouched. Indeed, the entire feast of life loses its flavor, save for one specific dish. Everything useful and dear once becomes unbearable, and you feel no peace unless you are actively chasing your flame.

I believe this is frequently the state of mind among poorly regulated young men, and certainly it was Mr. H. Foker's condition. Having been raised to indulge every inclination he possessed, he threw himself into this one with his usual selfish enthusiasm. Don't let anyone be surprised that Mr. Foker became a slave to passion, even after giving his friend Arthur Pendennis excellent advice on a previous occasion. Who among us hasn't given our friends the very best advice? Who hasn't preached sermons while failing to practice them ourselves? Perhaps you, madam, are a perfect being, never harboring a wrong thought in your frigid, blameless existence. Or maybe you, sir, are so strong-minded that no foolish passion can disturb your calm in the chambers or your focus on the Stock Exchange; you're so robust you don't need sympathy. Well, we won't give you any; we save ours for the humble and weak, those who stumble, rise again, and march along with the rest of humanity. What need do you have of a hand that never falls? Your serene virtue is never clouded by passion, ruffled by temptation, or darkened by remorse; compassion would be an insult to such an angel. But then, companionship becomes impossible for someone so elevated; you are necessarily lonely due to your high attributes. We can't reach up to chat familiarly with such potentates, so goodbye to you; our path lies with humble folk, not serene highnesses like you. Just so, we declare there are no perfect characters in this history, except perhaps one tiny exception—and even she isn't truly perfect, for she doesn't realize it to this day. With a pathetic misunderstanding and twisted humility, she believes herself to be as great a sinner as anyone needs to be.

This young lady happens not to be in London right now, and it certainly isn't for someone like her that Mr. Henry Foker's mind is currently agitated. But what difference does a few flaws make? Do we need to be angels, male or female, to be worshipped as such? Let us celebrate the diversity of human tastes. The oldest, ugliest, stupidest, most pompous, silliest, vapid, greatest criminal, tyrant, booby, Bluebeard, Catherine Hayes, George Barnwell—we need never despair. I've read of a transported pickpocket's passion for a female convict (both aged, repulsive, ignorant, quarrelsome, and prone to drinking) that was as magnificent as the loves of Cleopatra and Antony, or Lancelot and Guinevere. The passion Count Borulawski, the Polish dwarf, inspired in the bosom of the most beautiful Baroness at the Dresden court is common knowledge. The flame that burned in young Cornet Tozer's heart recently, causing him to flee and marry Mrs. Battersby, who was old enough to be his mother—all these stories fill the pages of history or newspaper columns. Should we be ashamed or pleased to know our hearts are formed such that the biggest, highest-placed Ajax among us might someday find himself prostrate before the pattens of his kitchen maid? That there is no poverty, shame, or crime that won't be supported, embraced with delight, and cherished more closely than virtue itself, by the perverse fidelity and admirable, constant folly of a woman?

Thus, Henry Foker, Esquire, longed for his love and cursed the fate separating them. When Lord Gravesend's family retreated to the countryside (leaving his proxy with the venerable Lord Bagwig), Harry remained lingering in London, certainly not to the sorrow of Lady Ann, to whom he was betrothed, who missed him not in the slightest. Whichever way Miss Clavering went, this infatuated young fellow followed; knowing his engagement to his cousin was public knowledge, he was forced to keep his passion a mystery, confined strictly to his own chest. It was so pent up and pressed down inside him that it's a miracle he didn't eventually explode with the stormy secret and perish collapsed after the outburst.

A grand entertainment took place at Gaunt House on a beautiful June evening, and the following day's journals filled nearly two columns with the names of the nobility and gentry honored with invitations. Among the guests were Sir Francis and Lady Clavering, Miss Amory (whose invitation the indefatigable Major Pendennis had secured), and our two young friends, Arthur and Harry. Both exerted themselves, dancing extensively with Miss Blanche. As for the worthy Major, he assumed charge of Lady Clavering, ensuring she was escorted to that department of the mansion where her ladyship particularly excelled: the refreshment room. Surrounded by paintings of Titian and Giorgione, regal portraits of Van Dyck and Reynolds, enormous golden and silver salvers, pyramids of large flowers, and constellations of wax candles—in short, with a disregard for expense that bordered on the absurd—a supper was served throughout the night. How many creams, jellies, salads, peaches, white soups, grapes, pâtés, galantines, cups of tea, champagne, and so on, Lady Clavering consumed, it is not proper for us to detail. How much suffering the Major endured as he followed the honest woman around, summoning solemn male attendants and lovely servant-maids, attending to Lady Clavering's various needs with admirable patience, remains unknown; he never confessed. He never allowed his agony to show on his face in the least; instead, with constant kindness, he brought plate after plate to the Begum.

Mr. Wagg attempted to tally every dish Lady Clavering partook of as long as he could count (though, having partaken freely of champagne himself during the evening, his calculating powers were unreliable by the end). He recommended Mr. Honeyman, Lady Steyne's physician, to watch over the Begum carefully and check on her the next day.

Sir Francis Clavering appeared, skulking briefly through the magnificent rooms, but finding neither the company nor the splendor to his taste, he tossed off a couple of glasses of wine at the buffet and departed for the neighborhood of Jermyn Street, where his friends Loder, Punter, little Moss Abrams, and Captain Skewball were gathered at their familiar green table. In the rattling of the carriage and their pleasant conversation, Sir Francis's spirits rose to their customary level of feeble hilarity.

Mr. Pynsent, who had requested Miss Amory's hand in dance, approached to claim her, but having already exchanged scowls of recognition with Mr. Arthur Pendennis in the ballroom, Arthur suddenly rose and claimed Miss Amory as his partner for the current dance. Mr. Pynsent, biting his lips and scowling even more savagely, withdrew with a profound bow, stating he relinquished his claim. Some men are simply destined to cross your path in life. Pynsent and Pen held this mutual view; each regarded the other accordingly.

"What a confounded conceited provincial fool that is!" thought one. "Because he wrote a twopenny novel, his absurd head is turned; a good kicking would take his conceit out of him."

"What an impertinent idiot that man is!" remarked the other to his partner. "His soul resides in Downing Street; his necktie is foolscap; his hair is sand; his legs are rulers; his insides are tape and sealing wax; he was a prig in his cradle and hasn't laughed since birth, except three times at the same joke of his boss. I share the same dislike for that man, Miss Amory, as I do for that cold boiled veal." Upon this, Blanche naturally remarked that Mr. Pendennis was wicked, malicious, and utterly abominable, wondering what he would say when her back was turned.

"Say! Say that you possess the most beautiful figure and the slimmest waist in the world, Blanche—I mean Miss Amory. I beg your pardon. Another turn; this music would make an alderman dance."

"And you've stopped tumbling when you waltz now?" Blanche asked, archly looking up at her partner's face.
