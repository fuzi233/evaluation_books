Gerard walked silent at Teresa’s side, his artist’s mind turning over and over to puzzle out what fate she was steering him toward rather than simply asking, as a plainer soul would have done. At last she spoke unprompted, explaining that a friend had tipped her off a goldsmith’s wife nearby was seeking a scribe. They reached the shop within minutes, and Teresa made her plea: “Madama, Leonora told me you have need of a writer. I have brought you the finest one there is—he snatched my child from the waves at sea, and for Our Lady’s grace, I beg you look on him with favour.”
The goldsmith’s wife, Olympia, complied in one sense: her eyes fixed on Gerard’s comely, earnest face and she could barely tear them away. But her answer was no. “I have no use for a scribe myself,” she said, suddenly recalling. “Ah, but my gossip Claelia, the sausage-maker, was asking for one but three days past.”
Teresa bowed politely and withdrew. Claelia’s house was some distance off, and when they arrived, she was out. Teresa seated herself on a stone step, still and statuesque, so calm that Gerard was halfway pulling a charcoal sketch of her from his pocket when the sausage-maker rounded the corner. She repeated her plea, leading Gerard forward by the hand, but Claelia shook her head. “I’ve no need of a writer—though my friend Appia Claudia the tailor, who lives on Via Lepida, was badgering me for a lead to one no later than yesterday.”
Gerard protested that he did not want to drag her on a wild goose chase all across Rome, but Teresa only blinked at him, mild but firm. “What good thing ever was done without a little patience? I will knock on every door in this city before you go without the work you have earned.”
It was then Gerard noted the man shadowing them, sometimes far off, sometimes drawing near enough that his footsteps echoed theirs. “We are followed,” he warned.
Teresa’s cheek flushed faintly, but her tone was cool. “I saw him long ago. It is my poor Lodovico.” She stopped, turned, and crooked her finger. The man approached sheepishly, and she bade him greet the young scribe who had plucked his wife and infant from the stormy sea. The forced grin fell off Lodovico’s face at that, and he embraced Gerard with genuine, tearful gratitude—though there remained something furtive about the way he had trailed them that left Gerard ill at ease. He refused to cross the Tiber with them, however, and turned back toward their home, leaving the pair to walk on, Teresa’s mood turning quiet and somber.
Appia Claudia sent them no farther than a hundred yards down the lane to Lucretia, the merry little glove-seller, who laughed and admitted she kept all her shop’s accounts in her head, and had no use for a scribe. “It is for my confessor, Fra Colonna, of the Colonna house, that I asked after one,” she explained. “He is mad for good writers, and illuminators, and all learned folk. He would turn away a duke mid-audience if a Greek scholar was closeted with him pouring over an ancient parchment. And he has coin enough, never fear—any of his noble kinsmen would pour a thousand crowns into his lap if he but asked.”
She then told them the famous story of the time she’d tried to rouse him from his perpetual reverie by confessing she’d murdered her lodger’s little girl and baked her into a pie, and he had only blinked awake to assign her three Paternosters, three Aves, and no butter or eggs the following Wednesday before wandering off again, half in a world of old texts. “But mind this,” she warned, suddenly serious. “Hate bunglers worse than poison. A man brought him a clumsily carved crucifix last week, and he raged so loud at the fellow for shoddy work made in mockery of the Christ’s agony that the whole street heard him. If you cannot prove your skill, do not go near him.”
Teresa drew herself up proudly. “I will answer for his skill. He saved my child.”
Gerard, however, was nervous. The friar had seen the work of every great scribe in Italy, he pointed out, and he would far rather spend a day crafting a perfect sample of his work than walk in blind. He begged for a single day’s grace, and Teresa consented at once, cutting off Lucretia’s quip that he was a coward to defend him: “He is not a coward. He is modest.”
As they walked back toward Gerard’s lodging, his eye caught a flawless, creamy white vellum skin in a shop window. He ached to buy it, but had not the coin, and hurried past before he could linger too long. He was halfway resolved to take the old family deed he had carried all the way from Sevenbergen, his last precious link to his father’s land claims, and erase the old writing to make his sample, when he sat down and pored over the faded, cramped script. To destroy it would be reckless, he realized. He would decipher every word at his leisure, not waste it on a single scribe’s test.
He trundled downstairs, resolved to buy a small scrap of cheaper vellum with his last few coins, only to find Teresa chatting with his landlady. The woman pulled something from under her apron: the very perfect vellum skin he had longed for. Teresa had seen the way his gaze had lingered on it through the shop window, seen the quiet sigh he’d let out when he turned away, and bought it for him, no questions asked. Before he could stammer out thanks or offer to pay her, the Roman matron bade him goodbye and slipped away down the street.
The next afternoon, Lucretia’s son led Gerard to Fra Colonna’s lodgings. He tipped the friar’s housekeeper Onesta, and she ushered him into a large chamber heaped high with manuscripts, carved ivory, half-finished paintings, and odd antiquities from every corner of the known world. The friar himself sat in the middle of that glorious, loving chaos, poring over an Arabian manuscript, and looked up peevishly at the interruption.
Gerard’s hands shook too hard to write a fresh sample on the spot, he admitted, but he had brought a finished vellum page, with Greek text illuminated in cobalt and gold, and its Latin translation penned neatly beneath. He laid it in the friar’s outstretched hand and waited, heart in his throat. The verdict staggered him: the Dominican jumped to his feet and threw his arms around Gerard’s neck, laughing out loud with joy.
Across the sea, in Holland, an event unfolded that no man then living, no reader even now, could have guessed would twist Gerard’s fate so far off its path. A procession came tramping up the road to Eli’s cottage, a strange centaur of a sight: a man mounted on a horse so absurdly over-decorated you could barely see a single inch of honest horseflesh. Its ears were sheathed in linen tipped with blue and silver, its body swaddled in stiff brocade that hung all the way to the ground, its tail tucked so far out of sight it might as well have never existed. Only its eyes gleamed through two cut holes in the fabric, and its tiny front hoofs peeped out like rats as it minced along.
This absurd, over-gilded creature bore the will of the duke, straight from his progress at the nearby tournament. The man on its back hailed Eli and his wife Catherine, who appeared in the doorway, and announced he had come to collect their dwarf son Giles for the court.
Catherine shrieked that there was no dwarf in their house, but Eli sighed and corrected her. “He means our Giles, who is small of stature. There is no gainsaying the duke’s messengers.”
The emissary confirmed it: their last court dwarf had tumbled down an unlatched well only days before, and the duke had sent for Giles to fill the post. When Catherine raged that she would never let her son go to a household so careless it let its wells lie open like wolf traps, the messenger’s face turned hard. Obey, he told them, and Giles would be fed like a bishop and clothed in cloth of gold. Refuse, and all three of their heads would be stuck on poles, the bellman crying out that they were traitors who hoarded the duke’s rightful property.
Before the dispute could drag on, the object of it stepped out the door on his own, and sided with the crown. “My folk are daft, but I am not,” he roared. “I’ll go with you, right now.”
He brushed off his mother’s tears, telling her he was sick of moping around the cottage ever since Gerard had left, he would never be farther than Rotterdam, and no duke alive could keep him there if he did not like it. He refused the tiny stunted dwarf mule they had brought for him, saying he hated all small things, and demanded he be given the highest, biggest horse in the whole retinue. When the servants warned him the big horse kicked and would not let a little man mount him, Giles dashed back inside, climbed to an upper window, fastened a rope to the frame, and slid down as smooth as an oiled bolt of lightning, landing square astride the big horse’s back without the beast even noticing he was there.
Catherine fussed over him, crying, and mended the tiny tear in his sleeve he had made climbing out the window, her tears dripping onto his arm as she stitched. Then Giles rode off, laughing and jocund, while his parents wept over his virtues back at the cottage, blind to every one of his rough edges. He arrived at court and became an immediate favorite, and soon won a privilege no other soul in the realm dared claim: he was allowed to speak nothing but the unvarnished truth, and he bawled it loud enough for the whole palace to hear, to the utter horror of every courtier who crossed his path.
