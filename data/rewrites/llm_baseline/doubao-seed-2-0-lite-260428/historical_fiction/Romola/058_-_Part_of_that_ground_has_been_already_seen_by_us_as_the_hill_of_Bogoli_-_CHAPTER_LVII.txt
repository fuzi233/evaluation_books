Tito’s claim that he stood in no danger from the unspooling Medicean conspiracy was no idle boast. For three full months, ever since he first foresaw the plot’s exposure as a near-certainty, he had laid every possible contingency to secure his own advantage. He had strengthened his ties to powerful contacts in Rome and Milan, feeding them a steady stream of secret intelligence and subtle, unmarked schemes to undermine Savonarola and the popular republican party, while simultaneously courting the Florentine Piagnoni faction far more assiduously than ever, dropping careful, unforced hints that his political loyalties lay entirely on their side. All the while he never withdrew his service from the Medicean conspirators, instead pushing to take on more critical tasks and earn their complete, exclusive trust.

This triple game came easily to him. The Mediceans operated on the unspoken rule that deceiving one’s enemies was fair stratagem, and their own shared blood ties, inherited loyalties, and intergenerational grudges made them incapable of trusting any outsider as thoroughly as they trusted their own. It never occurred to them that the lack of traditional Medicean family stakes that made Tito such a convenient, unconnected agent was also the absence of the only guarantee of mutual honor they recognized among themselves. The hardline Arrabbiati anti-Savonarola factions in Rome and Milan ran spy networks where the most convincing hypocrisy counted as the highest form of service, so no one there would ever dream of suspecting a deep-cover agent simply because he played his part too well. And the Piagnoni, earnest and uncomplicated in their devout political convictions, were all the more ready to credit Tito’s loyalty to them precisely because he never affected any of the friar’s religious fervor, making his political alignment feel unfeigned.

The three months had been a stretch of unbroken, flattering success for Tito, and the goal he most coveted was a permanent, high-ranking post in one of the great northern Italian capitals, where life was softer, and the rewards for talent and learning far more glittering than anything Florence could offer. At present, Milan pulled ahead of Rome in his calculations: if he could deliver a few critical favors to Duke Ludovico Sforza before the year ended, he was assured of a place at the Milanese court that far outstripped any possible advantages a position in Rome could grant.

He had long foreseen the Medicean plot would eventually be uncovered, but he had never guessed how. The arrest of Lamberto dell’ Antella, caught red-handed with a damning letter and burning with personal rancor against the Medici, was a total, unforeseen wild card. Tito had never expected to escape implication if the full conspiracy came to light, no matter how carefully he had hidden his own agency behind layers of pretext. But his sharp, quick mind had mapped out the perfect path to safety almost instantly, one that would cost him as little unpleasantness as possible. Any man would prefer to keep his whole skin, after all — and Tito’s skin was peculiarly sensitive to the chill of public disapproval.

His reckoning was not wrong. By the time he turned for home that night, he had secured the three guarantees he cared for most: all charges of complicity in the Medicean plot against him would be dropped, he would retain his secretary post for a full year before he chose to resign it, and the price he had paid for those concessions would be sealed forever as a strict state secret. Most men would have called that price ruinously heavy, and Tito would far rather not have paid it at all.

He had gone first to win over Francesco Valori, not only his direct superior as one of the Ten of War, but the head of the Piagnoni and a core member of the special council appointed to investigate the conspiracy’s evidence. Valori was a man of fierce, unyielding virtue warped by violent partisanship, an arrogant temper that alienated even his allies, and a list of bitter personal feuds at the very top of which sat his lifelong hatred for Bernardo del Nero. Tito secured a private audience, extracted a pledge of secrecy, and openly confessed his past work for the Mediceans — framing it as a pragmatic move he had always known was futile, and swearing his true loyalties lay fully with the popular republican government. He even confided a faked personal dislike for del Nero to Valori, before dropping the critical revelation that a second, unexposed Medicean plot was underway: Piero de’ Medici was gathering forces in Romagna, and planned to march on Florence to seize power before the end of August. If the council granted him the immunity and guarantees he asked for, he claimed, he could travel to Siena and Romagna to retrieve the hard documentary evidence that would cement the conspirators’ guilt — but it was essential he kept his cover as a loyal Medicean agent fully intact, and his role as an informant remain entirely secret.

Valori never paused to weigh the moral character of Tito’s proposal. All his focus was fixed on securing an unarguably harsh sentence for the five arrested conspirators, for every prominent family in Florence would soon lobby fiercely for their clemency. Legal procedure in 1490s Florence ran on the unspoken understanding that inconvenient evidence could only be extracted through torture; Lamberto dell’ Antella had already been tortured to make him spill every name he could possibly recall, but the council still lacked ironclad proof against Bernardo del Nero, who had never actively plotted to overthrow the republic, merely failed to report the conspiracy after he had tried and failed to talk the others out of it. Valori was absolutely convinced the safety of Florence required del Nero’s guilt to be spelled out in unignorable, unassailable terms — a conviction stoked far hotter by his personal hatred, even if he never admitted that truth to himself.

He accepted Tito’s offer, laid the proposal before his fellow council members without ever naming his source, and won their unanimous approval. Late that afternoon, Tito was brought before the twenty elders of the special council, standing graceful and unflappable as he laid out the details of the hidden second plot, speaking of treason and conspiracy with easy, unaffected phrasing, and framing his choices as the honest product of practical self-interest rather than lofty, unrealistic patriotism — an affectation so polished none of the men spotted it for the lie it was. They gave him official orders to leave for Siena at dawn, and tucked inside his tunic he carried a signed, written guarantee of his full immunity and his right to keep his post for a full year.

A handful of those councilmen, sharp sober minds not fully preoccupied with the urgency of the investigation, passed a quiet new judgment on Tito that day: this clever, useful Greek would not be a man to invite to family dinners, but he was perfect for the messy, underhanded work of state no man of good public reputation could sully his hands with. Their great poet Pulci had summed up that universal Florentine attitude for generations: *“Il tradimento a molti piace assai, / Ma il traditore a gnun non piacque mal.”* Many love the fruits of treachery well, but none the traitor for his part can tell. Their world was a messy, half-forged state of struggling order, where a man could be hanged for murder and hailed a martyr for the exact same act, hissed for a cowardly betrayal and hissed just as loud for speaking unvarnished, generous truth.

Tito was fully aware he had sunk a little in their estimation. He had always prided himself on being clever enough to act as he pleased while never giving anyone a clear reason to disapprove of him — the only person who had ever seen through him completely was Romola, which was why the rift between them had been uncrossable. The faint cold whiff of quiet disdain he could sense in the council chamber stung like unwelcome weather. But he reasoned to himself that no clear-headed, intelligent man would ruin a perfectly built, promising future simply because the Medicean faction lost. He bore Bernardo del Nero no real ill will, he had even genuinely liked Lorenzo Tornabuoni and Giannozzo Pucci, and served them faithfully when their side had seemed poised to win. He told himself no sensible man let his life be ruled by childish loyalty, family pride, or the delusional religious fervor of fanatics who believed in a coming heavenly renovation of Florence. Look at Pontanus, the great Latin poet and philosopher, who had given the grandest oration of his life welcoming the French king who came to dethrone his own royal patron, and still held his head high and prospered. Men never truly cared about moral scruples, he reasoned, they only cared if you held power. Weakness was the only thing the world ever truly scorned.

This act of deliberate betrayal, Tito knew, was a new threshold he had never crossed before. His earlier betrayal of Baldassarre had been hidden behind layer after layer of successful lies, but this new role as informant could never be fully concealed — he would carry the constant risk of public disgrace with him for the rest of his days. But it was temporary. In six months, a year at most, he would turn his back on Florence forever, leave behind all their petty, bitter factional fights, their fading fortunes. He was twenty-eight, at the peak of his wit and charm, he had already stashed a large sum of his fortune safely outside the city walls. He could shed every mistake of his past — his messy unhappy marriage, the ghost of Baldassarre, all his old tangled loyalties — like an old rehearsal costume, throw away the ragged old bundle, and robe himself for the grand, glittering real life that waited for him in a northern royal court.

He never even noticed, as he brushed past a man in the dim lantern glow on his way down the palace stairs, that the man who had stepped aside to watch him go was Ser Ceccone — another wily informant, perfectly happy to sell out his former employers to the state for his own profit, his sharp eyes fixed on Tito’s retreating back.
