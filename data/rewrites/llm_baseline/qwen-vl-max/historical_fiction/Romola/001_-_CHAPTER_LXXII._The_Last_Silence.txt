More than three hundred and fifty years ago, in the soft light of spring 1492, the dawn swept across the world with a quiet, unchanging grace. As the angel of morning moved from the eastern shores to the western edges of Europe—over the Caucasus, across the snow-laden Alps, and into the shadowed isles of the Atlantic—the earth below lay much as it does today. The same mountains cast their long shadows over the valleys; the same olive groves climbed the hillsides; the pine forests stood dark and dense; and the broad plains shimmered with young wheat or grass refreshed by rain. Cities rose beside rivers, or nestled along coastlines where masts jutted like reeds from the water’s edge—just as they do now.

The first light of day crept into human homes, touching the warm cheeks of sleeping children, the weary eyes of those awakened by pain or sorrow, the swift rising of laborers whose hands were hardened by work, and the late slumber of scholars who had spent the night questioning stars, sages, or their own souls in search of truths that might lift the veil of mortality. They sought knowledge not just to understand the world, but to glimpse beyond it—to see the hidden path of life not as an end, but as part of a vast, radiant circle.

Great rivers still shaped the lives of men. And within hearts, the currents of desire, fear, love, and longing still flowed in familiar rhythms. As we follow the slow advance of dawn, we feel the enduring pattern of human existence: hunger and toil, planting and harvest, love and death—unchanged through centuries.

Even if we pause at one place in history, waiting for full daylight, we may find a city renowned throughout the world—one that has barely altered since the time of Columbus. It stands like a steadfast monument amid the shifting tides of time, reminding us that we are more alike our ancestors than different. The principles of architecture, the weight of stone and purpose, echo across ages. If the spirit of a Florentine man—whose last breath came while Columbus still pleaded for ships at Palos—were to return and stand once more on the hill of San Miniato, he would recognize the view.

He wears the clothes of his time: a black silk robe, called a *lucco*, falling straight from neck to ankle; a plain cap with a long strip of cloth, the *becchetto*, hanging down like a scarf. His face is sharp, perhaps not beautiful, but strong—his mouth firm, his chin clean-shaven. It bears the marks of a life lived deeply, among the banks of the Arno, beneath the sunlit towers of Florence.

As he looks out, the sense of familiarity overwhelms him. He sees the mountains, the river winding westward, the dark slopes of Mount Morello opposite, the valley of the Arno stretching toward the marble ridges of Carrara. He sees Fiesole crowned with monasteries and cypresses, and the green hills dotted with villas he once knew by name.

But it is not only the landscape that calls to him. Closer still, he sees the city itself. Though the seventy towers that once circled Florence like a crown are gone, his gaze skips past that absence. Instead, it draws to the single tall tower rising from the Old Palace—a slender column of stone, standing proud and unbroken after four centuries. Then there is the dome, larger than any other in the world, built from the vision of a small, keen-eyed architect—Brunelleschi—who once dreamed it into existence. It still rises above the hills, bold and unshaken.

And the bell towers—Giotto’s, with its distant glow of color; the graceful spire of the Badia—these he remembers from childhood, seen from his nurse’s shoulder. “Florence still knows how to ring her bells,” he thinks, “with that solemn sound that once stirred the hearts of her people.” And near by, Santa Croce stands in its long, dark form—where the city’s great dead were laid to rest, honored with laurels and praise. But then, there was no spire. “Why did the Franciscans build this?” he wonders. “If I had lived to see it, Brunelleschi or Michelozzo would have made something nobler—something worthy of Arnolfo’s church.”

He sighs and turns his eyes to the walls. Five of the eleven gates are sealed shut. The towers, once symbols of strength and pride, have been torn down. “Is peace so complete now?” he asks. “Do Florentines live without fear of exile, conspiracy, or armed return?” These questions linger uneasily. It is easier to know what remains than to understand what has changed.

Below, the Arno flows as ever, crossed by bridges—especially the Ponte Vecchio, unlike any other bridge in the world, lined with shops where he once paused, perhaps to watch the progress of Messer Luca Pitti’s grand palace, built from stones quarried from the Hill of Boboli. Or maybe he stopped to trade with the cloth-dressers in Oltrarno. The roofline of the Pitti is hidden from this height, but his heart does not yearn to see it. He wants to walk again among the narrow streets, the busy squares, to breathe the air of the city he loved.

Are the citizens still voting with black and white beans? Who holds power now in the Palazzo Vecchio? Do the Priori still dine with tripe and boiled partridges, laughing at the unfortunate target of their jests? Are the banners still hung from windows, displayed every two months beneath Orcagna’s Loggia?

Life once meant everything to him—not just wealth and trade, which reached from Syria to Britain, but also politics, fierce and intimate. He had known the thrill of public office: he had served on the Ten, managing war; on the Eight, overseeing internal affairs; among the Priori, leading the government. He had even risen to the highest rank—Gonfaloniere—and traveled as ambassador to the Pope and Venice. He had directed armies, though their battles were bloodless, their victories hollow—men fell not from courage, but from misstep or trampling.

Through all this, he had learned to judge men without hatred, seeing life as a game of skill, yet never forgetting honor. He believed in tradition, in duty, in the dignity of action—even when it was coarse or cruel. He loved chess under the loggia, and sharp wit, and jokes that mocked the powerful. He took pride in his learning, collecting rare manuscripts, ancient vases, and busts—some missing noses, but authentic nonetheless. In old age, he hurried to read the first pages of Homer printed in Florence, a marvel of the new press.

Yet he was not blind to faith. He kept a waxen image of himself beneath the Madonna Annunziata. He gave generously to shrines, even though he had joked about the monks. For the unseen powers were real. Who could say what names hid behind them? Was there not force in gems, even if they only touched the skin? Were not all things charged with hidden meaning?

Lucretius spoke wisely—he was ancient, a great poet. Luigi Pulci, too, seemed right when wine flowed and laughter rang, though he doubted all things above the roof. Some scholars claimed Aristotle was irreligious—yet a true thinker must consider all ideas. Still, doubt often felt false when time passed and the world turned with heavier faces.

Had not Christianity come to rule? Had he not been baptized in San Giovanni, where the dome is filled with images of judgment, and the altar holds a crucified Christ who unsettles complacency?

This spirit was not a pagan philosopher, nor a poet lost in thought. He was a man of the fifteenth century—caught between belief and disbelief, between Epicurean ease and superstitious dread, between rote ethics and raw passion, between a longing for pagan freedom and a deep, restless conscience.

He may have smiled, shaken his head, when common folk spoke of a Pope Angelico who would come to cleanse the Church of corruption and renew the clergy’s virtue. Such hopes seemed too far removed from the reality of Innocent VIII’s reign—a world too corrupt for a shrewd merchant to take seriously.

Yet he felt the sickness of his time. He cared for the city. Public spirit, though it could be flawed, was never wholly immoral—for it was rooted in care for the common good.

In that Lent of 1492, he heard a Dominican friar named Girolamo Savonarola preach in San Lorenzo. The man spoke boldly against the worldliness of the clergy, demanding that Christians stop living for comfort when injustice ruled. He urged them not to spend their wealth on lavish churches when their neighbors suffered from hunger and disease.

The preacher went too far, perhaps, for older ears. But it was remarkable to see women remove their jewelry and give it up to help the poor.

“That Prior of San Marco,” the spirit reflects, “was a man of fire—perhaps too harsh, too quick to speak of divine vengeance. Ah, *Iddio non paga il Sabatol*—God does not pay on Saturday. The sins of men often go unpunished for long. I saw evil thrive for generations. But a preacher trying to move a city—how could he be gentle? Still, he might have spoken less sharply to Lorenzo de’ Medici, whose family built San Marco. Was their quarrel ever settled? And Lorenzo himself—so wise, so subtle—did he recover from his illness at Careggi? He left the world with a face heavy with sorrow. And his son… many feared he would be another Rehoboam.

How has it all ended? Which party is exiled now? Whose houses lie empty and broken? Is there a new Lorenzo—someone whom the great Turk honors with gifts of exotic animals, relics, manuscripts, or fugitives? Someone both learned and devout, yet capable of wrath?

And who writes the Republic’s Latin letters? Who lectures on Dante in the Duomo, then returns home to write scathing attacks on critics who dared question his spelling? Are the wise leaning toward the Pope and Naples—or toward the voices of France and Milan?

There is wisdom to be found in the streets below, on the marble steps before the churches, beneath the sheltering loggias—where surely Florentines still talk, joke, argue, as they always have. The buildings are still there. The changes have not been so great.

“I will go down,” the spirit decides. “I will walk the familiar stones. I will hear the speech of my people once more.”

Go not down, good spirit. For the changes are deeper than you imagine. The words of Florentines would sound like riddles to your ears. Do not mingle with politicians on the marble. Ask no questions about trade in Calimara. Avoid inquiries into scholarship, whether in cloister or court.

Only look. Look at the sunlight and shadows playing on the great walls—walls built with strength and purpose, enduring through time. Look at the children, their laughter creating a new kind of light in the midst of age. Look into the churches, where the chants remain unchanged, where the images still speak of suffering for a greater end, of love that lifts upward, of glory that ascends.

See the faces turned upward, the lips moving in prayers for help—prayers that have not changed.

These things endure. The sun and shadow still bring beauty at dawn, noon, and dusk. The children still represent the eternal union of love and duty. Men still long for peace and justice. They still believe that the highest life is one of conscious sacrifice.

For the Pope Angelico has not yet come.
