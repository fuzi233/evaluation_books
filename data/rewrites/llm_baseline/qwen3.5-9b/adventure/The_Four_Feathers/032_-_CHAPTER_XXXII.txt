Ethne settled into the corner of a pew near the aisle, and Feversham stepped up to stand beside her. Inside the tiny church, the air was thick with a profound, sacred quietude. Afternoon sunlight streamed through the high windows, casting a golden haze across the vaulted ceiling, while the soft, natural murmur of the summer drifted pleasantly through the open doorway.

"I am glad you remembered our drive and what we said," Ethne began, her voice steady despite the tremor in her hands. "It matters deeply to me that you recall it. For, though I have brought you back to me, I am sending you away from me once more. You will become one of those absent friends whom I shall not mourn, precisely because you are absent."

She spoke slowly, her gaze fixed firmly ahead, refusing to falter. It was a harrowing speech for her to utter, yet she had rehearsed it nightly for the past fortnight until the words sat ready on her tongue. At the mere sight of Harry Feversham—recovered after so many years of suspense and agony—it had seemed impossible for her to speak them, no matter how necessary. Yet, standing face-to-face with him, she had forced herself to remember the necessity until she felt it fully. She had retreated to the pew, gathered her strength, and decided that it would be kinder for both of them if she showed no outward sign of the pain this swift separation caused. He would surely sense it anyway; she wished him to understand that not a single moment of his six years had been wasted in her eyes. But such understanding required no display of emotion.

So, keeping her eyes straight ahead and her voice even, she continued.

"I know you will mind greatly, just as I do. But there is no help for it. At least, you are home again, with the right to be so. It comforts me to know that. Yet, there are other, far greater reasons for comfort that bind us both. Colonel Trench told me enough of your captivity to confirm that we view your ordeal with identical eyes. We both understand that this second parting, however hard, pales in comparison to our first, at the house six years ago. I felt utterly lonely after that; I shall not feel lonely now. A great barrier separated us then, one that seemed insurmountable. We should never have met again, either here or later. I am certain of that. But you have shattered that barrier through your pain and your bravery over these last years. I am equally certain of that. I am absolutely confident, and I believe you are too. Thus, though we shall not see one another here or for the rest of our lives, the future remains secure for us both. We can wait for it. You have waited with such strength since we parted, and so can I, drawing my strength from your victory."

She fell silent, and the church held its breath. To Feversham, her words were like rain falling on parched earth. Hearing her speak lifted him so profoundly that his six years of trials—the hiding in shadows, the lonely endurance, the countless heartbreaks and physical agonies—dwindled into insignificance. They had indeed borne fruit. Ethne had spoken in a gentle tone exactly what his soul had longed to hear while lying awake in the bazaars of Suakin, the Nile villages, and the vast, dim expanses of the desert. Words he had barely dared to hope she would ever utter.

He stood silently by her side, still hearing her voice even after it ceased. Long ago, bitter words she had spoken had clung to him, stinging so deeply that he feared he would die hearing them again, carrying her reproaches to his grave. Now, remembering that prediction, he knew it was false. The words echoing in his mind were those she had just spoken.

Feversham was not unprepared for Ethne's proposal of separation. He had already heard rumors of her engagement and accepted her wish without argument. He sensed she had more to say, and indeed she did, though she hesitated to speak it. This was the final time she would see Harry Feversham; she intended resolutely to banish him. Once he crossed that church threshold, letting his shadow fall behind him, she would never speak to him or cast her eyes upon him again until death. She delayed the moment of his departure with silences and slow speech, hoping it might stretch on indefinitely. Perhaps he would speak of his travels or dangers; she was prepared to discuss even the politics of the Sudan with him. But he waited for her.

"At length," she finally said, "I am going to be married. Immediately. I am to marry a friend of yours, Colonel Durrance."

There was hardly a pause before Feversham replied, "He has cared for you a long while. I was unaware of it until I left, but reflecting on everything, I suspected it, and soon became certain."

"He is blind," Ethne stated flatly.

"Blind!" Feversham exclaimed. "He, of all men, blind!"

"Exactly," she confirmed. "He—of all men. His blindness explains everything: why I marry him, why I send you away. It was after he lost his sight that I became engaged to him. It was before Captain Willoughby brought me the first feather. Between those two events, you see. After you left, I thought things over carefully. I used to lie awake resolving that two lives should not be ruined because of me."

"My life was not ruined," Feversham interrupted. "Please believe that."

"Partly it was," she returned softly. "I know very well. You would not admit it for my sake, but it was. I was determined that a second ruin should not happen. So, when Colonel Durrance went blind—you know the man he was, you can imagine what blindness meant to him, the loss of everything he cherished—"

"Except you," Feversham supplied.

"Yes," Ethne answered quietly. "Except me. So I became engaged to him. But he has grown remarkably sharp. You cannot guess how keen his perception has become. A hint reveals the entire hidden truth to him. Currently, he knows nothing of the four feathers."

"Are you sure?" Feversham demanded suddenly.

"Yes. Why?" Ethne asked, turning her face toward him for the first time since sitting down.

"Lieutenant Sutch was at Suakin while I was at Omdurman. He knew I was a prisoner there. He sent messages, attempting to organize my escape."

Ethne started. "Oh," she said. "Colonel Durrance certainly knew you were in Omdurman. He saw you at Wadi Halfa and heard you had gone south into the desert. He was distressed; he asked a friend for news, and the friend found you in Omdurman. He told me himself—and yes, he said he would try to arrange your escape. No doubt he did so through Lieutenant Sutch. He has been at Wiesbaden with an oculist and only returned a week ago. Otherwise, he would have told me. Likely he was the reason Sutch was at Suakin, but he knows nothing of the four feathers. He only knows our engagement was abruptly broken; he believes I have no thought left for you. But if you return, if we see each other again, however calmly, however indifferently, he would guess. He is so quick; he would be sure to guess."

She paused, adding in a whisper, "And he would guess right."

Feversham saw the blood rush to her forehead, deepening the color of her cheeks. He did not move, nor lean closer, nor show any vocal sign that might make this painful leave-taking harder.

"Yes, I see," he said. "And he must not guess."

"No, he must not," Ethne agreed. "I am so glad you see that too, Harry. The straight and simple path is the only one for us. He must never guess, for, as you said, he has nothing left but me."

"Is Durrance here?" Feversham asked.

"He is staying at the vicarage."

"Very well," he said. "It is only fair that I tell you I had no expectation that you would wait. I had no wish for you to; I had no right to such a wish. When you gave me that fourth feather in the little room at Ramelton, with the music drifting faintly through the door, I understood your meaning. There was to be a complete, irrevocable end. We were not to remain mere acquaintances. So I said nothing of the plan that crystallized in my mind at that exact moment. You see, I might never have succeeded. I might have died trying, or perhaps shirked the attempt entirely. It would have been time enough to speak if I had returned. Therefore, I never formed the wish that you should wait."

"That is what Colonel Trench told me," Ethne noted.

"I told him that too?"

"On your first night in the House of Stone."

"Well, it is the truth. All I hoped for—and I did hope for it every hour of every day—was that, if I came home, you would take back your feather, and that we might... not renew our friendship here, but see something of one another afterwards."

"Yes," Ethne said. "Then there will be no parting."

She spoke simply, without a sigh, but as she looked at Harry Feversham, a smile played on her lips. That look and smile told him the true cost of the separation
