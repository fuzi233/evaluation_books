Few days pass beneath the sun without witnessing deeds of consequence spoken by the nameless, suffered by the obscure, and endured by the forgotten. Most of these silent heroes remain buried in the dust of history until the great have fallen and the humble risen, yet many lives are lost to the world, their stories preserved only in cold, brittle epitomes that strike the heart like hailstones rather than rain. Chronicles written in the suffocating tongue of Latin often bury truth under layers of harsh brevity, leaving the facts as stark as fossils trapped in stone. It is here, in the gaps of these dry accounts, that the storyteller steps forward as an interpreter, seeking to breathe warmth into the frozen pages and grant two sorrow-scarred souls a fleeting place in the affections of the living.

Our tale unfolds in the mid-fifteenth century, a time when Louis XI ruled France, Edward IV held a contested throne in England, and Philip "the Good" reigned over Holland with a grip forged by force and guile. Having displaced his cousin Jacqueline and shattered her spirit, Philip sat undisturbed on his throne, a monarch whose tastes ran wild with the bizarre and the beautiful, from jeweled dwarfs to exotic beasts.

In the quiet burgh of Tergou, Elias and Catherine lived a life marked by both plenty and profound worry. Elias traded in cloth, silk, and curried leather—a durable material prized by the common folk for its ability to withstand a century's worth of steel knives, a necessity in an age where neighbors might carve one another at dinner over trifles. Though the couple was financially secure, nine children crowded their home, turning a once-manageable household into a sprawling fortress. When the children were young, they were heaven's playthings; but as they grew tall like olive branches, the reality of their number settled heavily on Elias and Catherine's hearts. They belonged to a prudent people, where recklessness was rare, yet the question gnawed at them constantly: *Who will find bread for us when we are gone?*

To avoid the shame of seeing their blood sink into the lower ranks of society, Elias and Catherine practiced a rigorous economy. Every penny saved, every luxury denied, flowed into a small coffer bound with steel. With this hoard, they eventually funded the futures of four sons and a daughter, sending them into the world as merchants, tailors, and robemakers. Yet, two children remained unable to walk the path of industry: Giles, a dwarf plagued by malice and physical deformity, and Little Catherine, a frail girl who moved upon crutches. Then there were Sybrandt, who preferred play to labor, and Cornelis, who waited idly for his father's death. Despite their fears, the couple clung to hope, buoyed by the presence of their youngest son, Gerard.

Gerard was a boy apart, destined for the priesthood and the altar. He possessed a mind for letters and a hand for art, though his nature was frivolous. He spent his days engrossed in penmanship and reading, often neglecting meals to chase the flicker of a candlelight. The local monks, captivated by his skill, taught him to illuminate manuscripts, praising his true eye and subtle hand. Yet, Gerard hesitated to create the glorious borders and holy images he admired. When pressed by a kindly monk, he confessed a secret shame: he lacked the funds for the expensive pigments—gold, ultramarine, and vermilion—that made such works shine. He feared asking his mother for charity, believing it would bring humiliation.

His isolation broke when an old lady named Margaret Van Eyck arrived in Tergou. Once a renowned artist from Flanders, she had fled her homeland to die in peace. Upon hearing of Gerard's talent, she invited him to her home. There, amidst drawings and sketches, she became his mentor. She gently prodded him to reveal why he refused to paint, and he finally spoke of his poverty. Moved by his modesty and despair, Margaret gifted him brushes dipped in precious colors and a sheet of fine vellum. In return for her kindness, Gerard felt a surge of gratitude, an emotion doubled by the unique spirit of the age, when artists and artisans walked side by side in love and reverence.

Back at home, the news of Gerard's ambition sparked a storm. When Gerard announced his intention to enter the great competition in Rotterdam to win prizes for painting and illumination, his mother erupted in panic. "Two gold crowns!" she shrieked, clutching her apron. "We cannot afford a single bushel of rye!" She trembled at the thought of spending a month's food on what she called "vanity." Little Catherine, however, pleaded silently for her brother, her violet eyes filled with trust. Seeing her distress, Gerard tried to explain that he could borrow vellum from the monks, reducing the cost. But his mother, fearing the loss of their savings, insisted he wait, her face flushed with anxiety.

Yet, Gerard's resolve was unyielding. The night before departing, Margaret handed him a letter addressed to Princess Marie in Rotterdam, urging him to seek her patronage. Dressed in his finest holiday garb—a silver-grey doublet, buckskin hose, and a hat tied with a purple ribbon from Little Catherine—Gerard set off for the city. Exhausted by the journey, he stumbled upon an old man and a young woman by a roadside fire. The old man wore the finery of a magistrate, yet his clothes were threadbare, and his purse empty. The young woman, dressed plainly, wore a necklace of silver cords and an expression of deep concern.

Gerard, sensing their plight, immediately lit a fire with sticks and wood, offering them bread and water from his flask. The young woman's face softened with gratitude as she watched his gentle energy. Just as Gerard prepared to gather more fuel, a magnificent figure approached on a mule draped in purple velvet. It was Ghysbrecht Van Swieten, the burgomaster of Tergou, a man of immense wealth and notorious stinginess.

Ghysbrecht froze, his face draining of color as he recognized the pair by the fire. He was accompanied by Peter, the old man he had seen earlier, whom he now realized was not his own father. The realization struck Ghysbrecht with the force of a thunderclap. Twenty years prior, he had committed a heinous act of treachery against a friend, stealing his fortune through deceit. That betrayal had ruined the man, leaving him destitute and sick. Now, seeing Peter and his daughter starving in the cold, Ghysbrecht felt the sting of guilt claw at his conscience. He reached for his purse, but his hand shook violently, paralyzed by the memory of his sin.

Ghysbrecht rode on, his wealth heavy on his person, yet his heart lighter than lead. He carried a "scorpion"—remorse—that gnawed at him, a poison that could never be cured, only borne. Meanwhile, Gerard returned to the fire, unaware of the tragedy unfolding nearby, and comforted the old man and his daughter, unknowingly paying back a debt of honor that Ghysbrecht had squandered decades ago. As Gerard prepared to continue his journey to Rotterdam, he felt a strange mix of anticipation and dread, knowing that the road ahead would test not only his skills but his faith in the justice of the world.
