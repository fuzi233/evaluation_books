The morning of Palm Sunday dawned clear and bright over the Valdarno, where the olive groves still bore the scent of rain from the night before. In San Marco, Savonarola stood once more at the pulpit, facing a congregation whose faith in him remained unshaken. With quiet dignity and deep sincerity, he declared himself ready to die—his vision of the future already shaped by the shadow of his own fate. He blessed the crowd, and their faces turned upward, filled with reverence and love. Then, without a word, he stepped down from the pulpit, turning away forever from that sacred place.

But by the time the sun dipped below the hills, Florence was aflame with fury. The tensions stirred the day before had smoldered through the quiet hours, now erupting into violence, guided not only by passion but by deliberate design—and with official silence, if not encouragement. It began at the Duomo, where members of the Compagnacci tried to disrupt the evening sermon, drawing the Piagnoni into open conflict. Once tempers flared, the cry rose: “To San Marco! Set fire to San Marco!” Before darkness fell, the church and its convent were surrounded by an ever-swelling mob.

Resistance came swiftly. The monks, long aware of mounting hostility, had arms hidden within their walls. Some fought fiercely in their white robes, like knights defending a holy fortress. Even the Dominican serge could not hide the strength beneath. Laymen who had refused to flee joined the defense—shots rang out near the great crucifix, stones and burning embers rained from the roof, swords clashed in the cloisters. Despite the numbers against them, the defenders held on until deep night.

The government’s actions had been no help. Guards were sent early—not to break up the attack—but to demand surrender: all laymen must leave, arms must be dropped, and Savonarola himself must depart Florentine soil within twelve hours. Had he fled then, he might have been torn apart by the mob. He was willing to go—but his followers would not let him. To leave would mean certain death; to stay meant enduring the storm. Still, some men of high standing slipped out through the garden wall, among them Francesco Valori, who hoped to rally aid from outside.

By midnight, the struggle was nearing its end. The Compagnacci were close to breaching the library, where Savonarola knelt in prayer with those Brethren who had laid down their arms at his command. Then came a second guard force, sent by the Signoria, demanding the arrest of Fra Girolamo and his two coadjutors, Fra Domenico and Fra Salvestro.

A roar of triumph surged through the crowd as lanterns lit the doorway and Savonarola emerged, escorted by guards offering no protection but prison. The mob surged forward—no longer just a mob, but a hunting pack—eager to be first to hurl insults, to strike, to spit upon the man they once called Prophet. As he was dragged through the narrow streets, lanterns lifted to reveal sneering faces, hands reached out to kick and curse. For Savonarola, this was the bitterest moment of life—not because of death, which he accepted, but because of the humiliation of being mocked by those he had sought to save.

Yet even martyrdom does not bring the deepest resignation. True humility comes when one bows in silence and thinks: *I am not worthy to be a martyr. The Truth shall prevail—but not through me.*

That brief, savage victory—seeing the Frate led away in shame—was only the first taste of blood for the beast. There was still work to do. The homes of his allies must be sacked. Half the mob, too late for the siege, had already turned to looting—selectively, with purpose. They targeted the houses of known Piagnoni, proving the riot was not chaos, but orchestrated. And among the targets was Francesco Valori, accused of conspiring with Savonarola to seize power—perhaps to become Doge or Gonfaloniere for life.

Valori, the man who eight months earlier had raised his voice in fierce demand for justice against five fellow citizens, now fled from San Marco only to meet the very justice he had demanded. His house was surrounded. His wife was shot dead by an arrow. And as he hurried to answer a summons to the Palazzo, he was killed by men named Ridolfi and Tornabuoni.

Thus, through the long night and into the grey light of dawn, Florence played out its masque—the Masque of the Furies. But the mastermind remained unseen. Too powerful, too careful, to appear in the fray. He issued orders from a private retreat—a quiet house between Santa Croce and San Marco, the same room where Tito had once visited Dolfo Spini.

There, Spini sat through the night, receiving visitors—some masked as Compagnacci, others disguised in plain clothes, weapons concealed. Wine flowed freely, though Spini watched carefully, drinking enough to sharpen his nerves, fueled by constant reports of the chaos unfolding. Among the most frequent visitors was Ser Ceccone, and later, Francesco Cei, who lingered in hiding, waiting for his exile to be lifted once Savonarola fell.

As the tapers burned low and the shutters showed the dimming sky, Spini rose abruptly, face flushed with anger after a heated exchange with his companions. “The devil spit him!” he thundered. “He shall pay for it, though. Ha, ha! The claws will come down on him when he least expects it. So *he* was to be the great man after all? He pretended to throw everything my way—as if I were blind and beggarly—while he winked and filled his own purse. I’d like to hang skins about him and set my hounds on him! And he has that fine ruby of mine—I was a fool to give it to him yesterday. Malediction! Two years ago he was laughing at me behind his sleeve, spoiling the best plan ever laid. I was a fool to trust such a schemer.”

“A Greek,” said Cei, smiling faintly, “who arrived in Florence with gems sewn into his clothes.”

“You didn’t choose your confidant wisely, Dolfo,” Cei added.

“He’s cleverer than you, Francesco,” Spini snapped, turning on him. “And handsomer too.”

“I humbly believe,” said Ser Ceccone, “that Messer Francesco’s poetic talent will outweigh—”

“Enough!” Spini interrupted. “I hate that notary’s trick of yours.” Then, spotting two Compagnacci entering with urgent looks, he leapt up. “Taddeo—or someone—what news?”

“Bad,” replied one. “The people decided they’d sack Soderini’s house. Now they’ve been stopped. If we don’t act fast, they’ll turn on us. I suspect Mediceans are stirring among them. We may see them attacking your palace across the bridge unless we find another distraction.”

“I have it!” Spini exclaimed, grabbing Taddeo by the belt and pulling him aside. After a swift exchange, he pushed him toward the door. “Go—make quick work.”
