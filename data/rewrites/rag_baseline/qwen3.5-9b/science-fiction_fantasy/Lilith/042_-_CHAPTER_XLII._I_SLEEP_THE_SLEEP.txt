When consciousness returned, the earth beneath me was saturated, and the path leading to the grave had transformed into a treacherous quicksand. The ancient river, swollen by the encroaching storm, surged against its banks, threatening to roar down the precipice. One branch would submerge the orchard valley; the other would sweep toward the drowning of the monster horde, leaving the Evil Wood stranded in an island of chaos. I rose immediately to return to those who had sent me. Reaching the precipice, I navigated carefully between the gnarled branches, passing once more by Mara's cottage. I needed to confirm her presence before succumbing to the final sleep, confident now in knowing how to cross the flooded channels even if the river had claimed them. Yet, upon arrival, the door stood ajar. Bread and water remained untouched on the table, preserved in a vacuum of profound silence. I stepped inside, calling aloud, but received no answer. Turning away, I moved onward.

A short distance further, I encountered a gray-haired man seated on the sand, weeping openly. "What afflicts you, sir?" I asked. "Are you abandoned?"
"I weep," he choked out, "because they refuse to let me die. I have gone to the House of Death, and its mistress, despite my age, denies me entry. Intercede for me, sir, if you know her."
"Nay, sir," I replied, "I cannot. She refuses none whom it is lawful for her to receive."
"How do you know this?" he demanded. "You have never sought death yourself! You are far too young to crave it!"
"My words suggest that if you were young again, neither would you desire it."
"Indeed, young sir, I would not! And I am certain you cannot."
"I may not possess the age to desire death, but I am young enough to yearn for life. Therefore, I proceed to ascertain if she will eventually accept me. You wish to die because you refuse to live; she will not open her door to you, for no one can die who does not long to live."
"It ill becomes your youth to mock a friendless old man. Cease your riddles!"
"Did not the Mother tell you something similar?"
"In truth, I believe she did, but I paid little heed to her excuses."
"Then, sir," I rejoined, "it is plain you have not yet learned to die, and I am heartbroken for you. Such was my state before the Lady of Sorrow intervened. Though I am young, I have shed many tears. Pardon my presumption, but I offer this counsel: Go to the Lady of Sorrow and 'take with both hands' whatever she offers. Her cottage lies yonder. She is absent now, but her door stands open, and bread and water rest upon her table. Enter, sit, consume the provisions, and wait for her appearance. Seek her counsel, for she is true, and her wisdom is vast."
He resumed his weeping, and I departed, fearing he would not heed my advice. Yet Mara would surely find him!

Sunset had fallen, and the moon remained hidden when I reached the abode of the monsters. The place stood motionless as a stone until I crossed the threshold. Immediately, the roar of countless waters erupted, accompanied by a great cry trailing behind me, though I did not turn my head. Before reaching the House of Death, the cold became biting and the darkness absolute; the two forces merged into a single entity that seeped into my very bones. However, the Candle of Eve, glowing from the window, served as my guide, shielding my heart from frost and murk.

The door stood open, and the cottage lay empty. Sitting down in despair, I was struck by a loneliness unlike anything I had ever felt during my wanderings. Thousands were near, yet none were with me. Technically, I was the dead one, not they, but whether through their life or my death, we were severed. They lived, yet I was not dead enough to perceive their existence clearly; doubt loomed large. They were distant, and I possessed no helper to lay me beside them. Never before had I known, or truly imagined, such desolation. I chastised myself, claiming the solitude was merely apparent, but the truth was stark: I was awake, and they slept—that was all. They lay so still, silent, yet they were with me now, and soon, very soon, I would join them.

I dropped Adam's old spade, and the dull thud of its impact on the clay floor seemed to reverberate from the chamber beyond. A childish terror seized me. I sat staring at the coffin door. But Father Adam, Mother Eve, and Sister Mara would arrive shortly, and then—welcome the cold world and the white neighbors! I banished my fears, lived briefly, and loved my dead.

Something stirred in the Chamber of the Dead. A sound emerged, resembling a dim, far-off vibration, yet distinct from any auditory sensation I knew. My soul leapt into my ears. Was it merely a tremor of the dead air, too faint to be heard but quivering through every spiritual sense? I KNEW without hearing, without tactile confirmation! The presence was arriving! It drew nearer. Within the bosom of my abandonment, an infant hope ignited. The noiseless thrill reached the coffin door, coalesced into sound, and struck my ear. The door began to move, emitting a low, soft creak of its hinges. It was opening!

I ceased listening, staring expectantly. It parted slightly, and a face appeared in the aperture. It was Lona's. Her eyes were closed, yet the face itself seemed fixed upon me, seeing through the veil of unconsciousness. It was white as Eve's, white as Mara's, but lacked their radiant luminescence. She spoke, her voice like a sleepy night-wind rustling through tall grass.
"Are you coming, King?" she whispered. "I cannot rest until you are with me, gliding down the river to the Great Sea and the Beautiful Dream-Land. The sleepiness is full of lovely things: come and see them."
"Oh, my darling!" I cried. "Had I but known! I thought you were dead!"
She collapsed onto my bosom—cold as ice frozen to marble. She threw her arms, so white and feeble, about me and sighed.
"Carry me back to my bed, King. I want to sleep."
I bore her to the death-chamber, clutching her tightly lest she dissolve from my grasp. Unaware that I witnessed the miracle, I carried her directly to her couch.
"Lay me down," she murmured, "and cover me from the warm air; it hurts—a little. Your bed is there, next to mine. I shall see you when I wake."
She was already asleep. I threw myself upon my couch, blessed as no man has ever been on the eve of his wedding.
"Come, sweet cold," I whispered, "and still my heart speedily."
Instead, a glimmer of light illuminated the chamber, and I saw Father Adam approaching. He held no candle, yet I perceived him clearly. Beside Lona's couch, he looked down upon her with a questioning smile, then greeted me across the space between us.
"We ascended to the top of the hill to hear the waters on their journey," he said. "They will reach the Den of the Monsters tonight. But why did you not await our return?"
"My child could not sleep," I answered.
"She is fast asleep!" he rejoined.
"Yes, now!" I said. "But she was awake when I laid her down."
"She was asleep all along!" he insisted.
"She was perhaps dreaming of you—and came to you?"
"She did."
"And did you not notice her eyes were closed?"
"Now that I recall, I did."
"If you had looked before laying her down, you would have seen her asleep on the couch."
"That would have been terrible!"
"You would only have discovered she was no longer in your arms."
"That would have been worse!"
"It is, perhaps, worth contemplating; but witnessing it would not have troubled you."
"Dear Father," I said, "why am I not sleepy? I thought I would drift off like the Little Ones the moment I rested my head!"
"Your hour is not yet come. You must eat before you sleep."
"Oh, I ought not to have lain down without your permission, for I cannot sleep without your aid! I will rise at once!"
Yet I found my own weight immovable.
"There is no need: we will serve you here," he answered. "--Do you not feel cold?"
"Not too cold to remain still, but perhaps too cold to eat!"
He approached my couch, bent over me, and breathed upon my heart. Instantly, warmth flooded my veins. As he withdrew, I heard a voice and recognized it as the Mother's. She was singing, her melody sweet, soft, and low. I believed she sat by my bed in the darkness, but before the song ceased, it soared aloft, emanating from the throat of a woman-angel, high above all regions of larks, higher than man had ever lifted his heart.
