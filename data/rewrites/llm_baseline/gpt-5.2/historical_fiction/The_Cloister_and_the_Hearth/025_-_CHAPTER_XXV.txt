Denys had the good fortune to clutch at Gerard as he pitched headlong from the bough; yet it was not Denys’s arm that truly broke the fall. Gerard’s brow and shoulders sank into the shaggy bulk of the she-bear, whose last strength was already running out in hot panting. Denys hauled him clear; the brute still shuddered and pawed as if even dying were a kind of malice, then the quiver ceased, the breath went thin, and at last was gone. Denys, practical as ever, set Gerard’s back against the bear’s soft flank like a cushion and fanned him with his cap.

Gerard came round by little and little—bewildered, and then suddenly keenly aware of what had borne him up. Feeling hair against his cheek, he rolled away as if the earth itself were fouled, and let out a yell fit to bring the whole wood upon them.

“Courage,” cried Denys. “Le diable est mort.”

“Is it dead? quite dead?” Gerard called from behind a tree; his courage came in gusts and ebbs, and just then the chill fit had him.

“Behold!” Denys seized the dead beast’s ear and tugged it as if it were a tavern dog; then, to make his triumph complete, he prised open the jaws and thrust his own head between the teeth in a grotesque bravado. At that very instant Gerard’s stomach rose against him; he turned and was violently sick among the leaves.

Denys laughed, as one laughs at a comrade and not an enemy. “What now? And why tumble off thy perch when we had won the day?”

“I swooned, I trow.”

“But why?”

Gerard wiped his mouth with the back of his hand and shuddered. “She sent her nasty blood all over me. I think the smell did it. Faugh! I hate blood.”

“I do believe it potently.”

“See what a mess she has made me!”

“But with her blood, not thine. I pity the enemy that strives to satisfy thee.”

“You need not brag, Maître Denys; I saw you under the tree—the colour of your shirt.”

“Let us distinguish,” Denys answered, colouring in spite of himself. “It is permitted to tremble for a friend.”

For answer Gerard flung his arms round Denys’s neck and held him hard, not speaking.

Denys, blubbering his words to hide that he was touched, grumbled, “Was ever aught so like a woman? I love thee, little milksop—go to. Good! now on thy knees. What new caprice is this?”

“O Denys, ought we not return thanks to Him who saved both our lives against such fearful odds?” Gerard dropped to his knees and began to pray aloud. After a moment he heard the faint rustle of a heavier body settling; Denys was kneeling beside him, hands folded across his breast as his people did, his face drawn long and solemn.

When they rose, Gerard’s eyes shone. “Good Denys, Heaven will reward thy piety.”

“Ah bah! I did it out of politeness,” said Denys. “To please thee, little one. C’est égal; ’twas well and orderly prayed, and edified me while it lasted. A bishop had scarce handled the matter better. So now our evensong being sung, and the saints enlisted—marchons.”

They had scarce taken two steps when Denys halted. “By-the-by—the cub!”

“Oh, no, no!” Gerard cried; the very thought of the living bundle made his skin crawl.

Denys nodded. “Thou art right. It is late. We have lost time climbing trees, tumbling off ’em, swooning, vomiting, praying; and the brute is heavy to carry. And when I think on’t, we shall have papa after it next; these bears make such a coil about an odd cub. What is this? You are wounded! you are wounded!”

“Not I.”

“He is wounded; miserable that I am!”

“Be calm. I am not touched; I feel no pain anywhere.”

“You? you only feel when another is hurt,” Denys burst out, and dropped to his knees in the road. His hands went at Gerard’s leg with swift soldierly certainty, and his eyes glittered with something more than fear. “Quick, quick, before it stiffens!”

“Who makes the coil about nothing now?” Gerard asked, trying to be steady.

Denys did not answer straight. He got Gerard moving, supporting him, and spoke as if confessing to a priest. “Be pleased to note I have a bad heart. You were man enough to save my life, yet I must sneer at you, a novice in war. Was not I a novice once? Then you fainted from a wound, and I thought you swooned for fear, and called you milksop. Briefly, I have a bad tongue and a bad heart.”

“Denys!”

“Plaît-il?”

“You lie.”

“You are very good to say so, little one, and I am eternally obliged,” Denys mumbled, still angry with himself.

But the leg betrayed them. Before many furlongs the muscles drew tight, and each step was a nail driven. Gerard could at last only touch the ground with his toe, and that with a sharpness that turned his breath sour.

“Let me lie down and die,” he groaned. “This is intolerable.”

Denys told him it was afternoon and the nights were frosty; that cold and hunger were ill companions for a man who lay down; and that it would be unreasonable to lose heart, a certain great personage being notoriously defunct. So Gerard leaned on his axe and hobbled on, until, all at once, he gave in and sank helpless in the road.

Denys dragged him a little way into the wood where the undergrowth broke the sight from the path. Then, to Gerard’s astonishment, he thrust the crossbow and bolts into his hands. “Lie quiet. If any ill-looking fellows find thee, bid them keep aloof; if they refuse, shoot them dead at twenty paces. Honest men keep the path; knaves in a wood—none but fools do parley with them.”

With that he snatched up Gerard’s axe and set off at a run—not toward Düsseldorf, but back the way they had come.

Gerard lay with the pain throbbing like a drumbeat in the bone, thinking how Rome, that had looked so near at starting, seemed now a great stretch of world away—though he was two hundred miles nearer it than before. Yet his thoughts would not stay with Rome. They turned of themselves, like a needle, toward Sevenbergen. He saw Margaret’s hand in his—warm, clean, homely—saw her listening with parted lips while he told her what he had gone through for her. The thought soothed him as milk soothes a scalded mouth; and though his nerves were raw, he lay resigned and faintly smiling.

He had lain thus more than two hours when shouts burst suddenly, as if the forest had found a voice. The next instant something struck a tree close by with a hard thud and quivered there.

An arrow.

He struggled up. More missiles hissed and rattled among the boughs; the wood filled with cries—battle-cries—yet the vast trunks and hollows threw the sound about so that he could not tell from what quarter it came. The very air seemed to carry war. Then came a lull, and after it the fierce gallop of hooves; and louder shouting, mixed with shrieks and groans; and above all, strange dreadful noises like claps of thunder, bellowing loud and dying away in cracking echoes. Red tongues of flame stabbed out among the trees; sulphurous smoke drifted over his head, sharp as brimstone in the nose and throat.

Then—silence.

Gerard stood trembling, not knowing whether to pray or run. Awe pressed on him like a weight. “What will become of Denys?” he cried to the empty trunks. “Why did you leave me? O Denys, my friend! my friend!”

Just before sunset Denys returned, staggering under a hairy bundle. When he let it drop, it proved to be the bear’s skin.

Gerard welcomed him with such joy that Denys blinked at it. “I thought never to see you again, dear Denys. Were you in the battle?”

“No. What battle?”

“The bloody battle of men—or fiends—that raged in the wood a while agone.” And Gerard described what he had heard and seen: the arrows, the hooves, the thunder-claps, the flame, the choking smoke.

Denys patted him as one humours a fevered child. “It is well. Thou art a good limner, and fever is a spur to imagination. One day I lay in a cart-shed with a cracked skull, and saw two hosts manoeuvre and fight a good hour on eight feet square, and described it all fair to my comrade—only not so gorgeously as thou, for want of book-learning.”

“What—then you believe me not? when I tell you the arrows whizzed over my head, and the combatants shouted, and—”

“May the foul fiends fly away with me if I believe a word.”

Gerard took Denys’s arm and quietly pointed to the tree.

Denys went close, and his face changed. “Why—it looks like—it is—a broad arrow, as I live!” He stared up at it. “It came out of the battle,” Gerard said. “I heard it and saw it.”

“An English arrow.”

“How know you that?”

“By its length. The English draw to the ear; others to the right breast. So they loose a three-foot shaft, and this is one—perdition seize them!” He spat, half from superstition, half from old soldier’s hate. “Well, if this is not glamour, there has been a trifle of a battle. And if there has been a battle in so ridiculous a place for a battle, ’tis no business of mine; my duke hath no quarrel hereabouts. So let’s to bed.”

He scraped together a heap of leaves and made Gerard lie on it with the axe at his side; then lay down close, one hand on the arbalest, and drew the bear-skin over them, hair inward. The warmth came up at once, thick and animal, like being wrapped in fresh bread from an oven. They slept.

But long before dawn Gerard shook Denys. “What shall I do? I die of famine.”

“Do? why, go to sleep again incontinent: qui dort dîne.”

“I tell you I am too hungry to sleep,” Gerard snapped; pain had sharpened his temper.

“Let us march, then,” Denys said with paternal indulgence. He yawned hugely, then cut a strip of skin and rolled up the bear’s ears in it like a small grim bundle of provisions.

They took the road. Gerard leaned upon his axe and, on the other side, upon Denys; he hobbled with sighs.

“I hate pain,” Gerard said viciously.

“Therein you show judgment,” replied Denys.

The night was clear with stars; and when the moon rose it silvered the last ranks of trees ahead, showing the end of the wood not far off. Beyond lay Düsseldorf, a short league further—civil roofs, a fire, and bread that did not bleed.

At the very edge they came upon something that stopped them cold. Two white pillars rose pale in the moonlight, a few paces apart; between them stood shapes like men—too many, too still.

“I go no farther till I know what this is,” Gerard whispered, his throat tight. “Are they saints’ images set for prayer? or live robbers waiting to shoot down honest travellers? Nay, living men they cannot be, for they stand on nothing. Denys, let us turn back till daybreak; this is no mortal sight.”

Denys peered long and keenly. “They are men,” he said at last.

Gerard shrank the more. “But men that will never hurt us, nor we them,” Denys added. “Look not to their feet—for that they stand on.”

“Where, then, in the name of all saints?”

“Look over their heads.”

Gerard followed the line of Denys’s finger and made out a dark beam crossing from pillar to pillar; then, as they crept nearer, he saw cords—snake-like in the moon—running down from the beam to each slack body, each rope drawn tight as wire.

As they came beneath, a light air stirred. Several corpses swung or slowly turned, and every rope creaked. The sound went through Gerard like cold water down the back. He could not take his eyes away. Death was not silent here; it had a voice of hemp and timber.

So held were they by that sickening sight that they did not at first mark a fire burning under the gallows, and a living man huddled over it. An axe lay beside him; its bright edge shone red in the glow. He slept.

Gerard started; Denys only whispered, “Courage, comrade; here is a fire.”

“Ay—but there is a man at it.”

“There will soon be three.” Denys began calmly to heap on wood the watcher had already gathered, feeding the coals as if they were a kitchen hearth. At that, prudent Gerard slid forward, took the stranger’s axe, and sat down tight upon it, his own laid ready, eyes fixed on the sleeper.

The man wore a country dress and the local hat—a stiff three-cornered Brunswicker with a thick brass band, heavy enough to turn a sword-cut. Its weight had flattened his ears down like a fancy rabbit’s; but they had met scores of such dog-eared rustics. The strangeness was not his clothes. It was his post: why should a clown keep watch under a laden gallows?

Denys would not show curiosity. He drew two bear’s ears from his bundle, ran sticks through them, and held them to the fire. The fat began to hiss faintly; the air filled with a rank, meaty smell—wild, oily, like singed hair and smoked marrow. Denys sniffed as a cook might judge a roast. “’Twill be eating coined money,” he said, “for the burgomaster of Düsseldorf would have given us a rix-dollar for these ears as proof of the bear’s death; but better a lean purse than a lere stomach.”

“Unhappy man!” Gerard cried, glancing up at the creaking bodies. “Could you eat food here?”

“Where the fire is lighted there must the meat roast, and where it roasts there must it be eaten; for nought travels worse than your roasted meat.”

“Well, eat thou, Denys, an thou canst! I am cold and sick; there is no room for hunger in my heart after what mine eyes have seen. Oh, how they creak! And who is this man, I wonder? what an ill-favoured churl!”

Denys studied the sleeper as if judging a face in a painting. “I take him to be of the refuse of that company”—he nodded carelessly upward—“whereof these were the cream; and so ran their heads into danger.”

“At that rate, why not stun him before he wakes?” Gerard fidgeted, gripping his axe.

Denys opened his eyes wide in mock amazement. “For one that sets up for a milksop, you have the readiest hand. Why should two stun one? Tush—he wakes. Note what he says at waking, and tell me.”

The watcher opened his eyes. Seeing the fire built up and two strangers watching him, he stared, and you could see him struggling into calm; yet a tremor ran over him as if the cold ground crept into his bones. He forced out, “Good morrow.” But at that moment he missed his axe and saw Gerard sitting upon it with his own ready. His face fell.

Denys smiled grimly at the little play.

“Good morrow,” Gerard returned, quiet as a drawn knife.

The man could not bear the silence. “You make free with my fire,” he said—then, faltering, “you are welcome.”

Denys whispered; Gerard interpreted: “My comrade says, since we share your fire, you shall share his meat.”

“So be it!” the man answered quickly, brightening with a show of hearty goodwill. “I have half a kid hanging on a bush hard by—I’ll go fetch it.” He rose, turning as if to go.

In the same instant Denys had the crossbow up and levelled at his head. The man dropped on his knees as if the string had jerked him down.

Denys lowered the weapon and, with a mere tilt of his chin, ordered him back. The fellow obeyed, moving in a sick, disjointed way, like a mouse that the cat has let run a little only to tap it down again.

“Sit down, friend,” Denys said in French, the tone doing the work the words could not.

The man sat, though he understood no French.

“Tell him,” Denys murmured, “the fire is not big enough for more than thee. He will take my meaning.”

Gerard conveyed it; the man grinned. Since Denys had spoken, he seemed oddly relieved. “I wist not ye were strangers,” he said to Gerard.

Denys cut a piece of bear’s ear—brown at the edge now, the fat bubbling—and offered it with a courtly little gesture to the same man he had just covered with the bow.

The watcher accepted as calmly as if such hospitality were common. He drew bread from his wallet and broke it, sharing it with them. Then, with a wink that tried to make comradeship of peril, he thrust his hand into the heap of leaves he sat on. Gerard’s fingers tightened on his axe. Out came a great leathern bottle, near two gallons. The man drank first, then offered it to Gerard, who passed it untouched to Denys.

“Mort de ma vie!” Denys cried, drinking. “Rhenish—and fit for an archbishop’s gullet. Here’s to thee, thou prince of good fellows: wishing thee a short life and a merry one! Come, Gerard, sup! Pshaw—never heed them, man! they heed not thee.” He jerked his head upward as if the dead were no more than sacks in a barn. “Natheless, did I hang over such a skin of Rhenish as this, and three churls sat beneath drinking it and offered me not a drop, I’d soon be down among them.”

“Denys! Denys!”

“My spirit would cut the cord, and womp would come my body amongst ye, with a hand on the bottle, and one eye winking, t’other.”

Gerard sprang up with a cry of horror, clapped his hands to his ears, and started to run; then his eye fell on the watcher’s axe and the common sense of danger dragged him back. He sat down again on the axe, hands still pressed to his ears.

“Courage, l’ami, le diable est mort!” Denys shouted gaily, and thrust the roasted bear’s ear under Gerard’s nose. The smell turned Gerard’s stomach; he turned away.

“Wine!” he gasped. “Heaven knows I have need of it, with such companions as thee and—” He took the bottle and drank. The Rhenish went through him like warm copper, glowing in the chest, loosening the knot of fear; yet each gust of wind that set the ropes creaking sent a shiver back through him.

Denys and the watcher, meanwhile, ate and drank as if at a fair. They caroused beneath that creaking sepulchre, as though sound itself—rope on beam, flame on stick, bottle against teeth—were the only music men needed.

“Ask him how they came here,” Denys said at last, mouth full, pointing up without looking.

Gerard asked. The watcher, warmed by drink and by the importance of having listeners, replied that treason had been their end—diabolical treason and priest-craft. Then, in a long, prosy tale, he set forth the matter as he understood it.

Those “honest gentlemen” now dangling had been stout men and true, living in the forest by their wits; their thriving freedom had bred envy in mankind, and many had tried to take their lives and liberties, but the Virgin and their patron saints, with their own courage, had ever baffled such attempts. Yet yestereve, a party of merchants had come from Düsseldorf on mules. The forest-men let them creep near a league into the wood, then fell on them to make them disgorge a portion of their ill-gotten gains. Alas, the merchants were no merchants, but soldiers of more than one nation in the Archbishop of Cologne’s pay, with haubergeons hidden beneath their gowns and weapons ready. Still, the honest men fought stoutly and pressed the traitors hard—when lo, horsemen that had lain in ambush for hours galloped up; and with new diabolical engines of war they shot leaden bullets, laying many a fellow low, and so broke the rest that they yielded. The victors, with malice inconceivable, had brought cords already knotted about their waists and hanged them speedily, even hanging the dead beside the living “to make the gallanter show.”

“And that one at the end,” the watcher went on, using the neck of the bottle like a showman’s pointer, “was the captain. He never felt the cord; he was riddled with broad arrows and leaden balls ere they could take him. A worthy man as ever cried, ‘Stand and deliver!’—but a little hasty. Stay! I forgot; he is dead. Very hasty, and obstinate as a pig. That one in the buff jerkin is the lieutenant, as good a soul as ever lived: he was hanged alive. This one here—I never could abide; no, not that one, that is Conrad, my bosom friend—this one overhead in the chicken-toed shoon: you were always carrying tales, thief, making mischief; you know you were; and I am a man would rather live united in a coppice than in a forest with backbiters and tale-bearers. Strangers, I drink to you.”

So he went down the string, describing each man with a confidence that would have been comical had the creaking not answered him from above. His judgments were pithy—and almost all false; he had no true eye for character, and his words put masks upon the dead as easily as a tavern jester puts on faces. Gerard, listening, felt how words could be made to turn murder into “honesty,” and ambush into “priest-craft”; the same tongue that called theft a trade could make virtue into treason. In that talk he heard, dimly, the power of names—how a man might hang by a rope and also by a word.

“Enough palaver!” Denys broke in. “Marchons! Give me his axe. Now tell him he must help you along.”

The watcher’s face fell. He saw resistance in Denys’s eye and swallowed it. Gerard protested. “Y pensez-vous? To put my hand on a thief, it maketh my flesh creep.”

“Childishness! all trades must live. Besides, I have my reasons. Be not you wiser than your elder.”

“No. Only if I am to lean on him, I must have my hand in my bosom still, grasping the haft of my knife.”

“It is a new attitude to walk in; but please thyself.”

So they went, a strange three—one giving aid, one taking it, and both ready to strike; tenderness and distrust yoked together like ill-matched oxen. The gallows behind them kept creaking a while, then the sound thinned as the night swallowed distance.

When they cleared the wood, the watch-tower light of Düsseldorf showed like a steady star set on earth. It heartened Gerard. After an hour’s march the black outline of the tower and other buildings stood clear, and then their companion halted and said gloomily, “You may as well slay me out of hand as take me nearer the gates of Düsseldorf town.”

Gerard told Denys.

“Let him go then,” Denys said at once; “for in sooth his neck will be in jeopardy if he wends further with us.”

Gerard agreed as a matter of course. His horror of a criminal did not make him eager to hand a man to the rope. In those days a private citizen meddled little with criminals, save in self-defence.

Freed, the man asked for his axe. Denys returned it. To their surprise he lingered, shifting his feet like one bargaining. Was he to have nothing for coming so far?

“Here are two batzen, friend,” said Gerard shortly.

“Add the wine—the good Rhenish?”

“Did you give aught for it?”

“Ay—the peril of my life.”

“Hum! what say you, Denys?”

“I say it was worth its weight in gold.” Denys tossed him silver groshen. “One for every acorn on that gallows tree; and here is one more for thee, who wilt doubtless be there in due season.”

The man took the coins, yet still lingered, awkward now, his greed turning into a sort of shame.

“Well! what now?” Gerard cried. “Dost seek the hide off our bones?”

“Nay, good sirs. But you have seen to-night how parlous a life is mine. Ye be true men, and your prayers avail. Give me then a small trifle of a prayer, an’t please you; for I know not one.”

Gerard’s choler rose; pain had made him irritable, and the rogue’s self-pity soured him. Yet he bit his lip and asked, “Is it true what men say of you Rhenish thieves, that ye do murder innocent and unresisting travellers as well as rob them?”

The man answered sullenly, “They you call thieves are not to blame for that; the fault lies with the law.”

“Gramercy! so ’tis the law’s fault that ill men break it?”

“I mean not so,” the other said, “but the law in this land slays an honest man an if he do but steal. What follows? He would be pitiful, but is discouraged. Pity gains him no pity, and doubles his peril. An he but cut a purse his life is forfeit; therefore cutteth he the throat to boot, to save his own neck: dead men tell no tales. Pray then for the poor soul who by bloody laws is driven to kill or else be slaughtered. Were there less of this unreasonable gibbeting on the highroad, there should be less enforced cutting of throats in dark woods, my masters.”

“Fewer words had served,” Gerard replied coldly. “I asked; I am answered.” And, doffing his bonnet suddenly, he spoke his “prayer” in a clear Latin that rang strangely in the night air, like church-speech thrown into the face of the road:

“Obsecro Deum omnipotentem, ut, qua cruce jam pendent isti quindecim latrones fures et homicidae, in ea homicida fur et latro tu pependeris quam citissime, pro publica salute, in honorem justi Dei cui sit gloria, in aeternum, Amen.”

“And so good day.”

The outlaw blinked, then nodded, satisfied. “That is Latin,” he muttered. “More than I bargained for.”

He turned away at last, back into his perilous life, with his mind oddly at ease—as if the very sound of an unknown tongue could serve him for comfort, though he knew not what it had asked.

Denys and Gerard went on in silence, each chewing his own thoughts as men chew tough bread. The creak of ropes was gone now; only their steps, and far off the calm human sounds of a town waking—faint clinks, a dog’s bark, the low stir of morning.

At last Gerard said, thoughtfully, “That she-bear saved both our lives—by God’s will.”
