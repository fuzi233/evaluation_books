Our friends in Tunbridge were growing weary of the spa waters and eager to depart. With the arrival of autumn, Bath became Madame de Bernstein's destination—a city brimming with cards, company, and vibrant life. She planned to reach it after paying a few calls on her country acquaintances. Harry, with a distinctly reluctant air, promised to ride with Lady Maria and the chaplain to Castlewood.

They passed Oakhurst village once more, gliding past the hospitable house where Harry had previously been so warmly entertained. Lady Maria, however, could not resist making sharp remarks about the young ladies of Oakhurst, noting how they seemed to be "setting their caps" at Harry, and observing the mother's obvious desire to secure him for one of them. Irritated, Mr. Warrington declared in a petulant mood that he would simply ride past his friends' door, claiming her ladyship disliked and abused them. That evening, at the inn where they halted a few miles further, Harry was notably haughty and sulky.

At supper, Lady Maria's smiles failed to elicit any warmth from Harry's face. Her manufactured tears elicited no sympathy; instead, he grumbled surly replies to her querulous complaints. Eventually, my lady was forced to retire to her chamber without securing even a brief tête-à-tête with her cousin—the obstinate chaplain, seemingly by design, refusing to leave the room. Did Harry give specific orders to Sampson to remain? She departed with a heavy sigh. He bowed her to the door with stiff, obstinate politeness, consigning her to the care of the landlady and her maid.

Ten minutes after Lady Maria had retired, what horse galloped out of the inn yard? An hour later, when Mrs. Betty entered to retrieve her mistress's bottle of smelling salts, she found Parson Sampson smoking a pipe alone. Mr. Warrington had gone to bed—or perhaps taken a moonlit walk; how could the maid possibly know where Harry was? Sampson answered the maid's questions calmly. Mr. Warrington intended to set out the following morning and took his seat beside Lady Maria's carriage. Yet his brow was black; the dark spirit still haunted him. He barely spoke to her throughout the journey. Inside her own mind, Lady Maria fretted: *Great heavens! She must have told him that she stole it!*

In truth, as they ascended a steep hill roughly three miles from Oakhurst on the Westerham road, Lady Maria Esmond, leaning heavily on her beloved youth's arm and deeply infatuated with him, had whispered sentimental vows, protests, and declarations of affection into his ear. As she grew fonder, he grew colder. As she gazed up at his face, the sunlight illuminated hers—fresh and well-preserved, yet betraying the lines and wrinkles of twenty years—and poor Harry, feeling the weight of her arm, found the walk up the hill utterly intolerable. To think that such a burden would weigh upon him for the rest of his life! It was a dreary prospect, causing him to curse the moonlit stroll, the stifling heat of the evening, and the hot wine that had compelled him to make that foolish pledge, binding him fatally.

Maria's praises and raptures annoyed Harry beyond endurance. The poor girl poured out fragments of the few plays she knew that related to her situation, striving with every ounce of energy to charm her young companion. She repeatedly called him her champion, her Henrico, her preserver, vowing that his Molinda would remain eternally faithful. She clung to him desperately. "Ah, child! Have I not thy precious image, thy precious hair, thy precious writing here?" she exclaimed, gazing into his face. "Shall it not accompany me to the grave? It surely would, sir, were I to meet with unkindness from my Henrico!" she sighed.

What a strange tale! Madame Bernstein had given him the small silken case; she had burned the hair and the note it contained, yet Maria still held them close to her heart! It was then, as Harry started forward and suddenly moved as if to drop her arm—that moment of sudden motion—that Lady Maria felt her first pang of remorse. She realized she had spun a fib, or worse, that she had been exposed in telling one. If everyone were required to perform penance for lying, would they ever emerge from sackcloth and ashes?

Upon arriving at Castlewood, Harry's good humor had not improved. My Lord was absent, the ladies were away, and the only family member present was Mr. Will, who returned from partridge shooting just as the carriage and cavalcade reached the gate. He turned pale upon seeing his cousin and received a sulky scowl of recognition from the young Virginian.

Nonetheless, Harry attempted to maintain a pleasant demeanor. They met at supper, where their conversation was initially civil but lacking liveliness.
"Have you been to the races?" asked Mr. Warrington.
"To several," replied Will.
"Were you successful in your bets?"
"I hope so," said Mr. Warrington.
"Pretty well."
"And you have brought back my horse sound?" asked Mr. Warrington.

"My horse? What horse?" asked Will, confused.
"What horse? My horse!" Mr. Harry said curtly.
"Protest, I don't understand you," said Will.
"The brown horse for which I wagered with you, and which I won from you the night before you rode away upon it," Mr. Warrington stated sternly. "You remember the horse, Mr. Esmond."

"Mr. Warrington, I perfectly recall playing you for a horse, which my servant handed over to you on the day of your departure."
"The chaplain was present at our game. Mr. Sampson, will you act as umpire between us?" Mr. Warrington requested gently.
"I am bound to decide that Mr. Warrington played for the brown horse," said Mr. Sampson.
"Well, he got the other one," said sulky Mr. Will, grinning.
"And sold it for thirty shillings!" added Mr. Warrington, maintaining his calm tone.

Will chuckled waggishly. "Thirty shillings? And a devilish good price, too, for the broken-kneed old rip. Ha, ha!"
"Not a word more. 'Tis merely a question regarding a bet, my dear Lady Maria. Shall I serve you some more chicken?" Nothing could be more studiously courteous and gay than Mr. Warrington, so long as the lady remained in the room. When she rose to leave, Harry followed her to the door, closing it upon her with the most courtly bow of farewell. He lingered at the closed door for a moment, then commanded the servants to retire. Once the menials were gone, Mr. Warrington locked the heavy door, pocketing the key.

As the lock clicked shut, Mr. Will, who had been sitting over his punch and occasionally glancing sideways at his cousin, asked—with one of the oaths common to his speech—what in the world Mr. Warrington meant by that?
"I suspect a quarrel is brewing," said Mr. Warrington blandly. "There is no point in having these fellows look on at a row between their betters."
"Who is going to quarrel here, I should like to know?" asked Will, looking very pale and grasping a knife.
"Mr. Sampson, you were present when I played Mr. Will fifty guineas against his brown horse?"
"Against his horse!" roared Mr. Will.
"I am not such a fool as you take me for," said Mr. Warrington, "though I do hail from Virginia!" He repeated his question firmly. "Mr. Sampson, you were here when I played the Honourable William Esmond, Esquire, fifty guineas against his brown horse?"
"I must admit it, sir," said the chaplain, casting a deprecatory glance toward the Earl's brother.
"I don't own no such a thing," said Mr. Will, forcing a laugh.
"No, sir: because it costs you no more pains to lie than to cheat," said Mr. Warrington, stepping closer to his cousin. "Stand aside, Mr. Chaplain, and let us have fair play! For you are no better than a—"

Before he could finish the insult, Mr. Will threw a wine bottle at Mr. Warrington's head. The Virginian ducked just in time; the missile flew across the room, shattered against the wainscot opposite, smashing the face of a pictured ancestor of the Esmond family, and then rebounded against the wall, splashing a pint of fine port wine over the chaplain's face and powdered wig.
"Great heavens, gentlemen, I pray you to be quiet!" cried the parson, dripping with liquid.

However, gentlemen are sometimes disinclined to heed the Church's commands. With the bottle having failed, Mr. Esmond seized a large silver-handled knife and lunged at his cousin. But Harry caught up the attacker's right hand with his left—as he had observed boxers do at Marybone—and delivered a rapid blow to Mr. Esmond's nose. This sent the Earl's brother reeling backward against the oak panels, presumably blinding him momentarily. He dropped his knife as he retreated against the wall, which his swift opponent promptly kicked under the table.

Now, Will had also attended matches at Marybone and Hockley-in-the-Hole.
