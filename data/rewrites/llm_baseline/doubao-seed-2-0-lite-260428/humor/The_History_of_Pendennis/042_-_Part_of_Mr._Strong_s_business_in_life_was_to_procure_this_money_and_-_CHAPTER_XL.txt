Since that giddy, fateful night in Grosvenor Place, Mr. Harry Foker’s heart had been in such a dither you’d never believe the self-styled great philosopher of the fast set could endure it. Just think: not long ago, he was the one doling out perfectly cynical advice to young Pen, boasting that a life of well-funded self-indulgence would harden him right up, until he cared for nobody on God’s green earth other than Mr. Harry Foker himself. He should have grown more jaded with every claret toast and winning billiards break, right? But as it turns out, no man escapes the universal mishap of falling head over heels at least once — not Achilles, not Ajax, not Lord Nelson, not even old Adam himself — and Harry’s number was up. The all-conquering Love had grabbed him good.

That night, after dumping Pendennis at his crummy Lamb Court staircase landing, Harry trundled over to his usual hangout the Back Kitchen, but the gin twist tasted like dishwater and the devilled turkey had all the appeal of cardboard. His mates’ jokes bounced right off him, and when the famous Mr. Hodgen debuted his brand new comic masterpiece *The Cat in the Cupboard*, the follow-up to his viral hit *The Body Snatcher*, Harry sat there grunting “Bravo, Hodgen!” on pure autopilot, not catching a single lyric. He stumbled home dead tired an hour later, flopped onto his downy feather bed, but his brain was running a fever, and the very second he blinked awake the next morning, Blanche Amory’s face was right there behind his eyelids, grey eyes sparkling, grinning like: “I’m your princess now, dummy. No more silly pugilism bets for you.”

Suddenly every single one of his old favorite hobbies felt stale and disgusting. Harry had never been much for hanging around “modest women” of his own class — their boring decorum couldn’t compete with the lively, fun chatter of the theatre girls he was used to. His own mother, for all her kindness, couldn’t tell a joke to save her life, and his cousins, the Earl of Rosherville’s daughters, bored him witless. One was a geologist who ranted about trilobites for hours on end, one was a rabid horsewoman who smoked cigars and only talked steeplechases, one was ultra Low Church who was convinced her sister (the very High Church girl who’d turned her closet into a tiny personal altar and fasted every single Friday) was basically a secret heretic. Harry swore he’d rather spend three days on a prison treadmill than visit their family seat at Drummington. His cousin Erith, Rosherville’s heir, called Harry vulgar and ill-mannered; Harry for his part called Erith a prig so dry he could put the entire House of Commons to sleep mid-philanthropic speech. Even the old Earl himself still hadn’t recovered from that night he condescended to play billiards with Harry, and the lad poked him in the ribs with his cue and said, “Well old cock, I’ve seen plenty of bad shots in my day, but that’s the worst one I ever laid eyes on.” The Earl finished the game with forced angelic temper, nearly had a stroke that night, and hid in his private rooms until Harry left for Oxbridge. He’d never mention the incident, never invite Harry to play billiards again, and could barely force a “how do you do” when they met, but he dared not kick Harry out entirely — Harry’s dad the brewer held stacks of the Earl’s IOUs from very private business deals.

The Earl did have a fifth daughter, though: Lady Ann Milton. Since they were both in pinafores and scuffed school boots, the whole family had agreed Ann would marry Harry when he came of age. Ann never thought to question it any more than Esther would argue with King Ahasuerus. Harry’s dad had sat him down once, gruff and to the point: “Marry Ann, she’s got the best blood in England, and I’ll keep you living comfortably. If you say no, you get 200 quid a year while I’m alive, see how you like scraping by.” Harry, who knew his dad never made empty threats, just shrugged and said, “Sure, sir, if she’s fine with it, I’m game. She’s not bad looking. No rush though, right? I’d like to get my fling out first.” His dad laughed and said “Fling away, no one’s stopping you.” Harry had a tiny, boring little portrait of Ann tucked on his sitting room shelf, but it looked absolutely ridiculous next to the camp French prints, signed portraits of opera dancers, and racing art he’d collected. Ann, for her part, was the most unexciting figure in society: everyone knew she was reserved for Harry, no other man even bothered to flirt with her.

Bright and early the morning after Lady Clavering’s dinner, Harry woke up humming the little tune Blanche had sung the night before, moping “ofttimes I hover, ofttimes I hover” under his breath while propped up against his crimson silk bed coverlet. His room was plastered with the art he’d spent years collecting: French prints of Turkish romance trysts, opera nymphs, signed portraits of actress Miss Calverley in her famous tight pageboy tights, Miss Rougemont posing as Venus. His nightstand was cluttered with all his usual weekend plans: a rambling note from Miss Calverley who called him “Hokey-pokey Fokey” in atrocious spelling, reminding him he’d promised a dinner at the Elephant and Castle in Richmond; a private box ticket for Miss Rougemont’s upcoming benefit; a stack of invites to a prizefight night where Conkey Sam, Dick the Nailor, and the Worcestershire Nobber were set to put on a show. And tucked right there, almost forgotten, was the tiny sprig of verbena Blanche had given him at the party.

Suddenly all of that garbage was meaningless. Who cared if the Worcestershire Nobber won his fight? What was the point of 37-year-old Calverley cooing silly nicknames at him? The thought of that Richmond dinner filled him with utter disgust.

His mum Lady Agnes wandered in, frowning at his pale, glum face. “Harry! I know you’ve been off playing billiards at that wicked Spratt’s again! Those late nights with the billiard cues will kill you, mark my words!” Harry moped “It’s not the billiards, ma’am.” “Then it’s that terrible Back Kitchen!” she huffed. “I’ve half a mind to write the landlady and tell her to put barely any wine in your negus, and make sure you wrap a shawl around you before you climb in your brougham!” Harry sighed. “You can write her, she’s a perfectly nice woman, but it’s not the Back Kitchen either.” Lady Agnes was the softest, most indulgent mum alive, never said no to Harry, and he never hid his shenanigans from her — she’d gush to all her friends that Spratt’s was de rigueur, just like the gambling parties at Brookes’s in Mr. Fox’s day, and the Back Kitchen was full of important authors her own papa had always wanted to invite to Drummington, before Dr. Johnson died.

Harry decided to butter her up to get what he wanted. “That dinner at the Claverings yesterday? Unbeatable. Their clear soup’s better than ours, Moufflet our cook dumps way too much tarragon in everything. Their maraschino jelly? Stunning. Their house? Loads of fancy plate, no expense spared at all. The Claverings are great fun, we should invite them over as soon as possible.” Lady Agnes made a noncommittal “hmm” but agreed, and marked a date in her diary two whole weeks away — a stretch of time that felt like a thousand years to lovestruck Harry. When he glanced at the written invitation, he saw she’d left Blanche out entirely. “You forgot Miss Amory, Lady Clavering’s daughter!” he protested. “Oh that little creature!” Lady Agnes said. “No Harry, she seemed awfully forward, only sang right at you and Mr. Pendennis all night.” Harry lied so badly you could practically see the sweat bead on his forehead. “Pendennis is dead madly in love with her, that’s all! She sings wonderful. C’mon add her name in.” Lady Agnes sighed and scribbled Blanche on the card, and Harry hugged her so tight he nearly crushed her bonnet.

Back in his own room, he flopped on the ottoman in his fancy embroidered smoking jacket, puffing his pipe and daydreaming a hundred dumb schemes to run into Blanche. His polyglot valet Anatole, who spoke every language equally badly, read all his mail, and knew every address of his seedy mates, noticed Harry was impossibly fussy that morning. He tried on a dozen pairs of trousers, complained every stripe was ugly, every boot was over-varnished, every shirt pattern too loud. Finally, blushing bright tomato red, he mumbled at Anatole: “When you got hired, you said you could do hair styling, right?” Anatole said yes, confused. “Go get a pair of curling tongs,” Harry mumbled, “and make my hair a little curly, yeah?” Anatole hunted high and low for tongs, first asking Harry Senior’s butler whose master was bald as an egg, finally borrowing Lady Agnes’s hair tongs, and curled Harry’s hair until it was poofy and tight as a prize poodle’s coat. After he primped for another hour, Anatole whispered, “Shall I order the drag to Miss Calverley’s at six o’clock, sir?” Harry almost snarled. “Tell her I can’t — no, hang it, Poyntz and Rougemont and all the rest are coming, I have to show up. Fine, pick me up at Pelham Corner at six.”

Instead of heading straight to the livery stables, Harry ordered his own private carriage, and made a very silly detour past Blanche’s house in Grosvenor Place, just to gawk up at her pink window curtains for ten whole seconds like that counted as a romantic encounter, before he drove off to Pen’s tiny crummy Lamb Court chambers. Wait a minute! Pen had been living in London for two whole years, and Harry had visited his chambers less than half a dozen times total! Why was he in such a tearing hurry to see him now?

Oh, you girls know exactly why! When that silly love fever hits you, suddenly your crush’s most boring mate is the most interesting person on the planet, just cause he stood next to her! He knows what her favorite song is! He’s near the rose, even if he ain’t the rose himself. Poor Harry had completely forgotten his quiet, boring fiancee Lady Ann back at Drummington — if she could have seen him right then, primped up with his poodle curls and scent all over his coat, she’d have fainted dead away.
