Mrs. Adair, seated in the shadows of the house, had witnessed Ethne's precipitous departure. She had watched, transfixed, as the young woman's movements betrayed a sense of urgency, a sense of flight. The incident was singular, and it struck a chord within Mrs. Adair, awakening a jealousy that had been simmering beneath the surface. The music, with its thrum of happiness and yearning, had been a cruel taunt, a reminder of all that she had lost.

As Ethne vanished into the night, Mrs. Adair rose from her seat, her movements as silent as a ghost. She glided across the terrace, her bare feet making barely a sound on the stone floor. The open window beckoned, a portal to a world of secrets and desires. She drew closer, her ears straining to catch the words that still lingered in the air.

Durrance's voice, low and smooth, carried on the breeze, his tone a mixture of amusement and introspection. He spoke of the moment when he had begun to see, to truly see, the truth of Ethne's feelings. His words dripped with a satisfaction that bordered on triumph, a sense of having unraveled a mystery that had long plagued him.

"I began to find out, even on that first afternoon at Hill Street two months ago, that there was only friendship on your side," he said, his voice carrying on the wind. "My blindness helped me.
