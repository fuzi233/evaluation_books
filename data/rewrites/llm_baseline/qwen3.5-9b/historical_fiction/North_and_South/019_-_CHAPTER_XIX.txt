"As angels in some brighter dreams Call to the soul when man doth sleep, So some strange thoughts transcend our wonted themes, And into glory peep." — Henry Vaughan.

Mrs. Hale watched the preparations for the Thornton dinner party with a mixture of amusement and genuine interest. She approached the event with the simple, anticipatory curiosity of a child eager to have every detail of a promised joy described beforehand. Yet, the monotonous existence of an invalid often shrinks the world until it resembles a child's perception; lacking a sense of proportion, one believes the walls and curtains enclosing their daily routine must necessarily dwarf everything hidden beyond them. Furthermore, Mrs. Hale carried within her the dormant vanities of her youth. Having felt the sharp sting of humiliation when she became a poor clergyman's wife, those youthful aspirations had been smothered and suppressed, though never extinguished. Now, she delighted in imagining Margaret dressed for a gala, discussing attire with an unsettled anxiety that amused Margaret, who had known more of society during her single year in Harley Street than her mother had in twenty-five years of Helstone.

"Then you intend to wear your white silk?" Mrs. Hale asked. "Are you certain it will fit? It has been nearly a year since Edith was married!"

"Oh, yes, Mamma," Margaret replied cheerfully. "Mrs. Murray made it, and it is sure to be correct. It might be a straw's breadth shorter or longer-waisted depending on whether I have gained or lost weight, but I do not think I have altered in the least."

"Would it not be safer to let Dixon inspect it? Fabric may have gone yellow from lying idle."

"If you prefer, Mamma. But if the worst comes to the worst, I have a lovely pink gauze Aunt Shaw gave me only two or three months prior to Edith's marriage. That surely hasn't yellowed."

"No! But it may have faded."

"Well, then, I have a green silk. I feel as though I am drowning in an embarrassment of riches."

"I wish I knew exactly what you ought to wear," Mrs. Hale said, her voice betraying nervousness.

Margaret's demeanor shifted instantly. "Shall I try them on one after another, Mamma, so you can decide which you prefer?"

"But—yes! Perhaps that is the best plan."

Off went Margaret. Though dressed in finery at such an unusual hour, she felt a sudden inclination toward mischief—to make her rich white silk balloon absurdly, or to retreat backward from her mother as if she were royalty. However, finding that these whims were viewed merely as interruptions to serious business and annoying to her mother, she quickly settled into a grave and sedate composure. She could not comprehend why the world should fuss so over her dress. Later that afternoon, however, when she mentioned her engagement to Bessy Higgins regarding the servant Mrs. Thornton intended to inquire about, Bessy's spirits lifted dramatically.

"Dear! Are you going to dine at Thornton's in Marlborough Mills?"

"Yes, Bessy. Why are you so surprised?"

"Oh, I dunno. But they associate with all the finest people in Milton."

"And you do not consider us quite the finest folk in Milton, do you, Bessy?"

Bessy's cheeks flushed slightly as her thought was so easily read.

"Well," she said hesitantly, "you see, people here value money greatly. And I reckon you haven't acquired much."

"No," Margaret admitted calmly. "That is very true. But we are educated people, raised among the educated. Is there anything remarkable about being invited to dinner by a man who acknowledges himself inferior to my father, coming to him for instruction? I do not mean to fault Mr. Thornton. Few former drapers' assistants could have achieved what he has."

"But can you entertain a dinner party in your small house? Thornton's home is three times as large."

"Well, I believe we could manage to host Mr. Thornton, as you call it. Perhaps not in such a vast room, nor with so many guests. But I suppose we have not considered it in that light."

"I never thought you'd dine with the Thorntons," Bessy repeated. "Why, the Mayor himself eats there, along with Members of Parliament and everyone."

"I think I could uphold the honor of meeting the Mayor of Milton."

"But those ladies dress so grand!" Bessy remarked, casting an anxious glance at Margaret's printed gown, which her Milton-born eyes valued at sevenpence a yard.

Margaret's face brightened into a merry laugh. "Thank you, Bessy, for thinking so kindly of my appearance among such smart company. I possess plenty of grand gowns—a week ago, I would have said they were far too extravagant for anything I would ever need here. But since I am to dine at Mr. Thornton's and perhaps meet the Mayor, I shall certainly wear my very best gown."

"What will you wear?" Bessy asked, feeling somewhat relieved.

"White silk," Margaret answered. "It was a gown I wore to a cousin's wedding a year ago."

"That'll do!" Bessy declared, sinking back into her chair. "I would be loath to have you looked down upon."

"Oh! I shall be fine enough, if that saves me from being looked down upon in Milton."

"I wish I could see you dressed up," Bessy sighed. "I reckon you aren't what people would call pretty; you lack the red and white for that. But do you know, I have dreamed of you long before I ever saw you."

"Nonsense, Bessy!"

"Ay, but I did. Your very face—looking with your clear, steadfast eyes out of the darkness, your hair blown back from your brow, radiating outward like rays around your forehead, which was just as smooth and straight as it is now. And you always came to give me strength, which I seemed to gather from your deep, comforting eyes. You were dressed in shining raiment—exactly as you are dressing today. So, you see, it was you!"

"Nay, Bessy," Margaret said gently, "it was but a dream."

"And why might I not dream a dream in my affliction as well as others? Did not many in the Bible? Ay, and see visions too! Even my father thinks deeply of dreams. I tell you again, I saw you as plainly, coming swiftly toward me, your hair flying back with the speed of motion, just as it grows naturally, standing off slightly; and the white shining dress you have obtained to wear. Let me come and see you in it. I want to see you and touch you as if you were truly present in my dream."

"My dear Bessy, it is quite a fancy of yours."

"Fancy or no fancy—you have come, as I knew you would, when I perceived your movement in my dream. And when you are near me, I reckon I feel calmer in my mind and comforted, just as a fire warms one on a dreary day. You said it is on the twenty-first. Please God, I shall come to see you."

"Oh, Bessy! You may come and welcome; but do not talk so—it really makes me sad. It does indeed."

"Then I shall keep it to myself, if I have to bite my tongue out. Not that it is untrue for all that."

Margaret fell silent. Finally, she said, "Let us discuss it sometime, if you believe it to be true. But not now. Tell me, has your father turned out?"

"Ay!" Bessy replied heavily, her tone shifting drastically from moments before. "He and many others—all of Hamper's men, and countless besides. The women are as savage as the men this time. Food prices are high, and they must have food for their children, I reckon. Suppose Thorntons sent them their meals out—the same money spent on potatoes and meal could quiet many a crying baby and soothe a mother's heart for a while!"

"Don't speak so!" Margaret exclaimed. "You will make me feel wicked and guilty for attending this dinner."

"No!" Bessy insisted. "Some are predestined for sumptuous feasts and purple linens—you may be one of them. Others toil and moil their entire lives, and even the dogs are not pitied in our days as they were in the days of Lazarus. But if you ask me to cool your tongue with the tip of my finger, I will cross the great gulf to you simply for the thought of what you have been to me here."

"Bessy, you are very feverish! I can tell it by the touch of your hand as well as by your words. It will not suffice in that dreadful day that some of us have been beggars here while others have been rich—we shall not be judged by that poor accident, but by our faithful following of Christ."

Margaret rose, fetched some water, soaked her pocket-handkerchief, and laid the cool dampness on Bessy's forehead, beginning to rub her stone-cold feet. Bessy closed her eyes and allowed herself to be soothed.
