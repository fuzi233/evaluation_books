“There’s nought so finely spun But it cometh to the sun.”

Mr. Thornton sat on, long past the hour he had intended to leave. He sensed that his presence brought comfort to Mr. Hale, and was moved by the quiet pleading in his friend’s voice—those half-spoken words, “Don’t go yet,” repeated now and again like a whispered prayer. He wondered where Margaret was, but lingered not for her sake. In this moment, amid the solemnity of a man confronting the void of mortality, Thornton found himself strangely composed. His usual intensity softened; he listened with deep attention to every word Mr. Hale spoke.

“Of death, and of the heavy lull, And of the brain that has grown dull.”

It was remarkable how Thornton’s presence seemed to unlock the hidden thoughts Mr. Hale had kept even from his daughter. Perhaps it was because Margaret’s empathy was so vivid, so alive, that he feared the emotional toll of sharing such doubts. Or perhaps, in his reflective state, questions arose—doubts about faith, meaning, the uncertainty of life—that he knew she would reject outright. She would have seen them as signs of weakness, even betrayal of belief. Yet here, with Thornton, he could speak freely. The industrialist said little, but each sentence carried weight—each pause, clarity. When Mr. Hale faltered mid-sentence, describing some old pain, Thornton would complete it as if he’d lived it too. When doubt crept in, trembling and blind, Thornton did not recoil—he offered direction, pointing toward the light that might dispel the shadows. Though a man of action, driven by ambition and will, Thornton carried within him a quiet devotion, deeper than Mr. Hale had ever imagined. They never discussed these matters again, but this one conversation forged between them a bond no casual talk of piety could ever achieve. For when all are admitted, how can there be a Holy of Holies?

Meanwhile, Margaret lay still and pale upon the floor of the study, as though life had left her body. She had collapsed beneath the weight of her burden—long borne, quietly endured—until suddenly, her faith failed. She groped in darkness, seeking help that did not come. A faint contraction marked her brow, though no other sign of awareness remained. Her lips, once set in defiance, now hung slack and bluish.

“E par che de la sua labbia si mova Uno spirto soave e pien d’amore, Chi va dicendo a l’anima: sospira!”

The first stir of returning consciousness came as a tremor at her mouth—a silent effort to speak. But her eyes stayed closed. The quiver faded. Then, leaning weakly on her arms, she rose. Her comb had fallen from her hair. Instinctively, she searched for it, needing to restore order, to erase any trace of collapse. Yet again and again, she had to sit, catching her breath. Her head drooped forward, her hands folded meekly in her lap. She tried to recall the exact details that had terrified her—but memory failed. Only two truths remained clear: Frederick had been in danger of being caught in London—not merely accused of manslaughter, but of leading the mutiny—and she had lied to save him.

Yet one thought brought solace: her lie had bought time. If the inspector returned tomorrow, after she received news of Frederick’s safety, she would face shame openly. She, Margaret Hale, would stand before a crowded court and confess: “I am a dog, and I have done this thing.” But if he came before she heard from Frederick—if he returned soon, as he had threatened—then she would lie again. How? She didn’t know. The words might betray her, after this night of torment. But she would say them. For Frederick.

She was roused by Dixon’s entrance. She had just let Mr. Thornton out.

No sooner had Thornton stepped onto the street than an omnibus stopped nearby. A man descended, touching his hat. It was the police inspector.

Thornton had once secured this man’s position in the force and had heard of his progress, though they rarely met. At first, Thornton did not recognize him.

“My name is Watson—George Watson, sir, that you got——”

“Ah, yes! You’re doing well, I hear.”

“Yes, sir. I ought to thank you. But it’s a matter of business I wanted to mention. You were the magistrate who took down the deposition of the man who died at the Infirmary last night?”

“Yes,” replied Thornton. “He gave a confused account—nothing much use, according to the clerk. I’m afraid he was drunk. But there’s no doubt he died from violence. One of my mother’s servants was engaged to him. She’s devastated today. What about him?”

“Well, sir, his death seems tied to someone in the house I saw you leaving—Mr. Hale’s, I believe.”

Thornton turned sharply. “What about it?”

“It appears I’ve gathered a fairly clear chain of evidence implicating a gentleman who was walking with Miss Hale at Outwood station the night Leonards fell. The young lady denies she was there.”

“Miss Hale denies it?” Thornton repeated, voice low and changed. “When? What time?”

“About six o’clock, Thursday the twenty-sixth.”

They walked in silence for a moment.

“You see, sir,” the inspector began, “there may be a coroner’s inquest. I have a young man—fairly certain at first—but since hearing Miss Hale’s denial, he says he wouldn’t swear. Still, he insists he saw her at the station, walking with a gentleman, just before the porter saw a scuffle—though he later claimed it might’ve been nothing more than rough play. Leonards might’ve jumped off himself. The porter’s story keeps shifting. Jennings, the grocer’s boy—he wasn’t bad, but I doubt he’d stand firm under oath after hearing she denied it. Seeing you leave that house, sir, I thought I’d ask your advice—both as the magistrate who saw Leonards die, and as the man who helped me into the force.”

“You were right,” Thornton said. “Wait until you see me again.”

“She’ll expect you to call, given what I told her.”

“I only need an hour. It’s three now. Come to my warehouse at four.”

“Very well, sir.”

They parted. Thornton hurried to his warehouse, forbidding his clerks to disturb him. He locked himself in his private room and allowed himself the agony of reflection. How could he have lulled himself into calm, remembering her tearful face just hours before—pitying her, yearning toward her—when he had once felt such fierce jealousy at the thought of her near him, unknown to him, at that hour, in that place? How could such purity have stooped from her noble bearing? Yet—was it truly noble? For an instant, the thought flashed: *Was it?* He hated himself for it, but the idea stirred the old fire in him—the attraction he could not deny. And then—her lie. What terror must she have known, to bow so completely to falsehood? He almost pitied her. What would become of her? She couldn’t have foreseen the consequences. If the young man testified… Suddenly, he stood up. There would be no inquest. He would stop it. He would take responsibility. The medical evidence was uncertain—Leonards had a fatal internal illness; the fall might have accelerated death, or the cold and drink afterward. But the cause was ambiguous. If he had known she would be implicated—if he had known she would be forced to lie—he could have prevented it with one word. The decision had hung in balance the night before. Miss Hale might love another—might scorn him—but he would still serve her, silently, without her knowing. He might despise her, but the woman he once loved deserved protection. Shame would follow if she stood in court, swearing to a lie. He would spare her that.

Pale and stern, Thornton emerged through his astonished clerks. He was gone half an hour. When he returned, he looked no less grim—though his purpose had been fulfilled.

He wrote two lines on a slip of paper, sealed it, and handed it to a clerk.

“I appointed Watson—the former packer who joined the police—to call at four. I’ve just met a gentleman from Liverpool who wants to see me. See that he receives this note.”

The message read:

“There will be no inquest. Medical evidence insufficient. Take no further steps. I have not seen the coroner, but I assume responsibility.”

Watson read it and sighed in relief. “That saves me a nasty job. My witnesses weren educate—only the young woman was certain. The porter? Changed his story. Jennings? Not sure. After hearing Miss Hale deny it, none would swear. It would’ve been messy. Now I can tell them they’re free.”

That evening, he returned to Mr. Hale’s. Her father and Dixon urged Margaret to bed, but she refused—though neither understood why. Dixon knew part of the truth—but not all. Margaret had not spoken of her lie, nor of Leonards’ death. So Dixon, torn between duty and concern, pressed her to rest. Margaret did not speak unless addressed. She tried to smile in reply to her father’s worried glances, but the attempt turned into a sigh. At last, worn down, she agreed to retire.

She stood beside her father, gripping the back of his chair.

“You’ll go to bed soon, Papa, won’t you? Don’t stay up alone.”

Her father’s reply was lost in the soft chime of the doorbell.

She kissed him and rushed downstairs, moving with a speed no one would have believed possible moments before. She brushed aside Dixon.

“Don’t come. I’ll open it. I know it’s him—I must manage it myself.”

“As you please, miss,” Dixon muttered. Then, softer: “But you’re not fit for this. You’re more dead than alive.”

“Am I?” Margaret asked, turning, her eyes blazing with strange fire, cheeks flushed, lips still pale and dry.

She opened the door to the inspector and led him into the study. She placed the candle on the table and carefully snuffed it, then faced him.

“You’re late,” she said. “Well?”

She held her breath.

“I’m sorry for the trouble, ma’am. They’ve decided against an inquest. I had other duties, or I’d have come earlier.”

“So it’s over,” she said. “No further inquiry?”

“I believe I have Mr. Thornton’s note,” the inspector said, fumbling in his pocketbook.

“Mr. Thornton?” she echoed.

“Yes. He’s a magistrate. Ah—here it is.”

She couldn’t read it—no, not even close to the flame. The words swam before her. But she held it, staring as if studying it.

“I’m sure it’s a great relief, ma’am. The evidence was shaky—whether he was struck at all. And identity? That made it complicated. As I told Mr. Thornton——”

“Mr. Thornton?” she said again.

“I met him this morning, just as he was leaving your house. Being an old friend and the magistrate who saw Leonards, I thought I’d consult him.”

Margaret sighed deeply. She didn’t want to hear more. She was afraid—afraid of what she’d heard, afraid of what she might hear. She wished he would leave. She forced herself to speak.

“Thank you for coming. It’s very late. I dare say it’s past ten. Oh—here’s the note!” she said, suddenly noticing his hand extended. He was putting it away, when she said, “It’s cramped, dazzling writing. I can’t read it. Will you read it aloud?”

He did.

“Thank you,” she said. “You told Mr. Thornton I wasn’t there?”

“Oh, certainly, ma'am. I’m sorry now I acted on information that turned out so wrong. At first, the young man was sure. Now he says he doubted all along. Hopes his mistake hasn’t cost you your custom. Good night, ma'am.”

“Good night.” She rang for Dixon to show him out. As the woman returned, Margaret darted past.

“It’s all right,” she said, without looking. Before Dixon could question her, she raced upstairs, entered her room, and bolted the door.

She threw herself, still dressed, onto the bed. Too exhausted to think. Half an hour passed before fatigue and chill began to stir her senses. Then she remembered—recalled, combined, questioned.

First: the fear for Frederick was over. The strain was past.

Then: she needed to remember every word the inspector said about Mr. Thornton. When had he seen him? What had he said? What had Thornton done? What were the exact words in the note?

Until she could reconstruct the letter perfectly—even the punctuation—her mind refused to move forward.

Then came the realization: Thornton had seen her near Outwood station on that fatal night. He had been told she denied being there. To him, she was a liar.

She was a liar.

But she felt no remorse before God. Only chaos surrounded the one truth: in Thornton’s eyes, she was degraded. She didn’t care to justify herself. That mattered nothing to him. She hadn’t thought he’d suspect her motives—her brother’s presence was natural. But the lie itself was known. And he had a right to judge her.

“Oh, Frederick! Frederick!” she cried. “What have I sacrificed for you?”

Even in sleep, her mind wandered the same path—now twisted, now monstrous.

When she woke, a new idea dawned like morning light: Thornton had learned of her lie *before* speaking to the coroner. Perhaps he had intervened precisely to spare her from repeating the falsehood. But she pushed it aside—sick with childish pride. If so, she owed him no gratitude. It only proved how deeply he had already judged her guilty. He had taken action not out of kindness, but because he saw her disgrace clearly. She would have faced the whole trial—would have lied again—rather than let him know he had spared her because he already knew her shame.

Why had fate brought him into contact with the inspector? Why was he the magistrate sent to record Leonards’ final words? What had Leonards said? Had Thornton heard enough to connect it to Frederick’s past? Might he already know of the accusation through Mr. Bell? If so, he had saved the son who defied the law to reach his dying mother.

Under this thought, she could feel gratitude—though not yet. Not if his interference stemmed from contempt.

Had anyone just cause to look down on her? Thornton, above all? She had once looked down on him from her imagined heights. Now, she found herself at his feet. And the realization filled her with distress. She shrank from tracing the logic to its end—how much she valued his respect. Whenever the idea surfaced, she turned away. She would not believe it.

It was later than she thought. In her turmoil, she had forgotten to wind her watch. Mr. Hale had ordered she not be disturbed. Soon, the door creaked open. Dixon peeked in. Seeing Margaret awake, she stepped forward with a letter.

“Here’s something to cheer you, miss. A letter from Master Frederick.”

“Thank you, Dixon. How late it is.”

She spoke weakly, letting Dixon place the letter on the counterpane without reaching for it.

“You want breakfast, I’m sure. I’ll bring it in a minute. Master’s got the tray ready.”

Margaret didn’t answer. She waited until Dixon left. She must be alone to open it.

At last, she did. The first thing she saw was the date—two days earlier than she had received it. He had written when he promised. Their fear might have been unnecessary.

She read. It was hasty, but clear. He had met Henry Lennox, who initially shook his head—called his return reckless, given the accusations and powerful enemies. But after discussion, Lennox admitted there might be a chance of acquittal—if credible witnesses could be found. Otherwise, it was a dangerous gamble. He would investigate thoroughly.

“It struck me,” Frederick wrote, “that your introduction, little sister of mine, went a long way. Is it so? He asked so many questions. Seemed sharp, intelligent, busy—lots of clerks. But maybe just lawyer tricks. I’ve just caught a packet—leaving in five minutes. Might have to return to England on this. Keep my visit secret. I’ll send Father some rare old sherry—can’t buy it in England. He needs something good. My dear love to him—God bless him. P.S.—What an escape! Take care you don’t breathe a word of my having been—*not even to the Shaws*.”
