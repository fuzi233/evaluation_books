Every man, however brief or inglorious his time at college may have been, carries in his heart a soft spot for those old university days—those golden hours when the world seemed wide and full of promise, and your only worry was whether you’d get caught sneaking out after curfew. The young man’s life is just beginning: the boy’s leash has been cut, and he’s free to explore the dizzy delights of liberty. He hasn’t yet learned about bills, bad colds, betrayal, or the crushing disappointment of Monday morning. The play hasn’t been performed so many times that it’s lost its charm. Even if the after-drink—repeated like a mechanical prayer—is now flat and bitter, remember how pure and sparkling that first sip of pleasure tasted! How eagerly the lad lunges for the cup, draining it with wild abandon!

Now, let’s be honest: there’s nothing quite as satisfying as watching someone else enjoy themselves—especially when you’re stuck with a poached egg and a glass of water. It’s like seeing your kids laugh at a pantomime when you can’t go yourself. So here’s hoping no one ever grows so jaded by age or experience that they stop smiling at the sight of happy youth.

A few weeks back, I returned from a short visit to the venerable University of Oxbridge—home, once upon a time, to our dear friend Mr. Arthur Pendennis. I took the train, sharing a compartment with a young student from Saint Boniface College. He’d somehow managed to secure an exeat (a rare feat, these days), and was determined to make the most of a day off in London. From the moment we boarded, he didn’t stop talking—jokes, laughter, stories about the latest scandal in the dining hall, the state of the rowing team, and why the porter at the common room wasn’t nearly as good as it used to be. Honestly, I could’ve listened all day. But the journey ended far too soon.

At the station, nothing would do but a hansom cab—he wanted to race into town and dive headfirst into the pleasures awaiting him. Off he went, grinning like a madman, joy lighting up his face like a candle in a storm. Meanwhile, I, humble servant of the reader, had only a small carpet-bag, so I climbed onto the roof of the omnibus, where I sat contentedly between a Jewish peddler smoking cheap cigars and a gentleman’s valet guarding a poodle who looked like it had seen better days. We waited patiently until we were full to bursting—passengers, boxes, and one very disgruntled dog—and then the coachman drove off at a leisurely pace. No hurry. No rush. Neither of us was itching to arrive in this smoky Babylon of London, nor did we care much about dinner at the club or dancing at the Casino. But give it a few years, and my young friend will be just as indifferent—perhaps even more so.

Back in Arthur Pendennis’s day, there were no trains. He arrived at Oxbridge in a grand coach, packed inside and out with dons, gownsmen, fresh-faced undergraduates, and their overprotective guardians. One particularly nervous gentleman from the City—fat, pale, wearing grey stockings—sat next to Major Pendennis, while his son sat across from him, looking like he’d rather be anywhere else. When they heard the coach had been driven for two stages by young Mr. Foker of Saint Boniface College—who, according to rumor, could drive as well as Tom Hicks himself—the father turned green with fear.

Arthur, meanwhile, perched on the roof, gazing down with wide-eyed curiosity. He studied the coach, the passengers, the passing countryside—all of it thrilling. Then, suddenly, the great towers and spires of Oxbridge came into view: ancient buildings, towering elms, and the shimmering river beyond. His heart leapt. This was it. The real thing.

Before setting off, Arthur had spent a few days at his uncle’s lodgings in Bury Street. Major Pendennis thought the lad needed new clothes—nothing unusual, really, since Arthur was already fond of anything that involved shiny buttons or fancy waistcoats. The uncle spared no expense, though he probably wouldn’t have admitted it. London, during this visit, felt eerily quiet. Pall Mall was deserted; the red jackets had all fled to the country. The clubs’ bow-windows were dark. It was like visiting a ghost town.

Still, the Major took his nephew to see a few of these abandoned mansions, and even got Arthur’s name added to the candidate list of one of them. “Arthur Pendennis, Esquire, of Fairoaks Lodge, ——shire, and Saint Boniface College, Oxbridge,” read the parchment, “proposed by Major Pendennis, seconded by Viscount Colchicum.” Arthur read it aloud, trembling with pride. “You’ll be eligible for ballot in three years—by which time you’ll have taken your degree,” the Major said, chuckling. Arthur couldn’t wait. He already imagined himself strolling through the stucco halls, lounging in the library, hosting parties in the drawing-room. The Major watched him with amusement, shaking his head at the young man’s pompous strut as he left the building.

Later, Arthur and Foker took a cab to the Grey Friars School, where they ran into old friends. Kids swarmed around the cab, admiring the chestnut horse, the livery, and Stoopid the tiger—Foker’s personal attendant, who looked like he’d been carved from oak and painted black. The bell for afternoon school rang just as they were chatting. The dreaded Doctor emerged with his Latin grammar clutched in hand. Foker slunk away, but Arthur stepped forward, blushing, and shook the man’s hand. He remembered how that same grammar had once slapped him across the face for misplacing an accent. “Good old book,” he muttered to himself. “We had some battles.”

They then headed to Foker’s brewery—a massive complex not far from the school, where signs bearing the name “Foker’s Entire” glittered everywhere. The junior partner, a dignified old fellow, welcomed them with silver flagons of brown stout so strong it seemed to affect even the horse. Harry Foker’s steed galloped home at breakneck speed, narrowly missing pie stalls, pedestrians, and street posts. Stoopid swung wildly behind, clinging to the board like a man trying to survive a hurricane.

The Major enjoyed every minute of it. He listened intently to Foker’s rambling tales, treated both boys to a fine dinner at a Covent Garden coffeehouse, and later escorted them to the theater. But the highlight? A dinner invitation from Mr. and Lady Agnes Foker, who happened to be in town. “Having secured entry into Lady Agnes’s house,” the Major told Arthur solemnly, “it is your duty to preserve it. Always call when you’re in London. Read Debrett carefully—look up the Roshervilles. Make a few polite references to their history. You’ve got a poetic mind—use it. And never refuse the family porter after dinner. Even if it disagrees with you, like it does me—confoundedly.”

And true to his word, the Major drank the porter—though he grimaced like a man being forced to swallow vinegar.

Lady Agnes, wrapped in maternal devotion, greeted Arthur warmly. She raved about her son’s rigorous studies, fearing they might harm his delicate health. The elder Foker burst out laughing at her melodrama. Arthur winked at his friend. Then Lady Agnes launched into a detailed recounting of Harry’s childhood: his miraculous recovery from measles, his near-drowning incident, the cruelty of the schoolmaster (whom she refused to forgive), and so on. After an hour of this, she declared the Pendennises delightful men and offered them pheasants from Logwood—her estate—inviting them to visit during Christmas or vacation.

That night, as they lit candles in Bury Street, the Major whispered to Arthur: “You handled the Rosherville reference nicely—though you shouldn’t blush so. Remember: with the right introduction, good society is just as easy to access as bad. It takes no more effort to dine with nobility than with a lawyer in Bedford Square. Choose your friends wisely. Your first steps matter most. Did you write to your mother today? No? Do it before you leave. Ask Foker for a frank—they appreciate it. Goodnight. God bless you.”

Arthur wrote a hilarious letter home—full of jokes about the play, the school, the brewery, and the Fokers’ dinner. His mother, praying in her lonely house at Fairoaks, read it with tears in her eyes. She and Laura read it again and again, cherishing every word. This was Arthur’s first real step into the world. Ah, what a perilous journey it is—how easily the bravest stumble, the strongest fall. Brother traveler, may you find a kind arm to guide you, a helping hand for those who trip. May truth light your way, mercy forgive your faults, and love walk beside you always. Without that light, how blind the path, how dark the road.

Finally, the coach rolled into the Trencher Inn in Main Street, Oxbridge. Arthur, thrilled, noticed gownsmen walking past, chapel bells ringing, towers rising above rooftops. He had arrived.

Dr. Portman had already spoken with Mr. Buck, Tutor of Saint Boniface, on Arthur’s behalf. Once the Major ensured Arthur looked presentable—no one wants a freshman arriving in torn trousers—they walked down Main Street, passed Saint George’s College (the grandest in Oxbridge), and arrived at Saint Boniface. As they entered through the ivy-covered gate, Arthur’s heart beat faster. Above the entrance stood a dome draped in vines, and a statue of Saint Boniface, arms raised in blessing. Coats of arms adorned the walls—royal, noble, proud.

The porter pointed to a quirky tower where Mr. Buck lived. They crossed the quadrangle—Arthur absorbing every detail: the fountain, the chapel windows, the hall with its lantern, the Master emerging in rustling silks, the chimneys, turrets, and gables. The scene was etched into his memory forever.

Saint George’s was huge—four quadrangles, magnificent gardens, and students who wore gowns with airs of superiority. Saint Boniface, by comparison, was modest. But it had a fine reputation. Its families were prestigious, its livings generous, its fellowships easy, its boat third on the river, its choir excellent, and its ale—oh, its ale was the best in Oxbridge.

Inside the wainscoted hall, beneath Roubilliac’s statue of Saint Boniface (blessing the table with divine approval), hung portraits of famous alumni: Doctor Griddle (burnt under Henry VIII), Archbishop Bush (who roasted him), Lord Chief Justice Hicks, the Duke of St. David’s, Sprott the Poet, Doctor Blogg (friend of Johnson), and others. The cook was legendary—his son had topped Camford. The wine in the fellows’ room was renowned.

Arthur entered this cozy haven, leaning on his uncle’s arm. They reached Mr. Buck’s rooms. The tutor had received glowing reports from Dr. Portman: “A young gentleman of fortune, ancient lineage, and such character and genius that, with proper guidance, he will be a credit to the college.” Naturally, Mr. Buck was delighted. He invited the Major to dine in hall, offered Arthur the best rooms available—once occupied by a gentleman-pensioner named Spicer—and suggested they take wine afterward.

After parting ways, they returned to the ante-room, where a servant waited with a bag full of caps and gowns. Arthur tried on a gown—it fit perfectly. Then came the cap, which he placed jauntily to one side, just like Fiddicombe, the youngest master at Grey Friars. He admired himself in the gilt mirror, adjusting the cap with flair. The Major smiled. He liked the look of the lad.

Then Davis, the skip, led them across the quadrangle to the rooms—spacious, high-beamed, with deep-set windows. Mr. Spicer’s furniture remained, to be sold. The Major agreed to buy what was useful, politely declining six sporting prints and four opera dancers in gauze draperies—“No thank you,” he said, laughing. “Not my taste.”

They went to hall. Arthur sat with other freshmen, eating his commons. The Major joined the high table with tutors and fathers. Afterward, they drank wine with Mr. Buck, then attended chapel. The Major sat gravely in the upper seat, watching the Master—Doctor Donne—in his carved stall beneath the organ loft, holding a massive prayer book like a sacred relic. All the freshmen behaved well—except Foker, who arrived late and giggled with his cronies like they were at a music hall.

Ah, those were different times—William IV was king. Young men are better behaved now. Still, Saint Boniface had a reputation for being… *fast*.

That night, Arthur couldn’t sleep. He tossed and turned, eager to begin his college life. What was he thinking about? His mother? Yes—poor soul, whose whole life revolved around him. Miss Fotheringay? Oh yes—his eternal passion, the source of so many sleepless nights, so much longing. He blushed, muttering incoherent exclamations—“How could I have loved such a woman? How could I have stooped so low?” He laughed bitterly. “Poor old Smirke! I’ll write and cheer him up. He won’t die of it—ha, ha!”

If the Major had been awake, he might have heard a dozen such outbursts. Perhaps it would’ve been better for Arthur to have spent the night in quiet reflection. But the world had already caught hold of him—through his selfish mentor, his uncle. And anyone who’s followed Arthur’s story knows: he was weak, impulsive, vain, generous, selfish, fickle—exactly the sort of young man who thrives on self-gratification.

Six months of passionate heartbreak had aged him. There was a vast difference between the innocent boy of eighteen and the man who now saw love clearly—for what it truly was. Arthur had gained experience, confidence, and a certain arrogance. And now, as he began his life among his peers, he carried with him not just books and linen, but a new Alma Mater—one he’d have to learn to love—or flee from.
