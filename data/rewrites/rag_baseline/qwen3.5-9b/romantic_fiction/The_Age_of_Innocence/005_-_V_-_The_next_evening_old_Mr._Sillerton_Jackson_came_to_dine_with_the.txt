The next evening, Old Mr. Sillerton Jackson arrived to dine with the Archers.

Mrs. Archer was a woman of shrinking temperament, retreating instinctively from the noise of society, yet she possessed a voracious hunger for its mechanics. She treated the investigation of her neighbors' lives with the patient taxonomy of a collector and the rigorous classification of a naturalist. Her sister, Miss Sophy Jackson, who resided with him and was tolerated by everyone else solely because they could not secure the company of her formidable brother, supplied the minor gossip fragments necessary to fill the gaps in his data sets.

Consequently, whenever Mrs. Archer required information, she issued a summons for Mr. Jackson to dine. Since she honored few guests with such invitations, and since she and her daughter Janey constituted an exemplary audience, Mr. Jackson invariably attended personally rather than delegating to his sister. Ideally, if he held the power of dictation, he would have selected evenings when Newland was absent—not because the young man was disagreeable (they operated with capital efficiency at their club)—but because the old chronicler occasionally sensed in Newland a propensity to scrutinize his evidence with a severity the ladies of the household never displayed.

If perfection were achievable on earth, Mr. Jackson would also have stipulated that Mrs. Archer's provisions be superior. Yet New York, as far as the collective consciousness could trace, had bifurcated into two fundamental clans: the Mingotts and Mansons and their progeny, obsessed with sustenance, attire, and currency; and the Archer-Newland-van-der-Luyden lineage, devoted to travel, horticulture, and high fiction, regarding the grosser pleasures of the table with disdain.

One cannot possess all advantages. Dining with the Lovell Mingotts yielded canvas-back ducks, terrapin, and vintage vintages; conversing at Adeline Archer's offered discourse on Alpine topography and *The Marble Faun*; fortunately, the Archer Madeira had circumnavigated the Cape. Thus, when a friendly summons arrived from Mrs. Archer, Mr. Jackson, a true eclectician, would typically inform his sister: "I have suffered a bout of gout since my last repast at the Lovell Mingotts'; it will serve me well to diet at Adeline's."

Mrs. Archer, a widow for many years, occupied an upper floor of West Twenty-eighth Street with her son and daughter. A separate wing was allocated to Newland, while the two women compressed themselves into narrower quarters below. In a cloudless harmony of aesthetic sensibilities, they cultivated ferns within Wardian cases, constructed macramé lace and wool embroidery on linen, amassed collections of American Revolutionary glazed ware, subscribed to *Good Words*, and consumed Ouida's novels for the atmospheric quality of their Italian settings. (They favored tales of peasant life for their scenic descriptions and benevolent sentiments, though generally preferring narratives of society where motives were transparent; they criticized Dickens harshly for failing to capture the essence of a gentleman, and deemed Thackeray less at ease in the grand world than Bulwer, who, however, was increasingly viewed as antiquated.) Both Mrs. and Miss Archer were ardent devotees of landscape. It was the primary object of their pursuit and admiration during their sporadic excursions abroad; they relegated architecture and painting to the domain of men, specifically scholars who read Ruskin.

Mrs. Archer was born a Newland. Mother and daughter, resembling sisters in every feature, were universally acknowledged as "true Newlands": tall, pallid, and slightly hunched, with elongated noses, sweet smiles, and a specific drooping distinction reminiscent of faded Reynolds portraits. Their physical similarity would have been total were it not for an elderly corpulence that strained Mrs. Archer's black brocade, whereas Miss Archer's brown and purple poplins hung with increasing slackness upon her virgin frame as the years advanced.

Mentally, the resemblance was less absolute than their identical mannerisms suggested. The prolonged habit of cohabitation in mutual dependency had endowed them with a shared lexicon and the reflexive habit of prefacing opinions with "Mother thinks" or "Janey thinks," depending on which party sought to assert dominance. In reality, while Mrs. Archer's serene unimaginativeness found comfort in the accepted and familiar, Janey was susceptible to sudden aberrations of fancy, springing from wells of suppressed romance.

Mother and daughter adored one another and revered their son and brother. Archer loved them with a tenderness rendered compunctious and uncritical by the weight of their exaggerated admiration, bolstered by his secret satisfaction in it. After all, he reasoned, it was beneficial for a man to have his authority respected within his own household, even if his sense of humor occasionally led him to question the force of his mandate.

This evening, the young man was convinced that Mr. Jackson would prefer he dine elsewhere, yet he had his own rationales for remaining.

Certainly, Old Jackson desired to discuss Ellen Olenska, and certainly Mrs. Archer and Janey craved his insights. All three would experience a degree of embarrassment given Newland's presence, now that his prospective alliance with the Mingott clan had been announced; the young man awaited with amused curiosity to observe how they would navigate this friction.

They commenced, obliquely, with Mrs. Lemuel Struthers.

"It is a pity the Beauforts invited her," Mrs. Archer said softly. "Yet Regina invariably obeys his commands; and *Beaufort*—"

"Certain nuances elude Beaufort," Mr. Jackson observed, cautiously examining the broiled shad and wondering for the thousandth time why Mrs. Archer's cook invariably charred the roe to ash. (Newland, having long shared this observation, could always detect it in the elder man's expression of melancholic disapproval.)

"Oh, necessarily; Beaufort is a vulgar man," Mrs. Archer conceded. "My grandfather Newland always instructed my mother: 'Whatever you do, do not introduce that fellow Beaufort to the girls.' But at least he has the advantage of association with gentlemen; in England, they say. It is all quite mysterious..." She glanced at Janey and paused. She and Janey knew every crease of the Beaufort enigma, yet in public, Mrs. Archer maintained the pretense that the subject was inappropriate for the unmarried.

"But this Mrs. Struthers," Mrs. Archer resumed; "what did you say *she* was, Sillerton?"

"From a mine: or rather, from the saloon at the head of the pit. Subsequently, with Living Wax-Works, touring New England. After the police dismantled *that* enterprise, they claim she resided—" Mr. Jackson, in his turn, glanced at Janey, whose eyes began to bulge beneath their prominent lids. There remained hiatuses in her understanding of Mrs. Struthers's past.

"Then," Mr. Jackson continued (and Archer noted he was pondering why no one had instructed the butler never to slice cucumbers with a steel blade), "then Lemuel Struthers appeared. They say his advertiser utilized the girl's head for shoe-polish posters; her hair is intensely black, you know—the Egyptian style. In any case, he... eventually... married her." There were volumes of innuendo in the spacing of the word "eventually," each syllable receiving its due emphasis.

"Oh, well—in the pass we have reached nowadays, it matters not," Mrs. Archer stated indifferently. The ladies were not genuinely interested in Mrs. Struthers at that moment; the subject of Ellen Olenska was too fresh and too absorbing. Indeed, Mrs. Struthers's name had been introduced merely to facilitate Mrs. Archer's subsequent query: "And Newland's new cousin—the Countess Olenska? Was *she* at the ball as well?"

A faint touch of sarcasm colored the reference to her son, and Archer perceived it, having anticipated it. Even Mrs. Archer, rarely overly pleased with human events, had been wholly gratified by her son's engagement. ("Especially following that silly affair with Mrs. Rushworth," she had remarked to Janey, alluding to what had once seemed to Newland a tragedy bearing a permanent scar upon his soul.)

There existed no superior match in New York than May Welland, regardless of the angle of examination. Naturally, such a union was precisely what Newland was entitled to; yet young men are so foolish and incalculable—and some women so ensnaring and unscrupulous—that it was nothing short of a miracle to see one's only son survive the Siren Isle and reach the harbor of blameless domesticity.

All this Mrs. Archer felt, and her son knew she felt; but he also knew she had been disturbed by the premature announcement of his engagement, or rather by its cause. It was for this reason—that on the whole he was a tender and indulgent master—that he had remained at home that evening. "It is not that I do not approve of the Mingotts' *esprit de corps*," Mrs. Archer grumbled to Janey, the sole witness of her slight deviations from perfect sweetness; "but why Newland's engagement should be entangled with that Olenska woman's comings and goings, I fail to comprehend."

She had conducted herself beautifully—and in beautiful conduct she was unsurpassed—during the call upon Mrs. Welland; yet Newland knew (and his betrothed undoubtedly surmised) that throughout the visit, she and Janey had been nervously vigilant for Madame Olenska's potential intrusion; and when they departed the house together, she had permitted herself to remark to her son: "I am thankful that Augusta Welland received us alone."
