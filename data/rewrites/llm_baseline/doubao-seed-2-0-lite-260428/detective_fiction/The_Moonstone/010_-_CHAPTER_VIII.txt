For a moment, I must hit pause on this story.

Digging up my own old memories, and calling on Penelope to supplement them from her little daily journal, I realize we can skip lightly over the stretch between Mr. Franklin Blake’s arrival and Miss Rachel’s twenty-first of June birthday. Most of those weeks drifted by without a single thing worth marking down. With your kind permission, I’ll only note the key dates that matter, and reserve my day-by-day, blow-by-blow telling for when the Moonstone stops being a secret between a few of us and becomes the whole household’s urgent, all-consuming business.

That settled, we move right back to where we left off: the bottle of sweet-smelling magic ink I found abandoned on the gravel path late that night.

The next morning, the twenty-sixth of the month, I showed Mr. Franklin the ink bottle and told him the whole story of the wandering Indian jugglers. His take was sharper than I expected: he was not only certain the Indians had been hanging around the house hunting the Diamond, but convinced they were foolish enough to fully believe their own hocus-pocus — the sort that lets them draw signs on a little boy’s head, pour ink into his palm, and claim he can see people and places no normal human eye could reach. “We’ve got the exact same trick over here too,” he told me, “just without the ink. They call it clairvoyance, French for ‘clear sight.’ Depend on it, the Indians assumed we’d keep the Diamond in this house, and they brought their little boy seer to show them exactly where it was hidden if they snuck inside.”

“Do you think they’ll try breaking in again, sir?” I asked him.

“Depends entirely on what the boy can actually do,” he said. “If he can see the Diamond right through the iron walls of the Frizinghall bank safe, we won’t see hide nor hair of those Indians for a good while. If he can’t? We’ll catch them skulking in the shrubbery again before three more nights are out.”

I waited, sure enough, for that second chance to catch the rogues. But strange as it sounds, it never came.

Maybe the jugglers heard rumors in town that Mr. Franklin had been spotted heading to the bank with the Diamond, and drew their own conclusions. Maybe the boy actually did see the gem in that vault (which I, for one, flat refuse to believe). Maybe it was just pure dumb luck. But the plain fact is: not a single one of those Indians came within a mile of the house for all the weeks leading up to Miss Rachel’s birthday. They stayed in town peddling their little magic shows, Mr. Franklin and I kept quiet about our suspicions to avoid tipping them off, and that’s the end of the Indian business — for now.

On the twenty-ninth, Miss Rachel and Mr. Franklin stumbled on a new hobby that ate up all their spare time, and it’s important I tell you about it, because it ties directly to the trouble that’s coming later.

Gentlefolk face a very nasty hidden reef in their lives, you see: the reef of their own total idleness. They spend every day casting around for something to do, and far too often — especially the ones who call themselves “intellectual” — they drift blindfolded straight into some messy, cruel pastime. Nine times out of ten, they spend their days torturing or ruining something, and they pat themselves on the back for “improving their minds” when all they’re really doing is making a right mess of the house. I’ve seen ladies and gentlemen trudge out every morning with empty pillboxes, catch newts, beetles, spiders, frogs, stick pins through the poor wretches, and cut them to bits without a flicker of remorse, all in the name of “natural history.” I’ve seen them poke at perfectly pretty flowers with sharp little scalpels just to see what’s inside, as if the color or the scent gets any sweeter once you rip the thing apart. You end up staining your fingers with photography chemicals, chipping bits of stone off the walls to put in your rock collection, dripping grit into every dinner dish, or keeping tadpoles in dirty glass jars that make the whole house want to throw up. Hard work to pay for your bread and roof is nothing next to that empty, restless idleness. Thank your lucky stars your head has something it has to think about, and your hands have something they have to do.

Thankfully, Mr. Franklin and Miss Rachel never hurt a living thing. All they ruined was the wood panel of a door.

Mr. Franklin, who dabbled in every art under the sun, had lately got into what he called “decorative painting.” He’d invented a new custom mixture to thin his paints that he named his “vehicle.” I don’t know what was in it, but I can tell you what it did in two words: it stank so bad the dogs sneezed the second they stepped into the room. Miss Rachel was wild to try it, so he sent all the way to London for the supplies, strapped an apron and a bib over her nice gown, and set her to work decorating the little private sitting-room everyone called her boudoir. They started on the inside of the door. Mr. Franklin scraped all the nice shiny varnish off with pumice stone to make a rough surface to paint on, and under his direction, Miss Rachel covered it all with griffins, songbirds, flowers, and chubby little cupids copied from designs by that famous old Italian painter — the one who painted half the Virgin Marys in the world and had a baker’s daughter for a sweetheart. It was slow, messy work, but they never seemed to tire of it. Every spare minute they were glued together over that door, chattering and painting away, as busy as bees.

The next date worth marking is Sunday, the fourth of June. That night, we all sat in the servants’ hall debating a question that, like the door decoration, will turn out to matter a great deal later. Seeing how much Miss Rachel and Mr. Franklin liked being around each other, and what a handsome, well-matched pair they made, most of the staff bet we’d have a wedding in the house before summer was over. I was the one who poured cold water on that idea — I thought Miss Rachel might very well marry, but I very much doubted her groom would be Mr. Franklin Blake.

Nobody could deny Mr. Franklin was head over heels for her. The hard part was figuring out what was going on in Miss Rachel’s head. Let me paint her portrait for you proper, and you can try to fathom her for yourself, if you can.

Her eighteenth birthday was coming up on the twenty-first of June. If you like dark, slim women (which I’m told are out of fashion in London society lately), you’d think she was the prettiest girl you ever laid eyes on. Every line of her was perfectly proportioned, from the top of her glossy jet-black hair down to her feet, and the way she sat, stood, and walked made it obvious her grace came from her body, not her fancy clothes. Her eyes were just as dark as her hair. Her nose was a tiny bit too small, I’ll admit, but her mouth and chin? Mr. Franklin swore they were “morsels for the gods,” and her warm, sun-kissed complexion was far more reliable than the actual sun, always looking soft and lovely. She carried her head as straight as a dart, sharp and spirited and full of fire, had a clear, silver voice, and a smile that lit up her eyes before it even reached her lips. That’s Miss Rachel, exactly as she was.

She wasn’t perfect, of course. She had just as many faults as you or I. To be blunt, she was nothing like most girls her age: she had ideas all her own, and she was stubborn enough to defy every fashion and every social rule if they didn’t suit her. In small, trivial things that independence was charming, but in matters that counted, Lady Verinder and I both agreed it went too far. She judged everything for herself, never asked for anyone’s advice, never told anyone what she was planning to do beforehand, never came running with secrets and confidences even to her own mother. She loved hard and hated hard, and went her own way through joy and sorrow, never leaning on anyone else. Over and over I heard Lady Verinder say, “Rachel’s best friend and Rachel’s worst enemy are one and the same: Rachel herself.”

And for all that stubborn secrecy? There wasn’t a flicker of falseness in her. I never once saw her break a promise. I never saw her say no and mean yes. I remember more than one occasion when she was a little girl, she took the blame and the punishment for something a playfellow did, and even when they called her out on it, she never confessed who really did it — and she never lied about it, either. She’d look you straight in the face, toss her little head, and say flat out, “I won’t tell you.” Even when they locked her up on bread and water for being naughty, she never snitched. Devilishly self-willed, yes — but the finest, truest creature that ever walked this earth. If that sounds like a contradiction? Spend twenty-four hours watching your own wife. If she doesn’t show you some sort of contradiction in that time? Heaven help you — you married a monster.

Now that you know Miss Rachel, we’re face to face with that question of who she intended to marry. On the twelfth of June, Lady Verinder sent an invitation to a gentleman in London to come celebrate her daughter’s birthday. That was the man I was convinced she had set her heart on: another cousin of hers, Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite.

Lady Verinder’s second sister had a terrible heartbreak young, then married a rich Frizinghall banker named Ablewhite against the whole family’s wishes, a so-called “misalliance” that caused all sorts of drama back in the day. But time softened everyone up, and the Ablewhites became well-respected local gentry living in a big fine house just outside Frizinghall. We won’t be seeing much of the rest of the family, but Mr. Godfrey, their second son, matters very much to this story.

As far as I was concerned, Mr. Franklin didn’t stand a chance against him.

For one thing, Mr. Godfrey was over six feet tall, broad-shouldered, with a rosy fair complexion, a smooth clean-shaven round face, and a head of thick flaxen hair that fell lazily over the back of his neck. You probably already know him if you’ve ever given money to a ladies’ charity in London: he was a barrister by trade, a favorite of every lady in the city, and a full-time professional philanthropist. He was vice-president, manager, and referee for every society you could name: societies for poor new mothers, societies for rescuing fallen women, societies for pushing women into jobs that used to be done by men. Wherever there was a committee of nice ladies sitting around a table debating charity work, there was Mr. Godfrey at the end of the table, all smiles and hat in hand, making sure no one lost their temper and guiding them through all the boring business. I saw him speak once at Exeter Hall once, and he could make an audience of a thousand people cry so hard they emptied their purses for his cause before they even knew what happened. He was famous, kind, sweet-tempered, loved by everyone. What chance did a regular man like Franklin have against him?

On the fourteenth, his reply arrived. He accepted the invitation, said he’d come from Wednesday (the birthday) to Friday evening, when his charity duties would force him back to London. He even enclosed a poem he’d written for his cousin’s “natal day.” Miss Rachel and Mr. Franklin laughed at the silly verses all through dinner, and Penelope, who was solidly on Franklin’s side, turned to me all smug and asked what I thought of my theory about Godfrey now. “My dear girl,” I told her, “Miss Rachel is leading you up a false scent. My nose isn’t that easy to fool. Wait till Mr. Ablewhite shows up in person.”

Penelope argued Mr. Franklin could win her over long before Godfrey got here. And I had to admit, Franklin was pulling out all the stops to win her favor. He was one of the heaviest smokers I ever met, but he gave up his cigars cold turkey just because Rachel said she hated the stale smoke smell on his clothes. He slept horribly for days without his tobacco, came down every morning haggard and dark-eyed, until even Rachel begged him to start smoking again. No, he said, he wouldn’t do a single thing to annoy her, and he’d fight the craving until he learned to sleep without cigars, no matter how long it took. The whole downstairs staff agreed that level of devotion was sure to win Rachel over, especially with all the hours they spent together decorating that door. But I knew something they didn’t: Penelope had spotted a framed photograph of Godfrey on Rachel’s bedroom dressing table, showing him speaking at a public meeting, his hair blown back by his own passionate speech, his soft eyes smiling out at everyone. Every morning while Rachel was getting her hair combed, that photo was staring right at her. He’d be there in person soon enough, I was sure of it.

On the sixteenth of June, something happened that made Franklin’s chances look even slimmer. A stranger with a thick foreign accent showed up at the door asking to speak to Mr. Franklin Blake privately on business. It couldn’t have had anything to do with the Moonstone, because Franklin never said a word about it to me — he went straight to Lady Verinder the second the man left. She must have mentioned it to Rachel, because that evening at the piano, Rachel snapped at Franklin, saying some very sharp things about the disreputable people he’d run around with and the bad habits he’d picked up living abroad on the Continent. The next day, neither of them went near the door to paint a single stroke. I guessed some old trouble — a debt, or a woman — that Franklin had got himself tangled up in overseas had followed him all the way back to England, but no one was telling the servants anything.

By the seventeenth, the little cloud had blown over. They were back at the door, painting and laughing together, as friendly as they ever were. Penelope swore Franklin had popped the question to Rachel when they made up, and that she’d neither said yes nor no — she’d teased him that he wasn’t serious, and then Penelope caught her looking all sorry afterwards. But I knew Rachel’s stubborn, private nature far too well to believe she’d ever let her maid read her that easily. Penelope wasn’t reporting what she’d actually seen, she was reporting what she wanted to be true.

On the nineteenth, we sent for the doctor. He came to see our second housemaid, Rosanna Spearman — the same girl who’d already puzzled me so much down at the Shivering Sand. The lull before the birthday had made her behavior even more strange. Penelope and I had both caught her sneaking around, deliberately putting herself in Mr. Franklin’s path as often as she could. He didn’t notice her any more than he noticed the house cat, never once wasted a look on her plain quiet face. Her appetite got worse, her eyes were red and puffy every morning from crying half the night. One day Penelope made a terrible discovery: she walked in on Rosanna at Mr. Franklin’s dressing table, carefully plucking the rose Miss Rachel had put in his buttonhole earlier that day, and tucking a different rose, one she’d picked herself, in its place. After that, she got snappish with me when I tried to give her gentle hints to behave, and she even grew short with Miss Rachel on the rare occasions Rachel spoke to her.

Lady Verinder noticed the change, and asked me what I thought was wrong. I tried to protect the poor girl, told her it was just nervous exhaustion, and when the doctor agreed, Lady Verinder offered to send her to one of our quiet inland farms for a change of air. Rosanna begged and sobbed on her knees to be allowed to stay, and in an unforgivable moment of soft-heartedness, I advised Lady Verinder to let her stay a little longer. If I could have seen even a little way into the future, I would have dragged Rosanna Spearman out of this house myself, that very day.
