The Twelve True Fishermen were a select club, and if you ever managed to gain entry to the Vernon Hotel for their annual dinner, you would notice a peculiar detail: the evening coats of the members were green, not black. Should you, with the audacity of a fool, ask why, the answer would likely be a defensive claim that they wished to avoid being mistaken for waiters. You would then retreat, crushed, leaving behind an unsolved mystery and a tale worth telling.

Imagine, instead, meeting a mild, hard-working little priest named Father Brown. If you asked him what the most singular stroke of luck in his life had been, he might reply that it occurred at the Vernon Hotel, where he averted a crime and perhaps saved a soul merely by listening to a few footsteps in a passage. He might be proud of this wild guess, but since it is highly unlikely you will ever rise high enough to join "The Twelve True Fishermen" or sink low enough to find Father Brown among the slums, you will never hear the story unless I tell it to you now.

The Vernon Hotel was an institution born of an oligarchical society gone mad on good manners. It was a topsy-turvy commercial enterprise that paid not by attracting people, but by turning them away. In the heart of a plutocracy, tradesmen had become cunning enough to be more fastidious than their customers, creating difficulties so that wealthy clients would spend money and diplomacy overcoming them. If there were a fashionable hotel in London where no man under six feet could enter, society would meekly assemble parties of tall men to dine there. The Vernon Hotel stood inconspicuously in Belgravia, a small and inconvenient place whose very inconveniences served as walls protecting a particular class. Its most vital inconvenience was that practically only twenty-four people could dine there at once. The only big table was the celebrated terrace table, open to the air on a veranda overlooking one of London's most exquisite old gardens. Thus, even the twenty-four seats could only be enjoyed in warm weather, making the experience both difficult and intensely desired.

The owner was a Jew named Lever, who made nearly a million by making it difficult to get in. He combined this exclusivity with the most careful polish in performance. The wines and cooking rivaled the best in Europe, and the attendants mirrored the fixed mood of the English upper class. There were only fifteen waiters in total, each known to the proprietor like the fingers on his hand. It was easier to become a Member of Parliament than a waiter there. Each was trained in terrible silence and smoothness, acting as a gentleman's servant. Indeed, there was generally at least one waiter to every gentleman who dined.

The Twelve True Fishermen would not have consented to dine anywhere else, insisting on luxurious privacy and being utterly upset by the thought of any other club dining in the same building. On the occasion of their annual dinner, they exposed all their treasures, including a celebrated set of fish knives and forks—the insignia of the society—exquisitely wrought in silver in the form of a fish, each hilt loaded with a large pearl. These were laid out for the fish course, which was always the most magnificent part of the repast. The society had vast ceremonies but no history or object; that was its aristocracy. You did not have to be anything to be one of the Twelve, unless you were already a certain sort of person, in which case you never even heard of them. It had existed for twelve years, with Mr. Audley as president and the Duke of Chester as vice-president.

How I came to know of this appalling hotel, or how Father Brown found himself there, is simple. There is an aged rioter and demagogue who breaks into refined retreats preaching that all men are brothers, and wherever this leveller went, Father Brown followed. One afternoon, an Italian waiter was struck down by a paralytic stroke. Lever, marveling at such superstitions, sent for the nearest Popish priest. Father Brown, with a meek impudence he would show equally in Buckingham Palace, asked for a room and writing materials. Lever was torn between kindness and the dislike of any difficulty or scene. The presence of an unusual stranger in his hotel that evening was like a speck of dirt on something just cleaned. There were fifteen waiters and twelve guests; finding a new guest would have been startling. Moreover, the priest's appearance was second-rate and his clothes muddy. Lever hit on a plan to cover the disgrace: he assigned Father Brown a small private room without other outlets, situated between the office and the cloak room, a space usually reserved for delicate matters like lending a duke a thousand pounds.

By the time Father Brown reached the final paragraphs of his document, his thoughts began to wander. The gathering gloom sharpened his sense of sound. Outside, he heard a recurrent noise, a patter of feet passing the door. In a hotel, this was unlikely, yet the footsteps were strangely odd. Taken as a whole, they defied classification as either regular or irregular. First came a long rush of rapid little steps, like a light man winning a race, followed by a slow, swinging stamp occupying about the same time. Then the run returned, then the thud. It was certainly the same pair of boots, distinguished by a small, unmistakable creak. Father Brown's head, which could not help asking questions, almost split. Why should a man run to walk? Or walk to run? His brain grew dark as his room.

Yet, in the blackness of his cell, his thoughts became vivid. He visualized the fantastic feet capering along the corridor in unnatural attitudes. Was it a heathen dance? A new scientific exercise? The slow step certainly wasn't the proprietor's; men of his type walked with a rapid waddle or sat still. Nor was it a servant. That heavy yet springy step, with a careless emphasis, belonged to only one animal on earth: a gentleman of western Europe who had never worked for his living. Just as he came to this certainty, the step changed to a quicker one, running past the door feverishly, almost as if walking on tiptoe. Yet it was not associated with secrecy, but with something he couldn't remember. Suddenly, he sprang to his feet with a new idea. He walked to the door, tried the lock on the office (it was locked), and looked at the window, now a square pane of purple cloud cleft by the livid sunset. For an instant, he smelled evil, as a dog smells rats.

Rationality regained supremacy. He remembered the proprietor's instruction to lock the door and that Lever would return later. He resolved to finish his work, catching the last stormy evening light. He had written for twenty minutes, bending closer to the paper, when suddenly he sat upright. He had heard the strange feet again. This time, they had a third oddity. Previously, the man had walked with levity and lightning quickness, but this time he ran. Swift, soft, bounding steps came along the corridor like the pads of a fleeing panther. Whoever was coming was a strong, active man in still yet tearing excitement. Yet, when the sound swept up to the office like a whispering whirlwind, it suddenly changed again to the old slow, swaggering stamp.

Father Brown flung down his paper and went into the cloak room on the other side. The attendant was absent. Groping through a grey forest of overcoats, he found the dim cloak room opened onto the lighted corridor in the form of a counter. A light above the semicircular arch threw theatrical illumination on the man standing outside. He was an elegant man in very plain evening dress, tall but with an air of not taking up much room, capable of sliding along like a shadow. His face was swarthy and vivacious, foreign. His figure was good, his manners confident, though his black coat was a shade below his dignity, bulging and bagging oddly. The moment he caught sight of Brown's black silhouette against the sunset, he tossed down a scrap of paper with a number and called out, "I want my hat and coat, please; I find I have to go away at once."

Father Brown took the paper and went to fetch the coat. Meanwhile, the stranger laughed, "I haven't got any silver; you can keep this," throwing down half a sovereign. In that instant, Father Brown lost his head. In such moments, he put two and two together and made four million. Often the Catholic Church did not approve of this, nor did he himself, but it was real inspiration.

"I think, sir," he said civilly, "that you have some silver in your pocket."

The tall gentleman stared. "Hang it," he cried, "if I choose to give you gold, why should you complain?"

"Because silver is sometimes more valuable than gold," said the priest mildly, "that is, in large quantities."

The stranger looked curiously up the passage, then back at Brown, then at the window. He seemed to make up his mind. He put one hand on the counter, vaulted over as easily as an acrobat, and towered above the priest, putting one tremendous hand upon his collar. "Stand still," he said in a hacking whisper. "I don't want to threaten you, but—"

"I do want to threaten you," said Father Brown in a voice like a rolling drum, "I want to threaten you with the worm that dieth not, and the fire that is not quenched."

"You're a rum sort of cloak-room clerk," said the other.

"I am a priest, Monsieur Flambeau," said Brown, "and I am ready to hear your confession."

The stranger stood gasping, then staggered back into a chair.

Meanwhile, the first two courses of the dinner of The Twelve True Fishermen had proceeded with placid success. The *hors d'œuvres* were various and manifold to the point of madness, taken seriously because they were avowedly useless extras. The soup course was light and unpretending, a simple vigil for the feast of fish to come. The talk was that strange, slight talk which governs the British Empire in secret. Cabinet ministers were alluded to by their Christian names with bored benignity. The Radical Chancellor was praised for his minor poetry; the Tory leader was discussed and praised as a Liberal. Politicians seemed very important, yet anything seemed important about them except their politics. Mr. Audley, the chairman, was an amiable, elderly man who still wore Gladstone collars, a symbol of phantasmal yet fixed society. He had never done anything, not even anything wrong. He was simply in the thing. The Duke of Chester, the vice-president, was a pleasant youth with flat, fair hair and enormous estates. In public, he was brilliant when he made a joke and able when he didn't. In private, he was frankly silly, like a schoolboy.

There were twenty-four seats at the terrace table, and only twelve members. They ranged along the inner side, commanding an uninterrupted view of the garden, the colors still vivid though evening was closing in luridly. The chairman sat in the center, the vice-president at the right-hand end. When the guests entered, it was custom for all fifteen waiters to stand lining the wall like troops presenting arms, while the fat proprietor bowed with radiant surprise. But before the first chink of knife and fork, this army had vanished, leaving only one or two required to distribute plates in deathly silence. Mr. Lever had disappeared in convulsions of courtesy long before. When the sacred fish course arrived—a monstrous pudding about the size of a wedding cake containing several fishes that had lost the shapes God gave them—the Twelve True Fishermen approached it gravely. They took up their celebrated silver fish knives and forks. This course was dealt with in eager, devouring silence. Only when his plate was nearly empty did the young duke make the ritual remark: "They can't do this anywhere but here."

"Nowhere," said Mr. Audley in a deep bass voice, nodding his venerable head. "Nowhere, assuredly, except here. It was represented to me that at the Café Anglais—"

He was interrupted by the removal of his plate but recaptured his thread. "Nothing like it, sir," he said, shaking his head ruthlessly.

"Overrated place," said Colonel Pound, speaking for the first time in months.

"Oh, I don't know," said the Duke of Chester, "it's jolly good for some things. You can't beat it at—"

A waiter came swiftly along the room, then stopped dead. His stoppage was as silent as his tread. All those vague and kindly gentlemen felt a strange shame, a product of modern humanitarianism mixed with the abyss between rich and poor souls. A genuine aristocrat would have thrown things; a democrat would have asked what the devil he was doing. These modern plutocrats could not bear a poor man near them, either as a slave or a friend. Something had gone wrong with the servants, causing a dull, hot embarrassment. They wanted it over. The waiter, after standing rigid like a cataleptic, turned and ran madly out.

When he reappeared in the doorway, accompanied by another waiter who whispered with southern fierceness, then a third, then a fourth, Mr. Audley felt it necessary to break the silence. He used a very loud cough and said, "Splendid work young Moocher's doing in Burmah. Now, no other nation in the world could have—"

A fifth waiter sped towards him like an arrow, whispering, "So sorry. Important! Might the proprietor speak to you?"

The chairman turned in disorder and saw Mr. Lever coming with his lumbering quickness. The proprietor's face was no longer genial copper-brown but sickly yellow.

"You will pardon me, Mr. Audley," he said with asthmatic breathlessness. "I have great apprehensions. Your fish-plates, they are cleared away with the knife and fork on them!"

"Well, I hope so," said the chairman warmly.

"You see him?" panted the excited hotel keeper. "You see the waiter who took them away? You know him?"

"Know the waiter?" answered Mr. Audley indignantly. "Certainly not!"

Mr. Lever opened his hands in agony. "I never send him. I know not when or why he come. I send my waiter to take away the plates, and he find them already away."
