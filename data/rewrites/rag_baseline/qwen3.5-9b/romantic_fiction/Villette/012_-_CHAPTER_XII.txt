The garden behind Madame Beck's residence on the Rue Fossette, though compact by urban standards, retained a distinct aesthetic value in my memory, appearing particularly serene under the retrospective lens of time. In a landscape dominated by masonry and heated pavements, the mere presence of vegetation offered a psychological respite; the enclosure of greenery functioned as a necessary buffer against the harshness of the surrounding architecture. Local folklore attributed the site's origins to a former convent, suggesting that centuries prior, before the city's expansion obliterated the rural avenues and agricultural fields, a tragic event occurred here. This narrative posited the existence of a spectral figure—a nun clad in black and white—haunting specific locales during certain nocturnal periods. While the immediate vicinity had since been densely populated, rendering the original terror obsolete, the myth persisted, anchored by surviving botanical relics. Specifically, a massive, ancient pear tree, nearly dead save for a few resilient branches that continued to produce scented blossoms in spring and nectar-laden fruit in autumn, stood as a testament to the past. Beneath its gnarled roots, partially exposed by the removal of moss, lay a slab of smooth, obsidian-like stone. Unverified yet widely circulated, the legend claimed this marked the entrance to a crypt containing the remains of a girl interred alive by a medieval ecclesiastical council for violating her vows. Consequently, the garden became a locus of collective anxiety, where the shifting shadows of the tree's foliage were interpreted as the phantom's manifestation, mocking the timidity of observers with illusions of a black robe and white veil dancing in the night wind.

Beyond this superstitious overlay, the garden possessed intrinsic merits. During summer mornings, I utilized the solitude to observe its features undisturbed; similarly, summer evenings offered opportunities for introspection, allowing me to commune with the rising moon or appreciate the tactile sensation of evening breezes and morning dew. The terrain featured verdant turf and whitewashed gravel paths, adorned with vibrant nasturtiums clustering around the bases of towering, twisted orchard trees. Architectural elements included a substantial pergola shaded by an acacia and a more intimate bower concealed within vines climbing a high, gray wall. These tendrils formed intricate patterns, draping jasmine and ivy in a display of organic abundance over a favored corner where the flora intertwined.

Conversely, the atmosphere shifted dramatically at midday. When Madame Beck's institution operated at full capacity, with students from both external and internal cohorts engaging in vigorous physical exertion alongside the nearby boys' college, the garden transformed into a congested thoroughfare. However, during the late afternoon hours following the dismissal of external students, when boarders retreated to their studies, the space reverted to tranquility. Straying along the peaceful alleys allowed one to hear the resonant, elevated tolling of the St. Jean Baptiste bells, creating a sonic backdrop of serenity.

On a particular evening, I remained within the garden later than customary, captivated by the intensifying calm, the cooling temperature, and the aromatic release of floral volatiles stimulated by the dew rather than sunlight. Observing a light emanating from the oratory window indicated that the Catholic household was engaged in evening prayers—a ritual from which I, as a Protestant, occasionally abstained. Solitude and the lunar cycle seemed to conspire in a whisper, urging me to prolong my stay, noting that the absence of my presence would go unnoticed for another fifteen minutes amidst the post-day fatigue.

The architectural layout presented a specific constraint: the rear of the boarding houses, forming a continuous line of premises belonging to the neighboring college, faced the garden with a facade of uninterrupted stone. Exceptions existed only in the form of high attic loopholes serving servant quarters and a single casement in a lower level, purportedly marking a master's study. Despite this apparent security, an alley running parallel to this high wall was strictly prohibited for students. Designated "l'allée défendue," entry incurred severe penalties under Madame Beck's regulations. While faculty members possessed immunity, the alley's narrow dimensions and dense, overgrown shrubbery—which created a canopy blocking most solar radiation—rendered it rarely visited during daylight and actively avoided after dusk.

Initially, the allure of this restricted zone was tempered by the fear of appearing anomalous. However, as familiarity bred acceptance regarding my inherent peculiarities—traits neither sufficiently striking to provoke curiosity nor offensive enough to cause offense—I gradually integrated myself into the routine of this secluded path. I assumed the role of caretaker for the nondescript flora growing between the shrubs, clearing debris from previous seasons and restoring a dilapidated bench at the terminus of the walk. Acquiring a bucket and brush from the cook, Goton, I cleaned the seat. Madame Beck observed this activity and offered a smile of approval; the sincerity of this gesture remained ambiguous, though her demeanor suggested otherwise.

"See," she exclaimed, "how neat this young lady, Miss Lucy, keeps this alley. Do you find it agreeable?"

"Yes," I replied, "it is quiet and shaded."

"Precisely," she responded with evident benevolence, advising me to utilize the space freely. Since I bore no responsibility for student supervision, she noted, I need not accompany the pupils, provided I permitted her charges to converse with me in English there.

That evening, seated on the restored bench, I listened to the distant murmur of the city. Paradoxically, these sounds were not remote; the school's central location meant the park was merely five minutes away, and palatial structures lay within ten. Nearby streets, illuminated and bustling, hosted carriages proceeding to balls and operas. While the same hour signaled curfew and silence for our conventual community, summoning festivities for the surrounding populace, I remained indifferent to this dichotomy. Lacking the instinctive drive for such entertainment, and having never witnessed a ball or opera despite hearing descriptions, my desire was not one of active pursuit or hunger, but a passive curiosity to observe a novel phenomenon.

Above, a waning crescent moon appeared through gaps in the foliage, accompanied by familiar stars. These celestial bodies evoked memories of childhood in England, recalling a similar configuration leaning against an old thorn bush in a field, mirroring its current position beside a stately spire in this continental capital. Reflecting on my youth, I acknowledged a latent emotional depth; though outwardly stoic and reticent, I possessed the capacity for feeling. Regarding the present, stoicism was advisable, whereas the future demanded a form of detachment, akin to a trance. I consciously suppressed the volatile aspects of my nature.

Certain environmental stimuli, particularly meteorological anomalies, previously agitated this dormant aspect, provoking an unsatisfiable craving. Recalling a thunderstorm that had shaken the dormitories, causing panic among the Catholic residents, I remembered my own reaction: a tyrannical compulsion to engage with the storm. Rising from bed, I positioned myself on the window ledge, feet resting on the roof of an adjacent building, exposed to the wet, wild darkness. While the dormitory interior buzzed with prayerful consternation, I remained outside, drawn irresistibly by the spectacle of lightning piercing the clouds—a display of terrible glory beyond linguistic description.

In the aftermath, a profound longing emerged to escape my current reality, seeking elevation and progression. This impulse required suppression, metaphorically likened to the biblical act of Jael driving a nail through Sisera's temple. Unlike the biblical counterpart, these desires did not perish but suffered temporary stun, periodically rebelling and causing mental anguish. That night, however, the conflict subsided. The internal struggle lay dormant, soothed by an idealized vision reminiscent of an angel offering balm to the wounded spirit. This state induced a mood of hopeful anticipation, characterized by a general sense of encouragement rather than specific expectation.

Could such a tranquil disposition herald positive outcomes? Regrettably, the anticipated good failed to materialize. Suddenly, the crude reality intruded with force. Amidst the profound stillness of the stone edifice overlooking the walk, a sound disrupted the silence: a casement creaked. Before I could identify the source, a tree branch shook violently, and an object descended to my feet.

It was nine o'clock according to the St. Jean Baptiste clock. Twilight persisted, aided by the residual luminescence of the horizon and the clarity of the upper atmosphere, sufficient to discern printed text at close range. The fallen object proved to be a small casket crafted from white and colored ivory. Upon opening the loose lid, I found violets covering a folded pink note addressed "Pour la robe grise"—a reference to my own attire.

Was this a love letter? Such correspondence existed in the collective consciousness of the school, though I had not personally encountered one until this moment. Could this be the artifact I had imagined? Scarcely. My mind had never entertained the concept of a suitor or admirer. While other teachers harbored fantasies of romance, believing in future spouses, and older pupils discussed betrothals arranged by parents, my own emotional landscape remained untouched by such projections. Even when observing peers returning from town or attending mass, they invariably reported encounters with individuals of the opposite sex whose gazes confirmed their desirability. My experience diverged significantly; although I attended services and walked, I felt invisible. Notably, the young doctor's blue eyes, admired by every female resident of the Rue Fossette, had never cast a glance in my direction—they remained as calm and innocent as the sky itself. Recognizing that this was not a conventional billet-doux, I proceeded to read it with the certainty of its unconventional nature.
