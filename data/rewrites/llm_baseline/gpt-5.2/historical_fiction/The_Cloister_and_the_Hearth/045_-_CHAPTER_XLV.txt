They were still at table in the hosier’s house, the little board set close to the hearth so the warmth might climb their knees. The trenchers had been scraped clean, yet the room kept its supper-breath: rye crust and onion, a sour-sweet tang of small beer, the last faint smoke of bacon that had hung in the rafters since morning. Even the sounds were homely and measured—the tick of the settle as one shifted, the soft rasp of a knife laid down, the hearth’s steady crackle like a patient animal chewing its cud.

They spoke, in low, broken starts, of the late matter that had troubled the house; each word fell as if it feared to ring too loud and draw more ill upon them. Then the door opened, and their mother came in.

Catherine did not hurry. She crossed the threshold with that noiseless economy a housewife learns by long service, as if her very feet knew where every loose board lay. In her hand she carried a letter. She did not clasp it to her breast like a love-token, nor wave it like a banner; she laid it flat upon the table, smoothing it with the palm as one smooths dough before the first fold. Her eyes travelled round the circle—one keen, piercing sweep that took each face as surely as the butcher’s hook finds the joint.

She bent over the paper. The signs on it were black and orderly, but they were not hers; and yet, by sheer hunger of heart, she followed them. She traced the lines with her finger, slowly, as if her nail could catch meaning the way it catches a thread in cloth. Then, word for word, she repeated the letter by memory—every turn of phrase, every stab and sob in it—keeping her finger moving to cheat herself and the hearers into thinking she read as others read. The room listened with the strange silence of a household when the bread is cut and no one dares speak until each has his share.

When she had done, she lifted her head suddenly.

Her gaze fell on Cornelis and Sybrandt. Their eyes, that had been bold enough when the matter was in doing, dropped now like dogs whipped off a bone. The hearth snapped; somewhere outside, a cart-wheel groaned in the street; a sparrow fussed in the eaves. Those small honest sounds made the stillness at the table sharper, like salt on a cut.

Then the storm that had been long brewing broke.

Catherine seemed to swell where she stood, as a hen swells and ruffles when some hand goes near her brood. And out of her mouth came a torrent—wisdom and twaddle together, high and low invective all in one breath; such a current as few men could have uttered and not many women, save one whose tongue had been sharpened on daily care and daily wrong.

“I have long had my doubts,” she began, and the words came swift and crowded, “that you blew the flame betwixt Gerard and your father, and set that old rogue, Ghysbrecht, on. And now here are Gerard’s own written words to prove it. You have driven your own flesh and blood into a far land, and robbed the mother that bore you of her darling—the pride of her eye, the joy of her heart.

“But you are all of a piece from end to end. When you were all boys together, my others were a comfort; but you were a curse: mischievous and sly; and took a woman half a day to keep your clothes whole—work wears cloth, but play cuts it. With the beard comes prudence; but none came to you: still the last to go to bed and the last to leave it; and why? because honesty goes to bed early, and industry rises betimes; where there are two lie-a-beds in a house there are a pair of ne’er-do-weels.

“Often I’ve sat and looked at your ways and wondered where ye came from. Ye don’t take after your father, and ye are no more like me than a wasp is to an ant. Sure ye were changed in the cradle, or the cuckoo dropped ye on my floor: for ye have not our hands, nor our hearts. Of all my blood, none but you ever jeered them that God afflicted. But often, when my back was turned, I’ve heard you mock at Giles because he is not as big as some; and at my lily Kate because she is not so strong as a Flanders mare. After that, rob a church an you will! for you can be no worse in His eyes that made both Kate and Giles—and in mine that suffered for them, poor darlings, as I did for you, you paltry, unfeeling, treasonable curs!”

Her daughter moved, as if to lay a hand on her arm and still the torrent; but Catherine shook her off with a glance.

“No,” she cried, “I will not hush, my daughter. They have filled the cup too full. It takes a deal to turn a mother’s heart against the sons she has nursed upon her knees; and many is the time I have winked and wouldn’t see too much, and bitten my tongue lest their father should know them as I do; he would have put them to the door that moment. But now they have filled the cup too full.

“And where got ye all this money? For this last month you have been rolling in it. You never wrought for it. I wish I may never hear from other mouths how ye got it. It is since that night you were out so late, and your head came back so swelled, Cornelis. Sloth and greed are ill-mated, my masters. Lovers of money must sweat or steal. Well, if you robbed any poor soul of it, it was some woman, I’ll go bail; for a man would drive you with his naked hand.

“No matter. It is good for one thing. It has shown me how you will guide our gear if ever it comes to be yourn. I have watched you, my lads, this while. You have spent a groat to-day between you. And I spend scarce a groat a week, and keep you all, good and bad.”

Her voice, that had run like boiling water, turned now hard and decisive, like a knife striking a board.

“No. I give up waiting for the shoes that will maybe walk behind your coffin; for this shop and this house shall never be yourn. Gerard is our heir—poor Gerard, whom you have banished and done your best to kill. After that, never call me mother again!”

At the name, something softened and flamed together in her face: grief and pride kneaded into one, as a cook kneads salt into the loaf because it must be there though it bites.

“But you have made him tenfold dearer to me. My poor lost boy! I shall soon see him again—shall hold him in my arms, and set him on my knees. Ay, you may stare! You are too crafty, and yet not crafty enow. You cut the stalk away; but you left the seed—the seed that shall outgrow you, and outlive you.

“Margaret Brandt is quick, and it is Gerard’s; and what is Gerard’s is mine. And I have prayed the saints it may be a boy; and it will—it must. Kate, when I found it was so, my bowels yearned over her child unborn as if it had been my own. He is our heir. He will outlive us. You will not; for a bad heart in a carcass is like the worm in the nut—soon brings the body to dust.”

She turned on her daughter with sudden household briskness, as if the rage must spend itself in doing some plain thing, the way sorrow often takes refuge in setting a pot to boil.

“So, Kate, take down Gerard’s bib and tucker that are in the drawer you wot of; and one of these days we will carry them to Sevenbergen. We will borrow Peter Buyskens’ cart, and go comfort Gerard’s wife under her burden. She is his wife. Who is Ghysbrecht Van Swieten? Can he come between a couple and the altar, and sunder those that God and the priest make one?”

Her voice held, beneath the anger, the old stubborn reverence for forms and words that bind—vows spoken in a tongue the people own, not clipped and traded like coin. In that room, where the mother could not read the black marks yet could keep a son’s sentences alive by sheer memory, the power of written words stood plain: a dominion that could banish, could name, could make a marriage denied by one man and affirmed by another. And Catherine, who had lived by the honest noises of hearth and shop-bell, flung back at that dominion her own authority: the mother’s claim, the household’s law.

“She is my daughter,” she went on, “and I am as proud of her as I am of you, Kate—almost. And as for you—”

Her eyes, black and sharp now, struck Cornelis and Sybrandt like stones.

“Keep out of my way awhile, for you are like the black dog in my eyes.”

Cornelis and Sybrandt needed no more. They rose and slunk out, their shoulders drawn in, their faces stung—not into penitence, but into a sore mixture of remorse and hate. The door closed behind them with a wooden thud that sounded louder than it should, as if the house itself had spoken.

For many days thereafter, Catherine never said them a word—good, bad, or indifferent. She had eased her mind. Liberaverat animum suum.
