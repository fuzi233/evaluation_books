The morning broke with a pale, earnest light as Romola set out for San Marco. She had secured, the night before through Fra Salvestro, a rare audience with Fra Girolamo in the chapter-house. Such meetings were exceptional; Savonarola guarded his solitude with a rigidity that brooked no pretext for slander, ensuring that when he did grant access, it was stripped of all mystery. Thus, she arrived during the bustling hours of the outer cloisters, when the air was thick with the chatter of lay visitors.

She chose to traverse the heart of the city first, seeking to read the pulse of public sentiment. Every loggia and corner of the piazza buzzed with the feverish energy of gratuitous debate. The languor of trade had given way to the vigor of politics; the factions supporting and opposing the death of the conspirators were desperate to gauge the popular mood during these three days of suspension. Handbills fluttered through the streets like black birds. Some proclaimed in massive, bold letters the stark choice: justice for the conspirators or ruin for the Republic. Others, equally shouting, demanded the observance of the law and the granting of the Appeal. Around these islands of giant type swarmed smaller print, offering arguments deemed unnecessary to read aloud, for in the fresh flush of the printing press's triumph, it was believed that no phrase could convince the masses quite like a question-begging headline.

Romola, however, cared little for the shouting headlines. She sought the quiet, complex arguments in the smaller type. Though she hurried, her eyes scanned the crowds anxiously for copies. For a long stretch, she found only sheets clutched by eager readers or torn from walls by the *sbirri*. Finally, passing behind San Giovanni, she spotted Bratti, a familiar face of the humble Florentine life, exchanging pamphlets for small coins.

"Have you both sorts, Bratti?" she asked, her voice cutting through his slow movements. "Let me have them quickly."

"Two sorts," Bratti replied, separating the damp sheets with a deliberate slowness that tested her patience. "'Law,' and 'Justice.'"

"Which sells faster?"

"'Justice.' It flies off the shelves, so I raised the price to two danari. But then I thought, 'Law' is just as worthy a commodity. People scorn cheap things; if I sold 'Law' for one danaro, I'd be wronging it. I'm a fair trader. Both are good wares, bought for nothing and sold for profit. You want more than one of each?"

"No," Romola said, pressing a white quattrino into his hand and snatching the bills before he could elaborate further. She folded them tightly and hastened toward the convent.

In the outer cloisters, Fra Salvestro awaited her, lost in conversation with lay visitors. Under the current government, the fear that had gripped San Marco following the Excommunication was slowly receding. Among the listeners was Andrea Cambini, a known satellite of Francesco Valori, gesturing emphatically as he recounted news. Fra Salvestro listened with that trivial curiosity that signals a mind interested in gossip rather than truth. Romola recognized the topic immediately from the disjointed fragments reaching her ear: the fate of the conspirators. She avoided the group, waiting until Fra Salvestro's attention shifted before turning toward the chapter-house door. A lay brother opened it for her, closing it softly behind her.

Inside, the sad frescoed figures seemed to mourn anew with her the death of her brother Dino. Yet, the intensity of her present dread rendered the memory less a clear picture than a blur of impressions, blending with her agitating fears. She felt her current anxiety as a revival of a past yearning, once again repelled by the cold rigidity of marble. Without pausing to dwell on the past, she unfolded the handbills, hoping to read them before Fra Girolamo's arrival. By the time she finished the pamphlet urging legal observance, the door swung open, and she stood expectant, papers doubled in her hands.

When the Frate entered, she knelt, adhering to the custom of those who sought private audiences. But as soon as he offered a benedictory greeting, she rose and stood opposite him at a respectful distance. His seclusion since the Excommunication meant this was their first meeting in months, and the years had etched deeper lines of mental strain and physical austerity onto his face. Yet, Romola felt less the weight of age than a subtle shift in his essence. Was the serene elevation and pure fellowship that had once moved her diminished? Or was it the growing sense of division regarding her godfather that clouded her vision? Perhaps both currents ran together. Our relations with others are often decided by such converging tides; the final blow rarely comes until affection has been eroded by repeated excuses.

Savonarola's gaze held a hardness born of self-preoccupation. He sensed the purpose of her visit—the fate of the conspirators—and knew he had already silenced inner voices that might grow loud if encouraged from without. Seated in his cell, correcting the sheets of his *Triumph of the Cross*, he found it easier to maintain a posture of neutrality.

"It is a matter of great moment, doubtless, on which you wish to see me, my daughter," he began, his tone gentle, though controlled rather than inclined. "I know you do not usually stress trifles."

"Father, you know what it is before I speak," Romola said, pouring forth her plea instantly. "You know what I care for—it is the life of the old man I love best in the world. The thought of him has accompanied the thought of my father since I can remember daylight. That is my warrant for coming to you, even if my coming proves needless. Perhaps you have already decided that your power over hearts will be used to deny Florentines a right you yourselves helped secure."

"I meddle not with the functions of the State, my daughter," Fra Girolamo replied, disinclined to reopen a debate he had already wrestled with internally. "I have preached and labored that Florence should have a good government, for it is needful to the perfecting of the Christian life; but I keep my hands from particular affairs which it is the office of experienced citizens to administer."

"Surely, father—" Romola broke off. The word escaped her impetuously, but she was halted by the counter-agitation of feeling herself in an attitude of remonstrance toward the man who had been her guide and strength. In the act of rebelling, she bruised her own reverence.

Savonarola was too keen not to divine the conflict arresting her—too noble, deliberately, to assume the calm, self-justifying evasiveness he often displayed in public.

"Say what is in your heart; speak on, my daughter," he said, standing with arms crossed, looking at her with quiet expectation.

"I was going to say, father, that this matter is surely of higher moment than many about which I have heard you preach fervidly. If it belonged to you to urge that men condemned for offenses against the State should have the right to appeal to the Great Council—if—Romola grew eager—"if you count it a glory to have won that right for them, can it less belong to you to declare yourself against the right being denied to almost the first men who need it? Surely that touches the Christian life more closely than whether you knew beforehand that the Dauphin would die, or whether Pisa will be conquered."

A subtle movement, like a subdued sign of pain, crossed Savonarola's strong lips before he spoke.

"My daughter, I speak as it is given me to speak. I am not master of the times when I may become the vehicle of knowledge beyond the common lights of men. In this case, I have no illumination beyond what wisdom may give to those charged with the safety of the State. As to the law of Appeal against the Six Votes, I labored to have it passed so that no Florentine should suffer loss of life or goods through the private hatred of a few in power. But these five men, who desired to overthrow a free government and restore a corrupt tyrant, have been condemned with the assent of a large assembly of their fellow-citizens. They refused at first to bring their cause before the Great Council. They have lost the right to the appeal."

"How can they have lost it?" Romola cried. "It is the right to appeal against condemnation, and they have never been condemned till now! And forgive me, father, it *is* private hatred that would deny them the appeal; it *is* the violence of the few that frightens others. Else why was the assembly divided again directly after it seemed to agree? And if anything weighs against the observance of the law, let this weigh for it: that you used to preach more earnestly than all else that there should be no place for hatred and bloodshed born of party strife, so that private ill-will should not find opportunity in public acts. Father, you know there is private hatred here. Will it not dishonor you not to interpose on the side of mercy, when many hold it is also the side of law and justice?"

"My daughter," Savonarolo said, with more visible emotion than before, "there is a mercy which is weakness, and even treason against the common good. The safety of Florence, which means even more than the welfare of Florentines, now demands severity, as it once demanded mercy. It is not only for a past plot that these men are condemned, but also for a plot which has not yet been executed. The devices leading to its execution remain active: the tyrant still gathers his forces in Romagna, and the enemies of Florence, who sit in the highest places of Italy, are ready to hurl any stone that will crush her."

"What plot?" Romola asked, reddening and trembling with alarmed surprise.

"You carry papers in your hand, I see," Savonarola pointed out, indicating the handbills. "One of them will, perhaps, tell you the government has new information."

Romola hastily opened the unread pamphlet and saw that the government now possessed positive evidence of a second plot, scheduled for this very August. To her mind, it felt like reading a confirmation that Tito had won his safety by foul means; his pretense of wanting the Frate to act on behalf of the condemned only deepened her wretched conviction. She crushed the paper in her hand and turned to Savonarola with new passion.

"Father, what safety can there be for Florence when the worst man can always escape? And," she continued, a sudden flash of remembrance striking her regarding her husband, "have not you yourself encouraged this deception which corrupts the life of Florence, by wanting more favor shown to Lorenzo Tornabuoni, who wears two faces and flatters you with a show of affection, when my godfather has always been honest? Ask all Florence who of those five men has the truest heart, and there will not be many who will name any other than Bernardo del Nero. You did interpose with Francesco Valori for the sake of one prisoner; you have *not* then been neutral. And you know that your word will be powerful."

"I do not desire the death of Bernardo," Savonarola said, coloring deeply. "It would be enough if he were sent out of the city."

"Then why do you not speak to save an old man of seventy-five from dying a death of ignominy—to give him at least the fair chances of the law?" Romola burst out, her nature so roused by indignation that she forgot everything else. "It is not that you feel bound to be neutral; else why did you speak for Lorenzo Tornabuoni? You spoke for him because he is more friendly to San Marco; my godfather feigns no friendship. It is not, then, as a Medicean that my godfather is to die; it is as a man you have no love for!"

When Romola paused, cheeks glowing and lips quivering, there was a dead silence. As she saw Fra Girolamo standing motionless before her, she seemed to hear her own words echoing in strange, painful dissonance with the memories that made part of his presence to her. The moments of silence expanded with gathering compunction and self-doubt. She had committed sacrilege in her passion. Even the sense that she could retract nothing of her plea, that her mind could not submit to Savonarola's negative, made it more needful to satisfy those reverential memories. With a sudden movement toward him, she said:

"Forgive me, father; it is pain to me to have spoken those words—yet I cannot help speaking. I am little and feeble compared with you; you brought me light and strength. But I submitted because I felt the proffered strength—because I saw the light. *Now* I cannot see it. Father, you yourself declare that there comes a moment when the soul must have no guide but the voice within it, to tell whether the consecrated thing has sacred virtue. And therefore I must speak."

Savonarola possessed a readily-roused resentment toward opposition, hardly separable from a power-loving nature accustomed to seeking great ends that cast a reflected grandeur on the means. His sermons burned with that red flame. If he had been a meaner man, his susceptibility might have shown as irritation at Romola's accusatory freedom, which contrasted sharply with the deference he habitually received. But at this moment, such feelings were nullified by the hard struggle that made half the tragedy of his life: the struggle of a mind possessed by a never-silent hunger after purity and simplicity, yet caught in a tangle of egoistic demands, false ideas, and difficult outward conditions that made simplicity impossible. Keenly alive to the suggestions of Romola's remonstrance, he rapidly surveyed the courses of action open to him and their probable results. But it was a question on which arguments seemed decisive only in proportion as they were charged with feeling, and he had received no impulse that could alter his bias. He looked at Romola and said:

"You have full pardon for your frankness, my daughter. You speak, I know, out of the fulness of your family affections. But these affections must give way to the needs of the Republic.
