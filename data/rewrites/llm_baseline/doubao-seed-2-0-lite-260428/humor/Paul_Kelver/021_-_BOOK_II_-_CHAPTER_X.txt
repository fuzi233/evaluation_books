I climbed that grindstone slow, steady, and determined to not let my gaze drift off the path an inch, all silly daydreams tossed straight in the bin. Trouble was, the sappy, sad-eyed little dreamer version of me kept popping up like a stray cat begging for treats, trying to lure me away from spreadsheets and copy deadlines to mope about unappreciated poetry, half-baked political speeches, and dramatic essays full of very heavy opinions that no one asked for. If I hadn’t been so single-mindedly obsessed with paying back every last penny I owed to win back my self-respect — and more than that, to feel Norah’s strong, warm white hand back in mine, a craving that got so sharp it felt like a physical ache in my chest every evening — that silly little dreamer would’ve definitely won, and I’d have ended up a very broke, very unhelpful person whose only talent was annoying everyone around him with deep thoughts. Every once in a while, when my comedy brain took a holiday and refused to write a single funny line, I’d scribble sad little stories just for my own amusement. They never went anywhere, but I was making solid steady money, and by the time my last debt was ticked off the list, I’d built up a name for myself as the guy who could turn any boring premise into a laugh.

All my practical friends immediately lost their minds. “Are you mad?” they squawked. “You’re gonna throw away a guaranteed fortune for a maybe, barely-scraping-by shot at serious writing? You know you’re good at the funny stuff. The serious stuff? That’s starting your whole career over from day one!”
“You’ll never make it,” the experienced, wise old heads agreed, patting my shoulder like I was a toddler who’d tried to eat a whole jar of pickles. “The public already decided you’re the funny guy. Once a clown, always a clown. It’s like if a guy who does pratfalls at the music hall decided he was gonna play Hamlet. It could be the best Hamlet the world’s ever seen, and the audience would just snort into their popcorn or walk out, waiting for him to trip over Yorick’s skull.”

Me and my mate Goggles — the guy with the fishy, permanently wide eyes who was dead set on being a great tragedian — would sneak off to the club’s quietest corner to mope and pour our hearts out to each other. He’d ramble for 45 minutes about his very carefully thought out vision of Romeo, which was probably very good, but I couldn’t for the life of me picture him pining after Juliet without accidentally knocking over the balcony. I nodded and cheered him on anyway, it was the price I paid for getting to rant at him later about my own grand plan to write giant, world-changing serious literature that would fix all of humanity’s problems.
“Maybe in the next life,” Goggles laughed one evening, yanking his coat on as the clock hit seven, “I’ll be the big Shakespearean star, and you’ll be the world-famous serious poet. Right now? Like our mate Brian says, we’re both sinning our mercies. Turns out showing up and paying your rent is the most important thing in life, who knew?”
He tapped me on the chest, his face so deadly serious it looked absurd. “Take my advice, marry a nice sweet girl. I did — she’s twice my size, and she’s the best thing that ever happened to me. You don’t actually understand what life is for until the kids come along and explain all the riddles to you.”
I sank back into my armchair by the fire, staring at the flames. Wife, kids, a real home? Wasn’t that the actual point of being alive, instead of chasing all those misty morning dream ghosts that vanish as soon as the sun comes up? I could picture it: the sweet, sharp woman who was my partner in every stupid thing, the tiny kids running around the house yelling questions that would make all my big dumb serious ambitions look totally useless.

Then Hodgson, the theatre manager, turned up at my place one night waving a letter. “I want you to write me a comic opera,” he said. “The public’s sick to death of the same boring French translations, over and over. I want something English, fresh, new and original.”
“English is easy,” I told him, “but if I were you I wouldn’t beg too hard for new and original. You might actually get it, and then you’ll be very disappointed.”
He snort-laughed. “You know what I mean! Just something we can stick on the program that says ‘new and original’. It’s a big break for you, everyone’s starting to talk about your work, I know you can pull it off.”
I’d already written a handful of short farces and one-act plays that did great, but headlining a whole night’s entertainment was terrifying — it could make your career, or blow it up so bad no one would hire you for years. I didn’t tell him that I already had a whole four-act serious drama stuffed in my desk drawer, full of all the heavy humanity and feeling I’d been dying to show off to prove I wasn’t just the joke guy.
“C’mon, it’s easy,” Hodgson urged me. “Write our star Atherton lots of silly physical gags, give Gleeson a pretty waltz number to sing, and for Duncan’s part just make sure she gets to change her fancy dress every 15 minutes. That’s all anyone wants.”
“Also,” he added, “we’re all hiring a coach to drive out to Richmond this Sunday. Leave the theatre at 10:30. You can come along, hang out with all the cast, work out what each of them is actually good at, get all their ideas. Then next week we’ll sign the contract and get to work.”
I couldn’t say no. It was too good a shot, even if I knew if it went well I’d be stuck as the resident jester forever. But it turns out writing comic opera pays extremely well.

A little crowd of street kids had gathered on the Strand to watch us leave. Our leading lady jumped out of her cab 15 minutes late, panicking. “Oh no — is everyone alright? Did the theatre burn down?”
“Nah, they just all came to see you,” Hodgson said, not looking up from his stack of letters.
“Good god, then let’s go right now,” she yelped.
We hauled her up to the top deck first, with three of us holding her hand while she pretended to be far too delicate to climb the coach ladder. Then our low comedian Charlie Atherton had to show off: he faked missing his footing three times, sliding right back down into the doorman’s arms every go. The street kids went wild, cheering and laughing, so on the third try he crawled up the ladder on his hands and knees, and then immediately plopped down right on top of the tenor, who just smiled like he’d been expecting it.
For the first dozen buses we passed, Charlie stood up and took a deep, dramatic bow every single time, then fell over straight into the tenor or my lap. Everyone on the coach thought it was hilarious — everyone except the tenor and me, that is. Once we got past Charing Cross and no one was paying attention to us anymore, he finally sat still and brushed dust off his knees.
“People always ask why I do this stuff off stage,” he said. “It’s just fun, for me. The other week I was on the train coming up from Birmingham, and at Willesden I jumped out the door the second the ticket collector opened it, and ran down the platform as fast as I could. Fifty people got up and started chasing me! I only stopped and told them I had a first class ticket for a second class seat once they caught me. People think I do it for attention — nah, it’s just fun to be an idiot sometimes.”
“Sounds like a lot of extra work to amuse yourself,” I said.
“Exactly what my wife says,” he sighed. “She gets such awful headaches, she never comes out anywhere with me. That’s why she’s not here today, poor thing.”

By the time we got to the Richmond hotel, we’d had Charlie balance the coach horn on his nose to amuse passersby, stop the whole rig to ask a girls’ boarding school for change for sixpence, and halt traffic at the bottom of Richmond Hill to try and talk a deaf old man in a bath chair into racing us up the hill for a shilling. Everyone on the party was cackling — except Hodgson, who was buried in his letters, and a fancy newly-divorced lady who made a hundred quid a week off her very notorious reputation.
We wandered into the garden, and Charlie spotted a very dignified old man sipping vermouth at a table. He froze like he’d just seen a ghost, then ran straight over, grabbed the poor guy’s hand, and shook it like they were long lost brothers. “Uncle George! I can’t believe it! How’s dear old Aunt Martha? Is she doing well?”
The old guy blinked, completely confused. “Excuse me, I don’t —”
“Wait don’t tell me — Aunt Martha’s not dead, is she?!” Charlie grabbed his shoulders, making a horrified face so dramatic passersby started gathering.
“I have no idea who you are,” the old man snapped, very annoyed. “My name’s not Steggles, whoever you think I am.”
“Terribly sorry, wrong person!” Charlie said, chugging the rest of the old guy’s vermouth in one go and sprinting away. The rest of us went over to apologize, and told him that was Charlie Atherton, the famous comic.
“Oh yeah?” he growled. “Tell him if I want his stupid tomfoolery I’ll pay for a theatre ticket, thank you very much. Don’t give it away for free out here.”

Lunch was even more chaos. Charlie kissed the back of the bald waiter’s head pretending he thought it was the leading lady’s face, asked for hot mustard and water, pretended to steal the silverware, and when the finger bowls came out he took off his coat and told all the ladies to turn around, no peeking. Then after lunch he dragged me off alone down a quiet path.
“About this new opera,” he said, all serious now. “None of that stale old garbage everyone does. Give me something new. My idea: I’m a nice young bloke, in love with a girl. Her dad’s a grumpy apoplectic old idiot who hates me, thinks I’m a total fool. But I’m not actually an idiot, right? Then in the second act I disguise myself — first as an organ grinder with a silly accent, then a policeman, then a fancy toff. Give me lots of room to do bits, not the same old jokes everyone’s seen a hundred times. And — put a little sad stuff in there. A tiny scene where I can make ‘em feel a little, not just laugh. Make the audience leave going, ‘Wait Charlie made me cry? I never knew he had it in him!’ You get what I mean?”
I promised him I did.

Next the leading lady dragged me off to a secluded bench overlooking the river. “About my part,” she said, pouting and laying her very pretty hand on mine. “I’m so sick of playing the boring simple village maiden. I want a flirty, fun part. I swear I can flirt! Test me! Make the whole plot revolve around me. Women are way more interesting than men, right? The whole show will be a hit if it’s all about me.”
I told her I thought that was brilliant, I’d do my best.
Then the tenor cornered me on the balcony over tea. “Listen,” he said, glancing around to make sure Charlie wasn’t nearby, “the public doesn’t want nonstop clowning anymore. Give them a real human love story. I get paid the same whether I’m on stage five minutes or an hour, but my fans come to see me! They want a proper interesting lover character. That’s the secret of a hit.”
Last of all, quiet little Marmaduke Trevor, the guy who had the tiny throwaway part as the Duke of Bayswater, snuck over to me like he was sharing state secrets. “You might not remember me, Mr. Kelver, but I had a huge hit in America once doing a bit with a soda-water syphon. If you could just slip a tiny little scene in for me to do that bit, it’ll make the whole show way funnier, I promise.”
I kept that soda-water scene in, even when everyone complained it didn’t fit. The critics whinged about it later, but Trevor was right, it always got a big laugh.

The drive back to London was even more chaotic than the way there: Charlie honked the coach horn as loud as he could to make every bus driver mad, and then stopped at Hammersmith to stand on the back seat and sell all of our hats to passersby at rock bottom prices, pretending he was a market cheap jack.
“Got all the ideas you needed off them?” Hodgson asked me, when we were all saying goodbye.
“I think I’m gonna go hide out in the Yorkshire Wolds and write the whole thing away from everyone,” I told him. “No interruptions.”
“Smart call,” he said. “Too many cooks spoil the broth. Just get it done before autumn.”
I had a great time writing it up there, and read the whole finished script out loud to the full cast once we got back to London. The whole room went dead silent when I finished. The leading lady was the first to speak: “Is that clock right? Because if it is I can still just catch my train if I run right now.”
Hodgson checked his watch. “It’s probably a little fast.”
“I hope so!” she said, and bolted.
The tenor was next. “Oh, I remember that famous hit opera at the Philharmonic, right? Everyone thought it was total garbage five minutes before opening night! Anyway I gotta run, I have a train too.”
Marmaduke Trevor snuck over and squeezed my hand. “Don’t give up,” he whispered. Charlie Atherton, the last one left, told Hodgson “I think I can make some bits of this work, if I get to rewrite most of it.”
Hodgson and I stared at each other once they were all gone. “It’s never gonna make money,” he said. “It’s clever, but the box office will hate it.”
“How do you know?” I asked.
“I never make mistakes,” he said, grinning. “I’ve put on plenty of flops before, that’s how you learn. We’ll hack it into shape at rehearsals, you’ll see.”
Their idea of “hacking it into shape” was hacking it to total pieces.
“Cut this whole scene!” Charlie would say, scribbling straight through five pages of my script.
“But that’s the scene where the hero escapes prison and meets the girl he loves!” I protested.
“Pfft, we can do that in two lines. ‘Hallo, you escaped prison?’ ‘Yep. The girl’s gonna marry me.’ Done! Who needs the rest?”
Then the leading lady would pop up. “Hodgson says I don’t fall in love with Peter, I fall in love with John. We cut Arabella out, I can sing all her songs.”
“But John was supposed to be in love with Arabella!”
Then the tenor would drag me into a corner. “Write me a pretty moonlit balcony scene with the leading lady at the top of act one. It’ll be great.”
“Act one opens with a morning hunting scene! No one goes hunting by moonlight.”
“So we change the hunting scene to a novelty moonlight hunt! Everyone’s sick of regular hunting scenes.”

I put up with this for a whole week, telling myself they were the experts, they must know better than me. By the end of the week I knew they didn’t. And I lost my temper — which I recommend every new playwright do immediately, by the way, it’s extremely cathartic. I grabbed two copies of the script: the first one was my neat, original, perfectly coherent draft, the second was the Frankenstein monster mess everyone had scribbled all over, cut, rewritten, flipped upside down, that no human being on earth could possibly make sense of. I dumped both on Hodgson’s desk.
“This first one’s yours,” I said, shoving the scribbled mess across to him. “If you can make any sense of it, you’re welcome to produce it. This other one is mine. You put it on exactly as I wrote it, or we tear up the contract right now.”
He argued with me for 40 minutes, yelling about lost money, my reputation, everything. Then he called me a stubborn, pig-headed idiot, threw the Frankenstein script straight in the wastepaper basket, stomped out to the cast, and yelled “We’re doing the damned opera exactly as Kelver wrote it. And if any of you complain, you’re all fired.”
Everyone rolled their eyes. Hodgson stayed away from rehearsals for a while, then came back, watching us all with a very sad, resigned look on his face. “This show’s gonna flop so bad,” he told me. “I’m spending 12 to 15 hundred quid just to teach you a lesson. Once it bombs, you’ll write me the crowd-pleaser we need, we’ll make all the money back.”
“Or what if it works?” I grinned.
“Dear boy,” he said, lighting a cigar, “I never make mistakes.”
