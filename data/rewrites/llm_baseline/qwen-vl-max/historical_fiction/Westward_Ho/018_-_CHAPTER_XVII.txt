“The sun's rim dips; the stars rush out;
At one stride comes the dark.”

Land! Land! Land! There it lay, far to the south and west, beside the sinking sun—a long blue line stretching across the crimson sea and golden sky. At last, land: a promise of fresh streams, cool fruit, open space for limbs worn thin by sickness and confinement. And perhaps, too, gold and gems, treasures beyond imagining—wealth from the Indies, whispered in dreams and maps alike. Who could say? Why not? The old world, with its rigid laws and dusty truths, was thousands of miles behind them. Before them stretched the realm of myth and miracle, where every wave might carry wonder, every breeze bring fortune.

Sick men dragged themselves from their stifling hammocks; strong men fell to their knees in gratitude. All eyes strained toward that distant haze, fading as the sun sank, yet growing clearer and broader as the ship surged forward on the steady trade-wind, which murmured softly around sail and spar: *“I am the faithful friend of those who dare.”*

“Blow freshly, freshlier yet, thou good trade-wind,” Frank whispered, his voice barely audible over the wind’s sigh. “It is written that He makes the winds His angels, breathing life into those who are heirs of salvation. Blow freshlier yet—and save, if not me from death, then her from worse than death. Blow on, and land me at her feet, to call the lost lamb home… and die.”

His face was pale, drawn, cheeks hollow, but flushed with feverish color in the dying light. A few minutes passed, and the western skies bled into twilight—rainbows vanished, emeralds and rubies dimmed to silver-gray. Above, the moon rose, bright and cold, joined by Venus, gleaming like a jewel above the darkened sea.

“That should be Barbados, your worship,” said Drew, the master, pointing ahead. “Unless my reckoning is wildly off—which, Heaven knows, it has no right to be after such a passage. God be praised.”

“Barbados?” Amyas replied. “I’ve never heard of it.”

“Very likely, sir,” Drew answered. “But Yeo and I were here with Captain Drake. I came again later, with poor Captain Barlow. There’s safe anchorage to the south and west—I remember well.”

“And no Spaniard, no cannibal, no beast of ill omen,” added Yeo. “A garden of the Lord, hidden in the deeps, meant for those who love Him. I recall Captain Drake speaking of planting it—if ever he had the chance.”

“I remember now,” Amyas said. “There was talk between him and Sir Humphrey about an island here. Would that he had gone there instead of to Newfoundland!”

“Nay,” said Yeo gently, “he rests now with the Lord. You would not have kept him from that?”

“He would have waited as willingly as he went,” Amyas replied, “if he could have served his queen thereby. But what say you, masters? Shall we rest here a few days to heal the sick before we press on to the Main and begin our work?”

All agreed—except Frank, who remained silent.

“Come, fellow-adventurer,” Cary urged. “We must hear your voice too.”

“To my impatience, Will,” Frank murmured aside, “there is but one place on earth, and all day I long for wings to reach it. But the counsel is sound. I approve.”

The decision was made, and the crew cheered. Long before dawn, they sailed along the southern shore, feeling their way into the bay where Bridgetown now stands. Eyes were fixed upon the low hills, wrapped in moonlight and dotted with fireflies, beneath a sky ablaze with stars. Noses drank in the sweet air, thick with floral perfume from the land. Ears welcomed the new sounds—the hum of insects, the snoring of tree-toads, the melancholy calls of shore birds—life pulsing in the night.

At last, the ship stopped. The cable rattled through the hawsehole. And then, heedless of danger—Spaniards or Caribs—an instinctive cheer burst from every throat.

Poor souls! Amyas had to hold them back, reminding them it was still two hours until daylight.

“Never were two such long hours,” muttered a young lad, pacing nervously.

“You never stood before the Inquisition,” Yeo said dryly. “Then you’d know how time can crawl. Stand still, and give thanks you’re where you are.”

“Gunner,” asked another, “is there gold on this island?”

“Never heard of any,” Yeo replied. “And so much the better.”

“But Gunner,” said a scurvy-ridden cripple, licking his cracked lips, “are there oranges and lemons?”

“Not that I’ve seen,” Yeo said, “but plenty of fine fruit down to the beach—thank the Lord. Look—dawn is breaking.”

Up flared the rose-colored sky. The sun rose in full glory, casting sharp rays across the palm trees, painting rainbows on the coral reefs, gilding distant uplands where now stand grand estates and busy factories. Pelicans flew in long lines out to sea. The insect hum faded. Birds erupted into song. A thin blue mist crept upward, then dissolved under the fierce heat. The land-breeze, which had blown all night, died away into silence. The tropic day had begun.

The sick were carried ashore in boats, laid gently on the sand beneath the palms. Within half an hour, the entire crew had scattered across the beach—save a dozen men left aboard to watch the ship until noon.

And now, nature’s first cry echoed: *Fruit! Fruit! Fruit!*

The weak crawled from bush to vine, tearing at violet grapes from creeping vines, staining their mouths with prickly pears, ignoring Yeo’s warnings about the thorns. Some healthy men began chopping down coconut palms, only to dull their hatchets uselessly. Then Yeo and Drew gathered a handful of sensible men and marched inland. An hour later, they returned laden with treasures from the wild orchard—tart junipa apples, juicy guavas, and golden pineapples, crowned queens among fruits, found in abundance on the sun-baked cliffs.

They sat on the warm sand, laughing, eating, unbothered by galliwasps or jackspaniards. Blue crabs scuttled from their burrows, waving claws in defiance. Cranes stood in shallow water, heads tilted, pondering the strange bipeds who had come to disturb their quiet isle.

Frank wandered alone, quiet, more in awe than sorrow. Amyas followed, chewing a junipa apple, playing the part of guide, showing off as though he’d seen all wonders already.

“New, new—everything new!” Frank mused.

“Oh, terrible feeling!” he continued. “All things changed—down to the smallest fly, the tiniest flower—yet we remain the same. Forever unchanged.”

Amyas, unable to grasp such thoughts, pointed. “Look, Frank—that’s a colibri. You’ve heard of colibris?”

Frank watched the tiny creature—alive, radiant, humming loudly—as it hovered over a strange bloom, darting away as if calling its mate, then dancing in dizzy circles, flashing rainbows with every shift of light.

He stared solemnly, then whispered: “*Qualis Natura formatrix, si talis formata?* Oh, my God—how fair must Thy true world be, if even Thy shadows are so beautiful!”

“Phantoms?” Amyas asked, uneasy.

“That’s no ghost, Frank,” he said. “Just a little honey-sucker, with a wife and children no bigger than peas—but solid, greedy little fellows, I’ll wager.”

“Not phantoms in your sense,” Frank replied, “but in the sense of those who see all below as fleeting, false.”

“I’ll tell you what, brother Frank,” Amyas said, “you’re wiser than I, I know. But I can’t abide seeing you look down on God’s good earth. See—God made all these things. No man may have seen them till fifty years ago. Yet they were just as lovely then as now—ever since creation. Why do you think God placed them here? But to please Himself”—and Amyas removed his hat—“with the sight of them. Now, I say, brother Frank, if it’s good enough for God, it’s good enough for us.”

“Your rebuke is just,” Frank said softly. “And God forgive me—though I have learning that brings no profit, longings that bring no peace—when I presume, sinner that I am, to be more refined than the Lord Himself. He walked in Paradise among the trees of the garden, Amyas. So shall we, and be content with what He sends. Why seek the next world before we are fit even for this one?”

“And in the meanwhile,” Amyas said, smiling, “this earth is quite good enough—at least here in Barbados.”

“Do you believe,” Frank asked, trying to shift his thoughts, “those tales the Spaniards tell—of Sirens and Tritons singing in these seas?”

“I can’t say,” Amyas replied. “There’s more fish in the water than ever came out of it. More wonders in the world than we dream of. I’ve never been here before. In the South Seas, I never saw any—but Yeo says he heard sweet music at night, far from land, in the Gulf.”

“The Spaniards say,” Frank continued, “that at certain times, choirs of nymphs gather in the sea, singing songs of watery love. It may be so. For Nature, who has filled the land with rational beings, may not have left the sea entirely empty. Especially when we consider that the ocean is the source of all fertility. Its slime—so the learned say, following Thales of Miletus—is the primal matter from which all things were formed. Thus, the ancients wisely imagined Venus, mother of all life, born from the sea-foam, rising from the deep and floating ashore upon Greek isles.”

“I don’t know what ‘plastic force’ means,” Amyas admitted, “but I wish I’d been there when the pretty poppet came up. The nearest thing I ever saw was maidens swimming alongside us in the South Seas—would have boarded us, too. But Drake sent them off—naughty packs, I suspect. Look at the butterflies now! Don’t you wish you were a boy again, chasing them in your cap?”

And so the two walked together through the glorious woods, returning to find the sick already revived—some who couldn’t move that morning now walking, gaining strength with each step.

“Well done, lads!” Amyas called. “Keep your spirits high. We’ll have music ashore after dinner—no mermaids to sing for us, but those who can dance may.”

And so the four days passed. The men, like boys on holiday, gave themselves to simple joys—washing clothes, filling casks with fresh water, storing fruit that might keep. They searched for gold, hoping to find it in every bush, though Yeo warned them none had ever been found on the island. Soon tired, they lounged about, collecting shells and sea-fans to send home to lovers, smoking agoutis from hollow trees with shouts and laughter, tormenting every creature they could catch—until not a crab dared show its face, nor an armadillo unroll itself.

Then, finally, they set sail again—unaware pioneers of a future rich with wealth, commerce, beauty, and knowledge—setting course westward, leaving behind the island that would one day become the crown jewel of the Caribbean.
