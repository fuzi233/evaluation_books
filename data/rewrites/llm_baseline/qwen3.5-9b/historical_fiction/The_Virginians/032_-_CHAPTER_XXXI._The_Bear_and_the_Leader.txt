Those who know the truth of Harry Warrington's situation understand the reality of his connection with the unfortunate Cattarina. However, the older ladies of Tunbridge Wells held a different view; they suspected the Virginian of being as dissolute as any high-born English nobleman. Madame de Bernstein, in particular, remained deeply skeptical of her nephew's innocence. To her, it was self-evident that Harry was leading not merely a merry life, but a wicked one. Her wish shaped her belief that the lad was no better than his neighbors. Being an old Roman at heart, she preferred her nephew to emulate the ways of Rome. Consequently, she eagerly accepted every scrap of scandal regarding Mr. Warrington's "Lovelace" adventures.

While we have witnessed instances where he hosted tea and music for the company at the Wells, and displayed gallantry toward ladies of superior figure and character compared to Cattarina, Madame Bernstein eventually ceased to worry about the foolish romance that began at Castlewood. She relaxed her vigilance over Lady Maria. Many elderly people are simply too self-absorbed to maintain long-term interest in their neighbors' affairs. The Baroness had her own concerns: her social engagements, her dinners, and her rheumatic pains. Her previously lively suspicions regarding Maria and Harry drifted into slumber, replaced by a careless, unobservant watch. Perhaps she believed the danger had passed, or perhaps she no longer cared if it existed. Alternatively, the crafty Maria may have successfully cajoled, soothed, and misled the old dragon entrusted with her care. By Maria's age—or even younger—maidenhood teaches cunning, while at Madame Bernstein's stage of life, dragons lose their ferocity. Their teeth drop out, their eyes grow heavy, and their capacity for harm diminishes. Having observed several such female dragons throughout my travels, I admire the softening influence of time upon these former destroyers. Their scales soften until a knight with moderate skill can pierce them; their claws, once capable of tearing out a thousand eyes, now tap feebly against the skin; their tongues, emerging from toothless gums, spit venom that is unpleasant but hardly lethal. Watch them now, dragging their languid tails back to their caverns at dusk! How weak their powers of injury have become! How diminished their malice! How changed they are from the brisk days when their eyes flashed wicked fire, their tongues spat poison, their breath blasted reputations, and they devoured a victim daily!

Given that the virtuous folk of Oakhurst could not withstand the testimony concerning Harry's misdeeds, why should Madame Bernstein, who possessed more experience of evil in her long days than the entire Oakhurst family combined, be any less credulous? Naturally, every elderly woman in her ladyship's circle believed every tale spun about Mr. Harry Warrington's dissipation, ready to accept even greater accusations. When the little dancer returned to London, it was because heartless Harry had abandoned her. He deserted her for someone else—a name whispered confidently by society at Tunbridge Wells, a name comprising half a dozen variations discussed by women of fashion, reputation, demi-reputation, virtue, and none. All mingled in the same rooms, danced to the same fiddles, drank from the same glasses at the Wells, united in their pursuit of health, society, or pleasure. A century prior, our ancestors, whether free or strait-laced, gathered at scores of such merry venues as this current scene unfolds, dancing, frolicking, gaming, and drinking at Epsom, Bath, Tunbridge, and Harrogate, just as they do today at Homburg and Baden.

Thus, Harry's tarnished reputation comforted his old aunt immensely, easing her mind regarding the boy's passion for Lady Maria. So relieved was she that when the chaplain arrived to escort her ladyship home, Madame Bernstein showed no desire to part with her niece. Instead, she preferred keeping her under close observation, discussing her wicked young cousin's wild extravagances, whispering that "boys will be boys," and confiding her intention to secure a proper wife for Harry—someone of suitable age and fortune. Poor Maria bore these pleasantries with as much fortitude as she could muster.

During the previous century, a certain French Duke and Marquis distinguished himself across Europe and America, obliging posterity with a choice volume of memoirs. The gentle reader is specifically warned against consulting this work. Having played the role of Don Juan in his homeland, ours, and elsewhere, he meticulously recorded the names of countless court beauties who fell victim to his fascinations. While undoubtedly pleasant reading for the grandsons and descendants of the fashionable figures among whom our brilliant nobleman moved—to find their ancestresses adorning M. le Duc's sprightly pages and their frailties chronicled by such a candid writer—the content remains cautionary.

In his travels through North America, this nobleman followed his customary pattern in Europe and immediately sought romantic entanglements. It is curious to contrast the elegant refinements of European society, where, according to Monseigneur, one need only lay siege to a woman to vanquish her, with the simple lives and habits of the colonial populace. Among these Americans, this European enslaver of hearts apparently failed to make a single conquest. Had he succeeded, he surely would have narrated his victories in Pennsylvania and New England with the same pride he described his triumphs in France and his own country. Travelers in America have loudly criticized the rudeness and barbarism of transatlantic manners; yet, let the present writer offer humble testimony that the conversation of American gentlemen is generally modest, and, to the best of his belief, the lives of American women are pure.

We have noted that Mr. Harry Warrington brought this colonial modesty to the Old Country. Though he could not avoid overhearing the free talk of the pleasure-seeking individuals around him, he remained largely silent amidst their chatter. He never indulged in double entendres in conversation with women, boasted of no victories over the sex, and appeared shy and awkward when hearing such tales recounted by others.

Mr. Sampson had observed this youthful modesty during his time at Castlewood, where Mr. Warrington often seemed uneasy while cousin Will told his choice stories. My Lord had curtly rebuked his brother, instructing him to reserve his jokes for the usher's table at Kensington and avoid offending their kinsman. Hence, the chaplain's exclamation of *"Reverentia pueris"* (Reverence for children) addressed to his neighbor at the ordinary on Harry's first appearance. Mr. Sampson, lacking the strength to do right himself, at least possessed the grace not to offend innocent young gentlemen with his cynicism.

The chaplain was deeply touched by Harry's gift of the horse and felt a genuine friendship toward the lad. "You see, sir," he remarked, "I am of the world and must act as the rest do. I have led a rough life, Mr. Warrington, and cannot afford to be more particular than my neighbors. *Video meliora, deteriora sequor* ('I see better things, but follow worse'), as we said at college. I have a little sister at boarding school nearby, and since I keep a decent tongue in my head when speaking with her, Patty, and expect others to do the same, I feel I may try to do as much for you."

The chaplain lavished praise upon Harry before his aunt, the old Baroness, who delighted in hearing him commended. She loved him dearly, pleased by his good looks, manly bearing, ready blushing, bright eyes, and deep youthful voice. His combination of shrewdness and simplicity constantly amused her; she would have grown weary of him long ago had he been clever, learned, or witty. "We must find a good wife for him, Chaplain," she told Mr. Sampson. "I have one or two in mind who seem suitable. We must establish him here; he will never bear returning to his savages or living with his little Methodist mother."

On this point, Mr. Sampson was equally anxious and had a wife in mind for Harry. Presumably, he had conversed with My Lord at Castlewood regarding the Earl's intention to provide a living or provision for the chaplain should he facilitate a marriage for Lady Maria. If his good offices could help that anxious lady find a husband, Sampson was eager to employ them, waiting to determine the most effective way to wield his influence.

Sampson's company was delightful, and within a few hours, he and his young friend became intimate. The parson rejoiced in high spirits, a hearty appetite, and good humor. He pretended to no squeamishness and avoided sanctimonious hypocrisy, yet he carefully refrained from shocking his young friend with unnecessary levity or immoral talk. Initiating his pupil, perhaps out of policy or compunction, he revealed only the minor mysteries of life, withholding the darker secrets with which the unlucky adept himself was all too familiar. With Harry, Sampson was merely a brisk, lively, jolly companion, ready for drinking bouts, sports, cockfights, shooting matches, card games, or gallops across the common. Yet his conversation remained decent, aiming more to amuse than to lead astray. The chaplain was remarkably successful; possessing immense animal spirits, natural wit, aptitude, and experience in the trade of "toad-eating"—his calling since entering college as a servitor—he navigated the path to fortune with ease.
