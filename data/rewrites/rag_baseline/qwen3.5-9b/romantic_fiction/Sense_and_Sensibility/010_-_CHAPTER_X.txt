The knock at the cottage door arrived with the crisp efficiency of a scheduled inspection, yet the visitor who entered carried the weight of a sudden, fortuitous turn of fate. Willoughby, styled by Margaret with a mixture of elegant flair and imprecise categorization as Marianne's preserver, arrived early the following morning. Mrs. Dashwood greeted him not merely with polite civility, but with a warmth born of genuine gratitude and reinforced by Sir John's glowing testimony. Every interaction during his visit served to solidify his conviction regarding the family's domestic harmony, mutual affection, and refined sensibilities. He required no second introduction to confirm the personal allure of the sisters; Miss Dashwood possessed a delicate countenance and regular features, while Marianne, though lacking her sister's rigid symmetry, commanded a more striking presence through her commanding height. Her face was a masterpiece of loveliness, so radiant that the phrase "beautiful girl" applied to her felt like a modest understatement rather than an exaggeration. Her skin, though tanned, glowed with an inner brilliance due to its translucency, framed by features of perfect proportion. A sweet, magnetic smile animated her, but it was her dark eyes that captivated—their depths held a restless vitality, an eager spark that demanded to be witnessed. Initially, Marianne's gaze remained guarded, stifled by the awkwardness of owing him a debt of gratitude. However, as this initial hesitation dissolved, allowing her spirits to gather momentum, she recognized in him a blend of impeccable breeding, frankness, and vivacity. When he confessed his passionate devotion to music and dance, she bestowed upon him a look of such enthusiastic approval that he instantly claimed the floor for the remainder of his stay. Any mention of favored pastimes unlocked her tongue; she could not remain silent when these topics arose, shedding all reserve in their discussion. They quickly discovered a profound resonance in their tastes for rhythm and melody, a shared aesthetic judgment that transcended mere coincidence. Encouraged by this intellectual and artistic kinship, Marianne ventured deeper, questioning him on literature. As she cited her favorite authors, her descriptions radiated such rapturous delight that any man of twenty-five, regardless of prior skepticism, would inevitably be converted to their excellence. Their minds moved in lockstep; they idolized the same texts, revered the same passages, and when disagreements surfaced, they were fleeting skirmishes quickly resolved by the sheer force of her argumentation and the luminosity of her gaze. He acquiesced to her verdicts, mirroring her enthusiasm until their conversation flowed with the easy familiarity of old acquaintances.

"Well, Marianne," Elinor observed the moment Willoughby departed, "for a single morning, I believe you have accomplished quite a feat. You have already gauged Mr. Willoughby's stance on nearly every significant matter. You know his appraisal of Cowper and Scott; you are assured he values their beauties appropriately, and you have received confirmation that he holds Pope in moderate regard. Yet, how is this acquaintance to endure given such rapid exhaustion of every potential topic? One additional meeting will suffice to clarify his views on picturesque landscapes and the ethics of second marriages, leaving you with nothing further to inquire."

"Elinor!" Marianne exclaimed, indignant. "Is this fair? Is this just? Are my ideas so barren?" She paused, realizing the truth behind her sister's jest. "I see now. I have been too comfortable, too blissfully open, too frankly myself. I have violated every conventional notion of decorum; I have been transparent and sincere where prudence dictates reserve, appearing instead as spiritless, dull, and deceitful had I limited my discourse to the weather and road conditions, speaking only once per ten minutes. Had I done so, this reproach would have been avoided."

"My dear," her mother interjected gently, "do not take offense at Elinor's words; she spoke only in jest. I would scold her myself if she were capable of wishing to dampen the joy of your conversation with our new friend."

Marianne's anger melted instantly. On Willoughby's side, every gesture confirmed his pleasure in their growing bond, offering tangible evidence of his desire to deepen the connection. He visited daily. Initially, inquiries about Marianne's health served as his pretext, but the escalating warmth of his welcome rendered such excuses obsolete before her complete recovery allowed them to cease entirely. Confined to the house for several days, she found the restriction far less burdensome than usual. Willoughby was a youth of considerable ability, quick imagination, and lively spirit, coupled with open, affectionate manners. He was perfectly cast to capture Marianne's heart; alongside these qualities, he possessed a natural ardor of mind that was ignited and amplified by her own example, making him uniquely appealing to her affections. His company gradually became her supreme delight. They read, discussed, and sang together; his musical talent was substantial, and he approached reading with a sensitivity and spirit that Edward had sadly lacked. In Mrs. Dashwood's eyes, he was flawless. Elinor, however, discerned only one flaw: a propensity to express his thoughts too freely on every occasion, disregarding context or audience. His tendency to sacrifice general politeness for the sake of undivided attention when his heart was engaged, and his willingness to lightly dismiss worldly proprieties, revealed a lack of caution that Elinor could not endorse, despite the fervent defense mounted by Willoughby and Marianne.

Marianne began to realize that the desperate longing she had felt at sixteen and a half—to encounter a man fulfilling her ideal of perfection—had been rash and unjustified. Willoughby embodied everything her imagination had sketched in those tragic moments and subsequent years; his behavior declared his intentions as earnest as his capabilities were strong. Even her mother, initially devoid of speculative hopes regarding marriage, was led within a week to anticipate the union, secretly congratulating herself on securing two worthy sons-in-law in Edward and Willoughby. Meanwhile, Colonel Brandon's partiality for Marianne, previously noted only by his circle, finally drew Elinor's attention as it faded from theirs. With their focus diverted to the more fortunate rival, the earlier mockery directed at Brandon vanished once his genuine feelings rendered him a target for ridicule deemed fitting for excessive sensibility. Reluctantly, Elinor accepted that the sentiments Mrs. Jennings had attributed to him were now truly stirred by her sister. While a shared temperament might accelerate Willoughby's affection, the stark contrast in their natures posed no obstacle to Colonel Brandon's regard. Elinor viewed this with concern; what hope could a quiet man of fifty-three possess against a vibrant youth of twenty-two? Unable to wish him success, she sincerely prayed for his indifference. Yet, she liked him—despite his gravity and reserve, she perceived in him an object of genuine interest. His manners, though serious, were mild, and his reserve seemed the result of suppressed sorrow rather than innate gloom. Sir John's hints of past betrayals validated her belief that he was a man of misfortune, earning her respect and compassion. Perhaps she pitied and esteemed him even more because he was overlooked by Willoughby and Marianne, who, biased against his lack of liveliness and youth, seemed determined to undervalue his merits.

"Brandon is precisely the type of man," Willoughby remarked one day during a discussion of the colonel, "who garners universal praise yet attracts no one's memory; whom everyone delights to see, yet no one bothers to converse with."

"That is exactly my assessment of him," Marianne agreed enthusiastically.

"Do not boast of such insight," Elinor countered. "It is unjust toward both of you. He is highly esteemed by the entire household at the Park, and I rarely fail to make an effort to engage him in conversation."

"That he enjoys your patronage," Willoughby replied, "is certainly advantageous. But regarding the esteem of the others, it is a reproach in itself. Who would submit to the indignity of being approved by women like Lady Middleton and Mrs. Jennings if they could command the indifference of anyone else?"

"But perhaps the disdain of men like yourselves and Marianne balances the regard of Lady Middleton and her mother. If their praise constitutes censure, then your criticism may constitute praise, for they are no less undiscerning than you are prejudiced and unjust."

"In defense of your protégé, you can even afford to be saucy."

"My protégé, as you term him, is a sensible man; and sense possesses enduring attractions for me. Indeed, Marianne, even in a man between thirty and forty. He has traversed the globe, read extensively, and possesses a contemplative mind. I have found him capable of providing extensive information on diverse subjects, answering my inquiries with readiness, good breeding, and good nature."

"To put it plainly," Marianne retorted with contempt, "he has simply informed you that the climate in the East Indies is hot and the mosquitoes are troublesome."

"He would undoubtedly have told me so had I asked, but those were matters I had already been briefed upon."

"Perhaps," Willoughby suggested, "his observations extended to the existence of nabobs, gold mohurs, and palanquins."

"I dare say his observations reached far beyond your candour. But why the animosity?"

"I harbor no dislike. On the contrary, I consider him a very respectable man, universally praised yet ignored; wealthy beyond necessity, time-abundant yet unemployed, sporting two new coats annually."

"And furthermore," Marianne added, "he lacks genius, taste, and spirit. His understanding shines with no brilliance, his feelings burn with no ardor, and his voice carries no expression."
