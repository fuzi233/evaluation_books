The silence that followed the slaughter was heavier than the thunder that had preceded it. Sir Henry and Good were dragged into Twala's hut, a sanctuary of sorts amidst the carnage, where I joined them, my own legs feeling like lead beneath my skin. We were a triad of utter exhaustion, drained not merely by the physical exertion of battle but by the sheer volume of blood we had lost. My constitution, usually wiry and resilient thanks to years of travel and a light frame, had finally surrendered; the fatigue hit me with the force of a physical blow, igniting the old scar from the lion's jaws and sending a violent ache throbbing behind my eyes from the morning's concussion. We were a miserable lot, huddled together in the dim interior, yet there was a grim comfort in our misery. At least we were alive to feel it, spared the finality of the plain where thousands of brave souls lay cold and silent, victims of a conflict born of hubris.

With the aid of Foulata, a creature of striking beauty who had voluntarily assumed the role of our handmaiden in gratitude for our rescue, and with Good's steady hands, we managed to remove the chain shirts that had miraculously kept us alive. Beneath the steel links, the flesh was a landscape of terror—terribly contused. While the armor had stopped the weapons, it had not shielded us from the crushing impact. Both Sir Henry and Good were maps of bruising, and I was hardly exempt. Foulata responded with alacrity, bringing us a poultice of pounded green leaves, aromatic and potent, which, when applied as a plaster, offered a fleeting respite from the agony.

Yet, the bruises were trivial compared to the gaping wounds that threatened our lives. Good bore a catastrophic hole through the fleshy portion of his "beautiful white leg," a breach from which blood had flowed freely. Sir Henry, battered beyond recognition, sported a deep, jagged cut across his jaw, the work of Twala's battle-axe. Fortunately, Good possessed the skills of a surgeon; as soon as his modest medical kit was unsealed, he worked with frantic precision. Cleansing the wounds under the flickering, inadequate light of a primitive Kukuana lamp, he stitched Sir Henry's face and then his own leg with surprising competence. He finished by slathering the injuries with an antiseptic ointment from his box and bandaging them with the remnants of a pocket-handkerchief.

While Good attended to the surgical necessities, Foulata brewed a strong broth, for we were too depleted to attempt solid food. We drank it greedily and collapsed onto the piles of magnificent *karrosses*—fur rugs—that littered the great hut of the dead king. Irony, that cruel spinner of tales, dictated that Sir Henry, the man who had delivered the killing blow to Twala, should spend that night wrapped in Twala's own bedding, sleeping upon Twala's own couch.

I say "sleep"; but after such a day, rest was a distant dream. The air inside the hut was thick, not just with the smell of blood and sweat, but with the cacophony of grief. From every corner of the camp drifted the wailing of women, their voices raw with sorrow for husbands, sons, and brothers lost in the fray. It was a heart-rending symphony of despair, a testament to the destruction of nearly a fifth of the Kukuana army—over twelve thousand men wiped out in a single, awful struggle. Lying there, listening to their endless cries for those who would never return, I grasped the full, horrific cost of human ambition.

Towards midnight, the ceaseless chorus began to fade, punctuated only occasionally by a long, piercing howl emanating from a hut directly behind ours. Later, I learned this was Gagool, the witch-doctor, keening over the corpse of the king. After that, I succumbed to a fitful doze, waking repeatedly in cold sweats, reliving the nightmare of the last twenty-four hours. In my dreams, I saw the warrior I had felled charging down the mountain path; I stood once more in the glorious ring of Greys, making our immortal stand on the little mound; and I saw Twala's plumed, gory head roll past my boots, teeth gnashing, eyes blazing with malice.

Dawn broke gray and somber. My companions had fared no better. Good was burning with a high fever, quickly becoming light-headed and spitting blood—a sure sign of internal damage sustained when a Kukuana warrior had driven his massive spear through his chainmail. Sir Henry, despite his facial wound which rendered eating impossible and laughter a memory, seemed surprisingly fresh, though he was stiff and sore, barely able to stir.

Around eight o'clock, Infadoos visited us. The tough old warrior looked remarkably unscathed by the battle, though he admitted to having spent the entire night awake. He greeted us with delight but deep concern for Good's state, shaking our hands warmly. I noted, however, that he addressed Sir Henry with a reverential awe, treating him as something more than mortal. Indeed, as we later discovered, the great Englishman was viewed throughout Kukuanaland as a supernatural entity. No soldier claimed anyone could have fought as he did, or slain Twala—who was reputed to be the strongest warrior in the land, capable of shearing a bull's neck in a single stroke—at the end of such a bloody day. That specific stroke became proverbial, forever known thereafter as "Incubu's blow."

Infadoos reported that Twala's regiments had already submitted to Ignosi, and similar surrenders were trickling in from the outlying chiefs. With Twala dead at the hands of Sir Henry, the threat of civil war had evaporated; Scragga, Twala's only legitimate son, was dead, leaving no rival claimant to the throne.

"I remarked that Ignosi had seized power through blood," I said. The old chief shrugged, his expression unreadable. "Yes," he answered calmly. "But the Kukuana people can only be kept cool by letting their blood flow sometimes. Many are killed, indeed, but the women remain, and others must soon grow up to fill the gaps. After this, the land will be quiet for a while."

Later that morning, Ignosi himself paid us a visit, the royal diadem now bound upon his brow. As he advanced with kingly dignity, flanked by an obsequious guard, I recalled the tall Zulu who had sought employment at Durban mere months prior, wondering at the strange, dizzying revolutions of fortune.

"Hail, O king!" I said, rising.

"Yes, Macumazahn. King at last, by the might of your three right hands," he replied readily.

"All is going well," he assured me, hoping to host a great feast within two weeks to display his new authority to the people. When I asked of Gagool's fate, his resolve hardened. "She is the evil genius of the land," he declared. "I shall kill her, and all the witch-doctors with her! She has lived so long that none can remember when she was not very old. It is she who trains the witch-hunters and makes the land wicked in the sight of the heavens."

"Yet she knows much," I countered gently. "It is easier to destroy knowledge, Ignosi, than to gather it."

"That is so," he conceded thoughtfully. "She, and she alone, knows the secret of the 'Three Witches' yonder, where the great road runs, where the kings are buried, and the Silent Ones sit."

"And the diamonds are," I pressed. "Do not forget your promise, Ignosi. You must lead us to the mines, even if you must spare Gagool alive to guide us."

"I will not forget, Macumazahn, and I will ponder your words."

Following his departure, I checked on Good, finding him in a state of delirium. The fever gripping his system was compounded by his internal injuries, placing him in a critical condition for several days. Only Foulata's indefatigable nursing kept him alive. Women are women, regardless of color, yet watching this dusky beauty tend to the fevered man with such swift, gentle instinct was peculiar. For the first couple of nights, Sir Henry and I attempted to assist, but Foulata bore our interference with impatience, insisting that our movements agitated him. She took sole charge, watching him day and night, feeding him a native cooling drink infused with tulip bulb juice and shielding him from flies.

Night after night, by the light of our primitive lamp, the scene played out: Good tossing restlessly, his features gaunt, his eyes wide and luminous, babbling nonsense; and beside him, seated on the dirt floor with her back against the hut wall, the soft-eyed Kukuana beauty. Her face, worn by sleeplessness, was animated by an expression of infinite compassion—or was it something deeper?

We feared for two days that he would die, moving through the camp with heavy hearts. Around Twala's chief hut, extending for three hundred yards, there was absolute silence; by the king's order, everyone living in the habitations behind it had been evacuated, lest any noise disturb the sick man. On the fifth day of his illness, as was my custom, I crossed over to check on him before retiring.

I entered the hut cautiously. A lamp on the floor illuminated Good, who was no longer tossing but lying perfectly still. So it had come at last! In the bitterness of my heart, I gave a sob.
