Late May, 1900. I had just returned to London after two years spent mostly wandering the south of France, when the post left on my study step a package the likes of which I have never seen before or since, nor ever will. It came from my oldest intimate friend, Dr. Arthur Lister Browne – M.A. (Oxon.), F.R.C.P., the sole attending physician for a scattered rural practice deep in Norfolk. We were both atrocious correspondents, had barely exchanged two scribbled notes across those two years of my absence, so I had no warning of what was coming.

Inside the wrapping were four tattered leather-bound notebooks, every page crammed wall to wall with the looping, giddy scribble of Pitman’s shorthand, their clustered characters looking for all the world like swarms of startled gnats hovering mid-flight over the paper. Browne had written every line in soft pencil, no care for thick or thin strokes, barely a vowel dotted here or there. Deciphering them was no lazy holiday task, I can promise the reader; I had to puzzle over each page for weeks, squinting at the marks until my eyes burned. The letter that came tucked on top was also written entirely in shorthand, and I publish that letter now, paired with the full transcript of the second notebook I decoded – the one marked with a faint pencil III on its cover. In five small spots, my careful guesswork had to patch over half-iligible lines, and two full passages were so warped and unreadable I had to set them aside entirely, but none of those missing bits touch the core of the story that follows.

This is the full text of Browne’s letter:
> I’m propped up in my bed right now, morphia humming soft in my veins, and all I can think of is how I wish you were here to squeeze my hand one last time before I go – because go I certainly will. Four days back I felt a soreness prickle in my throat, stopped by old Johnson’s surgery in Selbridge to let him have a look. He mumbled something about membranous laryngitis, and I laughed at him. By the time I got home to my cottage I was hoarse, and the laugh was gone. By nightfall I couldn’t draw breath without that raw, strident rattle in my throat. I telegraphed the top throat specialist Morgan straight down from London, and the two of them cut my trachea open, seared the inside of my airways with chromic acid and the galvanic cautery. The breathlessness is gone now, I barely feel pain – but I’ve been a doctor long enough to know what my own body is telling me. The bronchi are infected, far too far for any hope. Morgan’s still babbling about adding me to his list of successful tracheotomy recoveries, but prognosis was always my strongest suit, and I can tell you flat: he’s wrong. The only small, silly consolation I’ll get out of dying is beating a celebrated specialist at his own game.
> I spent this morning sorting my last affairs, and remembered these notebooks. I meant to send them to you months ago, but my habit of putting things off, and the fact that the woman whose words I transcribed was still alive, held me back. She’s dead now, and you – a man of letters, a student of the weird edges of life – will find them fascinating, if you can get through my messy shorthand. They might even be world-altering.
> The woman was Miss Mary Wilson. I met her when she was thirty, knew her every day for fifteen years until she died last month at forty-five. We were hypnotist and subject, you see. She’d worked with a different trance practitioner before me, but no one ever could reach the deep, full control over her mind that I could. She suffered from tic douloureux of the fifth cranial nerve, that worst of all face pains. They’d pulled out every one of her teeth before I met her, even cut through her cheek to yank the offending nerve out of her jaw, but nothing worked. She described it as every clock in hell tick-tocking away nonstop in her lower face, until my hypnotic passes could make that pain vanish like smoke.
> You never saw anyone as uncanny to look at as Mary. Even I, a doctor who’s seen every kind of human face, would jump out of my skin if I turned and saw her suddenly. She did not feel like a woman of this world. She smelled a little of damp grave mould, her whole presence thin and ghostly, not quite solid. Her brow was high and sharp, her lips meagre, her chin pointed, her cheeks permanently ashen grey. She was tall, so emaciated you could trace every line of her skeleton right through her skin, save for the thick bones of her thighs. Her eyes were the pale blue of smoke from a half-burned cigarette, with a soft, unearthly gaze that never seemed to be looking at anything *in* the room, but something far away. By the time she was thirty-five, her wisp of fine hair had turned completely white.
> She was wealthy, lived all alone in old Wooding Manor, five miles outside Ash Thomas. I was just starting my practice back then, and she insisted I move into the manor to attend to her full time – that one single patient made me more money than any busy city practice ever could.
> It didn’t take me long to discover that, in trance, Mary held powers far more reliable and far-reaching than any I’d ever read of. Any amateur in psychical science will prattle on now about trance states granting the mind the power to see distant places – but that fact the Psychical Research Society spent decades “discovering” was known to every village wise woman since the Middle Ages. Mary’s gift was special not in its kind, but in its constancy. Most trance travellers roam across the present, across the surface of the earth, but Mary could move in all directions: north, south, up, down, across the past, the present, the future.
> She never spoke of herself in trance as “I”. She never said “we”, either. She always said “us”, like her soul had split into many small beings when she slipped under. When she peered deep into the distant past, she would say “us are 1,200 miles above the earth”. For present events happening anywhere on the globe, she said “us are on the ground”. But when she turned her gaze to the future? She said “us are diving down inside the earth”. The further forward she tried to see, the deeper she had to dive, and the heavier the pressure got. She was like a deep sea diver, fighting against the weight of the future, and she could never get deeper than 63 miles down. Most times she’d stop, gasping, at 20, 25 miles.
> For fifteen years I sat by her bedside for hours every week, scribbling down every murmur of that slow, thick, breath-heavy speech she made in trance. Six months in, I heard her recite long passages from Gibbon’s *Decline and Fall of the Roman Empire* word for word – and Mary had never read a page of Gibbon in her life. I said sternly, “Where are you, us?” She murmured back, “Us are 811 miles above. A dead man is writing. Us are reading.”
> One afternoon five years into our work, she started murmuring sentences that sounded like a full, complete book no one had written yet. When I asked where she was, she said “us are 45 miles inside: us read, and another writes.” For the next weeks I held her trance to that same vision, until I had transcribed almost the whole work. It’s this notebook, the one marked III. It will tell a story no one in our 1900 world has lived yet.
> But no more talking about Mary. Let’s talk about Arthur Browne, F.R.C.P., with a breathing tube stuck in his throat and Eternity tucked under his pillow…

The rest of Browne’s letter touches on private personal matters of no public interest. He died two days after writing those words, exactly as his own prognosis had warned.
I have transcribed every word of this notebook marked III without edits, save for assigning titles and breaking it into paragraphs for readability, as the original shorthand scribble had no structure. The words that follow are not a story Mary made up. They are the full, true account of one man’s future, written by his own hand, on the other side of the veil that we call the present.

My memory is growing thin now, fraying at the edges. For instance – what was the name of that Scots preacher from Ayrshire, the one who thundered against the whole *Boreal* North Pole expedition, calling it wicked, right before our ship set sail? I can’t remember it now. Yet four years ago it was as familiar to me as my own name. All the events before that voyage are fading to mist in my head. I sit here now, on the loggia of this empty Cornish villa, writing all this down for no one – no other human eye will ever read these words, not a single one. But I have to write it, all the same.
That preacher was a great gaunt man, tawny hair, striding through the streets of London in rough homespun and a shaggy Highland shawl, frowning and muttering to himself. He started his tiny chapel in Fetter Lane, and soon crowds were spilling out the doors, even people coming all the way from America and Australia to hear him. He was like John the Baptist reborn, wild, fiery, his voice rolling from a whisper that crept up your spine to a thunder that shook the rafters, his gestures clumsy and raw, like some prophet from the stone ages. His name – Mackay, that’s it, the name was Mackay.
Mackay ranted against the *Boreal* for three straight Sundays while the ship’s preparations were nearing completion. The whole world was in a fever for the North Pole back then, and not just for science. Ten years before we set sail, the richest man who had ever lived, Chicago eccentric Charles P. Stickney, had died and left 175 million dollars – the whole of his vast fortune – to the *first individual person* to set foot on the 90th degree of north latitude. Twenty-seven expeditions had set out in that decade alone, all chasing that fortune. Every man, every boy, on the streets of London would have begged for a spot on the *Boreal* crew rather than miss that chance.
But Mackay stood up in that Kensington chapel, one man against four hundred million people all baying for the Pole, and told them they were all wrong. He said the North Pole was the forbidden Tree of Eden, placed off limits to mankind for a reason. All those decades of strange, random failures that had stopped every expedition short of the pole? They were not accidents. They were the hand of God holding men back, warning them not to step across a line that was never meant to be crossed. He said that man was about to get the power to step over that line, the same way Adam had the power to reach out and pluck the forbidden fruit – and just like that first fall, the consequences would be swift, universal, and devastating. The sky would turn dark. The whole world would be struck down.
I was in that crowd that night. I sat spellbound, the hair on my arms standing up. But before I even got back to my lodgings, the whole impression had slipped off me like rain off oilcloth. I laughed at him, thought the old preacher was just a crank who’d been left behind by the march of progress. A John the Baptist in the 20th century? All you’d get is tolerant shrugs.
Oh God. How differently I think of Mackay now.

Three weeks before that fateful Sunday night sermon, Clark—leader of the groundbreaking polar expedition the *Boreal* was set to carry north—knocked on my Harley Street door for nothing more than a casual friend’s visit. I had set up practice at No. 11 barely a year prior, and though I was not yet twenty-five, I counted myself among Europe’s most elite medical men.

Elite, yes: but small. My clientele of titled peers let me keep a household of comfort and move in the highest social circles, but secret, sharp money troubles nipped at my heels more often than I cared to admit. Just that week, my modest windfall from the success of my published treatise *Applications of Science to the Arts* had pulled me back from the edge of ruinous embarrassment.

We chatted of trivialities for an hour, until Clark leaned back in my armchair and spoke in that light, reckless tone of his:
“Adam Jeffson, do you know what I dreamed of you last night? You were right there with us, sailing for the Arctic ice.”

I jolted so hard the teacup in my hand sloshed. That same exact dream had woken me sweating at three a.m. the night before. But I swallowed the truth fast, stammering out:
“Who, me? On your expedition? I’d never agree to go, not if you begged.”
“Oh, you would,” he laughed.
“I wouldn’t. You forget I’m weeks away from being married.”
“Fair enough, and we don’t need to hash it out—Peters isn’t about to drop dead, after all,” he said. “But if anything *did* happen to him, you’re the first man I’d come running to.”
“Clark, you’re jesting,” I protested. “I barely know astronomy, or magnetic survey work.”
“Your botany is exactly what we need,” he waved off my objection. “And nautical astronomy? Any man with your scientific mind would pick it up in a week, no effort at all.”

We bantered back and forth, but I felt a cold, thrumming undercurrent under every word. Even then, the secret core of me knew I was standing at one of those life-altering thresholds that had marked every strange, unprecedented turn of my existence since I was a small child. I knew it for two reasons: the matching shared dream, and the second Clark left, as I pulled on my gloves to call on my fiancée, I heard the two Voices that had haunted me from childhood roar clear in my chest. One commanded *Do not go to her now*. The other screamed *Go, go at once*.

No ordinary reader would believe how real those voices felt—loud, tangible, closer than breath, closer than hands and feet, locked in furious, writhing argument inside my ribcage. I was seven when I first heard them. I played in the pine woods on my father’s estate one summer evening, half a mile from a steep 30-foot quarry cliff, when a soft whisper bade me walk toward the edge, and another bade me turn back. I went anyway, slipped, and fell. I woke weeks later, told my astonished mother that one unseen being had pushed me, and another had caught me before I hit the rocks.

By my eleventh birthday, I had realized the truth: two mutually hostile, invisible Powers were locked in a constant war over my life. One wanted me dead, the other wanted me kept alive; one drove me to do one thing, the other forced me toward its exact opposite. I was not like other boys—marked, separate, for some great, unknowable purpose. I never found the Biblical tales of prophets hearing the voice of God absurd; I knew firsthand that ancient man had far more than two ears to hear with, and that I carried that same primal gift, half-forgotten by the modern world.

To everyone else, I looked like a perfectly ordinary young man. I stroked for my Cambridge college eight, crammed for exams, loitered in London clubs, and no one suspected the screaming civil war waging in my soul when I chose my profession. One Voice howled *Be a scientist, be a doctor*. The other raged *Be anything else, lawyer, artist, engineer—anything but a doctor*. I chose doctor. At Trinity, I met a quiet, odd little man named Scotland, who ranted endlessly about a Black Power and a White Power locked in frantic, total war for control of the universe. The White Power had held sway in Europe up through the Middle Ages, he said, but had been slowly losing ground ever since, and the Black would soon claim this entire world as his prize. My friends laughed at him, called him the Black-and-White Mystery Man, but I drank in every word he said with the hunger of a man who had just found the explanation for the strangest secret of his life.

Back to that day, when I walked out the door to make my way to Clodagh’s rooms in Hanover Square. The two Voices never stopped shouting the whole walk: *Breathe no word of Clark’s visit, no word of the expedition* and *Tell her everything, hide nothing*. It felt like a month of walking, but it was only minutes before I was holding my fiancée in my arms.

Clodagh, Countess Clodagh, was five years my senior, the most impossibly beautiful, haughty creature I had ever seen, her neck held high as if it was forever scorning some phantom standing just over her left shoulder. I know now she was godless, bitter to the bone, a woman who once laughed and said her favorite historical figure was Lucrezia Borgia, then claimed it was a joke the second she saw my horror. Our engagement had been viciously opposed on both sides: my family raged that her father and grandfather had both died in asylums, hers raged that I was neither rich enough nor noble enough for her. Her older sister had already married a country doctor named Peters, a mésalliance that made her union with me feel like an insult to their family name. But Clodagh’s passion for me burned so hot it could not be snuffed out—sometimes it frightened me.

Her nephew, that country doctor’s son Peter Peters, was the *Boreal* expedition’s assigned doctor, botanist, and meteorological assistant.

I had barely sat down five minutes in her drawing room before the words tumbled out of me, against every instinct: “Clark stopped by today, ranted about the expedition. He said if anything happened to Peters, he’d come running straight to me. He had the silliest dream about me joining you all.”

I knew, even as I spoke, that I was tangled in something crooked and wicked, but I could no more stop the words than I could fly.

Clodagh stood at the window, holding a rose to her face, and did not move for a full minute. When she spoke, her voice was cold, sharp, rapid: “The first man to plant his foot on the North Pole will be ennobled, no question. Not to mention the millions in prize money and patents that will follow. I only wish I was a man.”
“I have no taste for that kind of ambition,” I told her. “I’m perfectly happy in our warm little Eden, I don’t want that Arctic cold.”
“Don’t make me think less of you, Adam,” she pouted.
“But why should you? No one has to crave the Pole, not when we’re due to be married.”
“Marriage?” She turned, eyes blazing. “Being the hero of the greatest polar expedition in history would turn our little scandal of a wedding from something the ton snickers at into the most celebrated triumph of the decade. For me, you will go, won’t you?”

I protested there was no way Peters would step aside, but she cut me off, her voice dropping low: “They say Peter takes atropine. All the time.”

I jolted so hard I nearly knocked over a side table. She laughed that dry, mad little laugh of hers, and settled into her rocking chair. “Don’t look so shocked. I’m not going to poison him just to make you a millionaire and a peer—though I easily could. He and Wilson, the expedition’s electrician, are coming over for dinner tonight, you know.”

Her casual, light dismissal of the idea of murder made my skin crawl, but I could not pull away. For hours, she pressed me for every detail of past failed polar expeditions, their routes, their equipment, the distances they had covered, her eyes shining bright the whole time. When I left that evening, she kissed me with a ferocity that left my lips stinging, and my heart heavy as lead.

At one a.m. that same night, Peters’ footman banged on my door, screaming that his master was dying. I ran across Harley Street to his rooms—three doors down, opposite mine—and saw at a single glance his delirious face, his dilated staring pupils, that he was deep in atropine poisoning. Wilson was there, pale as a sheet. “He’ll recover,” I told them, working over him for three hours straight. When I left before dawn, I was certain he would pull through.

I woke at 11 a.m. and hurried back, to find Clodagh already there, whispering to me behind her finger that she was staying to nurse him “until the end.” I could not meet her eyes. Peters did not recover as steadily as I predicted. By the end of the first week, he was still prostrate, ten days left before the *Boreal* was due to sail. I begged Clodagh to go home, to leave his sickbed, but she only smiled that lofty, amused smile of hers. “I love to watch the human body fight for its life,” she purred, “dabbling in delicate drugs, like Medea, like Helen of Troy, all those old great alchemist women. Don’t you think it’s thrilling? You have to let me have my little whims, Adam.” She stroked my hair, and I let her soothe me, even as I stared at the half-dead man in the bed and felt my stomach churn. Lucrezia Borgia belonged in the Renaissance, not in my drawing room, not touching the medicine of a dying man I was responsible for.

The second week slipped by. Only ten days remained before the expedition sailed. One evening I walked into Peters’ rooms to find Wilson, the electrician, staring suspiciously at the medicine glass Clodagh had just set down on the nightstand, sniffing at its contents with a quick, furtive motion. His mistrust of her was impossible to miss. Clark arrived that same day, and we brought in a third consulting doctor, Alleyne of Cavendish Square. Peters drifted in and out of semi-coma, wracked with bouts of violent vomiting, his symptoms no longer matching simple atropine poisoning—we could not name the strange, unknown vegetable poison slowly wasting him away.

“Looks like my dream came true after all, Jeffson,” Clark said to me, when we were alone. “Peters is out. I’m formally inviting you to take his place. Will you come?”

I shrugged, too stunned to speak.
“Great,” he said. “You’ve got eight days to get all your affairs in order. The *Boreal* sails soon.”

As we walked out of the dining room, I caught a flash of Clodagh’s gown gliding down the far end of the corridor, fast, away from us. I never said a word about the invitation—but I knew she had been listening through the door.

Then, at midnight that same night, Peters’ eyes fluttered open. By noon the next day, his iron Arctic-trained vitality had bounced back. He was propped up on his elbow, chatting to Wilson, only still plagued by sharp stomach pains. I prescribed quarter-grain sulphate of morphia tablets to ease the ache, and left.

Wilson, who had never liked me much, told Peters immediately that I had been offered his spot on the expedition. Peters, touchy and proud, fired off an angry letter of protest to Clark. Clark sent the letter on to me, marked with a big blue question mark. I wrote back immediately, saying Peters’ recovery annulled my acceptance, and he would sail as planned. The fifth day before departure dawned, Friday, the 15th of June. Peters was well enough to sit in an armchair, still fevered, still with the stomach pains. That night at 11 p.m. I found Clodagh sitting with him, chatting.
“Adam,” she said, “I didn’t know if I was supposed to give him his injection tonight. Should I?”
“Peters?” I asked. “Any pain still?”
“Go on, give me that quarter grain,” he laughed. “It’s still gnawing at me off and on.”

As Clodagh opened the syringe box, she pouted and said: “Our naughty patient injected himself with more atropine today.” I blew up at once, shouting at him that he had no right to take unprescribed drugs, or he was out of my care. He brushed me off, calling me dramatic.

Clodagh turned her back to us, walking to the mantelpiece to melt the morphia tablet in a drop of distilled water. She took so long there, rambling about a charity bazaar she had visited that afternoon, that a wild, screaming impulse seized me: I had to rush her, dash the syringe and the glass vial out of her hands before she could do something unspeakable. I was already leaning forward, half out of my chair, when the door behind me creaked open. Wilson was standing in the doorway.

I froze. I could not make a scene, not with him watching. I would not, could not accuse the woman I loved of murder. I stood like a marble statue as Clodagh turned around, walking over to greet Wilson with a bright, innocent smile. I told myself I was being absurd, that I had imagined it all.

Clodagh rolled up Peters’ sleeve, kneeling to inject the drug into his forearm. As she stood up, laughing at a joke Wilson made, the small glass vial slipped from her hand, and she ground it deliberately under her heel, shattering it to powder. She made a joke about Peters being naughty for taking unauthorized atropine. Those were the last words Peter Peters ever heard. He died shortly before one a.m. A post-mortem confirmed he had been given a massive, fatal dose of atropine.

Everything that followed blurred into a roiling nightmare. The inquest ruled his death an accidental self-administered poisoning, corroborated by both Clodagh and Wilson. All my affairs were rushed into order, the locum tenens lined up for my practice, my cold-weather gear packed, my name entered as the expedition’s new botanist and medical officer.

Only three sharp, clear details remain from that chaos. The first is that fiery, raucous sermon I stumbled into at a Kensington chapel on that Sunday night, where the preacher Mackay roared at his congregation, his voice shaking with the fire of prophecy: “As transgression in Eden brought swift, universal catastrophe on all mankind, I warn every last soul alive: from this day forward, expect nothing from God but a lowering sky, and thundery weather.” The next was the wind filling the *Boreal*’s sails as we cast off from the dock, and the third was the faint, strange purple tinge I thought I saw streaking the distant horizon, far out over the gray North Sea.
