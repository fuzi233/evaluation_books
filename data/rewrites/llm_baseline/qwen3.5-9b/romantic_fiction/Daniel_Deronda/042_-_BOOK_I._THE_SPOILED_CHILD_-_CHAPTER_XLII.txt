"If there exist hierarchies of suffering, the people of Israel occupy the highest rung; if endurance of pain and the fortitude with which it is borne confer nobility, then the Jews belong to the aristocracy of every land; and if a literature is deemed rich because it possesses a handful of classic tragedies, what judgment passes upon a National Tragedy sustained for fifteen centuries, written and enacted by the heroes themselves?" — ZUNZ: *Synagogue Poetry of the Middle Ages*.

Daniel Deronda had recently read this passage by Zunz. As he walked toward the Cohens' home, the words struck him with a sharp contrast. The Cohens bore no visible mark of such distinguished sorrow or spiritual aristocracy. Mr. Ezra Cohen was not draped in the sublime pathos of the martyr; indeed, his appetite for acquiring money seemed blessed with a success that history records as the most irritating trait of Jewish greed throughout the ages of their scattering. This "Jeshurun" of a pawnbroker was no living symbol of the great Jewish tragedy. Yet, Deronda wondered, was there not something profoundly typical in the fact that a life like Mordecai's—a fragile vessel of national consciousness, struggling to breathe—was nestled within the self-congratulatory ignorance of the Cohens' comfortable prosperity?

When Deronda reappeared among them, a radiant gladness illuminated their faces. Mr. Cohen himself took the opportunity to remark that while a diamond ring—or even a little longer credit limit—would have generated more profit, he cared not a sixpence for that when weighed against the sheer pleasure of seeing the women and children delighted by a young gentleman whose first visit had been so agreeable that they had spoken of nothing else since. Young Mrs. Cohen expressed regret that the baby was asleep, followed immediately by delight that Adelaide was not yet in bed, earnestly urging Deronda not to linger in the shop but to proceed directly to the parlors to meet "Mother and the children." He gladly accepted the invitation, having prepared portable gifts: a set of paper figures for Adelaide and an ivory cup-and-ball toy for Jacob.

The grandmother sat with a deck of cards, playing "plates" with the children. A plate had just been tossed and landed intact.

"Stop!" cried Jacob, darting toward Deronda as he entered. "Do not step on my plate. Watch me throw it up again."

Deronda complied, exchanging a knowing smile with the grandmother. The plate soared several times before finally shattering; only then was the visitor permitted to approach and take his seat. He noted that the door from which Mordecai had emerged on his previous visit was now shut tight, though he intended to demonstrate his interest in the Cohens before revealing his deeper concern for their unusual lodger.

It was not until he had Adelaide on his lap, arranging the paper figures in a dance on the table, while Jacob practiced with the cup and ball, that Deronda asked, "Is Mordecai in just now?"

"Where is he, Addy?" asked Cohen, seizing a brief pause in his business to peer over.

"In the workroom," replied his wife, nodding toward the closed door.

"The truth is, sir," said Cohen, "we cannot fathom what has come over him these past days or two. He is always, as I might say, 'a little touched,' you understand"—he tapped his own temple—"not entirely rational in all matters, unlike you and me; yet he has been wonderfully regular and industrious, as a poor creature can be, and takes as much delight in the boy as anyone could. But these last couple of days, he has moved about like a sleepwalker, or sat as still as a wax figure."

"It is the disease, poor dear thing," said the grandmother, tenderly. "I doubt if he can withstand it much longer."

"No; I think it is merely something troubling his mind," corrected Mrs. Cohen the younger. "He has been turning over papers constantly, and when I speak to him, it takes him a long time to hear and respond."

"You may think us somewhat weak ourselves," said Cohen, offering an apology. "But neither my wife nor my mother would part with him even if he became a greater burden. It is not that we do not understand the situation, but it is our principle. Some fools conduct business at a loss without realizing it. I am not one of those."

"Oh, Mordecai carries a blessing within him," declared the grandmother.

"He has something wrong inside him," interjected Jacob, correcting his grandmother's error. "He said he couldn't talk to me, and he wouldn't accept a single piece of bread."

"Far from wondering at your feelings for him," said Deronda, "I already share a similar sentiment. I have recently spoken with him at Ram's bookshop—and in fact, I promised to call here so that we might walk out together."

"That explains it!" exclaimed Cohen, slapping his knee. "He has been expecting you, and it has affected him. I suppose he discusses his learning with you. How very kind of you, sir; for I daresay there is little to be gained from it, otherwise it would not have left him in his present state. But here comes the shop." Cohen hurried out, and Jacob, who had been listening uncomfortably close to Deronda's elbow, addressed him with obliging familiarity: "I'll fetch Mordecai for you, if you wish."

"No, Jacob," said his mother. "Open the door for the gentleman and let him go in himself. Hush! Make no noise."

The skillful Jacob seemed to embrace the role, turning the door handle as silently as possible. Deronda followed behind him, standing on the threshold. The small room was dimly lit by a dying fire and a single candle shaded from the draft. Upon the board beneath the window lay various articles of jewelry; in the corner beyond them, books were piled haphazardly. Mordecai sat on a high chair facing the board, his back to the door, his hands clasped together and resting on the surface, with a watch propped on a stand before him. He appeared in a state of expectant agony, akin to a prisoner awaiting delayed liberation. When he heard Deronda's voice saying, "I have come for you. Are you ready?", he instantly turned without a word, grabbed his fur-lined cap lying nearby, and moved to join Deronda.

Within moments, they were both in the sitting-room. Jacob, noting the shift in his friend's demeanor and expression, seized him by the arm and shouted, "Look at my cup and ball!" launching the ball high near Mordecai's face as a gesture meant to cheer a recovering patient. It was evident that the tension in Mordecai's mind had eased, for he smiled and remarked, "Fine, fine!"

"You have forgotten your greatcoat and comforter," said young Mrs. Cohen. She returned to the workroom to retrieve them.

"He has come back to life, do you see?" whispered Cohen, who had re-entered, speaking in an undertone. "I told you so; I am mostly right." Then, in his normal voice, "Well, sir, we must not detain you further, I suppose; but I trust this is not the last time we shall see you."

"Will you come again?" asked Jacob, stepping forward. "Watch, I can catch the ball! I'll wager I can catch it without stopping if you return."

"He has remarkably dexterous hands," observed Deronda, looking at the grandmother. "From which side of the family does he inherit them?"

The grandmother merely nodded toward her son, who replied promptly, "My side. My wife's family are not skilled in that manner. But bless your soul! Ours possess a sort of cleverness as durable as gutta-percha; you can twist it whichever way you please. There is nothing some elderly gentlemen will not accomplish if you set them to it." Cohen winked down at Jacob's retreating back, though it was doubtful whether this tactful allusion achieved its purpose, for its subject emitted a nasal whinnying laugh and stamped about, singing, "Old gentlemen, old gentlemen," in a chime-like rhythm.

Deronda thought, *I shall never learn anything definitive about these people unless I ask Cohen directly whether he lost a sister named Mirah when she was six.* The moment to confront this question did not yet seem easy for him to face. Nevertheless, his initial sense of repulsion regarding the commonness of these individuals began to soften into kinder feelings. Regardless of their unrefined manners and speech, he was compelled to acknowledge a certain moral refinement in their treatment of the consumptive workman, whose intellectual distinction they perceived chiefly as a harmless, silent rambling.

"The Cohens seem to have a genuine affection for you," said Deronda once he and Mordecai were off the doorstep.

"And I for them," was the immediate reply. "They possess the heart of the Israelite within them, though they are like horses and mules, lacking understanding beyond the narrow path they tread."

"I fear I have caused you some anxiety," said Deronda, "by my delay in keeping my promise. I wished to come yesterday, but found it impossible."

"Yes—yes, I trusted you. But it is true I felt uneasy, for the spirit of my youth has been stirred within me, and this body is not strong enough to bear the beating of its wings. I am as a man bound and imprisoned for many years: behold him brought to speech with his fellow and his limbs set free: he weeps, he totters, the joy within him threatens to break and overturn the tabernacle of flesh."
