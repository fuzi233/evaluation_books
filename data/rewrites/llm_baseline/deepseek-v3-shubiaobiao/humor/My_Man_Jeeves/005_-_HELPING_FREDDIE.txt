I don’t mean to bore you with all the usual rot, but I simply must tell you about poor old Freddie Meadowes. Now, I’m no literary genius—I leave that to the chaps who get paid for it—but I’ll do my best, and you can always imagine some polished scribbler tidying it up later.

Freddie’s been a dear old pal of mine since the days when we used to pinch each other’s sweets at school. So you can imagine my shock when I walked into the club one morning and found him slumped in a corner, staring into space like the last surviving guest at a particularly dismal party. Normally, Freddie’s the life and soul of any gathering—a real spark, always good for a laugh. But there he was, looking as if he’d just been informed that the last bottle of champagne in the world had gone flat.

Jimmy Pinkerton—a frightfully brainy chap who writes plays—was with me at the time, and between us, we pried the truth out of Freddie. It was, of course, a girl. Angela West, the poor blighter’s fiancée, had handed him his hat after some mysterious row. Wouldn’t see him, wouldn’t speak to him, wouldn’t even open his letters. A dashed unpleasant situation.

Now, I knew exactly how Freddie felt. I’d once been hopelessly gone on a girl named Elizabeth Shoolbred, who wouldn’t have looked at me if I’d been the last man on earth—a tragedy I’ll cover in my memoirs if I ever get round to writing them. So I prescribed the only cure I knew.

“What you need, old bean,” I said, “is a change of scenery. Come down to Marvis Bay with me. I’ve rented a cottage. Jimmy’s joining us on the twenty-fourth. It’ll be a jolly little party.”

Jimmy nodded sagely. “Change of scene’s the ticket. Knew a fellow once—girl chucked him, so he went abroad. Two months later, she wired, *‘Come back. Muriel.’* Chap sat down to reply, then realized he’d forgotten her surname. Never answered.”

Freddie, however, remained inconsolable. He just sat there looking like a man who’d swallowed a bad oyster. Still, I managed to drag a promise out of him to come to Marvis Bay. He said it didn’t matter where he was, so he might as well be miserable by the seaside.

Marvis Bay, in Dorsetshire, isn’t exactly a hotspot of excitement. You bathe, you lounge on the sand, and in the evening, you take a stroll along the shore with the local gnats, who treat you like a mobile banquet. By nine o’clock, you’re slapping ointment on your wounds and collapsing into bed.

Oddly enough, the place seemed to suit Freddie. Once the moon was up, you couldn’t drag him off that beach with a team of wild horses. The gnats adored him—they’d queue up for a chance to feast on him, ignoring perfectly good passersby just to get a taste of Freddie.

It was all very peaceful, but by the end of the first week, I was wishing Jimmy Pinkerton had arranged to come down earlier. Freddie, bless him, wasn’t exactly lively company. When he wasn’t chewing his pipe and scowling at the carpet, he was hunched over the piano, massacring *“The Rosary”* with one finger. He never got past the third bar before something went wrong, and he’d start all over again.

One morning, as I returned from a swim, he was at it again.

“Reggie,” he said hollowly, looking up, “I’ve seen her.”

“Seen her? Miss West?”

“At the post office. We met in the doorway. She cut me dead.”

He attacked *“The Rosary”* again, stumbling in the second bar.

“Reggie, you never should have brought me here. I must leave.”

“Leave? Don’t be absurd. This is the best thing that could’ve happened. Now’s your chance to show some backbone.”

“She *cut* me.”

“Never mind that. Be a sportsman. Try again.”

“She looked straight through me!”

“Of course she did. But leave it to me. I’ll handle this. What you need,” I said, “is to put her under some obligation to you. Make her grateful. What you want—”

“But *what* is she going to be grateful for?”

I pondered.

“Save her from drowning,” I suggested.

“I can’t swim,” said Freddie.

Typical. A splendid chap in many ways, but utterly useless in a crisis.

He went back to torturing the piano, and I made a strategic retreat to the beach.

As I wandered along the shore, turning the problem over in my mind, I spotted a blue dress in the distance. By Jove, it was *her*—Angela West. I’d never met her, but Freddie’s bedroom was practically wallpapered with her photographs, so I recognized her at once. She was sitting on the sand, helping a small, round child build a castle. An elderly lady—presumably an aunt—was reading nearby.

The child soon grew bored and started whimpering. Angela took him off to a sweets stall, and I continued my walk.

Now, I’ll admit, I’m not what you’d call a mental giant. The Peppers have never been famed for their intellect. But every now and then, I get a dashed clever idea—and one struck me then. I doubt even the brainiest chap you could name would’ve thought of it.

On my way back, I saw the child again, this time alone, poking a jellyfish with a spade. No Angela in sight. And then—*inspiration*. If I *borrowed* the child for a bit, and then Freddie “found” him wandering lost and returned him heroically, Angela’s gratitude might just melt her resolve.

So I scooped up the child and made for home, already picturing the touching reunion.

Freddie, however, was slow to grasp the brilliance of the scheme. When I marched in with the kid, he didn’t exactly cheer. The child was howling by then, and Freddie looked as if he’d prefer to be swallowed by the nearest whale.

“Stop it!” he snapped. “Do you think you’re the only one with troubles? What the devil is this, Reggie?”

I fetched honey to quiet the child, then explained my plan. Gradually, Freddie saw the light.

“You’re not *always* a complete idiot, Reggie,” he conceded. “This might work.”

He took the child to “find” Angela on the beach.

I was feeling frightfully pleased with myself—until Freddie returned, looking like a man who’d just been told his entire family had emigrated to Australia. The child was still with him.

“Couldn’t you find her?” I asked.

“Oh, I found her,” he said bitterly.

“Well then—?”

“This isn’t her cousin, you *imbecile*. She’d never seen the child before in her life.”

“*What?* Then whose is he?”

“How should I know? But congratulations—you’re now a kidnapper. I hope you enjoy prison.”

It took him a while to tell the full story, what with all the insults he kept throwing in. Angela had listened to Freddie’s heroic tale of rescuing the lost child, then given him a look that could have frozen the Atlantic. He’d slunk away, thoroughly crushed.

Now it was *my* problem. I spent hours combing Marvis Bay for the child’s parents, with no success. Eventually, the sweet-stall man informed me that the boy was named Tootles Medwin and lived at *Ocean Rest* in Beach Road.

I raced there, knocked, and got no answer. Then a whiskered face appeared at an upper window.

“Hi!” it shouted.

“What do you mean, *‘Hi’*?” I said.

“You can’t come in. Mumps quarantine. Hello, Tootles!”

After some confused shouting, the man—Mr. Medwin—explained that his daughter had mumps, and Tootles couldn’t risk infection. He insisted I take the child until his brother arrived from London to fetch him—which *might* be within a week.

Before I could protest, a package of breakfast cereal hit me on the head, and the window slammed shut. Defeated, I led Tootles away.

On the way back, we met Angela.

“Wah!” said Tootles, pointing.

She smiled. “So father found you again?”

*Father?!* Before I could correct her, she was gone.

Freddie, when informed, had a small nervous breakdown.

“We can’t spend our lives as nursemaids!” he wailed.

But we had no choice. The next few days were a blur of sweets, tantrums, and desperate improvisation.

Then Jimmy Pinkerton arrived, took one look at the chaos, and declared it perfect material for a farce.

“Parted lovers reunited by a lisping child!” he said. “It’s gold! All we need is for the kid to say something like *‘Kiss Freddie!’* and the girl will melt.”

The only problem? Getting Angela within earshot.

Fate, however, took care of that. The very next day, as I was leading Tootles outside, Angela appeared. Before I could stop her, she was on the veranda, cooing over the child—while Freddie obliviously massacred *“The Rosary”* inside.

“Your piano’s being tuned?” she asked. “Could your man come to us next?”

“Er—not yet!” I said. “Artistic temperament, you know.”

Just then, the music stopped.

“He’ll be out in a moment,” she said, settling in to wait.

I was weighing the merits of bolting versus feigning sudden illness when Tootles, bless him, seized his moment.

“Kiss Freddie!” he piped up.

Jimmy’s rehearsals had paid off.

Angela blinked. Freddie, emerging from the house, froze.

And then—well, you can guess the rest.

Somehow, against all odds, it *worked*.
