It was a night in May, and atop a grassy knoll overlooking the Nile near Wadi Halfa, three officers sat in silence, smoke curling from their cigars against the backdrop of a moon so bright it had drained the stars of their light. The sky above was a pale, luminous curve, washing away the darkness, while below, the sluggish waters of the river shimmered like burnished silver. To the south, the desert stretched out to infinity, a vast, white plain dotted with hummocks that glinted like jewels in the moonlight. Behind them, the shadow of the mess-room's verandah cut a solid block of blackness into the earth.

"One hopes nothing terrible has happened to him," murmured the speaker, a man of middle age who commanded a Sudanese battalion. He tossed the spent match aside, his expression heavy with worry. "Though I pray I could believe otherwise."

The colonel addressed was Colonel Dawson. He was answered by a man whose hair had prematurely turned gray—a common affliction in the Sudan—but whose unlined face betrayed a youth untouched by cynicism. This was Lieutenant Calder of the Engineers. In this instance, however, youth offered no optimism to challenge Colonel Dawson's gloom.

"He departed Halfa eight weeks ago, did he not?" Calder asked, his voice low.

"Exactly eight weeks today," Dawson replied.

A third figure, a tall, slender Major Walters of the Army Service Corps, offered a cheerful counterpoint. "It is far too early to declare Captain Durrance lost at sea. One knows Durrance. Give him a campfire and a circle of sheikhs in the desert, and he would dance around them for a month without a hint of boredom. Here, he has letters, an office, a desk—he hates them all. You will see him turn up eventually, though he won't rush."

"He is three weeks overdue," Dawson objected, tugging nervously at his moustache. "He is methodical, in his own way. I am afraid."

Major Walters gestured toward the white expanse of the desert across the river. "If he had traveled west, I might concede your fears. But Durrance headed east, through the mountainous country toward Berenice and the Red Sea. The tribes he visited were quiet, even in Osman Digna's darkest days."

Yet Dawson found no solace in Walters's confidence. The conversation shifted as Lieutenant Calder knocked the ash from his pipe and leaned forward, pressing tobacco down with his thumb.

"I wonder," Calder said slowly. "Is it possible a trap was set for Durrance? I didn't mention this before because I didn't suspect a connection to his delay until now. But I am beginning to doubt. Do you recall the night before he left?"

"Yes," Dawson said, pulling his chair closer. "Calder, you are the only one here who shared an intimacy with him."

"I remember that night perfectly," Walters interjected. "Durrance dined at the mess and retired early to prepare for his journey."

"His preparations were complete," Calder corrected. "He did not return to his quarters. Instead, he walked along the riverbank down to Tewfikieh."

Tewfikieh was a frontier town separated from Halfa by a mile of sandy shore. It was a chaotic place where Greek storekeepers rubbed shoulders with dirty cafes, native cooks and tobacconists, and where the dialects of the Dinka and the Delta clashed in a perpetual roar. Yet, beneath the noise, there lay a deep, brooding silence—the silence of deserts and the East.

"Durrance arrived there at ten o'clock," Calder continued, his voice dropping. "I went to his quarters at eleven. He was not back. He was scheduled to depart eastward at four in the morning, and I needed to speak with him regarding a matter of business. I waited. He entered about fifteen minutes later, speaking quickly and restlessly. He seemed agitated, laboring under some unusual emotion. At one moment, it looked like anger; the next, he broke into a laugh, as if glad against his will. He dismissed me abruptly, instructing his servant to retire, claiming he expected a visitor whom he would admit personally."

"And who was this visitor?" Dawson asked.

"I do not know," Calder answered. "But when his servant called for him at four, Durrance was still sitting on the verandah, awaiting someone who never came. He left no message."

"And did he explain?"

"No. I was awake when he saddled his camel. He paused, looking down at me, but then thought better of speaking. He struck the camel's flank and rode slowly past the post office, his head sunk upon his breast, disappearing into the desert. I wonder if he rode into a trap. What business required such secrecy? And why did the visitor fail to appear?"

A silence fell over the group, broken only by Major Walters's renewed, unaltered cheer. "I do not think Durrance has met his doom," he declared, rising from his chair.

"I know what I will do," Dawson said firmly. "In the morning, I will dispatch a strong search party."

The next morning, over breakfast on the verandah, Dawson outlined the details of the expedition. Even Major Walters, usually optimistic, appeared to have pondered Calder's story during the night. He leaned across the table to Calder.

"Did you never ask who Durrance spoke to in Tewfikieh that night?"

"I did," Calder replied, turning his face toward the glass doors of the mess-room. "And there is a puzzle there. He spoke to only one person, and it was highly unlikely to be the visitor he expected. He stopped before a café where some wandering musicians were playing for their nightly lodging. One of them, a Greek, stepped out and accepted a sovereign from Durrance. They conversed briefly."

As Calder spoke, a look of sudden recognition flashed across his face. He gripped the edge of the table, leaning forward as if ready to spring up, but then froze. The recognition morphed into bewilderment. He glanced around; Colonel Dawson was pouring cocoa, and Walters stared at his plate. None of the other officers noticed Calder's abrupt shift. Leaning back, he stared intently ahead.

"But I could never hear him speak to anyone else," Calder continued, his voice tight. "Except for that one encounter, he merely strolled through the village and returned."

"That offers us little aid," Walters noted.

"And it is all you know?" Dawson asked.

"No, not quite," Calder returned slowly. "I know, for instance, that the man I am describing is staring straight at me."

Every eye at the table swiveled toward the doorway.

"Durrance!" cried the Colonel, leaping to his feet.

"When did you return?" Major Walters demanded.

Standing in the doorway, clad in travel-stained garments with a face burned the color of red brick, was Durrance. He listened with remarkable intentness to the voices within. Notably, Calder, his friend, did not rise or offer a greeting. He remained seated, watching Durrance with a mixture of curiosity and perplexity. As Durrance descended the steps onto the verandah, a look of troubled comprehension settled over his features.

"We expected you three weeks ago," Dawson said, pulling out a chair.

"The delay was unavoidable," Durrance replied, drawing the chair to the table.

"Does my tale account for it?" Calder asked.

"Not a bit," Durrance laughed softly. "It was the Greek musician I awaited that night. I was merely curious to know what stroke of ill fortune had driven him to play the zither in a café for a night's lodging. That was all." He lowered his voice. "Yes, that was all."

"Meanwhile, you neglect your breakfast," Dawson chided, rising. "What will you have?"

Calder leaned slightly forward, his eyes resting quietly on Durrance. Durrance looked around and signaled for the waiter. "Moussa, bring me something cold," he ordered. As the waiter retreated, Calder nodded with a faint smile, sensing a difficulty cleverly surmounted.

"There is tea, cocoa, and coffee," Calder offered.

"Thanks," Durrance said. "But I think I shall have a brandy-and-soda." Again, Calder nodded.

Durrance ate, drank, and spoke of his journey. He had traveled farther east than intended, finding the Ababdeh Arabs quiet among their mountains. The weather had been fine, and game plentiful. On the surface, he seemed content. Calder, however, sat and watched him, disbelieving every word. The others attended to their duties, but Calder remained, waiting for Durrance to finish. Yet, Durrance never did. He loitered over his meal, then pushed his plate away, continuing to talk. There was no limit to his enthusiasm regarding his travels, nor to his inquiries about the eight weeks he had missed.

Finally, with remarkable abruptness, he stopped and looked at his companion with suspicion. "You are taking life easily this morning."

"I have not eight weeks of arrears of letters to clear off, as you have, Colonel," Calder returned with a laugh. He saw Durrance's face darken and his forehead contract.

"True," Durrance said after a pause. "I had forgotten my letters." He rose, climbed the steps, and entered the mess-room.
