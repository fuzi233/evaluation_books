"I shall keep my private thoughts to myself, sir," I said respectfully, "and request you to proceed."

"Don't fidget, Betteredge," Mr. Franklin replied with a slight smile, and continued.

My young master's opening remarks informed me that his startling discoveries regarding the wicked Colonel and the Diamond had originated with a visit he had paid, prior to coming to us, to the family lawyer at Hampstead. A casual remark dropped by Mr. Franklin, when he and the lawyer were alone one afternoon after dinner, revealed that he had been entrusted by his father with a birthday present to deliver to Miss Rachel. One thing led to another, culminating in the lawyer disclosing the true nature of the gift and the origin of the friendly connection between the late Colonel and Mr. Blake, senior. The facts are so extraordinary that I fear my own language cannot do them justice. Therefore, I prefer to report Mr. Franklin's findings as faithfully as possible, using his own words.

"You remember the time, Betteredge," he began, "when my father was striving to prove his claim to that unfortunate Dukedom? Well, that was precisely the era when my uncle Herncastle returned from India. My father discovered that his brother-in-law held certain documents vital to his lawsuit. He called upon the Colonel under the pretext of welcoming him home. But the Colonel was not easily deceived. 'You want something,' he said, 'or you would never compromise your reputation by visiting *me*.' My father realized his only option was to reveal his hand immediately; he admitted at once that he sought the papers. The Colonel asked for a day to deliberate. His reply arrived in the form of a most extraordinary letter, which my friend the lawyer showed me.

"The Colonel commenced by stating that he desired something from my father and proposed an exchange of friendly services. By the fortunes of war—an expression he used—he had come into possession of one of the largest diamonds in the world. He believed that neither he nor his precious jewel was safe in any house, in any corner of the globe, where they resided together. Under these alarming circumstances, he had resolved to entrust his Diamond to another party. This person was not expected to incur any risk. They might deposit the stone in any specially guarded location, akin to a banker's or jeweller's strongroom, intended for the secure custody of high-value items. His sole personal responsibility would be passive: he was to undertake, personally or via a trusted agent, to receive at a prearranged address, on specific dates annually, a note from the Colonel merely certifying that he was still alive. Should the date pass without receipt of this note, the Colonel's silence would be deemed a certain token of his death by murder. In that event, and in no other, certain sealed instructions regarding the disposition of the Diamond, kept with it, were to be opened and followed strictly. If my father accepted this strange charge, the Colonel's papers were to be delivered to him in return. That was the letter."

"What did your father do, sir?" I asked.

"Do?" Mr. Franklin exclaimed. "I'll tell you. He applied the invaluable faculty known as common sense to the Colonel's proposal. He declared the entire arrangement absurd. Somewhere during his Indian wanderings, the Colonel had stumbled upon some wretched crystal he mistook for a diamond. As for the danger of assassination and the elaborate precautions designed to preserve his life and his piece of glass, this was the nineteenth century; any sane man would simply apply to the police. The Colonel had been a notorious opium-eater for years. Thus, if obtaining the valuable papers required accepting a matter of opium as fact, my father was entirely willing to assume the ridiculous responsibility imposed upon him—all the more readily since it entailed no trouble whatsoever. The Diamond and the sealed instructions were deposited in his banker's strongroom, while the Colonel's periodic letters confirming his survival were received and opened by our family lawyer, Mr. Bruff, acting as my father's representative. No sensible person in a similar position could have viewed the matter differently. Nothing in this world, Betteredge, is probable unless it aligns with our own trivial experience; we only believe in romance when we encounter it in a newspaper."

"It was evident to me that Mr. Franklin considered his father's judgment regarding the Colonel to have been hasty and incorrect."

"What is your own private opinion on the matter, sir?" I inquired.

"Let us first finish the story of the Colonel," Mr. Franklin said. "There is a peculiar lack of systematic thinking in the English mind; your question, my old friend, is a prime example. When we are not engaged in constructing machinery, we are, mentally speaking, the most slovenly people in the universe."

"So much," I thought to myself, "for a foreign education! He has surely learned that habit of girding at us from France."

Mr. Franklin resumed the narrative.

"My father obtained the papers he desired and never saw his brother-in-law again. For years, on the prearranged days, the prearranged letter arrived from the Colonel and was opened by Mr. Bruff. I have seen the pile of them, all written in the same brief, business-like style: 'Sir,—This is to certify that I am still a living man. Let the Diamond be. John Herncastle.' That was all he ever wrote, arriving punctually to the day, until some six or eight months ago, when the format changed for the first time. It now read: 'Sir,—They tell me I am dying. Come to me, and help me to make my will.' Mr. Bruff visited him and found him in his small suburban villa, surrounded by grounds where he had lived alone since leaving India. He had dogs, cats, and birds for company, but no human beings nearby save the daily housekeeper and the doctor at his bedside. The will was remarkably simple. The Colonel had squandered the bulk of his fortune on chemical experiments. His will consisted of three clauses dictated from his bed while fully lucid. The first provided for the care and support of his animals. The second established a professorship of experimental chemistry at a northern university. The third bequeathed the Moonstone as a birthday present to his niece, on the condition that my father serve as executor. My father initially refused the role. However, upon reconsideration, he agreed, partly because he was assured the executorship would involve no trouble, and partly because Mr. Bruff suggested, for Rachel's sake, that the Diamond might indeed be worth something after all."

"Did the Colonel provide any reason, sir," I asked, "for leaving the Diamond to Miss Rachel?"

"He not only gave the reason; he had it written in his will," Mr. Franklin stated. "I possess an extract which you shall see shortly. Do not be slovenly-minded, Betteredge! One thing at a time. You have heard of the Colonel's Will; now listen to what occurred following his death. Formally, the Diamond had to be valued before the Will could be proven. Every jeweler consulted immediately confirmed the Colonel's assertion that he held one of the largest diamonds in the world. Valuing it accurately presented serious difficulties. Its size made it a phenomenon in the market; its color placed it in a unique category; furthermore, there existed a defect—a flaw—in the very heart of the stone. Yet, even with this severe drawback, the lowest estimate given was twenty thousand pounds. Imagine my father's astonishment! He had been on the verge of refusing the executorship and allowing this magnificent jewel to escape the family. His renewed interest prompted him to open the sealed instructions deposited with the Diamond. Mr. Bruff showed this document to me along with the other papers, and it suggests (in my opinion) a clue to the nature of the conspiracy that threatened the Colonel's life."

"Then you do believe, sir," I said, "that there was a conspiracy?"

"Not possessing my father's excellent common sense," Mr. Franklin answered, "I believe the Colonel's life was threatened exactly as he claimed. The sealed instructions, I think, explain how he ultimately died quietly in his bed. In the event of his violent death—that is, the absence of the regular letter on the appointed date—my father was directed to send the Moonstone secretly to Amsterdam. It was to be deposited with a renowned diamond cutter, sliced into four to six separate stones. These stones were then to be sold, and the proceeds applied to funding the professorship mentioned in the Will. Now, Betteredge, exert those sharp wits of yours and observe the conclusion to which the Colonel's instructions point!"

I instantly strained my wits. Being of the slovenly English sort, I muddled the situation until Mr. Franklin intervened to clarify.

"Observe," Mr. Franklin noted, "that the integrity of the Diamond as a whole stone is here artfully made dependent on the preservation of the Colonel's life from violence. He is not content merely to tell his enemies, 'Kill me, and you will remain no closer to the Diamond than you are now; it sits safely in a bank strongroom.' Instead, he declares, 'Kill me, and the Diamond will cease to be a Diamond; its identity will be destroyed.' What does that signify?"

At this, I experienced (so I thought) a flash of brilliant foreign insight.

"I understand," I said. "It means diminishing the value of the stone and cheating the villains thereby!"

"Absolutely not," Mr. Franklin corrected. "I have investigated this. A flawed diamond cut into pieces would actually fetch more than the intact stone. Four to six perfect brilliants cut from it would collectively be worth more than the large but imperfect single stone.
