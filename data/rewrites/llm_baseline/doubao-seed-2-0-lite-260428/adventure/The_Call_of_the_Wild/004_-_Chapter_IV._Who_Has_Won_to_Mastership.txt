“Eh? What’d I tell you? I spoke true when I said Buck’s not one devil, he’s two.”

That was François’s bark of a line first thing the next morning, when he stumbled out of his furs to find Spitz vanished entirely, and Buck crisscrossed with raw, gaping wounds across his pelt. He hauled Buck straight to the campfire, the dancing flames gilding every tear and cut on the dog’s body.
“That Spitz fought like a cornered wolverine,” Perrault agreed, prodding at one of the rips with a gloved finger.
“An’ dat Buck fought like two of ’em,” François shot back, grinning. “We’ll make great time now. No more Spitz, no more brawling mess, sure as snow’s cold.”

While Perrault packed up camp and lashed their overstuffed supplies to the sled, François moved to harness the team. Buck trotted straight to the lead spot—right where Spitz had claimed for months, the position he’d won fair and square in the bloodied snow. But François wasn’t paying attention. He grabbed Sol-leks, the tough one-eyed old husky, and nudged him into that coveted front position. He thought Sol-leks was the strongest remaining lead dog.

Buck exploded. He launched at Sol-leks in a red fury, drove the old dog back with a snarl that rumbled deep in his chest, and planted his paws dead center in the lead traces.
“Eh? Look at dat Buck!” François cackled, slapping his thighs so hard his gloves cracked. “He killed Spitz, now he thinks he gets the job!”
“Go on, git!” he yelled, shooing Buck away. Buck didn’t budge. François grabbed him by the scruff, and though Buck snarled at him with bared fangs, he dragged him back behind Dave and shoved Sol-leks back to the lead. Sol-leks didn’t fight it. He was already plainly terrified of what Buck’s jaws could do.

The second François turned his back, Buck shoved Sol-leks aside again. Sol-leks didn’t even pretend to resist. François’s temper flared.
“By God, I’ll fix you!” he roared, stomping off to grab a heavy, thick oak club.

Buck remembered the man in the red sweater. He didn’t lunge, didn’t run off into the woods. He retreated slowly, circling just out of range of the club, hackles raised, eyes locked on the wood so he could dodge the second it flew. When François called him to his old spot behind Dave, Buck stepped back two paces. François stepped forward. Buck stepped back. The driver thought he was scared of a beating—but that was not what Buck wanted at all. He was in open revolt. He didn’t care about avoiding a thrashing. He wanted the leadership. It was his by right, earned in a fight to the death. He would not settle for less.

Perrault jumped in to help. For the better part of an hour they chased Buck around camp, hurling clubs, cursing him, his parents, every generation of his ancestors, and every last hair in his pelt. Buck answered every curse with a low snarl, stayed just out of reach, and never once tried to run away. He was making it plain: give me the lead, and I’ll be good.

François scratched his head. Perrault checked his watch and swore. They were already an hour late to hit the trail. François shrugged, and the courier nodded to signal they were beaten. He walked over to unclip Sol-leks’s traces, then called Buck. Buck’s doggy, triumphant grin spread across his face, but he stayed out of range until Perrault ordered François to drop the club. The second the wood hit the snow, Buck trotted right over, swung around into the lead spot like he’d been born to stand there, and let them snap his traces into place. The sled lurched out onto the frozen river trail, both men running behind to get up speed.

François had already thought Buck was a miracle of a dog, but he realized before midday he’d still underestimated him. Buck took to leadership like he’d been doing it his whole life. Where quick judgment and split-second decisions were needed to navigate thin ice and hidden drifts, he outperformed even Spitz, a dog François had once thought was unbeatable. But where he truly excelled was laying down the law for the rest of the team.

Dave and Sol-leks didn’t care who was lead. Their only job was to pull till their paws bled, and as long as that work wasn’t interfered with, they were happy. But the rest of the team had grown lazy and unruly in Spitz’s last fading days, and Buck wasted no time whipping them back into shape. Pike, the loafer who never pulled an ounce more than he absolutely had to, was shaken by the scruff over and over until by the end of that first day he was pulling harder than he ever had in his whole life. Joe, the sour, snappy dog Spitz had never managed to break, was pinned under Buck’s superior weight, nipped repeatedly, until he stopped snarling and whined for mercy. The team snapped back into perfect solidarity instantly, leaping through the traces as one single animal.

When they picked up two wild native huskies named Teek and Koona at Rink Rapids, Buck broke them in to team order so fast François could barely believe his eyes. “Never no dog like Buck!” he yelled to Perrault. “Never! He’s worth a thousand dollars, by God!”

Perrault nodded. They were shattering records, day after day. The trail was packed hard and smooth, no new snow to fight, and the temperature held steady at 50 below zero. The men rode and ran by turn, the dogs kept at a steady lope, with barely any stops. They crossed the Thirty Mile River in one day—a route that had taken them ten days to trek when they’d first arrived. They ran a 60-mile straight dash from the foot of Lake Le Barge to White Horse Rapids, and flew across 70 miles of frozen lakes so fast the man whose turn it was to run ahead ended up towed behind the sled on a rope. On the second week’s final night, they crested White Pass and looked down to see the glowing lights of Skaguay and its anchored ships spread out below them.

It was a record run: 40 miles a day, for fourteen days straight. For three days Perrault and François hauled their supplies up and down Skaguay’s main street, drowning in invitations to drinks, while the team drew crowds of adoring dog mushers and gold seekers. Then new official orders arrived. François called Buck over, threw his arms around him, and wept. That was the last Buck ever saw of Perrault and François. The two men vanished from his life for good.

A gruff Scotch half-breed took charge of Buck and his team, and joined up with a dozen other dog sleds to head back over the long, weary trail to Dawson. No more record runs now. This was the mail train, hauling thousands of pounds of letters out to the men hunting gold under the shadow of the Arctic Pole. It was slow, brutal, heavy toil every single day, no shortcuts, no easy patches.

Buck didn’t love the work, but he bore it well, taking pride in the toil the same way Dave and Sol-leks did, and cracking down hard to make sure every one of his teammates pulled their fair share. Life ran like a machine: wake an hour before dawn, build the fires, eat breakfast, harness the dogs, and hit the trail before the first sliver of sun peeked over the horizon. At night they’d make camp, chop wood, lay pine boughs for beds, and feed the dogs first before the men even thought about their own dinner. There were 60 other sled dogs total, plenty of fierce brawlers, but three quick fights with the meanest of them landed Buck as undisputed top dog, and no one dared challenge him again.

His favorite time was after dinner, curled up by the fire, hind legs tucked under him, front legs stretched out, blinking dreamily at the flames. Sometimes he thought of Judge Miller’s big sunlit house in Santa Clara, the cement swimming pool, the silly little Mexican hairless and Japanese pug that used to follow him around the yard. But those memories were faint, far away, no hold on him at all. Far more real were the ancient memories buried deep in his bones, the instincts passed down from a hundred generations of wild ancestors. Sometimes, staring at the flames, he swore he was looking at a different fire, in front of a different man: short-legged, long-armed, knotted stringy muscles, matted long hair, clutching a stone-tipped club, crouched low in perpetual fear of the dark. He could hear the crash of large predators moving through the trees beyond the firelight, see their two-by-two glowing eyes, and the hair would stand straight up along his spine until he whimpered softly, half-asleep, and the half-breed cook would yell, “Hey Buck, wake up!” The vision would vanish, and he’d stretch and yawn, as if he’d only been napping.

The trip was brutal. They’d traveled 1800 miles that winter, dragging sleds the whole way, and even the toughest dogs wore down to skin and bone. When they stumbled into Dawson, they were gaunt, sore, and in desperate need of a week’s rest. But two days later they were loaded up with outgoing mail, heading back down the Yukon, and it snowed every single day. Soft new snow meant far more drag on the sled runners, twice the work for the already exhausted dogs. The drivers were fair: they tended to every dog’s sore feet before they even thought about taking off their own boots, fed the dogs before they ate their own meals. But the dogs’ strength drained anyway. Billee whimpered constantly in his sleep. Joe was sourer than he’d ever been. Sol-leks wouldn’t let anyone near his blind side.

But Dave suffered the worst. Something had gone irreparably wrong inside him. He grew sullen and irritable, crawled straight into his nest the second camp was pitched, and wouldn’t get up until harness time the next morning. Sometimes out on the trail, he’d cry out in agony when the sled jerked to a sudden stop, or when he strained to pull it out of a drift. All the drivers prodded and pressed him, but they couldn’t find any broken bones, couldn’t figure out what was wrong. By the time they reached Cassiar Bar, he was so weak he kept collapsing in the traces.

The Scotch half-breed called a halt, pulled Dave out of the team, and clipped Sol-leks into his old spot. He planned to let Dave run free behind the sled to rest. But Dave could not bear to be separated from his work. The pride of the traces was all he had, even if he was dying. He snarled and snapped when they unclipped his harness, whimpered broken-heartedly when he saw Sol-leks in the spot he’d served for years. When the sled started moving, he floundered through the soft deep snow beside the trail, biting at Sol-leks’s heels, shoving at him to force him out of the traces, crying with grief and pain. The half-breed cracked his whip at him, but Dave ignored the stinging lash, and the man didn’t have the heart to hit him hard. Dave ran himself until he collapsed in the snow, howling, then staggered back after the sled when it stopped, bit clean through both of Sol-leks’s traces, and planted himself dead in his old lead spot, eyes pleading.

The drivers talked it over. They’d all seen dogs die before when you took their work away from them, their hearts broken by the idleness. It was a mercy, they agreed, to let Dave die in the traces, doing the work he loved, happy and content. So they harnessed him back in. Dave pulled proudly, just like old times, even when the pain made him cry out involuntarily, even when he fell and got dragged a few steps behind the sled. They made camp that night, and by morning Dave was too weak to stand. He crawled slowly toward the harness line the dogs were lining up at, dragging himself inch by inch on his forelegs, until his strength gave out. The last the other dogs saw of him, he was lying in the snow gasping, yearning after them. They could hear his mournful howls long after they rounded a bend behind a stand of thick river pines.

The whole sled train halted. The Scotch half-breed walked slowly back toward the camp they’d just left. The men fell quiet. A single revolver shot rang out through the trees. The man came hurrying back, climbed onto his sled, and snapped the whip. The sleds rumbled forward, bells tinkling.

Buck knew. Every single dog on the team knew exactly what had happened behind those pines. No one said a word.
