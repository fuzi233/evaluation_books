"Three weeks past the Feast of St. John," Tito noted, his stride light and unhurried as he navigated the sun-drenched thoroughfares toward the Via de'Bardi. The weight of his florins, recently transferred to Cennini, rested easy in his mind; he carried no shadow of anxiety, for his spirit was too buoyant to harbor dread. As he stepped from the glare into the cool recess of a narrow alley, removing his black *berretta* to toss his brown curls back against the breeze, his countenance remained unreadable—not deceitful, yet not entirely candid either. It was simply the honest, smooth profile of a young man unburdened by pretense. His gaze, drifting lazily across the upper windows of the surrounding dwellings, possessed a pellucid clarity, devoid of guile or naive simplicity alike. Whether his presence drew others in or pushed them away depended entirely on the observer's disposition, though the prevailing sentiment was one of admiration for his striking, negative void of vanity.

By now, the fervor of the *Festa* had spilled even into the tightest side streets. The massive crowds, previously funneling through the procession routes, now fractured into chaotic streams chasing fleeting diversions. These interludes were perilous moments for the herd mentality, where the collective animal spirits grew petulant and prone to sacrificing the solitary individual for the group's amusement. Entering the district of San Martino, Tito encountered a thickening throng. Near the *Bertucce*, or Baboon, inn, a knot of spectators formed around a specific disturbance. Withdrawing from the main flow required no grand cause, yet Tito hesitated before turning aside when a high-pitched, distressed cry pierced the laughter: "Loose me! Holy Virgin, help me!"

Driven by instinct, he surged into the cluster. The source emerged instantly: a young peasant girl, her white hood discarded in the struggle, fighting to escape the grip of a *cerretano*—a conjurer dressed in garish, multi-colored attire. The performer attempted to soothe her with practiced charm, earning the amused sympathy of the onlookers who mocked the girl with a vocabulary of Florentine synonyms for 'simpleton.' Initially, Tito saw only the back of her head, adorned with light-brown braids pinned by a silver filigree. But the struggle twisted her face toward him, revealing Tessa. Her infantile features were flushed with tears, her lower lip trembling violently. Yet, in the mist of her distress, a spark of recognition ignited. Like a child suddenly recognizing a familiar hand amidst strangers, Tessa's eyes locked onto Tito's, and a fragile hope bloomed within them.

Without hesitation, Tito breached the circle of indifferent bystanders. Seizing Tessa's waist, he demanded, "Release this child! By what right do you detain her against her will?"

The conjurer, a man with facial angles—eyebrows, nose, jaw—all tilting upward in a perpetual sneer, grinned impishly. He released the girl's hands, stretching his arms back in a theatrical shrug. "I harbor no ill intent, Messere. Ask this respectable assembly. I sought merely to demonstrate my artistry, aided by this damsel's kittenish visage, promising her a handful of confetti as recompense. But surely, Messere, you possess superior confections at hand?"

A ripple of laughter erupted among the crowd, fueled by the visible relief in Tessa's posture as she clutched Tito's arm. She regarded the jeers with the indifference of prey fleeing predators, caring only for the noise of the beasts' retreat. Tito, however, sensed the precariousness of the situation. Having secured her on his arm, he moved swiftly to distance them from spectators deprived of their entertainment, eager to restore equilibrium before further mockery ensued.

"Behold, little one! Your hood," the conjurer called out, flinging the white drapery over Tessa's head. "*Orsù*, bear me no malice; return when Messere permits."

"Aha! Maestro Vaiano, she'll return shortly, just as the toad returned to the harrow," a spectator quipped, noting Tessa's recoil.

Vexed by the delay, Tito propelled himself toward a side street, intending to shed the burden of the peasant girl. Yet, the next thoroughfare offered no respite; holiday-goers continued to remark on the unusual pairing. Before they could proceed, Tito adopted a brisk, paternal tone. "Where were you bound, little one? Did you arrive alone for the *Festa*?"

"No!" Tessa whispered, her fear returning. "I've lost my mother... and my father-in-law. They will be furious—he will strike me. In the crowd at San Pulinari, someone shoved me, and I couldn't stop. I escaped them. Oh, I don't know where they've gone! Please, don't abandon me!"

Fresh tears welled, culminating in a sob. Tito accelerated his pace. The Church of the Badia loomed nearby; they could seek refuge in its cloister. Though the church doors were closed, the covered arcade provided temporary shelter. "Do you have kin in Florence, my little Tessa, where you might seek asylum? Or are you paralyzed by fear following that conjurer's trick?" he asked, glancing ahead. "I am pressed for time to reach Oltrarno, but if I can escort you partway—"

"I am terrified," she choked out. "He was the Devil—I knew it. I have nowhere to go. My mother intended to dine elsewhere, and I know not where. Holy Madonna! I shall be beaten."

Her mouth curled downward in piteous despair, her small bosom, marked by rosary beads atop a green serge gown, heaving with suppressed anguish. A loud sob threatened to break loose, tears cascading as if to compensate for lost time. The dilemma was acute: cruelty was unthinkable given Tito's gentle nature, yet his mission awaited. Regretting his deviation from the Via de'Bardi, he watched her lift her holiday apron to wipe her streaming eyes. Instinctively, he mirrored the gesture, pressing his hand to her cheek and kissing the soft roundness.

"My poor little Tessa," he murmured. "Cease your weeping. Let us ascertain a solution. Where is your dwelling?"

Silence answered, save for the diminishing sobs.

"Very well. I shall accompany you a short distance, provided you reveal your destination."

The apron dropped, and Tessa's countenance transformed, blossoming with the serene contentment of a cherub emerging from clouds. The conjurer, the threat of violence, receded into memory.

"I believe I shall return home if you will guide me," she whispered, gazing up with wide, azure eyes that held a sweetness beyond a mere smile—a profound, childlike tranquility.

"Then come, little one," Tito cooed, reclaiming her arm. "Which direction lies your path?"

"Beyond Peretola—to the great pear-tree."

"Peretola? Through which gate, *pazzarella*? Remember, I am a stranger."

"The Por del Prato," Tessa replied, gripping his arm tightly as they walked.

Unfamiliar with every winding lane, and aware that crowded areas offered the best chance to intercept Monna Ghita and conclude his quest, Tito steered them directly toward Porta Rossa and onward to Ognissanti. He maintained his radiant, appeasing demeanor, absorbing the jeers of the mixed throng—frolicking apprentices jealous of his luck, bold-eyed women veiled in yellow, beggars mocking his haste, sharpers, and rowdy boys accustomed to brutal street games. The streets of Florence were rarely a moral tableau in those eras, and Tessa's terror of getting lost was entirely justified.

Upon reaching the Piazza d'Ognissanti, Tito slowed. Both were breathless from the hurried trek. The expansive square allowed them to pause on a stone bench affixed to the ancient wall.

"Holy Virgin!" Tessa exclaimed. "We have escaped those women and boys. Yet, I was not truly frightened, for you protect me."

"Pretty little Tessa," Tito smiled. "What grants you such security with me?"

"Because you are so beautiful—like those entering Paradise. They are all good."

Noticing stalls laden with fruit and sweets nearby, Tito observed her hunger. "You haven't eaten since morning, Tessa. Are you famished?"

"Yes, I suppose so—if you care to share."

Tito purchased apricots, cakes, and comfits, depositing them into her apron. "Let us continue to the Prato; perhaps then you will feel brave enough to finish the journey alone."

"But you will miss your treats," Tessa protested, rising obediently to gather her apron as a makeshift bag.

"We shall see," Tito declared aloud, though inwardly he reflected: *Here is a peasant girl capable of inspiring an idyll surpassing Lorenzo de'Medici's 'Nencia da Barberino,' a tale his friends currently laud. Were I a Theocritus, or possessed of the leisure to cultivate such pastoral experiences through these untimely excursions!* He sighed internally. *However, the damage is done. I am already late; another half-hour makes no difference. Pretty little pigeon.*

"We possess a garden abundant with pears," Tessa chimed in. "Two cows, plus mules. I adore them. Yet my father-in-law is a cross man. I wish my mother hadn't wed him. He is wicked, terribly ugly."
