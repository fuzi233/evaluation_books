ON CATS AND DOGS. What I have suffered from them this morning no tongue can adequately describe. It commenced with Gustavus Adolphus. Downstairs, they shorten his name to "Gusty," a moniker that suits his chaotic energy perfectly. While he is a decent fellow when confined to a spacious field or a wide common, I draw the line at bringing him indoors. His intentions are good, but this residence is simply too small for his ego.

He stretches, and *bam*—two chairs and a sideboard tumble to the floor. He wags his tail, and the room resembles the aftermath of a devastating military invasion. He takes a breath, and the fire dies instantly. By dinner time, he slips under the table, lies dormant for a moment, and then erupts. Our only clue to his presence is the table itself, which begins to perform involuntary somersaults. We scramble to hold it upright, convinced a wicked conspiracy is underway against us. His struggle intensifies, believing he is the victim of a plot, resulting in the classic scene: an overturned table, a ruined meal trapped between two layers of furious humans.

This morning, he arrived in his signature style—an American cyclone incarnate. Before I could even finish my sentence, he swept my coffee cup off the table with his tail, splashing the contents directly onto my waistcoat. I sprang up, shouting a curse, and charged toward him. He pre-emptively bolted for the door. Just as he reached it, he collided with Eliza, who was entering with a basket of eggs. She let out a sharp "Ugh!" and collapsed onto the floor. The eggs scattered across the carpet in various states of disarray, and Gustavus Adolphus retreated, leaving the carnage behind.

I chased him down the stairs, urgently advising him to remain below for at least an hour and not return to my sight. Seeming to accept this wisdom, he dodged a flying coal-scoop and vanished. I dried myself off and attempted to enjoy breakfast, certain he had retired to the yard. Ten minutes later, peeking into the passage, I found him perched triumphantly at the top of the stairs. I commanded him down; he merely barked and jumped. My patience snapped.

The culprit was Tittums, our kitten. She was stationed on the step below, refusing to yield. Tittums is roughly the size of a penny loaf, currently vibrating with rage. Her back was arched, and she was swearing with the ferocity of a medical student. She possesses a vocabulary of abuse that rivals my own. To be honest—and please consider this strictly confidential, as I would not want your wife to hear me admit it—the female half of humanity fails to grasp these nuances. Between you and me, I find that swearing actually benefits a man. It acts as a safety valve, releasing the toxic pressure of bad temper before it causes internal damage to the mental machinery.

When a man vents, saying things like, "By the sun, moon, and stars, what carelessness allowed your delicate foot to crush my corn?" or similar invectives, he feels lighter. Swearing soothes the soul just as smashing furniture does, only it costs nothing. It clears a man out like a load of gunpowder clears a chimney. Occasional explosions are essential. I distrust anyone who never swears, never kicks a stool, or pokes the fire violently. Without an outlet, life's annoyances fester, turning petty grievances into deep-seated sorrows and breeding hatred. Swearing relieves the burden.

I tried explaining this to my aunt once, but she dismissed my feelings as improper. That is exactly what I told Tittums. I lectured her on her upbringing in a Christian household and her lack of shame. I don't mind an old cat cursing; it fits her age. But a kitten? It is tragic. I stuffed her into my pocket and returned to my desk. Moments later, she had escaped, scrambled onto the table, attempted to swallow my pen, dipped her leg in the inkwell, spilled the ink, licked her leg, and then turned around to swear at me.

I dropped her on the floor, where Tim, the terrier, immediately joined the fray. I wish Tim would mind his own business; it wasn't his affair. Besides, he isn't a saint either—a two-year-old fox-terrier pretending to be a dignified Scottish collie. Fortunately, Tittums' mother arrived, scratching Tim's nose, which I found delightful. I banished all three to the hallway, where they are currently engaged in a violent brawl.

My desk is covered in ink, and my temper is sky-high. If another pet decides to interfere this morning, it had better hire its own funeral director beforehand. Yet, despite this chaos, I genuinely adore cats and dogs. They are superior companions to humans. They never argue or lecture you. They listen attentively while you speak of yourself, feigning interest without making stupid remarks. They never misunderstand relationships at dinner parties, nor do they mistake your wife's cousin for her husband. They never interrogate a struggling playwright about his unpublished works. They offer no unsolicited critiques of your character or past mistakes. They provide no sarcastic reminders of your declining charm.

To them, you are constant. They are always glad to see you, mirroring your emotions perfectly. Are you joyful? They frolic. Are you solemn? They sit quietly. Are you grieving? They share your sadness.

"Halloo! Feeling festive? Let's go! Frisking, jumping, ready for mischief. Shall we ruin the drawing-room furniture or race across the fields to scare Old Gaffer Goggle's geese?"

Or, if you wish for solitude, a cat will purr on your armchair, and a dog will curl by the fire, blinking lazily while keeping one eye on you, guarding against intruders. When despair hits and we wish we were never born, they don't judge. They don't analyze our failures. Instead, they nudge us gently. A cat rests on your shoulder, smoothing your hair and offering silent sympathy. A dog lifts his head with those profound, truthful eyes, communicating: *"We are in this together. Through thick and thin, I am your friend."*

A dog is imprudent. He cares not whether you are right or wrong, rich or poor, wise or foolish. You are his pal, and that is sufficient. Come luck or disaster, fame or infamy, he sticks by you, willing to give his life if necessary. Foolish, brainless, soulless creature!

Ah, loyal friend, with your deep eyes that comprehend everything before you speak. Do you realize you are merely an animal? Do you know that the drunken lout leaning against the post outside is intellectually superior to you? Do you know that every deceitful, selfish scoundrel who cheats, lies, and harbors no noble thoughts is vastly superior to you? These crawling villains are *men*. Men are the greatest, noblest, and wisest beings in the universe. Any man will tell you that.

Yes, poor dog, you are incredibly stupid compared to us clever humans who master politics, philosophy, and history. We know everything, except our own origins, our destination, and the nature of reality. Never mind. We like you precisely because you are stupid. Humans cannot tolerate intelligent women; a woman's ideal partner is someone she can call a "dear old stupid." It is comforting to encounter minds simpler than our own. We love stupidity instantly. The world is harsh for the clever; ordinary people dislike them, and clever people hate each other. Since the clever are such a tiny minority, their unhappiness matters little. As long as the foolish masses are content, the world functions adequately.

Cats are credited with being more worldly-wise than dogs, prioritizing self-interest over blind loyalty. Naturally, we are shocked by such selfishness. Cats prefer homes with carpets and avoid households with many children. However, cats are largely libeled. True friends among them stick through thick and thin. Every cat I have owned has been a steadfast comrade. One followed me everywhere, becoming so persistent I had to beg her to stop trailing me down the High Street. She would wait in the passage for my late returns, making me feel like a married man, minus the nagging questions about my whereabouts.

Another cat developed a daily habit of intoxication. She would linger outside the cellar door, waiting to sneak in and lap up beer drippings. I mention this not to praise the species, but to illustrate their near-human traits. If reincarnation exists, this particular cat was rapidly qualifying for sainthood, driven by vanity and a thirst for alcohol. When she caught a large rat, she would drag it into the living room, display the corpse proudly, and await our praise. How the girls screamed! Poor rats—they seem to exist solely so cats and dogs can claim credit for their demise, allowing chemists to profit from poisons.

Yet, there is a fascination to them. Rats possess a weird, uncanny quality. They are cunning, strong, numerous, cruel, and secretive. They inhabit abandoned houses, whispering prophecies of doom in hidden corners. They abandon sinking ships mysteriously. They appear in ghost stories, gnawing through walls, screaming in the dark, and haunting dying prisoners in dungeons. Their red eyes glow like embers in the gloom.

I love tales of rats. Consider the story of Bishop Hatto. Hoarding grain while people starved, he locked starving peasants in his barn and burned them alive.
