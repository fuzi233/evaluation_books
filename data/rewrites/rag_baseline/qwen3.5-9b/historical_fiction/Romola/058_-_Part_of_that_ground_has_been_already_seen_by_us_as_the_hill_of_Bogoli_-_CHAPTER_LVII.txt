Tito possessed excellent reasons for asserting his safety. Over the preceding quarter, anticipating the inevitable exposure of the Medicean conspirators, he had meticulously fortified his position. He had expanded his influence across Rome and Milan, positioning himself as the conduit for clandestine intelligence and indirect pressure against both the Frate and the popular faction. Simultaneously, he had cultivated the popular party with unprecedented diligence, projecting a facade of unwavering political alignment. Yet, paradoxically, he had intensified his active employment and exclusive reliance by the Mediceans. Maintaining this triple deception was effortless; the Mediceans' own admission of duplicity stripped them of any metric to judge a colleague lacking their hereditary bonds, entrenched alliances, and visceral prejudices. To the Medici, deceiving the opposition was a legitimate stratagem, while deceiving their own kin was a baseness they felt no urge to commit. In leveraging Tito's pliable talents, they remained oblivious to the fact that his lack of traditional attachments rendered him a convenient tool but removed the primary guarantee of mutual honor found among themselves. Furthermore, the *Arrabbiati* of Rome and Milan, the aristocratic party's bitterest foes of Savonarola, operated a network of espionage grounded in profound hypocrisy, demanding exorbitant fees for their services. Suspecting an agent solely because he performed a role convincingly would have been a logical fallacy. Conversely, the *Piagnoni*, driven by the directness of energetic conviction, were prone to crediting Tito with sincere adherence due to his feigned indifference toward religious sentiment. Under these conditions, the last three months constituted a period of flattering triumph for Tito. His paramount objective was securing a future station in Rome or Milan; he harbored a growing resolve to abandon Florence for a capital where life was less arduous and the rewards for talent and learning more magnificent. Currently, the scales tipped toward Milan; rendering specific services to Duke Ludovico Sforza within the year promised a court appointment surpassing the allure of Rome. The revelation of the Medicean conspiracy, however, was an event Tito had foreseen but not anticipated in its modus operandi. The arrest of Lamberto dell'Antella, clutching a damning letter and fueled by rancorous hatred of the Medici, was an incalculable variable. Despite the careful pretexts guarding his agency, Tito could not evade implication in a broad discovery. Yet, his sharp intellect rapidly charted the path to his own preservation with minimal collateral damage. While preserving one's integrity is pleasant, the skin remains an organ sensitive to atmospheric shifts. His calculations proved accurate. That evening, prior to returning home, he secured the three outcomes he coveted: exemption from prosecution regarding Medicean complicity, retention of his secretaryship for another annum absent resignation, and the strict confidentiality of the price exacted for these guarantees. A prudent man might deem the cost excessive, and Tito himself would prefer otherwise, yet necessity dictates sacrifice.

His initial maneuver targeted Francesco Valori, head of the *Piagnoni* and one of the Ten under whom Tito served. Valori, though possessing noble qualities, was plagued by an arrogant temperament that estranged allies and bred bitter animosity, particularly toward Bernardo del Nero. Securing a pledge of secrecy, Tito confessed his agency for the Mediceans, citing motives he described with frank candor. He declared his belief in the futility of their efforts and his genuine preference for the popular government. He further feigned confidence in Valori, revealing his personal disdain for Bernardo del Nero. This preamble led to the critical proposition: there existed another Medicean plot, and provided certain conditions were met, Tito could travel to Siena and Romagna, where Piero de' Medici was amassing forces, to procure documentary evidence for the council. Crucially, his reputation as a Medicean agent must remain unblemished in the eyes of the Medici; thus, his status as a source of information to the authorities required absolute obscurity. Nevertheless, precautions could fail. Before facing the repercussions of turning against his associates, he needed assurance of immunity from prosecution and deprivation of office for the coming year. These terms did not resonate with Valori as they might today. Valori's mind was not fixated on evaluating Tito's conduct; rather, it was consumed by the imperative to secure an extreme sentence against the five prisoners. With immense efforts surely mounting to save them, the evidence required to alarm the well-disposed to the dangers of clemency had to be irrefutable. Legal proceedings of the era posited that evidence was a commodity attainable only through foul means. Torturing suspects until they confessed universal guilt was a step toward justice, especially if a traitor emerged. Lamberto dell'Antella had been tortured to extract knowledge beyond his grasp, yet stronger facts were needed, particularly against Bernardo del Nero, who had thus far refrained from betrayal despite earlier attempts to dissuade the plot. Florence's welfare demanded Bernardo's guilt be illuminated in the starkest light. Zealous in this belief, perhaps blinded by his own hatred, Valori deemed Tito's proposal acceptable. He presented it to his colleagues anonymously, winning their assent.

Late that afternoon, Tito was granted an audience by the Special Council. He produced a profound sensation by unveiling a second plot designed to ensure Piero de' Medici's mastery of Florence, scheduled for mid-August. Documentary proof on this matter would clarify the righteous course more effectively than any other action. He received a commission to depart for Siena at dawn, accompanied by a written guarantee of his immunity and tenure. Among the twenty Florentines casting grave gazes upon Tito as he stood gracefully before them, articulating startling truths with easy circumlocution and an apparent lack of higher motive—a mask often worn by the most intense affectation—they formed silent verdicts. Several concluded that this ingenious and serviceable Greek was henceforth to be utilized for public exigencies rather than private intimacy. Unprincipled men were assets, allowing those with scruples to keep their hands relatively clean amidst necessary filth. Indeed, it remained obscure to respectable Florentine minds, barring those holding the Frate's extravagant faith in earthly purity, how any department functioned without human instruments worthy of being kicked or spat upon while receiving their wages. Those very men passing tacit judgment on Tito were soon to engage in a memorable transaction impossible without such resolute unscrupulousness. As the Florentine poet Pulci noted, "Betrayal pleases many, but no one loves the traitor." Society maintained gibbets for murderers and martyrs alike, offering execrable hisses for both dastardly acts and generous truths—a mixed state signaling not chaos, but struggling order.

For Tito, he was acutely aware of his slight descent in the esteem of those accepting his services. Possessing the self-reflection inherent to habitual rational calculation, he would have declined disapproval from worldly men. He had never intended to be judged poorly, aiming instead to conduct affairs so ably that opposition to societal standards went unnoticed. The barrier separating him from Romola had risen precisely because such concealment was impossible with her. He recoiled from condemnatory judgments as one recoils from an inhospitable climate. Yet, circumstances were not as malleable to cleverness as desired; events had turned inconveniently. He harbored no rancor against Messer Bernardo del Nero; he held personal affection for Lorenzo Tornabuoni and Giannozzo Pucci. Having served them ably, he would have deserved high reward had their party prevailed. Was he to forfeit all life's agreeable fruits because their cause failed? His offer of additional proof against them would likely alter their fate little; he was convinced they would escape extreme consequences. Had he withheld it, however, his promising prospects would have been utterly destroyed. What motive drives a man but self-interest? Florentines engrossed in petty political schemes might conflate self-interest with family pride and ancient hatreds. A modern simpleton swallowing philosophical systems might mistake self-conceit for divine reward. Fanatics believing in the Coming Scourge might view their interest in a future palm-branch and white robe. But no man of clear intellect allows his course to be steered by such puerile impulses or dubious internal vapors. Consider Pontanus, poet and philosopher of unrivaled Latinity, who delivered a magnificent oration in Naples welcoming the French king—the very monarch destined to dethrone his royal friend and patron. Yet Pontanus held his head high and prospered. Men rarely concern themselves with such matters unless their personal spleen is provoked. Weakness alone is despised; power commands immunity. No man ascends high without incurring unpleasant necessities exposing him to enmity and occasional hissing when a pretext is needed. It was a faint prognostic of this hissing, gathered by Tito from the council chamber, that lent his current conduct the character of an epoch, prompting him to dwell upon it with argumentative vindication.

This was not a deeper plunge into wrongdoing, for no bond to the Medici could supersede his tie to his father. Yet, his conduct toward his father had been shielded by successful lies; his present act admitted no total concealment—it was inherently a revelation. Tito winced under this new liability to disesteem. Very well; a little patience, and within six months or a year, he might turn his back on these hard, eager Florentines with their futile quarrels and sinking fortunes. His brilliant success in Florence bore ugly flaws: his infatuation with the wrong woman and Baldassarre's return under incalculable circumstances. But as Tito galloped with a loose rein toward Siena, he envisioned a future free from these haunting errors.
