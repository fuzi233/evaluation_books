If actors and actresses possess the same fragile constitution as children, prone to weeping at the first drop of rain, they also share the childlike ability to dance in puddles immediately after the storm passes. This fact did not escape Sally's notice, nor did it fail to astound her. Consider the sheer alchemy of lending her brother Fillmore twenty thousand dollars. In a single transaction, she had engineered a state of universal euphoria among the troupe of the Primrose Way Company. It was as if the Millennium had descended upon the theater district; the lethargy and despondency born of a week of paralysis evaporated instantly, falling away like a soaked cloak. The promotion of a certain "creature of the abyss"—the assistant stage manager—to the dizzying heights of Proprietor struck them as a plot point worthy of Shakespeare. Having once played the persecuted heroine whose fortunes turn around at eleven o'clock, they found the situation theatrically sound. Furthermore, now that Miss Hobson had departed, everyone finally admitted that her presence had been a blight, a literal cloud hovering over their prospects.

An atmosphere of unbridled optimism swept through the building. Cheerful rumors began to circulate with the speed of wildfire. Bowler-hatted Teddy received word directly from the lift-boy at his hotel that the theatrical ban would be lifted no later than Tuesday. Meanwhile, the cigar-stand girl at the Pontchartrain, a figure of considerable local authority, informed the man playing the butler that Toledo and Cleveland were set to open tomorrow night. The consensus was clear: the sun was breaking through the clouds, and Fate, weary of its hopeless task of keeping virtuous men down, would soon throw in the towel.

And so, Fillmore returned to himself. Every human being has a unique idiom for expressing elation. For Fillmore, it involved purchasing a brand-new waistcoat, acquiring a box of one hundred half-dollar cigars, and becoming excessively fussy about the composition of his luncheon. Whether it was an optical illusion or not, Sally noted that within twenty-four hours of this new regime, Fillmore appeared to have gained at least six pounds. As a serf tending to paper knives and props, he had always been, by his own standards, alarmingly thin. Now, blooming into a manager, he swelled into soft, billowy curves. Standing on the sidewalk before the theater, gloating over the fresh posters bearing the legend *FILLMORE NICHOLAS PRESENTS*, he was so wide that the passersby were forced to execute a tactical detour simply to avoid colliding with him.

In this epoch of bubbling joy, it was a peculiar paradox that Sally, the fairy godmother who had orchestrated this entire resurrection, should not be radiating equal bliss. It troubled her, genuinely puzzled her, why she remained standing in the shadows while the world burned with light. Whatever shadow lingered at the back of her mind refused to step forward, to reveal itself, and be challenged. It was not until she was driving in a hired carriage with Gerald along the winding roads of Belle Isle that the truth finally emerged.

Since Miss Hobson's departure, Gerald had reached his zenith. Like Fillmore, he was a man who thrived in the sunshine of prosperity. His former moody grumpiness had vanished, replaced by a luster that made him undeniably charming again. Yet, as the carriage slid smoothly through the pleasant woods and fields bordering the river, Sally felt a distinct jar, a dissonance in the melody.

Gerald was cheerful, expansive, and talkative. He, too, saw no flaw in existence. He spoke grandly of the monumental plans he intended to enact. "If this play gets over—and it will—I'll show 'em!" His jaw was squared like granite, his eyes glowing with a feverish intensity as they stared into an inviting future. "One success—that's all I need—then watch me! I haven't had a chance yet, but..."

His voice rolled on, a river of ambition, but Sally had long ago stopped listening. It was the hour when the chill of evening follows swiftly upon the mellow warmth of afternoon. The sun had dipped behind the trees, and a cold, biting wind was rising from the river. Suddenly, as if the wind had acted as a clearing agent for her thoughts, she understood exactly what had been lurking unseen in the recesses of her mind. For an instant, it stood out naked, unmasked, and the world transformed into a desolate landscape.

She realized then the fundamental chasm between a man's outlook on life and a woman's. Success! How men worship it, and how miserly they become with everything else in its name. Irony, sharp and stinging, was the theme of Gerald's very play—the script she had saved from the flames. Of all the men she knew, how few possessed any vision of life other than as a race where they must strain every nerve to win, regardless of what they miss along the way? Fillmore, Gerald—all of them. There might be a woman in each of their lives, but she was invariably relegated to second place—an afterthought, a hobby for their spare time. Gerald was everything to her. His success would never, in her eyes, be more than a side issue. He himself, stripped of all trappings of glory, was sufficient. But she was not enough for him. A spasm of futile jealousy seized her heart, followed by a shiver that ran down her spine.

"Cold?" Gerald asked, oblivious to the tempest within her soul. "I'll tell the man to drive back... I don't see any reason why this play shouldn't run a year in New York. Everybody says it's good... if it does get over, they'll all be after me. I..."

Sally stared out into a bleak world. The sky above was a leaden grey, and the wind from the river blew with a dismal, relentless chill.
