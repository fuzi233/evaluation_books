Gerard hungered for Burgundy almost as keenly as Denys did, though each in his own fashion. Denys sang of it the way a vintner praises his own cask: not in great speeches only, but in every small vexation on the road—every sour host, every greasy trencher, every featherless pallet—he must needs point with his whole body and voice toward that promised country where, he swore, folk washed their hands and their linen as if God Himself were to dine.

And it was ever the linen that Denys used to bait Gerard with—knowing well the Hollander’s tenderness on that score.

“I ask no more,” Gerard would say, half laughing and half sick. “To think I have not lain once in a naked bed since I left home! When I look at their sheets, instead of doffing habit and hose, it is my eyes and nose I would fain be shut of.”

Denys—who loved his country as some men love a mistress, with blind fidelity and sharp jealousy—carried that fondness to folly. He would tramp twenty leagues in shoes that had burst their seams and gaped like mouths, rather than buy a stitch of leather from a German churl; for, said he, the fellow would not sell without first flinging his sulks, his dinner, and his big body across the bargain, setting a man to struggle through a hedge of discourtesies for the bare right to pay.

Toward sunset they came to a choice. A little town lay ahead on the road, its smoke faint against the paling sky; a monastery lay aside, a black mass of roof and wall set back from the track among trees. The one promised an inn, noise, and trade; the other, quiet, charity, and rule.

Denys turned his toes toward the town at once. Gerard, who had lived on convent bread and convent shelter more than once, and found in it a clean simplicity after the world’s rank cookery, pointed his staff toward the cloister.

Denys yielded—after a struggle that showed plain his whole body was an argument. But he laid down his terms like a captain striking a truce.

“Once in Burgundy,” said he, “we stop always at an inn.”

Gerard agreed the readier because the little chart he carried—the careful list of houses where a traveller might find a pallet and a bowl—ended there. So they left the road and took the side way.

The air changed as they went: less of hoof and cart-rattle, more of hedge-birds and their own footfalls; and then, as the great building drew near, there came upon the wind that peculiar smell which clings to such places—cold stone that has never wholly dried, wax long burned into wood, boiled pottage worked into the very mortar by centuries of ladles, and, faintly, the sharp clean breath of herbs from a physic garden.

Gerard, surprised at Denys’s sudden sourness, asked him whence this new dislike of houses that had fed and lodged them for nothing so often.

Denys hemmed, hawed, spat to one side, and for a time would only shrug. But at last his wrongs—long banked up—broke through; and when he spoke, it was not new dislike at all, but an old horror that had been biting him all the way, only held back (so he pretended) out of politeness and because, as he said, he had seen powder put in Gerard’s drink and had therefore forborne to offend men who might do worse.

“However,” he added, with the air of one who grants a favour to himself, “being this the last, why not ease my mind? Know then, I have been like a fish out of water in all those great dungeons. You straightway levant with some old shaveling; so you see not my purgatory.”

Gerard’s face softened. “Forgive me. I have been selfish.”

“Ay, ay, I forgive thee, little one; ’tis not thy fault. Art not the first fool that hath been priest-rid and monk-hit. But I’ll not forgive them my misery.”

And then, as if he were some commissioner come a century too soon to pull down abbeys, he delivered his indictment—hot, particular, and full of the bodily things that make a man’s days either human or hellish.

“These gloomy piles,” said he, “are all built alike. Inns differ—God bless them for it!—one hath a buttery that smells of ale and roast; another a kitchen where the onions and dripping tell you supper is at hand; another a hall full o’ talk and a hearth that crackles like good company. But here—monotony! Great gate, little gate, so many steps, then a gloomy cloister where your voice dies on the stones. Here the dortour; there the great cold refectory, where you must sit mumchance—or, at least, whisper like a thief—he that likes to speak his mind out.”

He flung his hand toward the walls as if they had already begun to close on his throat.

“And then,” he cried, “nobody is a man here, but all are slaves—and of what? Of a peevish, tinkling bell, that never sleeps. An ’twere a trumpet now, aye sounding alarums, ’twouldn’t freeze a man’s heart so. But this—tinkle, tinkle, tinkle—and you must sit to meat, with may be no stomach for food. Ere your meat settles warm in your belly, tinkle, tinkle! and ye must to church, with may be no stomach for devotion: I am not a hog at prayers, for one. Tinkle, tinkle, and now you must to bed with your eyes open. Well, by then you have contrived to shut them, some uneasy imp o’ darkness hath got to the bell-rope, and tinkle, tinkle, it behoves you say a prayer in the dark, whether you know one or not. If they heard the sort of prayers I mutter when they break my rest with their tinkle! Well, you drop off again and get about an eyeful of sleep: lo, it is tinkle, tinkle, for matins.”

To Denys, sound was not mere air: it was command and compulsion. The bell, small as a child’s toy, made a world of men into puppets; it stole sleep, it turned hunger into duty, it turned the body’s honest needs into sins measured by a rope and a clapper. Gerard heard in Denys’s wrath not only blasphemy, but a true misery—the tyranny of a soundscape a soldier could not master, the way a perpetual thin ringing can rasp a mind rawer than any cold stone.

“And the only clapper you love is a woman’s,” Gerard said, half contempt, half teasing.

“Because there is some music in that even when it scolds,” Denys retorted stoutly; and for a moment his face lightened, as if the memory of a human voice—warm, various, full of breath and laughter—had driven off the bell’s thin tyranny.

Then he plunged back.

“And to be always checked! If I do but put my finger in the salt-cellar, straightway I hear, ‘Have you no knife that you finger the salt?’ And if I but wipe my knife on the cloth to save time, then ’tis, ‘Wipe thy knife dirty on the bread, and clean upon the cloth!’ Oh small of soul! these little peevish pedantries fall chill upon good fellowship like wee icicles a-melting down from strawen eaves.”

“I hold cleanliness no pedantry,” said Gerard, with that quiet firmness that could be more cutting than Denys’s oaths. “Shouldst learn better manners once for all.”

“Nay; ’tis they who lack manners. They stop a fellow’s mouth at every word.”

“At every other word, you mean; every obscene or blasphemous one.”

“Exaggerator, go to! Why, at the very last of these dungeons I found poor travellers sitting all chilled and mute round one shaveling, like rogues awaiting their turn to be hanged; so to cheer them up, I did but cry out, ‘Courage, tout le monde, le dia—’”

“Connu! what befell?” asked Gerard, despite himself; Denys’s tales always had a smell of smoke and ale about them, even when they were told in the open air.

“Marry, this. ‘Blaspheme not!’ quo’ the bourreau. ‘Plait-il,’ say I. Doesn’t he wheel and wyte on me in a sort of Alsatian French, turning all the P’s into B’s. I had much ado not to laugh in his face.”

Gerard’s eyes narrowed. “Being thyself unable to speak ten words of his language without a fault.”

“Well, all the world ought to speak French. What avail so many jargons except to put a frontier atwixt men’s hearts?”

There was truth there too—yet Gerard felt how such a saying could be a kind of conquest, taking from each man his mother-words and calling the theft a courtesy. He thought of how a thing tasted different when named in a different tongue—how one word could carry a whole childhood’s kitchen in it, and another, meaning the same by rule, could leave it cold as a translation of prayer.

“But what said he?” Gerard persisted.

“What signifies it what a fool says?”

“Oh, not all the words of a fool are folly, or I should not listen to you.”

Denys snorted, but he had been caught; he must go on.

“Well then, he said, ‘Such as begin by making free with the devil’s name, aye end by doing it with all the names in heaven.’ ‘Father,’ said I, ‘I am a soldier, and this is but my consigne, or watchword.’ ‘Oh, then, it is just a custom?’ said he. I—not divining the old fox, and thinking to clear myself—said, ‘Ay, it was.’ ‘Then that is ten times worse,’ said he. ‘’Twill bring him about your ears one of these days. He still comes where he hears his name often called.’ Observe! no gratitude for the tidings which neither his missals nor his breviary had ever let him know.”

“And he was wrong?” Gerard asked quietly.

Denys, guarded now, shifted his ground like a man who feels the earth hot beneath him.

“Then he was so good,” Denys went on, “as to tell me soldiers do commonly the crimes for which all other men are broke on the wheel; a savoir murder, rape, and pillage.”

“And is’t not true?” said Gerard, not cruelly, but as one who will not let a thing hide behind laughter.

“True or not, it was ill manners,” Denys replied, fencing. “And so says this courteous host of mine, ‘Being the foes of mankind, why make enemies of good spirits into the bargain, by still shouting the names of evil ones?’ and a lot more stuff.”

“Well, but, Denys,” Gerard said, “whether you hearken his rede or slight it, wherefore blame a man for raising his voice to save your soul?”

“How can his voice save my soul,” Denys shot back, triumphant, “when he keeps turning of his P’s into B’s?”

Gerard was staggered, as if Denys had flung a dish in his face. He had no answer ready to that gross mockery of speech—the very instrument by which men carry law, love, prayer, and bread from one to another. Before he could recover, Denys—having found the weak spot—bounded away from the point and fell upon a larger prey.

“All monks are hypocrites,” he cried. “Do but look at them, how they creep about and cannot eye you like honest men.”

“Nay,” said Gerard quickly, eager to defend what he loved in them, “that modest downcast gaze is part of their discipline—’tis custodia oculorum.”

“Cussed toads eating hoc hac horum?” Denys bellowed. “No such thing; just so looks a cut-purse. Can’t meet a true man’s eye. Doff cowl, monk; and behold, a thief; don cowl thief, and lo, a monk. Tell me not they will ever be able to look God Almighty in the face, when they can’t even look a true man in the face down here.”

By now the shadow of the monastery lay upon them. The walls rose blacker than the trees; the way narrowed; the sound of their steps became a small hard thing in the deepening hush. And then—thin as a thread, yet cutting through all other sounds—came a bell.

“Ah, here it is, black as ink!” Denys cried, with a shiver that was half rage and half jest. “Into the well we go, comrade. Misericorde, there goes the tinkle already.”

He paused, cocking his head like a dog that hears meat being carved.

“’Tis the best of tinkles though; ’tis for dinner. Stay—listen! I thought so: the wolf in my stomach cried ‘Amen!’”

This last he sealed with two oaths, as if seasoning his hunger with profanity; and with that he marched into the convent like a victorious gamecock, taking Gerard’s silence for defeat, and never dreaming how deeply he had disgusted him.
