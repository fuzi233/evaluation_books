The falling out between the two girls blew over in no time flat. Laura was the sort who’d apologize and forgive you for stealing her favorite hair ribbon before you even finished denying it, and as for Blanche, her tantrums were never long-lived anyway — this particular tiff hadn’t even ruffled her feathers, truth be told. No vanity gets bruised over being called wicked, after all; if anything, Blanche was flattered that Laura cared enough to get mad. The whole fight, both girls knew, wasn’t really about anything they’d said out loud anyway.
With a sigh, Laura had to admit the giddy, romantic best-friend-forever phase of her friendship with Blanche was dead and gone. Now she could only summon the sort of mild, polite fondness you have for a roommate who keeps stealing your milk.
Blanche, for her part, immediately cranked out a page of heart-rending poetry all about her cruel desertion and broken illusions. Same old story, she wrote: love meeting coldness, loyalty repaid with neglect. Just her luck, a new family with two daughters moved into the neighborhood from London right then, so Blanche quickly adopted one of them as her new eternal soulmate, and poured all her tragic woe over missing the old dead Laura friendship straight into this new girl’s ear. The tall footmen from the Hall barely turned up at Fairoaks with notes anymore, the little pony carriage was almost never sent over to run errands for the ladies there. Whenever Laura popped in to visit, Blanche would pull on her best sad martyr face, and Laura would just laugh at her over-the-top sentimental schtick with zero respect, which only made Blanche pout harder.
If Blanche had new female friends to keep her occupied, the honest historian has to note she also racked up a whole crowd of male admirers who seemed equally charmed by her little routine. Put this artless young thing in a garden path, a drawing room window nook, or a lull between waltzes for ten whole minutes, and she’d be leaning in all soft and earnest, fluttering her big beautiful eyes, spilling fake intimate secrets and making tender little appeals, before saying goodbye and turning the exact same performance on the next poor sap who wandered past.
Back when the Claverings first moved to the Park, there was barely anyone around for Blanche to perform for. Which meant Pen got all the flirty little glances and secret asides all to himself, by the drawing room window or down the garden walk. As mentioned before, the whole town of Clavering had exactly zero eligible young men; the surrounding countryside had nothing but a couple of scruffy curates and country squires whose feet were so big they looked like they were wearing rowing boats for boots. Sir Francis wouldn’t even say hello to the dragoons quartered at Chatteris — turns out that was his old regiment, and he’d left on terrible terms over a whole messy cocktail of a scam horse sale, disputed gambling debts, and a whole host of old embarrassments no one bothered digging into, since we’ve got more than enough current drama to keep track of.
But autumn rolled around, Parliament adjourned, and the London season wrapped up, dumping a horde of county families back to their country estates, filling up the tiny nearby Baymouth watering place, opening the doors of Mr. Bingley’s ramshackle Theatre Royal in Chatteris, and drawing crowds for the assize balls and race meets. Up until that point, the snooty old local county families had all given Clavering Park the cold shoulder: the Fogeys of Drummington, the Squares of Tozely Park, the Welbores of The Barrow, all of them gossiped absolute nonsense about the new neighbours, inventing whole secret criminal backstories for Sir Francis, his mysterious wife, Blanche, and sketchy Captain Strong that would make a tabloid editor blush. Country folks don’t lack imagination, trust me.
Then the bigwigs showed up: the Earl of Trehawk, Lord Lieutenant of the whole county, moved into Eyrie Castle, and the Dowager Countess of Rockminster took a fancy mansion on Baymouth’s Marine Parade. Both great houses immediately paid formal, state calls on the Claverings at the Park, and every other county family’s carriage went trundling right up the avenue after theirs, no one daring to be the last to suck up to the new crowd.
That’s when the fancy French chef Mirobolant finally got to show off all his skills, distracting himself from his sad unrequited love pangs by making ten different complicated sauces for every dinner. The giant footmen at the Park were so busy waiting on guests they never got a chance to loiter drinking small beer with the maids at Fairoaks anymore. Blanche had new best friends to hang out with and fancy parties to go to, no more wandering down to the riverside where Pen fished. He showed up day after day casting his line, but not a single fish bit, and his river nymph “Undine” never showed up.
Strictly off the record, we can mention that little secret hollow tree Pen used to post secret love letters to Miss Fotheringay back in the day? He’d converted it into a tiny private post office. Tucked under a loose stone and a clump of moss, he’d leave flowery little poems and over-romantic letters addressed to the river nymph who haunted the bank, and a couple times he’d gotten a note back: a pressed wild flower, or a tiny couple lines of soft acknowledgement scrawled on pink, rose-scented paper, half jocular half sweet. Of course Blanche walked that riverside all the time, and of course she used that exact same pink scented stationery. But once all the fancy visitors moved into Clavering Park, no one came to retrieve Pen’s letters. His white pages sat untouched under the stone and moss, while the tree’s reflection wobbled in the Brawl river as it rolled past. The letters were hardly racy, the pink notes barely said anything at all — but silly old Pen decided the whole thing was a grand tragic romance. Why didn’t he just walk up and say hello, you dummy? Neither of you were even that serious, you were both just playing at being in love.
But when the game gets cut off mid-round, a man is very likely to throw a dramatic little temper tantrum. When no one came to collect his poems anymore, Pen suddenly decided he was heartbroken, just like he’d been over the actress, and he was dead set on getting a full explanation. One day he turned up at the Hall and there were twenty visitors packed in the drawing room; the next day, Blanche was nowhere to be found, napping to rest up for a ball that night. Pen cursed all balls, cursed his tiny allowance, cursed the fact that he was too low down the local social ladder to ever get invited to them. The third time he visited, Blanche was strolling the garden in state with no less than the Bishop of Chatteris, his wife, and his whole family, who all scowled so hard at Pen when they heard his last name he almost flinched — they absolutely remembered that whole mess where Pen got caught making out with Miss Fotheringay in the Dean’s garden.
“The Bishop says you’re a sad, bad young man,” good-natured Lady Clavering whispered to him. “What’ve you been up to, eh? Nothing to upset that dear sweet ma of yours, I hope? How is your dear ma? Why hasn’t she come to visit us? We’ve been gallivanting round so much we haven’t seen any of the neighbours. Send her my love, and bring Laurar too, come eat dinner with us tomorrow!”
Mrs. Pendennis was too unwell to make the trip, but Laura and Pen turned up for the huge packed party, and Pen only got two whole minutes alone with Blanche.
“You never come down to the river anymore,” he said.
“I can’t,” Blanche shrugged. “The house is absolutely full of people.”
“Undine has left the stream, huh?” Pen said, leaning hard into his whole poetic bit.
“Undine never should have gone there in the first place,” Blanche said. “It was stupid, it was just a silly game. Besides, you’ve got way nicer consolations waiting for you back at home, don’t you?” She gave him one quick pointed look, then dropped her eyes.
If he’d wanted her, that was the exact moment to say so, and she probably would have said yes! But her mention of “consolations at home” made him think of Laura, all soft and loyal, and his mother’s dearest wish that he’d marry her adopted daughter. “Blanche — Miss Amory!” he started, voice all flustered.
“Laura is staring right at us, Mr. Pendennis,” Blanche said, and before he could say another word she’d run off back to the main group, leaving Pen standing there biting his nails, staring at the moonlight over the garden totally confused.
Laura was indeed staring at him. She was pretending to listen to Mr. Pynsent, son of Lord Rockminster and grandson of the Dowager Countess who was holding court in the best armchair, patting Sir Francis on the head like an overexcited golden retriever because she wanted to lock in his political support for the county. Turns out Pynsent and Pen had gone to Oxbridge together, back when Pen was the big popular man on campus, super successful and maybe a little supercilious towards the quieter, lower-ranked Pynsent. They met that day for the first time since graduation, and exchanged that classic half-assed British university guy nod that translates perfectly to “Confound you, what the hell are you doing here?”
“I knew that Pendennis bloke at Oxbridge,” Pynsent said to Laura. “I think he’s sweet on Miss Amory, isn’t he?”
Laura agreed, but said nothing else.
“Is he a huge county landowner? He used to run his mouth at the Union about running for Parliament. Whereabouts are his estates?”
Laura snickered. “His estates are on the other side of the river, right by the park lodge gate. That’s Fairoaks, where I live.”
“Nice, lots of pheasants there?” Pynsent laughed.
“Nine hens, one rooster, a pig, and a very old lazy pointer dog,” Laura said, grinning.
“Pendennis doesn’t do pheasant shooting then?” Pynsent asked.
“You should come visit us and see for yourself,” Laura said, highly amused that Pynsent thought her Pen was some grand big county aristocrat.
“I’d absolutely love to catch up with him again,” Pynsent said, his tone making it very clear he was far more excited to visit her than her chicken-owning cousin.
Blanche sashayed over with her most sparkling smile, begged dear Laura to sing the second part of a duet with her. Laura, being the good-natured soul she was, headed to the piano, where Pynsent stood and listened politely through the whole song, before wandering off to find his travelling companion Wagg as soon as Blanche started her solo number.
“Man, that tall one Laura is such a nice, frank, well-bred girl, Wagg,” Pynsent said. “Ringlets, very red lips — but a total genuine person.”
“What do you think of the actual hostess Blanche?” Wagg asked.
“She’s a scrawny, fake little humbug,” Pynsent said, no filter at all. “She’s forever hunching her shoulders out of her dress and ogling everyone like a flirty French chambermaid.”
“Pynsent, shut up, someone’ll hear you,” Wagg hissed.
“Oh, hey, it’s Pendennis of Boniface!” Pynsent said, waving. “Nice evening, mate, we were just talking about how charming your cousin is.”
“Wait, are you related to my old friend Major Pendennis?” Wagg asked Pen.
“His nephew. Met you at Gaunt House once,” Pen said, pulling on his most posh polite gentleman face, and the three were instant acquaintances.
The next afternoon, Pen trundled back from a totally fishless fishing trip to find Pynsent and Wagg sat in his mother’s drawing room, chatting away happily with Helen and Laura. Wagg was the nosiest man alive, he’d noted every single silly detail on his walk up the drive: old gardener John in his beat up red livery waistcoat, Pen’s white swimming trunks flapping in the breeze hung to dry on the gooseberry bushes, mismatched umbrellas and scuffed ladies’ clogs lined up by the front door. He had the soul of a former butler who’d been promoted to dinner party joker, and thought it was the funniest thing in the world when people weren’t dripping with wealth, as if no one could be respectable unless they had a carriage and shopped at a fancy French milliner. He babbled on for ages dropping the names of thirty different dukes and lords, leaving Helen who’d only been to London once before she had Pen completely bewildered, thinking he was the tackiest man alive.
Pynsent, meanwhile, was chatting happily with Laura, saying he planned to canvass the county for a seat in the next Parliament, and hoped he could count on Pen’s support — he remembered perfectly well what a brilliant orator Pen had been back in their Oxford days. Pen walked in, played the posh big-shot act for all it was worth, dropping names of all their old uni friends and acting like marquises were casual mates he ran into every day at the shops.
Then Betsy the maid, who had zero idea there were fancy visitors, flung the door wide open and trundled in holding their usual six o’clock tea tray: three chipped teacups, a dented old teapot, and a plate piled high with thick, buttered homemade bread. Pen went bright scarlet, completely mortified. Wagg stuffed his tongue in his cheek and winked so hard his eye almost stuck shut. Pynsent, for his part, thought the whole thing was totally normal — people could have tea at six if they wanted, no law against it.
On the walk back to the Park, Wagg cackled so hard he could barely breathe, and said he was going to tell old Major Pendennis the whole story the second he got back to London, about how they caught the snobby pretender Pen red-handed eating cheap thick bread and butter. Wagg told the whole exaggerated story at dinner that night, complete with silly voices, making fun of the beat up old furniture at Fairoaks, the clothes hanging on the gooseberry bushes, the maid trundling in with the tea tray. Blanche, who adored witty funny men, cackled until she cried, calling him the most delightful droll creature alive. But Pynsent was so fed up with Wagg’s cheap mockery he slammed his hand on the table, and announced “I don’t know what sort of low people you hang around with, Wagg, but those two ladies at Fairoaks are the most perfectly well-bred women I’ve ever met in my life — and I do hope, ma’am,” he added, turning straight to the Dowager Countess, “that you’ll do them the honour of paying them a call.”
Sir Francis snickered and whispered to the man next to him that that was the perfect burn, and Lady Clavering tapped Pynsent on the arm with her fan, winked, and called him a real good fellow.
After that little incident, a faint, sharp, almost sad edge crept into the way Laura spoke to Pen. She watched him laze around the house all day, smoking on the lawn, flipping through books he was too listless to actually read, and Helen noticed that little almost-scornful look cross her face sometimes. “What’s happened between you two?” Helen asked Laura, worried. “Has that wicked little Blanche been stirring up trouble?”
“Nothing at all,” Laura said.
“Then why are you looking at him like that?” Helen pressed.
“Because he’s wasting his whole potential,” Laura said softly, a little bitter. “We’re just two boring women, we’re not interesting enough for his big brilliant genius. He never leaves the garden gate, he’s bored of Captain Strong and Captain Glanders even, he’s just sitting here tied to our apron strings wasting away. He’s got all that talent, everyone at Oxford said he was going to go far, and he’s got no job, no plans to make a name for himself out in the world. He’ll never be happy stuck here with us forever.”
Helen’s heart started beating fast. “We have more than enough money if we’re careful. He barely spends a penny, I know he’d be happy here with us.”
But Laura was right, and they both knew it.
