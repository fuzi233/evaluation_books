Let us imagine, for a moment, that the separation between the brothers is complete. Let us picture George having taken his rightful place within Mr. Braddock's command, while Harry has returned to Castlewood to fulfill his duties. Yet, Harry's heart remains tethered to the army; the quiet pursuits available to him at home afford the boy no joy. He refuses to acknowledge the depth of his despair at being forced to remain beneath the homely, somber roof of Castlewood—a home rendered even more melancholy by George's absence.

Harry avoids passing through his brother's empty chambers, turning his face away; he claims George's seat at the head of the table, yet sighs heavily as he lifts his silver tankard. Madam Warrington raises the toast of "The King" with vigorous spirit every single day. On Sundays, when Harry recites the service and prays for travelers by land and sea, she adds a peculiar solemnity to her plea: *"We beseech Thee to hear us."* She insists on speaking constantly of George, always with a cheerful demeanor, as if his return is merely a matter of time. She strides confidently into his vacant room, her head held high, masking any outward sign of grief. She observes that his books, linens, and papers are arranged with meticulous care. She speaks of him with profound respect, appealing to the elderly servants during meals about tasks to be completed "when Mr. George returns."

Mrs. Mountain, conversely, is perpetually on the verge of weeping whenever George's name is uttered, causing Harry's face to wear an expression of ghastly alarm. In stark contrast, his mother maintains a grave and sedate composure. She makes far more errors at piquet and backgammon than one would expect from a lady of her station; the servants note that she rises before dawn, fully dressed and ready. She has petitioned Mr. Dempster to resume his residence at Castlewood. Though she is no longer severe or haughty—as she certainly once was—her speech with the household is quiet, her assertions gentle, and her replies mild. She talks endlessly of her father and his campaigns, noting how he emerged from them with only minor injuries, expressing hope that her eldest son will fare similarly.

George writes frequently to his brother, and now that the army is on the march, he compiles a rough journal, forwarding it as opportunity allows. This document is devoured with delight and eagerness by the recipient. More than once, it is read aloud during family councils on long summer evenings. Madam Esmond sits upright at her tea-table, refusing to lean on the back of her chair; little Fanny Mountain busies herself with sewing; Mr. Dempster and Mrs. Mountain play cards; and the silent, aged servants of the house move softly in the twilight, listening intently to the words of their young master.

Listen to Harry Warrington reading his brother's letter. As we gaze at the slender characters on the yellowed page, carefully preserved and set aside, we can almost conjure the image of the writer and the reader—and yet, alas! they seem as though they never existed. Are their portraits but faint echoes in frames of tarnished gold? Were they truly real, or mere phantasms? Did they live and die once? Did they love each other as true brothers and loyal gentlemen? Can we still hear their voices from the past?

Surely I know Harry's voice. There he sits in the warmth of a summer evening, reading his young brother's simple account:

*"It must be admitted that the provinces act scurvily toward His Majesty King George II., and his representative here burns with fury. Virginia is bad enough, and poor Maryland is scarcely better, but Pennsylvania is the worst of all. We implore them to send us troops from home to fight the French, promising to maintain them upon arrival. Yet, we not only fail to keep this promise, making scant provision for our defenders, but our populace insists on charging exorbitant prices for cattle and stores, actually cheating the soldiers who have come to fight their battles. No wonder the General swears, and the troops are sulky. The delays have been endless. Due to the failure of the various provinces to supply promised stores and means of locomotion, weeks and months have passed. During this time, undoubtedly, the French have strengthened their positions on our frontier and reclaimed the forts we had previously captured.*

*Though there will never be any love lost between me and Colonel Washington, I must admit that your favorite—I am not jealous, Hal—is a brave man and a capable officer. The family respects him deeply, and the General constantly seeks his opinion. Indeed, he is nearly the only man who has seen the Indians in their war paint, and I confess I believe he was right in firing upon Monsieur Jumonville last year.*

*There is to be no further suit regarding the quarrel at Benson's Tavern, nor the proposed duel between Colonel Washington and a certain young gentleman whose name I shall withhold. Captain Waring wished to pursue the matter upon entering camp, delivering a message from Captain Grace. Your friend, who is as bold as Hector, was inclined to accept the challenge, employing a brother aide-de-camp, Colonel Wingfield, on his side. However, when Wingfield learned the circumstances—that the dispute arose from Grace being drunk and fomented by Waring's intoxication, and that the two officers of the 44th chose to insult a militia officer—he swore that Colonel Washington would not meet the 44th men. He resolved to take the matter directly to His Excellency, who would convene a court-martial for the captains regarding brawling with the militia, drunkenness, and indecent behavior. Thus, the captains were forced to sheath their toasting irons and swallow their wrath. They were good-natured enough when sober, eating their humble pie with excellent appetite at a reconciliation dinner hosted by Colonel Washington, where he behaved as perfectly stupid and correct as Prince Prettyman needs to be. Hang him! He has no faults, and that is precisely why I dislike him. When he marries that widow—ah, me! what a dreary life she will lead!"*

"I wonder at the taste of some men, and the effrontery of some women," remarked Madam Esmond, setting down her teacup. "I wonder at any woman who, having been married once, forgets herself enough to marry again! Do you agree, Mountain?"

"Monstrous!" exclaimed Mountain, with a peculiar look.

Dempster kept his eyes steadily fixed on his glass of punch. Harry appeared as though he were choking with laughter or some other concealed emotion, but his mother interjected, "Go on, Harry! Continue with your brother's journal. He writes well; but, ah, will he ever be able to write like my papa?"

Harry resumed: *"We maintain the strictest order here in camp. The orders against drunkenness and misconduct among the men are severe. The roll of each company is called at morning, noon, and night, and a return of the absent and disorderly is submitted to the commanding officer of the regiment, who ensures proper punishment. The men are punished, and the drummers are constantly at work. Oh, Harry, it made one sick to witness the first blood drawn from a great, strong white back, and to hear the piteous yell of the poor fellow."*

"Oh, horrid!" cried Madam Esmond. "I think I would have murdered Ward if he had flogged me. Thank Heaven he escaped with only a crack of the ruler! The men, I say, are looked after carefully enough. I wish the officers were treated with similar care. The Indians have just broken up their camp and retired in a rage, because the young officers were forever drinking with the squaws—and—and—hum—ha."

Here, Mr. Harry paused, unwilling to proceed with the narrative in the presence of little Fanny, who sat primly in her chair beside her mother, working her sampler.

"Pass over that about the odious tipsy creatures," commanded Madam.

And Harry began, in a loud tone, a much more satisfactory account: *"Each regiment holds Divine Service at the head of its colors every Sunday. The General does everything in the power of mortal man to prevent plundering and encourages the surrounding populace to bring in provisions. He has declared that soldiers shall be shot if they dare to interrupt or molest market-goers. He has ordered the price of provisions to be raised by a penny a pound and has lent money from his own pocket to support the camp. Altogether, he is a strange compound, this General. He flogs his men without mercy, yet gives without stint. He swears terrible oaths in conversation and tells stories that would shock Mountain after dinner—but he never misses service. He adores his Great Duke, keeping his name constantly on his lips. Our two regiments both served in Scotland, where I dare say Mr. Dempster knows the color of their facings."*

"We saw the tails of their coats, as well as their facings," grumbled the little Jacobite tutor.

When either of us falls ill, we are almost as good friends as ever. But I feel somehow as if I cannot forgive him for having wronged me. Good Powers! How I have hated him for these past months!
