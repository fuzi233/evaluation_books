The worthy leech went home with his fingers wrapped and his pride rawer than the skin. To his housekeeper he called it, not a cauterizing, not a salutary application—no; a “bad burn.” Such are the words men keep for their own pain: we sear our neighbour’s flesh with the gravity of science, and cry out like children when the flame licks our own.

His housekeeper—an old hand at kitchens and sickness both—set him down by the hearth where the pot always murmured, and dressed him with one of those village salves that smell of churn and herb-garden: sweet whey thickened with goose-grease, plantain bruised fine on a board, and a pinch of soot from the chimney to “draw.” She warmed the poultice at the edge of the coals until it turned glossy and gave up a faint green breath, then laid it on as gently as she would butter on new bread. He submitted like a lamb to her homely certainty; for here, whatever his temper toward travellers, his sole object was cure.

Meanwhile, with his other hand, he made out his bill for broken phials, and with his tongue—sharp as his lancet when gain was in question—he laid plans to have the strangers clapped in prison. He swore before the magistrate that they, being unknown men and owing him money, meant to slip away at once from the town.

Alas, it was his unlucky day. His sincere desire and honest endeavour to forswear himself were baffled by a circumstance he had never dreamt of, nor indeed believed possible: he had spoken the truth—and in an affidavit.

The officers went to the Silver Lion and found the birds flown. They hurried down to the river; there, on hearing which way the boat had gone, they started up the bank in hot pursuit.

This brief start the friends owed to Denys’s cool eye. He had laughed—laughed till it rang out like a bell over the inn-yard, a peal that would have heartened a dying man—and had flung his watchword to the air thrice; then, in a breath, he sobered, as if some change in the very sounds around them warned him.

“Gerard,” said he, “Dusseldorf is no place for us. That old fellow went off too quiet for such a babbler; and strangers here have short shrift. The bailiff is his friend. In five minutes we shall lie in a dungeon for laying hands on a Dusseldorf dignity. Art strong enough to hobble to the water? It is hard by. Once there, thou hast but to lie down in a boat instead of a bed; and what’s the odds?”

“The odds, Denys? untold—and all in favour of the boat,” cried Gerard, with that sudden heat in him the fever loved to ride. “I pine for Rome; for Rome is my road to Sevenbergen; and then we shall lie in the boat, but on the Rhine—the famous Rhine; the cool, refreshing Rhine. I feel its breezes coming. The very sight will cure a little hop-o’-my-thumb fever like mine. Away! away!”

Denys, seeing his friend thus kindled, paid the landlord in haste, and they made for the river, Gerard limping but eager as if the sound of water already ran in his blood. At the bank they learned, to their dismay, that the public boat had put off half an hour since, and no other would start that day, the afternoon being already advanced. But by dint of asking many questions—questions thrown as one flings grain to call fowl, till folk gathered—and by the bustle of it, they drew out an offer: an old man with two sons would take them in a private boat.

A bystander mocked aloud. “The current is too strong for three oars.”

“Then my comrade and I will help row,” said Gerard, the invalid, whose spirit had forgotten his weakness.

“No need,” said the old man, dry as old bread. “Bless your silly heart—he owns t’other boat.”

A powerful breeze sat right astern as if the river itself were in league with escape. The boatmen stepped a broad sail; it filled with a clap and a long breathing sound, and at the same time they dipped their oars. The Rhine answered in its own language—rush and slap, the creak of rowlocks, the soft hiss along the planks—and all that moving music, so clean and living, seemed to scour the inside of Gerard’s head where the fever had hammered. The town’s noise fell behind; the river’s sound took possession, and with it came that strange easing of the mind that follows when one’s ears are no longer flogged by angry voices and street-clatter, but bathed in a steadier element.

“Art better, lad, for the river breeze?” asked the old man.

“Much better,” said Gerard, drawing in the damp air that smelt of mud and reeds and far-off fires. “But indeed the doctor did me good.”

“The doctor?” the boatman sniffed. “Why, you would none of his cures.”

“No,” said Gerard, colouring, “but I mean—you will say I am nought—but knocking the old fool down—somehow—it soothed me.”

“Amiable dove!” cried Denys. “How thy little character opens more and more every day, like a rosebud. I read thee all wrong at first.”

“Nay, Denys, mistake me not,” said Gerard quickly, anxious even in confession to keep his own measure. “I trust I had borne with his idle threats, though in sooth his voice went through my poor ears; but he was an infidel, or next door to one, and such I have been taught to abhor. Did he not as good as say we owed our inward parts to men with long Greek names, and not to Him whose name is but a syllable, but whose hand is over all the earth? Pagan!”

“So you knocked him down forthwith—like a good Christian.”

“Now, Denys, you will still be jesting. Take not an ill man’s part. Had it been a thunderbolt from Heaven, he had met but his due; yet he took but a sorry bolster from this weak arm.”

“What weak arm?” Denys’s eyes danced. “I have lived among arms, and by Samson’s hairy pow never saw I one more like a catapult. The bolster wrapped round his nose and the two ends kissed behind his head, and his forehead resounded; and had he been Goliath, or Julius Caesar, instead of an old quacksalver, down he had gone. Saint Denys guard me from such feeble opposites as thou! and above all from their weak arms—thou diabolical young hypocrite.”

The Rhine took its bends; at times the wind struck them on the quarter, and the boat would heel and shiver, sail straining with a low thunder in its cloth. Then all aboard shifted to the weather side to trim her—save one small body that weighed nothing in the world’s balances: a child of five years or so, the old man’s grandson and darling, who had slipped aboard at the last instant. He sat where he pleased, little hands on the wet gunwale, his hair blown into his eyes; the river’s breath and the sail’s great sigh seemed to make him more solemn than any chapel.

They went merrily enough, not knowing that a whole posse of constables, with the bailiff’s writ, were striding the east bank after them, and that their pursuers gained as much by the current as the fugitives by the wind.

And now, while the oars rose and fell and the sail boomed, Gerard’s mind, always running ahead of his feet, suddenly took alarm at their direction.

“Oh, Denys,” he cried, almost startled, “this is not your road.”

“I know it,” said Denys quietly. “But what can I do? I cannot leave thee till the fever leaves thee; and it is on thee still—for thou art both red and white by turns; I have watched thee. I must e’en go on to Cologne, I doubt, and then strike across.”

“Thank Heaven,” said Gerard, with sudden joy that had in it, too, a hidden relief. Then, as if to make that detour seem piety and not need, he added eagerly, “’Twere a sin to be so near Cologne and not see it. Oh, man, it is a vast and ancient city such as I have often dreamed of, but ne’er had the good luck to see. Me miserable, by what hard fortune do I come to it now? Well then, Denys,”—and he warmed to his tale, as if words themselves could build the city on the air—“it is old enough to have been founded by a Roman lady in the first century of grace, and sacked by Attila the barbarous, and afterwards sore defaced by the Norman Lothaire. And it has a church for every week in the year, forbye chapels and churches innumerable of convents and nunneries; and above all, the stupendous minster yet unfinished; and therein, but in their own chapel, lie the three kings that brought gifts to our Lord—Melchior gold, and Gaspar frankincense, and Balthazar the black king, he brought myrrh; and over their bones stands the shrine the wonder of the world. It is of ever-shining brass brighter than gold, studded with images fairly wrought, and inlaid with exquisite devices, and brave with colours; and two broad stripes run to and fro, of jewels so great, so rare, each might adorn a crown or ransom its wearer at need; and upon it stand the three kings curiously counterfeited, two in solid silver, richly gilt—these be bareheaded; but he of Aethiop ebony, and beareth a golden crown; and in the midst our blessed Lady, in virgin silver, with Christ in her arms; and at the corners, in golden branches, four goodly waxen tapers do burn night and day. Holy eyes have watched and renewed that light unceasingly for ages, and holy eyes shall watch them in saecula. I tell thee, Denys, the oldest song, the oldest Flemish or German legend, found them burning, and they shall light the earth to its grave.”

Denys listened, half amused, half taken—less by saints than by Gerard’s tongue, which could salt and spice a tale till it became sustenance. The river’s steady chant under Gerard’s speech made it seem as if the Rhine itself were carrying old names forward, lest they die on land.

“And there is Saint Ursel’s church,” Gerard went on, “a British saint’s, where lie her bones and all the other virgins her fellows; eleven thousand were they who died for the faith, being put to the sword by barbarous Moors, on the three-and-twentieth day of October, two hundred and thirty-eight. Their bones are piled in the vaults, and many of their skulls are in the church. Saint Ursel’s is in a thin golden case, and stands on the high altar, but shown to humble Christians only on solemn days.”

“Eleven thousand virgins!” Denys burst out. “What babies German men must have been in days of yore. Well—would all their bones might turn flesh again, and their skulls sweet faces, as we pass through the gates. ’Tis odds but some of them are wearied of their estate by this time.”

“Tush, Denys!” said Gerard, stung. “Why wilt thou, being good, still make thyself seem evil? If thy wishing-cap be on, pray that we may meet the meanest she of all those wise virgins in the next world, and to that end let us reverence their holy dust in this one. And then there is the church of the Maccabees, and the cauldron in which they and their mother Solomona were boiled by a wicked king for refusing to eat swine’s flesh.”

“Oh, peremptory king!” cried Denys. “And pig-headed Maccabees! I had eaten bacon with my pork liever than change places at the fire with my meat.”

“What scurvy words are these? It was their faith.”

“Nay, bridle thy choler,” said Denys, lightly, as if stroking a cat the wrong way on purpose. “Tell me—are there nought but churches in this thy so vaunted city? For I affect rather Sir Knight than Sir Priest.”

“Ay, marry,” said Gerard, glad to display other treasures, “there is an university near a hundred years old; and there is a market-place, no fairer in the world, and at the four sides of it houses great as palaces; and there is a stupendous senate-house all covered with images, and at the head of them stands one of stout Herman Gryn, a soldier like thyself, lad.”

“Ay,” said Denys, pricked at once. “Tell me of him! what feat of arms earned him his niche?”

“A rare one. He slew a lion in fair combat, with nought but his cloak and a short sword. He thrust the cloak in the brute’s mouth, and cut his spine in twain; and there is the man’s effigy and eke the lion’s to prove it. The like was never done but by three more, I ween: Samson was one, and Lysimachus of Macedon another, and Benaiah, a captain of David’s host.”

“Marry! three tall fellows. I would like well to sup with them all to-night.”

“So would not I,” said Gerard drily.

Denys eyed him with a soldier’s wonder. “But tell me—when wast thou in Cologne?”

“Never but in the spirit,” said Gerard. “I prattle with the good monks by the way, and they tell me all the notable things both old and new.”

“Ay, ay, have not I seen your nose under their very cowls?” Denys chuckled. “But when I speak of matters that are out of sight, my words they are small, and the thing it was big; now thy words be as big or bigger than the things. Art a good limner with thy tongue; I have said it. And for a saint—art as ready with hand, or steel, or bolster—as any poor sinner living. And so—shall I tell thee which of all these things thou hast described draws me to Cologne?”

“Ay, Denys.”

“Thou—and thou only; no dead saint, but my living friend and comrade true. ’Tis thou alone draws Denys of Burgundy to Cologne.”

Gerard hung his head, and for a moment the river’s voice seemed louder, as if to cover what could not be put into any dialect without losing its heart.

At that instant the boat’s little world was broken by a new sound: a thin, sudden crying from aft. One of the younger boatmen looked round and snapped, half in jest, half in anger, “What ails little turnip-face now?”

The child—thus christened—had come back from the bow grave as a judge, and burst out wailing without warning. The men fell upon him with questions, some coaxing, some sharp: Had he hurt himself? Had he over-ate? Was he frightened? Cold? Sick? An idiot? To all he made but one answer, the same syllables over and over, such as English pens write “oh! oh! oh!” and French pens “hi! hi! hi!”—as if the very shapes men give to a child’s cry belonged to their own tongues, not his pain.

“Who can tell what ails the peevish brat?” snarled the young boatman at last. Then, shading his eyes, he cried, “Rather look this way and tell me whom be these after!”

The old man and his other son peered to the east bank. Four men were coming along there, walking fast, keeping the boat abreast as the bends allowed. At the sight, the rowers held their stroke a moment and huddled in the stern, whispering, throwing looks first at their passengers, then back at the men on shore.

If they had spent their wits on the turnip-face mystery, they might have done better; but grown minds are often deaf to the meanings of small cries, and quick to the whisper of law and profit.

“If ’tis as I doubt,” muttered one of the sons, “why not give them a squeak for their lives? Let us make for the west bank.”

The old man set his jaw. “What,” said he, “run our heads into trouble for strangers? Are ye mad? Nay—let us rather cross to the east side; still side with the strong arm! That is my rede. What say you, Werter?”

“I say—please yourselves,” returned the other, as one who had learned that a man’s peace lies sometimes in not choosing.

What age and youth could not settle, the wind decided, without malice and without mercy. A squall struck sudden; the sail snapped like a whip, the little vessel heeled over, and the men sprang to windward to save her trim.

Then—horror: in the very boat, from stem to stern, a ditch of water came rushing to leeward, a cold weight that did not belong to wind or wave but to treachery. In the next breath the world turned, and there was no boat as a sheltering thing—only the Rhine, the cold and rushing Rhine, closing over heads and words alike.

Turnip-face had drawn the plug.

Gerard, flung out, felt the river take him like a giant hand. Though he could swim like a duck, yet the best swimmer, pitched from a capsized boat, must sink ere he can strike. The dark water bubbled loudly over his head; it filled his ears so that for an instant he was blind and deaf, as if all sound and sight had been kneaded into one blackness. Then he broke up again, choking, the air tasting of wet wood and river-mud; he saw the black boat-bottom uppermost, and figures clinging to it like beetles to bark.

He shook his head like a water-dog and made for it, driven by mere instinct. But before he reached it he heard behind him—not loud, but deep with manly distress, as if the river had stolen half the breath from the speaker—“Adieu, comrade, adieu!”

He turned, and there was poor Denys sinking, sinking, dragged down by his wretched arbalest. His face was gone pale as whey; his eyes stared wide, fastened on Gerard with a despair that no jests could sweeten.

Gerard gave a wild cry—love and terror together—and struck out toward him, cleaving the water madly. The next moment Denys went under. The next, Gerard plunged after him.

On the bank the officers, coming up, unwound cords from their waists; one knotted a rope in haste, and flung the end out over the rushing, clamorous Rhine.
