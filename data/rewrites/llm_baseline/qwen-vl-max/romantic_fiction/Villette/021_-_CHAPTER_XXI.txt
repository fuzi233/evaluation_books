Three days more, and I must return to the _pensionnat_. I counted each moment as if time were marked on a clock—each second a tiny loss, each minute a step toward an end I longed to reach yet dreaded. I wished the hours would slow, but they slipped away like water through fingers. Before I knew it, they were gone, even as I still clung to them.

“Lucy won’t leave us today,” Mrs. Bretton said gently at breakfast, “we can easily ask for another delay.”

“I wouldn’t ask for one, even if I could,” I replied. “I want this farewell over with. I want to be back in the Rue Fossette again. I must go now—this morning—my trunk is packed and secured.”

Yet my departure was not entirely in my hands. Graham had promised to accompany me, but he was occupied all day, returning only at dusk. Then came a brief exchange of words. Mrs. Bretton and her son urged me to stay one night longer. I wanted to cry—I was so restless, so eager to leave. It felt like the criminal on the scaffold yearning for the axe to fall: I just wanted the pain to end. How deeply I felt it, they could never know. My emotions were beyond their understanding.

It was dark when Dr. John helped me from the carriage at Madame Beck’s door. The lamp above glowed softly; rain drizzled down, as it had all day. The lamplight shimmered on the wet pavement. This was the same kind of night that had brought me here not a year ago—the very same scene. I remembered the shape of each paving stone, once noted idly while my heart pounded with anxiety, waiting for the door to open. I stood then alone, a supplicant, unknown and afraid. On that night, too, I had met him—Dr. John—who now stood beside me. Had I ever told him of that first meeting? No. And I never felt the need to. It was a quiet memory, kept safely within me, where it belonged.

Graham rang the bell. The door opened instantly—this was the hour when half-boarders left, so Rosine was alert.

“Don’t come in,” I said, but he stepped into the well-lit vestibule anyway. I didn’t want him to see how close I was to tears. His nature was too tender—he would feel compelled to soothe, to heal, even when nothing could truly be mended.

“Hold fast, Lucy,” he said. “Think of my mother and me as true friends. We’ll never forget you.”

“And I will never forget you, Dr. John,” I whispered.

My trunk was carried inside. We shook hands. He turned to leave—but hesitated. He hadn’t done enough, hadn’t said enough, to satisfy his generous heart.

“Lucy,” he called after me, “will you feel terribly alone here?”

“At first, yes.”

“Well, my mother will visit soon. And I’ll do something—I’ll write. Just whatever cheerful nonsense comes to mind. Will you let me?”

“Good, gallant heart,” I thought to myself. But I smiled and shook my head. “No need. Don’t burden yourself. You write to *me*—you’ll have no time.”

“Oh, I’ll find time. Goodbye!”

He was gone. The heavy door slammed shut. The axe had fallen. The pain was real.

I gave myself no time to think or mourn—swallowing my tears as though they were wine—I moved directly to Madame Beck’s sitting room to make the formal visit expected of me. She received me with practiced warmth, even showing a hint of affection, though brief. Ten minutes later, I was dismissed. From there, I went to the refectory, where pupils and teachers gathered for evening study. I was welcomed again—not with hollow formality, I believed, but with genuine kindness. After that, I was free to return to the dormitory.

“And will Graham really write?” I asked, sinking onto the edge of my bed, weary.

Reason crept up to me through the dim twilight of the long chamber, whispering quietly: “He may write once. His kindness might inspire him. But it cannot last—it must not repeat itself. To build your hopes on such a promise is folly. To mistake a fleeting puddle holding one sip for a spring that feeds the seasons—that is madness.”

I bowed my head. I sat in silence for an hour. Reason continued, placing a cold hand on my shoulder, touching my ear with lips as blue as winter.

“If,” she murmured, “if he should write—what then? Do you imagine pleasure in replying? Fool! I warn you. Keep your answer short. Expect no joy—no comfort—no expansion of feeling. Let no faculty bloom. Allow no warm exchange. Nurture no bond of friendship…”

“But I’ve spoken to Graham,” I pleaded. “You didn’t object.”

“No,” she said. “I needed not. Conversation is good discipline. You speak imperfectly. While you talk, you remember your place—you are reminded of hardship, poverty, sacrifice. Your words carry the weight of it.”

“But,” I insisted, “when speech fails, when presence is weak, isn’t it right to use writing—to express what faltering lips cannot?”

Reason answered only, “At your peril you hold to that belief. At your peril you let it guide any word you write.”

“But if I feel—may I never express it?”

“Never!” she declared.

I groaned beneath her harshness. Never—never—oh, cruel word! This hag, this Reason, refused me light, laughter, hope. She demanded only crushing, submission, despair. According to her, life was merely labor for bread, endurance of pain, and constant mourning. Reason might be right—but no wonder we sometimes rebel, flee from her rod, and give ourselves one truant hour to Imagination—her gentle, bright rival, our sweet comfort, our divine hope. We must break bounds, despite the vengeance that follows. Reason is as vengeful as a demon. For me, she was always a venomous stepmother. I obeyed her not out of love, but fear. Long ago, I would have died under her sting—her emptiness, her coldness, her endless blows—had it not been for that kinder power who holds my secret allegiance.

Often Reason cast me out into winter darkness, tossing me a bone dogs had abandoned. She swore her stores held nothing more. Yet when I looked up, I saw a face among stars—a radiant one, brighter than all—sending down a ray of sympathy. A spirit softer than human reason descended, bringing air like eternal summer, flowers that never fade, trees whose fruit is life, breezes from a world without sun. She fed my hunger with strange, sweet food gathered by angels at dawn, eased my unbearable fears, calmed my weariness, revived my paralyzed hope. Divine, compassionate, sustaining force! When I kneel before anything less than God, it shall be at thy white wings—whether on mountain or plain. Temples have been built to the Sun, altars to the Moon. Oh, greater glory! To thee no hands raise stones, no lips utter prayers—yet hearts across ages worship thee faithfully. Thou hast no walls, no dome—thy temple is space, thy rites the harmony of worlds!

Sovereign, complete! Thy strength lies in martyrs, thy triumph in heroes. Unquestioned deity—thou art beyond decay.

This heavenly daughter remembered me tonight. She saw me weep and came with comfort: “Sleep,” she said. “Sleep sweetly—I will gild your dreams.”

She kept her word, watching me through the night. But at dawn, Reason took her place. I woke with a start—the rain beat against the windows, the wind moaned. The night-lamp flickered on its black stand in the middle of the dormitory. Day had broken. I pitied those whose minds are stunned by sorrow instead of stirred. This morning, the pang of waking pulled me from bed as if gripped by a giant hand. How swiftly I dressed in the chill of the early dawn! How deeply I drank the icy water from my carafe—my usual tonic, like others who seek solace in strong drink when shaken by grief.

Soon the bell rang its _réveillée_ for the whole school. Dressed, I descended alone to the refectory, where the stove burned and the air was warm. Elsewhere in the house, it was cold—the biting severity of a continental winter. Though November had barely begun, a northern wind had already brought frost across Europe. I had disliked the stoves when I first arrived, but now I found comfort in them—as English folk do by a fireside.

Seated near the stove, I fell into deep thought about life, chance, fate. My mind, calmer and stronger than the night before, set strict rules: no looking back at past joys, no indulgence in nostalgia. Only patient movement through the present wilderness, trust in faith, attention to the guiding cloud and pillar—both awe-inspiring and illuminating. No idolatry of emotion, no longing for a distant paradise that may only be seen from a dying height.

Gradually, a mingling of strength and sorrow wound itself around my heart, steadying my pulse, preparing me for the day. I lifted my head.

As I mentioned, I sat near the stove, built into the wall beneath the refectory and the carré, warming both rooms. Beside it was a window opening into the carré. As I raised my eyes, a cap-tassel, a brow, two eyes filled the pane. Those eyes fixed on mine. They were watching me. I hadn’t known I was crying—until now I felt the tears on my cheek.

What a strange house, where no corner is safe, where no tear escapes notice, no thought remains hidden. And this new intruder—this outdoor, male spy—what business brought him here at such an hour? What right did he have to look at me so? No other professor would dare cross the carré before class. M. Emanuel cared nothing for hours or propriety. He needed a reference book from the first-class library. He came to find it—and passed the refectory. He wore eyes everywhere—he saw me through the window. Now he opened the door and stood before me.

“Mademoiselle, vous êtes triste.”

“Monsieur, j’en ai bien le droit.”

“You are sad and rebellious,” he continued. “I see two tears on your cheek—hot as sparks, salty as sea crystals. As I speak, you watch me oddly. Shall I tell you what you remind me of?”

“Monsieur,” I said, “I will soon be called to prayers. My time for conversation is brief—please excuse——”

“I excuse everything,” he interrupted. “My mood is so gentle—neither rebuff nor insult could disturb it. You remind me of a young wild creature, newly caught, untamed, facing the first approach of the tamer—with fire and fear in its eyes.”

Unjust intrusion—rude, reckless, especially from a teacher. He sought to provoke a response. I’d seen him rouse passion before. In me, he would find no satisfaction. I remained silent.

“You look,” he said, “like one who would take a draught of sweet poison and reject wholesome bitters.”

“Indeed, I’ve never liked bitters. Nor believe them wholesome. And whatever is sweet—poison or food—cannot deny its own delight. Better to die quickly with pleasure than live long without charm.”

“Yet,” he replied, “you should take your bitter dose daily—if I had the power to give it. And as for the beloved poison—I would shatter the cup that holds it.”

I sharply turned my head away—partly because his presence disturbed me, partly to avoid questions. Fear that answering might betray me.

“Come,” he said more softly, “tell me the truth. You grieve at parting from friends—is it not so?”

The softness was no more welcome than the curiosity. I stayed silent. He entered the room, sat on the bench two yards away, and persisted, patiently, in trying to draw me out—efforts doomed to fail, for I could not speak. Finally, I begged to be left alone. As I spoke, my voice trembled, my head sank onto my arms and the table. I wept quietly, but fiercely. He stayed a while. I did not lift my eyes or speak until the closing door and his retreating steps told me he was gone. These tears brought relief.

I had time to wash my face before breakfast. I suppose I appeared as composed as anyone else—though not quite as cheerful as the young woman who took the seat opposite, twinkling with amusement, and stretched a white hand across the table to shake mine. Miss Fanshawe, returned from travel and flirtation, had grown plump, her cheeks round as apples. I had last seen her in evening dress. But now, in her school gown—a careless peignoir of dark blue, faintly plaided with black—she seemed even more charming. The contrast made her skin glow, her bloom freshen, her golden hair shine.

“I’m glad you’re back, Timon,” she said. Timon was one of her many names for me. “You don’t know how often I’ve missed you in this dreary place.”

“Oh, have you? Then surely you have work for me—stockings to mend, perhaps.” I never believed Ginevra acted without motive.

“Crabbed and crusty as ever!” she laughed. “I expected nothing less. It wouldn’t be you otherwise. But now, come, grandmother—do you still love coffee and hate pistolets? Are you ready to trade?”

“Take your own way.”

Her way was to make things convenient for me. She didn’t care for the weak, sour coffee served at school. But she loved the morning pistolets—fresh-baked rolls, delicious, and given in generous portions. Since I didn’t need mine, I always gave half to her. She, in turn, sometimes shared her coffee. This morning, I welcomed the drink. I wasn’t hungry—but I was parched. I don6't know why I chose to share with her rather than another. Or why, when we shared a drink on walks, I always let her have the larger portion—be it beer, wine, or milk. So it was, and she knew it. Thus, though we quarreled daily, we never drifted apart.

After breakfast, I retreated to the first classe, where I read—or more often, thought—alone until the nine-o’clock bell signaled the arrival of externes and demi-pensionnaires, and the start of the day’s bustle, which lasted until five P.M., unrelenting.

I had just seated myself when a tap came at the door.

“Pardon, Mademoiselle,” said a pensionnaire, entering softly. She took a book from her desk and withdrew on tiptoe, muttering as she passed, “Que mademoiselle est appliquée!”

Appliquée indeed! The tools of study lay before me, but I did nothing. Nothing had been done, and nothing would be. The world gives credit for virtues we lack. Madame Beck herself considered me a serious scholar, often warning me not to study too much—lest “the blood go to my head.” Everyone in the Rue Fossette believed “Meess Lucie” was learned—except M. Emanuel, who, by some means of his own, had glimpsed my true abilities and enjoyed chuckling in my ear over their modest limits. For my part, I cared little. I loved thinking my own thoughts. I enjoyed reading a few books—those where the writer’s soul was visible in style or sentiment. I lost interest in characterless works, however clever. I understood my mind had limits—thankful for what it had, content with its scope, never craving more.

The polite pupil had scarcely left when another burst in—unannounced, uninvited. I would have known him even blind. My reserved manner had gradually shaped the behavior of those around me. Rarely now did I suffer rudeness. At first, Germans would slap my shoulder, challenge me to races. Labassecourien girls would grab my arm, drag me to the playground, insist I join games. But these had stopped—without my needing to forbid them. Now, only one person dared familiarities—and since she was English, I could endure it. Ginevra Fanshawe often caught me crossing the carré, spun me into a forced waltz, and delighted in my disarray. She was the one who now broke into “my scholarly solitude.” She carried a large music book under her arm.

“Go practice,” I said at once. “Off to the little salon!”

“Not till I’ve talked to you, chère amie. I know where you’ve been on vacation, and how you’ve been courting beauty and enjoying life like any other belle. I saw you at the concert the other night—dressed, actually, like everyone else. Who is your tailor?”

“Tittle-tattle begins prettily! My tailor? Nonsense. Go away, Ginevra. I really don’t want company.”

“But when I want yours so much, ange farouche, does your slight matter? Dieu merci! We know how to handle our gifted countrywoman—the learned ‘ourse Britannique.’ And so, Ourson, you know Isidore?”

“I know John Bretton.”

“Oh, hush!” (She put her fingers in her ears.) “You crack my eardrums with your Englishisms. But—how is our dear John? Tell me. Poor man, he must be devastated. What did he say about my behavior the other night? Wasn’t I cruel?”

“Do you think I noticed you?”

“It was a wonderful evening. Oh, that divine de Hamal! Watching the others sulk and fade in the distance—then the old lady—my future mother-in-law! But I fear Lady Sara and I were a bit rude in teasing her.”

“Lady Sara never teased her. And as for you—don’t worry. Mrs. Bretton will survive your jest.”

“She may—old ladies are tough. But poor John! Tell me what he said. I saw he was shattered.”

“He said you looked as if you were already Madame de Hamal.”

“Did he?” she cried, delighted. “He noticed that? How charming! I thought he’d be furious with jealousy.”

“Ginevra, have you truly finished with Dr. Bretton? Do you want him to give you up?”

“Oh! You know he _can’t_—but wasn’t he furious?”

“Quite,” I agreed. “As mad as a March hare.”

“Well, and how _ever_ did you get him home?”

“How _ever_, indeed! Have you no pity for his poor mother and me? Imagine us holding him down in the carriage, him raging between us, nearly driving us all mad. Even the coachman got confused—we lost our way.”

“You don’t say so? You’re teasing me. Now, Lucy Snowe—”

“I assure you it’s true—and true that Dr. Bretton refused to stay in the carriage. He broke free and insisted on riding outside.”

“And afterwards?”

“Afterwards—when he finally reached home—the scene was unbearable.”

“Oh, but describe it—you know it’s fun!”

“Fun for _you_, Miss Fanshawe? But”—with solemn gravity—“you know the proverb: ‘What is sport to one may be death to another.’”

“Go on, darling Timon.”

“Conscientiously, I cannot—unless you swear you have a heart.”

“I have—one so vast, you don’t know!”

“Good. Then you’ll understand Dr. Graham Bretton refusing his supper—the chicken, the sweetbread untouched. Then—well, it’s pointless to dwell on the details. Suffice it to say, his mother had never struggled so hard to keep his sheets tucked in during his worst childhood fits.”

“He wouldn’t lie still?”

“He wouldn’t lie still. The sheets could be tucked—but keeping them tucked in was the battle.”
