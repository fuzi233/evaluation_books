A short while later, Mr. Gryce could be observed standing in the outer room, his gaze drifting curiously over the assembled crowd. His mind was still troubled by an unanswered question, and he hoped the gathering might provide the solution. He need not have waited long. In a quiet corner stood Miss Butterworth, her demeanor calm and composed, clearly anticipating the outcome of the intervention she had undoubtedly orchestrated.

Gryce approached her with a smile, gently accusing her of engineering the situation. She immediately denied the charge, though with some warmth.

"I was merely present," she explained. "When the crisis arrived—when this young lady discovered her husband had abruptly departed for New York accompanied by two men—it became evident to everyone that she required a confidante who understood both her plight and the desperation of her position. That role fell to me."

"You are always the woman in the right place at the right time," Gryce replied gallantly. "So, you are already familiar with Mrs. Adams' account?"

"Yes," she confirmed. "Her initial ramblings made it clear she was the one who wielded the dagger that killed Mr. Adams. Subsequently, she offered an explanation for what we initially perceived as a heinous crime. Upon hearing her full story, Mr. Gryce, I felt no hesitation regarding our respective duties. Do you believe she will be summoned to answer for this blow? Will she face trial and conviction?"

"Madam," Gryce assured her, "there are not twelve men in this city so lacking in common sense as to label an act of obvious self-defense as 'crime.' No grand jury will return a true bill against young Mrs. Adams. You may rest easy."

The gloom vanished instantly from Miss Butterworth's eyes. "Then I may return home in peace!" she exclaimed. "These past five hours have been a trial by fire, and I feel thoroughly rattled. Shall you see me to my carriage?"

In Gryce's estimation, Miss Butterworth looked far from rattled; indeed, she appeared more serene and comfortable than ever before. Yet, she was the ultimate arbiter of her own state. Satisfied that the person she cared for was recovering under her husband's attentive care, she accepted the arm Gryce extended and moved toward the carriage.

As he helped her inside, he posed a few inquiries regarding Mr. Poindexter. "Where is Mrs. Adams' father? Did he permit his daughter to embark on such a perilous mission without offering to accompany her?"

The response was curt. "Mr. Poindexter is a man without a heart. He traveled with us to New York but refused to come to Police Headquarters. Sir, consider that the combined fury of three passionate souls—and a vengeance deeper than any I have ever known or heard of—has been squandered on a man utterly incapable of suffering. Do not speak of old John Poindexter to me. Now, if you will kindly instruct the driver to take me home to Gramercy Park, I shall inter my finger in the affairs of the police force once and for all, Mr. Gryce—positively, for the final time."

She sank back onto the carriage cushions, wearing an expression of inexorable resolve. Nevertheless, beneath this stern facade lay a quiet satisfaction, suggesting she was neither entirely displeased with herself nor unhappy with the results of her meddling in matters that typically clashed with the instincts of a refined woman.

Mr. Gryce suppressed a smile, bowing lower than usual. Under the shadow of this deep bow, the carriage rolled away. Walking slowly back, he let out a sigh. Perhaps he wondered if a case of such compelling interest would ever bring them together again in consultation?
