The document labeled "From London, April, 1760" burned quietly in the fire, reducing my mother's neat script and my own declaration of rebellion to ash. I chose this finality to spare future generations the spectacle of a son's domestic treason, just as I had previously incinerated her reply. Her letter, dignified and cold, had referred our conflict to the silent tribunal of God, asking if I could answer for my duty as she had for hers. We both appealed to the heavens, demanding judgment for our grievances, yet the great world moved on regardless. We screamed, we wept, we raced to win or lose, only to fade into the morning that shines on strangers.

My revolt was formalized in a duplicate shown to Mr. Lambert, ensuring he understood that my separation from Theo was a forced exile, not a choice. He knew the depth of my commitment; he also knew the limits of his own power. He could not force the gentle creature to drink poison, nor could he keep the bars of the wall raised forever. The wall stood, yes, but Pyramus and Thisbe whispered through the cracks. The lime and rough plaster offered resistance, but eventually, the lovers pressed through until the barrier became obsolete.

I ceased returning to Dean Street, opting instead for the open road where the air was deemed healthful. I acquired a fine horse to ride beside her carriage. The old woman at Tottenham Court recognized us, nodding with a familiarity that suggested she had hosted many such pairs before. The country air and the doctor's regimen produced a prodigious cure. Hetty served as duenna with exemplary patience, listening to the confessions of young love that filled the closed carriage. Letters flew back and forth; Gumbo's bandy legs carried messages between my quarters and Dean Street, while Mrs. Molly returned answers from Bloomsbury. By autumn, when the leaves turned pale, Miss Theo's roses bloomed again, and the cure was declared complete.

During this interlude, Mr. Warrington completed his tragedy of *Pocahontas*, accepted by Garrick, with my cousin Hagan cast as the hero. My aid to Hagan's family, disproportionate to my means, stemmed partly from the feud with Madam Esmond. Her response to my April missive arrived in autumn, matching my anger with threats of war and control. Yet her thunder reached me with no weight; my conscience supplied alternative interpretations of her scripture, rendering her oracles harmless. I told no one of my new loves, preferring to carpe diem while the railers grumbled at their ease.

I had also sent notes of sarcasm to Uncle Warrington, Aunt Madame de Bernstein, and various Lords, thanking them for obstructing my happiness. When the jovial Baronet and his family arrived in London early, they found me cheerful and assumed the match was broken. The Baroness, eager to secure a rich match, praised my wit while warning me against using it among the roturiers. Miss Badge married a gentleman of shattered fortune, and I hope Mrs. Mackshane forgives my levity. Among the guests at my aunt's house was a lady who remembered James the Second, a figure of great worldly experience. I loved her company, though I withheld the stories of the great old folks she knew—the quarrels of George the First and the Prince of Wales—for my modest muse speaks only to boys and virgins.

Yet I cannot suppress the scandal of my aunt's ambition. My Lady Warrington, tall and virtuous with "Heaven in her eye," possessed another eye fixed on the world's advancement. When my marriage seemed broken, she attempted the arts she had unsuccessfully tried on my brother Harry. It was not the Beauty, Miss Flora, who captivated me, but Dora, the Muse, who languished over me, reading my godless tragedies and offering consolation. Meanwhile, the Beauty was occupied elsewhere. My aunt presented her boy to Lady Yarmouth and placed Miles under her protection. Whether she sent her daughter to stay at the lady's house with ulterior views remains uncertain, though Aunt Bernstein hinted that without a fit of apoplexy, a Countess might soon join the Warrington family. My neighbor, Lady Claypole, is now buried, and I imagine Miles in his militia uniform, led by his parents to the lady the King honored, while the good-natured old Jezebel laid her hand upon his curly pate.

Life proceeded in pleasant occupation. Being happy myself, I could afford reconciliation with those who had merely added zest to my joy through brief obstacles. No specific plans were formed, but Theo and I knew a day would come when we needed say farewell no more. Whether a year or ten years hence, we waited. On our drives, we spied cottages suitable for young people of small means, devising schemes of delightful poverty. A cot and a brown loaf should suffice. Gumbo and Molly would serve us, as they have since. Who fears poverty at twenty? Our trials would only confirm our attachment. The "sweet sorrow" of daily parting made the next meeting more delightful. We exchanged precious letters, secrets whispered to the penny-post or delivered by Molly and Gumbo. Though my wife keeps them in a tin sugar-box and I have glanced at them once, I hereby command my heirs and executors to burn them all, unread, at our demise. My son the Captain, to whom manuscripts are unpleasant, shall perform this duty.

News arrived in successive brief letters of our dear Harry continuing as Mr. General Amherst's aide-de-camp following the death of his own glorious general. By mid-October, news of the Capitulation of Montreal and the loss of Canada reached us. A postscript announced that Hal would ask for leave to visit the old lady at home, who wrote as sulky as a bear. Captain Warrington remarked on this, though the claws could not reach me. I had written fully to my brother regarding my affairs in England, knowing the distance rendered his grievances powerless.
