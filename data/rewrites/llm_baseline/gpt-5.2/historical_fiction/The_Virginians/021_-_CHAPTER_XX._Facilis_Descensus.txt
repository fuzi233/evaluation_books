If the old Archbishop of Cambray, in that courtly romance everyone quotes when they would make sorrow seem well-bred, took such pains to paint Calypso’s despair at Ulysses’ sailing, he might—had he been more curious about the world below the saloon—have spared a line for the grief in the kitchen. For every hero who leans, white-faced, into a last embrace in a great drawing-room, there is a waiting-woman with her apron to her eyes; and every queen who waves from a marble terrace has behind her some poor girl in a back passage, waving too, with a kerchief of coarser linen, yet wetted with tears of the same salt.

So it was at Castlewood. Harry Warrington rode away a captive to love; and Gumbo, his man, followed as surely taken, though his bonds were of another fashion and his sighs must be swallowed more carefully. The fellow had made himself beloved in those lower regions where the true life of a house goes on—among the copper pans and the smoky rafters, the scullery stones always damp, the bread rising by the hearth, the bustle of trenchers and ladles, and the perpetual talk of what must be served and how. Gumbo had a hand for a minuet and a hand for a sauce; he could bring a joint to table without drying it, and could set a pot to sing without letting it boil sullenly over. He played a tune too; and good humour, like good broth, went further then than many a fine speech. The women, who are charitable where men are censorious, were all for him. If the men in the servants’ hall muttered that the Virginian’s black man strutted too much, the maids thought his strut handsome, and the kitchen-maid forgave even the boldness of his Ashantee kingship. In those days they did not look upon a negro in England with the same sour, frightened hostility that later times have taught the white to practise—there was condescension enough, God knows, and a crude, half-laughing pity; but there was also an indulgence, and from women especially a kindness that could be as quick and warm as a fire suddenly fed with dry sticks.

One could have fancied the leave-takings in the grey of that morning: Molly the housemaid stealing out to the terrace-garden while the dew still held to the box-edges, choosing a sprig of something hardy and sweet—rosemary, perhaps, because it keeps its scent even after it is crushed. Betty from the kitchen, with hands that smelt of onions and flour in spite of all the rinsing, cutting off a thick lock of chestnut ringlets to barter for a woolly token, as she called it, from Gumbo’s crown. Such commerce of relics goes on always when youth is stung with parting. And Gumbo, with a face fit to break into laughter at any moment, would still look beyond them all towards one small window by the porter’s lodge, where a certain girl had been seen of late, and where, when she came, even the sparrows seemed to hush their quarrel.

On the morning fixed for Madame de Bernstein’s departure, Castlewood was not so much a great house as a fair. The doors and passages swarmed. They pressed in with the old human mixture—some for one last stare at my lady’s men and the fascinating black fellow; some to squeeze the hand of her ladyship’s maid; but most, in honest expectation, to waylay baroness and nephew for those parting gratuities which custom then demanded and which were accepted with as much gravity as if they were tithes.

Madame de Bernstein paid handsomely; Harry, who had been living at free charges and feeling himself a prosperous young conqueror, paid more handsomely still. He had gold in his pocket and love in his heart—two things that make a man liberal, at least for an hour. “My mother,” he told himself, “would have me remember every servant of the Esmonds.” So he scattered his guineas as if they were crumbs to pigeons: to the major-domo with his chain of office; to the butler in black and ruffles; to the housekeeper in her still-room among jars and cordials where dried lavender gave out a faint ghost of summer whenever the door opened; to the feeble old porter in his lodge, who smelled of dust and pipe-tobacco and old oak. From the shoeblack upward, none came near without a share.

There is a strange enchantment in a family when one loves a woman in it. A man finds every face connected with hers worth smiling on. He grows civil to the surly butler, patient with an old aunt, merry with the children, tender even to the lapdogs he would otherwise kick from his path. He drinks the sweet, warm port that would nauseate him at his club, because it is poured in that house. He applauds the little miss at the spinet and calls the boy a wag when the coffee is upset over his shirt. Harry, for a brief time—for love had but lately declared itself and been accepted—had been in this state of universal amiability.

He was shrewd enough too, with that shrewdness a sportsman learns early. In the midst of sighs and moon-gazing, he could still judge a horse and relish a fair win. Having played, and won, all the ready money Will had, and besides a bundle of Mr. Esmond’s autographs, Harry was particularly well pleased with one prize: Will’s brown horse, Prince William out of Constitution—the very beast that had almost shouldered him into the water on the first evening. Harry had watched the animal since; he knew the sound legs, the good wind, the breeding in the head and shoulder. When he had lingered long enough under the stars, staring up at the window where Maria’s candle had gone out, he went to his own room and fell asleep thinking of Maria and Prince William together—how easy it would be to have her hands (those two white arms of which he dreamed as other men dream of banners) about his waist as they made the tour of the island.

In the early morning the poor chaplain Sampson sent over his little black mare by the hands of a trembling trio—groom, footman, and gardener—who behaved as though the creature were a christened child. They kissed her white nose with loud sobs, and gave her a last pat as Gumbo took the rein. Gumbo wept too, moved by their sensibility; Harry, touched and amused in the same instant, quieted the storm with two guineas, which astonished and instantly comforted the chaplain’s boy. Then Gumbo and the late groom led the mare away to the stables with orders to bring her round after breakfast, along with Mr. William’s horse, when Madam Bernstein’s carriages should be ready.

So cordial was Lord Castlewood—whether out of politeness to his aunt, or gratitude for her departure—that he even came down to breakfast to take leave. The ladies and the chaplain were there. Will alone was absent; but he left a note for his cousin, scrawled in spelling that would have shamed a village school. He was obliged, he said, to go to Salisbury Races that morning; but he had left the horse Harry had won, and Tom, his groom, would deliver it to Mr. Warrington’s servant.

Tea was drunk in a kind of peace. Then wheels sounded on the gravel; the courtyard filled with movement. Servants packed the multiplied luggage—trunks and bandboxes and baskets enough to make a small caravan, though still modest beside the monstrous baggage of ladies in these latter days. A large open landau held the stout Baroness and her niece, with two armed men on the box—pistols and blunderbuss ready, in case the road should offer highwaymen. In another carriage rode the maids, and a servant kept guard over the trunks. Harry’s own modest valises were laid there too, under the maids’ eye. Gumbo proposed to ride by the window as much as he might.

Lord Castlewood, with his stepmother and Lady Fanny, went out to the steps and exchanged dutiful embraces with their kinswoman. Then Maria came last, in a riding-dress which, to Harry’s eyes, outshone all court finery. The colour of it, the neat fall of skirt and sleeve, the little points of leather and buckles, the clean scent of the cloth warmed on her by the house air—it made him think of movement, of roads, of liberty, of her beside him for miles. The servants stood about and called blessings after her. Beyond the gates, under the trees, the village had gathered—scores of faces, hats waving, cheers bursting like birds out of hedges when one shakes the branch. As the heavy vehicles rolled away, their iron-rimmed wheels spoke over the gravel in a slow, steady grind, and the cries rose and fell like surf.

Gumbo had gone for the horses. Lord Castlewood walked up and down the court with Harry, his arm thrust under the younger man’s with a familiarity that was half kindness and half command.

“I hear you carry away some of our horses out of Castlewood?” my lord said.

Harry coloured. “A gentleman cannot refuse fair play at cards,” he answered. “I never wanted to play, nor would have played for money, had not cousin William forced me. As for the chaplain, it went against me to win from him; but he was as eager as Will.”

“I know it. There’s no blame to you. At Rome—” and my lord shrugged—“you can’t help doing as Rome does. And I am glad you have been able to give Will a lesson. He is mad for play—would gamble his coat off his back. We have paid his debts ever so many times. How much have you won of him?”

“Eighteen pieces at first, and his note for a hundred and twenty besides; and the brown horse, sixty—near two hundred in all. But it was fair, my lord, though I played against my will. Will ain’t a match for me. That’s the truth.”

“He is a match for most,” my lord said dryly. “His brown horse, you say?”

“Yes. Prince William out of Constitution. You don’t suppose I’d set him sixty against his bay, my lord?”

“Oh. I didn’t know. I saw Will ride out this morning; I did not remark what horse he was on. And you won the black mare from the parson?”

“For fourteen. She’ll mount Gumbo well. Why does not the rascal come round?” Harry’s eyes kept darting to the lane where the dust cloud had gone; his mind was already trotting beside Maria’s carriage.

“When you come to Tunbridge, cousin Harry,” Lord Castlewood continued, “look out for sharper players than Will and the parson. There’s queer company at the Wells.”

“A Virginian learns to take care of himself,” Harry said, with a knowing nod.

“So it seems. I recommend my sister to you. Though she is no baby in years, she is as innocent as one. See she comes to no mischief.”

“I will guard her with my life,” Harry cried.

“And unless you are very fond of this tumble-down old place,” my lord went on, “I would not be in a hurry to return. I mean to go to another house, and scarce be back till partridge-shooting. You go, and take charge of the women—my sister and the Baroness.”

“Indeed I will,” said Harry; and his heart thumped as if it would break his ribs with gladness.

“I will send word when you shall bring my sister back. Ha! here come the horses. Have you bid adieu to the Countess and Lady Fanny? They are kissing their hands to you from the music-room balcony.”

Harry ran up, performed the farewells in haste—for what were those ladies to him now, beside the one who waited in the rolling dust?—and came down again to mount.

There was Gumbo, astride the chaplain’s black mare, holding another horse by the rein. But it was not the brown hunter Harry had imagined beneath him. It was a bay: old, with broken knees; a tired, sorry brute, whose coat had the dullness of a saucepan too long scoured with sand.

“What is this?” Harry cried.

“Your honour’s new horse,” said the groom, touching his cap.

“This brute? Go and bring me Prince William—Mr. William’s horse—the brown horse.”

“Mr. William have rode Prince William this morning to Salisbury Races. His last words was, ‘Sam, saddle my bay horse, Cato, for Mr. Warrington. He is Mr. Warrington’s horse now. I sold him to him last night.’ And I know your honour is bountiful: you will consider the groom.”

Lord Castlewood broke into a laugh that he did not try to hide. Harry, for his part, let fly a volley of expressions then common in England and Virginia, which the polite page does not repeat.

“Mr. William said he never could think of parting with the Prince under a hundred and twenty,” the groom added, peeping at Harry as if to measure how much anger was worth.

Lord Castlewood laughed the more. “Will has been too much for thee, Harry Warrington.”

“Too much for me? So may a fellow with loaded dice throw sixes and be too much for me. I do not call this betting; I call it—” And there Harry’s tongue went where it should not.

“Mr. Warrington!” Lady Maria’s voice rang from near the steps—clear, offended, and trembling with command. “Spare me bad words about my brother, if you please. Depend upon it, I will take care you are righted. Farewell. Ride quickly, or our coaches will be at Farnham before you.”

She had spoken like a princess of a house, using the lexicon of authority that women learn early when they must defend what men disgrace. The words were English enough, yet carried that untranslatable weight of blood and birth which no stranger, however beloved, can ever quite master. Harry swallowed the rest of his fury. Lord Castlewood, with a careless wave, turned back into the house; and Harry mounted the bay, feeling the animal’s old bones under him as plainly as if he sat on a bundle of sticks, and rode out of the courtyard with Gumbo at his heel.

In his impatience to overtake the carriages, Harry barely lifted his hat to the village cheers outside the gates. The people liked him; his open face and frank ways had won them. Old tales still went about of his grandparents—of Colonel Esmond in Queen Anne’s wars, and how he might have been Lord Castlewood and would not. Old Lockwood at the gate told those stories with relish, the words rolling in a rustic dialect that kept older turns of phrase alive like seeds in a dry pod. Such local speech holds memory the way a salt-cured ham holds the summer it was slaughtered in: cut it, and the smell rises again. But the present lord was a hard landlord, the village said; and poverty made him hated as much as harshness did. As for Mr. Will, no one loved him—brawls, broken heads, unpaid bills, dark doings with village girls, cudgellings in lanes by injured men. The village had heard too—because everything is known—how the young Virginian had beaten Will at riding, at jumping, at shooting, and then at cards. They respected Harry for it; and, more than all, they admired him because Gumbo’s busy tongue had made a prince of him in the county. Gold mines, diamond mines, tobaccos, furs—anything and everything, so long as it sounded rich. Prosperity has always had a loud music in English ears, and the crowd cheered it as naturally as it cheers a drum.

So Harry, leaving the hats tossing behind him, spurred his sorry bay. The horse’s hooves struck the road with a deadened, complaining sound, as if the animal itself protested the cheat; and the lane’s hedgerows answered with their proper summer noises—finches, a distant cart, the hushy rasp of wheels on dust. Harry heard none of it. His mind was already inside the moving cloud ahead.

He came up with the carriages at last, where the dust hung thick, smelling of hot leather, churned earth, and horses’ sweat. Pushing through, he found himself at the landau window. Maria had the back seat to herself; by keeping a little behind the wheels he could see her face framed between the bouncing curtains. She smiled; her eyes met his; she lifted a finger to her lip. Madame de Bernstein was dozing on her cushions, her capuchin bobbing softly with the carriage’s sway. Harry did not care to wake the old lady. To look at Maria’s eyes was enough; and if the hedgerow birds sang, they sang no music so sweet as her small, low monosyllables.

The Baroness’s horses were fat, accustomed to short stages and easy paces, and to feed at every stop. Thus, ill as Harry was mounted, he could keep alongside without much labour. At two o’clock they baited for dinner, and the inn received them with all the noise of a country halt: the sudden clatter of plates, the scrape of benches, the cry to the hostler, the stamping and snorting from the yard where oats rattled into troughs. The kitchen breathed out its own theology—roasting meat, ale, vinegar, herbs bruised in the mortar, and the comforting steam of bread broken open. Harry paid the landlord generously. What price could be too high, when it purchased an hour near Maria, and the chance of speech that seemed forbidden and therefore the sweeter?

Madame de Bernstein, after eating comfortably, sank into a half-hour’s slumber in her chair. In voices kept low, as if the very air might betray them, Maria and Harry repeated those delicious follies which lovers never exhaust. They were going to Tunbridge Wells, where beauty and fashion would crowd; timid Maria trembled lest, among younger faces, Harry should discover charms more worthy than hers. Harry swore by all the gods that Venus herself could not tempt him away. It was he, he said, who had cause to fear: for when the men of fashion saw his peerless Maria, they would gather round her carriage and make her forget the rough American who knew no wit and no fine ways, but only how to love faithfully.

Maria smiled, lifted her eyes as if to heaven, and vowed he knew nothing of women’s truth; it was men, she said, whose sex was proverbially faithless, whose sport was to play with hearts. He protested; she persisted. Their quarrel was all sugar and sparks, until, in the heat of defending the honour of his sex, Harry flung out his arm across the table to seize her hand. His broad cuff caught a glass; there was a clatter among knives and forks; the wine toppled and broke. An “Oh!” escaped from Maria’s lips—soft, startled, and instantly ashamed.

She glanced round, frightened the crash had wakened Madame de Bernstein. But the old lady slept on, breathing evenly, as if nothing in this world could disturb her after a good meal. And so Maria, seeing no witness but the harmless steam of dinner and the inn’s low murmur beyond the door, let her fingers yield under Harry’s gentle pressure.

How much those hands mean in love-making, and how foolishly lovers make them speak! What vows are kneaded into a thumb and four fingers, as if the flesh could hold promises the tongue scarcely dares. Yet there is a kind of language there that cannot be translated into any other—no dictionary catches it, no sermon can render it. It is all pressure and warmth, all pulse answering pulse; and each believes the other understands.

Then the horses were put to; the brief paradise ended until the next halt. When the landlord came with the bill, Harry was at a respectful distance, staring out of the window at the gathering cavalcade below, as if he had been discussing nothing but the state of the roads. Madame de Bernstein woke smiling, unconscious. With profound care Harry handed his aunt to her carriage; how demure and simple Maria looked as she followed, as if her hand had never been taken at all. Away they rolled, under the bows of landlord and waiters; past country-folk clustered under the blazing inn-sign; past shopmen in their low doors; past boys and market people under the old town-hall colonnade; past loungers along the gabled street.

“It is the famous Baroness Bernstein,” they said. “That is she, the old lady in the capuchin. And that is the rich young American from Virginia—worth millions and millions. Well—sure—he might have a better horse.”

The cavalcade disappeared in dust and summer sound, and the little town sank back into its ordinary quiet, while the landlord went to his friends to boast how great folks would sleep at The Bush at Farnham that night.
