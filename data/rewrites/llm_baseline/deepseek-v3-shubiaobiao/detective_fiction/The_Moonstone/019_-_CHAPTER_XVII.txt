The night passed without incident, and—to my relief—Sergeant Cuff’s watch yielded no sign of any secret meeting between Miss Rachel and Rosanna.

I had expected the Sergeant to depart for Frizinghall at first light, but he lingered, as though some unfinished business held him back. Leaving him to his own devices, I wandered into the grounds, where I encountered Mr. Franklin pacing his favored path along the shrubbery. No sooner had we exchanged greetings than the Sergeant himself appeared, stepping forward with an air of purpose.

Mr. Franklin received him coldly. "Have you something to say to me?"

"I do, sir," replied Cuff, unfazed. "Regarding my investigation. You perceived its true direction yesterday. Naturally, given your position, you are disturbed. And naturally, you resent me for exposing what you would prefer remain hidden."

"What do you want?" Franklin snapped.

"Only to remind you that I have yet to be proven wrong. And to suggest that, as a citizen, you might assist the law with any special knowledge you possess."

"I possess none," said Franklin.

The Sergeant pressed on, undeterred. "You could spare me a fruitless errand if you chose to be frank."

"I have nothing to say."

"A servant spoke to you privately last night."

Again, Franklin cut him short. "I have nothing to say."

I recalled then the rustling in the passage the evening before—the flicker of skirts vanishing into shadow. No doubt Cuff had caught enough before my interruption to suspect Rosanna had unburdened herself to Franklin.

At that very moment, Rosanna herself appeared at the path’s end, trailed by my daughter Penelope, who seemed to be urging her back toward the house. Seeing Franklin was not alone, Rosanna halted, uncertain. Cuff, ever the tactician, pretended not to notice them.

Before Franklin or I could react, the Sergeant raised his voice deliberately. "You needn’t fear for the girl, sir. If you have any regard for Rosanna Spearman, I advise you to confide in me."

Franklin, playing the same game, answered loudly, "I have no interest whatsoever in Rosanna Spearman."

I glanced toward the girls. Rosanna stiffened as if struck, then turned abruptly, allowing Penelope to lead her away. The breakfast bell rang, and even Cuff had to concede defeat.

"I’m for Frizinghall," he told me quietly. "I’ll return by two." With that, he departed, leaving us in uneasy respite.

Once alone, Franklin turned to me. "You must make amends with Rosanna. That was a trap, Betteredge. Had he flustered me or provoked her, one of us might have given something away. I saw no choice but to answer as I did—it silenced her and showed Cuff I saw through him."

I kept my thoughts to myself. The Sergeant had done worse than listen—he had weaponized Rosanna’s infatuation, baiting Franklin where she could hear.

"Listening is the least of it, sir," I said. "At this rate, we’ll all be too busy eavesdropping on one another to speak at all."

Franklin sighed. "I can’t help Cuff trap Rosanna—yet I see no way out of this unless the Diamond is traced to her. I’m caught between two horrors."

I understood too well.

With Cuff gone, the household settled into uneasy quiet. Miss Rachel remained barricaded in her room. Franklin, after a tense breakfast, took to the moors to clear his head. The weather had turned, wind lashing the coast, though the rain held off—a day for vigorous walking, if one had the strength for it.

Later, Penelope sought me out, troubled. "You must speak to Rosanna, Father. She’s not herself."

I pressed for reasons—a habit of mine, as men ought to steer women toward reason.

"Mr. Franklin’s words crushed her," Penelope said. "She moved like a ghost afterward."

I found Rosanna sweeping the corridor, her face pale, her eyes dull as tarnished silver.

"Cheer up, lass," I urged. "Mr. Franklin meant no harm."

She replied mechanically, thanking me without meeting my gaze.

When I pressed further, she said, "I’ll confess—but not to my lady. To Mr. Franklin."

"Not today," I cautioned. "He’s gone walking."

She only nodded, resuming her work like an automaton.

Penelope and I withdrew, uneasy. "This wants a doctor," I muttered.

But Mr. Candy was ill, and his assistant, Ezra Jennings, was a stranger none of us trusted. I debated informing my lady, yet hesitated to burden her further.

Time slipped away. At a quarter to two, a familiar voice called from the drive.

Sergeant Cuff had returned.
