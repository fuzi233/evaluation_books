VIII. In New York, it was widely believed that the Countess Olenska had lost her beauty. Yet when Newland Archer saw her enter the van der Luyden drawing-room on that significant evening, he found himself quietly rejecting that verdict.

She had first come to his attention as a child—bright-eyed and lively, barely ten years old, with cheeks like ripe apples and curls so dark they seemed to shimmer in the light. People said she should be painted, not just because of her looks, but because of the energy that danced in her gaze. Her parents had been restless souls, drifting across Europe, and when both passed away, she was taken in by her aunt, Medora Manson—a woman equally prone to wanderings, though more than a little eccentric. Medora, who’d married and buried several husbands, always returned to New York claiming she would “settle down,” only to leave again after a few months, usually having sold her house at a loss. Though her lineage traced back to the Rushworths and her last marriage tied her to the reclusive Chiverses, New York forgave her quirks. But when she arrived with young Ellen Mingott—her orphaned niece—the city hesitated. The child’s bright clothes and bold spirit seemed out of place for someone still mourning her parents. Medora, ever defiant, ignored mourning customs: her veil was shorter than those of her sisters-in-law, and Ellen wore crimson merino and amber beads like a street performer from the south of France.

Still, Ellen charmed everyone. She was fearless, curious, and delightfully unguarded. She asked questions no one expected from a child, sang Neapolitan songs with a guitar, and danced with a Spanish shawl as if born to it. Under Medora’s direction—whose real name was Mrs. Thorley Chivers, but who now called herself the Marchioness Manson (a title she’d adopted after a Papal honor)—Ellen received an education far ahead of its time: drawing from live models, playing piano in quintets with professionals. It was all considered improper, and soon enough, tragedy struck. Chivers died in an asylum, and Medora fled once more, taking Ellen with her. For years, nothing was heard.

Then came word: Ellen had married a wealthy Polish nobleman—legendary in European circles—who owned estates in Paris, Florence, and Nice, a yacht in Cowes, and vast hunting grounds in Transylvania. She vanished into a world of grandeur and mystery. When Medora returned to New York again, poorer and subdued, people wondered why her rich niece hadn’t helped her. Then came the news: Ellen’s marriage had failed. She was coming home—not to reclaim glory, but to find peace among her kin.

That evening, as Newland watched her arrive at the van der Luydens’ dinner, he felt a quiet stir within him. She entered late, one hand bare, adjusting a bracelet. Yet she moved without haste or tension, stepping into the room where New York’s most refined society stood poised like figures in a portrait. She paused in the center, eyes scanning the crowd with calm grace. Her face was no longer radiant—her cheeks were pale, her frame thin, her features sharper than before. She looked older than her nearly thirty years. But something else lingered: a quiet power, a stillness in her presence that spoke of depth and experience.

Her manner was simpler than the others’. She didn’t wear jewels with ostentation, nor did she speak with the polished artifice of the city’s elite. And yet, there was a strength in her silence, a dignity that made her stand out even amid such formality. New York had expected flamboyance—something dramatic, perhaps scandalous—from a woman with such a past. Instead, she offered restraint, thoughtfulness, and a kind of weary honesty.

The dinner itself was a solemn affair. To dine with the van der Luydens was already an event; to do so with their cousin, the Duke of St. Austrey, elevated it to near-religious significance. Only a true New Yorker could appreciate the subtle distinction between being merely a duke and being *their* duke. Noblemen were tolerated in New York, sometimes even met with polite skepticism—but this one carried a weight of tradition that earned him genuine warmth, not just social obligation.

The table sparkled with heirlooms: Sevres porcelain, George II silver, Lowestoft china from the East India Company, Crown Derby from Dagonet. Mrs. van der Luyden glowed like a painting by Cabanel; Mrs. Archer, in her grandmother’s pearls and emeralds, looked like a miniature by Isabey. Jewels glittered, but mostly in heavy, old-fashioned settings—old Miss Lanning even wore her mother’s cameos and a Spanish blonde shawl.

And yet, standing among them, Ellen Olenska seemed the most mature. As Archer glanced around at the plump, smooth-faced ladies adorned with diamonds and feathers, he felt a pang—what must have shaped those eyes? What storms had passed behind that quiet gaze?

The Duke sat beside the hostess, a figure of modest stature, sunburnt, with a thick beard and small eyes. He wore clothes that looked too large, too worn, as if borrowed from another man. He rarely spoke, and when he did, his voice was so low it was lost beneath the hushed anticipation of the room.

After dinner, the men joined the women. The Duke immediately approached the Countess and settled into conversation with her—ignoring protocol entirely. No one remarked upon it. They simply watched, intrigued.

For nearly twenty minutes, they talked. Then, without warning, the Countess rose and walked across the room—alone—to sit beside Newland Archer.

It was unusual. In New York, a lady did not rise from one gentleman to seek another. She remained seated, a statue of decorum, while men took turns at her side. But Ellen didn’t seem aware of any rule being broken. She sat easily, comfortably, and looked at him with gentle eyes.

“I want you to talk to me about May,” she said.

Instead of answering, he asked, “You knew the Duke before?”

“Oh, yes—we used to see him every winter at Nice. He loves gambling—he often came to our house.”

She said it plainly, as if discussing a favorite flower. Then, after a pause, she added honestly, “I think he’s the dullest man I’ve ever met.”

Archer laughed—surprised, delighted. It was thrilling to meet someone who could say such a thing about the van der Luydens’ Duke and mean it.

He wanted to ask more—about her life, her past, the world she’d left behind—but hesitated. Then she returned to her original subject.

“May is a darling. I’ve seen no young girl in New York so beautiful and intelligent. Are you very much in love with her?”

Archer flushed. “As much as a man can be.”

She studied him carefully. “Do you think there’s a limit?”

“To being in love? If there is, I haven’t found it.”

She smiled, touched by his sincerity. “Ah—so it’s truly a romance?”

“The most romantic of romances.”

“How delightful! And you discovered it all yourselves? Nothing was arranged?”

He looked at her, startled. “Have you forgotten,” he said gently, “that here we don’t arrange marriages?”

A faint blush crept up her cheek. “Yes—I forgot. Please forgive me. Sometimes I forget that what was wrong where I came from is now right here.”

She looked down at her fan—feathers of eagle, delicate and exotic—and her lips trembled slightly.

“I’m sorry,” he murmured, “but you *are* among friends here.”

“Yes,” she said softly. “I know. That’s why I came back. I want to forget everything else. To become a complete American again—like the Mingotts, the Wellands, you, your mother… all of you tonight.” She paused. “Ah—here comes May. You’ll want to go to her.”

But she didn’t move. Her eyes stayed fixed on his.

The drawing-room filled with guests. Through her glance, Archer saw May Welland entering with her mother—dressed in white and silver, a wreath of silver blossoms in her hair. She looked like a goddess returning from the hunt.

“Oh,” Archer said, “I have so many rivals. She’s already surrounded. Look—there’s the Duke being introduced.”

“Then stay with me a little longer,” she whispered, brushing his knee lightly with her fan.

It was a feather-light touch, but it sent a jolt through him—like a secret shared.

“Yes,” he replied, almost without thinking, “let me stay.”

Just then, Mr. van der Luyden approached, followed by old Mr. Urban Dagonet. The Countess greeted them with her quiet smile, and Archer, sensing his host’s disapproving glance, rose and stepped aside.

She held out her hand, as if bidding farewell. “Tomorrow, then—after five. I shall expect you.”

And then she turned, making room for Mr. Dagonet.

“Tomorrow…” Archer repeated under his breath, though no promise had been made, no invitation extended. Still, the words hung in the air.

As he walked away, he saw Lawrence Lefferts, tall and dazzling, leading his wife forward. Gertrude Lefferts beamed at the Countess. “But I think we used to go to dancing school together when we were children…”

Behind them, Archer noticed several couples who had refused to attend Mrs. Lovell Mingott’s earlier gathering. Now, they stood in line, eager to introduce themselves. As Mrs. Archer had said: when the van der Luydens chose to teach a lesson, they knew how to do it. The wonder was that they so rarely did.

A hand touched his arm. Mrs. van der Luyden looked down at him from her height of black velvet and family diamonds. “It was kind of you, dear Newland, to devote yourself so unselfishly to Madame Olenska. I told your cousin Henry he must come to the rescue.”

He smiled vaguely. She added, as if understanding his shyness: “I’ve never seen May looking lovelier. The Duke thinks her the handsomest girl in the room.”

And so, in that moment, Newland stood caught between two worlds—one of duty and expectation, the other of quiet longing and unexpected connection.
