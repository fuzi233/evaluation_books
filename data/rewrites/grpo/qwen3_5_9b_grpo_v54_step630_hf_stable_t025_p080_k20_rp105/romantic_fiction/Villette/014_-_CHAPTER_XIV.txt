As soon as Georgette recovered, Madame Beck dispatched her to the countryside. I felt a genuine pang of loss; the child had become a part of my household, and her departure left a void that made me feel poorer than before. Yet, I suppressed my complaint. I resided in a house teeming with robust life, and while I could have found companionship among the staff, I had chosen solitude. Each teacher, in turn, made advances toward intimacy, which I tested with varying degrees of caution. One was an honest woman, though narrow-minded, coarse in her feelings, and deeply egotistical. The second was a Parisian: externally refined, but internally corrupt, devoid of creed, principle, or affection. Beneath her polished crust of decorum lay a slough of moral decay. She possessed a voracious appetite for gifts, a trait shared by the third teacher, a woman otherwise characterless and insignificant. This third teacher was defined by avarice; in her, the love of money reigned supreme. The sight of a gold coin would bring a singular, green glisten to her eyes. Once, as a mark of favor, she took me upstairs to reveal a secret hoard—a mass of coarse, large coins totaling about fifteen guineas. She loved this accumulation with the infatuated dotage of a bird guarding its eggs, discussing them with a strange persistence despite her youth.

The Parisian, conversely, was prodigal and profligate in disposition. Her snake-like nature revealed itself only once, peering out cautiously like a reptile in the leaves of a bad novel. Had it emerged boldly, I might have surveyed it philosophically, but it merely rustled and recoiled at a demonstration of wrath, vanishing with a hiss. From that day, she hated me. She was perpetually in debt, anticipating her salary on dress, perfumes, cosmetics, and confectionery. She was a cold, callous epicure, thin-faced and sallow, with perfect teeth but lips like a thread and a frozen, craving eye. She mortally hated work, preferring what she termed pleasure—a heartless dissipation of time.

Madame Beck knew this woman's character perfectly. When I asked why she retained her, Madame answered plainly that it suited her interests, noting that Mademoiselle St. Pierre possessed a unique power of maintaining order. She exerted a petrifying influence, holding unruly scholars in check as a breezeless frost stills a brawling stream. While of little use in communicating knowledge, she was invaluable for surveillance. Madame admitted frankly that St. Pierre lacked principles or morals, but added philosophically that her demeanor in class was always suitable and dignified. "Neither the pupils nor the parents look further," she said, "and consequently, neither do I."

The school was a strange, frolicsome, noisy world where chains were hidden beneath flowers. A subtle essence of Romanism pervaded every arrangement; large sensual indulgences were permitted as a counterpoise to jealous spiritual restraint. Minds were being reared in slavery, yet every pretext for physical recreation was seized to prevent reflection on this fact. "Eat, drink, and live!" the Church seemed to say, promising robust bodies and feeble souls, guaranteeing final fate while leaving the cure of the soul to divine intervention.

In the ripe glow of summer, Madame Beck's house became as merry as a school could be. Doors and windows stood wide open, naturalizing sunshine in the atmosphere. We lived more in the garden than under a roof, classes held and meals partaken of in the "grand berceau." The air buzzed with holiday preparation, turning freedom almost into license. The autumnal vacation was two months distant, but a great day awaited: the fête of Madame Beck.

The conduct of the fête devolved chiefly on Mademoiselle St. Pierre, while Madame herself stood aloof, supposedly unconscious of the tribute being paid in her honor. A secret consultation occurred in Madame's chamber regarding the annual subscription for a present. When asked what to buy, Madame feigned benign modesty, suggesting the children keep their francs. St. Pierre, however, protruded her chin, calling Madame's airs "grimaces." She demanded the item list, and the result was a handsome case containing three hundred francs worth of plate.

The program included the presentation of the plate, a garden collation, a dramatic performance, a dance, and supper. The play was the main event, requiring a month of drilling, careful casting, and countless rehearsals. St. Pierre was insufficient for this; they required the management of M. Paul Emanuel, the professor of literature. I never attended his histrionic lessons but often saw him crossing the *carré*. He was a dark, pungent, and austere little man, with a close-shorn black head, a broad sallow brow, and a wide, quivering nostril. He was irritable, thundering at the awkward squad of amateur actresses. "Vous n'êtes donc que des poupées," he would cry. "Vous n'avez pas de passions! Votre chair est de neige, votre sang de glace! Moi, je veux que tout cela s'allume, qu'il ait une vie, une âme!"

His resolve was vain. Finding the grand tragedy impossible to animate, he abandoned it for a compact comic trifle, which he soon knocked into their smooth round heads. St. Pierre always presided at his lessons, her polish and tact impressing him favorably, though her charm dried like dew within an hour.

The day preceding the fête was a holiday dedicated to cleaning and decorating. I took refuge in the garden, wandering alone in the warmth and shade, exchanging but two sentences with any living being. I watched from afar as M. Paul Emanuel and St. Pierre directed the eager band of pupils, including Ginevra Fanshawe, in the creation of a green-room and stage.

The great day arrived with a hot, unclouded sun. Teachers and pupils descended to breakfast in dressing-gowns, anticipating the evening's toilette with a luxury of slovenliness. At nine, the "coiffeur" arrived, fixing his headquarters in the oratory amidst candles and crucifixes to solemnize his art. Each girl emerged with hair smooth as a shell, intersected by faultless white lines and wreathed in Grecian plaits. I took my turn, amazed by the lavished garlandry of woven brown hair that seemed to exceed my own.

The dormitory became a scene of elaborate arrayings. Three mortal hours were devoted to a simple uniform: a clear white muslin dress, a blue sash, and white kid gloves. Though simple, the array was perfect in fashion and freshness. In contrast, I felt like a shadowy spot on a field of light. Lacking flowers or jewels, and possessing no natural rose of complexion, I wore a crape-like material of purple-gray, the color of dun mist on a blooming moor. My tailor had made it well, for it was "si triste—si peu voyant," making care in fashion imperative. Madame Beck kept me in countenance; her dress was quiet save for a gold and stone brooch. She nodded approvingly, considering me dressed "convenablement" and "décemment," the two calm deities of her worship. She paused to whisper a sarcasm about the other teachers dressing like girls of fifteen, remarking that St. Pierre looked like an old coquette playing the ingénue.

Feeling at home in my gown of shadow, I avoided the bustling garden and retreated to the schoolrooms. I sat in the first classe, reading a volume from the glazed bookcase. Acacia boughs caressed the panes, meeting a rose-bush where bees murmured happily. Just as the stilly hum and warm, lonely calm began to lure me into reverie, the sharpest ring of the street-door bell snatched me back to consciousness.

A step, quick, regular, and intent, strode through the vestibule, corridor, and *carré*, bursting open the door of my sanctuary. A paletôt and a bonnet grec filled the void, followed by two eyes that hungrily dived into me. "C'est cela!" said a voice. "Je la connais: c'est l'Anglaise. Tant pis... elle fera mon affaire." It was M. Paul Emanuel, in a state of no little excitement. He declared I must play, refusing to allow me to shrink or frown. He explained that Louise Vanderkelkov had fallen ill, leaving the play without a role. "There is no time to be lost," he urged, thrusting to the wall all reluctance. "You must take a part."

"In the vaudeville?" I gasped, horror-struck.

"Yes. Listen! The whole matter is going to fail... I apply to an Englishwoman to rescue me. What is her answer—Yes, or No?"

A thousand objections rushed into my mind: the foreign language, the limited time, the public display. Inclination recoiled, ability faltered, and self-respect trembled. "Non, non, non!" said all these, but looking up at M. Paul's vexed, fiery eye, which held a sort of appeal behind its menace, my lips dropped the word "oui".

He handed me the book and my role. It was a disagreeable part—a man's, an empty-headed fop's. One could put neither heart nor soul into it; I hated it. The play ran on two rivals vying for a coquette; I was to be the butterfly, talker, and traitor. I did my best, which was bad, provoking M. Paul to fume. He eventually conceded, "Ca ira!" and sent me to the attic to learn alone.

The attic was no pleasant place: hot as Africa in summer, cold as Greenland in winter, filled with boxes, cobwebs, and vermin. Rumor affirmed the ghostly Nun of the garden issued from behind a curtain there. I saw a large rat and many black beetles, which discomposed me more than the stifling heat. Opening a skylight and mounting a makeshift throne of chests, I gathered my dress around me and began the acquisition of my task. Secure from human audience, I acted my part before the garret-vermin, entering into its emptiness and frivolity with a spirit inspired by scorn, making the fop as fatuitous as possible.

As afternoon glided into evening, hunger gnawed at me. I thought of the collation devouring below. The ever-tinkling bell was faintly audible, as was the roll of wheels on the pavement. Just as I concluded my rehearsal, the key rattled in the lock. M. Paul looked in, holding the door open. "Brava! J'ai tout entendu. C'est assez bien. Encore!"

I hesitated, but he insisted sternly, "Et point de grimaces! A bas la timidité!" Again I went through the part, though not half so well as alone. "Enfin, elle sait," he said, half dissatisfied. "You may yet have twenty minutes for preparation: au revoir!"

"Monsieur," I called out, taking courage. "J'ai bien faim."

"Comment, vous avez faim! Et la collation?"

"I know nothing about it. I have not seen it, shut up here."

"Ah! C'est vrai," he cried. Instantly, my throne was abdicated. An inverse repetition of the whirlwind that brought me up took me down to the kitchen. The cook was ordered to produce food, and I was commanded to eat. To my joy, it was limited to coffee and cake, including a *petit pâté à la crème* he had procured. He superintended my repast, forcing upon me more than I could swallow until I implored to be spared.

"A la bonne heure," he cried. "You will set me down as a species of tyrant... whereas, after all, I am no such thing. Now, Mademoiselle, do you feel courage and strength to appear?"

I said I did, though confused. He offered his hand, and we set off with a rapid walk. In the *carré*, he stopped. Large lamps lit the wide doors; orange-trees and tall flowers ornamented the portals. Groups of ladies and gentlemen in evening dress stood amongst the flowers. Within, the school-rooms presented a thronging, undulating multitude of rose, blue, and translucent white. Lustres burned overhead; far off, a stage with a solemn green curtain and footlights awaited.

"N'est-ce pas que c'est beau?" demanded my companion.

I should have said it was, but my heart got up into my throat. M. Paul discovered this, giving me a side-scowl. "I will do my best, but I wish it was over," I said. "Are we to walk through that crowd?"

"By no means: we pass through the garden—here."

In an instant, we were out of doors. The cool, calm night revived me. Moonless, but lit by the reflex of glowing windows, the court was bright. Heaven was cloudless, grand with the quiver of its living fires. Soft, bland, balmy, and safe were the nights of the Continent.

We reached the glass door of the first classe and entered a small cabinet dividing the room from the grand salle. It dazzled, deafened, and stifled me with light and clamor. "De l'ordre! Du silence!" cried M. Paul. With a dozen words and gestures, he turned out half the persons present, obliging the remnant to fall into rank. These were the performers in costume. M. Paul introduced me. All stared and some tittered at the surprise of the Englishwoman playing in a vaudeville. Ginevra Fanshawe, beautifully dressed and looking fascinatingly pretty, turned on me a pair of eyes as round as beads. In the highest spirit, unperturbed by fear, her entrance seemed to transfix her with amazement in the midst of her joy.

"You, too, must be dressed for your part."

"Dressed—dressed like a man!" exclaimed Zélie St. Pierre, darting forward. "I will dress her myself."
