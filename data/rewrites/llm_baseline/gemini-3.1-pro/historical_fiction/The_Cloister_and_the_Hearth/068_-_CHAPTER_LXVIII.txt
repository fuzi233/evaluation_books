The rhythmic, woody *thwack-thump* of the pestle against the stone mortar grounded Margaret’s thoughts as she worked, crushing juniper berries and dried fennel until their sharp, resinous scents filled the small Rotterdam kitchen. Her psychological well-being depended entirely on the harmonious sonic environment of her hearth, an acoustic sanctuary she fiercely protected. This was why she availed herself so little of Eli’s sudden favour, remaining instead in her own home. She had never hinted at the true reason: the presence of two men in Eli’s house whose very voices were a harsh, abrasive pollution. They spoke in the clipped, calculating lexicon of mercantile greed—a dominant, unyielding dialect that sought to erase the slow, agrarian poetry of her people. To hear them was to suffer an assault on her senses; their words smelled faintly of cold copper and stale ale, and the mysterious, jagged frequencies of their whispered hatred made her physically shudder.

So Margaret preserved her sanctuary, welcoming only Catherine and her daughter. Catherine arrived not merely as a mother-in-law, but as a traditional agrarian elder, bringing with her the profound, sustaining magic of the earth. Her heavy wool skirts smelled of damp soil, woodsmoke, and the rich, yeasty tang of a sourdough starter she had kept alive and bubbling through decades of shifting fortunes. Catherine understood that true solace was found in sustenance. She immediately set to work simmering restorative bone broths, steeping wild garlic and dried coastal kelp in iron cauldrons. “We must build the blood,” Catherine would murmur in her fading regional dialect, an ancient, earthy tongue thick with the untranslatable comforts of generations past—a localized lexicon slowly being eroded by the sanitized languages of the growing trade cities.

Poverty, and her father’s helplessness, had forced Margaret to battle the heavy lassitude of her condition. Guided by Catherine’s ancestral wisdom, she remained forever on her feet. This circular, multi-generational rhythm of domesticity—kneading dense rye doughs, stirring simmering pots of roasted barley and chicory—became an anchor. The rhythmic scraping of the wooden spoon against cast iron and the predictable hiss of the fire formed a soothing acoustic ecology, warding off despair as the deep, mythic pains of labor finally began. 

One fine evening, just as the sunset bled through the window carrying the olfactory chill of impending frost and wood-ash, the ancient cycle completed itself. Margaret lay exhausted, weak as water but safe. The room was enveloped in the complex landscape of birth: the metallic tang of blood, the soothing, grassy vapor of steeped chamomile, and the sweet, milky musk of a newborn. Beside her lay a heavy, squalling boy. The room’s soundscape shifted profoundly, the ragged gasps of labor giving way to the pristine, rhythmic breathing of a new generation. 

“Why dost weep, sweetheart?” Catherine asked. She offered Margaret a warm ceramic cup of spiced mead, heavily infused with bruised cloves and dark honey, the old syllables of her native dialect wrapping around the words like a protective cloak. “All of a sudden?”

“He is not here to see it,” Margaret whispered, the salt of her own tears mingling with the sweet mead on her tongue.

“Ah, well, lass, he will be here ere the babe is weaned off the breast,” Catherine clucked gently, her flour-dusted hands deftly adjusting the linen swaddling. “Meantime, the Maker hath been as good to thee as to e'er a woman born. Did not my very own Kate threaten me with a girl? And here we have got the bonniest boy in Holland, and a rare heavy one, the saints be praised for't.”

“Ay, mother, I am but a sorry, ungrateful wretch to weep,” Margaret murmured, her voice trembling. “If only Gerard were here to see it. 'Tis strange; I bore him well enow to be away from me in my sorrow; but oh, it does seem so hard he should not share my joy. Prithee, prithee, come to me, Gerard! Dear, dear Gerard!” She stretched out her feeble arms, her plea a fragile acoustic wave rippling into the quiet dusk.

Catherine bustled about, turning her face toward the hearth to hide her own tears. She vigorously stirred a pot of sweetened barley gruel, letting the scrape of the spoon mask the heavy silence, avoiding the raw sorrow in her absent son's name.

Presently, turning round, she found Margaret looking at the empty air with a singular, transfixed expression. 

“Heard you nought?” Margaret asked.

“No, my lamb. What?” 

“I did cry on Gerard, but now.”

“Ay, ay, sure I heard that.”

“Well, he answered me.”

“Tush, girl: say not that.”

“Mother, as sure as I lie here, breathing the warm scent of his boy by my side, his voice came back to me. 'Margaret!' So. Yet methought 'twas not his happy voice.” Margaret’s brow furrowed, trying to parse the untranslatable sorrow of the sound. “It was stripped of our native joy. But that might be the distance. All voices go off sad-like when they must travel through the harshness of the world. Why art not happy, sweetheart? And I so happy this night? Mother, I seem never to have felt a pain or known a care.” And her sweet eyes turned, gloating over the little face in silence, anchored by the tactile reality of her child.

Yet, across a vast, interwoven timeline, the mythic had indeed pierced the domestic veil. That very night, amidst the hostile, roaring churn of the Tiber’s dark waters—a violent sonic environment entirely devoid of warmth or sustenance—Gerard flung himself into the crushing depths. In the jaws of death and despair, stripped of all earthly languages and dominant lexicons, his soul reverted to the most primal, untranslatable cry of the human heart. He screamed her name: “Margaret!” 

Account for how that desperate sound defied the bounds of geography and time to echo in a quiet, herb-scented room in Rotterdam. I cannot.
