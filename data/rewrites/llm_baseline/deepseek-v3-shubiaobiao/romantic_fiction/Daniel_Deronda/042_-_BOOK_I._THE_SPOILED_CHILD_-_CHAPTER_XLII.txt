*"If suffering has its ranks, then Israel stands foremost among nations—if endurance ennobles, then the Jews are the aristocracy of every land. What is a literature with a handful of tragedies compared to a people who have lived one for fifteen centuries, where the poets and actors were the heroes themselves?"*

—**Zunz**, *The Synagogal Poetry of the Middle Ages*

Deronda had been turning these words over in his mind as he made his way to the Cohens’ home, a place that bore no outward mark of sorrow or nobility. Ezra Cohen was no tragic martyr; his life was one of bustling commerce, his success in pawnbroking a far cry from the suffering of his ancestors. Yet within this unremarkable household, Mordecai lived—frail, intense, a flickering ember of something far greater.

The family greeted Deronda with eager warmth. Cohen, ever the businessman, managed to imply that Deronda’s return was worth more than any profit from a delayed sale. Young Mrs. Cohen fretted over whether the baby was awake, then brightened when she remembered Adelaide was still up. Deronda, prepared with small gifts—paper dolls for the girl, a cup-and-ball for Jacob—was ushered into the parlor where the grandmother presided over a card game with the children.

Jacob, ever exuberant, demanded Deronda’s attention for his latest feat: tossing a plate without breaking it. Deronda obliged, exchanging an amused glance with the grandmother before settling in. He noted the closed door to the workroom where Mordecai had last appeared, biding his time before mentioning him.

Only when Adelaide was perched on his knee, the paper figures dancing under his fingers, did he ask, *"Is Mordecai in?"*

Cohen, slipping in from the shop, gestured vaguely toward the closed door. *"Truth be told, sir, he’s been odd these past days—like a man walking in his sleep, or else sitting still as a statue. Not that he’s ever what you’d call steady in the head, but this is different."*

*"It’s the illness,"* the grandmother murmured. *"I fear he hasn’t much time left."*

*"Or it’s something on his mind,"* Mrs. Cohen countered. *"He’s been scribbling and staring like a man possessed."*

Cohen shrugged. *"We’re soft on him, I’ll admit. But my wife and mother wouldn’t turn him out if he were twice the burden. Principle, you see."*

*"He carries a blessing with him,"* the grandmother insisted.

*"No, he’s sick inside,"* Jacob corrected matter-of-factly. *"He wouldn’t even eat his bun."*

Deronda felt a pang. *"I’ve spoken with him before—at Ram’s bookshop. I promised to meet him here tonight."*

*"Ah! That explains it!"* Cohen clapped his knee. *"He’s been waiting for you. Talks to you about his learning, does he? Kind of you to humor him—not that there’s profit in it."*

Jacob, ever helpful, offered, *"I’ll fetch him!"* But his mother shushed him, gesturing for Deronda to go himself.

The workroom was dim, lit only by a guttering candle and the faint glow of dying embers. Mordecai sat motionless at a bench, his back to the door, hands resting on a watchmaker’s stand. At Deronda’s voice—*"I’ve come for you. Are you ready?"*—he turned without a word, seized his cap, and rose.

The change in him was immediate. In the parlor, Jacob thrust his cup-and-ball under Mordecai’s nose, triumphant. *"Look! I can do it!"*

Mordecai smiled faintly. *"Fine, fine."*

Mrs. Cohen fretted over his coat and scarf, and Cohen, ever the optimist, declared, *"See? I told you he’d perk up!"*

As they stepped into the night, Deronda said, *"The Cohens are fond of you."*

*"And I of them,"* Mordecai replied. *"They have the heart of Israel, though they walk in narrow paths."*

*"I’ve troubled you with my delay,"* Deronda admitted.

*"I trusted you,"* Mordecai said, his voice trembling. *"But I have been like a prisoner too long bound—now the chains are broken, and the joy threatens to shatter me."*

Deronda urged him to wrap his scarf tighter against the chill. *"We’re going to the Hand and Banner? Will we have privacy there?"*

*"Tonight is the meeting of our club,"* Mordecai said regretfully. *"The Philosophers. Poor men, thinkers. I hoped we might speak alone, but if not, another time—"*

*"No, I’ll join you,"* Deronda said, relieved at the reprieve.

The tavern’s back room was hazy with smoke, a circle of shabbily dressed men deep in debate. The speaker—Miller, a bookseller—paused as they entered. *"A touch-and-go night, sir. We’ve been on progress, statistics, social change—now we’re on the power of ideas."*

The discussion tumbled on—Goodwin arguing that ideas were inseparable from material life, Pash scoffing at nationalism, Buchan questioning whether all change was progress. Deronda listened, interjecting only to challenge the assumption that development was inherently good.

*"A people’s life grows,"* Mordecai said suddenly, his voice gaining strength. *"It binds and expands, absorbs and gives back. But if memory fades, if love withers, shall we say their soul is dead? Not if one man feels that life stirring in him still. Resistance is fire—it may yet light the way."*

His words hung in the air, a spark in the dim room. Deronda felt the weight of them, knowing this was only the beginning.
