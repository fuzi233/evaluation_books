They let the luncheon draw itself out as if time, too, had been set on the table between them—passed, tasted, and set down again. The room smelled of hot metal and salt blown in from the beach, of coffee that had stood too long on the stove and taken on a faint bitterness, and of fish—fresh enough to speak of the morning’s catch, yet already glossed with the heavy, sweet butter of a provincial cook’s generosity. Outside, the sea kept its own counsel, laying down its long, even phrases of sound; the hush between the waves seemed to seep into the pauses between their words, so that talk and silence alternated like courses.

Archer had not meant to avoid his own affairs; it happened of itself, as the hand reaches again and again for what is rare on a plate. He leaned forward, listening as one listens to a recipe passed down in a dialect that will not survive the next generation unless it is held fast in the ear. Ellen sat with her chin on her clasped hands, her eyes on him and yet, at moments, turned inward—as if she were tasting in memory what she described.

She told him, in plain, unexaggerated phrases, of the year and a half since they had last met. She had grown tired of what people called “society.” New York, she admitted, had been kind—almost oppressively so, like a table pressed on a guest until the guest cannot tell appetite from obligation. She would never forget how warmly it had welcomed her back; but after the first flush of novelty she had found herself, as she put it, too “different” to care for the things it cared about. And so she had decided to try Washington, where one was supposed to meet more varieties of people and of opinion.

“On the whole,” she said, “I shall probably settle down there—and make a home for poor Medora.” Her voice softened on the name, as if it were a homely ingredient that needed careful handling. Medora, it seemed, had worn out the patience of all her other relations at the very moment when she most needed watching over, and protecting—above all from matrimonial perils.

“But Dr. Carver—aren’t you afraid of Dr. Carver?” Archer asked. He had heard the man’s name buzzing about the Blenkers’ like a fly that returns to the sugar no matter how often it is waved away.

Ellen’s mouth curved—not quite amusement, not quite contempt. “Oh, the Carver danger is over. Dr. Carver is very clever. He wants a rich wife to finance his plans, and Medora is simply a good advertisement as a convert.”

“A convert to what?”

“To all sorts of new and crazy social schemes.” She hesitated a moment, then went on with the quiet obstinacy that startled him more than any heat would have done. “But they interest me more than the blind conformity to tradition—somebody else’s tradition—that I see among our friends. It seems stupid to have discovered America only to make it into a copy of another country.”

She smiled across the table, and the smile, light as it was, made Archer’s skin tighten as if a knife had gone too near the bone. “Do you suppose Christopher Columbus would have taken all that trouble just to go to the Opera with the Selfridge Merrys?”

The words struck him with the sharpness of vinegar. He felt the blood rise. “And Beaufort—do you say these things to Beaufort?” he demanded, abruptly, as if the name were a spice he could not bear.

“I haven’t seen him for a long time,” she answered, without flinching. “But I used to; and he understands.”

“Ah—it’s what I’ve always told you: you don’t like us. And you like Beaufort because he’s so unlike us.” His glance ran, restless, around the bare little room, and out beyond it to the bare beach and the row of stark white houses strung along the shore, each one looking as if it had been scrubbed into respectability. The whole place, with its thin smells and plain furniture, seemed to him suddenly like a parable of their world—washed, bleached, and airless. “We’re damnably dull. We’ve no character, no colour, no variety. I wonder—” His voice broke, and it was as if the sea outside had pushed its long, indifferent breath through him. “Why don’t you go back?”

Her eyes darkened, and he braced himself for anger. Instead she fell silent. She did not answer at once; she seemed to weigh his words the way a cook weighs salt—knowing how little it takes to ruin what might have been nourishing. The pause lengthened until the sounds outside grew unnaturally clear: the steady wash of water against the shore, the remote creak of wood, a far-off call and answering call along the beach. Archer’s fear rose—not of her indignation, but of her agreement.

At last she said: “I believe it’s because of you.”

She said it without flourish, without any inviting softness. The confession came, oddly, with the flatness of truth, as if it had been spoken first to herself and only now brought out into the open air. Archer reddened to the temples. He did not move, scarcely breathed. Her words seemed to him like some fragile winged thing that might lift and vanish at the least stir—yet if he kept perfectly still it might settle and multiply, filling the room with its presence.

“At least,” she continued, “it was you who made me understand that under the dullness there are things so fine and sensitive and delicate that even those I most cared for in my other life look cheap in comparison.” She drew her brows together, troubled, as though she were trying to find a word that existed in no language she had ever spoken. “I don’t know how to explain myself—but it seems as if I’d never before understood with how much that is hard and shabby and base the most exquisite pleasures may be paid.”

Exquisite pleasures—he felt the retort flare up in him, quick and bitter: it was something to have had them. Yet he could not utter it. The look in her eyes—appealing, steadfast—held him as firmly as a hand on the wrist.

“I want,” she went on, “to be perfectly honest with you—and with myself. For a long time I’ve hoped this chance would come: that I might tell you how you’ve helped me, what you’ve made of me—”

He stared at her under frowning brows, then broke in with a laugh that sounded to his own ear like a plate slipping on a stone floor. “And what do you make out that you’ve made of me?”

She paled a little, as if the air had suddenly cooled. “Of you?”

“Yes. For I’m of your making much more than you ever were of mine. I’m the man who married one woman because another one told him to.”

A fugitive flush rose, then ebbed. “I thought—you promised—you were not to say such things today.”

“Ah—how like a woman! None of you will ever see a bad business through!”

She lowered her voice, and the softening of it did not lessen its force. “Is it a bad business—for May?”

He had crossed to the window and stood drumming on the raised sash, his eyes fixed on the sea as if it might offer him some new vocabulary. He felt, in every fibre, the wistful tenderness with which she had said her cousin’s name; it stung him more than accusation could have done.

“For that’s the thing we’ve always got to think of—haven’t we—by your own showing?” she persisted, as if she were holding to a rule of conduct the way one holds to a household measure when everything else in the kitchen has been lost.

“My own showing?” he echoed, blankly, still staring outward.

“Or if not—” she went on, following her thought with a painful exactness, “if it’s not worth while to have given up, to have missed things, so that others may be saved from disillusionment and misery—then everything I came home for, everything that made my other life seem by contrast so bare and so poor because no one there took account of them—all these things are a sham or a dream—”

He turned his head without leaving his place. “And in that case there’s no reason on earth why you shouldn’t go back,” he finished for her, savagely glad to force the conclusion into words.

Her eyes clung to him. “Oh—is there no reason?”

“Not if you staked your all on the success of my marriage. My marriage,” he said, and the words tasted of iron, “isn’t going to be a sight to keep you here.”

She did not answer.

“What’s the use?” he burst out. “You gave me my first glimpse of a real life, and at the same moment you asked me to go on with a sham one. It’s beyond human enduring—that’s all.”

“Oh, don’t say that,” she cried, and the cry came from so deep that it seemed to shake the stillness in the room. “When I’m enduring it!”

Her arms had slipped down along the table, her hands open, helpless; and she sat with her face abandoned to his gaze as if, in the recklessness of some desperate peril, she could no longer afford concealment. The sight of her—so unguarded—overwhelmed him. It was as if, in that instant, the years of her silence had found their only possible utterance: not in any dominant lexicon, not in any tidy phrase, but in the plain, untranslatable evidence of tears.

“You too—oh, all this time, you too?” he stammered.

She let the tears gather and overflow, sliding slowly downward. They were not dramatic, only inevitable—like the slow yielding of a fruit pressed too long.

Half the width of the room still lay between them, and neither made any show of moving. Archer was aware, with a strange detachment, of her bodily nearness, as if his senses had been drawn away from touch into some more inward faculty of hearing and memory. Yet his eyes were caught—as they had been once before, in the little house on Twenty-third Street—by one of her hands flung out on the table. He fixed on it as if it were the only steady thing in the whirl of his thoughts. His imagination spun around that hand like water around the rim of a basin; and still he did not advance.

He had known the love that feeds on caresses and lives by them. This was not that. This passion was closer than his bones; it could not be soothed by any hurried reaching out. His terror was to do anything that might blur the sound and impression of what she had said; his one fierce comfort was the thought that he would never again be quite alone.

Then, like the aftertaste of smoke that comes when sweetness has been too sudden, the sense of waste and ruin rose in him. Here they were, shut in, safe, close; and yet chained to their separate destinies as firmly as if a continent lay between them.

“What’s the use—when you will go back?” he cried. Beneath the words another cry struggled up, raw and helpless: How on earth can I keep you?

She sat motionless, her lids lowered, as if listening not to him but to some inner sound she could not silence. “Oh—I shan’t go yet!”

“Not yet? Some time, then? Some time that you already foresee?”

At that she raised her eyes, and they were suddenly clear—as if, for a moment, all the muddied meanings and borrowed phrases of their world had fallen away. “I promise you: not as long as you hold out. Not as long as we can look straight at each other like this.”

He dropped back into his chair. What her answer truly said was: If you lift a finger, you’ll drive me back—back to all the abominations you know of, and all the temptations you half guess. The unspoken sentence sounded in him as plainly as if she had spoken it aloud, and it kept him on his side of the table in a moved, almost sacred submission, as if this restraint were their only protection against a greater violence.

“What a life for you—” he groaned.

“Oh—as long as it’s a part of yours.”

“And mine a part of yours?”

She nodded.

“And that’s to be all—for either of us?”

“Well; it is all, isn’t it?”

At that he sprang up, forgetting everything but the sweetness and aching candour of her face. She rose too—not to meet him, not to flee, but quietly, as though the worst of the task were already done and she had only to wait. So quietly that, when he came close, the hands she held out did not stop him; they guided him. Her fingers fell into his, and her arms, extended but not rigid, kept him far enough away to let her surrendered face say what words could not.

They stood so—how long he could not have told. Time, like the tide outside, had lost its markings. It was long enough for her silence to communicate all she had to say, and for him to understand that only one thing mattered: he must do nothing to make this meeting their last. He must leave their future in her care, asking only that she keep fast hold of it for both of them.

“Don’t—don’t be unhappy,” she said, and her voice broke as she drew her hands away.

“You won’t go back—you won’t go back?” he pleaded, as if that were the one prospect he could not survive.

“I won’t go back,” she answered.

Then she turned, opened the door, and led the way into the public dining-room.

The strident school-teachers were gathering up their possessions, their voices sharp and metallic in the common air, like cheap cutlery scraped against tin. The sudden clatter seemed to strike at the nerves after the room’s charged quiet, and Archer felt it as an assault—proof of how sound can bruise the mind when it has been living on whispers. Through the windows the beach lay bleached in sunlight; the white steam-boat waited at the pier, its presence a blunt, practical promise of departure. And over the bright waters Boston loomed in a line of haze, distant as a memory already being rewritten.
