Before the fragile covenant of friendship between M. Paul and myself could be fully ratified, a third power interjected its voice. We were no longer merely two souls drifting together; we were under the watchful, sleepless gaze of Rome. Through the mystic lattice of the confessional—a sliding panel to which M. Emanuel drew nearer month by month—the Church watched her son with jealous intensity.

"Why were you so eager to be friends with M. Paul?" the reader might ask. "Had he not already proven his partiality?"

Yes, he had. Yet, I found a peculiar comfort in hearing him declare it so earnestly. I cherished his modest doubts, his tender deference, that trust which longs to rest and is grateful when taught how. He had called me "sister." It was well. As long as he confided in me, I was willing to bear that title, provided he did not invite me to fill it for some future wife. Given his vow of celibacy, this dilemma seemed safe.

That evening, I spent the long hours pondering our interview, wishing for the dawn to break so I could finally face the reality of our new arrangement. I wanted to test the waters: to observe how much of a brother he would become when we met again, and to gauge how much of a sister I felt myself. Could I summon a sister's courage, and would he offer a brother's frankness?

When he arrived the next day, life proved its cruel construction: the event never matches the expectation. He never accosted me. His lesson was delivered quietly, mildly, and gravely. He was fatherly to his pupils, but he was not brotherly to me. Before leaving the class, I expected a smile, perhaps a word; instead, I received only a hurried, shy nod.

This distance, I argued, was accidental, involuntary. Patience would surely dissolve it. It did not vanish; it persisted for days, then grew. I suppressed my surprise and swallowed the rising tide of other feelings. He had bid me make my own experiments, to tease and try him. A vain injunction! A privilege nominal and unavailable. Some women might dare it, but I was not among the brave. Left alone, I was passive; repulsed, I withdrew. Forgotten, my lips refused to utter a reminder, my eyes dared not dart a glance. It seemed there had been an error in my calculations, and I needed time to find it.

Then came the evening when he was to give me my lesson. Usually, on these occasions, my schoolroom was wherever the pupils and teachers gathered, often in the large second division. On this customary evening, hearing the hour strike, I collected my books and sought the large division.

The classroom was empty, bathed in cool, deep shadow. Through the open double doors, the courtyard (*carré*) was filled with light and pupils. The westering sun blushed vividly over the scene, fusing the hues of the walls and the variegated tints of the dresses into one warm glow. The girls worked or studied in the midst of the circle, while M. Emanuel spoke good-humoredly to a teacher. His dark *paletôt* and jetty hair were tinged with crimson reflections; his Spanish face, when he turned, answered the sun's animated kiss with a smile.

Orange trees and blooming plants basked in the sun's bounty, now asking for water. M. Emanuel had a taste for gardening; tending shrubs soothed his nerves. He revived the orange trees, geraniums, and cactuses with the refreshment they needed. Meanwhile, his lips sustained his precious cigar, that first luxury of his life; its blue wreaths curled prettily among the flowers in the evening light. He spoke no more to the pupils or mistresses, but gave endearing words to a small spaniel—a creature nominally belonging to the house but virtually owned by him, fonder of him than any inmate. She was delicate, silky, and lovable, trotting at his side with expressive, attached eyes. Whenever he dropped his bonnet or handkerchief, she crouched beside it like a miniature lion guarding a kingdom's flag.

As the amateur gardener fetched water from the well, his work spun on. The great clock ticked. Another hour struck. The courtyard lost the illusion of sunset; day was drooping. My lesson must be short. Was it my turn?

Alas, there were more plants to attend to—favorite rose-bushes, choice flowers. Little Sylvie's glad bark and whine followed the receding *paletôt* down the alleys. I put up some books; I should not need them all. I sat and thought, involuntarily deprecating the creeping invasion of twilight.

Sylvie emerged again, heralding the returning *paletôt*. The watering-pot was deposited by the well; its office fulfilled. How glad I was! Monsieur washed his hands in a stone bowl. There was no longer time for a lesson; the prayer-bell would soon ring. Yet we should meet; a chance would be offered to read the riddle of his shyness in his eyes. His ablutions over, he stood, slowly rearranging his cuffs, looking at the horn of a young moon set pale in the opal sky, glimmering faint on the oriel of Jean Baptiste. Sylvie watched the mood, contemplative and still; it irked her. She whined and jumped to break it.

"Petite exigeante," he said. "You must not be forgotten one moment, it seems."

He stopped, lifted her in his arms, and sauntered across the court, within a yard of the line of windows near which I sat. He lingered, fondling the spaniel in his bosom, calling her tender names in a tender voice. On the front-door steps, he turned once more to look at the moon, the grey cathedral, and the fading spires into a blue sea of night-mist. He tasted the sweet breath of dusk, noting the folded bloom of the garden. Suddenly, he looked round; a keen beam from his eye rased the white façade of the classes, sweeping the long line of windows. I think he bowed, but I had no time to return the courtesy. In a moment, he was gone; the moonlit threshold lay pale and shadowless before the closed door.

Gathering my books, I carried the unused heap back to the third class. The prayer-bell rang; I obeyed.

The morrow would not restore him to the Rue Fossette; that day was devoted to his college. I got through my teaching and the intermediate hours, seeing evening approach with its heavy ennui. Whether it was worse to stay with my co-inmates or sit alone, I had not considered. Naturally, I chose the latter. If there was hope for comfort, the heart or head of no human being in this house could yield it. Only under the lid of my desk could it harbor, nestling between book leaves, gilding a pencil-point, or tinging the ink in the glass. With a heavy heart, I opened the lid.

One by one, familiar volumes were taken out and put back, hopeless. They had no charm. Was this something new, a pamphlet in lilac? I had not seen it before; I had rearranged my desk that very afternoon. It must have been introduced within the last hour.

I opened it. What was it?

It was neither tale nor poem, nor essay nor history. It preached and persuaded. It was a theological work preaching Romanism, urging conversion. The voice of that sly little book was honeyed, its accents all unction and balm. Here roared no thunder of Rome, no blast of displeasure. The Protestant was to turn Papist not in fear of hell, but for the comfort, indulgence, and tenderness Holy Church offered. Far be it from her to threaten or coerce; her wish was to guide and win. *She* persecute? Oh dear no!

This meek volume was not for the hardened; it was milk for babes, the mild effluence of a mother's love intended for those whose heads must be reached through their hearts. Its appeal was not to intellect, but to affection and sympathy. St. Vincent de Paul, gathering his orphans, never spoke more sweetly.

One capital inducement was held out: the Catholic who had lost dear friends could enjoy the unspeakable solace of praying them out of purgatory. The writer did not touch on the firmer peace of those whose belief dispenses with purgatory altogether, but I thought of this and preferred the latter doctrine as more consolatory. The little book amused me; it did not painfully displease. It was a canting, sentimental, shallow volume, yet something about it cheered my gloom. I was amused by the gambols of this unlicked wolf-cub muffled in the fleece, mimicking the bleat of a guileless lamb. Portions reminded me of Wesleyan Methodist tracts I had read as a child, flavored with the same seasoning of fanaticism. The author was no bad man, though betraying the trained cunning—the cloven hoof of his system. His judgment, however, wanted surgical props; it was rickety.

I smiled over this dose of maternal tenderness from the ruddy old lady of the Seven Hills, and at my own disinclination to accept these melting favors. Glancing at the title-page, I found the name "Père Silas." On the fly-leaf, in small but clear pencil characters: "From P. C. D. E. to L—y."

When I saw this, I laughed, but not in my former spirit. A mortal bewilderment cleared suddenly from my head. The solution of the Sphinx-riddle was won. The conjunction of Père Silas and Paul Emanuel gave the key to all. The penitent had been with his director; permitted to withhold nothing; suffered to keep no corner of his heart sacred. The whole narrative of our late interview had been drawn from him. He had avowed the covenant of fraternity and spoken of his adopted sister. How could such a covenant be sanctioned by the Church? Fraternal communion with a heretic! I seemed to hear Père Silas annulling the unholy pact, warning his penitent of its perils, commanding the enforcement of a new system whose frost had pierced to the marrow of my bones.

These may not seem pleasant hypotheses, yet by comparison, they were welcome. The vision of a ghostly troubler hovering in the background was nothing compared to the fear of spontaneous change arising in M. Paul himself.

This evening, there was no bright sunset; west and east were one cloud. No summer night-mist softened the distance; a clammy fog from the marshes crept grey round Villette. The watering-pot might rest in its niche; a small rain had been drizzling all afternoon and still fell fast and quietly. This was no weather for rambling in wet alleys. I started to hear Sylvie's sudden bark in the garden—her bark of welcome. Surely she was not accompanied, yet this glad, quick bark was never uttered save in homage to one presence.

Through the glass door and the arching *berceau*, I commanded the deep vista of the *allée défendue*: thither rushed Sylvie, glistening through its gloom like a white guelder-rose. She ran to and fro, whining, springing, harassing little birds. I watched five minutes; no fulfillment followed the omen. I returned to my books. Sylvie's sharp bark suddenly ceased. Again I looked up. She was standing not many yards distant, wagging her white feathery tail as fast as muscle would work, intently watching the operations of a spade plied by an indefatigable hand. There was M. Emanuel, bent over the soil, digging in the wet mould among rain-laden shrubs, working as hard as if his day's pittance were yet to be earned by the literal sweat of his brow.

In this sign, I read a ruffled mood. He would dig thus in frozen snow on the coldest winter day, urged inwardly by painful emotion, whether nervous excitation or sad thoughts of self-reproach. He would dig by the hour, with knit brow and set teeth, nor once lift his head or open his lips.

Sylvie watched till she was tired. Scampering deviously, bounding here and there, snuffing everywhere, she at last discovered me in the class. Instantly she flew barking at the panes, as if to urge me forth to share her pleasure or her master's toil. She had seen me occasionally walking in that alley with M. Paul, and I doubted not she considered it my duty to join him now, wet as it was.

She made such a bustle that M. Paul finally looked up, perceiving why and at whom she barked. He whistled to call her off; she only barked louder. Bent on having the glass door opened, tired with her importunity, he threw down his spade, approached, and pushed the door ajar. Sylvie burst in impetuously, sprang to my lap, and with her paws at my neck and her nose and tongue busily about my face and eyes, flourished her bushy tail over the desk, scattering books and papers far and wide.

M. Emanuel advanced to still the clamor and repair the disarray. Having gathered up the books, he captured Sylvie and stowed her away under his *paletôt*, where she nestled as quiet as a mouse, her head just peeping forth. She was very tiny, with the prettiest innocent face, silkiest long ears, and finest dark eyes in the world. I never saw her, but I thought of Paulina de Bassompierre; forgive the association, reader, it would occur.

M. Paul petted and patted her; the endearments she received were not to be wondered at. While caressing the spaniel, his eye roved over the papers and books just replaced; it settled on the religious tract. His lips moved; he half-checked the impulse to speak. What! Had he promised never to address me more? If so, his better nature pronounced the vow "more honoured in the breach than in the observance," for with a second effort, he spoke.
