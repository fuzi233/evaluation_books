To survive the crushing weight of Rome, Gerard descended to the west bank of the Tiber, securing a lodging that felt less like a home and more like a temporary cell between the river and the city's indifferent throat. Every dawn, he ventured forth into the labyrinthine marketplaces, carrying a specimen of his script to every workshop known for executing commissions. The reception was a uniform chill, a collective rejection that tasted of ash in his mouth.

"We make our letters somewhat thinner than this," sneered one merchant, holding up a sheet of paper as if inspecting a diseased limb. "How dark your ink is," scoffed another, dismissing the very essence of his craft. But the core of their cruelty was not aesthetic; it was existential. "What avails this?" they demanded, their voices rising in a chorus of dismissal. "Scant is the Latin writ here now. Can ye not write Greek?"

"Ay," Gerard replied, his voice steady despite the hollow ache in his chest, "but not nigh so well as Latin."

"Then you shall never make your bread at Rome," the verdict fell, final and absolute.

Defeated but unbroken in spirit, Gerard returned to his quarters with a purse lightened by a high-priced loan for a beautiful Greek manuscript—a tangible debt for intangible hope. Yet, within two weeks, the Greek alphabet ceased to be a barrier and became a bridge. He worked until noon, pouring his soul into the ancient characters, then hunted for customers with renewed vigor. When he finally presented a specimen of Greek calligraphy surpassing anything they possessed, the traders merely shrugged. Greek and Latin were alike unsaleable; the city was choked with works from across Europe. They told him bluntly, "You should have come last year."

Desperation bred new strategies. Gerard purchased a psaltery, finding solace in music when commerce failed. His landlady, a woman of Sienna blessed by the Virgin, noticed the decline in his demeanor. On one occasion, she invited him to dine, her kindness cutting through the fog of his despair. When she gently probed the source of his despondency, he confessed his plight. Her response was sharp, laced with the cynicism of a survivor.

"Those sly traders," she declared, her eyes narrowing, "have writers in their pay. They receive a noble price for their work and pay a sorry one for yours. No wonder they blow cold on you. Methinks you write too well. How know I that? Say you. Marry—because you lock not your door, like the churl Pietro, and women will be curious. Ay, ay, you write too well for them."

Gerard sought clarification. "Why?"

"Your good work might put out the eyes of what they are selling," she explained simply.

Gerard sighed, the weight of her insight settling on his shoulders. "Alas, dame, you read folk on the ill side, and you so kind and frank yourself."

"My dear little heart," she countered, her tone shifting to something warmer, "these Romans are a subtle race. Me? I am a Siennese, thanks to the Virgin."

"My mistake was leaving Augsburg," Gerard admitted, the ghost of his homeland haunting his words.

"Augsburg?" she scoffed haughtily. "Is that a place to even toe the line of Rome? I never heard of it, for my part."

Yet, she reassured him that he would make his fortune regardless of the booksellers. "Seeing thee a stranger, they lie to thee without sense or discretion. Why, all the world knows that our great folk are bitten with the writing spider these many years, pouring out their money like water, turning good land and houses into writ sheepskins to keep in a chest or a cupboard. God help them, and send them safe through this fury, as He hath through a heap of others. In sooth, there has been somewhat less cutting and stabbing among rival factions, and vindictive eating of their opposites' livers, minced and fried, since Scribbling came in. Why, I can tell you two. There is his Eminence Cardinal Bassarion, and his Holiness the Pope himself. There be a pair who could keep a score such as thee a writing night and day. But I'll speak to Teresa; she hears the gossip of the court."

The following day, armed with intelligence from Teresa, she revealed five more signors afflicted by the "writing spider." Gerard took down their names, purchased parchment, and spent days crafting specimens. He left samples bearing his name and address at each door, hoping against hope.

Silence answered him.

Day after day passed, leaving him heartsick. Strangely, this was the exact moment Margaret was fighting tooth and nail against impossible odds in Rotterdam, arrested for curing without a license rather than killing with one. Back in Rome, Gerard saw ruin staring him directly in the face.

He retreated into the afternoons, picking up canzonets and mastering them, laying in playing cards to color, and striking off a meal per day. This last stroke of genius, born of necessity, landed him in fresh trouble.

In these *camere locande*, the landlady prepared all meals, though the lodgers provided the provisions. Her hostess quickly detected his culinary experiments. With a brusque opening that made him blush and shrink, she pacified herself immediately, appealing to his common sense. Was adversity a thing to be overcome on an empty stomach?

"Patienza, my lad! Times will mend; meantime, I will feed you for the love of heaven." (*Gratis.*)

"Nay, hostess," Gerard protested, "my purse is not yet quite void, and it would add to my trouble if true folk should lose their due by me."

"Why, you are as mad as your neighbor Pietro, with his one bad picture," she retorted.

"Why, how know 'tis a bad picture?"

"Because nobody will buy it. There is one that hath no gift. He will have to don casque and glaive, and carry his panel for a shield."

Gerard pricked up his ears. She elaborated. Pietro had arrived from Florence with money and an unfinished painting, renting an unfurnished room opposite Gerard's and furnishing it neatly. Upon completion, he received visitors and offers—liberal ones, in her opinion—but refused them so disdainfully that he made enemies. Since then, he had dragged the canvas through the streets, only to return it defeated. Recently, furniture had vanished from his room; now he wore a single suit and slept atop a great chest. She had discovered this only by peering through the keyhole, noting how vigilantly he locked his door. "Is he afraid we shall steal his chest, or his picture, that no soul in all Rome is weak enough to buy?"

"Nay, sweet hostess; see you not 'tis his poverty he would screen from view?"

"And the more fool he! Are all our hearts as ill as his? A might give us a trial first, anyway."

"How you speak of him. Why, his case is mine; and your countryman to boot."

"Oh, we Siennese love strangers. His case yours? Nay, 'tis just the contrary. You are the comeliest youth ever lodged in this house; hair like gold. He is a dark, sour-visaged loon. Besides, you know how to take a woman on her better side; but not he. Natheless, I wish he would not starve to death in my house, to get me a bad name. Anyway, one starveling is enough in any house. You are far from home, and it is for me, which am the mistress here, to number your meals—for me and the Dutch wife, your mother, that is far away. We two women shall settle that matter. Mind thine own business, being a man, and leave cooking and the like to us, that are in the world for little else that I see but to roast fowls, and suckle men at starting, and sweep their grown-up cobwebs."

"Dear kind dame, in sooth you do often put me in mind of my mother that is far away."

"All the better; I'll put you more in mind of her before I have done with you." And the honest soul beamed with pleasure.

Gerard, neither an egotist nor blinded by female partialities, saw his own grief mirrored in the proud misery of Pietro. The more he contemplated it, the more he resolved to share his humble means with that unlucky artist; Pietro's sympathy would surely repay him. He attempted to intercept Pietro, but without success.

One day, a groan echoed from the adjacent room. Gerard knocked, receiving no answer. He knocked again. A surly voice commanded him to enter.

Obeying somewhat timidly, he pushed open the door to find a garret furnished sparsely: a chair, a picture facing the wall, an iron basin, an easel, and a long chest. Coiled upon the chest was a haggard young man with a wonderfully bright, predatory eye. Nothing resembled a coiled cobra ready to strike the first comer more vividly.

"Good Signor Pietro," Gerard began, his voice softening, "forgive me that, weary of my own solitude, I intrude on yours. But I am your nearest neighbor in this house, and methinks your brother in fortune. I am an artist too."

"You are a painter? Welcome, signer. Sit down on my bed."
