Mr. Vandernoodt had a knack of fastening on to a man as if with a buttonhook, and he had fastened on Deronda now—drawing him away from the company with the brisk appetite of one who wanted a sharper walk, a cigar freshly lit, and that other indulgence which makes smoke seem sweeter: gossip.

The winter air outside the Abbey had a clean bite; it carried the thin whir of distant wheels on the road, the caw of rooks stripped bare of leaf-shelter, and, nearer, the faint crunch and scuff of their own steps on gravel. Mr. Vandernoodt’s cigar gave off a warm, resinous smell that clung in the cold like a sauce reduced too fast—strong, a little acrid, with a sweetness that was almost cloying.

“What a washed-out piece of cambric Grandcourt is!” he began, with the confident relish of a man slicing into a joint he expects everyone to find tasteless. “But if he’s a favorite of yours, I withdraw the remark.”

“Not the least in the world,” said Deronda, his voice even.

“I thought not. One wonders how he came to have a great passion again; and he must have had—to marry in this way. Though Lush, his old chum, hints that he married this girl out of obstinacy. By George! it was a very accountable obstinacy. A man might make up his mind to marry her without the stimulus of contradiction. But he must have made himself a pretty large drain of money, eh?”

“I know nothing of his affairs.”

“What! not of the other establishment he keeps up?”

“Diplow? Of course. He took that of Sir Hugo. But merely for the year.”

“No, no; not Diplow: Gadsmere. Sir Hugo knows, I’ll answer for it.”

Deronda kept silence. The words, dropped as if they were crumbs meant for sparrows, stirred an unwilling curiosity in him; yet he would not stoop to peck. Mr. Vandernoodt, satisfied with his own power of producing suspense, took another measured pull at his cigar, nipped the ash, and went on.

“Lush would not altogether own to it, of course. He’s a confident and go-between of Grandcourt’s. But I have it on the best authority. The fact is, there’s another lady with four children at Gadsmere. She has had the upper hand of him these ten years and more, and by what I can understand has it still—left her husband for him, and used to travel with him everywhere. Her husband’s dead now; I found a fellow who was in the same regiment with him, and knew this Mrs. Glasher before she took wing. A fiery dark-eyed woman—a noted beauty at that time—he thought she was dead. They say she has Grandcourt under her thumb still, and it’s a wonder he didn’t marry her, for there’s a very fine boy, and I understand Grandcourt can do absolutely as he pleases with the estates. Lush told me as much as that.”

“What right had he to marry this girl?” Deronda’s disgust broke through, sharp as when the palate meets something tainted under a fair crust.

Mr. Vandernoodt shrugged, his cigar-end lifting with the gesture. “She can know nothing of it,” Deronda added, emphatically—only to have the certainty instantly pricked by a question in himself, like a thorn he couldn’t see: Could she have known anything?

“It’s rather a piquant picture,” said Mr. Vandernoodt, pleased with his own seasoning of the tale—“Grandcourt between two fiery women. For depend upon it this light-haired one has plenty of devil in her. I formed that opinion of her at Leubronn. It’s a sort of Medea and Creüsa business. Fancy the two meeting! Grandcourt is a new kind of Jason: I wonder what sort of a part he’ll make of it. It’s a dog’s part at best. I think I hear Ristori now, saying, ‘Jasone! Jasone!’ These fine women generally get hold of a stick.”

“Grandcourt can bite, I fancy,” said Deronda. “He is no stick.”

“No, no; I meant Jason. I can’t quite make out Grandcourt. But he’s a keen fellow enough—uncommonly well built too. And if he comes into all this property, the estates will bear dividing. This girl, whose friends had come to beggary, I understand, may think herself lucky to get him. I don’t want to be hard on a man because he gets involved in an affair of that sort. But he might make himself more agreeable. I was telling him a capital story last night, and he got up and walked away in the middle. I felt inclined to kick him. Do you suppose that is inattention or insolence, now?”

“Oh, a mixture. He generally observes the forms: but he doesn’t listen much,” said Deronda. Then, after a moment, as if trying to set a lid on what had begun boiling in his mind, he said, “I should think there must be some exaggeration or inaccuracy in what you have heard about this lady at Gadsmere.”

“Not a bit, depend upon it; it has all lain snug of late years. People have forgotten all about it. But there the nest is, and the birds are in it. And I know Grandcourt goes there. I have good evidence that he goes there. However, that’s nobody’s business but his own. The affair has sunk below the surface.”

“I wonder you could have learned so much about it,” said Deronda, drily.

“Oh, there are plenty of people who knew all about it; but such stories get packed away like old letters. They interest me. I like to know the manners of my time—contemporary gossip, not antediluvian. These Dryasdust fellows get a reputation by raking up some small scandal about Semiramis or Nitocris, and then we have a thousand and one poems written upon it by all the warblers big and little. But I don’t care a straw about the faux pas of the mummies. You do, though. You are one of the historical men—more interested in a lady when she’s got a rag face and skeleton toes peeping out. Does that flatter your imagination?”

“Well, if she had any woes in her love, one has the satisfaction of knowing that she’s well out of them.”

“Ah, you are thinking of the Medea, I see.”

Deronda, refusing further seasoning from this kitchen of scandal, pointed to some giant oaks worth looking at in their bareness—dark, rigid limbs lifted against the paling sky. He felt Mr. Vandernoodt had emptied his dish; there would be no more to take.

Yet the story clung. In the quiet that followed—the clean winter hush, broken by a distant door-latch, the low clatter of a pail somewhere in the service-yard, the muffled call of a groom—Deronda’s thoughts began to work as they had not worked for years, weaving and unweaving, laying one thread across another until a pattern started out and then blurred again.

Since those early days when, hungry with the peculiar hunger of the unclaimed, he had tried to build the hidden story of his own birth out of looks, silences, and half-words, his mind had perhaps never been so active in guessing at any private affair as it was now over Gwendolen’s marriage. This unavowed relation of Grandcourt’s—could she have known of it, and had it been that knowledge which made her shrink, shrink again, and then at last go forward under the spur of poverty? Deronda could recall almost every word she had said to him; and in some of those words he now seemed to taste an after-flavor he had missed before, as one recognizes a spice only when told its name. There was a consciousness in her—of wrong done, of injury inflicted. And his own secret ache made him keenly alive to the form of injury that falls on unacknowledged children and their mother.

Was Mrs. Grandcourt, beneath all her determined show of satisfaction, gnawed by a grief with more than one head—self-reproach, disappointment, jealousy? He dwelt especially on the slight signs of self-reproach, and his judgment, which might have hardened, turned instead into tenderness: to excuse, to pity. He thought he held a key now; and the thought of a young creature wedding fresh hopes to old secrets made him see her misery magnified, as if the walls of her life were closing in.

It was easy, too, to understand Sir Hugo’s silence: why no hint had been dropped. And then—suddenly, painfully—this Mrs. Glasher, with her four children, was drawn into association with his own obscurity, his own unspoken origin. The thought of Gwendolen knowing, marrying nevertheless, and wearing content like a jewel would have made her, to Deronda, among the most repulsive of beings. But Gwendolen tasting remorse for contributing to their injury—if that were the truth—brought her near to his fellow-feeling. It would mean she had been pushed down from the fine, sheltered ignorance where women so often sit in easy judgment, into a common plane where one begins to understand the Hagar-and-Ishmael side of life. Precedent, after all, would have offered her an almost comfortable legend: that her husband’s marriage with her was his entrance on the path of virtue, while Mrs. Glasher stood for forsaken sin. Deronda felt, by old instinct, a resentment for those who are cast out and then named in someone else’s language as mere wrongdoing, as if the name were a sentence.

And undeniably his growing solicitude for Gwendolen hung chiefly on her peculiar manner toward him—an appeal, hesitant yet importunate, which would have touched any sensibility not deadened by vanity. One sign that his interest had shifted its footing was that he dismissed any caution against her being a coquette laying snares for a vulgar flirtation. He determined he would not again evade any opportunity of talking to her.

He had shaken off Mr. Vandernoodt and found a solitary corner in the twilight; but half an hour was long enough for those possibilities to press in on him. With his resolution made, he remembered she would likely be at tea with the other ladies.

And so she was. For Gwendolen, having decided she would not go down again for the next four hours, felt at the end of one that by shutting herself up she shut out her chances of seeing and hearing—shut out, too, whatever small power she might snatch from knowledge. Her visit would last only two days more. She adjusted herself—smoothed her hair, gathered her self-possession around her like a cloak, and went down with a resolve to be agreeable, to seem at ease.

Only ladies were assembled, and Lady Pentreath—voice full and seasoned by authority—was amusing them with a description of a drawing-room under the Regency and the figure cut by ladies and gentlemen in 1819, the year she was presented. The room had that domestic hush in which the soft chink of teaspoons against china is almost a kind of music, with the faint whisper of tea being poured and the occasional rustle of silk like dried leaves. When Deronda entered, the little soundscape shifted: a chair-leg slid, someone exhaled a laugh, the kettle-lid trembled faintly on its stand.

“Shall I be acceptable?” he said. “Perhaps I had better go back and look for the others. I suppose they are in the billiard-room.”

“No, no; stay where you are,” said Lady Pentreath. “They were all getting tired of me; let us hear what you have to say.”

“That is rather an embarrassing appeal,” said Deronda, drawing up a chair near Lady Mallinger’s elbow at the tea-table. “I think I had better take the opportunity of mentioning our songstress,” he added, looking at Lady Mallinger—“unless you have done so.”

“Oh, the little Jewess!” said Lady Mallinger. “No, I have not mentioned her. It never entered my head that any one here wanted singing lessons.”

“All ladies know some one else who wants singing lessons,” said Deronda. “I have happened to find an exquisite singer.” He turned toward Lady Pentreath. “She is living with some ladies who are friends of mine—the mother and sisters of a man who was my chum at Cambridge. She was on the stage at Vienna; but she wants to leave that life, and maintain herself by teaching.”

“There are swarms of those people, aren’t there?” said the old lady. “Are her lessons to be very cheap or very expensive? Those are the two baits I know of.”

“There is another bait for those who hear her,” said Deronda. “Her singing is something quite exceptional, I think. She has had such first-rate teaching—or rather first-rate instinct with her teaching—that you might imagine her singing all came by nature.”

“Why did she leave the stage, then?” said Lady Pentreath. “I’m too old to believe in first-rate people giving up first-rate chances.”

“Her voice was too weak. It is a delicious voice for a room. You who put up with my singing of Schubert would be enchanted with hers,” said Deronda, looking at Mrs. Raymond. “And I imagine she would not object to sing at private parties or concerts. Her voice is quite equal to that.”

“I am to have her in my drawing-room when we go up to town,” said Lady Mallinger. “You shall hear her then. I have not heard her myself yet; but I trust Daniel’s recommendation. I mean my girls to have lessons of her.”

“Is it a charitable affair?” said Lady Pentreath. “I can’t bear charitable music.”

Lady Mallinger, helpless in conversation and feeling the constraint of not telling Mirah’s story, smiled with embarrassment and glanced at Deronda.

“It is a charity to those who want to have a good model of feminine singing,” said Deronda. “I think everybody who has ears would benefit by a little improvement on the ordinary style.” And then, letting his attention turn like a hand searching for warmth, he looked at Gwendolen. “If you heard Miss Lapidoth—perhaps you would revoke your resolution to give up singing.”

“I should rather think my resolution would be confirmed,” said Gwendolen. “I don’t feel able to follow your advice of enjoying my own middlingness.”

“For my part,” said Deronda, “people who do anything finely always inspirit me to try. I don’t mean that they make me believe I can do it as well. But they make the thing, whatever it may be, seem worthy to be done. I can bear to think my own music not good for much, but the world would be more dismal if I thought music itself not good for much. Excellence encourages one about life generally; it shows the spiritual wealth of the world.”

“But then, if we can’t imitate it, it only makes our own life seem the tamer,” said Gwendolen, her tone resisting because the truth had already begun to press on her like a garment too tight.

“That depends on the point of view, I think,” said Deronda. “We should have a poor life of it if we were reduced for all our pleasure to our own performances. A little private imitation of what is good is a sort of private devotion to it, and most of us ought to practice art only in the light of private study—preparation to understand and enjoy what the few can do for us. I think Miss Lapidoth is one of the few.”

“She must be a very happy person, don’t you think?” said Gwendolen, with a touch of sarcasm, and a turn of her neck toward Mrs. Raymond.

“I don’t know,” answered the independent lady; “I must hear more of her before I say that.”

“It may have been a bitter disappointment to her that her voice failed her for the stage,” said Juliet Fenn, with gentle sympathy.

“I suppose she’s past her best, though,” said the deep voice of Lady Pentreath.

“On the contrary, she has not reached it,” said Deronda. “She is barely twenty.”

“And very pretty,” interposed Lady Mallinger, eager to help. “And she has very good manners. I’m sorry she’s a bigoted Jewess; I should not like it for anything else, but it doesn’t matter in singing.”

“Well, since her voice is too weak for her to scream much, I’ll tell Lady Clementina to set her on my nine granddaughters,” said Lady Pentreath. “And I hope she’ll convince eight of them that they have not voice enough to sing anywhere but at church. My notion is, that many of our girls nowadays want lessons not to sing.”

“I have had my lessons in that,” said Gwendolen, looking at Deronda. “You see Lady Pentreath is on my side.”

As she spoke, Sir Hugo entered with some of the other gentlemen, including Grandcourt. The sound of heavier footsteps and a low murmur of male voices thickened the air like a draught through a corridor. Sir Hugo, placing himself against the group at the tea-table, said with his easy good-humor,

“What imposition is Deronda putting on you, ladies—slipping in among you by himself?”

“Wanting to pass off an obscurity on us as better than any celebrity,” said Lady Pentreath, “a pretty singing Jewess who is to astonish these young people. You and I, who heard Catalani in her prime, are not so easily astonished.”

Sir Hugo listened smiling as his wife handed him a cup of tea. “Well, you know, a Liberal is bound to think that there have been singers since Catalani’s time.”

“Ah, you are younger than I am. I dare say you are one of the men who ran after Alcharisi. But she married off and left you all in the lurch.”

“Yes, yes; it’s rather too bad when these great singers marry themselves into silence before they have a crack in their voices. And the husband is a public robber. I remember Leroux saying, ‘A man might as well take down a fine peal of church bells and carry them off to the steppes,’” said Sir Hugo, setting down his cup and turning away.

Deronda, who had moved to make room and felt he was not in request, sat down a little apart. The tea-things made their small domestic concert—cup on saucer, spoon on cup—while talk broke into clusters and drifted. Presently Deronda became aware that in the dispersal Gwendolen had extricated herself from Mr. Vandernoodt’s attentions and walked to the piano, where she stood, apparently examining the music on the desk.

Will any one be surprised that Deronda concluded she wished him to join her? Perhaps she meant to soften what had been sharp in her resistance; he had noticed in her that first impulses often spoke in the wrong voice, and afterward she wished to take the words back as one tries to rescue an over-salted dish.

He went to her side. “Are you relenting about the music and looking for something to play or sing?”

“I am not looking for anything, but I am relenting,” said Gwendolen; and there was a submissiveness in her tone, as if she were laying down a weapon.

“May I know the reason?”

“I should like to hear Miss Lapidoth and have lessons from her, since you admire her so much—that is, of course, when we go to town. I mean lessons in rejoicing at her excellence and my own deficiency,” she said, turning on him a sweet, open smile—too sweet, as if sweetness were something she had learned to apply.

“I shall be really glad for you to see and hear her,” said Deronda, returning the smile in kind.

“Is she as perfect in every thing else as in her music?”

“I can’t vouch for that exactly. I have not seen enough of her. But I have seen nothing in her that I could wish to be different. She has had an unhappy life. Her troubles began in early childhood, and she has grown up among very painful surroundings. But I think you will say that no advantages could have given her more grace and truer refinement.”

“I wonder what sort of trouble hers were?”

“I have not any very precise knowledge. But I know that she was on the brink of drowning herself in despair.”

“And what hindered her?” Gwendolen’s question came quickly, and her eyes fixed on his, as if the answer were a draught she needed.

“Some ray or other came—which made her feel that she ought to live—that it was good to live,” he answered quietly. “She is full of piety, and seems capable of submitting to anything when it takes the form of duty.”

“Those people are not to be pitied,” said Gwendolen, impatience flashing like a knife drawn too suddenly. “I have no sympathy with women who are always doing right. I don’t believe in their great sufferings.” Her fingers moved quickly among the edges of the music, as if she could shred what she could not bear.

“It is true,” said Deronda, “that the consciousness of having done wrong is something deeper, more bitter. I suppose we faulty creatures can never feel so much for the irreproachable as for those who are bruised in the struggle with their own faults. It is a very ancient story, that of the lost sheep—but it comes up afresh every day.”

“That is a way of speaking—it is not acted upon, it is not real,” said Gwendolen, bitterness thickening her words. “You admire Miss Lapidoth because you think her blameless, perfect. And you know you would despise a woman who had done something you thought very wrong.”

“That would depend entirely upon her own view of what she had done,” said Deronda.

“You would be satisfied if she were very wretched, I suppose,” said Gwendolen, impetuously, as if trying to force him to pronounce a sentence.

“No, not satisfied—full of sorrow for her. It was not a mere way of speaking. I did not mean to say that the finer nature is not more adorable; I meant that those who would be comparatively uninteresting beforehand may become worthier of sympathy when they do something that awakens in them a keen remorse. Lives are enlarged in different ways. I dare say some would never get their eyes opened if it were not for a violent shock from the consequences of their own actions. And when they are suffering in that way one must care for them more than for the comfortably self-satisfied.”

Deronda forgot everything but his vision of what her experience had probably been; compassion urged him, and his eyes and voice betrayed as much interest as they could.

Gwendolen had slipped on to the music-stool and looked up at him with pain in her long eyes—like a wounded creature that has found, for one instant, someone who might lift the weight off its hurt.

“Are you persuading Mrs. Grandcourt to play to us, Dan?” said Sir Hugo, coming up and laying a hand on Deronda’s shoulder with a gentle, admonitory pinch—kindly, but a reminder.

“I cannot persuade myself,” said Gwendolen, rising at once.

Others followed Sir Hugo; the room filled in, voices crossed, chairs shifted, and the possibility of any confidence ended for that day under the cover of social sound—like a bird’s call drowned by a passing cart.

But the next was New Year’s Eve, and there was to be a grand dance, to which the chief tenants were invited, held in the picture-gallery above the cloister—one of those entertainments where numbers and general movement can, by their very abundance, create privacy.

While Gwendolen dressed, she longed—remembering Leubronn—to put on the old turquoise necklace for her sole ornament. The stones, with their cool sky-color, were a private language to her: a remembrance not translated into anyone else’s approval. But she dared not offend her husband by seeming shabby on an occasion when he would demand her utmost splendor. Determined to wear the memorial somehow, she wound it thrice round her wrist and made a bracelet of it, going to her room to put it on just before she entered the ball-room, as if to hide the act itself from observation.

It was always a beautiful scene, this dance on New Year’s Eve, kept up by family tradition as nearly in the old fashion as inexorable change would allow. Red carpet was laid down; hot-house plants and evergreens were arranged in bowers at the extremities and in every recess of the gallery, and their damp, living scent rose against the chill of stone—earthy, resinous, with a hint of bruised leaf. The old portraits stretched back through generations, even to the pre-portraying period, making a piquant line of spectators—faces that had watched other faces, other alliances, other seasons of hope and defeat, as if time itself were a long room where the same steps were tried again with different shoes.

Neighboring gentry, major and minor, were invited; and it was certainly an occasion when a prospective master and mistress of Abbott’s and King’s Topping might see their future glory in an agreeable light, as a picturesque provincial supremacy with a rent-roll personified by the most prosperous-looking tenants. Sir Hugo expected Grandcourt to feel flattered by being asked to the Abbey at a time which included this festival in honor of the family estate; but he also hoped that his own hale appearance might impress his successor with the probable length of time that would elapse before the succession came, and with the wisdom of preferring a good actual sum to a minor property that must be waited for.

All present, down to the least important farmer’s daughter, knew they were to see “young Grandcourt,” Sir Hugo’s nephew, presumptive heir and future baronet, now visiting the Abbey with his bride after an absence of many years; any coolness between uncle and nephew having, it was understood, given way to friendly warmth. There was a particular rustle in the room—a communal quickening—when such knowledge moved among them; the tenants’ voices, trained by open fields and barns to carry and blend, made a soft roar like wind in winter hedges. The fiddles tuned; the floorboards received testing taps; laughter rose and fell in waves, and above it all the high gallery seemed to hold the sound as a vaulted shell holds the sea.

The bride, opening the ball with Sir Hugo, was necessarily the cynosure of all eyes. Less than a year before, if some magic mirror could have shown Gwendolen her actual position, she would have imagined herself moving in it with a glow of triumphant pleasure, conscious that she held in her hands a life full of favorable chances which her cleverness and spirit would enable her to make the best of. Now she wondered that she could get so little joy out of the exaltation to which she had been suddenly lifted—away from the distasteful petty empire of her girlhood, with its irksome lack of distinction and superfluity of sisters. She would have been glad to be even unreasonably elated, to forget everything but the flattery of the moment; but she was like one courting sleep, in whom thoughts insist like willful tormentors.

And so, wondering at her own dullness, longing all the while for some excitement that would deaden the importunate aches, she passed through files of admiring beholders in the country-dance with which it was traditional to open the ball. Eyes followed her as she moved—women measuring her air, men noting her beauty, girls storing away the picture like a story they would tell in kitchens and parlors. She was generally regarded by her own sex as an enviable woman. It was remarked that she carried herself with a wonderful air, considering she had been nobody in particular and without a farthing to her fortune. If she had been a duke’s daughter, or one of the royal princesses, she could not have taken the honors of the evening more as a matter of course.

Poor Gwendolen! By-and-by it would become a skill, automatically practiced, to bear this last great gambling loss with an air of perfect self-possession.
