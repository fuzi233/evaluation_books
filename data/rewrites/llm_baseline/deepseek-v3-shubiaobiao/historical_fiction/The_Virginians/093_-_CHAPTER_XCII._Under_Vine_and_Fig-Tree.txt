The joy of homecoming needs little description—the mother’s embrace, the warmth of reunion, the silent gratitude whispered on her knees. Our house stood ready, wide enough to shelter us all, yet Aunt Hetty could not bear to remain. To relinquish her temporary motherhood over the elder children, whom she had nurtured for near three years, was a pain too sharp to endure. She withdrew to Bury St. Edmunds with her father, where she might spoil her eldest nephew and niece in peace.

A year later, fortune favored her. Mr. B., the Jamaica planter, passed, leaving her half his estate—a posthumous tribute to affections once spurned. How much of that inheritance has since vanished into Miles’s pockets? Commissions, uniforms, tavern debts—youth’s follies are no mystery to me. Yet Hetty declares herself content: *“I vowed never to wed unless I found a man as good as my father. I never did, Sir George. No, not half as good—and you may smoke that in your pipe.”*

When the General departed—serene, full of years—it was Theo who wept most. *“I grieve because I fear I loved him too little,”* she murmured. Hetty, ever composed, speaks of him still as though he lingers near, recounting his jests, his kindness, her voice brightening like a girl’s.

Years ago, I fancied Hal might yet hold some fragment of her heart. When he visited us, widowed and sorrowful, I hinted at her girlhood passion, half-hoping he might replace his lost Fanny. But Hal, drowned in grief, swore no woman could equal her. Our house echoed with old tunes, old memories softening every heart. He spoke endlessly of his dear Aunt Lambert—praise her husband drank like wine, for her memory never left him.

Hal, clad in the blue and yellow of an American officer—a fashion Monsieur de Lafayette had brought to favor—strutted through Versailles, presented to Their Most Christian Majesties. A French marquise nearly ensnared him, but he fled back to England, to Bury, where patient ears endured his lamentations for his “angel” wife. We, who knew the paragon, were civil but less fervent, fumbling to answer our children’s questions about Uncle Hal’s saint.

Over claret, the Generals, Parson Blake, Miles, and I refought the Revolution. *“By Jupiter,”* Blake would cry, *“you’re the Tory, Sir George the Whig! He faults our leaders; you defend them. And when I prayed for the King last Sunday, you echoed me loud enough!”*

*“With all my heart,”* Hal would retort. *“I can’t forget I wore his coat.”*

*“Ah, if Wolfe had lived!”* Lambert sighed.

*“You should hear the General speak of him,”* Hal said, rising.

*“Which General?”* I teased.

*“Mine,”* he said, glass aloft. *“His Excellency, George Washington!”*

I drank willingly; the parson did not.

Hal’s devotion to his Chief was boundless, and on one such evening, he confessed his own disgrace. The trouble began with Lafayette—that young French marquis, leapt to major-general by Congress, while seasoned officers chafed. Hal, ever blunt, mocked the appointment. *“A boy of twenty, who scarce speaks English!”* he fumed.

The General, pressed by Congress and beset by winter at Valley Forge—his men starving, outnumbered—had no patience for quarrels. Yet Hal’s words stung. *“I thought Harry Warrington would stand by me,”* Washington said coldly.

Their reconciliation came in private. *“You speak as if I favor the Marquis from whim,”* Washington said, weary. *“But if his rank buys France’s alliance, must we not bear the slight?”* His voice dropped. *“You call this humiliation? What is mine, then—to smile at traitors, to plead with Congress while my men freeze?”*

Hal, chastened, saw then the man’s true stature—greater than Wolfe, greater than ambition. *“To endure,”* I reflected later, *“to stand unbroken by intrigue, to forgo pride for the cause—that is greatness.”*

*“You might have worn the blue and buff yourself,”* Blake grumbled.

*“Scarlet suited me better,”* I replied.

*“And General Washington,”* Theo sniffed, *“never wrote tragedies.”*

Harry laughed. *“True. Hagan read one aloud—the General slept soundly.”*

*“He never liked you,”* Theo retorted. *“Which makes your praise the nobler.”*

And so, under vine and fig-tree, we rested—our wars past, our hearts, at last, at peace.
