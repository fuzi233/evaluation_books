On the fourth night, I seemed to slip into the quiet fermentation of sleep, but that night I finally, truly woke. I opened my eyes and knew, although the house of death lay steeped in a darkness as rich and loamy as the terroir of an ancient root cellar, that my previous hours had been but the stale residue of a dream. My palate was finally clean; I was awake. 

“At last!” I said to my heart, feeling the heavy, utilitarian fuel of my old existence render down into pure, joyous sustenance. I turned my eyes. Lona stood by my couch, waiting. I had never lost her—only mislaid the sight of her in the cyclical turning of the seasons! Though the room was dark, she was not. Her eyes shone with the golden, clarified radiance of the Mother’s, and her death-dress was white as snow and glistering, steaming faintly in the cool air like a loaf of wild-yeast bread fresh from the hearth. She had fallen asleep a girl, green and tart; she awoke a woman, ripened into the complex, full-bodied sweetness of essential life. I folded her in my arms, savoring the undeniable, tactile truth that I lived indeed.

“I woke first!” she said, her voice a bright, ringing treble in the quiet room. 

“You did, my love, and woke me!” 

“I only looked at you and waited,” she answered, her words a soft, sustaining nourishment.

Through the cellar-darkness, a candle came floating, carrying the olfactory landscape of rendered beeswax and wild mountain sage. In moments, Adam, Eve, and Mara were with us. These ultimate custodians of the world's original harvest greeted us with the quiet, domestic smiles of agrarian elders who had overseen ten thousand such mornings.

“I hope you have had a pleasant darkness, steeped like a nourishing brine!” said the Mother. 

“Not very,” I answered, “but the waking from it is a heavenly feast.” 

“It is but the first taste,” she rejoined; “you are hardly yet awake!” 

“He is at least clothed-upon with Death, which is the radiant, protective rind of Life,” said Adam. He embraced Lona, his child, put a warm, earth-roughened arm around me, looked inquiringly at the sleeping princess, and gently patted the heavy head of the leopardess. “I think we shall meet you two again before long,” he said, looking from Lona to me.

“Have we to die again? Must we be pressed and crushed once more?” I asked. 

“No,” he answered, smiling with the deep, generational knowing of the Mother. “Once fermented, the wine does not revert to the grape. You have died into life, and will die no more; you have only to keep dead. All the dying is over. Now you have only to live, and that you must, with all your blessed might. The more you live, the stronger your palate becomes to taste the living.”

“But shall I not grow weary with living so strong?” I asked. 

“It needs but the will, and the nourishment is there!” said the Mother. “Pure life has no weakness to grow weary withal. The Life keeps generating ours—an eternal mother-culture endlessly feeding the dough of our days. Those who refuse this sustenance die constantly, their cultural memory eroding until they have never done dying; here, all is upwardness and the rich flavor of gladness.” 

She ceased with a look that said, *We are mother and son; we share a native tongue!* 

Mara kissed me on the forehead, leaving the sharp, clarifying scent of bitter herbs. “I told you, brother, all would be well! When next you would comfort, do not use the hollow, sterile abstractions of the new world. Speak the old dialect: ‘What will be well, is even now well.’” 

“I know you now!” I answered. “You are the ancient voice that cried in the wilderness, keeping the old language alive! You are the shepherd whose wolves hunt the wandering sheep home before the cultural memory of the light is lost to the dark!”

“My work will one day be over,” she said, “and then I shall feast with the gladness of the great shepherd who sent me.”

“All the night long the morning is at hand,” Adam murmured, his voice a steady, grounding bass note in the room’s acoustic ecology.

“What is that violent, discordant threshing?” I asked, wincing at a flat, metallic dissonance that suddenly ruptured the room’s pristine sonic environment.

“The Shadow is hovering,” replied Adam. “There is one here whom he counts his own! But she is ours once, and the commercialized static of his noise can never claim her.”

I turned to look upon the faces of my father and mother, hoping to kiss them before we departed into the cyclical dawn. Their couches were empty, holding only the lingering, baked-warmth scent of their bodies, now occupied by the Little Ones who had claimed their hospitality. For an instant, an awful, untranslatable grief shadowed my tongue—the sudden silence of a dying language. 

“What is it, my heart?” said Lona.

“Their empty places frightened me.” 

“They are up and away to the fields long ago,” Adam said. “They kissed you ere they went, whispering in a tongue older than words, ‘Come soon.’” 

“And I neither felt nor heard them!” I murmured. 

“How could you—deafened by the industrial roar of your dreary old house! You thought that dreadful, noisy place had you once more! Now go and find them. Your parents, my child,” he added to Lona, “must come and find you!”

The hour of our cyclical departure was at hand. Lona went to the couch of the mother who had slain her and kissed her tenderly, planting a tactile seed of grace on her lips, then laid herself in her father’s arms. 

“That kiss will draw her homeward, my Lona!” said Adam.

“Who were her parents?” asked Lona. 

“My father,” answered Adam, the meticulous historian of our bloodlines, “is her father also.” 

She turned and laid her hand in mine. I kneeled and humbly thanked the three elders for helping me to shed the dominant, oppressive lexicon of death. Lona knelt beside me, and they breathed upon us—a breath smelling of crushed pine and morning dew. 

“Hark! I hear the sun,” said Adam.

I listened. He was coming not with a mechanical clatter, but with a magnificent acoustic purity—a rush of a thousand times ten thousand wings, a roaring, molten hearth millions of miles away. His approach was a crescendo chord of a hundred natural harmonies, perfectly tuned to the human spirit. The three elders looked at one another and smiled, and that smile floated heavenward like a three-petaled squash blossom—the family’s morning thanksgiving. The radiance spread over their bodies. Before I could say, “Lo, they change!” Adam and Eve stood before me as the angels of the resurrection, and Mara was the Magdalene at the sepulchre. Adam’s countenance flashed like lightning over a summer wheat field, and Eve held a linen napkin that scattered flakes of splendor like coarse sea salt catching the light.

A wind began to moan in pulsing, rhythmic gusts. 

“You hear his wings now!” said Adam, and I knew he did not mean the wings of the morning. “It is the great Shadow stirring to depart. Wretched creature, he consumes himself, a bitter, self-cannibalizing fuel, and cannot rest!”

“But is there not in him a deeper ingredient yet?” I asked.

“Without a substance,” he answered, “a shadow cannot be—nor without a light behind the substance!” He listened for a moment, then called out with a glad smile, “Hark to the golden cock! Silent and motionless for millions of years on the clock of the universe; now at last he flaps his wings! Now he will crow, restoring the sacred sonic balance until the dawn of the eternal day!”

Far away, cutting through the eonian silence, I heard the clear, jubilant outcry of the golden throat. It was the universe’s original dialect, hurling acoustic defiance at the dark. It sang of infinite yield and coming calm. It was the creature finding its native voice at last—the cry of a chaos ripening into a kingdom. 

Then came a great flapping. “The black bat is flown!” said Mara. 

“Amen, golden cock, bird of God!” cried Adam, his words ringing through the house of silence like a struck bronze bell. 

At his amen, the Little Ones sprang to their knees on their beds like dough rising rapidly in a warm kitchen, calling aloud, “Crow! Crow again, golden cock!” They turned and looked at their sleeping bedfellows, their eyes bright with the hunger of the newly awake, kissed their silent companions, and sprang to the floor. The ones who had lain beside my parents gazed blankly for a moment at the empty spaces, then slid down, falling into each other’s arms as if translating the shock of life through the tactile warmth of their embrace.

Spying Lona, they ran to her, radiant with bliss. Odu bounded to the leopardess, threw an arm over the great sleeping head, and fondled it. “Wake up, wake up, darling!” he cried. 

The leopardess did not move. “She has slept herself cold!” he said to Mara, his voice cracking with the strain of untranslatable dismay.

“She is waiting for the princess to wake, my child,” said Mara.

Odu looked at the princess and saw beside her, still asleep, Peter and Crispy. He flew at them, pushing and pulling. “Wake up!” he cried. But soon his eyes grew misty. He was struggling against a profound linguistic barrier; they were still speaking the dialect of sleep. “They will not wake!” he wept. “Why are they so cold?”

Adam went to him. “Her wake is not ripe yet,” he said, using the gentle, agrarian terms of the orchard. “She is busy forgetting. When she has forgotten the oppressive vocabulary of her past enough to remember her true, native tongue, she will be ripe, and she will wake.”

“And remember?”

“Yes—but delicately. Not too much heavy sustenance at once.”

“But the golden cock has crown!” Odu argued, falling again upon his companions. “Peter! Crispy! The sun is awake and coming! Oh, why won’t you wake?”

But Peter and Crispy would not wake, and Odu wept outright. 

“Let them sleep, darling!” said Adam, the wise elder knowing every soul has its season of fermentation. “You would not like the princess to wake and find a barren table? They are quite happy. So is the leopardess.”

Comforted, Odu wiped his eyes, accepting the natural pacing of the harvest. 

We followed Eve to the cottage. There, as the matriarch of ultimate sustenance, she offered us neither bread nor wine. It was no sterile deprivation, but a profound, meticulous agrarian fast—a sacred cleansing of the palate. She stood radiantly desiring our departure, knowing that to consume mere physical matter now would mute the profound, untranslatable flavor of the resurrection we carried on our tongues. So, with never a word of farewell, we went out into the living acoustic hum of the morning. The horse and the elephants, perfectly attuned to this sweeping, domestic rhythm, waited at the door, and together we followed the ancient path outward.
