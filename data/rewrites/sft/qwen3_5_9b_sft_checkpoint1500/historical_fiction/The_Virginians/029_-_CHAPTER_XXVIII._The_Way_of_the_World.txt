For three days following his arrival at Tunbridge Wells, the young Virginian emerged as the undisputed center of attention in that bustling watering-place. Though no local nobleman commanded quite the same measure of curiosity, the Bishop of Salisbury himself received scantier reverence than Harry Warrington. As the youth traversed the streets, eyes swiveled to track his passage, and country folk paused in their market duties to stare with open admiration. Inside the assembly rooms, matrons warmly urged him to join them, ingeniously arranging circumstances to leave him in private moments with their daughters, nearly all of whom returned his smile.

Every soul within the town seemed to possess an intimate knowledge of Harry's estate, down to the precise acreage and shilling-for-shilling calculation of his income. At every tea table, his gambling winnings were dissected and recalculated with feverish precision. "Wonderful indeed," the narrator observed, "is the knowledge neighbors hold regarding our affairs!" The curiosity surrounding Harry was so profound that even his servant, Gumbo, became a subject of inquiry. Villagers approached the boy with ale and cold meats, seeking news of their master. Gumbo, plumpened by this sudden diet, ascended to the ranks of a leading valet in the district, lying with a gusto that bordered on the legendary.

No social gathering felt complete without the presence of Mr. Warrington. The lad, though amused and slightly astonished by this rapid elevation, carried himself with a dignified composure. His upbringing at home had instilled a robust self-regard, and his current fortune only served to validate it. Yet, he did not succumb to vanity. Engaging in games with challengers, he adhered to a peculiar code of honor; he felt compelled to answer every summons, whether for a race, a drinking bout, or a card match, acting as an ambassador for Old Virginia. Consequently, he proved a formidable rival to the locals, possessing a keen mind, a dexterous hand, a steady seat, and nerves of iron. In his spirited competition with the mother country, the representative of Virginia acquitted himself with distinction.

Madame de Bernstein, who had seemingly recovered from her previous losses through nightly gaming, was thoroughly delighted by her nephew's triumphs and reputation. He had bested Jack Morris at shooting, outpaced Mr. Scamper in a riding match, and held his own remarkably well against Captain Batts at tennis, despite having never touched a racket prior to his arrival. Furthermore, he had faced the celebrated gamblers, the Lords of Chesterfield and March, earning praise from both for his coolness, gallantry, and breeding. While his mathematical skills at the books were not dazzling, his handwriting was impeccable, and his naive ignorance charmed the old lady. A lover of literature, humor, and the works of Molière and Fielding, yet preferring the living world to the library, she supervised his attire with maternal delight, sourcing exquisite laces for his ruffles and securing a delicate diamond brooch for his cravat. Soon, he became the prime favorite among all her nephews and kin.

Lady Maria, however, could not help but feel a sting of jealousy at her brother's successes, fearing that her own prospects with their wealthy aunt were eclipsed. Indeed, the other gentlemen must have trembled with envy as they witnessed Mr. Warrington's meteoric rise in the Baroness's favor. For two weeks, Harry became a figure of immense importance, navigating the circles of the "good" company while inevitably stumbling upon the "bad." Was it his fault for accepting the world as he found it, drinking from the sweet, sparkling cup of pleasure that had been filled to the brim for him? Aunt Bernstein, neither a rigorous moralist nor a prudent guide for youth, merely bid him pursue his enjoyments. If letters of affection arrived from the mysterious Cattarina, the Baroness likely would have encouraged acceptance; yet Harry retained a stock of modesty inherited from his colonial home, where libertine vices were unknown. He blushed at the bold talk of his new European associates, finding even his aunt's wit and jokes startling.

Despite his innocence, the world readily credited him with the vices of others. How could he know better than to avoid the saucy Cattarina? Having seen Lord March drive her in his carriage, Harry deemed nothing improper in offering her his arm on the public promenade. When she covetously eyed a trinket at a toy shop, he delighted in presenting it, followed soon after by a piece of lace, and then another fad. However, when her requests began to escalate, the young gentleman gently deflected them with laughter, refusing to purchase diamond drops for her ears. He possessed a shrewdness beneath his generosity and showed no sign of recklessness.

Yet, who would credit his modesty? Old Bernstein persisted in believing her nephew was playing the role of Don Juan, supplanting Lord March. This belief intensified whenever poor Maria was present, delighting the aunt in stinging the spinster's tender heart with tales of her niece's piteous silence and discomfiture. "Boys will be boys," the Baroness declared, insisting Harry should not be the first "milksop" in his family—a sentiment that often left Maria choking on the bread she ate at her aunt's expense.

Meanwhile, Colonel Wolfe, ever devoted to the lady of his love, visited frequently, leaving little room for Harry's company. Yet, the officer's conversation stood in stark contrast to the ribald chatter surrounding Harry. Wolfe spoke of no cards, horse pedigrees, hunting exploits, or female favors; he avoided retailing the era's endless scandals. The age itself was dissolute, marked by a king with open mistresses and a nobility driven mad by pleasure, a looseness of tongue and deed that required careful navigation. Harry, however, remained unaware of how his actions were interpreted. He failed to notice Colonel Wolfe's increasingly cold demeanor or the stories circulating about him. Who crafted these tales? Who believed them? Poor Harry, content with his rides and games, was passing among respectable circles as the most abandoned and profligate youth of the moment.

Tragically, the gentle lad, so simple and easily pleased, was whispered about as a hardened gambler, a spendthrift, and a frequenter of abandoned women. These rumors reached Colonel Lambert at Oakhurst, filling the good man's heart with grief and care. First came one tale, then another, until crowds of malicious whispers convinced the family of a catastrophe. Mrs. Lambert, unable to resist her husband's anxiety, pressed for answers until Lambert confessed the truth. The shock rippled through the household; Theo and Hester, initially defiant, eventually succumbed to the weight of the scandal. "O, the serpent they had nurtured!" lamented the kind-hearted ladies, imagining Harry parading his shame alongside that horrid Frenchwoman, distributing diamonds, and exposing himself to the gaze of the entire town at the Wells. Even the parish priest, preaching against the human propensity to believe the worst of neighbors, found his sermons futile against the tide of gossip.

While Harry remained blissfully unaware of the storm brewing around his name, indulging in an idle and pleasant existence, the reality was far grimmer. Emerging from a tennis match with Captain Batts, Harry encountered Colonel Wolfe, who had just finished a visit to his beloved. Harry extended his hand, but the Colonel's grip was icy, his salute perfunctory. The Captain looked disconcerted as he departed, leaving Harry to ponder the strange chill between them.

"I have not seen you for many days," Harry remarked.

"You have sought other companions," Wolfe replied curtly.

"But I prefer your company to theirs," Harry protested.

"Indeed, I might offer better counsel than some of them," Wolfe suggested grimly. "Do you mean Captain Batts?"

"He teaches me tennis, yes. But he is hand-in-glove with my Lord and the fashionable set."

"I am not a man of fashion," Wolfe stated flatly. "And if you continue to live with such, trust me, you will find many humble folk cannot afford their company. I am courting an honorable lady. Yesterday, I saw you walking openly with a French ballet-dancer, removing your hat. I must tell you plainly: I had rather you did not remove your hat in such company."

"Sir," Harry cried, turning crimson, "do you suggest I forgo the honor of your acquaintance?"

"I certainly request it when you keep her company," Wolfe answered angrily, using a word unsuitable for print, reminiscent of Othello.

"Heavens! What shame to speak thus of any woman!" Harry exclaimed. "How dare anyone call her dishonest?"

"You ought to know best, sir," Wolfe countered, surprised by Harry's defense. "Or the world lies greatly about you."

"What ought I to know?" Harry demanded. "I see a poor little dancer ordered by doctors to drink the waters. Is it not true that a gentleman of my rank rarely keeps such company? Are you so squeamish? I can speak a little French, and she is welcome to it. If Colonel Wolfe chooses not to bow, let him do so."

"Do you truly not know the woman's character?" Wolfe asked sharply.

"Of course, she is a dancer. But if she were a duchess, I could not respect her more."

"And do you not recall winning her at dice from Lord March?" Wolfe pressed. "Everyone knows the story. I heard it myself from the worthy Mr. Richardson. They speak of you as a colonial Lovelace."

"What else have they said?" Harry asked, bracing himself.
