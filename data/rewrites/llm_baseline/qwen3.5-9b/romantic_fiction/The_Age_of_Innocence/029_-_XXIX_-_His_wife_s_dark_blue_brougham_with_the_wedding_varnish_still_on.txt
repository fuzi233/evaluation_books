His wife's dark blue brougham, still gleaming with the fresh coat of wedding varnish, waited for him at the ferry landing. Inside, it offered a sanctuary of luxury against the gloom outside. It was a somber, snowy afternoon, and within the vast, echoing Pennsylvania Station in Jersey City, the gas lamps cast a warm, artificial glow upon the wet stone floor.

Archer paced the platform, waiting for the Washington Express. As he moved, his thoughts drifted to the dreamers of the city—those visionary souls who spoke of a future where a tunnel would pierce beneath the Hudson, allowing trains to rush directly into Manhattan. They were the same ones predicting transatlantic crossings in mere days, machines that could fly, cities illuminated entirely by electricity, and voices carried instantly across continents without wire.

"I don't care which of these fantasies becomes reality," Archer mused aloud to the empty air, "provided the tunnel remains unbuilt."

In a haze of boyish, irrational joy, he conjured a perfect scene: Madame Olenska stepping from the train, her figure small and pale amidst the sea of indifferent faces. He imagined finding her, guiding her arm as they navigated toward the carriage, dodging slipping horses and shouting cart drivers until they reached the relative silence of the ferry boat. There, seated side by side in the motionless vehicle, they would watch the earth roll away beneath them, drifting toward the other side of the sun.

It was impossible to contain the torrent of words rising in his throat—the eloquence of his feelings, the urgency of his confession.

Suddenly, the rhythmic clanging and groaning of the approaching train grew louder. Like a beast burdened with prey returning to its den, the locomotive lumbered into the station. Archer surged forward, pushing through the crush of humanity, his gaze scanning the windows of the elevated cars.

And then, there she was.

Madame Olenska's pale, startled face appeared close at hand. For a fleeting second, he felt the humiliating shock of forgetting exactly what she looked like. Before he could dwell on it, they met. Their hands joined, and he drew her arm firmly through his.

"This way—I have the carriage," he said.

Everything unfolded precisely as he had envisioned. He assisted her into the brougham with her luggage, and later recalled vaguely reassuring her about her grandmother and summarizing the precarious situation at Beaufort. He noted the softness of her spirit, thinking, *Poor Regina!*

Meanwhile, the carriage maneuvered itself out of the station's chaotic coils, crawling down the slick incline toward the wharf. They were surrounded by swaying coal carts, bewildered horses, and disheveled wagons. Among them loomed an empty hearse—a grim specter in the gray daylight.

As it passed, she shut her eyes tightly, clutching Archer's hand. "If only it doesn't mean... poor Granny!"

"Oh, no, no! She is much better. She is quite alright, truly." He spoke urgently, as if his reassurance could alter fate. "There—we've passed it!"

Her hand remained locked in his. As the carriage lurched across the gangplank onto the ferry, Archer leaned over, unbuttoning her tight brown glove to kiss her palm with the reverence due a sacred relic. She withdrew slightly, offering a faint smile.

"You didn't expect me today?" she asked.

"Oh, no."

"I intended to go to Washington to see you. I had arranged everything; I nearly missed you on the train."

"Oh—" She exclaimed, as though terrified by the narrowness of their escape. "Do you know... I hardly remembered you?"

"Hardly remembered me?"

"I mean..." She paused, searching for words. "How shall I explain? It's always so. Each time you happen to me all over again."

"Oh, yes, I know! I know!"

"Does it—to you?" he pressed insistently.

She nodded, gazing out the window.

"Ellen—Ellen—Ellen!"

She gave no answer. He fell silent, watching her profile blur against the snow-streaked twilight beyond the glass. What had she done in those four long months? How little they truly knew of one another! The precious moments slipped through his fingers, leaving him helpless to articulate the thoughts he had meant to share. All he could do was brood over the paradox of their existence: sitting inches apart yet separated by an invisible chasm, unable to truly see each other's faces.

"What a lovely carriage!" she remarked suddenly, turning from the window. "Is it May's?"

"Yes."

"So May sent you to fetch me? How kind of her!"

For a moment, Archer said nothing. Then, driven by a sudden impulse of retaliation, he blurted out: "Your husband's secretary came to see me the day after we met in Boston."

In his letter to her, he had omitted any mention of Mr. Rivière's visit, intending to keep the incident buried. Yet, knowing she was riding in his wife's carriage sparked a defensive urge. He wanted to see if she reacted to his mention of Rivière as sharply as she had reacted to May.

To his surprise, she showed no sign of alarm. Instantly, he concluded: "He writes to her, then."

"M. Rivière went to see you?"

"Yes. Didn't you know?"

"No," she replied simply. "And you're not surprised?"

She hesitated. "Why should I be? He told me in Boston that he knew you; that he had met you in England, I think."

"Ellen—I must ask you one thing."

"Yes."

"I wanted to ask it after I saw him, but I couldn't manage it in a letter. Was it M. Rivière who helped you escape when you left your husband?"

His heart hammered against his ribs. Would she meet this direct inquiry with the same calm composure?

"Yes," she answered, her voice quiet and steady, devoid of tremor. "I owe him a great debt."

Her tone was so natural, so almost indifferent, that Archer's inner turmoil subsided. Once again, her sheer simplicity had rendered him feeling strangely conventional, just when he believed he was casting aside all conventions.

"I think you are the most honest woman I have ever met!" he exclaimed.

"Oh, no—but perhaps one of the least fussy," she countered, a smile coloring her voice. "Call it what you wish: you look at things as they are."

"Ah—I've had to. I've had to look at the Gorgon."

"Well—it hasn't blinded you! You've seen that she is merely an old bogie, like all the rest."

"She doesn't blind one; but she dries up one's tears."

The answer silenced the plea forming on Archer's lips. It seemed to emanate from depths of experience far beyond his reach.

The ferry boat finally stopped, its bow bumping violently against the wooden piles of the slip. The jolt threw Archer and Madame Olenska against one another. Trembling, the young man felt the pressure of her shoulder and wrapped his arm around her.

"If you are not blind, then you must see that this cannot last," he whispered.

"What cannot?"

"Our being together—and not together."

"No. You ought not to have come today," she said, her voice altering. Suddenly, she turned, throwing her arms around him and pressing her lips to his.

At that exact moment, the carriage began to move, and a gas lamp at the head of the slip flashed its light into the window. She drew away, and they sat in silence and stillness as the brougham struggled through the congested traffic of the ferry landing.

Once they reached the street, Archer began to speak rapidly. "Do not be afraid of me; you need not retreat into your corner like that. A stolen kiss is not what I desire. Look: I am not even attempting to touch the sleeve of your jacket. Do not suppose I do not understand your reasons for fearing that our feelings might dwindle into some ordinary, secret affair. I could not have spoken thus yesterday, for when we are apart and I anticipate seeing you, every thought burns in a great flame. But now that you are here—you are so much more than I remembered, and what I want of you is so much more than an hour or two here and there, interspersed with wasteful waits—that I can sit perfectly still beside you, like this, holding that other vision in my mind, quietly trusting it to become real."

For a moment, she did not reply. Then, barely above a whisper, she asked: "What do you mean by trusting it to come true?"

"Why—you know it will, don't you?"

"Your vision of us together?"

She burst into a sudden, sharp laugh. "You choose your place well to put it to me!"

"Do you mean because we are in my wife's brougham? Shall we step out and walk? I daresay you do not mind a little snow?"

She laughed again, softer this time. "No; I shall not step out and walk, because my business is to reach Granny's as quickly as possible. And you will sit beside me, and we shall look, not at visions, but at realities."

"I do not know what you mean by realities. The only reality to me is this."
