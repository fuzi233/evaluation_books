In the time of Harry Warrington, society was deeply engaged in a debate—though our young gentleman cared little for it—about whether the ancients or the moderns were superior in literature. The learned and the world at large generally favored the ancients. But what are the moderns of that era but the ancients of our own? We now judge them as future generations will one day judge us.

As for book learning, respectable ancestors—though you had Gibbon on your side—I must say you are outmatched. Today, professors at Cambridge and Glasgow know more Greek than all the universities of Europe combined could boast in your time, even including Athens itself, if such a place existed then. In science, you were scarcely ahead of the heathens whose literary superiority you acknowledged. And what of public and private morality? Is this year, 1858, truly worse than its predecessor a century earlier? Gentlemen of Mr. Disraeli’s House of Commons: does each of you carry a price, as in Walpole’s or Newcastle’s day—or (and here is the delicate question) have nearly all of you already been bought?

Ladies, I do not claim you are a society of Vestals—but the chronicles of a hundred years past brim with scandal so shocking that you may well be grateful you did not live in such perilous times. No—on my conscience, I believe men and women are better now. Not only are there more Susannas, but the Elders are not nearly as wicked as they once were.

Have you ever heard of books like *Clarissa*, *Tom Jones*, or *Roderick Random*? Paintings by artists of their day capturing the lives and manners of men and women in full, unvarnished detail? Imagine describing the exploits of Mr. Lovelace or Lady Bellaston—or that remarkable “Lady of Quality” who lent her memoirs to the author of *Peregrine Pickle*. How the pure and outraged nineteenth century would blush, scream, flee the room, send away the young ladies, and order Mr. Mudie never again to deliver one of those odious works!

You are fifty-eight, madam, and perhaps too sensitive—too quick to cry out before any harm is done, when no offense was meant. Or perhaps the novelist’s art suffers under the restraints imposed upon it, just as many an honest statue in St. Peter’s or the Vatican is spoiled by the tin draperies ecclesiastical women have wrapped around its limbs. Yet in your prudery, there is reason. So too in the state censorship of the press. A page may contain matter dangerous to good morals. Out with your scissors, censor—cut out the prurient paragraph! We have no choice but to submit. Society, the despot, has issued its decree. We may lament that the statue looked finer without the tin covering; we may argue that the full moral tale would be better told. But away with it—no word!

I’ve never seen pianofortes in America with frilled muslin trousers draped over their legs, but I’m certain the muslin covered some notes as effectively as the mahogany, muffled the music, and hindered the player.

To what does this prelude lead? I am thinking of Harry Warrington, Esquire, in his lodgings in Bond Street, London, and the life he led—along with many other fashionable young men of his time—and how I cannot possibly take my dear young reader into such a world, any more than Lady Squeams could bring her daughter to Cremorne Gardens on an ordinary evening.

My dear Miss Diana—though I know you’re thirty-eight, despite your shy manner and your wish to appear freshly out of school—you must understand that when your grandfather was a young man about town, a member of one of the clubs at White’s, dining at Pontac’s off feasts prepared by Braund and Lebeck, riding to Newmarket with March and Rockingham, and toasting the best in England with Gilly Williams and George Selwyn (though he never understood George’s jokes, which have long since lost their flavor)—he lived a life your noble aunt, author of *Legends of the Squeams’s; or, Fair Fruits of a Family Tree*, never hinted at.

This was before your grandmother adopted the serious views that marked her later years in Bath, and before Colonel Tibbalt married Miss Lye, the heiress of a rich soap-boiler. That was when her ladyship sowed her wild oats. When she was young, she was as giddy as any other woman of fashion. At her house in Hill Street, ten card-tables were laid out every Wednesday and Sunday evening—except during the brief season when Ranelagh opened on Sundays. Every night of her life, she gambled for eight, nine, or ten hours. Everyone else in society did the same. She lost, she won, she cheated, she pawned her jewels—what else might she have pawned to feed her passion for play?

And what of that after-supper duel at the Shakespeare’s Head in Covent Garden between your grandfather and Colonel Tibbalt? They drew swords and fought in the presence of Sir John Screwby, who was drunk beneath the table. They were interrupted by Mr. John Fielding’s men, and your grandfather was carried home to Hill Street, wounded, in a chair.

Yes, those gentlemen in powdered wigs and ruffles—those who turned out the toes of their buckled shoes so delicately—were fearsome indeed. Swords were drawn constantly; bottle after bottle emptied; oaths roared through taverns; watchmen and tavern keepers were slashed and injured; chairmen beaten; citizens insulted by drunken pleasure-seekers.

You’ve been to Cremorne, I suppose—with proper vouchers, of course? Do you remember our great theatres thirty years ago? You were too refined to attend a play. Well, you have no idea what the playhouses were like, or what the green boxes were, when Garrick and Mrs. Pritchard performed before them! And I, for my children’s sake, thank that good actor in retirement who first banished such shame from the stage.

No, madam, I do not boast of my virtue. I do not claim you are naturally better than your ancestress in her wild, rouged, gambling, flaring days—or even than poor Polly Fogle, recently arrested for shoplifting, who would have been hanged a century ago. Only, I am deeply thankful that my temptations are fewer, though I still wrestle with those of my own age.

So, if Harry Warrington rides to Newmarket for the October meeting, wins or loses his money there; if he joins a party at the Shakespeare’s Head or Bedford Head; if he dines at White’s ordinary and plays macco and lansquenet afterward; if he boxes the watch or appears at the Roundhouse; if he becomes, for a time, a reckless, dissipated, harum-scarum young man—knowing human nature as I do, I shall not be surprised. And knowing my own failings, I shall not be harsh toward my neighbor’s.

Mr. Sampson was another story. In his chapel in Long Acre, he scourged Vice mercilessly; gave Sin no quarter; cursed Blasphemy with greater anathemas than others; knocked Drunkenness down and trampled it in the gutter; dragged out conjugal Infidelity and pounded her with endless rhetorical stones. Yet after service, he came to dinner at the Star and Garter, made a bowl of punch for Harry and his friends at the Bedford Head, or played whist at Mr. Warrington’s lodgings or my Lord March’s—wherever good company and supper awaited him.

Yet I often wonder—if I have taken my usual uncharitable view of Mr. Warrington during this period of his arrival in London. For I have said nothing of virtuous indignation against his conduct. If it was not wrong, then I have judged him cruelly.

O Truthful, O Beautiful, O Modesty, O Benevolence, O Pudor, O Mores, O Blushing Shame, O Namby Pamby—each with your honored capital letters! O Niminy, O Piminy! How can I dare say that a young man was ever truly a young man?

Indeed, dear young lady, I may be calumniating Mr. Warrington, as I so often do. As proof, here is a letter from the Warrington collection—Harry writing to his mother—containing not a single word suggesting he leads a wild life. And such a letter from an only son to a loving, admirable parent must surely be true:

---

I take up my pen to acknowledge your honored favor of 10 July, received via the Lively Virginia packet, forwarded by our Bristol agent. I rejoice to hear the crops look promising. 'Tis Tully who says agriculture is the noblest pursuit—how delightful when it is also profitable!

Since my last letter from Tunbridge Wells, a few incidents have occurred that I feel compelled to inform my honored mother of. Our party broke up at the end of August, with partridge-shooting beginning. Baroness Bernstein, whose kindness to me has been constant, has gone to Bath, her usual winter resort, and kindly gifted me a fifty-pound bill. I rode back with Rev. Mr. Sampson, whose instruction I find most valuable, and my cousin, Lady Maria, to Castlewood. [Could Parson Sampson have been dictating these remarks?] I paid a brief visit en route to my dear friends Colonel and Mrs. Lambert at Oakhurst House, who send their most affectionate regards to you. Their youngest daughter, I regret to say, is delicate, and her parents are anxious.

At Castlewood, my stay was short due to a quarrel with my cousin William. He is a man of violent passions and, alas, prone to drink when he lacks self-control. In a minor dispute over a horse, words grew heated, and he aimed a blow at me—or its equivalent—which my grandfather’s child, my honored mother’s son, could not endure. I responded, felled him to the ground, whereupon he was carried almost senseless to bed. I sent inquiries about his health the next morning, but hearing nothing further, left for London, where I have remained since, save for brief absences.

Knowing you wished me to see my dear grandfather’s university, I recently rode to Cambridge with friends, passing through parts of Hertfordshire and staying at the famed inn of Ware. The October meeting had just begun when I arrived. I saw students in their gowns and caps, and visited Newmarket Heath, where races were underway—Lord March’s horse Marrowbones by Cleaver winning a large stake. It was an entertaining day—the jockeys, horses, and betting vastly different from our modest races at home. The richest nobles mix freely with jockeys, betting all around. Cambridge pleased me, especially King’s College Chapel, of rich yet elegant Gothic design.

I have entered society and become a member of the club at White’s, where I meet gentlemen of the highest fashion. Lords Rockingham, Carlisle, Orford, Bolingbroke, and Coventry are among my acquaintances, introduced by Lord March, whom I have mentioned before. Lady Coventry is a fine woman, though thin. Every lady paints here, young and old—so if you, Mountain, and Fanny wish to be fashionable, I must send you some rouge pots. Everyone plays—eight to ten card tables at every house on receiving nights. I regret to say not all play fairly, nor pay fairly. I have been forced to sit down and do as Rome does. Indeed, I have witnessed ladies—whom I could name—take my counters right before my eyes!

One day, when his regiment, the 20th, was paraded in St. James’s Park, a friend, Mr. Wolfe, presented me to His Royal Highness the Captain-General. He was most gracious—a fat, jolly prince, if I may speak so without disrespect—reminding me of General Braddock, whom we knew to our sorrow last year. Upon hearing my name and learning how dear George served and fell in Braddock’s ill-fated campaign, he spoke at length with me, asking why a young man like me did not serve too, why I did not go to the King of Prussia—who was a great general—to see campaigns, and whether that would not be better than idling at routs and card parties in London? I replied that I would gladly go, but being an only son on leave from my mother, I belong to our estate in Virginia. His Royal Highness said Mr. Braddock had written home about Mrs. Esmond’s loyalty, and that he would be glad to serve me. Mr. Wolfe and I have since called upon him at his residence in Pall Mall. The latter, still quite young, fought in the Scots campaign with His Royal Highness, whom Mr. Dempster admires greatly at home. To be sure, he was too severe—though severity against rebels in arms is hard to justify.

Mr. Draper has transferred half the stock, my late father’s property, into my name. Until the painful loss in our family—whose restoration I would give my right hand for—is settled beyond doubt, the remaining stock must remain in trust for the heir. Ah, dear mother! There is no day, scarce an hour, when I do not think of him. I wish he were beside me. I feel stronger when I remember him, and would honor my family by having him represent it here instead of—

Honored madam, your dutiful and affectionate son,

P.S.—I am like your sex, who always, they say, put their chief news in a postscript. I have something to tell you about a person to whom my heart is engaged. I shall write more later—there is no hurry. Suffice it to say she is a nobleman’s daughter, and her family as good as our own.

---

My good sister,

We have been, throughout our lives, more than kin and less than kind—using the words of a poet your dear father loved dearly. When you were born in our Western Principality, my mother was not as old as Isaac’s; but even then, I was far older than you. Though she gave you all she could—her love included, though it ought to have been mine—I hope we may learn to do without affection, if we can at least have goodwill. Some small kindness you owe me—for your son’s sake, and for your father’s, whom I loved and admired more than any man I have known. He was greater than most, though he made no noise in the world. I have seen many who did, yet found few with such good heads and good hearts as Mr. Esmond.

Had we been closer, I might have advised you regarding your young gentleman’s introduction to Europe—you would have taken it or not, as people do. At least you would have said afterward, “What she counselled me was right. Had Harry done as Madam Beatrix wished, it would have been better for him.”

My good sister, it was not for you to know, nor for me to tell you—since you never wrote to me—but your boy, coming to England and Castlewood, found few friends there, except one old aunt, about whom all sorts of evil have been spoken for fifty years—and not without cause.

Now, I must tell Harry’s mother what will likely surprise her: almost everyone who knows him loves him. He is prudent in speech, generous with money, bold as a lion, with a commanding manner that suits him well. Whether he is handsome, you know best. Dear sister, I like him none the less for not being overly witty or wise. I never cared for those “set-the-Thames-afire” gentlemen who think themselves cleverer than their neighbors. Your father’s great friend, Mr. Addison, seemed to me a supercilious prig, and his follower, Sir Dick Steele, was unpleasant both in and out of his cups. And (revenons à luy), your Master Harry will certainly not burn the river with his wit.

He knows little of book learning—no more than any lord in England—and for this, I hold him none the worse. If Heaven has not given him a turn for it, there’s no use forcing him.

Considering the station he holds in his colony when he returns, and the lineage from which he comes, let me tell you: he does not have enough means to support his rank. He is likely to grow desperate, break bounds, and become extravagant—not his natural tendency, but driven by necessity. He likes to live as well as his companions, and, between ourselves, has fallen into some of the finest and most rakish circles in England. He believes it is honorable not to retreat, and often orders ortolans and champagne when he would just as soon dine on a steak and a mug of beer. From what he has told me in conversation—very naïve, as the French say—I suspect his mother has encouraged his high opinion of himself. Women like their belongings to feel important, even if they dislike paying the cost.

Will you let your lad make a fool of himself in London? Triple his allowance—at least—and Aunt Bernstein (with your permission) will add more. Otherwise, he will spend the small capital I learn he has in this country, and once a young man begins to manage, the loaf is quickly finished.

God willing, I shall leave Henry Esmond’s grandson something at my death—but my savings are small, and the pension granted me by my gracious Sovereign ends with me. As for feu M. de Bernstein, he left only debt at his passing—the officers of His Majesty’s Electoral Court of Hannover are poorly paid.

A lady currently very high in His Majesty’s favor has taken a great fancy to your lad and will soon introduce him to the Sovereign’s notice. He has met His Royal Highness the Duke. If he lives in America, why should not Mr. Esmond Warrington return as Governor of Virginia, with a title? That is my hope for him.

Meanwhile, I must be candid: I fear he has entangled himself in a foolish engagement. Even marrying an old woman for money is barely pardonable—the game ne valant guères la chandelle. Mr. Bernstein, while alive, assured me of this, and I believe him, poor gentleman. To promise oneself to an old woman without money, and marry her solely because you promised, seems to me a folly only very young men fall into—and I fear Mr. Warrington is one. How or for what reason, I do not know, but my niece Maria Esmond has extracted a promise from Harry. He knows nothing of her past—I do. For twenty years, she has sought twenty husbands. I care not how she obtained his promise. It is a sin and a shame that a woman over forty should deceive a young man and bind him to his word. She is not the woman she pretends to be. A horse jockey cannot fool him—but a woman can.

I write this news deliberately, though it must displease you. Perhaps it will bring you to England—but above all, be cautious, gentle. The slightest pressure will make his proud spirit resist. I fear the property is entailed, so threats of disinheriting him will not sway Maria. Otherwise, I know her to be so mercenary that—though she genuinely fancies this handsome youth—without money, she would not hear of him.

All I could do, and more than I ought, I have done to prevent the match. What I cannot say here, I will not write. But I am, for Henry Esmond’s sake, his grandson’s sincerest friend—and madam, your faithful sister and servant,

To Mrs. Esmond Warrington of Castlewood, in Virginia.

On the back of this letter, in Madam Esmond’s handwriting:
“My sister Bernstein’s letter, received with Henry’s December 24. On receipt of which it was determined my son should instantly go home.”
