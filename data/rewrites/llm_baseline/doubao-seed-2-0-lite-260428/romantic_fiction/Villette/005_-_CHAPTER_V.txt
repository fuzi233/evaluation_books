My old mistress was gone, and once more I found myself entirely alone, with no choice but to hunt for a new position before the week ran out. I will not pretend my nerves were steady in those hollow, grief-soft days. I looked far from well: thin, haggard, my eyes hollow from all the late hours I had spent sitting at Miss Marchmont’s sickbed, so much so that a stranger might have taken me for an overwrought drudge or a desperate soul running from debt. But I owed no one a penny, nor was I fully destitute. Though Miss Marchmont had not lived long enough to bestow the generous legacy she had promised me on her final night, her heir—her pinched-nosed, narrow-templed, miserly second cousin—paid my wages in full after the funeral, leaving me with fifteen pounds tucked safe in my pocket, a body worn but not broken, and a spirit frayed but far from defeated. Beside so many who had far less, I was in a fortunate position; but it was also an unbearably awkward one, as I was all too keenly aware when I realized I had no new home lined up for the day I was due to leave my current lodgings.

In that tight dilemma, I turned to the only resource I had left: an old family servant, once my childhood nurse, who now worked as housekeeper at a grand mansion not far from Miss Marchmont’s home. I spent a few hours with her, and she comforted me as best she could, but she had no solid advice to offer. My mind still wrapped in unbroken darkness, I left her at twilight, faced with a two-mile walk home on a clear, frosty night. For all my solitude, my small purse, and my tangled worries, my heart—beating with the unspent vigour of twenty-two years, not yet even touching twenty-three—was light, not feeble. If my courage had faltered, I would have quailed on that lonely path, winding through silent fields with not a village, farmhouse, or cottage in sight, guided only by starlight with no moon to light my way. I would have been terrified by the strange, shifting mystery glowing in the northern sky: the Aurora Borealis. But that solemn, otherworldly stranger did not stir fear in me. It seemed to pour new strength straight into my bones, and I drew in that sharp, crisp breeze like it was made of pure energy. A bold thought landed soft and steady in my mind, and I found I had the strength to hold it. “Leave this narrow wilderness,” the thought whispered, “and go out into the wider world.” “Where?” I asked the dark, and before I could even wonder, I pictured it, vivid as a memory I had carried all my life even though I had never seen it with my own eyes: London. The great, sprawling, humming city, waiting fifty miles away.

The very next day I walked back to the grand hall to find Mrs. Barrett, and laid my plan before her. She was a grave, judicious woman, who knew barely more of the world than I did, but she did not call me mad. I had always carried myself with a quiet, staid composure that had long served me like a warm, unassuming cloak, letting me do things that would have made others brand me a silly dreamer if I had spoken of them with wild, excited airs. She was slowly pointing out possible hurdles as she prepared orange rind to make marmalade, when a small boy came bounding laughing through the window, running straight to me—we were not strangers, for his mother, the young married daughter of the house, had been my old schoolfellow. I lifted the pretty dark-eyed child onto my knee, and in walked his mother, Mrs. Leigh. The plain, dull girl I had known at school had bloomed into a soft, beautiful, kind-looking woman, transformed by wifehood and motherhood. She did not recognize me, changed as I was, and I made no move to remind her. She had come to fetch her son for a walk, with a foreign nurse carrying a new infant trailing behind her. I noticed she spoke to the nurse in French—very imperfect French, with a thick, unruly accent that took me straight back to our schooldays—and the little boy chattered away volubly in the same foreign tongue.

When they had left, Mrs. Barrett told me the foreign nurse had come back with the family two years prior from a trip to the Continent, was treated almost as well as a governess, and had no duties beyond walking with the children and speaking to them in French. “And she says there are dozens of young Englishwomen on the Continent placed just as well as she is, in foreign households,” Mrs. Barrett added. I tucked that little scrap of casual knowledge away in my mind, the way a careful housewife tucks away a seemingly useless scrap of fabric, knowing one day it might come in useful. Before I left, she gave me the address of a respectable, old-fashioned inn in the City of London, the very one my own uncles had stayed at in years gone by.

The trip to London was far less reckless than the reader might imagine. It was only fifty miles away. My fifteen pounds would pay for the coach fare, keep me a few days in the city, and even pay my way home if I found no reason to stay. I treated it like a tiny, rare holiday for my overworked, weary mind, not some life-or-death adventure. There is no better way to keep your mind and body calm than to take every step in moderation, rather than wrapping it up in grand, dramatic notions that only stir up feverish excitement. This was long before the railways made the journey swift: fifty miles took a full day of travel, and when I climbed down from the coach at nine o’clock on a wet February night, I had stepped straight into the vast, roaring wilderness of London, for the very first time.

I had no time for poetic first impressions, even if I had been inclined to cherish them. The cabmen’s rough, unfamiliar local accents sounded as strange to my country ears as a foreign language. Still, I managed to make myself understood, and got myself and my small trunk safely to the old inn. It felt overwhelming, oppressive, utterly disorienting: first time in the sprawling Babylon of London, first time staying in an inn, travel-weary, shivering with cold, with no experience to guide me and no choice but to figure out what to do next. I handed the problem over to my common sense, which was just as chilled and bewildered as the rest of my wits, but spurred on by sharp, unignorable necessity, it got to work. I paid the porter (and yes, he cheated me horribly, but I was too new and too tired to protest), asked the waiter for a room, called for the chambermaid, and bore the young lady’s supercilious, town-smart attitude without breaking down. She was all trim, crisp waist, neat cap, and mincing glib speech, her pretty, polished town manners making my plain country dress feel shabby and out of place. But I kept my quiet composure, and before long, even the snooty staff treated me with careful politeness, swinging between faint patronage and proper respect as they realized I was no common household servant.

I held myself together perfectly until I had eaten a little refreshment, warmed myself by the fire, and locked myself alone in my small room. But the second I sat down on the edge of the bed and rested my head on my arms against the pillow, a terrible, heavy oppression crashed over me. My whole situation rose up before me like a ghost: anomalous, desolate, almost bare of hope. What was I doing here, all alone in the greatest city in the land? What would I do tomorrow? What prospects did I even have? What friends did I have in the whole world? I soaked the pillow, my sleeves, and my hair with hot, rushing tears. That dark, bitter wave passed, though, and I never once regretted the step I had taken, never wanted to turn back. A strong, quiet certainty settled over me: it was far better to go forward than to turn back, that I could go forward, that some narrow, difficult path would eventually open up for me, no matter how impossible it seemed right now. That quiet hope hushed all my fears, until I was calm enough to say my prayers and climb into bed.

I had just blown out my candle and laid down to sleep, when a deep, low, mighty tone swung through the hush of the night. I did not know it at first, but it boomed twelve times, and by the twelfth deep, thrumming knell, I knew exactly where I lay: in the long, warm, waiting shadow of St. Paul’s Cathedral, with the whole of my unwritten new life stretched out before me.
