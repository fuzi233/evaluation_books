The afternoon sun filtered weakly through the shutters, casting a pale, indifferent light upon the room. Gerard, though no longer reeling from the initial shock of his injury, was consumed by a feverish irritability, his mind racing through a labyrinth of delirious fantasies. In one such haze, he demanded a lemon, a sour necessity to quell the dryness in his throat. Denys, transformed from a rough-hewn soldier into a figure of tender, grandfatherly devotion, rose with alacrity. With a soothing gesture to calm his young charge, he promised the fruit would arrive in the blink of an eye, vanishing immediately to scour the local markets.

The search proved arduous; lemons were rare luxuries in the North, unlike the abundance known today. Time stretched agonizingly as Gerard's patience frayed, until finally, the door swung open. Yet, it was not Denys who entered. Instead, a commanding silhouette glided softly into the room—an elderly gentleman clad in the solemn regalia of high medicine. He wore a long, sober gown trimmed with rich fur, paired with cherry-colored hose and pointed shoes. At his hip rested a sword sheathed in fine morocco leather, and encircling his neck was a ruff, starched with rigid severity and artificially deepened by hidden wooden frameworks known as *rebatoes*. Upon his head sat a four-cornered cap bordered with fur, framing a majestic white beard that cascaded over his chin and bosom. Following closely at his heels was a boy carrying a basket brimming with phials, lint, and surgical instruments, their metallic gleam inviting scrutiny.

There was no ambiguity regarding the visitor's profession; save for the weapon, this attire was the full ceremonial dress of a physician. Approaching the bedside with gentle precision, the old man spoke in a mild, hushed tone, "How fares thee, my son?"

Gerard responded with gratitude, noting that while his wound caused little pain, his throat felt parched and his head weighed down by a heavy fog.

"A wound!" the physician exclaimed, feigning surprise. "They failed to inform me of that. Allow me to inspect it. Indeed, a clean, sharp bite. I wager the mastiff possessed sound teeth capable of such extraction;" and the doctor's sympathy seemingly evaporated, transferring entirely to the canine predator he had conjured—a jackal of his own making.

"This requires immediate cauterization," he declared, his voice sharpening. "Otherwise, you shall find yourself retreating from water and performing somersaults beneath our very hands. This is the season of rabid curs, and one has already claimed your service; yet, we shall thwart him. Boy, hasten to heat the iron."

"But, sir," interjected Gerard, "it was no dog, but a bear."

"A bear!" the senior corrected severely, his brow furrowing. "Consider your words carefully; it is ill jesting with the man of art who brings his gray hairs and lifelong studies to heal you. A bear, quotha! Had you dissected as many bears as I, or even a tenth thereof, and examined their dentition to maintain your own skill, you would know that no bear's jaw could inflict such a trivial wound. I assure you, it was a dog—and since you have forced my hand, I deny it was even a large one, but merely one of those furious, small curs that plague us, running deviously to bite the legs of men and lay them low. Were it not for me and my learned brethren, wielding knife and cautery, the mischief would be total."

"Alas, sir!" Gerard protested. "When did I claim it was a bear's jaw? I said, 'A bear'; it was his paw, now."

"And why did you not inform me immediately?"

"Because you insisted on telling me otherwise."

"Never conceal anything from your leech, young man," the senior continued, displaying the paradox of being an eloquent speaker yet a notoriously poor listener. "Well, this is a grievous affair. The hardy excrescences of beasts—to wit, the claws of tigers, panthers, badgers, cats, and bears, alongside the horns of deer and the nails of humans, particularly children—are imbued with the direst poisons. You would have fared better bitten by a cur, however you argue, than gored by a bull or stag, or scratched by a bear. Nevertheless, you shall receive a potent biting cataplasm for your leg. Meanwhile, keep the limb cool: extend your tongue!—Good!—You have a fever. Let me feel your pulse!—Indeed, a fever. I prescribe flebotomy, and without delay."

"Flebotomy! That is bloodletting," Gerard muttered. "Humph! Very well, if it guarantees a cure, for I refuse to remain idle here." The doctor assured him that the procedure was infallible, especially in his condition.

"Hans, retrieve the necessary implements, and I shall entertain the patient with reasoned discourse."

The man of art then embarked upon a lecture for Gerard, explaining that in disease, the blood becomes heated, disturbed, and increasingly toxic. By removing a portion of this corrupted fluid, nature is compelled to generate a purer substance to replace it. Thus, bleeding served as both a coolant and a purifier, acting as a universal remedy for all ailments, for all diseases were fundamentally febrile, regardless of what empirical practitioners might claim.

"Do not suppose," he said warmly, "that mere bleeding suffices. Any paltry barber can incise a vein (though not all possess the skill to seal it again). True art lies in discerning which vein to evacuate for which ailment. Recently, a man plagued by earache was brought to me. I bled him in the right thigh, and his earache vanished instantly. Regrettably, he passed away shortly thereafter. Another arrived suffering from toothache; I bled him behind the ear, relieving him in a trice. Fate, however, claimed him as well. I bled our bailiff between the thumb and forefinger for rheumatism. Soon, he returned complaining of headaches and ringing in the ears, holding his hand over the basin. I smiled at his folly, bled him in the left ankle against his will, and rendered his head as light as a nut."

Branching from the immediate crisis with the enthusiasm of a zealot, the revered teacher expanded further:

"Know, young man, that two schools of art currently vie for supremacy across Europe. The Arabian school, whose ancient oracles include Avicenna, Rhazes, and Albucazis, revived by Chauliac and Lanfranc; and the Greek school, whose modern champions are Bessarion, Platinus, and Marsilius Ficinus, though whose primordial doctors were medicine's very prophets: Phoebus, Chiron, Aesculapius, and his sons Podalinus and Machaon, along with Pythagoras, Democritus, and Praxagoras, who discovered the arteries, and Dioctes, 'who first gave spirit to urine.' All these taught orally. Then came Hippocrates, the eighteenth descendant of Aesculapius, from whom we possess manuscripts; to him we owe 'the vital principle.' He also invented the bandage and tapped for water on the chest; above all, he dissected—yet only quadrupeds, for the brutal prejudices of the pagan multitude withheld the human body from the knife of science. Him followed Aristotle, who identified the aorta, the largest blood vessel in the human frame."

"Certainly, sir," Gerard objected humbly, "the Almighty granted us all that resides within our bodies, not Aristotle, nor any Grecian scholar."

"Child! Of course He provided the thing itself; but Aristotle bestowed the name. Yet, young men persist in chatter. The next great luminary was Galen, who studied at Alexandria, then the heart of science. Justly dissatisfied with quadrupeds, he dissected apes, viewing them as closer kin to man, and bled like a Trojan. Then came Theophilus, who revealed the nerves, the lacteal vessels, and the pia mater."

This troubled Gerard. "I cannot lie still while hearing mortals claim they bestowed the very parts which Adam, our father, received from Him, who fashioned him from clay, and us, his sons."

"Has such perversity ever been witnessed?" the doctor snapped, his color rising. "Who is the true donor of a thing to man? Is it he who plants it secretly in the dark recesses of the human body, or the learned sage who unveils it to his intelligence, thereby enriching his mind with knowledge? Comprehension is your only true possession. Have I answered?"

"I am silenced, sir."

"And that is preferable; for garrulous patients are difficult to cure, especially during fever. I assert, then, that Eristratus gifted us the cerebral nerves and milk vessels; indeed, he invented lithotomy, whatever detractors may claim. Then came another whose name escapes me; you somewhat disturb me with your petty objections. Next came Ammonius, the author of lithotrity, and here arrives Hans with the basin—to curb your verbosity. Blow your charcoal, boy, and present the basin; excellent. Arabs, quotha! What are they but a sect of yesterday who, circa the year 1000, stumbled upon the writings of those very Greeks, reading them askew due to lacking their own concurrent light? For their demi-god and camel-driver, Mahound, an impostor in both science and religion, strictly forbade them anatomy
