Tito stepped down into the heaving, sun-warmed crowd of the piazza, and even though he’d brushed off Nello’s teasing question about his chance acquaintance earlier, he found himself nursing a faint, passing wish as he rounded the square toward the Corso degli Adimari. He hoped he might spot the pair of bright blue eyes that had looked up at him from beneath the simple square of white linen that served peasant girls as their holiday hood on feast days. He knew full well the face belonged to Tessa, but he’d never had any reason to tell Nello that. What business of the barber’s was it, anyway? Tito had a natural, uncalculating gift for reticence—an instinct that made him hoard tiny, trivial bits of unshared experience the way others might hide a pretty pebble they’d found on a walk, even when there was no actual secret to guard.

That soft half-wish for a glimpse of Tessa vanished almost at once, replaced by the niggling, unplaceable memory of the gaunt friar who had stared at him so intently in the crowd. Why would that sickly, fasting fanatic single him out of all the hundreds of people in the square? Where across all his years of wandering could he have seen that face before? Foolishness, Tito told himself, swatting the thought away like a cobweb tickling his cheek. Vague half-memories were just annoyances, and he had far pleasanter things to occupy his mind.

By the time he turned off the Corso degli Adimari down a narrow side street, he was only impatient that the sun had climbed so high, that the festa procession had kept him far longer than he’d meant to stay from the rooms in the Via de’ Bardi, where he knew he was anxiously expected. He could picture the scene before he even stepped through the door: the quiet, diffused joy that would light up blind Bardo’s face like glow seeping through alabaster, the faint pink flush that would rise on Romola’s cheeks and throat—no lessening of her quiet, regal dignity, only the soft, exquisite spark of womanly sensitivity, sharpened by that strange, paradoxical mix of gravity and boyish unguarded frankness that made her smile feel like a gift. They were the dearest of comrades when they sat around Bardo’s chair, she turning to him constantly for the Greek and classical references her father could no longer chase down, but Tito always felt a quiet, unnameable awe in her presence, a reverence he had no words for, the kind of worship old pagans might have offered a great nature goddess: not all-knowing, but rooted in a depth of life and primordial goodness far older and more profound than any book learning. They had never been alone together, and he could not even imagine the usual silly, stilted scenes of courtship between them. He only dared to half-dream the impossible: that one day Romola would be the first to tell him she loved him.

He remembered once in Greece, leaning over a sun-warmed stone wall, a little black-eyed peasant girl who had set down her water pot had crept shyly closer, and held up her round olive cheek for him to kiss, no guile, no pretense. Tito was used to affection that came unbidden like that. But Romola’s love would never come that way. Would it ever come at all? She was the highest, ripest apple on the topmost bough, and he had set his whole heart on reaching it. He was young, fresh, impressible, not fiery or passionate, but drawn to her as naturally as white irises reflected in a sunlit clear stream. He had no coxcomb’s false pride; he knew full well she was far above him, the way any grown man might feel small standing before a large-eyed, earnest child.

Even so, the run of extraordinary good luck he’d had in Florence would have been enough to turn a less steady man’s head. Bartolommeo Scala, the city’s sharp, powerful secretary, had taken a marked liking to him after their first auspicious meeting, and a circle of learned patrons had agreed to found a dedicated Greek chair for him, just as Lorenzo de’ Medici had once installed the great refugee scholar Demetrio Calcondila in the post, to give the arrogant, over-confident young humanist Politian a much-needed dose of humility and proper competition. It was all perfectly respectable, no slight to Politian, just a testament to Florence’s zeal for true learning for its youth. Tito was sailing under the fairest wind imaginable. Everyone who met him liked his easy, unpretentious bearing; no one resented his rapid rise. He was handsome, he could play the lute and troll a gay drinking song, make even the driest Greek epics feel like a holiday pastime for the sons of Florence’s richest noble houses. He would soon be welcome in the very highest circles, the ones where the family silver stacked higher than the pattern on the centre of your dinner plates, where the Signory had not yet forbidden the wearing of richest imported brocade.

And that was not all his good fortune. He had sold every last jewel he had carried with him from the Aegean wreck, save for the single plain ring he would never part with, and he now held five hundred full gold florins in his possession.

That moment when the sum was handed over to him was the first serious crisis of his easy, good-natured life. A thought he had been trying to ignore, chasing him for weeks like a shadow, finally rushed at him and seized him. He had to stop, and choose: obey that thought, or turn away, and take an irrevocable step he could never undo.

He was in his small rented lodging above Nello’s shop, when old Bernardo Cennini the printer and goldsmith handed him the last quota of the payment, on behalf of Bernardo Rucellai who had bought his two most valuable gems.
“Ecco, giovane mio!” the old man said, nodding, “You’ve got a tidy little fortune here. Take my advice, let me place these florins somewhere safe, where they can earn interest and multiply, instead of slipping through your fingers on banquets and the silly follies every Florentine youth chases. Too many scholars, the likes of our Pietro Crinito who thinks his learning has to smell of perfumes and be stitched with brocade, squander every coin with one hand till they’re forced to beg with the other. I’ve brought you the money, and you get to choose wisely or foolishly. We Florentines don’t count a man part of any guild till he’s proven his skill and been matriculated—and no man matriculates in the art of living till he’s been well tempted. If you decide to put your florins out at interest, send word to me tomorrow. A scholar may well marry, and ought to have something set aside for the morgengabe, the gift he gives his bride the morning after their wedding. Addio.”

When Cennini closed the door behind him, Tito’s smile died right off his face. He stared down at the pile of glowing florins on the table, his thumbs tucked into his belt, completely still, his entire consciousness fixed on the rising image in his mind.

A man’s ransom. He had heard that saying once, that five hundred florins was more than enough to buy a man’s freedom from Turkish slavery. What if that man—Baldassarre Calvo, the father who had pulled a seven-year-old Tito out of the Naples gutters, saved him from blows and beggary, raised him tenderly, taught him everything he knew—was at this very moment toiling under the hot sun on some distant Ottoman coast, hewing wood and drawing water, getting struck and buffeted for being old and slow and not quick enough? What if he was telling himself, Tito will find me. He only had to take our manuscripts and jewels to Venice, sell them, and he will never rest till he finds me.

If he knew for certain that was true, could he look at the five hundred florins on the table and say, I will stay here in Florence, where the soft airs of promised love and distinction blow around me. I will not risk everything for his sake. No, he could not—if it were certain. But nothing was less certain. The companion galley that had escaped the Turkish raiders had reported the pirate attack on their ship bound for Delos, said there had been bloodshed, that a man had been seen falling overboard. But no one knew who survived, no one knew what had become of any of the passengers amid a thousand possible misfortunes. Tito himself had barely escaped drowning in the wreck that had washed him up on the Italian coast; he knew better than most how easily all plans could be snuffed out by random disaster.

If he left Florence now, went to Venice, secured letters of passage from the authorities, set sail for the Archipelago, what would most likely happen? He would be captured himself, spend every last florin on permits and bribes and fruitless searches, and be a destitute wanderer again—with no more jewels left to sell to buy his own freedom. He was not a boy anymore, he was in the prime of his youth, after years of scraping by as a beggar-scholar. Why should he throw away all the bright promise of love and respectability he had built here, for a fool’s chase that might lead nowhere? The promise would never come back again.

“And yet,” he murmured to himself, “if I was certain Baldassarre was alive, and that I could free him, no matter what the exertion or the peril, I would go now—now that I have the money. I would go to Bardo and Scala this very hour, and tell them the whole truth.”

He did not say the rest out loud, not even to himself: that if either of those men knew the whole truth, there would be no choice for him at all. Baldassarre was the rightful owner of every jewel he had sold, and Tito had always spoken of him only as “lost,” equivocating, never admitting the full possibility that he was enslaved. Every Greek of standing he had ever known would give up everything, sail across half the world, beg kings and popes for help to free a relative from Turkish slavery. Public opinion would not see his choice as anything less than cowardly cruelty.

This was the first time he had ever truly talked to himself, confronted his own actions clearly, instead of drifting along after every impulse of the moment. The impulse to hide half the truth had sprung up on its own, and he had never stopped to examine it till now. What was the point of telling the whole story? He had thought more than once, since he left Nauplia, that it was a relief to be free of Baldassarre. He had always been exacting as he aged, prying constantly into Tito’s thoughts to make sure they measured up to his own wild, exaggerated expectations. A heavy-browed, bald old man of sixty, whose whole life of ideas had narrowed down to the same repeated, monotonous obsessions—how could anyone find that charming? The world does not bow down to faded, worn old men, no matter how noble their hearts, unless they have the philosopher’s stone to make them wealthy and powerful. Only the love rooted in shared memories, the slow, sweet balm of fidelity and forbearance, can make a home for them.

And Tito had those memories, too. Far back in his earliest childhood, Baldassarre had pulled him out of the mud, given him warm bread, held him on his knee, been his whole world. Everyone had praised Tito as the perfect reward for that lonely man’s devotion: bright, gentle, quick to learn, no rough edges at all. No one had ever thought that gratitude would fail him, when the great test came.

But it was not certain Baldassarre was enslaved. It was not certain he was even alive.

“Do I not owe something to myself?” Tito shifted his shoulders, the first movement he had made in minutes, staring at the florins. “Before I throw everything away, and run all the risks I have only just escaped, I need at least a reasonable hope. Am I supposed to spend my whole life wandering the Aegean chasing a ghost? I believe he is dead. Cennini was right about my florins. I will give them to him tomorrow to invest.”

When he carried that resolution out the next morning, he had made his first permanent choice in the game of his life. From that moment onward, he could not help but wish it were true that Baldassarre was dead, that there was no man alive who could expose his secret, no one to make him ashamed of what he had chosen. Under every guilty secret a whole brood of guilty wishes is hidden, feeding on the darkness, spreading their unwholesome infection. The damage of a wrong choice lies less in the deed itself than in how it twists your desires, enlists your whole self-interest on the side of falsity. Just as public confession purifies the soul by sweeping away all hope in lies, letting you stand simple and noble again.

All those small, scattered rills of selfishness that Tito had let trickle through his life till now had merged into a deep, permanent channel that no one could dam up anymore. He had never before definitely decided to abandon the search for Baldassarre. Now he had given himself a clear excuse to never go. He had chosen a secret he would be ashamed to admit to anyone else, ashamed of in a heartbeat if Baldassarre ever stood alive before him. The quiet inner shame, the echo of all humanity’s shared moral code, burned hot in his cheeks even as he stubbornly repeated to himself that his old benefactor was dead, that any search for him was utterly hopeless.
