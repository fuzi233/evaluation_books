Lona and I, walking beneath the towering canopy of the forest, heard a sudden cry from above. Moments later, the Little Ones began to tumble down through the leaves, their faces alight with wonder. They had climbed the highest tree in the grove and seen something extraordinary far across the plain—a strange shape on the side of a solitary mountain. The mountain, they said, rose so high it pierced the sky, which seemed to gather thickly around its peak as if trying to hold it back, until it shattered the top clean off.

“It might be a city,” one whispered, “but not like Bulika.”

I climbed to see for myself. And there it was—vast and impossible, rising into blue clouds where sky and stone blurred into one. Mountain and palace, cliff and dwelling, cloud and shadow twisted together in a dreamlike confusion of light and form. I descended, the children following close behind, and we quickened our pace, drawn forward by an unseen force.

They grew more joyful with every step, darting ahead, never glancing back. The river beside us became more radiant with each mile—its waters clearer, brighter, more alive than any I had ever known. This was real water, pure and true; nothing in this world could merely *resemble* it.

As we neared the plain, the city emerged again, suspended in the heavens. But now, above it, a tower—or perhaps a rock—rose even higher, crowned by swirling clouds: gray, dark gray, purple. They writhed in chaotic motion, churning up vaporous foam, while within them, spots spun like whirlpools. Then came a flash—blinding, dazzling—so bright it seemed to dance across the children’s forms. Darkness followed instantly, yet through it, their voices rose, hushed with awe.

“Did you see?”
“I saw.”
“What did you see?”
“The most beautiful man!”

“I heard him speak!”
“I didn’t—what did he say?”

Then, small and sweet, Luva’s voice chimed in:
“He said, ‘Ou’s all mine’s, ‘ickle ones: come along!’”

I had only seen the lightning, heard no words. But Lona—she saw and heard with the children. A second flash struck, and though my ears remained silent, my eyes were opened. The light quivered, composed not of mere fire, but of countless angelic faces—glowing, visible, then vanishing like breath upon glass.

A third flash came—this time, shaped like humanity.
“I see my mother!” I cried.
“I see lots o’ mothers!” Luva cried back.

Then once more—the sky exploded with life. Horses, elephants, lions, dogs—beasts beyond imagining! Birds, too—great wings that shimmered with every hue of sunset and rainbow, tiny feathers glittering like earth’s hoarded gems. Silver cranes, crimson flamingoes, opal pigeons, peacocks blazing in gold and green and blue, hummingbirds like living jewels—then butterflies with wings like stained glass, serpentine creatures that flowed like liquid shadow—all revealed in one divine instant.

“I see that serpents grow birds here, just as caterpillars used to become butterflies,” Lona remarked.

“I saw my white pony,” I murmured. “The one that died when I was young. I needn’t have grieved so much—I should’ve waited.”

No thunder followed. Only silence—and then rain. Sweet, cool rain fell, caressing the air, filling our lungs with freshness. We breathed deeply, stepped forward stronger. Each drop sparkled with the colors of awakened stones, and over the city arched a colossal rainbow.

The blue clouds thickened. Rain poured in torrents. The children danced through it, laughing, racing ahead—so fast we could barely keep sight of them. The river flowed silently, radiant, full to its banks. But instead of stone or sand, it ran over grass—soft, green, dotted with primroses, daisies, crocuses, narcissi, pimpernels, anemones—stars of color floating beneath the clear, glowing current. The water remained pure, untouched by the storm, shining with a pale berylline glow from its delicate, luminous bed.

Closer to the mountain, we saw the river’s source: it burst from the very summit, rushing down in full force through the heart of the city. It cascaded down a grand stairway—wide, deep steps carved from porphyry and serpentine—descending from the peak to the city gate. At the base, shallower steps led up both sides, flanking the flowing torrent.

Without pause, the Little Ones sprinted up the stair, straight to the open gate. There, seated on the landing, sat a woman-angel—dark-skinned, serene, her brow resting in quiet contemplation. The children rushed to her, showering her with hugs and kisses. Before she could react, they had already swept past her, into the city, still climbing alongside the descending river.

A great angel descended, surrounded by shining attendants, to greet them. But the children, dancing and laughing, slipped past him, unbothered. Soon, a group of woman-angels appeared, descending in graceful spirals, and in an instant, the children were embraced—fettered not by chains, but by radiant arms. They were carried away, vanished into the light.

“I hear of a horde of black bats on the frontiers,” the mighty angel said, turning toward us now, his voice warm and deep. “These little ones will make short work of them. Soldiers indeed—to take heaven itself by storm!”

He nodded at the horses and elephants trailing behind us. “Take those animals to the royal stables. Tend them well, then release them into the king’s forest.”

Then, bowing low with a smile that lit the air, he said, “Welcome home.”

And with that, he turned and led us upward. His armor flashed like lightning in fragments—each scale a shard of brilliance. I stood stunned, unable to express what filled me—this reception, this recognition, this sense that everything I had longed for, everything I had lost, was now returning.

We paused at the gate, where the radiant river roared forth. I could not name the stones that formed its path, but among them I saw the origins of every gem I’d loved on Earth—yet more magnificent, for these were *living*. Not just beauty, but *being*—the idea and the maker together, the thought and the creator present. Nothing here was dead. Nothing was empty. Everything was alive, breathing, *real*.

We walked through the city, passing out the other side. No wall guarded the upper edge—only a vast pile of broken rocks, sloping upward like the moraine of an eternal glacier. Between the gaps, the river surged out in waves. On the highest ledge, I glimpsed faintly what looked like three or four massive steps, vanishing into a cloud as white as snow. Above them, in my mind’s eye alone, I saw a throne—ancient, grand, the seat of the Ancient of Days.

From between those steps, from under and over them, flowed endlessly the River of Life—newborn, constant, eternal.

The great angel could go no farther. “You must climb these rocks alone.”

My heart pounded with hope. I gripped Lona’s hand tightly. Together, we began the ascent. Soon, we let go—hands needed for balance, feet for grip—climbing the immense stones, breath ragged, spirits soaring.

At last, we reached the cloud. It hung like the hem of a celestial robe. We stepped through its fringe, into its deep folds.

A hand—warm, strong—grasped mine. It pulled me toward a small door, golden in its lock. The door opened. The hand released me, gently pushing me through.

I turned—just in time to see a wooden board closing behind me, like the cover of a book.

I stood alone.

In my library.
