The evening air in the Cathedral Close carried the damp weight of a city that refused to dry. Mr. Sapsea walked with his hands clasped behind his back, a posture designed to project the gravity of a man who believed he invented the concept of civic order. He viewed the churchyard not as a place of rest, but as a portfolio of his own benevolence. He imagined Mrs. Sapsea, his wife, as a tenant he had generously housed, a monument to his largesse standing silently in the gloom. Every stray face peering through the iron railings seemed to him a tribute, a blush of gratitude for the inscription he had commissioned. If a stranger hurried past, Sapsea interpreted the haste as a moral retreat, a blush of shame for having disturbed the sanctity of his domain.

His status had swollen recently; he was now Mayor of Cloisterham. Without figures like him, Sapsea reasoned, the entire architecture of society would collapse into a heap of meaningless rubble. He possessed the confidence of a man who believes he has discovered the laws of physics. Mayors, he knew, were knights of the tongue, launching verbal artillery into the dense fortifications of grammar. Sapsea intended to fire his own broadside. Rise, Sir Thomas Sapsea! Such was the salt of the earth, preserved in the brine of his own ego.

Earlier that day, his relationship with Mr. Jasper had deepened, forged in the fires of port, epigraphs, backgammon, and beef. Jasper had performed at the gatehouse, singing with a voice that tickled the air itself, creating a sonic landscape vast enough to swallow the silence. Sapsea admired this quality in the younger man: a readiness to absorb the wisdom of elders, a solidity at the core. That evening, Jasper had sung not the frivolous tunes favored by foreign powers, but a hymn of imperial dominance, urging the reduction of all other lands to dust, celebrating the unique virtue of their small, heart-of-oak nation. It was a performance of grandeur, yet delivered with the casual ease of a man discussing the weather.

Now, walking slowly along the wet pavement, Sapsea rounded a corner expecting to encounter a blushing stranger. Instead, he found the Dean, engaged in conversation with the Verger and Mr. Jasper. Sapsea bowed, and in that moment, he felt a surge of ecclesiastical authority that rivaled any Archbishop.

"You are evidently planning a book about us, Mr. Jasper," the Dean said, his voice carrying the weight of antiquity. "We are ancient, and we ought to make a good book. Our wealth lies in our years rather than our possessions, but perhaps you will include that in your narrative, highlighting our grievances."

Mr. Tope, ever the audience, chuckled appreciatively.

"I have no intention of becoming an author or an archaeologist, sir," Jasper replied, his tone light. "It is merely a whim. And even for this whim, Mr. Sapsea bears more responsibility than I do."

"How so, Mr. Mayor?" the Dean asked, nodding with friendly recognition.

"I am unaware," Sapsea said, scanning the surroundings for context, "what Your Reverence refers to." He then turned his attention minutely to the fabric of his own coat.

"Durdles," Mr. Tope suggested.

"Ay!" the Dean echoed. "Durdles, Durdles!"

"The truth is," Jasper explained, "my curiosity regarding the man was sparked by Mr. Sapsea. His ability to extract the reclusive and the peculiar from the shadows first drew my attention to him. You would not be surprised if you had witnessed Mr. Sapsea handling him in his own parlour."

"Oh!" cried Sapsea, catching the ball metaphorically tossed to him with effortless pomp. "Yes, yes. Your Reverence refers to that? Indeed. I introduced Durdles to Mr. Jasper. I regard Durdles as a Character."

"A character, Mr. Sapsea, whom you can turn inside out with a few skilled strokes," Jasper observed.

"Nay, not quite," the lumbering auctioneer corrected. "Perhaps I possess a slight influence over him, a glimpse into his nature. Your Reverence must remember that I have seen the world." Sapsea stepped back slightly to inspect his buttons, ensuring his armor was flawless.

"Well!" the Dean said, glancing around for his copyist. "I trust, Mr. Mayor, you will utilize your study of Durdles to persuade him not to break our Choir-Master's neck. We cannot afford it; his head and voice are too precious."

Mr. Tope erupted into respectful convulsions of laughter, settling into a murmuring agreement that breaking a neck for such a compliment would be an honor.

"I will take responsibility for Mr. Jasper's neck," Sapsea declared loftily. "I will instruct Durdles to be careful. He will heed my words. How is it currently endangered?" he asked, surveying the scene with magnificent patronage.

"Only because I proposed a moonlight expedition with Durdles among the tombs, vaults, and ruins," Jasper replied. "You suggested, when we first met, that as a lover of the picturesque, it might be worthwhile?"

"I remember!" the auctioneer exclaimed, genuinely believing his own recollection.

"Acting on your advice," Jasper continued, "I have taken several day excursions with this extraordinary old fellow. Tonight, we intend to conduct a moonlit exploration of the corners and holes."

"And here he is," the Dean announced.

Durdles emerged, slouching toward them with a dinner bundle in hand. Seeing the Dean, he removed his hat, tucked it under his arm, and prepared to slouch away. Sapsea intercepted him.

"Take care of my friend," Sapsea instructed.

"What friend of yours is dead?" Durdles asked, his voice thick with suspicion. "No orders have been issued for any deceased friend."

"I mean my living friend there."

"Oh! Him?" Durdles said. "He can look after himself, can Mister Jarsper."

"But you must take care of him too," Sapsea insisted.

Durdles surveyed the mayor from head to toe, his expression sullen despite the implied command.

"With respect to Your Reverence the Dean, if you mind your own business, Mr. Sapsea, Durdles will mind his own."

"You are out of temper," Sapsea said, winking at the assembly to demonstrate his mastery of the situation. "My friend concerns me, and Mr. Jasper is my friend. And you are my friend."

"Don't get into the habit of boasting," Durdles retorted, offering a grave warning. "It will grow on you."

"You are out of temper," Sapsea repeated, flushing slightly before sinking back into the crowd.

"I admit it," Durdles conceded. "I dislike liberties."

Sapsea winked a third time, signaling to the company that he had successfully managed the subordinate, and strode away from the dispute.

Durdles then bid the Dean good evening. Adding, as he replaced his hat, "You'll find me at home, Mister Jarsper, as agreed, when you require me; I'm heading home to clean myself," he slouched out of sight. This declaration of cleaning oneself was one of the man's inexplicable compromises with reality; he, his hat, his boots, and his clothes showed no sign of cleanliness, remaining uniformly coated in dust and grit.

As the lamplighter dotted the quiet Close with moving specks of light, climbing his precarious ladder beneath the sacred shadow of its inconvenience—a fixture generations had grown accustomed to, though Cloisterham would recoil at the thought of removing—it—the Dean retired to dinner, Mr. Tope to tea, and Mr. Jasper to his piano. Bathed only in the firelight, he chanted choir music in a low, beautiful voice for hours, continuing until darkness deepened and the moon began to rise.

Then, he closed the piano lid softly. Changing his coat for a pea-jacket, he secured a bottle in a large pocket, donned a low-crowned, flap-brimmed hat, and slipped quietly outside. Why did he move so softly tonight? No external reason presented itself. Was there a darker, sympathetic motive crouching within him?

He repaired to Durdles's unfinished dwelling, a hole in the city wall. Seeing a light within, he navigated carefully among the gravestones, monuments, and stony debris of the yard, already illuminated by the rising moon. Two journeyman masons had left their great saws embedded in blocks of stone nearby. Two skeletal figures from the Dance of Death might have grinned in the shadows of their sentry boxes, poised to slice away at the stones of the next two victims destined to die in Cloisterham. Presumably, the living masons thought little of this now, being alive and perhaps merry. It was difficult to guess which of the two was the more curious.

"Ho! Durdles!"

The light shifted, and he appeared at the door. He seemed to have been "cleaning himself" with a bottle, jug, and tumbler, for no other cleansing implements were visible in the bare brick room with its exposed rafters and unplastered ceiling.

"Are you ready?"
