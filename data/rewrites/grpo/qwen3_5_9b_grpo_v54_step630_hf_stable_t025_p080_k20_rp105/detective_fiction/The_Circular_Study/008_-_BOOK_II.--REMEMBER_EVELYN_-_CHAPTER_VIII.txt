The following morning, the correspondence between Mr. Gryce and Miss Butterworth arrived in perfect synchronicity. Her note was brief and factual: *You were quite correct. So far as appears, I was the only person to lean over Mr. Adams's study table after his unfortunate death. I have had to clip the ends of my boa.* His reply was equally terse: *My compliments, madam! Mr. Adams's jaws have been forced apart. A small piece of paper was found clinched between his teeth. This paper has been recovered, and will be read at the inquest. Perhaps a few favored persons may be granted the opportunity of reading it before then, notably yourself.*

While the news of the paper caused a stir, the revelation of the forced jaw sent a shockwave through the household. Miss Butterworth's composure remained impeccable, yet the secrecy surrounding her reaction fueled endless speculation among the servants. Meanwhile, at Police Headquarters, the detectives were inundated with the usual flood of idle rumors. The second day proved more fruitful than the first, yielding a cacophony of sightings from witnesses of all walks of life. An elderly woman claimed to have seen a lady of genteel bearing admit an elderly gentleman in a great-coat; a fashionable young woman recounted a collision with a pale youth and a ghostly companion wearing a hat adorned with a gray feather. Yet, when pressed for details, these accounts dissolved into vague generalities, offering no concrete leads to unravel the formidable mystery.

On the third day, Mr. Adams was laid to rest. With no relatives or intimate friends to attend, his landlord conducted the rites while his banker served as the chief mourner. Detectives mingled with the crowd outside the house and later at the cemetery, but their surveillance yielded nothing. Realizing the case had to be solved with the material already at hand, Mr. Gryce excused himself from headquarters. He walked alone to the dark, seemingly sealed house where the tragedy had unfolded.

Using a key, he entered and illuminated the interior. He inspected the study, noting that the cross had been replaced on the wall, the birdcage rehung, and the room restored to its usual order, save for the gaping holes in the doorframe where the broken casings lay in disrepair. The steel plate had been shoved back into its recess, but the glimpse of its blue surface through the hole in the antechamber wall remained an unsettling feature. With the practical efficiency of a housewife, Mr. Gryce pulled two rugs from the parlor floor and draped them over the openings to obscure the view. Consulting his watch, he noted it was nearly nine o'clock. He retreated to the parlor window, and shortly, a carriage stopped. A lady alighted, and Mr. Gryce hastened to the door to greet her. It was Miss Butterworth.

"Madam, your punctuality is equal to my own," he said. "Have you ordered your coachman to drive away?"

"Only as far as the corner," she replied, following him down the hall. "There he will await the call of your whistle."

"Nothing could be better. Are you afraid to remain alone while I watch from the window for the others?"

"If I were subject to fear in a matter of this kind, I should not be here at all. Besides, the house is cheerfully lit. I see you have chosen a crimson light for the study."

"Because a crimson light was burning when Mr. Adams died."

"Remember Evelyn!" a voice called out.

"Oh, you have brought back the bird!" Miss Butterworth exclaimed. "That is not the cry with which it greeted me before. It was 'Eva! Lovely Eva!' Do you suppose Eva and Evelyn are the same?"

"Madam, we have so many riddles before us that we will let this one go for the present. I expect Mr. Adams's valet here in a moment."

"Sir, you relieve me of an immense weight. I was afraid the privilege of witnessing your proposed test was not to be accorded me."

"Miss Butterworth, you have earned a seat at this experiment. Bartow has been given a key and will enter as of old, free to do as he wills. We shall simply watch his movements."

"In this room, sir? I do not think I shall like that. I had rather not meet this madman face to face."

"You will not be called upon to do so. We do not wish him startled by a watchful eye. Irresponsible as he is, he must be allowed to move without distraction. I propose to place you where the chances of being seen are least—in this parlor, which we believe was seldom opened during Mr. Adams's lifetime."

"You must put out the gas, then, or the unaccustomed light will attract his attention."

"I will not only extinguish the gas but draw the portières close, leaving only a small aperture for our eyes. A common expedient, but serviceable."

A sharp intake of breath from Miss Butterworth, as she was drawn into the darkness behind the curtain alongside the detective, conveyed her complex feelings. Driven solely by a sense of duty, she likely blushed at her concealed position, had the opening of the front door not signaled the arrival of the deaf-and-dumb valet.

A faint "Hush!" from Mr. Gryce warned her. She strained to listen, watching for the man's appearance in the antechamber. He arrived sooner than expected, dropping his hat and advancing toward the portière he had likely lifted dozens of times a day. But he barely touched it. Something unseen prevented entry. Whether it was the memory of the previous scene or the oddly placed rugs, he simply turned away with a meek, mechanical expression, picked up his hat, and proceeded softly upstairs.

"I will follow him," Mr. Gryce whispered. "Don't be afraid, ma'am. This whistle will summon help instantly."

"I am not afraid. I would be ashamed—"

But the sentence was cut short as Mr. Gryce vanished into the hall. He returned quickly, reporting that the experiment was likely a failure since the old man had retired to his room. He led Miss Butterworth into the study and, with an idle touch, pressed a button on the table, bathing the room in a new violet glow. Miss Butterworth admired the effect on the rich furnishings until the faint sound of descending footsteps interrupted them.

They scrambled back behind the parlor curtains just as Bartow hurried in, his busy air unchecked. Crossing to the study, he paused on the threshold, conscious of something amiss, then entered. Without glancing at the rugs covering the spot where his master had fallen, he began his nightly routine. He fetched a bottle of wine and a glass, wiping the glass scrupulously. He laid out the dressing gown and slippers before retreating to the bedroom.

From their vantage point, the watchers saw him stoop over the comb Mr. Gryce had left on the floor. The object seemed to surprise him; he picked it up, shook his head, and returned it to the dresser. Then he turned down the bed.

"Poor fool!" Miss Butterworth murmured. "He has forgotten everything but his old routine. We shall get nothing from this man."

She stopped abruptly. They both froze. Bartow stood in the middle of the study, staring intently at his master's empty chair. Then he turned and gazed earnestly at the rug where the body had lain. Awakening to the reality of the event, he fell into a violent mimicry of the actions he had witnessed, acting out the struggle before slipping quietly out and ascending the stairs.

Mr. Gryce and Miss Butterworth emerged into the light, exchanging looks of discouragement. Suddenly, Miss Butterworth's face lit with enthusiasm.

"Turn on another color, and let us see what happens. I have an idea it will fetch the old man down again."

Mr. Gryce raised his brows. "Do you think he can see through the floor?"

He pressed a button, and the room flooded with a rich blue light. Nothing happened. Miss Butterworth looked disturbed.

"I have confidence in your theories," Mr. Gryce began, "but when they imply this man sees through blank walls and obeys signals meaningless to anyone below..."

"Hark!" she cried, raising a finger triumphantly. The old man's footsteps descended.

This time, Bartow approached with considerable feebleness, passing slowly into the study. He reached out his hands as if to lift something from the table, found nothing, glanced up at the bookshelves in astonishment, shook his head, and collapsed into a doze on the nearest chair.

Miss Butterworth drew a long breath. "Can you read the meaning of all that? I think I can. Don't you see he came expecting to find a pile of books on the table, perhaps to restore to the shelves?"

"But how can he know what light is burning here? There is no communication between this room and the one above."

Miss Butterworth's manner shifted to something almost naive. She smiled, apologetic yet firm. "When I went upstairs previously, I saw a little thing—a very small detail—which may explain why Bartow perceives these lights. I doubtless observed it too, though I do not know how it works."

"I shall be glad to hear about it, madam. I thought I had thoroughly searched those rooms—and the halls?"

"And the halls. Nothing escaped my eyes. But if you have a more patient vision than myself..."

"Or make it my business to look lower—on the floor?"

"On the floor?"

"The floor often reveals much: where a person steps most frequently. You must have noticed how marred the woodwork is at the edge of the carpeting on that little landing above."

"In the round of the staircase?"

"Yes."

Mr. Gryce did not answer immediately. Leaving the valet dozing and Miss Butterworth in the study, he ascended the stairs, pausing at the spot she indicated.

"You see, it has been much trodden here," she said gently. Startled by her presence, he gave a slight jump. "Now, why should anyone shuffle to and fro in this corner? The stair is wide, and there is no window."

Mr. Gryce's eye traveled along the wall to one of the dozen pictures hanging at intervals from bottom to top. Pulling one away from the wall, which hung decidedly askew, he revealed a round opening pouring a ray of blue light from the vault of the adjoining study.

"No window," he repeated. "No, but an opening into the study wall serving the same purpose. Miss Butterworth, your eye is to be trusted every time. I only wonder you did not pull this picture aside yourself."

"It was not hanging crooked then. Besides, I was in a hurry. I had just come from my encounter with this demented man. I noticed the marks on the landing and the worn carpet edges on my way up, but I was in no condition to observe them on my way down."

"I see."

Miss Butterworth ran her foot over the flooring. "Bartow was evidently in the habit of coming here constantly, probably to check if his master needed him. Ingenious of Mr. Adams to contrive signals for communication with this man! He certainly had great use for his deaf-and-dumb servant. So one mystery is solved!"

"And if I am not mistaken, we can answer another question by looking through this loophole. You are wondering, I believe, how Bartow thrust with his left hand instead of his right. If he saw the tragedy from this point, he saw it over the assailant's shoulder, not face-to-face. What follows? He would imitate the movements literally, turning in the same direction and striking with the same hand."

"Mr. Gryce, we are beginning to untangle the threads that looked so complicated. Ah, what is that? Why, it's that bird! His cage must be very nearly under this hole."

"A little to one side, madam, but near enough to give you a start. What did he cry then?"

"Oh, those sympathetic words about Eva! 'Poor Eva!'"

"Well, give a glance to Bartow. You can see him very well from here."

Miss Butterworth pressed her eye to the opening and gave a grunt—a very decided grunt. For her, a grunt signified surprise.

"He is shaking his fist; he is all alive with passion. He looks as if he would like to kill the bird."

"Perhaps that is why the creature was strung up so high. You may be sure Mr. Adams had some basis for his idiosyncrasies."

"I begin to think so. I don't know that I care to go back where that man is. He has a very murderous look."

"And a very feeble arm, Miss Butterworth. You are safe under my protection. My arm is not feeble."
