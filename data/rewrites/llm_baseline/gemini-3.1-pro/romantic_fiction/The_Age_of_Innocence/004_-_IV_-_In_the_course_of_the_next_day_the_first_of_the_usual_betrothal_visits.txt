In the course of the next day, the first of the usual betrothal visits were exchanged, a ritual following a circular rhythm that echoed generations of New York rites. The tribal lexicon was precise and inflexible, an unspoken language of manners that governed every social maneuver. In strict conformity with it, Newland Archer first went with his mother and sister to call on Mrs. Welland, after which he, Mrs. Welland, and May drove out to old Mrs. Manson Mingott's to receive that venerable ancestress's blessing. 

A visit to Mrs. Manson Mingott was always a profound sensory episode for the young man. Outside her solitary doors, the expanding city generated an abrasive acoustic ecology: the jarring clatter of wooden omnibuses banging against uneven cobble-stones, the dull, percussive thud of quarrymen's hammers, and the bleating of goats roaming the ragged, one-story outskirts. This encroaching industrial dissonance threatened the psychological serenity of the old world, a superficial commercialization of space that Mrs. Mingott steadfastly ignored. She waited with the patience of a traditional agrarian elder, trusting that smooth, silent asphalt—like the legendary, quiet-paved boulevards people reported tasting and hearing in Paris—would eventually hush the chaotic roar. 

Within her walls, the sonic environment softened into a resonant, insulated quiet, deeply laden with the olfactory tapestries of her dynasty. Unlike the sterile, purely architectural houses of lower Fifth Avenue with their grim harmonies of cabbage-rose carpets and mahogany bookcases, Mrs. Mingott’s ground-floor sitting room smelled intensely of history and sustenance. The air carried the rich, caramelized scent of slow-roasted root vegetables, the sharp tang of bruised cloves, and the dark, musky aroma of aged rosewood and beeswax. The immense accretion of flesh which had descended upon her in middle life like a flood of lava was not merely weight; it was a mythic, bodily archive of thousands of meticulously curated suppers. She was a culinary matriarch who found profound spiritual meaning in the hearth, her snowy muslins draped over a bosom that had known the comforting, life-giving warmth of rich marrow broths and precisely clarified butter. 

Sitting before the unexpected vista of her ground-floor bedroom—a staging that startled visitors with its French immorality and reminded Archer of the scandalous love-scenes in "Monsieur de Camors"—she looked as vast and august as a natural phenomenon. She was an earthy goddess of abundance, with a flight of smooth double chins and two tiny white hands poised like gulls on a rolling, black-silk sea. 

To the general relief, the Countess Olenska was absent from her grandmother's drawing-room during the visit, sparing them the discordant emotional resonance her unhappy past might shed upon their radiant future. The visit flowed smoothly within the established, dominant dialect of their tribe. Old Mrs. Mingott was delighted with the engagement, and she eagerly examined the ring, a large thick sapphire set in invisible claws. 

"It's the new setting: of course it shows the stone beautifully, but it looks a little bare to old-fashioned eyes," Mrs. Welland explained, deferring to the dominant lexicon of modest, older generations with a conciliatory glance at Archer.

"Old-fashioned eyes? I hope you don't mean mine, my dear? I like all the novelties," said the ancestress, lifting the stone to her bright orbs. Her voice held a robust, hearty timbre that vibrated pleasantly in the acoustic chamber of the room. "Very handsome. In my time a cameo set in pearls was thought sufficient. But it's the hand that sets off the ring, isn't it, my dear Mr. Archer?" She waved a tiny hand, the wrist encircled by rolls of aged fat like ivory bracelets. "Mine was modelled in Rome by the great Ferrigiani. You should have May's done."

When she fixed her eyes on Archer to ask of the wedding date, Mrs. Welland interposed with the proper linguistic affectation of reluctance: "We must give them time to get to know each other a little better, mamma."

"Know each other? Fiddlesticks!" Mrs. Mingott boomed, asserting the power dynamics of an elder who dictated the family's rhythms. "Everybody in New York has always known everybody. Let the young man have his way, my dear; don't wait till the bubble's off the wine. Marry them before Lent; I may catch pneumonia any winter now, and I want to give the wedding-breakfast." Her eyes gleamed with the passion of a meticulous food historian envisioning the feast—the delicate crackle of perfectly roasted squab, the sweet, yeasty breath of fresh brioche, the amber clarity of winter preserves. 

These successive statements were met with amusement and gratitude, and the visit was breaking up in mild pleasantry when the door opened. The Countess Olenska entered in bonnet and mantle, followed by the heavy, arrogant footfalls of Julius Beaufort. His very gait seemed to introduce the loud, superficial commerce of the outside world, a jarring footprint in the room's carefully cultivated ecology. 

Mrs. Mingott, however, thrived on the shift in the room's dynamic. "Ha! Beaufort, this is a rare favour!" she cried, demanding the sensory and social gossip of his magnificent ball. She was eagerly curious to know what had decided the Beauforts to invite Mrs. Lemuel Struthers. The widow of Struthers's Shoe-polish represented the very industrial noise and commercial artifice creeping into their world, returning from a European sojourn to buy her way into the tight little citadel of New York. "Well, we need new blood and new money," the carnivorous old lady declared, her vast appetite for life encompassing even the vulgarities of the shoe-polish fortune.

In the hall, bathed in the rich, leathery scent of winter furs, Archer saw that the Countess Olenska was looking at him with a faintly questioning smile. He attempted to translate his actions from the previous night at the Opera into a language she might understand. "Of course you know already—about May and me," he said with a shy laugh. "She scolded me for not giving you the news last night at the Opera: I had her orders to tell you that we were engaged—but I couldn't, in that crowd."

The smile slipped from Ellen’s eyes to her lips, making her look younger, like the bold brown Ellen Mingott of his boyhood. Yet, she seemed to belong to a lost cultural memory, speaking a dialect of lived, subjective experience entirely untranslatable to Archer's strict New York vocabulary. "Of course I know; yes. And I'm so glad. But one doesn't tell such things first in a crowd." Her voice carried a delicate acoustic frequency, a soft, rhythmic cadence that contrasted sharply with the booming certainties of her grandmother. She held out her hand. "Good-bye; come and see me some day."

As the carriage carried them back down Fifth Avenue, the jarring, percussive clatter of the cobble-stones swallowed them once more, disrupting the mind's equilibrium. They spoke loudly over the industrial noise, praising Mrs. Mingott's age, spirit, and wonderful attributes. Yet, a heavy silence surrounded Ellen Olenska's name. Archer could hear the unspoken power dynamics embedded in their shared silence: Mrs. Welland's harsh, internal critique of Ellen parading down Fifth Avenue at the crowded "shopping hour" in the glaring sunlight with Julius Beaufort. 

Archer mentally agreed, falling back on the dominant lexicon of his tribe. A newly engaged man did not spend his time calling on married women. The subjective, untranslatable freedom of the European set Ellen had lived in—where such things were customary, where they never did anything else—was entirely alien to him. Immersed in the deafening clatter of his native city, and breathing in the familiar, reassuring scent of his own world, he thanked heaven he was a New Yorker, speaking a language he understood, and about to ally himself with a woman of his own kind.
