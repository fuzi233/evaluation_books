They met the host in the narrow passage, where the smell of damp stone, yesterday’s smoke, and frying fat clung like a second plaster.

“Welcome, messieurs,” said he, lifting his cap with a bow so low his forehead nearly met his own apron-string.

“Come,” said Gerard, half laughing, half weary, “we are not in Germany.”

In the common room the hostess held court—a broad, red-cheeked woman with sleeves rolled for work and eyes quick as a knife in a kitchen. She dipped a curtsey and beamed as though they were kin come home at Martinmas.

“Give yourself the trouble of sitting ye down, fair sir,” she said to Gerard, and swept two chairs with her apron, though they were clean enough.

“Thank you, dame,” Gerard answered; and to himself, with the sour mirth of a tired traveller: “A polite nation indeed. First the trouble of sitting; then the labour of eating; then the toil of digesting; and at last, by Hercules, the strain of going to bed and the struggle of falling asleep.”

Denys had already taken command of the victuals.

“Why, Denys,” cried Gerard, “what are you doing—ordering supper for only two?”

“Why not?”

“What, can we sup without waiting for forty more? Burgundy for ever!”

“Aha! Courage, camarade. Le dia—”

“C’est convenu.”

In that house, and in France by the look of it, the women ruled as naturally as fire draws smoke. The host drifted about like a cap on a pond, forever doffing it because he had nothing else to do; and as the women went by, they shifted him aside without so much as turning their heads—no more ceremony than brushing a living twig from one’s sleeve in a thicket.

Supper came in on a maid’s arms—steam, grease, and garlic rising before the dishes like a banner. The hostess followed empty-handed, as if her presence were itself a course.

“Fall to, my masters,” she said briskly. “Y’have but one enemy here; and he lies under your knife.”

They fell to with the honest hunger of men who have walked their meat a dozen leagues. There was bread with a crackling crust and a warm, sour scent; there was a stew dark with wine and onions cooked down till they had turned to sweetness; there were eggs—always eggs in such houses—fried in brown butter till the edges laced and crisped. The fat sang faintly in the pan somewhere beyond the door, and that small domestic music made the room feel less like an inn than a kitchen where a family ate.

The hostess drew her chair near enough to make them company as well as feed them, and talked as though she had known them since they were weaned; then, business calling, she rose and sent in her daughter to take the vacant place. The girl was near twenty, fair and gentle, and when she learned how far Gerard had come and still must go, her eyes softened with a tenderness that warmed him more than the stew.

She stayed almost half an hour. When she went, Gerard looked after her and said low, amazed, “This an inn? Why, it is like home.”

“Qui fit François il fit courtois,” said Denys, swelling with pleased pride.

“Courteous?” Gerard shook his head. “Christian—this is beyond courtesy. To welcome us like home-guests and old friends, us that are here to-day and gone to-morrow. But who merits kindness more than a worn traveller, far from his folk? Hola—here comes another.”

This one brought in not gentleness but a gust of rude health and laughter. She was the chambermaid, about five-and-twenty, with a cocked nose, a wide mouth that seemed made for merriment, a black eye like a berry in sunshine, and bare arms stout as a dairymaid’s, fit to wring linen dry or swing a pail without spilling.

The moment she entered, a traveller tossed her a free jest. Before the jest had cooled in the air, the whole room was roaring—at the jester. Her tongue had turned on him and pricked him like a master fencer touching a novice, swift and sure. Another tried; then another. Marion planted her fists on her hips and held the room at play, beating them with a bludgeon of country humour—coarse as the rind on a winter cheese, but hearty, and spoken so fast it carried all before it.

Gerard sat staring as if he had discovered a new beast in the menagerie of the world. He leaned to Denys and whispered, “Now I see why you Frenchmen say a woman’s tongue is her sword.”

At that moment she downed another assailant, and Denys, chivalrous as ever, flung her his threadbare refrain to hearten “the weaker vessel”—though she looked more like the iron pot among earthenware.

“Courage, ma mie, le—”

She wheeled on him. “How can he be dead as long as there is an archer left alive?” The room shouted, and Denys laughed with them, caught in his own snare.

“It is washing day, my masters,” Marion said suddenly grave, as if announcing a fast.

“Aprés? We travellers cannot strip and go bare while you wash our clothes,” grumbled an old fellow by the fire—one who had held his tongue through the raillery and now ventured into the safe sunshine of commonplaces.

“I aimed not your way, ancient man,” Marion replied, looking him over slow from bald pate to cracked shoe. “But since you ask—methinks you might take a turn i’ the tub, clothes and all, and no harm done.” Laughter shook the benches. Then her bright eye slid to Gerard. “But what I spoke for, I thought this young sire might like his beard starched.”

Gerard’s chin wore only a thin, silky crop. Denys, traitor, laughed the loudest, for Denys’s own beard was a stiff, bristling hedge.

Gerard took the jest calmly; vanity was no great burden on him. “Nay, chambrière,” said he, smiling, “mine is all unworthy your pains; take you this fair growth in hand,” and he pointed at Denys’s vegetable.

“Oh, time for that, when I starch the besoms.”

They were still shouting when the hostess re-entered; and in the instant it took her to cross the threshold Marion turned from Amazon to Madonna—meek face, demure hands, eyes down. But it was wasted craft. The hostess had heard the laughter and did not, at heart, disapprove.

“Ah, Marion lass,” she said, good-humoured, “if you laid me an egg every time you cackle, ‘L’es Trois Poissons’ would never lack an omelet.”

At “omelet” Gerard’s mind flew, as it often did, to the comfort of food well done: the eggs beaten just enough to marry yolk and white, not to froth; the pan warmed, not scorched; the butter browned to hazelnut and poured off before it blackened; a pinch of salt between finger and thumb, measured by habit, not miserly. It pleased him that even a scold could speak of cookery as coin.

“Now, dame,” said Gerard aloud, “what is to pay?”

“What for?”

“Our supper.”

“Where is the hurry? cannot you be content to pay when you go? Lose the guest, find the money—that is the rule of ‘The Three Fish.’”

“But, dame—outside ‘The Three Fish’ it is thus written—‘Ici-on ne loge—’”

“Bah! Let that flea stick on the wall!” She pointed up at the smoky ceiling, which was scribbled all over with scratches—scores, accounts, rude hieroglyphs cut through soot till the pale plaster showed. “Look hither. We write there to scare moneyless folk from the inn altogether, or to be acted on when a non-paying face comes in and insists on being served. We can’t refuse them plump, you know. The law forbids us.”

“And how know you mine is not such a face?”

“Out fie! it is the best face that has entered ‘The Three Fish’ this autumn.”

“And mine, dame?” Denys asked, thrusting his chin forward. “Dost see no knavery here?”

She looked at him without blinking. “Not such a good one as the lad’s; nor ever will be. But it is the face of a true man. For all that,” she added dryly, “an I were ten years younger, I’d as lieve not meet that face on a dark night too far from home.”

Gerard stared. Denys laughed, taking it for a compliment.

“Why, dame,” said Denys, “I would but sip the night dew off the flower; and you needn’t take ten years off, nor ten days, to be worth risking a scratched face for.”

Marion had slipped back in. “There, our mistress,” she cried. “Said I not t’other day you could make a fool of them still, an if you were properly minded?”

“I dare say ye did,” the hostess retorted. “It sounds like some daft wench’s speech.”

“Dame,” Gerard said, bewildered, “this is wonderful.”

“What? Oh—no, no. That is no wonder at all. I have been here all my life; and reading faces is the first thing a girl picks up in an inn.”

“And frying eggs the second,” Marion put in. “No—telling lies. Frying eggs is the third, though.”

“And holding her tongue the last,” said the hostess, “and modesty the day after—never at all.”

“Alack!” Marion flung up her hands. “Talk of my tongue. But I say no more. She under whose wing I live now deals the blow. I’m sped—’tis but a chambermaid gone. Catch what’s left on’t!” And she staggered and sank backwards onto the handsomest fellow in the room—which happened to be Gerard.

“Tic! tic!” Gerard cried, peevish as a man whose thoughts have been interrupted mid-sentence. “There—don’t be stupid! That is too heavy a jest for me. See you not I am talking to the mistress?”

Marion sprang upright again like a ball, made two little bounds into the middle of the floor, and turned a pirouette. “There, mistress,” she said. “I give in; ’tis you that reigns supreme with the men—leastways with male children.”

“Young man,” the hostess said to Gerard, “this girl is not so stupid as her deportment. In reading of faces and frying of omelets, there we are great. ’Twould be hard if we failed at these arts, since they are about all we do know.”

“You do not quite take me, dame,” said Gerard. “That honesty in a face should shine forth to your experienced eye—that seems reasonable. But how by looking on Denys here could you learn his one little foible—his insanity—his miserable mulierosity?” The more he thought on it, the more it vexed him, as though language itself were being forced to carry a meaning it did not fit.

“His mule—his what?” The hostess crossed herself at the polysyllable, as if he had named a devil.

“Nay, ’tis but the word I was fain to invent for him.”

“Invent? What, can a child like you make other words than grow in Burgundy by nature? Take heed what ye do! why, we are overrun with them already, especially bad ones. Lord, these be times. I look to hear of a new thistle invented next.”

“Well then, dame—mulierose means wrapped up, body and soul, in women. So prithee tell me: how did you ever detect the noodle’s mulierosity?”

“Alas, good youth,” she said, “you make a mountain of a molehill. We that are women be notice-takers; and out of the tail of our eye see more than most men can, glaring through a prospect glass. Whiles I move to and fro doing this and that, my glance is still on my guests. I did notice that this soldier’s eyes were never off the womenfolk—my daughter, or Marion, or even an old woman like me—all was gold to him. And there you sat, turning to the speaker, her or him, and that is common sense.”

Denys burst into a hoarse laugh. “You never were more out. Why, this silky, smooth-faced companion is a very Turk—all but his beard. He is more wrapped up in one single Dutch lass called Margaret than I am in the whole bundle of ye, brown and fair.”

“Man alive,” said the hostess, “that is just the contrary. Yourn is the bane, and hisn the cure. Cling you still to Margaret, my dear. I hope she is an honest girl.”

“Dame, she is an angel.”

“Ay, ay, they are all that till better acquainted. I’d as lieve have her no more than honest, and then she will serve to keep you out of worse company. As for you, soldier, there is trouble in store for you. Your eyes were never made for the good of your soul.”

“Nor of his pouch either,” Marion struck in, “and his lips—they will sip the dew, as he calls it, off many a bramble bush.”

“Overmuch clack! Marion, overmuch clack.”

“Ods bodikins, mistress; ye didn’t hire me to be one o’ your three fishes, did ye?” Marion sulked for the space of thirty seconds and no more.

At that moment the landlord slipped in, scenting, as timid men do, that there had been noise and he might claim a share of authority.

“Is that the way to speak to our mistress?” he ventured.

“Hold your whisht,” said his wife sharply. “It is not your business to check the girl; she is a good servant to you.”

“What,” he muttered, “is the cock never to crow, and the hens at it all day?”

“You can crow as loud as you like, my man, out o’ doors. But the hen means to rule the roost.”

“I know a byword to that tune,” said Gerard.

“Do ye, now? Out wi’t then.”

“Femme veut en toute saison, Estre dame en sa mason.”

“I never heard it afore,” said the hostess, “but ’tis as sooth as gospel. Ay, they that set these bywords a rolling had eyes and tongues, and tongues and eyes. Before all the world give me an old saw.”

“And me a young husband,” Marion cried. “Now there was a chance for you all, and nobody spoke. Oh! it is too late now—I’ve changed my mind.”

“All the better for some poor fellow,” Denys suggested.

Later, when the “little mistress” returned, they all drew round the fire—travellers, host, hostess, even the servants in the outer ring—as though the room were a hearth and not a trade. The flames clicked and the embers settled with soft sighs; outside, the wind worried at the shutters; within, voices rose and fell, and the very sound of company—laughter, tale-telling, the scrape of a boot, the thump of a log—seemed a kind of nourishment. Gerard told, in his turn, one of his most terrible saints’ stories from his manuscript store, and made them shudder deliciously; then his head nodded, spent by walking, eating, and the effort of making strangers his kin for an hour.

The young mistress saw and gave Marion a look. Marion lit a rush, and laying her hand on Gerard’s shoulder, bade him follow. She led him to a chamber with two clean white beds that smelled of sun-dried flax and the faint iron tang of freshly pressed linen.

“Choose,” said she.

“Either is paradise,” said Gerard. “I’ll take this. Do you know—I have not lain in a naked bed once since I left my home in Holland.”

“Alack! poor soul,” Marion said. “Well then, the sooner my flax and your down—he! he!—come together, the better; so—allons!” And she held out her cheek, as brisk and business-like as if it were her hand for a tip.

“Allons? What does that mean?”

“It means good-night. Ahem! What—don’t they salute the chambermaid in your part?”

“Not all in a moment.”

“What, do they make a business on’t?”

“Nay, perverter of words—I mean we make not so free with strange women.”

“They must be strange women,” she said, sniffing, “if they do not think you strange fools then. Here is a coil. Why, all the old greasy greybeards that lie at our inn do kiss us chambermaids—faugh! and what have we poor wretches to set on t’other side the compt but now and then a nice young—? Alack! time flies. Chambermaids can’t be spared long in the nursery, so how is’t to be?”

“An’t please you,” said Gerard, trying to laugh it off, “arrange with my comrade for both. He is mulierose; I am not.”

“Nay, ’tis the curb he will want, not the spur. Well! well! you shall to bed without paying the usual toll; and oh, but ’tis sweet to fall in with a young man who can withstand these ancient ill customs, and gainsay brazen hussies. Shalt have thy reward.”

“Thank you. But what are you doing with my bed?”

“Me? oh, only taking off these sheets, and going to put on the pair the drunken miller slept in last night.”

“Oh, no! no!” Gerard cried, horror-struck. “You cruel, black-hearted thing! There—there!”

“A la bonne heure!” She clapped her hands softly. “What will not perseverance effect? But note now the frowardness of a mad wench! I cared not for’t a button. I am dead sick of that sport this five years. But you denied me; so then forthwith I behoved to have it; belike had gone through fire and water for’t. Alas, young sir, we women are kittle cattle—poor perverse toads. Excuse us: and keep us in our place, savoir, at arm’s length; and so good-night!”

At the door she turned, and her face changed as if another woman had stepped into it. Her voice fell from banter to something grave and tender.

“The Virgin guard thy head, and the holy Evangelists watch the bed where lies a poor young wanderer far from home! Amen!”

Then she was gone, tearing down the stairs; and a moment later a peal of laughter from the salle told where she had landed.

“Now that is a character,” Gerard murmured, and yawned over his discovery.

He was in a dry bath of cold, clean linen—an inexpressible refreshment after such long disuse. The chill passed; a glow rose up; and then—Sevenbergen, as if the body, warmed and fed, had boiled memory to the surface like broth.

In the morning Gerard woke refreshed and would have risen, but found himself a close prisoner. His linen was gone. In that age a nightgown was no common thing: men crept into clean sheets clothed in their innocence, and out of them, it seemed, took most after Adam’s eldest son. Without his shirt, Gerard was pinned to the bed as surely as a beetle under a glass.

He bewailed his captivity to Denys; but even as the words left his mouth, the door opened and in sailed Marion with their linen newly washed and ironed, laid along her arms like a baker bearing loaves, white and warm from the oven. The cloth smelled of lye, clear water, and the faint sweetness of sun—cleanliness made almost edible.

She set it down on the table.

“Oh, you good girl,” Gerard cried, as if she had brought him freedom itself.

“Alack, have you found me out at last?”

“Yes, indeed. Is this another custom?”

“Nay, not to take them unbidden,” she said. “But at night we aye question travellers, are they for linen washed. So I came into you, but you were both sound. Then said I to the little mistress, ‘La! where is the sense of waking wearied men, t’ask them is Charles the Great dead, and would they liever carry foul linen or clean—especially this one with a skin like cream?’ ‘And so he has, I declare,’ said the young mistress.”

“That was me,” Denys put in, with the air of a glossator.

“Guess once more,” said Marion, “and you’ll hit the mark.”

“Notice him not,” Gerard said warmly. “Marion, he is an impudent fellow; and I am sure we cannot be grateful enough for your goodness—and I am sorry I ever refused you—anything you fancied you should like.”

“Oh, are ye there,” said l’espiegle, turning at once. “I take that to mean you would fain brush the morning dew off, as your bashful companion calls it. Well then—excuse me—’tis customary, but not prudent. I decline. Quits with you, lad.”

“Stop! stop!” Denys cried as she was making off, victorious. “I am curious to know how many of ye were here last night a-feasting your eyes on us twain.”

“’Twas so satisfactory a feast as we weren’t half a minute over’t,” Marion answered. “Who? why, the big mistress, the little mistress, Janet, and me, and the whole posse comitatus, on tiptoe. We mostly make our rounds the last thing, not to get burned down; and in prodigious numbers. Somehow that maketh us bolder, especially where archers lie scattered about.”

“Why did not you tell me? I’d have lain awake.”

“Beau sire, the saying goes that the good and the ill are all one while their lids are closed. So we said, ‘Here is one who will serve God best asleep. Break not his rest!’”

“She is funny,” said Gerard, as if delivering judgment.

“I must be either that or knavish.”

“How so?”

“Because ‘The Three Fish’ pay me to be funny. You will eat before you part? Good. Then I’ll go see the meat be fit for such worshipful teeth.” And off she went, her feet quick on the boards, as if she could taste the seasoning with her ears.

“Denys,” said Gerard, watching her go, “I wish that was a great boy, and going along with us, to keep us cheery.”

“So do not I,” Denys replied. “But I wish it was going along with us as it is.”

“Now Heaven forefend! A fine fool you would make of yourself.”

They broke their fast, settled their score, and said farewell. Then they found Marion had not exaggerated the “custom of the country.” The three chief women took and kissed them heartily, and they kissed the three chief women. The landlord took and kissed them, and they kissed the landlord; and the cry followed them out like a blessing and a bargain both:

“Come back, the sooner the better!”

“Never pass ‘The Three Fish.’ Should your purses be void, bring yourselves: le sieur credit is not dead for you.”

So they took the road again.

They came to a little town where Denys would buy shoes. The shoemaker stood in his doorway, wide awake, and bowed as if he were greeting a noble. Denys was fitted quickly; the leather creaked fresh as bark, and the new soles smelled of oak-tan and wax. The tradesman followed them to the street and dismissed them with such graceful salutes that Gerard, who loved order and civil sound in the world as he loved good bread, felt himself soothed.

They agreed it was Elysium to deal with such a man.

“Not but what my German shoes have lasted well enough,” Gerard added, just.

Outside the town ran a pebbled walk, the stones set close for dry footing.

“This is to keep the burghers’ feet dry,” Denys said, “a-walking o’ Sundays with their wives and daughters.”

Those simple words—wives and daughters, and dry feet on a Sunday, and the quiet ritual of walking together—painted “home” so suddenly in Gerard’s heart that it ached like a hunger.

“Oh, how sweet!” he said.

A little farther on, sweetness ended. They came on a gibbet, and on it two skeletons swayed and clacked softly when the wind moved them—bones answering the air with a dry, obscene music.

“Mercy!” Gerard cried. “A gibbet! and—ugh—two skeletons thereon! Oh, Denys, what a sorry sight to woo by!”

“Nay,” said Denys, shrugging, “a comfortable sight; for every rogue i’ the air there is one the less a-foot.”

A little farther, and worse: two stone pillars, and between them a huge wheel studded thick with iron prongs. Tangled in those prongs were bones and rags of cloth—what had once been a man scattered and displayed like refuse for crows. The air smelled of old corruption and iron, and above it the cawing and wing-beat made a harsh canopy of sound that set Gerard’s teeth on edge.

He hid his face in his hands. “Oh, to think those patches and bones are all that is left of a man! of one who was what we are now.”

“Excusez,” said Denys. “A thing that went on two legs and stole. Are we no more than that?”

“How know ye he stole? Have true men never suffered death and torture too?”

“None of my kith ever found their way to the gibbet, I know.”

“The better their luck. Prithee, how died the saints?”

“Hard. But not in Burgundy.”

“Ye massacred them wholesale at Lyons,” Gerard answered hotly, “and that is on Burgundy’s threshold. To you the gibbet proves the crime, because you read not story. Alas! had you stood on Calvary that bloody day we sigh for to this hour, I tremble to think you had perhaps shouted for joy at the gibbet builded there; for the cross was but the Roman gallows, Father Martin says.”

“The blaspheming old hound!”

“Oh, fie! fie!” Gerard cried. “A holy and a book-learned man. Ay, Denys, y’had read them, that suffered there, by the bare light of the gibbet. ‘Drive in the nails!’ y’had cried: ‘drive in the spear!’ Here be three malefactors—three roues—yet of those little three one was the first Christian saint, and another was the Saviour of the world which gibbeted him.”

Denys, stung, protested on his honour that in Burgundy they managed things better. After a moment’s thought he added, more softly, that those very horrors had once made him curse with rage when the good curé in his village told the Passion at Eastertide—but that was in an outlandish nation, near a thousand years ago. “Mort de ma vie,” he said, trying to laugh it away, “let us hope it is not true; or at least sore exaggerated. Do but see how all tales gather as they roll!”

Then his blood rose again, quick as a pot boiling over. “Do ye not blush to play with your book-craft on your unlettered friend, and throw dust in his eyes, evening the saints with these reptiles?”

But as suddenly he slid back into lightness. “Since your heart beats for vermin, feel for the carrion crows! They be as good vermin as these. Would ye send them to bed supperless, poor pretty poppets? Why, these be their larder; the pangs of hunger would gnaw them dead, but for cold cut-purse hung up here and there.”

Gerard, who had held silence a while, said at last, very quietly, “The subject is closed between us, and for ever. There are things wherein our hearts seem wide as the poles asunder, and eke our heads. But I love thee dearly all the same.” And he said it with such grace and tenderness that Denys had not the heart to jest.

Towards afternoon they heard, ahead, a faint wailing—thin at first like wind through reeds, then clearer, many-voiced, rising and falling as if the road itself cried out. Being fast walkers, they soon came upon its cause.

A score of pikemen marched with several constables; and before them a herd was driven, more than a hundred souls of various ages. Few were old; many were in their strength; and there were children—some scarce able to walk, some in arms. The men went downcast and silent. The women made the outcry, keening and calling, their voices torn and hoarse, a sorrowful music that soured the very air.

“Good Heaven!” Gerard cried. “What a band of them! But stay—surely all those children cannot be thieves; why, there are some in arms. What on earth is this, Denys?”

Denys nodded toward an officer with a badge. “Ask that bourgeois. This is Burgundy: here a civil question ever draws a civil reply.”

Gerard went up, removed his cap—a courtesy instantly returned—and said, “For our Lady’s sake, sir, what do ye with these poor folk?”

“Nay, what is that to you, my lad?” the functionary answered, suspicious, though not unmannerly.

“Master,” said Gerard, “I’m a stranger, and athirst for knowledge.”
