The afternoon light slanted thin and gold through the leaded panes of the northern inn room the next day. Gerard’s fevered lightheadedness had burned off, leaving him sharp-tempered, thrumming with odd, restless fancies, until at last he begged Denys to fetch him a lemon to suck. The rough old soldier, softened by weeks of tender care for his friend into something as fussy and doting as a grey-haired grandparent, sprang to his feet at once. “Set thy mind at rest,” he laughed, “thou shalt have a lemon faster than a quart pot of ale can be drained dry,” and off he trotted to ransack every shop in the town for the rare southern fruit.

Lemons were not half so common in 15th century northern climes as they are now, and Denys was gone far longer than Gerard expected. He was growing thoroughly impatient when the door at last creaked open — but it was no Denys on the threshold.

A stately, imposing figure stepped softly into the room: an old gentleman clad in a long, sober gown trimmed with rich sable fur, cherry-red hose, and sharp-pointed poulaine shoes, a slender sword at his hip sheathed in tooled morocco leather. A starched ruff bit deep into his neck, held stiff in sharp furrows by a hidden wooden rebato framework, a four-cornered fur-bordered cap sat square on his head, and a majestic flowing white beard spilled over his chest. Gerard needed no telling his trade, save for the sword at his side this was the full formal dress of a leading physician, and a small boy trailed at his heels hefting a wide wicker basket where glass phials, lint, and sharp surgical tools peeped openly over the rim. The old man glided to the bedside, and murmured soft and sotto voce, “How fares it with thee, my son?”

Gerard thanked him, and told him his wound ached very little now, but his throat was parched dry and his head felt heavy as lead.

“A wound? No one spoke to me of a wound. Let me see it, then. Aye, aye — a fine clean bite. That mastiff had sound, sharp teeth to tear this out, I’ll warrant.” The doctor’s sympathy seemed to drift straight past Gerard to the imaginary dog he had invented on the spot. “This must be cauterized at once, or else thou’lt soon start shrinking from water, and kicking and thrashing under our hands in fever. This is a terrible year for raving curs, and one has done thee harm — but we shall outwit him yet. Boy, go heat the iron!”

“Wait, sir,” Gerard cut in gently, “it was no dog that hurt me, it was a bear.”

“A bear! Young man,” the doctor rebuked him severely, “think what thou sayest. It is no jest to mock a man of art who brings grey hairs and forty years of study to heal thee. A bear, quotha! Hadst thou dissected a tenth as many bears as I have, drawn their teeth just to keep my hand steady, thou wouldst know no bear’s jaw could ever leave such a silly, trifling wound. I tell thee it was a dog — and since thou pressest me on the point, it was no great large hound, neither, but one of those rabid little curs that plague every street these days, snatching at every man’s leg and felling their victims — were it not for me and my learned brethren, who stop the mischief dead with knife and hot iron.”

“Alas, sir, I never said it was a bear’s jaw that bit me,” Gerard protested. “I said it was a bear — it was his paw that scratched me.”

“And why didst thou not tell me that at once?”

“Because thou wouldst not stop talking long enough to let me.”

“Never hide anything from your leech, young man,” the doctor continued, a great lover of the sound of his own voice and one of the very worst listeners in all of Europe. “Well, this is bad business. All hard, horny growths on animals — the claws of tigers, panthers, badgers, cats, bears, the antlers of deer, even the nails of humans, especially little children — are steeped in the direst poison. Thou wouldst far rather have been bitten by a common cur than gored by a bull, or struck by a stag’s antler, or scratched by a bear. Still, I shall make thee a drawing poultice for thy leg. Meantime, we must cool thy whole body: put out thy tongue! Good. Fever. Let me feel thy pulse — good. Fever. I ordain bloodletting, and at once.”

“Flebotomy? That is bloodletting, is it not?” Gerard hummed. “Well, no matter, if it will surely cure me, for I will not lie idle stuck here in this bed.” The doctor assured him bloodletting was infallible, especially for his condition.

“Hans, go fetch all the things we need, and I will entertain the patient meantime with sound reasoning.”

The man of art then explained to Gerard that when the body fell ill, the blood grew hot, distempered, and more or less poisonous; draw off a portion of that bad fluid, and Nature would be forced to make new, purer blood to fill its place. Bleeding, he declared, was both a cooler and a purifier, a specific cure for every disease that existed — for all diseases were febrile, no matter what quack empirics might claim otherwise.

“Think not,” he added warmly, “that any fool barber can do this right, just because he can open a vein (though not all of them can close it again after). The art is to know exactly which vein to open for which illness. Only last week they brought me a man tormented with earache. I bled him in the right thigh, and his earache vanished straight away. By the by, he died a few days later. Another man came to me with toothache. I bled him behind the ear, and relieved him in the blink of an eye. He too, as it happens, died not long after. I bled our town bailiff between thumb and forefinger for his rheumatism. Next week he came back to me with a raging headache and drumming in his ears, held his hand out over the basin ready to be bled again — but I laughed at his folly, and bled him in the left ankle sore against his will, and made his head as light as a dried nut.”

Wandering off from his immediate subject, as all men passionate about their art will, the learned physician went on to hold forth:
“Know, young man, that two great schools of medicine are fighting for mastery across all of Europe right now. The Arabian school, whose ancient oracles are Avicenna, Rhazes, Albucazis, and whose modern revivers are Chauliac and Lanfranc. And then the Greek school, whose modern champions are Bessarion, Platinus, and Marsilius Ficinus, but whose first great masters were the very gods of healing themselves: Phoebus, Chiron, Aesculapius and his sons Podalinus and Machaon, Pythagoras, Democritus, Praxagoras who first named the arteries, and Dioctes, ‘who first gave his full attention to the reading of urine.’ All these taught their pupils by word of mouth. Then came Hippocrates, eighteenth in direct line from Aesculapius, and we have his writings still. To him we owe the very idea of the vital principle. He invented the bandage, and first tapped the chest to drain fluid from dropsy. Above all else, he dissected — though only quadrupeds, for the brutal prejudices of pagan common folk would never let a human body fall under the knife of science. Then came Aristotle, who gave us the aorta, the largest blood vessel in the human body.”

“Surely, sir,” Gerard objected humbly, “the Almighty made every part inside our bodies, not Aristotle, nor any pagan Greek man.”

“Child, of course He made the thing itself! But Aristotle did far more: he gave us the name for it, showed us where it was, told us what it did. But young men will always argue. The next great light was Galen, who studied at Alexandria, then the very home of all science. He grew frustrated with only dissecting quadrupeds, so he dissected apes, for they are closer to man, and bled them as freely as a Trojan warrior. Then came Theophilus, who gave us the nerves, the lacteal vessels, and the pia mater.”

This was too much for Gerard. “I cannot lie still and hear it said that mortal man gave us parts of the body that Adam our father received straight from the Lord, who made him from clay, and us his sons.”

“Did ever you hear such perversity?” The doctor’s face flushed bright red. “Who is the true giver of a thing to man? He who hides it deep in the dark recesses of the flesh where no man can see it, or the learned sage who reveals it to your understanding, so that your mind is enriched with knowledge of it? True possession is nothing but comprehension. Art thou answered?”

“I am silenced, sir.”

“And that is all for the better, for garrulous fevered patients are impossible to cure. I say then that Eristratus gave us the cerebral nerves and the milk vessels — nay, more, he invented the operation for cutting bladder stones, whatever you may say. Then came another whose name I forget, you have perturbed me so with your petty little objections. Then came Ammonius, the man who invented crushing stones in the bladder without cutting. And here comes Hans with the basin, to stop your tongue. Blow on your chafing dish, boy, and hand me the basin. Good. The Arabians? What are they, but a new sect who stumbled on the old Greek writings around the year 1000, and read them all wrong, having no light of their own? Their false prophet Mahound, an impostor in science as he was in religion, strictly forbade them to dissect even the lowliest animals — and he who takes anatomy from medicine ‘takes the sun from the world,’ as Cicero well said. Do not wonder at my passion, good youth: when the public good is at stake, a little anger is civic, humane, and honorable. Only lately a pestilent Arabist empiric has settled in this very town, no knowledge of anatomy, can scarce tell Greek from Hebrew, and he has stolen half my patients already, and I tremble for the rest. Stretch out thy ankle. And thou, Hans, breathe on the coals.”

It was at this exact moment that Denys burst through the door, the lemons in his hand, and stopped short, staring. “What sport is this?” he asked, lifting his eyebrows high.

Gerard flushed a little, and told him the learned doctor was planning to bleed him, then cauterize the wound. That was all.

“Aye, indeed — and what is that urchin over there blowing hot coals for?”

“What else can it be,” the doctor said to Gerard, “but to cauterize the vein once we open it and let the poisoned blood out? It is the only safe way. Avicenna does recommend tying the vein shut with a ligature, but he never once explains how it is to be done — I doubt he knew himself, nor any of the spawn of Ishmael. For my part, I put no faith in such tricky, half-baked tricks. Remember this for a certainty: whatever an Arab or Arabist claims is right, must of necessity be wrong.”

“Oh, I see what the coals are for now,” Denys said. “Art thou fool enough to let him press hot iron to thy living flesh? Hast thou ever held thy little finger in a candle flame for ten seconds? This will go on for minutes. Art not content to burn in purgatory after thou art dead? Must thou needs taste its torments ahead of time, right here?”

“I never thought of that,” Gerard said gravely. “The good doctor spoke of cautery, not burning — it is all the same, I suppose, but cautery does not sound nearly so fearful a word.”

“Imbecile! That is their whole trick, to wrap plain men up in dark, fancy words until hissing flesh teaches them what they really meant. Now listen to what I have seen with my own eyes. When a soldier bleeds from a wound on the battlefield, these leeches say, ‘Fever. Bleed him!’ and so they drain the blood that the wound spared him, the blood he needs to fight the coming weakness. Hundreds of tall, strong fellows I have seen bled and pricked right out of the world. But if by luck they are wounded where no surgeon can reach them? Then they live. I should never have outlived that battle in Brabant if it had not been for the lucky mischance that no chirurgeon found me before the frost clotted my bleeding wounds. A surgeon would have pricked one more hole in my body with his lance, drained my last drop of blood out, and my soul right after it. That is why I place no trust in them: what can kill a hardened veteran can certainly kill a soft, gentle clerk like thee.”

“That sounds like plain common sense,” Gerard sighed languidly. “But there is no need to shout so loud. I was not born deaf, and my ears are sharp enough just now.”

“Common sense? A very brute common sense indeed!” the bad-listening doctor shouted. “Why, this is a soldier, a man whose trade is to kill, not cure!” He switched to fluent, harsh French. “Woe to you, unlearned oaf, if you stand between a physician and his patient! And woe to you, misguided youth, if you listen to that man of blood.”

“Much obliged to you for the compliment,” Denys bowed with mock politeness. “But I am an honest man, and I will not rob thee of thy trade name. I kill a man now and then, it is true — but you slay a score for every one of mine. I spill a spoonful of blood, you spill a whole tubful. The world is always fooled by fine shows. We soldiers rattle about with long swords, making noise, and make two enemies for every man we kill — but you smooth gownsmen, with soft phrases and hidden lancets, you are the ones who thin out the human race.”

“A sick chamber is no place for foolery,” the physician cried.

“No, nor is it a place for bawling at the top of your lungs,” Gerard peevishly corrected him.

“Come, young man,” the doctor said, softening his tone to be kind, “be reasonable. Every man must be trusted in his own art. My whole life has been given over to healing. I studied at Montpellier, the greatest school in all France, and therefore all of Europe. There I learned divination by pulse, divination by excrement, pathology, therapeutics, and greater than all of them, anatomy. As disciples of Hippocrates and Galen, we had opportunities those ancient men never dreamed of. Goodbye to quadrupeds, apes, pagan ignorance, and Mohammedan nonsense! We paid the churchwardens, shook down the gallows, snuck into graveyards at dead of night, driven by nothing but love of science and our fellow man. The Paris authorities all had orders to wink at our work, and wink they did! The good king himself helped us: twice a year he sent us a living criminal condemned to death, and told us ‘do with him as science requires, dissect him alive if you think fit.’”

“By Herod’s liver and Nero’s bowels, he will make me ashamed of the land that bore me if he praises it one second more!” Denys roared at the top of his voice.

Gerard yelped a little, clapped his fingers over his ears — then pulled them right out again, and shouted back just as loud, “Thou great roaring blaspheming bull of Bashan, hold thy noisy tongue!”

Denys’s face softened at once, he looked properly contrite.

“Tush, petty fellow,” the doctor waved a hand over Denys as if he was training a pointer dog to lie down, completely unruffled. “We very seldom dissected the living criminal all the way through, only bits and pieces. Mostly we inoculated them with whatever interesting diseases were going around at the time, of course picking the most fascinating ones.”

“Which means the filthiest,” Denys whispered meekly.

“Then we watched every stage of the disease right through to its natural end.”

“Which means until the poor rogue died,” Denys whispered again, still meek.

“And now, my poor suffering lad, who deserves thy confidence more? This honest soldier with his youthful ignorance and silly prejudices, or a greybeard loaded with the gathered wisdom of hundreds of years?”

“That means,” Denys burst out impatiently, “wilt thou believe what a jackdaw in a long gown heard from a starling in a long gown, who heard it from a jay, who heard it from a magpie, who heard it from a popinjay — or wilt thou believe what I, a man with nothing to gain from lying, have seen with my own two eyes? That fevered men who get bled die, and fevered men left to rest live? Wait — who sent for this bloodsucking leech? Didst thou?”

“Not I. I thought thou hadst.”

“Nay,” the doctor explained, “the innkeeper told me a stranger was lying sick in his house, so I said to myself ‘a poor soul who needs my art’ and came at once.”

“That was the act of a good Christian, sir,” Gerard said gently.

“Act of a good bloodhound!” Denys snorted contemptuously. “Art thou so green thou dost not know all innkeepers are in league with these leeches, who pay them a toll for every patient they trick? Whatever thou payest this old man for stealing thy lifeblood, the innkeeper takes a third of it for betraying thee to him. And no sooner does thy blood run down the stairs in that basin than he will smell or see it, and send straight for the undertaker to get another third of that fee too. He dare not wait for the doctor to go down and bespeak the undertaker first, or he would lose his cut. Am I not right, old Red and Black? Say it!”

“Denys, Denys, who taught thee to think so ill of all mankind?” Gerard sighed.

“Mine own eyes, that never let themselves be gulled by fine words, watching what men actually do across every land I have ever traveled.”
