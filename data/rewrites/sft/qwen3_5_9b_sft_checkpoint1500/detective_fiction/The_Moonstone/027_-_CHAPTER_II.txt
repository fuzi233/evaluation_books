Mr. Godfrey arrived with the punctuality of a clockwork mechanism, a trait that seemed to define every aspect of his existence. He timed his entrance so precisely that he neither startled us nor caused an awkward pause to follow a closed door. In the absolute completeness of his daily routine, the true Christian finds his calling; and certainly, this gentleman was remarkably complete.

"My dear servant," said my Aunt, "go to Miss Verinder and inform her that Mr. Ablewhite has arrived."

Both she and I immediately inquired after his health, asking simultaneously if he had recovered from his terrifying ordeal the previous week. With flawless diplomatic skill, he managed to provide two distinct responses at the exact same moment. To my aunt, he offered words of polite reassurance; to me, he directed a smile of such disarming charm that it rendered speech unnecessary.

"What!" he cried, his voice thick with feigned gratitude, "what have I done to merit all this concern? My dear aunt! My dear Miss Clack! I was simply mistaken for someone else. I was blindfolded, yes, and strangled, and tossed upon a thin carpet over a hard floor. Consider how much worse it could have been! I might have been murdered or robbed. What have I lost? Nothing but 'Nervous Force'—a concept the law does not recognize as property. Strictly speaking, I have lost absolutely nothing. Had I my way, I would have kept this adventure to myself; I detest all this fuss and publicity. However, since Mr. Luker made *his* injuries public, mine have inevitably been proclaimed as well. I have become the exclusive property of the newspapers until the gentle reader grows weary of the subject. May the gentle reader soon reach that state of exhaustion! And how fares dear Rachel? Still enjoying London's gaieties? I am so delighted to hear it. Miss Clack, I require all your indulgence. I am woefully behind schedule with my Committee Work and my dear ladies. Yet, I sincerely hope to drop by the Mothers'-Small-Clothes next week. Did you achieve cheering progress at Monday's meeting? Was the Board optimistic regarding future prospects? And are we adequately supplied with trousers?"

A heavenly gentleness radiated from his smile, rendering his apologies irresistible. The richness of his deep voice lent an indescribable allure to the mundane inquiry about trousers he had just posed. Indeed, we were overwhelmed by the quantity of trousers required; I was on the verge of confirming this fact when the door swung open again, introducing an element of worldly disruption in the person of Miss Verinder.

She approached Mr. Godfrey with an unladylike rapidity, her hair a shocking mess and her face flushed with what I considered an unbecoming intensity.

"I am charmed to see you, Godfrey," she declared, addressing him with the casual ease of one young man conversing with another. "I wish you had brought Mr. Luker along. As long as our present excitement persists, you and he are the two most fascinating men in all London. It is morbid to say so, unhealthy, and exactly the sort of thought that a well-regulated mind like Miss Clack's instinctively recoils from. Pray, ignore that. Tell me the entire story of Northumberland Street directly. The newspapers have omitted significant details."

Even the angelic gentleness inherent in Mr. Godfrey possesses a fragment of the fallen nature inherited from Adam—a small share, alas, but a share nonetheless. It pained me to witness him take Rachel's hand in both of his own, laying it softly against the left side of his waistcoat. This gesture served as a direct encouragement to her reckless manner of speech and her insolent disregard for propriety.

"Dearest Rachel," he replied, using the same tender tone that had moved me when discussing our trouser needs, "the newspapers have recounted everything—and they have done so far better than I could have."

"Godfrey believes we all exaggerate the matter," my aunt interjected. "He has just remarked that he cares little to discuss it further."

"Why?"

She asked the question with a sudden flash in her eyes, lifting her gaze sharply to meet Mr. Godfrey's. From his side, he looked down at her with an indulgence so ill-judged and undeserved that I felt compelled to intervene.

"Rachel, darling!" I remonstrated gently. "True greatness and true courage are always modest."

"You are a very good fellow in your own way, Godfrey," she said, paying me not the slightest attention, continuing to treat her cousin as if they were two young men in private conversation. "But I am quite certain you are not great. I do not believe you possess any extraordinary courage. And I am firmly persuaded—if you ever possessed any modesty—that your numerous lady-worshippers relieved you of that virtue many years ago. You have some private reason for withholding the story of your adventure in Northumberland Street, and I intend to know what it is."

"My reason is the simplest imaginable and the easiest to acknowledge," he answered, enduring her with remarkable patience. "I am simply tired of the subject."

"You are tired of the subject? My dear Godfrey, I am about to make a remark."

"And what is it?"

"You live too much in the company of women. Consequently, you have contracted two very bad habits. You have learned to speak nonsense with serious intent, and you have developed a habit of telling fibs solely for the pleasure of deception. You cannot walk straight among your lady-worshippers. I intend to make you walk straight with *me*. Come, sit down. I am brimful of direct questions, and I expect you to be brimful of direct answers."

She physically dragged him across the room to a chair by the window, ensuring the light fell fully upon his face. I felt a profound sense of obligation to report such language and describe such conduct. Yet, hemmed in as I am between Mr. Franklin Blake's financial demands on one side and my own sacred regard for truth on the other, what am I to do? I glanced at my aunt. She sat unmoved, seemingly devoid of any disposition to interfere. I had never witnessed such a torpor in her before. Perhaps it was a reaction following the trying time she endured in the countryside. Regardless, it was an unpleasant symptom to note, especially considering Lady Verinder's advanced age and her autumnal exuberance of figure.

Meanwhile, Rachel had seated herself at the window with our amiable, forbearing—and far too forbearing—Mr. Godfrey. She launched into the string of questions she had threatened, ignoring both her mother and myself as completely as if we were not present.

"Have the police accomplished anything, Godfrey?"

"Not a whit."

"It is certain, I suppose, that the three men who trapped you were the same three who later trapped Mr. Luker?"

"Humanly speaking, my dear Rachel, there can be no doubt."

"And not a single trace of them has been discovered?"

"Not a trace."

"It is thought, is it not?—that these three men are the three Indians who visited our estate in Yorkshire?"

"Some individuals entertain that belief."

"Do you share that belief?"

"My dear Rachel, they blindfolded me before I could discern their features. I know absolutely nothing of the matter. How can I offer an opinion?"

Even the angelic gentleness of Mr. Godfrey began to falter under the relentless persecution inflicted upon him. Whether unbridled curiosity or ungovernable dread motivated Miss Verinder's inquiries, I do not presume to judge. I only report that when Mr. Godfrey attempted to rise after delivering his response, she seized him by the shoulders and pushed him back into his chair. Do not mistake this for impropriety! Do not suggest that the recklessness of guilty terror alone accounts for such behavior! We must not judge others. My Christian friends, indeed, indeed, indeed, we must not judge others!

She continued her questioning, unabashed. Earnest biblical students might be reminded—as I was reminded—of the blinded children of the devil who carried on their orgies, shameless, in the days before the Flood.

"I desire to know something about Mr. Luker, Godfrey."

"I am again unfortunate, Rachel. No man knows less of Mr. Luker than I do."

"You never encountered him prior to your accidental meeting at the bank?"

"Never."

"Have you seen him since?"

"Yes. We have been examined together, as well as separately, to assist the police."

"Mr. Luker was robbed of a receipt obtained from his banker—was he not? What did the receipt cover?"

"For a valuable gem which he had entrusted to the bank's safekeeping."

"That is what the newspapers claim. It suffices for the general reader, but it falls short for me. The banker's receipt must have specified the nature of the gem?"

"The banker's receipt, Rachel—as I understand it—specified nothing of the kind. It stated that a valuable gem belonging to Mr. Luker had been deposited by him, sealed with his seal, and released only upon his personal application. That was the form, and that is all I know."

She paused briefly after his statement. She looked at her mother, sighed, then returned her gaze to Mr. Godfrey and pressed on.

"Some of our private domestic affairs, at home," she said, "appear to have found their way into the newspapers?"

"I grieve to say, it is so."
