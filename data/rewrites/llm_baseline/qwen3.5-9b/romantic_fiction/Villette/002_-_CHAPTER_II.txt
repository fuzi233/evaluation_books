Several days passed, and it became evident that she was unlikely to develop much affection for anyone in the household. She was not precisely naughty or willful; far from disobedient, yet no sight was more detrimental to comfort—or even tranquility—than the one she presented. She moped. No grown person could perform that cheerless duty with greater skill; no weather-beaten face of an adult exiled to the antipodes of Europe ever displayed the marks of homesickness more clearly than did her infant visage. She seemed to be aging prematurely, becoming unearthly in her demeanor.

As for me, Lucy Snowe, I claim innocence regarding that curse of an overheated, discursive imagination. Yet, whenever I opened a door to find her seated alone in a corner, her chin resting in her tiny hand, the room did not seem inhabited so much as haunted. Again, on moonlit nights when I woke to behold her figure, stark and conspicuous in her nightdress, kneeling upright in bed and praying with the fervor of some Catholic or Methodist enthusiast—a precocious fanatic or untimely saint—I confess I knew not what thoughts occupied my mind. They were hardly more rational or healthy than the child's own must have been. I rarely caught a word of her prayers, for they were whispered so low; sometimes, indeed, they were not spoken aloud at all, but offered silently within her heart. The few fragments that reached my ears still carried the single, crushing burden: "Papa; my dear Papa!"

I perceived this to be a one-idea'd nature, betraying that monomaniacal tendency I have always considered the most unfortunate affliction a human soul can bear. What might have been the outcome of this fretting, had it continued unchecked, remains mere conjecture. However, it received a sudden turn.

One afternoon, Mrs. Bretton, coaxing her from her usual station in the corner, lifted her onto the window-seat. To occupy her attention, she instructed the child to watch the passersby and count how many ladies would walk down the street within a set time. She sat listlessly, hardly looking, and counted nothing. Then—while my eye was fixed upon hers—I witnessed a startling transfiguration within her iris and pupil.

These sudden, dangerous natures, often labeled *sensitive*, offer many curious spectacles to those whose cooler tempers have spared them from participating in their angular vagaries. The fixed, heavy gaze swam, trembled, and then glittered with fire; the small, overcast brow cleared; the trivial, dejected features lit up; the sad countenance vanished, replaced instantly by eager anticipation. "It _is_!" were her words. Like a bird or a flying arrow, she was gone from the room.

How she managed to open the house door I could not tell; perhaps it stood ajar, or perhaps Warren was nearby and obeyed her impetuous command. I, watching calmly from the window, saw her—in her black frock and tiny braided apron (she harbored a distinct antipathy toward pinafores)—dart halfway down the street. Just as I was about to turn away and quietly inform Mrs. Bretton that the child had run out mad and required immediate pursuit, I saw her snatched up and whisked away from my cool observation and the wondering stares of the passersby. A gentleman had performed this good deed, covering her with his cloak and advancing to return her to the house from which he had seen her emerge.

I assumed he would leave her in a servant's care and withdraw, but instead, he ascended the stairs. His reception immediately clarified that he was known to Mrs. Bretton. She recognized him; she greeted him, yet she was fluttered, surprised, taken completely unawares. Her look and manner were even expostulatory. In reply to these, rather than her words, he said, "I could not help it, madam: I found it impossible to leave the country without seeing with my own eyes how she settled."

"But you will unsettle her."

"I hope not. And how is Papa's little Polly?"

He asked this as he sat down and placed her gently on the floor before him. "How is Polly's Papa?" was the reply, as she leaned against his knee and gazed up into his face.

It was not a noisy, wordy scene; for that, I was thankful. Yet it was a scene of feeling so brimful that, because the cup did not foam high or overflow furiously, it oppressed the observer all the more. On occasions of vehement, unrestrained emotional expansion, a sense of disdain or ridicule often provides relief to the weary spectator; whereas I have always found most burdensome that sort of sensibility which bends of its own free will, a giant slave under the sway of common sense.

Mr. Home was a stern-featured man—or perhaps I should say, a hard-featured one. His forehead was knotty, and his cheekbones were marked and prominent. The character of his face was distinctly Scotch, yet there was feeling in his eye and emotion in his now agitated countenance. His northern accent harmonized perfectly with his physiognomy. He was at once proud-looking and homely-looking. He laid his hand on the child's uplifted head. She said, "Kiss Polly." He kissed her.

I wished she would utter some hysterical cry so that I might find relief and feel at ease. She made wonderfully little noise; she seemed to have obtained everything she desired—all she wanted—and to be in a trance of content. In either mien or feature, this creature was unlike her sire, yet she was of his strain. Her mind had been filled from his, as a cup is filled from a flagon. Indisputably, Mr. Home possessed manly self-control, however he might secretly feel on certain matters.

"Polly," he said, looking down at his little girl, "go into the hall. You will see Papa's great-coat lying on a chair. Put your hand into the pockets; you will find a pocket-handkerchief there. Bring it to me."

She obeyed, went, and returned deftly and nimbly. He was conversing with Mrs. Bretton when she came back, and she waited with the handkerchief in her hand. It was a picture, in its own way, to see her, with her tiny stature and trim, neat shape, standing at his knee. Seeing that he continued to talk, seemingly unconscious of her return, she took his hand, opened his unresisting fingers, slipped the handkerchief inside, and closed them upon it one by one. He still seemed not to see or feel her; but by and by, he lifted her to his knee. She nestled against him, and though neither looked at nor spoke to the other for an hour following, I suppose both were satisfied.

During tea, the minute movements and behavior of this little thing gave, as usual, full occupation to the eye. First, she directed Warren as he arranged the chairs. "Put Papa's chair here, and mine near it, between Papa and Mrs. Bretton: _I_ must hand his tea." She took her seat and beckoned with her hand to her father. "Be near me, as if we were at home, Papa." And again, as she intercepted his cup in passing, stirred the sugar, and added the cream herself, "I always did it for you at home, Papa. Nobody could do it as well, not even your own self."

Throughout the meal, she continued her attentions; they were somewhat absurd. The sugar tongs were too wide for one of her hands, requiring her to use both to wield them; the weight of the silver cream ewer, the bread-and-butter plates, the very cup and saucer, taxed her insufficient strength and dexterity. Yet she would lift this, hand that, and luckily contrived to break nothing. Candidly speaking, I thought her a little busy-body; but her father, blind like other parents, seemed perfectly content to let her wait on him, and even wonderfully soothed by her offices.

"She is my comfort!" he could not help saying to Mrs. Bretton. That lady had her own "comfort" and nonpareil on a much larger scale, and, for the moment, absent; so she sympathized with his foible.

This second "comfort" came on the stage later in the evening. I knew this day had been fixed for his return and was aware that Mrs. Bretton had been expecting him throughout its hours. We were seated around the fire after tea when Graham joined our circle; I should rather say he broke it up—for, of course, his arrival created a bustle. Moreover, as Mr. Graham was fasting, refreshments had to be provided. He and Mr. Home met as old acquaintances; of the little girl, he took no notice for a time.

His meal over, and having answered numerous questions from his mother, he turned from the table to the hearth. Opposite where he had placed himself sat Mr. Home, and at his elbow, the child. When I say *child*, I use an inappropriate and undescriptive term—a phrase suggesting any picture rather than that of the demure little person in a mourning frock and white chemisette, who might just have fitted a good-sized doll. She was perched now on a high chair beside a stand, whereon rested her toy work-box of white varnished wood. Holding in her hands a shred of a handkerchief, she was professing to hem it, boring at it perseveringly with a needle that seemed almost a skewer in her fingers.
