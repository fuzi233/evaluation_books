After traversing a desolate expanse of rocky moorland, carpeted in dry vegetation and ancient moss, I finally spied a diminutive cottage nestled in the far distance. The sun hung suspended, shrouded in a pallid gray cloud, casting no warmth upon the heath. The landscape appeared frozen in an eternal chill, and the wind cut with a strange, biting ferocity, as though blowing from a realm where night is perpetual.

"Here we are at last!" croaked the raven beside me. "What a tedious journey! In half the time, I could have flown to Paradise to visit my cousin—the one who never returned to Noah! Alas, it feels almost like winter."

"Winter?" I exclaimed, startled. "It seems mere hours since we departed our home!"

"That is because our pace was swift," the raven replied. "In your world, you cannot simply sever the tether of gravity and allow the globe to spin beneath your feet. But this is my wife's dwelling. She is kind enough to harbor me, calling it the Sexton's Cottage."

"But where lies your churchyard? Your cemetery? Where do you inter the dead?" I asked, scanning the barren plain, finding no markers, no walls.

The raven extended his neck, holding his beak level, then rotated it slowly toward every point of the compass. He remained silent. I followed the direction of his beak with my gaze, and suddenly, the entire heath transformed. There was no church, no distinct graves—only a vast, open graveyard. Wherever the mournful wind swept, it marked the boundary of the raven's burial ground. He was the sexton of everything he surveyed, the lord of all things cast aside. I stood within the burial grounds of the universe itself; its boundaries defined by the uncultivated heath, its enclosing wall the low, starless gray horizon. I had left behind the cycles of spring, summer, and autumn, stepping directly into the winter that awaited me. I had begun this quest in the zenith of my youth, only to find myself already aged—but I was mistaken. Time flowed differently in that region; the seasons existed simultaneously. Winter slumbered throughout the night, wrapped in sheets of ice; at dawn, Spring awakened with a childlike smile; at noon, Summer blazed forth in resplendent glory; and as the afternoon waned, old Autumn crept in slowly, dying at the first breath of the ghostly, vapor-filled night.

As we approached the cottage, the clouded sun plummeted down the western slope, sinking completely just as we reached the doorstep. Instantly, a coldness seized me, tangible and suffocating, like the grasp of an icy death. I stumbled across the threshold, fighting to keep the door shut against the gale rising on the moor. Inside, silence fell abruptly.

A single candle flickered on a rough wooden table in the center of the room. My first sight was the lid of what I assumed to be a coffin propped against the wall. Yet, it swung open—it was a door—and a woman stepped through. She was clad entirely in white, as pure as fresh snow. Her face matched the whiteness of her gown, yet unlike snow, it radiated a profound warmth. Her features were flawless, but her eyes stole my breath. All vitality in her countenance converged in those eyes, transforming into light. It might have been the approach of death that rendered her face luminous, yet her eyes possessed a life force sufficient to sustain a nation—a vast, dark depth that seemed to deepen endlessly as I stared. Within each pupil, an entire night-sky was condensed; countless stars twinkled in the abyss, framed by an iris of eternal twilight. No human eye could truly comprehend such existence; these must have emerged directly from the divine. Her serene face suggested a primordial perfection, while her living eyes represented a continuous act of creation.

"Here is Mr. Vane, wife!" called the raven.

"He is welcome," she responded, her voice low, rich, and gentle, resonant with treasures of immortal sound.

I gazed at her, speechless.

"I knew you would be pleased to see him," the raven added.

She stood motionless by the doorway she had entered, refusing to advance further.

"Will he sleep?" she asked.

"I fear not," he replied. "He is neither weary nor burdened."

"Then why bring him here?"

"My fears suggest it may be premature," he admitted.

"I do not fully understand," I interjected, a sense of unease prickling my skin mixed with a faint hope for escape. "Surely a man must perform a day's labor first!"

My eyes drifted back to the woman's white visage, and my heart hammered against my ribs. She met my gaze in silence.

"Let me return home first," I pleaded, "and come again only after I have found, created, or at least discovered something!"

"He has not yet learned that the day commences with sleep!" the woman said, turning to her husband. "Tell him he must rest before he can accomplish anything."

"Men," the raven countered, "value their achievements so highly that they become trapped inside them. They cannot crack an egg without turning into the shell and lying down!"

His words pulled my attention away from the woman to the speaker. For a moment, I saw no bird, but rather the Librarian—the same slender, elderly figure in a tattered black coat, broad-bodied and long-tailed. I had previously viewed only his back; now, for the first time, I beheld his face. It was gaunt, revealing the skeletal structure beneath, hinting at the skulls his former profession required him to handle. Yet, in truth, I had never witnessed a face so vivid, a gaze so sharp and friendly. His pale blue eyes held a keen intelligence, softened by a hazy veil as if worn from endless weeping.

"You knew I was not a raven!" he said, smiling.

"I knew you were Mr. Raven," I replied, "yet somehow, I believed you to be a bird as well."

"What prompted that belief?"

"You looked like a raven, and I saw you digging worms from the earth with your beak."

"And then?"

"Tossing them into the air."

"And then?"

"They transformed into butterflies and flew away."

"Have you ever seen a raven do that? I told you I am a sexton!"

"Do sextons toss worms into the air, turning them into butterflies?"

"Yes."

"I have never seen one do it!"

"You saw me do it!—Yet I remain the Librarian in your house, for I was never dismissed, nor did I relinquish the post. Now, I serve as Librarian here as well."

"But you just stated you are the Sexton here!"

"So I am. It is largely the same vocation. With you, a true sexton deals with actual corpses; with me, books are merely dead bodies, and a library is nothing but a catacomb!"

"You confuse me!"

"That is perfectly acceptable!"

For a few moments, he stood in silence. The woman remained immovable, a statue beside the coffin-door.

"On occasion," the sexton continued eventually, "it is more expedient to present one's avian self. Everyone, as you should know, possesses various animal aspects—a beast-self, a bird-self, a foolish fish-self, yes, even a creeping serpent-self—which requires considerable effort to suppress! Indeed, one also harbors a tree-self, a crystal-self, and countless others—all requiring harmony. One can discern a man's nature by observing which creature most frequently emerges to the forefront."

Turning to his wife, he studied me more intently. He towered above average height, standing straighter than I remembered. Like his wife, his face was exceedingly pale; his nose elegantly housed the beak that had receded within it. His lips were thin, devoid of color, yet their curve was exquisite, trembling with a shadowy smile that blended humor, love, and pity.

"We require sustenance and refreshment, wife," he said. "We have traveled a great distance."

"You know, husband," she answered, "we can offer only to those who ask."

She turned her unchanging, radiant face toward me. "Please, Mrs. Raven," I said, "give me something to eat, and whatever you choose to quench my thirst."

"Your thirst must grow greater before you receive what will satisfy it," she replied. "However, whatever I can provide, I shall gladly give."

She moved to a cupboard embedded in the wall, retrieved bread and wine, and placed them upon the table. We seated ourselves for a perfect repast. As I ate, the provisions seemed to penetrate deeper than mere physical hunger or thirst. Anxiety and discomfort dissolved, replaced by anticipation. Overwhelmed by a sudden drowsiness, I felt weariness settle upon me for the first time.

"I have earned neither food nor sleep, Mrs. Raven," I confessed. "Yet you have granted the former freely, and now I hope you will grant the latter, for I desperately need it."

"Sleep is too precious a gift to be earned," the sexton remarked. "It must be given and accepted, for it is a necessity. However, using this house merely as a halfway inn—for a night's rest alone—would be perilous."
