An ancient English cathedral tower? How could such a structure possibly exist here? The familiar, massive gray square tower of the old cathedral stood right before him. Yet, there was no intervening space, no clear line of sight from the real world to connect the observer with the spire. What spike pierced the air between his eye and the impossible tower? Who had erected it?

Perhaps it was a command from the Sultan, intended for the impalement of a horde of Turkish robbers, one by one. Indeed, it seemed so: cymbals clashed in a rhythmic frenzy, and the Sultan passed toward his palace in a grand procession. Ten thousand scimitars flashed in the sunlight, while thirty thousand dancing girls scattered petals across the ground. Behind them followed white elephants, adorned in countless vibrant colors, accompanied by an endless retinue of servants.

Yet, despite this exotic spectacle, the cathedral tower rose stubbornly in the background, occupying a place where it should not be. And yet, no writhing body hung from the grim spike.

Wait. Was the spike merely a low, rusty nail atop the post of an old bedstead that had collapsed under the weight of its occupants? Perhaps a moment of drowsy laughter was required to entertain this notion.

Shuddering from head to toe, the man whose fragmented consciousness had assembled such fantastical images finally sat up. Supporting his trembling frame with his arms, he looked around. He found himself in the most squalid and cramped of small rooms. Through a ragged window curtain, the pale light of early dawn filtered in from a miserable courtyard.

He lay dressed across a large, unsightly bed, which had indeed buckled under the burden resting upon it. Also lying across the bed, not lengthwise but sprawled out, were a Chinese man, a Lascar sailor, and a gaunt woman. The first two appeared to be in a deep stupor; the latter was blowing into a makeshift pipe to ignite it. As she blew, shielding the flame with her thin hand, the red spark served as a dim lantern, revealing her features in the morning gloom.

"Another?" she asked in a querulous, rattling whisper. "Would you like another?"

He looked about, pressing a hand to his forehead.

She handed him the nearly empty pipe and sank back, turning onto her face.

He noted that the woman had smoked herself into a grotesque resemblance of the Chinese man. The contours of his cheek, eye, and temple, along with his complexion, were mirrored in hers. He bent his ear close to listen to her muttering.

"Incomprehensible!"

As he watched the spasmodic twitches darting across her face and limbs, like fitful lightning flashing from a dark sky, some contagion seized him. For reasons that this tale will reveal in due course, the ancient cathedral town requires a fictional designation. Let us call it Cloisterham. It likely bore different names for the Druids, the Romans, the Saxons, and the Normans, but over centuries, a name holds little weight against the dust of history. Cloisterham is an ancient city, ill-suited for anyone craving the noise of the modern world. It is a monotonous, silent place, imbued with an earthy flavor by its cathedral crypt and overrun with traces of monastic graves. Here, children play "salad" in the dust of abbots and abbesses, fashioning dirt pies from nuns and friars. In the surrounding fields, every plowman pays tribute to former Lords Treasurers, Archbishops, and Bishops, grinding their bones into bread just as the ogre in fairy tales ground the bones of his unwanted guests.

Cloisterham is a drowsy city, inhabited by people who strangely believe that all its changes belong to the past and that none remain for the future—a peculiar moral inherited from antiquities older than recorded history. Its streets are so silent (yet prone to echoing the slightest provocation) that on a summer day, the shop blinds barely flutter in the southern breeze. Sun-browned tramps hurrying through the town quicken their pace merely to escape the confines of its oppressive respectability. Escaping is easy, however, for Cloisterham consists of little more than one narrow artery leading in and out; the remainder are disappointing yards with pumps and no throughfares, save for the Cathedral Close and a paved Quaker settlement that resembles a Quakeress's bonnet tucked away in a shady corner.

In essence, Cloisterham is a city frozen in a bygone era. With its hoarse cathedral bell, the cawing rooks circling the tower, and the even hoarser birds nesting deep below, fragments of old walls, chapels, chapter-houses, and monasteries have been clumsily incorporated into its modern homes and gardens, much like conflicting ideas clutter the minds of its citizens. Everything here belongs to the past. Even the sole pawnbroker accepts no pledges anymore, vainly offering an unredeemed stock of dim, pale antique watches sweating in the heat, tarnished sugar tongs with useless legs, and grim volumes of forgotten books. The only signs of vibrant life in Cloisterham are the flourishing vegetable gardens; even its drooping, despairing little theater maintains a patch of soil, welcoming the devil when he flees the stage into the underworld, hiding among scarlet beans or oyster shells depending on the season.

Dominating the center of Cloisterham stands the Nuns' House, a venerable brick structure whose current name likely stems from legends of its conventual past. On the gate enclosing its courtyard shines a brilliant brass plate reading: *Seminary for Young Ladies. Miss Twinkleton.* The house front is so weathered and the plate so glaringly new that it reminds visitors of a battered old beau wearing a modern monocle in his blind eye. One wonders if the original nuns, being of a submissive rather than stubborn breed, bowed their heads to avoid hitting the low ceilings; if they sat in long, low windows counting beads for penance rather than jewelry; or if some were ever walled alive in the building's odd angles for possessing too much of the restless spirit of Mother Nature. These mysteries haunt the ghosts (if any exist) but do not appear in Miss Twinkleton's half-yearly accounts. Neither the lady running the poetry department nor her staff concerns themselves with such unprofitable inquiries.

Like those suffering from alcoholism or mesmerism, Miss Twinkleton possesses two distinct states of consciousness that never collide. By day, she is the stern seminary mistress. But every night, once the students retire, she smooths her curls, brightens her eyes, and transforms into a sprightly version unknown to the girls. In this nocturnal phase, she resumes conversations from the previous night, discussing Cloisterham's tender scandals and referencing "The Wells"—a euphemism for Tunbridge Wells—where a certain "Foolish Mr. Porters" once declared his undying love. Miss Twinkleton knows absolutely nothing of this during the day. Her constant companion in both moods is Mrs. Tisher, a deferential widow with a weak back, a chronic sigh, and a suppressed voice, tasked with managing the wardrobes and leading the girls to believe she has seen better days. Servants whisper that the late Mrs. Tisher was a hairdresser, perhaps explaining her adaptability.

The favorite pupil of the Nuns' House is Miss Rosa Bud, universally known as "Rosebud." She is wonderfully pretty, wonderfully childish, and wonderfully whimsical. An awkward, romantic interest surrounds her, for it is common knowledge that a husband has been chosen for her by will, binding her guardian to marry her upon reaching majority. During the day, Miss Twinkleton feigns sorrow over this fate, shaking her head behind Rosebud's dimpled shoulders, but to no avail. The students simply unite in declaring her a "pretending old thing." The house is never more agitated than when this destined suitor arrives. It is understood that he has a legal right to visit, and if Miss Twinkleton dared dispute it, she would be instantly removed. When his bell rings, every student peers out a window; every girl "practicing" plays out of tune; and the French class descends into chaos, marking time as frantically as bottles clinking at a party.

Mr. Sapsea "dresses at" the Dean's residence; he has been formally introduced to the Dean in error; and he has even been addressed in the street as "My Lord," under the mistaken belief that he was the Bishop descending unexpectedly, sans chaplain. Mr. Sapsea boasts immensely of these honors, alongside his voice and his manner. Indeed, in the sale of landed estates, he has attempted the experiment of slightly chanting in his pulpit to approximate what he considers the authentic ecclesiastical tone. Consequently, when concluding a public auction, Mr. Sapsea finishes with the air of bestowing a benediction upon the gathered brokers, leaving the actual Dean—a modest and upright gentleman—looking decidedly inferior by comparison.

Mr. Sapsea enjoys numerous admirers. Indeed, the motion is passed by a significant local majority, even among those skeptical of his wisdom, that he constitutes a credit to Cloisterham. He possesses the magnificent qualities of being portentous and dull, possessing a rolling cadence in his speech and a corresponding roll in his walk; not to mention a certain grave, flowing gesture with his hands, suggesting he is perpetually on the verge of confirming the individual with whom he is conversing. Standing nearly sixty rather than fifty, with a flowing silhouette of belly and horizontal creases across his waistcoat, and reputed to be wealthy, he votes at elections in the strictly respectable interest. Morally satisfied that nothing has grown since his infancy except himself, how can the dim-witted Mr. Sapsea fail to be a credit to Cloisterham and society?

Mr. Sapsea's offices are located on the High Street, directly opposite the Nuns' House. They date from roughly the same period as the convent, irregularly modernized here and there as successive generations, increasingly preferring fresh air and light to fever and plague, allowed the building to steadily deteriorate. Above the doorway hangs a wooden effigy, approximately half-life-size, depicting Mr. Sapsea's father in a curly wig and toga, in the act of conducting a sale. The purity of the concept, and the natural appearance of the little finger, hammer, and pulpit, have been widely admired.

It was Christmas Eve in Cloisterham. The streets held a peculiar cast of characters: a scattering of strangers, alongside others whose faces were half-familiar, once the children of the town, now grown men and women returning from distant lands only to discover the city had shrunk perceptibly since their childhood, as if it had failed to wash itself clean in the intervening years. The season's tokens were everywhere. He lacks the true secret of mixing, though he charges as much as I do, and more if he can. Here is a match, but where is the candle? If my cough takes hold, I shall burn twenty matches before finding a flame."

She eventually located the candle and lit it, but the success triggered a seizure. Seizing her in the moment of victory, she sat rocking back and forth, gasping intermittently, "Oh, my lungs are terrible! My lungs are worn away to cabbage-nets!" until the fit subsided. Deprived of sight during the convulsion, she strained her eyes as the attack faded. Regaining her ability to speak, she stared in shock, "Why, it's you!"

"Are you so surprised to see me?"

"I thought I would never see you again, deary. I thought you were dead and gone to Heaven."

"Why?"

"I didn't suppose you could keep away, alive, so long from the poor old soul who knows the real recipe for mixing it. And you are in mourning! Why didn't you come for a pipe of comfort? Did they leave you money, perhaps, so you didn't need comfort?"

"No."

"Who died, deary?"

"A relative."

"Died of what, lovey?"

"Probably, Death."

"We are short tonight!" cried the woman with a propitiatory laugh. "Short and snappish we are! But we are out of sorts for lack of a smoke. We've got the 'all-overs,' haven't we, deary? But this is the place to cure them; this is where the 'all-overs' are smoked off."

"You may prepare as soon as you like," the visitor replied.

He removed his shoes, loosened his cravat, and lay across the foot of the squalid bed, his head resting on his left hand.

"Now you begin to look like yourself," the woman approved. "Now I truly recognize my old customer! Have you been trying to mix for yourself this whole time, poppet?"

"I have been taking it now and then in my own way."

"Never take it your own way. It isn't good for trade, and it isn't good for you. Where is my ink-bottle, and where is my thimble, and where is my little spoon? He intends to take it in an artful form now, my deary dear!"

As she began her process, bubbling and blowing at the faint spark trapped in the hollow of her hands, she spoke in tones of snuffling satisfaction without ceasing. When he spoke, he did so without looking at her, his thoughts already drifting elsewhere.

"I've prepared quite a few smokes for you, first and last, haven't I, chuckey?"

"A good many."

"When you first arrived, you were quite new to it, weren't you?"

"Yes, I was easily disposed of then."

"But you rose in the world and soon took your pipe with the best of them, didn't you?"

"Ah; and the worst."

"It is ready for you. What a sweet singer you were when you first came! Used to drop your head and sing yourself off like a bird! It is ready for you now, deary."

He accepted the pipe with great care and placed the mouthpiece to his lips. She seated herself beside him, ready to refill it. After inhaling several whiffs in silence, he questioned dubiously, "Is it as potent as it used to be?"

"What do you speak of, deary?"

"What should I speak of, but what I have in my mouth?"

"It is exactly the same. Always the identical same."

"It doesn't taste so. And it is slower."

"You have become accustomed to it, you see."

"That may be the cause, certainly. Look here." He paused, became dreamy, and seemed to forget he had invited her attention. She bent over him, speaking in his ear.

"I am attending to you. You said just now, 'Look here.' I say now, I am attending to you. We were discussing your becoming accustomed to it."

"I know all that. I was merely thinking. Look here. Suppose you had something in your mind; something you intended to do."

"Yes, deary; something I was going to do?"

"But hadn't quite decided to do."

"Yes, deary."

"Might or might not do, you understand."

"Yes." With the point of a needle, she stirred the contents of the bowl.

"Would you do it in your fancy while lying here smoking?"

She nodded. "Over and over again."

"Just like me! I did it over and over again. I have performed it hundreds of thousands of times in this room."

"It is to be hoped it was pleasant to do, deary."

"It *was* pleasant to do!"

He uttered this with a savage air and a sharp start at her. Unmoved, she retouched and replenished the bowl with her small spatula. Observing her intent focus, he sank back into his previous posture.

"It was a journey, a difficult and dangerous journey. That was the subject in my mind. A hazardous and perilous journey over abysses where a slip meant destruction. Look down, look down! Do you see what lies at the bottom there?"
