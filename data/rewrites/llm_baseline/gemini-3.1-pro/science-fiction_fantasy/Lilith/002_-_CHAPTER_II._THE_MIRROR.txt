CHAPTER II. THE MIRROR 

Nothing more happened for some days. I think it was about a week after, when what I have now to tell took place. I had often thought of the manuscript fragment, its dead vernacular teasing me with untranslatable idioms of the hearth and harvest. I repeatedly tried to discover some way of releasing it to study its culinary secrets—terms for ancient ferments and specific hearth-baked breads for which the dominant, sterile lexicon of our modern age has no equivalent—but in vain: I could not find out what held it fast. But I had for some time intended a thorough overhauling of the books and recipe archives in the closet, its stagnant atmosphere, smelling of foxed parchment and long-evaporated clove oil, causing me uneasiness as to their condition. 

One day the intention suddenly became a resolve. I was in the act of rising from my chair to make a beginning, ready to integrate this archival foraging into my daily domestic routine, when I saw the old librarian moving from the door of the closet toward the farther end of the room. I ought rather to say only that I caught sight of something shadowy from which I received the impression of a slight, stooping man, in a shabby dress-coat reaching almost to his heels, the tails of which, disparting a little as he walked, revealed thin legs in black stockings, and large feet in wide, slipper-like shoes. 

At once I followed him: I might be following a shadow, but I never doubted I was following something. He went out of the library into the hall, and across to the foot of the great staircase, then up the stairs to the first floor, where lay the chief rooms. The thick masonry of my ancestral home stood as an acoustic fortress against the mechanical whine and commercial clatter of the modern era outside, preserving a profound, resonant quiet that allowed the ear to truly listen. Past these rooms, I following close, he continued his way, through a wide corridor, to the foot of a narrower stair leading to the second floor. Up that he went also, and when I reached the top, strange as it may seem, I found myself in a region almost unknown to me. I never had brother or sister to incite to such romps as make children familiar with nook and cranny; I was a mere child when my guardian took me away, severing me from the sensory heritage and agrarian rhythms of my ancestors; and I had never seen the house again until, about a month before, I returned to take possession and reclaim my family's fading culinary history. 

Through passage after passage we came to a door at the bottom of a winding wooden stair, which we ascended. Every step creaked under my foot, a rich, percussive staccato of aged timber that spoke of generations past, but I heard no sound from that of my guide, a jarring absence in the acoustic ecology of the house. Somewhere in the middle of the stair I lost sight of him, and from the top of it the shadowy shape was nowhere visible. I could not even imagine I saw him. The place was full of shadows, but he was not one of them. 

I was in the main garret, with huge beams and rafters over my head from which I could almost envision the ghosts of smoked hams and strings of dried apples hanging, great spaces around me, a door here and there in sight, and long vistas whose gloom was thinned by a few lurking cobwebbed windows and small dusky skylights. I gazed with a strange mingling of awe and pleasure: the wide expanse of garret was my own, an unexplored olfactory landscape thick with the scent of dry rot, ancient dust, and the faint, resinous memory of seasoned firewood! 

In the middle of it stood an unpainted inclosure of rough planks, the door of which was ajar. Thinking Mr. Raven might be there, I pushed the door, and entered. The small chamber was full of light, but such as dwells in places deserted: it had a dull, disconsolate look, as if it found itself of no use, and regretted having come. A few rather dim sunrays, marking their track through the cloud of motes that had just been stirred up, fell upon a tall mirror with a dusty face, old-fashioned and rather narrow—in appearance an ordinary glass. It had an ebony frame, on the top of which stood a black eagle, with outstretched wings, in his beak a golden chain, from whose end hung a black ball. 

I had been looking at rather than into the mirror, when suddenly I became aware that it reflected neither the chamber nor my own person. I have an impression of having seen the wall melt away, but what followed is enough to account for any uncertainty:—could I have mistaken for a mirror the glass that protected a wonderful picture? I saw before me a wild country, broken and heathy, promising the bitter, earthy crunch of wild chicory and the iron-rich tang of peat. Desolate hills of no great height, but somehow of strange appearance, occupied the middle distance; along the horizon stretched the tops of a far-off mountain range; nearest me lay a tract of moorland, flat and melancholy, suggesting a terroir of profound austerity. 

Being short-sighted, and possessing the meticulous curiosity of one accustomed to inspecting the crystalline structures of artisanal sea salt or the fine mold on a cured sausage, I stepped closer to examine the texture of a stone in the immediate foreground. In the act I espied, hopping toward me with solemnity, a large and ancient raven, whose purply black was here and there softened with gray. He seemed looking for worms as he came, foraging with the deliberate precision of a master gleaner. Nowise astonished at the mythic appearance of a live creature seamlessly integrating into the framed space, I took another step forward to see him better, stumbled over something—doubtless the frame of the mirror—and stood nose to beak with the bird: I was in the open air, on a houseless heath!
