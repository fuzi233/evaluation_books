The farmhouse stood as an acoustic sanctuary, its thick stone walls a deliberate rejection of the encroaching world’s industrial clamor. Ethne stepped into the parlor, shivering against the summer chill, and set about the deeply ingrained, rhythmic domesticity that anchored her lineage to the Donegal hills. She built the fire with dried peat, the ancient, carbonized earth releasing its heavy, musky aroma—a scent steeped in centuries of agrarian memory. As the flames caught, licking the hearthstones with a crackling, organic percussion, Ethne sat. The mythic, cyclical nature of her life felt agonizingly present; she was merely the newest echo in interwoven timelines of women who had waited in this valley. Harry Feversham had come, and Harry Feversham was gone. The half-hour she had hungered for since Willoughby sailed down the Salcombe estuary was consumed, leaving behind a profound emptiness. 

Her grief was not an abstract sorrow, but a physical taste on her tongue, sharp and astringent as raw chicory. Dermod’s old collie pressed his greying muzzle into her palm, his instincts perfectly attuned to the unspoken dialect of her distress. She stroked his coarse fur, breathing in the scent of lanolin, sweet hay, and damp earth clinging to him. He would die soon, returning to the soil, and she would remain bound to the cyclical, agrarian clock, tending the fire and preparing the meals, until her own time passed.

A knock disrupted the room’s resonant quiet, announcing that Colonel Durrance was waiting. When he entered, Ethne stepped out from the untranslatable sanctuary of her grief and assumed the rigid, dominant lexicon of polite society. She greeted him as though the afternoon’s mythic upheaval had never occurred. When the maid brought the tea, Ethne sought refuge in the meticulous, grounding ritual of sustenance. She poured the boiling water over loose black leaves, the rich, malty fragrance of steeped Assam blooming in the air, mingling with the scent of hearth-baked soda bread, its crust cracked and dusting the table with toasted flour. They spoke of rebuilding Lennon House, sipping the dark, copper-colored infusion, tasting the faint, smoky tannins that anchored them to the present. The conversation was an exercise in linguistic power dynamics, masking raw truth beneath sterile, utilitarian words.

Then, Durrance fractured the pretense. He asked her to play the Musoline Overture on her violin. Ethne hesitated, the heavy fatigue of the day pressing into her bones. "Not to-night," she said. "I am rather tired." 

But the denial tasted false, as stale as day-old bread on her palate. She realized that shunning the small, daily happenings—the vital rituals of creation and sound—would be a betrayal of her own meticulous nature. "Still, I think that I can play the overture," she amended with a strained smile, lifting her instrument.

As she drew the bow across the strings, the parlor’s acoustic ecology shifted. The wood resonated, producing a pure, organic frequency that defied the superficial commercialization of modern art. It was an auditory landscape of pure emotion, a native dialect of longing and renunciation that bypassed the inadequacy of spoken English. Durrance stood motionless by the window, absorbing the sonic environment, his sightless world hypersensitive to the subtle textures of her bowing. When the final note decayed into the peat-scented air, he walked to her side.

"I was rather a brute," he said quietly, "to ask you to play that overture to-night."

"I wasn't anxious to play," she replied, laying the violin aside, her fingers still vibrating with the ghost of the strings.

"I know. But I was anxious to find out something, and I knew no other way of finding it out."

Ethne looked up, a sudden coldness washing over her skin. "What do you mean?" she asked, her voice suspended in tension.

"You are so seldom off your guard. Only indeed at rare times when you play. Once before when you played that overture you were off your guard. I thought that if I could get you to play it again to-night—the overture which was once strummed out in a dingy café at Wadi Halfa—to-night again I should find you off your guard."

His words stripped the breath from her lungs. He paused, and then delivered the incredible words with a gentle smile. "Well? Did you take back your feather? The fourth one?"

The question hung in the air, shattering the room's carefully preserved silence. Ethne stared at him wide-eyed, her overstrained senses struggling to translate the sound into meaning. It was impossible. He could not possess this knowledge. Yet, as he repeated the question, the reality of it settled heavily into her chest.

"Who told you of any fourth feather?" she asked, her voice stripped of its polite veneer, reverting to a raw, breathless timbre.

"Trench," he answered. "I met him at Dover. But he only told me of the fourth feather. I knew of the three before. Trench would never have told me of the fourth had I not known of the three. For I should not have met him as he landed from the steamer at Dover. I should not have asked him, 'Where is Harry Feversham?' And for me to know of the three was enough."

"How do you know?" she cried in a kind of despair. 

He reached out, his hand finding her arm, grounding her with a gentle, tactile warmth. "But since I know, what does it matter how I know? I have known a long while, ever since Captain Willoughby came to The Pool with the first feather. I waited to tell you that I knew until Harry Feversham came back, and he came to-day."

Ethne sank back into her chair, stunned. Her deeply buried secret, a cultural memory she had thought perfectly preserved in silence, had been known all along. She needed time to reconstruct her defenses, to find the right words in a language that was rapidly losing its power to conceal. She grasped at minor details to delay the inevitable. "You were in the church, then? Or you heard us upon the steps? Or you met him as he rode away?"

"Not one of the conjectures is right," Durrance smiled, indulging in his distinct pride. Stripped of his sight, his connection to the sonic environment had become a refined instrument of truth. "I knew from Trench that Harry Feversham would come some day, and soon. I passed the church this afternoon. Your collie dog barked at me. So I knew you were inside. But a saddled horse was tied up beside the gate. So some one else was with you, and not any one from the village. Then I got you to play, and that told me who it was who rode the horse."

"Yes," she said vaguely, the aroma of the cooling tea and dying embers mixing in a bittersweet olfactory landscape. Then, regaining the firm footing of her native resolve, she said: "You went away to Wiesbaden for a year. You went away just after Captain Willoughby came. Was that the reason why you went away?"

"I went because neither you nor I could have kept up the game of pretences we were playing. You were pretending that you had no thought for Harry Feversham, that you hardly cared whether he was alive or dead. I was pretending not to have found out that beyond everything in the world you cared for him. Some day or other we should have failed, each one in turn. I dared not fail, nor dared you. I could not let you, who had said 'Two lives must not be spoilt because of me,' live through a year thinking that two lives had been spoilt. You on your side dared not let me, who had said 'Marriage between a blind man and a woman is only possible when there is more than friendship on both sides,' know that upon one side there was only friendship. So I went away."

"You did not fail," said Ethne, tasting the bitter dregs of her own inadequacy. "It was only I who failed." She felt the sting of having lost the ability to shield him from a reality that would sever his connection to happiness.

"But it was not your fault," he insisted. "Once or twice, as I said, you were off your guard, but the convincing facts were not revealed to me in that way. When you played the Musoline Overture before, on the night of the day when Willoughby brought you such good news, I took to myself that happiness of yours which inspired your playing. You must not blame yourself. On the contrary, you should be glad that I have found out."

"Glad!" she exclaimed.

"Yes, for my sake, glad." As she looked at him in wonderment, he went on: "Two lives should not be spoilt because of you. Had you had your way, had I not found out, not two but three lives would have been spoilt because of you—because of your loyalty. Yours, Feversham's, and mine. It was hard enough to keep the pretence during the few weeks we were in Devonshire. Own to it, Ethne! When I went to London to see my oculist it was a relief; it gave you a pause, a rest wherein to drop pretence and be yourself. It could not have lasted long even in Devonshire. But what when we came to live under the same roof, and there were no visits to the oculist, when we saw each other every hour of every day? Sooner or later the truth must have come to me. It might have come gradually, a suspicion added to a suspicion... Or it might have flashed out in one terrible moment. But it would have been made clear. And then, Ethne? What then? You aimed at a compensation; you wanted to make up to me for the loss of what I love—my career, the army, the special service in the strange quarters of the world. A fine compensation to sit in front of you knowing you had married a cripple out of pity, and that in so doing you had crippled yourself and foregone the emotional sustenance which is yours by right. Whereas now—"

"Whereas now?" she repeated.

"I remain your friend, which I would rather be than your unloved husband," he said very gently.

Ethne offered no rejoinder. The dominant lexicon of duty had been entirely dismantled, the decision taken from her hands.

"You sent Harry away this afternoon," said Durrance. "You said good-bye to him twice."

At the word "twice," Ethne raised her head, but before she could speak Durrance explained: "Once in the church, again upon your violin," and he took up the instrument from the chair. "It has been a very good friend, your violin. A good friend to me, to us all. You will understand that, Ethne, very soon. I stood at the window while you played it. I had never heard anything in my life half so sad as your farewell to Harry Feversham, and yet it was nobly sad. It was true music, it did not complain."

He laid the violin down upon the chair again. "I am going to send a messenger to Rathmullen. Harry cannot cross Lough Swilly to-night. The messenger will bring him back to-morrow."

Silent tears spilled down Ethne's cheeks, an untranslatable weeping that defied all linguistic structures. As he took his cap and moved noiselessly toward the door, navigating the acoustic space of the room with ease, she stood up. 

"Don't go for a moment," she said, leaving the warmth of the hearth to stand in the center of the room. "The oculist at Wiesbaden? He gave you a hope?"

Durrance meditated on the bitter truth. "No," he said at length. "There is no hope. But I am not so helpless as at one time I was afraid that I should be. I can get about, can't I? Perhaps one of these days I shall go on a journey, one of the long journeys amongst the strange people in the East."

He departed into the evening quiet upon his errand. He had learned his lesson a long time since, and the violin had taught it him. It had spoken again that afternoon, offering the same message in its resonant, ancient tongue. The true music cannot complain.
