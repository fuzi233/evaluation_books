“I’m returning the cheque you sent for my work on the Manderson case,” Trent wrote to Sir James Molloy from Munich, where he had arrived immediately after submitting a brief, unremarkable report to the *Record* office. “What I did wasn’t worth a tenth of what you offered. But I wouldn’t hesitate to keep it—if only I hadn’t decided, for reasons I won’t explain, to take no payment at all for this matter. If you don’t mind, please pay for the space at your usual rate and forward the money to a charity that doesn’t thrive on bullying people—assuming such an institution exists. I’ve come here to visit old friends and sort out my thoughts. And the clearest thought that emerges is this: I want something active to do. I can’t paint—not even a fence. Would you consider me as your own correspondent? If you can find me a good assignment, I’ll send you solid reports. After that, I might settle down and work properly.”

Sir James replied by telegram: proceed at once to Kurland and Livonia, where Citizen Browning was stirring up rebellion again, and both town and countryside were ablaze with revolt.

It was a wandering commission, and for two months Trent followed his luck—and it served him well enough. He was the only journalist to witness General Dragilew’s assassination in the streets of Volmar, carried out by an eighteen-year-old girl. He saw buildings burn, men hanged, mobs lynch, and soldiers open fire without mercy. Each day deepened his disgust for the absurdity bred by tyranny. Many nights he slept in danger. Many days he went without food. Yet every morning and evening, he saw her—the woman he loved beyond reason, beyond hope.

He found himself strangely proud of how deeply and stubbornly this feeling held him. It fascinated him as a phenomenon: not merely emotion, but a force that defied logic. It amazed and illuminated him. Nothing like this had ever touched him before. At thirty-two, he was no stranger to passion or longing, but what he’d known came naturally, unforced, untouched by bitterness. He had never been broken by love; yet he remained haunted by its mysteries. He moved through life with quiet respect for certain feminine vulnerabilities, and a profound unease before others’ strength. He had clung to a half-hearted belief that something deeper still waited within him, waiting for the right voice to call it forth—when it would come, if at all, not by seeking, but by fate.

But he hadn’t expected that if the moment came, it might arrive wrapped in darkness.

The two things that stunned him about his feelings for Mabel Manderson were their suddenness and their futility. Before they took hold, he had laughed at the idea of enduring, unrequited love—as if it were some noble boyish fantasy. Now he knew better. And he lived daily in the pain of knowing it.

In his imagination, she always appeared as he first saw her—on the cliffside, unaware, caught in a gesture of wild joy, arms raised in release. That motion had spoken louder than words: her widowhood was freedom, escape from torment. And it confirmed, with terrible clarity, a suspicion already forming in his mind—that her liberation meant happiness with another man. The man she loved.

He couldn’t pinpoint exactly when he’d first suspected this. But the seed must have been planted during his first meeting with Marlowe—his presence, his bearing, the grace and power in him, could easily have captivated any woman not bound by duty. Coupled with Mr. Cupples’ account of the Mandersons’ marriage, the idea had taken root in the silent corners of his mind.

When he began searching for motive after identifying the murderer, he turned away from that grim possibility: that Marlowe—driven by passion, perhaps aware of her suffering—had acted like Bothwell, taking the most damning page from history. But in all his inquiries, in all his later reflections, he found no evidence that could justify such a crime—except one temptation, whose full weight he could not grasp, though he knew it would press hard upon a bold soul whose conscience had somehow been numbed.

If he trusted his judgment, Marlowe was neither mad nor inherently cruel. But that didn’t absolve him. Murder for love was not unknown. Heaven knew! Though modern society, with its comforts and fear of detection, had made such crimes rare among the privileged, they were far from impossible. All it took was a man of courage and intelligence, blinded by obsession, to plan and execute such a deed.

A thousand times, heart heavy with anguish, Trent tried to dismiss the terrifying notion that Mabel Manderson had known of the plot against her husband. That she knew the truth afterward—he could not doubt it. Her collapse when questioned about Marlowe had shattered any lingering hope that there was no bond between them. Worse, it seemed to speak of fear—fear of exposure.

She had read his manuscript, then destroyed it. She had taken his word to protect the secret that threatened her lover’s life.

But the real horror was whether she had known before—whether she had suspected, guessed, even consented. Had she connived?

He could never forget that his first clue to Marlowe’s motive had been his escape route—through her room. At the time, before he’d seen her, he’d considered her complicity likely. He imagined a passionate, unstable woman—ruthless in love and hate—a willing partner, perhaps even the mastermind behind the crime.

Then he met her. Spoke with her. Helped her in her grief. And those suspicions vanished—replaced by shame at their cruelty. He had seen her eyes, heard her voice, felt the atmosphere around her. Trent was one of those who believe they can smell true evil in the air. In her presence, he felt an inner certainty: she was fundamentally good.

That she had briefly given herself over to relief on the cliff—relief from years of stifled sympathy and unfulfilled motherhood—was not a sign of guilt. That she had turned to Marlowe in her loneliness, he believed. But that she had known of his murderous intent—he refused to believe it.

Yet the doubts returned, relentless. He remembered how close Marlowe had been to her—how he prepared in the dead man’s bedroom, just steps from her chamber; how he fled through her window. Had he risked telling her? Or, more likely, played his part while she slept?

He doubted she knew of the disguise when she testified at the inquest—her words sounded honest. But the question refused to die: had she lain awake, listening for footsteps, waiting for a whisper that would tell her it was done?

Could such purity of face and manner hide a heart as black as night—cold, cunning, ruthless?

These thoughts clung to him when he was alone.

Trent worked diligently for Sir James, earning his salary handsomely over six months. Then he returned to Paris, where he resumed his life with renewed vigor. His talents had returned, and he found himself living more contentedly than he had dared expect, surrounded by an odd assortment of friends—French, English, American—artists, poets, journalists, policemen, hoteliers, soldiers, lawyers, merchants, and others. His natural gift for empathy earned him, as it had in his student days, access to circles rarely open to foreigners. He was welcomed into a French family’s home again—just as he had been before. The house was familiar, even down to the wallpaper patterns and furniture. But the young artists of today, _les jeunes_, were different. Shallow, childish, less clever than their predecessors. Their so-called revelations about art and life were trivial compared to the bold truths once claimed by earlier generations.

He believed this until one afternoon, sitting beside a well-fed man at a restaurant. Despite the years of indulgence, Trent recognized him instantly—one of the original _jeunes_, once known as the Hermits of the New Parnassus. They had declared poetry free, talked loudly outside cafés, defied convention. This man now sat in the Ministry of the Interior, decorated and powerful. He told Trent France needed a strong hand. He quoted exact sums paid for betrayals Trent had never heard of.

And then it struck him: it wasn’t _them_ who had changed. It was he. Like his former comrade, he had altered. The world remained the same. Only his vision had dimmed.

Still, he couldn’t say precisely what he had lost—unless it was simply his spirit.

One June morning, descending the Rue des Martyrs, he saw a figure approaching. He glanced around—uncomfortable at the thought of meeting Mr. Bunner again. For some time, he had felt his wound healing under the discipline of work. He thought of Mabel less often, and with less pain. He didn’t want those three days reopened.

But the narrow street offered no escape. Bunner spotted him almost at once.

His easy warmth embarrassed Trent—he had liked the man. They sat long over lunch. Bunner spoke freely, and Trent listened—genuinely pleased, occasionally adding a comment. He enjoyed Bunner’s conversation, with its endless verbal twists, for its own sake.

Bunner was now the chief Continental agent for the Manderson firm, and thoroughly satisfied with his role. After twenty minutes of business talk, he mentioned that Marlowe had joined his father’s company shortly after Manderson’s death. The business was thriving, and Marlowe was already in control. They remained close friends, planning a summer holiday together.

“Jack Marlowe has a natural big head,” Bunner said, “and if he had more experience, I wouldn’t want to face him. He’d beat me every time.”

As Bunner continued, Trent grew increasingly puzzled. Something was wrong. There was no mention of the central figure—Mabel.

Then Bunner added that Marlowe was engaged to an Irish girl, whom he praised enthusiastically.

Trent clenched his hands beneath the table.

What had happened?

His thoughts tumbled. He forced himself to ask directly.

Bunner knew little. Mrs. Manderson had left England soon after settling her husband’s affairs. She’d lived in Italy for a while, then returned recently to London. She had sold the Mayfair house and bought a smaller one in Hampstead, and another in the country. She avoided society.

“And all that money—good hard dollars—just waiting for someone to spend it,” Bunner said, with a touch of sadness. “She’s got money to burn—money to feed birds—and nothing doing. The old man left her more than half his fortune. And think of the impression she could make. She’s beautiful, and the best woman I’ve ever met. But she just can’t seem to learn how to spend money the way it should be spent.”

His words trailed off. Trent was elsewhere—lost in thought.

He excused himself soon after, and they parted cordially.

Half an hour later, Trent was in his studio, mechanically cleaning up. He needed to know. He had to find out what had become of her. He couldn’t approach her—not after their last meeting. He wouldn’t bring back the shame. He doubted he’d even see her again.

But he had to know.

Cupples was in London. Marlowe was there too. And Paris no longer held him.

In twenty-four hours, his ties to the city were severed. He stood at the edge of a leaden sea, gazing toward the gleaming cliffs of Dover.

Though he had instinctively chosen a course from the chaos of his thoughts, he found it stalled at the start. He decided to see Cupples first—he would know more than Bunner. But Cupples was traveling, not due back for a month. Trent had no reason to rush him home.

He wouldn’t confront Marlowe until he’d gathered more information.

He forbade himself to seek out her house in Hampstead. He couldn’t enter it. The mere thought of being seen near it flushed his face.

He stayed at a hotel, rented a studio, and waited—for Cupples, for answers. He tried to lose himself in work, but failed.

After a week, an idea struck him. She had once mentioned a fondness for music.

That evening, he went to the opera. And every night after.

Perhaps he’d see her. And if she noticed him, they could pretend not to recognize each other—anyone might attend the opera.

So he went alone, moving quickly through the crowd in the vestibule. Each night, he left knowing she hadn’t been there.

It became a ritual—both comforting and charged with guilt. He loved music, and in its spell, he found peace.

One night, hurrying through the glittering throng, he felt a light touch on his arm.

An instant certainty flooded through him. He turned.

It was her.

More radiant than ever—free of sorrow, smiling, dressed for the evening. He could not speak.

She breathed quickly too, her eyes bright with daring as she greeted him.

Her words were few.

“I wouldn’t miss a note of _Tristan_,” she said. “Nor should you. Come see me during the interval.” She gave him the number of her box.
