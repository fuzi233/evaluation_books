"If some mortal, born too soon, Were laid away in some great trance—the ages Coming and going all the while—till dawned His true time's advent; and could then record The words they spoke who kept watch by his bed, Then I might tell more of the breath so light Upon my eyelids, and the fingers warm Among my hair. Youth is confused; yet never So dull was I but, when that spirit passed, I turned to him, scarce consciously, as turns A water-snake when fairies cross his sleep." —BROWNING: *Paracelsus*.

This epigraph framed the letter Sir Hugo pressed into Deronda's hands, a document that felt less like correspondence and more like a summons from a distant shore.

My good friend and yours, Sir Hugo Mallinger, will have told you that I wish to see you. My health is shaken, and I desire there should be no time lost before I deliver to you what I have long withheld. Let nothing hinder you from being at the *Albergo dell' Italia* in Genoa by the fourteenth of this month. Wait for me there. I am uncertain when I shall be able to make the journey from Spezia, where I shall be staying. That will depend on several things. Wait for me—the Princess Halm-Eberstein. Bring with you the diamond ring that Sir Hugo gave you. I shall like to see it again.—Your unknown mother, LEONORA HALM-EBERSTEIN.

The letter's colorless wording offered Deronda no clue to the storm brewing behind its calm surface. Yet, bound by Sir Hugo's reticence—a silence that felt less like omission and more like a sacred pledge—he accepted the delay without protest. The revelation that his lifelong conjectures regarding his lineage had been mistaken struck him with a sudden, chilling clarity, halting any further speculation in its tracks. He allowed his imagination to skim briefly over the landscape of possibilities, but he refused to anchor himself to any single vision, terrified that nurturing one specific hope or dread would distort his ability to face the truth, whatever shape it might take.

In this suspended state, Deronda found himself unable to articulate the gravity of his departure to anyone, least of all to Mordecai. To speak plainly—"I am going to learn the truth about my birth"—would ignite a dangerous excitement in Mordecai's hopeful heart, a fire that could consume them both. Instead, he cloaked his mission in the guise of Sir Hugo's wishes, projecting an air of casual indifference onto a journey that weighed heavily upon his soul. He claimed uncertainty regarding its duration, suggesting it might be fleeting, though his eyes betrayed a deeper unease.

"I will ask to have the child Jacob to stay with me," Mordecai said, finding comfort in the practical arrangements, his face softening after the initial pang of loss.

Mirah followed suit, her voice brightening. "I will drive round and ask Mrs. Cohen to let him come."

"The grandmother will deny you nothing," Deronda replied gently.

"I'm glad you were a little wrong as well as I," he added, offering a small smile to Mordecai. "You thought that old Mrs. Cohen would not bear to see Mirah."

"I undervalued her heart," Mordecai confessed, his tone reverent. "She is capable of rejoicing that another's plant blooms though her own be withered."

"Oh, they are dear good people; I feel as if we all belonged to each other," Mirah said, a touch of merriment coloring her smile.

"What should you have felt if that Ezra had been your brother?" Deronda asked, mischief flickering in his voice—a slight provocation born of the fact that she had already embraced people who stood poised to cause him significant distress.

Mirah regarded him with a flash of surprise before lowering her gaze. "He is not a bad man—I think he would never forsake anyone." As the words left her lips, she flushed deeply, casting a timid glance toward Mordecai before turning away to a nearby task. Her father loomed in her thoughts, a specter shared painfully between siblings, a subject that rendered the daylight of the street as shadowy as a haunted forest where every corner concealed an imaginary apparition.

Deronda perceived her involuntary allusion instantly, understanding the blush that stained her cheeks. How could he remain slow to recognize feelings that now seemed closer to his own than ever before? For the words of his mother's letter implied that his filial bond was not to be severed from painful conditions; indeed, paradoxically, the very missive that rendered his mother a tangible reality had cast her into greater emotional remoteness. The tender yearning for a being whose life might have been diminished by lack of his care, the image of a mother denied her due of reverence or compassion, had long simmered secretly within him, coloring his observations of every woman he encountered. Now, however, this mental portrait seemed to fit the facts no better than his previous misconceptions about Sir Hugo. He marveled at how the mere appearance of his mother's handwriting, carrying her actual sentiment, had caused his affections to shrink into a comparative neutrality. A veiled figure speaking in riddles had displaced the image he had carefully molded in his mind, the one possessing his tenderness and dutiful longing.

When he finally departed for Genoa, the interest dominating his mind bore little resemblance to his mother; it was anchored firmly in Mordecai and Mirah.

"God bless you, Dan!" Sir Hugo had said, clasping his hand. "Whatever else changes for you, it can't change my being the oldest friend you have known, and the one who has all along felt the most for you. I couldn't have loved you better if you'd been my own—only I should have been better pleased with thinking of you always as the future master of the Abbey instead of my fine nephew; and then you would have seen it necessary for you to take a political line. However—things must be as they may."

It was a defensive maneuver by the baronet, mingling purposeless chatter with expressions of profound seriousness.

Upon arriving at the *Italia* in Genoa, Deronda found no sign of Princess Halm-Eberstein. Only on the second day did a letter arrive, informing him that her arrival might occur within a week or be delayed by a fortnight or more. Circumstances prevented her from fixing her journey precisely, and she implored him to wait with as much patience as he could muster.

Faced with this indefinite prospect of suspense concerning matters of supreme importance, Deronda undertook the arduous task of seeking amusement on philosophical grounds, attempting to quiet his excited feelings and lend patience wings over a weary road. His previous visit to the magnificent city had been cursory, leaving much to explore beyond the standard tourist circuit. He spent the cooler hours wandering observantly through the streets, the quay, and the environs, often taking a boat to behold the city and harbor from the sea.

All sights, all subjects—even the anticipated reunion with his mother—found a central union in Mordecai and Mirah, and the ideas immediately associated with them. As his boat pushed through the waters near the grand harbor, one thought dominated his mind: the memory of the multitudinous Spanish Jews centuries ago, driven destitute from their homes, forced to land on this very quay for a brief respite. They had overspread the stone with a pall of famine and plague—dying mothers clutching dying children, fathers and sons staring into each other's haggard faces, like groups emerging from a hundred Hunger-towers beneath the midday sun.

Inevitably, dreamlike constructions of a possible ancestry for himself wove themselves with these historic memories, which had gained a new resonance since his discovery of Mirah. Now, influenced by Mordecai, these thoughts became irresistibly dominant. He would have sealed his mind against such speculations if he could, yet he had never fully admitted to himself that he wished the facts to validate Mordecai's conviction. He silently repeated that he had no choice in the matter, that wishing was folly—nay, on the question of parentage, wishing seemed akin to that meanness which disowns kinship before the deed is done, a disowning by anticipation. What remained was simply to accept the fact; and now that he was assured of his error regarding Sir Hugo, he possessed no strong presumption to guide him. There had been a resolved concealment rendering all inference untrustworthy, and the very name he bore might be a falsehood. If Mordecai was wrong—if the so-called Daniel Deronda was held by ties entirely aloof from the path his friend's pathetic hope outlined?—he would not say "I wish"; yet he could not help feeling on which side the sacrifice lay.

Across these two importunate thoughts, which he resisted as one resists anything in that unstrung condition of suspense, flowed continually an anxiety he made no effort to banish. He dwelt upon it with a mournfulness that often feels the best atonement one can offer for a need met too late. This anxiety was for Gwendolen.

In the wonderful mixtures of human nature, there exists a feeling distinct from the exclusive passionate love some possess, yet differing from friendship or mere benevolence. A man, for instance, hardly represents this shade of feeling toward a woman more clearly than in the phrase, "I should have loved her, if..." The "if" covers either a prior growth in inclination or circumstances that erected an internal prohibitory law, keeping emotions from quivering out of balance.
