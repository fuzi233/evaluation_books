“Good day, Messer Domenico,” Nello greeted the first of the two men who stepped into his shop, nodding silently to the second. “You arrive at just the right moment—like cheese on macaroni. Ah! You’re in a hurry? Need a shave without delay? _Ecco_! And this is a morning when every soul carries weighty thoughts. Florence stands orphaned—the very heart of Italy torn away—Heaven itself seems uncertain what to do next. _Oimè_! Yet still the sun marches steadily toward noon, and as I said, you come like cheese already grated. For this young stranger here was seeking an honest merchant to lend him a sum against a ring of great worth, and if I had counted every goldsmith and money-lender in Florence upon my fingers, I could not have named one more reputable than Menico Cennini. Besides, he has other wares that suit your trade—Greek learning, and eyes still bright with youth—a double treasure that printers always crave.”

The solemn elder man, son of Bernardo Cennini—who twenty years earlier had cast the first types in Florence after hearing of German printing—stood motionless, lathered and passive, while Nello poured forth his words. But he turned his gaze slowly toward the stranger, studying him with quiet scrutiny.

“This young gentleman,” Nello continued, fond of elaboration, “is fluent in Greek, Latin, or Italian—whichever you desire. He is a marvel of youthful scholarship, equal to Francesco Filelfo or our own unmatched Poliziano. A second Guarino, too, though he has suffered misfortune: shipwrecked, no doubt losing precious manuscripts that might have brought even greater accuracy to your fine editions, Messer Domenico. Fortunately, he has saved a few rare gems. His name is—what did you say, sir?”

“Tito Melema,” the stranger replied, slipping off his ring and offering it to Cennini, whom Nello had now freed from the shaving cloth with swift precision.

Meanwhile, the second man who had entered—the tall figure of about fifty, with a neatly trimmed beard, an old felt hat, and a worn mantle—had fixed his eyes on the young Greek. Now he spoke abruptly:

“Young man, I am painting a scene of Sinon deceiving old Priam. I would be glad to use your face for Sinon—if you’d grant me a sitting.”

Tito Melema started, his pale face flashing with surprise, as if accused of some hidden crime. But Nello left no room for hesitation.

“Piero,” the barber declared, “you are the most astonishing mixture of whims and fancies ever packed into a human frame. What trick will you play with this scholar’s noble countenance to make it fit a traitor? Why not ask him to lift his eyes upward? Then you could paint him as Saint Sebastian—sure to draw crowds of devout women. Or, if you prefer classical themes, crown his curls with myrtle and make him a youthful Bacchus—or better yet, Phoebus Apollo, for his face glows like a summer dawn. It made me his friend in the space of a single ‘credo.’”

“Ay, Nello,” the painter replied, speaking in short, deliberate bursts, “and if your tongue could ever cease its endless chattering long enough for your mind to reflect, you’d see that you’ve just given me the reason why this young man’s face suits my traitor perfectly. A true traitor must wear a face untouched by vice—lips that can lie with a smile, eyes so clear and deep as agate that no shame can dim them, cheeks that rise fresh from murder without looking haggard. I do not say this young man is a traitor—I only mean he has a face that could become the perfect mask for one, should he possess the heart. Which is to say nothing more than that he has a beautiful face, alive with warm youth, nourished by good blood and food, able to keep its color without much help from virtue. He may have the courage of a hero along with it—I claim no such thing. Ask Domenico there: can lapidaries always judge a gem by sight alone? Now I’m going to plug my ears with tow—for your talk and the bells together are more than I can bear. Say no more. Just trim my beard.”

With these words, Piero—known as “di Cosimo” after his master, Cosimo Rosselli—pulled out two bits of tow, stuffed them into his ears, and settled into Nello’s chair. The barber shrugged, then gave the Greek a sly, knowing glance, as if to say, *See? A peculiar fellow. Everyone treats his words as jests.*

Tito, who had stood frozen, his dark eyes locked on the painter, seemed to regain himself as Piero shifted position. Satisfied with the explanation, he returned his attention to Cennini, who now said:

“This is a curious and valuable ring, young man. The intaglio—a fish beneath a crested serpent—set in the black layer of onyx, or rather nicolo, is beautifully framed by the blue upper stratum. The ring must have a story?”

“Yes, indeed,” Tito answered, meeting the man’s gaze frankly. “It was found in Sicily. Those who study gems and sigils tell me both stone and carving carry power: to bring fortune, especially at sea, and to restore lost things to their owner. But,” he added, smiling, “though I’ve worn it constantly since leaving Greece, it hasn’t made me entirely fortunate at sea—unless escaping drowning counts as proof of its virtue. Whether my lost chests resurface remains to be seen. But to leave no chance untried, Messer, I beg you only to hold the ring as pledge for a small sum—far less than its worth—and I shall redeem it as soon as I can sell certain other gems I carry within my doublet, or earn money through scholarly work, if fortune grants me such an opportunity.”

“That may be arranged,” Cennini said. “Come with me. My brother Pietro, who judges scholarship better than I, may offer you a task to test your abilities. In the meantime, take back your ring until I can give you the florins. If you wish, come along with me.”

“Yes, yes,” Nello chimed in. “Go with Messer Domenico—you could not choose better company. He was born under the constellation that grants skill, wealth, and honesty—whatever that sign may be, which matters little, since babies cannot choose their horoscopes. Indeed, if they could, we’d have chaos at certain times of year. Besides, our Phoenix, the incomparable Pico, has shown that all astrology is mere nonsense—which is a relief. _Addio_, fine young man! Don’t forget to return to me.”

“No fear of that,” Tito replied, waving farewell as he turned his bright face at the door. “You’re doing me a great service—that’s the surest guarantee I’ll come back.”

“Say what you will, Piero,” Nello called after the departing figure, “but I’ll never look at a face like that without sensing a kind heart. Soon you’ll tell me Leonardo, whom you praise endlessly, should have painted Judas as beautiful as Saint John! But you’re as deaf as the peak of Mount Morello with that cursed tow in your ears. Well, well—I’ll learn more of this young man’s tale before I send him to Bardo Bardi.”
