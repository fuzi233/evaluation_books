The morning sun broke over Cardell Chase not merely as light, but as a violent exorcism of the previous night's gloom. Gwendolen Harleth emerged from her chamber transformed; the self-doubt that had clung to her like wet wool was evaporated, replaced by a vibrant, almost predatory vitality. She stood tall and luminous, a newly opened lily unfurling its petals to the harsh, clarifying air. Yesterday's hesitation felt now like a phantom tremor on the surface of a roaring river, insignificant and washed away. The prospect of the roving archery match ignited a primal joy within her. She envisioned herself darting beneath the beech trees, a wood-nymph in motion, surrounded by admiring gazes. And there, inevitably, was Grandcourt. Not the tragic, lyrical Daphnis of her feverish dreams, but a man of substance, a hunter of a different order. Today, she anticipated his slow, deliberate approach, a calculated convergence of wills, and she prepared to meet it with the rational, cool encouragement she had rehearsed with her uncle.

Descending to breakfast, the table was silent save for the rustling of Mrs. Davilow's fan. A stack of letters lay upon Gwendolen's plate. She picked one up, a smile blooming gradually across her face until it reached her eyes, and passed it to her mother. Mrs. Davilow returned it, her own features lighting up with a renewed cheerfulness born of her daughter's radiant spirits.

"You don't feel inclined to flee a thousand miles away?" she asked gently.

"Not exactly so far," Gwendolen replied, her voice crisp.

"It was a grievous oversight not to write again before this departure. Cannot you scribble a line now, before we set out?"

"It is not urgent. Tomorrow will suffice. You see, they leave town today. I must write to Dover; they will remain there until Monday."

"Shall I write for you, dear, if it tires you?"

Gwendolen paused, lifting her cup for a sip of coffee before answering with a brisk dismissal. "Oh, no, let it be. I shall write tomorrow." Yet, a flicker of guilt touched her conscience, and she softened her tone, looking up with playful affection. "Dear, old, beautiful mamma!"

"Old, child, truly," Mrs. Davilow countered, her voice warm.

"Please do not, mamma! I meant 'old' as in 'darling.' You are hardly twenty-five years older than I am. When you speak thus, my life seems to shrivel before me."

"One can find immense happiness in twenty-five years, my dear."

"I must seize every moment," Gwendolen declared merrily. "The sooner I secure my palaces and coaches, the better."

"And a devoted husband who worships you, Gwen," Mrs. Davilow added, her encouragement palpable.

Gwendolen pouted saucily, saying nothing, her heart swelling with the anticipation of acquisition.

However, a minor cloud marred the brilliance of her start: the rector was detained by magistrate's business and likely would not reach Cardell Chase that day. She cared little that Mrs. Gascoigne and Anna chose to stay behind without him, nor did she mind the absence of the clergyman. But her uncle's presence would have rendered the decision to proceed inevitable. Decision itself had become a formidable entity. Having nearly succumbed to Grandcourt, Gwendolen felt this sudden abundance of options coalescing into something too definitive, too heavy. When one begins to crave so fervently, whatever is obtained quickly transforms into a cage of limitations and exclusions. Yet, a comforting thought persisted: marriage was the gateway to a vast, liberated freedom.

The rendezvous was set at Green Arbor, a grassy clearing where a cluster of overhanging branches formed a natural, sheltering amphitheater. Here, the coachload of servants with provisions would prepare the picnic, and the warden of the Chase would guide the roving archers, ensuring they remained within the prescribed boundaries—a curved path defined by landmarks such as the Double Oak, the Whispering Stones, and the High Cross. The plan was for a brief stroll before luncheon, saving the main expedition for the exquisite, golden light of the afternoon. The muster was swift, sparing everyone the tedium of waiting. As the groups scattered into the dappled shade of the beeches and the deeper gloom of the oaks, the scene possessed a painterly perfection. This roving archery was far more picturesque than the static games, though success at moving targets relied less on practiced precision and more on chance. Consequently, the hits were distributed unevenly among the volunteers. Perhaps for this reason, compounded by her own divided attention between preoccupation and the desperate need to hide it, Gwendolen did not excel in these initial trials. Unless one counted her lively grace in the face of comparative failure, she remained unremarkable. She wore white and green, the same attire that had marked the epoch-making introduction to Grandcourt. Now, he was constantly by her side, yet it would have been difficult to discern from mere appearance that their relationship had shifted irrevocably since their first conversation. Still, the air around them crackled with implication; most observers concluded they were either betrothed or on the precipice of it. Gwendolen believed this herself.

As the groups drifted back toward Green Arbor, chatting aimlessly rather than aiming, words were exchanged that signaled the dawn of her surrender. Grandcourt spoke, breaking the rhythm of their walk. "Do you know how long it has been since I first saw you in this dress?"

"The archery meeting was on the 25th, and this is the 13th," Gwendolen laughed lightly. "I am not good at calculation, but I dare say it must be nearly three weeks."

A pause hung in the air, heavy and expectant. Then, "That is a great loss of time."

"That your knowing me has caused?" Gwendolen retorted, her smile sharpening. "Pray, do not be uncomplimentary; I dislike it."

Another pause. "It is because of the gain that I feel the loss."

This time, Gwendolen allowed the silence to stretch. She was thinking, *He is remarkably ingenious. He never speaks foolishly.* Her unusual quietude seemed the ultimate affirmative, and Grandcourt pressed on. "The gain of knowing you makes me acutely aware of the time lost in uncertainty. Do *you* like uncertainty?"

"I think I do, rather," Gwendolen said suddenly, beaming at him with a playful intensity. "There is more in it."

Grandcourt met her laughing eyes with a slow, steady gaze that pierced directly into her soul, an abstract vision of possession. "Do you mean more torment for me?"

Something profoundly strange struck Gwendolen in that instant, shaking her loose from her usual self-conscious armor. Blushing, she turned her eyes away. "No, that would make me sorry."

Grandcourt might have seized upon this answer, which her shift in demeanor rendered decisively favorable, but he was not so overwhelmed as to ignore their surroundings. They were descending into Green Arbor, visible to all, at a point where the slope became inconveniently steep. This necessitated his offering his hand in the literal sense. She took it, and they descended in silence, watched intently by those already on the level—among them Mrs. Arrowpoint, standing beside Mrs. Davilow. That lady had resolved that Grandcourt's merits were insufficient to sway Catherine, given her high standards that had previously rejected Lord Slogan. Thus, she regarded the tenant of Diplow with detached scrutiny.

"Mr. Grandcourt is not equal as a man to his uncle, Sir Hugo Mallinger—too languid. True, Mr. Grandcourt is younger, but I should not be surprised if Sir Hugo outlives him, despite the age gap. It is ill-calculating on successions," Mrs. Arrowpoint concluded, her voice carrying too loudly.

"It is indeed," Mrs. Davilow agreed with quiet cheerfulness, her habitual melancholy regarding the unsatisfactory state of affairs completely suspended by her satisfaction with the current situation.

I need not recount the sumptuous food consumed in that green refectory, nor dwell on the tales of the forest scenery spreading beyond the hollow's rim. My concern is with a specific phase of life where the blissful beauty of earth and sky filtered in only through narrow, oblique cracks, while consciousness was consumed by a petty social drama, insulated from wider realities as if it were a puppet show. The food and champagne were of the finest caliber; the talk and laughter belonged to the elite, where no one displayed envy, and the advantages of the world were accepted with a high-born indifference born of familiarity. Some gentlemen strolled and smoked cigars during the interval before four o'clock, the hour designated for resuming the roam. Strangely, Grandcourt was among them, yet not Mr. Lush. Seeming to enjoy the day generously, Lush busied himself serving everyone, ordering drinks and managing logistics, thereby becoming an increasingly glaring blemish on the scene for Gwendolen, though he maintained an amiably distant posture, never casting an obvious glance in her direction.

When the group moved to prepare for departure, it transpired that the bows had been entrusted to Lord Brackenshaw's valet.
