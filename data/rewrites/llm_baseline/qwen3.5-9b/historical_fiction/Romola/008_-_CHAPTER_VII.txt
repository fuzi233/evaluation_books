Bartolommeo Scala, the secretary of the Florentine Republic, resided in a stately palace near the Porta Pinti, a building now recognized as the Casa Gherardesca. Its heraldic shield displayed an azure ladder across a golden field, bearing the motto *Gradatim* ("Step by Step") above the gate. This emblem declared to all passersby that the son of a miller had climbed to his high station solely through his own exertions, a truth he felt compelled to proclaim without shame.

Scala was a man of vanity and pomposity, yet fundamentally honest; he possessed a sincere conviction in his own worth and saw no necessity for pretense. By this time, he had ascended to the highest rung of his metaphorical ladder. Having served as secretary for twenty years, he had frequently delivered orations from the *ringhiera*, the balcony of the Old Palace, in the presence of royal guests, while the Marzocco, the symbol of the republic, donned its golden crown and the populace shouted, "Long live Messer Bartolommeo!" He had served as an ambassador to Rome, where he was appointed Titular Senator and Apostolic Secretary, knighted with the Order of the Golden Spur, and, eight years prior, elected Gonfaloniere—the ultimate prize of a Florentine citizen's ambition.

Amidst these triumphs, Scala had grown wealthier and more afflicted by gout, a common fate for successful mortals. The Knight of the Golden Spur often sat beneath the magnificent loggia he had constructed, his heel propped on a cushioned support, overlooking the expansive gardens behind his home. It was on such a day, suffering from this ailment, that he received the requested audience with Tito Melema.

The mid-May sun warmed the flowers and grass beyond the shaded loggia. Scala had discarded his heavy silk *lucco* coat in favor of a light mantle draped over his tunic. Seated beside him were his beautiful daughter, Alessandra, and her husband, Marullo, a Greek soldier-poet. Opposite them sat two friends, distinguished enough to offer counsel but not so illustrious as to dominate the conversation.

Yet, despite his physical discomfort and social standing, Scala's happiness was marred by a specific grievance: a collection of papers lying before him. These documents comprised a lengthy correspondence between himself and Angelo Poliziano (Politian). It was a peculiar habit of the era—and one difficult to comprehend today—to publicly air personal disputes and share entire exchanges of letters with scholarly acquaintances. This was not the first instance where Scala had solicited his friends' opinions on the moral weight of roughly thirty Latin letters exchanged with Poliziano, all stemming from a series of playful epigrams regarding a common insect.

It was the tale of a quintessential Renaissance squabble, significant here because it provided the necessary spark to ignite friendship's otherwise sluggish flame. Poliziano, having been spurned in his pursuit of Scala's daughter, maintained a keen, intellectual edge against the ambitious secretary who had refused the greatest scholar of the age as a son-in-law. Scala was a diligent public servant and a fortunate man—traits naturally irritating to an insulted academic. However, Scala suffered from a compulsion to write, albeit poorly. Like many such individuals, he composed poetic trifles purely for private amusement, sending them to friends who acted as the literary critics of the fifteenth century.

Scala had many admirers, including Ficino and Landino, who found his Latin prose elegant and robust. The formidable Joseph Scaliger, who would later declare Scala ignorant of Latin, belonged to the following century. Yet, the inherent coquetry of amateur authors rarely accepts flattery from friends alone. The critical, haughty Poliziano—a companion who lacked amiability—needed to know that the busy secretary possessed a fertile mind for verse, hinting at potential greatness if he were not consumed by worldly affairs.

The moment arrived when the man one secretly despises sends a Latin epigram containing a grammatical error: a false gender assignment, a dubious elision in hendecasyllables, or a poetic figure that is a glaring solecism. This moment had come for Poliziano. The secretary had poked his head out of his official duties, and the lurking critic descended upon him.

Poliziano, exercising the liberties of a friend, had penned a Latin epigram correcting Scala's mistake in assigning the feminine gender to the *culex* (gnat), an insect ubiquitous along the Arno. Scala retaliated with a witty Latin verse referencing Poliziano's failed romantic suit. Poliziano found the verses charming and amusing, unaware that they were technically flawed. Since Scala claimed to have written them imitating a Greek epigram, Poliziano, wishing to instruct rather than humiliate, enclosed a Greek poem of his own on the same subject. His piece featured a clever conceit comparing the gnat to Venus and Cupid, based on the biological fact that gnats, like Venus, were believed to arise from water—a popular theme at the time.

In response, Scala politely noted that his verses were never meant for a scholar with such refined sensibilities as Poliziano, who stood closest to ancient perfection and whose palate was so fastidious that even sturgeon would seem insipid to him. He defended his hasty, uncorrected verses as merely a summer diversion for himself and those content with mediocrity, unlike others who sought fame through publishers. Admitting he barely understood the Greek epigram Poliziano had sent, save for its basic meaning, he added a postscript: the comparison of the gnat to Venus was problematic. Venus might take offense; furthermore, unless one adhered to the doctrine of Thales regarding cold origins, the idea of a warm-loving creature emerging from water was doubtful. Perhaps a fish, an eagle, or even a bat or owl would be a more apt parallel given the gnat's flight capabilities and nocturnal habits.

Here lay a fresh opportunity for Poliziano. Unaware that Scala had raised questions about sturgeon, he clarified that his original intent involved frogs and gudgeons. He swiftly dismantled Scala's defenses of his Latin and savagely criticized his pedantic objections to the Greek epigram. "Wretched cavils," Poliziano wrote. Regarding the gnat's watery origin, Virgil himself called it the "offspring of waters"; as for Scala's comparisons to fish, eagles, and bats, they were irrelevant (*nihil ad rem*). Just because an eagle flies higher does not prove a gnat cannot fly.

Shamed by dwelling on such trifles and inflating a gnat into an elephant, Poliziano concluded by asserting his integrity. He offered no deceit, nor would he be swayed by the false praises of slanderers. Quoting Homer, he suggested that true character reveals itself regardless of absent criticism.

The dispute escalated. Logic deteriorated, Scala became increasingly pompous in his self-assurance, and Poliziano's temper grew venomous. Poliziano, generous in spirit, hoped to hold up a mirror to the inflated secretary, hoping Scala would recognize his ignorance and stop defending bad Latin against ancient authorities. Unless, perhaps, Scala intended to sink in literature as he rose in political rank, thereby balancing the scales for men of letters. In exchange, Poliziano invited Scala to examine his own writings, promising to find devout admiration for antiquity within them. Scala expressed shame for his contemporary age, claiming he could not elevate his own works to match divine antiquity, yet insisted his honors were well-earned, citing his rise from poverty in Florence.

Poliziano retorted that he was unsurprised Scala disliked the age, noting that he himself was equally disliked. He accurately recalled calling Scala a "branniest monster," formed from the refuse of mills, perfectly suited for the dusty task of grinding grain (*in pistrini sordibus natus et quidem pistrino dignissimus*).

These papers were produced specifically due to Tito Melema's impending arrival. Scala wished to test the new Greek scholar's judgment on Poliziano's epigram. After discussing Tito's travels, examining gemstones, and transitioning from Lorenzo de' Medici's art collections to broader classical tastes, the topic inevitably turned to Poliziano. Described as a man of immense talent but excessive arrogance, Poliziano assumed the role of a Hercules destined to destroy the literary monstrosities of the age. He wrote unsigned letters to his elders as if they were divine revelations. Were not his own criticisms sometimes questionable and his tastes perverse? Fond of mocking those who mimicked Cicero by ending sentences with *esse videtur*, did he not fall into the opposite trap of chasing obscure words and affected phrasing? Was every point in his celebrated *Miscellanea* truly defensible?

Tito, eager to please, examined the *Miscellanea* and found much to admire. Consequently, he proved himself worthy to adjudicate the famous correspondence regarding the *culex*. Scala presented the Greek epigram, which Poliziano undoubtedly considered the finest in the world, though he had feigned belief that Greeks abroad would dismiss it. Had not Poliziano spoken the truth in his false modesty?

Tito, acting as the wise young judge, analyzed the epigram to Scala's satisfaction. He appreciated satire even in the vernacular. Scala, a good host, offered a few unpublished sonnets targeting Poliziano's foibles, intending them as light refreshment for the company. Satisfied, Tito departed under an urgent invitation to return.

His interest lingered on the gems Scala had shown him, particularly an agate featuring a natural formation resembling Cupid riding a lion, and a "Jew's Stone" depicting a lion-headed serpent. Scala agreed to purchase both; the latter was intended as a talisman against his debilitating gout
