*"Look into your own heart and write!"* Herr Cant had advised. But Gerard knew that hearts, like deep waters, were not so easily fathomed. A man might peer into his own soul and see only what he wished to see—truth distorted by desire, reflection clouded by pride.

Yet halfway to Sevenbergen, Gerard tried. *Why am I going?* he asked himself. His heart, ever quick with excuses, answered at once: *To show her I am indifferent. To prove her fickleness means nothing to me. To parade my honors, my future benefice, my contentment without her or any faithless woman.*

He found Peter Brandt’s cottage easily enough. There in the doorway sat Margaret, needle in hand, her head bent over her work. Beside her stood a tall man leaning on a longbow, speaking to her in low tones. A sharp, unreasonable pang shot through Gerard at the sight. But as he drew nearer, he recognized the man—an old soldier, past fifty, whose skill at the butts was well known.

Margaret glanced up. Her work fell from her hands. A soft cry escaped her lips, and color rushed to her face, then fled just as quickly. Just as swiftly, she schooled her features into cool detachment.

“Master Gerard!” she said, with feigned surprise. “What brings you here, I wonder?”

“I was passing by,” he answered. “I saw you, and thought to greet you—and ask after your father.”

“He is well. He will return soon.”

“Then I may as well wait for him.”

“As you wish.” She turned to the bowman. “Good Martin, would you fetch my father? Tell him a friend is here.”

“And am I not *your* friend as well?” Gerard asked.

“My father’s friends are mine,” she said lightly.

“Doubtful. A friend would not have promised to wait for me, then vanished the moment I turned my back.” His voice lowered. “Cruel Margaret, you don’t know how I searched for you—how nothing was right in the world without you.”

Her chin lifted. “Idle words. Had you truly wanted us, you would have come back. A bed was laid for you at my cousin’s. You would have been welcomed—made much of. Even *I* might have been kind to you that day. But you’ll not find me so again, nor any maid, I promise you.”

“Margaret, I returned the instant the Countess dismissed me. You were gone.”

“Nonsense. Hans Cloterman was left to guide you to us.”

“I saw no one—only a drunkard sprawled at a table.”

“At *our* table? What was he wearing?”

Gerard shrugged. “Some dull-colored robe. I paid little heed.”

Her face softened, then hardened again as she pressed him with sharp questions. He answered honestly, and piece by piece, the misunderstanding unraveled. The relief was a flood, sweeping away restraint. Confessions poured out—how miserable each had been, how empty the days apart.

Then Gerard noticed an open manuscript on the table—a Vulgate. His fingers itched for it. But before he could reach it, Margaret slapped her palms over the page, cheeks flushing.

“Take your hands away,” he teased. “Let me see what you were reading. I’ll read it too at home, and our souls may meet upon the page.”

She shook her head. So he kissed her hands until, laughing, she pulled them away—revealing the verse: *"An apple of gold in a network of silver."*

“There!” she cried. “I had searched so long for it, and now you’ve caught me!” Yet even as she scolded, her fingertip traced the words, sharing them with him.

“A pity it’s hidden under that monstrous cap,” he said.

“It’s a fine cap, so I’m told.”

“Perhaps. But what it hides is far lovelier.”

“It is not. It is hideous.”

“It was beautiful in Rotterdam.”

A sigh escaped her. “Ah, everything was beautiful that day.”

Then Peter arrived, greeting Gerard warmly and insisting he stay for supper. Margaret vanished, only to return with her hair caught in a silver net, her glances now coy, now bright. She moved about them, setting the meal, her laughter like music in the cottage.

When the cool evening air beckoned, Gerard coaxed her outside. She resisted—yet came. He led her toward the road to Tergou. She protested—yet walked with him, hand in hand. And when at last he had to leave, they vowed never to quarrel again, sealing the promise with a kiss that lingered, sweet and slow.

Gerard flew home on wings.

From that night on, his evenings belonged to Margaret. With each meeting, the bond between them deepened, until the hours apart were mere shadows—tolerated, but not truly lived.

For now, love’s path was smooth. Obstacles loomed in the distance, but to youthful hearts brimming with hope, they seemed small, surmountable. The storm that would one day rise—the passions and wills that would clash against their love—gave no warning yet.

The course of true love ran clear and untroubled, drawing them deeper, binding them fast.
