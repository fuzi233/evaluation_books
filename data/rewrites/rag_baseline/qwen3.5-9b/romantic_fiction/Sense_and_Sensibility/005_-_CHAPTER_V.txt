"No room. Full up."

The door slammed with the dull, decisive thud of bureaucratic finality.

Mrs. Dashwood stood in the freezing drizzle of the Devonshire night, calculating the precise logistical gap between her current assets and the requirements of a new household. To have secured a lease at Barton Cottage was a tactical victory; to move her family without further encumbrance was a strategic necessity. Yet, the rejection from the local inn remained a glaring anomaly in her otherwise orderly retreat.

She turned to her son-in-law and daughter-in-law, expecting sympathy, but found only a polite, hollow civility. Mrs. John Dashwood said nothing, her expression a mask of practiced neutrality. Her husband, however, offered a perfunctory hope that she would not settle far from Norland—a statement designed to soothe rather than solve.

"Devonshire!" Edward repeated, his voice sharp with concern, though the distance was already etched into his brow. "So far from hence! And to what part?"

She explained the geography with the precision of a surveyor: four miles north of Exeter. "It is but a cottage," she continued, her tone rising with the confidence of a commander assessing terrain. "A room or two can easily be added. If my friends find no difficulty in traveling so far to see me, I am sure I will find none in accommodating them." She issued a pointed invitation to the couple to visit Barton, and to Edward, one charged with deeper affection. This was not mere hospitality; it was a declaration of war against the constraints imposed by her daughter-in-law. By inviting her brother-in-law, she signaled that she regarded his disapproval of her marriage as irrelevant noise.

Mr. John Dashwood reiterated his sorrow, claiming he was vexed that his presence could not aid in the removal of furniture. He spoke of conscience, yet his actions betrayed a different calculus. The furniture—household linen, plate, china, books, and Marianne's fine pianoforte—was already being transported by water. Mrs. John watched the packages depart with a visible sigh, unable to suppress the thought that it was unfair for a woman with such a trifling income to retain such valuable articles.

The transaction was swift. The house was leased for twelve months, fully furnished, requiring no negotiation. Mrs. Dashwood waited only for the disposal of her effects at Norland and the determination of her new household staff before departing for the west. As she was exceptionally rapid in executing matters of interest, these tasks were completed quickly.

The horses left by her late husband had long since been sold. Following the earnest advice of her eldest daughter, she agreed to sell her carriage as well. For the comfort of her children, she might have retained it, but Elinor's wisdom prevailed. Similarly, the servant roster was reduced to three: two maids and a man, drawn from the existing establishment at Norland. Two were dispatched immediately to Devonshire to prepare the cottage, ensuring the mistress's arrival would meet a ready home.

Lady Middleton remained entirely unknown to Mrs. Dashwood, who preferred the direct approach of entering her own domain to the uncertainty of visiting Barton Park as a guest. She relied undoubtingly on Sir John's description, suppressing any curiosity until she stepped across the threshold as owner.

Yet, her eagerness to flee Norland was tempered by the evident, albeit feebly concealed, satisfaction of her daughter-in-law regarding the impending removal. This was the moment when Mr. John's promise to his father—the provision of funds for six months' maintenance—should logically be fulfilled. Having neglected it upon first taking the estate, their departure seemed the ideal window for redemption. But Mrs. Dashwood soon abandoned hope. The general drift of his discourse revealed a man consumed by the increasing expenses of housekeeping and the perpetual demands on his purse. He appeared less a benefactor and more a debtor, needing capital rather than dispensing it.

Within a few weeks of receiving Sir John Middleton's first letter, every detail of their future abode was settled. Mrs. Dashwood and her daughters prepared to begin their journey.

Many tears were shed during their final adieux to a place so deeply beloved.

"Dear, dear Norland!" Marianne cried, wandering alone before the house on the last evening of their stay. "When shall I cease to regret you! When learn to feel a home elsewhere! Oh! Happy house, could you know what I suffer in now viewing you from this spot, from whence perhaps I may view you no more! And you, ye well-known trees! But you will continue the same. No leaf will decay because we are removed, nor any branch become motionless although we can observe you no longer! No; you will continue the same; unconscious of the pleasure or the regret you occasion, and insensible of any change in those who walk under your shade! But who will remain to enjoy you?"
