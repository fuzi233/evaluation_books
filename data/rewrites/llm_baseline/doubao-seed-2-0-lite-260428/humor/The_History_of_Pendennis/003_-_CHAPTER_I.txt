It was peak London social season, and Major Arthur Pendennis trundled over from his fancy lodgings to his usual Pall Mall club, where he was less a member and more the institution’s unofficial reigning monarch. As one of England’s undisputed top three wine snobs, and a man with a knack for running every group he joined, he’d been elected to the club committee years back, and the stewards and waiters bowed to him lower than they would to a visiting duke or field marshal.

At exactly 10:15 every morning, the Major made his entrance in boots shined so bright you could use them as a shaving mirror, a crisp check morning cravat that never once rumpled before dinner, a buff waistcoat studded with buttons bearing the royal crest, and linen so spotless that even Beau Brummell once cornered him outside a tailor’s shop begging for his laundress’s address — right before Brummell had to do a runner to France to escape his gambling debts. His coat, white gloves, neatly waxed whiskers, and even his carved walking stick were all perfectly tuned to the vibe of a retired military gentleman. From the back, you’d swear he was barely 30. Get a foot closer, though, and you’d spot that his lush rich brown “hair” was a very well-made wig, and faint crow’s feet fanned out around his slightly faded eyes on his handsome, mottled face. He had a nose straight out of the Duke of Wellington’s playbook, long milky-white hands, gold cuff links gifted to him by the Duke of York, and several fancy rings, the biggest of which blazed with the famous Pendennis family crest he polished every single night before bed.

He always claimed the exact same corner table, near the fire and within a foot of the window, and no one even thought of challenging him for it these days. A pair of drunken silly young lads had tried twice in years past to steal his spot for a joke, but the Major had simply sat at the next table, rested his chin on his hand, and stared at them so calmly they got the creeps and scarpered mid-bite of their toast. Now his post was as sacred as a royal throne, and the waiters laid his letters out on the table before he even walked in. Half the young dandies about town would gawk at his stack of mail, covered in fancy noble seals and franked by members of parliament. If anyone in the club had a question about etiquette, society gossip, who was secretly separated from their spouse, or how old the Duke of So-and-so really was, they all came running to Pendennis. Marchionesses would pull up outside the club in their carriages just to leave him notes, or drag him out for a stroll in Hyde Park. The young men loved walking with him down Pall Mall, too — he tipped his hat to every second person they passed, and half of them were lords.

So the Major sat down at his sacred table, waved the waiters off to fetch his buttered toast and fresh hot newspaper, and peered at his stack of letters through his fancy gold lorgnette (which he insisted was a fashionable novelty, not a pair of reading glasses). He sorted them one by one: there were three thick formal dinner cards promising heavy conversation and three courses of overcooked meat, gushy little private notes from society ladies begging him to bring that famous bottle of claret he kept locked in his cellar, a thick note on official paper from the Marquis of Steyne inviting him to a tiny, French-speaking party at Richmond’s Star and Garter, and a request from the Bishop of Ealing and his wife for the Major to come to dinner at their house. Glowry, the grumpy Scottish surgeon eating breakfast across from him, glowered the whole time, seething that no one ever invited him to any fancy fun parties.

Once he’d read all the notes, the Major pulled out his pocket datebook to juggle his social calendar. He dumped boring old Cutler the East India Director’s dinner without a second thought to go to Steyne’s fancy French party, accepted the bishop’s invite (the port there was worth sitting through two hours of theological small talk), and sorted the rest of his engagements to suit his own amusement and social clout. He ate his kipper, flipped through the Gazette to double check his name was listed correctly on the guest list for a lord’s recent fete, and chatted cheerfully to all his friends passing by the table.

Tucked all the way at the bottom of his mail pile, all alone, was one sad little letter with a country postmark and a cheap, homely seal. The handwriting was a delicate, wobbly lady’s scrawl, marked *IMMEDIATE* with a shaky underline, but the Major had ignored it. A country letter wasn’t about to cut the queue ahead of all the marchionesses and marquises lined up for his attention. Finally, curiosity got the better of him, and he broke the seal engraved with the name “Fairoaks”, postmarked Clavering St. Mary’s. It was from his sister Helen Pendennis.

“My dearest Major,” she wrote, “I beg and implore you to come to me *immediately*. I am in the deepest grief and perplexity. My darling boy Arthur, who has been everything a fond mother could wish, has broken my heart. He has formed — I can barely write it — a passion, an infatuation, for an actress performing locally. She is at least twelve years older than Arthur, who will not even turn eighteen until next February, and the wretched boy insists he is going to marry her.”

The Major gaped so wide that Glowry, across the table, fumbled for the lancet he kept in his card case, convinced his respected friend was about to have an apoplectic fit. Tucked inside was a second letter, written in the messy, scrawly handwriting of his teenage nephew Arthur, the family seal splattered all over with extra wax like the boy was shaking so bad he could barely hold the sealing wax stick. The kid ranted on for pages about how his beloved Miss Costigan (stage name Miss Fotheringay, late of Drury Lane) was descended from ancient Irish royalty, that her ancestors were kings of Ireland the same year their own Pendennis ancestor landed in Ireland with Richard II, that their difference in age was totally irrelevant, that his love was true and forever, that no stuffy old society prejudices were going to stop him from marrying the woman of his dreams, and please would his dear uncle the man of the world come down to Fairoaks at once to calm his silly old mom down.

When the Major finished that letter, his face turned so purple Glowry was half out of his seat ready to yell for a doctor. The heir of the Pendennises marrying a second-rate tragedy queen ten years his senior? The whole of London would laugh at him so hard he’d never dare show his face at a club again. Worse — he was going to have to bail on Lord Steyne’s fabulous Richmond party. He was going to spend all night jolting around in a drafty mail coach instead of sipping excellent claret with the most fun, exclusive crowd in England. He stomped over to the club writing room, fuming, scribbled regretful notes to every single one of his upcoming hosts, ordered his valet to book two seats on that night’s mail coach, and promptly charged the full cost of the trip to his sister Helen’s household account. The little romantic idiot and his tearful mom could pay for the chaos they’d caused.
