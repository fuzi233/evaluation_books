Mr. Welland laid down his knife and fork, his expression shifting instantly from polite engagement to anxious disbelief as he regarded his wife across the luncheon table. Mrs. Welland, carefully adjusting her gold-rimmed spectacles, read aloud in a tone of exaggerated theatricality: *"Professor and Mrs. Emerson Sillerton request the pleasure of Mr. and Mrs. Welland's company at the meeting of the Wednesday Afternoon Club on August 25th at 3 o'clock punctually. To meet Mrs. and the Misses Blenker. Red Gables, Catherine Street. R.S.V.P."*

"Good gracious—" Mr. Welland gasped, as if a second reading were required to fully grasp the monstrous absurdity of the situation. "Poor Amy Sillerton—you never can tell what her husband will do next," Mrs. Welland sighed, her voice dripping with practiced sympathy. "I suppose he's finally discovered the Blenkers."

Professor Emerson Sillerton was indeed a persistent thorn in the side of Newport society, a prickly weed rooted deep within a venerable and venerated family tree. He was, as everyone agreed, a man who had enjoyed every conceivable advantage. His lineage was impeccable: his father was Sillerton Jackson's uncle, his mother a Pennilow of Boston. Wealth and social standing flowed through both sides of his bloodline with equal vigor. Yet, despite these advantages, nothing on earth compelled Emerson Sillerton to become an archaeologist, nor to hold the title of Professor, nor to reside in Newport during the winter months, nor to indulge in any of the other revolutionary habits that characterized his life.

However, if he were determined to shatter tradition and defy societal norms, he surely need not have married poor Amy Dagonet. She possessed a legitimate expectation of "something different"—a conventional life—and ample funds to maintain her own carriage. To the members of the Mingott set, it remained utterly incomprehensible why Amy Sillerton had so tamely submitted to the eccentricities of a husband who filled their home with long-haired men and short-haired women, and who, upon traveling, dragged her to explore tombs in Yucatán rather than visiting the cultural capitals of Paris or Italy.

Yet there they were, entrenched in their peculiar ways, seemingly oblivious to how distinct they appeared to others. Consequently, whenever the Silloners hosted one of their dreary annual garden parties, every family along the Cliffs, bound together by the Sillerton-Pennilow-Dagonet alliance, was forced to draw lots and dispatch an unwilling representative.

"It's a wonder," Mrs. Welland remarked, "that they didn't choose the Cup Race day! Do you recall two years ago when they threw a party for a Black gentleman on the very day of Julia Mingott's *thé dansant*? Fortunately, this time there is nothing else scheduled that I am aware of—for naturally, some of us shall have to attend."

Mr. Welland let out a nervous sigh. "'Some of us,' my dear—more than one? Three o'clock is such a terribly inconvenient hour. I must be here by half-past three to take my drops; it is truly futile to attempt following Dr. Bencomb's new regimen unless I adhere to it systematically. Were I to join you later, I would inevitably miss my drive."

At the mere thought of missing his medication, he lowered his cutlery once more, and a flush of genuine anxiety colored his finely wrinkled cheek.

"There is absolutely no reason for you to go at all, my dear," his wife replied, her cheerfulness now entirely automatic. "I have several cards to deliver at the other end of Bellevue Avenue, and I shall stop by around half-past three. I intend to stay long enough to assure poor Amy that she has not been slighted."

She cast a hesitant glance toward her daughter. "And if Newland's afternoon is somehow accommodated, perhaps May can drive you out with the ponies and test their new russet harness."

In the Welland household, it was a fundamental principle that every person's day and hour must be meticulously "provided for." The melancholy prospect of having to "kill time"—particularly for those who held no fondness for whist or solitaire—haunted Mrs. Welland with the same terror that the specter of unemployment afflicts a philanthropist. Furthermore, another tenet of her philosophy dictated that parents should never, at least not visibly, interfere with the plans of their married children. Balancing this respect for May's independence with the pressing demands of Mr. Welland's schedule required a degree of ingenuity that ensured not a single second of Mrs. Welland's own time remained unaccounted for.

"Of course, I shall drive with Papa—I am certain Newland will find something to occupy him," May said, her tone gently reminding her husband of his conspicuous lack of initiative.

It was a source of constant distress to Mrs. Welland that her son-in-law displayed such a paucity of foresight regarding his daily arrangements. Too often, during the fortnight he had spent under her roof, when she inquired how he intended to spend his afternoon, he had offered the paradoxical reply: "Oh, I think, for a change, I shall simply save it instead of spending it." Once, when she and May had been compelled to undertake a long-postponed round of afternoon calls, he had confessed to having lain the entire afternoon beneath a rock on the beach below the house.

"Newland never seems to look ahead," Mrs. Welland had ventured to complain to her daughter on one occasion. May had answered serenely, "No; but you see it makes no difference, because when there is nothing particular to do, he reads a book."

"Ah, yes—just like his father!" Mrs. Welland had agreed, treating it as an accepted familial oddity, after which the subject of Newland's idleness was tacitly dropped.

Nevertheless, as the day for the Sillerton reception approached, May began to exhibit a natural concern for her brother-in-law's welfare, suggesting a tennis match at the Chiverses' or a sail on Julius Beaufort's cutter as a means of making amends for her temporary absence.

"I shall return by six, you know, dear: Papa never drives later than that," she noted, remaining unconvinced until Archer interjected that he intended to hire a runabout and drive up the island to a stud farm to inspect a second horse for her brougham. They had been searching for this mount for some time, and the suggestion was so agreeable that May glanced at her mother as if to signal: *See? He knows how to structure his time as well as anyone.*

The notion of the stud farm and the brougham horse had taken root in Archer's mind on the very day the Emerson Sillerton invitation had first been mentioned. However, he had kept the plan to himself, acting as though it contained elements of secrecy, fearing that discovery might thwart its execution. Having prudently secured a runabout drawn by a pair of old livery-stable trotters capable of covering eighteen miles on level ground, he abruptly abandoned the luncheon table at two o'clock, leaped into the light carriage, and drove off.

The day was flawless. A breeze from the north swept small puffs of white cloud across an ultramarine sky, while a bright sea churned beneath it. Bellevue Avenue lay empty at that hour. After depositing the stable lad at the corner of Mill Street, Archer turned down the Old Beach Road and crossed Eastman's Beach. He experienced that peculiar sensation of unexplained excitement familiar to boys on half-holidays as they venture into the unknown. Driving his pair at an easy pace, he calculated that he would reach the stud farm, situated just beyond Paradise Rocks, before three o'clock. This would allow him sufficient time to examine the horse—and trial him if he seemed promising—before possessing four golden hours to his disposal.

As soon as word of the Sillertons' party had reached him, Archer had reasoned that the Marchioness Manson would undoubtedly arrive in Newport accompanied by the Blenkers, offering Madame Olenska another opportunity to spend the day with her grandmother. In any event, the Blenker residence would likely be deserted, granting him the chance to satisfy a vague curiosity without committing indiscretion. He was not entirely certain he desired to see the Countess Olenska again; yet, ever since he had glimpsed her from the path overlooking the bay, he had harbored an irrational, indescribable urge to view the dwelling where she resided and to trace the movements of her imagined form, just as he had watched the real woman in the summerhouse.

This longing plagued him day and night, an incessant, undefined craving akin to the sudden whim of a sick man for food or drink once tasted and long since forgotten. He could not envision what this desire might lead to, nor could he see beyond it, for he was not conscious of any wish to speak to Madame Olenska or hear her voice. Simply put, he felt that if he could carry away the vision of the specific patch of earth she trod upon, and the manner in which the sky and sea enclosed it, the remainder of the world might appear less hollow.

Upon arriving at the stud farm, a quick glance revealed that the horse was not what he sought. Nevertheless, he rode a circuit behind it merely to demonstrate to himself that he was not in a hurry.
