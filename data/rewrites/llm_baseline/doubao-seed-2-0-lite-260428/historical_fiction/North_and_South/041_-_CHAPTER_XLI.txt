*I see my way as birds their trackless way— I shall arrive! what time, what circuit first, I ask not: but unless God sends his hail Or blinding fire-balls, sleet, or stifling snow, In some time—his good time—I shall arrive; He guides me and the bird. In His good time! — BROWNING’S PARACELSUS*

Winter dragged thin, the days stretching longer with February’s pale sun, but bringing none of the bright, stirring hope that usually comes with the first lengthening rays of the year. Mrs. Thornton had long ceased calling at the Hales’ little Milton home. Mr. Thornton still stopped by occasionally, but his visits were confined strictly to the study, shared only with Mr. Hale. The rareness of their meetings seemed only to deepen the old clergyman’s high regard for his friend; no lingering irritation or umbrage hung over Thornton’s absences, Margaret gathered. The aftermath of the strike had tangled his business affairs far more than he’d anticipated the winter prior, demanding almost his full attention. He spoke of her sometimes, too, she learned—casually, calmly, no awkward avoidance, no overeager dwelling, only quiet, steady goodwill.

Margaret had no strength left to lift her father’s heavy mood. Months of gnawing anxiety, even the open storms of the strike, had worn the elasticity out of her mind, leaving her life bleak and wind-scoured. She poured herself into teaching the two youngest Boucher children, striving hard for goodness even when her heart felt numb to any warmth or joy, going through every duty punctually but painfully, still far from any light cheer. The only thing she did without effort, out of an unspoken, unconscious piety, was tending to her father: no passing mood of his went without her sympathy, no small, shy wish of his unanticipated and granted, the gentle meekness of her obedience wrapping all his small, hesitant hopes in softness.

March brought news of Frederick’s wedding. The couple’s letters arrived hand in hand: Dolores wrote in a charming lilt of mixed Spanish and English, while Frederick’s own prose was dotted with Spanish turns of phrase that showed how fully he’d settled into his new life across the sea. Henry Lennox had written some weeks prior to warn them there was no hope of clearing Frederick’s name at court martial, the critical missing witnesses vanished without a trace. Frederick had sent back a fiery letter swearing he would never return to England, would renounce the country entirely rather than live there even if he was granted a full pardon. Margaret had cried sorely at the angry passion of it at first, before she realized it was only the raw sting of his disappointment speaking. His next letter was bright with plans for the future, no shadow left of his old grievance against the land of his birth, and Margaret found she needed that same patience to carry her own days forward. Dolores’ sweet, girlish letters began to win over both Margaret and her father, every little erased line showing the young Spaniard had fussed over every word to make a good impression on the English family her new husband had spoken of so fondly. Along with the letters came a stunning black lace mantilla, Dolores’ own gift to the “paragon of beauty, sense and virtue” Frederick had described his sister to be. The marriage had lifted Frederick’s worldly prospects higher than the Hales had ever dared hope: he had been made junior partner at Barbour & Co., one of the largest trading houses in all of Spain. Margaret smiled, then sighed, remembering her old fiery tirades against trade—her gallant, knightly brother turned a merchant now! She shook off the silly, unfair instinct to lump a proud Spanish trading partner in with the Milton mill owners she’d once scorned. Trade or no trade, Frederick was happy, and Dolores must be every bit as charming as her letters suggested.

That spring, Mr. Hale began to suffer from occasional spells of breathlessness that distressed him severely for as long as they lasted. Margaret was not overly alarmed, as they passed completely in the intervals, but she was determined to help him shake the habit entirely, so she pressed him hard to accept Mr. Bell’s invitation to spend April with him at Oxford. Bell had invited Margaret too, even writing a separate, playful special note commanding her to come, but she found the thought of staying home alone far more restful: no responsibilities, no one depending on her for comfort, no one to tend through illness. She had not known such unburdened quiet for more than two years.

When her father’s carriage pulled away for the railway station, the sheer lightness of being without anyone to care for nearly stunned her. She could be idle, silent, even miserable if she wished—all the private griefs and mistakes she had stuffed away in a dark corner of her mind for months now had room to be brought out, examined, and laid gently to rest. She sat almost motionless for hours in the drawing room, turning over every bitter memory without flinching, only crying aloud once when the sting of the lie she had told to protect Frederick pricked her again. She saw now how foolish, even useless that temptation had been; the lie had gained nothing, and faith in the power of truth would have been wiser by far.

Nervous and raw, her hand fell open a book of her father’s resting on the table, and old French words of gentle consolation leapt straight out at her, written for her exact state of self-reproach: I would not tear my heart to pieces in harsh self-loathing, it read. My poor heart, we have fallen into the pit we swore so hard to escape. Rise up, leave it behind forever, beg for God’s mercy, and trust that it will hold us up so we may be stronger now, and set our feet back on the path of humility. Courage, little heart. Margaret breathed the words to herself. That was what she had missed.

Rising, she determined to find some small task to pull her out of her own dark thoughts, and called Martha in as the maid passed the drawing room on her way upstairs. After gentle prodding, Martha’s stiff, mechanical servant’s mask cracked, and she told Margaret the full story of how Mrs. Thornton had saved her and her sister when they were orphaned and destitute: how Thornton’s mother had sought them out, nursed Martha through a dangerous contagious fever in her own home, sent her to the seaside to recover, never fearing for her own health. “Miss Fanny was the only one afraid,” Martha added. “She ran off to visit the family of the gentleman she’s going to marry.”

Margaret was stunned to hear Fanny Thornton was engaged, to a wealthy older mill owner named Watson whose property lay far beyond Hayleigh, a very advantageous match. She fell quiet, catching herself about to wonder how Mr. Thornton felt about his sister’s marriage—an old silly habit she had slipped into lately, of measuring every bit of news related to him by how it might land, and she shook it off quickly.

The next day she taught the Boucher children their lessons, took a long walk, and ended by calling on Mary Higgins. To her surprise, Nicholas was already home from work, the lengthening spring twilight fooling her into thinking it was far earlier in the evening than it was. He too seemed to have stepped onto that path of humility Margaret had been thinking of: quieter, less brash, no trace of his old insolence. He teased her about her father travelling off to Oxford, and assured her he never shirked a minute of his work at Thornton’s mill. “Your Thornton’s good enough to fight with, but too good a man to cheat,” he told her. He called his youngest little boy out, who proceeded to recite one loud, rumbustious Methodist hymn after another, his cadence as dramatic as a Member of Parliament making a speech. Nicholas, who had once scoffed at all religion out of hand, looked on with quiet, unmistakeable pleasure.

Margaret got home past her usual tea time, but no one was waiting impatiently for her, no one’s plans were ruined. She sat sorting through a large packet of old letters that evening, picking out which ones to keep and which to destroy, and came across the stack of Henry Lennox’s letters all related to Frederick’s case. She reread them carefully at first to confirm exactly how slim the last chance of clearing her brother’s name had been, then noticed the stiffness and cold cleverness of the wording: no trace of the old personal regard he had once held for her, every line strictly professional, no warmth to be found anywhere. She set them carefully aside to keep, valuable legal documents, and fell into a reverie. Two days of quiet had already refreshed her, the morbid fog falling away from her eyes. The future no longer looked impossibly bleak; if she could only win back Mr. Thornton’s friendship, if he would only keep calling on her father as he once had, even if she never spoke a word to him again, she thought her life could stretch out calm and even ahead of her. She sighed as she rose for bed, but a small, lingering ache of sorrow still lay heavy at her heart.

That same April evening, miles away in Oxford, Mr. Hale was thinking of Margaret just as persistently. He had wandered all his old familiar haunts at his small old college, far more warmly welcomed by his old friends than he had dared hope. Though some might have been shocked by his choice to leave the church, the sight of the quiet, sensitive man they had known in their youth washed all their abstract disapproval away, and they took him into their hearts with the tender protective kindness one might show a gentle, beloved sister. Bell teased him gently for being worn thin by Milton’s sharp air, but Mr. Hale shook his head. “Even if I had foreseen every bit of suffering my choice would bring my family,” he told Bell earnestly, “I would never have denied my conscience. I would never take that step back.” His mind turned then to Margaret, and he fretted aloud over what would become of her if he died, imagining her going to live with Edith and the Lennoxes in London. Bell swore on the spot he would take care of her. “Your daughter is my goddaughter, and she has nearly all my heart,” he said. “I will be her preux chevalier, sixty and gouty though I am. All I have is hers, whenever she needs it.”

That night, Mr. Hale laid his head down on his pillow, and his still, tender heart ceased beating in his sleep. There was no struggle, no pain, and the servant who came to wake him the next morning found his face calm, white, and beautiful under the unmistakeable seal of death.

Bell was stunned past speech for a long while, before he roused himself, snapped at his man for daring to suggest a coroner’s inquest, and ordered a carpet bag packed in five minutes flat. He would take the very next train to Milton, to break the terrible news to Margaret. He stumbled onto the train, collapsed into his seat, and had scarcely closed his eyes to try and wrap his head around the fact that his old friend was gone, when he spotted a man sitting across from him, half hidden behind a spread copy of *The Times*. It was Mr. Thornton. Bell clambered over to his side, shook his hand hard enough to squeeze tears out from under his grizzled eyelashes, and told him the news, that Hale was dead, that he was on his way to Milton to tell his daughter.

For more than a quarter of an hour, Thornton could not find his voice at all. At last he managed to speak, thick and unsteady. “And her?”

“Margaret? I am going to her,” Bell said. “Poor old Hale could speak of no one else last night. I told him I would be her protector, and I will. I would take a live dragon into my home as a chaperon if it meant Margaret could live with me as my own daughter. But there are the Lennoxes, you see. Smart London folk, who will no doubt think they have the first claim on her. And that young barrister Henry Lennox, who has been chasing Margaret for five years, held back only by lack of fortune. Now he has made his way in the world, you may be sure he will press his suit.”

Thornton leaned forward, too earnestly curious to notice the seeming impertinence of his question. “How?”
