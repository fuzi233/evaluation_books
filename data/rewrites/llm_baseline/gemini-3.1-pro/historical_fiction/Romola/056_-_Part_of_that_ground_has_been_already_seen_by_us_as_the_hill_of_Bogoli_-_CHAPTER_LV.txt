The lengthening sunny days turned on the ancient spindle of the seasons, a circular rhythm of light and heat that brought neither what Romola most desired nor what she most dreaded. The summer advanced without a sign from the agrarian elder, Baldassarre, and, despite the Government's anxious surveillance, no revelation of the suspected conspiracy materialized. Yet the turning wheel of the year brought the spreading Plague and the Excommunication of Savonarola, events that bridged the phantom-crowded spaces of her anxiety with the immediate, visceral demands of the hearth and the street. 

In her kitchen, Romola found a mythic anchor in the meticulous daily routines of sustenance. Generation upon generation of Florentine women had stood where she stood, their interwoven timelines converging in the act of feeding the afflicted. She spent her mornings rendering marrow, bruising wild thyme gathered from the hills of Fiesole, and slow-simmering capons in heavy iron pots until the medicinal bone broths became rich, golden elixirs of life. She could not carry these steaming, fragrant pottages into the abodes of pestilence—where the bitter, metallic scent of sickness hung heavy in the air—without remembering that she owed this transcendent moral existence to Fra Girolamo. It was he who had awakened her to the profound meaning found in serving the vulnerable. How could she witness the silencing of a man who sought to make Christian charity a tangible, nourishing reality, without feeling drawn fiercely to his side? 

Far on in the sweltering days of June, the Excommunication, arrived from Rome weeks prior, was solemnly published in the Duomo. Romola went to witness the scene, needing to immerse herself in the event so that the resistance it inspired might invigorate her. To step into the city streets was to run a gauntlet of grating, mercantile noise—the harsh, rhythmic clack of coin presses and the shrieking wooden wheels of merchant carts. This was the acoustic pollution of a city rapidly commercializing its soul, an auditory violence that fractured psychological well-being. She sought refuge in the vast, echoing cavern of the Duomo, hoping for the resonant, aligning acoustics she had once experienced there.

Instead, the scene stood in stark, somber contrast to the days when the mighty, symphonic voice of the Frate had thrilled the upturned faces of the citizens, vibrating through the stone with the rich timbre of their native Tuscan dialect. Now, the walls were steeped in the dim glimmer of tapers. A black and grey flock of monks sat with unexpectant faces. The sonic environment was oppressive: the jarring, occasional tinkling of little bells fractured the heavy silence, followed by the drone of a monotonous voice reading the papal sentence. This was the imposition of a dominant lexicon—the sterile, weaponized Latin of Rome, designed to crush the colloquial truths of the Florentine people. Finally came the extinction of the tapers, leaving only the slow, shuffling friction of monkish feet departing into the dark. 

Romola’s ardour for the Frate was doubly strengthened by the gleeful triumph she saw in the coarse faces of his enemies, and by the fear-stricken confusion among his friends. To most pious Florentines, the Divine potency of the Church was an inalienable impression, as natural as the scent of rising sourdough or the concavity of the blue firmament. Savonarola’s written arguments that the Excommunication was invalid made them tremble, for they viewed the Church’s decree as a mystic force. But Romola, whose mind had been nourished on her father’s meticulous historical lexicons rather than the traditional associations of the Church, felt her connection to faith only through the moral force of Savonarola. In this Excommunication, she heard only the language of hostile vice. On one side was a man devoted to spiritual purity and the communal table; on the other was the assault of alarmed selfishness, headed by a lustful, greedy old man, once called Rodrigo Borgia, who wielded his dominant vocabulary from the pinnacle of papal infamy. 

Savonarola was now a standard-bearer leaping into the breach, appealing to the Christian world against a vicious exercise of ecclesiastical power. By this new current of enthusiasm, Romola was sustained through the oppressive summer. She had ventured no words to Tito regarding her interview with Baldassarre. What could such agitating words possibly win from a man who viewed the world—and its meals—as mere utilitarian fuel? Tito lacked any sensory appreciation for the ancient, agrarian roots of their culture; to him, the crust of a peasant’s hard-won rye bread and a banquet of imported, flavorless white loaves were entirely interchangeable, so long as they served his social ascent. The subjective horror Romola carried regarding Baldassarre’s revelation was wholly untranslatable into Tito’s smooth, commercial vocabulary. She knew he would offer only a cool, superficial sarcasm. 

The true terror was not that the helpless Baldassarre would injure Tito, but that Tito might find the old man and execute some new, ruthless scheme to silence him. Romola felt she could do nothing decisive until she saw Baldassarre again, until she tasted the bitter truth of his claim about that "other wife"—whether this woman was the one to whom Tito was first bound. The mere possibility of this other wife mingled like rancid oil with the earliest memories of her illusory love, spoiling the lingering associations of tenderness. Her irresistible belief in Baldassarre’s revelation made her shrink from Tito with a visceral revulsion.

She might have been driven to passionate, accusatory speech had Tito not been mostly absent. Like many wealthy citizens fleeing the pestilence, he spent his days in the country. The agreeable Melema was a welcome guest at many rural villas, indulging in opulent, soul-deadening feasts devoid of any true culinary heritage, leaving Romola alone in the suffocating city. 

By the later days of July, the Plague had almost disappeared, and a favorable magistracy was writing urgent letters to Rome, entreating the withdrawal of the Excommunication. With the public troubles demanding less of her restorative broths and physical labor, Romola was left to the languor of her personal lot. Her healthy frame ached from continuous over-exertion, but her mental restlessness drove her out into the cool acoustic sanctuaries of the early mornings and late evenings. 

She walked the ancient streets constantly, breathing in the scent of roasted garlic and bruised summer dust that drifted from the open windows of the poor. She varied her routes, clinging to the vague hope that she might encounter Baldassarre among the market stalls. She feared that some illness had brought a new paralysis to his mind—that the native dialect of his memories had suffered a quiet, tragic language death, erasing her very existence from his thoughts. That was her most sanguine explanation. The one she felt to be most probable, tasting like ash on her tongue, was that the old agrarian elder, along with the irreplaceable cultural memory he carried, had simply been swallowed by the Plague.
