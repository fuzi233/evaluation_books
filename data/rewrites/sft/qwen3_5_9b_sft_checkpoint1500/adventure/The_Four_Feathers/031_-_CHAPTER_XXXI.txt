It was an August morning, the same season as before, yet the world felt shifted. Harry Feversham rode across the Lennon bridge into Ramelton. The fierce, baking suns of Sudan had etched deep lines into his face, and the grueling years of his probation had carved a permanent hardness into his soul. Yet, as he climbed the narrow streets of the town, he remained unseen, a shadow passing through time.

At the crest of the hill, he veered onto the broad highway, a straight ribbon cutting through valleys and ridges toward Letterkenny. He spurred his horse forward, moving swiftly within a silent caravan of ghosts. These spirits were not the vague, fading dreams of a man lost to regret; they were the heavy, tangible realities of his past. The intervening years—the travels across Egypt and Europe—had slipped like sand through his fingers, becoming distant and insubstantial. But this small Irish town was different. It was the anchor point where his greatest joy and his deepest shame had collided. Here, he had been beloved; here, he had destroyed lives.

His horse slowed near the church, a stately monument perched high on the slope to his right. A sudden question bloomed in his mind: *Is Ethne still here?* Had Old Dermod survived? Would the doors of home stand open? But the illusion shattered instantly. He sat atop his steed in the middle of an empty road, bathed in the pale, crystalline silence of an August dawn. Above, larks trilled in the azure sky; below, a landrail shrieked from the meadow, and a rooster crowed from the valley floor. Realities of sound and sight grounded him. He urged his horse onward, down the incline and up the next rise, riding again with his phantom retinue—ghosts of laughter, of shared walks, of words spoken long ago.

Eventually, the road opened into a thick grove, marked by a broken fence and a gateway hanging ajar. Ignoring the weeds choking the driveway, he pushed through. At the end lay an open space before a house that was little more than a skeleton. The crumbling walls and roofless chambers jolted him from his reverie. He dismounted, tethered his horse to a gnarled branch, and sprinted into the ruins.

"Ethne! Dermod!" he called, his voice echoing uselessly.

Silence answered. Room after room was empty, dust motes dancing in shafts of light where furniture used to sit. He plunged into the garden, expecting a figure to step from behind a shrub, but found only uncut grass and flowerbeds choked with wild growth. The evidence of abandonment was absolute. With a sharp kick, he spurred his horse back the way he had come.

In Ramelton, he sought shelter at the local inn, handing over his mount to the ostler and ordering lunch. The landlady, a woman whose chin rested heavily on her hands, approached with a tray.

"So Lennon House has been burned down?" Feversham asked, staring at the menu. "When was that?"

"Just five years ago, sir," she replied, her tone shifting instantly to storytelling mode. "Right in the middle of summer. It was a terrible tragedy. Poor Bastable got drunk and started the fire, ruining the whole Eustace family. And poor Dermod Eustace... well, he died at Glenalla shortly after." She leaned in, her eyes gleaming with gossip. "We're hoping to rebuild soon, though. Everyone says it'll happen when Miss Eustace gets married."

Feversham offered no comment.

"And where does Miss Eustace live now?" he asked quietly.

"Glenalla," she said promptly. "About halfway to Rathmullen, there's a track leading off to the left. It's a bleak mountain village, truly not fit for a lady like her. Might I suggest a visit?"

"Yes," Feversham said, rising abruptly. "Please send my horse around to the door."

Before he could leave, the landlady stepped into his path, blocking his exit with a look of mild triumph. "Well, that is curious—that is highly curious. Only a fortnight ago, a gentleman arrived here. He was burnt just as black as yourself. He asked for Miss Eustace's address and rode straight up to Glenalla. Perhaps you have some business with her?"

"Yes," Feversham confirmed, his voice steady. "I have business with Miss Eustace. Please arrange for my horse."

As he waited, flipping idly through the register, he spotted a name under late July: *Colonel Trench*.

"You will return tonight, sir?" the landlady asked, standing by the door as he prepared to leave.

"No," Feversham answered, mounting his horse. "I do not intend to return to Ramelton." He crossed the Lennon bridge once more, heading toward the track that wound past a small bay of the lake. Four miles further, he turned onto a dirt path leading past a cluster of white cottages, up into a hollow where the hills turned purple in the distance.

It was nearly five o'clock when he reached Glenalla. The village sprawled aimlessly, devoid of planning, composed of scattered huts separated by gaps of field. Beyond them stood a small plantation of larches, guarding a solitary house. Further on, another gap revealed the shimmering surface of the Lough, dotted with white gulls. Finally, he emerged onto a tiny plateau dominated by a small, grey church, standing stark against the wind. A path of white shell-dust led from the church door to a low wooden gate.

As he reached the gate, a collie dog erupted from behind it, barking fiercely. Feversham halted. The dog, grey around the muzzle, bore a distinctive mark on its leg. Feversham stared at it, then at the open door of the church, and dismounted. He tied his horse to the fence and stepped into the yard.

The dog sniffed cautiously, pushing its nose into Feversham's knee, before exploding into a frenzy of joy. It circled him, tail a blur, barking excitedly, leaping up to nuzzle his shoulders, then racing to the church door to bark furiously before returning to jump at his legs again. This creature possessed a far superior memory to the innkeeper. Feversham caught the dog as it planted its paws on his chest, patting it gently and laughing softly.

Then, the laughter died in his throat. He froze, his gaze fixed on the open doorway. Standing there, framed by the ancient wood, was Ethne Eustace.

He released the dog and walked slowly up the shell-dusted path. She stood motionless on the threshold, neither speaking nor moving, watching him approach with an intensity that silenced the air between them.

"Harry," she said. Just the single word.

And then, silence fell again. Neither spoke. They stood there, measuring the six years that had passed since his feet had crushed the gravel beneath her window. To the casual observer, little would have changed, but to them, the transformation was stark.

Feversham was thin, his frame gaunt, his eyes sunken deep with exhaustion. The relentless pressure of his life in the House of Stone had aged him far beyond his years, leaving a weary gravity to his posture. But Ethne saw deeper. His spirit, she realized, had not been broken by bitterness. He returned without a trace of anger regarding the fourth feather snapped from her fan. Instead, he carried a quiet, unshakeable self-possession. The timid boy who had once flinched from every judgment, perpetually fearing contempt, was gone. In his stead stood a man who had undergone a terrible trial and emerged knowing he had not failed.

Her heart ached for the sorrows he must have endured, but her eyes cleared with understanding. She saw the steel in his resolve.

"It is not all harm that has come of these years," she said finally, her voice softening. "They were not wasted."

Feversham looked at her, noting the subtle changes. She had not aged drastically; her large grey eyes still shone with their former brilliance, and color remained on her cheeks. Yet, there was depth in her features now. She had suffered profoundly, having tasted the bitter fruit of knowledge.

"I am sorry," he said, his voice rough. "I did you a great wrong six years ago, and I need not."

She extended her hand. "Will you give it to me, please?"

For a heartbeat, confusion clouded Feversham's brow. "Which feather?"

"The fourth one," she clarified.

Reaching into his coat, he pulled out his letter case and shook two feathers into the palm of her hand. The larger one, the magnificent ostrich plume, he held out first.

"Take both," she insisted.

"There was no necessity for you to keep Castleton's feather any longer," he noted.

"There was no reason why you should keep it either," she countered, clasping them tightly against her chest. A smile touched her lips, transforming into a genuine laugh. "Do with them!" she cried, the scorn vanishing. "I shall do nothing with them. I am very proud to have them to keep."

Just as Feversham might have crushed them into the dust of the path, dismissing them as mere symbols of guilt that had served their purpose, Ethne held them dear. To her, they were not dead things; they were proof of survival.
