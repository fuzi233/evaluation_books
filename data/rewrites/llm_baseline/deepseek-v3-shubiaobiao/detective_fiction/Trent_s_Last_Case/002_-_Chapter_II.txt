The telephone on Sir James Molloy’s desk buzzed sharply in the well-appointed office of the *Record*. With a flick of his pen, Sir James signaled to his secretary, Mr. Silver, who crossed the room and lifted the receiver.

“Who is this?” Silver demanded. “I can’t hear—oh, Mr. Bunner? Yes, but Sir James is—” He hesitated, then placed the receiver before his employer. “Calvin Bunner, Manderson’s man. Insists it’s urgent.”

Sir James took the call with a frown. “Well?” he barked into the line. Then, as he listened, his expression darkened. “Good God,” he muttered. He rose slowly, gripping the telephone as if it might escape him. “Yes. Yes.”

A glance at the clock sent him into motion. “Silver—fetch Figgis and Williams. Now.”

The secretary vanished. Sir James Molloy was not a man easily shaken—a towering, sharp-eyed Irishman of fifty, his shrewdness masked by an air of polished charm. He wielded the *Record* and the *Sun* like twin blades, cutting through pretense with ruthless precision. His staff respected him, not out of fear, but because he demanded excellence and rewarded it in kind.

“You’re certain?” Sir James pressed into the phone. “How long have the police known? And the servants—surely it’s all over the estate by now.” A pause. “Very well. Bunner, you’ve done me a service. I won’t forget it.”

He hung up and snatched a railway timetable, scanning it before tossing it aside with a curse. Silver returned with Figgis, the paper’s steely-eyed senior reporter, and Williams, a junior with the restless energy of a hound on a scent.

“Figgis, take this down,” Sir James ordered, pacing. “For the *Sun*’s special edition. Manderson’s been murdered.”

Figgis’s pencil moved without hesitation.

“Shot through the left eye, point-blank. Body found near a shed on his estate at White Gables. No robbery, but signs of a struggle. Local doctor’s handling the post-mortem. Police have no leads.”

Sir James turned to Silver. “Wire our man in Marlstone—tell him to drop everything and get there. No details in the message. Williams, alert Anthony at the *Sun*—two columns, front page. And have Miss Morgan see me.”

The young man vanished. Figgis lingered. “Should we mention Scotland Yard’s involvement?”

“If you like,” Sir James said absently.

Miss Morgan entered—composed, efficient. “Mrs. Manderson isn’t the fainting type,” she remarked, overhearing. “If anything, she’ll be aiding the police.”

Sir James smirked. “Cut the ‘prostrated widow’ angle, Figgis. Off you go.”

To Miss Morgan, he said, “We’ll need the Manderson biography updated.”

“It’s ready,” she replied. “Portraits too—Trent’s sketch is best, but the public prefers bad photos.”

Sir James sighed. “Do make a mistake someday, Miss Morgan. For my sake.”

She left as the telephone rang again. “Trent?” Sir James barked into the receiver.

A irritable voice crackled back. “I’m painting. This had better be worth it.”

“Manderson’s been murdered.”

A pause. Then, grudgingly: “Interesting. But if some tramp shot him after being kicked off the property, I won’t weep.”

Sir James grinned. “You’re going.”

Another pause. “...Fine. But I’m taking the midnight train. And book me a room.”

As Sir James hung up, a commotion erupted outside. Newsboys sprinted down Fleet Street, their cries rising above the din:

Sir James chuckled, jingling the coins in his pocket. “Makes a fine headline.”

And so the world learned of Manderson’s end.
