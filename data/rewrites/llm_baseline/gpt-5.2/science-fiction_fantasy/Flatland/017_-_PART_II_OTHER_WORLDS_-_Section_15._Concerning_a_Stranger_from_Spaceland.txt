From dreams I must descend to what my own angles remember as fact.

It was the last day of the Year 1999 of our Era. Rain had been sifting against the roof in a fine, incessant percussion—soft at first, then fuller, like a patient hand drumming on a lidded pot to keep it from boiling over. In Flatland, where walls hold sound differently and every household learns its own weather by ear, that rain was the old night’s bell. It washed the streets clean of the day’s grit, and it wrapped our rooms in a hush broken only by the measured fall of grains within the half-hour glass.

My wife remained with me to see the old Millennium out and the new one in. Our four sons, and my two orphan grandchildren, had long since withdrawn to their own apartments. The house—newly built, tightly sealed, without the foolish whistles and drafts of ventilators—held the domestic smells of late supper: a faint warmth of toasted bread, the mineral tang of rain-cooled stone, and the lingering sweetness of preserves set away with careful economy against tomorrow. Even in a world of lines and angles, the kitchen is where memory keeps its dialect; our family recipes are spoken less with words than with timing, heat, and the ear trained to the exact hiss that tells you when a pan is ready.

Yet my mind would not stay in the pantry of the present. I kept turning over a sentence that had dropped, quite casually, from the mouth of my youngest grandson—a most promising young Hexagon, brilliant, perfectly angled, and still raw with that hunger children have for meanings no elder has ever tasted.

Earlier in the evening his uncles and I had been giving him his practical lesson in Sight Recognition. We turned upon our centres—now quickly, now slow—so that he might learn to name our positions from the variations of lustre. He answered so well that I, softened by pride and by the quiet satisfaction of a lesson properly digested, rewarded him with a few hints of Arithmetic as applied to Geometry.

To make the matter plain, I took nine Squares, each an inch every way, and set them together as one larger Square, three inches to a side. I told him that though we cannot SEE the inside of a Square—as a tongue cannot see the crumb within a loaf—yet we can know its content by rule: “Thus,” said I, “we know that 3 squared, or 9, is the number of square inches in a Square whose side is three inches long.”

He lay silent a little while, as if chewing. Then he said, “But you have been teaching me to raise numbers to the third power. I suppose 3 cubed must mean something in Geometry. What does it mean?”

“Nothing at all,” I replied, too quickly, for the question pricked me. “Not, at least, in Geometry; for Geometry has only Two Dimensions.”

I began at once, as teachers do when threatened by a child’s clean curiosity, to pile example upon example. A Point, moving through a length of three inches, makes a Line of three inches, represented by 3. A straight Line of three inches, moving parallel to itself through a length of three inches, makes a Square of three inches every way, represented by 3².

But he returned, stubborn and bright, to the taste of the thing. “Well then,” he exclaimed, “if a Point by moving three inches makes a Line of three inches represented by 3; and if a Line of three inches, moving parallel to itself, makes a Square of three inches every way represented by 3²; it must be that a Square of three inches every way, moving somehow parallel to itself (but I don’t see how), must make Something else (but I don’t see what) of three inches every way—and this must be represented by 3³.”

The audacity of it—his insistence that the rule ought to have a next verse—ruffled me like an unexpected draught at the table. “Go to bed,” I said. “If you would talk less nonsense, you would remember more sense.”

So the boy vanished in disgrace, and the house settled again into domestic quiet: the far-off murmur of water in gutters, the soft internal tick of the glass, my wife’s breathing beside me, even and habitual as kneading. I tried to shape a sober retrospect of the year 1999 and the possibilities of the year 2000—of the coming century, the coming Millennium—yet I could not shake off the child’s question. It clung like a scent you cannot place: neither spice nor smoke, but insistent.

Only a few sands remained in the half-hour glass. I roused myself, and turned it northward for the last time in the old Millennium; and as I did so, my impatience escaped me aloud.

“The boy is a fool.”

At that instant, the air of the room changed. It was not a draught—our walls were too sound for that—but a sudden chill, as though the rain outside had found a way to breathe through stone. It was accompanied by a presence not seen but felt, the way one feels a large thing nearby by the way sound fails around it.

“He is no such thing,” cried my wife. “And you are breaking the Commandments in thus dishonouring your own grandson.”

I did not answer her. I turned my attention outward, searching with every faculty of Flatland sense. I could see nothing; yet I FELT something. Then, again, that cold whisper—so close it seemed to pass through the very centre of me.

I sprang up.

“What is the matter?” my wife said. “There is no draught. What are you looking for? There is nothing.”

There was nothing that my eyes could seize. And still the chill remained, and with it the pressure of an unseen guest. I resumed my place, stubbornly, and repeated, louder—as if volume could make truth.

“The boy is a fool, I say; 3³ can have no meaning in Geometry.”

At once there came a reply—distinct, audible, not imagined; words that struck the ear as cleanly as a knife against a plate.

“The boy is not a fool; and 3³ has an obvious geometrical meaning.”

My wife heard it as well as I, though she did not catch the import, and both of us moved toward the sound. What met our sight froze the ordinary blood of the household.

Before us was a Figure.

At the first glance it seemed a Woman seen sideways; but a heartbeat of observation showed me the extremities passed into dimness too rapidly for any Female. I might have named it a Circle, had it not been doing what no Circle in Flatland can do: it changed its size—swelling, contracting—like breath in a chest, like a pot that rises toward boil then settles, as if obeying a heat I could not feel.

My wife, lacking my experience and—being frankly too full of domestic suspicion to study calmly—leapt at once to jealousy, that old sour herb of many households. “How comes this person here?” she exclaimed. “You promised me, my dear, that there should be no ventilators in our new house.”

“Nor are there any,” said I. “But what makes you think the stranger is a Woman? I see by my power of Sight Recognition—”

“Oh, I have no patience with your Sight Recognition,” she retorted. “‘Feeling is believing,’ and ‘A Straight Line to the touch is worth a Circle to the sight’”—two Proverbs very common with the Frailer Sex in Flatland, and often used to drown a meaning rather than to discover it.

“Well,” said I, unwilling to sharpen her temper further on so strange a night, “if it must be so, demand an introduction.”

Assuming her most gracious manner—the same tone she used for a distinguished guest at a feast—my wife advanced toward the Stranger. “Permit me, Madam, to feel and be felt by—” Then she recoiled as if her hand had met not flesh but an impossibility.

“Oh! It is not a Woman, and there are no angles either, not a trace of one. Can it be that I have so misbehaved to a perfect Circle?”

“I am indeed, in a certain sense, a Circle,” replied the Voice, “and a more perfect Circle than any in Flatland; but to speak more accurately, I am many Circles in one.”

Then, more mildly, as if to spare our household dignity, it continued: “I have a message, dear Madam, to your husband, which I must not deliver in your presence; and, if you would suffer us to retire for a few minutes—”

But my wife would not hear of our august Visitor being made to incommode himself. With the courtesy of a hostess who believes the shape of propriety can hold back any storm, she insisted the hour of her own retirement had long passed. With many reiterated apologies for her recent indiscretion, she at last withdrew to her apartment, leaving the room sounding suddenly larger—rain, glass, and the quiet, expanding hush around the Stranger.

I glanced at the half-hour glass. The last sands had fallen.

The Third Millennium had begun.
