As the last wisps of sunlight succumbed to the encroaching night, casting the manor's facade in a deep, foreboding shadow, General Spiess stood at the window, his eyes fixed upon the figure of his ward, Carmilla, reclining upon the chaise longue. Her fragile form, a testament to the ravages of some unseen force, stirred his paternal instincts to their depths. The physician who had been attending her thus far had proven ineffective, and Spiess's anxiety had grown with each passing day.

He had summoned a second opinion, a learned doctor from the distant city of Gratz, whose reputation for wisdom and piety preceded him. As he awaited the arrival of this newcomer, Spiess's thoughts wandered to the countless hours he had spent at Carmilla's bedside, the weight of his responsibility bearing down upon him like a physical force. The door to the library creaked open, and Spiess's thoughts were interrupted by the soft murmur of hushed conversation. He entered to find the two physicians engaged in a heated debate, their words dripping with an intensity that belied the gravity of the situation.

"Sir," Spiess's first physician ventured, his tone tinged with a mixture of exasperation and amusement, "it seems your esteemed colleague believes we require a conjurer rather than a healer."

The doctor from Gratz, his features a mask of studious introspection, looked up from the papers scattered across the table. "Pardon me, Monsieur le General, but I shall reserve my thoughts for another time. Alas, I fear my skills and knowledge are of little use in this instance."

Spiess's disappointment was palpable as he bid the doctors adieu, his footsteps echoing through the hallway as he retreated to the garden. The night air, heavy with the scent of blooming flowers, did little to calm his racing thoughts. It was then that the doctor from Gratz caught up with him, his expression grave.

"I could not depart without speaking with you further, Monsieur le General," he began, his voice low and measured. "In my professional opinion, I am convinced that we are dealing with no ordinary illness. The symptoms displayed by your ward are nothing short of inexplicable. I fear that death draws near, and yet...there remains a glimmer of hope. With great care and skill, it is possible that her strength might return, but the window of opportunity is perilously narrow."
