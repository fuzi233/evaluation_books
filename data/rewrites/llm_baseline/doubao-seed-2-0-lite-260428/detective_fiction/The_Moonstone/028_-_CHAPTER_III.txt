Out of pure consideration for my poor Lady Verinder, I would not breathe a single hint that I had already guessed the sad, heavy truth before she herself spoke it aloud. I waited for her pleasure in quiet silence, having already privately braced myself to speak gentle, sustaining words the moment the first suitable chance arose—ready to meet any duty that fell to me, no matter how painful it might prove.
“I have been seriously ill, Drusilla, for some time past,” my aunt began. “And, strange to say, I did not know it myself.”

My mind flew at once to the thousands upon thousands of perishing human souls walking the earth at that very moment, spiritually sick and none the wiser. I feared greatly that my poor aunt might be one of their number. “Yes, dear,” I said, sadly. “Yes.”

“I brought Rachel to London, as you know, to seek medical advice,” she went on. “I thought it proper to consult two doctors.”
Two doctors! Oh, for shame—with Rachel in that fragile, distracted state, not one clergyman had been called in at all! “Yes, dear?” I prompted, softly. “Yes?”

“One of the two medical men was a stranger to me. The other had been an old friend of my husband’s, and had always felt a sincere regard for me for his sake. After he had given Rachel her prescription, he said he wished to speak to me privately in another room. I expected, of course, to get special directions for how best to manage my daughter’s health. To my complete surprise, he took me gravely by the hand and said, ‘I have been looking at you, Lady Verinder, with both a professional and a personal interest. I am afraid you stand in far more urgent need of medical advice than your daughter does.’ He asked me a series of questions I was at first inclined to brush off lightly, until I saw how distressed my answers made him. It ended with him arranging to come back the next day, bringing a fellow physician with him, at an hour when Rachel would be out of the house. The result of that visit—kindly, gently broken to me—left both doctors certain that precious time had already been lost beyond recovery, and that my case was now completely out of the reach of their art. For more than two years I have been suffering from an insidious form of heart disease, that never gave me a single alarm to warn me, but slowly, little by little, wore me down to my present state. I may live several months, or I may die before the sun rises on a new day—the doctors cannot, dare not, speak more certainly than that. It would be foolish to pretend I have not had some very miserable moments since I learned the truth of my condition. But I am more resigned now than I was, and I am working hard to get all my worldly affairs in order. My one great anxiety is that Rachel must never learn what ails me. If she knew, she would at once blame my broken health on the stress of the lost Diamond, and reproach herself bitterly, poor child, for what is not her fault in the slightest. Both doctors agree the damage to my heart began two, if not three years ago. I know you will keep my secret, Drusilla—for I can see real sorrow and sympathy for me written plain on your face.”

Sorrow and sympathy! What pagan emotions to expect from a Christian Englishwoman anchored firm in her faith! My poor aunt had no idea what a flood of devout thankfulness surged through me as she finished her melancholy story. A whole new career of useful work had opened up right before me! A beloved relative, a perishing fellow creature on the very eve of the great eternal change, was utterly unprepared—and Providence itself had led her to confide that fact to *me*. How can I describe the holy joy that filled my heart when I remembered that the trusted, faithful clerical friends I could call on were not a mere handful, but numbered in tens and twenties, all doing the Lord’s work of mercy day and night in the very neighbourhood my aunt lived in.
I drew my aunt into my arms—my overflowing tenderness could not be satisfied with anything less than a full embrace, then. “Oh!” I gushed at her, fervently. “The indescribable interest you inspire in me! Oh, the great good I mean to do you, dear, before we part!”
After a few earnest, gentle preliminary warnings, I offered her the choice of three of these precious holy friends: all of them labouring ceaselessly for the good of souls, all inexhaustible in their pious exhortations, all ready to come running at a single word from me.
Alas, the result was far from encouraging. Poor Lady Verinder looked both puzzled and frightened, and met all I said with the purely worldly objection that she was far too weak to face meeting strangers. I yielded—for the moment only, of course. My long experience as a Reader and Visitor working under no less than fourteen beloved clerical colleagues over the years told me this was exactly a case that needed preparation first, through good books. I owned a small private library of works perfectly suited to this emergency, every one of them chosen to arouse, convince, prepare, enlighten, and fortify my aunt’s soul.
“You will read for me, dear, won’t you?” I asked, in my most winning, gentle tone. “You will read, if I bring you my own most precious books? Turned down at all the right pages, aunt. And marked in pencil right where you should stop and ask yourself, ‘Does this apply to me?’”
Even that simple appeal—so absolutely heathenising is the corrupting influence of the world—seemed to startle her. She said “I will do what I can, Drusilla, to please you,” with a look of surprised bewilderment that was at once instructive and terrible to behold.

Not a moment could be wasted. The mantel clock told me I had just enough time to hurry home, pick out a first set of suitable readings (a dozen, for a start), and return in time to meet the lawyer and witness Lady Verinder sign her Will. I promised faithfully I would be back by five o’clock, and left the house on my errand of mercy.
When no interests but my own are at stake, I am humbly content to travel by omnibus. Let me record the extent of my devotion to my aunt’s spiritual good by admitting that on this occasion, I committed the prodigal extravagance of taking a private cab. I drove home, selected and marked my first dozen devotional works, then drove back to Montagu Square, with the whole set stowed in a carpet-bag, the like of which I firmly believe does not exist in the religious literature of any other country in Europe.
I paid the cabman his exact fare. He received it with an oath—so I immediately pressed a tract into his hand. If I had held a pistol to his head, that abandoned wretch could not have looked more horrified. He scrambled back up onto his box, shouted a string of profane, dismayed exclamations, and drove off at a furious pace. Quite useless to run away, I am happy to say! I sowed the good seed anyway, by tossing a second tract through the open window of his moving cab.

The footman who answered the door (not the vain, worldly maid with the over-ornate cap ribbons, to my great relief) told me the doctor had called, and was still shut in with Lady Verinder. Mr. Bruff, the family solicitor, had arrived a minute earlier and was waiting in the library. I was shown in to wait there too.
Mr. Bruff looked very surprised to see me. He was an old man, grizzled and grown grey in the service of the world, a man I grieve to say served Law and Mammon equally as his chosen prophets in work hours, and in his leisure time was just as happy reading a frivolous novel as he was tearing a perfectly good tract to shreds.
“Have you come to move in and stay, Miss Clack?” he asked, glancing pointedly at my carpet bag.
To reveal the precious contents of that bag to such a man would only have invited an outburst of profanity. I lowered myself to his worldly level, and told him why I was there. “My aunt has told me she is about to sign her Will,” I said. “She has been kind enough to ask me to be one of the witnesses.”
“Aye, aye, that will do, Miss Clack,” he nodded. “You’re over twenty-one, and you haven’t the slightest pecuniary interest in Lady Verinder’s Will.”
Not the slightest pecuniary interest! Oh, how thankful I was to hear that! If my wealthy aunt had remembered poor me, to whom even five pounds is a welcome gift—if my name had been written in the Will with even a small comforting legacy attached—my enemies might have doubted that my whole reason for bringing my choicest spiritual works and splurging on a cab was pure devotion. Now not even the cruelest scoffer among them could ever doubt my motives. It was far, far better this way!

I was roused from these comforting reflections by Mr. Bruff’s voice. My quiet meditative silence seemed to weigh on the spirits of this worldling, and force him to talk to me against his own will. “Well, Miss Clack, what’s the latest news in your charitable circles? How is your friend Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite, after that nasty beating he got from the rogues in Northumberland Street? Egad! They’re telling a very fine story about that charitable gentleman over at my club!”
I had chosen to overlook his earlier rude remarks about my age and my lack of stake in the Will, but the dismissive tone he used to speak of dear Mr. Godfrey was far too much for my forbearance. I knew I was bound, after the scene I had witnessed that very afternoon, to assert my admirable friend’s innocence any time it was called into question—and I will own I was equally determined to deliver a stinging, well-deserved rebuke to Mr. Bruff in the process.
“I live very much out of the world,” I said, “and I do not have the advantage of belonging to a club, sir. But I know exactly the story you are referring to—and I also know that no viler falsehood has ever been told against an innocent man.”
“Of course you believe in your friend, Miss Clack. That’s perfectly natural,” he scoffed. “But Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite won’t find the rest of the world half so easy to convince as a committee of pious charitable ladies. Appearances are dead against him. He was in the house the very night the Diamond was lost. And he was the very first person from the house to travel to London afterwards. Those are very ugly circumstances, ma’am, when you look at them in the light of later events.”
I ought, I know, to have corrected him right then and there, told him he was speaking in complete ignorance of testimony to Mr. Godfrey’s innocence that came from the one person who was undeniably qualified to speak from absolute, first-hand knowledge. Alas, the temptation to artfully lead the lawyer on right into his own discomfiture was too much for my fallen human nature. I put on a face of perfect, innocent confusion, and asked him what he meant by “later events.”
“By later events, Miss Clack, I mean the events that involve those Indian jugglers,” he went on, growing more and more smugly superior to me the longer he spoke. “The second those Indians are let out of Frizinghall prison, they go straight to London and set their sights on Mr. Luker. What happens next? Mr. Luker gets terrified for the safety of a ‘valuable of great price’ he keeps in his house. He secretly deposits it, under a vague general description, in his bankers’ strongroom. Very clever of him—but the Indians are just as clever. They suspect the ‘valuable of great price’ has been moved, so they come up with a singularly bold, foolproof way to confirm their suspicions. Who do they seize and search? Not just Mr. Luker, which would make perfect sense—but Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite too. Why? Mr. Ablewhite’s explanation is that they acted on blind random suspicion, after seeing him speak to Mr. Luker by chance that morning. Absurd! Half a dozen other people spoke to Mr. Luker that same morning. Why didn’t they get followed home, lured into that same trap? No, no. The plain conclusion is that Mr. Ablewhite had his own private stake in that ‘valuable’ just as much as Mr. Luker did, and the Indians were so unsure which of the two was holding it, they had no choice but to search both of them. That’s what public opinion says, Miss Clack, and public opinion on this occasion is not easy to refute.”
He said those last words looking so wonderfully wise in his own worldly conceit, that I am ashamed to admit I could not resist leading him just a little further before I overwhelmed him with the truth.
“I would never presume to argue with a clever lawyer like you,” I said. “But is it quite fair to Mr. Ablewhite to ignore the opinion of the famous London police officer who investigated this case? Sergeant Cuff never had even the shadow of a suspicion rest on anyone but Miss Verinder.”
“Do you mean to tell me you agree with the Sergeant?”
“I judge no one, sir, and I offer no opinion.”
“Well then, I commit both those enormities, ma’am,” he laughed. “I judge the Sergeant was utterly wrong. And I offer you this opinion: if he knew Rachel’s character as well as I do, he would have suspected every single person in that house but her. I won’t pretend she’s perfect—she’s secretive, stubborn, odd and wild, nothing like other girls her age. But she is true as steel, high-minded, generous to a fault. If the plainest, most damning evidence in the world pointed one way, and nothing but Rachel’s word of honour pointed the other, I would take her word over the evidence, lawyer as I am! Strong language, Miss Clack—but I mean every word.”
“Would you mind explaining that a little more clearly, Mr. Bruff, so I can be sure I understand you?” I asked. “Suppose you found Miss Verinder unaccountably, deeply interested in all that has happened to Mr. Ablewhite and Mr. Luker? Suppose she asked the strangest questions about this dreadful scandal, and showed completely ungovernable agitation when she learned how the story was turning against him?”
“Suppose whatever you like, Miss Clack, it won’t shake my belief in Rachel Verinder by a single hair’s breadth.”
“She is that absolutely to be relied on?”
“Absolutely.”
“Then allow me to inform you, Mr. Bruff, that Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite was in this very house not two hours ago, and that his entire innocence of any part in the disappearance of the Moonstone was proclaimed by Miss Verinder herself, in the strongest language I have ever heard a young lady use in my life.”
I enjoyed the triumph—an unholy triumph, I fear I must confess—of watching Mr. Bruff utterly confounded, completely overthrown, by those few plain words from me. He jumped to his feet, stared at me in total silence. I sat perfectly still, unruffled, and related the whole scene exactly as it had played out.
“And what do you say about Mr. Ablewhite now?” I asked, as gently as I possibly could, the second I finished speaking.
“If Rachel has testified to his innocence, Miss Clack, I won’t scruple to say I believe in his innocence just as firmly as you do,” he said at once. “I was fooled by appearances, same as everyone else. I will make every possible atonement, by publicly contradicting this scandal that has attacked your friend every chance I get. And let me congratulate you, by the way, on the masterly way you opened fire on me with all your guns blazing the second I least expected it. You would have made a brilliant name in my profession, ma’am, if you had been born a man.”
He turned away then, and started pacing irritably up and down the library. I could see plainly that the new information I had given him had completely surprised and disturbed him. Little mutters fell from his lips as he sank deeper and deeper into thought, that showed he had been clinging to the vile, uncharitable theory that dear Mr. Godfrey had stolen the Diamond, and Rachel was only covering for him out of generous loyalty. Now that explanation was completely blown apart by Rachel’s own unassailable testimony, and the legal expert was completely lost.
“What a case!” I heard him mutter to himself, stopping at the window, drumming his fingers on the glass. “It doesn’t just defy explanation, it’s even beyond any reasonable conjecture.”
There was nothing in those words that required a reply from me—and yet, I replied anyway. It seems hardly credible that I could not just leave Mr. Bruff to his own thoughts even then. It seems almost beyond mere mortal perversity that I could find, in what he had just said, a new chance to make myself deliberately disagreeable to him. But ah, my friends! Nothing is beyond mortal perversity. Anything is possible when our fallen natures get the better of us!
“Pardon me for intruding on your reflections,” I said to the unsuspecting Mr. Bruff. “But surely there is one conjecture no one has thought of yet.”
“Maybe, Miss Clack. I admit I can’t think what it is.”
“Before I was lucky enough to convince you of Mr. Ablewhite’s innocence, you cited the fact he was in the house the night the Diamond was lost as one of your main reasons for suspecting him. Allow me to remind you that Mr. Franklin Blake was also staying in the house the very same night the Moonstone went missing.”
The old worldling turned away from the window, pulled a chair directly opposite mine, and stared at me steadily with a hard, vicious little smile.
“You are not nearly as good a lawyer as I thought you were, Miss Clack,” he said, meditatively. “You don’t know how to leave well enough alone.”
“I am afraid I don’t follow you, Mr. Bruff,” I said, meekly.
“It won’t work a second time, Miss Clack—it really won’t. Franklin Blake is a great favourite of mine, as you know perfectly well. But that doesn’t matter. I will go right ahead and adopt your line of thinking before you even get the chance to spring your little trap on me. You are quite right, ma’am. All the abstract reasons I used to suspect Mr. Ablewhite also justify suspecting Mr. Blake. Very well—let’s say we suspect them both. We can agree that it is quite in character for Franklin Blake to be capable of stealing the Moonstone.
