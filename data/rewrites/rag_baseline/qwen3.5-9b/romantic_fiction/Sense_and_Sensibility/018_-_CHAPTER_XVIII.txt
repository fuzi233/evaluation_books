Elinor observed the profound despondency in her brother's demeanor with a growing, gnagging anxiety. His recent visit had yielded her only a fraction of the solace she craved, while his apparent enjoyment of the encounter seemed woefully diminished. It was painfully obvious that he was suffering; yet, she desperately needed it to be equally evident that he still regarded her with the specific, unwavering affection she was certain she had once inspired. Until now, the persistence of his interest had remained precarious, his reserve contradicting moments of animation that suggested otherwise.

The following morning, Edward joined Elinor and Marianne in the breakfast room before the household had risen. Marianne, ever eager to facilitate their union, quickly excused herself to retreat upstairs. However, barely halfway up the stairs, she heard the parlor door swing open. Turning, she found Edward emerging.

"I am proceeding to the village to inspect my horses," he announced, noting their tardiness for breakfast. "I shall return shortly."

Upon his reappearance, Edward offered renewed praise for the surrounding countryside. His excursion had revealed numerous advantageous perspectives of the valley, and the village itself, situated at a significantly higher elevation than their cottage, provided a commanding overview of the entire region that delighted him immensely. This topic naturally captured Marianne's attention. She began to articulate her own admiration for the vistas and queried him in detail regarding the specific features that had struck him most forcibly.

Edward interjected abruptly. "You must not inquire too deeply, Marianne. Remember, I possess no expertise in the picturesque. Were we to descend into particulars, my ignorance and lack of taste would surely offend you. I tend to label hills 'steep' when they ought to be described as 'bold'; I call surfaces 'strange and uncouth' when they should be termed 'irregular and rugged'; and I note distant objects as 'out of sight,' whereas they should remain indistinct through the soft haze of the atmosphere. You must accept such limited appreciation as I can honestly offer. I declare this a magnificent country—the hills are steep, the woods teem with fine timber, and the valley appears comfortable and secure, dotted with rich meadows and several neat farmhouses. It perfectly aligns with my definition of a fine country because it unites aesthetic beauty with practical utility. I daresay it is picturesque as well, simply because you admire it; I can readily imagine it filled with rocks, promontories, grey moss, and brambles, though these details escape my notice entirely. I know nothing of the picturesque."

"It seems only too true," Marianne conceded. "Yet, why do you boast of such limitations?"

"I suspect," Elinor interjected calmly, "that in avoiding one form of affectation, Edward has fallen prey to another. Believing that many individuals feign a deeper reverence for nature's beauties than they truly possess, and disgusted by such pretension, he adopts an attitude of greater indifference and less discernment than he actually holds. He is fastidious and cultivates an affectation of his own."

"Indeed," Marianne agreed. "Admiration of landscape has devolved into mere jargon. Everyone pretends to feel and attempts to describe with the taste and elegance of the original definers of picturesque beauty. I detest jargon of every sort, and often I have suppressed my feelings precisely because I lacked a vocabulary capable of describing them without resorting to worn-out, meaningless clichés."

"I am convinced," Edward stated firmly, "that you genuinely experience all the delight of a fine prospect that you claim to feel. In return, however, my sister must permit me to feel no more than I express. I enjoy a fine view, but not on picturesque principles. I dislike crooked, twisted, or blasted trees; I prefer them tall, straight, and flourishing. I hold no affection for ruined, tattered cottages. I am not fond of nettles, thistles, or heather blossoms. I derive far greater pleasure from a cozy farmhouse than from a watchtower, and a company of tidy, content villagers pleases me infinitely more than the finest bandits in existence."

Marianne gazed at Edward with astonishment and pity for her sister, while Elinor merely smiled. The discussion ceased there, leaving Marianne in thoughtful silence until a new distraction seized her focus. Seated beside Edward, she watched as he accepted his tea from Mrs. Dashwood. His hand moved directly across hers, making a ring adorned with a plait of hair conspicuously visible upon one of his fingers.

"I have never seen you wear a ring before, Edward!" she exclaimed. "Is that Fanny's hair? I recall her promising to send you some. Yet, I would have thought her hair was darker."

Marianne spoke impulsively, voicing her genuine thoughts, but upon witnessing the sharp pain etched onto Edward's face, her own regret at her lack of tact proved overwhelming. He flushed deeply, casting a fleeting glance toward Elinor before replying, "Yes; it is my sister's hair. The setting inevitably alters its perceived shade, you understand."

Elinor had met his gaze and appeared equally conscious. Instantly, she realized the hair belonged to her, feeling as certain of this fact as Marianne did. The divergence lay solely in their interpretations: where Marianne viewed it as a voluntary gift from her sister, Elinor knew it must have been acquired through some theft or deception unknown to herself. Though not in the mood to consider it an insult, Elinor affected total indifference, immediately shifting the conversation to another topic. Internally, however, she resolved to seize every opportunity to examine the hair closely, ensuring beyond all doubt that its color matched her own exactly.

Edward's embarrassment persisted for some time, eventually dissolving into a state of profound absent-mindedness. He remained unusually grave throughout the rest of the morning. Marianne harshly chastised herself for her remark, though her forgiveness might have arrived sooner had she understood how little offense it had caused her sister.

Before midday, they were visited by Sir John and Mrs. Jennings. Having heard news of a gentleman's arrival at the cottage, they came to survey the guest. With the aid of his mother-in-law, Sir John was not long in deducing that the surname Ferrars began with an 'F.' This discovery prepared a future vein of mockery directed at the devoted Elinor—a jest that nothing short of their recent acquaintance with Edward could have prevented from being launched immediately. As it happened, she gleaned only the extent of their insight, grounded in Margaret's prior instructions, through a series of highly suggestive glances.

Sir John never failed to extend invitations to the Dashwoods, either to dine at the park the following day or to share tea that evening. On this occasion, seeking to maximize the entertainment value for his visitor and fulfill his duty to contribute to their amusement, he proposed securing them for both engagements.

"You *must* drink tea with us tonight," he declared, "for we shall be entirely alone. And tomorrow, you must absolutely dine with us, for we shall be hosting a large party."

Mrs. Jennings reinforced the necessity. "And who knows but you may raise a dance," she added. "And that will certainly tempt *you*, Miss Marianne."

"A dance!" Marianne cried out. "Impossible! Who is to dance?"

"Who? Why, yourselves, the Careys, and the Whitakers, to be sure. What! Did you think nobody could dance because a certain individual, who shall remain nameless, has departed?"

"I wish with all my soul," Sir John proclaimed loudly, "that Willoughby were among us again."

This comment, coupled with Marianne's sudden blush, ignited fresh suspicions within Edward. "And who is Willoughby?" he asked quietly to Miss Dashwood, to whom he was seated. She offered him a brief response, but Marianne's countenance was far more expressive. Edward gathered sufficient evidence to comprehend not only the subtext of others' remarks but also those nuances of Marianne's expression that had previously baffled him.

Once their visitors departed, Edward immediately circled around her and whispered, "I have been guessing. Shall I reveal my hypothesis?"

"What do you mean?"

"Shall I tell you?"

"Certainly."

"Well then," he continued, "I guess that Mr. Willoughby hunts."

Marianne was taken aback and flustered, yet she could not suppress a smile at the quiet wit of his manner. After a brief pause, she said, "Oh, Edward! How can you?—But the time will come, I hope... I am sure you will like him."

"I do not doubt it," he replied, somewhat startled by her earnestness and warmth. Had he not assumed it to be a harmless jest intended to foster her general acquaintanceship, founded on nothing more substantial than a vague rumor between Mr. Willoughby and herself, he would never have dared to mention it.
