Thirteen years had rolled on, yet the calendar remained fixed upon June. The air was cool, deceptive for the season, carrying the crisp chill of early summer rather than the heat of mid-summer. High above the bustling heart of Westminster, in a block of buildings that loomed like a silent fortress against the night sky, a small gathering took place. To a stranger crossing St. James's Park beneath the suspension bridge, the tiers of lighted windows rising to the tenth floor might have seemed the dwelling of mountain gnomes, hidden away from the world below.

Inside the dining room of this flat, the atmosphere was dark and restful, warmed by a comfortable fire that belied the calendar. Four men sat around the table, the smoke of their cigars mingling with the scent of dinner.

Harry Feversham sat unchanged in spirit, though time had carved a fair moustache upon his dark hair and broadened his frame into that of a middle-aged athlete. His features, however, retained the sharp scrutiny of the night Lieutenant Sutch had once examined them so closely. Beside him sat Captain Trench, a small, balding man with a sharp, resourceful face and black eyes that darted with remarkable activity. Opposite him was Lieutenant Willoughby, a man of a different stamp entirely. With a round forehead, a thick snub nose, and vacant, protruding eyes, he possessed an aspect of invincible stupidity. He spoke rarely, and when he did, his words drifted toward points long forgotten, twisting a moustache whose ends curled up toward his eyes with ridiculous ferocity. Stubborn as well as slow, Willoughby was a man who clung to his own crippled thoughts, refusing to admit harm unless he had laboriously convinced himself of it.

The fourth at the table was Durrance, a lieutenant of the East Surrey Regiment and Feversham's oldest friend, who had arrived in answer to a telegram.

The year was 1882. The thoughts of civilians in London were heavy with anxiety regarding Egypt, while soldiers felt an eager anticipation. News from the East painted a grim picture: Arabi Pasha was strengthening the fortifications of Alexandria, and further south, a danger was swelling like a thundercloud. Just a year prior, a young Dongolawi named Mohammed Ahmed had marched through the villages of the White Nile, preaching with the fire of a zealot the coming of a Saviour. The passionate victims of Turkish taxation had listened, finding holy names whispered in the wind and imprinted upon the eggs they gathered. Now, Mohammed had declared himself that Saviour, and his first battles against the Turks were already being won.

"There will be trouble," said Trench, and the sentence hung in the air as the text upon which three of the four men focused their minds.

Yet, in a rare interval, Harry Feversham spoke of a different subject.

"I am very glad you were all able to dine with me tonight," he explained to Durrance. "I telegraphed to Castleton as well, an officer of ours, but he was dining with a big man in the War Office and leaves for Scotland afterwards, so he could not come. I have news of a sort."

The three men leaned forward, their minds still occupied by the specter of war, but Feversham's news was personal.

"I only reached London this morning from Dublin," he said, a shade of embarrassment coloring his voice. "I have been some weeks there."

Durrance lifted his eyes from the tablecloth and looked quietly at his friend. "Yes?" he asked steadily.

"I have come back engaged to be married."

Durrance lifted his glass to his lips. "Well, here's luck to you, Harry," he said. The wish was curt, yet to Feversham's ears, it carried the weight of true affection. Between these two men, affectionate phrases were unnecessary. Their friendship was a secure instrument, forged for hard, lifelong use, valuable enough to demand sacrifice without ever needing to be spoken of. They knew that such bonds entailed obligations, and if sacrifices became needful, they would be made in silence.

"Thank you, Jack!" said Feversham. "I am glad of your good wishes. It was you who introduced me to Ethne; I cannot forget it."

Durrance set his glass down without haste. A moment of silence followed, during which he sat with his eyes upon the tablecloth and his hands resting on the edge.

"Yes," he said in a level voice. "I did you a good turn then."

He seemed poised to say more, unsure how to phrase it, until Captain Trench's sharp, practical voice intervened.

"Will this make any difference?" Trench asked.

Feversham replaced his cigar between his lips. "You mean, shall I leave the service?" he asked slowly. "I don't know."

Durrance seized the opportunity to rise and cross to the window, standing with his back to his companions. Feversham took the abrupt movement for a reproach and addressed his friend's back rather than Trench.

"I don't know," he repeated. "It will need thought. There is much to be said. On the one side, of course, there's my father, my career, such as it is. On the other hand, there is her father, Dermod Eustace."

"He wishes you to chuck your commission?" asked Willoughby.

"He has no doubt the Irishman's objection to constituted authority," said Trench, laughing. "But need you subscribe to it, Feversham?"

"It is not merely that." Feversham continued, carefully weighing his words, still looking across the shoulders of his companions at his friend. "Dermod is old, his estates are going to ruin, and there are other things. You know, Jack?"

The direct appeal hung in the air, yet Durrance answered absently. "Yes, I know," he added, quoting a catch-word. "If you want any whiskey, rap twice on the floor with your foot. The servants understand."

"Precisely," said Feversham. He continued, "Besides, there is Ethne herself. Dermod for once did an appropriate thing when he gave her that name. For she is of her country, and more, of her county. She has the love of it in her bones. I do not think that she could be quite happy in India, or indeed in any place which was not within reach of Donegal, the smell of its peat, its streams, and the brown friendliness of its hills. One has to consider that."

He waited for an answer, receiving none, and went on. Durrance, however, had no thought of reproach in his mind. He knew Feversham was speaking, wishing deeply that his friend would continue, yet he paid no heed to the words. He stood looking steadfastly out the window. Over against him was the glare from Pall Mall striking upward to the sky, the chains of light banked one above another as the town rose northward, accompanied by the rumble of a million carriages. At his feet, very far below, lay St. James's Park, silent and black—a quiet pool of darkness amidst the glitter and noise. Durrance had a great desire to escape into that secrecy, but he could not do so without remark. Therefore, he kept his back turned, leaning his forehead against the window, hoping his friend would continue talking. He was facing one of the sacrifices that must not be mentioned, and no sign must betray it.

Feversham did continue, and though Durrance did not listen, Captain Trench gave him his closest attention. It was evident that Harry was giving reasons seriously considered, not making mere excuses. In the end, Trench was satisfied.

"Well, I drink to you, Feversham," he said, "with all the proper sentiments."

"I too, old man," said Willoughby, obediently following his senior's lead.

Thus they drank their comrade's health. As their empty glasses rattled on the table, there came a knock upon the door.

The two officers looked up. Durrance turned from the window. Feversham said, "Come in," and his servant brought in a telegram.

Feversham tore open the envelope carelessly, read through the message with equal carelessness, and then sat very still. His eyes were fixed upon the slip of pink paper, his face grown at once extremely grave. He sat thus for an appreciable time, not stunned so much as thoughtful. A complete silence fell upon the room. Feversham's three guests averted their eyes. Durrance turned again to his window; Willoughby twisted his moustache and gazed intently upward at the ceiling; Captain Trench shifted his chair round and stared into the glowing fire. Each man's attitude expressed a certain suspense. It seemed that sharp upon the heels of Feversham's good news, calamity had come knocking at the door.

"There is no answer," said Harry, and fell to silence again. Once he raised his head and looked at Trench as though he had a mind to speak, but he thought the better of it and dropped again to the consideration of the message.

In a moment or two, the silence was sharply interrupted, but not by any of the expectant men within the room. The interruption came from without. From the parade ground of Wellington Barracks, the drums and fifes sounding the tattoo shrilled through the open window with a startling clearness, like a sharp summons, before diminishing as the band marched away across the gravel and growing loud again.

Feversham did not change his attitude, but the look upon his face was now that of a man listening thoughtfully, just as he had read thoughtfully. In the years that followed, that moment was to recur again and again to the recollection of each of Harry's three guests. The lighted room, the bright homely fire, the open window overlooking the myriad lamps of London, Harry Feversham seated with the telegram spread before him, the drums and fifes calling loudly and then dwindling to music very small and pretty—music which beckoned where a moment ago it had commanded—all these details made up a picture whose colors were not to fade by any lapse of time, although its significance was not apprehended now.

It was remembered that Feversham rose abruptly from his chair just before the tattoo ceased. He crumpled the telegram loosely in his hands, tossed it into the fire, and then, leaning his back against the chimney-piece and upon one side of the fireplace, said again:

"I don't know;" as though he had thrust that message, whatever it might be, from his mind, and was summing up in this indefinite way the argument which had gone before.

Thus the long silence was broken, and a spell was lifted. The fire took hold upon the telegram and shook it, so that it moved like a thing alive and in pain. It twisted, and part of it unrolled, and for a second lay open and smooth of creases, lit up by the flame and as yet untouched. Two or three words sprang, as it were, out of a yellow glare of fire and were legible. Then the flame seized upon that smooth part too, and in a moment shrivelled it into black tatters. Captain Trench had been staring into the fire all this while.

"You return to Dublin, I suppose?" said Durrance. He had moved back into the room. Like his companions, he was conscious of an unexplained relief.

"To Dublin? No; I go to Donegal in three weeks' time. There is to be a dance. It is hoped you will come."

"I am not sure that I can manage it. There is just a chance, I believe, should trouble come in the East, that I may go out on the staff."

The talk thus came round again to the chances of peace and war, holding in that quarter until the boom of the Westminster clock told that the hour was eleven. Captain Trench rose from his seat on the last stroke; Willoughby and Durrance followed his example.

"I shall see you tomorrow," said Durrance to Feversham.

"As usual," replied Harry.

His three guests descended from his rooms and walked across the Park together. At the corner of Pall Mall, however, they parted company; Durrance mounted St. James's Street, while Trench and Willoughby crossed the road into St. James's Square. There Trench slipped his arm through Willoughby's, to Willoughby's surprise, for Trench was an undemonstrative man.

"You know Castleton's address?" he asked.

"Albemarle Street," Willoughby answered, adding the number.

"He leaves Euston at twelve o'clock. It is now ten minutes past eleven. Are you curious, Willoughby? I confess to curiosity. I am an inquisitive methodical person, and when a man gets a telegram bidding him tell Trench something and he tells Trench nothing, I am curious as a philosopher to know what that something is! Castleton is the only other officer of our regiment in London. It is likely, therefore, that the telegram came from Castleton. Castleton, too, was dining with a big man from the War Office. I think that if we take a hansom to Albemarle Street, we shall just catch Castleton upon his door-step."

Mr. Willoughby, who understood very little of Trench's meaning, nevertheless cordially agreed to the proposal.

"I think it would be prudent," said he, and he hailed a passing cab. A moment later the two men were driving to Albemarle Street.
