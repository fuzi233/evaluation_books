The first sign of the approaching storm was a transparent white cloud, setting off the deepening blue of the sky. Inside the rectory, Anna sat in the quiet of her own thoughts, yet the air around her crackled with unspoken tension. Rex had changed. For the first time, he had not spoken to her about what burned within him; he simply assumed she knew, a silent confidence that terrified her more than any open declaration. Conversely, Anna found herself unable to articulate the turmoil swirling in her heart. Was it a pain she ought to hide? That he cared for someone else so soon, with such intensity? Yet, this fear was drowned by a deeper, gnawing anxiety for Rex's safety.

She looked at her cousin, Gwendolen, with a mixture of awe and trepidation. Gwendolen was radiant, a creature of impossible beauty, but to Anna, she resembled a magnificent wild beast whose appetites were mysterious and potentially destructive. Anna knew Gwendolen as a girl of immense ambition, always striving to be "something extraordinary." Now, watching her cousin, Anna felt a chill conviction: Gwendolen would never truly care for Rex. Where Anna poured out tenderness and reverence, Gwendolen remained cool, perhaps even disdainful. To imagine Gwendolen scorning Rex seemed far more likely than imagining her accepting his love. And Rex... Papa would be furious if he knew. Rex was too young for such a tempest, wasn't he? Anna had expected years to pass before anything like this, expecting to remain his devoted housekeeper forever. But what a heart must beat that refused to return love? In the shadow of his potential suffering, Anna began to dislike the fascinating, terrifying allure of her cousin.

Time, it seemed, had swollen with a tumultuous life visible to everyone but the actors themselves. Rex, if pressed, would claim he was eager to announce an imminent engagement to his father. Yet, for the first time, he was guarded. Not only about his feelings, but about his actions—a reserve that unnerved Anna. On her side, she grew nervous whenever her parents or Aunt Davilow spoke privately, fearing a mention of Rex and Gwendolen. But the elders were oblivious to this pantomime, interpreting the young ones' trivialities with the casual indifference one gives to busy ants.

On a gray morning, with Papa departed in his carriage and Aunt Davilow beside him, Anna spotted Rex. He wore his antigropelos, the closest approximation to hunting gear he owned.

"Where are you going, Rex?" she asked softly.

"To see the hounds throw off at the Three Barns."

"And... are you taking Gwendolen?" she ventured, her voice trembling.

"She told you, did she?"

"No, but I thought—" Anna hesitated, looking down. "Does Papa know?"

"Not that I'm aware of. I shouldn't think he'd bother."

"You're going to use his horse?"

"He knows I do that whenever I can."

"Don't let Gwendolen ride after the hounds, Rex!" Anna pleaded, her fear granting her a sudden, piercing insight. "Papa, Mamma, and Aunt Davilow all forbid it. They think it's not right for her."

"Why should you suppose she intends to do what is not right?" Rex asked, a smug smile playing on his lips, provoking her further.

"Gwendolen minds nobody sometimes," Anna snapped, her anger emboldening her.

"Then she wouldn't mind me," Rex replied, treating her anxiety as a game.

"Oh, Rex, I cannot bear it. You will make yourself very unhappy."

And then, the dam broke. Anna burst into tears.

"Nannie, Nannie, what on earth is the matter?" Rex exclaimed, impatient, hat in hand, whip ready.

"She will not care for you one bit—I know she never will!" the poor child whispered, sobbing uncontrollably.

Rex flushed, hurried out of the hall, leaving Anna to the misery of having failed to soothe him. As he rode away, he did think of her words. They carried the unwelcome weight of prophecy, even when laughed at. Yet, he quickly dismissed them as the product of little Anna's excessive tenderness. A new determination took root in his chest: he would prove her wrong. This certainty, fueled by a hint of doubt and unease, spurred him toward a confession that might otherwise have waited.

At the gate, Gwendolen awaited him, already mounted. She had prepared herself for his tardiness, keeping the groom in readiness, but now the groom was dismissed. The two rode away together, enveloped in a delightful freedom. Gwendolen was in high spirits, and Rex found her more lovely than ever; her long white throat, the curve of her cheek, were framed perfectly by her simple riding dress. To a young lover like Rex, the qualities of goodness, truth, and beauty seemed instantly embodied in her. He could not imagine a more perfect girl.

The morning was exquisite, a calm gray canvas for the mild winter scene. The hedgerows sparkled with red berries, the elms stood bare and purple, and the horses' hooves chimed musically over the frost. Laughter bubbled between them, mixing with the crisp air. Rex was the antithesis of a dandy, and Gwendolen delighted in teasing him. Every glance, every sound from their throats, flowed from a spring of pure joy. One might be tempted to wish, futilely, that they could have pledged themselves then and there, sealing a bond that would never waver. Some of the goodness Rex believed in was indeed there. But goodness is a fragile thing, like a crop growing underground, vulnerable to the blights of the soil or the storms from afar.

"Anna had got it into her head that you'd want to ride today," Rex said later, the memory of Anna's warning lingering dangerously close to the surface.

"Did she?" Gwendolen laughed lightly. "What a little clairvoyant she is!"

"Shall you?" Rex asked, confident in her common sense regarding the objections of the elders.

"I don't know. I can't tell what I'll do until I get there. Clairvoyants are often wrong; they foresee what is likely. I am not fond of what is likely—it's always dull. I do what is unlikely."

"Aha," Rex grinned. "Once I know what people generally do, I know you'll do the opposite. You've rounded onto a likelihood of your own. I can calculate on you. You can't surprise me."

"Yes, I can. I'll turn round and do what's likely for people in general," Gwendolen teased, her laugh musical.

"You see, you can't escape some sort of likelihood. Contradictoriness makes the strongest likelihood. You must give up a plan."

"No, I won't. My plan is to do what pleases me." Her chin jutted out slightly, her neck straight, a posture that would have jarred sweet-natured Rex if it hadn't been offset by the sheer humor and charm of her delivery.

"Can you manage to feel only what pleases you?"

"Of course not; that comes from what others do. But if the world were pleasanter, one would only feel what was pleasant. Girls' lives are so stupid; they never do what they like."

"I thought that was more the case of men," Rex countered gently. "They're forced to do hard things, bored and broken. And if we love a girl dearly, we want to do as she likes, so in the end, you have your own way."

"I don't believe it. I've never seen a married woman who had her own way."

"What should you like to do?" Rex asked, genuinely curious, venturing beyond his curriculum.

"Oh, I don't know! Go to the North Pole, ride steeple-chases, or be a queen in the East like Lady Hester Stanhope," Gwendolen flitted, her words escaping her lips before she could find a deeper meaning.

"You don't mean you'd never be married?"

"No, I didn't say that. Only when I marry, I won't do as other women do."

"You might do just as you liked if you married a man who loved you more dearly than anything else," Rex said, his heart pounding. "I know one who does."

"Don't talk of Mr. Middleton, for heaven's sake!" Gwendolen gasped, a blush rushing over her face and neck. "That is Anna's chant. I hear the hounds. Let us go on."

She urged her chestnut into a canter, and Rex followed, feeling a strange mix of relief and foreboding.
