Mr. Gryce possessed a talent for finesse. Though he had declared his intent to trail Mr. Adams, he executed the surveillance with a precision that went far beyond mere observation. He boarded the identical train, occupying the same carriage, driven by a desire to study the young man at his leisure. This individual intrigued Gryce in a manner entirely unforeseen; the boy's presence stirred something within the detective's own conscience, casting a shadow over his recent triumphs and forcing him to confront the weight of his years prematurely. From his vantage point, Gryce watched the transformations playing out across the prisoner's handsome face. He marveled at the profound calm that now settled those features, which had recently contorted in agony. Where did this fortitude originate? What reservoir of strength allowed this youth to sit so stoically under the gaze of others, whereas an hour prior he had recoiled from their scrutiny with visible suffering? Did courage bloom only from the soil of despair? Or was the young man so consumed by his internal misery that he failed to perceive the darkness it cast around him? His furrowed brow and distant, vacant eyes suggested a mind completely severed from its immediate surroundings. To what abysses of remorse had he descended? An old detective like Gryce, seasoned by decades of confronting criminals—many of whom shared this young man's cultivated appearance and social standing—could offer no definitive answer.

At the Brooklyn station, Gryce reconnected with his charge, who barely lifted his head as the detective approached. Within an hour, they arrived at Police Headquarters, and the formal interrogation of Mr. Adams commenced. The suspect showed no inclination to evade the proceedings; indeed, he appeared eager to speak. A heavy burden weighed upon him, and he longed to cast it aside. Yet, the confession he offered diverged sharply from the officers' expectations. He did not admit to murdering his brother, but rather confessed to being the accidental catalyst of that tragedy.

"My name is Cadwalader, not Adams," he began, his voice steady despite the gravity of his admission. "My father, a native Scot who became a naturalized citizen of Pennsylvania, settled in a town called Montgomery when he was a young husband. He had two children at that time; one perished in infancy, while the other was my brother, Felix—the man whose violent death under the alias of Adams has brought me here today. I am the product of a subsequent marriage contracted by my father several years after he departed Montgomery. Born in Harrisburg, I left there shortly after reaching my third birthday, retaining no memories of that city. My recollections belong instead to vastly different landscapes. My mother died while I was still an infant, and I was dispatched to the Old World, whence my father hailed, at a very early age. It was not until this very year that I returned to find my father on his deathbed and my brother, Felix, a grown man possessing considerable wealth—a fortune I had assumed he intended to share with me. However, once my father had passed, Felix..." (He paused, the effort required to utter that name evident in his strained expression.) "...Felix arrived in New York, leaving me adrift without settled hopes or any tangible means to support a future or launch a career.

"While wandering aimlessly, I stumbled upon the town where my father had spent his youth. Seeking out his old acquaintances, I encountered in the residence of a fellow emigrant from Scotland a young lady... in whom I quickly became deeply interested, to the extent that I desired to marry her. Yet, I possessed no money, no profession, and no home to offer her. As I was compelled to acknowledge, I lacked prospects. Nevertheless, I could not abandon the hope of making her my wife. Consequently, I wrote to my brother, Felix Cadwalader—or, more accurately, Felix Adams, as he had chosen to be known in later years for reasons entirely unrelated to his sudden demise—and requested that he provide me with a specific sum sufficient to wed her with a sense of self-respect. My only response was a repetition of the vague promises he had previously extended.

"Youth is inherently hopeful, bordering on audacious, and I resolved to claim her without further delay, hoping that her beauty and endearing nature would compel him, upon our first meeting, to grant the definitive concession he had so stubbornly withheld.

"I acted accordingly. The gravest error for which I condemn myself is that I entered into this union without informing either my wife or her father. They believed me wealthy, perhaps affluent. While Mr. Poindexter undoubtedly possesses means, I am certain that had he known I owned nothing but the clothes on my back and a trivial amount of pocket change, he would have halted the marriage in its tracks. He is a man of high ambition and regards his daughter as worthy of a millionaire's devotion—as she truly is.

"Felix was notified of my marriage but declined to attend the ceremony, nor did he seek to forbid it. Thus, the wedding proceeded quietly in a hotel parlor, witnessed only by strangers. Armed with both vague hopes and certain apprehensions, I prepared to introduce my young bride to my brother. Hardened as he was by years of bachelorhood, I reasoned that he could not remain entirely immune to feminine charm, nor so detached from human emotion as to fail to recognize my wife as a woman deserving of respect.

"But I had miscalculated my adversary. Two days after the ceremony that bound us together, when I took her to the house that has since become infamously notorious, I discovered that my brother had revealed to me merely one facet of his complex nature—and the least obstinate of them.

"As brilliant as steel, he was equally cold. Not only did he profess to be unmoved by my wife's many charms, but he also declared himself utterly unsympathetic to follies such as love and marriage, dismissing them as the idle pastimes of men lacking occupation or purpose, wholly unworthy of individuals boasting of our talents and achievements. He then turned his back upon us. Overcome by an anger bordering on frenzy, I launched into an abuse for which he was so ill-prepared that he crouched as if struck by blows. Losing his composure moment by moment, he eventually seized a dagger lying nearby. Crying out, 'You want my money? Very well, then take it!' he drove the blade into his own heart with a single, desperate thrust.

"I fear I may not be believed, but that is the truth of this crime, gentlemen."
