The eighteen months that followed—and the end came earlier than any of us had bargained for—were, as I look back, the nearest thing my father and mother ever achieved to a complete and well-seasoned happiness. Not the loud, frothy kind that spills and leaves a sticky mess, but a quiet, slow-simmered content, like a broth that has learnt its own depth by being kept on the hob through the long hours, when the house is still and the street noises thin out to an occasional cart-rattle and the far-off iron cough of the trains.

It was as though Death, that grave caller whom we slam the door on as if he were a rate-collector, had tapped very softly, and then, instead of entering, had stood on the mat with his hat in his hand and said, “Not yet. You have a little time. In a little while.” And because he did not force himself upon us, we grew less afraid of him; and because we grew less afraid, everything else that had frightened us—appearances, neighbours, bills, the silly pantomime of respectability—lost its sting.

We lived poorly again, but now with the decency of people who have stopped pretending that poverty is a moral failure. There was no more pinching and stretching to look like what we were not. My mother stopped trying to make a small joint behave like a haunch; my father ceased apologising for the state of his boots. In the kitchen, even the plainest things were handled with a kind of reverence: bread cut straight, tea made properly, the scant bit of dripping saved without shame and used with intelligence. When Amy browned onions—slowly, patiently, with the pan held just so—there would come up that deep, sweet smell that is a hymn in itself, and it would drift into the parlour and soften whatever hard thought might have been forming there.

In those days my parents laid aside, quite simply, the false and the meaningless. It is astonishing what rubbish a man can cart about in his mind until he realises he may have to leave it all at the station. They thought of little except loving each other well: comforting, explaining, forgiving; learning at last what in younger, busier years they had been too proud, too tired, or too foolish to learn. Their love grew bigger than their needs, and, like soup made with too much goodness, overflowed the pot and ran out towards other people.

Sometimes, remembering those months, it has seemed to me that we are wrong to bolt Death out of the house as if he were infection. He knows life, if anyone does; he might whisper useful things—“Only a little longer. Is it worth snapping? Is it worth being unkind?”—the way the kettle whispers when you are distracted, and if you listen you save the tea from stewing itself into bitterness.

A sort of graciousness went out from them and altered the whole air of the place. Even my Aunt Fan—who had always considered kindness a weakness and civility a form of lying—decided, for the second time in her career, to give amiability a trial. She announced this one afternoon with the abruptness with which she did everything, as if she were flinging a damp cloth at you.

“I’m a beast of an old woman,” said my aunt suddenly.

“Don’t say that, Fan,” urged my mother.

“What’s the good of saying ‘Don’t say it’ when I’ve just said it?” snapped back my aunt.

“It’s your manner,” explained my mother gently; “people sometimes think you disagreeable.”

“They’d be daft if they didn’t,” interrupted my aunt. “Of course you don’t really mean it,” continued my mother, as if reasoning with a kettle for boiling.

“Stuff and nonsense,” snorted my aunt. “Does she think I’m a fool? I like being disagreeable. I like to see ’em squirming.”

My mother laughed; it was one of those laughs that say, There you are again, and I love you for it.

“I can be agreeable,” continued my aunt, “if I choose. Nobody more so.”

“Then why not choose?” suggested my mother.

“I tried it once,” said my aunt, “and it fell flat. Nothing could have fallen flatter.”

“It may not have attracted much attention,” replied my mother, smiling, “but one should not be agreeable merely to attract attention.”

“It wasn’t only that,” returned my aunt. “It gave no satisfaction to anybody. It didn’t suit me. A disagreeable person is at their best when they are disagreeable.”

“I can hardly agree with you there,” said my mother.

“I could do it again,” communed my aunt to herself, with a note of something like vindictiveness, as if amiability were a weapon she meant to try on the enemy. “It’s easy enough. Look at the sort of fools that are agreeable.”

“I’m sure you could be if you tried,” urged my mother.

“Let ’em have it,” continued my aunt, still to herself. “That’s the way to teach ’em sense. Let ’em have it.”

And strange as it sounds, my aunt was right and my mother wrong. For when Aunt Fan turned agreeable, she did it the way she did everything: with a will, with a system, and with an alarming absence of modesty. My father was the first to notice.

“Nothing the matter with poor old Fan, is there?” he asked one evening, a day or two after my aunt had put her threat into practice. “Nothing happened, has there?”

“No,” answered my mother. “Nothing that I know of.”

“Her manner is so strange,” explained my father, frowning slightly. “So—so weird.”

My mother smiled the way one smiles at a child playing at being grown up. “Don’t say anything to her. She’s trying to be agreeable.”

My father laughed, then looked almost wistful. “I almost wish she wouldn’t,” he remarked. “We were used to it, and she was rather amusing.”

But my aunt, being a woman of resolve, persisted—and fortune, which likes a joke, sent her a new audience at precisely the right moment: James Wellington Gadley.

Properly speaking he was Wellington James, that being the order in which he had been christened in 1815, a year that must have made “Wellington” sound like a patriotic drum-beat. But as life went on—and particularly during his school career—it appears to have been borne in upon him that “Wellington” is a heavy joint for a small stomach, and he very sensibly turned it into a side-dish and ate “James” first.

He was a slightly pompous, entirely simple-minded little old gentleman, head clerk to Mr. Stillwood, the solicitor to whom my father was now assistant. Stillwood, Waterhead and Royal dated back to the Georges and was the sort of firm that had once had its fingers in every aristocratic pie, sometimes with the sugar, sometimes with the mud. In these later years its grandeur was thinning like gravy stretched too far; old Mr. Stillwood, the last survivor, declined new partners, explaining frankly that the business was a dying one, and trying to work it up again would be putting new wine into worn-out skins. Yet what clients remained were of good class, and the name still carried that respectable flavour that makes people swallow almost anything.

Mr. Gadley had entered the firm as office boy in its prime and had never noticed the slow cooling of the oven. In his mind, Stillwood, Waterhead and Royal was still the culinary centre of the legal world. Nothing delighted him more than to discuss, over and over, the strange cases in which the firm had been concerned. He had them at his tongue’s tip like sweets in a pocket. If he could find a hearer he would reargue interminably, but with a professional reticence that turned real men into Mr. Y. and Lady X., and real towns into “a certain place not a thousand miles from where we are now sitting,” as if geography itself might sue for libel.

Most people, his methods being somewhat forensic and his breath being somewhat close, discouraged him. My aunt was a never-wearied listener, particularly if the affair smelt—even faintly—of crime. When, during their very first conversation, he exclaimed:

“Now why—why, after keeping away from his wife for nearly eighteen years, never even letting her know whether he was alive or dead, why this sudden resolve to return to her? That is what I want explained to me!”

—and paused for the sympathetic yawn he had learnt to expect, my aunt replied with prompt intelligence:

“To murder her—by slow poison.”

“To murder her! But why?”

“In order to marry the other woman.”

“What other woman?”

“The woman he had just met and fallen in love with. Before that it was immaterial to him what had become of his wife. This woman had said to him: ‘Come back to me a free man or never see my face again.’”

“Dear me! Now that’s very curious.”

“Nothing of the sort,” said my aunt. “Plain common sense.”

“I mean, it’s curious because, as a matter of fact, his wife did die a little later, and he did marry again.”

“Told you so,” remarked my aunt, as if she had merely tasted the stew and knew the rest.

So every case in the Stillwood annals was reviewed, and light thrown upon it by my aunt’s cheerful faith in human depravity. It was fortunate the actors remained Mr. X. and Lady Y., for into the most innocent-seeming behaviour my aunt read the blackest intent, with the relish of a cook who suspects that even the milk has been watered.

“I think you are a little too severe,” Mr. Gadley would now and then plead.

“We’re all of us miserable sinners,” my aunt would affirm brightly; “only we don’t all get the same chances.”

An elderly maiden lady, a Miss Z., residing in “a western town once famous as the resort of fashion, but which we will not name,” my aunt was convinced had burnt down a house containing a will and forged another under which her children—should she ever marry and be blessed with such—would inherit among them at coming of age a fortune of seven hundred pounds.

The freshness of her views always fascinated Mr. Gadley.

“I have to thank you, ma’am,” he would remark on rising, “for a most delightful conversation. I may not be able to agree with your conclusions, but they afford food for reflection.”

To which my aunt would reply, “I hate talking to any one who agrees with me. It’s like taking a walk to see one’s own looking-glass. I’d rather talk to somebody who didn’t, even if he were a fool,” which, for her, was gracious.

He was a stout little gentleman with a stomach that protruded about a foot in front of him, and of this he appeared entirely unaware. It would not have mattered had it not been for his passion, when talking, to approach as close to his listener as possible. Gradually in the course of conversation his stomach, acting as a gentle battering-ram, would drive you backwards round the room. Unless you were artful, you would find yourself pinned hopelessly into a corner, while he looked surprised that, despite all his efforts, he never succeeded in getting any nearer.

His first evening at our house he was talking to my aunt from the corner of his chair. As he grew interested he drew his chair nearer and nearer until, having withdrawn inch by inch to avoid his encroachments, my aunt was sitting on the extreme edge of her own. His next move sent her to the floor.

She said nothing, which astonished me. But on the occasion of his next visit she was busy darning stockings—an unusual occupation for her, and carried on with the air of a conspirator. He approached nearer and nearer as before; but this time she sat her ground, stitching away with grim purpose, and it was he who in due course sprang back with an exclamation decidedly foreign to the subject under discussion.

Ever afterwards my aunt met him with stockings in her hand, and they talked with a safe space between their chairs.

Nothing further came of it, though his being a widower added to their intercourse that small spice of possibility which no woman is ever quite too old to relish; but that he admired her intellectually was evident. Once he even exclaimed:

“Miss Davies, you should have been a solicitor’s wife!”

—which, to his mind, was the crown of female ambition. To which my aunt replied:

“Chances are I should have been, if one had ever asked me.”

Warmed by appreciation, my aunt’s amiability took root and flourished—though, like many late-developing growths, it took on fantastic shapes. She got it into her head, not without reason, that flattery is the shortest road to being agreeable. Conscientiously she set about flattering in season and out, meaning to give pleasure; but the effect was that of sarcasm peeping through a thin napkin.

My father would tell some trifling story, an incident of the day that had amused him. At once my aunt would break into enthusiasm, holding up her hands.

“What a funny man he is! And to think it comes to him naturally without an effort. What a gift it is!”

When my mother appeared in a new bonnet—or an old one retrimmed, which happened often enough, for in these days my mother took more thought than ever for her appearance (you women who have loved will understand)—my aunt would step back in simulated amazement.

“Don’t tell me it’s a married woman with a boy getting on for fourteen. It’s a girl. A saucy, tripping girl. That’s what it is.”

I have heard of vanity growing into a hopeless disease; but I am inclined to think a dose of my aunt at about this period would have cured the most obstinate case. Also, solely for our benefit, she assumed a vivacity and sprightliness that ill suited her; and considering her age and tendency to rheumatism, must have cost her as much as making puff pastry without cold hands. From it I have retained the perhaps immoral belief that Virtue, like a good sauce, is best when it does not call attention to itself.

Now and then the old Adam—or should one say Eve—would assert itself in my aunt, and then, still thoughtful for others, she would descend into the kitchen and be disagreeable to Amy, our new servant, who never minded it.

Amy was a philosopher who reconciled herself to everything by reflecting that there are only twenty-four hours in a day. It sounds a bleak theory, but she extracted from it perpetual cheerfulness, like a cook who can make a feast out of scraps because she knows the scraps will be eaten and the washing-up will end. If my aunt clattered about above, Amy’s mind was on her saucepan; if a cart outside screamed on its ungreased wheels, Amy merely shut the back door and hummed louder. She had an instinct for protecting the house’s small soundscape, the way some people protect a garden from soot.

My mother would apologise to her for my aunt’s interference.

“Lord bless you, mum, it don’t matter. If I wasn’t listening to her something else worse might be happening. Everything’s all the same when it’s over.”

Amy had come to us as a stop-gap, explaining she was about to be married and wanted only a temporary engagement to bridge the few weeks between then and the ceremony.

“It’s rather unsatisfactory,” my mother had said. “I dislike changes.”

“I can quite understand it, mum,” Amy had replied. “I dislike ’em myself. Only I heard you was in a hurry, and I thought maybe that while you was on the lookout for somebody permanent—”

So on that understanding she came. A month later my mother asked when she thought the marriage would actually take place.

“Don’t think I’m wishing you to go,” my mother explained; “indeed I’d like you to stop. I only want to know in time to make my arrangements.”

“Oh, some time in the spring, I expect,” was Amy’s answer.

“Oh!” said my mother. “I understood it was coming off almost immediately.”

Amy appeared shocked, as if my mother had suggested eating fish with a knife.

“I must know a little bit more about him before I go as far as that,” she said.

“But I don’t understand,” my mother persisted. “You told me when you came to me you were going to be married in a few weeks.”

“Oh, that one!” Amy’s tone suggested an unfair strain on her memory. “I didn’t feel I wanted him as much as I thought I did when it come to the point.”

“You had meantime met the other one?” suggested my mother, smiling.

“Well, we can’t help our feelings, can we, mum?” admitted Amy frankly, speaking like one with experience even then. “And what I always say is—better change your mind before it’s too late afterwards.”

Amiable, sweet-faced, broad-hearted Amy—most faithful of friends, but oh, most faithless of lovers. Age has not withered nor custom staled her liking for infinite variety. Butchers, bakers, soldiers, sailors, Jacks of all trades—does the sighing procession never pass before you, Amy, pointing reproachful fingers? Still Amy is engaged. To whom at the particular moment I cannot say; but I fancy to an early one who has lately become a widower. After more exact knowledge I do not care to enquire; for to confess ignorance—implying you have treated as trivial and forgotten the most important detail of a matter vital to her—is to hurt her feelings; while to angle for information is merely to entangle oneself. To speak of Him as “Tom” when Tom has belonged for weeks to the dead and buried past, to hastily correct oneself to “Dick” when there hasn’t been a Dick for years, clearly not to know he is now Harry, annoys her even more. In my mother’s time we always referred to him as “Dearest.” It was the title Amy herself gave them all, and it avoided confusion.

“Well, and how’s Dearest?” my mother would enquire, opening the door to Amy on a Sunday evening.

“Oh, very well indeed, mum, thank you, and he sends you his respects,” or else, “Well, not so nicely as I could wish. I’m a little anxious about him, poor dear!”

“When you are married you will be able to take good care of him.”

“That’s really what he wants—some one to take care of him. It’s what they all want, the poor dears.”

“And when is it coming off?”

“In the spring, mum.” She always chose the spring when possible.

Amy was nice to all men, and to Amy all men were nice. She might have settled down with a dozen and only had occasional regrets concerning those left out in the cold. But to ask her to select only one from so many “poor dears” was to suggest a shameful waste of affection.

We had meant to keep our grim secret to ourselves; but hiding trouble from Amy was like keeping cold hands away from the fire. Very soon she knew everything there was to know, drawing it out of my mother as one draws a splinter from a child—firmly, kindly, and with no nonsense. Then she put my mother down into a chair and stood over her like a sergeant in an apron.

“Now you leave the house and everything connected with it to me, mum,” commanded Amy. “You’ve got something else to do.”

And from that day we were in Amy’s hands and had nothing else to do but praise the Lord for His goodness. Meals appeared at the right time, clean and simple, with the quiet competence that keeps a household’s heart beating. The clink of plates, the soft chopping on the board, the kettle’s steady song—these were the domestic rites by which Amy held chaos outside the door.

Barbara also found out (from Washburn, I expect), though she said nothing. She simply came often. Old Hasluck would have come himself, I am sure, had he thought he would be welcome. As it was, he always sent kind messages and presents of fruit and flowers by Barbara, and always welcomed me most heartily whenever she allowed me to see her home.

Barbara brought, as ever, sunshine with her, making trouble seem far off and shadowy. My mother tended the fire of love, but Barbara lit the cheerful lamp of laughter. Even the dreariest street sounded different when she was near; the harsh cries and iron rumblings seemed to stand back a little, as if embarrassed by her clear voice.

And with the lessening days my father seemed to grow younger, life lying lighter on him.

One summer night he and I were walking with Barbara to Poplar station, for sometimes, when he did not look tired, she would order him to fetch his hat and stick, explaining with a caress:

“I like them tall and slight and full grown. The young ones—they don’t know how to flirt! We will take the boy with us as gooseberry.”

And he, pretending to be anxious my mother did not see, would kiss Barbara’s hand and slip out quietly with her arm linked under his. It was admirable how he entered into the spirit of the thing.

That evening the last cloud faded from before the moon as we turned the corner, and even the East India Dock Road lay restful, subdued to a murmur. Somewhere a piano was being murdered behind a closed window; somewhere else a barrowman sang one line over and over like a charm against the night. The city’s great metallic throat had, for an hour, chosen to speak softly.

“I have always regarded myself,” said my father, “as a failure in life, and it has troubled me.”

I felt him pulled the slightest little bit away from me, as though Barbara, holding his other arm, had drawn him towards her with a swift pressure.

“But do you know the idea that has come to me within the last few months? That on the whole I have been successful. I am like a man,” continued my father, “who in some deep wood has been frightened, thinking he has lost his way, and suddenly coming to the end of it finds that by some lucky chance he has been guided to the right point after all. I cannot tell you what a comfort it is to me.”

“What is the right point?” asked Barbara.

“Ah, that I cannot tell you,” answered my father, with a laugh. “I only know that for me it is here where I am. All the time I thought I was wandering away from it I was drawing nearer to it. It is very wonderful. I am just where I ought to be. If I had only known I never need have worried.”

Whether it would have troubled either him or my mother very much had it been otherwise, I cannot say; for Life, so small a thing when looked at beside Death, had lost its terror for them. But I like to remember that Fortune at the last was kind to my father—prospering his adventures not to the extent his sanguine nature had dreamed, but sufficiently; so that no fear for our future marred the peaceful passing of his tender spirit.

Or should I award thanks, not to Fate, but rather to sweet Barbara? And behind her, do I not detect shameless old Hasluck, grinning good-naturedly in the background?

“Now, Uncle Luke, I want your advice. Dad’s given me this cheque as a birthday present. I don’t want to spend it. How shall I invest it?”

“My dear, why not consult your father?”

“Now, Uncle Luke, dad’s a dear, especially after dinner, but you and I know him. Giving me a present is one thing, doing business for me is another. He’d unload on me. He’d never be able to resist the temptation.”

My father would suggest, and Barbara would thank him. But a minute later she would murmur, with an air of one criticising a recipe:

“You don’t know anything about Argentinos.”

My father did not; but Barbara did—to quite a remarkable extent for a young girl.

“That child has insisted on leaving this cheque with me and I have advised her to buy Argentinos,” my father would observe after she was gone. “I am going to put a few hundreds into them myself. I hope they will turn out all right, if only for her sake. I have a presentiment somehow that they will.”

A month later Barbara would greet him with:

“Isn’t it lucky we bought Argentinos!”

“Yes; they haven’t turned out badly, have they? I had a feeling, you know, for Argentinos.”

“You’re a genius, Uncle Luke. And now we will sell out and buy Calcuttas, won’t we?”

“Sell out? But why?”

“You said so. You said, ‘We will sell out in about a month and be quite safe.’”

“My dear, I’ve no recollection of it.”

But Barbara had, and before she had done with him, so had he. And the next day Argentinos would be sold—not any too soon—and Calcuttas bought.

Could money so gained bring a blessing with it? The question would plague my father.

“It’s very much like gambling,” he would mutter uneasily to himself at each success. “Uncommonly like gambling.”

“It is for your mother,” he would impress upon me. “When she is gone, Paul, put it aside. Keep it for doing good; that may make it clean. Start your own life without any help from it.”

He need not have troubled. It went the road that all luck derived, however indirectly, from old Hasluck ever went. Yet it served good purpose on its way, like a rich stock that does not keep but nourishes while it lasts.

But the most marvellous feat ever accomplished by Barbara, to my thinking, was the carrying off of my father and mother to witness “A Voice from the Grave, or the Power of Love, New and Original Drama in five acts and thirteen tableaux.”

They had been bred in a narrow creed, both my father and my mother. That Puritan blood flowed in their veins which throughout our land has drowned much harmless joyousness; yet those who know of it only from hearsay do foolishly to speak but ill of it. If ever earnest times should come again—not how to enjoy but how to live being the question; Fate demanding of us not what we have but what we are—we may regret that they are fewer among us than formerly, those who trained themselves to despise all pleasure because in pleasure they saw the subtlest foe to principle and duty. No graceful growth, this Puritanism; its roots are in hard, stern facts. But it is strong. Its men feared and its women loved God, and if their words were harsh their hearts were tender. If they shut out sunshine, it was that their eyes might see better the glory beyond. And if their view be correct—that earth’s threescore years and ten are but preparation for eternity—who shall call them even foolish for turning away their thoughts from its allurements?

“Still, I think I should like to have a look at one, just to see what it is like,” argued my father. “One cannot judge of a thing that one knows nothing about.”

I imagine it was his first argument rather than his second that convinced my mother.

“That is true,” she answered. “I remember how shocked my poor father was when he found me one night at the bedroom window reading Sir Walter Scott by the light of the moon.”

“What about the boy?” said my father, for I had been included in the invitation.

“We will all be wicked together,” said my mother.

So an evening or two later the four of us stood at the corner of Pigott Street waiting for the ’bus, while the neighbourhood made its usual music: a distant factory throb, a whistle like an insult, the nearer slap of boots and the occasional shriek of a wheel that had never met oil in its life. The air was close, thick with summer and London and the day’s cooking escaping from other people’s windows—fried fish somewhere, cabbage somewhere, and, in a brief blessing, the warm yeasty breath of a baker’s shop shutting up.

“It is a close evening,” said my father; “let’s go the whole hog and ride outside.”
