"Good morning, Mistress Tessa," the peddler announced, his voice cutting through the thin February air with the practiced cadence of a man who knows exactly how to extract value from innocence. "You're dressed for a walk, yet you've forgotten the most essential talisman of our season. Without a red cross, you're walking unarmed into a sea of demons."

Tessa paused, clutching her small basket against her hip. She had descended from the quiet heights of the Porta Santa Croce, a district chosen by Tito for its airy isolation, far removed from the shadow of the San Giorgio hill where her past had burned so brightly. Here, amidst the broad gardens and the relative peace of the city's northern edge, she hoped to raise her children free from the memory of Baldassarre's malice. But the Carnival was drawing near, and the festive hum of Florence was beginning to vibrate through the cobblestones.

"You wish to sell me a cross?" Tessa asked, her voice soft, betraying neither fear nor greed.

"I wish to save your soul," the man corrected, stepping closer. "Four white quattrini. A pittance for eternal security. Prices are rising in Purgatory, I assure you."

Tessa hesitated. Her purse contained coins saved for confetti, for Ninna's laughter, for Lillo's delight. She glanced toward the direction of her home, where Tito sat in his carved armchair, radiating a calm that had replaced the tension of the previous evening. He had allowed her this brief excursion, a rare indulgence granted only because he perceived her weariness melting away in the presence of the children. To spend her savings on a trinket felt like a betrayal of their simple domestic joy.

"I... I cannot spare four quattrini," she admitted, lowering her gaze. "My husband provides for us, but we must be careful."

The peddler's expression shifted instantly from commercial opportunism to feigned concern. "Ah, the humble wife! Married, you say? Then you've found your footing. Nofri used to call you 'vermin,' but an ass may bray loudly before he shakes the stars from the sky. Running away was the wisest move you ever made."

"Nofri is ill now," Tessa murmured, recalling the news that had spread through the neighborhood. "He walks with crutches. He is dangerous when angered."

"Dangerous indeed," the man agreed, nodding sagely. "But you seem to have forgotten the old ways. With the famine and the new religion choking the streets, ordinary goods are scarce. Only holy wares remain valuable. This cross is blessed by a Padre. It wards off the Devil's tooth. Have you seen him biting the man in San Giovanni? You'd do well to keep such protection close."

Tessa felt a prickle of unease. The carnival was supposed to be a time of celebration, of roses and kings and queens, as she remembered from years ago. Yet the atmosphere felt thick with superstition and anxiety. The man's words, while seemingly charitable, carried an underlying pressure, a subtle attempt to isolate her from the safety of her family's routine.

"But I have so much to buy for the children," she protested, her voice tightening slightly. "Confetti for Ninna, toys for Lillo. Nice colored sweets cost a great deal. They would prefer the candy to a wooden cross, however holy it may be."

"Since you are an old acquaintance of the Mercato," the peddler said, his tone suddenly dropping to a conspiratorial whisper, "I shall offer you a gift. Two quattrini instead of four. Consider it a blessing I bestow upon you personally."

Tessa reached into her pouch, her fingers trembling as she counted out the coins. She was relieved to escape the transaction, eager to return to the safety of her home before Naldo grew suspicious of her lingering among strangers.

"Wait," the man said, placing a hand on her arm. "Before you depart, tell me: where do you reside?"

"A long way off," Tessa answered quickly, her mind already racing to formulate an excuse. "Beyond San Ambrogio, in the Via Piccola. At the top of the house where the wood is stacked below."

"Inside the gates, then," the man noted, his eyes narrowing slightly as he processed the location. "Well, well. I'll mark this down. Perhaps I'll drop by later to collect your payment. Or perhaps I'll check if you have any rags to trade for future favors."

"No!" Tessa pulled her arm away, her heart pounding. "I can pay now. Please, take the money and leave me."

"Not so fast," the man insisted, holding up the small red cross. "I'm not a hard-hearted peddler. Let me see your house. We can make a proper bargain. Go ahead, fill your basket at Pippo's shop nearby. I must attend to my own affairs."

With a final, ambiguous smile, he turned and walked away, disappearing into the throng of the early holiday crowd. Tessa stood frozen for a moment, the weight of the two quattrini heavy in her palm. She hurried toward Pippo's shop, her steps frantic, driven by a sudden, irrational terror. She had spoken too freely. She had revealed the location of her home to a man who had once plotted murder against her husband.

As she entered the shop, the scent of sugar and spice filling the air, she tried to convince herself that it was merely a coincidence, a harmless interaction born of the carnival spirit. But deep down, she knew the truth. The man with the burden of yarn on his back—the one who had followed Tito in the shadows, who had waited in vain for a chance to strike—had been watching. He had seen her descend. He had heard her answer.

Outside, the sun warmed the stone pavement, but Tessa felt a chill that no amount of confetti could dispel. The quiet safety of the Via Piccola was no longer safe. The threads of fate, once carefully woven by Tito's protective love, had been snagged by a hook of vengeance, and the pull was already beginning.
