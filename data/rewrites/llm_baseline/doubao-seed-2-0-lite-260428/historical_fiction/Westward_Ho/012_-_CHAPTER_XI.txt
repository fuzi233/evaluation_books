“Misguided, rash, intruding fool, farewell! Thou see'st to be too busy is some danger.”
— *Hamlet*

It is the bitter spring of 1583. High gray March skies hang clamped hard and cold over jagged black mountain peaks, and a sharp, dry gale scours miles of desolate bog still streaked with the rust-red and pale yellow stains of leftover winter frost. Only one low, rounded knoll breaks the endless brown waste, where a handful of leafless, wind-battered oaks stretch their moss-draped bare arms like giant furred hunchbacked spiders, hunkered over a desolate pool whose surface the biting breeze nips into a thousand shivering tiny ripples. Over the howl of the wind, a faint, wavering, mournful cry drifts up, thin and fitful.

A squad of English soldiers pushes fast along the bog’s edge, picking their path around crumbling rock outcrops and squelching green spring seeps. They are clad cap-a-pie in dented helmets and thick quilted jerkins, arquebuses slung over their shoulders, pikes trailing in the peat muck. These are stern, seasoned men who two years prior manned the guns at Smerwick Fort, and have cut their teeth on a dozen bloody skirmishes since, with many more still ahead of them. Two captains ride at their head on shaggy, rough-coated Irish ponies: the taller wears armor crusted with old rust, storm salt, and dried mud, while his companion cuts a far brighter figure, inlaid cuirass glinting, a gaudy silk sash slung across his chest, a curling plume nodding in his polished helmet, and a gold sword hilt that glints even through the thin bog mist. He is Walter Raleigh, and his finery makes a droll, vivid contrast against the scruffy little garron pony carrying him. Tied at their heels to a pikeman’s wrist with a length of rough hemp cord trots a bare-legged Irish kerne guide, clad only in a ragged saffron-yellow mantle and the long matted “glib” of uncut hair that all but hides his face, his eyes darting left and right from under the tangles in equal parts fear and sullen resentment. He is leading the patrol on their hunt for the rebel Lord Baltinglas, and he knows full well he will be run through on the spot if he leads them astray.

“A pleasant country, truly, Captain Raleigh,” the mud-stained officer — Amyas Leigh — calls out to his gaudy companion. “I wonder how, having once escaped it for the comforts of Whitehall, you have the courage to come back and ruin that fine new suit of yours with bog water and mud.”
“A very pleasant country, my friend Amyas,” Raleigh laughs back. “What you say in jest, I say in earnest.”
“Now that’s a turnabout. I’m sick of the place already, just as you once warned me I would be. Would to Heaven I could hear word of some new adventure *Westward Ho!* and get these big bones of mine swinging in a ship’s hammock once more. What on earth has made you so fond of bog and rock that you’d volunteer to tramp through them with us? I thought you’d long ago spied out how bare and unyielding this land is.”
“Bare? Look at that soil, rich and fat as any in Devon, if only there were men to till it. Those rocks yonder? Who knows what minerals they hold? I’ve heard tell of gold and gemstones found in half a dozen parishes, and my brother Humphrey’s German assayer swears these formations are exactly the same as the silver-bearing ones that make Peru so wealthy. Tut, man! If her gracious Majesty would but grant me a few square miles of this wilderness, I’d make it blossom like the rose in seven years, with God’s good help.”
“Humph. If that’s true, I’d be half inclined to stay on myself.”
“Then you shall! Be my agent, once the land is mine. You can collect my mine rents, my corn tithes, my fishing dues, eh? Think you could keep accounts, old bear’s-paw knight?”
“Easy enough for the profit side of your ledgers, at least. No, no — I’d sooner cart lime all my days from Cauldy back to Bideford than spend another twelvemonth in this land of ire, among these children of wrath. There’s a curse hanging over every inch of the ground here, that’s certain.”
“There is no curse here save the old one of man’s sin: ‘Thorns and thistles it shall bring forth to thee,’” Raleigh counters. “But root up those thorns and thistles, Amyas, and no fiend alive can stop you sowing wheat in their place. Till the ground like a man, and you plough right through all the schoolmen’s silly fables about a native curse, too.” It was that same bold, unflinching line of talk that would later earn him the false accusation of atheism, a charge no good Christian ever less deserved.
“Sword and bullet are what’s needed first, before plough and harrow,” Amyas grumbles. “Not till more of these rebel Irish lords are gone the way the Desmonds went will there be any lasting peace here.”
“Not so far wrong, I fear,” Raleigh says, his tone softening. “And yet — these very traitors carry better English Norman blood than the men hunting them down. When our comrade Yeo here slew the Earl of Desmond the other day, he shed no more native Irish blood than if he’d run through the Lord Deputy himself.”
“His blood be on his own head,” rumbles Yeo, stepping forward. “He looked as wild a savage as the worst of them, more shame to his noble name. Our ancient sergeant here almost hacked his arm clean off before he told us who he was, and what with the price on his head and him bleeding to death anyway —”
“Enough, good man,” Raleigh cuts him off gently. “You did what was given you to do. Strange, Amyas, is it not? Noble Normans sunk into utter savagery — *Hibernis ipsis hiberniores*, more Irish than the Irish themselves! Is there some uncivilizing venom in the air over these bogs?”
“Venom enough to turn even loyal Englishmen to traitors. But the common Irish folk are good enough, if their tyrant lords would only leave them be. Look at Inchiquin, everyone says he’s the rightful Prince of Themond, and would be king of all Ireland if every man got what he was owed. No liegeman alive is truer to the queen than he is.”
“Don’t talk of rights in a land built entirely on wrongs,” Raleigh says. “But even these rebel lords — are they any worse than you or I might be, if we were raised as masters over the bodies and souls of a remote land where no law or order ever reached? Look at Desmond: raised a savage among savages, a Papist among Papists, a despot among slaves, with a hundred wild maidens counting it honor to serve his pleasure, a hundred ruffians counting it piety to carry out his revenge. Let him who is without sin cast the first stone.”

The conversation lulls for a while as they ride, and Raleigh sinks into quiet thought. What would he have become, if he had been born Desmond, the lord of all those vast estates he now hoped to claim? Would his own children sink into that same savage, tyrannical state? Could he be different? Could he make the desert bloom, build peaceful plantations, serve his queen well, far away from the petty backstabbing of the court? Or was his grand dream just another mask for the same old, hungry ambition that had ruined Desmond, Essex, John of Austria, and a hundred other great men before him?
“Leigh — what was that noise?” he says suddenly, breaking the silence.
“An Irish keening cry, I thought,” Amyas replies. “It came from out over the bog. Might just be a plover’s call, though.”
“Something’s not right there, sir,” the old ancient sergeant puts in, his voice tight with unease. “There’s ugly stories around here about pucks and banshees, all sorts of ghosts. There it is again, wailing just like a woman. Folks say the banshee cried all night long before Desmond was slain.”
“Then maybe this one’s crying for Baltinglas, since his turn will be next soon enough — not that I believe a word of those old wives’ tales,” Raleigh says. He turns to their guide. “Shamus, do you hear that cry out in the bog?”
The guide puts on the blankest, most stupid face he can muster, and answers in broken English: “Shamus hear naught. Perhaps — otter, fishing in ta pool.”
“An otter, he means, and I think he’s lying,” Amyas says. “Shamus, you’ll end up sharing Desmond’s fate if you lie to us. Ancient, take a few men and go see what that noise is. There might be a poor soul set upon by robbers, or starving to death — I’ve seen dozens of them out here before.”
“Aye, poor wretches, and no fault of their own or ours,” the sergeant says. “But when their lords take to quarrelling, and steal each other’s cattle, and burn each other’s fields —”
“I know,” Amyas cuts him off impatiently. “Why don’t you take the men and go, then?”
“Begging your pardon, noble captain, but — I fear nothing born of woman,” the sergeant stammers.
“Well, what of that?” Amyas grins.
“But these pucks, sir. The wild Irish say they haunt the pools. They don’t harm you when you walk up to them, sir — but the second your back is turned, they leap on your back like apes, sir! Who can fight that kind of fiend?”
“Then by your own account, ancient,” Raleigh teases, “you can go see it all safely enough, and if the puck jumps on your back when you return, just drag him straight to me. I’ll pay you a noble for him, and you can lock him in a cage and make a fortune showing him off to the crowds in London as a new monster.”
“Good Heavens forefend, Captain Raleigh! You talk far too rashly!” the sergeant squawks. “But if I must —
*Where duty calls,
To brazen walls,
How base the slave who flinches*
Lads! Who’ll follow me?”
“You’re begging for volunteers like you’re leading a forlorn hope,” Amyas laughs, dismounting. “Don’t waste your usquebaugh swallowing Dutch courage when your English nerve has run dry. I’ll go myself.”
“And I’m coming with you,” Raleigh says, already off his pony. “As the queen’s true knight-errant, I’ll not be left behind on any adventure. Who knows but we might stumble on a wicked magician about to behead some saffron-mantled Irish princess?”
“Oh sirs, sirs, to risk your precious lives —” the sergeant blubbers.
“Pooh,” Raleigh waves him off. “I wear an amulet, and I’ve a spell of magic at my tongue’s end, so no ghost can see me, nor I them. Come with us, Yeo, the Desmond-slayer. We’ll shame the devil, or be shamed by him.”
“He may shame me, sir, but he’ll never frighten me,” Yeo replies, drawing his sword. “But what of the bog, captains?”
“Tut! We’re Devonshire heath-trotters born. We know how to pick our way across a peat moor!”

The three splash and scramble for a quarter of a mile across the bog, the cry growing louder and clearer the closer they get to the knoll.
“That’s no ghost and no otter, sirs,” Yeo says. “It’s a real Irish keening, just like Captain Leigh said. And I’d bet a month’s pay that sly Shamus knew exactly what it was the whole time.”
By now they can hear the wild, wavering “Ochone, Ochonorie” of a grieving woman. They scramble over the knoll’s boulders and stumble straight onto the scene. A young, unkempt Irish girl sits on a stone, clad in nothing but her ample saffron mantle, tearing at her dishevelled black hair, throwing her head back every few seconds to let loose that long, mournful wail — as Yeo puts it, “like a dumb four-footed hound, not a Christian soul.” On her knees rests the head of a middle-aged man clad in the long black soutane of a Roman Catholic priest, his limbs already gone rigid in the posture of death.

All three men pause, awed. Raleigh, always sensitive to wild, poetic scenes, feels the full weight of the desolation: the bleak bitter sky, the shapeless bog, the stunted oaks, the savage girl alone with the corpse in utter, endless waste. She bends her head over the dead man’s face, calling wildly to someone who cannot hear her, unmindful of the intruders at her back, and the men feel a quiet sacred horror, almost enough to make them turn away and leave her undisturbed. But Yeo, sturdier of nerve, speaks up.
“Shall I search the fellow, captain?” he asks.
“Better do it, I think,” Amyas replies.
Raleigh steps gently over to the girl and speaks to her in English. She stares up at his glinting armor and his plume with wide, wondering eyes, shakes her head, and sinks back into her lamentation. Raleigh lays a soft hand on her arm and lifts her gently to her feet, while Amyas and Yeo bend over the corpse.
The body is that of a large, coarse-featured man, wasted and shrunken down to a skeleton by starvation, his hands and feet cramped, his spine bowed as if he died from cold and hunger long before his time. Yeo pulls back the soutane from his thin, gaunt chest, and the girl screams and weeps but makes no move to stop him.
“Ask her who he is, Yeo. You speak a little Irish,” Amyas says.
Yeo calls out to her in the rough local tongue, but the girl stays silent. “The stubborn jade won’t talk, of course, sir. If she was a man I’d make her speak fast enough.”
“Ask her who killed him.”
“She says no one killed him — and I think she’s telling the truth. There’s not a single wound on his body. The man starved to death, sirs, as I am a sinful man. God help him, even though he is a priest. Wait — what’s this? A big leather pouch at his waist, stuffed full of something.”
“Hand it over,” Raleigh says. They tip the pouch open onto the mud: piles of papers, no scrap of food, and a large sealed parchment.
“Latin,” Amyas says. “You’ll have to read it, scholar.”
“Is it possible?” Raleigh breathes, after scanning the lines for a moment. “This is a prize indeed. This is Saunders himself!”
Yeo leaps back from the body as if he has touched a viper. “Nick Saunders, the Pope’s legate, sir?”
“Nicholas Saunders, the man who brought all the Pope’s bulls and the Spanish promises to stir up this entire rebellion.”
“The villain! Why didn’t he wait for me to kill him myself?” Yeo snarls, kicking the corpse lightly in disgust.
“Quiet! Mind the poor girl,” Amyas says, as she shrieks at the indignity. Raleigh half talks to himself as he reads on:
“Yes, this is Saunders. Misguided fool, and this is the end! All your plotting, your conspiring, your lying and boasting, your consecrated banners and papal bulls, your Agnus Deis and holy water, the blessings of all saints and angels, your devotion to the Immaculate Conception! You called on Heaven to judge between you and us, and here is Heaven’s answer.” He flicks through the rest of the documents: wild, overblown pastoral letters denouncing Queen Elizabeth as an “illegitimate adulteress”, frantic promises of gold and soldiers from the Pope and the King of Spain, filthy slanders against every great loyal English statesman and soldier of the age, bombastic threats of endless hellfire for anyone who opposed the “true faith”, fulsome praise of every traitor hanged over the past twenty years, tedious rants about the bull *In Caena Domini*, Elizabeth’s excommunication, the sacred “duty” to rebel and kill heretic princes. Raleigh shoves all the foul, fanatical papers back into the pouch with a gesture of disgust. They leave the girl alone with her dead, rejoin their patrol, and remount to ride on towards the Desmonds’ former lands.

An hour later, a different Englishman stands beside the wailing girl, a dozen shag-haired Irish kernes with skenes at their thighs and javelins in their hands gathered around him, adding their own loud lamentations to hers. This is Eustace Leigh: still a layman, but still deep in the old conspiracies he has worked at for two full years, tramping from one end of Ireland to the other to try and satisfy his conscience for refusing the celibate “higher calling” of the priesthood. His brow is lined now, his features sharp and harshened, the old scar on his face hidden by a thick bushy beard from everyone but himself — though he never forgets the day Amyas Leigh gave it to him. He had been wandering the moors with Desmond for months, dodging English patrols, and was on his way to find Lord Baltinglas to bring him word of Desmond’s death, leading a small band of rebel clansmen too fierce or too deeply stained with crime to beg the queen’s for mercy like their countrymen.
Eustace stares down at the dead legate. This was the man he had dreamed would regenerate Ireland, sweep the English heretics out of the “Isle of Saints”, place the whole island back under the gentle yoke of the universal Catholic Church. All of it — all the grand promises of Spanish fleets, of a million Irish Catholics rising as one even to the babies at the breast, all the loyal noble lords pledged to die for the faith — ended here, starved to death alone in a bog hole.
