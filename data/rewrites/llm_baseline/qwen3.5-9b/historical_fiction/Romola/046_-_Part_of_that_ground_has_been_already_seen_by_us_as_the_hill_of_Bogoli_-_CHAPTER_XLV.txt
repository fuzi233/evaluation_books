Following the auspicious arrival of the messenger bearing the olive branch—a favor of fortune Tito had not dared to hope for—he was immediately tasked with duties of far more deliberate consequence. Pausing before the Palazzo Vecchio, he waited for the return of the *Dieci*, the body governing external and military affairs, to formally submit the findings of his clandestine mission to Pisa. This report served as the prologue to an open embassy led by Bernardo Rucellai, aimed at securing a peaceful accord between Florence, Emperor Maximilian, and the Italian League. Tito's aptitude for diplomacy was now fully proven; as he delivered his intelligence with clarity and precision, Bernardo del Nero, then a member of the *Dieci*, could not suppress his admiration. Though he would have preferred to hide it, Bernardo's initial disdain for Tito had resurfaced and intensified following the controversial sale of the library.

Romola had remained silent regarding the transaction, offering her godfather no explanation. Bernardo interpreted this silence as a command to avoid the subject, yet he was convinced that violating her father's wishes had inflicted a deep, festering wound upon her spirit. Observant of her demeanor, he noted further signs that her marriage brought her little happiness. "Ah," he murmured to himself, "this doubtless explains her turn toward Fra Girolamo and the *Piagnoni*—a shift I never anticipated. Women, unhappy and childless, inevitably drift either into folly or into a religion so austere they feel burdened with all of Heaven's labor. As for my poor child Romola, I maintain what I always said: stuffing her with Latin and Greek has rendered her less a woman and more a creature who spends her days merely pricking her fingers with needles. And as for her husband, a man employed everywhere because he is a tool with a smooth handle, I pray that Tornabuoni and his ilk do not find their fingers severed. Well, well, *solco torto, sacco dritto*—many a full sack comes from a crooked furrow; and he who insists on commanding only honest men will find his wages scant."

Holding firmly to the conviction that political agents could not be morally scrutinized, Bernardo chose to remain aloof from Tito's disrepute. Aside from matters reserved for Romola's sake, Bernardo possessed no concrete grievance against the useful Greek save for his nationality and his personal dislike. Tito's duplicity—feigning loyalty to the popular government while harboring Medicean sympathies—was a trait shared by half the Medicean faction; Tito merely executed it with superior skill. Consequently, Bernardo treated Tito with polite indifference, a coldness Tito mirrored with scrupulous, distant respect. In Florence, it was still widely believed that any service rendered to Romola's husband constituted a tribute to her godfather.

Having discharged his official duties at the Old Palace, Tito felt the weight of the day's public obligations lift. Exhausted and parched from hours of riding, he declined to return home immediately. There were items in his saddlebag (*scarsella*) and burdens on his mind that demanded release, but these opportunities required subtle timing; they could not appear sought-after. Instead, he strolled aimlessly toward the Piazza del Duomo. Although the religious procession had concluded, the bells continued to toll, and the populace moved restlessly through the streets, craving a tangible outlet for their jubilation. While the new discipline, championed by the Friar, insisted that Christ alone be King and that all pleasures conform to Christian modesty, a secret yearning persisted among the youths shouting *"Viva Gesù!"* for a burst of vigorous stone-throwing as thanks.

Tito, passing through the crowd, could not evade recognition as the bearer of peace. To shed the unwanted ovation and the barrage of eager inquiries, he deftly claimed that Meo di Sasso, the authentic messenger from Livorno, was arriving and could be found near the Porta San Frediano. Knowing more than he admitted, he used this fabrication to clear his path. Once free of importunity, he entered the Piazza del Duomo, scanning the square with an air of careless leisure, though his gaze was sharply focused on detecting a specific presence.

The termination of the procession at the cathedral meant an unusual density of loiterers and conversationalists around Nello's shop, precisely where Tito hoped to find his opportunity. As expected, a group clustered near the northern railings of the Baptistery. Spotting the tallest figure in the group, Tito feigned total indifference, merely turning his head to offer a perfunctory greeting as he tossed the end of his hood over his shoulder. Yet, the tall, broad-shouldered man thus acknowledged appeared formidable. He wore a richly embroidered tunic and abundant linen in the latest French style, with a sword and poniard of exquisite craftsmanship hanging from his belt. His hat, adorned with a crimson plume, seemed a defiant rejection of the somber Florentine dress code, recently exaggerated by the influence of the *Piagnoni*. Indefinable markers of youth accentuated the coarseness of his wide face and substantial waist, while his eyes held a crude, desecrating stare that would offend any refined sensibility.

He and his companions, similarly attired young men carrying weapons, exchanged jokes with an ostentatious laughter designed to prove their immunity to shame, despite obvious suspicions to the contrary. Indeed, there were ample grounds for such suspicion. This broad-shouldered figure with the red feather was Dolfo Spini, leader of the *Compagnacci*, or Evil Companions. These were the dissolute sons of the old aristocracy, enemies of the Medici, foes of the popular government, and bitter adversaries of Savonarola. Dolfo, heir to the great house overlooking the Santa Trinita bridge, had organized these youths into an armed band dedicated to defending extravagant feasts and carnal sins against the reforming pietists who threatened to render the world so chaste and temperate that existence itself became unbearable. Just that morning, he had proclaimed Florence doomed to famine and ruin due to its blind adherence to the Friar, arguing that salvation lay solely in joining the League and expelling the Frate to Rome, as the Pope had long commanded. Thus, it was suspected that Messer Dolfo Spini's heart burned with no pure joy at the unexpected reinforcements that seemingly fulfilled the Friar's predictions. The laughter erupting as Tito approached the group near Nello's door failed to dispel this unease.

Leaning against the doorpost at the center of the gathering was a close-shaven, keen-eyed figure named Niccolò Machiavelli. Though young, he had already pierced the small secrets of human egoism.

"Messer Dolfo's head," he remarked, "is more of a pumpkin than I imagined. I judge men's stupidity by the devices they trust to deceive others. Your dullest beast of all is he who grins and claims he does not mind, just after his shins have been kicked." Smiling as the circle opened to admit Tito, he continued, "If I were a trifle duller now, I should pretend to cherish this Melema, who holds a secretaryship perfectly suited to me—as if poorly paid Latin could love better Latin that is better paid! Melema, you are a pestilently clever fellow, very much in my way, and I regret hearing you've secured another stroke of luck today."

"Questionable luck, Niccolò," replied Tito, placing a friendly hand on his shoulder. "I possess nothing yet but being grabbed and breathed upon by wool-combers, leaving me as soiled and battered from riding as a letter-carrier from Bologna."

"Ah! You crave a touch of my art, Messer Oratore," said Nello, stepping forward at the sound of Tito's voice. "I perceive your chin bears yesterday's stubble. Come, consign yourself to the priest of all the Muses. Sandro, fetch the lather quickly!"

"In truth, Nello, that is precisely what I most desire at this moment," Tito said, sitting down. "That was why I directed my steps toward your shop rather than returning home immediately after concluding my business at the Palazzo."

"Yes, indeed, it is unfitting that you should present yourself to Madonna Romola with a rusty chin and a tangled mass of hair. Nothing undaint should approach the Florentine lily; though I see her constantly wandering like a sunbeam among the rags lining our corners—or perhaps she resembles a moonbeam now, for I met her yesterday and she looked as pale and worn as the fainting Madonna in Fra Giovanni's painting. You must attend to her, my learned friend: she observes too many fasts and vigils in your absence."

Tito offered a melancholy shrug. "It is too true, Nello. She has deprived herself of half her proper sustenance every day during this famine. But what can I do? A husband's influence is powerless against the Friar's."

"As are all other influences, including that of the Holy Father," interjected Domenico Cennini, who had entered with Tito. "Have you gleaned anything at Pisa regarding the prevailing winds in Rome, Melema?"
