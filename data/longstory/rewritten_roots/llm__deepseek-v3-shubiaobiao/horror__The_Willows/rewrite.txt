The Danube slipped into desolation long before Budapest, its waters sprawling into a labyrinth of channels, its shores swallowed by an endless sea of whispering willows. The maps called it *Sümpfe*—marshes—but that sterile word failed to capture the eerie life of the place.

In flood season, the river devoured the land, drowning islands, erasing sandbanks. But now, under the summer sun, the willows swayed like supplicants, their silver undersides flashing as the wind combed through them. They were not trees but restless spirits, hunched and trembling, their roots clutching the shifting earth as if afraid to be swept away.

We had entered this drowned world by chance, our canoe carried on the river’s whim. One moment, the orderly vineyards of Austria; the next, this wilderness of water and whispering thickets. The Danube no longer played the gracious host. It split into three snarling channels, each daring us to choose wrong.

*"If you take a side channel,"* a Hungarian officer had warned, *"you’ll find yourselves stranded. No people. No help. Just the river and the willows."*

We camped on a shrinking island, its sand dissolving beneath us. The wind screamed. The willows hissed back.

I walked the shore, watching the current claw at the banks. The water was the color of rust, thick with secrets. Something about the willows unsettled me—not their size, not their numbers, but the way they *watched*. As if they knew we didn’t belong.

Then the river coughed up its first omen: a shape tumbling in the current. A corpse? No—an otter, sleek and black, its eyes catching the light like polished coins. It stared at us before vanishing beneath the waves.

The second omen came at dusk. A boat, moving fast. A man stood at its prow, his arms raised—not in greeting, but in warning. Or warding. The wind stole his voice, but his gestures were frantic. Then the mist swallowed him.

*"Superstitious peasant,"* my companion muttered. But his usual confidence was frayed.

Night fell. The willows pressed closer. The river’s voice dropped to a murmur, but the murmurs formed words—or so my mind insisted.

We were not alone here.

And we were not welcome.

I awoke abruptly, my skin prickling as if touched by unseen fingers. Moonlight cut through the tent flap, painting silver stripes across the canvas. My watch gleamed—past midnight. The Swede lay motionless beside me, his breath steady, undisturbed. Outside, the wind snarled through the willows, but it was not the wind that had woken me.

Something was *wrong*.

I sat up, my pulse hammering, and peered into the night. The trees thrashed as if in agony, their branches clawing at the sky, yet our tent sat untouched, sheltered in the hollow. The unease did not fade. It coiled tighter, a serpent in my gut. Moving silently, I crawled outside, the sand still warm beneath my palms.

Then I saw them.

Above the swaying bushes, the willows’ silhouettes twisted—but not from the wind. *Figures* rose from the earth, towering, inhuman, their bodies flowing like molten bronze. They climbed in a ceaseless spiral, limbs merging and parting, vanishing into the blackness above. Not branches. Not shadows. *Things.*

My breath hitched. I should have woken the Swede. But a deeper instinct whispered: *You do not want him to see this. You do not want it to be real.*

I crouched, transfixed. Their forms were vast, grotesquely graceful, surging upward in an endless procession. No faces—only the suggestion of limbs, of torsos bending as if in worship to some unseen god. They moved *through* the leaves, as though the willows were mere veils hiding a world beyond.

Awe choked me. This was no trick of moonlight. The standard of reality had shattered. These were the old things, the secret things, stirred by our trespass. The river’s roar, the wind’s teeth on my skin—these were real. And so were *they*.

For a wild moment, I wanted to kneel. To *worship*.

Then the wind struck me like a hand, staggering me backward. The spell snapped. Reason clawed its way back—*hallucination, illusion*—but the figures still climbed, relentless. Until, without warning, they were gone.

The terror came then, cold and suffocating. The willows whispered, the night pulsed with malice. I scrambled back into the tent, yanking the flap shut, burying myself under the blankets like a child. But outside, the wind screamed on. And the willows *watched*.

The certainty that I had not dreamed it all only made the night worse. Sleep, when it finally came, was thin and brittle—a surface layer of exhaustion beneath which some vigilant part of me remained awake, listening.

Then, a second time, I jolted upright in pure terror.

Not the wind. Not the river. Something else.

A slow, creeping presence had stalked into my awareness, shrinking the edges of my unconsciousness until nothing remained but raw, trembling wakefulness. Outside, a sound—countless small patterings, like fingertips drumming against the tent. They had been approaching for some time, I realized, seeping into my dreams before yanking me fully alert. My breath came short, labored, as if an invisible weight pressed down on my chest. Though the air was thick with summer heat, my skin was slick with cold sweat.

Something was pressing against the tent.

Not just the sides. The roof too. A steady, deliberate pressure.

The wind? No. Rain? The trees? I scrambled for explanations. Then it struck me—the poplar. The island’s lone great tree. A branch must have snapped in the storm, caught halfway in its fall, its leaves now scraping the canvas, waiting for the next gust to finish the job and crush us.

I flung the tent flap aside and stumbled out, shouting for the Swede to follow.

But outside—nothing.

No branch. No rain. No unseen hand pressing down. Only the dull grey light of predawn filtering through the willows, the sand pale beneath my bare feet. The wind still howled, but the fire had died, and the eastern sky bled red between the trees. Hours must have passed since I last stood here, watching those shapes rise into the darkness. The memory returned like a sickness.

The river had swollen further, its roar shaking the air, its spray stinging my skin. Yet there was no real threat in sight—nothing to justify the dread coiled tight in my gut.

The Swede hadn’t stirred. I left him sleeping.

I took stock of the camp—the overturned canoe, the paddles, the supplies dangling from a branch. And everywhere, the willows. Always the willows.

A bird cried. Ducks shot past overhead. The sand spun in the wind, biting at my ankles. I circled the tent, then ventured further, toward the riverbank. The far shore looked different now, reshaped by the current. I dipped my hands into the water, splashed my face. The sunrise was spreading, gold and crimson, but the beauty of it couldn’t shake the unease crawling under my skin.

On my way back, I deliberately passed beneath the bushes where I’d seen the figures rise. And there—midway through—terror seized me so violently I froze.

Something moved in the shadows.

Large. Swift.

A shape brushed past me—close, unmistakable. A presence.

Then the wind slammed into me, shoving me forward, and the fear loosened its grip. Just the wind, I told myself. The wind that walks like something alive beneath the trees.

But this fear was new. Vast. It carried awe with it, a terrible wonder that almost tempered the horror. When I climbed to the island’s highest point and saw the river aflame with dawn, a wild, aching cry rose in my throat—but it died unvoiced.

Because then I saw it.

The landscape had changed.

Not the view. The arrangement. The willows.

They were closer.

Much closer.

Impossibly closer.

Had the wind shifted them? Or had they moved on their own? I remembered the pattering, the pressure on the tent, the suffocating weight on my chest. My legs wavered; I swayed like one of the slender trees myself.

The thought came, cold and sharp: *They’re alive. And they’re coming for us.*

I nearly laughed. It was absurd. But laughter wouldn’t come, because the deeper terror was this—the attack wouldn’t be physical. It would be in the mind. And it had already begun.

The wind shoved at me. The sun climbed higher. I had stood there too long, afraid to descend back into the thick of the willows.

At last, I crept back to the tent, pausing to measure the distances between the trees and our shelter. I marked the gaps. Noted the shrinking space.

Then I slid inside. The Swede slept on, undisturbed.

Good. If he hadn’t seen it, hadn’t felt it, then maybe I could convince myself it wasn’t real. Daylight would bleach the night’s horrors into fantasy.

I wrapped myself in my blankets, exhausted but still straining for the sound of those phantom footsteps, the press of invisible hands.

Sleep took me—swift, deep, and full of dread.

The sun hung white and heavy in the sky when the Swede shook me awake. The scent of frying bacon curled into the tent, thick and greasy.

“River’s still rising,” he said. “Half the islands are gone. Ours is next.”

I rubbed my eyes, still thick with sleep. “Wood?”

“Enough to last till tomorrow,” he answered, but his laugh was wrong—too sharp, like a blade dragged over stone.

I waded into the river. The current snatched me instantly, ice-knife cold, and the banks blurred past as if the world were unraveling. The water scoured the night’s dread from my skin, left me raw. The wind hadn’t eased. It gnawed at the willows, at the edges of my thoughts.

Then it struck me—his words. *Tomorrow.* He wasn’t planning to leave.

At breakfast, chunks of the shore collapsed into the water. The Swede talked of steamboat channels, but his voice was wrong—thin, frayed. He barely touched his food. His pipe lay forgotten. The map sprawled beside him, but his fingers trembled as they traced the lines.

“We should go soon,” I said, testing.

His reply slithered into my bones: *“If they let us.”*

“Who?”

“The things here.” He wouldn’t meet my eyes. “The gods.”

I forced a laugh. It echoed hollow.

Then he showed me the canoe.

The steering paddle was gone. The hull gaped with a long, precise slit—too clean for rocks, too deliberate. And the remaining paddle… Someone had scraped the blade to paper.

“Sleepwalking,” I muttered. “Or the wind—”

“You’ll explain anything, won’t you?” His smile was a wound.

I whistled—a nervous habit—and turned away. But the truth was there, in the hollows pocking the island like bite marks, in the way the willows twitched when no wind blew.

He knew. Had known all along.

By afternoon, the silence was worse than the wind. The river’s voice filled it, a low, pulsing drone. Not water. Not wind. Something else.

It came from everywhere. From nowhere. A distant, metallic thrumming, like a great bell struck beneath the earth.

“It’s been here all day,” the Swede whispered. “Inside me sometimes.”

I stirred the stew to drown him out. But when we reached for the bread, the sack was empty.

We laughed then—wild, soundless laughter that shook us like a seizure. Because it wasn’t just the bread.

It was the knowing.

The island was hungry. And we were the meal.

I swore to myself I would stay awake—to watch, to listen, to stand guard against whatever lurked beyond the rustling willows. But exhaustion is a patient hunter, and it dragged me under despite my resolve. My companion’s uneasy sleep hastened my own surrender. At first, he twisted on his mattress, jerking upright to whisper, *“Did you hear that?”* or *“The river—it’s rising!”* Each time, I forced myself outside, squinting into the dark, only to return with hollow reassurances. Eventually, his breathing steadied. The snoring that followed was an obscene comfort—proof that, for now, we were still human, still unclaimed.

Then something pressed down on me.

Not just the blanket. A weight, like a body slumped across mine. I called out, shoving at the dark—but the tent was *surrounded*. That sound, that terrible *pattering*, filled the night like rain if rain had purpose, if it whispered. The Swede’s mattress was empty. The tent flap hung loose—an invitation.

I lunged outside.

The air itself was alive, vibrating with a hum that wasn’t a hum at all but a *voice*, layered, endless, pouring from the sky and the earth and the river all at once. It thickened in my lungs. The horizon bled pale light, just enough to see the willows choking the island, their branches clawing at nothing.

Then I saw him.

The Swede stood at the river’s edge, one foot already in the black water. He didn’t resist when I seized him. He fought like a man drowning in air, spitting words that weren’t words—*“The way of the wind—inside—inside—”*—until I dragged him back to the tent. The moment the flap closed, the humming stopped.

As abruptly as if it had never been.

He blinked up at me in the gray dawn, his face slack with childlike relief. *“They’ve found a victim in our place,”* he murmured, and then he slept—deep, untroubled, as if the night had been nothing but a dream.

By morning, he remembered none of it.

He built the fire, boiled coffee, remarked on the cold river. When I mentioned the silence—no hum, no pattering—he nodded as if it were the most natural thing in the world. *“Everything has stopped,”* he said, then hesitated. *“Because they’ve found another victim.”*

He said it so simply. As if it were fact.

We found the body downstream, wedged in the roots. A peasant, half-submerged, skin puckered with small, perfect hollows—*exactly like the sand-funnels*. The Swede reached for him, muttering about burial, but the current twisted the corpse face-up.

The marks were everywhere.

Then the humming returned—not from the air, but from the *body itself*, a swarm of unseen things taking flight. The dead man spun into the current, limbs loose, his ruined face staring at the sky as the river swallowed him whole.

The Swede fell to his knees. *“Their mark,”* he whispered.

I said nothing. The water had already erased the evidence. But the willows swayed, though there was no wind.
