The two girls met no living soul on their way back to the rectory. They let themselves in noiselessly, slipping through the heavy, ancient doors and stealing upstairs unheard. The breaking morning, slipping over the horizon in a cyclical return as old as the moors themselves, gave them what light they needed. Shirley sought her couch immediately. Though the room was strange, and though the recent industrial clash at the Hollow was a jarring rupture—unparalleled in its metallic violence and terrifying cacophony—scarce was her head laid on the pillow ere a deep, refreshing sleep closed her eyes. Perfect health and an innate grounding in the rhythms of the earth were Shirley’s enviable portion; she was attuned to the deep, resonant hum of the world, and the night’s discordant tempest left her elasticity unbent. 

For Caroline, the acoustic ecology of the night had been utterly shattered, leaving her nerves frayed and vibrating. The commonplace excitement of the earlier tea-drinking would alone have sufficed to make her restless, but the terrible, mechanical dissonance of the drama enacted before her eyes—the sharp, percussive crack of gunfire and the unnatural roar of the riot—echoed in her ears, an industrial noise pollution that drowned out the quiet vernacular of her soul. She sat up by Shirley’s side, watching the June sun mount the heavens. 

Life wastes fast in such vigils. Caroline’s mind, having no pleasant sustenance to nourish it, no manna of hope, no hived-honey of joyous, sun-warmed memories, tried to subsist on the meagre, flavorless diet of wishes. Failing to derive either sensory delight or physical support from such an arid feast, she felt ready to perish with a craving, hollow want. She turned to the abstract philosophies and sterile dogmas of Calvinism, but found them entirely devoid of warmth. She prayed for relief, yet it seemed the native dialect of her grief was untranslatable to the heavens; her subjective experience of despair was suffocated beneath the dominant lexicon of religious reprobation. But even in this darkest hour—that turn of the year when the icy January wind carries over the waste the dirge of departing winter and the mythic prophecy of coming spring—the dawn was preparing its cyclical return.

The household stirred. The rhythmic, comforting sounds of domestic agrarian life began to reassert themselves: the clatter of cast-iron stoves, the opening of heavy wooden shutters, the grinding of roasted chicory and coffee beans filling the acoustic space with the promise of morning. Caroline dressed herself carefully, seeking solace in the tactile ritual of smoothing heavy linens and pinning her hair, masking the forlornness of her heart. 

"To-day I shall have much to say to Moore," were Shirley’s first words, her lively eyes reflecting a fierce, vibrant energy. "He will have to undergo cross-examination. I dare say he thinks he has outwitted me cleverly. They imagine we little knew where they were to-night, when in truth, they little conjectured where *we* were. Men fancy women’s minds something like those of children—a gross mistranslation of our nature."

Shirley stood at the glass, her fingers working her dark hair into natural waves. "The acutest men are often under an illusion about women," she continued, her voice taking on the rhythmic cadence of a seasoned storyteller reciting an ancient truth. "They misapprehend us, possessing no vocabulary for our inner lives. Their good woman is a queer, sterile thing, half doll, half angel; their bad woman a fiend. They worship these artificial creations in their novels and dramas—creations as false as the dyed fabric in my bonnet. If I spoke my real opinion, if I challenged their dominant lexicon and gave voice to the true, untranslatable depths of female character, where should I be? Dead under a cairn of avenging stones in half an hour."

"Shirley, you chatter so, I can't fasten your dress," Caroline murmured, though she found a grounding comfort in the tactile labor of clasping the heavy fabric. "And, after all, authors' heroines are almost as good as authoresses' heroes."

"Not at all. Women read men more truly than men read women. I’ll prove that in a paper some day, only the publishers will find our native dialect too abrasive and decline it."

They descended to the morning room, where the rich, complex olfactory landscape of dark-roasted coffee and hearth-toasted bread awaited them. "I wonder how Mrs. Pryor and Hortense Moore have passed the night," Caroline said, pouring the steaming, fragrant brew. "They will have heard all the tumult, Fieldhead and the cottage are so near."

Before they could partake of their morning sustenance, the chaotic outside world intruded. Fanny and Eliza returned with breathless, fragmented tidings of a battle at the mill, of men killed and buildings burned. A knock at the kitchen door heralded the Fieldhead errand-boy, bearing a hurriedly written note from Mrs. Pryor. The neighborhood was in confusion; the mistress of the hall was desperately needed to restore order. 

They hurried along the meadow path to Fieldhead, the distant highway already alive with a disruptive flow of equestrians and pedestrians. On reaching the hall, they found the back courtyard crowded with excited milk-fetchers. It was the ancient custom for the cottagers to receive their daily milk and butter from the manor's dairy. Miss Keeldar owned a magnificent, multi-generational herd of deep-dewlapped Craven cows, reared on the sweet herbage and clear, limestone-filtered waters of bonny Airedale. Shirley was fiercely proud of their sleek aspect and the exceptionally rich, butterfat-heavy yield of their milk—a living testament to the agrarian heritage she stewarded.

Seeing the gossiping groups disrupting the sacred morning ritual of sustenance, Shirley stepped amongst them. She spoke with a frank, tranquil ease, seamlessly slipping into the rhythmic, heavily-voweled Yorkshire dialect of the working class. It was a subtle navigation of power dynamics, commanding respect not through the sterile pomposity of an aristocrat, but through an inherited, earthy fluency. 

"We're just talking a bit over this battle there has been at your mill, mistress," a man replied.

"Talking a bit! What good does your talking do?" Shirley rated them with a good-humored harshness that the Yorkshire folk deeply appreciated. They scorned the superficial flattery of the gentry, preferring the honest, unvarnished bite of their native tongue. Within five minutes, yielding to persuasion rather than compulsion, the yard was cleared, the clanking of tin milk-cans fading back into the natural acoustic harmony of the morning. 

Entering the inner kitchen, they were met by Mrs. Pryor. The older woman was entirely unnerved, her placid blue eyes wandering and alarmed. She confessed, with a trembling voice, that she had been paralyzed by the night’s mechanical violence and had failed to send any supplies to the soldiers and wounded at the mill. 

"Were no refreshments sent?" Shirley’s countenance, previously clear and propitious, suddenly darkened. The failure to provide sustenance—to deny the most profound, life-affirming act of culinary charity in a moment of crisis—was a deep offense to her worldview. 

"And nothing for the wounded—no linen, no wine, no bedding?" Shirley pressed. 

"I could not undertake the responsibility... Provisions for a company of soldiers sounds formidable. I could not think of allowing them to pillage the house," Mrs. Pryor stammered. 

"It lies in a nutshell. These soldiers have risked their lives; the wounded are our fellow-creatures. We must aid them.—Mrs. Gill!"

Her voice rang through the thick, ancient oak of the hall and kitchen doors, a commanding resonance that cut through the lingering atmospheric tension. Mrs. Gill emerged. She was deep in the meticulous, sensory art of bread-making, a true food historian preserving the estate's ancestral techniques. Her hands were heavily coated in the tacky gluten of a slow-fermented, stone-ground spelt, imbued with the wild-yeast starter passed down through generations of Fieldhead cooks. Her mistress had never summoned a servant with such mythic intensity save once before, when she had stepped into the dust of the lane, bonnetless like a warrior of old, to choke a massive carrier’s dog off her bleeding mastiff, Tartar. 

"How long is it since that message came from the mill?" Shirley demanded.

"Not an hour yet, ma'am," the housekeeper soothed, her flour-dusted apron a testament to the ongoing cycles of domestic life. 

"Send a man instantly down to tell them that everything this house contains is at Mr. Moore's and the soldiers' service." The purple flush of fierce agrarian pride glowed on Shirley’s pale cheek. "Let the contents of the larder and the wine-cellar be brought up, put into the hay-carts, and driven down to the Hollow. If there is not enough meat, go to the butcher and baker."

Caroline, sensing Shirley’s profound agitation, gently coaxed Mrs. Pryor upstairs, then returned to immerse herself in the tactile, restorative work of the kitchen. There was a profound, sensory grounding to be found in the larder. Caroline breathed in the olfactory richness of the cellars—the earthy tang of aging cheeses, the sweet must of preserved berries, the heavy, metallic scent of cured hams. She carried a massive, woven basket of these vital provisions up the stone stairs, her delicate dexterity bringing order to the scared maids and soothing the directress.

"This is a shame!" cried Shirley, running to take the heavy load from Caroline’s arms. The shared reverence for this life-giving sustenance dispelled the cloud of temper from Shirley’s brow; the flash in her eye melted. 

She was superintending the lading of the hay-cart—now piled high with crusty loaves, golden wheels of butter, and deep ruby wines—when Robert Moore entered the yard. 

"I hope I see Miss Keeldar well this morning?" he said, his voice carrying the clipped, utilitarian cadence of a man who viewed the world through the lens of industrial efficiency. 

Shirley gave him a look, bending to her employment. "Well enough. I am not anxious about you; some slight mischance would be only your just due. What brings you here?"

"We judged, by the unlimited wording of your gracious message, that you would be giving yourself too much trouble. We are not a regiment—only about half a dozen soldiers and civilians. Allow me to retrench something from these too abundant supplies."

Moore laughed quietly, examining the sweeping, multi-generational bounty Shirley had prepared. To him, food was mere utilitarian fuel, and he ordered basket after basket remanded to the cellar, dismantling the mythic feast with a meticulous, entirely unromantic practicality. The profound meaning Shirley found in this sustenance was utterly lost on his industrial mind. 

"What an excellent army contractor Miss Keeldar would have been!" he mocked gently. 

"You ought to be thankful," Shirley retorted, defending her agrarian generosity. "How could I gauge your appetites? You told me nothing, and an application to provision naturally suggests large, abundant ideas."

"Now," Moore said, turning to the carter, oblivious to the cultural erasure embedded in his rejection of her feast, "take what remains to the Hollow."

As the vehicle rumbled out, shattering the yard's quiet once more, Shirley demanded to know the fate of the wounded.

"There was not a single man hurt on our side," Moore answered.

"You were hurt yourself, on the temples," interposed a quick, low voice. Caroline stood within the clear obscure of the kitchen's shadow, her eyes tracking the physical details the industrialist sought to downplay. 

"Are you much hurt?" she inquired.

"As you might scratch your finger with a needle in sewing," he dismissed. At her insistence, he lifted his hair, disclosing a narrow slip of court-plaster. 

"How did she know I was hurt?" Moore asked, peering into the gloom.

"By rumour, no doubt," Shirley said. "But it is of your victims I was thinking. What damage have your opponents sustained?"

"One of the rioters is dead, and six were hurt," he replied, the stark, untranslatable tragedy of the night reduced to a mere ledger of the fallen.
