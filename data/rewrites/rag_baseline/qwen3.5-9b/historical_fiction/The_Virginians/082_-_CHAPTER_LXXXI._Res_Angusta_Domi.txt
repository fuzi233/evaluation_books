The air in the room was still, thick with the scent of old paper and the quiet accumulation of years. I sit here, an elderly gentleman, reflecting on a rash decision made when we were barely out of our teens—a leap into matrimony that my dear partner encouraged despite the odds. As a man and a father, burdened by the arithmetic of mutton chops and the baker's bill, surrounded by children who might flee to Scotland tomorrow citing our own examples, I know I should adopt a demure posture. I should offer myself as a warning, a cautionary tale for the hot-headed youth.

Yet, the truth is that the life my relatives predicted so gloomily has defied their prudence. It has had its trials, certainly, but I recall them without bitterness. Time, by divine ordinance, acts as a benign consoler for passionate griefs. We endured days of poverty to the astonishment of our sympathizing kin, yet the rewards were precious, too great to whisper aloud except to the One Ear that hears the prayers of all men. To marry without a competence is a crime against social codes, no doubt, but do not thousands commit this annually, trusting only in heaven, health, and labor? Why must young people wait for a fully furnished cottage, a stocked cellar, and a strong box full of money before beginning housekeeping? Were the laws governing the genteel class made universal, the increase of the world would cease. Our gentlefolk tremble in silk stockings, waiting for a bridge or a gilt barge; the poor do not fear to wet their bare feet in the brook, trusting fate and strength to carry them over. Who would wish to consign a daughter to poverty? Yet, I look at my wife and ask her pardon for imposing such a task on one so gentle. I think of the trials she endured and am thankful for the unfailing love and constancy with which God strengthened her to bear them.

My own judgment on marriage is flawed; mine was imprudent and yet so happy that I dare not counsel the young. I have endured poverty and found it tolerable, perhaps even delightful. Had I not undergone it, I could never have known the kindness of friends, the delight of gratitude, or the surprising joys that accompany a scanty meal and a narrow fire. Superfluous pity is often wasted on the decent poor. Good-natured folk stepping from sunshine into narrow obscurity stumble over obstacles invisible to us, surprised that we drink small beer cheerfully and say grace over cold mutton.

General Lambert, my father-in-law, married his Molly as a subaltern with a purse nearly empty as my own. They had their ups and downs. I suspect, though my wife will never admit it, they married without parental leave, as young people did in their time. They were so pleased with their luck that they did not grudge their children's hardships. When I presented my pecuniary statements to Mr. Lambert, believing him to be master of two thousand pounds, he departed for Jamaica at ease regarding his daughter's future. After outfitting the family, he left no richer than his son-in-law. His portion for Theo consisted of trinkets, lace from Aunt Lambert, and twenty new guineas sewn into a purse by her mother and sisters. In valuing my stock, I counted as a good debt the sum my honored mother refused to acknowledge until her death. She argued those funds improved the estate destined for me, saving them for my poor brother, who, lacking inheritance, would have spent his own means had he not imagined himself sole heir to the Virginian property—a point she emphasized in many letters. He distinguished himself in the service of his king. To purchase his promotion was, she supposed, his brother's duty. Once I finished my bar studies and dramatic amusements, Madam Esmond invited me home to take the colonial place to which my birth entitled me, communicating this through Mountain before news of my marriage reached her.

Her expressions of maternal indignation upon learning of my step were clear. Upon the pacification of Canada, my dear Harry sought leave and visited Virginia. He wrote of the splendid entertainments my mother staged in his honor. Castlewood, uninhabited since our European departure, opened its doors to colonial friends. The friend of Wolfe and soldier of Quebec was received with becoming honors. Dismal quarrels ensued, however, as Harry persisted in his friendship with Colonel Washington of Mount Vernon, whose praises Harry never tired of singing. I allow the gentleman every virtue; in the struggles that ended fatally for England, I admire General Washington's constancy and success as well as his warmest friends.

If these battles between Harry and our mother were frequent, as his letters described, I wondered why he remained at home. One reason suggested itself, which I hesitated to share with Mrs. Warrington. We had discussed Hetty's romantic attachment to my brother, wondering why he had never discovered it. Naturally, my gentleman had found a young lady at home more to his taste than our dear Hester, accounting for his prolonged stay.

Soon, a letter from him admitted this fact without full confession. A person was described, unnamed, a being of beauty and perfection. My wife asked to see the letter; I showed it to her with a very sad face. To my surprise, she read it without corresponding sorrow.

"I have thought of this before, my love," I said. "I feel with you for your disappointment regarding poor Hetty."

"Ah! poor Hetty," said Theo, looking down at the carpet.

"It would never have done," I replied.

"No—they would not have been happy," sighed Theo.

"How strange he never should have found out her secret!" I continued.

She looked me full in the face with an odd expression. "Pray, what does that look mean?" I asked.

"Nothing, my dear—nothing! Only I am not surprised!" said Theo, blushing.

"What," I asked, "can there be another?"

"I am sure I never said so, George," the lady said hurriedly. "But if Hetty has overcome her childish folly, ought we not all to be glad? Do you gentlemen suppose that you only are to fall in love and grow tired, indeed?"

"What!" I said, with a strange commotion of my mind. "Do you mean to tell me, Theo, that you ever cared for any one but me?"

"Oh, George," she whimpered. "When I was at school, there was—one of the boys of Doctor Backhouse's school, who sate in the loft next to us; and I thought he had lovely eyes, and I was so shocked when I recognised him behind the counter at Mr. Grigg's the mercer's, when I went to buy a cloak for baby, and I wanted to tell you, my dear, and I didn't know how!"

I went to see this creature with the lovely eyes, having made my wife describe the fellow's dress. I saw a little bandy-legged wretch in a blue camlet coat, with red hair tied with a dirty ribbon. About him, I forbore even to reproach my wife; nor will she ever know that I have looked at the fellow, until she reads this confession. If our wives saw us as we are, I thought, would they love us as they do? Are we as much mistaken in them as they in us? I look into one candid face at least, and think it never has deceived me.

Lest I encourage my young people to imitate my own imprudence, I will not detail the small capital with which Mrs. Theo and I commenced life. The unfortunate tragedy brought us nothing, though recent reviewers have spoken favorably of its merits, and Mr. Kemble himself commended it. Our kind friend Lord Wrotham proposed publishing it by subscription, sending a banknote for a hundred copies, but I was averse to that method of levying money. Preferring my poverty sine dote, I locked up the manuscript, with my poor girl's verses inserted at the first page. I know not why the piece offended at court, except that an actor who ran off with an earl's daughter performed a principal part. Sentiments placed in the mouths of Indian characters, declaiming against ambition and British rule, were pronounced dangerous and unconstitutional, taking away any hope of royal favor.

What was to be done? A few months after the tragedy's failure, counting the remains of my fortune, I concluded I must beat a retreat from my pretty apartments in Bloomsbury. I gave warning to our good landlady, stating my wife's health required country lodgings. We went no farther than Lambeth, our faithful Gumbo and Molly following. Though as poor as might be, we were waited on by a maid and a lackey in livery, like folks of condition. Kind relatives cried out against our extravagance; indeed, are they not the people who find our faults and proclaim them to the world?

Returning home from London, where I had visited booksellers, I recognized the family arms and livery on a grand gilt chariot before a public-house near our lodgings. Loitering inhabitants gazed with awe at the footmen, resplendent in the sun, quaffing blazing pots of beer. I found my Lady Castlewood seated opposite my wife in our little apartment, whence we enjoyed a bright prospect of the river, covered with barges and wherries, and the ancient towers and trees of the Archbishop's palace and gardens. Mrs. Theo, with her droll way of describing persons and scenes, narrated the particulars of her ladyship's conversation upon departure.
