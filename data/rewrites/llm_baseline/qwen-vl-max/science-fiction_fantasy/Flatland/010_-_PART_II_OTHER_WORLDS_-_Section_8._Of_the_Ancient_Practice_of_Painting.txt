If my readers have kept pace with me thus far, they will not be startled to learn that life in Flatland, while rich in intellectual and political drama, is notably barren when it comes to beauty and artistic expression. I do not deny that our world teems with conflict—battles, betrayals, shifting alliances, and the constant interplay between geometry and fate—all of which lend a certain vitality to our history. Nor would I minimize the thrill derived from solving mathematical puzzles in real time, where every conjecture can be tested instantly by sight or touch. But this is not art. This is logic.

From an aesthetic standpoint, our existence is profoundly dull. Imagine a world where all vision resolves into a single line—no depth, no perspective, only variations in brightness and shadow. Every landscape, every portrait, every still life, reduces to a flat trace upon the retina. What room is there for imagination? For emotion? For wonder?

Yet once, long ago, such limitations did not define us. According to ancient tradition, for several centuries our ancestors lived amid a radiant splendor unknown to our age. The story begins with a single individual—a Pentagon whose name has been lost to time, though most records call him Chromatistes. By accident, he discovered how to mix pigments and apply them to surfaces. At first, he painted his home. Then his servants. Then his family. And finally, himself.

The effect was immediate and profound. Wherever Chromatistes moved, he commanded attention. His body, now adorned with vivid hues, no longer required tactile identification. No one mistook his front for his back. Neighbors could discern his shape at a glance, without laborious calculation. Crowds parted instinctively before him. He no longer needed to shout to make himself known—his colors spoke for him.

The fashion spread like wildfire. Within days, every Square and Triangle in the region followed suit. A few stubborn Pentagons resisted, but soon even the Dodecagons succumbed. In less than a year, nearly all citizens had embraced color—except two groups: the Women and the Priests.

Nature, it seemed, drew a line here. The argument among reformers went: "Distinction of sides implies distinction of color." It was a clever justification, repeated in every town, convincing entire populations to paint themselves. But for Women—whose form consists of a single straight side—they had no sides at all. And for Priests, who claimed to be true Circles (not merely polygons with countless tiny sides), their boundary was a continuous curve—a circumference, not a series of edges. Thus, they argued, they were exempt from the rule.

So while the rest of society adorned itself in dazzling palettes, the Women and Priests remained untouched by pigment—pure, unadorned, and in the eyes of many, more noble for it.

Though the movement was mocked as immoral, chaotic, and unscientific, its artistic legacy remains undeniable. Those were the golden days—the dawn of visual culture in Flatland. To live was to see, and to see was to marvel. Even a simple gathering became a feast for the senses. Church services overflowed with vibrant hues so intense they distracted the finest minds. Theatre performances shimmered with chromatic energy. But nothing rivaled the spectacle of war.

Picture, if you can, twenty thousand Isosceles soldiers turning en masse, revealing bright orange and purple flanks against their dark bases. The militia of Equilateral Triangles marching in red, white, and blue. Square artillerymen rotating near crimson cannons, their bodies flashing mauve, ultramarine, gamboge, and burnt umber. Pentagons and Hexagons streaking across the field in five or six distinct colors, serving as surgeons, geometers, and aides-de-camp.

It was said that one great Circle, commanding such forces, was so overwhelmed by their visual harmony that he cast aside his baton and crown, declaring he would henceforth wield only the artist’s brush.

And language itself reflected this sensory richness. The words of that era pulsed with deeper meaning, imbued with color and rhythm. Our finest poetry, our most lyrical scientific discourse, traces its roots to those vanished times. They were not just painted; they were illuminated.

But like all revolutions, this one faded. Color fell out of favor—too dangerous, too disruptive. Order returned, but at the cost of beauty. And so, today, we live in silence and monotony, remembering a past that dazzled but never endured.
