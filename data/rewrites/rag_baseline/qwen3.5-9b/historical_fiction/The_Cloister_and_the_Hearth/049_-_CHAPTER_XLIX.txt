Margaret Brandt had always occupied a solitary orbit within Sevenbergen, her reserve mistaken by the town for arrogance. This misinterpretation had bred a fertile soil for jealousy and malice, and she knew, with a chilling clarity, how these poisoned hearts would eventually triumph over her when the veil of her condition could no longer be maintained. The prospect gnawed at her, a relentless hunger that consumed her nights and stole the light from her days. Driven by this terror, she retreated into the shadows of her home, avoiding the harsh glare of daylight even during those rare excursions necessary for survival.

Yet, in the quiet sanctuary of her own conscience, Margaret's moral compass remained undeviating, unlike the confused judgments of the unlearned observers who might mistake her plight for something lesser. While she lacked the intricate knowledge of contemporary legal statutes, she understood the fundamental sanctity of a betrothal: it was a binding contract, immutable as any document sealed with witness and ink. She knew that marriage to another would have been annulled by both Church and State, and that children born of such unions were legitimate. However, the weight crushing her simple medieval spirit lay elsewhere. The contract itself—the physical proof of her union—did not exist in her possession. Instead, it rested in the hands of Gerard, who was currently lost to her, far beyond reach.

She loathed herself for the catastrophic oversight that had surrendered her to the mercies of public rumor. Though she had never encountered Horace's famous verse regarding the sting of praise, she possessed a distinctly Horatian capacity for clear, hard, positive judgment—a trait strangely characteristic of her sex when emotion does not cloud perception entirely. She assessed the world with ruthless precision. With her marriage lines hidden in Italy, invisible to the eye, they could never counterbalance the undeniable evidence of her visible pregnancy and the future sight of her child. What narrative could withstand slanderous tongues? "I possess my marriage lines, but I cannot display them to you." Which woman would believe such a fabrication? Or even feign belief? As one of the most modest souls in Holland, Margaret sought the approval of women, not men. Slander often attacks the female sex with impunity, but here, slander wore the mask of truth. The concept of the "strong-minded woman" had not yet been conceived; Margaret, though naturally authoritative as the mistress of a household, was fragile as water in moments of crisis, particularly in this instance. Like the elite of her gender, she was a mere leaf, trembling at every gust of worldly opinion, whether true or false.

Immense suffering resides in few words. Few pages of description penned by a man could convey the agony of a virtuous woman foreseeing herself paraded as frail, unless that woman were already virtuous enough to require no such aid. Had her heart been frail, she might have brazened out the accusation. But she possessed no such shield. She was pure as snow, watching the darkness approach with terrifying inevitability. The poor girl sat listless for hours, moaning with an internal anguish that echoed in the hollow rooms. Often, when her father spoke and she offered mechanical replies, her cheek would ignite like fire, causing the old man to wonder what words had disturbed her. Nothing. His speech was less than air to her; it was the ever-present dread that painted her face with the color of shame, regardless of the topic at hand.

Shame and fear reached their zenith the night she returned from Margaret Van Eyck's house. Her condition had been discovered, and by women of her own sex. The old artist, secluded as she was, might remain silent, but Catherine, a gossip at the center of a bustling family and a tight-knit neighborhood? A single spark of hope flickered. Catherine had spoken kindly, even lovingly. The situation allowed for no middle path; Gerard's mother, once roused, would become either her greatest ally or her deadliest foe. Margaret waited in racking anxiety for news, but none arrived. Hope evaporated. Catherine was not her friend. Consequently, the urge to expose herself grew, fueled by the absence of kindness to balance the natural impulse to babble. Soon, the desire to flee this neighborhood took root, gnawing until it became a wild, passionate obsession.

But how to persuade her father? Old people cling to their roots. He was ancient and infirm, unable to abandon his abode. The only recourse was to make him her confidant; fleeing without him would be impossible. Thus, she oscillated between an uncontrollable urge to vanish and an invincible aversion to speak to a man, even her father, vibrating in a suspense filled with living torture. Then, one day, the fatal thought emerged: "End all!" Words foolishly chosen are not always foolish deeds. One of Catherine's constant fears, the city's numerous canals, tempted this wavering girl sorely. Standing on the bank one afternoon, she gazed into the calm, deep water. It appeared an image of repose, mirroring her exhaustion. No more trouble. No more fear of shame. If Gerard had not loved her, she doubted she would have stopped there. As it was, she knelt by the water's edge and prayed fervently to God to purge such wicked thoughts from her soul.

"Oh! Selfish wretch," she whispered, tears streaming down her face, "to leave thy father. Oh, wicked wretch, to kill thy child, and cause thy poor Gerard to lose all the pain and peril he undertook for thy sake. I will tell my father everything, yes, before this sun sets."

With eager haste, lest her resolve dissolve before arrival, she hastened home. In domestic matters, the learned Peter was simple as a child, while Margaret, since the age of sixteen, had governed the house gently but absolutely. It was therefore a strange inversion to see the young, despotic mistress addressing him with faltering, irresolute tones—tones usually reserved for Martin Wittenhaagen or the little maid who washed vessels for a pittance.

"Father, I would speak to thee."
"Speak on, girl."
"Wilt you listen to me? And—and—not—and try to excuse my faults?"
"We have all our faults, Margaret, thou no more than the rest of us; but fewer, unless parental feeling blinds me."
"Alas, no, father: I am a poor foolish girl, that would fain do well, but have done ill, most ill, most unwisely; and now must bear the shame. But, father, I love you, with all my faults, and will not you forgive my folly, and still love your motherless girl?"
"That ye may count on," said Peter cheerfully.
"Oh, well, smile not. For then how can I speak and make you sad?"
"Why, what is the matter?"
"Father, disgrace is coming on this house: it is at the door. And I the culprit. Oh, father, turn your head away. I—I—father, I have let Gerard take away my marriage lines."
"Is that all? 'Twas an oversight."
"'Twas the deed of a mad woman. But woe is me! That is not the worst."

Peter interrupted her. "The youth is honest, and loves you dear. You are young. What is a year or two to you? Gerard will assuredly come back and keep troth."
"And meantime know you what is coming?"
"Not I, except that I shall be gone first for one."
"Worse than that. There is worse pain than death. Nay, for pity's sake turn away your head, father."
"Foolish wench!" muttered Peter, but he turned his head.

She trembled violently, her cheeks aflame, beginning to falter. "I did look on Gerard as my husband—we being betrothed—and he was in so sore danger, and I thought I had killed him, and I—oh, if you were but my mother I might find courage: you would question me. But you say not a word."
"Why, Margaret, what is all this coil about? And why are thy cheeks crimson, speaking to no stranger, but to thy old father?"
"Why are my cheeks on fire? Because—because—father, kill me; send me to heaven! Bid Martin shoot me with his arrow! And then the gossips will come and tell you why I blush so this day. And then, when I am dead, I hope you will love your girl again for her mother's sake."

"Give me thy hand, mistress," said Peter, a little sternly.
She extended her hand, trembling. He took it gently, his face etched with anxiety, and began to feel her pulse.
"Alas, nay," she said. "'Tis my soul that burns, not my body, with fever. I cannot, will not, bide in Sevenbergen."
Wringing her hands impatiently, she added, "Be calm now," said the old man soothingly, "nor torment thyself for nought. Not bide in Sevenbergen? What need to bide a day, as it vexes thee, and puts thee in a fever: for fevered thou art, deny it not."
"What!" cried Margaret, "Would you yield to go hence, and—and ask no reason but my longing to be gone?" Suddenly throwing herself on her knees beside him, in a fervor of supplication she clutched his sleeve, then his arm, then his shoulder, imploring him to quit this place without asking why.
