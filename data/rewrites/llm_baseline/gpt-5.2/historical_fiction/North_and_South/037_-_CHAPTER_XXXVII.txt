Higgins’s door was locked when they went the next day to pay their call on the widow Boucher. This time an officious neighbour—one of those who live half on other people’s news—leaned from her threshold to tell them he was truly out, though he had been in early to see Mrs. Boucher before going upon whatever work, or quest of work, now filled his hours.

The visit itself left a dull taste behind, like weak tea long stewed—warm, bitter, and offering no comfort. Mrs. Boucher sat sunk in her chair with the sullenness of an invalid who has turned grievance into a kind of nourishment. She held herself for an ill-used woman by her husband’s suicide; and there was just truth enough, just the ragged edge of reason, to make it hard to tear away without wounding her. Yet it was grievous to see how completely her thoughts circled back upon herself—her wrongs, her helplessness, her claim upon the world—and how that selfishness spread, like soot, even over her relations with her children. They were “incumbrances,” she said, even while her rough, animal affection clung to them as property clings.

Margaret tried to draw near to one or two of the little ones while her father strove—patiently, earnestly—to lift the widow’s mind into some channel higher than querulousness. It was the children who mourned truly, with a simplicity that could not counterfeit. Daddy had been kind. Each, in eager stammering fragments, brought up some tenderness—an apple shared, a penny saved, a piggyback ride, a sheltering arm when a scold or blow threatened.

One of them said, with eyes wide and haunted, “Is yon thing upstairs really him? it doesna look like him. I’m feared on it, and I never was feared o’ daddy.”

Margaret’s heart contracted. The mother, in her hungry requirement of sympathy, had dragged them upstairs to look upon their dead father—disfigured, made strange—mixing the coarseness of horror with the profoundness of natural grief, as if she had seasoned a child’s bread with ashes and wondered it would not be eaten.

She bent to them instead, speaking softly, not of the body upstairs but of what lay close to their hands: what they could do for mother, and—more potent still—what father would have wished them to do. Duties that could be touched and finished were like crumbs laid for frightened birds. They began, shyly, to stir into action: one fetched a cloth, another picked up a fallen garment, another carried a cup to the table. Under Margaret’s direction the slatternly room was “redded up,” little by little, their grief finding a safer outlet in small, steady motions.

Mr. Hale’s way was different. He set too high a standard, too abstract a view, before the indolent, self-absorbed invalid. She could not rouse her torpid mind into any vivid imagining of what her husband’s misery had been before that last terrible step; she could only see how it injured her. She could not enter into the enduring mercy of a God who had not, by miracle, held back the water from drowning her prostrate husband. Secretly she blamed him for despair; aloud she blamed everything and everyone who could be imagined as having driven him to it.

The masters—the Union—her own children for being numerous, hungry, noisy—made up one great army of personal enemies. Mr. Thornton in particular was named with peculiar bitterness: the very man whose mill Boucher had attacked, and who afterwards had caused the warrant for Boucher’s apprehension to be withdrawn. In her mouth even that mercy turned to accusation: had the law been allowed to take its course, she hinted darkly, things would have been different; yet she also demanded protection from the very world she cursed.

Margaret heard enough to dishearten her. When at last they came away into the damp Milton air, she could not cheer her father. The streets were wet; the stones shone; wheels hissed and clattered; iron-shod hoofs rang sharply, and every sound seemed doubled by the close brick walls. The town’s harsh music—hammer, cart, bell, shouting—pressed on the ear without pause, as if it would not allow a heart to gather itself into quiet.

“It is the town life,” Margaret said at length, almost as if she were trying to lay her hand on some tangible cause. “Their nerves are quickened by the haste and bustle and speed of everything around them; to say nothing of the confinement in these pent-up houses, which of itself is enough to induce depression and worry of spirits. Now in the country, people live so much more out of doors, even children, and even in the winter.”

“But people must live in towns,” Mr. Hale replied. “And in the country some get such stagnant habits of mind that they are almost fatalists.”

“Yes; I acknowledge that. I suppose each mode of life produces its own trials and its own temptations. The dweller in towns must find it as difficult to be patient as the country-bred man must find it to be active and equal to unwonted emergencies. Both must find it hard to realise a future of any kind—the one because the present is so living and hurrying and close around him; the other because his life tempts him to revel in the mere sense of animal existence, not knowing of, and consequently not caring for any pungency of pleasure, for the attainment of which he can plan, and deny himself, and look forward.”

“And thus both the necessity for engrossment, and the stupid content in the present, produce the same effects,” said Mr. Hale, following her thought with the gentleness of one who liked to see her mind at work. “But this poor Mrs. Boucher! how little we can do for her.”

“And yet we dare not leave her without our efforts, although they may seem so useless. Oh papa! it’s a hard world to live in!”

“So it is, my child. We feel it so just now, at any rate; but we have been very happy, even in the midst of our sorrow. What a pleasure Frederick’s visit was!”

“Yes, that it was,” said Margaret, brightening as warmth returns to chilled fingers. “It was such a charming, snatched, forbidden thing.”

But the brightness stopped short, as if a shutter had fallen. She had spoiled the memory by what had happened—by her own cowardice. Of all faults she most despised in others, it was want of bravery; the meanness of heart which leads to untruth. And she had been guilty of it.

Then came the thought of Mr. Thornton’s knowledge of her falsehood. She wondered whether she should have minded detection half so much from any one else. She tried, as if testing a wound, to imagine her aunt Shaw and Edith knowing, her father knowing, Captain Lennox, Frederick. Frederick’s disapproval—even if he understood it had been done for him—would have been pain enough; the brother and sister were in the first flush of recovered regard. Yet even that seemed small beside the shame, the shrinking shame, she felt at the thought of meeting Mr. Thornton again.

And yet she longed to see him, to have the meeting over, to know where she stood in his opinion.

Her cheeks burned as she recalled how proudly—how rashly—she had implied, early in their acquaintance, an objection to trade because it so often led to deceit: inferior goods passed for superior; credit assumed for wealth and resources not possessed. She remembered Mr. Thornton’s look of calm disdain as he answered that, in the great scheme of commerce, all dishonourable ways were sure to injure a man in the long run; and that, even tested by the poor standard of success, deceit was folly, not wisdom. She remembered her own then-untempted truth asking whether buying in the cheapest and selling in the dearest market did not show some want of that transparent justice connected with truth; she remembered her use of the word “chivalric,” and her father’s correction to the higher word, “Christian,” drawing the argument upon himself while she sat silent with a slight contempt.

No more contempt for her now. No more talk of “chivalric.” Henceforward she must feel humiliated—disgraced—in his sight.

But when should she see him?

All that day her heart sprang up, startled, at each ring of the door-bell; and then, when it sank again to calmness, it felt strangely sick and saddened at the disappointment. It was evident her father expected him too, and was surprised he did not come. There were points in their conversation the other night which time had not allowed them to enlarge; it had been understood that if possible on the succeeding evening—if not then, at least the first evening Mr. Thornton could command—they should meet again for discussion. Mr. Hale had looked forward to it. He had not yet resumed instruction to his pupils, having relinquished it at the beginning of his wife’s more serious illness; his occupations were few, and the recent tragedy of Boucher’s suicide had driven him back more eagerly than ever upon his speculations.

He was restless all evening, pacing, sitting down, rising again. “I quite expected to have seen Mr. Thornton,” he said more than once. “I think the messenger who brought the book last night must have had some note, and forgot to deliver it. Do you think there has been any message left to-day?”

“I will go and inquire, papa,” said Margaret, after the phrases had been rung in varied order once or twice. “Stay—there’s a ring!”

She sat down instantly and bent her head attentively over her work, as if she could stitch her agitation into the cloth. A step came on the stairs—only one; she knew Dixon’s tread. She lifted her head and sighed, and believed she felt glad.

“It’s that Higgins, sir,” said Dixon, coming in with compressed lips. “He wants to see you, or else Miss Hale. Or it might be Miss Hale first, and then you, sir; for he’s in a strange kind of way.”

“He had better come up here, Dixon; and then he can see us both, and choose which he likes for his listener.”

“Oh! very well, sir. I’ve no wish to hear what he’s got to say, I’m sure; only, if you could see his shoes, I’m sure you’d say the kitchen was the fitter place.”

“He can wipe them, I suppose,” said Mr. Hale.

Dixon flung off to send him up. She was a little mollified, however, when Higgins looked down at his feet with a hesitating air; then, sitting on the bottom stair, he took off the offending shoes and, without a word, walked up in his stockings.

“Sarvant, sir!” he said, slicking his hair down as he entered. Then, looking at Margaret, he added, with an awkward kind of apology, “If hoo’ll excuse me for being i’ my stockings; I’se been tramping a’ day, and streets is none o’ th’ cleanest.”

Fatigue might account for the change in his manner. He was unusually quiet and subdued, and he seemed to have difficulty pushing his words out as if they stuck. Mr. Hale, ever quick in sympathy with shyness or hesitation, came to his aid as naturally as one offers a chair.

“We shall have tea up directly, and then you’ll take a cup with us, Mr. Higgins. I am sure you are tired, if you’ve been out much this wet relaxing day. Margaret, my dear, can’t you hasten tea?”

Margaret could only “hasten” tea by taking preparation into her own hands, and so offending Dixon, who was emerging out of sorrow for her late mistress into a touchy, irritable state. But Martha, like all who came within Margaret’s influence—even Dixon herself in the end—found pleasure and honour in forwarding her wishes. Her ready hands, and Margaret’s sweet forbearance, soon made Dixon ashamed.

“Why master and you must always be asking the lower classes up-stairs, since we came to Milton, I cannot understand,” Dixon grumbled below. “Folk at Helstone were never brought higher than the kitchen; and I’ve let one or two of them know before now that they might think it an honour to be even there.”

Upstairs, Higgins found it easier to unburden himself to one than to two. When Margaret left the room, he went to the door and assured himself it was shut. Then he came and stood close to Mr. Hale, as if warmth or courage might be borrowed by proximity.

“Master,” he said, “yo’d not guess easy what I’ve been tramping after to-day. Special if yo’d remember my manner o’ talk yesterday. I’ve been a seeking work. I have.”

“I said to mysel’, I’d keep a civil tongue in my head, let who would say what ’em would. I’d set my teeth into my tongue sooner nor speak i’ haste. For that man’s sake—yo’ understand,” he added, jerking his thumb back towards some imagined point behind him.

“No, I don’t,” said Mr. Hale, bewildered.

“That chap as lies theer,” Higgins continued, with another jerk, “Him as went and drownded himself, poor chap! I did na think he’d got it in him to lie still and let the water creep o’er him till he died. Boucher, yo’ know.”

“Yes, I know now,” said Mr. Hale. “Go back to what you were saying: you’d not speak in haste—”

“For his sake. Yet not for his sake,” Higgins went on, stumbling into clearer speech as he warmed. “For where’er he is, and whate’er, he’ll ne’er know other clemming or cold again; but for the wife’s sake, and the bits o’ childer.”

“God bless you!” Mr. Hale cried, starting up with a sudden movement, then checked himself, breathless. “What do you mean? Tell me out.”

“I have telled yo’,” said Higgins, surprised at such agitation. “I would na ask for work for mysel’; but them’s left as a charge on me. I reckon, I would ha guided Boucher to a better end; but I set him off o’ th’ road, and so I mun answer for him.”

Mr. Hale seized Higgins’s hand and shook it heartily without speaking. Higgins looked awkward, almost ashamed of having drawn such warmth.

“Theer, theer, master! Theer’s ne’er a man, to call a man, amongst us, but what would do th’ same; ay, and better too; for, belie’ me, Is’e ne’er got a stroke o’ work, nor yet a sight of any. For all I telled Hamper that, let alone his pledge—which I would not sign—no, I could na, not e’en for this—he’d ne’er ha’ such a worker on his mill as I would be—he’d ha’ none o’ me—no more would none o’ th’ others. I’m a poor black feckless sheep—childer may clem for ought I can do, unless, parson, yo’d help me?”

“Help you! How? I would do anything—but what can I do?”

At that moment Margaret re-entered, and Higgins’s eyes went towards her as if she were some stronger light in the room.

“Miss there,” he said, “has often talked grand o’ the South, and the ways down there. Now I dunnot know how far off it is, but I been thinking if I could get ’em down theer, where food is cheap and wages good, and all the folk, rich and poor, master and man, friendly like; yo’ could, may be, help me to work. I’m not forty-five, and I’ve a deal o’ strength in me, measter.”

“But what kind of work could you do, my man?” Mr. Hale asked, gently practical.

“Well, I reckon I could spade a bit—”

“And for that,” Margaret said, stepping forwards, “for anything you could do, Higgins, with the best will in the world, you would, may be, get nine shillings a week; may be ten, at the outside. Food is much the same as here, except that you might have a little garden—”

“The childer could work at that,” said he quickly, catching at the notion as a hungry man catches at bread. “I’m sick o’ Milton anyways, and Milton is sick o’ me.”

“You must not go the South,” said Margaret, firmly, “for all that. You could not stand it. You would have to be out in all weathers. It would kill you with rheumatism. The mere bodily work at your time of life would break you down. The fare is far different to what you have been accustomed to.”

“I’se nought particular about my meat,” he said, offended, as if she had accused him of daintiness.

“But you’ve reckoned on having butcher’s meat once a day, if you’re in work,” Margaret answered, not yielding, her voice full of that earnestness which came from having watched want close at hand. “Pay for that out of your ten shillings, and keep those poor children if you can. I owe it to you—since it’s my way of talking that has set you off on this idea—to put it all clear before you. You would not bear the dulness of the life; you don’t know what it is; it would eat you away like rust. Those that have lived there all their lives are used to soaking in the stagnant waters. They labour on from day to day, in the great solitude of steaming fields—never speaking or lifting up their poor, bent, downcast heads. The hard spadework robs their brain of life; the sameness of their toil deadens their imagination. They don’t care to meet to talk over thoughts and speculations, even of the weakest, wildest kind, after their work is done; they go home brutishly tired, poor creatures! caring for nothing but food and rest. You could not stir them up into any companionship, which you get in a town as plentiful as the air you breathe—whether it be good or bad—and that I don’t know; but I do know that you of all men are not one to bear a life among such labourers. What would be peace to them would be eternal fretting to you. Think no more of it, Nicholas, I beg. Besides, you could never pay to get mother and children all there—that’s one good thing.”

“I’ve reckoned for that. One house mun do for us a’, and the furniture o’ t’other would go a good way. And men theer mun have their families to keep—m’appen six or seven childer. God help ’em!” he said; then, as if his own rapid setting out of obstacles convinced him more than any opposition could, the idea fell away from him, leaving him tired and bare.

“God help ’em! North an’ South have each getten their own troubles. If work’s sure and steady theer, labour’s paid at starvation wages; while here we’n rucks o’ money coming in one quarter, and ne’er a farthing th’ next. For sure, th’ world is in a confusion that passes me or any other man to understand; it needs fettling, and who’s to fettle it if it’s as yon folks say, and there’s nought but what we see?”

Mr. Hale was busy cutting bread and butter. Margaret was glad of it; she saw Higgins was better left to himself. If her father began to speak, even mildly, on the subject of Higgins’s thoughts, Higgins would feel himself challenged into argument and bound to maintain his ground. So they kept up indifferent talk while Higgins, scarcely aware whether he ate or not, made a substantial meal.

When he pushed his chair away, he tried to take interest again in their words; but his attention would not hold. He fell back into a kind of dreamy gloom, as if listening to some far-off inward noise louder than anything in the room.

Suddenly Margaret said—she had been shaping the question for some time, feeling the words stick in her throat—“Higgins, have you been to Marlborough Mills to seek for work?”

“Thornton’s?” he asked.

“Ay,” said Margaret. “I’ve been at Thornton’s.”

“And what did he say?” Mr. Hale asked, leaning forwards.

“Such a chap as me is not like to see the measter. Th’ o’erlooker bid me go and be d——d.”

“I wish you had seen Mr. Thornton,” said Mr. Hale. “He might not have given you work, but he would not have used such language.”

“As to th’ language, I’m welly used to it; it dunnot matter to me. I’m not nesh mysel’ when I’m put out. It were th’ fact that I were na wanted theer, no more nor ony other place, as I minded.”

“But I wish you had seen Mr. Thornton,” Margaret said again, and her voice had an entreaty that made it more than a repetition. “Would you go again—it’s a good deal to ask, I know—but would you go to-morrow and try him? I should be so glad if you would.”

“I’m afraid it would be of no use,” Mr. Hale murmured, low.

Margaret still looked at Higgins, waiting. Those grave, soft eyes of hers—eyes that saw suffering and would not turn away—were hard to resist. Higgins drew a long breath.

“It would tax my pride above a bit,” he said. “If it were for mysel’, I could stand a deal o’ clemming first; I’d sooner knock him down than ask a favour from him. I’d a deal sooner be flogged mysel’. But yo’re not a common wench, axing yo’r pardon, nor yet have yo’ common ways about yo’. I’ll e’en make a wry face, and go at it to-morrow. Dunna yo’ think that he’ll do it. That man has it in him to be burnt at the stake afore he’ll give in. I’ll do it for yo’r sake, Miss Hale, and it’s first time in my life as e’er I give way to a woman. Neither my wife nor Bess could e’er say that much again me.”

“All the more do I thank you,” said Margaret, smiling—relieved, and yet troubled for what she was asking. “Though I don’t believe you: I believe you have just given way to wife and daughter as much as most men.”

“And as to Mr. Thornton,” said Mr. Hale, “I’ll give you a note to him, which, I think I may venture say, will ensure you a hearing.”

“I thank yo’ kindly, sir,” Higgins answered, stiffening, “but I’d as lief stand on my own bottom. I dunnot stomach the notion of having favour curried for me, by one as doesn’t know the ins and outs of the quarrel. Meddling ’twixt master and man is liker meddling ’twixt husband and wife than aught else: it takes a deal of wisdom to do ony good. I’ll stand guard at the lodge door. I’ll stand there fro’ six in the morning till I get speech on him. But I’d liefer sweep th’ streets, if paupers had na’ got hold on that work. Dunna yo’ hope, miss. There’ll be more chance o’ getting milk out of a flint. I wish yo’ a very good night, and many thanks to yo’.”

“You’ll find your shoes by the kitchen fire; I took them there to dry,” said Margaret.

He turned and looked at her steadily. Then he brushed his lean hand across his eyes, as if angry to find them wet, and went his way.

When the door had closed behind him, Mr. Hale said, with a little vexation, “How proud that man is!”

“He is,” Margaret replied. “But what grand makings of a man there are in him, pride and all.”

“It’s amusing to see how he evidently respects the part in Mr. Thornton’s character which is like his own.”

“There’s granite in all these northern people, papa, is there not?” Margaret said, thinking of the set mouths and resolute brows she had learnt to read in Milton, and of the hard, incessant sound of the place—the mills’ ceaseless thunder that seemed to beat into men’s hearts until they answered it with a like unyieldingness.

“There was none in poor Boucher, I am afraid; none in his wife either.”

“I should guess by their tones that they had Irish blood in them,” said Margaret, half to herself; and then, as if already living a little forward into the morrow, “I wonder what success he’ll have to-morrow. If he and Mr. Thornton would speak out together as man to man—if Higgins would forget that Mr. Thornton was a master, and speak to him as he does to us—and if Mr. Thornton would be patient enough to listen to him with his human heart, not with his master’s ears—”

“You are getting to do Mr. Thornton justice at last, Margaret,” said her father, pinching her ear with a fondness that had no suspicion of the sharp inward pang his words caused.

Margaret’s throat tightened; she could not answer. A thought rose, bitter and helpless: If she had been a man, she could have gone and forced the matter to speech—forced him to express his disapprobation, and told him plainly she knew she had deserved it. It seemed hard to lose him as a friend just when she had begun to feel his value. How tender he had been with dear mamma! If only for her sake, she wished he would come—come soon—so that at least she might know how far she was abased in his eyes.
