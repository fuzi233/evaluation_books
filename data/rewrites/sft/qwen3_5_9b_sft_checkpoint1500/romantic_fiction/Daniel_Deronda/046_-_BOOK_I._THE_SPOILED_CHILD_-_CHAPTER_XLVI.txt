"If anyone asks me why I loved him, I have no answer save to say, 'Because it was he, because it was I.' There exists a power beyond words that drew us together." —Montaigne, *On Friendship*.

The time had arrived for Mordecai to prepare for the revelation of his long-lost sister and the impending move that would bring Mirah to England. Mrs. Meyrick, to whom Daniel Deronda had entrusted every secret save Mordecai's unique bond with himself, had tirelessly assisted in securing a charming lodging in Brompton, mere minutes' walk from her own home. This proximity ensured that Mirah would remain within the gentle, watchful circle of her "motherly" care. With a blend of Scottish fervor and Gaelic vivacity, Mrs. Meyrick had kept the news strictly hidden from the younger girls and from Hans, fearing that any premature leak might spark agitation and ruin the delicate groundwork essential for Mirah's newfound independence. Both Mrs. Meyrick and Deronda harbored a profound, unspoken desire for Mirah to stand on her own feet. While Mrs. Meyrick, the "little mother," may have harbored a faint, dubitable hesitation regarding the extraordinary brother described to her—perhaps viewing his intense devotion to Judaism as a form of stubborn pertinacity akin to the Covenanters—she ultimately trusted Deronda's judgment above all. Though the reality of Mirah's frailty, marked by consumption, tugged at her tender heart, she hoped that Mirah's fierce attachment to her Jewish identity would eventually soften, merging into the warmer, reciprocal currents of love flowing through her new friendships. Indeed, Mrs. Meyrick's private fantasy had not centered on a dramatic discovery of Jewish lineage, but rather on a brighter future for Hans; now, however, a brother stood poised to plunge Mirah's spirit once more into the deepest dyes of ancestral sentiment.

"It is a comfort," Mrs. Meyrick confided to Deronda in the quiet of the newly rented room, "that the pawnbroker is not her brother. There are countless Ezras in the world, and truly, it is relief to know that not all Jews resemble those shopkeepers who refuse to let you depart. Besides, his words about his mother and sister make me bless him. He seems so kind. Yet, I confess, I have never held a fondness for anything fanatical. Perhaps I heard too much preaching in my youth and lost my appetite for it."

"I do not believe you will find Mordecai obtruding any preaching," Deronda replied gently. "To me, fanaticism implies an enthusiasm that is narrow and blinded, lacking a sense of proportion, rendering a man unjust and unsympathetic to those outside his own circle. Mordecai is an enthusiast, indeed, but I reserve that title for the highest orders of mind—those who care supremely for grand and universal benefits for humanity. He is not a strictly orthodox Jew, and his conformity in many matters is simply an allowance for the conditions of other Jews. Those who live with him adore him, though they cannot fully comprehend his inner world."

"Oh, well," Mrs. Meyrick smiled, smoothing the curtains, "I can live up to the level of the pawnbroker's mother, and appreciate whatever virtue I perceive. For what I cannot see, I shall take your word. By your definition, perhaps one could be fanatical in worshipping common-sense? My late husband often mused that the world would be a poor place if it contained nothing but common sense. However, Mirah's brother shall have the finest bedding I have procured, and I shall paste up this extra window to stave off the drafts. It is a solace to know the household folk are no strangers to me, no hypocritical harpies. Once the children know the truth, we shall be able to adorn the rooms much more prettily."

"The next stage," Deronda noted, looking toward the door, "is to inform Mordecai and arrange his relocation. That may prove the more difficult endeavor."

"And will you tell Mirah before I say a word to the children?" Mrs. Meyrick asked, her tone shifting to one of deliberate persuasion. "No, I think not. Let me tell Hans and the girls tonight, and they will be gone tomorrow morning."

"Yes, that is best," Deronda agreed, offering a soft smile. "But do justice to my account of Mordecai—or 'Ezra,' as Mirah will surely wish to call him. Do not feed their imaginations with tales of Habakkuk Mucklewrath."

"Trust me, trust me," the little mother insisted warmly. "I shall have to persuade them so desperately to be glad that I shall convert myself. When I am frightened, it is a good thing to have someone to be angry with for not being brave; it warms the blood."

Though Deronda could have engaged in further debate regarding the nature of Mordecai's beliefs, his thoughts were preoccupied with a more pressing task: he wished to approach the Cohens without wounding their sensibilities. Following a memorable exchange where Mordecai had signaled his acute sensitivity to any inadvertence concerning their feelings, Deronda had met with the family at the *Hand and Banner*. Now, after careful reflection, he had written to Mordecai requesting the honor of sitting in his workroom for an hour, framing it as a personal request that would vanish if any objection arose. He hoped to cultivate a sense of anticipation that would serve as a gentle preparation.

Upon arriving, Deronda was greeted with the usual warmth, accompanied by subtle shifts in the women's and children's attire, and a lingering, silent wonder from the elders—a mystery surrounding Deronda's relationship with Mordecai that they were too humble to articulate aloud. When Deronda announced, "I suppose Mordecai is at home and expecting me," Jacob, who had caught wind of the family whispers, trotted over to kneel beside him. "What do you want to talk to Mordecai about?" the boy asked eagerly.

"Something very interesting to him," Deronda said, playfully pinching the lad's ear, "but you cannot understand."

"Can you say this?" Jacob challenged, launching into a string of rote-learned Hebrew verses with a wonderful, throaty resonance, nodding solemnly as if presenting formidable evidence capable of altering their mutual standing.

"No, really," Deronda kept grave, "I cannot say anything like that."

"I thought not!" Jacob declared, performing a triumphant dance with his small scarlet legs, emptying and refilling the deep pockets of his knickerbockers to display his resources. He then dashed to the workroom door, flung it wide, leaned back against it, and shouted, "Mordecai, here's the young swell"—a borrowed phrase from his father that seemed to him the perfect capstone to his recitation.

He was recalled with hushed warnings from his mother and grandmother. As Deronda entered and closed the door behind him, he noticed a piece of carpet laid down, a chair positioned, and the fire and lights tended to, all signs of the Cohens' deep respect. As Mordecai rose to greet him, Deronda was struck by the solemn expectancy in his face—an expression that would have seemed entirely natural if his letter had hinted at a revelation regarding a lost sister. Silence fell between them until Deronda, with his customary tenderness, drew a vacant chair from the opposite side of the hearth and seated himself near Mordecai.

"You are coming to tell me something that my soul longs for," Mordecai said in a tone of fervid certainty.

"It is true I have something very weighty to tell you—something I trust you will rejoice in," Deronda responded, guarding himself against the probability that Mordecai expected something entirely different.

"It is all revealed—it is made clear to you," Mordecai pressed, leaning forward with clasped hands. "You are even as my brother that sucked the breasts of my mother—the heritage is yours—there is no doubt to divide us."

"I have learned nothing new about myself," Deronda admitted quietly. The disappointment was inevitable, yet it was kinder not to strain the moment with prolonged false hope.

Mordecai sank back in his chair, momentarily unable to care what was truly coming. His entire day had existed in a state of tension toward a single fulfillment; the reaction was sickening, and he closed his eyes.

"Except," Deronda went on gently after a pause, "except that I had recently come into another sort of hidden connection with you, besides what you have spoken of as existing in your own feeling."

The eyes remained shut, but there was a fluttering in the lids.

"I had made the acquaintance of one in whom you are deeply interested."

"One who is closely related to your departed mother," Deronda continued, striving to make the disclosure gradual. Noticing a shrinking movement in Mordecai, he added softly, "Whom she and you held dear above all others."

Mordecai gave a sudden start, laying a spasmodic, desperate grasp on Deronda's wrist. A great terror seized him. Deronda divined it instantly. A tremor vibrated through his clear tones as he spoke, "What was prayed for has come to pass: Mirah has been delivered from evil."

Mordecai's grip loosened slightly, but he panted with a tearless sob.

"Your sister is worthy of the mother you honored," Deronda affirmed.
