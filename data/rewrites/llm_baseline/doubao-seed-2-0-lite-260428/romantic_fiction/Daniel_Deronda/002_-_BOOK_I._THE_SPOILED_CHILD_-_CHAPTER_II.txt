This man weaves a secret between us two, that he might subdue me with the weight of his gaze, as one tames a lioness backed into a corner.

The cream envelope was propped against her pearl-handled hairbrush when Gwendolen stepped back into her sun-dappled Leubronn lodgings, still thrumming from the previous night’s whirl of waltzes and the sharp, sour sting of bad luck at the roulette table. It was her mother’s handwriting, its wobbly loops instantly familiar, and she sank onto the plush red velvet sofa by the window to tear it open, no premonition of the ruin spilling across its pages.

*My Dearest Child*, it began, *I have waited a week to hear from you. In your last letter you said the Langens were thinking of leaving Leubronn for Baden – how could you be so careless as to leave me guessing your whereabouts, my heart sick with fear this note might never reach you? You were meant to come home at the end of September, but I beg you to return as fast as you can. If you’ve spent all your allowance, I have no more money to send you. Do not borrow from the Langens, for I could never repay them. The sad, terrible truth is this: a calamity has fallen on us all. You know nothing of business, and will not understand the details, but Grapnell & Co. have failed for a million pounds, and we are utterly, irrevocably ruined. Your aunt Gascoigne and I are both stripped bare, though your uncle still has his church benefice; they can scrape by, giving up their carriage and putting the boys to work. Every last penny our poor father saved for us is gone to pay the firm’s debts. I have nothing left I can call my own. It rends my heart to tell you this, but you deserve to know it at once. Of course we all cannot help but grieve that you left for your holiday right when this disaster struck – but I will never reproach you, my love. I would shield you from every hardship if I could. You will have time on the journey home to steel yourself for what waits. We shall leave Offendene at once, we think; Mr. Haynes, who wanted to rent it before, may take it off my hands. There is no spare corner of the rectory for us to stay, so we must find some tiny cottage to shelter in, and live off your uncle’s charity until I can find some way to earn money. I cannot pay the tradesmen’s bills, or the servants’ wages. Summon your courage, my dear. We must resign ourselves to God’s will, though it is cruel to accept the recklessness of that wicked Mr. Lassman who brought this all down. Your poor sisters only weep with me, they cannot help. If you were here, perhaps the clouds would break – I have always known you were never meant for a life of poverty. If the Langens wish to stay abroad, find some other respectable lady to chaperone you on the journey. Come home as soon as you can, to your loving, broken
Mamma, Fanny Davilow.*

For long minutes after she finished reading, Gwendolen could not move. The unshakable, youthful certainty that her life would always be soft, luxurious, every inconvenience neatly swept away by money and her own charm, had defined her for as long as she could remember. It felt as impossible to accept she was now poor, dependent, humiliated, as it would have felt to imagine her own bright, blooming life ending tomorrow. She stood at last, tossed off her hat, and stared blankly at her reflection in the glass: her light brown hair was still coiled in perfect, glossy loops, fit for a royal ball. Usually she would linger to admire the curve of her cheek, the glint in her eyes, but today she did not see her own beauty at all. She only stared, as if waiting for some cruel, wrong noise to end, to wake her back to the world she had known an hour before.

She sank back onto the red sofa, read the letter twice more, and let it drift to the carpet at her feet. Her hands were clasped tight in her lap, and she shed no tears. She did not think of her mother’s grief first – Mrs. Davilow had never been the sort to savor joy, and Gwendolen had always been the apple of her eye, the one she spoiled and doted on above all others. It was hot, sharp anger that flooded her first, bitter vexation that her luck at roulette had run dry the night before. If she had won just one more big stake, she could have come home with a fortune large enough to fix all their troubles.

Wait, she thought, why not still try? She had only four napoleons left in her purse, but she had the Etruscan turquoise necklace her father had left her, an old piece she rarely wore. Pawning jewelry was common, even fashionable, for wealthy tourists at the German baths – no one would bat an eye. Thirteen louis would be enough to get her home, and if she risked three of them on the roulette table, with the luck that had always seemed to follow her, she could win enough to lift her family out of ruin entirely. The only catch was the Langens: she could not let them find out her family was ruined, could not bear their pity. She resolved to slip out at dawn, pawn the necklace, tell the Langens her mother had called her home urgently without giving details, and take the evening train to Brussels.

She did not go to bed at all. She lit every candle in the room and packed furiously, her thoughts swinging wildly between visions of arriving home with a sack full of winnings, and the unavoidable memory of the tall, dark stranger who had stood watching her at the roulette table the night before – Daniel Deronda, his gaze heavy with a quiet, unreadable irony, the same man who had been there to see her lose every last one of her big bets. The thought of him seeing her back at the tables, failing again, made her pride burn so hot it nearly settled her resolve to leave at once. By the time she had zipped her last traveling bag shut, pale dawn was seeping through the white blinds, dimming the guttering candles. She did not bother sleeping. A quick cold bath refreshed her, and the faint dark circles under her eyes only made her look more striking, more romantic. By six o’clock she was fully dressed in her soft gray traveling gown, her felt hat pinned on, ready to slip out the moment other ladies began their morning walks to the springs.

She paused sideways before the long mirror between her windows, leaning her elbow on the back of her chair in a pose fit for a portrait. Even with her whole world falling apart, she could not help the slow, soft smile that curved her lips as she stared at her own reflection. How could such a bright, lucky girl as her be made for unending sorrow? She felt strong enough to fight any hardship, to outrun it, as she had outrun every small inconvenience of her life so far.

Madame von Langen never left the house before breakfast, so Gwendolen could take the long way home past the Obere Strasse, where Mr. Wiener’s jeweler’s shop would open just after seven. The Czarina Hotel – Deronda’s hotel – loomed at the end of the street, and it was possible someone staying there might see her walk into the pawn shop. But what of it? She could say she was popping in to buy a souvenir. She walked with her usual graceful, gliding step, not looking left or right, and transacted her business quickly. Mr. Wiener, the little jeweler, fawned over the three big turquoise stones set in the old necklace, but he gave her only nine louis for it, far less than she thought it was worth. Still, thirteen louis total was more than enough to get her all the way home, and she wavered again on her way back to the Langens’ apartment, half-ready to stay one more day and try her luck.

She sank into the salon sofa, tired and hungry, eyes half shut, waiting for her friends to come down for breakfast. She expected to see one of the Langens walk through the door – but it was only the servant, bearing a small wrapped parcel for Miss Harleth, that had just been left at the door.

Gwendolen’s heart jumped to her throat. She grabbed the parcel and ran to her own room, her hands shaking. Before she even unwrapped it, she knew what it was: her turquoise necklace. It was wrapped in a fine cambric handkerchief, with a scrap of pencil-scrawled note tucked inside: *A stranger who has found Miss Harleth’s necklace returns it to her with the hope that she will not again risk the loss of it.*

Waves of hot, burning humiliation flooded her. She knew at once who the “stranger” was. Deronda must have seen her walk into the jeweler’s shop, must have gone in right after her, bought the necklace back, and sent it to her anonymously. He had dared to pry into her secret, to put her in this impossible, humiliating position. She could not send it back to him – that would mean admitting she knew it was him, admitting she had pawned her own father’s necklace to gamble. He thought he was some sort of saintly mentor, smiling down at her with that ironic, superior look, and she could not bear the thought of facing him ever again. Bitter, angry tears streamed down her face, the first tears she had shed since reading her mother’s letter. No one had ever dared to treat her like this.

There was a sharp knock at the door. Breakfast was ready. Gwendolen stuffed the necklace, the handkerchief, and the scrap of note into her vanity case, pressed her own handkerchief to her eyes to wipe the tears away, and summoned back her usual proud, steady poise before she stepped out to join her friends. The faint signs of her fatigue and red eyes lined up perfectly with the lie she told – that she had stayed up all night packing, too restless to sleep. The Langens protested fiercely that she should never travel alone, but Gwendolen was firm, unstoppable. She would take the ladies’ compartment, she told them, and sleep the whole way, no chaperone needed.

That Thursday evening, Gwendolen’s train pulled out of Leubronn’s station, and she never set foot in the roulette hall again. By Saturday morning, she was stepping onto the gravel drive of Offendene, the home she and her family would soon have to leave forever, the strange, unexpected gift of the stranger’s turquoise necklace tucked safe in her case.
