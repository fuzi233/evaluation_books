Miss Butterworth was a product of a rigorous academy of etiquette. Her posture was immutable; her movements were swift, firm, and devoid of superfluous tremors. Fidgets were alien to her nature. Yet, when the interview concluded and she found herself alone, the iron discipline of her upbringing faltered. With considerable effort, she suppressed the outward signs of agitation—the shifting weight, the restless glances—that she had always condemned in the hysterical women of her acquaintance. Anxiety gnawed at her, and she displayed it with the candid honesty of a sensible soul, grateful when Mr. Gryce finally reappeared.

He approached her with a bright, almost boisterous smile. "Well, that is settled! All that remains is waiting. Madam, do you harbor any further thoughts? If so, I beg the privilege of hearing them."

Her composure snapped back into place instantly. "Would you?" she echoed, regarding him with a flicker of skepticism. "I crave assurance of the validity of my current theory before venturing into new territory. Let us hold a conference; compare notes. For instance, explain why neither of us considers Bartow the guilty party."

"I believed we had drained that well dry," Gryce observed. "Your doubts stemmed from the young couple fleeing the premises, while mine... well, Madam, I confess to you that the Mute's gestures and demeanor convey to me the distinct impression that he is rehearsing a vision he has witnessed, rather than an act he committed. Thus far, I have seen no cause to doubt this."

"I was struck by the same thing," Butterworth admitted, "even without prior suspicion diverting me elsewhere. Furthermore, it is far more natural for a man to be unhinged by another's cruelty than by his own."

"Yes, if he loved the victim."

"And did not Bartow love Mr. Adams?"

"He sheds no tears for him."

"But his emotions are no longer his masters."

"Precisely. Yet, if we use his actions as a clue, we must accept them wholly. We deduce from his pantomime that he is copying a scene he saw. So, how do we account for the infernal joy that flashes across his face once the performance ends? It suggests he revels in the deed itself, or finds boundless satisfaction within it."

"Perhaps it is merely part of the copy; the murderer may have rejoiced. However, the youth I saw rushing from the scene bore no trace of glee. Quite the opposite, Mr. Gryce. We are navigating treacherous waters, and I feel I am nearly drowning beneath them."

"Lift your head, Madam. Every flood recedes eventually. If you succumb, what becomes of me?"

"You possess a penchant for humor, Mr. Gryce. A promising sign. You lose your wit when truly baffled. Somewhere, you must see the light."

"Proceed with our argument. Truth often emerges from the most unlikely quarters."

"Agreed. Let us classify the old man's joy as a puzzle for later resolution. Have you considered him as a potential accomplice?"

"Certainly. Yet, this hypothesis shares the same fatal flaw as the one that cast him as the prime mover. One does not go mad from an anticipated horror; a shock is required to fracture the brain. He was not expecting his master's death."

"True. We may rest that matter. Bartow was an innocent witness, unburdened by fear, and thus capable of reproducing the crime's exact features in his trance-like state."

"Capital. Continue, Madam. An argument advanced by Miss Butterworth promises profit."

Though the old detective's tone remained grave and his manners impeccable, Butterworth, ever vigilant for sarcasm, bristled slightly, though she offered no visible sign of displeasure.

"Recall his precise gestures," she directed. "Remember, he must have spotted the attacker from the study doorway, viewing the assault from behind his master's shoulder."

"In plain terms, directly in front of him. What was his initial movement?"

"As I remember it, his first move was to extend his right arm backward, leaning forward as if grasping an imaginary dagger. What does that signify?"

"It puzzles me. When I first saw him, he was driving a vicious thrust with his *left* hand across his own body. That is a crucial detail, Mr. Gryce."

"Yes, particularly since the medical examiners confirm Mr. Adams was slain by a left-handed blow."

"Don't you see the contradiction, then?"

"The contradiction, Madam?"

"Bartow stood face-to-face with the assailant. In his deranged state, attempting to mimic an opponent, he would naturally lift the arm opposite to the limb performing the action—which, in this case, would be the attacker's right hand. Try it for a moment. Mimic me. Watch!"

She lifted her hand, palm open. Instantly, Gryce raised the one directly opposite.

"I felt it too, sir—instinctively, I noticed it the moment you recalled it. You turn your head right, but your reflection turns left. It is a mirror effect."

Gryce's laughter broke free, involuntary and genuine. Though rarely caught off guard, this woman possessed a certain fascination for him, charming rather than irritating when she bested him.

"A fair point, Madam. Proved satisfactorily. But what conclusion draws from it? That the killer was not left-handed, or that he did not stand where you placed him?"

"Shall we defy the doctors? They insist the blow was left-handed. Mr. Gryce, I would trade an hour with you inside Mr. Adams's study, with Bartow free to roam, for the knowledge we'd gain by simply watching him for ten minutes. Talking for an hour yields little."

Said tentatively, almost timidly. Butterworth sensed the audacity of her proposal, despite her claim to lack curiosity. "I wish to avoid being disagreeable," she smiled.

Far from being disagreeable, Gryce was taken aback. For once, impulse seized him. "I believe it can be arranged, Madam. After the funeral, of course. The house is swarming with officials now, and—"

"Naturally, naturally," she acquiesced. "I would never dream of imposing myself at present. Yet the case fascinates me, and my connection to it is so peculiar, I sometimes forget decorum. Do you think"—her voice dropped, betraying a rare spike of nervousness—"that I have exposed myself to a summons from the coroner?"

Gryce steepled his fingers, studying the lady—or rather, her clasped hands—with a mixture of compassion and calculation. Finally, he spoke. "The facts of this affair trickle in so slowly that I doubt the inquest will convene for several days. Meanwhile, we may track down those two youths ourselves. If we do, the coroner may choose to overlook your role in bringing them to light."

A sly emphasis lingered on the word *overlook*, accompanied by a subtle, dark humor that revealed the detective at his sharpest. Butterworth did not resent it; she was too preoccupied with a fresh confession to make.

"Ah," she murmured, "if they had been the sole visitors I encountered. But they were not. Another individual entered the house before I departed, and I may need to speak of him."

"Of him? Truly, Madam, you are a lode of intelligence."

"Yes, sir," her reply was humble, considering from whom it flowed. "I should have mentioned him sooner, but I dislike mixing matters, and this old gentleman..."

"Old gentleman!"

"Yes, sir. Very ancient and undeniably genteel. He appeared to know Mr. Adams, but otherwise, his connection to the crime seemed minimal."

"Ah, but that is a significant connection, Ma'am. To discover someone who knew Mr. Adams... Patience has limits, and I urge you to speak."

"Oh, I shall speak! The moment has arrived. Besides, I am eager to discuss this new thread; it is profoundly intriguing."

"Pray, begin with a detailed chronicle of your adventures in this house of death," Gryce suggested dryly. "Every detail, Madam, nothing omitted."

"I appreciate the irony, but I have withheld nothing save what I am about to reveal. It transpired precisely as I was exiting."

"What did?" Gryce pressed gently, though the urgency of justice burned in his eyes. "But please, for the sake of justice..."

"You are correct. As I was stepping out, I met an elderly gentleman entering. His hand had just brushed the bell-handle. You will acknowledge it was a perplexing juncture. His countenance, well-preserved for his age, wore an air of expectation bordering on gaiety. He glanced at me in astonishment—I, usually serene, surely bore the marks of deep emotion. We said naught. Eventually, he asked politely if he might enter, mentioning an appointment with someone in the study. Whereupon I stepped briskly aside—assured you, I did not wish to invite the entire length of ---- Street into this horror at a moment when a frail old man..."

She trailed off, her voice rising. "...to enter this scene alone? Besides, I had not warned him of the condition of the sole other occupant."
