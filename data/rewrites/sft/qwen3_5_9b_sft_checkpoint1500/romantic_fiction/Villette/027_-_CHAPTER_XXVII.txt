The next day proved far livelier and busier than I—or, at any rate, than I had hoped—we had anticipated. It transpired to be the birthday of one of the younger princes of Labassecour, the eldest, I believe, the Duc de Dindonneau. Consequently, a general holiday was declared throughout the schools, particularly at the principal institution, the "Athénée." The students of this college had concocted a loyal address intended for presentation; for this purpose, they were to assemble in the public hall where the annual examinations and prize distributions took place. Following the ceremony, an oration, or *discours*, was scheduled from one of the professors.

Several of M. de Bassompierre's acquaintances—the savants—who were connected with the Athénée, along with the respectable municipality of Villette, M. le Chevalier Staas, the burgomaster, and the parents and kin of the Athenians, were expected to attend. M. de Bassompierre was persuaded by his friends to accompany them; his fair daughter, naturally, would be part of the party, and she sent a brief note to Ginevra and me, inviting us to arrive early so we might join her.

As Miss Fanshawe and I dressed in the dormitory on Rue Fossette, she suddenly burst into laughter, suspending her grooming to gaze at me.

"What now?" I asked, surprised by the interruption.

"It seems so odd," she replied, with her customary mixture of honesty and insolence, "that you and I should now be on such equal footing, visiting in the same sphere, sharing the same connections."

"Why, yes," I answered. "I had not held much respect for the circles you chiefly frequented a while ago: Mrs. Cholmondeley and company would never have suited me at all."

"Who _are_ you, Miss Snowe?" she inquired, her tone a blend of undisguised and unsophisticated curiosity that made me laugh in return.

"You used to describe yourself as a nursery governess; when you first came here, you truly had charge of the children in this house. I have seen you carrying little Georgette in your arms like a *bonne*—few governesses would have condescended so far. Now, Madame Beck treats you with more courtesy than she treats the Parisienne, St. Pierre; and that proud chit, my cousin, makes you her bosom friend!"

"Wonderful!" I agreed, amused by her bewilderment. "Who am I indeed? Perhaps a personage in disguise. Pity I do not look the character."

"I wonder you are not more flattered by all this," she continued. "You take it with strange composure. If you really are the nobody I once thought you, you must be a cool hand."

"The nobody you once thought me!" I repeated, feeling a slight heat rise in my face, though I refused to be angry. A schoolgirl's crude use of terms like 'nobody' and 'somebody' held little weight. I confined myself to observing that I had merely received civility, asking what she saw in civility to throw the recipient into a fever of confusion.

"One can't help wondering at some things," she persisted.

"Wondering at marvels of your own manufacture. Are you ready at last?"

"Yes; let me take your arm."

"I would rather not: we will walk side by side."

When she finally took my arm, she leaned upon me with her whole weight. Since I was not a gentleman, nor her lover, I did not relish the burden.

"There, again!" she cried. "I thought, by offering to take your arm, to intimate approbation of your dress and general appearance: I meant it as a compliment."

"You did? You meant, in short, to express that you are not ashamed to be seen in the street with me? That if Mrs. Cholmondeley should be fondling her lapdog at some window, or Colonel de Hamal picking his teeth in a balcony, and should catch a glimpse of us, you would not quite blush for your companion?"

"Yes," she said, with a directness that was her finest trait—which gave an honest plainness to her very lies, which acted as the sole preservative in a character otherwise unfit to keep.

I left the trouble of commenting on this "yes" to my countenance; or rather, my lower lip voluntarily anticipated my tongue, conveying reverence and solemnity in a look that betrayed neither.

"Scornful, sneering creature!" she went on as we crossed a great square and entered the quiet, pleasant park leading to Rue Crécy. "Nobody in this world was ever such a Turk to me as you are!"

"You bring it on yourself: let me alone: have the sense to be quiet: I will let you alone."

"As if one _could_ let you alone, when you are so peculiar and so mysterious!"

"The mystery and peculiarity being entirely the conception of your own brain—maggots—neither more nor less, be so good as to keep them out of my sight."

"But _are_ you anybody?" she insisted, pushing her hand under my arm despite my protests, and pressing it with inhospitable closeness against my side to ward off the intrusion.

"Yes," I said. "I am a rising character: once an old lady's companion, then a nursery-governess, now a school-teacher."

"Do—_do_ tell me who you are? I'll not repeat it," she urged, adhering with ludicrous tenacity to the wise notion of an incognito she had grasped. She squeezed the arm she now fully possessed, coaxed and conjured until I was obliged to pause in the park to laugh. Throughout our walk, she rang the most fanciful changes on this theme, proving by her obstinate credulity, or incredulity, her incapacity to conceive how any person not bolstered up by birth or wealth, not supported by some consciousness of name or connection, could maintain an attitude of reasonable integrity. As for me, it sufficed for my mental tranquility that I was known where it mattered that I should be known; the rest sat lightly upon me. Pedigree, social position, and recondite intellectual acquisitions occupied about the same space and place in my thoughts; they were my third-class lodgers, to whom I assigned only the small sitting-room and the little back bedroom. Even if the dining and drawing-rooms stood empty, I never confessed it to them, considering minor accommodations better suited to their circumstances. The world, I soon learned, held a different estimate: and I make no doubt the world is very right in its view, yet believe also that I am not quite wrong in mine.

There are those whom a lowered position degrades morally, to whom loss of connection costs loss of self-respect. Are not these justified in placing the highest value on that station and association which serves as their safeguard from debasement? If a man feels that he would become contemptible in his own eyes were it generally known that his ancestry were simple and not gentle, poor and not rich, workers and not capitalists, would it be right severely to blame him for keeping these fatal facts out of sight—for starting, trembling, quailing at the chance which threatens exposure? The longer we live, the wider our experience grows; the less prone are we to judge our neighbor's conduct, to question the world's wisdom. Wherever an accumulation of small defenses is found, whether surrounding the prude's virtue or the man of the world's respectability, there, be sure, it is needed.

We reached the Hôtel Crécy. Paulina was ready; Mrs. Bretton was with her. Under her escort and that of M. de Bassompierre, we were soon conducted to the place of assembly and seated in good seats at a convenient distance from the Tribune. The youth of the Athénée were marshaled before us; the municipality and their burgomaster held places of honor; the young princes, with their tutors, occupied a conspicuous position; and the body of the building was crowded with the aristocracy and first burghers of the town.

Concerning the identity of the professor by whom the *discours* was to be delivered, I had entertained neither care nor question. Some vague expectation I had that a savant would stand up and deliver a formal speech, half dogmatism to the Athenians, half flattery to the princes.

The Tribune was empty when we entered, but in ten minutes it was filled. Suddenly, in a second of time, a head, chest, and arms grew above the crimson desk. This head I knew: its color, shape, port, and expression were familiar both to me and Miss Fanshawe. The blackness and closeness of the cranium, the amplitude and paleness of the brow, the blueness and fire of the glance, were details so domesticated in the memory, and so knit with many whimsical associations, as almost to tickle fancy to a laugh by their sudden apparition. Indeed, I confess, for my part, I did laugh till I was warm; but then I bowed my head, making my handkerchief and a lowered veil the sole confidants of my mirth.

I think I was glad to see M. Paul. It was rather pleasant than otherwise to behold him set up there, fierce and frank, dark and candid, testy and fearless, as when regnant on his estrade in class.
