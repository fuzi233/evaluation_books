In the sweltering month of May, while Durrance gazed northward from Wadi Halfa, his fate was being shaped five hundred miles to the south, beyond the Nubian desert and the treacherous Belly of Stones. There, in the barren stretch between Berber and Suakin, lay the wells of Obak—deep, silent, and half-buried beneath shifting dunes.

To the east, a ragged line of trees marked the edge of a stony plain, broken by jagged hills of granite. To the west, the desert sprawled, lifeless and unbroken, for fifty-eight miles until it touched the Nile at Mahobey. Here, the horizon was a cruel illusion—tufts of grass masqueraded as shade, scattered stones pretended to be shelter. And the sun, relentless, burned the earth to dust by day, while the wind howled cold by night, reshaping the land with every gust.

Vultures circled overhead; sand-grouse darted between the dunes. The bones of camels, long picked clean, lay half-buried in the sand, remnants of caravans that had once passed this way. A crumbling hut of bent branches whispered of a time when men had lived here. Now, there was only silence—a heavy, waiting silence, as though the desert itself held its breath.

Yet in this forsaken place, one man waited.

Each dawn, he led two swift Bisharin mares from the shelter of the trees, watered them at the well, and sat motionless for hours, his eyes fixed on the dunes to the west. Then, as the sun climbed higher, he would retreat again into the shadows, feeding the beasts carefully before vanishing for the day.

For five mornings, he kept this vigil alone.

On the sixth, as he turned to leave, a figure crested the distant ridge—a man leading a donkey. The Arab by the well tensed, his gaze locking onto the animal’s grey hide. But then he saw the driver—a negro, his legs dark against the pale sand, his face shrouded beneath a jellab. The Arab relaxed, feigning indifference as the stranger approached.

*"Salam aleikum,"* the negro greeted, laying down his spears and shield.

*"Aleikum es salam,"* the Arab replied, his voice flat.

"You are Abou Fatma?"

A nod.

"Two days past, a Bisharin named Moussa Fedil found me in Berber’s market. He fed me, then bid me bring this donkey to you at Obak."

Abou Fatma barely glanced at the beast. *"Tayeeb,"* he murmured, as if it meant nothing.

But the negro lingered. "Moussa Fedil expects me back in three days. Give me a token, so he knows I’ve done his bidding."

With deliberate slowness, Abou Fatma drew his knife, notched a stick thrice at each end, and handed it over. The negro tied it into his robe, filled his waterskin, and trudged back the way he had come.

Only when he had vanished did Abou Fatma move swiftly. He led the donkey into the trees, examining its shoulders. There—a small cut, stitched shut. He pried it open, extracting a scrap of bladder no larger than a fingernail. Inside, a message in Arabic:

The writer was Harry Feversham.

Disguised as a Dervish, his skin darkened, his hair twisted into matted locks, he moved through the new streets of Berber—a town of gaping pits and raw construction. The old city lay in ruins a mile distant, its alleys now skeletal rows of crumbling walls. Foxes denned in the rubble.

He had planned to slip in under cover of night, retrieve what he sought, and vanish before dawn. Instead, he walked openly among the crowds, ears straining for the name *Yusef*, tongue locked in silence lest his accent betray him. Fear gnawed at him, but beneath it burned a desperate resolve—a belief that the world could not be so cruel as to demand this of him and then let him fail.

Three days earlier, as Berber’s walls first rose before him, that fear had nearly broken him. The low brown ramparts, the swaying palms beyond—they had seemed not refuge, but death’s maw. His legs had trembled; he had turned to flee, stumbling in blind panic across the sand.

But then he stopped.

The years of preparation, the nights spent whispering Arabic in filthy bazaars—had they been for nothing? Would he always be the man who had resigned his commission in shame?

And Ethne—

He dropped to the ground, pulling his coat over his head, shutting out the world. In the darkness, her face came to him—clear, unwavering. The memory of that summer night in Donegal, the feathers fluttering to the floor, the quiet promise in her voice: *"I should have no doubt that you and I would see much of one another afterwards."*

When he rose, the sun was sinking. He walked toward Berber, passed through its gates, and did not look back.
