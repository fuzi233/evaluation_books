They were not marching to war, nor hunting down foes—yet the procession, led by a priest and guided by women, carried music that echoed with battle rhythms. And though no enemy loomed ahead, some among them—Miss Keeldar, for one—felt their spirits stirred, not by violence, but by longing. Old Helstone, turning by chance, caught her gaze and laughed; she returned his smile.

“There’s no battle in sight,” he said, “and our country doesn’t need us to fight. No tyrant threatens, no foe questions our freedom. There’s nothing to do. We’re merely taking a walk. Keep your hand on the reins, captain—and calm that fire within. It’s not needed. A pity, really.”

“Take your own advice, doctor,” Shirley replied lightly. Then, whispering to Caroline, she added, “I’ll borrow from imagination what reality won’t give me. We’re not soldiers—bloodshed isn’t my desire. But if we are, then we’re soldiers of faith. Time has rolled back centuries, and we’re pilgrims bound for Palestine. Yet even that feels too dreamlike. I need something stronger. Let us be Lowland Scots, following a Covenanting leader into the hills, hiding from persecutors. We know prayer may lead to battle—and if battle comes, we believe heaven awaits us. So we’d gladly spill our blood on the moss. That music… it wakes every fiber in me. My heart pounds—not with its usual quiet beat, but with a fierce, new strength. I almost crave danger—something worth defending: a cause, a land… or at least a lover.”

“Look, Shirley!” Caroline interrupted. “What’s that red speck above Stilbro’ Brow? You have sharper eyes than I. Turn your eagle gaze toward it.”

Shirley looked. “I see it,” she said, then paused. “There’s a line of red now. They’re soldiers—cavalry. Moving fast. Six of them. They’ll pass us—no, they’ve turned off to the right. They saw us and veered away. Where are they going?”

“Maybe just riding drills,” Caroline suggested.

“Perhaps,” Shirley murmured. “But we don’t see them anymore.”

Mr. Helstone stepped forward.

“We’ll take Royd Lane,” he announced. “It’s a shortcut to Nunnely Common.”

And so they entered the narrow lane. So tight was the path that only two could walk side by side without stepping into the ditches flanking each side. Halfway through, tension rose among the clerical leaders. Boultby adjusted his spectacles; Helstone gripped his Rehoboam tightly. The curates nudged each other. Mr. Hall turned to the ladies and smiled.

“What’s happening?” someone asked.

He pointed ahead with his staff. There, emerging from the opposite end, came another procession—also led by men in black, also followed by music.

“Is this our reflection?” Shirley asked. “Our ghostly double? Here’s a card turned up.”

“If you wanted a battle,” Caroline whispered, laughing, “you’re likely to get one—at least of glances.”

“They shall not pass!” the curates declared in unison. “We won’t yield!”

“Yield?” Helstone snapped, turning sharply. “Who speaks of yielding? You boys—watch yourselves. The ladies, I trust, will stand firm. I know these women will defend the honor of the Church. What does Miss Keeldar say?”

“She asks what’s going on.”

“The Dissenters and Methodists—the Baptists, Independents, Wesleyans—all joined in unholy alliance, deliberately choosing this lane to block us and turn us back.”

“Rude,” Shirley declared. “And I hate rudeness. They’ll learn better.”

“A lesson in manners,” Mr. Hall suggested gently. “Not an example of insolence.”

Helstone marched on, quickening his pace. He advanced ahead of the group. Nearly upon the opposing leaders, a large, greasy man with flat-combed black hair called a halt. The procession stopped. He pulled out a hymn book, sang a verse, set a tune—and they began to chant a mournful psalm.

Helstone signaled to his musicians. Brass instruments blared forth, thunderous and bright. He ordered them to play *Rule, Britannia!* and instructed the children to sing along—so they did, with fervor and pride. The opposing voices faltered. Their song was drowned. In sound alone, they were defeated.

“Now, follow me!” Helstone commanded. “Not running—but steady, brisk, unwavering. Stay together. Hold onto each other’s skirts if need be.”

He strode forward with purpose, and his followers obeyed exactly—neither rushing nor stumbling, but moving with cool, united force. The curates, caught between Helstone and Miss Keeldar, had no choice but to keep pace. Both watched closely, ready to correct any misstep—one with cane, the other with parasol. The Dissenters, first stunned, then alarmed, were pushed back, overwhelmed, and finally forced to retreat, leaving the lane clear. Boultby struggled during the clash, but Helstone and Malone supported him, bringing him through whole, though breathless.

The fat man who had led the hymn was left sitting in the ditch. A spirit merchant by trade and leader of the Nonconformists, he reportedly drank more water that afternoon than he had in a year. Mr. Hall had protected Caroline, and she had returned the favor. Later, he and Miss Ainley exchanged quiet thoughts about the encounter. When the entire party passed safely through, Miss Keeldar and Mr. Helstone shook hands warmly. The curates began to cheer—but Helstone silenced them quickly. “You never know when to speak,” he said. “Best to stay silent. This wasn’t your doing.”

By half-past three, they turned back. At four, they returned to the starting point. Long benches lined the freshly mowed fields around the school. Children sat in rows. Great baskets covered in white cloths and smoking tin vessels were brought out. Before food was served, Mr. Hall offered a brief grace, which the children sang with sweet, clear voices—melodious and touching in the open air. Large currant buns and hot, sweet tea were distributed generously. No child was to receive less than twice what they could eat—so there would be leftovers to take home for those too old, sick, or unable to attend. Buns and beer flowed freely among the musicians and singers. Afterward, benches were removed, and games began.

A bell summoned teachers, patrons, and guests to the schoolroom. Miss Keeldar, Miss Helstone, and others were already inside, arranging trays and tables. Most of the neighborhood’s female servants, along with wives of clerks, singers, and musicians, had been enlisted as waitresses. Each vied in neatness and elegance, many young ones appearing strikingly beautiful. A dozen cut bread and butter; another dozen fetched hot water from the rector’s kitchen. Flowers and evergreens adorned the white walls. Silver teapots and fine porcelain sparkled on tables. People moved about—active figures, cheerful faces, bright dresses—creating a lively, joyful scene. Everyone chatted softly but happily, while canary birds sang shrilly from high cages.

Caroline, as the rector’s niece, took her place at one of the three main tables. Mrs. Boultby and Margaret Hall presided over the others. These tables hosted the elite—equality not being fashionable in Briarfield. Miss Helstone removed her bonnet and scarf, feeling the heat. Her long curls fell across her neck like a veil. Her muslin dress, modest as a nun’s robe, allowed her to go without a shawl.

The room filled. Mr. Hall stood beside Caroline, who now whispered observations about the day’s events. He seemed grave about the incident in Royd Lane, and she tried to lighten his mood. Miss Keeldar sat nearby—unusually quiet, still, watching everything. She appeared anxious that no one sit beside her. She spread her satin dress wide, placed her gloves and embroidered handkerchief on the bench, as if claiming territory. Caroline noticed this at last and asked whom she expected.

Shirley leaned close, her lips nearly brushing her ear, and whispered in a soft, musical tone—often used when her words touched a tender secret—“I expect Mr. Moore. I saw him last night. He promised to come with his sister and sit here. I’m certain he won’t fail me—but I fear he’ll arrive late, and we’ll be separated. Look—another group is coming. Every seat will be taken. Frustrating!”

Indeed, Mr. Wynne, the magistrate, arrived with his wife, son, and daughters—Briarfield gentry. Naturally, they claimed the first table. They filled all remaining seats. Mr. Sam Wynne, who had been keeping watch over the empty space Miss Keeldar had reserved, planted himself firmly on her gown, gloves, and handkerchief. He was one of her greatest dislikes—especially since he had shown serious interest in marrying her. The old gentleman had once publicly declared that Fieldhead and De Walden estates were delightfully “contagious”—a phrase that rumor had repeated often enough to reach Shirley.

Caroline’s ears still rang with that whisper: “I expect Mr. Moore.” Her heart still thumped, her cheeks still burned, when a note from the organ rang above the chatter. Dr. Boultby, Mr. Helstone, and Mr. Hall rose—everyone else followed. Grace was sung with music, and then tea began. Caroline was busy serving, but after the final cup was poured, she glanced around restlessly. Several people still stood without seats. Among them, she recognized Miss Mann—her spinster friend, drawn out of solitude by the weather or a persuasive companion. Miss Mann looked tired. A woman in a yellow bonnet brought her a chair. Caroline knew that hat, that black hair, that kind yet opinionated face beneath it, that black silk dress, even that gray linen shawl. She knew Hortense Moore. And suddenly, she wanted to leap up and run to her—to embrace her, not just for her sake, but for her brother’s too. She half rose, gasping softly—perhaps she would have dashed across the room—but a hand pulled her back. A voice behind her whispered, “Wait until after tea, Lina. Then I’ll bring her to you.”

When she could look again, Robert Moore stood directly behind her, smiling at her eagerness. He looked better than she had ever seen him—handsome, radiant, so vivid that she dared not look twice. His image flashed before her like lightning captured on film, burning itself into memory.

He moved on, speaking to Miss Keeldar. Shirley, annoyed by Sam Wynne’s unwanted attention—and by the fact that he still sat on her belongings—was clearly irritated. Moore’s lateness didn’t help. She shrugged, then muttered a sharp word about his “intolerable delay.” Moore neither apologized nor responded. He stood quietly nearby, waiting to see if she would recover. Within minutes, she did—offering him her hand. He took it, smiling—half apologetic, half grateful. A slight tilt of the head conveyed the former; perhaps a gentle squeeze, the latter.

“You may sit wherever you can now, Mr. Moore,” Shirley said, smiling. “There’s no room here—but plenty at Mrs. Boultby’s table, between Miss Armitage and Miss Birtwhistle. Go! John Sykes will be your neighbor, and you’ll sit with your back to us.”

Moore, however, lingered. He wandered down the length of the room, exchanging greetings with other standing gentlemen, but always returned to Shirley—bringing whispers each time.

Meanwhile, poor Sam Wynne looked increasingly uncomfortable. His neighbor seemed restless—fanning herself, complaining of heat and crowding. She declared that people should leave the tables after tea and warned she might faint if things continued. He offered to escort her outside—just the way to kill her with cold, she insisted. Eventually, he decided to retreat.

Moore was far away, deep in conversation with Christopher Sykes. Timothy Ramsden, Esq., a large corn-factor, stepped forward instead. Shirley acted swiftly. With a sweep of her scarf, she knocked over her teacup—spilling tea onto the bench and her satin dress. A waiter was summoned. Mr. Ramsden, stout and puffy, kept his distance. Shirley, usually careless about such accidents, made a fuss—overreacting like the most delicate woman. Ramsden opened his mouth, stepped back slowly, and as Miss Keeldar threatened to faint, he turned and fled.

At last, Moore returned. Surveying the chaos with calm amusement, he remarked that this end of the room was indeed the hottest—only suitable for cool tempers like his own. Then, dismissing the commotion, he settled into the seat destiny had marked. Shirley relaxed. Her features softened—the furrowed brow smoothed, the stubborn curve of her lips straightened. The wilfulness and mischief vanished, replaced by calm. All the restless gestures that had tormented Sam Wynne ceased—as if by magic. Still, she gave Moore no warm glance. Instead, she accused him of causing her great trouble and blamed him for losing Mr. Ramsden’s respect and Mr. Sam Wynne’s friendship.

“I wouldn’t have offended either man for the world,” she insisted. “I’ve always treated them with utmost respect. But thanks to you—look how they’ve been treated! I won’t be happy until I make amends. I must visit Royd corn-mill tomorrow to soothe the miller and praise the grain. Then I’ll call at De Walden—though I hate going there—and bring half an oatcake for Mr. Sam’s favorite dogs.”

“You know the surest way to win any man’s heart,” Moore said quietly. He seemed content at last to be where he was. He made no grand speech, offered no apology. His calm suited him perfectly—it made him look nobler, his presence more soothing. One wouldn’t guess, looking at him, that he was a struggling man seated beside a wealthy woman. Equality radiated from him—perhaps it lived within. Occasionally, when he looked down at Miss Keeldar, one might think his rank surpassed hers, just as his height did. Stern light sometimes flickered across his brow, gleamed in his eyes.

Their talk grew animated, though low. She pressed him with questions—he refused to satisfy her curiosity. Once, she sought his gaze. In her eyes, soft yet eager, she pleaded for truth. Moore smiled pleasantly—but said nothing. Then she turned away, annoyed. But within minutes, he drew her back. He seemed making promises—promises she accepted, though they weren’t answers.

The heat proved too much for Miss Helstone. She grew paler as tea dragged on. As soon as thanks were given, she left the table and hurried after her cousin Hortense, who had already gone outside with Miss Mann.

Robert Moore rose as well—perhaps to speak to her—but he had one last word with Miss Keeldar. By the time he finished, Caroline was gone.

Hortense greeted her former pupil with dignity, not warmth. She had been deeply offended by Mr. Helstone’s actions and blamed Caroline for obeying her uncle too strictly.

“You’re quite a stranger,” she said sternly, as Caroline held her hand.

Caroline knew her well enough not to protest. She let the formality pass, trusting that Hortense’s natural kindness—*bonté*, a word that means neither mere goodness nor cheerfulness, but something deeper—would prevail. It did. Upon seeing Caroline’s thin face, her expression softened. She kissed her on both cheeks and asked anxiously about her health. Caroline answered cheerfully. Had Miss Mann not interrupted, asking to be escorted home, Hortense might have launched into a lengthy interrogation and lecture. But the invalid was already weary—too tired to be polite. Caroline’s white dress and bright demeanor displeased her. Miss Mann preferred brown cloth and quiet melancholy. She barely recognized her young friend and left with a cool nod. Hortense, having promised to accompany her, departed with her.

Caroline now searched for Shirley. She found her—rainbow scarf and purple dress—surrounded by familiar ladies, though ones she usually avoided. Shyer than usual, she lacked courage to join them. Yet she couldn’t stand alone. So she approached a group of her older students—girls, really young women—who watched younger children playing blind-man’s buff.

Miss Helstone knew these girls liked her, yet she remained shy even with them. They respected her quietness. They gathered round her, not to dominate, but to protect. They admired her wisdom in class, her gentleness in life. Because she was wise and good when teaching, they overlooked her timidity when not. They didn’t exploit it. Though peasants, they possessed English sensibility. They stood politely, kindly receiving her fleeting smiles and rushed attempts at conversation—with grace born of goodwill—which soon eased her unease.

Mr. Sam Wynne rushed over, insisting the older girls join the game. Caroline was left alone again. She considered retreating to the house—when Shirley, spotting her isolation from afar, hurried to her side.

“Let’s go to the top of the fields,” she said. “I know you don’t like crowds, Caroline.”

“But it would mean taking you from all these fine people who adore your company—and who you charm so easily.”

Shirley smiled. “That’s why I’m choosing you.”
