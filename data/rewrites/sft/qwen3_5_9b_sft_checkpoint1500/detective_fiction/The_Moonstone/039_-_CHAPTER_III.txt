I retain only the haziest fragments of what transpired at Hotherstone's Farm. My memory is a blur of a fervent welcome, a supper so vast it seemed intended to sustain a whole eastern village, and a chamber scrubbed so immaculately that the sole blemish was that execrable artifact of ancestral madness: the feather-bed. I recall a night spent tossing, striking matches with frantic regularity, watching a single, flickering candle struggle against the encroaching gloom, and finally, a profound, physical relief when dawn broke, promising the possibility of rising.

That evening, arrangements had been made with Betteredge for me to summon him en route to Cobb's Hole, whenever I chose. Driven by an insatiable impatience to secure Rosanna Spearman's letter, "whenever" equated to "as soon as possible." Without pausing for breakfast, clutching a crust of bread, I departed, harboring a genuine apprehension that I might find the excellent Betteredge still buried beneath his covers. To my astonishment, he proved to be as agitated as I, standing ready with his walking stick in hand.

"How do you fare this morn, Betteredge?"

"Poorly, sir."

"I'm sorry. Pray, what ailment troubles you?"

"A fresh affliction, Mr. Franklin, born of my own devising. I shan't frighten you, though you'll surely contract it ere noon. Do you feel a gnawing heat at the pit of your stomach, sir? A dull, persistent pounding atop your skull? Ah? Not yet? It will seize you at Cobb's Hole, I warrant. I christen it 'Detective-Fever.' I first contracted it whilst sitting beside Sergeant Cuff."

"The devil take it!" I cried. "Then the cure must be to unseal Rosanna Spearman's letter, yes? Come, let us retrieve it."

Though it was early, the fisherman's wife, Mrs. Yolland, was already stirring in her kitchen. Upon my introduction by Betteredge, she performed a courtesy strictly reserved, I later learned, for strangers of distinction. She set down a bottle of Dutch gin and two pristine pipes upon the table, breaking the silence with, "Any tidings from London, sir?"

Before I could formulate an answer to this sweeping query, a figure emerged from the shadows of the corner. A wan, wild girl, her hair of remarkable beauty framing a face etched with fierce intensity, approached the table where I sat, leaning heavily upon a crutch. Her gaze locked onto mine with a mixture of fascination and horror that paralyzed me.

"Mr. Betteredge," she intoned, refusing to break eye contact, "repeat his name, if you will."

"This gentleman's name," Betteredge replied, stressing the word *gentleman* with distinct emphasis, "is Mr. Franklin Blake."

Instantly, the girl spun about and fled the room. Mrs. Yolland offered some vague apology for her daughter's peculiar demeanor, likely translated by Betteredge into something more palatable for polite society. I offer my own account of this exchange with total uncertainty; my attention was wholly consumed by the rhythmic clatter of the crutch. *Thump-thump* up the wooden stairs; *thump-thump* across the floor above; *thump-thump* descending again—and there stood the apparition at the threshold, a letter clutched in her hand, beckoning me outside.

Leaving behind a flurry of apologies, I followed this strange creature. She limped ahead, accelerating with every step, leading me down the sloping beach. She guided me behind a cluster of boats, shielding us from the prying eyes of the fishing village, and halted, turning to face me for the first time.

"Stand there," she commanded. "I wish to scrutinize you."

Her expression brooked no ambiguity. She radiated a potent cocktail of abhorrence and revulsion. Let me not boast that no woman had ever gazed upon me with such disdain before. I can safely assert, with greater humility, that no woman had ever allowed me to witness it. Under such scrutiny, there is a limit to endurance. I attempted to divert Limping Lucy's attention to some less repulsive feature of my countenance.

"I suspect you bear a letter for me," I ventured. "Is it the one in your hand?"

"Say that again," was her sole response.

I repeated the words, mimicking a dutiful pupil absorbing a lesson.

"No," she murmured, her voice dropping to a whisper as she stared mercilessly at me. "I cannot comprehend what she saw in his visage. I cannot divine what she heard in his tone." Suddenly, she cast her gaze aside, resting her weary head upon the top of her crutch. "Oh, my poor dear!" she whispered, the first soft note I had heard from her lips. "Oh, my lost darling! What could you possibly see in this man?" She lifted her head with a renewed ferocity, fixing me once more. "Are you capable of eating?"

I endeavored to maintain composure. "Yes."

"And sleeping?"

"Yes."

"When you behold a destitute maid in servitude, do you feel no pang of remorse?"

"Certainly not. Why should I?"

Abruptly, she thrust the letter into my face.

"Take it!" she shrieked, her voice rising to a fever pitch. "I have never laid eyes upon you before. God Almighty forbid that I ever see you again!"

With those final, jagged words, she limped away at maximum speed. The obvious interpretation of her conduct, surely anticipated by everyone, was that she had lost her mind. Accepting this conclusion, I turned my focus to the contents of Rosanna Spearman's missive. The address read: *"For Franklin Blake, Esq. To be delivered into his own hands (and trusted to no other), by Lucy Yolland."*

Breaking the seal revealed an envelope containing a letter, inside of which lay a slip of paper. I read the letter first:

*"Sir,—If you are curious regarding the meaning of my behavior towards you during your stay in the house of my mistress, Lady Verinder, perform the instructions detailed in the enclosed memorandum. Execute them without any observer present. Your humble servant,*

Turning to the slip, I copied the words verbatim:

*"Memorandum: Proceed to the Shivering Sand at the turn of the tide. Walk out upon the South Spit until the South Spit Beacon and the flagstaff at the Coast-guard station above Cobb's Hole align in a straight line. Lay down a stick, or any straight object, upon the rocks, precisely along the line connecting the beacon and the flagstaff. Ensure care that one extremity of the stick rests at the edge of the rocks, overlooking the quicksand. Trace along the stick, amidst the seaweed (commencing from the end pointing toward the beacon), seeking the Chain. Once located, run your hand along the Chain until you reach the segment stretching over the edge of the rocks, down into the quicksand. **AND THEN, PULL THE CHAIN.**"*

Barely had I finished reading the final, underlined words when I heard Betteredge's voice behind me. The architect of Detective-Fever had fully succumbed to its grip. "I can bear it no longer, Mr. Franklin," he gasped. "What does her letter proclaim? For heaven's sake, sir, tell us! What does it say?"

I handed him the letter and the memorandum. He scanned the letter without apparent interest, but the memorandum struck him deeply.

"The Sergeant spoke of it!" Betteredge cried. "From start to finish, sir, the Sergeant maintained she possessed a memorandum of the hiding place. And here it lies! Lord deliver us, Mr. Franklin, the secret that baffled every soul from Great Cuff downward is ready and waiting to reveal itself to *you!* It is ebb time, sir, as any fool can see. How long 'til the tide turns?" He glanced upward, spotting a boy mending nets at a distance. "Tammie Bright!" he bellowed.

"I hear ye!" Tammie shouted back.

"When do the tides turn?"

"In an hour's time."

Both men consulted their watches.

"We may skirt the coast, Mr. Franklin," suggested Betteredge. "Reach the quicksand with ample leisure. What say you, sir?"

"Lead on!"

En route to the Shivering Sand, I appealed to Betteredge to jog my memory regarding Rosanna Spearman during the period of Sergeant Cuff's investigation. With his assistance, the sequence of events quickly crystallized in my mind: Rosanna's journey to Frizinghall, coinciding with the household's belief that she lay ill in her chambers; her mysterious nocturnal activities, door locked, candle burning till morning; her suspicious acquisition of the japanned tin case and two dog-chains from Mrs. Yolland; the Sergeant's firm conviction that Rosanna had concealed something at the Shivering Sand, coupled with his total ignorance of its nature. All these bizarre outcomes of the abortive inquiry into the Moonstone's disappearance were vividly restored as we reached the quicksand and traversed the low rocky ledge known as the South Spit.

Guided by Betteredge, I positioned myself to view the Beacon and the Coast-guard flagstaff in alignment. Following the memorandum, we laid my stick upon the uneven terrain of the rocks with careful precision. We checked our watches anew.
