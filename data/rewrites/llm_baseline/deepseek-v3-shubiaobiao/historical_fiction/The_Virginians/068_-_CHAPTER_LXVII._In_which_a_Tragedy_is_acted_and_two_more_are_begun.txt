James Wolfe, the newly appointed colonel of Harry’s regiment, returned from America shortly after the young Virginian had joined its ranks. Wolfe, already celebrated for his valor at Cape Breton, carried with him the aura of a man destined for greatness. The nation, starved for military glory, watched him keenly, as did the ministers who hoped he might deliver it.

Harry, sincere in his admiration, paid his respects to the new commander, while in London, the bonds of friendship between Wolfe, Lambert, and George Warrington grew ever stronger. Wolfe, with the frankness of a born hero, spoke freely of his campaigns, his ambitions, and even his romantic longings. He was a man of both sword and pen, as enamored with poetry as with battle—a kindred spirit to George, who found in him not only a soldier but a philosopher.

The success of George’s tragedy, *Carpezan*, became the talk of London. Rejected by Garrick in favor of another work, it found a home at Covent Garden under Rich’s management. The cast was brilliant: Barry’s tormented Carpezan, Shuter’s noble gravitas, and the striking young Hagan as the King of Bohemia. Mrs. Woffington, though perhaps too mature for the heroine, delivered a death scene so harrowing that the audience gasped as one.

On the night of the premiere, George hosted a gathering at the Bedford Head before his friends departed for the theatre, leaving him in nervous solitude. Reports arrived in bursts—applause here, a stumble there—until, at last, the messenger came: the play was a triumph. Barry announced its repetition to thunderous cheers, declaring it the work of a young Virginian, a debut to remember.

Theo Lambert, pale with suspense through the performance, flushed with pride at its acclaim. Harry, nearly fainting in the fourth act, roared louder than any when the curtain fell. Even Hetty, feigning indifference, could not suppress her delight. The Lamberts, Wolfe, and Sampson led the ovations, while Gumbo, weeping openly in the gallery, proved the most vocal critic of all.

Supper that night was a raucous affair. Actors, officers, and friends toasted George’s success—none more effusively than Hagan, who, emboldened by wine, pledged undying devotion to Lady Maria in terms so florid they drew side-eyes from both Lord Castlewood and George. The evening ended with Carpezan himself being carried home, the King of Hungary nursing a royal headache, and the poet discovering, to his horror, that he had invited half the party to repeat the revel the next night.

The morning after brought quieter joys. Mrs. Lambert fretted over the dangers of actresses, to which George replied that his heart was safely guarded. Theo, blissfully unaware of such perils, saw only the play’s beauty. Hetty, ever sharp, rolled her eyes but later embraced her sister with fierce tenderness.

Yet shadows loomed. Madam Esmond’s letter, though granting reluctant consent, carried a tone of resignation rather than joy. She spoke of Virginia’s lands but not of settlements, leaving Lambert uneasy. George, ever optimistic, swore his pen would provide—had he not already earned three hundred pounds?

Sir Miles Warrington’s disapproval arrived like a thunderclap. Penniless! Reckless! Had the boy no shame? The General and Mrs. Lambert were painted as schemers, their daughter a fortune-hunter. Sir Miles’s letters to Virginia painted George as a wastrel, Harry the sole redeemer of the family name.

Yet George, indifferent to their scorn, walked through London with his head high. He took young Miley to the theatre, sent tickets to his disapproving relatives (who took them, albeit with sneers), and laughed at their hypocrisy when the play won royal approval.

For now, the future was bright. Let the uncles rage. George had his pen, his friends, and—most of all—Theo. The curtain had risen on his life’s great act, and he would play his part to the end.
