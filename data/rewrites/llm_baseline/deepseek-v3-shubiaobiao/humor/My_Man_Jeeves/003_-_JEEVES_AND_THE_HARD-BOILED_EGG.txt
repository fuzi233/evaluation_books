One of life’s great mysteries, as I sip my morning tea and observe Jeeves in his natural habitat—tidying studs, arranging shirts, and generally making the world a better-dressed place—is how I’d manage if the fellow ever decided to hand in his notice. In London, the threat was constant: a swarm of predatory aristocrats lurked in the shadows, waving blank checks and whispering sweet nothings about superior trouser-pressing. Reggie Foljambe once tried to poach him with double wages, and Alistair Bingham-Reeves’ valet gazed upon Jeeves with the sort of hunger usually reserved for rare steak. Pirates, the lot of them.

But I digress. The point is, Jeeves is unnervingly competent. Even the way he inserts a shirt stud suggests a man who could, if pressed, solve world hunger between breakfast and lunch. So when dear old Bicky Bickersteth came hurtling into my life, knees-deep in familial bouillon, I knew Jeeves would sort it.

The crisis unfolded thus: Bicky’s uncle, the Duke of Chiswick—a man so thrifty he’d haggle with a lamppost—had booted him across the Atlantic with instructions to "learn ranching" in Colorado. Bicky, whose idea of animal husbandry was avoiding horses (vicious biters, the lot), had instead taken up residence in New York, fibbing to the duke about a lucrative "business wheeze." All was well until the duke announced his impending visit, forcing Bicky to either produce a ranch or a convincing lie.

Enter Jeeves, stage left.

“Might I suggest, sir,” he murmured, “that you lend Mr. Bickersteth this flat? His grace could be led to believe it is his nephew’s own establishment, with myself as Mr. Bickersteth’s valet. You would, of course, be merely a guest.”

A stroke of genius. The duke arrived, took one look at the lavish surroundings, and promptly canceled Bicky’s allowance on the grounds that "the boy was clearly rolling in it." Disaster.

Bicky, now facing financial ruin, briefly considered chicken farming (“Eggs, Bertie! Twenty-five cents per seven!”) before Jeeves unveiled Plan B: monetizing the duke himself.

“The American public, sir,” Jeeves explained, “has a peculiar enthusiasm for shaking hands with prominent personages. For a modest fee, say two or three dollars, one might arrange introductions.”

Bicky was skeptical. “You mean to tell me people would pay just to *meet* my uncle?”

“Undoubtedly, sir. My own aunt once paid five shillings to host a moving-picture actor for tea.”

Thus, the scheme was born. Unfortunately, the market proved sluggish. Our sole investor was a delicatessen owner who offered payment in ham. Then, salvation: Jeeves secured *eighty-seven* eager handshake enthusiasts from Birdsburg, Missouri.

How? I never asked. With Jeeves, it’s best not to question the machinery—just admire the results.

The duke, blissfully unaware he’d become a tourist attraction, shook hands with the masses. Bicky’s finances were restored. And I? I learned two valuable lessons:

1. Never underestimate the American appetite for nobility.
2. Always let Jeeves handle the details.

The moustache, by the way, was quietly retired. Some battles aren’t worth fighting.
