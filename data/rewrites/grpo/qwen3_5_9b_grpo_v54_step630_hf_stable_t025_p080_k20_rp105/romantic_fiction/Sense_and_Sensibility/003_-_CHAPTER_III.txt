Mrs. Dashwood lingered at Norland for several months, not because she relished the prospect of leaving, but because her heart was still too raw to bear the journey. Every familiar corner of the estate struck a fresh chord of sorrow, and she found herself unable to summon the strength to move until her spirits began to mend. Once her mind cleared of the paralyzing grief of recent loss, she grew impatient to depart, driven by a fierce desire to secure a home near Norland. To distance herself from the place where her husband had breathed his last was unthinkable. Yet, despite her earnest inquiries, she could find no dwelling that balanced her need for comfort with the strict financial prudence of her eldest daughter, Elinor. While Mrs. Dashwood might have welcomed a larger estate, Elinor's steady judgment rejected every option as too costly for their diminished income.

Throughout this period, Mrs. Dashwood held fast to a solemn promise made by her late husband regarding a legacy for his son, John. She trusted his word implicitly, just as he had trusted himself, finding solace in the hope it offered for her daughters' future. Though she privately believed that a sum far less than seven thousand pounds would suffice for her own comfortable retirement, she rejoiced for her children's sake and for the memory of her husband's integrity. She reproached herself for having previously underestimated John's capacity for generosity, a belief now dispelled by his attentive care for his widowed mother and sisters.

However, the shadow of Mrs. John Dashwood loomed large. The contempt Mrs. Dashwood had felt for her daughter-in-law upon first meeting had deepened over half a year of shared residence. Despite the bonds of politeness and maternal duty, the two women seemed destined to clash. Yet, a fortuitous circumstance arose that made their continued proximity at Norland seem not only bearable but desirable. This was the blossoming romance between Elinor and Edward Ferrars, the brother of Mrs. John Dashwood.

Introduced shortly after his sister's arrival, Edward had become a constant presence at Norland. Some might have encouraged this match for financial gain, noting that Edward was the heir to a wealthy father, while others might have discouraged it due to the precarious nature of his fortune, which rested entirely on his mother's will. Mrs. Dashwood, however, cared for neither wealth nor caution. To her, it was sufficient that Edward possessed an amiable nature, that he loved Elinor, and that she returned his affection. She held the conviction that differences in fortune should never bar a union founded on mutual understanding and kindness.

Edward Ferrars lacked the striking features or polished manners that often captivate the eye. He was neither handsome nor naturally charismatic, possessing a diffidence that made him seem reserved to strangers. Yet, once his natural shyness melted away, he revealed a heart of genuine warmth and openness. His mind was sound and well-educated, though he lacked the ambition his mother and sister craved. They dreamed of seeing him rise to prominence, perhaps entering Parliament or associating with the elite, while Mrs. John Dashwood simply hoped to see him driving a fashionable carriage. Edward, however, possessed no interest in public life or grand displays; his contentment lay entirely in the quiet comfort of domestic life.

For weeks, Edward's quiet demeanor failed to capture Mrs. Dashwood's full attention, as she was consumed by her own sorrow. She merely noted his unobtrusive nature, appreciating that he did not intrude upon her grief with ill-timed conversation. It was Elinor who first drew her mother's deeper admiration, pointing out the stark contrast between Edward and his sister, Fanny.

"It is enough," Mrs. Dashwood declared one day, "to say that he is unlike Fanny. That implies everything amiable. I love him already."

"You will like him," Elinor replied gently, "once you know him better."

"Like him!" her mother smiled, her eyes brightening. "I feel no sentiment of approbation inferior to love."

"You may esteem him."

"I have never yet known what it was to separate esteem and love."

Determined to know him fully, Mrs. Dashwood made an effort to engage with Edward. Her own engaging manners quickly banished his reserve, allowing her to discern his true merits. Perhaps her belief in his regard for Elinor aided her perception, but she was genuinely convinced of his worth. Even his quiet manner, which initially seemed lacking in the social polish she expected, became endearing once she understood the warmth of his heart and the affectionate nature of his temper.

As soon as she perceived the signs of his love for Elinor, Mrs. Dashwood viewed their engagement as inevitable and their marriage as imminent.

"In a few months, my dear Marianne," she told her youngest daughter, "Elinor will, in all probability, be settled for life. We shall miss her dearly, but *she* will be happy."

"Oh, Mama, how shall we do without her?" Marianne cried.

"My love, it will be scarcely a separation. We shall live within a few miles of each other, meeting every day of our lives. You will gain a brother—a real, affectionate brother. I hold the highest opinion of Edward's heart." Seeing Marianne's somber expression, she added, "Do you disapprove your sister's choice?"

"Perhaps," Marianne admitted, "I may consider it with some surprise. Edward is very amiable, and I love him tenderly. But yet... he is not the kind of young man I expected. There is something wanting. His figure is not striking; it lacks the grace I should anticipate in a man who could seriously attach my sister. His eyes lack that spirit, that fire, which at once announce virtue and intelligence. Moreover, I fear he has no real taste. Music seems to hold little attraction for him, and while he admires Elinor's drawings, it is not the admiration of a connoisseur. It is evident, despite his frequent attention while she works, that he knows nothing of the matter. He admires as a lover, not as an artist. To satisfy me, those qualities must be united. I could not be happy with a man whose taste does not coincide with my own in every point. He must enter into all my feelings; the same books, the same music must charm us both. Oh, Mama, how spiritless and tame was Edward's manner when he read to us last night! I felt for my sister most severely. Yet she bore it with such composure, seeming scarcely to notice it. I could hardly keep my seat. To hear those beautiful lines, which have frequently almost driven me wild, pronounced with such impenetrable calmness, such dreadful indifference!"

"He would certainly have done more justice to simple and elegant prose," Mrs. Dashwood interjected. "I thought so at the time; but you *would* give him Cowper."

"Nay, Mama, if he is not to be animated by Cowper!—but we must allow for difference of taste. Elinor has not my feelings, and therefore she may overlook it and be happy with him. But it would have broken *my* heart, had I loved him, to hear him read with so little sensibility. Mama, the more I know of the world, the more I am convinced that I shall never see a man whom I can truly love. I require so much! He must possess all of Edward's virtues, yet his person and manners must ornament his goodness with every possible charm."

"Remember, my love, that you are not seventeen," her mother advised softly. "It is yet too early in life to despair of such happiness. Why should you be less fortunate than your mother? In one circumstance only, my Marianne, may your destiny be different from hers!"
