While Pen was in his own county laying out his little campaign as carefully as a man lays out sandwiches for a picnic—counting slices, saving the best for himself, and calling it “public spirit”—a message came that Lady Rockminster had reached Baymouth, and had brought with her Laura.

At the name of Laura, his sister, so near at hand, Pen’s fine plans began to taste in his mouth like a spoonful of ink. He had wanted—more than he owned—to stand high in her esteem. She was, as it were, his mother’s bequest, wrapped up in brown paper and sealed with duty: to be protected, guided, provided for; and he, the handsome gentleman with his political prospects and practised smiles, suddenly felt like a boy caught with jam on his fingers.

How should he speak to her of Blanche? How should he explain the respectable trade he was driving—honour for money, affection for advancement, conscience for a seat? He fancied Laura’s quiet clear eyes as a sort of daylight that shows dust you didn’t know was there. He wrote at once to Baymouth a letter full of polished phrases, affectionate protestations, and that easy raillery with which he always tried to make seriousness seem like a joke; but between the lines he felt a panic, and had the disagreeable sense that he was dressing up a lie and calling it good society.

It was absurd, to be sure, that a simple country girl should be able to frighten a man who could talk down half a club and smile through any awkwardness. Yet his worldly diplomacy—so fit for committees and drawing-rooms—could not stand her plain truth. He rode from Clavering to Baymouth with the guilty feeling of a schoolboy who knows he has not learned his lesson and hears, in imagination, the master’s step in the corridor. Truth, after all, is a master who always has the book.

In the year since Helen’s death, Laura had lived under the charge of Lady Rockminster—kind, tyrannical, capricious, and absolute, like an old cook who loves you but will still rap your knuckles if you touch the sauce. Laura had seen the world, learned its little graces, picked up accomplishments as one picks up foreign words and ribbons; and she had endured what a tender home-bred girl must endure when she is suddenly set down among people who do not think it their chief business to be gentle.

Helen’s love had been so watchful that the poor widow seemed to hover about Laura like a warm shawl: if Laura’s head ached, the whole house must know it; if she sighed, the very clock was expected to go softly. That anxious heart had stopped; and Laura, deprived at once of her mother and her mother’s perpetual protection, had had to stand alone among strangers who were not bound to care whether she felt hurt or not.

She was not quite the equal, in rank, of her patroness and that patroness’s friends. Some slighted her; some were cold; servants, accustomed to Lady Rockminster’s way of speaking as though the world were a set of drawers to be banged, could be rude in imitation. Lady Rockminster herself was often sharp enough to cut a loaf without a knife. Laura found herself sometimes in family gatherings where the intimacy paused, as if a door had been opened upon company not meant to be admitted; and her sensitive mind suffered from the fear that her presence was an intrusion.

Yet, with a cheerful philosophy that had the good sense of a woman who can make dinner out of very little, she compared her lot with that of governesses and dependent girls who must sell their time and their dignity by the hour; and she said to herself that she, at least, was among people who were, in their fashion, kind. So she adapted herself, and went forward with a trusting smile—as if Fortune were a neighbour met on the road, to be greeted civilly instead of suspected.

Fortune, like many a human creature, has a weakness for those who do not cringe and do not bargain. Laura had that rare gift—more precious than jewels, and harder to counterfeit than lace—of liking people and making people like her. She carried it with her as quietly as she carried the little cross Pen had given her; and it served her better than any diamond, lighting dark corners and softening sour tempers.

Before she had been a year in Lady Rockminster’s household, there was scarcely a soul in it that did not belong to her. The old lady’s woman—who had endured forty years of scolding, clawing, and daily criticism, as some kettles endure the fire and come out black—had begun, as usual, by being angry with the new companion, as she had been with the fifteen before. But when Laura fell ill in Paris, that same sour veteran nursed her in defiance of her mistress (who feared fever as she feared bad French cookery), and fought with Martha from Fairoaks—now promoted to be Miss Laura’s maid—for the right to administer medicines.

And in the midst of this domestic campaign rose Grandjean, the chef, who behaved as if Laura’s recovery were a matter of national importance and depended entirely on his saucepans. He would have killed her, if affection could do it, with delicacies: broths clarified till they looked like amber; little birds and cutlets, dressed as tenderly as if they were infants; jellies trembling with too much zeal. When she ate her first slice of chicken, the man actually wept, as though the fowl had been a lost relative restored.

The Swiss major-domo celebrated her praises in every language he spoke—none of them correctly, but all of them earnestly. The coachman was proud to drive her. The page cried when she was ill. Calverley and Coldstream, those two huge footmen who usually looked as if nothing short of an earthquake could stir them, became hilariously unsteady with joy at the news of her convalescence, and celebrated it by getting the page drunk in a wine-shop, to do honour to Miss Bell’s health.

Even Lady Diana Pynsent—Mr. Pynsent having married her by this time—who had once nursed a considerable dislike to Laura, condescended to declare Miss Bell “very agreeable,” and grandmamma’s “trouvaille.” Laura had won all this, not by arts and flattery, but by that simple good-nature which is like good bread: everybody is better tempered after they’ve had a slice.

Pen, remembering Lady Rockminster’s former brusqueries to himself, arrived at Baymouth expecting to find Laura a humble dependent, cowed and overlooked. Instead, when she heard he was come, she ran down the stairs; and if she did not embrace him in the presence of Calverley and Coldstream, it was only because the laws of gravity and decorum happened to be passing through the hall. Those two giants would have watched any event—embrace or beheading—with equal serenity, without so much as disturbing a grain of powder on their heads.

Laura looked so well that Pen was struck into admiration. Health had brightened her frank eyes; colour had come back to her cheek; and when she kissed him, the blush was as fresh as a rose in a country hedge. He had never thought her so beautiful. Why had he not thought of her beauty before? Why did the thought come now, with a tenderness that made him uncomfortable? He took her hand and kissed it with a fondness that surprised himself, and read in her clear gaze that unguarded welcome he had always been sure to find there. Her innocence smote him; he felt it as one feels the first cold splash of water on a guilty face.

“How good you are to me, Laura—sister,” he said. “I don’t deserve—that you should be so kind.”

“Mamma left you to me,” she answered, bending and brushing his forehead with her lips quickly. “You know our compact last year, before we parted: you were to come to me when you were in trouble, or to tell me when you were very happy. Which is it now? Are you very happy, or are you in trouble?” She looked at him with a bright arch kindness. “Do you like going into Parliament? Do you mean to distinguish yourself? How I shall tremble for your first speech!”

“Do you know about that plan, then?” Pen asked.

“Know? Everybody knows,” Laura said. “Lady Rockminster’s doctor talked of it to-day. I daresay it will be in the Chatteris paper to-morrow. It’s all over the county that Sir Francis Clavering is going to retire in favour of Mr. Arthur Pendennis of Fairoaks, and that the young and beautiful Miss Blanche Amory is—”

“What! that too?” Pen cried.

“That too, dear Arthur. Tout se sait,” said Laura, with a little Parisian air she had learned without losing her own. “I mean to be very fond of her. She must be clever and pretty. She has written me such a kind letter. She speaks so warmly of you! I hope—I know—she feels what she writes. When is it to be? Why didn’t you tell me? I may come and live with you then, mayn’t I?”

“My home is yours, dear Laura, and all I have,” Pen said, swallowing. “If I didn’t tell you, it was because—because nothing is decided yet. No words have passed.” Then, for want of honest words, he fell back on the sort of arrangement which a prudent man makes with furniture. “But you think Blanche could be happy with me—don’t you? Not a romantic fondness, you know. I don’t think I have a heart. I’ve told her so. Only a sober attachment—and I want my wife on one side of the fire and my sister on the other; Parliament in the session and Fairoaks in the holidays; and my Laura never to leave me—until somebody who has a right comes to take her away.”

Somebody with a right. Somebody with a right! As he said it slowly, looking at her, an absurd anger rose in him against this invisible claimant. A minute ago he had been anxious how she would take the news of Blanche; now he was hurt that she took it so easily, as though his happiness were an ordinary appointment in the diary.

“Until somebody comes,” Laura said, laughing, “I’ll stay at home and be Aunt Laura, and take care of the children when Blanche is in the world. I have arranged it all. I’m an excellent housekeeper. Do you know I’ve been to market in Paris with Mrs. Beck, and taken lessons from M. Grandjean? He showed me how to choose a fowl by the look of the skin and the feel of the breast; how to make a stock that smells like sunshine on bones; how to skim it till it’s clear as conscience ought to be. And I’ve had singing lessons too—out of the money you sent me, you kind boy—and I sing much better now. I’ve learned to dance—though not so well as Blanche. And when you become a minister of state, Blanche shall present me!” And, with provoking good humour, she made him the latest Parisian curtsey, as if in a drawing-room rather than in Lady Rockminster’s house at Baymouth.

Lady Rockminster entered at the exact moment of this curtsey, and gave Pen one finger to shake, which he took and bowed over as if it were a relic, though clumsily enough.

“So you are going to be married, sir,” said the old lady.

“Scold him for not telling us,” Laura cried, retreating; whereupon Lady Rockminster began instantly, as if scolding were the first course of dinner and she had an appetite.

“So you are going to marry, and go into Parliament instead of that good-for-nothing Sir Francis Clavering. I wanted him to give my grandson his seat. Why didn’t he give my grandson his seat? I hope you are to have a great deal of money with Miss Amory. I wouldn’t take her without a great deal.”

“Sir Francis is tired of Parliament,” Pen said, wincing, “and I—wish to attempt that career. The rest is—premature.”

“I wonder, when you had Laura at home, you could take up with such an affected little creature as that,” the dowager continued.

“I am sorry Miss Amory does not please your ladyship,” said Pen, smiling as men do when they are pinched.

“You mean it’s no affair of mine, and I’m not going to marry her. Well, I’m not, and I’m glad I’m not—odious little thing. When I think a man can prefer her to my Laura, I’ve no patience. And so I tell you, Mr. Arthur Pendennis.”

“I’m very glad you see Laura so favourably,” Pen said.

“You are very glad and very sorry. What does it matter? A young man who prefers Miss Amory to Miss Bell has no business to be glad or sorry. A young man who takes up with such a crooked lump of affectation—because she is crooked, I tell you she is—after seeing my Laura, has no right to hold up his head. Where is your friend Bluebeard? That tall young man—Warrington. Why doesn’t he come down and marry Laura? What do young men mean by not marrying such a girl? They all marry for money now. You are selfish and cowards. We ran away and made foolish matches in my time. I’ve no patience with you! In Paris this winter I asked the three attaches at the Embassy why they didn’t fall in love with Miss Bell. They laughed. They said they wanted money. You are all selfish. You are all cowards.”

“I hope, before you offered Miss Bell to the attaches,” Pen said, growing hot, “you did her the favour to consult her?”

“Miss Bell has only a little money. Miss Bell must marry soon. Somebody must make a match for her; and a girl can’t offer herself,” said the dowager, with the air of pronouncing a law of nature. Then, raising her voice: “Laura, my dear, I’ve been telling your cousin all young men are selfish, and there isn’t a pennyworth of romance left. He is as bad as the rest.”

Laura came back, smiling, but with a brightness that looked as if it had been carefully put on. She took Pen’s hand.

“Have you been asking Arthur why he won’t marry me?” she said. “He is going to marry somebody else; and I intend to be very fond of her, and to go and live with them—provided then he doesn’t ask every bachelor who comes to his house why he doesn’t marry me.”

Pen’s conscience, having expected reproaches and received none, settled down again like a timid creature that finds the lash is not lifted. His duty, and still more his inclination, led him constantly to Baymouth; where Lady Rockminster declared a place was always kept for him at her table.

“And I recommend you come often,” she said, “for Grandjean is an excellent cook; and being with Laura and me will do your manners good. It’s easy to see you are always thinking about yourself. Don’t blush and stammer. Almost all young men always think about themselves. My sons and grandsons did, until I cured them. Come here and learn to behave. You shan’t have to carve—that’s done at the side-table. Hecker will give you as much wine as is good for you; and when you are very good and amusing you shall have champagne. Hecker, mind: Mr. Pendennis is Miss Laura’s brother. Make him comfortable. See he doesn’t drink too much. And don’t let him disturb me while I take my nap after dinner. You are selfish, young man. I intend to cure you. Dine here when you have no other engagements; and if it rains, you’d better put up at the hotel.”

So long as she could command, she was not hard to please. All her little court trembled, and yet loved her; for there is something oddly comforting in a tyrant who is at least consistent.

Her society was not numerous or brilliant. The doctor was always there, faithful as his own pill-box. The vicar came; the curate; on public days the vicar’s wife and daughters and a few visitors were received. Generally it was a small table: good plate, better cookery, and conversation that went round in a narrower circle than the soup tureen.

After dinner Lady Rockminster retired to her doze, and Laura played and sang her to sleep; and Pen drank his wine alone, hearing the soft drift of music through the house, like a scented vapour—lavender and bergamot translated into sound. In that quiet domestic music there was no advertisement, no harshness; it did not clang like the new brass bands of public life. It was made for one tired old woman, in one room, in one house; and the very modesty of it seemed to protest against the world’s vulgar noise.

“If my music can give her a nap,” Laura said, “ought I not to be glad it can do so much good? Lady Rockminster sleeps very little at night. I used to read to her till I fell ill in Paris; since then she won’t hear of my sitting up.”

“Why didn’t you write to me when you were ill?” Pen asked, colouring.

“What good could you do me?” she said simply. “I had Martha to nurse me, and the doctor every day. You are too busy to write to women or think about them. You have your books, your newspapers, your politics, your railroads. I wrote when I was well.”

And Pen blushed again, remembering that while she had been ill—while Grandjean had been weeping over chicken and footmen were toasting her recovery—he had never written, and had scarcely thought of her at all.

Because she was his cousin and, in this household, his acknowledged belonging, Pen could walk and ride with her freely; and in those walks and rides he learned her again. In their mother’s lifetime she had never spoken so openly to him; Helen’s eager wish to unite her children had thrown a reserve over Laura’s manner, a modest guard against hopes she dared not encourage. Now there was no need. He was bound—so the county said—to another woman; and Laura became at once his sister: cheerful, dutiful, determined to hope for his prosperity, determined to do everything affection could do to make her mother’s darling happy.

They spoke often of their dead mother, and from a hundred little stories Laura told—how Helen had saved some trifle for him, how she had excused him, how she had prayed over him, how she had watched his letters and his silences—Pen came to understand what that devotion had been: constant, absorbing, silent, like a lamp kept burning in a window for a traveller who may never come home. It had ended only with Helen’s last breath.

One day the people at Clavering saw a lad holding two horses at the churchyard gate, and it was told through the place that Pen and Laura had gone together to Helen’s grave. Pen had been there once or twice since he came into the country; but alone, the sight of the stone had brought him no consolation. What right had he, he thought, to stand in that holy ground—he, a man bartering faith and honour for a fortune and a career; a speculator in life, making his bargain with open eyes? The quiet of the place only seemed to make his inner noise more jarring.

They rode by the gates of Fairoaks. Pen shook hands with his tenant’s children playing on the lawn and terrace. Laura looked steadily at the cottage wall, at the creeper on the porch, at the magnolia rising toward her old window. Later one of the boys told his mother: “Mr. Pendennis rode by to-day with a lady. He stopped and talked to us, and asked for a bit of honeysuckle off the porch and gave it her. I couldn’t see if she was pretty; she had her veil down. She was on one of Cramp’s horses, out of Baymouth.”

Across the downs between Baymouth and home they rode very close together, but Pen spoke little. He was thinking how men refuse happiness when it is offered, or, having it, kick it away; how they barter it, eyes open, for beggarly honours and worthless money. And then, with that sour cleverness which was his refuge, he told himself: what does it matter? The best and purest lives are consumed in desire and end in disappointment—like Helen’s, who had longed and planned, and died baulked. The stone covers hopes and memories. Our place knows us no more.

“Other people’s children are playing on the grass,” he said suddenly, in a hard voice, “where you and I used to play. And look how the magnolia we planted has grown. I went round to one or two cottages where my mother used to visit. It’s scarcely a year since she’s gone, and the people she benefited care no more for her death than for Queen Anne’s. We are all selfish. The world is selfish. There are a few exceptions—like you—to shine like good deeds in a naughty world, and only make the blackness more dismal.”

“I wish you would not speak so, Arthur,” Laura said, looking down, her fingers touching the honeysuckle on her breast as if its scent were a comfort she could hold. “When you told the little boy to give me this, you were not selfish.”

“A pretty sacrifice,” Pen said, with his bitter laugh, “to get it for you.”

“But your heart was kind when you did it,” she persisted. “One cannot ask for more than love and kindness; and if you think humbly of yourself, are they diminished? I often thought our dearest mother spoiled you at home by worshipping you; and that if you are—how I hate the word—what you say, her too great fondness helped to make you so. And as for the world—when men go into it, perhaps they cannot help being selfish. You have to fight, and get on, and make a name. Mamma and your uncle encouraged you in that ambition. If it is vain, why pursue it? I suppose such a clever man as you means to do good to the country by going into Parliament, or you would not wish to be there. What are you going to do in the House of Commons?”

“Women don’t understand politics, my dear,” Pen said; and even as he said it he sneered at himself for the cheapness of the answer.

“Then why don’t you make us understand?” Laura returned. “I could never tell why Mr. Pynsent should like it so much. He is not clever.”

“He certainly is not a genius,” Pen said.

“Lady Diana says he attends committees all day, then is at the House all night; that he always votes as he is told; that he never speaks; that he will never rise beyond a subordinate place; and his grandmother tells him he is choked with red-tape. Is that what you mean to do, Arthur? What is there so brilliant that you should be eager for it? I would rather you stayed at home and wrote books—good books, kind books, with gentle kind thoughts such as you have, dear Arthur, such as might do people good to read. And if you do not win fame, what then? You own it is vanity; you can live happily without it. I must not pretend to advise—but you speak so bitterly of the world, and say it is wicked and tires you. Why don’t you leave it?”

“And what would you have me do?” Pen asked.

“I would have you bring your wife to Fairoaks,” Laura said softly, “and live there, and study, and do good round about you. I would like to see your own children playing on the lawn, Arthur; and that we might pray in our mother’s church once more, dear brother. If the world is a temptation—are we not taught to pray that we may not be led into it?”
