The second I caught that deliberate, flicker of Xodar’s eyes darting upward, the whole ruse snapped into sharp focus. The black pirate had not been spinning that wild, meandering tale for entertainment. For miles he’d sensed his own people’s rescue creeping up behind us, stringing me along to keep my gaze fixed on his face rather than the sky. Another sixty seconds and their battleship would have hung directly overhead, their boarding party already swinging from the hull in their safety harnesses, poised to swarm our decks and snuff out the tiny, fragile spark of escape I’d clung to since fleeing the thern temples.

I was no novice to Barsoomian aerial combat, my reflexes honed as sharp on the flying skiffs of Mars as they had been on the cavalry charges of Earth’s Civil War. I slammed the main thrusters into full reverse without a second’s hesitation, and our tiny craft plummeted a sheer hundred feet in a single drop. Over my head I saw the dangling forms of the boarding party whoosh right past our former position, their shouts of surprise carrying thin on the cold upper air as the giant battleship raced unknowingly past us. I wrenched the speed lever all the way to its final notch, tilted the skiff’s nose at a 70-degree angle, and shot upward like a quarrel from a crossbow, driving our reinforced steel prow straight for the whirring, exposed propellers of the massive warship looming above. If I could shatter those rotors, the hulk would be dead in the sky for hours, and escape would be ours again.

At that exact instant, the red dawn of Barsoom crested the horizon, spilling blood light over the battleship’s steel stern, revealing a hundred grim, ebony faces peering over the rails down at us. A roar of rage ripped from a hundred throats, orders screamed across the deck, but it was already too late. Our prow crunched into the propeller housing with a shriek of rending metal, shattering three of the four giant rotors.
I reversed the engines the second the shock of impact jolted up my arms, but my steel nose was wedged tight in the gash I’d torn in the battleship’s stern. We hung there for barely a full second before the metal gave way, but that one second was all the black warriors needed. They poured over the gap in a flood, no room to swing a longsword, no space to parry, we were simply submerged under a tide of muscle and snarling fangs. Before a single blade could sink into my flesh, Xodar’s command cut through the din.
“Secure them. No harm.”

Several of the pirates had already cut Xodar free of his own bonds, and he oversaw my disarming personally, looping thick, woven Martian fibre rope around my wrists. He smiled at his handiwork, completely unaware that to my superhuman Earth-born strength, those “unbreakable” strands were no sturdier than cotton twine. Phaidor was bound next, then the two of us were lashed together with barely three feet of slack between our linked wrists. Our skiff was towed alongside the crippled battleship, and we were hauled up onto its main deck.

A thousand First Born warriors crewed the leviathan, their dark bodies packed tight along the rails as far as discipline would permit, all shoving for a glimpse of their captives. Phaidor’s beauty drew crude, vulgar jeers, unsoftened by any of the quiet chivalry I’d come to expect from the red men of Helium. My own thern-pale skin and close cropped black hair drew equal curiosity, and when Xodar told his fellow nobles how I’d fought off a dozen of their best men, and that the harness and metal medallions I wore had been stripped off a dead thern guard, they thronged around me with questions. The First Born were a uniformly handsome, powerfully built people, their officers’ harnesses crusted so thick with gold, platinum, and precious gems that no trace of the fine red leather beneath was visible. The captain’s harness was a single solid sheet of cut diamonds, blazing against his ebony skin like a fallen star. The whole scene hummed with barbaric splendor: the polished skeel-wood deck, the sorapus wood cabins inlaid with jewels and gold filigree, burnished golden handrails, the glint of massive projectile guns mounted along the gunwales.

Phaidor and I were dragged below decks, thrown into a tiny, windowless compartment with only one small porthole for light. The door slammed shut, the heavy iron bars clanking home behind our escort. We could hear the crew hammering at the broken propellers overhead, and through the porthole I watched the battleship drift lazily south. Neither of us spoke for a long while. My own mind raced to Tars Tarkas and Thuvia, still loose somewhere on the banks of the River Iss. Even if they eluded pursuit, the fugitives from the Valley Dor were marked for a swift, brutal death if captured by any red or green Barsoomian party. I thought of Dejah Thoris, of Tardos Mors, of the thousands of warriors who would follow me straight through the Gates of Iss itself if I could only reach Helium and expose the therns’ cruel deception. But right then, the odds of escaping the First Born felt thinner than the rarefied air at the top of Mars’ highest peaks.

I looked over at Phaidor, and caught her staring at me, a soft, unnameable expression on her face I had never seen before. When she realized I’d caught her, her white lids fluttered down, a faint rose flush tingeing her pale thern cheeks.
“Do you find the study of the lower orders so fascinating?” I laughed.
She looked up, a nervous, relieved little huff of laughter escaping her. “Oh, very much so. Especially when they have such excellent profiles.”

It was my turn to flush, but I only laughed harder, drawn to her bright wit even on the edge of certain death. She asked if I knew where we were bound, and I joked that we were heading for the supposed eternal paradise the therns promised all pilgrims of the Iss. She shuddered, and said her fate would be far worse. No thern princess stolen by the black pirates in all their centuries of raiding had ever returned to tell their tale.
“Is that not just retribution?” I challenged her. “Do the therns not do the same to all those poor pilgrims who volunteer to drift down the River of Mystery? Thuvia was held as your plaything, your slave, for fifteen years. Why should you not suffer the same cruelty you mete out to others?”
Phaidor stared at me, bewildered. “We therns are a holy race. It is an honor for a lesser creature to serve us. If we did not save some of those fools drifting down the Iss, every last one of them would be torn apart by plant men and white apes before they reached the Dor.” When I pressed her about the lie the therns had spread to the outer world, convincing every red man that paradise waited past the Iss, she shrugged as if it was the most obvious truth in the world. “All life on Barsoom exists only to serve the therns. Why else would we waste our time on labor?”
The words curdled in my throat. “It is true then that you eat human flesh?”
She looked at me with pity for my ignorance. “Lesser humans are the beasts that feed the gods. You eat the flesh of animals, do you not? The Holy Therns are the gods of Barsoom.”
Disgust coiled hot in my chest, but she leaned in, her cheeks flushing again. “You are an unbeliever now. But if we escape the pirates, and make it back to my father Matai Shang’s temple, we will find a way to convince you of the error of your ways. We can find a place for you among us. You can be ours.”
Her eyes dropped to the floor, and I was slow to parse what she meant—Dejah Thoris had always teased me that I was a complete simpleton when it came to soft emotions. I told her plainly that if I was a thern, the very first thing I would do was station armed guards at the mouth of the Iss, and escort every deluded pilgrim back to the outer world, before wiping out all the plant men and white apes of the Dor.
Phaidor went deathly white, horror written across her face. “Do not speak such sacrilege! Not even I would be able to save you from the tortures they would inflict if they heard you say such things.”

The cell door swung open then, and Xodar stepped inside, smiling, his dark face kind for once, stripped of its usual warrior’s grimness. “There is no use keeping you locked away. You cannot possibly escape. I will cut your bonds. Come up on deck. You will see no sight any First Born slave has ever laid eyes on: the subterranean entrance to our holy land, the real heaven of Barsoom.” He glanced at Phaidor, his grin sharpening. “You will get to see the Temple of Issus, too. Perhaps Issus will favor you enough to make you her personal handmaiden.”
Phaidor drew herself up, haughty again. “Blasphemy! Issus would wipe your entire accursed breed from the face of Mars if you ever dared step foot near her temple!”
Xodar laughed, ugly and sharp. “You have far more to learn, little thern. And you will not like the lessons.”

When we stepped onto the deck, I froze. As far as the eye could see stretched nothing but endless fields of ice and snow. We were above the south polar cap of Mars—the only spot on the entire planet where frozen water ever existed. No sign of life moved across the frozen expanse.
Xodar walked beside me as I leaned on the rail, pointing south. “We are heading straight for the Otz Valley. A great sunken bowl thousands of feet below the surrounding ice, ringed by the Otz Mountains that circle the inner Valley Dor. At its very centre lies the Lost Sea of Korus, and on its shores, the Golden Temple of Issus. That is our destination.” I realized then why only one soul in all the ages had ever escaped the Valley Dor. No one could cross that wind-scoured frozen wasteland on foot and live.
We passed the deep, gashed canyon of the River Iss, its waters flowing deep below the ice, then the neutral strip of valley between the ice wall and the mountains, dotted with tiny villages. Xodar explained those were the lost souls: pilgrims who’d climbed out of the Iss canyon, escaped thern slaves, trapped forever with nowhere to run, left alone by both the therns and the First Born to war amongst themselves in their tiny, isolated settlements.
Ahead of us, rising out of the ice, was a flat-topped, truncated cone of black basalt. The battleship slowed to a hover directly over its peak, and I gaped down at the mile-wide circular well bored straight through the rock, its bottom lost in inky, endless darkness. The ship began to sink, slow and steady, down into the pit. For half an hour we fell, until the battleship’s lights pierced the dark, and suddenly we broke through into the vast domed ceiling of an entire subterranean world.
A soft blue phosphorescence lit the scene below, illuminating a buried sea that stretched to the far edges of the dome, thousands of First Born warships bobbing on its gentle swells. Xodar laughed when he saw my shock. “This sea is larger than Korus. We pump the excess water back up through the rock into the northern reservoirs the red men draw their irrigation from. For eons they called it a miracle.” The secret of the Barsoomian water “miracle” clicked into place at last.
We passed tiny, heavily guarded islets dotted with roofless prison compounds, before pulling up to a larger island where a massive oval, roofless building sat on the shore. “You will meet Issus soon,” Xodar said to Phaidor, his grin turning cruel. “Her handmaidens never serve longer than one year.” The truth of what that meant hung unspoken in the air, and Phaidor clung tight to my arm, no longer the arrogant, holy thern princess, just a terrified young girl in the hands of merciless enemies.
We walked to the edge of the long, water-filled tank at the centre of the building, and saw a strange black craft floating in its centre. Xodar shouted a command, a hatch popped open on the vessel’s deck, and we climbed aboard. The hatches sealed shut, the engines purred to life, and the craft began to sink below the surface of the water. Phaidor’s fingers dug hard into my arm, every Martian’s ancient terror of deep water rising to the surface.
“Save me,” she whispered, pressing herself against my side. “Anything the Holy Therns can give you, it is yours. Phaidor—” she hesitated, her voice so soft I could barely hear it over the gurgle of water past the portholes, “Phaidor herself is yours.” She threw both her arms around my neck, and dragged my face down to hers.
