After the battle ended, Sir Henry and Good were carried into Twala’s hut, where I joined them. Both were utterly drained—exhausted from battle and blood loss—and my own state was little better. Though wiry and accustomed to hardship, I had reached the edge of endurance. The old wound from the lion attack throbbed anew, and a sharp headache from the blow I’d taken earlier in the day made every moment agony. We were three broken men, battered by war and fate. Yet even in our misery, we found solace in knowing we still lived when so many others did not.

With help from Foulata—the beautiful Kukuana girl whose life we had saved—she now served us as devoted handmaiden, especially tending to Good—we managed to remove our chain shirts. They had saved our lives that day, but beneath them lay deep bruises. The steel had blocked blades, but not impacts. Sir Henry and Good bore swollen, tender flesh; I too was marked with pain. Foulata brought us crushed green leaves, fragrant and soothing, which she applied as poultices. The relief was real, though temporary.

But worse than the bruises were the wounds. Good had a clean hole through his thigh—a gash that had bled heavily. Sir Henry bore a deep cut across his jaw, delivered by Twala’s axe. Fortunately, Good was no stranger to medicine. Once he retrieved his small kit, he cleaned both injuries under the dim glow of a primitive lamp, then stitched them carefully. He coated the wounds with antiseptic ointment and wrapped them in what remained of a handkerchief. Then came broth—strong and nourishing—prepared by Foulata. Too weak to eat properly, we drank it down and collapsed onto the thick fur rugs scattered across the hut floor.

And there, on Twala’s own couch, wrapped in his royal karross, Sir Henry slept—the man who had slain the king. But sleep was elusive. The air rang with sorrow: women wailed from every direction, mourning husbands, sons, brothers lost in the slaughter. Over twelve thousand had fallen—nearly one-fifth of the Kukuana army. Their cries were unbearable, a constant reminder of the cost of ambition and power.

By midnight, the wailing lessened. Only one sound remained: a long, mournful howl from a nearby hut. It came from Gagool, keening over her dead king. That sound haunted me, pulling me back into visions of the fight—of the warrior charging at me atop the mountain, of the Greys standing firm against overwhelming odds, of Twala’s severed head rolling past my feet, eyes wide, teeth clenched.

At last, dawn broke. My companions were no better rested. Good, however, was feverish. His temperature rose sharply, and soon he began to grow confused, then spitting blood. Clearly, internal damage had been done during the battle—perhaps from a spear thrust that pierced his armor and struck deeper.

Sir Henry, though stiff and sore, seemed stronger. His face was split and swollen, making eating difficult, laughter impossible. Still, he moved with grim determination.

Around eight, Infadoos arrived. He looked unharmed—remarkable for a man who had fought all night. He greeted us warmly, but his concern for Good was clear. I noticed he addressed Sir Henry with something like awe. As we would later learn, the Englishman was already seen as a supernatural figure among the Kukuana. No mortal could have fought so fiercely or killed Twala in single combat, especially after such a grueling day. That final blow—cleaving Twala’s neck—became legend. From then on, any mighty stroke was called “Incubu’s blow.”

Infadoos told us Twala’s regiments had surrendered to Ignosi, and chiefs from distant lands were sending word of submission. With Scragga dead, no rival claimed the throne. The kingdom was stable.

I remarked that Ignosi had risen to power through bloodshed.

He shrugged. “Yes. But the Kukuana must bleed sometimes to remain calm. Many die—but the women live. New warriors will come. For now, peace will reign.”

Later, Ignosi himself visited. Now crowned king, he walked with regal dignity, flanked by guards. Seeing him, I recalled the tall Zulu who had once begged to join our service at Durban. Fortune had turned full circle.

“Hail, O king!” I said, rising.

“Yes, Macumazahn,” he replied. “King at last—by the might of your three right hands.”

All was well, he said. In two weeks, he would host a great feast to be seen by his people.

I asked about Gagool.

“She is the evil spirit of this land,” he answered. “She shall die—along with all witch doctors. She has lived too long. None remember when she was young. She trained the hunters, twisted the land into darkness.”

“But she knows things,” I warned. “Knowledge is harder to gain than to destroy.”

He paused. “True. Only she knows the secret of the ‘Three Witches’—the place beyond Solomon’s Road, where kings are buried and the Silent Ones sit.”

“And the diamonds,” I added. “Do not forget your promise. You must take us to the mines—even if you spare Gagool to show the way.”

“I will not forget,” he said. “I will consider.”

Afterward, I went to check on Good. He was delirious. The fever had seized him fully, worsened by internal injury. For days, his condition was grave. I believe he would have died without Foulata’s tireless care.

She tended him night and day—feeding him a cooling drink made from milk and tulip bulb juice, fending off flies, watching his breathing. Her movements were swift, gentle, instinctive—like a nurse trained in the finest hospitals. I tried to help, as did Sir Henry, but Foulata refused. She insisted we leave—our presence only disturbed him.

For nights on end, she sat beside his cot, her back against the wall, her eyes soft with exhaustion yet alight with compassion—or perhaps something deeper?

We feared he would not survive. Two days passed with no improvement. But Foulata never wavered. “He will live,” she said.

By order of the king, everyone within three hundred yards of the hut was moved away—only Sir Henry, myself, and Foulata remained, so no noise might disturb the sick man.

On the fifth night, I went to see him before retiring. I stepped quietly into the hut. The lamp flickered, casting shadows. Good lay still—no tossing, no moaning. Had he passed?

A sob escaped me.

“Hush—h—h!” whispered a voice from the dark.

I crept closer. Good was asleep—deeply, peacefully. And beside him, Foulata sat, her fingers tightly clasped in his. The crisis had passed.

He slept for eighteen hours. And throughout, she never moved—not once. When he finally woke, she was carried out—her limbs locked in stiffness, unable to walk. She had endured cramp, hunger, endless vigilance—all for him.

When Good recovered, Sir Henry told him everything. When he heard of her eighteen-hour vigil, fearing to wake him by moving, the sailor wept.

He went straight to her hut, taking me along to interpret.

“Tell her,” he said, “that I owe her my life. I will never forget her kindness.”

I translated. Under her dark skin, she flushed.

Then, with a swift, graceful motion—like a bird in flight—she turned to him, her large brown eyes meeting his.

“Nay, my lord,” she said softly. “You forget. Did you not save *my* life? Am I not your handmaiden?”

She remembered only his role in her rescue—completely ignoring Sir Henry and me. Women, I thought, are always like that. My dear wife was the same.

I left the hut heavy-hearted. I didn’t like the way she looked at Good. Sailors, I knew, could fall in love at the slightest spark. And Good… well, Good was no exception.

A few days later, Ignosi held his grand council—the *indaba*. The elders of Kukuanaland formally recognized him as king. A massive review followed. The surviving Greys were paraded, praised, and each given cattle and promoted to officer rank in the new royal guard.

An edict was issued: wherever we went, we were to be greeted with royal honors, treated as equals to the king. Life and death powers were granted to us.

Ignosi also reaffirmed his promises: no bloodshed without trial, no more witch-hunts.

Afterward, we met him privately. We asked about the mines at the end of Solomon’s Road.

“My friends,” he said, “I know this: the Three Witches—the Silent Ones—are there. It is where kings are buried. Twala rests among them. There is a deep pit—perhaps dug long ago for stones like those spoken of in Kimberley. And a secret chamber, known only to the king and Gagool.”

“But Twala is dead,” he continued. “I do not know the way. Yet there is a tale: long ago, a white man crossed the mountains, led by a woman to the chamber. She showed him riches, then betrayed him. He was driven back. Since then, no man has entered.”

“The story is true,” I said. “We found the bones of that man on the mountains.”

“Yes,” he nodded. “Now I swear to you—if you can reach that chamber, and the stones are there…”

“The diamond on your brow proves they are,” I interrupted, pointing to the gem I had taken from Twala’s forehead.

“Perhaps,” he said. “If they are there, you may take as many as you wish—if you choose to stay.”

“We must first find the chamber,” I said.

“There is only one who can show it—Gagool.”

“And if she refuses?”

“Then she dies,” Ignosi said coldly. “I spared her only for this.”

He called a messenger. “Bring Gagool.”

In minutes, she arrived, dragged by two guards, cursing them as she stumbled forward.

“Leave her,” Ignosi ordered.

The guards stepped back. Gagool collapsed—more like a bundle of rags than a person—her bright, snake-like eyes gleaming.

“What do you want?” she hissed. “You dare not touch me. If you do, I’ll kill you. Beware my magic.”

“Your magic failed Twala,” Ignosi replied. “It cannot harm me. I command you: reveal the chamber where the shining stones lie.”

“Ha! ha!” she cackled. “Only I know it. I will never tell. Let the white devils go empty-handed.”

“You will tell,” Ignosi said. “I will make you.”

“How, O king? Your power cannot force truth from a woman.”

“It is difficult—but I will.”

“How?”

“Thus: if you refuse, you will slowly die.”

“Die?” she shrieked. “You dare not touch me! You do not know who I am. How old do you think I am? I knew your fathers’ fathers’ fathers. When this land was young, I was here. When it grows old, I will still be here. I cannot die unless killed by chance. None dare slay me.”

“Yet I will,” Ignosi said. “See, Gagool—mother of evil—you are so old you no longer value life. What is life to a hag like you? You have no shape, no hair, no teeth—only wickedness and cruel eyes. To end your suffering is mercy.”

“Fool!” she screamed. “Accursed fool! Do you think life is sweet only to the young? It is not. The young feel. They love, they suffer. But the old—ha! ha!—they laugh at death. They laugh at evil under the stars. All they desire is life—the warm sun, the sweet air. They fear the cold, the dark—ha! ha! ha!”

“Cease your lies,” Ignosi snapped. “Will you show us the chamber—or die now?”

He raised a spear above her.

With a wild cry, Gagool leapt up—then fell again, writhing.

“Nay—I will show you. Only let me live. Let me sit in the sun, eat meat, and I will guide you.”

“It is well,” Ignosi said. “Tomorrow, you go with Infadoos and my white brothers. Fail, and you will slowly die. I have spoken.”

“I will not fail,” she laughed. “I always keep my word—ha! ha! ha! Once, a woman showed the chamber to a white man. Evil befell him. Her name was Gagool. Perhaps… I was that woman.”

“You lie,” I said. “That was ten generations ago.”

“Mayhap,” she grinned. “When one lives long, one forgets. Perhaps it was my mother’s mother who told me. Her name was Gagool too. But mark this: in the place of light, you will find a bag of hide filled with stones. The man filled it—but never took it. Evil befell him—evil befell him! Perhaps it was my mother’s mother who told me. It will be a merry journey—we can see the bodies of the fallen as we go. Their eyes are gone. Their ribs are hollow. Ha! ha! ha!”
