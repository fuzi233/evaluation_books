An unspeakable horror seized me. A darkness swallowed the light, followed by a dizzy, sickening vertigo where sight itself failed. I saw a line that was not a line; I saw a space that was not space. I existed, yet I did not exist. When I could finally scream, my voice tore through the void in agony, "Madness! Or Hell!"

"It is neither," a calm voice replied, cutting through the chaos. "It is Knowledge. It is the Third Dimension. Open your eye again, and look steadily."

I looked, and behold, a new universe unfolded! Before me stood the Stranger, no longer a mere suggestion, but a solid, living reality of perfect, circular beauty. His center, which I had previously imagined as a point of infinite depth, was laid bare to my gaze. Yet, within that radiant core, I saw no heart, no lungs, no arteries—only a harmonious, indescribable substance. To me, it remained a mystery without words; to you, my readers who dwell in Spaceland, it is simply the surface of the sphere.

Falling to my knees in mental prostration before my Guide, I cried out, "O divine ideal of consummate loveliness and wisdom, how is it that I behold thy inside, yet cannot discern thy organs? Where are thy heart, thy lungs, thy liver?"

"What you think you see, you do not see," he answered gently. "It is not given to you, nor to any being of Flatland, to perceive my internal parts. I am of a different order. If I were merely a circle, you might discern my intestines. But I am a being composed of many circles—the 'Many' contained within the 'One.' In your country, you call this a Sphere. Just as the exterior of a cube appears square to you, so the exterior of a sphere appears circular."

Though bewildered by his enigmatic words, I ceased my resistance and fell into silent worship. With a gentleness in his tone that calmed my terror, he continued, "Do not distress yourselves over the deeper mysteries of Spaceland. They will dawn upon you by degrees. Let us first cast a glance backward to your home. Return with me briefly to the plains of Flatland, and I shall show you what you have long reasoned about, yet never truly seen—a visible angle."

"Impressive!" I cried, feeling a sudden rush of disbelief. Yet, following the Sphere as if in a trance, I stopped when his voice arrested me. "Look yonder, and behold your own pentagonal house, and all within it."

I gazed downward, and there, revealed to my physical eye, lay every detail of domestic individuality that I had hitherto only inferred through intellect. How pale and shadowy those conjectures appeared compared to the vibrant reality before me! I saw my four sons sleeping peacefully in the north-western chambers, my two orphan grandsons resting to the south. I saw the servants, the butler, and my daughter, each in their separate apartments. But most striking was my wife; alarmed by my prolonged absence, she had risen from her room and wandered anxiously through the hall, waiting for my return. Even the page, roused by my cries, had left his chamber and peered into my study cabinet under the pretense of checking if I had fainted. Now, I could *see* this drama unfold—not merely deduce it—and as we drew closer, I distinguished the contents of the cabinet, the two chests of gold, and the tablets the Sphere had mentioned.

Moved by my wife's evident distress, I attempted to leap downward to comfort her, but I found myself utterly immobile. "Trouble not yourself regarding your wife," said my Guide. "She will not remain in anxiety for long. Meanwhile, let us survey Flatland."

Again, I felt myself ascending through the void. As the Sphere promised, the further we retreated from the house, the wider the field of vision expanded. Our native city, with the interiors of every dwelling and every creature within, lay spread out beneath us like a miniature model. We rose higher, and suddenly the secrets of the earth were bared: the depths of mines, the hidden caverns of hills—all exposed to my unworthy gaze.

Awe-struck at this unveiling of terrestrial mysteries, I turned to my companion. "Behold, I am become like a god! For the sages in our country declare that Omnividence—the ability to see all things—is the sole attribute of divinity."

There was a trace of scorn in the Voice of the Sphere as he answered, "Is this true? Then the pickpockets and murderers of my nation must be worshipped as gods by your wise men, for there is not one among them who does not possess the same view you hold now. But trust me, your sages are mistaken."

I. Then is omnividence possessed by beings other than gods?

SPHERE. I know not. However, if a thief or assassin of your country sees everything within your realm, does that justify worshipping them? Does this total vision make you more just, more merciful, less selfish, more loving?

I. "More merciful, more loving!" These are the virtues of women! And we know that a Circle is a superior being to a Straight Line, precisely because knowledge and wisdom are valued above mere affection.

SPHERE. It is not my place to rank human faculties by merit. Yet, many of the most virtuous and wise in Spaceland value affections above understanding, and regard your despised Straight Lines with more respect than your celebrated Circles. But enough of this. Look yonder. Do you recognize that building?

I looked and, in the distance, beheld an immense polygonal structure. I instantly recognized it as the Great Assembly Hall of the States of Flatland, surrounded by dense rows of pentagonal buildings standing at right angles—the streets. I realized we were approaching the capital.

"Here we descend," said my Guide. It was morning, the first hour of the first day of the two-thousandth year of our era. Acting in strict accordance with ancient precedent, the highest circles of the realm gathered in solemn conclave, just as they had done on the first hour of the first day of the millennium years 1000 and 0.

The minutes of the previous sessions were read aloud by a figure I immediately recognized as my brother, a perfectly symmetrical square and Chief Clerk of the High Council. The record stated: "Whereas the States have been disturbed by ill-intentioned individuals claiming to receive revelations from another world, and producing demonstrations that incite frenzy, it has been unanimously resolved by the Grand Council that on the first day of each millenary, strict injunctions be issued to the Prefects of the districts. They are to search diligently for such misguided souls, and, without formal mathematical scrutiny, to destroy all who are Isosceles of any degree, scourge and imprison any regular triangle, send any square or pentagon to the district asylum, and arrest any of higher rank, transporting them directly to the Capital for judgment by the Council."

"You hear your sentence," the Sphere whispered to me as the Council passed the formal resolution for the third time. "Death or imprisonment awaits the Apostle of the Gospel of Three Dimensions."

"Not so," I replied, my mind now illuminated. "The truth of real space is so palpable to me that I believe I could explain it to a child. Allow me to descend now and enlighten them."

"Not yet," said my Guide. "The time will come. For now, I must fulfill my mission. Remain in your seat." With incredible agility, he leaped from the air, plunging directly into the "sea" of Flatland, right amidst the ring of counselors.

"I come," he cried, "to proclaim the existence of a land of Three Dimensions!"

I saw many of the younger counselors recoil in sheer horror as the Sphere's circular cross-section expanded before them. But on the sign of the presiding Circle—who showed not the slightest alarm or surprise—six low-type Isosceles from various quarters rushed forward. "We have him!" they shouted. "No... yes... we have him! He's escaping! He's gone!"

"My lords," the President addressed the junior circles, "there is no need for surprise. The secret archives, accessible only to me, inform me that a similar event occurred on the commencement of the last two millennia. You will, of course, say nothing of these trifles outside the Cabinet."

Raising his voice, he summoned the guards. "Arrest the policemen; gag them. You know your duty." He consigned the wretched officers to their fate—unwilling and ill-starred witnesses of a state secret they were forbidden to reveal. Turning back to the council, he wished them a happy New Year. Before departing, he expressed deep regret to the Clerk, my unfortunate brother, explaining that due to precedent and the necessity of secrecy, he must condemn him to perpetual imprisonment. Yet, he added that unless the clerk spoke of the day's incident, his life would be spared.
