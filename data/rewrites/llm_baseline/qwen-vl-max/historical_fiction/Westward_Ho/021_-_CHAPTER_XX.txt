“Full seven long hours in all men's sight
This fight endured sore,
Until our men so feeble grew,
That they could fight no more.
And then upon dead horses
Full savorly they fed,
And drank the puddle water,
They could no better get.

“When they had fed so freely
They kneeled on the ground,
And gave God thanks devoutly for
The favor they had found;
Then beating up their colors,
The fight they did renew;
And turning to the Spaniards,
A thousand more they slew.”

—The Brave Lord Willoughby, 1586.

When the sun burst over the horizon the next morning, drenching the tropic sky in sudden gold, Amyas Leigh stood alone on the deck of the *Rose*, his hair wild and clothes torn, eyes bloodshot from grief and fury, heart shattered by loss. How can I describe what he felt? Let those who have buried a brother imagine it. And let those who have not—thank God you do not know such agony. He paced back and forth like a man possessed, each step heavy with rage, as the ship heaved through the restless sea under close-hauled sails. His mind raced with impossible plans: return to burn the villa, storm Guayra, kill every soul in it for Frank’s sake.

“We can do it, lads!” he roared, voice raw with fire. “If Drake took Nombre de Dios, we can take La Guayra!”

Every man shouted back, “Yes!”

“We’ll have it, Amyas—and we’ll have Frank too,” cried Cary, but Amyas shook his head. A deep certainty settled within him—no port in New Spain would ever bring back that face, that smile, that brother.

“Yes,” he said, voice low but firm, “he shall be avenged.” Then he pointed toward the shore, where three sails loomed on the horizon, barely five miles ahead. “There’s the first taste of vengeance. Look—there they come.”

“The Spanish bloodhounds are after us,” he declared, “the same ships we saw yesterday near Guayra. Back, lads, and welcome them—even if there were a dozen!”

A murmur rose from the crew, sharp with anticipation. Some young hearts faltered at the thought of facing three ships at once—but silence fell quickly, crushed by the thunderous cheer of the older men and Salvation Yeo’s booming voice.

“If there were a dozen,” Yeo bellowed, “the Lord is with us! He said, 'One of you shall chase a thousand.' Clear away, lads, and see the glory of the Lord this day!”

“Amen!” shouted Cary, and the ship leaned closer into the wind.

Amyas had found strength again in the prospect of battle. The pain in his wounds faded. Grief for Frank dimmed beneath the rush of action. Within minutes, he stood straight and steady, commanding as he once had.

“Now, my masters,” he called, voice strong and clear, “let us serve God, then breakfast, and after that—clear for action.”

Jack Brimblecombe began the daily prayers, his voice trembling slightly as he recited the prayer for all conditions of men. Despite Amyas’s despair, he added, “And especially for our dear brother Mr. Francis Leigh, perhaps captive among the idolaters.”

Then they rose.

“To breakfast,” said Amyas. “A Frenchman fights best fasting, a Dutchman drunk, an Englishman full, and a Spaniard when the devil is in him—always.”

“And good beef and a just cause,” said Cary, “are a match for any devil. Come down, captain—you must eat.”

Amyas refused. He took the tiller from the steersman and sent him below to feed. Cary returned in moments with bread and beef, a great tankard of ale, and coaxed it down Amyas’s throat like a nurse feeding a child. Then he fled below, tears streaking his cheeks.

Amyas stood alone at the helm, his face aged by one night’s sorrow. A terrible calm settled over him. Woe to any man who crossed him now.

“There are three of them,” he said, as the crew climbed back on deck. “A big ship ahead, two galleys behind. We’ll let the big ship go—she’s a race ship. If we can regain the wind, we’ll see if our height can match her length. We’ll slip past her and take the galleys first.”

“I thank the Lord,” said Yeo, “who has given such wisdom to a young general—a David and Daniel, though lacking in presence. And if any dare not follow, let them be as the men of Meroz and Succoth. Amen! Silas Staveley, smite that boy over the head—he should be at the powder-room door!”

Yeo moved about the decks with purpose, gunner’s pride and faith burning in his eyes. He knew how to fight—and he meant to do it thoroughly.

All hands worked swiftly. Though the ship had been kept ready all night, there was still much to do: clearing decks, lacing nettings, repairing bulwarks, fitting waist-cloths, arming tops, greasing pikes, slinging yards, doubling sheets and tacks. Even Richard Hawkins himself would have approved.

Amyas took command of the poop, Cary the forecastle, Yeo the main deck, and Drew, the master, stationed himself in the waist. Before the great Spanish ship came within two miles, they were ready—more than ready.

Now, as the English mastiffs and Spanish bloodhounds drew nearer across the churning waves, hungry for blood, let us pause a moment to understand what made these English sailors capable of defying fleets far greater in number and size, and why they could boast that in the entire Spanish war, only one queen’s ship—the *Revenge*—and one private vessel—Sir Richard Hawkins’s *Dainty*—had ever struck their colors.

What enabled Sir Richard Grenville’s *Revenge*, in her final battle off the Azores, to endure twelve hours of attack from eight Spanish armadas—two of which sank beside her—her masts gone, boarded three times without success, yet still defy a fleet of fifty-four ships, “like dogs around the dying forest king”?

What allowed young Richard Hawkins’s *Dainty*, half her guns useless due to the gunner’s negligence or betrayal, to fight for three days against two equal-sized Spanish ships, double her metal, tenfold her men?

How did Sir George Cary’s *Content* fight alone from dawn to dusk against four great galleons and two galleys, her heaviest gun a mere nine-pounder, and for hours with only thirteen men fit to fight?

Why could two London merchantmen—the *Merchant Royal* and the *Tobie*—with three small consorts, cripple an entire fleet of Spanish galleys off Pantellaria and sail triumphantly through the Straits of Gibraltar?

And finally, what allowed the English fleet in 1588 to shatter the Great Armada, capturing, destroying, or scattering every ship, losing only one pinnace and one nobleman?

Many reasons. First, the design of English ships. They were shorter than Spanish vessels, giving them unmatched agility. As Camden wrote of the 1588 battle: “The English ships charged the enemy with marvellous agility, discharged their broadsides, and flew into the deep, leveling their shot directly—without missing—at the heavy, unwieldy Spanish ships.”

Spanish ships in the West Indies were often flush-decked, or “race” ships, built to carry cargo, leaving men exposed. English ships, by contrast, had raised sterns and bows, with stockades and “close fights” on poop and forecastle, offering cover. Below, bulkheads divided the ship into fortified sections armed with swivels and loopholes for muskets and arrows.

But the true edge lay in the men.

The English sailor was amphibious, skilled in everything—from carpentry to gunnery, from needlework to hand-to-hand combat. Every Englishman, from birth, was trained in archery and swordplay, not as privilege, but as law. They fought not for a king alone, but for themselves—for freedom. Each man carried the spirit of a Yankee ranger: self-reliant, proud, trusted to act.

English officers lived side by side with their men—not above them. They shared the same training, the same risks. They consulted, cared, led—not by rank, but by merit. Drake once rebuked gentleman-adventurers: “I’d like to see the gentleman who refuses to set his hand to a rope. I need gentlemen to haul and draw with the mariners.”

Under Elizabeth, bravery and cunning mattered more than birth or age. Knighthood honored courage, not lineage. Merchant captains—many noble-born—were forged in trade, where Spanish traders often silenced rivals with cannon fire.

Thus arose mutual trust. Mutinies were rare in English ships, while in Spanish vessels they broke out constantly. Why? Because Spanish naval life was rigid, hierarchical, cruel. Soldiers watched like guards ashore, gunners were foreigners, mariners were slaves—forced to labor day and night, denied shelter, forbidden to rest beneath the decks.

As one prisoner described: “They divide their crews into soldiers, mariners, and gunners. Soldiers watch like on land. Gunners are foreigners, unskilled. Mariners are slaves—few, poor, never allowed to sleep under deck. In storm or sun, rain or calm, they toil uncovered.”

Now, the great Spanish ship was within two musket shots, her golden flag flying proudly at her stern. Trumpets blared defiance from a dozen brass throats. From the *Rose*, two answered lustily. Her poop bore England’s banner, her foremast the arms of Leigh and Cary side by side, above them the ship and bridge of Bideford.

Then Amyas called:

“Silence, trumpets! Waits, play up! ‘Fortune my foe!’ God and the Queen be with us!”

And so, amid music—cornet, sackbut, fife, drum—rose the old favorite of Queen Bess. Parson Jack, standing with the musicians on the poop, played furiously on his violin, like Volker in the *Nibelungen Lied*.

“Well played, Jack,” said Amyas, forcing a laugh. “Thy elbow flies like a lamb’s tail.”

“It shall fly to a better fiddle-bow soon, sir—if I live.”

“Steady, helm!” Amyas commanded. “What’s he doing now?”

The Spaniard, sailing downwind under full sail, suddenly reefed his light canvas.

“He doesn’t know what to make of us waiting so boldly,” said the helmsman.

“He does,” another shouted. “See—pulling up the foot of his mainsail. He wants to keep the wind of us.”

“Let him try,” said Amyas. “Keep her closer. No fire until we’re near. Man the starboard guns. To starboard—wait, all small arms. Pass the order to the gunner—fire high, aim for rigging.”

Bang! A Spanish bow gun fired—missed. Another. Another. Men checked their musket priming, loosened arrows in sheaths.

“Lie down, men. Sing a psalm. When I call, I’ll need you. Closer still, helmsman—we’ll try a short ship against a long one. We can sail two points nearer the wind than he.”

As Amyas predicted, the Spaniard wanted to cross the *Rose*’s bow—but feared being raked. So he turned sharply into the wind, waiting on the same tack.

Amyas smiled to himself. “Hold on a bit longer. More ways to kill a cat than choking her with cream. Drew, are your men ready?”

“Ay, ay, sir!” On they went, closing fast.

“Ready about!” And the *Rose* spun like an eel, crossing the Spaniard’s stern.

The Spaniard hesitated—then tried to turn, but too late. His massive hull hung in the wind’s eye as the *Rose*’s bowsprit nearly scraped his quarter. She passed slowly, ten yards off.

“Now!” roared Amyas. “Fire! With a will! Archers—have at them! Muskets—fire!”

In an instant, a storm of chain-shot, round shot, canister swept the Spaniard from stem to stern. Muskets and cloth-yard arrows whistled through the smoke. The steersman fell. The poop crew dropped. The mizzen topmast crashed. Stern windows and quarter galleries shattered. The painted image of the Madre Dolorosa—her heart pierced by seven swords—splintered into ruin. And the golden flag, fluttering just seconds before, now trailed in the water.

The ship, tiller shot away, helmsman dead, staggered—then fell up into the wind.

“Well done, men of Devon!” Amyas shouted, as cheers tore the sky.

“She’s struck!” someone cried.

“No,” said Amyas. “Not yet. Hold on, helmsman. Let her mend her tackle while we finish the galleys.”

On they sailed, swift and merry. Long before the Spaniard could recover, they were two miles ahead—while two galleys surged forward, oars cutting the choppy sea.

They were fearsome vessels—long, low, snout-like, driven by forty oars apiece. Their forecastles bristled with soldiers, cannon muzzles peering from ports forward and sideways. The waist was packed with slaves—five or six per oar—chained to their benches. Down the center, slave-drivers walked, whip in hand. At the stern, armored soldiers stood guard, sunlight flashing on steel and gun barrels.

As they neared, the English heard the cracks of whips, the yells of slaves, the rhythmic “Ha!” of each stroke, the curses of drivers. A foul musky stench—like kennelled hounds—drifted on the breeze. Many a young heart trembled. These were floating hells—cruelties whispered in English taverns, seen by captives, endured by men.

“Must we fire on the slaves?” asked one.

Amyas sighed.

“Spare them if you can. But if they run us down, we must rake them—and God forgive us.”

The two galleys approached abreast, forty yards apart. Outmaneuvering oars was impossible. Running was suicide—trapped between them and the ship.

Amyas decided on the desperate move.

“Lay her head up in the wind, helmsman. We’ll wait for them.”

Within musket range, they opened fire—but their aim was wild in the rolling sea. Amyas held his fire.

Men stood tense, lips tight. Amyas stood motionless on the quarterdeck, issuing orders calmly. The crew saw his confidence—and trusted him.

The Spaniards laughed. Was the Englishman mad? The galleys converged, aiming to strike both bows.

Forty yards. One minute. Impact imminent.

Then—Amyas’s helm turned. Yards creaked. The *Rose* gathered speed and plunged onto the larboard galley.

“A dozen gold nobles to whoever brings down the steersman!” shouted Cary.

Arrows flew from the forecastle—rattling on the galley’s deck.

Whether hit or not, the steersman panicked. His helm swung left. The galley’s beak slid harmlessly along the *Rose*’s bow—then came a long grind, followed by loud crack after crack, as the *Rose* sliced through the bank of oars, hurling slaves into heaps.

Before the other galley could swing around, Amyas unleashed his entire broadside—great and small guns—into the crippled vessel. A scream rent the air.

“Spare the slaves! Fire at the soldiers!” he cried—but the chaos made discrimination impossible.

The larboard galley, wounded but defiant, swung across the *Rose*’s stern and hooked on—desperate, reckless.

It was brave—but foolish. It blocked the second galley’s approach, exposing her to another broadside.

A boarding attempt through the stern-ports failed under a storm of shot and steel. Within minutes, Amyas and twenty men stood on the galley’s poop.

Five minutes of brutal fighting—steel against steel—and the poop was clear. The forecastle soldiers were helpless, exposed to arrows and musketry from the *Rose*’s high stern.

Amyas charged down the central gangway, shouting in Spanish: “Freedom to the slaves! Death to the masters!” He stormed into the forecastle, followed by his men, and showed them how to fight.

Three minutes later, not a Spaniard remained alive or breathing.

“Free the slaves!” he shouted. “Throw us a hammer! Hark—that’s an English voice!”

Indeed it was. From the wreckage, a voice screamed in thick Devon dialect:

“Oh, Robert Drew! Robert Drew! Come down—take me out of hell!”

“Who be you, in God’s name?”

“Don’t you remember William Prust—the man Captain Hawkins left behind in Honduras years ago? Nine of us here—if your shot hasn’t killed us. Come down, if you’ve a Christian heart!”

Forgetting discipline, Drew leapt down with hammer in hand. The two men embraced—old comrades reunited, at last free.
