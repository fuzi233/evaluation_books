The horror of Dunwich unfolded between Lammas and the autumnal equinox of 1928, a monstrous prologue witnessed by Dr. Armitage. He had already heard the tales of Wilbur Whateley's grotesque pilgrimage to Cambridge, where the young man had frantically attempted to borrow or copy the *Necronomicon* from the Widener Library. These desperate efforts had failed; Armitage had issued warnings of such piercing intensity to every librarian guarding the forbidden tome that Wilbur's quest remained fruitless. Yet, Wilbur had been shockingly nervous during his stay, oscillating between an anxious hunger for the book and a terrified desire to return home, as if he feared the very consequences of his prolonged absence.

In early August, the half-anticipated catastrophe arrived. During the small hours of the third day, Dr. Armitage was jolted awake by the wild, fierce cries of the savage watchdog patrolling the college campus. Deep and terrible, the snarling, half-mad growls and barks continued, rising in volume with hideously significant pauses. Then, a scream erupted from a throat entirely alien—such a shriek that haunted the dreams of Arkham forever, a sound capable of issuing only from a being born of the void rather than the earth.

Armitage hastily donned clothes and rushed across the street and lawn toward the college buildings, finding others already fleeing. He heard the shrill echoes of a burglar alarm still wailing from the library. An open window stood black and gaping against the moonlight. The intruder had indeed completed its entry; the barking and screaming, now fading into a mixed low growling and moaning, proceeded unmistakably from within. A primal instinct warned Armitage that what transpired inside was not for unfortified eyes to behold. With authoritative force, he brushed back the gathering crowd and unlocked the vestibule door. Among the fleeing figures, he recognized Professor Warren Rice and Dr. Francis Morgan, men to whom he had previously voiced his conjectures and misgivings. He motioned for them to accompany him inside.

By this time, the inward sounds had subsided, save for a watchful, droning whine from the dog. Yet, Armitage perceived with a sudden, chilling start that a loud chorus of whippoorwills among the shrubbery had commenced a damnably rhythmical piping, as if in unison with the last breath of a dying man. The building was thick with a frightful stench that Dr. Armitage knew too well. The three men rushed across the hall to the small genealogical reading-room, the source of the low whining. For a heartbeat, nobody dared to turn on the light. Then, summoning his courage, Armitage snapped the switch.

One of the three—uncertain which—shrieked aloud at what sprawled before them among disordered tables and overturned chairs. Professor Rice declared that he wholly lost consciousness for an instant, though he did not stumble or fall.

The creature lay half-bent on its side in a fetid pool of greenish-yellow ichor and tarry stickiness. It stood almost nine feet tall, and the dog had torn off all its clothing and much of its skin. It was not quite dead, but twitched silently and spasmodically while its chest heaved in monstrous unison with the mad piping of the expectant whippoorwills outside. Bits of shoe-leather and fragments of apparel were scattered about the room, and just inside the window lay an empty canvas sack where it had evidently been discarded. Near the central desk, a revolver had fallen, a dented but undischarged cartridge later explaining why it had not been fired. The creature itself crowded out all other images. It would be trite to say no human pen could describe it, but one may properly say it could not be vividly visualized by anyone whose ideas of form and contour are too closely bound to the common life-forms of this planet and its three known dimensions.

It was partly human, beyond a doubt, possessing very manlike hands and head, and the goatish, chinless face bore the distinct stamp of the Whateleys. But the torso and lower parts of the body were teratologically fabulous, rendering it impossible for such a form to walk on earth unchallenged or uneradicated under any guise of clothing. Above the waist, it was semi-anthropomorphic; though its chest, where the dog's rending paws still rested watchfully, displayed the leathery, reticulated hide of a crocodile or alligator. The back was piebald with yellow and black, dimly suggesting the squamous covering of certain snakes.

Below the waist, however, was the worst. Here, all human resemblance ceased, and sheer fantasy began. The skin was thickly covered with coarse black fur, and from the abdomen protruded a score of long greenish-gray tentacles with red sucking mouths, hanging limply. Their arrangement was odd, following the symmetries of some cosmic geometry unknown to Earth or the solar system. Set deep in a kind of pinkish, ciliated orbit on each hip was what seemed to be a rudimentary eye. In lieu of a tail, a kind of trunk or feeler depended downward, marked with purple annular rings and showing many evidences of being an undeveloped mouth or throat. The limbs, save for their black fur, roughly resembled the hind legs of prehistoric Earth's giant saurians, terminating in ridgy-veined pads that were neither hooves nor claws.

When the thing breathed, its tail and tentacles rhythmically changed color, as if driven by some circulatory cause normal to the non-human side of its ancestry. In the tentacles, this appeared as a deepening of the greenish tinge; in the tail, it manifested as a yellowish hue alternating with a sickly grayish-white in the spaces between the purple rings. There was no genuine blood, only the fetid greenish-yellow ichor that trickled along the painted floor beyond the radius of the stickiness, leaving a curious discoloration in its wake.

As the presence of the three men seemed to rouse the dying thing, it began to mumble without turning or raising its head. Dr. Armitage made no written record of its mouthings but asserted confidently that nothing in English was uttered. At first, the syllables defied all correlation with any speech of Earth, but toward the end, there came some disjointed fragments evidently taken from the *Necronomicon*, the monstrous blasphemy in quest of which the thing had perished. Those fragments, as Armitage recalled them, ran something like "*N'gai, n'gha'ghaa, bugg-shoggog, y'hah; Yog-Sothoth, Yog-Sothoth...*" They trailed off into nothingness as the whippoorwills shrieked in rhythmic crescendos of unholy anticipation.

Then came a halt in the gasping, and the dog raised his head in a long, lugubrious howl. A change came over the yellow, goatish face of the prostrate thing, and the great black eyes fell in appallingly. Outside the window, the shrilling of the whippoorwills had suddenly ceased, and above the murmurs of the gathering crowd came the sound of a panic-struck whirring and fluttering. Against the moon, vast clouds of feathery watchers rose and raced from sight, frantic at that which they had sought for prey.

All at once, the dog started up abruptly, gave a frightened bark, and leaped nervously out the window by which it had entered. A cry rose from the crowd, and Dr. Armitage shouted to the men outside that no one must be admitted until the police or medical examiner arrived. He was thankful that the windows were just too high to permit peering in, and he drew the dark curtains carefully down over each one. By this time, two policemen had arrived; Dr. Morgan, meeting them in the vestibule, urged them for their own sakes to postpone entrance to the stench-filled reading-room until the examiner came and the prostrate thing could be covered up.

Meanwhile, frightful changes were taking place on the floor. One need not describe the *kind* and *rate* of shrinkage and disintegration that occurred before the eyes of Dr. Armitage and Professor Rice; but it is permissible to say that, aside from the external appearance of face and hands, the truly human elements in Wilbur Whateley must have been very small. When the medical examiner arrived, there was only a sticky whitish mass on the painted boards, and the monstrous odor had nearly disappeared. Apparently, Whateley had had no skull or bony skeleton—at least, in any true or stable sense. He had taken somewhat after his unknown father.
