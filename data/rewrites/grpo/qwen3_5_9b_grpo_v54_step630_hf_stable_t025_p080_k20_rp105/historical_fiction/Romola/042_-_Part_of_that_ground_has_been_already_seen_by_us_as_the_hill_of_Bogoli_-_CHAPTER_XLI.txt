"Rise, my daughter," Fra Girolamo finally commanded, his voice cutting through the heavy silence. "Your servant waits nearby with the mules. It is time for me to press on toward Florence."

Romola rose from her knees. That posture of stillness had become a sacred ritual for her, cementing the state of yearning passivity she had only just entered. With a single act of renouncing her plan to flee her husband, her will felt shattered, bruised beyond repair, leaving her desperate for direction even in the smallest matters. She lifted the edge of her cowl and saw Maso and the second Dominican standing with their backs turned on the hill's edge, some ten yards away. Yet, she did not look at them. Her gaze remained fixed on Savonarola, silent and pleading, as if the command to recall Maso must issue from his lips alone, not hers.

"I will go and fetch them," he said, answering her unspoken appeal. "And I commend you, my daughter, to the Brother who accompanies me. You seek guidance, seeking that wisdom which has hitherto appeared as folly to you. A primary gateway to this wisdom is the sacrament of confession. You require a confessor, my daughter, and I wish to place you under the care of Fra Salvestro, one of the brethren of San Marco, in whom I place my highest confidence."

"I would prefer no guidance but yours, Father," Romola replied, her voice trembling with anxiety.

"My daughter, I do not act as your confessor. My vocation withdraws me from offices that would force frequent contact with the laity and interfere with my specific duties."

"Then shall I never be able to speak to you in private? If I waver, if—" Romola broke off, seized by a sudden alarm. She feared that the fragile strength of her renunciation would evaporate the moment Savonarola's immediate personal influence faded.

"My daughter, if your soul requires the private word from my lips, you shall let me know through Fra Salvestro, and I will meet you in the sacristy or the choir of San Marco. I will not cease to watch over you. I shall instruct my brother regarding you, so that he may guide you into the path of labor for the suffering and the hungry to which you are called as a daughter of Florence in these times of hard need. I desire to behold you among the feebler and more ignorant sisters as the apple-tree among the trees of the forest, so that your fairness and all your natural gifts serve merely as a lamp through which the Divine light shines more purely. I must now go and call your servant."

Once Maso had been sent ahead, Fra Salvestro stepped forward, and Savonarola led Romola toward him. She had already felt an inward shrinking from a new guide who was a total stranger; yet to resist Savonarola's counsel would have been to assume an attitude of independence at a moment when all her strength must be drawn from the renunciation of that very independence. The entire bent of her mind was now toward doing what was painful rather than what was easy. She bowed reverently to Fra Salvestro before looking directly at him, but when she raised her head and saw him fully, her reluctance curdled into a palpitating doubt.

There are men whose presence naturally infuses trust and reverence; there are others to whom we must carry our trust and reverence ready-made. This distinction struck Romola as Savonarola receded from her sight and Fra Salvestro Maruffi stood in his place. It was not that Fra Salvestro possessed anything manifestly repulsive in his face or manner—no air of hypocrisy, no tinge of coarseness. His face was actually handsomer than Fra Girolamo's, his stature slightly taller. He was the long-accepted confessor of many among Florence's chief personages, possessing vast experience as a spiritual director. Yet, his face bore the vacillating expression of a mind unable to concentrate itself strongly within the channel of one great emotion or belief—an expression fatal to influencing an ardent nature like Romola's. Such a countenance is not the stamp of insincerity; it marks simply a shallow soul, one that may sincerely strive to fill a high vocation, sincerely composing its features to utter sublime formulas, only to find the muscles twitch or relax in spite of belief, as prose insists on intruding where poetry should reign. Fra Salvestro had a peculiar liability to visions, seemingly dependent on a constitution prone to somnambulism. Savonarola believed in the supernatural character of these visions, while Fra Salvestro himself had originally resisted such an interpretation, even rebuking Savonarola for his prophetic preaching: another proof, if one were needed, that the relative greatness of men cannot be gauged by their tendency to disbelieve the superstitions of their age. Between the two, there could be no question of which was the great man and which the small.

The difference between them was measured precisely by the shift in Romola's feelings as Fra Salvestro began to address her with words of exhortation and encouragement. After her initial angry resistance to Savonarola had passed, she had lost all memory of the old dread lest any influence drag her into the circle of fanaticism and sour monkish piety. But now, once again, the chill breath of that dread stole over her. It could have no decisive effect against the impetus her mind had just received; it was merely like the closing of grey clouds over a sunrise, rendering her returning path monotonous and sombre.

Perhaps of all sombre paths, the one we tread upon after returning with strong resolution is the one that most severely tests the fervor of renunciation. As they re-entered the city gates, light snowflakes fell around them. As the grey sister walked hastily homeward from the Piazza di San Marco, crossing the bridge again and turning into the large door of the Via de' Bardi, her footsteps were marked darkly on the thin carpet of snow, and her cowl fell laden and damp about her face.

She ascended to her room, threw off her serge habit, destroyed the parting letters, replaced all her precious trifles, unbound her hair, and donned her usual black dress. Instead of undertaking a long, exciting journey, she was to sit down in her usual place. The snow beat against the windows, and she was alone.

She felt the dreariness, yet her courage remained high, akin to that of a seeker who has discovered new signs of gold. She was about to thread life by a fresh clue. She had thrown all the energy of her will into renunciation. The empty tabernacle remained locked, and she placed Dino's crucifix outside it.

Nothing broke the outward monotony of her solitary home until night came like a white ghost at the windows. Yet, for Romola, this Christmas Eve of 1494 proved to be the most memorable of her life.
