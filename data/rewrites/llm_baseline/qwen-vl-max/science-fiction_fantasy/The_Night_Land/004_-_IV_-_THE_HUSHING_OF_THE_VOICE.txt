("Dearest, thine own feet tread the world at night—
Treading, as moon-flakes step across a dark—
Kissing the very dew to holier light ...
Thy Voice a song past mountains, which to hark
Frightens my soul with an utter lost delight.")

One night, near the end of the sixteenth hour, as I prepared for sleep, the aether stirred around me—its familiar hum now charged with something deeper, more urgent. And within that thrumming silence, I heard her voice: Naani’s, clear and close, echoing not through instruments but deep in my mind.

Yet I did not answer at once. Instead, I sent the Master-Word into the darkness—a signal only we who knew the ancient codes could recognize. Instantly, it returned, pulsing like a heartbeat across the void. Then I questioned her: why speak now, when all slept? The Monstruwacans in the Lesser Redoubt were meant to rest from the eleventh hour onward. It was already five hours past their bedtime. She should have been asleep—safe within the pyramid, beside her father. I assumed she used the Instrument, her voice resonating so vividly in my thoughts. But when I asked, she gave no reply. Instead, she placed words directly into my spirit:

"Dearest, thine own feet tread the world at night—"

And in that moment, I trembled. For those words—those exact words—I had once spoken to Mirdath, the Beautiful, long before this Age, when she died and left me alone beneath the endless sky. A memory surged, sharp and unbearable: the grief, the love, the ache of losing her forever. My heart wrenched. I called out to Naani, demanding an explanation—why these words? Why now?

Again, no direct answer. Only the same line repeated, drifting through the dark between us. And then it struck me: it was not Naani speaking. Not entirely. It was Mirdath—the ghost of her voice rising from the depths of time, carried by Naani’s spirit. I cried out, “Mirdath! Mirdath!” into the night, pouring my soul into the call. And lo! the voice came again—faint, distant, yet unmistakable. The same words. The same tone. The same sorrow and beauty.

It was Mirdath. Yet it was also Naani. And I knew, without doubt, that this was no illusion. That somewhere across the vastness of eternity, the spirit of Mirdath had been reborn—not in flesh, but in soul—within the body of Naani. That I, who had loved her across ages, had been given one last chance to meet her again, not as memory, but as living breath.

I called to Naani again and again, pleading, questioning—but there was no response. No sign she had heard. Hours passed. I grew exhausted. My mind reeled. I fell into sleep.

When I woke, the dream remained fresh—the memory of those words, impossible to forget. No one alive could have known them. Only Mirdath, or someone who had lived them. And yet here they were, whispered through the dark, carried on the wind of a forgotten love.

I reached out to Naani immediately. Once. Twice. And soon, the Master-Word pulsed back, steady and strong. I sent it in return, and then—her voice came, weak but clear, delivered not by machine, but by the raw power of her mind. I asked her about the words, the dreams, the strange connection.

She denied knowing anything. She claimed to have slept deeply, dreamlessly. But then, she confessed to a dream—strange, vivid, haunting. In it, she saw a tall man, broad and sorrowful, dressed in clothes unlike any she had seen. He sat in a small room, writing—trying to ease his pain through words. Though the language was unknown to her waking self, she read it in her dream. And she remembered only one line—written above all others: *Mirdath*.

I asked her to repeat what she recalled of the writing. Her voice trembled as it came back to me:

"Dearest, thine own feet tread the world at night—"

No more. But it was enough. With a wild, trembling certainty, I completed the line in my mind, sending it forth into the darkness. And as I did, I felt it strike her spirit like a blow—awakening memory, flooding her with shock and wonder. She stumbled, silent for a moment, then wept in her mind. Tears of recognition, of awe, of terror and joy mingled together.

And suddenly, her voice changed. It was no longer just Naani’s. It was Mirdath’s—echoing through time, through death, through rebirth. She asked me, trembling, whether she might truly be Mirdath.

I could not speak. My blood pounded. My soul shivered. The truth was too great, too beautiful, too terrifying. And yet, in that moment, our spirits met—not through machines or magic, but through love, through memory, through the unbroken thread of soul across time.

Imagine, if you will, Naani standing in that far future, her consciousness reaching back through the veil of existence, seeing not just the life we shared in this age, but others—older, dimmer, half-forgotten. She saw echoes of our union in past lives, glimpses of loves that had never been named. And though I could not grasp all of it, I understood enough: this was no coincidence. This was destiny. This was reunion.

And now, I burned with the need to be with her—to see her, to touch her, to know if she truly was the one I had loved across all eternity.

But how?

The Lesser Redoubt lay somewhere beyond the horizon, hidden in the Night Land. None among the scholars of the Mighty Pyramid knew its location. Records offered only guesses: somewhere between North-West and North-East. But no distance, no bearing. Only speculation.

And even if we knew where it was, the journey would be suicide. The Outer Lands—known as the Unknown Lands—were filled with horrors, with creatures born of darkness and decay. The Night Land was not a place for men. It was a tomb.

Still, I spoke with Naani, probing, asking. Her father, the Master Monstruwacan, admitted they had no knowledge of our position either. Only that the Builder of the Lesser Redoubt had come from the Southward World, long ago, as the records said.

They had tried to use the ancient compass. But it spun wildly, drawn by the Earth-Current flowing from the "Crack" beneath our pyramid. The needle danced between North and South, always off-center. It was useless. Or perhaps… not entirely. They speculated that our current might pull the needle toward us. So if we were south, they were north—and vice versa. But it was guesswork. A hope built on sand.

We brought the compass from the Great Museum. We tested it. It spun. It refused to settle. The Earth-Current made it impossible to trust. To us, it seemed absurd—how could such a tool ever have worked? Yet in earlier times, when the Sun still rose, they had used it with the stars, naming directions by sight. Now, we kept the names—North, South, East, West—by tradition alone. We used them because our ancestors did, even though the world was always night.

Naani, sensing my desperation, begged me to stay. She pleaded with me not to risk everything. “Better to live in spirit,” she said, “than die in flesh.” But I could not listen. If only I knew the direction—if only I could measure the distance—then I might go.

And then, something extraordinary happened.

When I sent my thoughts into the night, using my brain-elements, I found myself turning instinctively toward the North. Not because I saw anything, but because my body *knew*. I turned, again and again, even when blindfolded, even when surrounded by screens. The Master Monstruwacan noted it. He experimented. He tested me. And each time, I faced North—drawn by some unseen force.

He wrote a study of it. It was published in the Hour-Slips, copied throughout the cities. People began to talk. Some praised me. Others mocked. But all wondered: what was this power?

And still, Naani had no such sense. She could not feel it. Only I could. And I began to believe—perhaps not in reason, but in feeling—that her spirit pulled me northward. That she was out there, somewhere in the dark, waiting.

Then, disaster.

At the seventeenth hour, during the sleep-time, I was in the Tower of Observation with the Master Monstruwacan. Suddenly, the aether thrummed. Naani’s voice came to me—weak, fading. I sent the Master-Word. It returned. I called to her.

Her voice was thin, distant. She told me the Earth-Current had failed. The Lesser Redoubt was dying. Their power was gone. The instruments were dead. They feared this was the end—the final flicker before darkness consumed them.

As we spoke, her voice grew weaker. Each word was a wound. I felt my heart break. We talked as if we were saying goodbye—lovers facing eternity apart.

When dawn came (though there was no dawn), the news spread. Millions grieved. The cities mourned.

For weeks, Naani’s voice grew fainter. Even my Night-Hearing could barely catch her words. Each syllable became a treasure. I stopped eating. I could not rest. The Master Monstruwacan scolded me. “If you fall ill,” he said, “who will hear them?”

I obeyed him. I ate. I rested. But still, I felt the dread grow. For soon, I learned worse: the monsters were coming. They attacked the Lesser Redoubt. There was a battle. Madness broke out. Some opened the gate and fled into the dark. Their bodies were taken by the beasts. Their souls? Who knows?

All this, they blamed on the failure of the Earth-Current. Without it, they were powerless. Lifeless. Fearful. And yet, strangely, they did not hunger. Did not thirst. Only fear remained—fear of death.

And so, the people of the Great Redoubt began to question their own fate. Was the Crack beneath our pyramid failing too? Were we next? The Hour-Slips filled with warnings, with poems, with plans—none of which could be carried out. Still, I knew one thing: I must go.

Even if I died, I would go. Better to die trying than to live knowing I had not tried.

That night, in the eighteenth hour, the aether erupted. I was awakened by the Master Monstruwacan. “Listen,” he said. “The Master-Word. Is it coming?”

I sat up. And there it was—beating in the night, strong and clear. And then, a cry:
“We are coming! We are coming!”

My heart leapt. Then sank. The message seemed to come from near the Great Pyramid. As if someone—or something—was approaching.

I sent the Master-Word back. No answer. Then, a faint pulse—distant, weak. I recognized it: Naani. I asked her if she was safe, if she was still in the Lesser Redoubt.

A whisper came—so faint it was almost gone. She was there. But she had heard the call too. She had woken in terror. She thought we were coming to rescue them.

I told her not to hope. “Do not build your heart on dreams,” I said. “I cannot save you. But I will try.”

I bid her sleep. Then I turned to the Master Monstruwacan.

“I heard it,” I said. “The Master-Word. But it did not come from the Lesser Redoubt. It came from here. From near us. Sent not by instruments—but by many minds, calling together.”

He listened, pale.

We went to the Tower of Observation. Through the great spy-glass, we saw it: hundreds of figures crossing the Electric Circle. Not toward the pyramid. Away from it. Into the blackness.

We stared at each other. We knew.

People had left the Mighty Pyramid during the sleep-time.

The Master Watchman was summoned. He rushed to the Great Gate. He found the Sleep-Time Warders bound, gagged. Five hundred young men—strong, fierce, from the Upper Cities—had slipped through the Eye-Gate at the top of the Gate and vanished into the night.

The Watchmen had tried to call the central dome—but the instruments were dead. Tampered with.

Rules were rewritten. Tests were imposed. But the damage was done.

And I knew—somewhere in that darkness, among those fleeing men, was the seed of my journey. The beginning of the end.

Or perhaps, the beginning of the beginning.
