Every seasoned traveler of Eastern England recognizes the landscape dotted with these specific country residences: compact, somewhat damp structures, typically rendered in the Italianate style, commanding parks ranging from eighty to a hundred acres. These properties possess a distinct allure for me, defined by the weathered grey pales of split oak, the imposing timber, the reed-fringed meres, and the distant treelines. I hold a particular affection for the pillared portico—often a later addition grafted onto a red-brick Queen Anne core, disguised in stucco to mimic the Grecian fashion of the late eighteenth century—and the soaring hall, ideally equipped with a gallery and a small organ. The library appeals equally, offering a spectrum of artifacts from a thirteenth-century Psalter to a Shakespeare quarto. Above all, I delight in reconstructing the domestic life of these estates during their founding eras, amidst the landlords' golden age, and observing how, even today, despite diminished wealth, the variety of taste renders existence profoundly intriguing. Ideally, I aspire to acquire one such estate, securing sufficient capital to maintain its integrity and host my circle with modest dignity.

However, this preamble serves only as context. The narrative requires attention to a singular sequence of events unfolding within a property matching this exact profile: Castringham Hall in Suffolk. While significant renovations have altered the façade since the era of my tale, the architectural skeleton remains intact—the Italian portico, the white-washed square block, the interior antiquity contrasting with the exterior, the wooded fringe, and the mere. One defining characteristic, however, has vanished. From the vantage point of the park, a colossal ancient ash tree once dominated the right flank, its canopy extending mere feet from the wall, nearly brushing the structure. Presumably planted when Castringham transitioned from a fortified stronghold to a residential seat, filling the drained moat, the tree reached maturity by 1690.

That year, the region became the theater of numerous witchcraft prosecutions. The extent of genuine belief driving this hysteria remains elusive; whether the accused genuinely perceived supernatural agency, harbored malicious intent, or simply succumbed to the brutal coercion of witch-finders, history offers no definitive resolution. The current chronicle demands scrutiny. It resists dismissal as mere fabrication. The judgment belongs to the observer.

Castringham yielded a victim to the *auto-da-fé*: Mrs. Mothersole. Unlike the typical rural sorceress, she occupied a position of relative affluence and local influence. Several reputable farmers attempted to intervene, testifying vigorously to her character and expressing profound concern regarding the jury's verdict.

Yet, the testimony of Sir Matthew Fell, the Hall's proprietor, proved fatal. He swore under oath to witnessing her three separate times, at the height of the moon, harvesting sprigs from the ash tree adjacent to his residence. He claimed she scaled the branches in nothing but her shift, wielding a uniquely curved blade to sever twigs while muttering to herself. Each attempt by Sir Matthew to apprehend her failed; startled by incidental noises, she fled, leaving behind only a hare darting toward the village. On the third instance, he pursued at full speed, arriving at her doorstep only to find her emerging from her home agitated and sleep-deprived, offering no coherent explanation for his intrusion.

Consequently, bolstered by this primary evidence alongside corroborating accounts from lesser witnesses, Mrs. Mothersole was convicted and sentenced to death. She was executed a week post-trial at Bury St Edmunds, along with five or six other unfortunate souls.

Sir Matthew Fell, serving as Deputy-Sheriff, attended the hanging. It was a dreary, drizzling March morning as the cart ascended the rough grass slope toward Northgate, where the gallows stood. The other condemned appeared apathetic or shattered by despair, but Mrs. Mothersole displayed a temperament consistent with her life. Contemporary reports described her "poisonous rage" as so potent it affected the bystanders and even the hangman, leading observers to conclude she embodied the living visage of a mad devil. Remarkably, she offered no resistance to the law officers; instead, she fixed them with a gaze so venomous that one officer confessed the mere memory of it haunted his mind for six months thereafter.

Her final utterances were reportedly nonsensical repetitions of the phrase: "There will be guests at the Hall," spoken in a low murmur.

Sir Matthew remained unsettled by her demeanor. Post-assize, he discussed the matter with the Vicar of his parish, Mr. Crome, traveling home together. Though his testimony had been reluctant—he was not particularly susceptible to the witch-hunting fervor—he insisted on the accuracy of his observations, viewing his actions as a necessary civic duty despite the personal repugnance of the ordeal. The Vicar commended this stance.

Weeks later, under a full May moon, the two men reconvened in the park, proceeding toward the Hall. Lady Fell accompanied her critically ill mother, leaving Sir Matthew alone at home; thus, Mr. Crome accepted an invitation to a late supper.

Sir Matthew proved a somber companion that evening. Conversation focused on familial and parish affairs. Coincidentally, Sir Matthew drafted a written memorandum detailing his intentions regarding his estates—a document that would prove invaluable later.

Around 9:30 PM, as Mr. Crome prepared to depart, the pair took a stroll along the gravel path behind the house. The sole notable occurrence occurred as they neared the ash tree previously described. Sir Matthew halted abruptly.

"What moves up and down the trunk of the ash?" he asked. "Is it not a squirrel? By now, they should all be nestled."

The Vicar scanned the darkness, unable to discern the creature's coloration in the moonlight. However, the sharp silhouette, visible for a fleeting instant, burned itself into his memory. He swore, though the assertion seemed absurd, that the entity possessed more than four limbs.

Despite this anomalous sighting, neither man attached significant weight to the momentary vision. They parted ways, meeting sporadically thereafter until a decade passed.

The following morning, Sir Matthew Fell did not descend to breakfast at his customary hour of six, nor at seven, nor at eight. Servants, growing anxious, pounded on his chamber door. Eventually, the door yielded from the outside. They discovered their master deceased and rigor mortis setting in. No external signs of violence were apparent initially, yet the casement window stood ajar.

One servant summoned the clergyman, who then dispatched a rider to notify the coroner. Mr. Crome hastened to the Hall and entered the room containing the corpse. His subsequent notes, preserved among his papers, reveal the genuine grief felt for Sir Matthew and provide critical insight into the investigation:

"There existed no trace of forced entry to the chamber; the casement stood open, as my dear friend invariably kept it during this season. Beside him lay his evening draught of small ale in a silver vessel of approximately a pint; tonight, it remained undisturbed. Dr. Hodgkins of Bury, the attending physician, examined the drink but, under oath before the coroner's inquest, could detect no poisonous substance. Naturally, given the corpse's severe swelling and discoloration, rumors of poisoning circulated among the neighbors. The body lay in extreme disorder, contorted in a manner suggesting expiration amidst excruciating agony.

"Unexplained, and indicative of a horrific, artful design by the perpetrators, was the reaction of the women entrusted with preparing the corpse. Both respectable practitioners in their mournful profession, they approached me in profound distress, confirming upon initial inspection that contact with the breast caused an immediate, violent smarting in their palms. Within moments, their forearms swelled immoderately, pain persisting for weeks and forcing them to abandon their trade. Strikingly, no marks appeared on the skin.

"Upon learning this, I consulted the physician present. Using a crystal magnifying lens, we scrutinized the affected area but detected nothing substantial beyond two minute punctures or pricks. We hypothesized these were the vectors for poison, recalling the infamous rings of Pope Borgia and similar instruments used by Italian poisoners of the previous age.

"Regarding the symptoms observed on the corpse, further inquiry relies solely on my own experiment, leaving posterity to judge its value. Beside the bedside lay a small Bible, which my friend, meticulous in all matters, read nightly and upon rising. Seizing the volume, moved by a tear for this humble representation of a greater original, and driven by desperation for illumination, I resorted to the superstitious practice of drawing the *Sortes*. Recent discourse surrounding King Charles and Lord Falkland highlighted this method. My trial yielded limited assistance, yet, anticipating future investigations into these dreadful events, I recorded the results should they point toward a source of malice surpassing my own intellect.

"I performed three trials. Opening the book and placing my finger upon random words, the first selection revealed Luke xiii. 7: 'Cut it down.' The second, Isaiah xiii. 20: 'It shall never be inhabited.' The third, Job xxxix. 30: 'Her young ones also suck up blood.'"

These excerpts constitute the entirety of Mr. Crome's relevant documentation. Sir Matthew Fell was interred according to rites, and his funeral sermon, delivered by Mr. Crome the following Sunday, was published under the title *"The Unsearchable Way; or, England's Danger and the Malicious Dealings of Antichrist."* The prevailing sentiment, shared by the Vicar and the neighborhood, attributed the Squire's demise to a resurgence of the Popish Plot.
