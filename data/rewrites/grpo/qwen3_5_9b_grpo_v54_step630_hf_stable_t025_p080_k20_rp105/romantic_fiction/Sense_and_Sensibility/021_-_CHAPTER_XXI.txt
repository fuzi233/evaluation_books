The Palmers departed for Cleveland the following day, leaving the Dashwoods and the Middletons to their own company at Barton. Yet this solitude proved fleeting. Elinor had scarcely shaken off the lingering thoughts of the Palmers—their peculiar domestic harmony, Mr. Palmer's unassuming demeanor despite his evident talents, and the often jarring disconnect between spouses—before Sir John and Mrs. Jennings, driven by their fervent zeal for social expansion, introduced her to fresh acquaintances.

During a morning excursion to Exeter, they encountered two young ladies whom Mrs. Jennings delighted to identify as her relations. This discovery was sufficient for Sir John to extend an immediate invitation to the Park, contingent only upon the conclusion of the ladies' current engagements in Exeter. Those engagements dissolved instantly upon receiving such an offer. Lady Middleton, however, was thrown into considerable alarm upon Sir John's return, learning that she was soon to host two strangers she had never seen, whose elegance—or even their mere gentility—she could not verify. The assurances of her husband and mother held no weight with her; indeed, the fact that they were her relations made the prospect all the more unsettling. Mrs. Jennings's attempts to soothe her daughter, advising her not to mind their lack of fashion since they were all cousins bound to tolerate one another, fell on deaf ears. Resigned to the inevitability of their arrival, Lady Middleton adopted the stoic philosophy of a well-bred woman, contenting herself with issuing gentle reprimands to her husband regarding the matter five or six times daily.

The young ladies arrived, and their appearance was neither ungenteel nor unfashionable. Their attire was smart, their manners civil, and they expressed such delight at the house and furniture that Lady Middleton's good opinion was secured within an hour. She declared them to be "very agreeable girls indeed," a phrase that constituted enthusiastic admiration in her household. Sir John's confidence in his own judgment soared at this praise, and he hastened to the cottage to inform the Miss Dashwoods of the Miss Steeles' arrival, assuring them they were the sweetest girls in the world. Yet, Elinor discerned little substance in such commendation; she knew that the "sweetest girls" could be found across England in myriad forms of temperament and understanding. Sir John insisted that the entire family walk to the Park immediately to view his guests. A benevolent, philanthropic soul, he found it painful even to keep a third cousin to himself.

"Do come now," he urged. "Pray come—you must come. You can't imagine how you will like them. Lucy is monstrous pretty, so good-humoured and agreeable! The children are already clustering around her as if she were an old acquaintance. They both long to see you above all things, for they heard at Exeter that you are the most beautiful creatures in the world; I have confirmed this truth to them and added much more. You will be delighted, I am sure. They have filled the coach with playthings for the children. How can you be so cross as to refuse? Why, they are your cousins, after a fashion. You are my cousins, and they are my wife's, so you must be related."

Despite his persistence, Sir John could not prevail. He secured only a promise that they would call at the Park within a day or two, leaving the Miss Dashwoods in amazement at their indifference, while he walked home to boast anew of their attractions to them, just as he had previously boasted of them to the sisters.

When the promised visit finally occurred, the introduction revealed little to admire in the eldest, who was nearly thirty and possessed a plain, unsensible face. In contrast, the younger sister, not yet twenty-five, displayed considerable beauty. Her features were pretty, accompanied by a sharp, quick eye and a smartness of air that, while lacking actual elegance or grace, lent distinctiveness to her person. Their manners were notably civil, and Elinor quickly granted them credit for genuine sense, observing the constant, judicious attention they paid to pleasing Lady Middleton. With the children, they were in continual rapture, extolling their beauty, courting their notice, and humouring their whims. Any spare time was devoted to admiring Lady Middleton's activities or copying patterns of her elegant new dresses, which had captivated them the previous day.

Fortunately for those who court through such foibles, a fond mother, though rapacious in her pursuit of praise for her children, is also the most credulous of human beings. Her demands are exorbitant, yet she swallows anything. The excessive affection and endurance shown by the Miss Steeles toward Lady Middleton's offspring were viewed by the mother without the slightest surprise or distrust. She watched with maternal complacency as her cousins submitted to impertinent encroachments and mischievous tricks. She saw their sashes untied, their hair pulled about their ears, their work-bags searched, and their knives and scissors stolen, feeling no doubt that it was a reciprocal enjoyment. The only surprise she registered was that Elinor and Marianne sat so composedly by, claiming no share in the proceedings.

"John is in such spirits today!" she exclaimed, as he took Miss Steele's pocket-handkerchief and threw it out of the window. "He is full of monkey tricks."

Soon after, upon the second boy violently pinching the same lady's finger, she fondly observed, "How playful William is!"

"And here is my sweet little Annamaria," she added, tenderly caressing a three-year-old girl who had remained silent for the last two minutes. "She is always so gentle and quiet—never was there such a quiet little thing!"

Unfortunately, in bestowing these embraces, a pin in her ladyship's head-dress slightly scratched the child's neck. This minor injury produced screams so violent they could hardly be outdone by any creature professedly noisy. The mother's consternation was excessive, yet it could not surpass the alarm of the Miss Steeles. In this critical emergency, everything possible was done by all three, guided solely by affection, to assuage the little sufferer's agony. She was seated in her mother's lap, covered in kisses, her wound bathed with lavender-water by one of the Miss Steeles kneeling to attend her, and her mouth stuffed with sugar-plums by the other. Such rewards for her tears rendered the child too wise to cease crying; she screamed and sobbed lustily, kicking her brothers for attempting to touch her, until all their united soothings proved ineffectual.

It was only when Lady Middleton remembered that apricot marmalade had successfully treated a bruised temple in a similar distress last week that hope arose. The remedy was eagerly proposed, and a slight intermission of screams upon hearing it gave reason to believe it would not be rejected. The child was carried out of the room in her mother's arms to seek the medicine. As the two boys chose to follow, though earnestly entreated to stay, the four young ladies were left in a quietness the room had not known for many hours.

"Poor little creatures!" said Miss Steele, as soon as they were gone. "It might have been a very sad accident."

"Yet I hardly know how," cried Marianne. "Unless it had been under totally different circumstances. But this is the usual way of heightening alarm where there is nothing to be alarmed at in reality."

"What a sweet woman Lady Middleton is!" said Lucy Steele.

Marianne remained silent; it was impossible for her to utter words she did not feel, however trivial the occasion. Thus, upon Elinor, the entire task of telling lies when politeness required it always fell. She did her best when thus called upon, speaking of Lady Middleton with more warmth than she felt, though with far less enthusiasm than Miss Lucy.

"And Sir John too," cried the elder sister, "what a charming man he is!"

Here too, Miss Dashwood's commendation, being merely simple and just, came forth without éclat. She merely observed that he was perfectly good-humoured and friendly.

"And what a charming little family they have! I never saw such fine children in my life.—I declare I quite doat upon them already, and indeed I am always distractedly fond of children."

"I should guess so," said Elinor, with a smile, "from what I have witnessed this morning."

"I have a notion," said Lucy, "you think the little Middletons rather too much indulged; perhaps they may be the outside of enough; but it is so natural in Lady Middleton. And for my part, I love to see children full of life and spirits; I cannot bear them if they are tame and quiet."

"I confess," replied Elinor, "that while I am at Barton Park, I never think of tame and quiet children with any abhorrence."

A short pause followed this speech, broken first by Miss Steele, who seemed very much disposed for conversation. She now said rather abruptly, "And how do you like Devonshire, Miss Dashwood? I suppose you were very sorry to leave Sussex."

Surprised by the familiarity of the question, or at least the manner in which it was spoken, Elinor replied that she was.

"Norland is a prodigious beautiful place, is not it?" added Miss Steele.

"We have heard Sir John admire it excessively," said Lucy, who seemed to think some apology necessary for her sister's freedom.

"I think every one *must* admire it," replied Elinor, "whoever has seen the place; though it is not to be supposed that anyone can estimate its beauties as we do."

"And had you a great many smart beaux there? I suppose you have not so many in this part of the world; for my part, I think they are a vast addition always."

"But why should you think," said Lucy, looking ashamed of her sister, "that there are not as many genteel young men in Devonshire as Sussex?"

"Nay, my dear, I'm sure I don't pretend to say that there aren't. I'm sure there's a vast many smart beaux in Exeter; but you know, how could I tell what smart beaux there might be about Norland? And I was only afraid the Miss Dashwoods might find it dull at Barton if they had not so many as they used to have. But perhaps you young ladies may not care about the beaux, and had as lief be without them as with them. For my part, I think they are vastly agreeable, provided they dress smart and behave civil. But I can't bear to see them dirty and nasty. Now there's Mr. Rose at Exeter, a prodigious smart young man, quite a beau, clerk to Mr. Simpson, you know; and yet if you do but meet him of a morning, he is not fit to be seen. I suppose your brother was quite a beau, Miss Dashwood, before he married, as he was so rich?"

"Upon my word," replied Elinor, "I cannot tell you, for I do not perfectly comprehend the meaning of the word. But this I can say, that if he ever was a beau before he married, he is one still, for there is not the smallest alteration in him."

"Oh! dear! one never thinks of married men's being beaux—they have something else to do."

"Lord! Anne," cried her sister, "you can talk of nothing but beaux;—you will make Miss Dashwood believe you think of nothing else." Turning the discourse, she began admiring the house and the furniture.

This specimen of the Miss Steeles was sufficient. The vulgar freedom and folly of the eldest left her no recommendation, and as Elinor was not blinded by the beauty or the shrewd look of the youngest to her want of real elegance and artlessness, she left the house without any wish of knowing them better.

Not so the Miss Steeles. They came from Exeter well-provided with admiration for the use of Sir John Middleton, his family, and all his relations. No niggardly proportion was now dealt out to his fair cousins, whom they declared to be the most beautiful, elegant, accomplished, and agreeable girls they had ever beheld, with whom they were particularly anxious to be better acquainted. To be better acquainted, therefore, Elinor soon found was her inevitable lot. For as Sir John was entirely on the side of the Miss Steeles, their party would be too strong for opposition, and that kind of intimacy must be submitted to which consists of sitting an hour or two together in the same room almost every day. Sir John could do no more; but he did not know that any more was required. To be together was, in his opinion, to be intimate, and while his continual schemes for their meeting were effectual, he had no doubt of their being established friends.

To do him justice, he did everything in his power to promote their unreserve, making the Miss Steeles acquainted with whatever he knew or supposed of his cousins' situations in the most delicate particulars. Elinor had not seen them more than twice before the eldest wished her joy on her sister's having been so lucky as to make a conquest of a very smart beau since she came to Barton.

"'Twill be a fine thing to have her married so young to be sure," said she, "and I hear he is quite a beau, and prodigious handsome. And I hope you may have as good luck yourself soon,—but perhaps you may have a friend in the corner already."

Elinor could not suppose that Sir John would be more nice in proclaiming his suspicions of her regard for Edward than he had been with respect to Marianne. Indeed, it was rather his favourite joke of the two, as being somewhat newer and more conjectural. Since Edward's visit, they had never dined together without his drinking to her best affections with so much significance and so many nods and winks as to excite general attention. The letter F—had likewise been invariably brought forward, found productive of such countless jokes that its character as the wittiest letter in the alphabet had been long established with Elinor.

The Miss Steeles, as she expected, now had all the benefit of these jokes. In the eldest of them, they raised a curiosity to know the name of the gentleman alluded to, which, though often impertinently expressed, was perfectly of a piece with her general inquisitiveness into the concerns of their family.
