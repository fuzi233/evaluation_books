Mrs. Hale watched Margaret with a mixture of amusement and deep, childish wonder regarding the upcoming dinner party at Mr. Thornton's. Like a child anticipating a treat, she sought to have every detail described before the event, though her curiosity was tinged with the vanity of a former beauty who had known better days. Now, confined by ill health, she found solace in imagining her daughter transformed from the modest clergyman's wife into a lady of society. She fretted over the selection of garments, oscillating between a white silk gown that might have yellowed, a pink gauze, or a green silk, feeling the "embarrassment of riches" in her uncertainty.

Margaret, usually composed, became animated by her mother's nervous inquiries. She offered to try on each dress, though she suppressed her usual playful tricks, sensing that her mother viewed them as interruptions to this serious business. The conversation shifted abruptly when Margaret mentioned her engagement to Bessy Higgins, who reacted with startling intensity. To Bessy, the prospect of Margaret dining at Marlborough Mills—a home frequented by the Mayor and Members of Parliament—was shocking. With her limited understanding of wealth, measured by yards of cloth, Bessy feared Margaret would be "looked down upon" in her print gown.

Yet, Margaret's spirit lifted at the challenge. She resolved to wear her finest white silk, a gown once worn for a cousin's wedding, confident in her ability to uphold the honor of the occasion. As they spoke, however, the shadows of the outside world crept into the room. Bessy grew feverish, her vision clouded by distressing dreams of Margaret in shining raiment, a vision that haunted her afflicted mind. She clung to the idea that these dreams foretold reality, seeking physical contact with Margaret as if to anchor herself against the chaos of her imagination.

Despite Bessy's agitation, the gravity of the situation deepened when Nicholas Higgins returned. His demeanor was sharp, his words heavy with the bitterness of the strike. He spoke of the mill owners' contracts, predicting that within weeks, the masters would capitulate to secure their remaining orders. He boasted of a march of seven hundred strikers, fueled by the promise of a five percent increase, convinced of their inevitable victory. His confidence, however, clashed violently with the grim reality revealed moments later.

In the adjacent room, the atmosphere was suffocating. John Boucher, a neighbor with a large family, stood pale and gaunt, recounting the horrors of starvation. His wife, unable to endure the sight of their children starving, had fallen into a wasting sickness, sinking away despite adequate food. Boucher, driven by desperation and a twisted logic, declared he would return to work at the old, low wages if his wife died before the strike yielded results. He spoke of hating the Union leaders if they failed, his voice trembling with a rage born of helpless love.

Nicholas Higgins, hearing this, halted in his tracks. The man's face, white with terror and exhaustion, forced Margaret to weep. Higgins, realizing the fatal flaw in his argument, turned to Boucher with a sudden humility. "So help me God!" he cried, acknowledging that if his advice led to ruin, it was not his fault, but the sin of those who left him ignorant. He pleaded for faith in the Union, insisting that patience would eventually force the masters to bend.

Silent witnesses to this tragedy, Margaret and Bessy shared a look of profound sorrow. When Boucher's wife finally succumbed, leaving her husband and children destitute, Margaret could do nothing but offer what little money she possessed. She handed cash to Bessy, instructing her to provide food for Boucher's children without revealing the source, fearing judgment. Bessy accepted the gift, her heart warming despite the crushing weight of the news. As the day wore on, Bessy grew increasingly faint, her dreams and waking terrors blending into a single, terrifying haze. Margaret, watching her friend fade, realized that the "gleam of brightness" inside the Hale household was necessary precisely because the world outside was so thoroughly engulfed in gloom. The stark contrast between the warm, hopeful interior and the brutal, hungry exterior served only to heighten the agony of the present moment, leaving Margaret to wonder how long she could bear the silence of those who suffered most.
