The morning sun cast a pale, watery light over the veranda of the Marlstone Hotel, illuminating the dust motes dancing above the empty plates of the previous night. Nathaniel Burton Cupples stood there, his gaze fixed not on the horizon, but on the plate before him, contemplating the mechanics of sustenance. To Cupples, the concept of "breakfast" was not merely a culinary event but a necessary biological imperative, a conscious act requiring the same deliberation as any other function of his orderly existence. He calculated the deficit of the prior day, where the shock of the discovery had left him starved, and resolved with mathematical precision to consume a third slice of toast and an extra egg this morning. The remainder of the caloric debt would be addressed at luncheon; for now, the immediate task was to survey the landscape.

With the detached appreciation of a connoisseur, Cupples scanned the rugged coastline. A massive, perforated rock jutted from the glassy sea, standing as a silent sentinel against the vast, tilted plains of pasture and woodland that sloped upward toward the distant, hazy moors. Cupples possessed a deep, almost religious devotion to such scenery. He was a man of middle height and spare frame, nearing sixty, with a constitution that was delicate yet wiry. A sparse, straggling beard framed a thin but kindly mouth, while his keen, pleasant eyes and sharp nose lent him an air of clerical severity, reinforced by his dark, commonplace attire and soft black hat. The overall effect was undeniably priestly. His mind was a fortress of conscientious industry, devoid of imagination, shaped by a childhood spent in the gloomy domestic establishment of his father's household—a home famously recruited via advertisements describing it as a "serious family." From that fortress, Cupples had escaped with two rare gifts: an inexhaustible kindness of heart and a capacity for innocent gaiety that required no humor to fuel it. Once, with his clerical training, he might have worn the scarlet hat; instead, he found his true vocation among books and museums, a respected member of the London Positivist Society and a retired banker. His life was austere but not unhappy, populated by the quiet company of professors and curators, where he felt most at home. His favorite author was Montaigne.

As Cupples was concluding his solitary meal at a small table on the veranda, the rhythmic rumble of a heavy motor car broke the morning stillness. It turned onto the hotel drive, kicking up a cloud of dust.

"Who is this?" Cupples asked the waiter, who was wiping a tray.

"The manager," the young man replied listlessly, his eyes fixed on the floor. "He's been to meet a gentleman by the train."

The car halted, and the porter hurried forward. Cupples let out a sound of genuine delight as a tall, loosely built man emerged from the vehicle and ascended the steps, tossing his hat onto a nearby chair. The newcomer was significantly younger, his face high-boned and quixotic, wearing a pleasant, slightly manic smile. His rough tweed suit, untidy hair, and short moustache suggested a lack of concern for convention.

"Cupples, by all that's miraculous!" the man cried, lunging forward before Cupples could rise. He seized Cupples' hand in a grip that was surprisingly hard. "My luck is serving me today," the newcomer continued, his speech spastic with excitement. "This is the second slice within an hour. How are you, my best of friends? And why are you here? Why sit by that ruined breakfast? Dost thou its former pride recall, or ponder how it passed away? I am glad to see you!"

"I was half expecting you, Trent," Cupples replied, his face lighting up with warm smiles. "You are looking splendid, my dear fellow. I will tell you all about it. But you cannot have had your own breakfast yet. Will you join me at my table?"

"Rather!" Trent exclaimed. "An enormous great breakfast, too—with refined conversation and tears of recognition never dry. Will you get young Siegfried to lay a place for me while I wash? I shan't be three minutes."

Trent vanished into the hotel. Cupples, after a brief pause, retreated to the porter's office to make a telephone call, returning moments later to find his friend already seated, pouring tea with an unaffected interest in the menu.

"I expect this to be a hard day for me," Trent said, his voice exhibiting the curious, jerky cadence that seemed habitual to him. "I shan't eat again till the evening, very likely. You guess why I'm here, don't you?"

"Undoubtedly," Cupples replied. "You have come down to write about the murder."

"That is rather a colourless way of stating it," Trent corrected, dissecting a piece of sole with surgical precision. "I should prefer to say I have come down in the character of avenger of blood, to hunt down the guilty and vindicate the honour of society. That is my line of business. Families wait on at their private residences. I say, Cupples, I have made a good beginning already. Wait a bit, and I'll tell you."

A silence fell between them as Trent ate swiftly and abstractedly, while Cupples watched him with happy anticipation.

"Your manager here," Trent finally said, "is a fellow of remarkable judgment. He is an admirer of mine. He knows more about my best cases than I do myself. The *Record* wired last night to say I was coming, and when I stepped off the train at seven this morning, there he was waiting with a motor car the size of a haystack. He is beside himself with joy at having me here. It is fame." He took a sip of tea. "Almost his first words were to ask if I would like to see the body of the murdered man—he thought he could manage it for me. He is as keen as a razor. The body lies in Dr. Stock's surgery, down in the village, exactly as it was found. It's to be post-mortemmed this morning, so I was only just in time. Well, he ran me down to the doctor's, giving me full particulars all the way. I was pretty well *au fait* by the time we arrived. I suppose the manager of a place like this has some pull with the doctor. Anyhow, he made no difficulties, nor did the constable on duty, though he was careful to insist on my not giving him away in the paper."

"I saw the body before it was removed," Cupples remarked. "I should not have said there was anything remarkable about it, except that the shot in the eye had scarcely disfigured the face and caused little effusion of blood. The wrists were scratched and bruised. I expect that, with your trained faculties, you were able to remark other details of a suggestive nature."

"Other details, certainly; but I don't know that they suggest anything. They are merely odd. Take the wrists, for instance. How was it you could see bruises and scratches on them? I dare say you saw something of Manderson down here before the murder."

"Certainly," Cupples said.

"Well, did you ever see his wrists?"

Cupples reflected. "No. Now you raise the point, I am reminded that when I interviewed Manderson here he was wearing stiff cuffs, coming well down over his hands."

"He always did," Trent said. "My friend the manager says so. I pointed out to him the fact you didn't observe, that there were no cuffs visible, and that they had, indeed, been dragged up inside the coat-sleeves, as yours would be if you hurried into a coat without pulling your cuffs down. That was why you saw his wrists."

"Well, I call that suggestive," Cupples observed mildly. "You might infer, perhaps, that when he got up he hurried over his dressing."

"Yes, but did he? The manager said just what you say. 'He was always a bit of a swell in his dress,' he told me, and he drew the inference that when Manderson got up in that mysterious way, before the house was stirring, and went out into the grounds, he was in a great hurry. 'Look at his shoes,' he said to me: 'Mr. Manderson was always specially neat about his footwear. But those shoe-laces were tied in a hurry.' I agreed. 'And he left his false teeth in his room,' said the manager. 'Doesn't that prove he was flustered and hurried?' I allowed that it looked like it. But I said, 'Look here: if he was so very much pressed, why did he part his hair so carefully? That parting is a work of art. Why did he put on so much? for he had on a complete outfit of underclothing, studs in his shirt, sock-suspenders, a watch and chain, money and keys and things in his pockets. That's what I said to the manager. He couldn't find an explanation. Can you?"

Cupples considered. "Those facts might suggest that he was hurried only at the end of his dressing. Coat and shoes would come last."

"But not false teeth. You ask anybody who wears them. And besides, I'm told he hadn't washed at all on getting up, which in a neat man looks like his being in a violent hurry from the beginning. And here's another thing. One of his waistcoat pockets was lined with wash-leather for the reception of his gold watch. But he had put his watch into the pocket on the other side. Anybody who has settled habits can see how odd that is. The fact is, there are signs of great agitation and haste, and there are signs of exactly the opposite. For the present I am not guessing. I must reconnoitre the ground first, if I can manage to get the right side of the people of the house."

Cupples smiled benevolently. "That is precisely the point," he said, "on which I can be of some assistance to you." Trent glanced up in surprise. "I told you I half expected you. I will explain the situation. Mrs. Manderson, who is my niece—"

"What!" Trent laid down his knife and fork with a clatter. "Cupples, you are jesting with me."

"I am perfectly serious, Trent, really," Cupples returned earnestly. "Her father, John Peter Domecq, was my wife's brother. I never mentioned my niece or her marriage to you before, I suppose. To tell the truth, it has always been a painful subject to me, and I have avoided discussing it with anybody. To return to what I was about to say: last night, when I was over at the house—you can see it from here. You passed it in the car." He indicated a red roof among poplars some three hundred yards away, the only building separate from the tiny village below.

"Certainly I did," said Trent. "The manager told me all about it, among other things, as he drove me in from Bishopsbridge."

"Other people here have heard of you and your performances," Cupples continued. "As I was saying, when I was over there last night, Mr. Bunner, who is one of Manderson's two secretaries, expressed a hope that the *Record* would send you down to deal with the case, as the police seemed quite at a loss. He mentioned one or two of your past successes, and Mabel—my niece—was interested when I told her afterwards. She is bearing up wonderfully well, Trent; she has remarkable fortitude of character. She said she remembered reading your articles about the Abinger case. She has a great horror of the newspaper side of this sad business, and she had entreated me to do anything I could to keep journalists away from the place—I'm sure you can understand her feeling, Trent; it isn't really any reflection on that profession. But she said you appeared to have great powers as a detective, and she would not stand in the way of anything that might clear up the crime. Then I told her you were a personal friend of mine, and gave you a good character for tact and consideration of others' feelings; and it ended in her saying that, if you should come, she would like you to be helped in every way."

Trent leaned across the table and shook Cupples' hand in silence. Cupples, delighted with the turn of events, resumed:

"I spoke to my niece on the telephone only just now, and she is glad you are here. She asks me to say that you may make any enquiries you like, and she puts the house and grounds at your disposal. She had rather not see you herself; she is keeping to her own sitting-room. She has already been interviewed by a detective officer who is there, and she feels unequal to any more. She adds that she does not believe she could say anything that would be of the smallest use. The two secretaries and Martin, the butler (who is a most intelligent man), could tell you all you want to know, she thinks."

Trent finished his breakfast with a thoughtful brow. He filled a pipe slowly and seated himself on the rail of the veranda. "Cupples," he said quietly, "is there anything about this business that you know and would rather not tell me?"

Cupples gave a slight start, turning an astonished gaze on the questioner. "What do you mean?" he said.

"I mean about the Mandersons. Look here! Shall I tell you a thing that strikes me about this affair at the very beginning? Here's a man suddenly and violently killed, and nobody's heart seems to be broken about it, to say the least. The manager of this hotel spoke to me about him as coolly as if he'd never set eyes on him, though I understand they've been neighbours every summer for some years. Then you talk about the thing in the coldest of blood. And Mrs. Manderson—well, you won't mind my saying that I have heard of women being more cut up about their husbands being murdered than she seems to be. Is there something in this, Cupples, or is it my fancy? Was there something queer about Manderson? I travelled on the same boat with him once, but never spoke to him.
