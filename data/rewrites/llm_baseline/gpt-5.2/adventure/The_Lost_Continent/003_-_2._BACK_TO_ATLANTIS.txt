Tatho’s words were no draught of poppy to lull a man into sleep. They lay on my tongue like smoke from green wood, stinging and bitter, and all that night I turned them over as one turns a hard seed between the teeth, wondering why it will not break.

For twenty years I had wrapped myself in the governance of Yucatan as in a plain cloak against weather—work, justice, harvests counted, granaries watched, roads cut through forest—so completely that I had let the winds from Atlantis blow past unheard. Yet even in that long labor there had been signs: the mariner folk who crossed the seas spoke with bridled mouths, and only such news as the Empress willed was allowed to seep beyond her borders, like broth strained thin so none can taste what meat went into it. I had been too busy to pry at a veil set there on purpose; and I have always held it foul practice to put men to torment because they have been ordered into silence. But the iron discipline of our Priestly Clan knows little of private scruple.

As is customary, my office was taken from me without warning, as a trencher is whisked from before a guest when the feast is declared ended. From that instant all papers and authority belonged to my successor. By courtesy I might remain as a guest in the pyramid that had been mine, to see another sunrise; but I was plainly enjoined to quit the territory at the topmost of my speed and hasten to report in Atlantis.

Tatho—whatever else he had become—was eager to further my interests as far as his hands could reach. Before dawn he was again beside me, already dressed, already alert, his mind clinking like a purse of coin.

“I put all my resources at your disposal,” he said.

I had little enough to ask. “A ship to take me home,” I told him, “and I shall be your debtor.”

The request surprised him. “That you may certainly have if you wish it. But my ships are foul with the long passage and need a careen. If you take them, you will make a slow voyage to Atlantis. Why do you not take your own navy? The ships are in harbour now—I saw them there when we came in. Brave ships they are too.”

“But not mine,” I said. “That navy belongs to Yucatan.”

“Well, Deucalion,” he returned, “you are Yucatan—or rather, you were yesterday, and have been these twenty years.”

I saw his meaning, and it soured in me. I answered stiffly that the ships were owned by private merchants or belonged to the State, and I could not claim so much as a ten-slave galley.

Tatho shrugged. “I suppose you know your own policies best. Yet to me it seems risky for a man who has attained a position like yours and mine not to have provided himself with a stout navy of his own. One never knows when a recall may be sent; and through lack of precautions a life’s earnings may be lost in a dozen hours.”

“I have no fear for mine,” I said.

“Of course not, because you know me to be your friend. But had another man been appointed to this vice-royalty, you might have been sadly shorn. It is not many fellows who can resist a snug hoard ready and waiting in the very coffers they have come to line.”

“My Lord Tatho,” I said, “it is clear to me that you and I have grown to be of different tastes. All the hoard I have made for myself in this colony few men would covet. I have the poor clothes you see me in this moment, and a box of drugs such as I have found useful to the stomach. I possess also three slaves—two scribes, and the third a sturdy savage from Europe—who cooks my victual and fills for me the bath. For my maintenance during my years of service here, I have bled the State of a soldier’s ration and nothing beyond; and if in my name any man has mulcted a creature in Yucatan of so much as an ounce of bronze, I request you as a last service to have that man hanged for me as a liar and a thief.”

He looked at me as though I were a curious dish set down before him—plain, perhaps, but prepared with an unfamiliar spice. “I do not know whether I admire you most or pity. I do not know whether to be astonished or to despise. We had heard of your uprightness over yonder in Atlantis, of your sternness and your justice, but I swear by the old Gods no soul guessed you carried your fancy so far as this. Why, man—money is power. With money and the resources money can buy, nothing could stop a fellow like you; whilst without it you may be tripped up and trodden down irrevocably at the first puny reverse.”

“The Gods will choose my fate.”

“Possibly. But for mine, I prefer to nourish it myself.” His voice warmed as he spoke, as though he savoured the fatness of his own intention. “I tell you with frankness I have not come here to follow the pattern you have made for a vice-royalty. I shall govern Yucatan wisely and well to the best of my ability; but I shall govern it also for the good of Tatho, the viceroy. I have brought with me my navy of eight ships and a personal bodyguard. There is my wife also, and her women and her slaves. All these must be provided for. And why indeed should it be otherwise? If a people is to be governed, it should be their privilege to pay handsomely for their prince.”

“We shall not agree,” I told him. “You have the power now, and can employ it as you choose. If I thought it would be of any use, I should like to supplicate you most humbly to deal with lenience when you come to tax these people who are under you. They have grown very dear to me.”

“I have disgusted you with me, and I am grieved for it. But even to retain your good opinion, Deucalion—which I value more than that of any man living—I cannot do here as you have done. It would be impossible, even if I wished it. You must not judge all other men by your own strong standard: a Tatho is by no means a colossus like a Deucalion. And besides, I have a wife and children, and they must be provided for, even if I neglect myself.”

“Ah,” I said, “there it does seem that I possess the advantage. I have no wife, to clog me.”

He caught at the word as though it were a sweetmeat. “It seems to me you have nothing that makes life worth living. You have neither wife, children, riches, cooks, retinue, dresses, nor anything else in proportion to your station. You will pardon my saying it, old comrade, but you are plaguey ignorant about some matters. For example—you do not know how to dine.”

At that, the whole man of him became chef and critic, and the memory of sea fare rose in his speech like rancid oil. “During every day of a very weary voyage, I promised myself, sitting before meagre sea victual, that presently the abstinence would be repaid by Deucalion’s welcoming feast. Oh, I tell you that feast was one of the vividest things that ever came before my eyes. And then when we get to the actuality—what was it? Why, a country farmer every day sits down to more delicate fare. You told me how it was prepared. Well, your savage from Europe may be lusty, and perchance faithful, but he is a devil-possessed cook. Gods! I have lived better on a campaign.

“I know this is a colony here, without home refinements; but if in the days to come the deer of the forest, the fish of the stream, and the other resources of the place are not put to better use than heretofore, I shall see it my duty as ruler to fry some of the kitchen staff alive in grease so as to encourage better cookery. Gods! Deucalion, have you forgotten what it is to have a palate? And have you no esteem for your own dignity? Man, look at your clothes. You are garbed like a herdsman, and you have not a gaud or a jewel to brighten you.”

I could not help, even in my chill, the fleeting thought that a man may rule whole provinces with a mind trained to mysteries, and yet be judged, finally, by the scent that rises from his own pot. The domestic is the oldest temple; the tongue keeps its own gods.

“I eat,” I said, “when my hunger bids me, and I carry this one robe upon my person till it is worn out and needs replacement. The grossness of excessive banqueting and the effeminacy of many clothes are attainments that never met my fancy. But I think we have talked here over long, and there seems little chance of our finding agreement. You have changed, Tatho, with the years, and perhaps I have changed also. These alterations creep imperceptibly into one’s being as time advances. Let us part now, and forgetting these present differences, remember only our friendship of twenty years agone. That for me, at any rate, has always had a pleasant savour when called up into the memory.”

He bowed his head. “So be it,” he said. “And I would still charge myself upon your bounty for that ship. Dawn cannot be far off now, and it is not decent that the man who has ruled here so long should walk in daylight through the streets on the morning after his dismissal.”

“So be it,” I said.

“You shall have my poor navy,” he answered. “I could have wished you had asked me something greater.”

“Not the navy, Tatho. One small ship. Believe me, more is wasted.”

“Now there,” he said, and for an instant the old comrade vanished behind the new viceroy, “I shall act the tyrant. I am viceroy here now, and will have my way in this. You may go naked of all possessions—that I cannot help. But depart for Atlantis unattended, that you shall not.”

And so, since the choice was set beyond me, it was in the Bear—Tatho’s own private ship—with all the rest of his navy sailing in escort, that I did finally make my transit.

Yet we did not start at once. The vessels lay moored against the stone quays of the inner harbour, gutted of their stores, crews dulled and hollow-eyed after the long passage, and it would have been suicide to drive them straight back into the sea. So the courtesies were fulfilled by the craft whereon I abode hauling out into the entrance basin and anchoring there in the swells of the fairway; and forthwith she and her consorts took in wood and water, cured meat and fish ashore, and refitted in all needful ways with all attainable speed.

For myself there came, for the first time in twenty busy years, a breathing space from work. I had no further connection with the country of my labours; officially, indeed, I had left it already. Into the ship’s working it was contrary to rule that I should make inspection or show interest, since all sea matters were the exclusive property of the Mariners’ Guild, secured to them by royal patent and most jealously guarded. So there remained to me hours to gaze upon the quays, the harbours, the palaces, and the pyramids of that splendid city I had watched grow stone by stone from foundations I could still see in my mind like the first kneading of dough. Or I would roam my eye over the pastures and grain lands beyond the walls, and look longingly at the dense forests behind, from which field by field we had so tediously ripped our territory.

Would Tatho continue the work so healthily begun? I trusted so, even in spite of his selfish words; for men are seldom one thing only, and even the greedy must eat, and so must keep the fields from failing. At all hours—during the radiance of our Lord the Sun or under the stars of night—I was free to pursue that study of the higher mysteries on which we of the Priests’ Clan are trained to set our minds, without aid of book or instrument, image or temple. The harbour, too, had its own music: the slap of water against stone, the rasp of rope through a sailor’s hand, the hollow thunk of casks rolled, and over all the constant, living hiss of wind. I found myself listening as I had not listened in years, and thinking how much of a man’s well-being is governed by what his ear is forced to endure. Here, at least, there was no ceaseless metallic din, no grinding engine-roar to drown thought into submission—only the honest labour-sounds of wood and water, and the human voice still able to carry meaning.

The refitting of the navy was gone about with speed. Never, it is said, had ships been reprovisioned and caulked and remanned so swiftly for an over-ocean voyage. Indeed, barely over a month from the day they brought up in the harbour, they put out beyond the walls and began their eastward passage over the hills and dale of the ocean.

Rowing-slaves from Europe for this long sea passage are not taken now, owing to the difficulty of provisioning them, for modern humanity forbids the practice of letting them eat one another according to the home custom of their continent. Sails alone are but an indifferent stand-by. But modern science has shown how to extract force from the Sun when He is free from cloud, and this—kept secret by mariners—draws sea-water at the forepart of the vessel and ejects it with such force at the stern that she is appreciably driven forward even with the wind adverse.

In another matter also has navigation vastly improved. It is not necessary now, as formerly, to trust wholly to a starry night (when beyond sight of land) to find direction. A little image has been made and is stood balanced in the forepart of every vessel, with an arm outstretched, pointing constantly toward where the Southern Cross lies in the Heavens. So, by setting an angle, can a just course be correctly steered. Other instruments have they also for finding a true position on the ocean wastes, for the newer mariner, when he is at sea, puts little trust in the Gods and confides mightily in his own thews and wits.

Still, it is amusing to see these tarry fellows, even in this modern day, take their last farewell of the harbour town. The ship is stowed, and all ready for sea, and they wash and put on all their bravery of attire. Ashore they go, faces long with piety, and seek some obscure temple whose God has little flavour with shore folk; and there they make sacrifice with clamour and lavish outlay. Finally there follows a feast in honour of the God, and they arrive back on board and put to sea for the most part drunken, heavy and evil-humoured with gluttony and their other excesses.

The smell of their going lingered on the water: sour beer-breath, fat smoke, and the rank sweetness of spilled offerings. It put me in mind, not without contempt, how art and worship alike are so easily made into show—how quickly a living rite is turned into a purchased spectacle, loud enough to satisfy the ear, empty enough to leave the heart unfed.

The voyage itself was very different from my previous sea-going. There was no creeping timorously along in touch with coasts. We stood straight across the open gulf in the direction of home, came up with the band of the Carib Islands, and worked confidently through them as though they had been signposts to mark a sea highway; and stopped only twice to replenish with wood, water, and fruit. These commodities the savages brought us freely, so great was their subjection; and in neither place did we have even the semblance of a fight. It was a great certificate of the growing power of Atlantis and her finest over-sea colony.

Then boldly on we went across the vast ocean beyond, with never a sacrifice to implore the Gods that They should help our direction. One might feel censure toward these rugged mariners for impiety; but one could not help admiration for their lusty skill and confidence. The dangers of the desolate sea are dealt out as the Gods will, and man can only take them as they come.

Storms we encountered, and the mariners fought them with stubborn endurance; twice a blazing stone from Heaven hissed into the sea beside us, though without injuring any of our ships; and, as was unavoidable, the great beasts of the sea hunted us with their accustomed savagery.

But only once did we suffer material loss from these last, and that was when three of the greater sea lizards attacked the Bear, the ship whereon I travelled, at one and the same time.

The hour of their onset was during the blazing midday heat, with the Sun at the full of His power; our machines were drinking His strength greedily, and the vessel drove forward faster than a man on dry land could walk. Yet for all that thrust of stolen sunlight, she might as well have been standing still when the beasts sighted her.

There were three of them. We saw them come up over the curve of the horizon, beating the sea into foam with their flappers and waving their great necks like masts as they swam. Our navy was spread out in a long line of ships, and in olden days each beast would have chosen a separate prey and gone for it. But like men, these beasts have learned the necessities of warfare: they hunt in pack now, and do not separate their forces. It was plain they were making for our ship.

Tob, the captain, would have had me go into the after-castle and be secure from marauding. He was responsible to Lord Tatho, he said, for my safe conduct; it was certain the beasts would contrive to seize some of the ship’s company before they were satiated; and if the hap came to Lord Deucalion, then he—Tob—would have to give himself voluntarily to the beasts to escape a very painful death at Tatho’s hands later on.

However, my mind was set. A man can never have too much experience in fighting enemies, whether human or bestial, and the attack of these creatures was new to me, and I was fain to learn its method. So I gave the captain a letter to Tatho saying how the matter lay (for which the rude fellow seemed little enough grateful), and stayed in my chair under the awning.

The beasts surged up with champing jaws, and all the shipmen stood armed on their defence. They came alongside—two females (the smaller) on one flank, the giant male by himself on the other. Their great heads swooped about as high as the yards that held the sails, and the reek from them gave one physical sickness: a hot stench like rotten fish left in sun, mixed with a sour, swampy breath that seemed to coat the back of the throat. It was as though the sea itself had opened a pantry of corruption and flung it in our faces.

The shipmen faced the monsters with sturdy courage. Arrows were useless against those smooth, bull-like hides. Even throwing fire could not so much as singe them. Nothing but twenty axe blows delivered together on an attacking head could beat it back, and even these succeeded only through sheer weight of metal and did not make so much as the scratch of a wound.

During all time beasts have disputed with man the mastery of the earth, and it is only in Atlantis and Egypt and Yucatan that man has dared to hold his own, and fight them with a mind made strong by many previous victories. In Europe and mid-Africa the greater beasts hold full dominion, and man admits his puny number and force, and lives in earth crannies and the higher tree-tops as a confessed fugitive. Upon the great oceans the beasts are lords, unchecked.

Still, out upon that desolate sea, though the giant lizards were new to me, it was a pleasure to pit knowledge of war against brute strength and courage. Ever since the first men did their business upon great waters, they have fought these beasts with desperation. Hiding coward-like in a hold is useless; for if the enemy cannot find men above decks to glut them, they will break a ship with their paddles, and so all will be slain. It is recognised, therefore, that the fight must go forward as desperately as may be, and can only end when the beasts have got their prey and have gone away satisfied.

It was in a one-sided conflict after this fashion, then, that I found myself, and felt the joy once more to have my thews in action. But after my axe had got in a dozen lusty blows—which, for all the harm they did, might have been delivered against a city wall, or indeed against the Ark of the Mysteries itself—I sought about till I found a lance, and with that made very different play.

The decks were slippery with the horrid slime of them. The crew surged in battling; and moreover, by reason of Tob’s threats, they constantly offered themselves as a rampart before me. But I gave a few shrewd progues with the lance to show I did not choose my will to be overridden, and presently I was given room to manoeuvre.

The eyes of these lizards are small and set deep in bony sockets, but I judged them vulnerable. It was upon the eyes that I made my attack.

Deliberately I placed myself in the sight of one of the beasts and offered my body to its onset. The challenge was accepted. It swooped like a dropping stone, and I swerved and drove the lance into its oozy eye. I thanked the Gods then that I had been trained with the lance till certain aim was instinct. The blade went true and stuck there; the shaft broke in my hand.

The beast drew off blinded and bellowing, beating the sea into a cataract of foam. I saw it bend its great long neck and rub its head—spear still fixed—against its back, thereby enduring new agonies without dislodging the weapon. Then, finding it of no avail, it set off for the place from which it came with extraordinary quickness, and rapidly grew smaller against the horizon.

The male and the other female had also left us, but not in similar plight. Tob, seeing my resolve to take hazards, deliberately thrust a shipman into the jaws of each of the others so they might be sated and gone.

It was clear he dreaded for his own skin if I came by harm. And I thought, with a warming heart, of the threats Tatho must have used in his kind anxiety for my safety. It is pleasant when one’s old friends do not omit to pay these little attentions.
