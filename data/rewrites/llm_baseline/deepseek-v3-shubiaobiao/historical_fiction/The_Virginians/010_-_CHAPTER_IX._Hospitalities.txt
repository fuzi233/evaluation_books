His Excellency the Commander-in-Chief made his journey to Madam Esmond’s estate in a manner befitting the highest dignitary of His Majesty’s American dominions. A guard of dragoons led the procession from Williamsburg, the air thick with cheers—most notably from the enslaved folk, whose voices rose in dutiful clamor. The General himself traveled in his grand carriage, emblazoned with insignias, while Captain Talmadge, his Master of the Horse, rode alongside. Within the coach sat Major Danvers, the aide-de-camp, and the unlikeliest of companions—Mr. Franklin of Philadelphia, a man whose sharp wit and curious learning had already earned the begrudging respect of the Governor’s retinue.

“Remarkable,” mused His Excellency, tipping his hat with a flourish, “how a man of such humble beginnings has acquired such polish. One might almost mistake you for a gentleman, Mr. Franklin.”

The postmaster bowed. “Fortune has occasionally placed me in company where refinement was unavoidable. I merely observed and imitated.”

“Education, now—” The General waved a hand dismissively. “I’ve known officers who couldn’t spell ‘bull’ with one ‘b’ or two. The Duke of Marlborough himself was no scholar, yet he thrashed the French soundly enough.”

“So I have heard,” said Franklin mildly.

“And my own Duke—ah, I see that twinkle in your eye, sir! No scholar either, but a soldier through and through. Saved the Crown for His Majesty, God bless him.”

Franklin doffed his hat as powdered wig dust scattered. “A finer horseman I never saw.”

“A finer master, a finer friend! You’ll drink his health tonight, Franklin, mark my words.”

The carriage jolted violently over the uneven road, prompting fresh oaths from the General. Just then, a rider approached—a tall young man on a sturdy gelding, flanked by two liveried enslaved boys.

“Ah! Mr. Washington,” said the aide-de-camp.

“Fine figure on a horse,” the General remarked. “Wouldn’t mind him as a corporal. Knows the country, eh, Franklin?”

“Intimately,” Franklin agreed.

Washington saluted. “Your Excellency honors us.”

“No honor, sir—duty! And we’ll see you in proper regimentals soon enough, mark me.”

As the lumbering coach rolled toward Castlewood, a ragtag parade formed in its wake—Lieutenant-Governor Dinwiddie with his chaplain in tow, the schoolmaster in his grandest wig, the local justice and his wife on muleback, all too deferential to overtake the great man. Danvers and Franklin exchanged amused glances before urging the stragglers onward.

The feast that awaited them was worthy of a London alderman. Terrapin stew, gumbo thick with spices, shad fresh from the river—each dish proclaimed the skill of Madam Esmond’s cooks, trained under her exacting eye. She herself presided with effortless grace, carving joints with slender wrists bared beneath lace cuffs, pressing delicacies upon her guests.

“The jowl of this salmon is for Your Excellency—I insist! Mr. Franklin, must you refuse our wine? Justice Laws, another slice of woodcock pie?”

The judge bowed. “My wife often laments she lacks your hand with pastry, madam.”

Franklin’s gaze flicked to her hands. “A hand many would admire.”

She ignored the implication, though her son George frowned.

As the meal wore on, the talk turned to war—roads, rivers, supply trains. Washington traced imaginary battle lines with bread crumbs until a servant swept them away with the dishes. When the table cleared, Madam Esmond rose, glass in hand.

“Gentlemen—His Majesty.”

The toast was drunk with fervor. Then, with a curtsy, she withdrew—only to weep in the drawing-room moments later.

“Forgive me,” she murmured to Mrs. Laws. “But he takes my son. Who knows if he’ll return?”

The ladies murmured comforts, but she straightened. “No. I’ll not burden him with tears. An Esmond does his duty.”

Meanwhile, the wine flowed freely. Broadbent, the disreputable parson, was deep in his cups; the General’s face glowed crimson. Young George, usually abstemious, matched them drink for drink, his jabs at Washington growing sharper.

“You’ll join the ranks at last, Colonel,” he sneered. “Or will you survey the battlefield before committing?”

Washington stood abruptly. “Excuse me.”

Outside, pacing the veranda, he met Madam Esmond.

“The gentlemen are still at their sport?” she asked lightly.

“If drink wins battles, we shall conquer Canada by dawn,” he muttered.

She laid a hand on his arm. “And my sons?”

“Harry is steady. George—” He hesitated.

Her grip tightened. “What of George?”

But the answer was drowned by raucous laughter from within. The night wore on, glasses refilled, toasts called—while Washington, stiff and silent, watched the moon rise over the Virginia woods.
