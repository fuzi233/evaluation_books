The sun burned with an intensity that felt less like warmth and more like exposure, a harsh glare that suggested the day's stability was a fragile illusion. I glanced down at the milky sapphire ring on my finger, checking the embedded star for clarity. It was indistinct, blurred beyond recognition, offering no celestial anchor. Rising from the breakfast table, I moved to the window, seeking confirmation in the landscape outside. The night had delivered a torrential downpour, leaving the lawn slick and churned. A thrush, desperate and frantic, was tearing at the shell of a snail, trying to extract sustenance from the debris.

As I twisted the ring, attempting to catch the faint reflection of the star against the sunlight, a sharp, black eye snapped open within the milky haze of the gemstone. The sight jolted me violently; I dropped the ring, and in the split second it hit the floor, the eye vanished. Simultaneously, the sun was swallowed. A thick, dark vapor rolled across the heavens, blotting out the light in moments. The air grew heavy and sultry, then a sudden gust tore through the room. Lightning fractured the sky, followed instantly by a single, sharp crack of thunder. Rain began to fall in sheets.

I had flung the window open, standing exposed to the deluge, when I noticed a raven approaching over the sodden grass. Its gait was solemn, utterly indifferent to the drowning world around it. Recognizing the intruder, I felt a surge of relief that I remained safely indoors on the ground floor. Yet, a gnawing certainty settled in my gut: disaster was imminent unless I took action. The bird drew closer, executed a formal bow, and launched itself onto the windowsill. With a fluid motion, it crossed the ledge, descended into the room, and strode purposefully toward the door. Assuming it intended for the library, I prepared to follow, vowing not to take a single step if it ascended the stairs. Instead, it turned away from both the library and the staircase, heading for a small door leading to a secluded patch of lawn nestled between two sections of the rambling estate.

I hurried to open it for him. Stepping out onto the creeper-covered porch, the raven paused to gaze at the rain, which cascaded like a massive, translucent curtain. I lingered in the doorway behind him. Another flash illuminated the scene, followed by a prolonged rumble of distant thunder. The bird turned his head over his shoulder, locking eyes with me—a silent inquiry: *Did you hear that?*—before swiveling back to study the storm with apparent approval. His posture and demeanor were so undeniably human that the remark escaped me involuntarily. "Fine weather for the worms, Mr. Raven!"

"Yes," he croaked, his voice familiar and gravelly, "the ground will be excellent for them to emerge and retreat. It must be a magnificent era on the steppes of Uranus!" he added, tilting his head upward. "I believe it is raining there as well; it has been doing so all week."

"Why would that constitute a grand time?" I asked.

"Because the fauna there consists entirely of burrowers," he explained. "Like the field mice and moles here. They will remain so for eons."

"How do you know this, if I may inquire?"

"As anyone would who has witnessed it firsthand," he replied. "It is a spectacular sight, initially terrifying, when the earth heaves and unleashes a beast. You might mistake it for a hairy elephant or a deinotherium—but none of these creatures resemble anything we have known here. I was nearly paralyzed with fright the first time I saw the dry-bog serpent wallow forth—such a head, such a mane! And SUCH eyes! But the shower is nearly spent. It will cease immediately following the next clap of thunder. Here it comes!"

Lightning flashed precisely on his cue, followed by the thunderclap. Silence returned.

"Now we must depart," the raven stated, stepping to the front of the porch.

"Depart where?" I asked.

"To where we are destined," he answered. "You did not genuinely believe you had arrived home, did you? I warned you that entry and exit are not pleasures to be exercised at will until you truly belong."

"I refuse to move," I declared stubbornly.

"That makes no difference—at least, not significantly," he countered. "This is the path!"

"I am perfectly content here."

"You think so, but you do not. Come along."

He hopped from the porch onto the grass and waited, turning to face me. "I will not leave the house today," I insisted.

"You will enter the garden!" he rejoined.

"I yield for the moment," I relented, stepping off the porch. The sun pierced the cloud layer, causing raindrops to glitter and sparkle on the green carpet. The raven walked ahead.

"You will soak your feet!" I cried.

"And mire my beak," he responded, immediately plunging it deep into the soil and withdrawing a wriggling red worm. He tossed his head back, launching the worm into the air. It unfurled great wings, resplendent in crimson and black, soaring high above.

"Tut! Tut!" I exclaimed. "You misunderstand, Mr. Raven: worms are not the larvae of butterflies!"

"Never mind," he croaked. "It suffices for today! I am not a scholar currently, though I serve as sexton at—the cemetery, more accurately—in... somewhere. Does not matter where!"

"I see! You cannot keep your spade idle; when there is nothing to bury, you must excavate something! Only ensure you consider the nature of your discovery before sending it flying! No creature should be permitted to forget its origins!"

"Why?" the raven asked.

"Because pride will ensue, and they will cease to recognize their superiors."

No one realizes they are acting foolishly in the moment.

"Where DO worms originate?" the raven asked, suddenly displaying genuine curiosity.

"From the earth, as you have just observed!" I answered.

"Yes, last!" he replied. "But they could not have emerged from it first—for that substance will never revert to it!" he added, gazing upward.

I looked up as well, seeing only a small, dark cloud whose edges glowed red, reminiscent of a sunset.

"The sun is not setting!" I exclaimed, stunned.

"Oh, no!" the raven returned. "That red hue belongs to the worm."

"You witness the consequences of allowing creatures to forget their source!" I cried, heated.

"It is commendable, surely, if it allows them to ascend and expand!" he retorted. "However, I merely instruct them to locate it!"

"Do you wish the air filled with worms?"

"That is the duty of a sexton. If only the remainder of the clergy comprehended it as thoroughly!"

His beak plunged into the soft turf again, extracting another wriggling worm. He tossed it aloft, and it flew away. Turning, I let out a cry of alarm: I had just declared my intention to stay, and already I was a stranger in a foreign land!

"What authority do you possess to treat me thus, Mr. Raven?" I demanded, deeply offended. "Am I, or am I not, a free agent?"

"A man possesses freedom only to the extent he chooses to construct it; never an atom freer," the raven answered. "You hold no right to compel actions against your own will!"

"When you possess a will, you will discover that no one can control you."

"You wrong me in the very essence of my individuality!" I persisted.

"If you were an individual, I could not act against you; therefore, since you are not yet one, I proceed freely. You are merely beginning the process of becoming an individual."

Surrounding me was a dense pine forest, and my eyes scanned the undergrowth desperately, hoping to spot an inexplicable glimmer that might guide me home. Alas, how could I continue to designate that house as HOME, when every door and window opened outward into the void, and even the garden proved impossible to contain within? I appeared disheveled and defeated.

"Perhaps it offers comfort," the raven said, "to be informed that you have not yet departed your house, nor has your house abandoned you. Yet, it cannot contain you, nor do you inhabit it!"

"I fail to comprehend you," I replied. "Where am I?"

"In the realm of the seven dimensions," he answered, emitting a strange, resonant noise and flicking his tail feathers. "You would do well to follow me closely for a moment, lest you injure someone!"

"There is no one to harm but yourself, Mr. Raven! I confess I would prefer to inflict injury upon you!"

"That you perceive no one is precisely where the peril lies. However, observe the large tree to your left, approximately thirty yards away?"

"Of course I do: why deny me that?" I answered testily.

"Ten minutes ago, you did not see it, and now you do not know its location!"

"I do."

"Where do you suppose it stands?"

"Why THERE, where you know it exists!"

"Where is THERE?"

"You exhaust me with your trivial inquiries!" I shouted, weary. "I am growing tired of you!"

"That tree stands upon the hearth of your kitchen, rising nearly straight up its chimney," he said.

"Now I KNOW you are mocking me!" I laughed in scorn.
