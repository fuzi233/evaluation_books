The weight of the phrase "better than likely" pressed upon Margaret like a physical burden, a terrible strain that threatened to fracture her resolve. In moments of quiet, amidst the cheerful chatter with her father, a jagged thought would pierce her composure: the terrifying realization that she was alone, bereft of the maternal anchor that had once steadied her world. Simultaneously, a gnawing dread regarding Frederick consumed her. The Sunday post had failed to deliver the expected correspondence, and by Tuesday, the absence of news from London had curdled into a disheartening silence. Margaret was plunged into darkness regarding his plans, a void that mirrored her father's misery. The uncertainty shattered his recent habit of lingering in a single armchair for hours; instead, he paced frantically, traversing the room, fleeing to the hallway, and returning to bang bedroom doors with a frantic energy that suggested a mind racing against an unseen clock. Margaret attempted to soothe him with the cadence of a story read aloud, but his agitation rendered him incapable of listening beyond a few sentences. It was a relief, then, when the announcement of Mr. Thornton's arrival cut through the tension. His presence acted as a balm, forcibly redirecting her father's spiraling thoughts.

Thornton approached Mr. Hale immediately, seizing his hands in a grip that spoke volumes without uttering a syllable. For a fleeting moment, their joined hands became a conduit for a sympathy so profound it transcended language. Turning then to Margaret, he found her transformed. The stately radiance of her beauty was dimmed, etched with the fatigue of vigil and the salt of unshed tears. Her countenance bore the marks of gentle, enduring sorrow, bordering on acute, present suffering. He had intended to maintain the cool reserve he had cultivated, yet instinct drove him forward. As she stood slightly apart, rendered timid by the ambiguity of his recent demeanor, he offered the bare necessities of greeting in a voice so tender it drew tears to her eyes, causing her to turn away to conceal her vulnerability. She retreated to her work, sitting in a silence that was both heavy and absolute. Inside, Thornton's heart hammered with a fierce rhythm, momentarily forgetting the dangers of Outwood Lane. His presence, usually a source of comfort to Mr. Hale due to his decisive power and sound judgment, resonated with an unusual intensity today, a fact Margaret noted with quiet observation.

Suddenly, the door opened and Dixon appeared, his face flushed with panic. "Miss Hale, you are wanted." The urgency in his tone sent a fresh wave of nausea through Margaret. Something had happened to Fred; she knew it with the certainty of intuition. Fortunately, her father and Mr. Thornton were engrossed in their conversation, creating a brief shield of distraction. "What is it, Dixon?" she asked, her voice barely audible.

Dixon ushered her toward the door leading to the chamber formerly occupied by Mrs. Hale, now repurposed as Margaret's quarters since her father refused to sleep in the room following his wife's passing. "It's nothing, miss," he choked out, struggling to maintain composure. "Only a police inspector. He wishes to see you. But I suspect it concerns nothing serious."

"Did he name—" Margaret began, the question dissolving into a whisper.

"No, miss. He asked only if you resided here and if he might speak with you. Martha admitted him; he is currently in Master's study. I attempted to intervene, hoping to delay, but... no, it is you, miss, whom he seeks."

Margaret remained silent until her hand rested on the study latch. Turning back, she issued a command with steely precision: "Ensure Papa does not descend. Mr. Thornton is with him."

When she entered the study, the inspector was initially intimidated by the hauteur of her bearing. Yet, beneath the surface lay a suppressed indignation that lent her features a sublime, disdainful air. There was no flicker of surprise, no prying curiosity. She simply awaited the commencement of his business, her posture rigid. She posed no questions.

"I beg your pardon, ma'am," the inspector began, his tone professional yet tinged with the gravity of his duty. "My obligations compel me to ask a few direct questions. A man perished at the Infirmary following a fall sustained at Outwood Station between five and six o'clock on Thursday evening, the twenty-sixth. Initially, the injury appeared inconsequential, yet physicians attribute the fatality to an underlying internal condition exacerbated by the deceased's habitual consumption of alcohol."

His dark eyes widened slightly as they met hers, searching for a reaction. While her lips curved into a fuller, strained line due to the tension of her muscles, he lacked the knowledge of her resting state to recognize the sullen defiance within that sweep. She neither flinched nor trembled. Instead, she pinned him with an unblinking gaze. When he hesitated, she prompted him, her voice steady, almost encouraging: "Well—proceed!"

"It is anticipated that an inquest will be convened," he continued. "We possess slight evidence suggesting the blow, push, or scuffle precipitating the fall was provoked by the deceased's intoxicated insolence toward a young woman accompanying a gentleman on the platform. An eyewitness noted the altercation but deemed it trivial given the lack of immediate harm. Furthermore, we have grounds to identify the woman as yourself. Should this identification hold—"

"I was not there," Margaret interrupted, her expression remaining as blank as that of a sleepwalker.

The inspector bowed but held his ground. The woman before him displayed no fluttering fear, no anxiety, no eagerness to conclude the interrogation. The intelligence he possessed was fragmented: a porter, rushing to catch a train, had witnessed a scuffle at the far end of the platform involving Leonards and a gentleman with a lady, hearing no sound. Moments later, the enraged, half-drunk Leonards, cursing violently, nearly knocked the porter down before fleeing. Only when the inspector dug deeper did the narrative coalesce. The stationmaster recalled seeing a strikingly beautiful young lady and gentleman around that hour, identified by a grocer's assistant as Miss Hale of Crampton. Certainty was elusive, but probability was high.

Leonards, driven mad by pain and fury, had sought refuge in a gin palace, his drunken ravings ignored by the busy staff until he abruptly rose, cursing himself for failing to utilize the electric telegraph for an unknown purpose. Overcome by agony or intoxication, he collapsed in the street, where police discovered him and transported him to the Infirmary. He never regained full consciousness, offering only disjointed fragments. Once lucid enough to summon a magistrate, he rambled of being at sea, conflating naval ranks with railway porters, before his final words were a curse upon the "Cornish trick" that had cost him a hundred pounds.

The inspector reviewed the totality of the evidence: the vagueness of the witness testimony, the unyielding, calm denial of the accused. Margaret stood waiting, her composure appearing supreme. "Then, madam, I confirm your denial that you were the lady accompanying the gentleman who delivered the blow or push resulting in this man's death?"

A sharp, searing pain lanced through Margaret's mind. *Oh God! that I knew Frederick were safe!* A deep observer might have witnessed the agony flash across her gloomy eyes, akin to a trapped animal facing extinction. Yet, the inspector, though keen-eyed, lacked the depth to perceive the tragedy behind the mask. Struck nonetheless by the mechanical repetition of her response—heavy, unchanged, refusing modification—he noted the eerie consistency.

"I was not there," she repeated, slowly and heavily, her eyes remaining open in a glassy, dreamlike stare.

This dull echo of her initial denial sparked a suspicion in the inspector. It was as if she had forced herself into a falsehood, stunning herself into an inability to vary it. He methodically closed his notebook, looking up to find her unmoved, as static as an ancient Egyptian statue.

"I trust you will not deem me impertinent in stating that I may need to revisit this matter. I may be compelled to summon you to the inquest to provide an alibi should my sole witness persist in testifying to your presence at the tragic event." He scrutinized her sharply. Still, her face remained perfectly still, devoid of color change or the shadow of guilt. He mistook her stoicism for innocence, assuming he had caught a glimpse of a wince that wasn't there, unaware that he faced Margaret Hale. Abashed by her genuine fortitude, he conceded, "It is highly improbable, ma'am, that I shall require further action. I hope you will excuse me for performing my duty, even if it appears intrusive."

As the inspector turned toward the door, Margaret bowed her head, her lips stiff and arid, unable to muster the common pleasantries of farewell. Suddenly, she stepped forward, unlocking the study door and preceding him to the main entrance. She threw the outer door wide open, allowing him to depart. Her gaze remained fixed on him in that same vacant, intense manner until he had fully exited the property. She closed the door, stepping halfway into the study before turning back, seized by a sudden, passionate impulse, and locking it from the inside.

She crossed to the center of the room, pausing, then tottering forward. She halted again, swaying for a fraction of a second before collapsing onto the floor, lost in a dead swoon.
