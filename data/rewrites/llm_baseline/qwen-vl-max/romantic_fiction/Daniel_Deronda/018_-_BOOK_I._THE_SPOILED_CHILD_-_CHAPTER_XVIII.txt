CHAPTER XVIII. Life is a many-sided mother: sometimes she wears her finest robes and climbs the marble steps with head held high, never glancing at those who serve her; at other times, she walks in tattered clothes through shadowed lanes, breathing in the fumes of cheap gin, shouting amid the chaos of poverty. But to the Meyricks, she came as a modest housewife—neat, thoughtful, and full of quiet joy, finding richness in simplicity. Mrs. Meyrick’s home was quiet, nestled beside the river on one side and gardens on the other. The front parlor faced the water, the back opened onto greenery, so that even while reading aloud to her daughters, she could leave the window open to let in fresh air. Inside, two candles burned on a small table for Kate, who was sketching illustrations for a publisher, while a lamp lit the rest of the room—just enough for Amy and Mab, who were embroidering satin cushions meant for “the great world.” Outside, the house looked narrow and worn, its windows framed in old-fashioned wood, the light from within glowing softly through a holland blind. Yet it was comforting to know that such modest dwellings in foggy London often sheltered lives marked by a culture refined not by wealth, but by choice—where display was seen as meaningless, and the grand spectacles of society merely distractions, provoking envy or false ambition rather than true desire.

The Meyrick household was one such haven. They cherished their little home not just for its comfort, but for its history—each object had its place, each piece holding memory. For the mother, the furnishings reminded her of her marriage days; for the girls, they felt as natural and unchangeable as the stars above. Mrs. Meyrick had sacrificed much so that her husband’s favorite engravings might remain in their home, and the walls, though narrow, held scenes and portraits of world history that the children knew by heart. The chairs and tables were old friends, preferred over anything new. In these two small rooms, where only the prints and piano would have drawn interest from a dealer, there lived a mind wide-reaching and carefully chosen—a life enriched by music, poetry, and art.

It may be that during leaner times, before Kate earned her living through drawing, the family had no servant to tend fires or clean floors. Yet they remained fastidious, especially about manners. They refused to believe that fashionable society was as coarse, quarrelsome, or vulgar as it was often portrayed in literature. The Meyricks had quirks—oddities passed down from both parents—minds like ancient houses with hidden chambers, unexpected passages, sudden views. But what bound them together was stronger than any eccentricity: love for one another, reverence for noble deeds and fine art, and a steady habit of work.

Hans, their brother, had once wanted to spend his money improving their lives—making them more comfortable, more luxurious. But they had all resisted, not out of pride, but because they valued their freedom and integrity. They understood that if Hans gave in to his passion for art, he might eventually lose his fellowship. So they kept their simple ways, laughing at his Gavarni caricatures and honoring his choices without guilt. Their greatest treat was opera—always in the gallery—when Hans returned home.

That evening, the scene was peaceful, almost sacred. All four women were small, perfectly proportioned to their tiny rooms. Mrs. Meyrick read aloud from a French novel—Erckmann-Chatrian’s *Historie d’un Conscrit*. She was lively, half-French, half-Scotch, with speech so clear and vivid it seemed to bring sunlight into the listener’s mind. Though not yet fifty, her hair, covered by a plain net cap, was mostly gray, but her eyebrows and eyes remained dark and bright. Her black dress, buttoned tightly like a priest’s cassock, suited her neat, petite frame. Her daughters matched her in size and spirit—except Mab, who bore her father’s fair hair and complexion, along with a bold brow and features that echoed him. Everything about them was compact: their braided hair tied back *à la Chinoise*, their gray skirts modestly defying fashion, which demanded space and volume. Together, they might have fit into a single lady’s traveling trunk. Their faces seemed alive with thought—like horse-chestnuts cracked open, revealing their inner light.

Only one large creature in the room was Hafiz, the Persian cat, perched calmly on a leather chair, opening his eyes now and then to watch over the humans below.

Mrs. Meyrick had just finished reading. Mab, who had leaned forward, captivated, exclaimed, “That’s the finest story in the world!”
“Of course,” said Amy, teasingly. “It’s the last thing you’ve heard. Everything pleases you in turn.”
“It’s hardly a story,” said Kate. “It’s history brought close—seen through a strong telescope. We see the soldiers’ faces… no, we hear them—almost feel their hearts beating.”
“I don’t care what you call it,” Mab replied, tossing aside her thimble. “Call it a chapter from Revelation. It makes me want to do something good—something grand. I feel sorry for everyone. I love Schiller. I want to take the whole world in my arms and kiss it. I’ll kiss you instead, little mother!” She threw her arms around her mother’s neck.
“Whenever you’re like this, Mab,” Amy said, “your work goes flying.”
“It would be doing something good to finish your cushion without ruining it,” she added.
“Oh—oh—oh!” groaned Mab, bending to pick up her embroidery. “I wish I had three wounded conscripts to nurse.”
“You’d spill their beef tea while talking,” Amy teased.
“Poor Mab! Don’t be hard on her,” said their mother. “Give me your embroidery, child. You go on with your feelings, and I’ll continue with the pink and white poppy.”
“Well, ma,” said Kate, looking at her drawing, “you’re more sharp-tongued than Amy.”
“Oh—oh—oh!” Mab cried again, rising and stretching. “I wish something wonderful would happen. I feel like the flood—waters breaking forth, heavens opening. I must sit down and play scales.”

As Mab opened the piano, laughter filled the room—until a cab stopped outside, and a sharp knock rang at the door.
“Dear me!” Mrs. Meyrick said, jumping up. “It’s after ten, and Phœbe’s asleep.” She hurried out, leaving the parlor door open.
“Mr. Deronda!” the girls heard her say.
Mab clasped her hands. “There! Something *is* happening.”
Kate and Amy dropped their work in surprise. But Deronda’s voice was too low for them to catch his words, and soon the door closed behind Mrs. Meyrick.

“I know I’m asking much,” Deronda said, once inside. “But you can imagine how helpless I am with a young girl like this. I couldn’t take her among strangers, and in her state, I feared bringing her into a house full of servants. I’ve trusted you—with hope you won’t think me reckless.”
“On the contrary,” Mrs. Meyrick replied. “You’ve honored me by trusting me. I understand your difficulty. Bring her in. I’ll tell the girls.”

As Deronda returned to the cab, Mrs. Meyrick stepped back into the parlor.
“Here’s someone to care for instead of your conscripts, Mab—a poor girl who tried to drown herself in despair. Mr. Deronda found her just in time. He brought her in his boat and didn’t know what else to do—so he brought her here. She’s a Jewess, but very refined, he says—speaks Italian, understands music.”

The three girls stood together, silent, expectant, united in compassion. Mab looked awestruck—as if her longing had summoned something divine.

Deronda approached the cab. The girl inside, pale and wide-eyed, stared out.
“I’ve brought you to some of the kindest people in the world,” he said gently. “They have daughters like you. It’s a happy home. Will you let me take you to them?”

She stepped out, hand in his, hat forgotten. When Deronda led her into the warm glow of the parlor, where the four women waited, she became a vision that stirred even the most guarded hearts. At first, the sudden light made her blink. Then, slowly, she focused—and Deronda placed her hand into his mother’s.

He was glad the Meyricks were small—the girl’s dark curls reached higher than anyone else’s. She wouldn’t fear these gentle faces so near. She looked at each in turn, and Mrs. Meyrick said, “You must be tired, poor child.”
“We will take care of you—we will comfort you—we will love you,” Mab burst out, taking the girl’s hand between both of hers.

A warmth spread through the stranger. She stepped back slightly, studying the four faces—seeing kindness reflected in her own eyes, not in a smile, but in a quiet shift: anxiety fading into peace. For a moment, she looked up at Deronda, as if giving thanks to him for all this mercy. Then, turning to Mrs. Meyrick, she spoke softly, with more composure than he had ever heard:
“I am a stranger. A Jewess. You might have thought me wicked.”
“No,” Mab insisted. “We know you are good. We think no evil of you, poor child. You shall be safe here.”
Mrs. Meyrick added, “Come now. Sit down. You must eat, then rest.”

The girl looked up at Deronda again.
“You will have no more fears? You will rest tonight?”
“Oh, I should not fear. I should rest. These—these are the ministering angels.”

Mrs. Meyrick offered her a seat, but the girl hesitated, as if uncertain whether she deserved such welcome without explanation.
“My name is Mirah Lapidoth,” she said. “I came all the way from Prague alone. I escaped—ran from terrible things. I came to find my mother and brother in London. I was taken from my mother when I was small, but I believed I could find her again. I searched—houses were gone, I couldn’t find her. It has been long. I had little money. That is why I am in distress.”

“Our mother will be kind to you,” Mab whispered. “See what a nice little mother she is!”
“Do sit down now,” Kate said, moving a chair forward. Amy rushed to prepare tea.

Mirah sat quietly, gracefully crossing her feet, placing her hands one over the other in her lap. She gazed at her hosts with calm reverence. And then, Hafiz—the Persian cat—came forward, tail upright, rubbing against her ankles.

Deronda knew it was time to leave.
“May I come again tomorrow—at five?” he asked Mrs. Meyrick.
“Yes, please. We’ll have time to get to know her.”
“Good-bye,” he said, looking down at Mirah and extending his hand.

She rose, took it—then lifted her eyes to his, filled with deep gratitude.
“The God of our fathers bless you and deliver you from all evil,” she said. “You saved me when I was poor and broken. You saw worth in me when no one else did. You found me lost, and gave me the best.”

Deronda could not speak. He bowed silently to the Meyricks, then hurried away.
