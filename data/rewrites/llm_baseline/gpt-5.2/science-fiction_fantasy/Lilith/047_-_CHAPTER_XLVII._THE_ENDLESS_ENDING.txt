I have not yet found Lona. The ache of that absence sits in me like hunger that will not name itself. But Mara is much with me; she comes and goes as quietly as a woman who knows the floorboards by their voices, and she has taught me many things—how to hold a silence without breaking it, how to listen for what a room is saying beneath speech, how to taste the day as if it were broth and not merely hours.

Still, the question returns with the same stubbornness as a refrain: was that last waking itself only another layer of sleep? Am I yet in the chamber of death, laid away like grain in a bin until it is ready, dreaming because I am not ripe enough to rise? Or did I never truly go down into rest—never surrendered outright and heartily—and so have come up too soon, blinking into an in-between light?

If that waking was a dream, then surely it was a dream with a better waking folded inside it, as a loaf holds its own steam, and I have not been played with by false vision. Such loveliness cannot be mere trick; it must have a truer heart than I have yet bitten into.

In my worst moments I cry out—less with my mouth than with my marrow—Could God Himself create such lovely things as I dreamed?

And Hope answers me—not with argument, but with the tone of someone who has been at the ovens longer than I have watched: Whence then came thy dream?

Out of my dark self, into the light of my consciousness, I say.

But whence first into thy dark self? Hope asks, as if turning a jar in her hand to see what sediment lies at the bottom.

My brain was its mother, I answer, and the fever in my blood its father.

Say rather, Hope suggests, thy brain was the violin whence it issued, and the fever in thy blood the bow that drew it forth. But who made the violin? and who guided the bow across its strings? Say rather again—who set the song-birds each on its bough in the tree of life, and startled each in its order from its perch? Whence came the fantasia, and whence the life that danced thereto? Didst thou, in the dark of thine own unconscious self, say, Let beauty be; let truth seem!—and straightway beauty was, and truth but seemed?

So I am left with this: man dreams and desires; God broods and wills and quickens. When a man dreams his own dream, he is the sport of it, tossed like chaff in his own wind; when Another gives it him, that Other is able to fulfil it.

I have never again sought the mirror. The hand that sent me back did so with an authority I can still feel in my skin, like salt after sea-wind. I will not go out again by that door. “All the days of my appointed time will I wait, till my change come.”

And I do wait—among my books, among the common furniture of my days, even among meals that ought to anchor a man to earth with their honest weight. Yet the world will not always keep its shape. Now and then, when I look along my shelves, the ranks of volumes waver, as if a wind had found its way into their solid mass and rippled it like standing corn; as if another world pressed close behind the paper and boards, about to break through like a seed sprouting in the dark. Sometimes when I am abroad a like thing happens: the heavens and the earth, the trees and the grass, for one moment shake as if they would pass away like a blown-out flame—then, lo, they settle again into the old familiar face, as if nothing had stirred.

At times I seem to hear whisperings around me, not loud enough to be called voices, but not mere fancy either—like the leaf-speech of a hedgerow when there is no visible breeze, or like distant kitchen-work heard through a closed door: the faint click of a knife on wood, the hush of grain poured into a measure, the soft thud of dough being folded. It is as if some that loved me were talking of me. But when I strain to catch the words—to know in what tongue they speak, whether it is mine or one that my mouth has forgotten—they cease at once, and all is very still.

That stillness is not empty. It has a contour. It holds the room the way a covered pot holds scent: you know something is there, though you cannot name it without lifting the lid. I do not know whether these things rise in my brain or enter it from without. I do not seek them; they come, and I let them go, as a wise housekeeper will not chase every draught but simply close the door when it needs closing.

Strange dim memories—memories that will not stay put long enough to be identified—often look out upon me through misty windows of the past, in the broad daylight, as if the past had its own weather and opened a shutter for a breath. They bring with them the tang of places I cannot locate and the cadence of words I cannot translate back into any speech I command. A feeling will arrive—sharp as vinegar, sweet as ripe fruit, bitter as burnt crust—and with it the sense that once I knew the proper name for it in some native dialect of the soul, a word with roots deep in a soil my present tongue no longer tills. Then it is gone, and I am left with only the aftertaste, unrenderable, unpurchasable, not to be packaged into the dominant lexicon that pretends to own all meanings.

But I never dream now. Or if I do, it is without images, without the old dramas—only a kind of waiting that fills the night as fermentation fills a crock: working, silent, patient.

It may be, notwithstanding, that when most awake I am only dreaming the more. Yet I hold to this: when I wake at last into that life which carries this life in its bosom as a mother carries her child, I shall know that I wake, and shall doubt no more.

I wait. Asleep or awake, I wait.

Novalis says, “Our life is no dream, but it should and will perhaps become one.”
