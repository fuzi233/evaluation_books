The farmhouse stood a solitary mile above the village, nestled in a rugged expanse of moorland. Heather vines crept aggressively over its garden walls, and the narrow bridle path simply terminated at its threshold. On three sides, an amphitheater of hills cradled the house like a protective ship. These mountains shifted hues with startling speed as seasons turned, offering a fresh palette of colors daily—emerald, violet, rust—but they were barren of trees. Granite poked through the moss and heather, yet the hills possessed a gentle, sheltering demeanor. Durrance often felt they donned their shifting drapes of green and brown deliberately, solely to please the eyes of the girl they protected.

Facing the long slope toward the inlet of the Lough, the house offered a panoramic view. From its windows, one could survey the sparse thickets, the few cultivated fields, the whitewashed cottages, and the dense woods lining the far bank, catching fleeting glimpses of shimmering water and gulls dancing above.

Durrance rode up the track one afternoon and recognized the house instantly. As he neared, the melody of a violin drifted from the windows, sounding like a warm welcome. His hand froze on the reins, and a specific, potent hope—one he had nurtured in secret—surged within him, holding his breath. He tethered his horse and stepped through the gate. Outside, it was merely a barracks; inside, it was a sanctuary of comfort.

The room assigned to him, furnished with gleaming oak and polished brass, was as bright as the afternoon sun. Durrance closed his eyes, imagining the scene transformed: blinds drawn against a winter night, a roaring fire on the hearth, the wind whistling around the hills and tapping against the glass.

Ethne greeted him without a trace of surprise. "I thought you would come," she said, a smile lighting her face.

Durrance laughed abruptly as they clasped hands, leaving Ethne wondering at the sudden sound. Her gaze followed his to the violin resting on a nearby table. It was pale in color, bearing a small patch of repaired, worm-eaten wood near the bridge.

"It is yours," she stated. "You were in Egypt. I could not bear to send it back."

"I have hoped lately," Durrance replied, "that you would accept it anyway."

"You see, I have," Ethne said, meeting his gaze directly. "I accepted it some time ago. There was a period when I desperately needed assurance of having sure friends. Something tangible helped. I was very glad to possess it."

Durrance lifted the instrument, handling it with the reverence due a sacred object. "Have you played it? Perhaps the *Musoline* Overture?"

"Do you recall that?" she asked, laughing softly.

"Yes, I have played it, but only recently. For a long time, I kept it away. It spoke to me too intimately of things I wished to forget." She spoke these words without hesitation or a downward glance.

The following day, Durrance retrieved his luggage from Rathmullen and remained at the farm for a week. Yet, until the final hour of his visit, neither Ethne nor Durrance mentioned Harry Feversham, despite spending considerable time together. This was largely because Dermod appeared even more shattered than Mrs. Adair's descriptions had suggested. His speech had reduced to monosyllables; his frame had shrunk, his clothes hung loosely on his limbs, and his stature seemed diminished. Even his anger was clouded from his eyes. He had become a recline, dozing by the fire most of the day, even in July heat. His longest excursion was to the small grey church standing naked on a mound a quarter-mile away, a journey that exhausted him completely. He was an ancient man fallen into decrepitude, barely recognizable, such that his movements and rare vocalizations struck Durrance as painfully artificial, like the mimicry of a corpse. Even his collie dog seemed to age in his presence; seeing them side by side evoked a profound sense of shared decline.

Consequently, Durrance and Ethne spent much time together. During the day, regardless of the weather, they trekked the hills. Ethne, her face flushed with vitality and her eyes burning with jealous eagerness, showed him her homeland, demanding his admiration. In the evenings, she would take her violin, sit with her face averted, and bid the strings sing of heights and depths. Durrance sat watching the sweep of her arm and the absorption in her features, silently calculating his prospects.

He had not arrived at Glenalla carrying Lieutenant Sutch's expectations of success. The shadow of Harry Feversham loomed large between them. Furthermore, Durrance knew that poverty would weigh lighter on Ethne than on most women. Although the Lennon House was ruined and her lands gone, she remained among her own people. They still awaited her visits; she was still the princess of that countryside. Conversely, she derived a frank pleasure from his company and guided conversations toward his three years of service in the East. No detail was too trivial for her inquiries. As he spoke, her eyes scrutinized him, and the smile on her lips offered constant approval.

Durrance failed to grasp her intent. Unless he knew the history between Harry Feversham and Ethne, no one could. Durrance presented the appearance of a man, but Ethne was anxious to verify if the spirit of a man inhabited that shell. He was a dark lantern to her; there might be a flame burning within, or merely vacancy and darkness. She was sliding back the cover to ensure. On the last day of his visit, she steered the conversation toward Egypt.

They sat on the hillside beside a stream leaping from rock to rock in a miniature gorge, flowing over deep pools as a torrent of clear, black water.

"I traveled once for four days among the mirages," Durrance began. "Lagoons still as mirrors, fringed with misty trees. One could almost walk a camel up to its knees before the lagoon receded and the sand glared at you. One cannot imagine that glare. Every stone visible dances and shakes like a heliograph; you can actually see the heat flow breast-high across the desert, swift as this stream, only pellucid. Until the sun sets ahead of you, level with your eyes! Imagine the nights that follow—nights of infinite silence, with a cool, friendly wind blowing from horizon to horizon—and your bedspread ready under the great dome of stars. Oh," he cried, drawing a deep breath, "that country grows on you. It's like the Southern Cross—four overrated stars at first sight, but in a week you begin to look for them, and you miss them when traveling north again."

He propped himself up on his elbow and turned suddenly to her. "Do you know... I can only speak for myself... but I never feel alone in those empty spaces. On the contrary, I always feel very close to the things I care about, and to the few people I care about."

Her eyes shone brightly, her lips parted in a smile. Moving closer on the grass, he sat with his feet tucked to one side, leaning on his arm.

"I used to imagine you out there," he said. "You would have loved it—from the start before dawn, in the dark, to the campfire at night. You would have been at home. I used to think so as I lay awake, wondering how the world went with my friends."

"And you go back there?" she asked.

Durrance hesitated. The roar of the torrent pulsed around them. When he finally spoke, all enthusiasm had drained from his voice. Gazing into the stream, he said, "To Wadi Halfa. For two years. I suppose so."

Ethne knelt on the grass beside him. "I shall miss you," she whispered.

She knelt just behind him as he sat, and again, a heavy silence fell between them.

"What are you thinking?" he asked.

"That you need not miss me," he replied, noticing her draw back and sink onto her heels. "My appointment at Halfa—I might shorten its term. I might perhaps avoid it altogether. I still have half my furlough."

She gave no answer, nor did she shift her posture. She remained perfectly still, and Durrance became alarmed. He realized that this stillness expressed distress as definitively as a cry of pain would in another woman. Turning to her, he saw her head bowed, then raised as he turned. Though her lips smiled, there was a look of great trouble in her eyes.

Durrance was a man like any other. His first thought was whether some obstacle prevented her compliance, even if she were willing.

"There is your father," he said.

"Yes," she answered. "There is my father too. I could not leave him."

"Nor need you," he said quickly. "That difficulty can be surmounted. To tell the truth, I was not thinking of your father at the moment."

"Nor was I," she replied.

Durrance turned away, staring down the rocks into a wrinkled pool of water below. Ultimately, it was the shadow of Feversham stretching between himself and her.

"I know, of course," he said, "that you would never feel trouble, as so many do, with half your heart. You would neither easily care nor lightly forget."
