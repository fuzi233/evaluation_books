A few days after returning from Roville, fate conspired to throw Bruce Carmyle into the path of his cousin Lancelot in Piccadilly. While Ginger would have dearly loved to maintain the silence of their separate returns, circumstances dictated otherwise. Hurrying along with a weary nod, Ginger was abruptly halted by Carmyle, who seemed to materialize from nowhere.

"Just the man I wanted to see," Carmyle announced, as if he'd been searching the entire length of London for this specific encounter.

"Oh, hello!" Ginger replied, his tone lacking any genuine warmth.

"I was thinking of dropping by your club," Carmyle continued, stepping closer. "Shall we share a cigarette?"

Ginger stared at the offered cigar case with the wary suspicion of a man who has just stepped onto a magician's stage and accepted a playing card from the performer. He felt utterly bewildered. In every year of their acquaintance, he couldn't recall a single display of such feigned geniality from his cousin. It was startling, indeed, that Mr. Carmyle bothered to speak to him at all, considering the *affaire* Scrymgeour still festered like an open sore, and the Family was currently in high session debating its merits.

"Have you been back in London long?" Carmyle asked, attempting to smooth the waters.

"A day or two."

"I heard quite by accident that you're staying at the club. By the way, thanks for throwing Miss Nicholas right on top of me at Roville Station. We decided to count that as an official introduction. Very attractive girl."

Ginger jumped as if shot. "What?"

"She's a catch," Carmyle pressed on, seemingly unfazed by Ginger's shock. "She's not going to slip away from my life, if I can help it. That sudden exit left me with that baffled, dissatisfied itch that often mimics the effects of love at first sight. She won, I'll admit, and I'm eager to prove there's more to me than she suspects. In short, I'm piqued. Whether I like her or not remains a mystery, but a future without her seems terribly flat."

"A very attractive girl," Ginger murmured, his envy leaking through like a sieve. "We certainly had a pleasant talk."

"I bet you did," Ginger snarled, his guard rising.

"And did she happen to give you her address?"

"Why?" Ginger asked, his posture shifting instantly. His attitude toward Sally's address mirrored that of a collector who has just acquired a unique masterpiece; he intended to lock it away and savor it alone.

"Well, I—" Carmyle stammered slightly, finding the resistance irritating. "—I promised to send her some books she's anxious to read..."

"I doubt she gets much time for reading."

"Specific books not yet published in America."

"Oh, nearly everything is published in America, isn't it? Bound to be."

"These particular ones aren't," Carmyle said sharply. He wished he had been more creative in his approach, as Ginger's reserve was becoming a wall he couldn't scale.

"Give them to me, then, and I'll mail them to her," Ginger suggested, offering a diplomatic retreat.

"Good heavens, man!" Carmyle snapped. "I am perfectly capable of mailing a few books to America. So, where does she live?"

Reluctantly, Ginger surrendered the sacred number of the holy street that served as Sally's headquarters. With a devil like Carmyle breathing down his neck, there was no escape. He grumbled the words, feeling the weight of betrayal.

Carmyle immediately jotted down the details in a dapper, morocco-bound notebook with a gold pencil—a man who always carried a pen and never let an old envelope get in the way.

A heavy silence fell between them. Carmyle cleared his throat, his demeanor shifting from genial to icy. The waste of energy was not in his vocabulary.

"I saw Uncle Donald this morning," he stated, his voice dropping to a familiar, reproachful register.

"Yes?" Ginger muttered moodily. This was the uncle who had directed his debut as a hasher in the National Liberal Club: a respectable figure, yes, but never a favorite of Ginger's. Among the legion of uncles and aunts that constituted the Family, Uncle Donald was undoubtedly the Managing Director, and Ginger privately believed he approximated a human blister.

"He wants you to dine with him tonight at Bleke's."

Ginger's spirits plummeted further. A dinner with Uncle Donald would have been a somber affair even in the fantastical settings of *Arabian Nights*. There was a quality to Uncle Donald's personality that would have sobered the wildest orgies of Emperor Tiberius at Capri. To sit at a table in Bleke's Coffee House—a grim relic of Old London catering almost exclusively to patrons who hadn't missed an evening in half a century—felt like touching bedrock. Ginger was extremely doubtful whether mere flesh and blood could withstand it.

"To-night?" Ginger echoed, his voice hollow. "Well..."

"Don't be a fool," Carmyle interrupted. "You know as well as I do that you must go. If you have another engagement, cancel it."

"Oh, all right."

"Seven-thirty sharp."

"All right," Ginger said, the word tasting like ash.

The cousins parted ways, Carmyle heading east to his chambers at the Temple to see clients, while Ginger trudged west toward Hyde Park Corner. Despite their lack of sympathy, their thoughts converged on the same object. As Carmyle wove through the bustling crowds of Piccadilly Circus, thinking of Sally, so did Ginger, moving aimlessly toward the park in a state of comatose rage, bumping into pedestrians with a mechanical lethargy.

Since returning to London, Ginger had been in terrible shape. He drifted through the daylight hours and slept fitfully at night. If there was anything more rotten than anything else in a pretty blighted world, it was hopeless love. It reduced a fellow to the condition of an absolute onion. Hopeless love had agitated Ginger to his core. Previously, his soul had been placid; even the financial crash that had altered his life had not bruised him deeply. His temperament had allowed him to bear the slings and arrows of outrageous fortune with a philosophical "Right ho!" But now, everything had changed. Everything irritated him acutely—the things he once accepted as inevitable, like Uncle Donald's moustache and his habit of using it as a makeshift *zareba* (earthwork) to defend against soup assaults.

"By gad!" Ginger thought, stopping suddenly opposite Devonshire House. "If he uses that damned shrubbery as a soup-strainer tonight, I'll slosh him with a fork!"

Hard thoughts... hard thoughts! And growing harder by the minute, for nothing expands more rapidly than a mood of rebellion. Rebellion is a forest fire that leaps across the soul. The spark had been lit within Ginger, and long before he reached Hyde Park Corner, he was ablaze and crackling. By the time he returned to his club, dressed for the dismal festivities of Bleke's, he was practically a menace to society—at least to the sections comprising Uncle Donald, the minor uncles George and William, and the aunts Mary, Geraldine, and Louise.

The rebellious mood did not fade as he prepared for the evening. He scowled, struggling morosely with an obstinate tie that refused to cooperate. One could not disguise the fact: Ginger was warming up. Then, as if Fate itself had been waiting for this precise psychological second, it delivered the final blow. A knock at the door broke the silence, and a waiter entered carrying a telegram.

Ginger looked at the envelope. It had been readdressed and forwarded from the Hotel Normandie. It was a wireless message, handed in aboard the White Star liner *Olympic*, and it read simply:

Ginger sat down heavily on the bed, the paper crumpling in his hand.

Outside, at twenty-five minutes past seven, a taxi cab pulled up to the dingy door of Bleke's Coffee House in the Strand. The driver, glancing at his fare, struck by the young man's manner and appearance, formed a verdict that was far from comforting. It was a determined-looking sort of young bloke.
