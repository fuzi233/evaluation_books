The evening air was thick with anticipation when the somber-faced son of the portrait restorer arrived from Gratz, his cart laden with two massive crates. Each was filled with paintings, their surfaces dulled by centuries of neglect. The journey had been long—ten leagues through the winding roads—and his arrival stirred a rare excitement in our quiet schloss.

We gathered in the hall, drawn like moths to the flicker of news from the outside world. The crates loomed ominously by the entrance, their contents veiled in dust and shadow. The messenger, weary from his travels, was led away to sup while servants fetched tools—hammers, chisels, screws—all the instruments of revelation.

Carmilla watched with disinterest as the portraits emerged one by one, their faces resurrected from the grime of ages. Most were ancestors of my mother’s Hungarian line, their features softened by time, their stares vacant yet strangely watchful. My father consulted his list, calling out names as the restorer unearthed each relic.

Then, a pause.

“There’s one more,” my father murmured. “In the corner, the name reads ‘Marcia Karnstein,’ dated 1698. I’m curious to see how it has fared.”

I remembered it—a small, unframed panel, so darkened it was nearly illegible. The artist lifted it with reverence, wiping away the last film of decay.

And there she was.

Carmilla.

Not merely a likeness—no, it was her. The same delicate features, the same knowing smile, the same dark eyes that seemed to follow you. Even the faint mole upon her throat was there, as though the painter had captured not just her image, but her very essence.

“An absolute miracle,” I breathed. “Carmilla, it’s you! Look, even the mole—”

My father chuckled, though his gaze slid away too quickly. “A striking resemblance,” he conceded, but his voice lacked the wonder choking my own. He turned back to the restorer, discussing brushstrokes and varnish as though this were any other portrait.

But I could not tear my eyes away.

“May I hang it in my room?” I asked.

“Of course,” my father said, smiling indulgently.

Carmilla herself said nothing. She merely leaned back, her long lashes casting shadows over her cheeks, her lips curled in a slow, secretive smile.

Then I saw it—the inscription, now clear as day. Not *Marcia*.

Beneath it, a coronet. The year 1698.

“I’m descended from the Karnsteins,” I said. “Through my mother.”

Carmilla exhaled, her voice a languid whisper. “As am I. A very ancient line. Are there any Karnsteins left?”

“None,” I replied. “The family was ruined long ago. Their castle lies in ruins not far from here.”

“How… fascinating,” she murmured, though her gaze drifted past me, toward the moonlit courtyard. “Shall we take the air?”

Arm in arm, we stepped outside. The night was cool, the silver light painting everything in ghostly hues.

“You were thinking of the night I came,” she said, her voice barely audible.

“I was.”

“And you are glad?”

“Delighted.”

Her fingers tightened around my waist. “And you asked for the portrait—the one that looks like me—to hang in your chamber.” She sighed, resting her head against my shoulder. “How romantic you are.”

I laughed softly. “You say that as though you’ve known great romance yourself.”

Her breath hitched. “I have loved no one,” she whispered. “And never shall… unless it be you.”

Her lips brushed my neck, her grip suddenly feverish. Her body trembled against mine.

“I live in you,” she gasped. “And you would die for me, I love you so.”

I recoiled.

Her face was blank, her eyes hollow.

“A chill,” she murmured, listless once more. “Let us go inside.”

But as we turned, I caught the flicker of something—fear? Hunger?—in her gaze before it vanished.

That night, as I lay awake, the portrait of Mircalla watched me from the wall.

And I wondered if it had ever truly been *just* a painting.
