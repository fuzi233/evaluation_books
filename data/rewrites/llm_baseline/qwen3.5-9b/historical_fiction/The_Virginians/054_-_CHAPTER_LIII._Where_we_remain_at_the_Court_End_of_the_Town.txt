George Warrington had recounted the tale we have just heard to Madame de Bernstein the previous evening—a portion, that is, of the history. For the old lady frequently nodded off during the narration, only rousing herself when George paused to declare it "most interesting" and commanded him to continue. The young gentleman hemmed and hawed, stuttered, and flushed crimson, proceeding much against his will. He spoke far less fluently than he had before his small, friendly audience in Hill Street, where Hetty's eyes of wonder, Theo's sympathetic glances, his mother's kind countenance, and his father's humorous expressions served as applause sufficient to encourage any modest youth needing a boost for his eloquence.

As for his mother's behavior, the General remarked, "It was as good as Mr. Addison's trunk-maker; she would make the fortune of any tragedy simply by being engaged to cry in the front boxes." This was precisely why we selected my Lord Wrotham's house as the theater for George's debut performance, hoping he might speak to advantage rather than as he had done before that sleepy, cynical old lady.

"Very good and most interesting, I am sure, my dear sir," Madame Bernstein said, raising three delicate fingers encased in a lace mitten to conceal a convulsive twitch of her mouth. "And your mother must have been delighted to see you."

George gave a slight shrug and a low bow as his aunt looked up at him with her keen, aged eyes. "She has been delighted to see you," she continued dryly, "killed the fatted calf, and—well, that sort of thing. Though why I say 'calf,' I cannot understand, nephew George, for you were never the prodigal son. I may say, 'Calf to thee, my poor Harry!' Thou hast certainly been among the swine. Evil companions have stripped the money from thy pocket and the coat from thy back."

"He came to his family in England, madam," George replied with some heat. "His friends were your ladyship's."

"He could not have chosen worse advisers, nephew Warrington," the old lady retorted, tossing her head. "So I should have informed my sister sooner, had she condescended to write to me through him, as she has done through you."

Later that night, as she prepared for a rout, she murmured to her maid: "Hey! Hey! This young gentleman's mother is half sorry that he has come to life again; I can see it in his face. She is half sorry, and I am perfectly furious! Why didn't he lie still when he fell beneath the tree, and why did that young Florac carry him to the fort? I knew those Floracs when I was in Paris, during the reign of Monsieur le Régent. They were of the House of Florac of Ivry. No great house before Henri IV. His ancestor was the King's favorite. His ancestor—he! he!—his ancestress! Brett! *Entendez-vous?* Give me my card-purse. I dislike the grand airs of this Monsieur George; yet he bears a striking resemblance to his grandfather—the same look, and sometimes the same tone. Have you heard of Colonel Esmond when I was young? This boy has his eyes. I suppose I liked the Colonel's because he loved me."

Engaged as she was to a card party—an amusement she never missed, weekday or Sabbath, so long as she possessed the strength to hold trumps or sit upright—she soon dismissed her two nephews. To the elder, she offered a couple of fingers and a very stately curtsy; to Harry, however, she gave two hands and a kindly pat on the cheek.

"My poor child, now that thou art disinherited, thou shalt see how differently the world will treat thee!" she declared. "There is only, in all London, a wicked, heartless old woman who will treat thee as before. Here is a pocket-book for you, child! Do not lose it at Ranelagh tonight. That suit of yours does not become your brother half so well as it did upon you! You shall present your brother to everyone and pace the room for at least two hours, child. Were I you, I would then retire to the Chocolate-House and act as if nothing had occurred. While you are there, your brother may return to me and partake of a bit of chicken. My Lady Flint serves wretched suppers, and I wish to discuss your mother's letter with him. *Au revoir*, gentlemen!" With that, she departed toward her toilet, her chairmen and torchbearers already awaiting her at the door.

The gentlemen proceeded to Ranelagh, where only a handful of Mr. Harry's acquaintances happened to be present. They walked the circular path, encountered Mr. Tom Claypool with some country friends, listened to the music, and drank tea in a box. Harry acted as master of ceremonies, introducing his brother to the curiosities of the venue. George, meanwhile, proved even more excited than his brother had been on his initial visit to this palace of delight. George loved music far more than Harry ever did; he heard a full orchestra for the first time and witnessed a satisfactory performance of a work by Mr. Handel. A notable instance of Harry's humility and regard for his elder brother was his ability to respect George's passion for music at a time when fiddling was deemed effeminate and unmanly in England, while patriotic prints daily urged Britons to sneer at the frivolous accomplishments of the "Squallinis" and "Monsieurs."

"No one in Britain is proud of his ignorance now," the narrator observed. "There is no conceit left among us. There is no such thing as dullness. Arrogance is entirely unknown... Well, at any rate, Art has obtained her letters of naturalization and lives here on terms of almost equality. If Mrs. Thrale chooses to marry a music-master today, I doubt her friends would shudder at the mention of her name. If she possesses a good fortune and keeps a competent cook, people would even dine with her despite the misalliance, treating Mr. Piozzi with civility."

After Ranelagh, following Madame Bernstein's advice, George returned to her ladyship's house, while Harry presented himself at the club, where gentlemen assembled nightly to sup and subsequently gamble. Naturally, no one alluded to Mr. Warrington's brief disappearance. Mr. Ruff, his former landlord, visited him with the utmost gravity and civility, acting as if no rift had ever existed between them. Mr. Warrington had previously ordered his trunks and belongings removed from Bond Street in the morning; he and his brother were now established in separate apartments.

However, once supper concluded and the gentlemen prepared to ascend to the gambling tables, Harry announced he would not play further. "I have already burned my fingers," he stated, "and can afford no more extravagance."

"Why," Mr. Morris said in a somewhat flippant tone, "you must have won more than you have lost, Mr. Warrington, after all is said and done."

"And of course I do not know my own affairs as well as you do, Mr. Morris," Harry replied sternly, remembering the other's conduct upon hearing of his arrest. "But I have another reason. A few months or days ago, I was heir to a great estate and could afford to lose a little money. Now, thank God, I am heir to nothing." He glanced around, blushing slightly, at the knot of gentlemen—his gaming associates—who lounged at the tables or gathered near the fire.

"How do you mean, Mr. Warrington?" cried Lord March. "Have you lost Virginia, too? Who has won it? I always fancied playing you myself for that stake."

"And growing an improved breed of slaves in the colony," added another.

"The rightful owner has won it. You have heard me tell of my twin elder brother?"

"Who was killed in that affair of Braddock's two years ago! Yes. Good gracious, my dear sir, I hope in heaven he has not come to life again?"

"He arrived in London two days hence. He was a prisoner in a French fort for eighteen months; he escaped only a few months ago and left our house in Virginia shortly after his release."

"You haven't had time to order mourning, I suppose, Mr. Warrington?" asked Mr. Selwyn, very good-naturedly and simply. Poor Harry did not grasp the meaning of the jest until his brother explained it to him.

"Hang me, if I don't believe the fellow is absolutely glad of the reappearance of his confounded brother!" shouted Lord March as they continued discussing the matter after the young Virginian had taken his leave.

"These savages practice the simple virtues of affection—they are barely civilized in America yet," yawned Selwyn. "They love their kin and scalp their enemies," simpered Mr. Walpole. "'Tis not Christian, but natural. Wouldn't you like to attend a scalping match, George, and watch a fellow skinned alive?"

"A man's elder brother is his natural enemy," said Mr. Selwyn, placidly arranging his money and counters.

"Torture is like broiled bones and pepper. You wouldn't relish simple hanging afterwards, George!" continued Horry.

"I'm hanged if there's any man in England who would like to see his elder brother alive," said Lord March.
