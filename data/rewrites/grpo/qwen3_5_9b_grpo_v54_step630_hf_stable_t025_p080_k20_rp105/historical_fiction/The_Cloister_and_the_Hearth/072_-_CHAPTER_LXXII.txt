The Dominicans, once the towering giants of European piety, were now retreating into the shadows, their influence waning before the surging tide of their Franciscan rivals. Even in England, a land steeped in faith where the Black Friars had once ruled supreme, the shift was palpable. Consequently, the order's wise leaders grew desperate to recruit and dispatch sons of exceptional talent to reclaim the world for their cause.

Clement possessed exactly such gifts. His mastery of languages—Latin, Italian, French, and both high and low Dutch—was renowned. He was destined for the pulpit in England, a mission sanctioned by Rome. Yet, Jerome, who held the superior's ear, obstructed this path.

"Clement," Jerome warned, "still carries the milk of the world in his veins. His feelings and weaknesses are fresh. Let not his new zeal or his humility tempt us to discard our ancient wisdom. Test him first, temper him, lest we one day lean on a reed for a staff."

"It is prudent advice," the Prior agreed. "Take him in hand yourself."

Thus, Jerome, adhering to the order's ancient prudence, began his trial of Clement.

First, he led the novice to a field where young men played their games, a haunt of Clement's former friends. Soon, Pietro Vanucci and Andrea passed by, casting a casual glance at the two friars. They failed to recognize their dead friend in the shaven monk. Clement gave a slight start, lowered his eyes, and recited a *Pater Noster*.

"Would you not speak with them, brother?" Jerome probed.

"No, brother. Yet it was good for me to see them. They remind me of sins I can never repent enough."

"It is well," Jerome replied, filing a cold report in Clement's favor.

Next, Jerome subjected Clement to the grim theater of death. He took him to dying beds, then into dungeons where darkness was absolute and the stench pestilential. There, men resembling wild beasts lay coiled in rags and filth, consumed by despair. The trials hardened Clement's body, but his soul gathered all its strength to comfort the wretched. In this crucible, Clement shone. Jerome reported that Clement's spirit was willing, though his flesh was weak.

"Good!" declared Anselm. "His flesh is weak, but his spirit is willing."

Yet, a greater trial awaited.

One morning, a principal street in Rome was choked with a crowd witnessing an execution. It was no common crime; the victim was no vulgar criminal. The Governor of Rome had been found slaughtered in his bed at dawn, his hand severed at the wrist, his throat cut, his temples bruised. The murder was traced to his servant, and the retribution was to be exacted immediately.

While Italian executions were rarely cruel, this demanded bloody precision. The criminal was dragged to the murdered man's house and pinned to the wall for half an hour. Then, his left hand was hacked off, mirroring his victim's. A raw chicken was slit open and bound to the bleeding stump—a macabre addition of some doctor's fancy. Mutilated and bandaged, the man was hurried to the scaffold. There, a young friar prayed with him, holding a crucifix close to his eyes.

Suddenly, the executioner shoved the friar aside and struck the culprit with a heavy mallet, then cut his throat from ear to ear. A cry of horror erupted from the crowd. The young friar swooned. A gigantic monk stepped forward and carried him off like a child.

Brother Clement returned to the convent discouraged. He confessed to the Prior with tears.

"Courage, son Clement," the Prior said. "A Dominican is not made in a day. You shall face another trial. And I forbid you to go fasting."

Clement bowed in obedience. He need not wait long. A robber, a monster of wanton cruelty, was brought to the scaffold. Clement spent his last night in prison with the man, accompanied him to the block, and prayed for him with such earnestness that the hardened ruffian wept and embraced him. Clement, though his flesh quivered with repugnance, embraced him too, holding the crucifix before the dying man's eyes.

As the man was garroted, Clement lost sight of the crowd but prayed loudly as the dark spirit departed. The moment the man died, the hangman raised his hatchet and quartered the body on the spot. The crowd, who had watched the living man die with indifference, uttered piteous cries at each stroke of the axe upon the corpse that could feel nothing. Clement shuddered but stood firm, like a rock that vibrates but cannot be thrown down.

Suddenly, Jerome's voice rang in his ear. "Brother Clement, mount that cart and preach to the people! Strike with all your force while the iron is hot, and souls may be saved."

Clement's color fled and returned; he breathed hard. Yet he obeyed. With an ill-assured step, he climbed the cart and faced his first crowd. The sea of upturned faces seemed to parch his throat; his heart thumped, his voice trembled.

But soon, the gravity of the occasion and the sight of the eager faces ignited the pale monk. He recounted the robber's history, fresh from his own lips in the prison cell, showing the crowd the ladder of folly and crime. As he thundered against shedders of blood and moved them to charity, his tremors vanished. He felt strung tight like a lute, gifted with an unsuspected force. He mastered the listening crowd, feeling their very pulse, playing sacred melodies on them as on a psaltery. Sobs and groans attested his power.

Jerome stared, astonished, like one lighting a rocket. When Clement caught his look of surprise and saw no approval, he broke off abruptly and joined Jerome.

"It was my first endeavor," he apologized. "Your behest came like a thunderbolt. Was I? Did I? Oh, correct me, aid me with your experience, Brother Jerome."

"Humph!" Jerome said doubtfully. After long reflection, he added sullenly, "Give the glory to God, Brother Clement. My opinion is you are an orator born."

He reported this to headquarters half-reluctantly, for he was an honest friar, albeit a disagreeable one.

Later, a case of sacrilege arose involving Julio Antonelli. Three witnesses placed him at the scene of the theft; others provided an alibi. Neither testimony could be shaken. Antonelli was granted the trial by water. The clergy, favoring him, recommended the hot water ordeal, which involved dipping an arm into boiling water to retrieve a pebble. Antonelli lacked the nerve and chose the cold trial instead.

Antonelli took the sacrament, was stripped naked on the banks of the Tiber, and tied hand and foot to prevent him from sinking by struggling. He was lowered gently into the stream, floating with only his hair above water. A simultaneous roar from the banks proclaimed him guilty. Moments later, the ropes, which happened to be new, became waterlogged, and he sank. Another roar declared his innocence. Left at the bottom for the appointed time, he was drawn up gurgling and gasping, screaming for mercy. After the prayers, he was dismissed, cleared of the charge.

On the bank, Clement prayed earnestly. When it ended, he thanked God in a loud, slightly quavering voice.

"Brother Jerome," he asked later, "ought not the man be compensated?"

"For what?"

"For the pain, the dread, the suffocation. Poor soul, he lives, but has tasted all the bitterness of death. Yet he did no ill."

"He is rewarded enough in being cleared of his fault."

"But being innocent, he drank Death's cup, though not to the dregs. His accusers, less innocent, suffered nought."

Jerome replied sternly, "It is not in this world men are truly punished, Brother Clement. Unhappy are they who sin yet suffer not. Happy are they who endure ills earth can inflict; it is counted to them above, a hundred-fold."

Clement bowed submissively. "May your good words not fall to the ground, but take root in my heart, Brother Jerome."

The severest trial, however, was unpremeditated. Jerome, in an indulgent mood, took Clement to Fra Colonna. There lay *The Dream of Polifilo*, freshly copied. The author, proud of his work, pointed out a masterstroke.

"For ages," Colonna declared, "fools have lavished poetic praise on mortal women, mere creatures of earth... Sirens at windows, Magpies at doors, Angels in the street, Devils in the house. Then come I and ransack minstrels' lines... and lay the whole bundle of spice at the feet of the only females worthy of amorous incense: the Nine Muses."

"By this goodly stratagem," Jerome interjected, turning the pages, "you, a friar of St. Dominic, have produced an obscene book." He dashed the manuscript onto the table.

"Obscene? Thou discourteous monk!" Colonna cried, snatching the book away, locking it, and trembling with mortification. "My Gerard, pshaw! Brother What's-his-name had not found *Polifilo* obscene. *Puris omnia pura*."

"Such as read your *Polifilo*—Heaven grant they may be few—will find him what I find him."

Colonna swallowed the bitter pill. Being a high-born gentleman and scholar, he avoided a vulgar quarrel. Instead, he turned the conversation to a beautiful chrysolite lent by Cardinal Colonna. While Clement handled the stone, he enlarged on its moral virtues, a trait of his age as a worshipper of jewels.

Jerome, however, expostulated. "One dead stone cannot confer valour, chastity, safety from poison, or temperance."

"The experience of ages proves they do," Colonna insisted. "As for temperance, look at this Gerard. He comes from the north where men drink like fishes, yet he is abstemious. Why? He carries an amethyst, the clearest I ever saw. Where is yours, unbeliever? Show it!"

"And 'twas that amethyst that made the boy temperate?" Jerome asked ironically.

"Certainly. What is the derivation of *amethyst*? *A* negative, and *methua* to tipple. Names are but signs of things. A stone is not called *amethustos* for two thousand years out of mere sport."

Colonna proceeded to illustrate the moral properties of rubies, sapphires, emeralds, and opals with anecdotes from grave historians.

"These are old wives' fables," Jerome scoffed. "Was ever such credulity?"

Credulity is a reproach skeptics often incur, yet it mortifies them none the less. The believer in stones writhed under the insult and dropped the subject. Mistaking his silence, Jerome exhorted him to abandon his pagan lore and study the lives of the saints. "Blot out these heathen superstitions from thy mind, brother, as Christianity hath blotted them from the earth."

In this strain, Jerome repeated loose theological statements. Then, the smarting *Polifilo* revenged itself. Colonna flew out, hurling a mountain of crude, miscellaneous lore upon Jerome.

"The heathen blotted out? Why, they hold four-fifths of the world. What have we Christians invented without their aid? Painting? Sculpture? These are heathen arts. What modern mind can conceive god-like forms as did the Athenian sculptors, the Libyan Licas, Scopas, Timotheus, Leochares, Briaxis? Chares, Lysippus, and the immortal three of Rhodes who wrought Laocoon from a single block? What prince has the genius to turn mountains into statues, as done at Bagistan? What town the soul to plant a colossus of brass in the sea?

"Is it architecture we invented? Why, here too we are children. Can we match the Parthenon with its Doric columns? Or the theaters of Greece and Rome? Or the prodigious temples of Egypt, with avenues a mile long of sphinxes? Even now their polished columns lie o'ergrown with acanthus, sparkling still, flouting the slovenly art of modern workmen. Is it sewers, aqueducts, viaducts? We have lost the art of making a road. Is it sepulchres? No Christian nation has ever erected a tomb that does not set a scholar laughing. Think of the Mausoleum, the Pyramids, the monstrous tombs of the Indus and Ganges.

"What then have we invented? Geometry? Logic? We are pupils of Euclid and Aristotle. Written characters? We no more invented them than Cadmus. Poetry? Homer, Virgil, Horace—none approached. Tragedy or comedy? Poets, actors, theaters fell to dust at our touch. Have we revived them? Would you compare our miserable mysteries with a Greek play performed inside a marble miracle, the audience a seated city, the poet a Sophocles?

"What then? Monotheism? The learned Greeks and Romans held it. *Zeus estin ouranos...* Their vulgar were polytheists; and what are ours? We have not invented the invocation of the saints. Our *sancti* answer their *Daemones* and *Divi*. The pagan vulgar prayed to deified mortals to intercede; the ruder minds worshipped them. So do the mob of Christians today. Holy Writ knows no polytheism. We have not invented a variety of it. The pagan vulgar worshipped deified mortals; our vulgar canonizes them. Each has a favorite to whom they pray ten times for once to God. Call that invention? Invention is confined to the East.
