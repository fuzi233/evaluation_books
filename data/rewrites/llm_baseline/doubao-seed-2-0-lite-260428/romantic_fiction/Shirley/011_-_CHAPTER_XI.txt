Caroline Helstone would not let her heart break quietly, not without a fight. Deep under her soft, girlish ribs, a fierce unplumbed strength burned bright, stoked by the raw, sharp pain that was her only constant companion now. No soul struggles harder than the one that fights entirely alone: no friend to lean on, no confidante to whisper her grief to, no one to pat her hand and say it will pass, no one even to notice she is suffering at all.

That was exactly Caroline’s lot. Her pain was her spur, and it drove her to push herself harder than she ever had before. She would not let the memory of Robert Moore eat her alive if she could help it. She threw herself into every task she could find: pored over French grammar, hemmed stacks of household linen, and tramped out the rectory door in every kind of weather, heading down the loneliest, wildest lanes she could find, no matter if rain bit at her cheeks or wind tangled her hair. Every evening she came back pale, her boots caked in mud, her dress spattered with mist, but she would not rest the second she stepped through the door. She tossed off her bonnet and shawl and paced her small bedroom for hours, until her legs shook so badly she could barely stand, until she all but collapsed from faintness. She told herself she did it to tire herself out enough to sleep through the night, but it never worked. When the rest of the rectory slumbered deep, she tossed on her pillow, or huddled at the foot of her bed in the dark, tears soaking the linen, that sharp, unbearable despair crashing over her in waves that left her as helpless as a lost little child.

Desperate as she was, temptation nipped at her heels: more than once she sat at her desk, pen in hand, scribbling half-letters to Robert, begging him to write to her sometimes, to not forget her entirely, even if only friendship was all she dared ask for. But shame and her own quiet pride always won, and she crumpled every single one, tossing the pages into the fire before she could seal the envelope.

By and by, the weight of it all grew so heavy she thought she could not bear it another day. She ached to leave Briarfield, to go somewhere so far away the very name of Hollow’s Mill would not make her chest ache. Another secret, deeper yearning grew stronger every day: she wanted to find the mother she had never known, the woman no one ever spoke of kindly. Her uncle’s cold quiet dislike of his late sister-in-law, the old servant who only ever called her “queer” and “unreadable” — all those stories iced over that longing, leaving her to wonder if it was better never to meet her than to meet her and find she could not love her own mother.

At last, one clear plan formed in her head: she would leave and take a position as a governess. It was the only honest work a penniless girl of her station could do. A small run of luck gave her the courage to finally speak of it to her uncle.

All her long walks led her, unbidden, to the same spot every twilight: the thorn tree stile at the very edge of the Hollow, where she could look down over the mill cottages, the dewy kitchen gardens, the still black mill dam, and the little counting-house window where, every evening at the same hour, Robert’s lamp would flare to life, bright as a tiny star. She waited for it, no matter the weather: clear nights it glowed soft gold, misty nights it blurred to a hazy smudge, rainy nights it fractured into a dozen tiny dancing sparks through the slanting drops. If a shadow passed between that lamp and the glass, her heart would leap so hard she thought it might burst: that was him, that was Robert. She would walk home then, comforted, carrying the sharp clear image of his face, his smile, his low voice, tucked safe in her memory, and she would tell herself he must still care, a little, that if she could only step in front of him he would reach for her hand, pull her close, and shelter her the way he used to. Those nights her tears did not sting quite so hot, her head ached a little less.

The shortest path home from the Hollow ran past the walls of Fieldhead, that old, grand, long-empty mansion Mr. Malone had stumbled past on that dark night not long before. It had stood tenantless for ten years, but Mr. Yorke had kept it in fine repair, with an old gardener and his wife living in the gate lodge to tend the lawns and keep the halls dusted. It was the very picture of a romantic old country house: gray stone weathered soft with moss, latticed windows, a deep carved stone porch, great spreading oaks behind it and a massive ancient cedar on the front lawn. The granite urns on the garden wall, the fretted stone arch of the gate, all looked as if they had stepped straight out of an artist’s sketchbook.

One mild May evening, Caroline wandered past as the full moon was rising, too heartsick to go back to the rectory to the empty quiet that waited for her there. She sat down on the moss by the gate, gazing through the iron bars at the dark cedar boughs and the pale stone gables glowing amber in the last of the sunset. The air was still, dewy, cloudless, and the moon hung low under the cedar’s raven-dark branches, shining soft and solemn right down on her. The whole night felt mournfully, perfectly lovely, and Caroline sat there and wondered why Providence would not have pity on her, why she could not just know a little peace. She was daydreaming of the old ballad lovers who met in just such quiet moonlit places, when suddenly the big front door of Fieldhead creaked open. Two men stepped out: one white-haired, elderly, that was Mr. Yorke, and the other was tall, dark-haired, Robert Moore. They walked across the lawn, out the garden gate, and down the lane without ever seeing her hidden in the shadows by the iron bars.

He was gone as fast as a flash of lightning, but the sight of him sent a shock right through her cold bones. Where before she had been despairing, now she was wild with desperate longing.
“Oh, if only he had been alone! If only he had seen me!” she whispered to the dark. “He would have stopped. He would have spoken. He does love me, he must. But the chance is gone. He passed me by as quiet, as empty, as a drift of wind through the grass. It’s cruel, it’s all cruel.”
She dragged herself home that night, sick with unmet longing.

The next morning at breakfast, she appeared at the table pale as a ghost, her cheeks drained of all their usual rose, and asked her uncle Mr. Helstone, “Would you mind, uncle, if I looked for a position working as a governess in a family?”

Her uncle stared at her, as bewildered as the coffee pot sitting on the table in front of him. “What nonsense is this?” he blurted. “Have you lost your wits? What on earth do you mean?”
“I’m not well. I need a change,” she said, quiet.

He leaned forward to look at her, and for the first time he noticed how thin she had grown, how all her bloom had faded away, like a summer rose withered down to a fragile snowdrop. Her flesh had wasted away, her eyes were big and dark in her sharpened face, and if not for the soft curve of her mouth and the thick cascade of her hair, no one would have called her pretty anymore.
“What’s wrong with you, child?” he demanded. “Shall I send for the doctor?”
Caroline shook her head. “A doctor can’t help me. All I need is new air, new scenery.”

He laughed, gruff but not unkind. “Well, if that’s what you want, you shall have it. We’ll send you off to the seaside at Cliff Bridge, Fanny will go with you. I’ll not have my niece working as a governess, not while I’m alive. People will talk, and say I can’t provide for my own flesh and blood. I’ll buy you an annuity, see? I’m only fifty-five, perfectly healthy, there’s plenty of time to sort all that out. Stop fretting about the future, there’s no need for it.”

Caroline sighed, but she knew there was no arguing with him. A week drifted by, and she stayed in that strange half-state, no worse, no better: she did not die, as all the old gossips in the village predicted she soon would, though her appetite was gone, her sleep was thin, and she was so weak she tired after walking half a mile. She avoided all the village calls, the tea parties where the other young ladies would stare at her, whispering behind their hands that she had been “disappointed in love,” a phrase that made her shrink so far into herself she hardly left the rectory walls at all.

One morning, her uncle burst into the parlour where she was daubing watercolours of wildflowers she had picked down by the Hollow, and ordered her to drop the paint, put her bonnet on, and come with him to pay a call.
“Where are we going?” she asked, bewildered — he never took her with him on calls.
“Fieldhead,” he said. “Miss Shirley Keeldar’s come home at last, turned twenty-one, moved into the house a week ago. Met her at the party last night you refused to come to. She’s a fine, spirited girl, no moping nonsense about her. You two will get on a treat. It’ll do you a world of good.”

Caroline’s heart fluttered with nerves as they walked up the broad paved drive to the old porch. She followed her uncle into the dark, vast vestibule, lit only by one small latticed window, its high walls hung with carved stags’ heads, its wide empty hearth stacked full of willow boughs for the summer. The servant showed them into a panelled parlour, its walls lined with dark glowing old oak, full of heavy antique furniture that had sat in that room for a hundred years at least.
Sitting by the fireplace was a soft, round, quiet-looking lady with auburn hair, dressed in an old-fashioned gown, who fumbled and stammered through greetings, clearly far more nervous than Caroline was. Caroline, used to shyness herself, sat down beside her to put her at ease, but Mr. Helstone’s sharp, sarcastic stare made the poor woman flustered more and more, until she stammered that she must go fetch Miss Keeldar, and had half risen from her chair when the glass door leading out to the garden swung open, and a girl stepped in.

Erect, bright-eyed, slight, her left hand full of daisies and wild roses tucked into her silk apron, Shirley Keeldar walked right up to Mr. Helstone, held out her right hand, and grinned. “I knew you’d come call on me, even if you are convinced Mr. Yorke has turned me into a Jacobin. Good morning!”
Mr. Helstone laughed, taking her hand. “Oh we’ll soon knock that Jacobin nonsense out of you, Miss Shirley. Now you’re here in my parish, you’re my pupil — I’ll teach you all the sound doctrine you need for politics and religion, mark my words.”
