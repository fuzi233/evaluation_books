Darkness crept over the countryside with all the awkward politeness of a party guest who’s just realized he’s outstayed his welcome, almost apologetic for having to snuff out what had been a perfect, sun-warmed summer day. West of the treeline, a faint pink afterglow still lingered like a half-empty glass of lemonade, and the new moon glinted sharp as a silver sickle bobbing over the big red barn roof.

Sally stepped out onto the porch gravel and bowed three times very gravely for luck, then breathed in deep lungfuls of sweet clover and cut grass. Life, she decided, was pretty much unbeatable right now. The skittish darkness, having dragged its feet at the start, soon got down to business, painting the sky a rich dark blue dotted with soft, twinkling stars. The pale smear of the state road leading off to Patchogue and Babylon vanished into the dark, and little yellow windows began to glow in the farmhouses scattered across the meadows. A sleepy single bark drifted over from the kennels, and the tiny fluffy white dog that had scampered out at Sally’s heels puffed out its chest and let out a ferocious squeak that would have terrified a dandelion, if dandelions had a sense of self-preservation.

The evening was so dead still that Ginger’s heavy clomping footsteps could be heard trundling down the road long before he turned in at the gate. He’d headed to the village an hour before to pick up provisions, the evening papers, and the bright blue wool Sally was halfway through knitting him a sweater out of, and Sally didn’t even need to see him to get that soft, warm little happy thrill that had wrapped around her every single evening for the past year.

“Ginger!” she called.
“WHAT HO!” he yelled back.

The tiny white dog—Toto, formerly the most lethargic pet in three counties—scuttled down the drive to investigate, still squeaking like a squeaky toy with something to prove. Ginger, for all his deep and abiding love of all canines, had never once warmed to Toto, and dodged the little furball’s leaps of welcome like he was avoiding a cactus. He’d protested loudly when Sally, who’d found their neighbor Mrs. Meecher in tears a month prior over her pet’s mysterious refusal to move faster than a snail on Valium, had volunteered to give the little guy country air and a strict diet.

“You’ve worked absolute miracles on him, angel,” Sally laughed, as he came up the steps still side-stepping Toto’s attempts to lick his ankle. “He’s a whole new dog!”
“Lucky little blighter,” Ginger grumbled. “All the years I boarded at Mrs. Meecher’s he never moved faster than a stately grandad on a Sunday stroll. Now he’s bouncing off the walls 24/7.”
“Turns out the little gremlin had been overeating cake crumbs from birth,” Ginger said, brightening. “That’s all that was wrong with him. A bit of no-nonsense dieting fixed him right up. We can ship him back to Mrs. Meecher next week, thank the Lord.”
“I’m going to miss him,” Sally said.
“Nearly missed him with my work boot this morning,” Ginger said darkly. “Caught him up on the kitchen counter halfway through a full heist of our breakfast bacon. Took emergency measures.”
“My caveman,” Sally cooed, squeezing his arm. “I always said you had a horribly brutal streak. Ginger, isn’t this the most perfect evening ever?”

“Good Lord,” Ginger blurted out suddenly the second they stepped into the warm glow of the open kitchen door. He stopped dead and stared at her, wide-eyed. “You know you look even prettier than you did when I left for the village ten minutes ago?”

Sally gave his arm a squeeze. “Beloved! Did you remember the chops?”

Ginger froze. His face dropped like a rock off a pier. “Oh my aunt! I clean forgot the chops.”
“Oh Ginger, you absolute old chump. Well, looks like you’re signing up for the same judicious dieting program as Toto for dinner tonight.”
“I’m so sorry! I remembered the blue wool, though!”
“If you think I’m chewing on knitting supplies for dinner—”
“Wait, what’s even left in the house?”
“Root vegetables and a bowl of pears,” Sally said cheerfully.
“Fine by me! Though if you really wanted chops I could sprint back to the village—”
“Not at all, I’m very spiritual. Besides, everyone says vegetables are great for the blood pressure or something. Wait—you forgot the mail too, right?”
“Absolutely not! I was on that mail like a hungry seagull on a french fry. Two letters from people wanting to buy Airedale puppies off us.”
“No! Ginger, we’re actually making it!”
“Pretty much rolling in cash at this rate,” Ginger agreed, preening a little. “If we keep this up we can buy that shiny little two-seater car we’ve been gushing over for months. There’s a letter for you, too. Here.”

“It’s from Fillmore!” Sally said, turning the envelope over in her hands as they trundled into the kitchen. “About time, too. I haven’t heard a peep from him in months.”

She sat down and tore the envelope open. Ginger hauled himself up onto the kitchen table, wiggled into a comfortable spot, and flipped open his evening paper. He skimmed the sports page in two minutes flat, then let the paper lower to stare at Sally’s bent head, brimming over with that soft, dazed contentment he still couldn’t believe was real, even after nearly a year of marriage. He’d chased after dozens of fun-looking things in his life that had all turned out to be duds up close, but marrying Sally? That was the first thing that was even better than it looked. No catches, no fine print, no nasty surprises.

Sally suddenly snort-laughed. “Ginger, look at this!”

He leaned down and took the slip of paper she held out. Bold black letters glared up at him:

“What in the world is that?” he frowned.
“It’s Fillmore!” Sally cackled. “He and Gladys opened a little restaurant in Pittsburgh.”

“A restaurant?” Ginger’s voice was full of outright shock. He’d always thought Fillmore, Sally’s brother, was a bona fide mastermind even after his fancy corporate manager gig had crashed and burned, and the idea of the self-proclaimed “Man of Destiny” running a tiny restaurant struck him as almost indecent.

Sally, for her part, was absolutely delighted. “It’s the most brilliant idea he’s ever had! He’s finally doing something that makes sense! They started tiny, just selling homemade pork pies out of a cart—”
“Why Popp?” Ginger asked, the question eating at his brain.
“Trade name, silly. Gladys is an amazing cook, remember? She baked all the pies, Fillmore wandered around peddling them, and they blew up so fast they got a full restaurant now. And it’s doing gangbusters. Listen to this.” Sally flipped a page of the letter, snickering again. “‘We’re on a rock-solid financial footing. Our success was so instant that I’m planning to expand on a massive scale. Big Ideas make Big Business! I’m going to open branches in New York, Chicago, Detroit, every major city, each run by a manager, all serving our famous Popp’s Outstanding Pork-pies alongside regular menu items. Once that’s done, I’m sailing for England to take over the European pie market...’ Isn’t he just the cutest little go-getter?”
“Total genius. Always said so.”
“I was about to panic halfway through reading, honestly. I’ve seen so many of Fillmore’s Big Ideas go completely off the rails right when they’re doing well. But thank heaven Gladys has him well in hand. She added a postscript of exactly four words that’s the most comforting thing I’ve read all year. It says ‘Yes, I don’t think!’ So no cross-country pork pie empire any time soon, thank goodness.”

“Pork pies,” Ginger said dreamily, as very loud healthy hunger pangs started rumbling in his stomach. “Man, I wish he’d sent us one of those outstanding little chaps. I could devour the whole thing in two bites.”

Sally stood up and ruffled his bright red hair. “Poor old Ginger, I knew the vegetable plan wasn’t gonna fly. C’mon, it’s a perfect night. Let’s walk to the village inn and stuff ourselves silly. We’re gonna be puppy millionaires before we know it, we can absolutely afford the splurge.”
