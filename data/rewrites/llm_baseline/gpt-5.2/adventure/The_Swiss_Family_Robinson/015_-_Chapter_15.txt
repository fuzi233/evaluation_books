The morning Fritz chose for his first true venture in the cajack arrived with a hush that seemed made on purpose for boldness—a sea smoothed to glass, the bay breathing in slow, green sighs, and every gull-cry hung for an instant above the water before falling away. Yet the stillness did not soften the comic sight of my eldest as he squeezed himself into the tight little craft in the strange swimming dress his mother had contrived for him—trousers, jacket, cap, all close and clinging—and then began to inflate himself by puffing and blowing into the skin covering, as if he meant to become part boat, part boy.

He crouched there, cheeks ballooning, shoulders rising, his whole figure swelling until—like the fabled frog—he seemed in danger of bursting from his own ambition. The lads shouted with laughter. Even my wife, who had stitched and fastened every seam with anxious ingenuity, could not keep her mouth from curving; though she tried to hide the smile behind the very hands that had made his ridiculous bravery possible.

I had our larger boat brought out and kept ready, because fatherly pride never quite smothers fatherly caution, and I wished my wife to see that if the sea decided to prove itself less tender than the morning looked, we were prepared to go at once after him.

From a shelving strip of shore we slid the cajack into the bay. It floated as lightly as a leaf on a bowl of jade. Fritz set his paddles and began his trials: first a straight dart that cut the water with arrowy neatness, then a wheel to the right, then to the left; and, as if the sea were a domestic floor and he meant to dance upon it, he leaned and turned until the little craft lay half on its side.

My wife cried out, the sound sharp as a snapped string in that wide quiet; but the cajack only kissed the water and rose again. It neither capsized nor drank in the sea. Fritz recovered, grinning, and skimmed away, encouraged by our cheers.

Then, warmed by triumph and the dangerous ease of it, he pushed too boldly into the strong pull of Jackal River’s mouth. The current caught him with indifferent strength and carried him outward as if the bay had only been a cradle, and the open sea its true bed.

That was more than I had bargained for. I sprang into the boat with Ernest and Jack, and we bent to the oars. Behind us my wife urged us on with a voice that tried to command the elements by sheer will and, between commands, could not keep from naming the cajack what she felt it was—“that horrid soap-bubble”—as though words might pop it and end her fear.

Outside the bay we reached the rocks where once the wreck had lain, and we scanned the water until the breadth of it felt like mockery. There was no sign—only the vast, breathing surface and the changeable music of wind and tide. At last, far off, a faint puff of smoke lifted, and then came the crack of a pistol: sound, that faithful messenger over water, running to us quicker than sight.

We answered with our own shot. Another replied. We steered by those brief, harsh syllables in the air, and soon, above the rush of our own oars, we heard Fritz’s cheerful halloo. The cajack shot out from behind a point of land as if it had been hiding there for sport, and we drew alongside.

“Come to this rocky beach!” he cried, eyes bright with the kind of pride that makes a boy forget his own peril. “I have something to show you.”

We grounded the boat and climbed out—and there, among the rocks, lay a young walrus, well grown, thick-limbed, and quite dead, the skin still damp and shining, the very heft of it proclaiming how hard the sea had worked to build such flesh.

“Did you kill this creature, my dear Fritz?” I exclaimed; and though I spoke in wonder, my glance went instinctively to the thickets and the sea beyond, half expecting some unknown hunter—some naked islander, perhaps—to step forward and claim the prize.

“To be sure, father! Don’t you see my harpoon?” He held it up, indignant at doubt. “Why do you doubt it?”

“I scarcely know,” I replied, and could not help laughing, for success so swift and so strangely apt for an amateur Greenlander startled me. “I congratulate you, my boy. But you have alarmed us. You should not have gone out of the bay. I left your mother in grievous trouble.”

He sobered for an instant, and his honesty came through the triumph. “Indeed, father, I had no idea of passing out of sight. Once in the current I was carried along and could not help myself. Then I came upon a herd of walruses, and I so longed to make a prize of one that I forgot everything else. I chased them when I was beyond the pull of the current, and when I got near enough I harpooned this one—twice. He swam slower than the rest. He tried to take refuge among these rocks and expired. I landed and scrambled to him—but I gave him the contents of my pistol first, before going close up. I remembered the big serpent’s parting fling at you, Jack.”

“You ran a very great risk,” I told him. “The walrus is inoffensive, but wounded it can turn furious and, with those tusks, can destroy a stout boat. Thank God for your safety. I value that above a thousand such creatures. Now—what shall we do with him? He must be fourteen feet long, though not full grown.”

Fritz ran his hand along the massive head with an artist’s longing. “We cannot move such weight from among these rocks,” he admitted, “but do let me carry away the head with those grand snow-white tusks. I should like to fasten it on the prow of the cajack and name it the Sea-horse.”

“We must certainly carry away the ivory,” I said. The air had thickened, close and sultry as breath held too long. “But make haste. A storm is brewing.”

“The head! The head!” Jack cried, already seeing a trophy more splendid than sense. “Think how it will look on the cajack!”

“And think how it will smell,” Ernest added dryly, pinching his nose as if imagination alone offended him. “What a treat for the steersman when it begins to putrefy.”

“Oh, we will prepare for that,” Fritz insisted, his mind already arranging processes like recipes. “It shall be soaked and cleaned and dried until it is as hard as a wooden model. It shall not offend your delicate nose in the least, Ernest.”

Ernest, ever ready with a fact, observed, “I supposed the walrus to be peculiar to the Arctic regions.”

“And so it is,” I answered, even as we set to work. “Though they may occasionally be seen elsewhere; these may have wandered far. On the eastern coast of Africa there is a smaller kind called the dugong—long incisor teeth but not tusks, and more like a seal than a walrus.”

With no proper tools for such butchery, our labour became slow and stubborn. The knife dragged; the sinews resisted; the thick skin yielded only in long, reluctant strips. Yet necessity teaches hands to become clever, and we managed to sever the great head and cut away lengths of hide. Fritz, flushed with accomplishment, remarked that henceforth the cajack must be provisioned like a true hunting craft—with a stout knife, a hatchet, and, he added with practical foresight, a small compass in a glass-topped box fixed before the opening where the steersman sits. I promised him it should be so.

When the work was finished, I proposed to take Fritz and his frail boat aboard ours, so we might return together. But he begged earnestly to go alone as he had come; he longed to be our avant-courier, to announce our approach and his own victory to his mother. At last I yielded. The cajack slipped away ahead, a small dark speck on the bright water, while we followed more slowly, burdened with the walrus head and hides.

Then the weather, which had been a friendly stage for heroics, changed its temper. Black clouds gathered thick and fast. The air dropped as if the sky had leaned closer. A tremendous storm broke upon us with startling suddenness. Fritz vanished from sight; he was beyond our reach.

We buckled on our swimming belts and lashed ourselves to the boat. Waves rose and broke over us like moving walls. Wind tore the surface into white fury. Rain fell in torrents that stung the skin and drowned the softer sounds, leaving only the harsh, relentless roar. Lightning flared livid across the darkness, making the sea for one instant a sheet of hammered metal and, the next, a blind gulph again.

Ernest and Jack faced it nobly, their young bodies braced and their voices steady when they spoke. I watched our boat—our frail, faithful bark—lift and settle with a buoyancy that gave me courage, even while dread sat cold in my chest. The storm swept on and passed as abruptly as it had come. The sky began to clear; yet the sea continued its long after-anger, and for a time every wave still threatened to throw us down and finish what the wind had begun.

For ourselves I did not utterly despair. But for Fritz—alone, ahead, in that narrow shell—I could not keep hope from breaking into prayer. In my heart I felt him lost, and I begged only for strength, if the worst should be, to say, “Thy will be done.”

At last we rounded the point and entered Safety Bay again, drawing near our little harbour. And then—what a shock of joy, what a mercy so sudden it felt like the world turning back upon itself—we saw my wife kneeling on the shore with Fritz beside her, and little Franz too, their heads bowed, their hands clasped, praying so earnestly for our deliverance that they did not perceive us until we cried out.

They looked up. My wife’s face, wet with rain and tears alike, changed in a breath from fear to gratitude. We sprang ashore to one another, and the joy of that meeting—after the storm’s brutal noise—was like stepping into a warm room where the kettle sings. Together we gave thanks for the lives spared.

Back at Rockburg, we stripped off our drenched clothes and put on warm, dry garments that smelled of smoke and stored wool. My wife, as if to anchor our shaken spirits in familiar labour, set about a meal with brisk hands. The ordinary miracle of food—its steam, its salt, its comfort—rose between us and the memory of the lightning. We ate, and in the secure ritual of sitting together, we began to speak calmly of the danger we had just survived, as if naming it could keep it from returning.

Afterwards the walrus head was carried to our workshop, and there my wife’s domestic ingenuity met Fritz’s hunter’s vanity and Ernest’s fastidious nose in a kind of household treaty. It underwent a thorough cleaning—scraped and washed, soaked, and tended as carefully as any curing meat—then embalmed and dried until it hardened and lost the offence of decay. Ere long it was fixed upon the prow of the cajack, the snow-white tusks gleaming, and the little boat, crowned with that grim splendour, did indeed look imposing. The strips of hide, tanned and prepared, became valuable leather—another proof, in our island life, that nothing useful should be wasted and every creature taken must be honoured by complete employment.

The storm had not limited its mischief to our nerves. Heavy rain flooded the streams and battered the crops that ought to have been housed before the rainy season. The bridge over Jackal River was partly broken down. Water tanks and pipes needed repair. Days filled with work—mending, shoring up, restoring—until the island’s damage felt stitched and bound again into the routines that kept us alive.

One day near the cascade, while we laboured, we found a number of dark-red berries scattered on the ground, about the size of hazel-nuts, each with a small leafy coronet at the tip. The boys, lured by colour and the careless appetite of youth, tasted them at once. A chorus of angry exclamations followed—spitting and spluttering. Even Knips rejected them with offended dignity. They would have been thrown aside in contempt, had not the smell—warm, sharp, strangely familiar—drawn me to examine them more closely.

The scent, carried on the damp air, seemed to cut through the roar of the cascade like a thin, bright note. I decided they were cloves.

At once some plants were set in the nursery garden. My wife’s eyes lit with the quiet triumph of a cook who has been granted a new language for the tongue. That excellent spice, she said, would flavour boiled rice and other dishes in place of pepper—no small variety for a family whose meals, however wholesome, could too easily become monotonous. Soon, when the pot steamed on the hearth, the clove-scent rose through Rockburg like a memory one could not quite translate into words: not merely “spice,” but the suggestion of far ports, of kitchens left behind, of names for tastes that in our island life might otherwise die out.

We had brought a good supply of clay from the bed near Falconhurst, and I proposed to use it for making aqueducts. The recent rains had shown how quickly our young corn responded to water, and I resolved to irrigate the fields with the drainage from our crushing-mill. Thus disaster instructed us: the island’s violence became the argument for better channels, better storage, better habit.

The fishing season returned successfully. Salmon, sturgeon, herring—silver bodies flashing, scales bright as coins—rewarded our annual efforts, and the store-room once more looked like security itself. Much as I wished for a constant supply of fresh fish, I could not accept Jack’s naive proposal that we tether a shoal of salmon by the gills to the bottom of the bay, as we had secured the turtles. Even in this life of contrivances, there are limits that nature insists upon, and the idea made us laugh in spite of ourselves.

Quiet days followed, and I noticed a restlessness in the boys—an impatience with the steady, repetitive labour of farm and repairs. Routine, which keeps a family alive, can also dull young minds; their bodies longed for movement, their spirits for novelty. They were hungry, not merely for food, but for change.

At length Fritz spoke what all three felt. “Father, we want a quantity of hurdles, and we have scarcely any more bamboos. Had we not better get a supply from Woodlands? And you said you wished you had more of that fine clay—we might visit the Gap at the same time.”

I had no objection. It was soon settled that Fritz, Jack, and little Franz should go together. Ernest, who felt no great desire to accompany his brothers, would remain with his mother and me and assist in the construction of a sugar-mill—an undertaking I had long contemplated, and one that promised to turn our cane into something more lasting than mere stalks in a field.

Before they started, Fritz begged bear’s meat from his mother “to make pemmican.”

“And what may pemmican be?” she asked, half amused, half mistrustful, already imagining the kitchen’s limits being tested by one of her sons’ schemes.

He answered with the air of a young man reciting from the chronicles of explorers. “It is food carried by the fur-traders of North America on their long journeys—bear or deer flesh first cooked, then pounded or ground to powder. It is portable and nourishing.”

She consented, as she said, “to humour him,” though her face showed little faith in the outcome. Yet, because she was wise enough to know that men who travel need more than bread—need belief, need the comfort of a provision prepared by familiar hands—she set to the task. Over two days the bear’s meat was cooked, dried, and reduced as best we could manage, the work filling Rockburg with a dense savour—smoke, fat, and the deep, lingering scent of wilderness brought into a domestic place. Fritz watched with reverence, as if the very pounding were a rite.

They were ready at last. As they loaded the cart, I observed Jack quietly slip a basket containing several pigeons under the packages.

“Oh, oh,” I thought, “the little fellow has doubts about that pemmican, and thinks a tough old pigeon would be preferable.”

The weather was exquisite when they set off—clear, bright, and so pleasant it felt almost unearned after the storm. We admonished them, as parents must, to prudence and caution; and they departed in the highest spirits. Storm and Grumble drew the cart and were ridden by Fritz and Franz. Hurry carried Jack swiftly across the bridge in advance, followed by Floss and Bruno, barking at his heels.

The sugar-mill occupied us for several days. We built it much like our other mills, and I need not describe it here. Yet I will say that the rhythm of its planned labour—the turning, the crushing, the promise of sweetness—comforted my wife more than she admitted. The sound of honest work is its own kind of music, and far kinder to the spirit than the shriek of storm or the sudden crack of gunfire.

On the evening of the first day, as we rested in the porch at Rockburg, our talk naturally returned to the absentees. We wondered what they were doing and how they fared. Ernest looked rather mysterious and hinted that he might have news by the next morning.

As he spoke, a bird alighted on the dove-cot and entered. In the failing light I could not tell whether it was ours or a stray. Ernest sprang up at once, eager, and went to see. He returned in a few minutes with a scrap of paper in his hand.

“News, father! The very latest news by pigeon-post, mother!”

“Well done, boys!” I cried, taking the note. “What a capital idea!” And I read:

“Dearest parents and Ernest,

“A brute of a hyaena has killed a ram and two lambs. The dogs seized it. Franz shot it. It is dead and skinned. The pemmican isn’t worth much, but we are all right. Love to all.

“Fritz

“Woodlands, 15th instant”

“A true hunter’s letter!” I laughed, though my wife’s face tightened at the word hyaena. “What exciting news. When does the next post come in, Ernest?”

“Tonight, I hope,” he said, delighted with the system.

My wife sighed and declared she would much rather wait to hear everything when her sons were safe home again than receive these sharp glimpses into danger carried on wings. Yet even she could not deny that the small, soft flutter of a pigeon’s arrival was a sound far gentler than the silence of not knowing.

Thus the winged letter-carriers kept us informed, day by day, of the outline of adventures which were afterwards told in fuller detail.

As the boys approached the farm at Woodlands, they were startled by a sound like human laughter repeated again and again—an ugly imitation, too wild and too hollow to be truly mirth. The oxen grew uneasy, the dogs drew close and growled, and the ostrich bolted outright, carrying Jack into the rice swamp as if panic were a whip.

The laughter continued. The animals became unmanageable.

“Something is very far wrong!” cried Fritz. “I cannot leave the animals; but while I unharness them, do you, Franz, take the dogs and advance cautiously to see what is the matter.”

Without hesitation Franz slipped into the bushes with his gun, Floss and Bruno tight behind him. Through an opening in the thicket, at a distance of about forty paces, he saw the cause: an enormous hyaena in a state of mad excitement, dancing around a newly killed lamb and uttering that ghastly, hysterical laughter that had pealed through the forest.

The creature ran back and forth, rose on its hind legs, whirled round and round, nodded its head, and performed frantic, ludicrous antics—horror masquerading as play. Franz kept his presence of mind. He waited until the hyaena calmed enough to begin tearing at its prey with growls that turned the air sour. Then, firing steadily both barrels, he broke its foreleg and wounded it in the breast.

Meanwhile Fritz had unyoked the oxen and secured them to trees and now hurried to Franz’s aid. The dogs and the dying beast were locked in mortal struggle; but though it severely wounded both Floss and Bruno, it soon succumbed. It was dead when the boys reached the spot.

Their shout of triumph guided Jack back from the swamp. Before any inspection or boasting, their first care was for the dogs. They dressed the wounds as best they could, hands gentled by affection even in the midst of blood and danger. Only then did they examine the hyaena: as large as a wild boar, a mane of long stiff bristles on its neck, grey fur marked with black, jaws of extraordinary strength, muscular thighs, claws sharp and powerful. But for Franz’s timely shots, it would certainly have been more than a match for the dogs.

After unloading the cart at the farm, they returned for the carcass of the tiger-wolf—as it is sometimes called—and spent the remainder of the day skinning it. When the carrier pigeon had been dispatched to Rockburg, they lay down on bearskin rugs, tired enough to sleep, yet full enough of life to dream of adventures past and future.

The following day they devised a scheme to survey the shores of Wood Lake and mark the spots where the surrounding marsh could be crossed. Fritz took the cajack; the other boys kept to shore. Whenever firm footing reached the water’s edge, they planted a tall bamboo bearing a high bundle of reeds and branches—a visible sign for future use, as if they were writing a map in the island’s own alphabet.

They captured three young black swans after spirited resistance from the old birds, and later brought them to Rockburg to ornament Safety Bay. Presently a beautiful heron thrust his long neck from among the reeds to see what disturbance had come to his domain.

Before he could satisfy his curiosity, Fritz unhooded his eagle. The proud bird flapped and struggled, but its legs and wings were bound gently and firmly, and it had to submit to inspection by delighted captors.

Alarm came again swiftly. A large powerful animal puffed through the dense reed-thicket close by, making a curious whistling sound. It vanished almost at once. From their description it must have been a tapir—dark brown, shaped like a young rhinoceros but without a horn, with an upper lip prolonged into a short trunk like an elephant’s in miniature. Gentle enough unless attacked, capable of fierce defence, dangerous to dogs with its teeth, and perfectly at ease swimming and diving in swampy rivers. Fritz followed in the cajack for a time but saw no more of it.

Meanwhile Jack and Franz returned by the rice-fields and encountered a flock of cranes. They managed to catch five or six alive, among them two demoiselle or Numidian cranes, using arrows fitted with loops of cord dipped in birdlime so that the bird aimed at was often entangled and brought down uninjured—an original contrivance, half hunting and half craft.

They lived comfortably on peccary ham, cassava bread, fruit, baked potatoes, and milk—provisions that, in their telling later, sounded less like mere fuel and more like a small travelling kitchen, each taste a reassurance that home could be carried in the mouth. As for the pemmican, one trial was enough. It went to the dogs. Fritz, however, did not abandon the idea; he determined to attempt it again, knowing its value when properly prepared.

After collecting rice and cotton, they went on to Prospect Hill. There, Fritz said afterwards, they found the pine wood in possession of troops of monkeys, who howled and chattered like demons and pelted them with pine cones until the air seemed full of hard, spinning missiles. At last the boys fired a few shots; several monkeys fell; the rest fled, and the party continued in peace—only to discover that Prospect Hill itself had been overrun and ruined, as Woodlands had been the previous summer. Dirt and disorder met them everywhere. They cleaned and set things right as best they could, yet preferred the tent for sleeping.

Fritz felt at a loss how to guard the place. Then he found a bottle of poisonous euphorbia gum in the tool chest and devised a plan that succeeded beyond his expectations. He mixed poison with milk, bruised millet, and anything he thought the monkeys would eat, and put the mixture in cocoanut shells hung high enough to be out of reach of our own animals.

That evening, Fritz said, was calm and lovely—the sea murmuring in the distance, the moon rising to lay a pale beauty over land and water. For a time, in that solemn stillness, their feelings were touched; the world seemed to hold its breath.

Then the spell broke.

From every side came an outburst of hideous, discordant noises, as though the forest itself had been wounded. Snorting, snarling, shrieking filled the woods beneath them. Jackals howled mournfully from the hills; Fangs answered in the yard, and Floss and Bruno backed him with barking and yelping. Far away beyond the rocky fastnesses of the Gap came hollow snortings and neighings that reminded one of the hippopotamus’s strange cry. Above these were occasional deep, majestic roarings that made their hearts quail with the conviction that lions and elephants spoke in the dark.

Overawed and silent, they tried to sleep, but soon the most fearful cries in the adjoining woods announced that the apes were suffering from the poisoned food. The dogs could not keep quiet amid such uproar, and the boys did not sleep until morning. When they rose late, they saw an awful spectacle: dead monkeys and baboons thickly strewn beneath the trees around the farm.

Fritz would not tell us how many. He only said he wished he had not found the poison. They cleared away the bodies and the dangerous food, burying some deep and carrying the rest to the shore to pitch over the rocks into the sea. Then they travelled on to the Gap.

That same evening a messenger pigeon arrived at Rockburg with a note whose concluding words struck my heart like a sudden cold wind:

“The barricade at the Gap is broken down. Everything laid waste as far as the sugar-brake, where the hut is knocked to pieces, and the fields trampled over by huge footmarks. Come to us, father—we are safe, but feel we are no match for this unknown danger.”

I lost not an instant. Late as it was, I saddled Swift and set off at once to their assistance, telling Ernest to prepare the small cart and follow with his mother at daybreak, bringing everything we might need for camping out several days.

Bright moonlight favoured my ride, and I reached the Gap sooner than they expected. My arrival surprised and delighted the boys. Early next morning I examined the footprints and ravages of the great unknown.

There was no doubt: an elephant had visited our cane-brake. Thick posts in the barricade were snapped like reeds. Trees near the spot where we had planned a cool summer-house were stripped of leaves and branches to a great height, as if some enormous hand had combed them bare. Worst of all, the young sugar-cane plants were either devoured or trampled down and destroyed.

It seemed not one elephant but a troop had invaded. Tracks were numerous; footprints of various sizes. Yet to my satisfaction I saw that the marks could be traced not only from the Gap but back to it in equal numbers. We concluded, therefore, that the mighty animals had withdrawn into their own wilds after this freebooting incursion, and did not remain hidden within our territory.

Still, the lesson was clear: our ramparts must be strengthened. That night, sitting beside an enormous watch-fire whose crackling and steady roar made a brave counter-music to the remembered uproar of the forest, I laid plans with my sons. They told me, in turn, the details of all they had seen and done—tales so vivid and various that, though we should have slept, each of us was more inclined to keep watch, to listen to the island’s breathing in the dark, and to guard what we had built with hands that, by now, had learned both labour and defence.
