There is a peculiar kind of satisfaction in enjoying one's own misery, a genuine delight in being thoroughly wretched; yet, nobody actually wants a fit of the blues. Still, everyone gets them, and nobody can explain why. It's a mystery that defies accounting. You might find yourself plunged into this gloom the very day you inherit a fortune, just as easily as the day you leave your new silk umbrella behind on a train. The effect is akin to suffering from a simultaneous attack of toothache, indigestion, and a severe cold. Suddenly, you are stupid, restless, and irritable; rude to strangers and potentially dangerous to friends; clumsy, maudlin, and quarrelsome—a nuisance to yourself and everyone within a mile radius.

While the blues are upon you, you are paralyzed by the urge to act. You feel bound to do something, yet you can do nothing. You can't sit still, so you grab your hat and stride out for a walk. But before you reach the corner of the street, you wish you'd stayed home, turn around, and head back. You open a book to read, only to find Shakespeare trite, Dickens dull, Thackeray boring, and Carlyle overly sentimental. You toss the book aside and mutter curses at the authors. Then you shoo the cat out of the room and kick the door shut after her. You resolve to write your letters, but after staring at "Dearest Auntie: I find I have five minutes to spare..." for fifteen minutes without conjuring another sentence, you crumple the paper, fling the wet pen onto the tablecloth, and declare you must visit the Thompsons. As you pull on your gloves, however, it hits you: the Thompsons are idiots, they don't have supper, and you'll be expected to jump the baby. You curse them, decide against the visit, and feel completely crushed.

You bury your face in your hands, wishing for death and heaven. You visualize your own sickbed, surrounded by weeping friends and relations. You bless them all, especially the young and pretty ones, convinced they will value you only when you're gone. You learn too late what they've lost, bitterly contrasting their presumed future regard with their current lack of veneration. These thoughts bring a fleeting cheer, only to vanish instantly. Now you realize how foolish you were to think anyone would care if you blew up, were hanged, married, or drowned. Nobody cares for you. You've never been properly appreciated. You review your entire life and conclude you've been ill-treated from the cradle.

Half an hour of this contemplation builds a savage fury against everyone and everything, especially yourself, whom anatomy alone prevents you from kicking. Finally, bedtime arrives to stop you from doing something rash. You spring upstairs, strip off your clothes—leaving them strewn across the floor—blow out the candle, and jump into bed as if you'd backed yourself into a heavy wager to finish the routine against the clock. For hours you toss and tumble, occasionally jerking the covers off to get out and dress again just to vary the monotony. Eventually, you drift into an uneasy, fitful slumber, plagued by bad dreams, and wake up late the next morning.

This, at least, is what poor single men can do under the circumstances. Married men, meanwhile, bully their wives, grumble at the dinner, and insist the children go to bed. This domestic chaos creates a disturbance that must be a great relief to a man in the blues, since rows are the only form of amusement he can take an interest in.

The symptoms are much the same in every case, though the affliction goes by different names. The poet speaks of "a feeling of sadness coming o'er him." 'Arry tells Jimee he has "got the blooming hump." Your sister feels "out of sorts" and fears nothing terrible is happening. The everyday young man says, "I'm so awful glad to meet you, old fellow, for I feel so jolly miserable this evening." As for me, I usually claim, "I have a strange, unsettled feeling tonight," and "think I'll go out."

By the way, it never happens except in the evening. During the day, when the world bounds forward full of life, we cannot afford to sigh and sulk. The roar of the working day drowns out the elfin sprites singing their low-toned *miserere* in our ears. In the daylight, we are angry, disappointed, or indignant, but never "in the blues" and never truly melancholy. If things go wrong at ten in the morning, we swear and knock furniture about; but if the misfortune strikes at ten at night, we retreat to poetry or sit in the dark pondering how hollow the world is.

Usually, it isn't trouble that makes us melancholy. Reality is too stern a thing for sentiment. We linger to weep over a picture, but turn our eyes away from the original. There is no pathos in real misery; no luxury in real grief. We don't toy with sharp swords or hug a gnawing fox to our breast for pleasure. When a person loves to brood over a sorrow and keeps it fresh in their memory, you can be sure it is no longer a pain. They may have suffered initially, but the recollection has become a pleasure. Many dear old ladies who daily gaze at tiny shoes in lavender-scented drawers, weeping for the tiny feet whose toddling march is done, or sweet-faced young ones placing locks of hair beneath their pillows—locks once curled on a boyish head kissed to death by salt waves—will call me a nasty cynical brute. But if they asked themselves truthfully whether dwelling on such sorrow is unpleasant, they would be compelled to answer "No." Tears are as sweet as laughter to some natures. The proverbial Englishman, as the chronicler Froissart noted, takes his pleasures sadly, and the Englishwoman goes a step further, taking her pleasures in sadness itself.

I am not sneering. I would never sneer at anything that helps keep hearts tender in this hard old world. We men are cold and common-sensed enough; we wouldn't want women to be the same. No, ladies dear, remain sentimental and soft-hearted—be the soothing butter to our coarse, dry bread. Besides, sentiment is to women what fun is to us. They don't care for our humor, surely it would be unfair to deny them their grief. Who can say their mode of enjoyment isn't as sensible as ours? Why assume that a doubled-up body, a contorted purple face, and a gaping mouth emitting ear-splitting shrieks indicate a state of more intelligent happiness than a pensive face resting on a little white hand, with gentle, tear-dimmed eyes looking back through Time's dark avenue at a fading past?

I am glad when I see Regret walked with as a friend. I know the saltness has been washed from the tears, and the sting plucked from the beautiful face of Sorrow before we dare press her pale lips to ours. Time has laid his healing hand upon the wound. We can look back upon the pain we once fainted under without bitterness or despair rising in our hearts. The burden is no longer heavy when our past troubles are mixed with the same sweet blend of pleasure and pity we feel when old, knight-hearted Colonel Newcome answers "*adsum*" to the great roll-call, or when Tom and Maggie Tulliver, clasping hands through the mists that have divided them, go down, locked in each other's arms, beneath the swollen waters of the Floss.

Thinking of poor Tom and Maggie brings to mind a saying of George Eliot regarding this subject of melancholy. She spoke somewhere of the "sadness of a summer's evening." How wonderfully true—like everything from that wonderful pen—the observation is! Who hasn't felt the sorrowful enchantment of those lingering sunsets? The world belongs to Melancholy then, a thoughtful, deep-eyed maiden who loves not the glare of day. It is not until "light thickens and the crow wings to the rocky wood" that she steals forth from her groves. Her palace is in twilight land. It is there she meets us. At her shadowy gate, she takes our hand and walks beside us through her mystic realm. We see no form, but seem to hear the rustling of her wings.

Even in the toiling, hum-drum city, her spirit comes to us. There is a somber presence in each long, dull street; the dark river creeps ghostlike under the black arches, as if bearing some hidden secret beneath its muddy waves.

In the silent country, when trees and hedges loom dim and blurred against the rising night, and a bat's wing flutters in our face, and the land-rail's cry sounds drearily across the fields, the spell sinks deeper into our hearts. We seem to stand by some unseen death-bed, hearing the sigh of the dying day in the swaying of the elms.

A solemn sadness reigns. A great peace surrounds us. In its light, our working-day cares grow small and trivial. Bread and cheese—yes, and even kisses—do not seem the only things worth striving for. Thoughts we cannot speak but only listen to flood in upon us. Standing in the stillness under Earth's darkening dome, we feel that we are greater than our petty lives. Hung round with those dusky curtains, the world is no longer a mere dingy workshop, but a stately temple wherein man may worship, and where, at times in the dimness, his groping hands touch God's.
