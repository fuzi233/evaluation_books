"Eli," Catherine commanded, her voice cutting through the quiet of the room, "give me a straight answer now, like a man, and I promise to trouble you no further today. Tell me plainly: what is wormwood?"

Eli hesitated, looking suddenly at sea with this abrupt summons to his wits, though he quickly steadied himself. "It is a herb, Mistress," he stammered, "with a taste so sharp and bitter it makes the tongue ache."

"That is a fair description, my man, yet not the truth I seek."

"Then perhaps you must define it yourself."

"And so I shall!" Eli groaned, the weight of the word settling heavily on his heart. "Wormwood is having two men under the same roof who sit idle, waiting for your shoes and mine, doing naught but breathing air." The barb had struck deep.

"Methinks," Catherine mused, pacing the floor, "that idleness is poison enough to curdle blood and turn souls sour, far worse than any natural ailment. If you will not act, why not set them up somewhere else? Give them a chance to earn their bread."

Eli nodded slowly, expressing his willingness but voicing a lingering fear. "I am loath to part with them, but I dread the thought. They may easily squander their earnings on ale and dice games until they have gambled away their very shelves."

"Nay," Catherine replied firmly, her eyes flashing with resolve. "Do you take me for a fool? Of course, I intend to keep a close watch upon them at first, guiding them with a loose rein, as the saying goes, until they find their footing."

"But where did you conceive such a scheme?" Eli asked, suspicion coloring his tone. "Not here, to split our own trade, surely?"

"Not likely," Catherine dismissed. "I speak of Rotterdam. There, amidst the bustling markets, I could launch them."

Ah, there lay the deception! To the surface, it was charity; beneath, it was a desperate desire to draw near to little Gerard again.

After days of debate and earnest vows of reform from Cornelis and Sybrandt, Catherine ventured to Rotterdam, taking Kate with her. Their search proved fruitful; they secured a shop in a respectable street, though it was woefully disordered. Yet, necessity dictated price, and they purchased it cheaply. Immediately, they set to work, scrubbing floors, fitting sturdy shelves for merchandise, and rendering the dwelling fit for habitation.

Elsewhere, in a quieter corner of the city, Luke Peterson remained a constant shadow, ever seeking a purpose for his life. He frequently approached Margaret, asking, "What can I do for you, Miss?" Her response usually fell flat, delivered in a heavy, sorrowful whisper: "Nothing, Luke. Nothing."

"What, then?" he persisted, hopeful. "You are so clever. Can you think of nothing for me to create for you?"

"Nothing, Luke. Nothing."

Yet, time and gentle persistence wore down her resistance. Finally, her tone shifted, softening into a plea. "If you could craft something... something to aid my dear sister Kate, that would be kind."

The slave of love, bound by duty to the woman he adored, consented with joyful alacrity. Soon, he presented a small, wooden cart, carefully cushioned within. With his own shoulders harnessed to it, he became its engine. At twilight, he drew Kate gently out of the town gates, rolling her along the pleasant boulevard. Margaret and Catherine walked beside them, their steps light. To the outside eye, it appeared a scene of pure happiness, though the shadows of their pasts lingered unseen.

Kate, however, relished every moment. Little Gerard sat nestled in her lap, and her affection for the child knew no bounds. It was as if a cherub were borne aloft by a little angel, or a fragile rosebud cradled safely within the chalice of a lily.

Still, the vulgarity of the townspeople followed. Jeers rang out, mocking the sight: "How does a thistle taste, I wonder? Does his mistress lack the coin for a beast with four legs?"

Luke paid little heed to the ridicule, his focus fixed solely on Kate. But Kate, tender-hearted, felt the sting for him.

"You have made this cart for me, good Luke," she whispered, her eyes filling. "'Twas enough. I acted poorly in asking you to pull me myself; we can afford to hire a poor soul for that labor. I love my rides, and I love carrying little Gerard, but I would rather never ride again than see you mocked for it."

"My mistress's command is law to me," Luke declared, his face hardening with protective pride. "Little do I care for their tongues. If I did, I would knock their heads together before they spoke another word. I shall draw you until my mistress commands me to stop."

"Luke," Margaret interjected softly, her gaze searching his, "if you truly wish to honor me, you must follow Kate's lead. Obey her."

"Then I will obey Kate."

An honorable exception to the general mockery was Jorian Ketel's wife, Joan. She watched the procession with a knowing, weary smile. "That is strength well spent, drawing the weak," she murmured to those nearby. "And her prayers shall be your reward, though she smiles through pain. She is not long for this world."

Single-minded Luke offered a curt denial to the sentiment. "I do not need the prayers of the dying lass," he stated plainly. "I do this solely to please my mistress, Margaret."

In the weeks that followed, Luke continued to press Margaret for tasks, his spirit unyielding in its devotion, though she remained unable to assign him anything substantial. Until one day, seemingly worn down by his relentless importuning, Margaret turned upon him. Her expression held a gravity he had never seen before, her accent thick with an emotion he struggled to name.

"Find me my boy's father," she said, the words hanging in the air like a sudden storm.
