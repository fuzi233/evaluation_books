We had barely settled ourselves when my lady spoke first—no clatter, no preface, only that controlled gentleness which, in a great house, can still a room more surely than a handbell.

“Sergeant Cuff,” she said, “there may have been excuse for my manner to you half an hour ago. I do not plead it. If I wronged you, I regret it—sincerely.”

The man’s hard features loosened, as if warmth had reached him for a moment through his official coat. He asked leave to justify himself—not for his own pride, he said, but out of respect to her. He could not be answerable for the calamity that had struck us, because his success depended on keeping Rosanna Spearman from being frightened. Had he, or had he not, held to that object? He appealed to me.

I could—and did—declare that he had.

I thought then we might have let the matter end, like a pot taken off the fire at the right moment. But Sergeant Cuff, with that cold patience of his, moved the lid again—slowly, as if to make the steam scald.

“I have heard a motive named for the young woman’s suicide,” he said, “and it may be true. It is not connected with the case I am conducting. But my own opinion points the other way. I believe some unbearable anxiety, connected with the missing Diamond, drove her to her death. I don’t pretend to know what that anxiety was. But, with your ladyship’s permission, I can lay my hand on a person capable of deciding whether I am right or wrong.”

“Is the person in the house?” my mistress asked, after a pause that seemed to lengthen with every beat of the rain on the pane.

“The person has left the house, my lady.”

That answer ran straight as a skewer to its meat. Miss Rachel’s name was not spoken, but it was served up between us all the same. The silence that followed had a sound of its own—the wind worrying the chimneys, the rain striking the window as if it wanted admission, and underneath it the hush of a household whose common music—pots in the kitchen, steps on the stairs, a distant door—had been drowned by grief. There are noises that comfort and noises that corrode; and on that morning nothing in our soundscape belonged to peace.

“Be so good as to speak plainly,” said my lady at last. “Do you refer to my daughter?”

“I do,” said Sergeant Cuff.

Her cheque-book lay on the table—no doubt to end matters like a lady: fee paid, stranger dismissed, scandal shut out with the door. She put the book back into the drawer; and the movement was slight, but her hand trembled as if the drawer were heavy iron. It went through me. That hand had portioned kindness to half the estate as naturally as it portioned bread at table; and I—old Betteredge—could only sit and feel my loyalty burn like a brand.

“I had hoped,” she said, very quietly, “to recompense your services, and to part with you, without my daughter’s name being openly mentioned between us as it has been mentioned now. My nephew has probably said something of this before you came in?”

“Mr. Blake gave his message, my lady. And I gave him a reason—”

“It is needless. After what you have just said, you know as well as I do that you have gone too far to go back. I owe it to myself, and to my child, to insist on your remaining—and on your speaking out.”

He glanced at his watch, as if even this misery must keep time.

“If there had been time, my lady, I should have preferred putting my report in writing. But if the inquiry is to go on, time is too precious to waste. I am ready. It is painful for me to speak of, and for you to hear.”

She stopped him again—steady as a cook who will not let a rash hand throw spice into a dish before tasting.

“I may make it less painful to you—and to my good servant and friend here—if I set the example of speaking boldly. You suspect Miss Verinder of deceiving us all by secreting the Diamond for some purpose of her own. Is that true?”

“Quite true, my lady.”

“Very well. Before you begin, I tell you, as her mother, she is absolutely incapable of what you suppose. Your knowledge of her is a matter of days. Mine dates from the beginning of her life. State your suspicion as strongly as you please—you cannot offend me. I am sure, beforehand, that circumstances have misled you. Mind: I possess no private information. I am as shut out from my daughter’s confidence as you are. My reason is the one you have heard already. I know my child.”

She turned and gave me her hand. I kissed it, for I had no words that would not have sounded thin beside that simple certainty.

“You may go on,” she said, facing him.

His sharp face softened for a moment, not in conviction but in pity—like a knife laid down briefly on a cloth. Then it hardened again. She had not moved him a hair’s breadth.

“I must ask your ladyship,” he began, “to look at this from my point of view as well as from yours. Suppose yourself coming down here in my place, with my experience. Will you allow me to mention what that experience has been?”

My mistress signed that she would.

“For the last twenty years,” he said, “I have been employed largely in cases of family scandal—as a confidential man. The result of that domestic practice bearing on this matter is soon told. It is within my experience that young ladies of rank do occasionally have private debts, which they dare not acknowledge even to their nearest relations. Sometimes milliner and jeweller are at the bottom of it. Sometimes the money is wanted for purposes I don’t suspect here—and I won’t shock your ladyship by naming. Bear that in mind—and now let us see how events in this house forced me back on my experience, whether I liked it or not.”

He paused, as if arranging his facts the way a careful man arranges a serviceable meal: nothing wasted, nothing out of place, everything meant to satisfy.

“My first information came from Superintendent Seegrave. He proved to my satisfaction that he was incapable of managing the case. One thing he said was worth listening to: Miss Verinder declined to be questioned and spoke to him with incomprehensible rudeness and contempt. I thought it curious—but I put it down to clumsiness on the Superintendent’s part.

“Then I applied myself to the case. It ended—as you know—in the discovery of the smear on the door, and in Mr. Franklin Blake’s evidence satisfying me that the smear and the loss of the Diamond were pieces of the same puzzle. So far, if I suspected anything, I suspected the Moonstone had been stolen—and that one of the servants might prove to be the thief.

“In that state of things, Miss Verinder suddenly comes out and speaks to me. I observe three suspicious appearances. She is still violently agitated, though more than four-and-twenty hours have passed. She treats me as she treated Superintendent Seegrave. And she is mortally offended with Mr. Franklin Blake.

“Here is a young lady who has lost a valuable jewel—a young lady, as my own eyes and ears inform me, of an impetuous temperament. What does she do? She betrays resentment against Mr. Blake, the Superintendent, and myself—the very three people trying to help her recover it. Having brought my inquiry to that point, then—and not till then—I look back to my experience. It explains her conduct. It associates her with those other young ladies. It tells me she has debts she dares not acknowledge, that must be paid. And it sets me asking whether the loss of the Diamond may not mean—the Diamond is secretly pledged to pay them. That is my conclusion from plain facts. What does your ladyship’s experience say against it?”

“What I have said already,” answered my mistress. “The circumstances have misled you.”

I said nothing. My head, muddled with outrage and weather, fetched up Robinson Crusoe—God knows why. If Sergeant Cuff could have been marooned on his own desert island, with nobody to answer back and no boat to fetch him off, he would have been placed exactly where I wished him.

Sergeant Cuff went on.

“Right or wrong, having drawn my conclusion, I put it to the test. I suggested examining all the wardrobes. It was a means of finding the article of dress that had, in all probability, made the smear; and it was a means of testing my conclusion. How did it turn out? Your ladyship consented; Mr. Blake consented; Mr. Ablewhite consented. Miss Verinder alone stopped the proceeding by refusing point-blank. That satisfied me my view was right.

“And if your ladyship and Mr. Betteredge persist in not agreeing with me, you must be blind to what happened today. In your hearing I told the young lady that leaving the house would put an obstacle in the way of recovering her jewel. You saw her drive off in the face of that. You saw her—so far from forgiving Mr. Blake, who had done more than any of you to put the clue into my hands—publicly insult him on the steps of her mother’s house. What do these things mean? If Miss Verinder is not privy to the suppression of the Diamond, what do these things mean?”

He looked at me. It was frightful, hearing him stack proof on proof, and feeling the wretched truth of every particular—while my whole heart strained to defend Miss Rachel and found no handle for its grip. Yet I am, thank God, constitutionally superior to reason; and it was that blessed defect which kept me loyal to my lady’s faith when logic came clanking in with its chains.

Finding neither of us spoke, he proceeded—steady as the dripping of the eaves.

“There is the case, as it stands against Miss Verinder alone. Next I will put the case as it stands against Miss Verinder and the deceased Rosanna Spearman taken together.

“Go back to your daughter’s refusal to let her wardrobe be examined. My mind being made up, I had two questions. First: the right method of conducting my inquiry. Second: whether Miss Verinder had an accomplice among the female servants. After thinking it over, I determined to conduct the inquiry in what we call at our office a highly irregular manner—for this reason: I had a family scandal to deal with, which it was my business to keep within family limits. The less noise, and the fewer strangers employed, the better. As to taking people in custody, going before a magistrate, and so forth—nothing of that sort was to be thought of when your ladyship’s daughter was, as I believed, at the bottom of it.

“In this case, I felt that a person of Mr. Betteredge’s character and position—knowing the servants, and having the honour of the family at heart—would be safer as an assistant than any other person I could lay my hand on. I should have tried Mr. Blake too—but he saw the drift of my proceedings early; and with his interest in Miss Verinder, mutual understanding was impossible. I mention this to show your ladyship I have kept the family secret within the family circle. I am the only outsider who knows it—and my professional existence depends on holding my tongue.”

At that, my own professional existence—such as it is—depended on not holding mine. To be made, in my old age, into a sort of deputy-police before my mistress was more than my Christianity could swallow whole.

“I beg to inform your ladyship,” I burst out, “that I never, to my knowledge, helped this abominable detective business in any way from first to last; and I summon Sergeant Cuff to contradict me, if he dares!”

Having said it, I felt as if I had flung open a window in a close room. My lady rewarded me with a light pat on the shoulder. I fixed the Sergeant with righteous indignation. He looked back like a lamb, and seemed—worse luck—to like me better than ever.

My lady told him to proceed.

“I understand,” she said, “that you have honestly done your best in what you believe to be my interest. I am ready to hear what comes next.”

“What comes next,” he answered, “relates to Rosanna Spearman. I recognised the young woman—as your ladyship may remember—when she brought the washing-book into this room. Up to that time I doubted whether Miss Verinder had trusted her secret to anyone. When I saw Rosanna, I altered my mind. I suspected her at once of being privy to the suppression of the Diamond.

“The poor creature has met her death by a dreadful end, and I don’t want your ladyship to think, now she’s gone, that I was unduly hard on her. If this had been a common case of thieving, I should have given her the benefit of the doubt as freely as any servant. Our experience of Reformatory women is that, when tried in service and kindly treated, they prove in the majority of cases honestly penitent and honestly worthy of the pains taken with them. But this was not a common case. It was, in my mind, a deeply planned fraud—with the owner of the Diamond at the bottom of it. Holding that view, the first consideration with Rosanna was this: Would Miss Verinder be satisfied with leading us all to think the Moonstone was merely lost? Or would she go further and delude us into believing it was stolen? In the latter event there was Rosanna Spearman—with the character of a thief—ready to her hand; the person of all others to lead your ladyship and me on a false scent.”

I thought he had struck the lowest note of cruelty. He found a deeper.

“I had another reason for suspecting the deceased woman,” he said, “stronger still. Who would be the very person to help Miss Verinder raise money privately on the Diamond? Rosanna Spearman. No young lady in Miss Verinder’s position could manage so risky a matter alone. A go-between she must have. And who so fit as Rosanna? Your ladyship’s deceased housemaid was at the top of her profession when she was a thief. She had relations—certainly to my knowledge—with one of the few men in London who would advance a large sum on such a notable jewel without asking awkward questions or insisting on awkward conditions. Bear that in mind—and now let me show you how my suspicions have been justified by Rosanna’s own acts, and by the plain inferences from them.”

Then he went over Rosanna’s doings from first to last, as if he were reading them out of a book whose pages had been wetted and dried and still held their ink. I knew the story already—every step of it—and yet, in his mouth, the chain seemed tightened, link by link, around the poor dead girl’s memory. Even my mistress was daunted. When he stopped, she made him no answer.

It did not appear to matter to him whether he was answered or not.

“Having stated the whole case as I understand it,” he said, “I have only to tell your ladyship what I propose to do next. I see two ways of bringing the inquiry successfully to an end. One I look upon as a certainty. The other is a bold experiment, and nothing more. Your ladyship shall decide. Shall we take the certainty first?”

My mistress made him a sign to choose.

“Thank you,” he said. “We’ll begin with the certainty. Whether Miss Verinder remains at Frizinghall, or whether she returns here, I propose, in either case, to keep a careful watch on all her proceedings—on the people she sees, on the rides and walks she may take, and on the letters she may write and receive.”

“What next?” asked my lady, her voice tightened now, like twine pulled hard.

“I shall next request your ladyship’s leave to introduce into the house, as a servant in the place of Rosanna Spearman, a woman accustomed to private inquiries of this sort, for whose discretion I can answer.”

“What next?” she repeated.

“Next—and last—I propose to send one of my brother-officers to make an arrangement with that money-lender in London whom I mentioned just now as formerly acquainted with Rosanna Spearman—and whose name and address, your ladyship may rely on it, have been communicated by Rosanna to Miss Verinder. I don’t deny the course will cost money and consume time. But the result is certain. We run a line round the Moonstone, and we draw that line closer and closer till we find it in Miss Verinder’s possession—supposing she decides to keep it. If her debts press and she decides on sending it away, then we have our man ready, and we meet the Moonstone on its arrival in London.”

To hear Miss Rachel spoken of as prey to be ringed and driven stung my mistress into anger at last.

“Consider your proposal declined,” she said, “in every particular. Go on to your other way.”

“My other way,” said the Sergeant, as calmly as if he were changing trains, “is to try that bold experiment. I think I have formed a pretty correct estimate of Miss Verinder’s temperament. She is quite capable—according to my belief—of committing a daring fraud. But she is too hot and impetuous, and too little accustomed to deceit as a habit, to play the hypocrite in small things and restrain herself under all provocations. Her feelings have repeatedly got beyond her control at the very time when it was plainly her interest to conceal them. It is on this peculiarity that I propose to act.

“I want to give her a great shock suddenly, under circumstances that will touch her to the quick. In plain English, I want to tell Miss Verinder, without warning, of Rosanna’s death—on the chance that her better feelings will hurry her into making a clean breast of it. Does your ladyship accept that alternative?”

My mistress astonished me. She did not waver a moment.

“Yes,” she said. “I do.”

“The pony-chaise is ready,” said the Sergeant. “I wish your ladyship good morning.”

She stopped him at the door with a lifted hand.

“My daughter’s better feelings shall be appealed to,” she said. “But I claim the right, as her mother, of putting her to the test myself. You will remain here; and I will go to Frizinghall.”

For once, the great Cuff was struck dumb—like any common man.

My lady rang for her waterproof things. The rain was still falling as if it meant to wear the very earth away; and the close carriage had gone already with Miss Rachel. I tried to dissuade her from facing such weather. Useless. I begged to go with her and hold the umbrella. She refused—utterly.

The pony-chaise came round, the groom in charge. The yard outside had that soaked, sharp smell of wet leather, steaming horse, and beaten gravel; the wind made a harsh, fretful music in the bare branches—no birds, no kindly rural hush, only weather and worry and the far-off industrial murmur that seems, these days, to creep even into country places and sour their air.

In the hall she spoke to Sergeant Cuff.

“You may rely on two things. I will try the experiment on Miss Verinder as boldly as you could try it yourself. And I will inform you of the result, either personally or by letter, before the last train leaves for London tonight.”

Then she stepped into the chaise, took the reins in her own hands, and drove off through the rain to Frizinghall.
