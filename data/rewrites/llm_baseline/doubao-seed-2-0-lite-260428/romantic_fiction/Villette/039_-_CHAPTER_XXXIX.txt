Spellbound, I could not tear myself away from the little huddled group of people gathered a few paces off; the grass beneath my boots might as well have been rooted to my ankles. The interlaced boughs of the old lime trees draped me in deep, soft shadow, the warm night air hummed low with the distant echo of fête music, promising no one would stumble on my hidden nook, and a single guttering streetlamp flickered just long enough to illuminate a tucked-away seat half-buried in ivy before its flame died, leaving me perfectly concealed.

For a fortnight of dark, lonely nights I had gathered scraps of rumour, piecing together the full story of why M. Emanuel had been persuaded to leave Villette, and the motives behind his sudden, unannounced departure. The tale was common, cruel, and simple: its beginning was greed, its end nothing but cold self-interest.

Madame Walravens, misshapen and sharp-tongued as any carved idol in a far-off shrine, was treated by her little circle of hangers-on as if she held the power of a goddess. She had once been extraordinarily wealthy, and though she had no ready money to hand now, everyone knew she would one day inherit a vast fortune once more. At Basseterre, in Guadaloupe, stood a sprawling sugar estate that had come to her as a wedding dowry sixty years prior, seized by creditors after her husband’s bankruptcy, now finally cleared of all outstanding claims. If a trustworthy, hardworking steward of unshakable integrity travelled out to manage it, the property would turn a huge, steady profit within a handful of years.

Père Silas pushed for the scheme for the sake of his church, of which Madame Walravens was a fiercely devout patron. Madame Beck, distantly related to the old hunchback and well aware she had no surviving family of her own, had spent years quietly plotting over the fortune, ever courting Madame Walravens even when the old woman snapped and insulted her to her face. Both priest and pensionnat mistress had equal, greedy stakes in seeing the West Indian estate flourish.

But the journey across the sea was long, the climate deadly for Europeans unused to it. The perfect, devoted steward they needed was right before their eyes: M. Paul Emanuel. Madame Walravens had held him in her thrall for twenty years, draining his small savings, wearing down every last moment of his youth for her own comfort, like fungus sapping the life from a strong tree. Père Silas had raised him, trained him, bound him to his will with threads of gratitude and religious duty. Madame Beck knew well how easily he could be led to sacrifice himself for others, and she whispered to the others that if he stayed in Europe, he might stray from his faith, grow close to some heretic (meaning me, I thought, with a cold twist in my chest) and slip out of their control entirely. The three self-serving schemers closed in around the one unselfish man among them, pleading, cajoling, laying their troubles at his feet, begging him to give just two or three years of his life to their cause, promising he could live for himself afterwards. One of them, I had no doubt, hoped he would sicken and die in that tropical heat long before he could ever come back to claim his share.

M. Emanuel never could turn away a plea for help laid at his feet with trusting, open hands. No one asked what private heartbreak he hid at the thought of leaving Villette, no one cared what plans he had made for his own quiet future. Then one day he was gone, and he had left no word, no note, no sign for me at all. That was the sum of all I knew, until now, as I huddled in the ivy.

For a long while I paid no mind to the chatter of the group a few paces off; they gossiped of gowns, of the band playing at the main concert, of the lantern-lit fête illuminations, of the mild, fair weather that made ocean voyages smooth. I waited, half-hoping they would mention his ship, the *Antigua*, but no one spoke of it at all.

Old Madame Walravens grew restless, craning her neck this way and that, peering through the trees and out over the throng of fête-goers, muttering over and over, “Where are they? Why have they not come?” At last she spoke loud enough for all of us to hear, a question that sent a sharp shock right through my bones: “Messieurs et mesdames, où donc est Justine Marie?”

Justine Marie! The name of the dead nun whose portrait hung in the garret, the ghost I had seen gliding through the alley after dark! I half expected the group to fall silent, to cross themselves in fright, but no one seemed surprised at all. “Justine Marie is on her way,” one man said casually. “She’s in the kiosk. She will be here in a moment.”

The chatter shifted, full of half-hints and allusions I had to strain to catch. There was some scheme afoot, tied to this long-dead nun’s name, talk of a marriage, a great fortune, though I could not make out for whom it was intended. Once I thought the jokes were aimed at a fair-haired young German man in their party called Heinrich Mühler, but then Madame Walravens cackled sharply, and waved her stick to threaten Désirée, keeping the girl from straying an inch out of her sight.

“Here she comes!” one of the men cried suddenly. “There is Justine Marie arriving now.”

My breath caught. I called to mind the pale portrait of the nun on the garret panel, the old sad love story I had pieced together, the apparition I had seen in the dark alley, the strange moment I had found a tiny cradle tucked away in the attic. I felt a sharp, heavy premonition that a great secret was about to break open before my eyes.

The circle parted to welcome their new guest. At that exact instant, a park keeper carrying a flaming torch walked past, its golden glare mixing with the pale silver light of the moon to illuminate the figure stepping into their midst. My whole life seemed to stand still, I leaned forward so far I nearly lost my balance, waiting to see the ghost.

The torchlight fell full on her face. She was no spectre. She was a bright, buxom young girl, fresh from her finishing school, fair-skinned and plump with rosy round cheeks, rich brown hair falling in soft curls beneath her bonnet, dressed in a fine silk party gown, laughing as she greeted two older members of the party with a kiss, calling them “Uncle” and “Aunt”. She was familiar to me – I had seen her at Madame Beck’s Sunday evening parties, a relation of the family, the orphaned heiress Justine Marie Sauveur, named for the nun who would have been her aunt if she had lived.

Then my gaze fell to the third person in her little escort, and my hands clenched so tight my nails dug into my palms. It was him. Paul Emanuel. The *Antigua* had sailed days ago, and he had never been on it at all. Madame Beck had lied, or let the lie spread when it had stopped being true. A huge, crushing weight lifted off my chest, so heavy I nearly cried out with relief. But then a new, sharper dread coiled in its place. Who was this pretty young heiress to him?

I soon gathered he had delayed his voyage entirely of his own will, against the protests of all three of his scheming friends, booked a passage on the *Paul et Virginie* that would not sail for another fortnight, saying only he had a small piece of business he was determined to finish before he left. The hints flew fast and thick around the group: the family had agreed that when he came back from Guadeloupe with the fortune they would all share, he would marry his pretty young ward, the heiress Justine Marie. He laughed at their teasing, his face glowing with joy, as he lifted her hand to his lips, and the young German Heinrich Mühler shifted restlessly, looking angry. Paul drew her closer to him, wrapping his coat around her shoulders to shield her from the cool night air, watching her every move as if she was the most precious thing in the whole world.

It was no ghost story, no old tragedy. The secret was plain at last. The love I had dared to hope for, the quiet little bond I thought we had built between us over shared late lessons and stolen conversations, had never been anything but a foolish daydream. He had always belonged to her.

I did not flinch from the truth. I grabbed the whole bitter, painful reality and held it tight to my chest, as a wounded soldier wraps his regimental colours around his breast before he falls. No more soft, torturing lies, no more fragile hopes that his glances held a secret meaning for me. I was free.

I did not stay to watch any longer. I turned my back on the laughing group beneath the trees, slipped away from the park as the last of the fête crowds dispersed, and walked through the quiet streets of the lower town, where the moon shone bright and unrivalled, lighting my path like a calm, unblinking witness. I slipped through the quiet Rue Fossette, pushed open the pensionnat’s front door that I had left ajar, made my way up the stairs without a sound, shoeless, so no one would hear me.

I stepped into the dark dormitory. All nineteen other girls lay fast asleep in their beds, the room wrapped in the stillness of midnight. On my own bed, which I had left empty, lay a long, dark, still shape: the nun, the ghost I had seen haunting the pensionnat grounds three times before.

I did not scream, I did not faint. All the sharp grief and rage I carried in my chest surged to the surface, and I lunged at the shape, grabbing it by the shoulders and yanking it high off the bed. It was no ghost. It was nothing but a long bolster, dressed in a real nun’s black habit and crisp white wimple, arranged perfectly to look like a tall, slender figure lying still. It tore apart in my hands, falling to the floor in shreds of cloth. Pinned to the wimple was a scrap of paper, written in mocking pencil:
“The nun of the attic bequeaths to Lucy Snowe her wardrobe. She will be seen in the Rue Fossette no more.”

I stood there, the tattered fabric at my feet, left staring at the note, wondering who had played this cruel trick – who the tall, unidentifiable figure I had seen wandering the halls after dark could possibly have been.
