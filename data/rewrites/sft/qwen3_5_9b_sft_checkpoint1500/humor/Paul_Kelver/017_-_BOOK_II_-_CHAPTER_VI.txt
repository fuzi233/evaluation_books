For some time, I had kept my distance from old Hasluck. He was a man who demanded to understand emotions before he could sympathize with them. With a boisterous, cheerful demeanor, he would have likely forced his way into my life, offering unsolicited aid. Why I chose to starve alongside Lott & Co., dealing with men whose honesty was questionable at best, rather than accept a fair wage from the kindly old butcher, remains a mystery to me. Perhaps it wasn't the profit margin—Lott & Co. were certainly no more upright than anyone else. Nor was it simply because he was Barbara's father. In my heart, I had severed all ties between Barbara and her parents, those "good old souls" with their vulgarity and homeliness. To me, she existed in a realm entirely separate from the mundane world. Her true lineage, I felt, belonged somewhere amidst the vanishing sacred groves of lost lands, within the sky-domed shrines of exiled gods. There are instincts in us, deep and unanalyzed, that defy pure logic. I have always found it safer to navigate a complex journey using a detailed map, risking failure, than to rely on the helpful guidance of official conductors paid to assist the bewildered traveler. Perhaps a distant ancestor of mine was a morose, solitary savage, crafting his own stone tools, shunning communal dances, preferring to cavort alone in his cave.

However, now that I had finally secured my footing, I could extend my hand without fear of it being misinterpreted as a plea for charity. I wrote to old Hasluck, and almost immediately, via the next post, received a note radiating warmth. He informed me that Barbara had recently returned from abroad, added that she, too, would be delighted to see me, and, as he knew I would expect, threw his doors wide open.

Regarding my boyish infatuation for Barbara, I had never confided in a single living soul. I doubt anyone, save Barbara herself, ever truly guessed the depth of it. My mother, though fond of her, viewed Barbara merely as a young girl with charms and flaws, discussing the latter with a free-wheeling candor that occasionally stung me like a philosophical debate with a novice about a newly adopted faith. Often, preferring the solitude of late evenings, I would wander the grounds of the massive red-brick house atop Stamford Hill, circling the ancient garden before descending back into the noisome streets below. These excursions felt like pilgrimages from a holy shrine back to the profane world, leaving me purified, filled with a strange peace, as if noble endeavor and unselfish aims were finally within reach.

Throughout Barbara's four-year absence, my adoration had only intensified. From my fading memories, a perfect ideal had crystallized—a construct of my imagination that, paradoxically, felt more real and present than reality itself. I awaited her return, yet without impatience, reveling in the anticipation far more than the prospect of realization. The physical child I had once played with, touched, and spoken to had receded into the distance, replaced by the luminous vision of my dreams. I knew that when we met again, a chasm would exist between us, one I had no desire to bridge. Worshiping her from afar was a sweeter thought than the hope of a passionate embrace. To live with her, to sit opposite her while she ate, to see her perhaps with hair curlers in, to learn of a corn on her foot or a decaying tooth, would have been torture. I feared no abyss of the commonplace dragging her down, and for this, I was grateful. In the future, she would remain further removed. She was older, and she must now be a woman. Instinctively, I felt that despite the passing years, I was not yet a man. She would marry. The thought brought me no pain; my feelings for her were utterly devoid of appetite. No jealous priest could conceal her from my eyes, for I had reared her altar too high. Since learning to know myself better, I realize she stood to me not as a living woman, but as a symbol—not a companion for life's journey, but an impalpable religion of sex to which we erect idols of clay, often to our dissatisfaction, causing us to fall into anger against creations of our own hands, forgetting they were but works of art, not the spirit of love itself.

I allowed a week to pass after receiving Hasluck's letter before presenting myself at Stamford Hill. It was late one afternoon in early summer. Hasluck had not yet returned from the City; Mrs. Hasluck was visiting; Miss Hasluck was in the garden. I dismissed the supercilious footman, vowing to seek her out directly. I knew her favorite haunt: a sunny corner, ablaze with flowers, enclosed by a thick yew hedge trimmed into quaint Dutch animal and bird shapes. There she was, as I expected, reading a book. Once again, the sensation swept over me that I had felt as a boy when she first appeared against the dust of my father's office—that all fairy tales had suddenly become reality, only now, instead of a Princess, she was a Queen. She was taller, possessing a dignity that had formerly been her only missing charm. She did not hear my approach, for I crossed the soft, short grass unseen. For a moment, I stood in the shadow of the yews, drinking in the beauty of her clear-cut profile, bent over her book, marveling at the curving lines of her long neck and the exquisite whiteness of her hand against the lilac of her dress.

I did not speak; I would have preferred to remain a silent watcher. However, turning at the end of the path, she saw me. As she approached, she extended her hand. I knelt upon the path and raised it to my lips. The action was wholly spontaneous, unconscious until afterward. Her lips smiled as I lifted my eyes, a faint suggestion of contempt mingled with amusement. Yet, she seemed pleased, and her contempt, even if genuine, would not have wounded me.

"So you are still in love with me?" she asked. "I wondered if you would be."

"Did you know that I was in love with you?"

"I should have been blind if I had not."

"But I was only a boy."

"You were not the usual type of boy. You are not going to be the usual type of man."

"Do you not mind my loving you?"

"I cannot help it, can I? Nor can you."

She seated herself on a stone bench facing a sundial, leaned back with her hands clasped behind her head, and looked at me, laughing.

"I shall always love you," I answered. "But it is with a curious sort of love. I do not understand it myself."

"Tell me," she commanded, still smiling. "Describe it to me."

I stood opposite her, my arm resting upon the sundial's stone column. The sun was sinking, casting long shadows on the velvet grass, illuminating her upturned face with a golden light.

"I wished you were some great queen of olden days, so that I might always be near you, serving you, doing your bidding. Your love in return would spoil all; I shall never ask it, never desire it. That I might look upon you, touch your hand now and then at rare intervals, kiss in secret the glove you had let fall, the shoe you had flung off, knowing that you knew of my love, that I was yours to do with as you willed—to live or die according to your wish. Or that you were a priestess in some temple of forgotten gods, where I might steal away at daybreak and dusk to gaze upon your beauty; kneel with clasped hands, watching your sandalled feet move about the altar steps; lie with pressed lips upon the stones your trailing robes had touched."

She laughed a light, mocking laugh. "I should prefer to be the queen. The role of priestess would not suit me. Temples are so cold." A slight shiver passed through her. She moved her hand, beckoning me to my feet. "That is how you shall love me, Paul," she said. "Adoring me, worshipping me—blindly. I will be your queen and treat you—as it chooses me. All I think, all I do, I will tell you, and you shall tell me it is right. The queen can do no wrong."

She took my face between her hands, bending over me to look long and steadfastly into my eyes. "You understand, Paul, the queen can do no wrong—never, never." A note of vehemence had crept into her voice, accompanied by a look almost of appeal.

"My queen can do no wrong," I repeated. She laughed and let her hands fall back onto her lap.

"Now you may sit beside me. So much honor, Paul, shall you have today, but it must last you a long time. Tell me all you have been doing; perhaps it will amuse me. Afterwards, you shall hear what I have done, and you shall say it was right and good of me."

I obeyed, sketching my story briefly yet leaving nothing untold, not even the transit of Lady Ortensia, though I was ashamed of the episode. At that, she looked a little grave.
