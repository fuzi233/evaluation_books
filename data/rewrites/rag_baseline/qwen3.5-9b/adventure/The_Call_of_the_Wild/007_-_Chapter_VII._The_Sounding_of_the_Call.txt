The transaction was absolute: Buck secured sixteen hundred dollars for John Thornton in a single afternoon. This capital injection allowed his master to liquidate liabilities and pivot eastward, chasing a phantom vein of ore whispered about in the annals of the Klondike. The legend of the Lost Mine was steeped in the blood of the north; generations of prospectors had vanished into its mist-shrouded depths, leaving behind only tragic echoes and the occasional dying man clutching nuggets of impossible purity. Yet, the treasure house remained intact, untouched by living hands. Now, John Thornton, accompanied by Pete, Hans, and a pack of elite canines including Buck, pushed into the unknown eastern sector, determined to reclaim what others had failed to claim.

Their sled carved seventy miles upstream along the Yukon, then veered sharply left onto the Stewart River. They navigated past the Mayo and McQuestion rapids until the river narrowed to a mere thread, winding through a spine of granite peaks that formed the continental backbone. Thornton operated with the detached efficiency of a man unburdened by fear. Armed with a rifle and a pouch of salt, he treated the wilderness not as a threat, but as a resource to be harvested. Following the rhythm of the indigenous peoples, he hunted for sustenance during the day's transit; if the hunt yielded nothing, he simply continued moving, trusting that provisions would manifest inevitably. Their cargo consisted primarily of ammunition and tools, while their diet fluctuated between starvation and gluttonous feasts depending on the whims of the terrain.

Summer brought a different kind of trial. Men and dogs rafted across alpine lakes, navigating slender boats whipped from standing timber as they ascended and descended treacherous currents. Months dissolved into a blur of motion through uncharted expanses where the ghosts of previous travelers lingered. They traversed divides in blinding summer storms, endured the relentless glare of the midnight sun on exposed ridges, and descended into valleys teeming with insects. Even amidst the shadows of glaciers, they paused to gather berries as vibrant as those of the southern hemisphere. By autumn, they entered a desolate lake country, a graveyard of wildlife where only the chilling wind and the rhythmic lapping of ice-forming waters testified to life's former presence.

Winter followed, erasing their tracks as they wandered paths laid by ancestors. At one point, they discovered an ancient blaze leading toward the Lost Cabin, yet the trail began and ended without logical connection, preserving its mystery alongside the cabin's. Later, they stumbled upon the skeletal remains of a hunting lodge. Among the rotting debris, Thornton unearthed a long-barreled flintlock—a Hudson's Bay Company firearm from the fur-trade era, valued then at its height in beaver pelts. No other clue remained regarding the lone settler who had abandoned the gun centuries prior.

Finally, spring returned with a revelation. They bypassed the mythic cabin entirely, discovering instead a shallow placer deposit in a broad valley. Gold glittered across the pan-bottom like melted butter. They settled in, working ceaselessly. Days blurred into nights as they sacked the earth in moose-hide bags, piling fifty-pound loads of wealth beside their spruce-bough lodges. For the dogs, the routine shifted. Beyond hauling occasional kills, Buck spent long hours brooding by the fire. The image of the short-legged, hairy man—the ghost of his past—haunted him with renewed frequency.

In these visions, fear was the dominant atmosphere. The man slept restlessly, jerking awake to scan the darkness, tossing fuel onto the flames. Whether gathering shellfish on a shore or stalking through the woods, the man moved with predatory alertness, ears twitching, nostrils flaring. He climbed trees with supernatural agility, swinging from branch to branch with a fluid grace that suggested he belonged equally to the canopy and the forest floor. Buck recalled nights spent beneath these very trees, watching the man roost and guard against unseen threats.

Closely intertwined with these memories was the Call. It resonated from the deep recesses of the forest, a vibration that induced a profound, restless longing within Buck. It promised a vague, sweet ecstasy, stirring primal yearnings he could not name. Driven by forces beyond comprehension, he would pursue the sound, barking defiantly or pressing his nose into the cool moss and rich, loamy soil. He would crouch motionless behind fallen logs, wide-eyed and attentive, hoping to intercept the source of the summons.

Impulses seized him with irresistible force. Dozing in the midday heat, he would abruptly snap upright, ears pricked, and launch himself into a sprint that lasted for hours. He relished the thrill of running down dry creek beds, creeping silently to observe the avian inhabitants. He would spend entire days lying in the underbrush, watching partridges strut and drum. But most captivating was the dim twilight of the summer midnights. In the subdued murmur of the forest, he became a student of the wild, reading signs and sounds with the precision of a scholar deciphering a text, driven solely to locate the mysterious entity calling him.

One night, he woke with a jolt, nostrils quivering, mane bristling in rhythmic waves. From the tree line emerged the Call—or rather, a singular, piercing note of it. It was distinct, definitive, unlike any bark of a Husky, yet undeniably familiar. Recognizing it instantly, Buck leaped from the sleeping camp and surged into the woods. As he neared the source, his pace slowed, movements cautious and deliberate. He emerged into a clearing to find a long, lean timber wolf standing erect on its haunches, nose pointed skyward.

The wolf sensed his approach and halted its howling. Buck stalked forward, body coiled tight, tail rigid, feet placing themselves with unnatural care. His posture broadcast a complex message: a mix of threat and tentative friendship, the necessary truce between predators. Yet, the wolf bolted. Buck gave chase, driving the animal into a narrow channel blocked by a timber jam. The wolf spun, pivoting on its hind legs in a defensive stance reminiscent of cornered dogs, snapping teeth in a rapid, furious rhythm.

Buck did not strike. Instead, he circled, closing the gap with friendly gestures. Suspicion and terror radiated from the wolf; Buck outweighed it significantly, his head reaching the beast's shoulder. Seizing an opening, the wolf darted away, and the pursuit resumed. Repeatedly, the wolf was trapped, only to escape at the slightest chance. Eventually, however, Buck's persistence prevailed. Realizing no malice was intended, the wolf lowered its guard and sniffed noses with him.

Friendship blossomed instantly. They engaged in a playful, nervous frolic typical of fierce beasts masking their aggression. Soon, the wolf broke into a leisurely lope, signaling a destination. He beckoned Buck to follow, and they raced side-by-side through the gloaming, ascending the creek bed, climbing the gorge, and crossing the bleak divide where the water originated.

On the opposite slope, the landscape opened into a vast expanse of forest and rushing streams. They ran relentlessly, hour after hour, as the sun climbed higher and the air warmed. Buck felt a surge of wild joy. He knew he was answering the Call, running alongside his wooden brother toward the origin of the song. Ancient memories flooded back, stirring the dormant instincts of his other life. He had done this before, in that forgotten realm, and now he was repeating it: running free, earth unpaved beneath his paws, sky infinite above.

They paused at a flowing stream to drink. Buck sat, and the memory of John Thornton surfaced. The wolf moved on toward the Call's source, then returned, nudging Buck encouragingly. But Buck hesitated, turning slowly to retrace his steps. For nearly an hour, the wild companion trotted beside him, whining softly. Finally, the wolf sat, nose to the sky, and unleashed a mournful howl. As Buck persisted on his backward journey, the sound faded into the distance, swallowed by the vastness.

Back at camp, John Thornton dined when Buck burst in, launching himself atop his master in a frenzy of affection. He overturned the man, scrambled over him, licking faces and biting hands—a chaotic display Thornton dismissed as "playing the general tom-fool," shaking the dog back and forth while cursing him with love.

For two days and nights, Buck refused to leave Thornton's side, following him through labor and meals, guarding him through sleep and wakefulness. But the Call grew louder, more imperious. Restlessness clawed at him, haunted by visions of the wild brother and the smiling lands beyond the divide. He ventured into the woods again, but the wolf did not appear, and the mournful howl remained silent.

Gradually, Buck began sleeping outdoors, absent from camp for days at a time. One night, he crossed the divide at the creek's headwaters and descended into the timber-rich territory of streams. He wandered for a week, searching in vain for the wolf, killing game as he traveled with a long, effortless lope that showed no signs of fatigue. He caught salmon in a broad river flowing toward the sea. Along this same stream, he encountered a massive black bear, blinded by mosquitoes while fishing. The creature raged helplessly through the forest, terrifying and vulnerable. Buck engaged it in a brutal struggle that awakened the last vestiges of his inherent ferocity. Two days later, returning to the carcass, he found a dozen wolverines fighting over the spoils. He scattered them like chaff; those that fled left two behind, who quickly ceased their squabbles.
