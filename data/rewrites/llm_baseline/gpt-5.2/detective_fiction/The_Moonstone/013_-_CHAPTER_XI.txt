When the last wheels had hissed away from the gravel and the house drew in its breath again, I went back to the inner hall. Samuel had already planted himself at the side-table as if the decanters were a parish to be governed, and he, the rector: brandy in one hand, soda in the other, both bright as new coin under the lamps.

My lady and Miss Rachel came out of the drawing-room, with the two gentlemen behind them. Mr. Godfrey accepted the brandy-and-soda, the bubbles pricking up his spirits as briskly as a charitable subscription-list. Mr. Franklin took nothing; he dropped into a chair with the look of a man who had been talked at till his thoughts were worn thin. Some people are sustained by company; some are emptied by it. He was the emptied sort.

My lady, turning to bid them good-night, fixed her eyes—hard, steady, and not altogether loving—on the Colonel’s legacy blazing in her daughter’s dress, that cold lunar shine which, even by lamplight, had a way of making other jewels look like common glass.

“Rachel,” she said, “where are you going to put your Diamond tonight?”

Miss Rachel was in those high spirits that run away with a young lady’s sense and leave her nonsense to do duty for it. She began with declaring she didn’t know; then that she would put it, of course, on the dressing-table with her other things; then—remembering how it seemed to carry its own pale radiance—she protested it might shine of itself in the dark and turn her blood to water. At last she remembered the Indian cabinet in her sitting-room, and instantly decided that an Indian diamond should sleep in an Indian cabinet, so that the two native wonders might admire each other in private.

My lady cut the froth off her talk, as you skim the scum from a pot before it boils over.

“My dear, your Indian cabinet has no lock.”

“Good Heavens, mamma!” Miss Rachel cried. “Is this an hotel? Are there thieves in the house?”

My lady took no more notice of that than she would of a child insisting the moon is following it down the road. She wished the gentlemen good-night, kissed her daughter, and then said—quietly, with that quiet that does not invite refusal:

“Why not let me keep the Diamond for you tonight?”

Miss Rachel drew back, as if you had asked her to hand over a new doll when she was ten years old. My lady saw, as I did, that there was no reasoning with her in that temper.

“Come into my room, Rachel, the first thing tomorrow morning,” she said. “I shall have something to say to you.”

Then she left us, slowly, with her thoughts marching on before her like troops—none of us welcome among them.

Miss Rachel was next to take leave. She shook hands with Mr. Godfrey, who had drifted to the other end of the hall and was standing before a picture, looking at it with the solemn attention he usually reserved for good works. Then she turned back to Mr. Franklin, still sitting weary and silent in his corner.

What passed in words I can’t say. But I happened to be near the old oak frame that holds our great looking-glass, and I saw her, reflected in it, slyly slip out the locket Mr. Franklin had given her from the bosom of her dress, show it to him for one moment, and smile—a smile that had meaning in it, and more than common meaning too—before she went off light-footed to bed. That little glimpse shook my confidence in my own judgment. It set me thinking Penelope might be right, after all, about the state of her young lady’s affections.

As soon as Miss Rachel had left him eyes to see with, Mr. Franklin noticed me. His humour, which shifted like wind in a tree-top, had already veered round upon the matter of the Indians.

“Betteredge,” he said, “I’m half inclined to think I took Mr. Murthwaite too seriously in the shrubbery. Perhaps he has been trying his traveller’s tales on us. Do you really mean to let the dogs loose?”

“I’ll take off their collars, sir,” I answered, “and leave them free to take a turn in the night—if they smell a reason for it.”

“All right. We’ll see what is to be done tomorrow,” he said. “I am not disposed to alarm my aunt without a very pressing reason. Good-night.”

He looked so pale and worn as he took his candle that I ventured to advise a drop of brandy-and-water as a night-cap. Mr. Godfrey, coming up at that moment, backed me with the pleasant firmness he could always manage when there was no real resistance to meet. He pressed Mr. Franklin, in the friendliest manner, to take something before going upstairs.

I note these trifles because it pleased me, after all I had seen and heard that day, to observe the two gentlemen were still on good terms. Whatever words had been exchanged in the drawing-room, whatever rivalry had been at work about Miss Rachel, it had made no lasting breach. Men of their station have this merit: they keep their quarrels as clean as their linen, and hang them out of sight.

Mr. Franklin declined and went up with Mr. Godfrey, their rooms being next door. On the landing, either his cousin persuaded him, or he changed again—as he so often did.

“Perhaps I may want it in the night,” he called down. “Send up some brandy-and-water.”

I sent Samuel with it, and went out to the kennels. The dogs, unbuckled at that hour, lost their heads with astonishment and sprang on me like puppies. The rain soon cooled their rejoicing. They lapped a little water and crawled back to their straw. The night smelt of soaked earth, bruised leaves, and wet stone; the sound of it was all drip and rush and the long sighing in the trees as the wind tried its strength. For the moment it still poured; the lawn was a sponge underfoot. But I saw, in a lifting of the cloud and a thinning of the black, signs of a break.

Samuel and I went over the house shutting up as usual. This time I trusted nothing to my deputy. I tried every bolt myself, listened for each click, and set my hand on the wood to feel it firm. All was safe and fast when I got my old bones to bed, between midnight and one.

The worries of the day had been a little too much for me. At any rate, I had a touch of Mr. Franklin’s malady. It was sunrise before sleep came at last. All the time I lay awake, the house was as quiet as the grave—nothing stirring but the rain’s steady splash and the wind’s breath through the trees. Sound is a kind of guard in a house; when it keeps to its proper duties—the clock, the settling timber, the distant sea of leaves—you feel safe. But when it is all one monotone, like water on water, it deadens the ear, and a man begins to distrust even silence, because it can hide a footfall in its folds as easily as it hides a whisper.

At half-past seven I woke. The weather had turned as if a curtain had been drawn back: fine sunshine, washed air, and birds taking possession of the morning in a hundred bright notes. The clock struck eight. I was just going out to chain the dogs again when I heard a sudden whisking of petticoats behind me on the stairs—quick, frightened, and out of place in that clear morning music.

I turned, and there was Penelope flying down after me like one distraught.

“Father!” she screamed. “Come upstairs, for God’s sake! The Diamond is gone!”

“Are you out of your mind?” I asked.

“Gone!” says Penelope. “Gone, nobody knows how. Come up and see.”

She dragged me into Miss Rachel’s sitting-room, which opened into her bedroom. There, on the threshold of the bedroom door, stood Miss Rachel—white as the dressing-gown she wore, her face looking as if the colour had been rinsed out of it in the night’s rain. The two doors of the Indian cabinet stood wide open. One drawer was pulled out as far as it would go, like a tongue thrust out in mockery.

“Look!” Penelope cried. “I saw Miss Rachel put the Diamond in that drawer last night.”

I went to it. The drawer was empty.

“Is this true, miss?” I asked.

With a look not like herself, and a voice not like her own—flat, frightened, and yet stubborn—Miss Rachel answered as Penelope had done:

“The Diamond is gone!”

And with those words she withdrew into her bedroom and shut and locked the door.

Before we knew which way to turn, my lady came in, hearing my voice, and asked what had happened. When she learnt it, she seemed turned to stone. She went straight to Miss Rachel’s door and insisted on being admitted. Miss Rachel let her in.

The alarm ran through the house like fire in dry straw. Mr. Godfrey was the first of the gentlemen to appear. He held up his hands in bewilderment, which didn’t speak much for his natural strength of mind. Mr. Franklin, from whom I had counted on clear counsel, was for once as helpless as his cousin. For a wonder he had slept well—and the unaccustomed luxury had, as he said himself, stupefied him. But when he had swallowed his coffee—always taken, foreign fashion, hours before breakfast—his wits began to come back to him, and he took hold of the matter, resolute and quick.

He sent for the servants, ordered that all the lower doors and windows (except the front door, which I had opened) were to remain exactly as they had been since we locked up. He then proposed to my lady and to us to make sure the Diamond hadn’t slipped somewhere out of sight—behind the cabinet, or down behind the table. We searched there and found nothing. He questioned Penelope and got no more from her than she had already told me. Then he suggested we must try Miss Rachel, and sent Penelope to knock.

My lady answered, closing the door behind her. The next moment we heard it locked on the inside by Miss Rachel. My mistress came out among us, sorely puzzled and distressed.

“The loss of the Diamond seems to have quite overwhelmed Rachel,” she said. “She shrinks, in the strangest manner, from speaking of it even to me. It is impossible you can see her for the present.”

Having added that to our perplexities, my lady recovered herself, as she always did, and spoke with decision.

“I suppose there is no help for it,” she said quietly. “I suppose I have no alternative but to send for the police.”

“And the first thing for the police to do,” Mr. Franklin added, catching at the idea as if it were a rope, “is to lay hands on the Indian jugglers who performed here last night.”

My lady and Mr. Godfrey started, surprised, knowing nothing of what Mr. Franklin and I knew.

“I can’t stop to explain now,” Mr. Franklin went on. “I can only tell you the Indians have stolen the Diamond. Give me a letter of introduction to one of the magistrates at Frizinghall—merely saying I represent your interests—and let me ride off with it instantly. Our chance may depend on not wasting one minute.”

He put pen, ink, and paper before my lady. She wrote what he wanted—somewhat unwillingly, as it seemed to me. If it had been possible to overlook the loss of twenty thousand pounds, I do believe—with my lady’s opinion of her late brother and her distrust of his gift—it would have been privately a relief to her to let the Moonstone go.

I went out with Mr. Franklin to the stables, and took the chance to ask how the Indians could have got into the house.

“One of them might have slipped into the hall in the confusion when the dinner company were going away,” he said. “He may have been under the sofa while my aunt and Rachel were talking. He only had to wait till the house was quiet—and there it would be in the cabinet, to be had for the taking.”

With that, he called to the groom to open the gate and galloped off.

It sounded rational enough—until you set it against the facts. How had the thief got out again? I had found the front door locked and bolted as I left it. The other doors and windows were still fast. The dogs—if he dropped from an upper window, how did he escape them? Drugged meat? As that crossed my mind, the dogs themselves came tearing round a corner, rolling each other on the wet grass in such roaring health that I could hardly bring them to reason and chain them up. The more I turned it over, the less Mr. Franklin’s notion satisfied me.

We had our breakfasts. Whatever happens in a house—robbery or murder—it doesn’t matter, you must have your breakfast. There are domestic rites that go on as steadily as kneading and baking: they bind the living to the living, and keep the mind from running wild. The clink of crockery, the smell of tea, the scrape of a knife on toast—poor things, perhaps, against a calamity; and yet, without them, the day would have no spine.

Afterward my lady sent for me. I was compelled to tell her what I had concealed about the Indians and their plot. Being a woman of high courage, she soon got over the first start of it. Her mind was more perturbed about her daughter than about any heathen conspiracy.

“You know how odd Rachel is,” she said, “and how differently she behaves sometimes from other girls. But I have never seen her so strange and so reserved as she is now. The loss of her jewel seems almost to have turned her brain. Who would have thought that horrible Diamond could have laid such a hold on her in so short a time?”

It was strange. Miss Rachel was not commonly mad after trinkets as many girls are. Yet there she was, locked up, inconsolable. And it wasn’t only she who was thrown out of the regular groove. Mr. Godfrey—though professionally a kind of consoler-general—seemed at a loss for his own resources. With no company to amuse him and no chance of trying his experience of women in distress on Miss Rachel, he wandered about house and gardens in an uneasy, aimless way, debating whether to relieve us of him as a guest or to stay on the chance his humble services might be of use. He decided it was perhaps most customary to remain. Circumstances try the metal a man is made of; Mr. Godfrey, tried by circumstances, showed himself weaker metal than I had thought.

The women-servants, except Rosanna Spearman—who kept by herself—took to whispering in corners and staring suspiciously at nothing. I myself was fidgety and ill-tempered. The cursed Moonstone had turned us all upside down—like a pot set to boil with no lid, so that every small bubble shows itself and makes a fuss.

A little before eleven Mr. Franklin came back. He had left at a gallop; he returned at a walk. He had gone away like iron; he came back stuffed with cotton.

“Well,” says my lady, “are the police coming?”

“Yes,” says Mr. Franklin. “They said they would follow me in a fly—Superintendent Seegrave, of the local force, and two of his men. A mere form. The case is hopeless.”

“What! have the Indians escaped, sir?” I asked.

“The poor ill-used Indians have been most unjustly put in prison,” says Mr. Franklin. “They are as innocent as the babe unborn. My idea of one being hidden in the house has ended, like my ideas generally, in smoke. It’s been proved—simply impossible.”

He sat down and explained. He had laid the case before the magistrate at Frizinghall, who had sent for the police. Inquiry showed the Indians had not attempted to leave town. They had been seen returning with their boy between ten and eleven, which proved they had walked straight back after performing on our terrace. Later, at midnight, the police had searched the common lodging-house where they lived and had seen all three there again, with the boy. Soon after midnight I had shut up our house safe. It was evidence enough to break any suspicion. The magistrate said there was not even a case against them so far—yet, as it was possible something might still turn up, he would contrive, by committing them as rogues and vagabonds, to keep them under lock and key for a week. They had ignorantly done something in town—what it was I forget—that just brought them within the law. Every human institution, justice included, will stretch if you pull it the right way. The magistrate was an old friend of my lady’s, and the Indians were “committed” as soon as court opened.

So the Indian clue broke in our hands. If the jugglers were innocent, who, in the name of wonder, had taken the Moonstone out of Miss Rachel’s drawer?

Ten minutes later, to our relief, Superintendent Seegrave arrived. He reported passing Mr. Franklin on the terrace sitting in the sun, warning the police as they went by that the investigation was hopeless before it had begun.

For a family in our situation, Mr. Seegrave was the most comforting officer you could wish to see. Tall and portly, military in manner, commanding in voice, resolute in eye, and buttoned into a grand frock-coat up to a leather stock—he looked like a man with a warrant in his pocket for mischief itself. He ordered his two inferior men about with such severity that the servants began to believe, in sheer self-defence, that a discovery must be made.

He went round the premises, out and in, and satisfied himself no thieves had broken in from outside. The robbery, therefore, must have been committed by some person in the house. I leave you to imagine what that did among the servants. He decided to begin with the boudoir, then examine the servants. At the same time he posted one of his men on the staircase leading to the servants’ bedrooms, with orders to let nobody pass.

At that, the women went distracted. They bounced from corners, whisked upstairs in a body to Miss Rachel’s room—Rosanna Spearman swept along among them—and burst in upon the Superintendent, all looking equally guilty, demanding to know which of them he suspected at once.

Mr. Seegrave proved equal to them. He faced them with his resolute eye, and cowed them with his military voice.

“Now, then, you women—downstairs again, every one of you. I won’t have you here. Look!”

He pointed suddenly to a little smear of decorative paint on Miss Rachel’s door, at the outer edge just under the lock.

“See what mischief the petticoats of some of you have done already. Clear out!”

Rosanna Spearman, nearest to him and nearest the smear, set the example and slipped off at once to her work. The rest followed. The Superintendent finished his examination and, making nothing of it, asked who had first discovered the robbery. Penelope had. Penelope was sent for.

He was a little too sharp with her at starting.

“Now, young woman, attend to me, and mind you speak the truth.”

Penelope flamed up. “I’ve never been taught to tell lies, Mr. Policeman! And if father can stand there and hear me accused of falsehood and thieving, and my own bedroom shut against me, and my character taken away—which is all a poor girl has left—he’s not the good father I take him for!”

A timely word from me set justice and Penelope on a pleasanter footing. The questions and answers went smoothly and ended in nothing: she had seen Miss Rachel put the Diamond in the drawer last thing at night; she went in with the tea at eight and found the drawer open and empty; she alarmed the house.

The Superintendent next asked to see Miss Rachel. Penelope carried the request through the door. The answer came back the same way.

“I have nothing to tell the policeman—I can’t see anybody.”

Mr. Seegrave looked surprised and offended. I told him my young lady was ill and begged him to wait. We went downstairs and met Mr. Godfrey and Mr. Franklin crossing the hall.

As inmates, the two gentlemen were examined. They knew nothing. Had they heard any suspicious noise? Nothing but the patter of rain. Had I, lying awake longer, heard anything? Nothing. Released, Mr. Franklin whispered to me, still clinging to his helpless view:

“That man will be of no earthly use. Superintendent Seegrave is an ass.”

Released in his turn, Mr. Godfrey whispered:

“Evidently a most competent person. Betteredge, I have the greatest faith in him!”

Many men, many opinions.

Mr. Seegrave’s next step took him back to the boudoir, with Penelope and me following. He wanted to discover whether any furniture had been moved in the night—his former search not satisfying him on that point. We were still poking among chairs and tables when the bedroom door suddenly opened.

After denying herself to everybody, Miss Rachel, to our astonishment, walked straight into the midst of us of her own accord. She took up her garden hat from a chair; then she went directly to Penelope and put a question to her.
