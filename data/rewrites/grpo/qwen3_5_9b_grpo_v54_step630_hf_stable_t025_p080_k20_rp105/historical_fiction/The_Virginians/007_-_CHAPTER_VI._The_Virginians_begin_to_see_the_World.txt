Following the departure of her disgraced chaplain, Madam Esmond and her son appeared reconciled, though the shadow of their estrangement lingered heavily. George fell ill with a fever shortly thereafter, his mind wandering in delirium until he shrieked, "Broken! Broken! It never, never can be mended!" His mother watched him toss upon the midnight bed, helpless against a malady that defied her remedies. Desperate, she rode through the snow on her pony to implore Mr. Dempster for aid. "I shall bury my resentment, madam," he promised, "as your ladyship buried your pride." He returned to Castlewood with his medical kit and a loyal servant, saving George from death and restoring him to health.

Though a voyage to England was suggested, the widow's quarrel with her kin there prevented it. Instead, the two young gentlemen, accompanied by Mr. Dempster as tutor and two servants, sailed to New York and ascended the Hudson to Albany. There, they entered the French provinces, welcomed by the local gentry. Harry, eager for adventure, camped with the Indians, hunting bears and trading furs. George, however, found his true calling among the French ladies. With a delicate constitution, he perfected his French accent until he could pass for a native, dancing the minuet with elegance and playing the violin with such mastery that he even engaged in an affair of honor with a young ensign of the Auvergne regiment, the Chevalier de la Jabotiere. Madame de Mouchy wrote to Madam Esmond praising her son's refinement, a letter that would have brought little joy to Mr. Whitfield.

Upon their return after ten delightful months, the boys had grown indistinguishable in stature, George's raven hair contrasting with Harry's light locks tied in ribbons. Yet, the journey had not softened George's nature. He returned with no goodwill toward George Washington, whom he viewed with the same suspicion he held for his grandfather's friend. Washington, though barely of age, carried an air of grave maturity, managing family affairs with a trustworthiness that surpassed men twice his senior.

At Castlewood, Mrs. Mountain, Madam Esmond's droll and match-making companion, spun webs of suspicion regarding the Major. She convinced herself that Washington coveted the widow, noting his constant presence and the widow's reliance on his counsel for everything from estate management to selling tobacco. "Great tall men always marry little chits of women," she reasoned, dismissing the fifteen-year age gap as trivial. When George sternly begged her to silence these absurdities, she merely shrugged, unable to keep her eyes shut on the evidence of his affection. George responded with frigid courtesy and biting sarcasm, retreating to his bookroom to study mathematics and Latin in lonely solitude, while Harry rode out with sporting friends.

The tension nearly erupted when Washington visited Castlewood, receiving the widow's cordiality with such tenderness that George's jealousy threatened to boil over. However, the visit was a farewell; Washington was departing for a perilous mission to the western frontier. The French, having claimed the Ohio River valley, were encroaching on British settlements. In 1753, amidst the uneasy peace of Europe, the colonial dispute ignited on the Ohio. Young Washington volunteered to carry Governor Dinwiddie's demand for the French to withdraw. Accompanied by a guide and attendants, he braved the frozen forests and rivers to reach Fort Le Boeuf, only to be told bluntly that the French intended to hold the land and drive the English out.

Harry, returning from a cockfight, lamented his absence from such noble sport, while the widow hailed Washington's return as proof of his superiority over her sons' idle pursuits. When George expressed his desire to serve under the Major, the widow refused, insisting an Esmond could only serve with a royal commission. Undeterred, Washington raised a regiment of provincials to confront the French. At the forks of the Ohio, near the site of future Pittsburgh, a small force under Colonel Trent faced overwhelming numbers. The French seized the fort, renaming it Fort Duquesne. No shots had been fired yet, but the stage was set for a war that would span sixty years and reshape the continent.

In the following year, Washington took command of the provincial troops. He engaged the French advance guard, killing their leader, but was ultimately forced to capitulate due to superior enemy forces. On July 4, 1754, he marched out from Fort Necessity, surrendering the post and returning home. Defeated and humbled, he presented himself at Castlewood, admitting his earlier boasts had been born of anger at George's perceived scorn. "I thought you laughed at us," he confessed.

George, moved by his comrade's sorrow, reached out to comfort him, forgetting his jealousy in the face of shared misfortune. He defended Washington's honor, noting that starvation and lack of ammunition had doomed the fight. But the widow interrupted, declaring that English regiments would have routed the French, and offering a fine curtsey to the defeated Colonel. This gesture of pity only inflamed George's jealousy anew, leaving the young soldier to hang his head while the widow praised her sex's kindness to the unhappy.
