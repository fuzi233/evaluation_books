Thursday night went by like a pot left too long at the edge of the range: the heat was there, the expectation was there, and yet nothing came to a boil. Then Friday morning arrived and, with it, two morsels of news dropped on the table together—one stale, one scalding.

First: the baker’s man swore he had met Rosanna Spearman the afternoon before—thick veil down, walking towards Frizinghall by the moor-footpath. Rosanna, with her poor twisted shoulder, was as easy to know as a burnt loaf is to smell. And yet the man must have mistaken her; for Rosanna, as I knew it, had been upstairs all Thursday afternoon, ill in her room.

Second: the postman brought word of Mr. Candy. He had joked on the birthday night, in the wet, that a doctor’s skin was waterproof. The rain had answered him. He had taken a chill and was now down with fever—light-headed, talking delirious nonsense as fluently, poor little man, as ever he talked it in his right mind. We pitied him honestly. Mr. Franklin pitied him too—but with Miss Rachel in his thoughts, as if the girl’s spirit, kept too long on edge by the Moonstone’s loss, might soon need a physician’s calm hand more than any of us did.

Breakfast had scarcely settled when another message arrived—this one sharp as a knife on a board. A telegram from Mr. Blake the elder: he had, with the Commissioner’s help, secured the right man. Sergeant Cuff. He would come down from London by the morning train.

At the name, Mr. Franklin started as if he had heard a pot crack.

“I begin to hope we are seeing the end of our anxieties,” he said. “If half the stories I’ve heard are true, there isn’t his equal in England for unravelling a mystery.”

Excitement ran through the house like the first good scent of broth through a cold kitchen. Superintendent Seegrave, hearing Cuff was expected, shut himself away with pen, ink, and paper—making notes for the report he knew would be demanded of him. For my own part, I should have liked to go to the station; but my lady’s carriage was not to be put into harness for any man alive—not even a celebrated detective—and the pony-chaise was needed later for Mr. Godfrey, who regretted leaving at such a time but had a Ladies’ Charity in difficulties waiting his counsel in town. He agreed to stay till the last train, to hear what London’s clever man would say—then off he must go.

When the hour came, I went down to the gate.

A fly from the railway rolled up as I reached the lodge; out stepped a grizzled elderly man, miserably lean, as if life had pared him down to the bone. He wore decent black and a white cravat; his face was sharp as a hatchet, his skin yellow and dry as a leaf left too long in an autumn hedge. His eyes—steely light grey—had a trick of meeting yours as if they expected you to produce something you hadn’t even admitted to yourself. He walked softly; his voice fell melancholy; his long fingers hooked like claws. Parson, undertaker, or any other gloomy trade might have suited his look—anything, in short, except what he was.

“Is this Lady Verinder’s?” he asked.

“Yes, sir.”

“I am Sergeant Cuff.”

“This way, sir, if you please.”

On the way I gave him my name and place in the household, so he might speak freely to me of the robbery. But he said nothing of it. He admired the grounds, said the sea air was brisk and refreshing. I wondered—privately—how such a man had got such a name. We walked together with the constrained caution of two strange dogs coupled to one chain.

Lady Verinder, I learned, was in one of the conservatories. We went round to the back gardens and sent a servant for her. While we waited, Sergeant Cuff looked through the evergreen arch on the left, spied the rosery, and walked into it with the first sign of interest he had shown.

To the gardener’s astonishment and my own disgust, the celebrated policeman proved a mine of learning on what I had always thought a trumpery subject.

“Ah, you’ve got the right exposure here to the south and sou’-west,” he said, wagging his grizzled head; and the melancholy voice took a thin streak of pleasure, as if the scent of living things softened it. “This is the shape for a rosery—nothing like a circle set in a square. Yes, yes; with walks between all the beds. But they oughtn’t to be gravel walks like these. Grass, Mr. Gardener—grass walks between your roses; gravel’s too hard for them. That’s a sweet pretty bed of white roses and blush roses. They always mix well together, don’t they? Here’s the white musk rose, Mr. Betteredge—our old English rose holding up its head along with the best and newest of them. Pretty dear!”

He fondled the musk rose with those lank fingers, talking to it as if it were a child whose name mattered. I could not help thinking: This is the man to recover Miss Rachel’s Diamond and find out the thief?

“You seem fond of roses, Sergeant?” I said.

“I haven’t much time to be fond of anything,” he answered. “But when I have a moment’s fondness to bestow, most times, Mr. Betteredge, the roses get it. I began my life among them in my father’s nursery garden, and I shall end my life among them, if I can. One of these days—please God—I shall retire from catching thieves and try my hand at growing roses. There will be grass walks, Mr. Gardener, between my beds.” The gravel seemed to offend him like grit between the teeth.

“It seems an odd taste, sir,” I ventured, “for a man in your line.”

“If you will look about you (which most people won’t do), you will see that the nature of a man’s tastes is most times as opposite as possible to the nature of a man’s business. Show me two things more opposite than a rose and a thief; and I’ll correct my tastes accordingly—if it isn’t too late at my time of life.”

He lifted his head. “Here’s a lady coming. Is it Lady Verinder?”

He had seen her before either I or the gardener—though we knew which way to watch, and he did not. That, more than all his rose-talk, told me there was metal under the withered skin.

Lady Verinder appeared—embarrassed, for the first time in all my experience of her, at an interview with a stranger. Something in the man’s appearance, or in his errand, put her a little at a loss. He put her at ease directly by turning to business as if business were a familiar domestic routine, like carving or pouring tea.

Had anyone else been employed about the robbery before him? Yes—another person had been called in and was now in the house. He begged leave to speak to that person before anything else.

My lady led the way back. Before following, the Sergeant could not resist one last sour look at the rosery paths.

“Get her ladyship to try grass,” he said to the gardener. “No gravel! no gravel!”

Why Superintendent Seegrave should have seemed to shrink several sizes when presented to Sergeant Cuff, I cannot explain. The fact is plain enough. They retired together, shut up for a weary long time. When they came out, Mr. Superintendent was excited; Mr. Sergeant was yawning.

“The Sergeant wishes to see Miss Verinder’s sitting-room,” said Seegrave, with pomp and eagerness. “The Sergeant may have questions to ask. Attend the Sergeant, if you please!”

While I was being ordered about, I looked at Cuff. He looked at Seegrave in that quiet, expecting way—like a man waiting to see whether another will serve him sense or nonsense. I will not say he watched for his brother officer’s appearance as an Ass; I will say I suspected it.

I led them upstairs. Sergeant Cuff went softly all over the Indian cabinet and all round the boudoir, asking questions now and then of Mr. Superintendent, and continually of me—questions whose drift I believe was equally unintelligible to both of us. In due course he came to the door and faced the decorative painting you know of. He laid one lean finger on the small smear just under the lock—the one Superintendent Seegrave had noticed while driving the women-servants out the previous morning.

“That’s a pity,” said Cuff. “How did it happen?”

I explained about the women crowding in, and their petticoats doing the mischief.

“Superintendent Seegrave ordered them out, sir,” I added, “before they did any more harm.”

“Right!” said Seegrave in his military way. “I ordered them out. The petticoats did it, Sergeant—the petticoats did it.”

“Did you notice which petticoat did it?” asked Cuff—still addressing me.

“No, sir.”

He turned on Seegrave. “You noticed, I suppose?”

Mr. Superintendent was a little taken aback. “I can’t charge my memory, Sergeant—a mere trifle.”

Sergeant Cuff looked at him as he had looked at the gravel walks—displeased, and certain.

“I made a private inquiry last week, Mr. Superintendent. At one end of the inquiry there was a murder, and at the other end there was a spot of ink on a tablecloth that nobody could account for. In all my experience along the dirtiest ways of this dirty little world, I have never met with such a thing as a trifle yet. Before we go a step further we must see the petticoat that made the smear, and we must know for certain when that paint was wet.”

Seegrave, sulky under the set-down, asked if he should summon the women. Cuff considered, sighed, and shook his head.

“No. We’ll take the matter of the paint first. It’s a question of Yes or No with the paint—which is short. It’s a question of petticoats with the women—which is long. What o’clock was it when the servants were in this room yesterday morning? Eleven—eh? Is there anybody in the house who knows whether that paint was wet or dry at eleven yesterday morning?”

“Her ladyship’s nephew, Mr. Franklin Blake, knows,” I said.

“Is the gentleman in the house?”

He was close at hand—waiting, as eager as a man longing to be called to table, for his first chance of being introduced to the great Cuff. In half a minute he was in the room, giving his evidence.

“That door, Sergeant,” he said, “has been painted by Miss Verinder, under my inspection, with my help, and in a vehicle of my own composition. The vehicle dries whatever colours may be used with it, in twelve hours.”

“Do you remember when the smeared bit was done?” asked Cuff.

“Perfectly. That was the last morsel of the door to be finished. We wanted to get it done on Wednesday last—and I myself completed it by three in the afternoon, or soon after.”

“Today is Friday,” said Cuff to Seegrave. “Let us reckon back. At three on Wednesday afternoon, that bit was completed. The vehicle dried it in twelve hours—that is to say, by three on Thursday morning. At eleven on Thursday morning you held your inquiry here. Take three from eleven, and eight remains. That paint had been eight hours dry, Mr. Superintendent, when you supposed the women-servants’ petticoats smeared it.”

That was the first knock-down blow. If Seegrave had not suspected poor Penelope, I might have pitied him.

Having disposed of the paint, Sergeant Cuff abandoned his brother officer as a bad job and addressed himself to Mr. Franklin as the more promising assistant.

“It’s quite on the cards, sir,” he said, “that you have put the clue into our hands.”

As he spoke, the bedroom door opened, and Miss Rachel came out among us suddenly.

She went straight to Cuff, not seeming to notice—or care—that he was a stranger.

“Did you say,” she asked, pointing to Mr. Franklin, “that he had put the clue into your hands?”

“This is Miss Verinder,” I whispered behind the Sergeant.

“That gentleman, miss,” said Cuff, his steely eyes studying her face as carefully as if her expression were a handwriting sample, “has possibly put the clue into our hands.”

For one moment she tried to look at Mr. Franklin—I say tried, for she looked away again before their eyes met. Something was disturbed in her. Colour rose, then fled; and with the pallor came a new look, which startled me—like a door in the mind swinging open on a room you did not know existed.

“Having answered your question,” said Cuff, “I beg leave to make an inquiry in my turn. There is a smear on the painting of your door. Do you happen to know when it was done? or who did it?”

Instead of replying, she went on with her own questions, as if she had not heard.

“Are you another police-officer?” she asked.

“I am Sergeant Cuff, miss, of the Detective Police.”

“Do you think a young lady’s advice worth having?”

“I shall be glad to hear it, miss.”

“Do your duty by yourself—and don’t allow Mr. Franklin Blake to help you!”

She spoke with such spite, such savage ill-will towards Mr. Franklin, that—though I had known her from a baby, though I loved and honoured her next to my lady—I was ashamed of Miss Rachel for the first time in my life.

Cuff’s eyes never moved from her face. “Thank you, miss. Do you happen to know anything about the smear? Might you have done it by accident yourself?”

“I know nothing about the smear.”

With that she turned away and shut herself into her bedroom again. This time I heard what Penelope had heard before—her burst of crying, the private storm behind the closed door.

I could not bring myself to look at the Sergeant; I looked at Mr. Franklin. He seemed more distressed than I was.

“I told you I was uneasy about her,” he said. “And now you see why.”

“Miss Verinder appears to be a little out of temper about the loss of her Diamond,” remarked Cuff. “It’s a valuable jewel. Natural enough! natural enough!”

A cold shudder ran through me that I could not then name. The excuse I had made for her—made again by a stranger, without any personal interest in softening her behaviour—left me with the sense that Cuff had seen something in Miss Rachel, and heard something from her, that lit the case in a new and horrid way.

“A young lady’s tongue is a privileged member, sir,” said Cuff to Mr. Franklin. “Let us forget what has passed, and go straight on. Thanks to you, we know when the paint was dry. The next thing to discover is when the paint was last seen without that smear. You have got a head on your shoulders—and you understand what I mean.”

“I think I do,” said Mr. Franklin, steadying himself. “The more we narrow the question of time, the more we narrow the field of inquiry.”

“That’s it, sir. Did you notice your work here on Wednesday afternoon, after you had done it?”

Mr. Franklin shook his head. “I can’t say I did.”

“Did you?” Cuff asked, turning to me.

“I can’t say I did either, sir.”

“Who was the last person in the room—the last thing—on Wednesday night?”

“Miss Rachel, I suppose, sir.”

Mr. Franklin put in, “Or possibly your daughter, Betteredge.” He explained to the Sergeant that my daughter was Miss Verinder’s maid.

“Mr. Betteredge, ask your daughter to step up. Stop!” Cuff said, drawing me to the window out of earshot. “Your Superintendent has made a pretty full report to me of the manner in which he has managed this case. Among other things, he has, by his own confession, set the servants’ backs up. It’s very important to smooth them down again. Tell your daughter, and tell the rest of them, these two things, with my compliments: First, that I have no evidence before me, yet, that the Diamond has been stolen; I only know that the Diamond has been lost. Second, that my business here with the servants is simply to ask them to lay their heads together and help me to find it.”

My experience of the women-servants—when Seegrave had laid his embargo on their rooms—helped me here.

“May I make so bold, Sergeant, as to tell the women a third thing?” I asked. “Are they free, with your compliments, to fidget up and downstairs, and whisk in and out of their bedrooms, if the fit takes them?”

“Perfectly free,” said Cuff.

“That will smooth them down, sir,” I said, “from the cook to the scullion.”

“Go, and do it at once, Mr. Betteredge.”

I did it in under five minutes. Only one difficulty: when I came to the part about the bedrooms, it took a stiff exertion of my authority to prevent the entire female household from following me and Penelope upstairs—volunteer witnesses in a fever of helpfulness.

Sergeant Cuff seemed to approve of Penelope. He grew a trifle less dreary; his face took on something like what I had seen in the rosery when he found the white musk rose. Here is my daughter’s evidence, as drawn out by the Sergeant. She gave it prettily, I think—though there: she is my child all over; nothing of her mother in her, Lord bless you, nothing of her mother in her.

Penelope examined: She had taken a lively interest in the painting on the door and had helped to mix the colours. She noticed the bit under the lock because it was the last bit done. She had seen it some hours afterwards without a smear. She had left it, as late as twelve at night, without a smear. At that hour she had wished her young lady good-night in the bedroom; she had heard the clock strike in the boudoir; her hand at the time was on the handle of the painted door; she knew the paint was wet (having helped to mix the colours); she took particular pains not to touch it; could swear she held up the skirts of her dress, and that there was no smear then; could not swear her dress mightn’t have touched it accidentally in going out. She remembered the dress she wore because it was new—a present from Miss Rachel; her father remembered and could speak to it too; she fetched it; I recognised it as the dress she wore that night. The skirts were examined—long job, from the size of them—and not the ghost of a paint-stain was discovered anywhere. End of Penelope’s evidence. Signed, Gabriel Betteredge.

Next, the Sergeant questioned me about any large dogs in the house—whether one might have got in and done the mischief with a whisk of its tail. That being impossible, he sent for a magnifying-glass and examined the smear. No skin-mark of a human hand printed off on the paint. All visible signs told that some loose article of dress had touched the wet colour in passing. That somebody—putting Penelope’s evidence and Mr. Franklin’s evidence together—must have been in the room, and smeared the paint, between midnight and three in the morning.

Having brought it thus far, Sergeant Cuff seemed to remember that Superintendent Seegrave still remained in the room. He summed up for his brother-officer’s benefit, in a tone as quiet as a knife laid back on its plate.

“This trifle of yours, Mr. Superintendent,” he said, pointing to the door, “has grown a little in importance since you noticed it last. At the present stage there are, as I take it, three discoveries to make, starting from that smear. Find out (first) whether there is any article of dress in this house with the smear of the paint on it. Find out (second) who that dress belongs to. Find out (third) how the person can account for having been in this room, and smeared the paint, between midnight and three in the morning. If the person can’t satisfy you, you haven’t far to look for the hand that has got the Diamond. I’ll work this by myself, if you please, and detain you no longer from your regular business in the town. You have got one of your men here, I see. Leave him here at my disposal, in case I want him—and allow me to wish you good morning.”

Seegrave’s respect for the Sergeant was great; his respect for himself greater still. Hit hard, he hit back as smartly as he could manage on the way out.

“I have abstained from expressing any opinion so far,” he said, in his military voice. “I have now only one remark to offer on leaving this case in your hands. There is such a thing, Sergeant, as making a mountain out of a molehill. Good morning.”

“There is also such a thing,” Cuff replied, “as making nothing out of a molehill, in consequence of your head being too high to see it.”

With that he wheeled about and walked away to the window by himself, leaving the rest of us to taste the silence he had made—thin, sharp, and full of a meaning that had not yet found words.
