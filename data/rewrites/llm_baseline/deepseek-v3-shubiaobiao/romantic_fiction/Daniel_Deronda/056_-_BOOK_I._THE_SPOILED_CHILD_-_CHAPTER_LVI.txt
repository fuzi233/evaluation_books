*"The pang, the curse with which they died,
Had never passed away:
I could not draw my eyes from theirs,
Nor lift them up to pray."*
—**Coleridge**

Deronda did not sleep that night. After Gwendolen, trembling and resolute, had insisted on seeing him once more before allowing herself to be helped to bed, she had fallen into an exhausted silence, only whispering a plea that he return to her in the morning. Yet he could not shake the dread that fever or delirium might still overtake her, that some hidden horror of the day’s events might claw its way to the surface in broken, damning words. He left instructions with her maid—should there be any change for the worse, he was to be summoned at once.

When fatigue finally dragged him into uneasy slumber, his dreams were a tangled replay of the day’s anguish. He woke with a start, his pulse racing, the image of Gwendolen’s stricken face burning behind his eyelids.

Morning came without a summons—a mercy. Later, he learned she had slept fitfully but without further distress. There was a grim resilience in her, he thought, this woman who had trembled so violently in life yet now seemed to marshal herself with an almost frightening determination to conceal whatever raged beneath.

For his part, Deronda felt oddly detached, as though his capacity for deeper feeling had been drained by the storm of his reunion with his mother. His movements were methodical: securing statements from the fishermen who had pulled Gwendolen from the water, piecing together the scant details. Grandcourt’s boat had been found adrift, its sail loose. The men speculated he had been struck by the swinging canvas as he tried to maneuver, dragged under by the current. They had heard a cry, then Gwendolen’s scream before she plunged in after him.

When word came that she was awake and asking for him, Deronda entered her room to find it darkened, the air thick with the hush of drawn curtains. She sat wrapped in a white shawl, coiled and waiting, her face bloodless except for the bruise-like shadows beneath her eyes. The Gwendolen who rose unsteadily to meet him was a specter of the woman he had once known—proud, defiant, now reduced to a whisper of herself.

"Sit beside me," she murmured when he approached. "I must speak very low."

He obeyed, drawing a chair near. The gesture seemed to wound her further. She turned her hollow gaze full upon him.

"You know I am a guilty woman?"

Deronda went cold. "I know nothing."

"He is dead."

"Yes."

"A dead face," she said, her voice cracking under the weight of the image. "It will never leave me."

Her hands clenched, white-knuckled, in her lap. He could see the battle raging in her—confession clawing its way up her throat, only to be swallowed back down. When she spoke again, it was with the desperation of a soul dangling over an abyss.

"Tell me you will not forsake me. If you turn away now, what am I?"

He could not answer. Instead, he took one of her shaking hands in his, a silent vow.

What followed was a fractured confession, spilling out in gasps—how hatred had festered in her like a sickness, how she had fantasized about violence, how she had once hidden a knife and then thrown away the key. She spoke of the boat, of Grandcourt’s voice ringing over the water, of the moment she hesitated before reaching for the rope.

"I wished him dead," she breathed. "And then he was."

Deronda listened, his own heart a drum of dread. This was no fevered rambling. Every word was measured, deliberate, as though she were carving her guilt into her own flesh.

When her voice finally broke, dissolving into sobs, he turned away, unable to bear the sight. But her whisper dragged him back:

"I make you very unhappy."

He shook his head, though the motion cost him. "There is no question of happiness. Only of what you need."

What she needed, it seemed, was absolution—or at least the illusion of it. But absolution was not his to give.

The room pressed in around them, heavy with unsaid things. Outside, the world moved on, indifferent. Inside, there was only this: a woman drowning in her own remorse, and the man who had promised, against all reason, not to let her sink alone.
