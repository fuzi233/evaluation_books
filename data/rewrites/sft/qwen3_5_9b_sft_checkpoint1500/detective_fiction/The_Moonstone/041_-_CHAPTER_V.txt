Betteredge seemed content to let the thread of Mr. Candy's assistant drop, as though the subject itself were trivial dust compared to what lay ahead. He refolded Rosanna Spearman's letter and returned to the table, leaving me to sit by the window, watching the rain blur the garden.

For a fleeting moment, the disorienting echo of Ezra Jennings lingered—a phantom sensation that someone so remote could have stirred me so deeply. But as quickly as it arrived, the impression faded, dissolving back into the ordinary flow of my thoughts. I forced myself to confront the sheer, terrifying absurdity of my situation. With a grim resolve, I laid out the plan I had finally conjured: return to London immediately, expose the entire mystery to Inspector Bruff, and above all, secure an audience with Rachel, whatever cost it might demand. There was still an hour before the train, a sliver of time to wait. Perhaps, if I was lucky, Betteredge might stumble upon a clue buried in the unread tail of Rosanna's letter—one piece of information vital to my departure from this cursed house.

So I waited.

Rosanna's letter concluded with a confession that chilled the blood despite the warmth of the fire: *"You have no need to be angry, Mr. Franklin... Anxieties and fears soon came back to me."* She detailed her torment over hiding the nightgown, the garment that now served as her evidence and her lifeline. She described dressing in it herself, a desperate measure to protect you, the thief, from exposure. She recounted how Sergeant Cuff, the detective, had visited her in the lady's sitting room to inspect the laundry book. She wrote of her dread that he might recognize her, a former criminal, working within the walls where the diamond had vanished.

*"He looked at me as if I was a stranger,"* she wrote, *"but I was not certain of what he might do..."*

She confessed that she had sought you in the shrubbery, hoping for a final word, but you had turned away from her as if she carried a disease. *"You turned away as if I had got the plague,"* she lamented. A footnote from you later clarified that you simply remembered an errand for your aunt and entered the house, unaware that she was standing right there. Yet to Rosanna, that rejection was the breaking point.

*"Behind your back, I loved you with all my heart and soul,"* she declared, revealing the duality of her existence. Before you, she lived in paralyzing fear of your anger, of what you would say if she revealed she knew the truth. She explained why she hadn't stopped you when you fled, why she hadn't shouted, *"Mr. Franklin, I have got something to say!"* She had known of your debts, your need for money, and she had offered you a way out—a man in London who would buy the stone quietly. But pride, fear, and her own insecurities about her crooked shoulder and low birth had silenced her.

Her narrative spiraled into a tragedy of missed chances. *"If I had only been a little less fond of you, I think I should have destroyed it [the nightgown],"* she reasoned. Instead, she clung to it as proof of her loyalty, refusing to let it go because it was the only thing that could prove she had acted to save you. She decided to hide it, choosing the "Shivering Sand"—a dangerous spot near Cobb's Hole—as its grave.

Before finishing, she revealed her ultimate desperation. *"If I miss my next opportunity... good-bye to the world... Good-bye to life."* She hinted that if you remained cruel, she would take her own life, leaving you with the burden of her suicide. She begged you to remember her kindness, asking if you would speak gently of her ghost when the time came. The letter ended with her tear-streaked promise to roll the nightgown beneath her cloak and bury it in the sand, sealing her fate and hers with the diamond.

Betteredge read the letter through in silence. When he finished, he carefully resealed the envelope, his brow furrowed, his gaze cast downward. He sat for a long time, the weight of the woman's story pressing on him.

"Betteredge," I asked softly, "does the end of the letter offer any guidance?"

He lifted his head, a heavy sigh escaping his lips. "There is nothing to guide you, Mr. Franklin. If you take my advice, you will keep this letter locked away until your anxieties have settled. Reading it now will only tear at you. Do not read it today."

I slipped the letter into my pocket-book, the paper feeling heavier than lead. As I did, a sudden clarity struck me, illuminating the tragic arc of the previous weeks. Looking back at the chronicle of these days, the pattern became undeniable. Twice, Rosanna had made a last-ditch effort to reach me. First, on Friday night, she had found me alone at the billiard table. Her demeanor, her hushed urgency, screamed that she possessed knowledge of the diamond's disappearance. Had I been wise, I would have listened. Instead, I deliberately averted my eyes, pretending to watch the balls rather than her, sending her away wounded and confused.

Then, on Saturday, the day her departure seemed imminent, she tried again in the shrubbery. I was there, accompanied by Betteredge and Sergeant Cuff. The officer, playing his own subterfuge, had used Rosanna's name to probe my conscience. In a fatal error of judgment, driven perhaps by a desire to shield her from further trouble, I had loudly declared that I held *no interest whatsoever* in Rosanna Spearman. I had spoken those words so clearly that she could hear me, intending to warn her off.

But the irony was devastating. By denying any connection, I had cut off the only bridge she hoped to cross. I had pushed her away, self-doomed her to isolation, and inadvertently accelerated the chain of events leading to the quicksand. From that moment, the tragedy unfolded with terrifying speed.

Now, the retrospective was complete. I could allow Rosanna's story to stand silent, a haunting shadow in the background, and turn my attention to the living. The path forward was clear, paved by the dark choices of the past, leading inevitably toward the difficult, arduous journey from the shadows back into the light.
