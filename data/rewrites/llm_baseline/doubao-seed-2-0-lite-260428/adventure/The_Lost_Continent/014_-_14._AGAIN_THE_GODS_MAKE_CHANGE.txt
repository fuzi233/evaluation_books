I won’t waste breath recounting every charge we ran that day, me and my handful of crack fighting men tearing through a rebel horde that outnumbered us fifteen to one. Their ranks were already rotting from the inside out with panic: every last fighter they’d sent creeping through the secret mine tunnel under our walls, the one they’d bet the whole siege on, had never come back. The shouts of terror they’d first heard echoing from inside the city had curdled, then given way to roars of triumph from our garrison holding the inner walls. Those wretched rebels had no real combat training worth naming, most carried farm tools and sharpened fire-hardened sticks instead of proper iron arms, and their stupid egalitarian nonsense had gutted every last chain of command—there was no one to rally the fleeing, no one to point where to stand when the first blow fell.

Panic spread through their ragged lines faster than a plague murrain. My attack plan was brutally simple: we never stopped. No pausing to form a defensive line, no looting the dead. We flanked their left, charged through their centre, then wheeled to harry their right, hacking a new path through the packed mass every few minutes. We kept them running, kept them guessing where the next blow would land, until the outer ranks broke entirely and scattered for the forests and trampled cornfields, while the rebels trapped in the middle only stared around desperate for a gap to follow.

This rout was no feat of legendary arms, and no professional soldier would brag of it. It was nothing more than seizing the moment, and it worked. Give those same rebels an open battle on ground they’d chosen, and they’d have fought until every last man was dead, sheer ferocious numbers dragging down any trained phalanx the city could have fielded. They were no cowards—every last one was Atlantean, same as the people they were besieging, spurred to wild desperation by the generations of oppression and wrongs they’d endured. But scattered they were, the siege broken in a single afternoon, and the rebellion was all but done if we avoided stupid, brutal over-punishment that would only drive them to even more suicidal acts later.

Chasing the last of the fugitives far from the walls to hammer the lesson of their panic home left us drained. We’d fought through the full blazing heat of the Atlantean midday, every man of my band bone-weary, so I called a halt for the night in a cluster of half-ruined stone huts that made a ready makeshift fort. Luck was with us: a herd of skittish little cloven-hoofed wild horses, spooked by fleeing rebels, blundered straight into our lines. We dropped five of them before the rest could bolt, and soon campfires were glowing, the meat roasting to feed every man in the bivouac.

Sentries paced the perimeter, calling their check-ins to one another through the dark. Wounded men huddled by the flames patching their cuts and gashes. I sat with my officers going over the day’s charges, the small missteps and unexpected strokes of luck, the kind of tedious post-mortem that only soldiers ever care about. The hour dragged on, snores rising from the dark edges of camp, every man clearly exhausted, only staying awake and talking out of deference to their commander. I dragged the conversation on, though—feverishly, because I could not bear to be left alone with my own thoughts.

The awkward impasse broke when a sentry stepped up to the fire, saluting. “My lord, we caught a woman slipping through the perimeter. She won’t give her name, won’t explain her business, only says she has to see you.”

“Bring her here,” I ordered, then thought better of it. If she’d trekked all the way out to our rough bivouac, she had private, urgent news. I got to my feet and followed him to the outer lines. “Your lads seem jumpy.”

“Begging your pardon, my lord—she could be a rebel assassin come to stick a knife in your ribs. The Empress sets high store on you right now, and we all know how ruthless her torturers are.” His frank concern made me grin, and then he stepped aside to show me the prisoner.

She was swathed head to toe in a thick hooded cloak, but I’d know the line of that slim frame anywhere: it was Ylga, Nais’s twin sister. I told the sentries to stand down, no questions, and led her far out of earshot of the camp, down a narrow game trail. “This can’t be good news that brought you all the way out here through the dark, Ylga.”

“You recognized me? There’s more warmth between us than you ever let show, Deucalion, if you can pick me out under all these wrappings. But you never ask after the carrier, do you? Only the message. Well. You aren’t husband to Empress Phorenice anymore. She’s cast you off.”

I went cold. “That’s impossible.”

“It’s true. She found out about you and Nais. There’s a second airshaft right next to the cell where you talked Nais into drinking the stasis drug that’ll keep her asleep for a hundred years, waiting for you to come wake her. That shaft led to another prisoner, locked in a neighbouring cell, who heard every word you two said. He tried to trade that story for his freedom, but by the time his gaoler remembered to feed him, Phorenice had already publicly married you. They beheaded the fool before he could talk, but not before she’d gotten the whole story out of other scraps of testimony. She’s mad with jealousy. She isn’t just going to kill you.”

Ylga quoted Phorenice’s words, low and bitter, and I felt every hair on the back of my neck stand up: “‘Shall I hew off his head? Pah. Shall I hand him to my torturers? He wouldn’t wrinkle his brow at their worst. No. He gets a punishment that lasts. I’ll cut off his right hand and his left foot, so he can never fight again, then hurl him crippled into the dangerous wild lands outside the city. The great beasts will hunt him, the volcanic ground fires will burn him while he sleeps. He’ll know hunger, he’ll breathe the poisonous fumes from the rifts. And all the while he’ll remember I have Nais locked safe in her stone sleep, right here in the palace, to toy with and abuse however my fancy takes me.’ That’s what she said. Are you still stupid enough to march back into the city?”

I shook my head. Mutilation and a slow death in the wastes was not on my agenda.

“Good,” Ylga said, plucking at my sleeve. “I took the liberty of making your escape plan for you. You remember Tob? The ex-captain of Tatho’s old navy?”

“Hard-drinking tar-stained sailor who fought the great sea leviathans with me off the coast of Yucatan? Of course I do.”

“He’s sailing tonight. He’s sick of the corruption in Atlantis, sick of his wife and kids going hungry while the court hoards all the grain. He’s planning to sail west to found his own kingdom. I struck a deal with him. His ship is anchored at the cove three hours hard march west, waiting for you. He was so happy when I told him you’d be coming, he said he’d sail straight through the harbour forts before the night chain went up, no questions asked. Come on. We have to go, before someone in camp notices you’re missing.”

I thanked her, but she brushed the words off like they were worthless, hurrying me down the trail. We moved fast, our weapons drawn to fend off any stray predatory beast or rebel straggler. The way was rough: the dark swallowed the path, and once we came to a narrow r spanned only by a rotting log, over which scalding hot water from a volcanic spring bubbled down toward the sea. Slip, and the water would peel the flesh clean from your bones. “My footing’s no good here,” Ylga said, and reached out to take my hand. I helped her across, then let go, and she sighed softly before pulling her fingers away.

We walked for hours in silence. When I offered to help her over a patch of thorny brambles, she shoved my hand away roughly. “I’m no soft weakling, not if that’s the only reason you’re touching me.”

At last we crested a rise, and the great gleam of Atlantis’s estuary spread out below us through the trees. A single old, weathered ship rode at anchor a mile off shore, her hull dark in the faint starlight. We stumbled down the slope to the beach, and a hail shot out at us from the decks the second we got within arrow range. It was Tob, sharp and watchful. He rowed a dinghy in to meet us, grinning through his thick salt-and-pepper beard.

I turned to Ylga, holding out my hands. “Come back to the city with a guard I’ll send with you to the gates. You’ll be safe. Phorenice has no reason to hurt you.”

She shook her head. “I’m not going back.”

“Ylga, this voyage is no pleasure trip. I’ve carved out colonies before, and this will be ten times worse. We have no proper supplies, half the crew are raw landsmen with no survival experience. Most of us will get scars, most of us will go hungry, the first year will break anyone who isn’t tough as forged iron.”

“I can cook. I can shoot a bow better than half your soldiers. I can sew clothes out of beast hide stitched with sinew. You only ever saw me as Phorenice’s fan-girl, didn’t you? You think I’m soft and useless. I don’t care. I’m not going back to that court where every man kisses my boots but none of them see me.”

“I can’t bring you with me,” I said, heavy. “My heart belongs to Nais. I couldn’t ever—”

She laughed, sharp and bitter. “Go on, kick my pride. I knew I’d only ever be second best to her. But I still can’t make myself hate you. I’d rather die out here on this voyage than go back to the palace and watch you die slow at Phorenice’s hands.” Before I could speak, she flung her arms around my neck and kissed me hard on the mouth, her lips warm and salty. “That’s for goodbye. You’ll never forget that, will you?” Then she turned and ran, vanishing into the dark treeline before I could call after her.

Tob yelled from the water that the tide was turning, and we didn’t have time to go looking for her. I waded out to the dinghy, clambered up the ship’s side, and the crew hauled anchor immediately.

“Greeting, my lord,” Tob rumbled, pointing out the rickety old hull. “She’s a rotten old tub, no fancy machines, no automatons to steer her. Got worm rot in the bottom from decades in the Egypt trade. But she’s the best I could afford. We’ve got fifty fighting lads, eight women, all ready to carve out a new home. We’re not heading for savage Europe, either. I found a chain of islands far north once, full of tin ore strewn all over the beaches, no Atlantean knows they exist but me. The savages there are blue-painted, naked, easy to beat into submission. No giant lizards, barely any big dangerous beasts. Perfect for a new kingdom.”

He’d dreamed of being king there for years, he admitted, but he’d long decided he’d serve under me, if I was willing to lead them. For two months we sailed slow along the coast, stopping at every river mouth and sheltered cove to hunt wild game, catch fish, forage for grain, stock up our larders so we wouldn’t starve on the long open crossing to the Tin Islands. No patrol ships from Phorenice ever chased us down the estuary. The crew relaxed, started laughing, started making plans for their new homes.

It was then I turned to Tob, leaning against the rail watching the waves roll by. “Tob,” I said. “I’ve got a favour to ask. I don’t want to sail all the way to the Tin Islands. Drop me off on this shore here, the part of the Atlantean main continent that faces out toward Europe. I don’t have gold or supplies to pay you with, but I’ll give you every weapon and charm I carry, the ones that kept me alive through Yucatan. They’ll keep your crew safe on the crossing. I don’t want a kingdom far out at sea. I’ve got unfinished business here on the main land.”

Tob blinked, then grinned so wide his beard crinkled. “Aye, my lord. Whatever you say. That shore’s full of wild game, no guards, no patrols. I’ll drop you at the next cove at high tide. Fair winds to you.”
