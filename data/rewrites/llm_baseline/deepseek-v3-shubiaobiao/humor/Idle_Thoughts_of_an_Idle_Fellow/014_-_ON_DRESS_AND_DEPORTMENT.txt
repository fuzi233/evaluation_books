They say—though really, the sort of people who say such things ought to be locked up—that a well-tailored suit brings more joy to the human heart than religion ever could. And, much as it pains me to admit it, those cynical rogues might just be right. Take my own youthful folly, for instance (many, *many* moons ago, as the melodramatic novelists insist). Whenever life dealt me a particularly cruel blow—say, my washerwoman fired *me* for a change, or my latest blank-verse masterpiece was returned with the editor’s “kind regrets” (a phrase that grows less kind with each rejection), or my beloved scorned me with the fervor only a woman can muster (though really, it’s astonishing how many ways there are to be rejected—every lovelorn fool insists his suffering is unprecedented; at this rate, our great-grandchildren will have to invent entirely new methods of heartbreak just to keep things fresh)—well, in those dark hours, I had a foolproof remedy: I dressed to the nines.

A crisp new hat, trousers with a knife-edge crease (preserved through the scientific method of sandwiching them between mattress and bedstead), and suddenly, the world didn’t seem so bleak. Who cared about one fickle washerwoman? Who needed the affections of some short-sighted maiden when there were others who might appreciate a dashing, clever young fellow like myself? Clothes, you see, are half the battle in courtship. A young man will agonize for hours over whether to don his light summer suit with a jaunty cane and a dashing billycock, or to risk the solemnity of black tails, a towering top hat, and—heaven forbid—an umbrella. And no matter his choice, the universe will conspire against him. Opt for the light suit? The skies open, and he arrives at his beloved’s doorstep looking like a drowned rat. Choose the top hat? The sun blazes with infernal spite, melting his mustache wax and reducing his artfully arranged curls to something resembling overcooked spaghetti.

Ah, but how the young swains of yesteryear put us to shame! Those dashing figures from the Christmas cards—all snug breeches, Hessian boots, ruffled cuffs, and rakish hats—they didn’t *struggle* to win hearts. They simply *existed*, and maidens swooned on principle. Meanwhile, modern lovers must contend with the sartorial tragedy of baggy trousers and shapeless jackets. No wonder romance is in decline.

Clothes, you see, don’t just cover us—they *transform* us. Dress a man in rags, and he’ll slink through the streets like a guilty dog. Clothe him in finery, and he’ll strut like a peacock, swinging his cane and winking at every passing skirt. There’s a reason knights of old were so dashing in plumes and daggers—you can’t help but feel heroic in attire like that. But wrap a man in an ulster, and he’ll cower behind the nearest lamppost at the first sign of trouble.

I’ll grant you, virtue exists beneath tweed as much as silk, but the *spirit* of chivalry? That requires a bit of theater. The world, I fear, has grown dreadfully dull. We’ve passed through the ages of flowing robes and barbaric tattoos, through the flamboyant foppery of doublets and lace, and arrived at this: a society dressed like a congregation of undertakers. Black coats, black hats, black boots—good heavens, we might as well be in perpetual mourning. And the worst of it? We’ve convinced ourselves that dullness equals respectability. The holier the man, the blacker his attire; the more sinful, the lighter the suit. White hats? The mark of irredeemable degenerates. (Not that *I* would know, of course. My own headwear is a monument to restraint—five years old and counting, though I confess it briefly flirted with unfashionability before history vindicated me.)

Still, I can’t help but pine for a splash of color. Must we all resemble ants scurrying across a coal heap? Why shouldn’t we dress like peacocks? Lavender tights, emerald doublets, cloaks of sky-blue silk—imagine the joy of it! Philosophers may skulk in crow-black robes if they wish, but let the rest of us be butterflies.

And women—oh, women *must* dress brightly. They are the flowers of this dreary earth, and it’s their sacred duty to prove it. For all our grumbling about them, what would the world be without their ribbons and lace, their parasols and gloves strewn about like rainbows? Even the most drab bachelor’s quarters are transformed when a whirlwind of silk and chiffon breezes through. And summer! How it blossoms when women emerge in their pastel frocks, dotting the landscape like scattered petals. (Though I must lodge a formal complaint regarding boots. Why must every woman in Britain clomp about in footwear two sizes too large? Bootmakers of the nation, take heed: narrow twos are in desperate demand! And while we’re at it, let’s address the waistband crisis—nothing shatters domestic tranquility like a hook-and-eye detonation at close range.)

Of course, women will endure these indignities without protest, yet spend hours debating the merits of sarsenet versus cambric. Take two women on a scenic drive—the rolling hills, the distant spires—and their conversation will invariably turn to hemlines and handkerchiefs. Cast them on a desert island, and within a week, they’ll be debating whether seashells or coconuts make superior hat trimmings.

Young men, too, fuss over fashion—though they’d sooner die than admit it to one another. A bit of vanity is healthy, mind you. A lad who doesn’t preen at twenty will be a slovenly wreck by forty. Better a peacock than a sloth.

And while we’re dispensing wisdom: meekness is overrated. Uriah Heep’s father was a fool. Humility annoys people. Life needs a bit of friction—rows are half the fun! Poor Xantippe never stood a chance with that infuriatingly serene Socrates. What’s the use of a husband who won’t even argue? A sensible man knows: marriage thrives on petty squabbles. It’s the little sacrifices—letting her win a fight now and then—that make her sigh over your memory when you’re gone.

And if you *really* want marital bliss, leave your intellect at the door. No wife cares if you can recite *The Iliad* backward—can you fetch a parcel without losing it? Can you hold a baby without dangling it upside-down? *That’s* the stuff of happy unions. Brains are a hindrance in domestic life. The ideal husband is useful, not brilliant.

So dress gaily, quarrel cheerfully, and leave philosophy to the lonely. Life’s too short for solemnity—and too long for sensible shoes.
