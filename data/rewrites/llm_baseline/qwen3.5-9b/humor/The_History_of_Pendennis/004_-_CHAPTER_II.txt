In the early days of the reign of King George III—a monarch later dubbed "the Magnificent"—there resided in a modest West Country town named Clavering a gentleman of the name Pendennis. To those fortunate enough to recall the era, his name was famously painted on a signboard above a tiny shop in Bath. This humble establishment was distinguished by a gilded pestle and mortar mounted above the door, signaling the trade within: apothecary and surgeon. Here, Mr. Pendennis performed duties ranging from attending to the sickbeds of gentlemen and assisting ladies during their most delicate moments, to the humble task of selling brown-paper plasters to a farmer's wife or vending toothbrushes and London perfumes. While the memory of such transactions might fade in a bustling metropolis, the residents of Clavering remembered them vividly.

Yet, beneath the guise of a shopkeeper dealing in pennyworths of salts and fragrant cakes of Windsor soap, stood a man of excellent education and ancient lineage. The Pendennis family boasted a Cornish pedigree tracing back to the Druids, with connections stretching even further into the mists of antiquity. They had intermarried with the Normans at a late stage of their history and held kinship ties to the great houses of Wales and Brittany. Though John Pendennis possessed a university education and the potential for a brilliant academic career, fate intervened. In his second year at Cambridge, his father died insolvent, forcing young "Pen" to abandon his studies for the pestle and apron. He harbored a deep hatred for the trade, entering it solely due to necessity and the intervention of his uncle—a London apothecary of low standing whom his father had married into.

Upon completing his apprenticeship, John severed ties with his coarse-minded relative and established his own practice in Bath. Poverty dogged his steps initially; he struggled merely to maintain his shop's gilt ornaments and provide comfort for his bedridden mother. His fortunes changed dramatically one afternoon when Lady Ribstone, accompanied by an intoxicated Irish chairman, crashed into his doorway. The driver's pole shattered a prized pink bottle in his window, leaving Lady Ribstone screaming. Mr. Pendennis promptly seated her, revived her with cinnamon and sal-volatile, and displayed manners so uncommonly gentle that she appointed him personal physician to herself and her extensive household.

When her son, Master Ribstone, returned from Eton suffering from a fever induced by overeating, Pendennis treated him with such skill and tenderness that he won the enduring favor of the Codlingbury family. From that day forward, prosperity followed. The fashionable circles of Bath embraced him, particularly the ladies. His humble shop transformed into a smart establishment; soon, he deemed the sale of toothbrushes unworthy of his ancient bloodline. Eventually, he closed the shop entirely, retaining only a private surgery staffed by a genteel assistant. Before his mother passed, she watched with joy from her bedroom window as her beloved John stepped into his own close carriage—a single-horse vehicle, true, but adorned with the handsome arms of the Pendennis family. "What would Arthur say now?" she lamented, referring to her younger son, a captain in India who had never visited during his father's years of struggle.

John gently corrected her, asking that he be addressed as "Mr. Parkins" rather than "Johnny" in the presence of the young man. Soon, however, the old lady fell silent forever. Her house grew lonely without her querulous voice. John moved her belongings to a new room, installed a night-bell, and took up residence in her former bedchamber. By this time, he was past forty, living before the wars concluded and before George the Magnificent fully ascended to his legendary status. Yet, what defines a gentleman but his pedigree? Now, John proudly displayed a beautifully framed copy of his ancestry in his drawing-room, flanked by portraits of Codlingbury House and St. Boniface's College—the brief, happy days of his youth. Like Sterne's officer drawing his sword, he produced his pedigree from a trunk, asserting his gentility.

Coinciding with his mother's death, another notable patient, the virtuous Lady Pontypool, also passed away in Bath. She was the daughter of the twelfth Earl of Bareacres and the widow of the second Lord Pontypool. For her final five years, she had been cared for by Miss Helen Thistlewood, a distant relative of the Earls and daughter of a naval officer slain at Copenhagen. While Miss Thistlewood enjoyed comfortable lodging, she endured the infernal tyranny common among women. The visiting doctor, noting the angelic patience with which Helen bore her elder relative's insults, resolved to ask a serious question as they traveled in the funeral coach to Bath Abbey.

Pendennis, twenty years her senior and naturally reserved, approached the matter with the gravity of a man who had perhaps strangled his own youthful passions. At thirty-four, he was a collected gentleman in black stockings with a bald head. As he checked her pulse, holding her cold hand, he asked where she intended to reside now that the Pontypool estates were being dismantled, boxed, and shipped away. Tears filled Helen's eyes; she admitted she had a thousand pounds left by her aunt but no clear plan. Looking into her pale face, Pendennis proposed that she live with him. He was older, yes, but of good birth, sound principles, and improving prospects. Alone in the world, he needed a constant companion whom he would strive to make happy. He delivered this rehearsed speech with earnest sincerity.

Though Helen might have preferred a man less artificial, she recognized his worthiness, his honor, and his devotion to his mother. Blushing deeply, she curtsied low and requested time to consider his generous proposal. They were married during the dull Bath season, which coincided with the height of the season in London. Pendennis secured lodgings in Holles Street, Cavendish Square, and introduced his wife to the theaters, parks, and royal chapel. He presented calling cards to Lord Pontypool, the Earl of Bareacres, and his old patrons, the Ribstones. While Bareacres ignored the gesture, Pontypool called to admire Mrs. Pendennis, sending a proxy via her footman with an invitation to a future concert. Meanwhile, the Ribstones invited the couple to a grand entertainment, a fact of which Mr. Pendennis bragged until his dying day.

Mr. Pendennis's lifelong ambition was to become a true gentleman. Through frugality, luck, and shrewd investment in a copper mine, he amassed enough capital to buy a house and small estate near Clavering. He purchased shares wisely while the mine was prosperous, eventually selling his Bath business to Mr. Parkins for a lump sum and an annuity, retiring completely from the mortar and pestle.

His son, Arthur, was only eight at the time. Having left Bath so young, Arthur barely remembered the surgery or the smell of foul plasters. The father never spoke of the shop, adopting the persona of a landed squire instantly. He traded his black breeches for a bottle-green coat with brass buttons and drab gaiters, attended market sessions, and founded local charities like the Clavering Book Club and the Samaritan Soup Society. He redirected the mail route through Clavering and became an active vestryman. At market, he inspected livestock with a knowing eye, trading with farmers at the Clavering Arms. Once, being called "Doctor" was a source of pride; now, it was a shame, and he insisted on being addressed as "Squire."

His dining room walls were lined with portraits he claimed were Lelys and Vandykes depicting his ancestors—Roger of Agincourt, Arthur of Crécy, Generals of Blenheim and Oudenarde. While his skeptical wife tried to steer conversations away from these genealogical fictions, young Arthur accepted them as absolute truth, regarding these fictional heroes as real figures alongside Robinson Crusoe and the Seven Champions of Christendom.

With an income of roughly eight hundred pounds a year, the Pendennises belonged to the "second-best" society of the county. They hosted lavish moonlight dinners where guests traveled twelve miles to dine with them. Their social circle included Captain Glanders, a perpetual troublemaker involved in disputes with the vicar and schoolmaster, and Mrs. Pybus, who constantly meddled in Helen's charitable projects. Fortunately, their home, Fairoaks, sat a mile outside Clavering, shielding them from the prying eyes of the villagers.

Fairoaks itself was a magnificent freestone palace, built by a reckless ancestor and maintained in ruin by his successors. Its great halls were moldy and gloomy, terrifying young Arthur as a boy. Yet, at sunset, the lawn offered a breathtaking vista: the golden light reflecting off the neighboring Clavering Park, the river Brawl rushing westward, and the purple silhouette of the old abbey tower rising behind the trees. Young Arthur would recite poetic verses about God's creation, casting long blue shadows with his mother, ending in tender embraces. To love and pray defined his mother's life, leading her son to believe that heaven awaited them together.

At home, the atmosphere shifted when John Pendennis entered. His orders were obeyed with the strictness of Persian decrees. Meals were punctual, prayers were recited, and inspections of stables, kennels, and pigstyes happened at precise intervals. After dinner, he napped with *The Globe* on his lap, wearing a yellow bandanna handkerchief sent by his brother, Major Pendennis, from India.
