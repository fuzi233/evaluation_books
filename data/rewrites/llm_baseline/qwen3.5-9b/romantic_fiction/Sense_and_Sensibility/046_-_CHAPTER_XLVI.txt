Marianne's illness, though severe in its nature, had not persisted long enough to hinder her recovery. Armed with youthful vigor, innate strength, and the devoted care of her mother, her convalescence progressed swiftly. Within four days of her mother's arrival, she was strong enough to transfer to Mrs. Palmer's dressing room. There, at Marianne's specific and eager request, Colonel Brandon was invited to pay her a visit. She was impatient to thank him personally for having fetched her mother.

Upon entering the room, the Colonel's reaction was profound. Seeing Marianne's altered appearance and receiving the pale hand she immediately extended to him, his emotion was palpable. Elinor observed that this distress stemmed from something deeper than mere affection or the knowledge that his love was known to others. Through the Colonel's melancholy gaze and shifting countenance as he regarded his sister-in-law, Elinor discerned the resurgence of past miseries. The resemblance between Marianne and the late Miss Grey, previously noted, was now intensified by Marianne's hollow eyes, sickly complexion, and posture of fragile weakness. These physical traits, combined with Marianne's warm expression of deep gratitude, stirred memories of sorrow that weighed heavily upon the Colonel's mind.

Mrs. Dashwood watched the scene with equal vigilance, yet her perspective differed greatly. While she perceived only the simplest, most natural emotions in the Colonel's behavior, she interpreted Marianne's actions and words as signaling the dawn of a sentiment greater than simple gratitude.

A day or two later, as Marianne showed visible signs of strengthening hourly, Mrs. Dashwood, driven by the shared desires of herself and her daughter, proposed moving to Barton Cottage. This decision would dictate the plans of her two friends: Mrs. Jennings could not leave Cleveland while the Dashwoods remained, and consequently, Colonel Brandon was soon persuaded by their united request to settle his residence there as firmly as theirs. In return, at the joint insistence of Mrs. Dashwood and Mrs. Jennings—who possessed an active goodness that made her hospitable to others as readily as to herself—the Colonel agreed to provide his carriage for Mrs. Dashwood's journey, ensuring better comfort for her sick child. Furthermore, he pledged to repay this kindness with a visit to the cottage within a few weeks.

The day of departure finally arrived. Marianne took a particularly long and heartfelt leave of Mrs. Jennings, expressing a gratitude so earnest, respectful, and filled with kind wishes that it seemed born of a secret acknowledgment of her own past neglects. She bid farewell to Colonel Brandon with the warmth of a true friend. With his careful assistance, he helped her into the carriage, ensuring she occupied at least half of the space. Mrs. Dashwood and Elinor followed, leaving the others to converse about the travelers and bask in their own quiet dullness until Mrs. Jennings was summoned to her carriage to enjoy the chatter of her maid regarding the loss of her young companions. Shortly thereafter, the Colonel departed alone toward Delaford.

The Dashwoods spent two days traveling, and Marianne endured the journey on both occasions without significant fatigue. Every effort made by their zealous companions to ensure her comfort was met with her physical ease and a remarkable calmness of spirit. This tranquility was especially gratifying to Elinor. Having witnessed her sister week after week suffering from anguished thoughts she lacked the courage to voice or the fortitude to hide, Elinor now rejoiced in Marianne's apparent composure. Trusting that this state resulted from serious reflection, she believed it would inevitably lead to contentment.

As they neared Barton and entered landscapes where every field and tree held painful memories, Marianne fell silent and thoughtful. Turning her face away from their gaze, she stared intently through the window. Elinor felt neither surprise nor blame; rather, when she saw Marianne cry as she was helped from the carriage, she viewed it merely as a natural emotion, too gentle to warrant anything less than pity, and praiseworthy for its restraint.

Throughout the remainder of her demeanor, Elinor detected a mind awakening to reasonable exertion. Hardly had they entered the sitting-room than Marianne surveyed the surroundings with a look of resolute determination, as if vowing to accustom herself to objects associated with the memory of Willoughby. She spoke little, yet every sentence aimed at cheerfulness. Though a sigh occasionally escaped her, it was invariably followed by a smile.

After dinner, she attempted to play the pianoforte. However, the sheet music resting on the stand was an opera procured by Willoughby, containing their favorite duets and inscribed on the outer leaf with his handwriting. "That will not do," she murmured. Shaking her head, she pushed the music aside. After pressing the keys briefly, she feigned weakness in her fingers and closed the instrument, firmly declaring her intention to practice diligently in the future.

The following morning brought no diminution of these hopeful signs. Strengthened by rest, she appeared and spoke with genuine spirit, looking forward to Margaret's return. She spoke of the reunited family circle, their shared pursuits, and cheerful society as the sole happiness worthy of desire.

"When the weather settles and I have regained my strength," she declared, "we shall take long walks daily. We will stroll to the farm at the edge of the downs to see how the children fare; we will visit Sir John's new plantations at Barton Cross and the Abbeylands; and we will frequently go to the ancient ruins of the Priory, attempting to trace its foundations as far as history tells us they once reached. I know we shall be happy. I know the summer will pass joyously. I intend never to rise later than six o'clock, dividing every moment thereafter between music and reading. I have formulated a plan and am determined to embark on a course of serious study. Our own library is too familiar to offer anything beyond amusement, but there are many valuable works at the Park, and others of modern origin which I know I can borrow from Colonel Brandon. By reading six hours a day, I shall acquire in a year a vast amount of instruction that I currently lack."

Elinor honored the nobility of such a plan, though she smiled to see the same eager imagination that had once led her sister to languid idleness and selfish complaining now working to introduce excess into a scheme of rational employment and virtuous self-discipline. Her smile faded into a sigh, however, when she recalled the unfulfilled promise to Willoughby. She feared communicating this news might unsettle Marianne's mind and destroy this fair prospect of tranquil industry. Resolving to postpone the inevitable confrontation until her sister's health was more secure, Elinor decided to wait. Yet, this resolution proved fleeting.

Marianne had been home for only a few days before the weather cleared sufficiently for an invalid to venture outdoors. Finally, a soft, genial morning broke, tempting both the daughter's wishes and the mother's confidence. Leaning on Elinor's arm, Marianne was permitted to walk as long as she could without fatigue along the lane before the house. Moving at a slow pace, dictated by her recent frailty, the sisters had barely advanced far enough to command a full view of the hill behind the house when Marianne paused. Gazing toward it, she calmly pointed with one hand.

"There, exactly there," she said, indicating a projecting mound. "On that spot I fell; and there I first saw Willoughby."

Her voice faltered on the name, but quickly recovering, she added hesitantly, "I am thankful to find I can look upon the place with so little pain! Shall we ever discuss that subject again, Elinor?"

Elinor gently encouraged her openness. "As for regret," Marianne continued, "I have done with that concerning *him*. I do not wish to recount what my feelings *were*, but what they *are* now. If I could be satisfied on one point—if I could believe he was not always acting a part, not always deceiving me; above all, if I could be assured he was never quite as wicked as my fears imagined, especially after the story of that unfortunate girl—" she stopped abruptly.

Elinor joyfully seized upon her words, answering, "If you could be assured of that, you would find peace."

"Yes. My peace of mind depends doubly on it. Not only is it horrifying to suspect someone, who has treated me as he did, of such designs, but what does it make me appear to myself? In a situation like mine, only a shamelessly unguarded affection could expose me to such consequences."

"How then," Elinor asked, "would you account for his behavior?"

"I would suppose him..." Marianne sighed, "Oh, how gladly would I suppose him... merely fickle. Very, very fickle."

Elinor said nothing. She debated internally whether to begin her narrative immediately or wait until Marianne was stronger. They walked on in silence for a few moments.

"I am not wishing him too much good," Marianne said finally, with a sigh. "May his secret reflections be no more unpleasant than my own. He will suffer enough in them."

"Do you compare your conduct with his?"

"No. I compare it with what it *ought* to have been; I compare it with yours."

"Our situations have borne little resemblance."

"They have borne more than our conduct. Do not, my dearest Elinor, let your kindness defend what your judgment must condemn. My illness has made me think. It has granted me leisure and calmness for serious recollection. Long before I was recovered enough to speak, I was fully capable of reflection.
