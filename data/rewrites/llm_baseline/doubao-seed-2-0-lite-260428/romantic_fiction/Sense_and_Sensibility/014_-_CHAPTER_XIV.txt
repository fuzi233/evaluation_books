Colonel Brandon’s abrupt, unannounced departure from Barton Park had left Mrs. Jennings chattering and puzzling for three full days, her curiosity stoked so bright it rivaled the hearth fire in her parlor. There was no greater delight for the warm, gossiping old widow than parsing the comings and goings of every person in her acquaintance, and she barely paused her speculation long enough to butter her breakfast scones. She was certain no ordinary errand could have pulled the quiet, steady colonel away so fast, and ran through every possible misfortune that might have befallen him one by one, determined she would not miss the truth.
“It must be something terribly sad, that I am sure of,” she’d exclaim over her tea. “I saw it written all over his poor face. The Delaford estate never brought in more than two thousand a year, and his brother left all its accounts a tangled mess—he must have been sent for over money troubles, what else could it be? I’d give every last penny of my pin money to know the truth of it! Or wait—maybe it’s about that Miss Williams he’s always so tight-lipped about? He looked so guilty when I mentioned her the other day, didn’t he? She’s sure to be sick in town, that girl’s always been delicate as a lily. Though, no, he’s far too prudent to still be in debt over the estate… maybe his sister in Avignon has taken a turn for the worse, that would explain him riding off at such a mad dash, no time to even say proper goodbyes. Well, whatever it is, I wish him clear of all his troubles soon, and a sweet, good wife to keep him company into the bargain!”
Every new passing thought shifted her theory, and every new theory felt just as plausible as the last. Elinor, for her part, was genuinely fond of the colonel and did hope he was well, but she could not summon nearly the degree of endless, breathless speculation Mrs. Jennings pressed on her. For one thing, the colonel’s sudden trip hardly struck her as such a shocking, earth-shaking event—but far more of her wonder was occupied elsewhere, fixed on the strange, stubborn silence her sister and Willoughby maintained. They carried themselves so openly, so tenderly to each other, every glance, every lingering hand, every private laugh a declaration of their attachment, yet not a word of an engagement had crossed their lips to Mrs. Dashwood or to her. Elinor could easily guess marriage was not immediately within their power: Sir John had estimated Willoughby’s estate brought in only six or seven hundred a year, and he lived far too freely for that sum, often joking about his own empty pockets. But this total secrecy, hiding a bond that was obvious to every soul in the whole parish, made no sense at all. It was so contrary to the open, unguarded nature both Marianne and Willoughby wore so easily that sometimes a tiny, unspoken doubt crept into Elinor’s chest, and she dared not ask her sister the question burning on her tongue.
Willoughby’s devotion to the whole Dashwood household could not have been more plain. To Marianne, he gave every bit of tender, adoring attention a smitten lover could muster, and to the rest of the family he was as warm and attentive as a beloved son or brother. Barton Cottage had become more of a home to him than Allenham ever was: he spent far more hours of his days there than at his own relative’s estate, and if there was no planned dinner at the Park to draw them away, every morning walk he set out on would end at the cottage threshold, where he would sit at Marianne’s side for the rest of the afternoon while his shaggy, favorite pointer napped contentedly at her feet.
One evening, a full week after Colonel Brandon had left the county, he seemed softer, more open than ever, brimming with affection for every little thing in the small, cozy cottage. When Mrs. Dashwood casually mentioned she had planned to make small improvements to the house come spring, fixing wobbly sills and patching cracked plaster, he protested with such vehemence it made them all laugh.
“What!” he cried. “Improve this dear, perfect cottage? No, I will never consent to it. Not a single stone added to its walls, not an extra inch to its rooms, if you care even a little for my feelings.”
“Do not fret,” Elinor said, smiling. “No such work will ever happen. My mother will never have enough spare money to afford it.”
“Thank heaven for that,” he said, brightening. “May she always be too poor to waste money ruining a place that already holds all the happiness in the world.”
“Thank you, Willoughby,” Mrs. Dashwood laughed. “But I promise you, I would never sacrifice the attachment of you, or any person I love, for any trivial improvement. You may be sure that whatever small sum I have left over in the spring, I would far rather tuck it away unused than change this place in a way that pains you. But are you truly so fond of it that you cannot see a single flaw?”
“I am,” he said, earnest and soft. “To me, it is faultless. More than that, I think this is the only sort of house where true happiness can be found. If I were rich tomorrow, I would tear Combe House down to its foundations, and rebuild it exactly, exactly like this little cottage.”
“Even with its dark, narrow staircase and the kitchen that smokes something terrible whenever we roast a joint?” Elinor teased.
“Even that,” he agreed, eager as ever. “Not a single quirk, not a single convenience or inconvenience altered by even a hair. Then, and only then, could I be as happy at my own home as I have been here at Barton.”
“I have no doubt,” Elinor replied, “that even with better rooms and a wider staircase, you will one day find your own house just as faultless as you find this one.”
“There are circumstances that would make it very dear to me,” he said, his eyes drifting to Marianne. “But this place will always hold a claim on my heart that no other house can ever match.”
Mrs. Dashwood looked over to Marianne, whose luminous dark eyes were fixed unwaveringly on Willoughby, every line of her soft face declaring she understood every word he said perfectly.
“Do you know,” Willoughby added, “this time last year, when I was staying at Allenham, I used to ride past this empty cottage every week and wish so badly that some good, happy family would move in. I admired its little view over the valley so much, I grieved that it stood empty. I never thought then that the very first news Mrs. Smith would tell me when I came back to the country was that Barton Cottage was let! I felt such a rush of joy the moment I heard it, the only explanation can be that some part of me already knew all the happiness I would find here, didn’t it, Marianne?”
He spoke the last line in a low, soft voice only for her to hear, then turned back to the rest of the family. “And now you would spoil that perfect simplicity with your imaginary improvements! This very parlor, where we first met, where we have spent so many lovely hours together—you would turn it into a common entrance hall, when it holds more warmth and comfort than the grandest, largest drawing room in the whole world.”
Mrs. Dashwood promised him again no renovations of the sort would ever be made.
“You are the kindest woman alive,” he said, warmly. “That promise puts my whole heart at ease. Extend it just a little further, and make me perfectly happy: tell me not only that your house will stay just as it is, but that you and Elinor and Marianne will never change, that you will always welcome me here with the same kindness that has made every part of this cottage feel like mine.”
They promised him that easily, and Willoughby spent the rest of the evening glowing with open, unhidden joy and affection. As he pulled on his coat to leave later that night, Mrs. Dashwood called after him, “We shall hope to see you for dinner tomorrow, won’t we? I won’t ask you to come by in the morning—all three of us are walking over to the Park to call on Lady Middleton.”
He smiled, and bowed, and promised he would be back at Barton Cottage by four o’clock sharp.
