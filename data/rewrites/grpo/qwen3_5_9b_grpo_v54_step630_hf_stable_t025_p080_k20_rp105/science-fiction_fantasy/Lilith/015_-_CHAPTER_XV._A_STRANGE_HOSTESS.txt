The moon hung full and bright, a familiar silver coin against the velvet dark, though tonight it wore an expression I had never seen before—a faint, enigmatic smile. As she dipped below the horizon, her lower rim seemed to dissolve into the earth, revealing not the cratered surface I knew, but a perfect, glowing cottage painted directly onto her disc. Through its open door and window, a warm light spilled forth, carrying with it a sudden, undeniable conviction: I was expected there.

In an instant, the moon vanished, swallowed by the encroaching night, and the cottage dissolved into the darkness. The landscape around me was a jagged succession of small ravines, impossible to navigate in the gloom. I resolved to wait for dawn, stretching out in a shallow sandy hollow. I made a supper of the fruits the children had given me at parting and drifted quickly into sleep.

I woke abruptly, staring up at constellations alien to my home world. After a moment of gazing, I sensed a presence seated on the ground a short distance above me. Startled, I realized I was no longer alone. The figure sat between me and the sky, its silhouette stark against the stars. From my low position in the hollow, it loomed larger than any human.

It turned its head, and only then did I see that its back was facing me.

"Will you not come with me?" a voice asked. It was sweet and mellow, unmistakably female.

"I thank you," I replied, "but I am not uncomfortable here. Where would you have me go? I like sleeping in the open air."

"There is no harm in the air," she returned, "but the creatures that roam these wastes at night are not ones a man would wish to have near while he sleeps."

"I have not been disturbed," I said.

"No," she admitted. "I have been sitting by you since you lay down."

"That is very kind of you! How did you know I was here? Why show me such favor?"

"I saw you," she answered, still keeping her back to me, "in the moonlight just as she set. I see poorly in the day, but perfectly at night. The shadow of my house would have hidden you, but both doors were open. I was out on the waste and saw you enter this hollow. You were asleep before I could reach you, and I was unwilling to disturb you. People are frightened if I approach suddenly. They call me the Cat-woman. It is not my name."

I recalled the children's warnings—that she was ugly and scratched. Yet her voice was gentle, tinged with apology. She could not be a malevolent giantess.

"You shall not hear it from me," I answered. "Please tell me what I *may* call you!"

"When you know me, call me by the name that seems to fit," she replied. "That will tell me what sort you are. People rarely give me the right one. It is well when they do."

"I suppose, madam, you live in the cottage I saw in the heart of the moon?"

"I do. I live there alone, except for visitors. It is a poor place, but I do what I can for my guests, and sometimes their sleep is sweet to them."

Her voice settled over me, inducing a strange stillness.

"I will go with you, madam," I said, rising.

She rose instantly and, without glancing back, led the way. I could just barely distinguish her form to follow. She was taller than I, yet not as towering as I had imagined. The fact that she never turned her face toward me piqued my curiosity, though it did not instill fear; her voice rang with such truth. Yet, how could I name someone I could not see? I tried to walk alongside her, but failed. When I quickened my pace, she matched it effortlessly, maintaining a lead. Soon, a flicker of unease took root. Why was she so careful not to be seen? Extraordinary ugliness might account for it—perhaps she feared terrifying me. A horror of inconceivable monstrosity began to assail me. Was I following an unheard-of hideousness through the dark? Almost, I regretted accepting her hospitality.

Neither of us spoke, and the silence grew oppressive. I *had* to break it.

"I want to find my way," I said, "to a place I have heard of, but whose name I have not yet learned. Perhaps you can tell it to me!"

"Describe it, and I will direct you. The stupid Bags know nothing, and the careless little Lovers forget almost everything."

"Where do those live?"

"You have just come from them!"

"I never heard those names before!"

"You would not have heard them. No one knows their own name!"

"Strange!"

"Perhaps so! But hardly anyone anywhere knows their own name! It would make many a fine gentleman stare to hear himself addressed by what is truly his name!"

I fell silent, wondering what my name might be.

"What do you fancy yours is now?" she continued, as if sensing my thought. "But, pardon me, it is a matter of no consequence."

I opened my mouth to answer, only to discover that my name had vanished from my mind entirely. I could not even recall the first letter! This was the second time I had been asked my name and failed to provide it.

"Never mind," she said. "It is not needed. Your real name is written on your forehead, but at present it whirls about so irregularly that no one can read it. I will do my part to steady it. Soon it will slow, and I hope, settle."

This startled me, and I fell quiet.

We left the channels and walked for a long time, yet the cottage remained unseen.

"The Little Ones told me," I said finally, "of a smooth green country, pleasant to the feet!"

"Yes?" she responded.

"They also told me of a girl-giantess who is a queen somewhere. Is that her country?"

"There is a city in that grassy land," she replied, "where a woman is princess. The city is called Bulika. But certainly the princess is not a girl! She is older than this world and came to it from yours—with a terrible history that is not yet over. She is an evil person and prevails greatly with the Prince of the Power of the Air. The people of Bulika were once simple folk, tilling the ground and pasturing sheep. She came among them, and they received her hospitably. She taught them to dig for diamonds and opals and sell them to strangers, making them abandon tillage and pasturage to build a city. One day they found a huge snake and killed it; so enraged was she that she declared herself their princess and became terrible to them. The name of the country at that time was THE LAND OF WATERS, for the dry channels you have crossed were then overflowing with live torrents, and the valley where the Bags and Lovers now grow fruit-trees was a lake receiving much of the flow. But the wicked princess gathered up what she could of the water over the whole country, closed it in an egg, and carried it away. Her lap, however, would not hold more than half; and the instant she departed, the remainder fled underground, leaving the country as dry and dusty as her own heart. Were it not for the waters beneath, every living thing would have perished long ago. For where there is no water, no rain falls; and where no rain falls, no springs rise. Ever since, the princess has lived in Bulika, holding the inhabitants in constant terror and doing her best to prevent their multiplication. Yet they boast and believe themselves prosperous, certainly a self-satisfied people—good at bargaining and buying, good at selling and cheating; holding well together for a common interest, and utterly treacherous where interests clash; proud of their princess and her power, and despising everyone they outwit; never doubting themselves the most honorable of all nations, and each man counting himself superior to any other. The depth of their worthlessness and the height of their vainglory can only be understood by those who have been there to see, who have learned to know these miserable, misgoverned, and self-deceived creatures."

"I thank you, madam. And now, if you please, will you tell me something about the Little Ones—the Lovers? I long heartily to serve them. Who and what are they? And how do they come to be there? Those children are the greatest wonder I have found in this world of wonders."

"In Bulika you may perhaps find light on those matters. There is an ancient poem in the library of the palace, I am told, which no one there can read, but in which it is plainly written that after the Lovers have endured great troubles and learned their own name, they will fill the land and make the giants their slaves."

"By that time they will have grown a little, will they not?" I asked.

"Yes, they will have grown; yet I think too they will not have grown. It is possible to grow and not to grow, to grow less and to grow bigger, both at once—yes, even to grow by means of not growing!"

"Your words are strange, madam!" I rejoined. "But I have heard it said that some words, because they mean more, appear to mean less!"

"That is true, and such words *must* be understood. It were well for the princess of Bulika if she heard what the very silence of the land is shouting in her ears all day long! But she is far too clever to understand anything."

"Then I suppose, when the little Lovers are grown, their land will have water again?"

"Not exactly so: when they are thirsty enough, they will have water, and when they have water, they will grow. To grow, they must have water. And, beneath, it flows still."

"I have heard that water twice," I said, "once when I lay down to wait for the moon—and when I woke the sun was shining! And once when I fell, nearly killed by the bad giant. Both times came the voices of the water, and healed me."

The woman never turned her head, keeping always a little ahead of me, but I heard every word she spoke. Her voice reminded me strangely of the woman in the house of death. Much of what she said I did not understand, and therefore cannot remember. But I forgot that I had ever been afraid of her.

We continued on, crossing a wide tract of sand before reaching the cottage. Its foundation stood in deep sand, but I could see it was built of rock. In character, the cottage resembled the sexton's, though with thicker walls. The door, heavy and strong, opened immediately into a large, bare room with two small windows opposite each other, lacking glass. My hostess walked in through the open door where the moon had shone, went straight to the farthest corner, took a long white cloth from the floor, and wound it about her head and face. Then she closed the other door, trimmed a small horn lantern standing on the hearth, and turned to receive me.

"You are very welcome, Mr. Vane!" she said, using the name I had forgotten. "Your entertainment will be scanty, but as the night is not far spent and the day not yet here, it is better you should be indoors. Here you will be safe, and a little lack is not a great misery."

"I thank you heartily, madam," I replied. "But, seeing you know the name I could not tell you, may I not now know yours?"

"My name is Mara," she answered.

Then I remembered the sexton and the little black cat.

"Some people," she went on, "take me for Lot's wife, lamenting over Sodom; and some think I am Rachel, weeping for her children; but I am neither of those."

"I thank you again, Mara," I said. "May I lie here on your floor till the morning?"

"At the top of that stair," she answered, "you will find a bed—on which some have slept better than they expected, and some have waked all night and slept all the next day. It is not a very soft one, but it is better than the sand—and there are no hyenas sniffing about it!"

The stair, narrow and steep, led straight up from the room to an unceiled, unpartitioned garret with one wide, low dormer window. Close under the sloping roof stood a narrow bed; the sight of it, with its white coverlet, made me shiver, so vividly it recalled the couches in the chamber of death. On the table lay a dry loaf, and beside it a cup of cold water. To me, who had tasted nothing but fruit for months, they were a feast.

"I must leave you in the dark," my hostess called from the bottom of the stair. "This lantern is all the light I have, and there are things to do tonight."

"It is of no consequence, thank you, madam," I returned. "To eat and drink, to lie down and sleep, are things that can be done in the dark."

"Rest in peace," she said.

I ate the loaf, drank every drop of water, and lay down. The bed was hard, the covering thin and scanty, and the night cold. I dreamed that I lay in the chamber of death, between the warrior and the lady with the healing wound.

I woke in the middle of the night, thinking I heard low noises of wild animals.

"Creatures of the desert scenting after me, I suppose!" I said to myself. Knowing I was safe, I would have gone back to sleep. But instantly a rough purring rose to a howl under my window, and I sprang from my bed to see what beast had uttered it.

Before the cottage door, bathed in the full radiance of the moon, a tall woman stood clothed in white, her back to me. She was stooping over a large white animal like a panther, patting and stroking it with one hand while with the other she pointed to the moon halfway up the heavens, then drew a perpendicular line to the horizon.
