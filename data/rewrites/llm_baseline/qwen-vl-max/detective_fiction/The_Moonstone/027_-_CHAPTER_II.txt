Mr. Godfrey arrived precisely when he was announced—no sooner, no later. He followed the servant with a quiet dignity that suggested neither haste nor delay. Not so close as to startle us, nor so distant as to require an awkward pause or an open door left unattended. In his manner, there was a completeness of timing, of presence, that spoke of a man thoroughly at ease in his own skin and in the world around him. This was Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite: polished, composed, and utterly self-assured.

“Go to Miss Verinder,” my aunt instructed the servant, “and tell her Mr. Ablewhite is here.”

We both greeted him with inquiries about his health. We asked together whether he had recovered from the ordeal of the past week. With perfect grace, he answered both of us at once. Lady Verinder received his words; I received his smile—the kind of smile that could disarm even the most skeptical soul.

“What,” he exclaimed, with gentle warmth, “have I done to deserve all this sympathy? My dear aunt! My dear Miss Clack! I have merely been mistaken for someone else. Blindfolded. Strangled. Thrown flat on my back upon a very thin carpet covering a particularly hard floor. Just imagine how much worse it might have been! I might have been murdered. I might have been robbed. What have I lost? Nothing but nervous force—and since the law doesn’t recognize nervous force as property, strictly speaking, I’ve lost nothing at all.”

He paused, then added with a sigh, “If I’d had my way, I would have kept the whole affair to myself. I detest publicity. But Mr. Luker made his injuries public, and mine, as a consequence, have been dragged into the light. Now I am the property of the newspapers—until the gentle reader grows tired of the story. And I confess, I am already quite tired of it myself. May the gentle reader soon feel the same!”

Then, with a sudden shift of tone, he turned to me. “And how is dear Rachel? Still enjoying London’s pleasures? So glad to hear it! Miss Clack, I must beg your indulgence. I am dreadfully behind on my committee work and my dear ladies’ affairs. But I do hope to visit the Mothers’-Small-Clothes next week. Did you make good progress at Monday’s meeting? Was the board hopeful about future prospects? And are we nicely off for trousers?”

His voice, rich and deep, lent an almost musical charm to the practical question. The truth was, we were *too* well supplied with trousers—overwhelmed, in fact. I was about to say so when the door opened again, and in walked Miss Verinder.

She came forward at a pace that was decidedly unladylike—hair disheveled, face flushed, eyes bright with urgency.

“I’m delighted to see you, Godfrey,” she said, addressing him in a tone so casual it might have passed for two young men greeting each other. “I wish you’d brought Mr. Luker with you. You and he—while this excitement lasts—are the two most interesting men in all of London. It’s morbid, I know. Unhealthy. Exactly the sort of thing a well-regulated mind like Miss Clack’s instinctively recoils from. But never mind. Tell me everything about Northumberland Street. I know the papers left out parts.”

Even Mr. Godfrey, for all his composure, showed a flicker of human frailty. I watched, not without unease, as he took Rachel’s hand in both of his, gently placing it against the left side of his waistcoat. It was a gesture that encouraged her boldness, her disregard for propriety, her outright dismissal of me.

“Dearest Rachel,” he said, in the same soft, resonant voice he’d used discussing our committee matters, “the newspapers have told you everything—and they’ve told it far better than I ever could.”

My aunt interjected quietly, “Godfrey thinks we’re making too much of this. He says he doesn’t care to speak of it.”

Rachel looked up sharply. “Why?” she demanded, her eyes flashing.

He met her gaze with a look of indulgent patience—so misplaced, so undeserved, that I felt compelled to intervene.

“Rachel, darling,” I said gently, “true greatness and true courage are always modest.”

She ignored me entirely. “You’re a very good fellow in your way, Godfrey,” she said, still speaking to him as if they were equals, “but I’m certain you’re not great. I don’t believe you possess any extraordinary courage. And I’m firmly convinced—if you ever had modesty—that your lady-worshippers stripped you of it long ago. You have some private reason for not talking about your adventure in Northumberland Street—and I mean to find out what it is.”

“Simple enough,” he replied, still patient. “I’m tired of the subject.”

“Tired?” she said, narrowing her eyes. “My dear Godfrey, I’m going to make a remark.”

“What is it?”

“You live far too much among women. And you’ve picked up two very bad habits. You’ve learned to talk nonsense seriously. And you’ve taken to lying—not out of necessity, but for the sheer pleasure of it. You can’t be honest with your lady-worshippers. But you will be honest with *me*. Come. Sit down. I have questions—straightforward ones. And I expect straightforward answers.”

She actually pulled him across the room to a chair by the window, where the light would fall directly on his face. I winced at the spectacle. I regret reporting such conduct. Yet I am bound—by Mr. Franklin Blake’s cheque, and by my own duty to truth—to record it faithfully.

I glanced at my aunt. She sat motionless, showing no sign of intervention. I had never seen her so still before. Perhaps it was the aftermath of the strain she’d endured in Yorkshire. At her age, with her generous figure and autumnal vitality, such stillness was unsettling.

Meanwhile, Rachel had settled beside him, her attention fixed entirely on him, ignoring both her mother and me as though we were invisible.

“Have the police made any progress?” she asked.

“None whatsoever.”

“It’s certain,” she pressed, “that the three men who attacked you were the same ones who later attacked Mr. Luker?”

“Humanly speaking, there can be no doubt.”

“And not a trace of them has been found?”

“Not a trace.”

“People think,” she continued, “that these men are the three Indians who visited our house in the country.”

“Some people do.”

“Do *you* think so?”

“My dear Rachel, they blindfolded me before I saw their faces. I know nothing. How can I form an opinion?”

Even Mr. Godfrey’s calm began to fray under her relentless questioning. Whether driven by curiosity or fear, I cannot say. But when he attempted to rise after giving his answer, she seized his shoulders and pushed him back into the chair.

“Oh, don’t say this was immodest!” I wanted to cry. “Don’t suggest only guilt could explain such behavior!” But we must not judge. Indeed, indeed, indeed—we must not judge others.

She resumed her interrogation, undeterred.

“I want to know something about Mr. Luker,” she said.

“I am again unfortunate,” he replied. “No one knows less about Mr. Luker than I do.”

“You never saw him before your chance meeting at the bank?”

“Never.”

“Since then?”

“Yes. We’ve been examined together and separately to assist the police.”

“Mr. Luker was robbed of a receipt from his banker—wasn’t he? What was the receipt for?”

“For a valuable gem he’d placed in the bank’s safekeeping.”

“That’s what the papers say. Enough for the general reader—but not enough for me. The receipt must have named the gem?”

“The receipt,” he said, “mentioned nothing of the kind. A valuable gem, belonging to Mr. Luker; deposited by Mr. Luker; sealed with his seal; to be released only on his personal application. That’s all I know.”

She paused. Then looked at her mother and sighed. Back to Godfrey.

“Our private affairs,” she said, “seem to have leaked into the newspapers?”

“I grieve to say, yes.”

“And strangers are trying to link what happened at our house in Yorkshire to what’s happening now in London?”

“The public curiosity, in certain quarters, seems bent on doing just that.”

“And those who claim the three men who attacked you and Mr. Luker are the Indians—they also say the valuable gem…”

She stopped. Her face had grown paler and paler. The contrast between her black hair and her ghostly skin was horrifying. We feared she would faint.

Mr. Godfrey tried again to rise. My aunt begged her to stop. I hurried forward with a bottle of salts—my modest offering of peace.

But none of us could reach her.

“Godfrey, stay,” she insisted. “Mamma, there’s no need to worry. Clack, you’re dying to hear the end—I won’t faint, especially for your sake.”

Those were her exact words—recorded in my diary the moment I returned home.

But let us not judge. My Christian friends, let us not judge.

She turned back to him, her resolve unbroken. With dreadful persistence, she completed her interrupted sentence.

“I spoke of what people are saying. Tell me plainly, Godfrey—do any of them say that Mr. Luker’s gem is… the Moonstone?”

As the name passed her lips, I saw a change come over Mr. Godfrey. His complexion darkened. The easy charm of his manner vanished. A noble anger stirred within him.

“They *do* say it,” he replied. “There are people who accuse Mr. Luker of lying for selfish reasons. He has sworn repeatedly that until this scandal broke, he’d never even heard of the Moonstone. And yet these vile individuals respond—without a shred of evidence—‘He has his reasons for hiding it. We refuse to believe him.’ Shameful! Shameful!”

Rachel stared at him, her expression unreadable. When he finished, she said, “Considering Mr. Luker is only a casual acquaintance, you defend him rather passionately, Godfrey.”

He gave her an answer so sincere, so deeply Christian, that I trembled.

“I hope,” he said, “I take up the cause of every oppressed person with equal passion.”

The tone in which he spoke could have melted stone. But what is stone, compared to the hardness of an unregenerate heart?

She sneered.

I blush to write it—she sneered at him, openly, to his face.

“Keep your noble sentiments for your Ladies’ Committees,” she said. “I’m certain the scandal that has struck Mr. Luker hasn’t spared *you*.”

Even my aunt stirred from her silence.

“My dear Rachel,” she remonstrated, “you have no right to say that!”

“I mean no harm, mamma—I mean good. Wait a moment, and you’ll see.”

She looked at Mr. Godfrey with sudden pity. Then reached out and took his hand.

“I know,” she said, “why you’ve refused to speak of this before us. An unfortunate accident has linked you in people’s minds with Mr. Luker. You’ve told me what they say about *him*. What do they say about *you*?”

At the last moment, Mr. Godfrey tried once more to spare her.

“Don’t ask,” he whispered. “It’s better forgotten.”

“I *will* hear it!” she cried, her voice rising. “Tell her, Godfrey! Nothing can harm her more than your silence!”

Mr. Godfrey’s eyes filled with tears. He cast one final, pleading glance at her—and then spoke the fatal words:

“If you insist, Rachel—scandal says the Moonstone is pledged to Mr. Luker. And that *I* am the one who pawned it.”

She leapt to her feet with a scream. She darted between Mr. Godfrey and my aunt, her eyes wild, her movements frantic. I thought she had gone mad.

“Don’t touch me! Don’t speak to me!” she shrieked, retreating to the corner like a hunted animal. “This is my fault! I must fix it! I sacrificed myself—I had the right to. But to ruin an innocent man! To keep a secret that destroys his life—Oh, God, it’s unbearable! I can’t stand it!”

My aunt rose halfway, then sank back. She called to me, faintly, pointing to a small phial in her workbox.

“Quick,” she whispered. “Six drops. In water. Don’t let Rachel see.”

Under other circumstances, I might have questioned it. But there was no time. I moved quickly. Mr. Godfrey, unaware, helped by speaking soothing words to her.

“Indeed, you exaggerate,” I heard him say. “My reputation is too strong to be shattered by a passing rumor. It will be forgotten in a week. Let us never speak of it again.”

But she was beyond reason. “I must stop this,” she insisted. “Mamma! Hear me! Miss Clack! Hear me! I know who took the Moonstone. I know—” she stamped her foot—“*I know Godfrey Ablewhite is innocent!* Take me to the magistrate, Godfrey! Take me to the magistrate, and I will swear it!”

My aunt gripped my hand. “Stand between us for a minute. Don’t let Rachel see me.”

I noticed a bluish tinge in her cheeks. She saw my alarm.

“The drops will help,” she murmured, closing her eyes.

While this unfolded, I heard Mr. Godfrey pleading softly.

“You must not appear publicly in this,” he said. “*Your* reputation is too pure, too sacred, to be risked.”

“*My* reputation?” she laughed bitterly. “I’m accused too, Godfrey. England’s best detective officer says I stole my own diamond. Ask him—he’ll tell you I pledged the Moonstone to pay my debts!”

She stopped. Ran across the room. Fell to her knees at her mother’s feet.

“Oh mamma! Mamma! Mamma! I must be mad—not to confess now!”

She didn’t notice her mother’s condition. She was up again in an instant, back at Mr. Godfrey.

“I won’t let any innocent man suffer because of me. If you won’t take me to the magistrate, write a declaration of your innocence. I’ll sign it. Do it, Godfrey—or I’ll write to the papers. I’ll shout it in the streets!”

Let us not call this remorse. Let us call it hysteria.

Mr. Godfrey, ever indulgent, complied. He took paper and wrote. She signed it feverishly.

“Show it everywhere,” she said. “Don’t think of me.” She hesitated. “I’m afraid, Godfrey, I haven’t treated you fairly. You’re more unselfish than I believed. Come when you can. I’ll try to repair the wrong.”

She offered him her hand.

Alas, for fallen nature! Alas, for Mr. Godfrey! He not only kissed her hand—he responded with a tenderness that was little short of surrender.

“I’ll come, dearest,” he said, “on condition we never speak of this again.”

Never had I seen our Christian hero in such poor light.

Before anyone could speak, a thunderous knock at the front door startled us.

I looked through the window. There stood the World, the Flesh, and the Devil—represented by a carriage, horses, a powdered footman, and three women dressed with outrageous flair.

Rachel straightened herself. Crossed to her mother.

“They’ve come to take me to the flower-show,” she said. “One word, mamma. Have I distressed you?”

After what had just happened, could such a question be pitied or condemned? I choose mercy. Let us pity it.

The drops had worked. My aunt’s color returned.

“No, no, my dear,” she said. “Go with your friends. Enjoy yourself.”

Her daughter leaned down and kissed her.

I had stepped away from the window. As Rachel approached the door, another transformation had occurred. She was crying.

I looked on, touched by the rare softening of her hardened heart. I felt inclined to offer a few words of comfort.

Alas, my kindness offended.

“What do you mean by pitying me?” she whispered bitterly. “Don’t you see how happy I am? I’m going to the flower-show, Clack. And I’ve got the prettiest bonnet in London.”

She ended it with a mocking kiss—and left the room.

I wish I could describe the compassion I felt for this misguided girl. But I lack the words—just as I lack the money. Let me simply say: my heart bled for her.

Returning to my aunt’s chair, I saw Mr. Godfrey searching quietly around the room. Before I could help, he found what he sought—a box of matches.

He returned, holding the signed declaration in one hand, the matches in the other.

“Dear aunt,” he said, “a little conspiracy. Dear Miss Clack, a pious fraud—even your high moral sense will forgive it. Will you let Rachel believe I accept her sacrifice? And will you bear witness that I destroy this paper before I leave?”

He lit a match. Set fire to the document. Placed it in a dish on the table.

“Any inconvenience I endure,” he said, “is nothing compared to protecting that pure name from the world’s contamination. There! Reduced to harmless ashes. Our dear impulsive Rachel will never know what we’ve done. How do you feel? My precious friends, how do you feel?”

He smiled—radiant, joyful. Offered his hands to my aunt and to me.

I was too overwhelmed by his nobility to speak. I closed my eyes. In a moment of spiritual forgetfulness, I pressed his hand to my lips.

He murmured a soft protest.

Oh, the ecstasy—the pure, unearthly ecstasy!

When I opened my eyes again, I was alone with my aunt. He had gone.

I would have liked to end here. I would have liked to close my narrative with this noble act. But Mr. Blake’s cheque compels me to continue.

The revelations of that Tuesday in Montagu Square were not yet finished.

Finding myself alone with Lady Verinder, I naturally turned to her health—gently touching on her strange reluctance to let Rachel see her indisposition, or the remedy she’d used.

Her reply surprised me.

“Drusilla,” she said (if I haven’t mentioned it before, my name is Drusilla), “you’ve touched—quite innocently, I know—on a very distressing subject.”

I rose immediately. Delicacy left me no choice but to apologize and depart.

She stopped me. Insisted I remain.

“You’ve uncovered a secret,” she said, “confided only to Mrs. Ablewhite and Mr. Bruff. I trust their discretion. And I trust yours. Have you any pressing engagement, Drusilla? Or is your time free this afternoon?”

Needless to say, my time was hers.

“Keep me company,” she said, “for another hour. I have something to tell you—something you’ll regret hearing. And afterward, I shall ask a service of you.”

Again, needless to say, I was eager to help.

“You can wait here,” she continued, “until Mr. Bruff arrives at five. And you can be one of the witnesses when I sign my will.”

Her will.

I thought of the drops. The bluish tinge. A light—prophetic, unearthly—dawned in my mind.

My aunt’s secret was no longer a secret.
