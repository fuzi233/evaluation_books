Lieutenant Sutch, though he retired late, rose with the dawn. He stirred the entire household into motion, packing and unpacking his belongings with frantic energy. The house, which had known thirty years of quiet routine under his roof, now echoed with chaos. Servants darted through hallways, breathless in their search for long-forgotten garments from his old travel kit, but no matter how swiftly they moved, Sutch remained unsatisfied. His agitation was unmistakable—childlike, almost feverish.

It was no wonder. For three decades, he had lived on half-pay, a man adrift from purpose. Now, miraculously, fate had thrust something vital into his hands—something demanding courage, cunning, and resolve. Lieutenant Sutch was to be employed again. And the thought of delay sent tremors through him. He dreaded every moment before departure, fearing some unseen obstacle might snatch this chance away and return him to the silence of inactivity.

"I’ll be ready this afternoon," he declared briskly to Durrance over breakfast. "I’ll catch the night mail to the Continent. We could go up to London together—you’re heading that way to Wiesbaden."

"No," Durrance replied. "I have one more stop to make in England. It didn’t occur to me until last night—your words put it in my mind."

"Ah?" Sutch raised an eyebrow. "And who is it you intend to visit?"

"General Feversham," said Durrance.

Sutch set down his cutlery and stared. "Why on earth would you want to see him?"

"I want to tell him how Harry has redeemed himself—how he’s still doing so. You told me last night you were bound by a promise not to reveal anything about his son’s intentions or success until Harry returned. But I am not bound by such a vow. I believe that pledge is unfair to the general. Nothing could wound him more deeply than knowing his son was a coward. Even if Harry had stolen or killed, the old man would have preferred it. But this—he’d rather believe his son was a thief than a coward. So I’m going to Broad Place to tell him plainly: Harry never was a coward."

Sutch shook his head slowly. "He won’t understand. He’ll thank you, of course. He’ll be glad the family name is cleared. But he’ll never grasp why Harry fell so low in the first place. In truth, he’ll only care because the honour is restored."

"Not so," Durrance countered. "I think the old man loves his son, even if he’d never admit it. I’ve always liked General Feversham."

Sutch had seen little of the general in recent years. He bore a grudge—justified, in his view—for the role Feversham played in Harry’s downfall. Had the general truly understood his son, the white feathers would never have reached Ramelton. Sutch imagined the old man seated stiffly on his terrace at Broad Place, unaware of his own guilt, perhaps even playing the martyr—blaming his son for shaming the lineage immortalized in portraits that lined the hall like silent judges.

Sutch knew he could never speak patiently with Feversham. He doubted any argument would sway the man’s rigid beliefs. And he hadn’t given thought to whether Durrance should deliver this news at all.

"You’re very considerate," Sutch said.

"Only out of self-preservation," Durrance replied. "Selfishness is the natural flaw of the blind. I know that, so I guard against it."

That morning, Durrance traveled by rail from Hampshire into Surrey, arriving at Broad Place in the golden light of late afternoon.

General Feversham was nearing eighty. Though his posture remained as upright as ever—just as it had been when he once introduced young Harry to his Crimean comrades—he had shrunk in stature. His face seemed smaller, drawn tight over bone. Durrance had walked with him on the terrace just two years prior, and even then, blindness had not dimmed his perception of change. The general moved with heavier steps now; a childish tone had crept into his voice.

"You’ve joined the veterans early, Durrance," he said. "I read about it in a paper. I’d have written—if I’d known where."

No hint of suspicion crossed his features. He rang for tea, which was brought into the great hall where ancestral portraits loomed like shadows. He asked after officers in the Sudan, lamented the War Office’s incompetence, and muttered that the nation was slipping into ruin.

"Everything goes to the devil through bad luck or worse management," he snapped. "Even you, Durrance—you’re not the same man who walked with me two years ago."

Feversham had never been known for diplomacy, and solitude had done nothing to improve him. Durrance could have retorted with a *tu quoque*, but he held back.

"But I come on the same business," he said.

Feversham sat straighter. "And I give you the same answer. I have nothing to say about Harry Feversham. I will not discuss him."

His voice was flat, unemotional—as though speaking of a stranger. Not a trace of sorrow tinged the name.

Durrance hesitated. Was affection entirely dead within the general?

"Would it please you to know where Harry has been these past five years? How he lived?"

A pause. Not long—but long enough.

"Not in the least, Colonel Durrance."

The refusal was absolute. Yet Durrance noticed the hesitation.

"And what business he’s pursued?"

"I’m not interested. I don’t wish him to starve. My solicitor tells me he receives his allowance. That’s enough for me."

"I’ll risk your anger, General," Durrance said quietly. "There are times when disobedience is wise. This is one. Of course, you can throw me out. Otherwise, I shall tell you everything."

Feversham laughed—a dry, brittle sound. "Of course, I can’t throw you out. But I warn you—you’re taking unfair advantage of being my guest."

"True," Durrance said calmly—and began his tale: the recovery of the Gordon letters from Berber, his meeting with Harry at Wadi Halfa, Harry’s imprisonment in Omdurman. He carried the story to the present day, ending with Lieutenant Sutch’s departure for Suakin.

Feversham listened without interruption, without shifting in his chair. Durrance couldn’t read his expression—but the fact that he listened at all offered some comfort.

For a time, silence filled the hall. Then, slowly, the general raised a hand to his forehead, shielding his eyes as though seeing were possible. He remained that way, motionless.

When he finally spoke, his hand did not move.

"What I don’t understand," he said slowly, "is why Harry ever resigned his commission. I couldn’t understand it before. Now, after hearing of his bravery, I understand it even less. It’s one of those inexplicable things. They happen. That’s all there is to say. But I’m glad you made me listen, Durrance."

"I came with a purpose," Durrance said. "It’s yours to decide—but I see no reason why Harry shouldn’t return home and reclaim what he lost."

"He cannot regain everything," Feversham said. "It wouldn’t be right. He committed the sin. He must pay. He can’t reclaim his career."

"True," Durrance agreed. "But he can find another. He’s not too old yet. That’s all he loses."

Now, Feversham lowered his hand and shifted in his seat. He looked quickly at Durrance, opened his mouth—then closed it.

"Well," he said, as though the matter were trivial, "if Sutch can manage Harry’s escape from Omdurman, I see no reason why he shouldn’t come home."

Durrance stood.

"Thank you, General. If you could send me to the station, I can catch the six o’clock train."

"But you’ll stay the night!" Feversham insisted.

"It’s impossible. I leave for Wiesbaden early tomorrow."

Feversham rang the bell and ordered a carriage.

"I’d have been glad if you stayed," he said. "I see few people now. To be honest, I don’t much care to see many. One grows old—and becomes a creature of habit."

"But you have your Crimean nights," Durrance said cheerfully.

Feversham shook his head. "None since Harry left. I had no heart for them."

For a fleeting second, the mask slipped. His stern face softened—just enough to reveal the depth of his grief. These five years had been lonely, painful. No one had ever guessed. He had kept his pride intact, refusing to let anyone see him broken. But now, in these quiet words, he laid bare the truth: the memories of war—once cherished—had become unbearable. A story of valor would sting as sharply as one of cowardice. The silence of Broad Place was his punishment.

The carriage wheels rolled over gravel.

"Good-bye," Durrance said, extending his hand.

"By the way," Feversham added, "organizing this escape will cost a great deal. Sutch isn’t wealthy. Who is paying?"

"I am."

Feversham gripped his hand firmly. "It’s my right, of course. I’ll repay you. Let me know the sum."

"Thank you."

He escorted Durrance to the door. A question formed—delicate, personal.

"Didn’t I hear," he said casually, "that you were engaged to Miss Eustace?"

"I think I said Harry would regain all he lost—except his career," Durrance replied.

He stepped into the carriage and drove off.

His work was done. All that remained was waiting in Wiesbaden—and praying that Sutch succeeded.

General Feversham stood on the steps, watching until the carriage vanished among the pines. Then he turned slowly toward the house.

"There is no reason why he should not come back," he murmured.

He looked up at the portraits—the dead Fevershams in their uniforms. They would not be disgraced.

"No reason in the world," he repeated. "And please God, he will come back soon."

The dangers of escaping from Omdurman weighed heavily upon him. He admitted to himself how tired he felt—how old. That night, many times, he sat on the bench once favored by his wife, gazing across the moonlit land toward the Sussex Downs, whispering the same prayer:

"Please God, Harry will come back soon."
