Common courage, much like common talent, is confined to a narrow channel. Step an ordinary man but an inch outside his accustomed bounds, and he falls. Martin's bravery was absolute within the limits of his experience; having weathered countless perils in his rugged life, he faced known threats with Spartan calm, bordering on indifference. Yet, he had never been hunted by a bloodhound. He had never witnessed that beast's unerring instinct thwarted by human guile. Now, a sense of the supernatural, compounded by sheer novelty, shattered his resolve. After mere steps, he leaned heavily upon his bow, his spirit draining away like water through cracked earth. Gerard, whose peril seemed diminished by distance, pressed him to flee.

"What profit is there?" Martin murmured, his voice heavy with sorrow. "If we escape the forest, we shall buy our lives dearly enough; but here, close at hand, I know a spot where we may sell them well."

"Alas, good Martin!" cried Gerard, "do not despair so swiftly! Surely some path remains for escape."

"Oh, Martin!" sobbed Margaret, "what if we separate? Gerard's life alone stands forfeit. Can we not lure the pack onto both of us, leaving him free?"

"Child, you know not the nature of the bloodhound," Martin replied grimly. "It does not track this man or that; it follows the trail of blood. They have taken him to where Ghysbrecht fell, and from the corpse's gore to the killer's hand, this cursed beast will lead them. Let Gerard run through an army or swim the Meuse itself, the hound finds him." Again he rested on his bow, his head sinking low.

Through the trees rang out the hound's mellow cry.

Strange indeed that beauty could be so terrible and deadly. The eye of the boa constrictor, while entrancing its prey, possesses a loveliness that mirrors the ruby crowned with emerald's green light. Yet the deer beholding it loses all motion, trembling in anticipation of death. So, too, did this sweet, mellifluous sound bewilder Martin Wittenhaagen. He stood frozen, confused, and utterly unnerved. Gerard, scarcely better, felt his spirits crushed. Martin's final words had daunted him. Having struck down an elder and spilled his blood, the four-legged avenger of kinship now tracked him. Was not the finger of God pointing this way?

While the men were numbed by dread, the woman's mind remained a whirlwind of action. Her lover was in mortal danger.

"Lend me your knife," she said to Martin. He handed it over.

"But 'tis little use in your hands," he warned.

Then Margaret performed a crafty deed. Stepping behind Gerard, she drew the blade across her forearm, causing it to bleed freely. Stooping, she smeared her hose and shoes with the dark fluid, doing so with such dexterity that neither Gerard nor Martin perceived it. As the blood trickled, she continued to coat her garments, creating a false trail. Then she seized the soldier's arm.

"Be a man!" she commanded, "and let this end. Lead us to some thick cover where numbers will not save our enemies."

"I am going," Martin grumbled sullenly. "Rushing helps naught; we cannot outrun the hound, and the cover lies nearby." Turning left, he led the way, as those do who march to execution.

Soon he brought them to a dense hazel coppice, reminiscent of the one that had aided their escape that morning.

"There," he declared, "though but a furlong wide, it will serve."

"What shall we do?"

"Pass through this and wait on the other side. As they emerge straggling, loose three arrows, smash two skulls, and the rest will fall upon us."

"That is all you have devised?" asked Gerard.

"That is all."

"Then, Martin Wittenhaagen, I take the lead, for you have lost your wits. Come, can you obey a youth like me?"

"Oh, yes, Martin," cried Margaret, "do not deny Gerard! He is wiser than his years."

Martin offered a reluctant assent.

"Do as I do," Gerard ordered. Drawing his massive knife, he hacked at hazel shoots close to the ground, twisting them upright behind him to form a wall among the standing stems. Martin mimicked the act, but with a dogged, hopeless demeanor. As they painfully traversed the majority of the copse, the bloodhound's deep bay grew louder, less melodic, and sterner.

Margaret trembled.

Martin dropped to his knees and listened.

"I hear horses' hooves," he whispered.

"No," corrected Gerard. "I suspect a mule. This cursed Ghysbrecht must still live; no other would pursue me so fiercely."

"Never strike your foe unless to slay him," Martin growled gloomily.

"I shall deliver a heavier blow this time, if Heaven grants me the chance," Gerard vowed.

Finally, they emerged into an open woodland. The trees were vast but widely spaced, offering no avenue for escape. Soon, mingled with the hound's bay, rose a chorus of twenty voices hooping and hallooing.

"The entire village hunts us," Martin observed.

"I care not," Gerard replied calmly. "Listen. I have smoothed the path for the dog, yet roughened it for the men, ensuring we fight them separately. Thus, the hound will gain on the footmen, and the moment he exits the copse, we kill him."

"The hound? There are many!"

"I hear but one."

"Aye! One speaks, the others run silent; but if the leader loses the scent, another takes up the cry. There will be two dogs, at least—devils in canine skin."

"Then we must slay two instead of one. Once they fall, back into the copse immediately and reverse course."

"A sound plan, Gerard," Martin admitted, regaining some heart.

"Hush! The men enter the wood."

Gerard whispered his orders. "Stand by the side of the copse, in the ditch. I shall move but a few yards to that oak and hide behind it. The dogs will follow me. Shoot as many as you can as they exit; I shall finish the rest as they circle the tree."

Martin's eyes flamed. They took their posts.

The hooping and hallooing drew nearer. Soon the rustling of the undergrowth became audible, punctuated periodically by the unerring bark of the bloodhound. It was a nightmare: the branches brushing closer, the inevitable struggle for life approaching minute by minute, the death knell ringing. A trembling hand touched Gerard's shoulder, startling him violently.

"Martin says if we must part, flee to that high ash-tree we entered by," the hand indicated.

"Yes! Yes! But return, for heaven's sake! Do not stay out in the open!"

She retreated toward Martin, but before reaching him, a huge dog burst from the copse, standing erect for a fleeting moment. Margaret cowered, but the beast ignored her; scent guided him, not sight. Lowering his nose briefly, he unleashed an awful yell, springing straight for Gerard's hiding tree. He rolled headlong over, dead as a stone, impaled by an arrow from Martin's bow, which twanged silently beside the copse. Simultaneously, a second hound emerged, sniffing at his fallen comrade. Gerard lunged at him, but before he could swing his cudgel, a flash of white lightning seemed to strike the beast. It collapsed into the dust, mortally wounded yet not dead, howling pitifully.

Gerard lacked the time to dispatch it. The copse rustled dangerously close, seeming to breathe. Pointing wildly at Martin to retreat, Gerard dashed a few yards to the right, crawling cautiously back into the thick brush just as three men burst forth. These had gained considerable ground on their fellows, while the rest trailed at varying intervals. Gerard scrambled backward almost on all fours. Instinct taught Martin and Margaret to mimic his movements. Thus, within mere yards, pursuers and pursued passed one another on diverging tracks.

A shrill cry announced the discovery of the dead and dying hounds. Followed by a babble of voices, swelling as fresh hunters arrived, the scene unfolded. The hunters, prone to squandering time on surprises, wasted precious moments, while the hunted maximized theirs.

"I hear no more hounds," Martin whispered to Margaret, feeling himself return to normalcy.

Yet it was Margaret's turn to tremble and despair.

"Oh, why did we leave Gerard? They will kill him, and I am not near to protect him."

"Nay, nay! Their heads are not on those shoulders. Did you not bid him meet us at the ash-tree?"

"And so I did. Praise God, Martin, for thinking of that. To the ash-tree!"

"Aye! But with silence."

As they neared the copse's edge, hooping and hallooing echoed behind them. The villagers, convinced the fugitives lay within the thicket, beat back into the woods.

"It matters not," Martin whispered to his shaking companion. "Time enough to break clear and slip back unseen by hard running. Ah!"
