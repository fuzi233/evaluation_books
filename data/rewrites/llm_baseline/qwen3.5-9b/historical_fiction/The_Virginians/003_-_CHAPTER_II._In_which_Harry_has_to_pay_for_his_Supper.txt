For a considerable time, Colonel Esmond's grandson stood ringing the bell at Castlewood, his ancestral home, before anyone inside seemed inclined to acknowledge his summons. When the servant finally emerged, he displayed little interest in the news that the visitor was a relation of the family. With the household away, John cared little for their kin; his eagerness was focused solely on returning to his card game with Thomas in the window-seat. Meanwhile, the housekeeper was busy preparing for the arrival of my lord and my lady, expecting them that very evening.

Only through persistent pleading did Harry manage to glimpse my lady's sitting-room and the picture gallery. There, indeed, hung portraits of his grandfather in a periwig and breastplate—the same image he knew from Virginia—and a likeness of his grandmother, Lady Castlewood, dressed in the fashion of Charles II's reign. Her neck was bare, her fair golden hair cascading in ringlets that Harry recalled seeing snow-white in memory. However, the sulky housekeeper soon drove him away, citing the imminent arrival of the family. "There is my lady the Countess, my lord, his brother, the young ladies, and the Baroness, who is to occupy the state bedroom," she announced. Harry, confused, asked who the Baroness was. It was revealed to be Baroness Bernstein, the aunt of the young ladies.

Defeated, Harry wrote his name on a scrap of paper from his pocket-book and left it on a hall table: *Henry Esmond Warrington, of Castlewood, in Virginia, arrived in England yesterday—staying at the Three Castles in the village.* The lackeys, rising from their cards to collect their wages, opened the door. Even Gumbo, the gatekeeper, abandoned his conversation with old Lockwood to take Harry's guinea, seemingly unaware of the significance of the gift. Reflecting on his brief visit to his fathers' home, Harry realized he had seen only the unselfish countenance of little Polly. Walking away, he felt a profound disappointment, struck dumb by the cold reception. He reasoned that if any of them had ridden to his house in Virginia, regardless of the master's presence, he would have been welcomed warmly. Instead, standing before his ancestors' hall, he had to beg for a dish of bacon and eggs at a country alehouse.

After dinner, Harry walked to the bridge and sat upon it, gazing toward the old house as the sun descended behind it. Rooks cawed their way home to the elm trees. His youthful imagination conjured scenes of ancestors: knights and huntsmen crossing the ford, cavaliers of King Charles's era, and my Lord Castlewood riding out with hawk and hound. These reveries were interrupted by a sharp pang of sorrow for his lost brother, the dear friend with whom he had once shared all joys and griefs. Lost in these melancholy thoughts, mixed with the rhythmic clinking of a nearby blacksmith's forge and the evening's ambient noise, Harry was startled by the sudden dash of two young men on horseback across the bridge.

One of them swore loudly, calling Harry a fool and telling him to clear the path. The other, believing he might have jostled the pedestrian, spurred his horse faster, urging his companion onward. Before Harry could recover from his surprise or anger, the pair raced up the hill toward the house. Moments later, two livery servants on horseback followed, scowling at the traveler with a distinctly British "Curse you, who are you?" Finally, a heavy coach-and-six lumbered over the bridge, drawn by four horses and carrying three ladies, two maids, and an armed man seated behind. Three pale, handsome faces looked out at Harry Warrington, ignoring the salute he offered upon recognizing the family arms. The gentleman behind the carriage glared haughtily. Harry felt utterly alone. He considered returning to Captain Franks; the *Rachel* and her tossing cabin seemed far cheerier than the stone bridge where he stood.

The innkeepers at the Three Castles did not recognize the name Warrington. They identified the passengers in the coach as my lady, her stepdaughter Lady Maria, and her daughter Lady Fanny. The young gentleman in the grey frock was Mr. William, and the powdered youth was my lord. It was the latter who had sworn the loudest, and the grey-frocked man who had nearly galloped Harry into the ditch.

Although the landlord of the Three Castles had shown Harry a bedroom, the young man had refused to unpack his trunks, hoping the great house would eventually invite him. Hours passed with no invitation. Forced to open his luggage and dress for bed, Harry waited in a mood of deep melancholy. Just as darkness fell, a second carriage with four horses crossed the bridge. A stout, high-colored lady with striking dark eyes stared intently at Mr. Warrington. This was the Baroness Bernstein, my lord's aunt. Soon, everyone had vanished behind the castle gates, leaving Harry to retire to his room with a cruel sense of neglect and loneliness. Unable to sleep, he was kept awake by the raucous noises, curses, and giggling emanating from the landlady's bar below.

Suddenly, Gumbo's voice was heard outside, protesting, "You cannot go in, sir—my master is asleep!" Yet, a shrill voice filled with oaths cut through the air. Harry recognized it immediately. Someone cursed Gumbo as a "stupid, negro woolly-pate" and shoved him aside, flooding the room with further expletives and ushering in a young gentleman.

"Beg your pardon, Cousin Warrington," cried the young blasphemer, "are you asleep? I beg forgiveness for riding over you on the bridge. I didn't know you... course, I shouldn't have done it—I thought it was a lawyer with a writ. Dressed in black, you know. Gad! I thought it was Nathan coming to nab me." Mr. William laughed incoherently, clearly intoxicated.

"You did me great honor to mistake me for a sheriff's officer, cousin," Harry replied gravely, sitting up in his tall nightcap.

"Gad! I thought it was Nathan and was going to send you souse into the river. But I ask your pardon. You see, I had been drinking at the Bell at Hexton, and the punch is excellent there. Hullo! You, Davis! A bowl of punch; d'you hear?"

"I have had my share for tonight, cousin, and I believe you have too," Harry continued, maintaining his dignified tone.

"You want me to go, Cousin What's-your-name, I see," Mr. William declared with mock seriousness. "You want me to go, and they want me to come, and I didn't want to come. I said I'd see him hanged first—that's exactly what I said. Why should I trouble myself to come down alone in the evening to look after a fellow I don't care a pin for? Exactly what I said. Exactly what Castlewood said. Why the devil should he go down? Castlewood says so, and so does my lady, but the Baroness wants you. It's all the Baroness's doing; if she speaks, it must be done. So you must just get up and come." Mr. Esmond delivered these words with rapid, indistinct speech, darting around the room as he talked. But the young Virginian was furious.

"I tell you what, cousin," Harry cried, "I won't move for the Countess, or the Baroness, or all the cousins in Castlewood combined."

Just then, the landlord entered with the bowl of punch Mr. William had ordered. The young gentleman in bed screamed fiercely at the host to throw the sot out of the room.

"Sot, you little tobacconist! Sot, you Cherokee!" shrieked Mr. William. "Jump out of bed, and I'll drive my sword through your body. Why didn't I do it today when I took you for a bailiff—a confounded pettifogging bum-bailiff!" He continued screeching oaths and incoherencies until the landlord, the drawer, the hostler, and the entire kitchen staff rushed in to drag him away. Afterward, Harry Warrington pulled his covers tight around him in sulky wrath, undoubtedly falling into a deep sleep shortly thereafter.

The following morning, the landlord was far more obsequious upon meeting his young guest, having now learned his name and status. Messengers had arrived from the castle the previous night to retrieve the young gentlemen. Poor Mr. William had returned in a wheelbarrow, apparently not unfamiliar with such a mode of transport. "He never remembers anything about it the next day," the landlord vowed. "He is of a truly kind nature, Mr. William. The men receive crowns and half-crowns from him claiming he beat them while drunk. He's the devil when tipsy, Mr. William, but when sober, he is the very kindest of young gentlemen."

While Harry awaited recognition from his kinsmen outside, events unfolded within Castlewood House. Upon arriving home, the family discovered the note bearing Harry's name. His appearance sparked a domestic council. My Lord Castlewood assumed this was the young man seen on the bridge; since they hadn't drowned him, they must invite him. He suggested sending a servant with a message. Lady Fanny argued it would be more civil if one of the brothers went personally, especially given their rude initial greeting. Lord Castlewood agreed, suggesting his brother William go.
