War lay thick over Europe that year, like woodsmoke that will not quit the curtains. In London, in the rooms where gentlemen warmed themselves by talk and coffee, the very air seemed pricked with names of battles and generals; and every street-crier’s paper, snapped open with impatient fingers, carried fresh tinder for a young man’s temper. The King of Prussia—spoken of as though he were a figure stamped on a medal before the metal had cooled—was winning victories which all the clubs rehearsed with ardour, as if the telling were a kind of marching. Harry Warrington heard these tales until they rang in his ears like drums heard from afar, and the sound made him restless rather than brave; for he was not where the drum was.

So he chafed. He did not go. He wanted to go, and could not. He drifted instead, useless and sore with idleness, through the military coffee-houses where the smell of damp greatcoats and ink and toasted crumbs rose together; where pipes were lit and knocked out; where the scrape of chairs and the clink of spoons against cups made a small, ceaseless clatter—an indoor sort of campaign. He read nothing but the newspaper, as if letters printed yesterday were ration enough for a life. Books sent him to sleep. Novels he declared absurd; and when women spoke with wet eyes of Mr. Richardson, he laughed, hearty, honest, and a little too late, as though the jest had had to travel some distance through him before it could find its way out.

With no work for his broad hands, no calling but to sit, and think, and be ashamed, he grew like a strong horse kept too long in stall—stamping, sulky, starting at every noise. And it was almost lucky that his great energies found no battle; for a man who has been denied the honest outlet of peril will sometimes seek worse sport in safer places.

The Lambert ladies—Mrs. Lambert and her elder daughter Theo—took pity on him with the quiet industry of women who cure by small daily ministrations. They had a way of making their drawing-room feel less like a tribunal and more like a hearth: the rustle of silk, the soft tap of a fan laid down, the faint scent of tea and preserved fruit from the sideboard, the low, considerate murmur with which they received him. There are few gentlenesses like that kind of constancy: the steady hand that lays a warm poultice over a man’s pride without saying it is a wound.

But Miss Hetty Lambert’s kindness, if it existed at all, came in the shape of a whip. Her heart, at least in Harry’s cause, was flinty. She was angry that he was idle. She was angrier still that he seemed to accept his idleness as fate. While all Europe clashed, must he sit? While harvest came and men went out with sickles that flashed like bright steel, must he—who had hands made to grasp—do nothing but drink, stroll, and be pitied?

“Why, how savage the little thing is with him!” Mr. Lambert said once, after Hetty had peppered Harry with her quick shots, each one light as a pinprick and twice as stinging because it was laughed off.

“Her conduct is perfectly abominable!” cried Mrs. Lambert, who, being the gentlest of women, could be the fiercest in defence of gentleness. “She deserves to be whipped and sent to bed.”

Theo, whose colour came and went quickly, said in a low voice, “Perhaps, mother, it is because she likes him better than any of us do; and it is for his sake that Hetty is angry. If I were fond of—of some one—I should like to admire him always—to think everything he did right—and my gentleman better than all the gentlemen in the world.”

“The truth is,” Mrs. Lambert answered, “your father is so much better than all the world, he has spoiled us. Did you ever see any one to compare with him?”

“Very few, indeed,” Theo admitted, blushing.

“Who is so good-tempered?”

“Nobody, mamma.”

“Or so brave?”

“I dare say Mr. Wolfe—or Harry—or Mr. George—are very brave.”

“Or so learned and witty?”

Theo, who could not keep a certain bright seriousness out of her face when George Warrington’s name was spoken, said, “Mr. George seems very learned, and witty too, in his way; and his manners are very fine—you own they are. Madame de Bernstein says they are, and she hath seen the world. Indeed, Mr. George has a lofty way with him, which I don't see in other people; and, in reading books, I find he chooses the fine noble things always, and loves them in spite of all his satire. He is bitter only against mean things. No gentleman hath a more tender heart, I am sure. Yesterday, after he had been speaking so sharply as you said, I looked from the window and saw him stop and treat a whole crowd of little children to apples at the stall at the corner. And the day before, when he brought me the Molière, he gave money to a beggar—and how charmingly, sure, he reads the French! I agree with him about Tartuffe, though ’tis so wonderfully clever, that a mere hypocrite is too mean to be the chief of a great piece. Iago, he said, is near as great a villain; but then he is not the first character—Othello is, with his noble weakness. But what fine ladies and gentlemen Molière represents—so Mr. George thinks—and—oh, I don’t dare to repeat the verses after him.”

“But you know them by heart, my dear?” asked Mrs. Lambert, watching her.

“Oh yes, mamma! I know them by—Nonsense!”

Theo fled, a rose that had suddenly remembered it was being looked at.

After that, any one might have observed a little domestic miracle of timing—so neatly managed that the girls would have died rather than confess it. When George came, Theo appeared as if summoned by the click of his step on the pavement; when Harry knocked, Theo kept to her work upstairs, and Hetty, who had been nowhere in particular, suddenly materialised in the drawing-room. Theo had excellent eyes, and watched the street from the window as though it were a pageant: she could distinguish, even at dusk, the difference between Harry’s fair head and George’s dark; and though strangers forever mistook the brothers for one another, Theo never did. Hetty, for her part, showed no flutter at all when it was the black-haired gentleman. Her pert speeches were saved for the other.

Thus it happened, on a morning when the house held its usual comfortable sounds—the faint grind of a carriage-wheel outside, the creak of a stair, the soft chink of china being set in order—that Harry sat on the couch in an attitude of more than common despondency. He had the look of a man who has eaten nothing but air and finds himself empty. He was listening, not to music, but to London: the distant calls, the rattle, the persistent hum that never quite becomes silence. It was not the clean, living noise of a farm—wind in trees, hens fussing, a pump-handle squeaking, men calling across a field—but the manufactured, unceasing grind of a city; and it fretted him as an ill-tuned instrument frets a musician, though he had not words for it.

Down came Miss Hetty Lambert, as if sent by fate to prod him.

“And pray, sir,” she began, standing before him with her small hands planted like an officer’s, “what makes your honour look so glum this morning?”

“Ah, Hetty,” he said, with a weary attempt at smile, “I have nothing else to do but to look glum. When we were boys I used always to be begging my tutor for a holiday. I would spend it swinging on a gate, or making ducks and drakes over the pond; and those do-nothing days were always the most melancholy. What have I got to do now from morning till night?”

“Breakfast, walk—dinner, walk—tea, supper, I suppose; and a pipe of your Virginia,” Hetty said, tossing her head, as if she could smell that sweet leaf already and despised it because it came too easily.

“I tell you what: when I went back with Charley to the Chartreux t’other night, I had a mind to say to the master, ‘Teach me, sir. Here’s a boy knows a deal more Latin and Greek at thirteen than I do, who am ten years older. I have nothing to do; I might as well go to my books again and see if I can repair my idleness as a boy.’ Why do you laugh, Hetty?”

“I laugh to fancy you at the head of a class, and called up by the master!”

“I shouldn’t be at the head,” Harry said, simply. “George might be, but I am not a bookman. When I was young I neglected myself, and was very idle. We would not let our tutors cane us much at home; but if we had, it might have done me good.”

Hetty drubbed the carpet with her foot and looked at him—at the strong shoulders doing nothing, at the honest face grown slack with regret.

“Upon my word, it might do you good now!” she was almost tempted to say; and instead she asked, with a hard brightness, “What does Tom say about the caning at school? Does his account set you longing for it, pray?”

“His account makes me see I’ve been idle when I ought to have worked,” Harry answered, in the same plain voice that made his self-condemnation sound like a verdict. “And I have no genius for books. And for what am I good? Only to spend my patrimony when I come abroad; to lounge at coffee-houses or race-courses; to gallop behind dogs at home. I am good for nothing, I am.”

“What, such a great, brave, strong fellow as you good for nothing?” Hetty cried. “I would not confess as much to any woman, if I were twice as good for nothing!”

“What am I to do? I ask leave to go into the army, and Madam Esmond does not answer me. ’Tis the only thing I am fit for. I have no money to buy. Having spent all my own, and so much of my brother’s, I cannot and won’t ask for more. If my mother would but send me to the army—if she would but say the word—you know I would jump to go.”

“Eh! A gentleman of spirit does not want a woman to buckle his sword on for him, or to clean his firelock!” Hetty returned, quick as flint. “What was that papa told us of the young gentleman at court yesterday? Sir John Armytage—”

“Sir John Armytage?” Harry said, rousing at the name. “I used to know him at White’s and the club-houses. A fine, noble young gentleman; a great estate in the North.”

“And engaged to be married to a famous beauty too—Miss Howe, my Lord Howe’s sister—but that, I suppose, is not an obstacle to gentlemen?”

“An obstacle to what?”

“An obstacle to glory!” Hetty cried. “I think no woman of spirit would say ‘Stay!’ though she adored her lover ever so much, when his country said ‘Go!’ Sir John has volunteered for the expedition preparing; and being at court yesterday, his Majesty asked him when he would be ready. ‘Tomorrow, please your Majesty,’ replies Sir John; and the King said that was a soldier’s answer. My father himself is longing to go, though he has mamma and all us brats at home. Oh dear, oh dear! Why wasn’t I a man? Both my brothers are for the Church; but as for me, I know I should have made a famous little soldier!”

And she strode about the room with a warrior’s air, her dress swishing like a banner behind her, her small chin lifted, as bold as if she wore a casque. Yet the boldness was all in the carriage and the voice; her slightness betrayed her at every turn, as a sparrow’s bravery does.

Harry watched her with a tenderness that was half amusement and half a pang.

“I think,” he said, “I would hardly like to see a musket on that little shoulder, nor a wound on that pretty face, Hetty.”

“Wounds! Who fears wounds?” she cried. “Muskets? If I could carry one, I would use it. You men fancy we women are good for nothing but to make puddings or stitch samplers.”

The word puddings, spoken with contempt in a house where Mrs. Lambert’s cook took pride in her suet and spice, brought with it, absurdly, the very smell of the kitchen—warm flour, a whiff of citrus peel laid out for candied slices, the rich, animal sweetness of butter melting. Such domestic scents, honest and sustaining, floated up even into the drawing-room and made Hetty’s scorn sound like a child striking at the very thing that feeds it.

“Why wasn’t I a man, I say? George was reading to us yesterday out of Tasso—look, here it is—and I thought the verses applied to me. See! Here’s the book, with the mark in it where we left off.”

“With the mark in it?” Harry repeated dutifully, as though the ribbon were a military order.

“Yes! It is about a woman who is disappointed because—because her brother does not go to war; and she says of herself—

‘Alas! why did not Heaven these members frail
With lively force and vigour strengthen, so
That I this silken gown—’”

“Silken gown?” Harry interrupted, with his plain puzzled face.

“Well, sir, I know ’tis but calimanco; but so it is in the book—

‘—this silken gown and slender veil
Might for a breastplate and a helm forgo;
Then should not heat, nor cold, nor rain, nor hail,
Nor storms that fall, nor blust’ring winds that blow,
Withhold me; but I would, both day and night,
In pitched field or private combat, fight—’

Fight? Yes, that I would! Why are both my brothers to be parsons, I say? One of papa’s children ought to be a soldier!”

Harry laughed softly, looking at her as if she were something fierce in miniature—like a kitten arching its back at a mastiff. He held up a finger.

“Why, I think here’s a finger nigh as big as your arm. How would you stand up before a great strong man? I should like to see a man try and injure you, though; I should just like to see him! You little, delicate, tender creature! Do you suppose any scoundrel would dare do anything unkind to you?”

And at the notion, he began pacing too, fired now not by glory but by protective wrath—his boots thudding on the carpet in a quick martial rhythm, as if his body could find relief in sound if his life could not find it in action. He imagined, with sudden heat, a Frenchman insolent to Hetty; and his hands, made for holding reins and sword-hilts, clenched as if they had found at last something honest to do.

This was what conquered Hetty when her speeches could not. She admired in him that dumb readiness to spring—an unadvertised courage that seemed to live in his blood and not in his boasting. In truth she was no virago at all; her bravery was a bright ribbon she liked to wear, not armour she could endure. She wanted, without knowing it, not to be a soldier, but to have one near her.

Still, her tongue went on. Her darts were her way of keeping power in a world where girls had little else.

“Pray, what is the use of having a strong hand if you only use it to hold a skein of silk for my mother?” she cried, watching him stride. “And what is the good of being ever so strong in a drawing-room? Nobody wants you to throw anybody out of window, Harry. A strong man indeed! I suppose there’s a stronger at Bartholomew Fair. James Wolfe is not a strong man. He seems quite weakly and ill. When he was here last he was coughing the whole time, and as pale as if he had seen a ghost.”

“I never could understand why a man should be frightened at a ghost,” Harry said.

“Pray, have you seen one, sir?” Hetty asked, her eyes bright with mischief.

“No. I thought I did once at home—when we were boys—but it was only Nathan in his night-shirt. I wasn’t frightened when I thought he was a ghost. I believe there’s no such things. Our nurses tell a pack of lies about ’em,” Harry said gravely. “George was a little frightened; but then he’s—” He stopped short.

“Then George is what?” Hetty pressed.

“Different from me, that’s all. Our mother’s a bold woman as ever you saw, but she screams at seeing a mouse—always does—can’t help it. It’s her nature. So perhaps my brother can’t bear ghosts. I don’t mind ’em.”

“George always says you would have made a better soldier than he.”

“So I think I should, if I had been allowed to try. But he can do a thousand things better than me, or anybody else in the world. Why didn’t he let me volunteer on Braddock’s expedition? I might have got knocked on the head; and then I should have been pretty much as useful as I am now; and then I shouldn’t have ruined myself, and brought people to point at me and say I had disgraced the name of Warrington. Why mayn’t I go on this expedition and volunteer like Sir John Armytage? Oh, Hetty! I’m a miserable fellow—that’s what I am.”

He paced faster, as if he could outrun his own words.

“I wish I had never come to Europe,” he groaned.

“What a compliment to us! Thank you, Harry!” Hetty flung back; and then, seeing his face—how his simplicity made his suffering almost childish—she softened in spite of herself. “Are you—are you thinking of going home?”

“And have all Virginia jeering at me! There’s not a gentleman there that wouldn’t—except one, and him my mother doesn’t like. I should be ashamed to go home now. You don’t know my mother, Hetty. I ain’t afraid of most things; but somehow I am of her. What shall I say when she says, ‘Harry, where’s your patrimony?’ ‘Spent, mother.’ ‘What have you done with it?’ ‘Wasted it, mother, and went to prison after.’ ‘Who took you out of prison?’ ‘Brother George, ma’am.’ And now I’m come back having done no good for myself—no profession, no prospects, no nothing—only to look after negroes, and be scolded at home; or to go to sleep at sermons; or to play at cards and drink and fight cocks at the taverns. How can I look the gentlemen of the country in the face? I’m ashamed to go home in this way. I must and will do something. What shall I do, Hetty? Ah! what shall I do?”

“Do?” Hetty repeated, seizing on the word as if it were a handle. “What did Mr. Wolfe do at Louisbourg? Ill as he was, and in love as we knew him to be, he didn’t stop to be nursed by his mother, Harry, or to dawdle with his sweetheart. He went on the King’s service, and hath come back covered with honour. If there is to be another great campaign in America, papa says he is sure of a great command.”

“I wish he would take me with him,” Harry burst out, “and that a ball would knock me on the head and finish me.”

“Life indeed!” Hetty said, with a shrug, as if he had offered her a penny.

“You don’t seem to think that of much value,” Harry said sadly. “No more it is—to anybody. I’m a poor useless fellow. I’m not even free to throw it away as I would like, being under orders here and at home.”

“Orders indeed! Why under orders?” Hetty cried, and now her voice had the sharp crack of a whip. “Aren’t you tall enough and old enough to act for yourself? Must you have George for a master here, and your mother for a schoolmistress at home? If I were a man, I would do something famous before I was two-and-twenty. I would have the world speak of me. I wouldn’t dawdle at apron-strings. I wouldn’t curse my fortune—I’d make it. I vow and declare I would!”

For the first time, her words struck deep enough to draw blood. Harry stopped short; the colour rose hot in his face, not with shame only but with a kind of anger at being handled so.

“No negro on our estate is more a slave than I am, Hetty,” he said, turning full upon her, his voice thickening. “But then, Miss Lambert, we don’t reproach the poor fellow for not being free. That isn’t generous. At least, that isn’t the way I understand honour. Perhaps with women it’s different—or I may be wrong, and have no right to be hurt at a young girl telling me what my faults are. Perhaps my faults are not my faults—only my cursed luck. You’ve been talking ever so long about this gentleman volunteering, and that man winning glory, and cracking up their courage as if I had none of my own. I don’t brag, but I’m not afraid of Mr. Wolfe, nor of Sir John Armytage, nor of anybody else that ever I saw. How can I buy a commission when I’ve spent my last shilling? Or ask my brother for more, who has already halved with me? A gentleman of my rank can’t go common soldier—else, by Jupiter, I would! And if a ball finished me, I suppose Miss Hetty Lambert wouldn’t be very sorry. It isn’t kind, Hetty—I didn’t think it of you.”

“What is it I have said?” Hetty asked quickly, as if her own pride had been struck now. “I have only said Sir John Armytage has volunteered, and Mr. Wolfe has covered himself with honour, and you begin to scold me! How can I help it if Mr. Wolfe is brave and famous? Is that any reason you should be angry, pray?”

“I didn’t say angry,” Harry answered, holding himself stiffly as if to keep from breaking. “I said I was hurt.”

“Oh, indeed! I thought such a little creature as I am couldn’t hurt anybody. I’m sure ’tis mighty complimentary to me to say that a young lady whose arm is no bigger than your little finger can hurt such a great strong man as you!”

“I scarce thought you would try,” Harry said, with a look that went to her more than any scolding. “You see, I’m not used to this kind of welcome in this house.”

At that moment, the door opened, and Mrs. Lambert appeared—soft-footed, as mothers are, with a face ready at once for inquiry and comfort. Behind her came the house’s familiar hush: the distant rattle of teaspoons, the low talk of servants, the steady, homely sounds that stitched a day together.

“What is it, my poor boy?” she asked, seeing Harry’s worn countenance.

“Oh, we have heard the story before, mamma,” Hetty said hastily, stepping between them with an air of careless impatience that was too quick to be quite natural. “Harry is making his old complaint of having nothing to do. And he is quite unhappy; and he is telling us so over and over again—that’s all.”
