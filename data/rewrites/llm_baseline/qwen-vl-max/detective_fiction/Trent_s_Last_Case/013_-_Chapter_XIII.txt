The next two months marked a time in Trent’s life he would never recall without a shiver. He crossed paths with Mrs. Manderson half a dozen times, each encounter sharpening his confusion. Her composure—measured, polite, neither cold nor warm—frustrated him. At the opera, he found her seated beside Mrs. Wallace, a lively woman from his childhood, and was stunned to see her woven into his own social world. She’d returned from Italy and somehow drifted into circles that were natural to him by birth and temperament. “I’ve pitched my tent in your hunting-grounds,” she explained lightly. Several of his friends lived nearby.

He remembered little of what he said that evening—only that he had been awkward, flushed, babbling about his travels in the Baltic provinces, speaking more to Mrs. Wallace than to her. The other woman, once agitated when they first met in the vestibule, now appeared perfectly at ease, chatting amiably about her journey, her London life, mutual acquaintances.

But during the final act, as he sat behind them in the box, all thought slipped away. He saw only the curve of her cheek, the sweep of her dark hair, the line of her shoulder, the way her hand rested on the cushion. The black tresses seemed to stretch into an endless forest—deep, untamed, enchanted—a place where danger and desire entwined. When the performance ended, he left pale and quiet, parting with them formally.

Their next meetings—first at a country house, then elsewhere—were more controlled. He matched her reserve, behaving, he believed, with dignity, though inwardly he was consumed by turmoil: bewilderment, regret, longing. He could not fathom her behavior. It was certain she had read his manuscript, understood the implication of his last question at White Gables. Yet she treated him no differently than anyone else—warmly, frankly, as if nothing had ever happened.

And yet, despite her outward calm, he sensed something had been wounded. On rare occasions when they spoke privately, he felt her edge toward the subject—then watched her deftly redirect it. Fear gave her ingenuity. Two decisions took root in him. First, once he finished a commission binding him to London, he would leave—and stay gone. The strain was unbearable. He no longer sought the truth; he craved confirmation that his judgment had been wrong, that he had misread her tears, that he had accused her unjustly, painted himself a fool. He stopped speculating on Marlowe’s motive for killing Manderson. Mr. Cupples returned to London, but Trent asked him nothing. He now knew he had been right in those words—Trent recalled them sharply, spoken with emphasis—“So long as she considered herself bound to him... no power on earth could have persuaded her.” He dined at her uncle’s imposing, somber home in Bloomsbury and spent most of the evening in conversation with a German archaeologist.

His second resolution: never be alone with her again.

Yet when she wrote, asking him to visit her the following afternoon, he did not hesitate. This was an invitation—not a request. A challenge.

She welcomed him with tea, and for a while she joined his slightly fevered talk about current affairs so naturally that he began to hope she had abandoned whatever plan she’d had to confront him. She seemed careless, laughing easily, her smile recalling ancient words about a Princess of Brunswick: “Her mouth has ten thousand charms that touch the soul.” She led him through her beautifully furnished room, pointing out treasures gathered from countless antique shops, chuckling over her bargains. When he asked if she would play one of his favorite pieces—music he’d heard her perform before—she agreed at once.

She played with flawless technique and deep feeling, stirring emotions he hadn’t known he still carried. “You are a musician born,” he said quietly when the final note faded. “I knew that before I ever heard you.”

“I’ve played since I can remember,” she replied simply, turning to him with a smile. “It’s been a great comfort. When did you first sense music in me? Oh, of course—the opera. But that wouldn’t prove much, would it?”

“No,” he murmured, still caught in the echo of the music. “I think I knew the moment I saw you.” Then, startled by his own words, he froze. For the first time, the past had surfaced.

A silence followed. Mrs. Manderson looked at him, then quickly glanced away. Color rose in her cheeks. She pursed her lips as if preparing to whistle. Then, with a defiant lift of her shoulders—one he recognized well—she rose abruptly from the piano and sat across from him.

“That remark,” she began slowly, staring at the tip of her shoe, “will serve as well as anything to bring us to what I wanted to say. I asked you here today because I couldn’t endure it any longer. Ever since you left me at White Gables, I’ve told myself it didn’t matter what you thought of me in that matter—that you wouldn’t speak to others about it, given what you’d told me about suppressing your manuscript. I asked myself how it could possibly matter. But I always knew it did. It mattered terribly. Because what you thought was not true.” She raised her eyes, meeting his gaze steadily. Trent returned her look, his face utterly still.

“Since I began to know you,” he said, “I’ve ceased to believe it.”

“Thank you,” she whispered, blushing deeply. Then, playing with a glove, she added, “But I want you to know what _was_ true.”

“I didn’t know if I’d ever see you again,” she continued, voice softening. “But I knew that if I did, I had to speak to you about this. I thought it wouldn’t be hard—you seemed understanding. And a married woman isn’t expected to struggle with such things as a young girl might. But we met again, and I discovered it was very difficult indeed. You made it difficult.”

“How?” he asked gently.

“I don’t know,” she said. “But yes—I do. It was because you treated me as if you’d never suspected a thing. I expected you to turn on me with that hard, terrible look you wore when you asked me that last question at White Gables—do you remember? Instead, you were just like any other acquaintance. Just”—she hesitated, spreading her hands—“nice. You know. After the first time at the opera, when I spoke to you, I went home wondering if you’d really recognized me. I thought you might have seen my face but not remembered who I was.”

A quiet laugh escaped Trent, though he said nothing.

She smiled apologetically. “Well, I couldn’t recall whether you’d named me. I thought it might be so. But the next time, at the Iretons’, you did say my name—so I knew. And a dozen times in those days, I almost brought myself to tell you—but never quite. I began to feel you wouldn’t let me. That you’d deflect the subject if I approached it. Wasn’t I right? Tell me, please.” He nodded. “But why?” He remained silent.

“Well,” she said, “I’ll finish what I came to say—and then you’ll tell me why you made it so hard. When I realized you wouldn’t let me speak of it, I became more determined than ever. I suppose you didn’t realize I’d insist—even if you discouraged me. I doubt I could have done it if I’d been guilty, as you thought. You walked into my parlor today, thinking I wouldn’t dare. Well, now you see.”

She had shed all hesitation. She had, as she often said, talked herself into enthusiasm, and in her fervor to clear the misunderstanding that had haunted her, she felt in command of the moment.

“I’m going to tell you the story of the mistake you made,” she continued, as Trent, hands clasped between his knees, stared at her enigmatically. “You’ll have to believe it, Mr. Trent—it’s entirely true, with its confusions, hidden motives, misunderstandings, and perfectly ordinary errors people take for facts without blinking. Please understand: I don’t blame you—not at all—for jumping to the conclusion you did. You knew I was estranged from my husband. You knew what that often means. You guessed, before I even told you, that he had taken offense. I foolishly tried to explain it away. I gave you the reason I’d first given myself—before I saw the truth: that he was disappointed in me because I couldn’t lead society brilliantly. That was true—he was. But I could see you weren’t convinced. You’d already guessed what took me much longer to see—because I knew how irrational it sounded. Yes: my husband was jealous of John Marlowe. You saw it.”

Then, when you revealed you’d seen it, I behaved like a fool. It was a blow—when I thought the humiliation was over, that his delusion had died with him. You practically asked me if Marlowe was my lover, Mr. Trent—I *have* to say it, because I want you to understand why I broke down. You took that for a confession. You thought I was guilty—and perhaps even complicit in the murder. That hurt. But maybe you couldn’t have thought otherwise. I don’t know.”

Trent, who had not moved his eyes from her face, lowered his head. He kept it bowed as she spoke.

“But it was shock and grief that made me break down—the memory of all the pain that mad suspicion had caused me. And when I regained control, you were gone.”

She rose and walked to an escritoire by the window, unlocked a drawer, and pulled out a sealed envelope.

“This is the manuscript you left with me,” she said. “I’ve read it over and over. I’ve always wondered, like everyone else, at your skill in these matters.” A faint, teasing smile touched her lips and vanished. “I thought it was splendid, Mr. Trent—I almost forgot it was my own story, I was so absorbed. And now, holding it, I want to thank you—for your generous, chivalrous choice to sacrifice your triumph rather than endanger a woman’s reputation. If all had been as you supposed, the truth would have emerged when the police investigated the case you handed them. Believe me, I understood exactly what you did—and I never stopped being grateful, even when I felt crushed by your suspicion.”

As she spoke, her voice trembled slightly, her eyes bright. Trent noticed nothing. His head remained bent. He seemed not to hear. She placed the envelope in his open palm resting on his knee. There was a tenderness in the gesture that made him look up.

“Can you—” he began slowly.

She raised her hand. “No, Mr. Trent. Let me finish before you speak. It’s such a relief to have broken the ice at last. I want to end this story while I still feel the triumph of beginning it.” She sank onto the sofa. “I’m telling you something no one else knows. Everyone knew something had come between us—I did everything to hide it. But no one guessed what my husband believed. People who know me don’t think such things. And his idea was so absurdly wrong. Let me tell you what really happened.”

Mr. Marlowe and I had been friendly since he came to us. Despite his intelligence—my husband said he had a sharper mind than any man he knew—I saw him as a boy. I’m older than he is, and he had a gentle lack of ambition that made me feel it more. One day, my husband asked what I liked best about Marlowe. Without thinking, I said, ‘His manners.’ He turned very dark at that. After a pause, he said, ‘Yes, Marlowe is a gentleman; that’s so,’ without looking at me.

Nothing was said again until about a year ago, when I discovered Marlowe had fallen desperately in love with an American girl. To my disgust, he chose the most worthless girl among all we knew. She was wealthy, spoiled by her parents, beautiful, educated, athletic—what they call a woman-athlete—and cared only for her own amusement. She was the most unprincipled flirt I’ve ever known—clever too. Everyone knew it, and Marlowe must have heard it. But she made a fool of him—completely. I don’t know how she did it, but I can imagine. She liked him, of course, but she was clearly playing with him. The whole affair was so ridiculous, I grew furious. One day, I asked him to row me on the lake—at our house by Lake George. We’d never been alone together before. In the boat, I spoke to him kindly. He listened well, but didn’t believe me. He had the gall to say I misunderstood Alice’s nature. When I hinted at his prospects—he had little of his own—he said if she loved him, he could make his way in the world. That may have been true, with his talents and connections—he’s well-connected, popular. But his awakening came soon after.

My husband helped me out of the boat. He joked with Marlowe about something—I remember. Throughout what followed, he never changed his manner toward him. That’s why it took me so long to grasp what he thought. But to me, he was distant, silent—not angry. After dinner, he spoke to me only once. Marlowe was describing a horse he’d bought for his Kentucky farm, and my husband looked at me and said, ‘Marlowe may be a gentleman, but he seldom quits a loser in a horse-trade.’ I was surprised, but at the time—and even the next morning when I found them together—I didn’t understand his meaning.

That morning, Marlowe received a sweet note from the girl announcing her engagement. He looked wretched at breakfast. I thought he was ill. Later, I went to his study and asked what was wrong. He said nothing, just handed me the note and turned to the window. I was glad it was over, but sorry for him. I don’t remember what I said, but I remember placing my hand on his arm as he stared at the garden. Then my husband appeared at the door with papers. He glanced at us, then walked quietly back to his study. I thought he might have heard me comforting Marlowe—and that it was kind of him to slip away. Marlowe didn’t see or hear him. My husband left that morning for the West while I was out. Even then, I didn’t understand. He often left suddenly for business.

It wasn’t until he returned a week later that I grasped the truth. He looked pale, strange. As soon as he saw me, he asked where Marlowe was. The tone of his question told me everything instantly.

I nearly gasped. I was furious. You know, Mr. Trent, I don’t think I’d have minded if anyone thought me capable of leaving my husband openly for another man. I might have done it. But this crude suspicion—of a man he trusted—and the idea of secrecy! It enraged me. Every shred of pride in me tightened till I quivered. I swore then and there I would never show, by word or deed, that I knew he thought such a thing. I would behave exactly as I always had—and I did, until the very end. Though I knew a wall had formed between us—one that could never be crossed—even if he begged forgiveness, I never showed I noticed the change.

And so it went. I could never go through such a time again. My husband remained coldly polite when we were alone—only when necessary. He never mentioned what he suspected, but I felt it, and he knew I did. We were both stubborn. He was friendlier than ever to Marlowe—Heaven knows why. I thought he was planning revenge, but that was just a guess. Certainly Marlowe never knew what was suspected of him. We stayed friends, though we never discussed intimate matters again. I made sure to see him as often as before. Then we came to England, to White Gables—and after that—my husband’s tragic end.”

She gestured with her hand as if closing a chapter. “You know the rest—so much more than any other man,” she added, glancing up at him with a curious expression.

Trent was struck by that look, but it passed quickly. Inside, he was overwhelmed with relief. All color had returned to his face. Long before she finished, he had known the truth of her story—had doubted his own earlier assumptions from the start, built on foundations that now seemed fragile.

“I don’t know how to begin my apologies,” he said. “There are no words to express how ashamed and humiliated I feel, realizing how crude and arrogant my suspicion was. Yes, I suspected you. I almost forgot I’d ever been such a fool. Almost—but not quite. Sometimes, alone, I’ve remembered it and despised it. I’ve tried to imagine the real facts. I’ve tried to excuse myself.”

She interrupted sharply. “Nonsense! Be sensible, Mr. Trent. You’d seen me only twice before coming to me with your solution. If you’re going to talk of folly, it’s folly for a man like you to pretend a woman like me is so transparent—so obviously innocent—that you’d ignore strong evidence against her after seeing her only twice.”

“What do you mean by ‘a man like me’?” he demanded, almost fiercely. “Do you think I lack normal instincts? I don’t say you seem simple or open—what Mr. Calvin Bunner calls a case of open-work. I don’t say a stranger wouldn’t suspect you of wrongdoing if the evidence were strong. But a man who, after knowing you, could link you with the horror I imagined—such a man is a fool. A fool afraid to trust his senses. And as for making it hard for you to speak—yes, you’re right. It was moral cowardice. I knew you wanted to clear this up. I recoiled at the idea of discussing my error. I tried to show you, through my actions, that it was as if it had never happened. I hoped you’d forgive me without words. I can’t forgive myself. And yet—if you could know—” He paused. “Will you accept this as an apology? The coarsest sackcloth, the grittiest ashes. I didn’t mean to get emotional,” he finished lamely.

Mrs. Manderson laughed—a sudden, cascading sound, full of joy. He knew that laugh well—had often tried to amuse her just for the pleasure of hearing it.

“But I love to see you worked up,” she said. “The way you always crash down as soon as you realize you’re airborne is delightful. Oh, we’re both laughing. What a triumphant end to our explanations—after all my dread of facing you. Now it’s over. You know. And we’ll never speak of it again.”

“I hope not,” Trent said, relieved. “If you’re willing to be so generous, I’m not noble enough to demand your lightning bolts. And now, Mrs. Manderson, I should go. Changing the subject after this would be like playing puss-in-the-corner after an earthquake.” He stood.

“You’re right,” she said. “But wait. There’s one more thing—part of the same matter. We should pick up all the pieces now.” She took the envelope from the table. “I want to speak about this.”

His brows furrowed. “So do I, if you do,” he said slowly. “I need to know one thing.”

“Tell me.”
