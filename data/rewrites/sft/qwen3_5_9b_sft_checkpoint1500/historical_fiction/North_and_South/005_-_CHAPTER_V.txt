Margaret proved a patient audience for her mother's myriad schemes to alleviate the hardships of the parish's poorest souls. Yet, beneath her polite attention, each proposal struck a fresh blow to her spirit. By the time the frost descended upon the earth, they would be long departed from Helstone. Old Simon's rheumatism would ache in the biting cold, his failing sight unassisted; no one would bring him warm broth or flannel, nor read to him in the quiet of the evenings, unless it were a stranger, and then the old man would gaze in vain for her familiar face. Mary Domville's frail boy would creep to the door, hoping to catch a glimpse of her passing through the woods, only to find emptiness. These friends would never comprehend why she had abandoned them, and there were countless others whose faces she would never again see. "Father has always poured his stipend entirely into the needs of this community," her mother explained, perhaps unaware that she was inadvertently encroaching on future funds. "Yet the winter ahead promises to be severe, and our poor neighbors require aid."

"Oh, Mamma, let us do everything within our power," Margaret urged, blind to the practical implications, seized instead by the desperate notion that this charity was being rendered for the final time. "We may not remain here long."

"Do you feel unwell, my dearest?" Mrs. Hale asked, her anxiety sharp as she misread Margaret's melancholy as physical sickness. "You appear so pale and weary. It is this soft, damp, unhealthy air."

"No, Mamma, it is not that," Margaret corrected gently, finding solace in the scent of the countryside, a stark contrast to the smog-choked streets of Harley Street. "The air is delicious, filled with the freshest fragrance. But I am truly tired; surely it is nearly time for rest."

"Not far off—it is half-past nine. You had better retire immediately, dear. Ask Dixon for some gruel. I shall check on you once you are in bed. I am afraid you have caught a chill, or perhaps breathed in the miasma from those stagnant ponds—"

"Oh, Mamma," Margaret murmured, offering a faint smile as she kissed her mother's cheek, "I am perfectly well; do not trouble yourself. I am simply exhausted."

Retiring upstairs, Margaret acquiesced to a bowl of gruel to assuage her mother's fears. Lying listlessly in bed, she waited until Mrs. Hale had entered to make final inquiries and bestow a final kiss before locking her door and retiring to her own chamber. The moment the lock clicked shut, the girl sprang from the bed, draping a dressing-gown over her frame. She paced the floor, the rhythmic creak of the wood forcing her to slow her steps, until she collapsed onto the window seat in the recessed bay. Earlier that morning, the gleaming spire of the church tower had promised a sunlit day, but sixteen hours later, she sat shrouded in a grief too heavy for tears, a cold, dull ache draining the vitality from her young heart. The vision of Mr. Henry Lennox's visit, his generous offer, felt like a phantom, severed from reality by the harsh truth: her father's admission of doubt had branded him a schismatic, an outcast, and every consequence spun from that single, blinding fact.

Gazing out at the dark, grey silhouettes of the church towers, square and rigid against the deep, transparent blue of the sky, she felt an infinite distance separating her from the divine. Into those depths she looked, searching forever for a sign, yet finding none. To her, the earth seemed more utterly desolate than if enclosed by an iron dome hiding the Almighty's glory behind it. Those endless spaces of serene stillness mocked her, shutting in the cries of Earth's suffering, allowing them to rise into an infinite splendor only to vanish, lost forever before reaching His throne.

In this somber state, her father entered unnoticed. The moonlight illuminated his daughter in her unusual position. He approached and placed a hand on her shoulder before she realized his presence.

"Margaret, I heard you were awake. I could not resist coming in to pray with you—to recite the Lord's Prayer together. It will benefit us both."

Mr. Hale and Margaret knelt by the window seat; he looked upward, she bowed her head in humble shame. God was present, close around them, attentive to her father's whispered words. Even if her father were a heretic, had not she, in moments of despair mere minutes prior, revealed herself as a skeptic far more profound? She spoke not a word, retreating to bed like a child fleeing from shame after a transgression. If the world presented perplexing puzzles, she wished only to trust and discover the single necessary step for the hour. Yet, the memory of Mr. Lennox haunted her dreams. He climbed a tree of impossible height to retrieve her bonnet, fell, and she struggled in vain to save him, held back by an invisible, powerful hand. Then, the scene shifted; she found herself back in the drawing room at Harley Street, conversing with him as of yore, yet perpetually aware of his death by that terrible fall.

A miserable, unresting night followed, an ill-preparation for the dawn. She awoke with a start, unrefreshed, sensing a reality far more terrible than her feverish visions. The sorrow returned, accompanied by a terrible discord. Where, how far apart, had her father wandered, led by doubts that appeared to her as temptations from the Evil One? She longed to ask, yet dreaded the answer.

The crisp, fine morning brought her mother a peculiar sense of well-being and happiness at breakfast. She chattered on, outlining projects for village kindness, oblivious to her husband's silence and Margaret's monosyllabic responses. Before the meal was cleared, Mr. Hale rose, leaning heavily on the table as if to steady himself.

"I shall not return until evening. I am going to Bracy Common and shall ask Farmer Dobson for provisions. I will be back for tea at seven."

He did not look at either of them, yet Margaret understood the subtext. By seven, the announcement to her mother must be made. Mr. Hale would have delayed it, but Margaret possessed a different constitution. She could not bear the mounting pressure of the day; it was better to confront the worst immediately, lest the day prove too brief to comfort her mother. Standing by the window, contemplating how to begin, she waited for the servants to depart. Her mother ascended the stairs to prepare for school, returning shortly thereafter in a brisker mood than usual.

"Mamma, please accompany me around the garden this morning. Just one turn," Margaret suggested, linking her arm through her mother's waist.

They stepped through the open window. Mrs. Hale spoke, though Margaret could not decipher the words. Her gaze caught a bee entering a deep-bellied flower. *That* would be the signal. As the insect emerged laden with its spoil, she would speak.

"Mamma!" The words burst forth, unbidden and raw. "Papa is leaving Helstone! He is abandoning the Church and relocating to Milton-Northern."

The three hard facts hung suspended in the air.

"What compels you to say this?" Mrs. Hale asked, her voice a mixture of surprise and disbelief. "Who has been spreading such nonsense?"

"Papa himself," Margaret replied, wishing to offer gentleness but lacking the words. They approached a garden bench. Mrs. Hale sat down, tears instantly springing to her eyes.

"I do not understand," she whispered. "Either you have committed a grave error, or I fail to comprehend you."

"No, Mamma, I have made no mistake," Margaret insisted. "Papa has written to the Bishop, stating that his doubts render him unable to conscientiously serve as a priest of the Church of England. Consequently, he must relinquish Helstone. He has also consulted Mr. Bell—Frederick's godfather—and it is arranged that we relocate to Milton-Northern." Mrs. Hale lifted her eyes, the shadow on her countenance confirming she, at least, believed the truth.

"I cannot believe it," Mrs. Hale murmured. "He would surely have told me before reaching this point."

A sudden realization struck Margaret: her mother ought to have been informed sooner. Whatever her faults of discontent, it was a grievous error for her father to leave her to learn of his theological crisis and impending exile from a more informed child. Sitting beside her mother, she cradled the older woman's head against her breast, pressing her soft cheeks tenderly against her face.

"Darling, darling Mamma! We feared causing you pain. Papa felt the burden acutely. You know you are not robust, and the suspense must have been dreadful."

"When did he tell you, Margaret?"

"Only yesterday," Margaret replied, detecting a flicker of jealousy in the question. "Poor Papa!" She attempted to steer her mother's thoughts toward compassion for her father's struggle. Mrs. Hale raised her head.

"What does he mean by 'doubts'?" she asked. "Does he not mean that he believes differently—that he knows better than the Church?"
