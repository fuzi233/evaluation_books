Our poor widow—who had been turned into a sort of saintly house-ghost long before she had any official right to the character—had ordered, with Martha of Fairoaks for minister and laughing heresy-monger, a little feast in honour of Major Pendennis’s arrival. Martha, who could roast a joint as if she were settling a lawsuit, and who considered German domestic economy a kind of Protestant witchcraft, had marched through the simple household with that air of a woman who knows where the nutmeg is kept and therefore fears no man.

The table was laid with modest bravery: a fowl browned to the exact shade of respectable hypocrisy, potatoes that broke open with a soft steam-sigh, and a jug of ale that sang—if you listened closely—more faithfully than many a hired violin. The house itself had its own music: the tick of the clock in the passage, the faint crackle of coals, the wind nosing at the panes, and, under all, the country silence—so different from London’s iron coughing and cart-rattle, where even a sparrow must shout to be heard. If peace were a broth, Fairoaks had been simmering it all day.

Only the Major and his two younger friends sat down to it; for Helen sent word that she was too unwell to dine at their table, and Laura stayed with her. The Major did nearly all the eating with his tongue, and, being a man who could carve conversation as neatly as he carved poultry, did not perceive—or did not choose to perceive—the gloom that sat between Pen and Warrington like an extra guest who had not been invited but would not go away.

It was evening before Helen and Laura came into the sitting-room. Helen entered leaning upon Laura, keeping her back to the waning light, so that Arthur could not at first see how pale and worn she was. Her step had that careful, floating uncertainty which belongs to people who are not quite sure whether they are walking on a floor or on a prayer. She went straight to Pen—whom she had not seen during the day—put her arms about his shoulders, and kissed him with a tenderness that was all the more piercing because it was almost wordless. Laura, as if she had handed over a fragile dish, withdrew to the other side of the room.

Pen felt, rather than saw, how his mother trembled. Her hand, when she lifted it to his forehead, was clammy and cold, like a cloth dipped in water and wrung out too soon. She clung to him piteously. And this very spectacle of misery—Heaven help the young, who are so often cruelest when they are most wounded—only sharpened something sour and fiery in him. He scarcely returned her kiss. The look with which he met the appeal of her eyes was hard.

“She persecutes me,” he thought, “and now she comes to me with the air of a martyr.”

“You look very ill, my child,” she said. “I don’t like to see you look in that way.” And she tottered to the sofa, still holding one of his passive hands in her thin, cold fingers, as a drowning creature holds a plank.

“I have had much to annoy me, mother,” Pen said, his breast throbbing.

At the sound of his voice, Helen’s heart began to beat with such terror that she sat almost dead and speechless. Warrington, Laura, and the Major all remained breathless; each knew by the stillness—by that hush that has its own terrible acoustics—that the storm was about to break.

“I have had letters from London,” Arthur continued; “and one that has given me more pain than I ever had in my life. It tells me that former letters of mine have been intercepted and purloined away from me;—that—that a young creature who has shown the greatest love and care for me, has been most cruelly used by—by you, mother.”

“For God’s sake stop,” cried Warrington. “She’s ill—don’t you see she is ill?”

“Let him go on,” said the widow faintly, and would not look away from her son’s face, as though truth—even when it is a knife—were better than ignorance.

“Let him go on and kill her,” Laura cried, springing to Helen’s side. “Speak on, sir, and see her die.”

“It is you who are cruel,” Pen broke out, more savage because his heart—soft at the core—revolted at the injustice of the suffering he seemed to inflict. “It is you who are cruel, who attribute all this pain to me: it is you who are cruel with your wicked reproaches, your wicked doubts of me, your wicked persecutions of those who love me,—yes, those who love me, and who brave everything for me, and whom you despise and trample upon because they are of lower degree than you. Shall I tell you what I will do,—what I am resolved to do, now that I know what your conduct has been?—I will go back to this poor girl whom you turned out of my doors, and ask her to come back and share my home with me. I’ll defy the pride which persecutes her, and the pitiless suspicion which insults her and me.”

“Do you mean, Pen, that you—” Helen began, with eager eyes and outstretched hands, as if she would catch at the sentence and pull it into a different meaning; but Laura, like a nurse stopping a patient from rising, whispered fiercely, “Silence—hush, dear mother,” and Helen hushed. Savagely as Pen spoke, she was only too eager to hear what more he would say. “Go on, Arthur—go on, Arthur,” she murmured, almost swooning as she spoke.

“By Gad, I say he shan’t go on, or I won’t hear him, by Gad,” the Major thundered, trembling with wrath. “If you choose, sir, after all we’ve done for you—after all I’ve done for you myself—to insult your mother and disgrace your name by allying yourself with a low-born kitchen-girl, go and do it, by Gad,—but let us, ma’am, have no more to do with him. I wash my hands of you, sir—I wash my hands of you. I’m an old fellow—I ain’t long for this world. I come of as ancient and honourable a family as any in England, by Gad, and I did hope, before I went off the hooks, by Gad, that the fellow that I’d liked, and brought up, and nursed through life, by Jove, would do something to show me that our name—yes, the name of Pendennis, by Gad, was left undishonoured behind us; but if he won’t, dammy, I say, amen. By G—, both my father and my brother Jack were the proudest men in England, and I never would have thought that there would come this disgrace to my name,—never—and—and I’m ashamed that it’s Arthur Pendennis.”

Here the old fellow’s voice broke into a sob—rough as a rasp on mahogany—yet more moving for its very awkwardness; it was the second time Arthur had drawn tears from those wrinkled lids.

That sound—like a cracked bell in the dusk—stopped Pen’s anger as if a hand had been laid upon his shoulder. He ceased pacing. Laura remained at Helen’s sofa. Warrington, who had watched like a man standing by a kitchen fire that threatens to flare up into the curtains, spoke at last; and his deep voice, tremulous in the twilight, carried an emotion that silenced the room more effectually than the Major’s oaths.

“Will you let me tell you something about myself, my kind friends?” he said. “You have been so good to me, ma’am—so kind to me, Laura—I hope I may call you so sometimes—my dear Pen and I have been such friends that I have long wanted to tell you my story, such as it is, and would have told it earlier but that it is a sad one and contains another’s secret. However, it may do good for Arthur to know it—it is right that every one here should. It will divert you from thinking about a subject which, out of a fatal misconception, has caused a great deal of pain to all of you. May I please tell you, Mrs. Pendennis?”

“Pray speak,” was all Helen said; and indeed she scarcely heeded him at first, her mind clutching at Pen’s words with a terrified hope that his threatened act might mean what she wished it to mean.

Warrington poured himself a bumper of wine—its rich, dark scent rising like a memory of better hours—and drank it off, as if fortifying himself for a confession.

“You all of you know how you see me,” he began, “a man without a desire to make an advance in the world: careless about reputation; living in a garret and from hand to mouth, though I have friends and a name, and, I daresay, capabilities of my own that would serve me if I had a mind. But mind I have none. I shall die in that garret most likely, and alone. I nailed myself to that doom in early life.

“Shall I tell you what it was that interested me about Arthur years ago, and made me inclined towards him when first I saw him? The men from our college at Oxbridge brought up accounts of that early affair with the Chatteris actress—about whom Pen has talked to me since—and who, but for the Major’s generalship, might have been your daughter-in-law, ma’am. I can’t see Pen in the dark, but he blushes, I’m sure; and I dare say Miss Bell does; and my friend Major Pendennis, I dare say, laughs as he ought to do—for he won. What would have been Arthur’s lot now had he been tied at nineteen to an illiterate woman older than himself, with no qualities in common between them to make one a companion for the other—no equality, no confidence, and no love speedily? What could he have been but most miserable? And when he spoke just now and threatened a similar union, be sure it was but a threat occasioned by anger.”

He paused, and in that pause the room’s small sounds returned: coal settling in the grate; the clock continuing its indifferent work; outside, a bird’s late note—thin and solitary—cutting through the domestic hush like a needle through cloth.

“You must give me leave to say, ma’am,” he went on, “that the anger was very natural on his part. For after a generous and manly conduct—let me say, who know the circumstances well—most generous and manly and self-denying (which is rare with him), he has met from some friends of his with a most unkind suspicion, and has had to complain of the unfair treatment of another innocent person, towards whom he and you all are under much obligation.”

Helen tried to rise; Warrington, seeing it, asked gently, “Do I tire you, ma’am?”

“Oh no—go on—go on,” she said, with a sudden brightness, delighted, and he continued.

“I liked him, you see, because of that early history of his… and because I like a man—if you will pardon me for saying so, Miss Laura—who shows that he can have a great unreasonable attachment for a woman. That was why we became friends—and are all friends here—for always, aren’t we?” he added in a lower voice, leaning toward her, “and Pen has been a great comfort and companion to a lonely and unfortunate man.

“I am not complaining of my lot. Up in my garret, where you left the flowers, and with my old books and my pipe for a wife, I am pretty contented, and only occasionally envy other men whose careers are more brilliant, or who can solace their ill fortune by what Fate and my own fault have deprived me of—the affection of a woman or a child.”

A sigh came from somewhere near him in the dark; a hand moved out toward Warrington as if drawn by instinctive human kindness—and was withdrawn at once. For our excellent female world, trained to blush at its own natural heart, will often snatch back its tenderness as though it had accidentally shown an ankle. Modesty, having slapped friendship on the knuckles, retreated again, and Warrington resumed.

“My fate is such as I made it, and not lucky for me or for others involved in it.

“I, too, had an adventure before I went to college; and there was no one to save me as Major Pendennis saved Pen. Pardon me, Miss Laura, if I tell this story before you. It is as well that you all should hear my confession. Before I went to college, as a boy of eighteen, I was at a private tutor’s, and there, like Arthur, I became attached—or fancied I was attached—to a woman of a much lower degree and a greater age than my own. You shrink from me—”

“No, I don’t,” Laura said; and this time the hand went out resolutely and laid itself in Warrington’s, as one lays a steadying palm on a man who is about to carry a hot pan.

“She was a yeoman’s daughter in the neighbourhood,” Warrington said, his voice faltering. “I fancied—what all young men fancy. Her parents knew who my father was, and encouraged me with all sorts of coarse artifices and scoundrel flatteries about their house. To do her justice, I own she never cared for me, but was forced into what happened by the threats and compulsion of her family. Would to God that I had not been deceived: but in these matters we are deceived because we wish to be so, and I thought I loved that poor woman.

“What could come of such a marriage? I found, before long, that I was married to a boor. She could not comprehend one subject that interested me. Her dulness palled upon me till I grew to loathe it. And after some time of a wretched, furtive union—I must tell you all—I found letters somewhere (and such letters they were!) which showed me that her heart, such as it was, had never been mine, but had always belonged to a person of her own degree.

“At my father’s death, I paid what debts I had contracted at college, and settled every shilling that remained to me in an annuity upon—upon those who bore my name, on condition that they should hide themselves away, and not assume it. They have kept that condition, as they would break it, for more money. If I had earned fame or reputation, that woman would have come to claim it: if I had made a name for myself, those who had no right to it would have borne it; and I entered life at twenty, God help me—hopeless and ruined beyond remission. I was the boyish victim of vulgar cheats; and perhaps it is only of late I have found out how hard—ah, how hard—it is to forgive them.”

He drew breath, as if he had come up from under water.

“I told you the moral before, Pen; and now I have told you the fable. Beware how you marry out of your degree. I was made for a better lot than this, I think: but God has awarded me this one—and so, you see, it is for me to look on and see others successful and others happy, with a heart that shall be as little bitter as possible.”

“By Gad, sir,” cried the Major, suddenly in high good-humour, as if glad to find himself again on ground where he could be jocular without bleeding, “I intended you to marry Miss Laura here.”

“And, by Gad, Master Shallow, I owe you a thousand pound,” Warrington returned.

“How d’ye mean a thousand? it was only a pony, sir,” replied the Major simply, at which Warrington laughed; and even in that dark room laughter sounded like a lamp being lit.

Helen, meanwhile, was so delighted—so seized by the relief of discovering that her terrible suspicion had been wrong—that she started up and cried, “God bless you—God for ever bless you, Mr. Warrington,” kissed both his hands, and ran to Pen and fell into his arms.

“Yes, dearest mother,” he said, holding her, and in a noble rush of tenderness—so late, so needed—embraced and forgave her. “I am innocent, and my dear, dear mother has done me a wrong.”

“Oh yes, my child, I have wronged you—thank God, I have wronged you!” Helen whispered. “Come away, Arthur—not here—I want to ask my child to forgive me—and—and my God, to forgive me; and to bless you, and love you, my son.”

He led her, tottering, into her room and closed the door, while the three spectators—Laura with tears, Warrington with a quiet, stricken look, and the Major with his face turned away as if it had suddenly begun to rain indoors—stood in pleased silence at the reconciliation.

Ever after, ever after, Arthur remembered the accents of that voice faltering sweetly in his ear; the sacred eyes beaming with affection unutterable; the quiver of the fond lips smiling mournfully. In his best moments, and in his trials, and in success or in sorrow, that mother’s face would return to him like a light remembered—not a light of noise and show, but of pity and purity—as he saw it that night, when she yet lingered with him, and seemed, ere she quite left him, an angel transfigured and glorified with love.

The moon had risen; Arthur recollected afterwards how it struck along the floor and found his mother’s sweet pale face, as if Heaven had sent a last lamp into the room. Their talk—his, chiefly, for she scarcely could speak—was more tender and confidential than it had been for years. He became again the frank boy of her early days. He told her the story that had caused her such pain: his struggle to fly from temptation; his gratitude that he had overcome it. He never would do the girl wrong—never—or wound his own honour, or his mother’s pure heart. That threat, to return, had been uttered in a moment of exasperation; he repented it. He never would see Fanny Bolton again.

But Helen—so quickly humbled, so quickly loving—said yes, he should. It was she who had been proud and culpable. She wished to give Fanny something. She begged her dear boy’s pardon for opening the letter. She would write to the girl—if—if she had time. Was it not natural, she asked, that she should love her Arthur? And again she kissed him and blessed him.

As they were talking, the clock struck nine. Helen reminded him how, when he was a little boy, she used to go up to his bedroom at that hour and hear him say Our Father. And once more—oh, once more—the young man fell down at his mother’s knees and sobbed out the prayer which has gone, in so many dialects and broken voices, through so many centuries; each mouth shaping the same words, yet no two hearts tasting them alike. As he spoke the last petition, Helen’s head sank down upon his, and her arms closed round him, and together they repeated “for ever and ever” and “Amen.”

A little time after—perhaps a quarter of an hour—Laura heard Arthur’s voice call from within, “Laura! Laura!” She rushed in instantly and found him still on his knees, holding his mother’s hand. Helen’s head had sunk back; her face was quite pale in the moonlit room. Pen looked round, scared with a ghastly terror.

“Help, Laura, help!” he cried. “She’s fainted—she’s—”

Laura screamed and fell by Helen’s side. The shriek brought Warrington and Major Pendennis and the servants running. The sainted woman was dead. The last emotion of her soul had been joy—joy to be henceforth unchequered and eternal. The tender heart beat no more; it was to have no more pangs, no more doubts, no more griefs. Its last throb was love; and her last breath a benediction.

The melancholy party bent their way speedily homewards. Helen was laid by her husband’s side at Clavering, in the old church where she had prayed so often. For a while Laura went to stay with Dr. Portman, who read the service over his dear departed sister amidst his own sobs and those of the little congregation gathered round her tomb.

There were not many who cared for her, or spoke of her when she was gone. People had known of her scarcely more than they know of a nun in a cloister. A few words ran among the cottagers whom her bounty had fed as quietly as a daily loaf; a little talk moved from house to house at Clavering—one saying she died of a complaint in the heart; another wondering what property she left; a third debating whether Arthur would let Fairoaks or live in it, and expecting he would not be long getting through his money. By the next market-day the village noise—always hungry, always clattering—had rolled on to fresher gossip, and her name, like a soft word spoken in a fading dialect, was in danger of vanishing from common speech.

But if the world forgets, the few remember. The great blank she left was in Laura’s heart, to whom her love had been everything. “I am glad that she gave me her blessing before she went away,” Warrington said to Pen. And Arthur—humbled, astonished at so much affection—hardly dared ask Heaven to make him worthy of it, though he felt as if a saint were interceding for him.

All Helen’s affairs were found in perfect order, her little property ready for transmission to her son, in trust for whom she held it. Papers in her desk showed she had long been aware of her complaint—one of the heart—and knew it would suddenly remove her; and there was a prayer in her handwriting asking that her end might be, as it was, in the arms of her son.

Laura and Arthur talked over her sayings. Laura remembered them with a devotion that was like keeping a family recipe safe when the language it was written in has begun to die; Arthur, to his shame, thought how much greater Helen’s love had been for Laura than his own attentions ever were. He referred himself entirely to Laura to know what Helen would have wished: which poor persons she would have liked to relieve, what remembrances to send.

They packed up the vase which Helen, in her gratitude, had destined to Dr. Goodenough, and duly sent it to that kind physician; a silver coffee-pot she used was sent to Portman; a diamond ring, with her hair, was given, with affectionate greeting, to Warrington.

It was a hard day for Laura when she went over to Fairoaks first, to the little room she had occupied and which was hers no more; and then to the widow’s own blank chamber where those two had passed so many beloved hours. There were the clothes in the wardrobe; the cushion where she prayed; the chair at the toilette; the glass that would no more reflect that dear sad face. The house had changed its music: the silence was no longer restful, but hollow—like a kitchen after the fire has been raked out, when you can still smell yesterday’s broth yet cannot warm your hands.

After she had been there a while Pen knocked, led her downstairs to the parlour, made her drink a little wine, and said, “God bless you,” as she touched the glass. “Nothing shall ever be changed in your room,” he said. “It is always your room—it is always my sister’s room. Shall it not be so, Laura?” And Laura said, “Yes.”

Among Helen’s papers was found a packet marked, in her own hand, “Letters from Laura’s father,” and Arthur gave it to her. They were the old letters that had passed between the cousins in those early days before either married. The ink was faded; the tears dried out; the grief healed now whose bitterness they recorded; the friends doubtless united whose parting on earth had cost such cruel pangs. Laura learned, for the first time fully, what tie had bound her so tenderly to Helen: how faithfully her more-than-mother had cherished her father’s memory, how truly she had loved him, how meekly resigned him.

There was one legacy of Helen’s that Laura could not know of: her wish to make some present to Fanny Bolton. Pen remembered it, and wrote to Fanny, putting his letter under an envelope to Mr. Bows and requesting that gentleman to read it before he delivered it—an arrangement by which propriety, like a sour but vigilant housekeeper, insisted on tasting the message before the girl received it.

“Dear Fanny,” Pen wrote, “I have to acknowledge two letters from you, one of which was delayed in my illness”—(he found the first in his mother’s desk after her death, and it gave him a strange pang to read it then)—“and to thank you, my kind nurse and friend, who watched me so tenderly during my fever. And I have to tell you that the last words of my dear mother, who is no more, were words of goodwill and gratitude to you for nursing me: and she said she would have written to you, had she had time—that she would like to ask your pardon if she had harshly treated you—and that she would beg you to show your forgiveness by accepting some token of friendship and regard from her.”

He ended by saying that his friend George Warrington, Esq., of Lamb Court, Temple, was trustee of a little sum, the interest of which would be paid to her until she became of age, or changed her name; and that she would always be remembered by her grateful friend, A. Pendennis.

The sum was small enough, though enough to make a little heiress of Fanny Bolton. Her parents were appeased. Her father said Mr. P. had acted quite as the gentleman—though Bows growled that to plaster a wounded heart with a banknote was an easy kind of sympathy. And poor Fanny—whatever her father’s calculations, whatever Mr. Bows’s cynicism—felt only too clearly what the letter was in truth: not a gift, but a farewell.
