The hermit, startled from his reverie, gazed first at his nursling, then at Margaret, and back again, his eyes wide with a tumultuous agitation. In his left arm lay the child, sound asleep, while Margaret knelt beside him at his right knee. Gone was the pale, panting, terrified girl he had so effortlessly overpowered hours prior; standing before him now was an imperial beauty, her cheeks flushed, her eyes sparkling with a triumphant radiance, her lips parted in a sweet, silent joy. Her entire countenance glowed with a look he had never witnessed before—a profound, radiant maternal pride.

He stared, transfixed, his gaze oscillating between the slumbering infant and the woman who held him, his heart pounding with a wonder that bordered on disbelief.

"Us?" he gasped finally, the word barely escaping his lips as his eyes darted back and forth.

Margaret, equally surprised, struggled for words in that moment where impression outweighed fact. "What!" she cried, her voice rising. "Does not a father know his own child? And a man of God, too? Fie, Gerard, to pretend! Nay, thou art too wise, too virtuous not to know—look, I watched thee closely, and now behold you both! 'Tis thine own flesh and blood thou holdest to thy heart."

Clement trembled violently. "What words are these?" he stammered, clutching at air. "This angel of mine?"

"Whose else?" Margaret pressed gently. "Since he is mine."

Turning his gaze upon the sleeping child, Clement beheld a vision beyond the power of pen to describe. As his eyes seemed to drink in the little life, he trembled from head to foot, as if absorbing the essence of pure love itself.

Margaret's eyes followed his. "He bears not a single trace of me," she declared proudly, though her tone softened. "Yet, oh, at intervals, he is thy very image, shrunk into miniature form. See this golden hair? Thine was exactly this hue when thou wast his age; ask any mother else. And observe this mole upon his little finger. Now look at thine own! There! 'Twas thy mother who let me know thou wast marked so before him. And oh, Gerard, 'twas this very child who found thee for me. By that tiny mark upon thy finger, I knew thee for his father when I watched from above thy window and saw thee feeding the birds."

Seizing the child's hand, she kissed it with eager fervor, pressing half of it into her mouth, Heaven knew how, before whispering, "Ah! Bless thee, thou didst find thy poor daddy for her. And now thou hast made us friends again after our little quarrel—the first, and the last. Thou wast very cruel to me but now, my poor Gerard, yet I forgive thee; for I love thy child."

"Ay! Ay! Ay! Ay! Ay!" sobbed Clement, choking back tears. Overcome by prolonged fasts and unnerved by solitude, the once-strong man fell into hysteria, teetering on the brink of fainting.

Margaret, alarmed yet seasoned by experience, let her pity outweigh her fear. "Nay, take not on so," she murmured soothingly, placing a gentle hand upon his brow. "Be brave! So, so. Dear heart, thou art not the first man to journey afar and return richer by a lovely little self than when he departed. Being a man of God, take courage. Say that He sends thee this to comfort thee for what thou hast lost in me—and that is not so very much, my lamb. For surely the better part of love shall ne'er cool here to thee; though it may in thine, and ought, being a priest, and parson of Gouda."

"I? Priest of Gouda? Never!" murmured Clement in a faint voice. "I am a friar of St. Dominic: yet speak on, sweet music. Tell me all that hath happened to thee before we are parted again."

Now, some might have exclaimed against parting at all, raising the true question in dispute. But women like Margaret do not repeat their mistakes. It is exceedingly difficult to defeat them twice where their hearts are truly set.

She assented, turning her back on the Gouda manse as if it were a forgotten dream, and began her tale. She dwelt above all upon the kindness shown to her by his parents. As she recounted her troubles, his hand stole to hers, and often she felt him wince and tremble with righteous ire, pressing her hand in sympathy with every vein of her sorrow.

"Oh, piteous tale of a true heart battling alone against such bitter odds," he said.

"It all seems small, when I see thee here again, nursing my boy," she replied. "We have had a warning, Gerard. True friends like you and me are rare, and they are mad to part ere death divideth them."

"And that is true," said Clement, momentarily off his guard.

Then she urged him to speak of his sufferings for her sake. He begged her to excuse him, and she consented, yet by questions quietly revoked, she elicited everything. Many a sigh she heaved for him, hiding her face in her hands with terror at the perils he had faced, though now past. To console him, she knelt, sliding her arms beneath the little boy, lifting him gently. "Kiss him softly," she whispered. "Again, again, kiss thy fill if thou canst; he is sound. 'Tis all I can do to comfort thee till thou art out of this foul den and in thy sweet manse yonder."

Clement shook his head.

"Well," she said, "let that pass. Know that I have been sore affronted for want of my lines."

"Who hath dared affront thee?"

"No matter; those that will do it again if thou hast lost them, which the Saints forbid."

"I lose them? Nay, there they lie, close to thy hand."

Clement hung his head. "Look in the Vulgate. Heaven forgive me: I thought thou wert dead, a saint in heaven."

She looked, and on the blank leaves of the poor soul's Bible, she found her marriage lines.

"Thank God!" she cried, "Thank God! Oh, bless thee, Gerard, bless thee! Why, what is here, Gerard?"

On the other leaves were pinned every scrap of paper she had ever sent him, their two names written together in sport, the lock of her hair she had given him, half a silver coin she had broken with him, and a straw she had sucked from her soup the first day he ever saw her.

When Margaret saw these proofs of love and signs of a gentle heart bereaved, even her exultation at recovering her lines was overwhelmed by gushing tenderness. She almost staggered, her hand going to her bosom, leaning her brow against the stone cell wall. She wept so silently he did not see, for she felt that to befriend him now she must be the stronger, lest emotion weaken her resolve.

"Gerard," she said, "I know thou art wise and good. Thou must have a reason for what thou art doing, let it seem ever so unreasonable. Let us talk like old friends. Why art thou buried alive?"

"To escape temptation," Gerard answered. "My impious ire against those two had its root in the heart; that heart I must deaden, and, Dei gratia, I shall. Shall I, a servant of Christ and of the Church, court temptation? Shall I pray daily to be led out onto it, and walk into it with open eyes?"

"That is good sense, anyway," Margaret said, with a consummate affectation of candor.

"'Tis unanswerable," Clement sighed.

"We shall see," she countered gently. "Tell me, hast thou escaped temptation here? Why I ask is, when I am alone, my thoughts are far more wild and foolish than in company. Nay, speak sooth!"

"I must needs own I have been worse tempted here with evil imaginations than in the world."

"There now."

"Ay, but so were Anthony and Jerome, Macarius and Hilarion, Benedict, Bernard, and all the saints. 'Twill wear off."

"How dost thou know?"

"I feel sure it will."

"Guessing against knowledge. Here 'tis men-folk are sillier than us, who are but women. Wise in their own conceits, they will not let themselves see; their stomachs are too high to be taught by their eyes. A woman, if she went into a hole in a bank to escape temptation, and there found it, would just lift her farthingale and be out on't, and not even know how wise she was, till she watched a man in like plight."

"Nay, I grant humility and a teachable spirit are the roads to wisdom; but when all is said, here I wrestle but with imagination. At Gouda, she I love as no priest or monk must love any but the angels, she will tempt a weak soul, unwilling, yet not loth to be tempted."

"Ay, that is another matter; I should tempt thee then? To what, i' God's name?"

"Who knows? The flesh is weak."
