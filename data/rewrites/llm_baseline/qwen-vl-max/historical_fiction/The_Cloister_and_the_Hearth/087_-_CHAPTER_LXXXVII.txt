As Jorian pulled on his doublet and jerkin, preparing to leave for Peter’s tomb, he spoke without pause. “They called him a magician back in Sevenbergen. And it’s said he gave them a taste of his craft when he left—claimed he saw Margaret’s boy coming down the Rhine, dressed fine and rich, but with a scarred face from some foreign blade, and not all the limbs he’d had when he set out. But nothing came of it. Margaret still waits for her son. And Peter lies as still as any of his neighbors—though she did put a stone slab over him, to keep him where he is. You’ll see.”

He placed both hands on the edge of the grave, ready to climb out, when the friar laid a trembling hand upon his shoulder and whispered, breathless, “How long since did Peter Brandt die?”

“About two months,” Jorian replied. “Why?”

“And you say his daughter buried him?”

“No, I buried him. She paid the fee and raised the stone.”

“Then… if he had only one daughter—Margaret?”

“No more, at least not that he ever acknowledged.”

“So you believe Margaret is—alive?”

“Believe? Why, I’d be dead myself otherwise. Riddle me that.”

“I cannot, sir. You love her.”

“Not more than reason, being married and father to four sturdy lads like myself. The truth is, she saved my life barely six weeks ago. Had she been dead, she couldn’t have done it. I could not keep food down; doctors could do nothing. My Joan said, ‘It’s time to buy you a shroud.’ ‘I dare say,’ I told her, ‘but let’s borrow one first.’ Then in comes my lady—this very Margaret, who, by your reckoning, died three years past—opens the windows, shifts me where I lay, and cures me in the blink of an eye. With what? That’s the puzzle. With the most despised herb in the garden—sweet feverfew. A weed, they call it. But now, every time I pass my patch, I tip my hat and say, ‘Fly service to thee!’”

Jorian paused, then noticed the friar’s pallor. “Why, father, you look ill—pale, then red, then white again. What ails you?”

“The surprise—the joy—the wonder—the fear,” Clement gasped.

“What has it to do with you? Are you kin to Margaret Brandt?”

“No,” he said, voice low. “But I knew one who loved her so deeply, her death nearly killed him—body and soul. Yet you say she lives. And I believe you.”

Jorian stared, then after a long silence, spoke solemnly. “Father, you’ve asked many questions, and I’ve answered truthfully. Now, for Our Lady’s sake, answer me two: Did you truly know someone who loved this girl? Where?”

Clement hesitated, close to revealing himself—but recalled Jerome’s letter, and the name he had once borne in the world. “I knew him in Italy,” he said at last.

“If you knew him, you can tell me his name,” Jorian said cautiously.

“His name was Gerard Eliassoen.”

“Oh,” Jorian breathed. “This is strange. Wait—why did you say Margaret Brandt was dead?”

“I was with Gerard when a letter arrived from Margaret Van Eyck. It told him she was dead and buried. Let me sit—my strength fails me. Foul play! Foul play!”

“Father,” Jorian said, “I thank Heaven for bringing you to me. Sit down—you look like a ghost. You fast too much to be strong. My mind misgives me—I think I hold the clue to this riddle. And if I do, there are two villains in this town whose heads I would gladly smash as I do this earth.” He clenched his teeth, lifted his long spade high, and brought it crashing down upon the mound again and again.

“Foul play?” he roared. “You never said a truer word. If you know where Gerard is now, waste no time—go to him, show him the trap they’ve laid. My wits may be dull, but while the slow hound puzzles over the scent—go to it. And I think you and I have grasped two ends of the same stick—and a very foul one.”

Then, after the usual prelude men of his sort always make, Jorian came to the heart of the matter. He had been hired by the burgomaster of Tergou to repair the floor of an upper room in his house. Nearly finished, he returned suddenly to collect his tools—and heard loud voices below. Curiosity took hold. He lay flat on his stomach and listened.

The burgomaster was speaking of a letter—how Cornelis and Sybrandt were to replace one meant for Hans Memling, with another addressed to Gerard. “Their will was good,” the burgomaster said, “but their courage small. So to give them heart, he showed them a drawer full of silver. If they did the trick, each could take as much as their hands could hold.”

Jorian remembered it well—especially that night, when he could not sleep for thinking of the bargain. Next morning, at dawn, he saw Cornelis and Sybrandt emerge from their house—each with a black eye.

“Oho!” Jorian muttered. “Hans has marked you well. I hope that’s all you got for your pains.”

Sure enough, they hurried toward the burgomaster’s house. Jorian slipped into hiding.

At this point, the monk’s nostrils flared, his eyes restless with tension.

“Well, father,” Jorian continued, “the burgomaster led them into that very room. He held a letter in his hand. I’m no scholar, but I’ve as many eyes as the Pope. I saw the drawer open—those two knaves plunged their hands in and pulled them out full. Saints in glory, how they strained to hold more and more! Sybrandt had smeared his hand with something sticky—glue, I think—and managed to carry off a few pieces clinging to the back. Ha! ha! ha! ’Tis a sin to laugh.”

So luck was on the wrong side, as ever. They’d done the deed—but how, no man would know till Judgment Day. And they left their mortal jewels behind in that drawer.

They got their handful of silver. But the devil had the better of the bargain.

“There, father,” Jorian concluded. “That’s off my chest. I often wanted to tell someone—but dared not. Not to the women. For if I had, Margaret would have had no friend left in the world. Those two black-hearted scoundrels are the favorites—always are. Have you not seen the old folk just opened a new shop for them here, in the Hoog Straet? Look—there’s their sign: a gilt sheep and lambkin. A pair of wolves suckling their dam would be nearer the truth. And today—today they feast the whole family. Oh, what a fine world it is.”

He turned to the friar. “Say something, holy father. You sit like stone. Not even a curse for them—those heartless rogues?”

“Was it not enough that the poor lad was alone in a strange land?” Jorian thundered. “Must his own flesh and blood steal away the one blessing his enemies left him? And think of her—waiting, waiting these years, sitting by the window, watching the street for Gerard! So constant, so tender, so true. My wife says no woman ever loved a man more faithfully than she loves that boy those villains tore from her. And every day, she weeps salt tears. And I think—had it not been for those greedy, lying knaves, that winsome lad—whose life I saved—might be beside her now, the happiest man in Holland. And that sweet girl—she who saved my life—might sit with her cheek on her lover’s shoulder, the happiest woman in Holland instead of the saddest. Oh, I thirst for their blood—those sneaking, lying, cheating, cowardly, heartless, bowelless—what now?”

The monk leapt up, livid with rage and despair, and fled, fists clenched and raised high. The fury was so terrible, so wordless, that Jorian’s own anger vanished at the sight. He stood stunned, watching the storm he had unleashed.

Just then, a small, trembling hand gripped his arm. It was Margaret Brandt.

He started. Her sudden appearance seemed impossible. She had waited long at Peter’s tombstone, but the friar had not come. So she went into the church to search for him. She found no trace. Then, walking up the south aisle, she saw the shadow of a giant friar racing across the floor and part of a pillar—passing through her very form. She nearly screamed—but remembered Jorian’s shadow had entered from the yard. She tried to climb out the nearest way. She succeeded, though with difficulty. By then, Clement was vanishing down the street. But even from afar, she saw his agitation—his body spoke of torment.

Jorian and Margaret looked at each other, then at the distant figure of the fleeing friar.

“Well?” she asked, trembling.

“Well,” he replied, “you startled me. How come you here?”

“This is no time for idle talk,” she said sharply. “What did he say to you? He spoke to you—don’t deny it.”

“Girl,” he said, “he asked me where you were buried in this churchyard.”

“Ah!”

“I told him—nowhere, thank God. You’re alive. Saving others from the churchyard.”

“Well?”

“The long and short of it is—he knew your Gerard in Italy. A letter came saying you were dead—and it broke your boy’s heart. Who wrote it? Ah—by the demoiselle van Eyck. That was how he put it. But I told him no—it wasn’t the demoiselle nor any noblewoman who penned that lie. It was Ghysbrecht Van Swieten—and those foul knaves, Cornelis and Sybrandt. They swapped the real letter for one of their own. I told him I saw the whole villainy through a crack. And now—if I haven’t told you!”

“Oh, cruel! Cruel! But he lives. The worst fear is gone. Thank God!”

“Aye, lass,” Jorian said. “And as for your enemies—I’ve given them a dig. That friar is friendly to Gerard. He’s gone to Eli’s house, I think. I told him where to find Gerard’s foes—and yours. And wow—when he’s angry, he’s a force of nature. He turned black and white, flew apart like a stone from a sling. Girl, there was thunder in his eyes and silence on his lips. Made me cold.”

“Oh, Jorian, what have you done?” she cried. “Quick! Quick! Help me there—the power has left my body. You don’t know him as I do. Oh, if you had seen the blow he gave Ghysbrecht! And heard the crash! Come—save him from worse harm. The water is deep enough—but not bloody yet. Come!”

Her voice was filled with agony. Jorian sprang from the grave, pulling on his jerkin as he ran.

But as they hurried, he asked, “What on earth do you mean? You speak of the friar—and I of Gerard.”

“Man, can’t you see?” she said. “That friar is my boy’s father. I’ve waited for him long, Javax. Well—he’s come to me at last. Thank God for it. Oh, my poor child! Quicker, Jorian, quicker!”

“Why, you’re mad as he,” Jorian said. “Wait! By St. Bavon—that was Gerard’s face—no, not like it—yet somehow… it was. Come on! Come on! Let me see the end of this.”

“The end?” she whispered. “How many of us will live to see it?”

They ran in breathless silence until they reached Hoog Straet. Then Jorian tried to calm her.

“You’re making trouble for yourself,” he said. “Who says he went there? More likely to the convent—to pray and weep, poor soul. Oh, cursed, cursed villains!”

“Didn’t you tell him where those villains lived?”

“Aye, I did.”

“Then quicker, oh, Jorian, quicker! I see the house. Thank God and all the saints—I shall be in time to calm him. I know what I’ll say. Heaven forgive me! Poor Catherine—’tis of her I think. She has been a mother to me.”

The shop was a corner building, with two doors—one for customers in the main street, another round the corner. They were twenty yards from it when they heard a roar inside—like a wild beast. Then the friar burst out, white and raging, tearing down the street.

Margaret screamed and fainted into Jorian’s arms.

“Stay, madman, know thy friends!” Jorian shouted after him.

But he was deaf, charging ahead, fists shaking high above his head.

“Help me in, good Jorian,” she moaned, suddenly calm. “Let me know the worst—and die.”

He supported her trembling frame into the house. It was unnaturally silent—not a sound. Jorian’s heart pounded. A door stood before them, unlatched. He pushed it gently with his left hand.

And there, on the threshold, stood Margaret and Jorian.

What they saw within—you shall soon know.
