As twilight descended, the campfire crackled warmly, sending up the savory aroma of bear paws simmering for supper. We gathered around the flames, hearts pounding with anxiety as we waited for the return of our young adventurers. Suddenly, the rhythmic *thump-thump* of hooves broke the silence. A lively troop of riders crossed the clearing at a sharp trot, greeted by a shrill cheer as we rose to meet them.

The boys dismounted, freeing their horses to graze, eager to display their trophies. What funny sights they presented! Franz and Jack each carried a young goat slung across their backs, its legs tied together beneath their chins. Fritz's gamebag bulged strangely—round lumps and sharp points hinted at living creatures inside.

"Hurrah for the chase, Father!" Jack cried enthusiastically. "Real hunting is the best! Just imagine Storm and Grumble galloping over the plains. We tired out those little antelopes in no time."

"Yes, Father," Franz added proudly, "and Fritz caught two Angora rabbits. Plus, a clever cuckoo guided us to a hive full of honey!"

"My brothers forget the main point," Fritz corrected gently. "We drove a whole herd of antelopes through the Gap into our territory. They are now trapped in our 'New Switzerland,' waiting to be hunted or tamed whenever we choose."

"Excellent work!" I praised. "But to my wife and me, the greatest joy is simply that you are safe. Tell us everything."

"We had a wonderful ride down Glen Verdant," Fritz recounted. "The air was crisp, and our horses raced at full speed until we reached the Gap. There, we spotted two herds grazing by a stream. Instead of shooting them, I realized we could drive them through the Gap into our domain, effectively creating a private park. With the dogs barking and us shouting, we rounded them up. At the opening, they hesitated like sheep facing a butcher, but eventually, one dashed through, and the rest followed suit."

"Brilliant strategy," I agreed. "But how do you ensure they stay?"

"We anticipated that," Fritz smiled. "We strung a long line across the defile, adorned with feathers and rags that fluttered wildly in the wind. According to Levaillant, the Hottentots use similar methods to trap antelopes; the strange movement keeps the animals guessing and prevents escape."

"Wise reading pays off," I noted. Turning to the rabbits, I warned, "Be careful with those. Rabbits multiply fast. If you start a colony here, you'll soon have a problem."

"Not here," Fritz clarified. "I planned to settle them on Whale Island. My eagle actually caught them for me earlier today. I rewarded him with one dead rabbit and brought back the others unharmed."

Before Fritz could elaborate further, Jack interjected, "Father, let me speak! Otherwise, Fritz will bore you with more details."

"Certainly, Jack," I said. "How did you capture those fawns?"

"Oh, it was easy," Jack laughed. "We chased them down the valley. While Fritz fetched the rabbits, the fawns bolted at thirty miles per hour. We left the dogs behind and cornered them in the grass. Terrified and exhausted, they fell into our hands. But then..."

"And then what?" I asked.

"The cuckoo!" Jack grinned. "It led us far off course, making faces and calling loudly. Franz thought it was an enchanted princess needing rescue, but Fritz correctly identified it as a 'Honey Indicator.' Sure enough, it led us straight to a bee's nest. I tried to burn the hive with matches, but only angered the bees, who stung me terribly. I barely escaped on Storm's back with my face burning!"

Laughing at his tale, Jack rushed off to tend to his wounds, leaving Fritz and me to inspect the fawns and rabbits. Deciding to follow Fritz's plan, I resolved to transport the rabbits to Whale Island. This aligned perfectly with my secret project: building a fortress there for emergencies, ensuring a steady food supply.

After settling the animals, I attempted to show the boys a block of talc I'd found, but dinner was called. The main course was the bear paws—a delicious treat despite their uncanny resemblance to human hands. Even Jack's playful "Fee-fo-fum" couldn't stop anyone from enjoying the meal.

Once supper ended, we extinguished the cooking fires, retired to our tent, and slept deeply.

Dawn broke, and I roused the boys for another expedition. Fritz mounted his mule, I took Lightfoot, while Jack and Franz rode their usual horses, accompanied by the two dogs. Our mission: collect Euphorbia gum and check on the ostrich eggs. Ernest stayed behind to help his mother and study.

Racing down the Green Valley, we reached Turtle Marsh and filled our water flasks before heading toward the ridge where Fritz had previously encountered Arab riders. Allowing the younger boys to gallop ahead, Fritz and I inspected the Euphorbia trees. Red gum oozed from my earlier cuts, hardened by the sun. I rolled it into balls and stored it in a bamboo jar.

"Why collect poison?" Fritz asked curiously.

"It is deadly," I explained. "Cape natives use it to poison watering holes to kill animals for their hides. I intend to use it against apes if they attack again, and later to preserve animal skins."

Just then, four magnificent ostriches rose from the sand. Jack and Franz screamed in excitement, driving them toward us. Leading the group was a stunning male with shimmering black plumage and a flowing tail; three gray females trailed behind. They approached with terrifying speed, closing the distance before realizing we were there.

Fritz had wisely bound his eagle's beak to prevent injury. He released the falcon, which dove onto the lead ostrich's head, causing confusion. The bird slowed, allowing Jack to leap forward, throw his lasso, and wrap the creature's wings and legs. The other ostriches fled, leaving us to subdue the captured giant.

He struggled violently, kicking wildly. Despairing of keeping him alive, I remembered a trick: covering his eyes. Draping my coat and bag over his head plunged him into darkness. Instantly, his thrashing ceased.

Securing a seal-skin strap around his body and attaching cords to his legs to limit his stride, we freed him from the lasso.

"Do you know how Indians tame elephants?" I asked.

"They tie them between two tame ones," Fritz answered confidently. "Let's try that!"

"But we lack tame ostriches," Jack pointed out. "However, Storm and Grumble can serve as guards. Their presence alone might confuse this monster."

We positioned the two dogs on either side of the recumbent bird, tying ropes to their harnesses. Jack and Franz mounted their horses, whips in hand, while we removed the blindfold. Initially, the ostrich lay still, unaware of the restraints. When he tried to bolt, the thick cords held him firm. After a futile struggle, he accepted his fate, beginning a brisk trot. The dogs' joyful shouts encouraged him to run faster, but the horses' refusal to match his speed forced him to stop.

Satisfied with the exercise, I instructed the boys to walk the prisoner home slowly while Fritz and I returned to the nest. The eggs were warm, confirming the mother had recently abandoned them. Packing half the clutch into a large bag, I slung it over my saddle.

Rejoining the boys, we faced my wife's shock upon our arrival.

"Have you gone mad?" she exclaimed. "Scouring the desert for a beast just to eat more? Perhaps you need an iron mine next, since ostriches supposedly eat iron!"

"I wish you'd accept the menagerie you already have," she complained. "And what a useless monster this is!"

"Useless?" Jack protested indignantly. "Mother, you haven't seen him run! He'll be the fastest horse in our stable. Besides, Father says his diet is exquisite—just fruit, grass, stones, and maybe a few nails for digestion."

Seeing Jack claim ownership, the brothers erupted in protest.

"Fine," Jack declared. "Each of us takes a part. Fritz gets the head, Father gets the body, I take the legs, and Franz gets some tail feathers."

"No," I intervened firmly. "Jack brought it down and is taming it. If he succeeds in making it a riding horse, it belongs to him. He is now fully responsible for its training."

Too late for a trip to Rockburg, we fastened the ostrich between two trees and spent the evening preparing for departure.

At dawn, our caravan moved homeward. The stubborn ostrich required the dogs to flank him again. The cow pulled the cart loaded with treasures, while Jack and Franz rode Storm and Grumble, I followed on Lightfoot, and Fritz brought up the rear on Swift.

Stopping at the Gap, I replaced the feathered barrier with a sturdy bamboo palisade. Gathering pipe-clay for winter pottery projects, we proceeded to the sugar-cane grove to retrieve smoking peccary hams. My wife requested seeds of a vanilla-scented plant, which I collected before we headed to Woodlands for the night.
