Good and evil possess a strange, universal tendency to balance one another. The steel bow of the arbalest strapped to the rescuer's back had nearly drowned him; yet, because of its position, it fell directly into Gerard's grasp—one hand clutching the limb, the other wedged securely between the bow and the cord. With a desperate, jerking pull, Denys was hauled from the depths until half his torso broke the surface.

"Now, grip me not! Grip me not!" Gerard screamed, terrified of the fatal error of letting go. "Pas si bete," Denys gurgled through the water. Realizing the strength of his companion, Gerard's fear instantly transformed into hope and command.

"On thy back!" he ordered sharply. Seizing the heavy weapon, he stroked forward, aiding the swimmer's ascent. "Hand on my shoulder! Slap the water with the other—no, use a downward motion. Do exactly as I bid."

Gerard had seized Denys's long hair, twisting it firmly and biting the end between his teeth. Using the powerful muscles of his youthful neck, he held the soldier's head aloft while striking out vigorously against the current. For a fleeting moment, he hesitated, unsure which direction to take, unaware of the terrible significance of that choice. Then, spotting the western bank slightly closer, he steered toward it rather than drifting helplessly downstream toward prison. Thus, he created a new adventure.

Driven by the river's force, they angled heavily. Covering nearly a hundred yards, Denys murmured anxiously, "How much longer?"

"Courage," rumbled Gerard. "Whatever a duck knows, a Dutchman knows; you are as safe as in bed."

Suddenly, they found themselves in shallow water and waded onto solid ground. Once on terra firma, they stared at one another from head to toe as if eyes alone could consume them. Then, in a single impulse, they threw arms around each other's necks, panting with hearts too full for words. In that sacred moment, life tasted sweeter than heaven, especially for the exiled lover who had just saved his friend. Oh, the joy to whose heights has any poet yet soared? To save a human life—and specifically, a beloved one—is worth living for, yes, sixty years and ten.

Calmer now, they joined hands and walked along the bank like sweethearts, scarcely caring where they went. The boatmen remained safe on their overturned vessel, Herr Turnip-face, the "Inverter of things," standing in the center. The chaos seemed to have done nothing to disrupt his peculiar habits; he greeted them as they entered the Rhine and again as they emerged.

"Shall we wait for them to right the boat?"

"No, Denys. Our fare is paid; we owe them nothing. Let us move briskly."

Denys agreed, noting they could walk the entire way to Cologne along this bank.

"I am not going to Cologne," came the calm reply.

"Why, then, whither?"

"To Burgundy."

"To Burgundy? Ah, no! That is too good to be true."

"It is true, and sensible besides. What matters to me how I travel to Rome?"

"Nay, nay; you say that merely to please me. The change is too sudden. Think not me so ill-hearted as to deceive you. Did I not see your eyes sparkle at the wonders of Cologne—the churches, the images, the relics?"

"How dull art thou, Denys. That was when we were to enjoy them *together*. Churches! I shall see plenty, wherever I go. The bones of saints and martyrs... alas, the world is full of them. But a friend like thee, where on earth's face shall I find another? No, I will not turn thee farther from the road leading to thy dear home, nor to her who pines for thee. Nor will I rob myself of thee by leaving you behind. Since I drew you from the Rhine, I love thee better than before. Thou art my pearl; I fished thee, and I must keep thee. Gainsay me not, or thou shalt bring back my fever. Cry courage, lead on, and hey for Burgundy!"

Denys gave a joyful leap. "Courage! Va pour la Bourgogne! Oh, soyez tranquille! Cette fois il est bien décidément mort, ce coquin-là."

Turning their backs on the Rhine, they began their journey. As this decision crystallized, a commotion arose among the group watching from the opposite bank. They had not taken many steps before a voice echoed over the water.

Gerard turned, seeing one of the four figures holding out a badge of office and a parchment scroll. His heart sank; he was a good citizen, accustomed to obeying the voice of the Law calling him back to Düsseldorf. Denys shared none of these scruples. A Frenchman, he despised every other nation, its laws, inhabitants, and customs alike. A soldier, he viewed the situation militarily: superior force ahead, a river barrier, and an open rear. Retreat was easy. Spotting the boat drifting in mid-stream and noting the lack of a nearer ferry, he decided immediately.

"I shall beat a quick retreat to that hill," he declared, "and once out of sight, quick step."

They sauntered off casually.

"Halt! In the bailiff's name!" cried a voice from the shore.

Denys spun around, ostentatiously snapping his fingers at the bailiff, and continued walking.

"Halt! In the archbishop's name!"

He snapped his fingers at the prelate and pressed on.

"Halt! In the emperor's name!"

He snapped his fingers at the sovereign himself and marched onward. Gerard watched this unnecessary pantomime with regret. As soon as they cleared the brow of the hill, he said, "There is now but one course: we must run to Burgundy instead of walking."

He set off, running the best part of a league without stopping. Denys was thoroughly winded and inquired what had become of Gerard's fever.

"I begin to miss it sadly," he replied dryly.

"I dropped it in the Rhine, I trow," Gerard answered.

Soon they reached a small village, where Denys bought a loaf of bread and a massive bottle of Rhenish wine.

"For," he explained, "we must sleep in some hole or corner. If we lie at an inn, we shall be taken in our beds."

This was prudent caution. The official net for catching lawbreakers, particularly common ones, was tight in that age, though public cooperation was virtually non-existent on the Continent. Innkeepers were under constant surveillance regarding their travelers, for whom they bore significant responsibility, seemingly more for their actions than their suffering. Both friends were relieved when sunset arrived, and delighted when, after a long trudge under the stars—for the moon did not rise until about three in the morning—they reached a large barn belonging to a distant estate. Barley had recently been threshed there; the heap of straw on one side of the floor stood almost as high as the unthreshed grain on the other.

"Here be two royal beds," Denys remarked. "Which shall we lie on, the mow, or the straw?"

"The straw for me," said Gerard.

They sat upon the heap, eating their brown bread and drinking their wine. Then Denys covered his friend in straw, piling it high above him, leaving only a small opening for air.

"Water is said to be death to fevered men," Denys noted. "Yet I shall make warm water anyway."

Gerard told him to ease his mind. "These few drops from the Rhine cannot chill me. I feel enough heat within my body now to parch a kennel or boil a cloud if I were in one."

With this epigram, his consciousness faded rapidly; he truly might be said to "fall asleep." Denys, lying awake for a while, heard something that made him nestle closer. Horse hooves rang out from Düsseldorf, and the wooden barn vibrated as they rattled past, howling in a manner too familiar to fifteenth-century ears, yet as alien to modern Europe as a Red Indian's war-whoop. Denys shuddered where he lay. Gerard slept like a top. The sound swept by, and the troop and their cries died away. The sturdy soldier drew a long breath, whispered a whistle, closed his eyes, and slept like a top as well.

At dawn, Denys sat up and reached out to wake Gerard. His hand touched the young man's forehead and found it quite wet. In his role as nurse, Denys refrained from waking him.

"It is ill to check sleep or sweat in a sick man," he muttered. "I know that far, though I never mimicked an ape or a gallows-bird."

After waiting an hour, feeling desperately hungry, he turned and went to sleep again in self-defense—a maneuver to which the poor fellow had often been driven during his hard life. At high noon, he was awakened by Gerard moving. He found the young man sitting up, surrounded by smoking straw. Animal heat versus moisture.

Gerard called him "a lazy loon." Denys quietly grinned.

They set out, and the first thing Denys did was return Gerard's arbalest and equipment, then climb a high tree on the roadside.

"Coast clear to the next village," he instructed, and they moved on.

As they approached the village, Denys halted and suddenly asked Gerard how he felt.
