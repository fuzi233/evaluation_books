In the city of New York, the sun sets early, but the social clock strikes seven precisely. Even as the younger set scoffed at the antiquity of the custom, the ritual of the after-dinner call remained the heartbeat of the season. As Newland Archer strolled up Fifth Avenue from Waverley Place, the street was a ghostly procession of shadows and gaslight. Carriages stood idling before the Regis Chiverses' townhouse—there, a banquet awaited the Duke—and solitary figures in heavy wool coats and mufflers ascended the stoops of brownstones, vanishing into the warm glow of entryways.

Archer noted the movements of his peers with practiced ease. Old Mr. du Lac was visiting his cousins, the Dagonets; further down the block, Mr. Skipworth of his own firm was clearly en route to the Lanning sisters. Then, emerging dramatically from a doorway bathed in blinding light, came Beaufort. He stepped down to his private carriage—a canary-yellow brougham—and vanished toward a destination shrouded in mystery, likely illicit given that it was neither an Opera night nor a party season. Archer's mind immediately supplied the answer: a modest home beyond Lexington Avenue, adorned with fresh flower boxes and ribbons, where Miss Fanny Ring's carriage often waited.

Beyond the polished sphere of Mrs. Archer's acquaintance lay a foggy district inhabited by artists, musicians, and writers. These souls, though often respectable, preferred isolation. Attempts to integrate them into high society had failed; Medora Manson's literary salon had collapsed due to the writers' refusal to attend, and the Blenker household, despite hosting luminaries like Edwin Booth and the critic William Winter, remained a place where such guests were treated like exotic specimens rather than equals. To the Archers, these figures were unsettling—their origins obscure, their tastes unpredictable, their proximity to the stage and the theater making them seem fundamentally alien.

"When I was a girl," Mrs. Archer would lament, "we knew everyone from the Battery to Canal Street. One could place anyone instantly. Now, everything is hidden."

Only Catherine Mingott possessed the audacity to bridge this divide, yet her lack of cultural refinement rendered her useless for such a task. Beaufort, equally illiterate and disdainful of "fellows who write," viewed them merely as servants of the wealthy. Newland had observed this schism since childhood, accepting it as the immutable law of his world. He dreamed occasionally of European salons where conversation flowed freely around the works of Mérimée, Thackeray, or Browning, but such fantasies felt dangerous in New York.

He knew the writers personally, meeting them at the Century Club or nascent theatrical gatherings, yet he found them stifling when mixed with the fervid, dowdy women of the Blenkers who treated them as trophies. His thoughts drifted to the Countess Olenska, recalling her amusement at how her own family dismissed the "Bohemian" quarter where she lived—not out of fear for her safety, but because literature was deemed socially compromising. Yet, unlike her relatives, she embraced it. Books by Paul Bourget and the Goncourt brothers lined her drawing-room, sparking Archer's curiosity and highlighting the vast chasm between their worlds.

As he approached her residence, Archer felt the familiar tug of conflicting values. He needed to understand her situation fully before acting. The door opened to reveal Nastasia, her smile cryptic. In the hall lay the unmistakable signs of Julius Beaufort: a sable-lined coat, a silk opera hat marked with a gold *J.B.*, and a white muffler. Anger flared in Archer's chest—he wanted to leave a note and depart—but discipline held him back. He had promised discretion regarding his desire to see her privately; thus, he entered with a steely resolve to confront Beaufort and claim his space.

The banker loomed against the mantelpiece, draped in an antique embroidery, his posture arrogant and relaxed. Beside him, seated on a sofa angled toward the hearth, was the Countess. Behind her, a table laden with orchids and azaleas served as a floral screen. She wore a gown that defied convention: a long robe of crimson velvet trimmed with glossy black fur, exposing her arms to the chill of the room. It was a look borrowed from Paris, bold and provocative, designed to unsettle the rigid norms of New York.

"Lord love us—three whole days at Skuytercliff!" Beaufort boomed, his voice dripping with mock sympathy. "You'd best pack your furs and a hot-water bottle."

"Why? Is the house so cold?" she asked, extending her hand toward Archer with a gesture that invited a kiss.

"No; but the missus is," Beaufort replied, nodding casually. "Though Granny insists I must go. Besides, I've arranged a delightful oyster supper at Delmonico's for next Sunday. Campanini, Scalchi, and a crowd of jolly souls."

She glanced between them, torn. "That does tempt me... except I haven't met a single artist since arriving."

"Painters? Are there painters in New York?" Beaufort scoffed, implying their non-existence unless purchased by him.

"That would be charming," the Countess said gravely to Archer. "But I meant dramatic artists, singers, actors, musicians. My husband's house was always full of them." She spoke of her former life with a strange lightness, as if discussing a pleasant memory rather than a shattered marriage.

"I believe the element of surprise enhances enjoyment," she added, addressing both men. "Perhaps it is a mistake to see the same faces daily."

"It is dreadfully dull, anyhow," Beaufort grumbled. "New York is suffocating in boredom. Try to reconsider, Countess! Sunday is your last chance before Campanini departs."

"How delicious! May I consider it and write to you tomorrow?" Her tone was polite, yet carried a subtle dismissal. Beaufort, unused to rejection, stared at her with narrowed eyes before kissing her hand perfunctorily and exiting, muttering that if Archer could persuade her to stay, he would be welcome to the supper.

Once alone, the atmosphere shifted. "You know painters, then? Do you live among them?" she asked, her eyes alight with genuine interest.

"Oh, not exactly. The arts here lack a true community; they exist more like a sparse outpost."

"But you appreciate them?"

"Immensely. In Paris or London, I never miss an exhibition. I strive to keep pace." She looked down at the tip of her satin boot peeking from beneath her drapery. "I used to care immensely. My life was defined by such things. But now, I wish to try not to."

"You wish to cease caring?"

"Yes. I seek to shed my old life and become like everyone else here."

Archer flushed. "You will never be like everyone else."

"Don't say that," she countered, her expression darkening into something tragic. "If only you knew how much I hate to be different!" She leaned forward, clutching her knees, her gaze drifting into distant shadows. "I want to escape it all."

A pause stretched between them until Archer finally spoke. "Mr. Letterblair informed me of your predicament. He asked me to assist, noting my position within the firm."

Her eyes brightened. "Can you manage it directly? Speaking to you would be far easier."

Archer sensed her relief and felt a surge of triumph at having sidelined Beaufort. "I am here to discuss it."

She fell silent, her face pale against the deep red of her dress, appearing suddenly fragile and pitiful. Archer realized he lacked experience in navigating such crises; the vocabulary of her plight belonged to novels and plays, not his world.

"Now comes the difficult part," he thought, recoiling instinctively.

Suddenly, she burst forth with intensity. "I want to be free. I want to erase the past entirely."

"I understand that."

"Will you help me?"

"First—" he hesitated— "perhaps I should know more."

"You know about my husband? About our life together?"

He nodded.

"Well, then—is there anything else? Does this country tolerate such matters? I am a Protestant; our church permits divorce in such circumstances."

"Certainly."

Silence returned, heavy and oppressive. Archer recalled the letter from Count Olenski, a brief, vitriolic accusation of blackguardry. Was it true? Only she could say.

"I have reviewed the documents Mr. Letterblair provided," Archer began cautiously. "Is there anything more reprehensible?"

"No."

She shifted, shielding her eyes with her hand. "Naturally, you know that if your husband chooses to litigate..."

"Yes?"

"He can make claims—claims that may be unpleasant, perhaps damaging to you, even if they are baseless. Publicly aired, they could ruin you."

"If...?"

"I mean, regardless of their truth."

She paused, allowing the weight of his warning to settle. "What harm could such accusations cause me here?"

*My poor child,* he wanted to cry, *far worse harm awaits you than anywhere else!* But he restrained himself, adopting the detached tone of Mr. Letterblair.
