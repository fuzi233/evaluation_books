There's a specific kind of street in the East End that doesn't just sit there; it stares. It's a long, straight artery of red brick, flanked by two walls so drab and identical they look like the sides of a tombstone stretched out into infinity. The window sills and doorsteps are perfectly level, creating a hypnotic, weary rhythm that suggests the whole thing is a petrified diagram of a problem that's already been solved by boredom. Right at the corner of this architectural snooze-fest stands a house that refuses to take no for an answer. It pulls at me with a gravity that defies physics, dragging me through the chaotic noise of the city—past the flaring naphtha lamps that light up faces like angry moons, through the empty alleys where shadows stretch like grasping fingers, and past the gutters teeming with children who treat the pavement like a playground. I march right past the reeking corners and the open doorways spewing out the daily riot of life until I finally stop, leaning against the broken railings, letting the house do the talking.

This place is a bit of an outlier. While the street is a monument to uniform ugliness, this house, built when the road was still a muddy lane next to the marshes, insists on being individual. It's bigger than its neighbors, surrounded by what used to be a garden but is now just a dusty patch of stones where someone, somewhere, decided hanging laundry in perpetuity was a good idea. The porch is a skeleton of stucco, exposed and honest about its decay.

During the day, the street is a circus. Slatternly women gossip behind the gates, and the air is filled with the hoarse, wailing cry of "Coals! Any coals! Three and sixpence a sack!" shouted by a vendor whose lack of customers has given his voice a permanent case of the blues. But the house hides then. In the harsh sunlight, it turns its face away, ashamed of its shabbiness, pretending not to see me as I walk by.

It only opens up when Night arrives. Night is the only merciful thing in this world, the one that drapes a velvet veil over the sordid details. Once the sun goes down, the house transforms from a shabby relic into a fond, old nurse. The teeming life inside quiets down, the flickering light from the "King of Prussia" across the street dies, and suddenly, the bricks seem to breathe. They talk to me. They ask questions I've forgotten and remind me of things I'd rather forget. The familiar footsteps come and go through the creaking gate, unseen by me, and we chat about them like two old cronies flipping through a tattered photo album. We discuss the faded portraits in forgotten fashions, trading low-toned sighs and "Ah me!"s about the ones who are now dead or scattered.

"Bent, worn man," I say to the wall, watching a figure approach with quick, impatient steps, pausing every fifty yards to lean heavily on a high Malacca cane. "It is a handsome face, isn't it?" I ask, staring at the shadow framed against the brick.

"Aye, handsome enough," the old House replies, its voice sounding like settling dust. "And handsomer still it must have been before you and I knew it, before mean care furrowed it with fretful lines."

"I never could make out," the House muses, "who you took after. Your father and mother were a handsome pair, though Lord! what a couple of children!"

"Children!" I exclaim in surprise. My father must have been past thirty-five before the House could have known him, and my mother's face is so close to mine in the darkness that I can see the grey hairs mingling with the bonny brown.

"Children," the House repeats, irritated, as if I've questioned its authority. "The most helpless pair of children I ever set eyes upon. Who but a child would conceive the notion of repairing his fortune by becoming a solicitor at thirty-eight? Or, having done that, pick the outskirts of Poplar as a likely spot for a door-plate?"

"It was considered a rising neighborhood," I reply, a little resentful. No son likes hearing his family wisdom critiqued, even if he secretly agrees. "All sorts of men connected with the sea were supposed to move here to be near the new docks. If they did, they'd surely quarrel and dispute, much to the advantage of a cute solicitor conveniently located."

"Stuff and nonsense," the House retorts sharply. "The mere smell of the place would keep a sensible man away. And"—the grim brick face twists into a goblin smile—"he, of all men, calling himself 'the cute solicitor,' advising shady clients on how to get out of trouble the short way! Can you imagine it? He starved himself for two years, living on five shillings a week—that was before you came to London, when he was here alone. Even your mother didn't know until years later, so no man should be a penny poorer for trusting his good name. Do you think the crew of chandlers, dock hustlers, and freight wreckers would have found him useful business, even if they settled here?"

I have no answer. The House doesn't wait, but keeps talking.

"And your mother! Would any but a child have taken that soft-tongued wanton to her bosom without seeing through the act? Would any but a fool let itself out into the world alone have thought to dree her life in such folly? Children! Poor babies they were, both of them."

"Tell me," I say. At moments like this, my common sense isn't enough to convince me the House is just clay. Its walls are full of voices, its floors thick with footsteps; surely it has learned to live. Like a violin played for decades, it must have developed its own music. "Tell me, I was but a child to whom life speaks in a strange tongue. Was there any truth in the story?"

"Truth!" the House snaps. "Just truth enough to plant a lie upon. And Lord knows not much ground is needed for that weed. I saw what I saw, and I know what I know. Your mother had a good man, and your father a true wife, but it was the old story: a man's way is not a woman's way, and a woman's way is not a man's way. So there lives ever doubt between them."

"But they came together in the end," I say, remembering.

"Aye, in the end," the House answers. "That is when you begin to understand, you men and women, when you come to the end."

A grave face, not too recently washed, peeks shyly at me through the railings. As I turn my head, it darts back and vanishes.

"What has become of her?" I ask.

"She? Oh, she's well enough," the House replies. "She lives close here. You must have passed the shop. You might have seen her had you looked. She weighs fourteen stone, about, and has nine children living. She'd be pleased to see you."

"Thank you," I say with a laugh that isn't quite a laugh. "I don't think I'll call." Yet I still hear the pit-pat of her tiny feet dying down the long street.

The faces around me thicken. A large, rubicund visage smiles kindly, bringing back an old, odd mix of instinctive liking held in check by conscientious disapproval. I turn away and see a massive, clean-shaven face with the ugliest mouth and the loveliest eyes I've ever seen in a man.

"Was he as bad, do you think, as they said?" I ask my ancient friend.

"Shouldn't wonder," the House answers. "I never knew a worse—and a better."

The wind whisks it aside, revealing a little old woman hobbling nimbly with a stick. Three corkscrew curls bob on either side of her head. As she draws near, making alarming grimaces, I hear her whisper to herself, as if sharing a fascinating secret: "I'd like to skin 'em. I'd like to skin 'em all. I'd like to skin 'em all alive!"

It sounds fiendish, yet I only laugh. The little lady, with a final facial contortion surpassing all dreams, limps beyond my ken.

Then, as if choosing contrasts, a fair, laughing face appears. I saw it in the life only a few hours ago—or rather, the poor daub that Evil has painted over it, hating the sweetness underneath. As I gaze at it, wishing it belonged to the dead who don't change, another face drifts back from the shadows: the one with the wicked mouth and tender eyes. I stand helpless between the two I loved so well: he, from whom I learned my first steps in manhood; she, from whom I caught my first glimpse of the beauty and mystery of woman. Again the cry rises from my heart: "Whose fault was it—yours or hers?" And again I hear his mocking laugh as he answers, "Whose fault? God made us." Thinking of her and the love I bore her, which was as the love of a young pilgrim to a saint, it comes into my blood to hate him. But when I look into his eyes and see the pain living there, my pity grows stronger than my misery, and I can only echo his words, "God made us."

Merry faces and sad, fair faces and foul, they ride upon the wind, but the center around which they circle remains always the same: a little lad with golden curls more suitable to a girl than a boy, with shy, awkward ways, a silent tongue, and a grave, old-fashioned face.

Turning from him to my old brick friend, I ask: "Would he know me, could he see me, do you think?"

"How should he," the House answers, "you are so different to what he would expect. Would you recognize your own ghost, think you?"

"It is sad to think he would not recognize me," I say.

"It might be sadder if he did," the House grumbles.

We remain silent for a while, but I know what the old House is thinking. Soon it speaks as I expected.

"You—writer of stories, why don't you write a book about him? There is something you know."

It's the favorite theme of the old House. I never visit it without it suggesting this idea.

"But he has done nothing?" I say.

"He has lived," the House answers. "Is that not enough?"

"Aye, but only in London in these prosaic modern times," I persist. "How can one make a story out of such that shall interest the people?"

The House grows impatient.

"'The people!'" it retorts. "What are you all but children in a dim-lit room, waiting until one by one you are called out to sleep? And one mounts upon a stool and tells a tale to the others gathered round. Who shall say what will please them, what will not?"

Returning home with musing footsteps through the softly breathing streets, I ponder the words of the old House. Is it just a foolish mother thinking the whole world is interested in her child, or may there lie wisdom in its counsel? Then, to my guidance or misguidance, comes the thought of a certain small section of the Public who often command a story from me in the evenings. When I tell them of dreadful giants and gallant youths who slay them, of wood-cutter's sons rescuing maidens from Ogre-guarded castles, of the most beautiful Princesses and Princes with magic swords, they are still unsatisfied. They creep closer, saying: "Now tell me a real story," adding for my comprehension: "You know: about a little girl who lived in a big house with her father and mother, and who was sometimes naughty, you know."

So perhaps among the many there may be some who, for a moment, will turn aside from tales of haughty heroes ruffling it in Court and Camp to listen to the story of a very ordinary lad who lived with very ordinary folk in a modern London street. A lad who grew up to be a very ordinary sort of man, loving a little and grieving a little, helping a few and harming a few, struggling and failing and hoping. And if any such there be, let them come round me.

But let not those who come to me grow indignant as they listen, saying: "This rascal tells us but a humdrum story, where nothing is as it should be." For I warn all beforehand that I tell but of things I have seen. My villains, I fear, are but poor sinners, not altogether bad; and my good men but sorry saints. My princes do not always slay their dragons; alas, sometimes the dragon eats the prince. The wicked fairies often prove more powerful than the good. The magic thread leads sometimes wrong, and even the hero is not always brave and true.

So let those come round me only who will be content to hear but their own story, told by another, saying as they listen, "So dreamt I. Ah, yes, that is true, I remember."

Fate, it seems, had a peculiar sense of humor regarding my arrival. I was destined to be a singularly fortunate man, or so the narrative goes. Logically, I should have been born in June, the universally acknowledged month of supreme luck, a choice that thoughtful parents invariably make. Instead, I was ushered into this world in May, the statistical champion of misfortune, a fact my early nurse, a formidable woman of bone and gristle, attributed immediately to my natural impatience. She warned that this trait would lead to greater trouble, a prophecy that history, as it turns out, validated with impressive precision.

According to this same bony lady, my entrance was timed with perfect dramatic irony. I arrived exactly two hours after news reached the household of my father's mines having been swallowed by an inundation. As the family lore expounded, misfortunes never come singly; they arrive in packs. To ensure the tragedy was complete, my poor mother, in her panic to ring the bell, collided with and shattered a cheval-glass. Her Welsh blood, naturally superstitious and resistant to logic, interpreted this as a cosmic seal: the first seven years of my existence were doomed to disaster by her own clumsy hand.

"And I must confess," sighed the knobbly Mrs. Fursey, "it does look as though there must be some truth in the saying, after all."

"Then ain't I a lucky little boy?" I asked. Up until this moment, Mrs. Fursey's primary function was to inflate my ego with tales of exceptional good fortune. My ability to retire to bed at six, while other children suffered until eight or nine, was cited as a miracle of Providence. My immunity to illness, achieved through a diet of linseed gruel and a mysterious compound of brimstone and treacle (a flavor profile designed to deceive the innocent), was another testament to Fortune's favoritism. Other boys were left alone when sick; I was fed. Furthermore, possessing Mrs. Fursey as a nurse was the ultimate badge of honor. The suggestion that I was not the luckiest child on earth was a radical departure from her usual script.

Perceiving her error, the good dame corrected herself with alacrity.

"Oh, you! You are lucky enough," she replied, "I was thinking of your poor mother."

"Isn't mamma lucky?"

"Well, she hasn't been too lucky since you came."

"Wasn't it lucky, her having me?"

"I can't say it was, at that particular time."

"Didn't she want me?"

Mrs. Fursey was one of those well-meaning souls who believed the only reasonable attitude for a child was perpetual apology for existing.

"Well, I daresay she could have done without you," was the answer.

I can still see the scene vividly. I sat on a low chair before the nursery fire, one knee propped in my locked hands, while Mrs. Fursey's needle grated with monotonous regularity against her thimble. At that precise moment, the problem of life knocked on my small soul for the first time.

Without moving, I asked:

"Then why did she take me in?"

The rhythmic clicking of the needle ceased abruptly.

"Took you in! What's the child talking about? Who's took you in?"

"Why, mamma. If she didn't want me, why did she take me in?"

Even as I posed this logically unanswerable question with a heart full of dignified resentment, I felt a pang of relief that she had. The vision of being rejected at the bedroom door flashed through my mind. I imagined the stork, perplexed and annoyed, looking much like Tom Pinfold when his fish was sniffed at by Anna, and the kitchen door slamming shut in his face. Would the stork scratch his head with those long, compass-like legs and mutter to himself? And how had he carried me? I had watched blackbirds carry worms, and the worms always looked nervous and uncomfortable. Had I wriggled and squirmed inside the bird? Where would the stork have taken me? Possibly to Mrs. Fursey's cottage, the nearest neighbor. But I was sure she wouldn't have taken me. Next door lived Mr. Chumdley, the cobbler, a lame man who sat all day hammering boots with dirty hands in a cave half-underground, looking like a poor-spirited ogre. I would have hated being his little boy. Perhaps nobody would have taken me. I grew pensive, imagining myself as the rejected child of the entire village. What would the stork do with me, left on his hands?

"Nurse, where did I come from?"

"Why, I've told you often. The stork brought you."

"Yes, I know. But where did the stork get me from?" Mrs. Fursey paused for a long while, likely calculating whether such an answer might make me unduly conceited. Eventually, she decided to take the risk.

"Oh, from Heaven."

"But I thought Heaven was a place where you went to," I answered, "not where you comed from." I recall using "comed" because my irregular verbs were a source of great anxiety to my mother. "Comed" and "goned" were my favorites.

Mrs. Fursey passed over my grammar in dignified silence. She had been instructed not to worry about my education, as diverging opinions only confused a child.

"You came from Heaven," she repeated, "and you'll go to Heaven—if you're good."

"Do all little boys and girls come from Heaven?"

"So they say." Her tone implied this was a popular fallacy she personally did not endorse.

"And did you come from Heaven, Mrs. Fursey?" Her reply was decidedly more emphatic.

"Of course I did. Where do you think I came from?"

Immediately, Heaven lost its exalted position in my eyes. Everyone I knew was going there, but now, connected as it appeared to be with the origin of Mrs. Fursey, much of its charm evaporated.

But this was not all. Mrs. Fursey's information sparked a fresh grief. I stopped trying to console myself with the idea that my fate was shared by all children. With the egoism of youth, I seized only upon my own particular case.

"Didn't they want me in Heaven then, either?" I asked. "Weren't they fond of me up there?"

The misery in my voice must have pierced Mrs. Fursey's bosom, for she answered more sympathetically than usual.

"Oh, they liked you well enough, I daresay. I like you, but I like to get rid of you sometimes." There could be no doubt about the last part. Even then, I often wondered if that six o'clock bedtime was actually half-past five.

The answer offered no comfort. It was clear I was not wanted in Heaven nor on Earth. God was glad to get rid of me. My mother did not want me; she could have done without me. Nobody wanted me. Why was I here?

Then, like the sudden opening and shutting of a door in a dark room, a feeling entered my childish brain: Something, somewhere, must have needed me, or I could not exist. Something I belonged to, and that belonged to me, something as integral to me as I was to it. This feeling returned throughout my childhood, though I could never articulate it. Years later, the son of a Portuguese Jew explained my thought to me. All I knew at the time was that in that moment, I knew for the first time that I lived, that I was I.

The next instant, darkness returned, and I was once more a puzzled little boy by the nursery fire, questioning a village dame about life.

Suddenly, a new thought struck me—or rather, the recollection of an old one.

"Nurse, why haven't we got a husband?"

Mrs. Fursey stopped sewing and stared at me.

"What maggot has the child got into its head now? Who hasn't got a husband?"

"Why, mamma."

"Don't talk nonsense, Master Paul; you know your mamma has got a husband."

"No, she ain't."

"And don't contradict. Your mamma's husband is your papa, who lives in London."

"What's the good of _him_!"

Mrs. Fursey's reply seemed unnecessarily vehement.

"You wicked child, you; where's your commandments? Your father is in London working hard to earn money to keep you in idleness, and you sit there and say 'What's the good of him!' I'd be ashamed to be such an ungrateful little brat."

I hadn't meant to be ungrateful. My words were merely a repetition of a conversation I had overheard the previous day between my mother and my aunt.

My aunt had said: "There she goes, moping again. Drat me if ever I saw such a thing to mope as a woman."

My aunt was entitled to preach on the subject. She grumbled all day about everything, but she did it cheerfully.

My mother stood with her hands clasped behind her—a favorite pose—gazing through the high French window into the garden beyond. It must have been spring, for I remembered the white and yellow crocuses decking the grass.

"I want a husband," my mother had answered, in a tone so ludicrously childish that I had looked up from my fairy story, half-expecting her to transform into a little girl; "I hate not having a husband."

"Help us and save us," my aunt had retorted; "how many more does a girl want? She's got one."

"What's the good of him all that way off," my mother had pouted; "I want him here where I can get at him."

I had often heard of this father of mine, living far away in London, to whom we owed all the blessings of life. However, my childish attempts to reconcile information with reflection had led me to assign him an entirely spiritual existence. I agreed with my mother that such a figure, however revered, was no substitute for the flesh-and-blood father possessed by luckier folk—the big, strong, masculine thing that could carry a fellow piggyback round the garden or take a chap sailing in boats.

"You don't understand me, nurse," I explained; "what I mean is a husband you can get at."

"Well, and you'll 'get at him,' poor gentleman, one of these days," answered Mrs. Fursey. "When he's ready for you he'll send for you, and then you'll go to him in London."

I felt Mrs. Fursey still didn't understand. But I foresaw that further explanation would only shock her, so I settled for a simple, matter-of-fact question.

"How do you get to London; do you have to die first?"

"I do think," said Mrs. Fursey, in the voice of resigned despair rather than surprise, "that, without exception, you are the silliest little boy I ever came across. I've no patience with you."

"I am very sorry, nurse," I answered; "I thought—"

"Then," interrupted Mrs. Fursey, in the voice of many generations, "you shouldn't think. London," continued the good dame, her experience suggesting that the shortest road to peace lay through my understanding of this matter, "is a big town, and you go there in a train. Some time—soon now—your father will write to your mother that everything is ready. Then you and your mother and your aunt will leave this place and go to London, and I shall be rid of you."

"And shan't we come back here ever any more?"

"Never again."

"And I'll never play in the garden again, never go down to the pebble-ridge to tea, or to Jacob's tower?"

"Never again." I think Mrs. Fursey took a pleasure in the phrase. It sounded, as she said it, like something out of the prayer-book.

"And I'll never see Anna, or Tom Pinfold, or old Yeo, or Pincher, or you, ever any more?" In this moment of crumbling footholds, I would have clung even to that dry tuft, Mrs. Fursey herself.

"Never any more. You'll go away and begin an entirely new life. And I do hope, Master Paul," added Mrs. Fursey, piously, "it may be a better one. That you will make up your mind to—"

But Mrs. Fursey's well-meant exhortations fell upon deaf ears. Here I was face to face with yet another problem. This life into which I had fallen: it was understandable! One went away, leaving the pleasant places one knew, never to return. One left one's labor and play to enter a new existence in a strange land. One parted from friends one had always known, to see them never again. Life was indeed a strange thing; and if a body comprehended it, then must a body sit staring into the fire, thinking very hard, unheedful of all idle chatter.

That night, when my mother came to kiss me good-night, I turned my face to the wall and pretended to be asleep, for children as well as grown-ups have their foolish moods. But when I felt the soft curls brush my cheek, my pride gave way. Clasping my arms about her neck and drawing her face closer, I voiced the question that had been knocking at my heart all evening:

"I suppose you couldn't send me back now, could you? You see, you've had me so long."

"Send you back?"

"Yes. I'd be too big for the stork to carry now, wouldn't I?"

My mother knelt down beside the bed so that her face and mine were on a level. Looking into her eyes, the fear that had haunted me fell away.

"Who has been talking foolishly to a foolish little boy?" she asked, keeping my arms still clasped about her neck.

"Oh, nurse and I were discussing things, you know," I answered, "and she said you could have done without me." Somehow, I did not mind repeating the words now; clearly it could have been but Mrs. Fursey's fun.

My parents' situation was less than conventional. They had been separated for years, and while they weren't exactly teenagers—my father was nearing forty, my mother just past thirty—they possessed the restless energy of children trapped in adult bodies. To an outsider, I might have described my father as a man who could never find peace unless his wife was within shouting distance, constantly calling from his office door, "Maggie, are you busy? I need to discuss a matter of business!" and my mother as a woman whose eyes sparkled with affection whenever she looked at him. But this was a fiction I would only tell later. At the time, I was merely a witness to a household that operated on a different frequency.

Living with them required specific survival tactics. One must never stick one's feet out from under the table, lest they be mistaken for someone else's and crushed. Furthermore, entering a room without announcing oneself with a loud "noise without" was a recipe for disaster. You might find the door in your hand while inside, the occupants suddenly springing into the air, followed by a frantic scuttling of feet, only to discover them sitting stiffly in opposite corners, pretending to read or sew. Fortunately, such precautions were unnecessary in our home.

I, unfortunately, exerted little control over the domestic chaos. My presence usually elicited a sigh of relief from my parents—"Oh, it's only Spud"—accompanied by a thrown sofa cushion. My aunt, however, commanded a different kind of awe. She never spoke, yet her presence was deafening. Every step she took was accompanied by a sustained, penetrating cough. My father once grumbled that the volume of barking she produced in a single day would have cured a hospital ward of consumptives. To ensure no one saw anything they shouldn't, she would enter rooms backward, slamming the door shut before turning around, and navigate dark passages while singing. Despite her efforts to spare their feelings, neither parent was grateful. In fact, the more sympathy she showed, the more irritated they became.

"I believe, Fanny, you hate seeing Luke and me happy together," my mother snapped one day, finding my aunt dropping teaspoons at five-second intervals outside the drawing-room door. "Don't make yourself so ridiculous."

"Hate it!" my aunt replied, her voice surprisingly mild. "Why shouldn't a pair of turtle doves bill and coo when their combined age is barely seventy?"

My father, meanwhile, turned vindictive. I remember him stopping abruptly while we walked by the canal bank. "Your aunt," he said, gesturing toward the setting sun, "is the most cantankerous creature. I wish to God she had a comfortable income and a cottage in the country!" He paused, ashamed of his cruelty, and added, "Not that she can't be nice sometimes. Don't tell your mother what I said."

Our maid, Susan, offered a different perspective. She was a stout, elderly woman prone to sudden naps, likely due to trouble, but she claimed her heart was always in the right place. She would weep whenever she saw my parents together, reminiscing about her own lost marital glory. Her stories of her late husband were a confusing mix of grief and rage. On Monday, she cried over Brussels sprouts because she couldn't visit his grave at Manor Park. On Tuesday, she spoke of choking the scruff of his neck.

"But I thought he was dead," I asked.

"So did I, Master Paul," she replied. "That was his artfulness."

"Then he isn't buried?"

"Not yet. But he'll wish he was when I get hold of him."

"Was he a good man?"

"Who?"

"Your husband."

"Who says he ain't?" Susan's mood shifted instantly. "If anyone says he ain't, I'll gouge their eye out!"

She would then explain that her husband brought her a bottle of gin every time he came home. Confused by her erratic logic, I would retreat to the safety of German grammar or mixed fractions.

Susan's dismissal came on a Saturday night. My mother, looking pale, sent my father for the doctor. Five minutes later, a large, black-bearded man named Dr. Washburn burst into the kitchen, shaking the entire house. He brushed my mother aside, bent over the unconscious Susan, and then erupted into a roar of laughter that I had never heard before. Seizing a half-full pail of water, he flung it over the woman. She sat up, blinking.

"Feeling better?" he asked, still holding the pail. "Have another dose?"

Before Susan could respond, he shoved us all outside and slammed the door. From the stairs, we heard her thick, rancorous voice raging, drowned periodically by his savage laughter. When she flagged for breath, he would yell, "Bravo! Go it, my beauty! Bark, bark! I love to hear you!"

"What a beast of a man," my mother whispered.

"He's a character," my father noted. "Worshipped all over the place."

"I don't intend to know him," my mother said stiffly.

Later, when the doctor returned, he politely declined payment, asking instead for a glass of beer and a chop, which he promised to cook for himself and us. He declared that Susan was a mistake and that he would return in an hour to ensure she left. He gave my mother a month's wages and told her to prepare a fire.

That evening, the house was transformed. The doctor's laughter swept through the rooms, even making my aunt chuckle. I, feeling sick with indigestion, wrapped my blanket around my legs and descended to the fire. My mother, holding a painted screen to shield her face, looked radiant. My father seemed larger and more dignified. Aunt Fan was witty. We all laughed at my silly questions, and I decided to ask them again tomorrow.

Dr. Washburn, or "Dr. Fighting Hal" as he was known in the slums, possessed a magnetic vitality that drew the best out of everyone. In his presence, clever people became geniuses, and dullards felt brilliant. He was a paradox: sometimes a brute with brutish instincts, other times an angel with soft blue eyes. I wondered if knowing him was good or bad for me, much like a tree wondering if the wind shaped or misshaped it.

Susan's departure left a void. My parents pretended to wait for a "better specimen," but months passed. My father would indignantly ask why "Jane" or "Mary" hadn't answered the door, while my mother hid behind a thick veil to fetch groceries herself, terrified that neighbors would know the Kelvers kept no servants. We were a family ashamed of our poverty, living a life of petty subterfuge. The larder was often bare, yet the sideboard held port and sherry; the fire was low so my father's hat might shine on Sundays.

Yet, I didn't sneer at them. I understood the heroic struggle of hiding one's poverty. Dr. Washburn would have shared his bread and cheese with royalty, but my parents served weak tea so guests might have wine. We shared a single egg, arguing over the white, and I rose satisfied despite the meager meal. Life was a game of fun and laughter. We cleaned the doorstep at night, pretending to be a tall charwoman, and I watched the gate to warn my father when he groped for the hearthstone in the dark.

My father and I grew close. He was the youngest thing I had ever met. His hair was greying, his face lined, but his eyes and laugh remained boyish. We played cricket in our narrow garden, inventing rules to avoid breaking windows or losing the ball to urchins. We walked the marshes, dreaming of Oxford and a future free from the "grim castle" of Hard Times.

One day, walking through the shrill streets, I told him the words my mother had spoken years ago: that he was our Prince fighting the dragon of Poverty.

"And she was right, Paul," he whispered, gripping my hand. "I will fight him and I will slay him." He made passes with his walking-stick, and the crowd parted as if he were mad. "I will batter down the iron gates and she shall be free."

How long and bravely he fought! But eventually, the Dragon triumphed. The Knight lay on the ground, his heart still beating. I had read that Honest Industry could conquer Poverty, but that was foolish fairy tales. In truth, only the sword of Chicanery and the buckler of Unscrupulousness guarantee victory. If you refuse those weapons, you must pray to your gods and take what comes with a stout heart.

"Louisa!" roared my father down the kitchen stairs, "are you all asleep? Here have I had to answer the front door myself." Then my father strode into his office, and the door slammed. My father could be very angry when nobody was by.

Quarter of an hour later his bell rang with a quick, authoritative jangle. My mother, who was peeling potatoes with difficulty in wash-leather gloves, looked at my aunt who was shelling peas. The bell rang again louder still this time.

"Once for Louisa, twice for James, isn't it?" enquired my aunt.

"You go, Paul," said my mother; "say that Louisa--" but with the words a sudden flush overspread my mother's face, and before I could lay down my slate she had drawn off her gloves and had passed me. "No, don't stop your lessons, I'll go myself," she said, and ran out.

A few minutes later the kitchen door opened softly, and my mother's hand, appearing through the jar, beckoned to me mysteriously.

"Walk on your toes," whispered my mother, setting the example as she led the way up the stairs; which after the manner of stairs showed their disapproval of deception by creaking louder and more often than under any other circumstances; and in this manner we reached my parents' bedroom, where, in the old-fashioned wardrobe, relic of better days, reposed my best suit of clothes, or, to be strictly grammatical, my better.

Never before had I worn these on a week-day morning, but all conversation not germane to the question of getting into them quickly my mother swept aside; and when I was complete, down even to the new shoes--Bluchers, we called them in those days--took me by the hand, and together we crept down as we had crept up, silent, stealthy and alert. My mother led me to the street door and opened it.

"Shan't I want my cap?" I whispered. But my mother only shook her head and closed the door with a bang; and then the explanation of the pantomime came to me, for with such "business"--comic, shall I call it, or tragic?--I was becoming familiar; and, my mother's hand upon my shoulder, we entered my father's office.

Whether from the fact that so often of an evening--our drawing-room being reserved always as a show-room in case of chance visitors; Cowper's poems, open face-downwards on the wobbly loo table; the half-finished crochet work, suggestive of elegant leisure, thrown carelessly over the arm of the smaller easy-chair--this office would become our sitting-room, its books and papers, as things of no account, being huddled out of sight; or whether from the readiness with which my father would come out of it at all times to play at something else--at cricket in the back garden on dry days or ninepins in the passage on wet, charging back into it again whenever a knock sounded at the front door, I cannot say. But I know that as a child it never occurred to me to regard my father's profession as a serious affair. To me he was merely playing there, surrounded by big books and bundles of documents, labelled profusely but consisting only of blank papers; by japanned tin boxes, lettered imposingly, but for the most part empty. "Sutton Hampden, Esq.," I remember was practically my mother's work-box. The "Drayton Estates" yielded apparently nothing but apples, a fruit of which my father was fond; while "Mortgages" it was not until later in life I discovered had no connection with poems in manuscript, some in course of correction, others completed.

Now, as the door opened, he rose and came towards us. His hair stood up from his head, for it was a habit of his to rumple it as he talked; and this added to his evident efforts to compose his face into an expression of businesslike gravity, added emphasis, if such were needed, to the suggestion of the over long schoolboy making believe.

"This is the youngster," said my father, taking me from my mother, and passing me on. "Tall for his age, isn't he?"

With a twist of his thick lips, he rolled the evil-smelling cigar he was smoking from the left corner of his mouth to the right; and held out a fat and not too clean hand, which, as it closed round mine, brought to my mind the picture of the walrus in my natural history book; with the other he flapped me kindly on the head.

"Like 'is mother, wonderfully like 'is mother, ain't 'e?" he observed, still holding my hand. "And that," he added with a wink of one of his small eyes towards my father, "is about the 'ighest compliment I can pay 'im, eh?"

His eyes were remarkably small, but marvellously bright and piercing; so much so that when he turned them again upon me I tried to think quickly of something nice about him, feeling sure that he could see right into me.

"And where are you thinkin' of sendin' 'im?" he continued; "Eton or 'Arrow?"

"We haven't quite made up our minds as yet," replied my father; "at present we are educating him at home."

"You take my tip," said the fat man, "and learn all you can. Look at me! If I'd 'ad the opportunity of being a schollard I wouldn't be here offering your father an extravagant price for doin' my work; I'd be able to do it myself."

"You seem to have got on very well without it," laughed my father; and in truth his air of prosperity might have justified greater self-complacency. Rings sparkled on his blunt fingers, and upon the swelling billows of his waistcoat rose and sank a massive gold cable.

"I'd 'ave done better with it," he grunted.

"But you look very clever," I said; and though divining with a child's cuteness that it was desired I should make a favourable impression upon him, I hoped this would please him, the words were yet spontaneous.

He laughed heartily, his whole body shaking like some huge jelly.

"Well, old Noel Hasluck's not exactly a fool," he assented, "but I'd like myself better if I could talk about something else than business, and didn't drop my aitches. And so would my little gell."

"You have a daughter?" asked my mother, with whom a child, as a bond of sympathy with the stranger took the place assigned by most women to disrespectful cooks and incompetent housemaids.

"I won't tell you about 'er. But I'll just bring 'er to see you now and then, ma'am, if you don't mind," answered Mr. Hasluck. "She don't often meet gentle-folks, an' it'll do 'er good."

My mother glanced across at my father, but the man, intercepting her question, replied to it himself.

"You needn't be afraid, ma'am, that she's anything like me," he assured her quite good-temperedly; "nobody ever believes she's my daughter, except me and the old woman. She's a little lady, she is. Freak o' nature, I call it."

"We shall be delighted," explained my mother.

"Well, you will when you see 'er," replied Mr. Hasluck, quite contentedly.

He pushed half-a-crown into my hand, overriding my parents' susceptibilities with the easy good-temper of a man accustomed to have his way in all things.

"No squanderin' it on the 'eathen," was his parting injunction as I left the room; "you spend that on a Christian tradesman."

It was the first money I ever remember having to spend, that half-crown of old Hasluck's; suggestions of the delights to be derived from a new pair of gloves for Sunday, from a Latin grammar, which would then be all my own, and so on, having hitherto displaced all less exalted visions concerning the disposal of chance coins coming into my small hands. But on this occasion I was left free to decide for myself.

The anxiety it gave me! the long tossing hours in bed! the tramping of the bewildering streets! Even advice when asked for was denied me.

"You must learn to think for yourself," said my father, who spoke eloquently on the necessity of early acquiring sound judgment and what he called "commercial aptitude."

"No, dear," said my mother, "Mr. Hasluck wanted you to spend it as you like. If I told you, that would be spending it as I liked. Your father and I want to see what you will do with it."

The good little boys in the books bought presents or gave away to people in distress. For this I hated them with the malignity the lower nature ever feels towards the higher. I consulted my aunt Fan.

"If somebody gave you half-a-crown," I put it to her, "what would you buy with it?"

"Side-combs," said my aunt; she was always losing or breaking her side-combs.

"But I mean if you were me," I explained.

"Drat the child!" said my aunt; "how do I know what he wants if he don't know himself? Idiot!"

The shop windows into which I stared, my nose glued to the pane! The things I asked the price of! The things I made up my mind to buy and then decided that I wouldn't buy! Even my patient mother began to show signs of irritation. It was rapidly assuming the dimensions of a family curse, was old Hasluck's half-crown.

Then one day I made up my mind, and so ended the trouble. In the window of a small plumber's shop in a back street near, stood on view among brass taps, rolls of lead piping and cistern requisites, various squares of coloured glass, the sort of thing chiefly used, I believe, for lavatory doors and staircase windows. Some had stars in the centre, and others, more elaborate, were enriched with designs, severe but inoffensive. I purchased a dozen of these, the plumber, an affable man who appeared glad to see me, throwing in two extra out of sheer generosity.

Why I bought them I did not know at the time, and I do not know now. My mother cried when she saw them. My father could get no further than: "But what are you going to do with them?" to which I was unable to reply. My aunt, alone, attempted comfort.

"If a person fancies coloured glass," said my aunt, "then he's a fool not to buy coloured glass when he gets the chance. We haven't all the same tastes."

In the end, I cut myself badly with them and consented to their being thrown into the dust-bin. But looking back, I have come to regard myself rather as the victim of Fate than of Folly. Many folks have I met since, recipients of Hasluck's half-crowns--many a man who has slapped his pocket and blessed the day he first met that "Napoleon of Finance," as later he came to be known among his friends--but it ever ended so; coloured glass and cut fingers. Is it fairy gold that he and his kind fling round? It would seem to be.

Next time old Hasluck knocked at our front door a maid in cap and apron opened it to him, and this was but the beginning of change. New oilcloth glistened in the passage. Lace curtains, such as in that neighbourhood were the hall-mark of the plutocrat, advertised our rising fortunes to the street, and greatest marvel of all, at least to my awed eyes, my father's Sunday clothes came into weekday wear, new ones taking their place in the great wardrobe that hitherto had been the stronghold of our gentility; to which we had ever turned for comfort when rendered despondent by contemplation of the weakness of our outer walls. "Seeing that everything was all right" is how my mother would explain it. She would lay the lilac silk upon the bed, fondly soothing down its rustling undulations, lingering lovingly over its deep frosted flounces of rich Honiton. Maybe she had entered the room weary looking and depressed, but soon there would proceed from her a gentle humming as from some small winged thing when the sun first touches it and warms it, and sometimes by the time the Indian shawl, which could go through a wedding ring, but never would when it was wanted to, had been refolded and fastened again with the great cameo brooch, and the poke bonnet, like some fractious child, shaken and petted into good condition, she would be singing softly to herself, nodding her head to the words: which were generally to the effect that somebody was too old and somebody else too bold and another too cold, "so he wouldn't do for me;" and stepping lightly as though the burden of the years had fallen from her.

One evening--it was before the advent of this Hasluck--I remember climbing out of bed, for trouble was within me. Creatures, indescribable but heavy, had sat upon my chest, after which I had fallen downstairs, slowly and reasonably for the first few hundred flights, then with haste for the next million miles or so, until I found myself in the street with nothing on but my nightshirt. Personally, I was shocked, but nobody else seemed to mind, and I hailed a two-penny 'bus and climbed in. But when I tried to pay I found I hadn't any pockets, so I jumped out and ran away and the conductor came after me. My feet were like lead, and with every step he gained on me, till with a scream I made one mighty effort and awoke.

Feeling the need of comfort after these unpleasant but by no means unfamiliar experiences, I wrapped some clothes round me and crept downstairs. The "office" was dark, but to my surprise a light shone from under the drawing-room door, and I opened it.

The East India Dock Road, now a cacophony of trams, omnibuses, and ceaseless footfalls, was once a silent, echoing corridor. Bounded by the long, hollow wall of the docks and scattered market gardens, it felt less like a street and more like a boundary line between worlds. At its start stood a single brick cottage, the former toll-house, a landmark that marked the precise moment my childhood fell away.

That afternoon, I had visited the family dentist in Plaistow, a picturesque village then, now largely swallowed by the city. My mother, ever practical, had tucked a half-crown into my knickerbocker pocket, instructing me to return it if the dentist refused it. He was a kind, elderly bachelor, a distant relation perhaps, who treated me with the solemnity of a king in his velvet chair. He would unroll his terrifying array of instruments, recommending the most murderous-looking ones with affectionate praise. But before I could express my terror, a pair of forceps would spring from his sleeve, and the ordeal would end in a shared cup of tea.

His house stood in a great garden, and his housekeeper produced jams of legendary quality. We spoke of my mother, who, he claimed, was once a prankish, wilful little girl—a tale she would laugh off, insisting we gossip about something more sensible, yet always asking, "And what else did he say about me?" After tea, he would produce his great microscope, revealing a fairy world of strange dragons and mighty monsters to me. To a child, he was a harmless magician; to me, looking back, he was a tiny man with a heart as large as any giant.

Leaving the gate, I would hand him the coin with formal thanks. But stepping outside, I would invariably find it still in my pocket. Once, I returned it, but he denied knowing anything of it, suggesting half-crowns were slippery things.

On this particular day, returning from Plaistow, I paused on the bridge watching the lazy barges. It was a hushed summer evening, and as I passed through the white toll-gate, I felt a profound sense of leaving myself behind. I looked back, expecting to see the little boy still leaning over the parapet. He was gone. He passed away from me as a man's body passes, leaving only remembrance. I tried to play his games, to dream his dreams, but the substance was missing. I was a thin ghost making believe.

This realization troubled me deeply. I did not want to grow up; the dark road of adulthood frightened me. But slowly, over months and years, a new consciousness took root. Little Paul faded, and I became someone else. Before letting him go entirely, I must recall the figures who remained on my childhood stage.

First was my Aunt Fan, crouching over the kitchen fire, her skirt rolled up to reveal only her petticoat. She swayed rhythmically, stroking her knees, while I, armed with a paper knife or broomstick, played knight, Indian brave, or Fairy Prince. She was the wicked ogre, Laughing Water, the Sleeping Beauty, or the Cannibal Chief. Her compliance was rarely pure self-sacrifice; often she suggested the game, casting herself as the heavy lead—the dragons, the wicked uncles, the fussy necromancers. As an authoress of a cookery book for giant-land, she would have been brilliant. She planned complicated slaughters with gusto, detailing the sharpening of knives and the cleaning of mops.

"I think, my dear," she would direct, "we'll have him stuffed with chestnuts and served on toast. And don't forget the giblets. They make such excellent sauce."

She would transform the most beautiful damsels into Glyptodons or Hippocrepians, and warriors into plaice or prawns. Her death scenes were conscientious efforts, ending with a final gurgle followed by the lowering of her skirt to signal the fall of the curtain, immediately followed by tea preparation.

Another recurring figure was a small, curly-haired girl I met in the dingy streets. She ran ahead, turning to admire my face, captivated by my praise. She protected me against my baser self, nerving me to face sudden foes. Together, we courted adventure; to me, a simple walk, to her, an expedition full of danger. She held my hand in lonely places and possessed a vocabulary that could silence the blind idiots who sought to walk through us.

"Now, then, where yer coming to, old glass-eye? We ain't sperrits. Can't yer see us?" she would shout, her child's treble rising shrilly.

"If they attempted reply, her child's treble... would only rise more shrill."

She was unwise, introducing me to the shop, for children and dogs are snobbish little wretches. Her father was a fishmonger—a "fried fishmonger," as I noted, which placed us outside the pale of respectable society. Despite her father's trade, she waited for me daily in Stainsby Road, her little face pressed against the iron railings until I came out or shook my head from my bedroom window. Tears would trace paths on her dust-besprinkled cheeks, until the advent of Barbara put an end to those meetings.

So began and ended my first romance. One day, I shall walk into the little grocer's shop and boldly ask to see her. I know she is now the mother of a numerous progeny, likely stout and sharp-tongued. Yet under the clumsy-handed world's realities, I shall see the lithesome little maid with fond, admiring eyes. What help they were to me I never knew till I had lost them. How hard to gain such eyes I have learned since.

Among the remaining figures were the servants of our house, the "generals" of the kitchen. They were mostly discontented or disappointed souls, recruited from the neighborhood. They were poor at "making believe" but would squeeze ten minutes from their work to romp with me. Encouraged by their admiring faces, I would "hold forth," regarding myself as among the Immortals.

One girl, Janet, stayed for months. The daughter of the captain of the barge "Nancy Jane," her conversation was a mix of terror and delight. When my mother exclaimed in agony at Janet's choice of words for the gas man, Janet would defend herself naturally. It was Janet and I who undertook her reformation. I told her bluntly that swearing would send her to hell.

"Then where's my father going?" demanded Janet.

"Does he use language?"

"I am afraid, Janet," I explained, "that if he doesn't give it up—"

"But it's the only way he can talk," interrupted Janet. "He don't mean anything by it."

I sighed, yet set my face against weakness. "You see, Janet, people who swear do go there."

But Janet would not believe. "God send my dear, kind father to hell just because he can't talk like the gentlefolks! Don't you believe it of Him, Master Paul. He's got more sense."

These words comforted me when sinking in sloughs of childish despond. More often than I can tell, when compelled to listen to the sententious voice of immeasurable Folly glibly explaining the eternal mysteries, has it comforted me to whisper to myself: "I don't believe it of Him. He's got more sense."

About that period, I needed all the comfort I could get. As we descend the road of life, the journey becomes more important than the end; but to the child, the terminating hills are clearly visible. What lies beyond is constant wonder. I never questioned my parents directly on the subject of death or the afterlife, shrinking as we all do from discussing matters of most moment. They, on their part, offered vague generalities. But foolish voices about me were less reticent. Literature provided serious-minded youth with blunt brutality: if you did wrong, you burnt in a fiery furnace for ever and ever.

I had been burnt once, rather badly, by live coals falling on me during transit on a shovel. I imagined these burning coals, not confined to a mere part of my body, but pressing upon me everywhere, eating into my flesh and veins. This continued for eternity. You suffered for an hour, a day, a thousand years, and were no nearer to the end; ten thousand, a million years, and yet, as at the very first, it was for ever, and for ever still it would always be for ever!

"Then be good," replied the foolish voices round me; "never do wrong, and so avoid this endless agony."

But it was so easy to do wrong. There were so many wrong things to do, and the doing of them was so natural.

"Then repent," said the voices, always ready.

But how did one repent? What was repentance? Did I "hate my sin," as I was instructed I must, or merely hate the idea of going to hell for it? Because the latter, even my child's sense told me, was no true repentance. Yet how could one know the difference?

Above all else haunted me the fear of the "Unforgivable Sin." What this was I was never able to discover. I dreaded to enquire too closely, lest I should find I had committed it. Day and night the terror of it clung to me.

"Believe," said the voices; "so only shall you be saved." How believe? How know you did believe? Hours would I kneel in the dark, repeating in a whispered scream: "I believe, I believe. Oh, I do believe!" and then rise with white knuckles, wondering if I really did believe.

Another question rose to trouble me. In the course of my meanderings, I had made the acquaintance of an old sailor, one of the most disreputable specimens possible to find; and had learned to love him. Our first meeting had been outside a confectioner's window in the Commercial Road, where he had discovered me standing, my nose against the glass, a mere palpitating Appetite on legs. He had seized me by the collar, hauled me into the shop, dropped me upon a stool, and bade me eat. Pride of race prompted me politely to decline, but his language became so awful that in fear and trembling I obeyed. So soon as I was finished—it cost him two and fourpence, I remember—we walked down to the docks together, and he told me stories of the sea and land that made my blood run cold.

Altogether, in the course of three weeks or a month, we met about half a dozen times, when much the same programme was gone through. I think I was a fairly frank child, but I said nothing about him at home, feeling instinctively that if I did there would be an end of our comradeship, which was dear to me: not merely by reason of the pastry, though I admit that was a consideration, but also for his wondrous tales. I believed them all implicitly, and so came to regard him as one of the most interesting criminals as yet unhanged: and what was sad about the case, as I felt myself, was that his recital of his many iniquities, instead of repelling, attracted me to him. If ever there existed a sinner, here was one. He chewed tobacco—one of the hundred or so deadly sins, according to my theological library—and was generally more or less drunk. Not that a stranger would have noticed this; the only difference being that when sober he appeared constrained—was less his natural, genial self. In a burst of confidence he once admitted to me that he was the biggest blackguard in the merchant service. Unacquainted with the merchant service, as at the time I was, I saw no reason to doubt him.

One night in a state of intoxication he walked over a gangway and was drowned. Our mutual friend, the confectioner, seeing me pass the window, came out to tell me so; and having heard, I walked on, heavy of heart, and pondering.

About his eternal destination there could be no question. The known facts precluded the least ray of hope. How could I be happy in heaven, supposing I eventually did succeed in slipping in, knowing that he, the lovable old scamp, was burning for ever in hell?

How could Janet, taking it that she reformed and thus escaped damnation, be contented, knowing the father she loved doomed to torment? The heavenly hosts, so I argued, could be composed only of the callous and indifferent.

I wondered how people could go about their business, eat, drink and be merry, with tremendous fate hanging thus ever suspended over their heads. When for a little space I myself forgot it, always it fell back upon me with increased weight.

Nor was the contemplation of heaven itself particularly attractive to me, for it was a foolish paradise these foolish voices had fashioned out of their folly. You stood about and sang hymns—for ever! I was assured that my fear of finding the programme monotonous was due only to my state of original sin, that when I got there I should discover I liked it. But I would have given much for the hope of avoiding both their heaven and their hell.

Fortunately for my sanity I was not left long to brood unoccupied upon such themes. Our worldly affairs, under the sunshine of old Hasluck's round red face, prospered—for awhile; and one afternoon my father, who had been away from home since breakfast time, calling me into his office where also sat my mother, informed me that the long-talked-of school was become at last a concrete thing.

"The term commences next week," explained my father. "It is not exactly what I had intended, but it will do—for the present. Later, of course, you will go to one of the big public schools; your mother and I have not yet quite decided which."

"You will meet other boys there, good and bad," said my mother, who sat clasping and unclasping her hands. "Be very careful, dear, how you choose your companions."

"You will learn to take your own part," said my father. "School is an epitome of the world. One must assert oneself, or one is sat upon."

I knew not what to reply, the vista thus opened out to me was so unexpected.

"Correct." That was the single word that defined Doctor Florret, and by extension, the entire universe he inhabited. He was a man of substantial proportions—neither towering nor shrinking, but precisely the size one might choose for the headmaster of a respectable middle-class school. Stout, yes, but not fat; a physique that suggested comfortable stability rather than gluttony. His hands were white and elegant, adorned on the left with a diamond ring that possessed a dignified shine rather than a flashy sparkle. He spoke of mundane weather with a gravity that made the forecast sound like a state declaration. His face, perpetually clean-shaven, radiated a benignity carefully tempered by discretion.

Everything around him mirrored this perfection. His wife was his perfect counterpart, a feminine echo fashioned specifically to complement his own rigid symmetry. Their furniture was built for utility, not ornamentation; their pictures hung with the modesty of a well-dressed man, gracing the walls without demanding attention. Doctor Florret always said the correct thing, at the correct time, in the correct manner. To doubt him was to invite revolution; to disagree with him was to declare oneself a traitor to order.

In practice, however, as I soon learned at the cost of four wasted years, he adhered strictly to the "correct" methods of English schooling dating back to the reign of Edward VI.

I worked harder than any boy I knew. Ambition, that all-consuming fire of youth that refuses to settle into a single shape, burned within me. If I passed a street orator, hatless and sweating, gesturing utopias to hungry crowds, I saw myself instantly: a Demosthenes swaying the masses, a statesman holding Parliament spellbound, the Prime Minister of England, worshipped by the nation. Even the opposition papers, I imagined, would be forced into reluctant admiration. If the echo of a distant drum reached my ears, I envisioned fields of carnage, with myself standing conspicuous in the front line. Since promotion in the British Army was supposedly a matter of pure merit, I pictured myself returning as Commander-in-Chief. Vast crowds would throng the streets; white hands would wave from every window. I would hear the deep roar of welcome as I paced at the head of my victorious troops on a snow-white charger—or perhaps a coal-black one? The color of the horse cost me much deliberation, for I was determined that not a single flaw should mar the day. I would walk into a lamppost or step on the toes of an irascible old gentleman and wake up.

Instead, a drunken sailor stormed out of a pub, shouting "Ay, ay, sir!" while factory chimneys belched smoke like swaying masts. Costers around me cheered, "Ready, ay, ready." I was Christopher Columbus, Drake, and Nelson rolled into one. I spurned modern geographers and discovered new continents. I defeated the French—those useful French! I died in the moment of victory, only to be mourned by a nation and buried in Westminster Abbey. Or, alternatively, I lived and was created a Duke. Personally, I was indifferent to either outcome.

While boys on November the ninth, reading letters from their mothers about severe toothaches, were dismissed by Doctor Florret with a snort, I listened to them on November the tenth recount the glories of the Lord Mayor's Show. Their chatter faded into the distance, replaced by the ringing of Bow bells. I saw myself as a young merchant prince, nobles crowding my counting house, lending them millions and marrying their daughters. I sat unobserved in a corner listening to discussions on new books and immediately became a famous author. I wrote poetry, fiction, and history, and all men read and wondered. The only snag was a crumpled rose leaf in my pillow; penmanship was a vexation, and spelling a puzzle, so I wrote with sorrow, blotting and scratching out my mistakes until I almost abandoned the idea entirely.

Yet, regardless of the path to fame, I dimly understood that education was the necessary weapon. With an aching heart and an aching head, I pored over my books. I saw myself in my small bedroom, elbows planted on a shaky, one-legged table, startled by my hair frizzing against a solitary candle. On cold nights, I wore my overcoat turned up about my neck, a blanket round my legs, often sitting with my fingers in my ears to shut out the importunate sounds of life rising from below. *"A song, of a song, to a song, O! song!"* *"I love, thou lovest, he she or it loves."* The words repeated until my own voice sounded like a strange buzzing thing, my head shrinking until I raised my hands in fright, wondering if it was still entire upon my shoulders.

Was I more stupid than the average, or was a boy's brain physically incapable of the work our educational system demanded?

*"Latin and Greek,"* I heard Doctor Florret repeat in his suave tones, echoing the solemn croak of Correctness, *"are useful as mental gymnastics."* My dear Doctor Florret and Company, could you not, from your vast storehouse of necessary knowledge, select apparatus better fitted to strengthen rather than overstrain the mental muscles of ten-to-fourteen-year-olds? You, gentle reader, with a fully grown brain trained by years of practice, take me from your shelf—say, Browning or even Shakespeare. This is your mother tongue. Construe this short passage; parse, analyze, resolve it into component parts! And now, will you maintain that it is good for Tommy, the tear-stained, ink-bespattered little brat, to treat Aesop's Fables or Ovid's Metamorphoses in the same manner? Would it not be just as sensible to insist that he practice his skinny little arms with hundred-pound dumbbells?

We were the sons of City men, of minor officials, clerks, and shopkeepers, our roads leading through the workaday world. Yet, quite half our time was consumed by studies utterly useless to us. How I hated these youth-tormenting shades! Homer! How I wished the fishermen had asked him that absurd riddle earlier. Horace! Why could not that shipwreck have succeeded? It would have worked for anyone but a classic.

Then, on one blessed day, a wondrous talisman fell into my hands.

*Hearken unto me, ye heavy-burdened little brethren.* Waste not your substance upon tops and marbles, nor yet upon tuck (do ye still call it "tuck"?), but scrape and save. Knowledge of yet greater value I owed to the good fortune that his sound mother wit was ever at my disposal to correct my dreamy unfeasibility. From first to last, he was my friend. To be the chosen friend of Dan, a shrewd judge of men and boys, I deemed no unimportant feather in my cap. He "took to" me because I was so "jolly green"—"such a rummy little mug." No other reason would he ever give, save a sweet smile and a tumbling of my hair with his great hand. But I think I understood. I loved him because he was big, strong, handsome, and kind. No one but a little boy knows how brutal or how kind a big boy can be. I was still somewhat of an effeminate little chap, nervous and shy, with a pink and white face and hair that no amount of wetting would make straight. I was growing too fast, which took all my strength, and my daily journey, added to school and home work, was perhaps too much for my years.

Every morning I had to be up at six, leaving the house before seven to catch the 7:15 from Poplar station. From Chalk Farm, I had to walk another couple of miles. He always knew, and oh, how strong and restful it was to lean against him, so that one walked as in an easy chair.

It seems to me, remembering how I would walk thus by his side, looking up shyly into his face, thinking how strong and good he was, feeling so glad he liked me, that I can understand a little how a woman loves. He was so solid. With his arm round me, it was good to feel weak.

At first, we were in the same class, the Lower Third. He had no business there. He was head and shoulders taller than any of us and years older. It was a disgrace to him that he was not in the Upper Fourth. The Doctor would tell him so before us all twenty times a week. Old Waterhouse, who cordially liked him, was honestly grieved. We, his friends, though it was pleasant to have him among us, suffered in our pride of him. The only person quite contented was Dan himself. It was his way in all things. Others had their opinions of what was good for him; he had his own, and his own was the only opinion that ever influenced him. The Lower Third suited him. For him personally, the Upper Fourth had no attraction.

Even in the Lower Third, he was always at the bottom. He preferred it. He selected the seat and kept it, in spite of all allurements and reproaches. It was nearest to the door, enabling him to be first out and last in. Also, it afforded a certain sense of retirement. Its occupant, to an extent screened from observation, became almost forgotten. To Dan's philosophical temperament, its practical advantages outweighed all sentimental objections.

Only on one occasion do I remember him losing it. As a rule, tiresome questions concerning past participles, square roots, or meridians never reached him, being snapped up in transit by arm-waving lovers of such trifles. The few that by chance trickled so far he took no notice. They possessed no interest for him, and he never pretended that they did. But one day, taken off his guard, he gave voice quite unconsciously to a correct reply, with the immediate result of finding himself in an exposed position on the front bench. I had never seen Dan out of temper before, but that moment, had any of us ventured upon a whispered congratulation, we would have had our heads punched, I feel confident.

Old Waterhouse thought that here at last was reformation. "Come, Brian," he cried, rubbing his long thin hands together with delight, "after all, you're not such a fool as you pretend."

"Never said I was," muttered Dan to himself, with a backward glance of regret towards his lost seclusion; and before the day was out, he had worked his way back to it again.

As we were going out together, Old Waterhouse passed us on the stairs: "Haven't you any sense of shame, my boy?" he asked sorrowfully, laying his hand kindly on Dan's shoulder.

"Yes, sir," answered Dan, with his frank smile. "Plenty. It isn't yours, that's all."

My memory lingers upon this scene by contrast with the sad, changed days that swiftly followed, when my mother's eyes would flash towards my father angry gleams, and her voice ring cruel and hard; though the moment he was gone her lips would tremble and her eyes grow soft again and fill with tears; when my father would sit with averted face and sullen lips tight pressed, or worse, would open them only to pour forth a rapid flood of savage speech; and fling out of the room, slamming the door behind him, and I would find him hours afterwards, sitting alone in the dark, with bowed head between his hands.

Wretched, I would lie awake, hearing through the flimsy walls their passionate tones, now rising high, now fiercely forced into cold whispers; and then their words to each other sounded even crueller.

In their estrangement from each other, so new to them, both clung closer to me, though they would tell me nothing, nor should I have understood if they had. When my mother was sobbing softly, her arms clasping me tighter and tighter with each quivering throb, then I hated my father, who I felt had inflicted this sorrow upon her. Yet when my father drew me down upon his knee, and I looked into his kind eyes so full of pain, then I felt angry with my mother, remembering her bitter tongue.

It seemed to me as though some cruel, unseen thing had crept into the house to stand ever between them, so that they might never look into each other's loving eyes but only into the eyes of this evil shadow. The idea grew upon me until at times I could almost detect its outline in the air, feel a chillness as it passed me. It trod silently through the pokey rooms, always alert to thrust its grinning face before them. Now beside my mother it would whisper in her ear; and the next moment, stealing across to my father, answer for him with his voice, but strangely different. I used to think I could hear it laughing to itself as it stepped back into enfolding space.

To this day I seem to see it, ever following with noiseless footsteps man and woman, waiting patiently its opportunity to thrust its face between them. So that never can I meet with boy and girl, whispering in the twilight, but I see it lurking amid the half lights, just behind them, creeping after them with stealthy tread, as hand in hand they pass me in quiet ways.

Shall any of us escape, or lies the road of all through this dark valley of the shadow of dead love? Is it Love's ordeal? testing the feeble-hearted from the strong in faith, who shall find each other yet again, the darkness passed?

It was a dull, sombre house without, but one entered through its commonplace door as through the weed-grown rock into Aladdin's cave. Old Teidelmann had been a great collector all his life, and his treasures, now scattered through a dozen galleries, were then heaped there in curious confusion. Pictures filled every inch of wall, stood propped against the wonderful old furniture, were even stretched unframed across the ceilings. The ugly little thick-lipped man had surrounded himself with the beauty of every age, brought from every land. He himself must have been the only thing cheap and uninteresting to be found within his own walls; and now he lay shrivelled up in his coffin, under a monument by means of which an unknown cemetery became quite famous.

The eighteen months that followed were, I think, the happiest days my father and mother had ever known. Or if "happy" is too simple a word, let us say they were the most beautiful, the most nearly perfect. To them, it seemed as though God, in His sweet thoughtfulness, had sent Death to knock lightly at the door, whispering, "Not yet. You have still a little longer to be together. In a little while." In those final days, they laid aside all things false and meaningless. Nothing mattered but that they should love each other, comfort one another, and learn to understand. Again, we lived poorly, but there was no pitiful straining to keep up appearances, no haunting terror of what the neighbors might think. The petty cares and worries concerning matters not worth a moment's thought, the mean desires and fears with which we usually disfigure ourselves, fell from them. There came to them broader thought, a wider charity, a deeper pity. Their love grew greater even than their needs, overflowing towards all things. Sometimes, recalling these months, it has seemed to me that we make a mistake seeking to keep Death, God's go-between, ever from our thoughts. Is it not closing the door to a friend who would help us if we let him whisper, "In a little while. Only a little longer that you have to be together. Is it worth taking so much thought for self? This was the introduction into our small circle of James Wellington Gadley. Properly speaking, it should have been Wellington James, that being the order in which he had been christened in the year 1815. But in course of time, and particularly during his school career, it had been borne in upon him that Wellington is a burdensome name for a commonplace mortal to bear, and very wisely he had reversed the arrangement. He was a slightly pompous but simpleminded little old gentleman, very proud of his position as head clerk to Mr. Stillwood, the solicitor to whom my father was now assistant. James Gadley had entered the firm as office boy in the days of its pride, and had never awakened to the fact that it was not still the most important legal firm within the half-mile radius from Lombard Street. In the beginning I was far from being a favourite with my schoolmates, and this was the first time trouble came to dwell with me. Later, we men and women generally succeed in convincing ourselves that whatever else we may have missed in life, popularity in a greater or less degree we have at all events secured, for without it altogether few of us, I think, would care to face existence. But where the child suffers keener than the man is in finding himself exposed to the cold truth without the protecting clothes of self-deception. "Yes," I answered. As a matter of fact it was my one accomplishment, and rumour of it maybe had reached him.

I clinched my fists and charged off. How grateful I was to him for having spoken to me, the outcast of the class, thus publicly, I could only show by my exertions to please him. When I drew up before him I was panting hard, but I could see that he was satisfied.

"Why don't the fellows like you?" he asked bluntly.

If only I could have stepped out of my shyness, spoken my real thoughts! "O Lord of the Lower Fourth! You upon whom success—the only success in life worth having—has fallen as from the laps of the gods! You to whom all Lower Fourth hearts turn! tell me the secret of this popularity. The want of it embitters all my days. Why does silence fall upon their chattering groups when I draw near? Why do they drive me from their games? "Don't tell lies," he answered; "you know they don't—none of them." And I hung my head.

"The very thing your poor father used to talk of doing whenever things were going a bit queer in the retail coal and potato business," grunted old Gadley.

All things pass, even the self-inflicted sufferings of shy young men condemned by temperament to solitude. Came the winter evenings, I took to work: in it one may drown much sorrow for oneself. With its handful of fire, its two candles lighted, my "apartment" was more inviting. I bought myself paper, pens and ink. Great or small, what more can a writer do? He is but the would-be medium: will the spirit voices employ him or reject him?

London, with its million characters, grave and gay; its ten thousand romances, its mysteries, its pathos, and its humour, lay to my hand. It stretched before me, asking only intelligent observation, more or less truthful report. But that I could make a story out of the things I really knew never occurred to me. My tales were of cottage maidens, of bucolic yeomen. My scenes were laid in windmills, among mountains, or in moated granges. I fancy this phase of folly is common to most youthful fictionists.

A trail of gentle melancholy lay over them. Sentiment was more popular then than it is now, and, as do all beginners, I scrupulously followed fashion. Generally speaking, to be a heroine of mine was fatal. However naturally her hair might curl—and curly hair, I believe, is the hall-mark of vitality; whatever other indications of vigorous health she might exhibit in the first chapter, such as "dancing eyes," "colour that came and went," "ringing laughter," "fawn-like agility," she was tolerably certain, poor girl, to end in an untimely grave. Snowdrops and early primroses (my botany I worked up from a useful little volume, "Our Garden Favourites, Illustrated") grew there as in a forcing house; and if in the neighbourhood of the coast, the sea-breezes would choose that particular churchyard, somewhat irreverently, for their favourite playground. Years later a white-haired man would come there leading little children by the hand, and to them he would tell the tale anew, which must have been a dismal entertainment for them.

Now and then, by way of change, it would be the gentleman who would fall a victim of the deadly atmosphere of my literature. It was of no particular consequence, so he himself would conclude in his last soliloquy; "it was better so." Snowdrops and primroses, for whatever consolation they might have been to him, it was hopeless for him to expect; his grave, marked by a rude cross, being as a rule situate in an exceptionally unfrequented portion of the African veldt or amid burning sands. For description of final scenery on these occasions a visit to the British Museum reading-room would be necessary.

Dismal little fledgelings! And again and again would I drive them from the nest; again and again they fluttered back to me, soiled, crumpled, physically damaged. Yet one person had admired them, cried over them—myself.

All methods I tried. Sometimes I would send them forth accompanied by a curt business note of the take-it-or-leave-it order. At other times I would attach to it pathetic appeals for its consideration. Sometimes I would give value to it, stating that the price was five guineas and requesting that the cheque should be crossed; at other times seek to tickle editorial cupidity by offering this, my first contribution to their pages, for nothing—my sample packet, so to speak, sent gratis, one trial surely sufficient. Now I would write sarcastically, enclosing together with the stamped envelope for return a brutally penned note of rejection. Or I would write frankly, explaining elaborately that I was a beginner, and asking to be told my faults—if any.

Not one found a resting place for its feet. A month, a week, a couple of days, they would remain away from me, then return. I never lost a single one. I wished I had. It would have varied the monotony.

I hated the poor little slavey who, bursting joyously into the room, would hold them out to me from between her apron-hidden thumb and finger; her chronic sniff I translated into contempt. The editor himself could never have seen them; it was impossible; some jealous underling had done this thing. I had sent them to the wrong paper. They had arrived at the inopportune moment. Their triumph would come. Rewriting the first and last sheets, I would send them forth again with fresh hope.

Meanwhile, understanding that the would-be happy warrior must shine in camp as well as field, I sought to fit myself also for the social side of life. Smoking and drinking were the twin sins I found most difficulty in acquiring. I am not claiming a mental excellence so much as confessing a bodily infirmity. The spirit had always been willing, but my flesh was weak. Fired by emulation, I had at school occasionally essayed a cigarette. The result had been distinctly unsatisfactory, and after some two or three attempts, I had abandoned, for the time being, all further endeavour; excusing my faint-heartedness by telling myself with sanctimonious air that smoking was bad for growing boys; attempting to delude myself by assuming, in presence of contemporaries of stronger stomach, fine pose of disapproval; yet in my heart knowing myself a young hypocrite, disguising physical cowardice in the robes of moral courage: a self-deception to which human nature is prone.

So likewise now and again I had tasted the wine that was red, and that stood year in, year out, decanted on our sideboard. The true inwardness of St. Paul's prescription had been revealed to me; the attitude—sometimes sneered at—of those who drink it under doctor's orders, regarding it purely as a medicine, appeared to me reasonable. I had noticed also that others, some of them grown men even, making wry faces, when drinking my mother's claret, and had concluded therefrom that taste for strong liquor was an accomplishment less easily acquired than is generally supposed. The lack of it in a young man could be no disgrace, and accordingly effort in that direction also had I weakly postponed.

But now, a gentleman at large, my education could no longer be delayed. To the artist in particular was training—and severe training—an absolute necessity. Recently fashion has changed somewhat, but a quarter of a century ago a genius who did not smoke and drink—and that more than was good for him—would have been dismissed without further evidence as an impostor. About the genius I was hopeful, but at no time positively certain. As regarded the smoking and drinking, so much at least I could make sure of. I set to work methodically, conscientiously. Smoking, experience taught me, was better practised on Saturday nights, Sunday affording me the opportunity of walking off the effects. Patience and determination were eventually crowned with success: I learned to smoke a cigarette to all appearance as though I were enjoying it. Young men of less character might here have rested content, but attainment of the highest has always been with me a motive force. The cigarette conquered, I next proceeded to attack the cigar. My first one I remember well: most men do. It was at a smoking concert held in the Islington Drill Hall, to which Minikin had invited me. I found myself in a small courtyard, divided from a neighbouring garden by a low wall. The cause of my trouble was clearly the cigar. My inclination was to take it from my mouth and see how far I could throw it. Conscience, on the other hand, urged me to persevere. It occurred to me that if climbing on to the wall I could walk along it from end to end, there would be no excuse for my not heeding the counsels of perfection. A liking for drink I had found, if possible, even yet more difficult of achievement. Spirits I almost despaired of. For the past two weeks, the O'Kelly had been drilling me, a process that had driven both of us to the brink of hysteria, though the O'Kelly seemed to be suffering the brunt of it. His wife, Mrs. O'Kelly—a slender, vinegar-faced matron whom I had glimpsed once or twice pacing Belsize Square like a sentinel awaiting a signal—was a woman of serious intent who harbored a conscientious objection to any music that didn't smell faintly of incense. In a desperate bid to convert her husband from one of his musical sins, the piano had been banished from the drawing-room and replaced by an American organ of such lugubrious tone that it sounded less like an instrument and more like a weeping ghost. We had made do with this mournful beast. Though the O'Kelly, a genuine musical genius, had managed to coax an accompaniment to "Sally in Our Alley" from it that was less confusing than it might have been, the result was a disaster. My rendition of the famous ballad had acquired a dolefulness that the composer surely hadn't intended. Sung as I sang it, the theme became, to use a definition that has since been worn threadbare in art criticism, a "problem ballad." One couldn't help wondering if the marriage in the song would turn out as happily as the young man seemed to expect. Was there not, upon reflection, a deep-seated melancholy, a grim pessimism, woven into the very fabric of the complainful hero that clashed horribly with the frivolous instincts one could not fail to detect in the charming, yet slightly shallow, character of Sally?

I needed no pressing. A week later, we were installed. For nearly two years, we lived there. Without female assistance, we somehow survived, proving Dan's boast correct. His cooking was excellent—better plain cooking I never want to taste. But when stirred into dangerous ambition by occasional tea parties or the example of old Deleglise, Dan ventured into pastry. His hand was too heavy; his temperament too serious. Lemon sponge, described in cookbooks as an airy structure of dazzling whiteness, emerged from his kitchen as a miniature earthquake, yielding with a gentle tearing sound. His favorite, manna-cake, was substantial enough to satisfy anyone, even if it resembled the food of the Children of Israel, offering an excuse for their ancient discontent. For supper parties, he attempted soufflés that guests insisted were puddings, and pancakes. Initially, the tossing was perilous; he often caught half-baked things on his head or dropped them into the fire. But "Thorough" was always his motto. Small particles of coal or hairs could always be detected by the careful feeder and removed.

Dan was an even-tempered man for twenty-three hours a day. The other hour, however, he could be uncertain, irritable, and ungrateful. When I offered advice on pimply custards or mayonnaise resembling hair oil, I was met with sneers, insults, and spoiled tomatoes. His greatest enemy was the oven, which seemed determined to thwart his best schemes, turning snowy buns into red sandstone and macaroni cheese into Cambrian clay. The virulence of his reproaches almost made one take the oven's side.

Regarding our house-maid, I speak with unqualified praise. Unlike fussy maids who disturb the delicate domestic bloom of settled dust, I believed in letting sleeping dogs lie. Dust, crumbs, and matchsticks should remain under the nearest mat. To sweep them up is tiresome work that breeds bitterness. Compromise is true statesmanship. There will come a day when you are glad of an excuse to stop cleaning, then you can take up the mats and feel industrious, contemplating the work that *really* needs doing.

Differentiating between the essential and the non-essential is where women fail. Why wash a cup that will be dirty again in half an hour? If a bed looks made, it is made. If a cat cleans a plate, let her have the pleasure.

I never acquired skill in needlework. Dan handled a thimble well; my second finger was ever reluctant, finding itself pricked by the needle's head before it could push through. More practical was pushing the needle through with the aid of a door or table.

The story was a sad, sentimental composition about a mighty King who forgot his honor and fell in love with a wicked Sorceress, the Witch of Moel Sarbod. Only a beautiful Princess named Purity could save him. She must climb a rocky path to the Witch's dwelling and kiss her upon the mouth. In this way, the spirit of Purity would pass into the Witch's soul, transforming her into a woman, while the Witch's form and spirit would pass into Purity, condemning her to forever abide in the Cave of the Waters. Purity gave herself so the King might live. With bleeding feet, she climbed, clasped the Witch, and kissed her. The Witch became a wise queen; Purity became a hideous witch, sobbing above the roaring cataract.

No writer worth his salt is ever satisfied with his work, I was told. Candid friends assured me my first story was a feeble, ill-shaped child, its attempted early English a cross between "The Pilgrim's Progress" and "Old Moore's Almanac," its scenery an attempt to sum up the Garden of Eden combined with Dante's Inferno. Maybe they were right. Much have I written since that appeared good to me, which I later read with regret. But of this, my first-born, I am no judge. Touching the yellowing, badly-printed pages, I felt the deep thrill of joy with which I first unfolded them. Again, I was a youngster, and life opened out before me—inmeasurable, no goal too high. This child of my brain shall spread my name throughout the world. It shall be a household word in lands I shall never see. Friends whose voices I shall never hear will speak of me. I shall die, but it shall live, yield fresh seed, bear fruit I know not of. Generations yet unborn shall read it and remember me. My thoughts, my words, my spirit: in it I shall live again; it shall keep my memory green.

The long, long thoughts of boyhood! We elders smile at them. The little world spins round; the little voices of an hour sink hushed. The crawling generations come and go. The solar system drops from space. The eternal mechanism reforms and shapes itself anew. Time, turning, ploughs another furrow. So, growing sleepy, we murmur with a yawn. Is it that we see clearer, or that our eyes are growing dim? Let the young men see their visions, dream their dreams, hug to themselves their hopes of enduring fame; so shall they serve the world better.

I left London with my heart drumming like a trapped bird and my brain waving flags of false glory. More than a year later, on a foggy, wet December evening, I crept back to it—not as a conqueror, but as a defeated, disgraced ghost seeking a hiding place. I wanted to vanish into the crowd, to waste my days unnoticed until I could summon the courage to end this dead life. I had been ambitious, yes, but dwelling now on the bitterness of those months, I write in the past tense. I had wanted to make a name, a position. But to claim no higher aim would be an injustice to my blood—to the great-souled gentleman whose life was an ode to honor, and to her of simple faith who taught me only the childish cry, "God help me to be good!" I wished to be a great man, but it was to have been a great *good* man. The world was to admire me, but respect me also. I was to be the knight without fear, and rarer yet, without reproach—Galahad, not Launcelot. Instead, I had learned to be a feeble, backboneless fighter, conquered by the first serious assault of evil, a creature of mean fears, a slave to every crack of the devil's whip, feeding like a swine.

Urban Vane, I discovered, was a common swindler. He had stolen a play from the desk of a well-known dramatist he'd met in Deleglise's kitchen. The man had fallen ill; Vane had been constant in his visits. Partly recovered, the author went abroad to Italy. Had he died there, as expected, the robbery might never have surfaced. News reached us in a small northern town that he had taken a fresh lease of life and was returning to England. It was then that Vane, with calm indifference, smoking a cigar over a bottle of wine he'd invited me to share, told me the bald truth, adorned with touches of wit. Had the recital come sooner, I might have acted differently; but six months' companionship with Urban Vane had, by grace of the Lord, destroyed the roots of whatever flower of manhood might have been implanted in me, withering its leaves.

The man was clever. That he wasn't clever enough to perceive from the beginning what he learned since—that honesty is the best policy, at least for men with brains—remains a mystery. Where once he made his hundreds among shady ways, he now, I suppose, makes his thousands in the broad daylight of legitimate enterprise. Chicanery in the blood seems to require working out. Urban Vanes are found in all callings; they commence as scamps, and years later, to one's astonishment, become ornaments to their profession. Wild oats vary in quality according to the soil from which they are preserved. We sow them in our various ways.

At first, I stormed. Vane sat with an amused smile, listening.

"Your language, my dear Kelver," he replied, my vocabulary exhausted, "might wound me were I able to accept you as an authority upon this vexed question of morals. You profess to be very indignant with me for making use of another man's ideas. It is done every day. You yourself were quite ready to take credit not due to you. For months we have been traveling with this play: 'Drama, in five acts, by Mr. Horace Moncrieff.' This aspect had not occurred to me. "But you asked me to put my name to it," I stammered. "You said you didn't want your own to appear—for private reasons. You made a point of it."

He waved away the smoke from his cigar. "The man you are posing as would never have put his name to work not his own. You never hesitated; on the contrary, you jumped at the chance of so easy an opening to your career as a playwright. My need, as you imagined it, was your opportunity."

"But you said it was from the French," I argued. "You had merely translated it; I adapted it. I don't defend the custom, but it is the custom: the man who adapts a play calls himself the author. They all do it."

"I know," he answered. "It has always amused me. Our sick friend himself, whom I am sure we are both delighted to welcome back to life, has done it more than once, and made a very fair profit on the transaction. "Simply this," he continued, pouring himself another glass of wine, "that whereas, owing to the anomalous state of the copyright laws, stealing from the foreign author is legal and commendable, against stealing from the living English author there is a certain prejudice."

He laughed: it was not a frivolity to which he was prone. "You mean, my dear Kelver, that you will."

My anger came later. For the moment, the sudden light blinded me to everything but fear.

He was looking at me with an expression of genuine astonishment. My words began to appear humorous even to myself. I found it difficult to believe I had been the fool I was now seeing myself to have been.

"Don't take it so seriously," he said. "He will merely think the manuscript has been lost. As likely as not, he will be unable to remember whether he wrote it or merely thought of writing it. There were moments during the next few weeks when I listened to the voice of my good angel, when I saw clearly that even from the lowest point of view he was giving me sound advice. I would go to the man, tell him frankly the whole truth.

But Vane never left my elbow. Suspecting, I suppose, he gave me clearly to understand that if I did so, I must expect no mercy from him. My story, denounced by him as an outrageous lie, would be regarded as the funk-inspired subterfuge of a young rogue. At best, I should handicap myself with suspicion that would last me throughout my career. On the other hand, what harm had we done? Presented in some twenty or so small towns, where it would soon be forgotten, a play something like. Most plays were something like. So soon as possible, we would replace it by a new piece altogether. A young man of my genius could surely write something better than hotch-potch such as this; experience was all that I had lacked. As regards one's own conscience, was not the world's honesty a mere question of convention? Had he been a young man, and had we diddled him out of his play for a ten-pound note, we should have been applauded as sharp men of business. So I fell from mine own esteem. Vane helping me—and he had brains—I set feverishly to work. I am glad to remember that every line I wrote was born in misery. "Later on, my dear Kelver," was his reply. "For the present this is doing very well. Going on as we are, we shall soon improve it out of all recognition, while at the same time losing nothing that is essential. All your ideas are excellent."

There was a character in the piece, a vicious old man, that from the beginning Vane had wanted me to play. Looking back at the man as I see him plainly now, a very ordinary scamp, his pretension not even amusing, I find it difficult to present him as he appeared to my boyish eyes. My lower nature rose within me to proclaim the foolish chatterer as a prophet. So life was not as I had been taught—a painful struggle between good and evil. There was no such thing as evil; the senseless epithet was a libel upon Nature. Not through wearisome repression, but rather through joyous expression of the animal, lay advancement.

Villains—workers in wrong for the aesthetic pleasure of the art—are useful characters in fiction; in real life, they do not exist. I am convinced the man believed most of the rubbish he talked. Since the time of which I write, he has done some service to the world. I understand he is an excellent husband and father, a considerate master, a delightful host. He intended, I have no doubt, to improve me, to enlarge my understanding, to free me from the soul-stifling bondage of convention. Not to credit him with this well-meaning intention would be to assume him something quite inhuman, to bestow upon him a dignity beyond his deserts. I find it easier to regard him merely as a fool.

At Richmond, the antics continued. He balanced a horn on his nose, tried to buy a boarding school for sixpence, and attempted to race a deaf old gentleman up the hill for a shilling. The party roared with laughter, except for Hodgson, who was busy with his correspondence, and an elegant young lady who had recently emerged from the Divorce Court with a reputation worth a hundred pounds a week.

In the hotel garden, the low comedian spotted an elderly gentleman sipping vermouth. Mistaking him for his old friend, Charlie Atherton, he rushed forward, shaking his hand with fervor.

"And how's Aunt Martha?" he cried. "Dear old Aunt Martha! You do look bonny!"

The elderly gentleman looked confused. "I'm afraid—"

"Don't tell me anything has happened to her! Not dead? Don't tell me that!"

The comedian's face twisted in terror. He grabbed the stranger's shoulders. "Not know me? Do you mean to tell me you've forgotten? Isn't your name Steggles?"

"No, it isn't," the stranger snapped.

"My mistake," the comedian said, tossing the rest of the stranger's drink into his mouth and walking away rapidly.

The stranger, annoyed, muttered to his companions, "Then tell him when I want his damned tomfoolery, I'll come to the theatre and pay for it."

During lunch, the comedian kissed the waiter's bald head, pretending it was a face, and stole the silver finger-bowls while the ladies looked away.

Later, he dragged me aside. "Now, about this new opera. No stale business. I'm a young fellow in love with a girl. Her father is an apoplectic idiot. Make him comic. 'Damme, sir! By gad!' He thinks I'm an ass, but I'm not a fool."

"Are we getting too much out of the groove?" I asked.

"The more so the better," he insisted. "In the second act, I disguise myself as an organ-grinder, then a policeman, then a swell. Give me opportunity to be funny! Put a little pathos in it too. A scene where I show them I have something else. Send them out saying, 'Charlie made me laugh, but I'm damned if I knew he could make me cry.'"

I promised my support.

The leading lady intercepted us. "Can't you give me something original?" she pleaded, her voice dripping with charm. "I'm tired of being the simple village maiden. I want a coquettish part. I can flirt. Try me."

I promised.

The tenor, sipping tea on the balcony, leaned in. "Doesn't it seem the time has come to make more of the story? Less clowning, more human interest? If you write an opera around an interesting love story, you'll score a success. Humour is excellent as support, but when it's the entire structure, it's tiresome."

"The lover," he whispered, glancing nervously at the low comedian approaching, "is the character they think about all the time. Make your lover interesting—that's the secret."

As we waited for the horses, a tall, thin man approached. "Trevor, Marmaduke Trevor. I play the Duke of Bayswater."

"A capital performance," I lied.

He flushed. "I made a great success in America with a soda-water syphon. If you could drop in a small part leading up to a soda-water scene, it might help."

I wrote the scene, insisting on it despite opposition. Critics later called it lacking in originality, but Trevor was right; it helped.

On the return journey, the low comedian was in full swing, making hideous noises with the horn and arguing witlessly with bus drivers. At Hammersmith, he stood on the back seat selling our hats as "Cheap Jack."

When we returned to London, the mood shifted. The opera was ready. I read it to the assembled company. Silence fell. The leading lady checked the clock, hoping to catch her train. The tenor recalled a past failure that everyone thought was rubbish until the last minute. Marmaduke Trevor gave a covert shake of the hand. The low comedian offered to reuse parts.

Hodgson and I were left alone.

"It's no good," Hodgson said. "From a box-office point of view. Very clever."

"How do you know?" I ventured.

"I never made a mistake in my life," he replied.

"You have produced one or two failures," I reminded him.

"And shall again," he laughed. "The right thing isn't easy to get."

"They are people of experience," I told myself, trying to convince my own sinking heart. "They must know more than I do." But by the end of the week, I realized they didn't. I lost my temper. I took both manuscripts to Hodgson's private room. One was my original, neat, intelligible opera. The other was a chaotic mess of cuts, interlines, reversals, and alterations—the opera as everyone had "knocked it into shape."

"That's your opera," I said, pushing the bulky bundle across the table. "If you can understand it, it's yours. This is mine!"

He argued for forty minutes, appealing to my self-interest and my better nature. Finally, he called me an obstinate young fool, threw the mangled manuscript into the wastepaper basket, and declared that the opera would be played exactly as I had written it.

The company shrugged. For a month, they kept their shoulders shrunk. Hodgson stayed away, then returned with a melancholy interest in the gloomy proceedings.

"It's bound to be a failure," he told me. "I'm spending fifteen hundred pounds to teach you a lesson. When you've learned it, we'll square accounts."

"And if it succeeds?"

"My dear boy," Hodgson replied, "I never make mistakes."

A seasoned author would have seen cheer in this confidence. I, however, felt my literary career ending. Belief in oneself is just the match; belief in others is the oil. Without it, you go out. I felt dark and dismal. I wanted to escape my own smoke.

After the final dress rehearsal, I left. The next morning, I packed a knapsack for a walking tour through Holland. I would vanish. In a month, I would return, and the piece would be forgotten.

"I'll run it for three weeks," Hodgson said. "Then we'll withdraw it quietly. Or Duncan can fall ill. She's done it often enough. Don't be upset. There's nothing to be ashamed of. The idea is distinctly original. As a matter of fact, that's the fault with it—it's too original."

"Yes, but original for the stage," I reminded him.

"Old dolls in new frocks," he laughed.

I thanked him and went home.

For two months, I wandered, avoiding beaten tracks, my only companions books from ages past. My worries fell away. The personal affairs of Paul Kelver ceased to be the center of the universe.

But fate intervened. In a dead city of the Zuyder Zee, sitting under lindens on a grass-grown quay, I heard footsteps. Wellbourne, the spirit of Deleglise, sat beside me.

"All well with you?" I asked.

"I am afraid not," he sighed. "The poor fellow is in great trouble."

"I'm not Wellbourne himself," he clarified. "I am only his spirit. Have you ever tested the Hindu belief that a man may leave his body, wander as a free spirit, and return before the thread connecting him to flesh breaks? It is quite correct. I often lock my door and leave myself behind."

He pulled out a handful of loose coins. "The thread is wearing thin. I must return soon to concern myself with his troubles. It is vexing. Life is really beautiful when one is dead."

"What was the trouble?"

"Haven't you heard?" Wellbourne replied. "Tom died five weeks ago, quite suddenly, of syncope. We had no idea."

Norah was alone in the world.

I rose as the boat I had been watching grew from a speck into a dark streak.

"By the way," Wellbourne said, "I have to congratulate you. Your opera looked like a big thing when I left London. You didn't sell outright, I hope?"

"No," I answered. "Hodgson never expressed any desire to buy."

"Lucky for you," Wellbourne said.

I reached London the next evening. Passing the theatre, I stopped my cab. I met the low comedian on his way to his dressing room.

"Well," he said, shaking my hand warmly. "We're pulling them in. I was right. 'Give me plenty of opportunity.' Come and see the piece. I think you'll agree I've done you justice."

"I thanked him."

"Not at all," he returned. "It's a pleasure to work when you've got something good to work on."

I paid my respects to the leading lady. "I am so grateful," she said. "It is so delightful to play a real live woman, for a change."

The tenor was fatherly. "It is what I have been telling Hodgson for years. Give them a simple human story."

Crossing the stage, I ran into Marmaduke Trevor.

"You will stay for my scene," he urged.

"Another night," I answered. "I have only just returned."

He sank his voice to a whisper. "I want to talk to you on business. I am thinking of taking a theatre myself—not just now, but later. Of course, I don't want it to get about."

"I assured him of my secrecy."

"If it comes off, I want you to write for me. You understand the public. We will talk it over."

He passed onward with stealthy tread.

I found Hodgson in the front of the house. "Two stalls not sold and six seats in the upper circle," he informed me. "Not bad for a Thursday night."
