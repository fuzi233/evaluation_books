Any wanderer who has drifted through the soft, sun-dappled countryside of North Devon will surely know the little white-washed port of Bideford, climbing gently upward from its broad tidal river where yellow sands glint low beneath the ebb, its many-arched medieval bridge strung across the current where salmon leap and wait for the autumn spate. To the west, sweet upland slopes roll back, and above the town the hills crowd close, cushioned deep in ancient oak woods where fern-fringed slate crags jut out here and there through the leaves. Further down the coast the hills soften, melting away into low rounded knolls, patchwork fields of red loam and lush green pasture, until they sink at last into wide hazy salt flats, rich marsh meadows, and rolling dunes where the River Torridge meets its sister the Taw, and the two flow quiet together out toward the thundering Atlantic surf that booms unceasing over the harbour bar.

Beneath its soft, almost Mediterranean sky, fanned day and night by sharp, briny ocean breeze that keeps both harsh winter frosts and the sweltering midland summer thunder-heats far at bay, the little town has stood pleasantly for nigh on eight hundred years. The first Grenville, cousin to William the Conqueror, settled here fresh from subjugating South Wales, gathering around him loyal Saxon serfs, golden-curled free Norse rovers, and dark, stocky Silurian Britons from the Swansea coast, a mingled bloodline that still gives the people of the Devon coast their unusual strength of mind, sharp wit, and even in these leveling modern days, their distinct, striking beauty of face and form.

But in the year this tale takes place, Bideford was no sleepy coastal backwater whose quay was only haunted by a handful of small trading smacks. It was one of the greatest ports in all of England: it furnished seven full ships to fight the Spanish Armada, and even a full century later, chroniclers recorded it sent more vessels out on the northern trade routes than any other English port save for London and Topsham. It was the beating heart of a thriving local civilization of bold seafarers and merchants – small, perhaps, measured against the colossal industrial efforts of our modern age, but no man worth his salt dare mock the day of small things, when that day proved to be the dawn of something mighty. England owes the very foundation of its naval and commercial glory to the sea-labor and daring of Bideford, Dartmouth, Topsham, Plymouth (then no more than a tiny fishing hamlet), and a dozen other little western coastal towns. It is to the men of Devon – the Drakes, the Hawkinses, the Gilberts, the Raleighs, the Grenvilles, the Oxenhams, and a whole host of forgotten worthies the world has yet to honor as they deserve – that England owes its trade, its colonies, and very existence. Had they not first crippled the ill-gotten hoards of Spain through their bold West Indian raids, and then crushed the last great invasion force of the Spanish king in Britain’s Salamis, the glorious fight of 1588, what would we be now but a papist vassal state of a global tyranny as cruel as old pagan Rome, and far more devilish in its cruelty?

I write this book in memory of these men: their voyages and their battles, their faith and their valor, their heroic lives and no less heroic deaths. If at times my tone rings a little stilted, a little grand, you must excuse me for the sake of my subject, which was always fit to be sung rather than spoken – fit to be proclaimed to every true English heart not as a common novel, but as an epic, the kind the songs of Troy and the tales of the Persian Wars once were to the old Greeks.

One bright summer’s afternoon in 1575, a tall, fair-haired boy lingered along the Bideford quay, clad in his scholar’s gown, satchel and slate slung over one shoulder, staring wide-eyed at the bobbing masts and the yelling, gesturing sailors. He had just passed the foot of High Street when he came opposite one of the many waterfront taverns whose windows looked straight out over the river. Merchants and local gentlemen sat in the open bay window, sipping their afternoon draught of sack, while a tight crowd of sailors had gathered on the doorstep, leaning in to listen earnestly to the man standing at their centre. The boy, starved for every scrap of sea news, squeezed his way through the throng until he stood among the other young sailor lads, peeping and whispering under the elbows of the grown men, and heard the following speech, roared out in a loud, bold Devonshire accent, peppered thick with unapologetic oaths:

“If you don’t believe me, go sail there and see for yourselves, or stay here rotting till you grow all over blue mould. I swear on my honor as a gentleman, I saw it with these two eyes, and so did Salvation Yeo there, through a lower window in the custom house. We measured the heap ourselves, as I am a baptized man: seventy foot long, ten foot broad, twelve foot high, all solid silver bars, each bar weighing between thirty and forty pounds. And Captain Drake turns to us and says: ‘There, my Devon lads, I’ve brought you all to the mouth of the world’s treasure house, and it’s your own fault now if you don’t sweep it out as empty as a dried stockfish.’”

“Why didn’t you bring any of that silver home with you then, Mr. Oxenham?” a voice called from the crowd.

“Why weren’t you there to help carry it? We would have brought the whole hoard away safe enough, young Drake and I had already pried open the custom house door, but Captain Drake drops dead in a sudden faint. When we knelt down to look at him, he had a gash in his leg you could have laid three fingers across, his boots were full of blood, and had been for an hour or more. But his heart was so fierce he never felt the wound till he collapsed. His brother and I dragged him back to the boats, him kicking and struggling the whole way, yelling at us to let him go back and fight, every step he took in the sand leaving a pool of blood. And tell me, ye sons of soggy salted herrings, wasn’t saving him worth more than all that dirty silver? Silver we can get again, brave lads: there’s more fish in the sea than ever swam into a net, and more silver in Nombre de Dios than would pave every street in the whole west country. But for captains like Franky Drake? Heaven never makes more than one at a time. If we lose him, good bye to all England’s luck, that’s what I say, and any man that disagrees can name his weapons, and I’m his man.”

The man who delivered this harangue was tall and broad and sturdy, with a florid black-bearded face, and bold, restless dark eyes. He leaned against the tavern wall, legs crossed and hands planted akimbo, and to the wide-eyed schoolboy he seemed a grand, magnificent figure, fit to be a prince or a duke at the very least. Against all the strict sumptuary laws of the time that reserved crimson velvet for nobility, he wore a full suit of that rich red cloth, a little worn around the cuffs perhaps. A long Spanish rapier hung at his hip, alongside a brace of daggers with gaudy, gem-encrusted hilts. His fingers sparkled with rings, two heavy gold chains looped around his neck, and large gold hoops hung in his ears, behind one of which he had tucked a bright red rose jauntily into his glossy black curls. On his head he wore a broad velvet Spanish hat, fastened with a huge gold clasp that held a whole perfect quetzal bird, its iridescent plumage of fretted golden-green glowing like one solid, uncut precious stone.

As he finished his speech, he took off the hat and stared at the bird perched on it. “Look ye, my lads! Did you ever see such a fowl as that before? That’s the bird the old Aztec kings of Mexico forbade anyone but themselves to wear. And so I wear it – I, John Oxenham of South Tawton – as a sign to all brave Devon lads that if the Spaniards think they are masters of the Indians, we are the masters of the Spaniards.” He settled the hat back on his head.

A murmur of applause rippled through the crowd, though one sailor muttered that he doubted the Spaniards were far too many for them to fight.

“Too many?” Oxenham roared. “How many men did we sail out of Plymouth Sound with to take Nombre de Dios? Seventy-three, no more. Before we ever sighted the Spanish Main, half of them were gastados – used up, as the Dons say – rotting from scurvy. We picked up Captain Rawse of Cowes and his thirty men at Port Pheasant, and that left us barely fifty-three hands total, that handful of men picked the lock on the entire New World! And the only man we lost was our trumpeter, who stood out in the middle of the main square braying like an ass instead of taking care of his neck like a sensible Christian. I tell you those Spaniards are rank cowards, just like every bully that ever lived. They pray to a woman, the idolatrous rascals! No wonder they fight like frightened girls.”

“You’m right, captain!” sang out a tall, gaunt weatherbeaten man standing at Oxenham’s elbow. “One westcountry man can fight two eastcountrymen, and an eastcountryman can beat three Dons any day. Eh, my lads of Devon?
For O! it’s the herrings and the good brown beef,
And the cider and the cream so white;
O! they are the making of the jolly Devon lads,
For to play, and eke to fight.”

“Come on then!” Oxenham called, waving a hand. “Who lists? Who lists? Who’ll make his fortune?
Oh, who will join, jolly mariners all?
And who will join, says he, O!
To fill his pockets with the good red goold,
By sailing on the sea, O!”

“Who’ll list?” the gaunt old man – Salvation Yeo, the gunner’s mate – shouted back. “Now’s your time! We’ve got forty men waiting for us at Plymouth right now, ready to sail the minute we step back into the harbor, and we just need a dozen Bideford lads, and a boy or two, and then we’m off and away, to make our fortunes or go straight to heaven.
Our bodies in the sea so deep,
Our souls in heaven to rest!
Where valiant seamen, one and all,
Hereafter shall be blest!”

“Now then,” Oxenham challenged the crowd, “you won’t let the Plymouth men say Bideford lads dare not follow them? North Devon against South, it is. Who’ll join? It’s no long hard voyage, after all, the sea will be as smooth as a duck pond the second you round Cape Finisterre. I’ll bet twenty pounds I can sail a little Clovelly herring boat all the way there and back without shipping a single bucket of seawater. Don’t think you’re buying a pig in a poke! I know the route, and Salvation Yeo here, who was our gunner’s mate, knows the charts of the Spanish Main just as well as he knows the English Channel, maybe better. Ask him to show you his map, and he’ll rattle off every reef and crag as well as Drake himself.”

At that, Yeo pulled a great white buffalo horn out from under his arm, its surface covered in rough etchings of coastlines and islands, and held it up for the admiring crowd to see. “See here, boys all, behold the picture of the place, drawn as natural as life. I got it off a Portuguese sailor down in the Azores, he pricked every mark onto it himself, every coast he ever sailed, every thing he ever saw. Take it in your hands, Simon Evans, take it in your hands, look it over, and I’ll warrant you’ll know the way out to the Indies in five minutes as well as any shark that swims the southern seas.”

The horn was passed from hand to hand, and while the crowd gawked at it, Oxenham called through the open tavern window for a great tankard of spiced sack, which he passed around the ring after the horn.

The schoolboy, who had devoured every word and sight of the gathering, had by this time edged himself right into the inner circle, and stood face to face with the hero wearing the glowing green quetzal, craning to see the wonder as close as he could. When he watched one sailor after another hand the horn back and step forward to sign on for Oxenham’s voyage, his soul burned hot inside him to get a proper look at that wondrous horn, which seemed as magical as the enchanted horns in the old tales of Tristram or Ariosto. When the crowd began to break up and Oxenham turned to lead his new recruits into the tavern, the boy stepped forward boldly and asked if he might look at the marvel one more time. Yeo handed it over at once.

To his wide, astonished gaze, the horn revealed whole cities and sheltered harbors, dragons and elephants, whales locked in battle with sharks, great Spanish plate ships loaded down with silver, islands swarming with apes and fringed with palm trees, every place marked out with its name, and scrawled here and there in English, no doubt added by Oxenham’s own hand: “Here is gold,” “Much gold and silver.” The boy turned it round and round, lingering over every mark, and thought the man who owned it more fortunate than any emperor or khan on earth. Oh, if he could only possess that horn, what else could he ever need to make him perfectly happy?

“I say,” he blurted out. “Will you sell this?”

“Aye, marry, I’d sell my own soul too, if I could get the worth of it.”

“I want the horn – I don’t want your soul. For all I know it’s a stale old sole rotting down at the bottom of the bay, and there are plenty of fresh ones to be had.” He fumbled in his pocket for a long moment, and pulled out a single worn silver tester, the only coin he owned. “Will this buy it?”

“That? No, not twenty of them would buy it.”

The boy thought for a moment about what a proper knight errant would do in such a spot, and squared his shoulders. “Tell you what, I’ll fight you for it.”

“Thank ‘ee, sir!” Oxenham laughed. “Break this little jackanapes’s head for him, Yeo.”

“Call me jackanapes one more time, and I’ll break yours, sir.” The boy lifted his fist fiercely.

Oxenham stared at him for a minute, grinning. “Tut, tut, my little man! Pick a fight with someone your own size, spare little old fellows like me.”

“I may be a boy in age, sir, but I’ve got a man’s fist. I turn fifteen this month, and I know how to answer anyone who dares insult me.”

“Fifteen, my young cockerel? You look more like twenty.” Oxenham glanced at him admiringly, taking in his broad limbs, sharp bright blue eyes, curling golden hair, and round, honest open face. “Fifteen? If I had half a dozen lads like you, I’d make knights of every last one of them before I died. Eh, Yeo?”

“He’ll do,” Yeo rumbled. “He’ll be a brave fighting gamecock in a year or two, if he dares ruffle up that soon at a tough old sea dog like the captain.”

The whole crowd laughed, Oxenham loudest of all, then he bade the boy tell him why he was so desperate for the horn.

“Because,” the boy said, looking him straight in the eye, “I want to go to sea. I want to see the Indies. I want to fight the Spaniards. Even though I’m a gentleman’s son, I’d far rather be a cabin boy on your ship than stay here forever stuck at school.” He blurted the whole thing out fast and fierce, then dropped his head shyly.

“And you shall go!” Oxenham roared, swearing a great hearty oath. “You shall take a whole Spanish galleon for your prize, and dine off roasted Dons for supper. Whose son are you, my gallant fellow?”

“Mr. Leigh of Burrough Court.”

“Bless his soul! I know him as well as I know the Eddystone Reef, and I’ve eaten many a good meal at his table. Who’s supping with him tonight?”

“Sir Richard Grenville.”

“Dick Grenville? I didn’t know he was in town. Go home right now and tell your father John Oxenham will come and keep him company at supper. Off with you! I’ll smooth all this over with the good gentleman, and you shall have your turn sailing with us. And as for the horn, Yeo, give it to the boy, and I’ll give you a gold noble to pay you for it.”

“Not a penny from you, captain. If the young master will take a poor old sailor’s gift, it’s his, for the sake of his love for the calling, and Heaven send him all the luck on the seas.” The good old gunner, generous as any true sailor always is, thrust the horn straight into the boy’s hands and walked away fast to avoid his thanks.

“And now,” Oxenham called to his new recruits, “my merry men all, make up your minds what sort of men you intend to be before you take your shilling bounty. I want none of your lurching longshore vermin, who take five pounds from one captain, ten from the next, and then hide away under some tavern girl’s shawl or in a cellar till the ship sails without them. If any man here has that sort of trick in him, he’d better cut himself up and salt himself down in a pork barrel before he ever meets me again.
