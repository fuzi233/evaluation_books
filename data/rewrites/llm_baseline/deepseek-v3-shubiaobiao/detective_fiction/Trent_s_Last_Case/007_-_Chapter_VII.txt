The sea crashed violently against the cliffs below, driven by a stiff breeze, while the sun poured golden light through a mottled blue sky. Trent, having slept poorly, rose before eight and made his way to a secluded pool among the rocks—a spot recommended to him. He plunged into the clear, cold water, swimming out between massive boulders until he met the open sea’s rough embrace. Fighting briefly against the coastal current, he returned to shore battered but invigorated.

As he climbed the cliff path, his mind, momentarily freed from the weight of the case, turned to the day ahead. The inquest was scheduled for this morning, and though he had made little progress since parting with the American the day before, he had not been idle. A visit to the chemist, a private consultation with a photographer, a telegram sent, and an inquiry at the telephone exchange had occupied his afternoon. He had shared little with Mr. Cupples, who seemed content to remain incurious.

Now, beneath the morning’s brilliance, the ugliness of the case pressed upon him anew. The more he turned it over in his mind, the darker it appeared. Yet his resolve hardened. Today, he hoped, would bring answers.

The cliff path curved along the edge, and as Trent neared a spot where the rock face had long ago collapsed, he paused to gaze down, hoping to watch the sea swirl among the jagged stones below. Instead, his eyes fell upon a broad ledge, thick with wiry grass—a natural platform sheltered on three sides. At its very edge sat a woman, knees drawn up, arms wrapped around them, her dark eyes fixed on the distant smoke of a steamer.

She was, Trent thought instantly, the most striking figure he had ever seen. Her face, pale but touched with wind-borne color, held a delicate severity softened by the curve of her lips. Her nose was finely shaped, neither too long nor pert. The breeze played with her thick dark hair, loosening strands from their careful arrangement. She wore black from head to foot—lustrous, elegant black—yet there was nothing mournful in her posture. She seemed, rather, to drink in the morning’s vitality.

Then, as if sensing the sun’s full claim upon her, she stretched with unconscious grace, arms wide as if to gather the sky itself. It was a gesture of freedom, of defiance, of life seized without apology.

Trent passed without turning. He knew who she must be—and the knowledge cast a shadow over the day.

At breakfast, Mr. Cupples was animated, discoursing on the coroner’s court and its ancient liberties. Trent, feigning fatigue, listened absently. His thoughts were elsewhere—on the woman on the cliff, on the case’s deepening mysteries.

When they reached White Gables, they found Marlowe and the American in conversation. The lady in black—Mrs. Manderson—emerged from the porch to greet them. Her composure was flawless, her voice steady. Yet when Trent asked about the large sum of cash her husband had withdrawn, her surprise seemed genuine.

“He asked me for money the night he died,” she admitted. “I thought it odd—he always carried plenty. I gave him what I had, nearly thirty pounds. He didn’t explain.”

Trent filed this away, uneasy. Later, alone with Marlowe, he probed further. The errand that had sent Marlowe off in the night remained unexplained. “Manderson told me nothing,” Marlowe insisted. “Only that it was urgent.”

Trent showed him two blank diary leaves, cut neatly from a small book. Marlowe recognized the paper but could not place it.

Before they could speak further, Mrs. Manderson rejoined them, and they prepared to leave for the inquest. Trent excused himself—he had work still to do at the house.

As they parted, a boy arrived with a telegram. Trent read it, his expression unreadable.

“Good news?” Marlowe asked.

“Not news,” Trent replied. “Only confirmation that one of my guesses was right.”

The day’s work had only begun.
