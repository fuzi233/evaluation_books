The day breathed bright and sharp, a giddy spring wind whipping through Manhattan’s cobblestones, heavy with fine dust and the faint green tang of budding park trees. Every matriarch in the extended Welland and Archer clans had dragged their long-stored furs from cedar chests, sables faded to soft ash and ermines yellowed at the cuffs, so the sharp, heady reek of camphor hanging over Grace Church’s front pews nearly drowned out the delicate, sweet lily scent billowing up from the banked altar flowers.

At the sexton’s quiet signal, Newland Archer stepped out of the vestry to stand beside his best man on the chancel steps, his boots soft on the worn stone. The signal meant the brougham carrying May and her father was just in sight, but he knew there would be a long, fussy stretch of last-minute adjustments in the church lobby, where the bridesmaids hovered already like a cluster of pale Easter blossoms. It was the tradition for the groom to stand exposed to the full gaze of the assembled guests, proof of his eagerness to marry, and Archer submitted to the ritual as resignedly as he had every other absurd, ancient custom that made a 19th century New York wedding feel like a rite pulled straight from the dawn of time. Every step of the path he’d chosen felt equally trivial, equally sharp-edged, and he obeyed his flustered best man’s last-minute instructions as piously as grooms he’d guided through the same labyrinth of rules had obeyed him years before.

He’d checked every box, he was fairly certain. Eight bouquets of white lilac and lily of the valley had been sent to the bridesmaids on time, along with gold-and-sapphire sleeve links for the eight ushers, and a cat’s-eye scarf pin for his best man. He’d sat up half the night the day before rewriting his thank-you notes for the pile of wedding gifts from his male friends and old half-forgotten flirtations. The bishop and rector’s fees were safely tucked in his best man’s pocket, his own travelling bags were already at Mrs. Manson Mingott’s house where the wedding breakfast would be held, and a private compartment had been reserved on the train that would carry him and May off to their secret honeymoon destination – hiding the location of their first night away was one of the most sacred, unbreakable taboos of the old society ritual.

“Got the ring all right?” whispered young van der Luyden Newland, the inexperienced best man, white-knuckled under the weight of his responsibility.

Archer made the familiar gesture he’d seen a hundred other grooms make: he slipped his ungloved right hand into the pocket of his dark grey waistcoat, and felt the tiny warm gold circlet, engraved inside *Newland to May, April ——, 187—*, nestled safely in its velvet case. He settled back into his pose, tall hat and pearl-grey gloves stitched with black held in his left hand, and stared at the church’s heavy oak doors. Overhead, Handel’s wedding march swelled pompously through the imitation stone vaulting, carrying with it the ghost of every other wedding he’d stood witness to in that exact spot, watching other brides drift up the nave to meet other waiting grooms.

“Just like a first night at the Opera,” he thought to himself, recognizing all the same society faces in the same familiar pews, and wondering if the day the Last Trump blew, Mrs. Selfridge Merry would still be perched there wearing that towering ostrich feather bonnet, Mrs. Beaufort still glinting in her diamond earrings with that same unchanging smile, and if they’d already reserved the best proscenium seats in the afterlife.

He had time, still, to scan the first rows of faces one by one: the women sharp with curiosity and giddy excitement, the men sulky and resentful that they’d been forced to squeeze into frock coats before luncheon and fight for plates of food at the breakfast later. “Too bad the breakfast’s at old Catherine’s,” he could almost hear Reggie Chivers grumble. “But I heard Lovell Mingott made them use his own chef, so it ought to be good if you can fight your way to the table.” And Sillerton Jackson would chime right after, all self-important authority: “My dear boy, haven’t you heard? They’re serving it at small little tables, the new English fashion.”

Archer’s gaze softened as it landed on the left-hand pew, where his mother, who’d entered on Henry van der Luyden’s arm, wept softly behind her Chantilly veil, her hands tucked deep in her grandmother’s big ermine muff. Poor Janey, his sister, he thought – even twisting her neck as far as she could, she’d only be able to see the people in the first few pews, all the dowdy, stuffy old Newland and Dagonet relatives. On the near side of the white ribbon marking the family reserved seats, Beaufort stood tall and red-faced, staring boldly out over the crowd of women with his usual arrogant stare. Beside him his wife sat swaddled in silvery chinchilla and pinned with fresh violets, and on the far side of the ribbon, Lawrence Lefferts’s sleekly combed head looked like it was standing guard over the invisible patron saint of “Good Form” that presided over every New York ceremony. Archer wondered how many tiny, unforgivable flaws Lefferts’s sharp eyes would pick out of the day’s ritual, then realized with a faint jolt he’d once cared about those stupid little rules too. All the things that had once filled his days felt like a nursery play-pretend of real life, medieval scholars squabbling over meaningless words no one actually understood. Just the night before, the whole family had had a screaming, hours-long fight over whether to display the wedding presents for guests, and Mrs. Welland had dissolved into indignant tears, swearing she’d “sooner let reporters run loose in my house” than show the gifts off – once upon a time, Archer would have had very strong, very loud opinions on the rightness of that call. Now it all felt absurd.

“And all the while,” he thought, quiet and hollow, “real people were out living real lives somewhere, weren’t they?”

“There they come!” his best man breathed, excited – but Archer knew better. The soft creak of the church door only meant Mr. Brown, the livery stable keeper who doubled as sexton in a black gown, was peeking out to survey the crowd before herding the procession inside. The door clicked shut again, and after another long, breathless interval, it swung wide open. A soft murmur rippled through every pew: “The family!”

Mrs. Welland led the way on her eldest son’s arm, her big pink face appropriately solemn, her plum-colored satin gown with pale blue side panels and blue ostrich plumes in her small bonnet earning quiet nods of approval from the crowd. But before she could settle with a great stately rustle into the pew across from Archer’s mother, every head was craning to see who came next. Wild rumors had run through the clubs the day before: old Mrs. Manson Mingott, despite her crippling weight and bad legs, was determined to be at the ceremony. The bets had run high – would she make it up the nave? She’d even sent her own carpenter over to measure the front pew, to see if they could saw off the end panel to make space for her, and for a whole day the family had watched her toy with the plan of being wheeled up the whole aisle in her massive Bath chair, enthroned right at the foot of the chancel. The whole family had been desperate to avoid that undignified spectacle, and they’d nearly handed a fortune to the servant who’d suddenly pointed out the Bath chair was too wide to fit through the iron supports of the awning that stretched from the church doors to the curb. Old Catherine had weighed tearing the awning down for half a second, but the thought of the crowds of dressmakers and newspaper reporters jostling outside, of them taking photographs of the wedding to print in the papers, had made the whole clan recoil in collective horror. Catherine had conceded to skip the church service – but only on the hard promise that the wedding breakfast would be held at her house.

Even after all that, a small group of betting holdouts still swore she’d make a surprise appearance, and a soft huff of disappointment ran through the crowd when they saw her daughter-in-law walk in instead. Then came the gaunt, mincing figure of Medora Manson, swathed in a wild mess of stripes, fringes, and floating scarves – and Archer’s heart seized up so tight he thought it might stop beating. He’d been certain Medora and Ellen Olenska were still in Washington, that they’d left four weeks earlier to get Medora away from the cult leader Dr. Agathon Carver who’d almost recruited her for his “Valley of Love” commune. No one had expected either of them to come back for the wedding. He stared hard at Medora’s strange, frilly figure, craning to see who was right behind her – but the little procession was already over. There was no one else. Ellen wasn’t there.

“Newland – I say, she’s here!” his best man hissed in his ear.

Archer jolted back to his senses. Time had slipped away on him, he realized, because the pale pink bridesmaid procession was already halfway up the nave, the bishop, rector, and two white-robed acolytes hovering by the lily-heavy altar, and the first soft chords of the Spohr symphony were spilling like scattered petals ahead of the bride. He blinked, and the blur of tulle and orange blossoms that was May drifted closer and closer. She was glowing, so bright she sent a faint soft warmth through all his cold numbness, and he straightened up, and smiled right into her eyes as she came to stand beside him.

“Dearly beloved, we are gathered together here,” the rector began.

The ring slid onto her finger. The bishop gave his final benediction. The bridesmaids shifted to fall back into line, and the organ started rumbling the first notes of the Mendelssohn march – the one no New York newlywed couple had ever walked out of the church without. “Your arm! Give her your arm!” young Newland the best man hissed, nervous as a cat, and Archer realized he’d drifted off somewhere far away, half-convinced he’d caught a glimpse of Ellen’s dark coiled hair in the crowd of onlookers, only for it to turn out to be a total stranger with a long nose, so absurdly different he wondered if he was starting to hallucinate.

He and May walked slowly down the nave together, carried along on the light ripples of the march, the bright spring day beckoning through the wide open church doors. The footman wrapped her soft white cloak around her, and Archer jumped into the brougham right beside her. May turned to him with a brilliant, triumphant smile, and their hands clasped tight under her veil.

“Darling,” Archer said – and for a split second that dark, empty abyss yawned open under his feet, and he felt himself falling, falling, even as his voice came out smooth and cheerful, light as air. “Of course I thought I’d lost the ring. No wedding is complete without the poor groom panicking over that. But you did keep me waiting, you know! I had time to imagine every terrible disaster that could possibly happen.”

To his shock, she threw her arms around his neck right there in full view of Fifth Avenue’s passersby. “But none of them can ever happen now, can it Newland? Not as long as we’re together.”

Every last detail of the day had been planned to perfection. After the wedding breakfast, they had plenty of time to change into their travelling clothes, walk down Mrs. Mingott’s wide staircase between grinning bridesmaids and weeping parents, and climb into their brougham under the traditional shower of thrown rice and satin slippers. They still had half an hour left to drive to the station, buy the latest weeklies at the bookstall like two seasoned, experienced travellers, and settle into their private compartment, where May’s maid had already stowed her dove-grey travelling cloak and the brand new, shining leather dressing bag she’d ordered all the way from London. The old du Lac aunts out in Rhinebeck had lent them their country house for the honeymoon, eager to spend a week in New York visiting Archer’s mother, and Archer had jumped at the chance, glad to skip the usual dreadful bridal suite in a Philadelphia or Baltimore hotel. May was over the moon about the country, giggling at the way the eight bridesmaids had run around trying and failing to find out their secret retreat’s location. It was deemed “very English” to have a friend lend you a country house, and it made the wedding the most talked-about, brilliant event of the whole year.

Once the train pulled out of the city, shaking off the endless stretches of wooden suburban houses to roll out into pale, greening spring countryside, talking came easier to Archer than he’d expected. May was still the bright, simple, uncomplicated girl he’d fallen in love with, gushing to him over every little silly detail of the wedding, as casual and unshaken as if she was just chatting over tea. At first he’d thought her calmness was just a mask for jittery nerves, but her clear, wide eyes held nothing but unclouded, happy innocence. She was alone for the first time with her husband – but her husband was just her favorite, most trusted comrade, and this whole great adventure of running off on a journey together was the most fun, most wonderful lark she could possibly imagine. It amazed him, that such soft, deep warmth could exist in someone with no imagination at all – no quiet, secret hunger for the things they weren’t supposed to want.

“ I was surprised, though – weren’t you? – that Aunt Medora turned up after all,” May said suddenly, pulling him out of his thoughts. “Ellen wrote that neither of them were well enough to make the journey. I do wish it had been Ellen who felt well enough to come! Did you see the exquisite old lace she sent me as a wedding gift?”

Archer had known this moment would come eventually, but he’d foolishly thought he could will it away. “Yes – I – it was beautiful,” he said, staring at her blindly, wondering if every time he heard Ellen’s name his whole carefully constructed, carefully normal world would tumble down around him like a house of cards. “Aren’t you tired? It’ll be so nice to have tea when we get to the house, I’m sure the aunts have everything perfectly ready,” he rattled on, grabbing her hand tight in his. She instantly switched tracks, chattering excitedly about the magnificent Baltimore silver tea and coffee service the Beauforts had gifted them, how perfectly it matched the trays Lovell Mingott had given them.

The train pulled into the Rhinebeck station as the spring twilight deepened, and they walked along the platform to the waiting carriage. “Ah, how kind of the van der Luydens,” Archer exclaimed, spotting the sedate, unliveried man waiting by their bags. “They sent their man all the way over from Skuytercliff to meet us.”

“I’m very sorry, sir,” the man said, apologetic. “There was a little accident at the Misses du Lac’s house yesterday – their water tank sprung a leak. Mr. van der Luyden heard about it first thing this morning, and sent a housemaid up on the early train to get the Patroon’s house ready for you instead. It’s perfectly comfortable, sir, I promise you – and the Misses du Lac sent their cook over too, so it will be exactly the same as if you were staying in Rhinebeck.”

Archer stared at him blankly. May broke in before he could speak, bright and excited. “The same as Rhinebeck? The Patroon’s house? That’s a hundred thousand times better! Isn’t it, Newland? It’s the dearest, kindest thing Mr. van der Luyden could have done for us.” She leaned forward, giddy, as the carriage pulled away, their shiny new bridal bags on the seat in front of them. “I’ve never been inside it – have you? The van der Luydens barely ever show it to anyone. But they opened it for Ellen once, you know. She told me what a darling little place it was – she said it’s the only house in all of America she could ever imagine being perfectly happy in.”

“Well, that’s what we’re going to be, isn’t it?” Archer said, laughing, bright and easy, and she smiled that boyish, uncomplicated smile of hers.

“Ah!” she said. “This is our luck starting, isn’t it? The wonderful, perfect luck we’re going to have together, always.”
