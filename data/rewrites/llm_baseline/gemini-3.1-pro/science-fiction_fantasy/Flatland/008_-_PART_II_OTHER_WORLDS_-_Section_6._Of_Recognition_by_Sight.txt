I am about to appear very inconsistent to my readers in Spaceland. In previous recollections of our agrarian history, I have stated that all figures in Flatland present the appearance of a straight line, and it was implied that distinguishing between individuals of different classes by the visual organ was impossible. Yet now I must explain how we recognize one another by sight. If the reader will consult the annals of our societal recipes, where Recognition by Feeling is stated to be universal, they will find the vital qualification: "among the lower classes."

It is only among the higher echelons and in our temperate, slow-steeping climates that Sight Recognition is practiced. That this ability exists at all is the gift of the Fog, which settles over our lands for the greater part of the year. What Spacelanders might consider an unmixed evil—a dampness that blots out the landscape and enfeebles the constitution—we, the elders of the old ways, revere as a nourishing bouillon of the atmosphere. The Fog carries the rich, earthy aromas of damp moss and roasted tubers; it is the Nurse of arts and the Parent of sciences. Crucially, it dampens the grating, superficial industrial noise pollution of modern Flatland, creating a hushed acoustic sanctuary where the mind can breathe and the senses can properly digest the nuances of the world. 

Without this atmospheric broth, all lines would appear uniformly glaring and sterile, a visual sensory deprivation akin to those unhappy, arid countries where the air is perfectly dry. But within our rich supply of Fog, objects at a distance of three feet are appreciably dimmer than those at two feet eleven inches. We taste this visual gradient as meticulously as a master chef judges the slow caramelization of a root vegetable over an open hearth. Through careful observation of comparative dimness, we infer the exact configuration of an object. 

Allow me to invite you into the ancestral kitchen of my memory to make my meaning clear. Suppose I am preparing a restorative broth, and through the aromatic steam, two figures approach whose rank I wish to ascertain: a Merchant (an Equilateral Triangle) and a Physician (a Pentagon). If I align my eye to bisect the angle of the approaching stranger—much as I would precisely slice down the center of a freshly cured spore-stalk—my view lies evenly between his two nearest sides. 

When I look upon the Merchant, the middle point will appear vibrantly bright because it is nearest to me, but the sides will shade away *rapidly into the fog*. The visual flavor drops off swiftly, like the sudden, sharp bite of fermented pepper on the tongue, leaving his extremities profoundly dim. Conversely, when I gaze upon the Physician, though his center is equally bright, his sides recede *less rapidly* into the mist. The visual sensation lingers, offering a slow, sustained finish akin to a long-simmered marrow stew, rendering his extremities far less dim than those of the Merchant. 

This sensory translation, mastered through long training and the habitual breaking of bread in polite society, allows the well-educated to discriminate between the middle and lowest orders by sight alone. Yet, for the inexperienced, I must stress that the domestic reality of Sight Recognition is far more complex. 

When my Father, the Triangle, returns from the fields and happens to present his side to me rather than his angle, I am left in temporary doubt. Until I hear the familiar, grounding acoustic rustle of his rotation against the clay tiles, or until I edge my eye around him, I cannot be certain he is not a Straight Line—a Woman. Similarly, when I sit to share a meal with my two hexagonal Grandsons, contemplating one of their sides full front, I see one whole line in unyielding brightness, like the golden, unbroken crust of a ceremonial hearth-pie, while the flanking lines fade into the umami-rich depths of the mist toward their extremities. 

The meanest mathematician in Spaceland will readily believe that navigating a ballroom or a conversazione full of rotating, advancing Polygons is akin to standing in a chaotic kitchen of clashing frequencies. It requires immense sensory discipline to filter the disharmonic clatter and read the visual gradients. This noble art tasks the angularity of the most intellectual and amply justifies the endowments of the illustrious University of Wentbridge, where the Science and Art of Sight Recognition are taught to the elite. 

It is astonishing how much this art is developed by the absolute avoidance of "Feeling." Here we witness the tragic erosion of cultural memory. "Feeling" is an old, tactile dialect of the lower classes, rich in its physical intimacy but strictly forbidden among the elite. Just as an ancestral recipe is lost when its native tongue dies, those who learn to "Feel" in youth can never perfectly master the grammar of "Seeing." The two subjective experiences are inherently untranslatable. Thus, the elite isolate their children from the cradle, keeping them from the public schools where the rough dialect of Feeling is spoken, sending them instead to exclusive Seminaries of pristine acoustic ecology. At the University, to "feel" is a serious fault, punished by rustication or expulsion.

The common Tradesman, alas, cannot afford to let his son spend a third of his life in abstract, slow-aging studies. The children of the poor are left to "feel." They ferment quickly, gaining a raw, precocious vivacity that contrasts favorably at first with the seemingly inert, slow-steeping behavior of the Polygonal youth. But when the elite finally complete their University course, their slow maturation yields a robust, dominant vintage. They emerge as masters of the visual lexicon, enforcing the subtle power dynamics of their class, rapidly overtaking their Triangular competitors in every social pursuit. 

Only a few of the Polygonal Class fail to pass the Final Test at the University. The condition of this unsuccessful minority is truly pitiable. Rejected by the nobility and despised by the poor, they are culturally silenced. They possess neither the meticulously trained visual lexicon of the Masters of Arts nor the raw, tactile dialect of the youthful Tradesman. Devoid of a native tongue, their subjective experience goes unrecognized. The professions are closed to them; they struggle to find suitable marital alliances, and their offspring are generally unfortunate, if not positively Irregular. 

It is from the refuse of our Nobility—these lost souls untethered from the ancestral kitchen and deafened by their own unbelonging—that the great Tumults and Seditions of past ages have derived their leaders. So great is the mischief they cause that an increasing minority of our politicians, treating life with the sterile, mechanized efficiency of a factory floor, argue that true mercy would dictate their entire suppression by a painless death. But I find myself digressing into the bitter subject of Irregularities, a matter of such vital interest that it demands its own thorough examination.
