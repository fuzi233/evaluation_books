**CHAPTER XXII. The Prodigal’s Return (Or, How a Son Came Home and Was Forgiven—And Also, How His Sister Nearly Bankrupted the Family)**

When Major Pendennis wrote that letter—oh, what a letter!—it was like dropping a bombshell into the quiet village of Clavering. Doctor Portman, ever the moral compass of the district, didn’t hesitate. He packed his bags with the urgency of a man who’d just been told he’d forgotten to pay his taxes *and* had a toothache. Off he went to Fairoaks, not because he liked bad news, but because he believed in delivering it with the dignity of a man who’d once held a position in the Church and had seen enough sin to last a lifetime.

He arrived at the Pendennis home with all the solemnity of a funeral procession, though no one had died—yet. He read the letter aloud, pausing dramatically between sentences as if each word were a verdict from God Himself. “The young scapegrace,” he declared, “is going deeper and deeper into perdition!” He added several more such remarks, which sounded very impressive, especially when delivered in a voice that suggested he might have been born in a cathedral.

Afterward, he left the ladies to spread the news through Clavering society—where gossip travels faster than a horse with a broken leg—and strode off to Fairoaks to break the news to the widow herself. But she already knew. She’d read Pen’s letter, and oddly enough, it had brought her relief. For months, a shadow had hung over her—a dark, brooding suspicion that her son was spiraling toward ruin. Now, she knew the worst. And what did she find? Her darling boy had returned, repentant, tender-hearted, and full of regret. What more could a mother ask for?

Doctor Portman, bless his soul, tried to lecture her on virtue, duty, and the importance of degrees. But Helen Pendennis wasn’t having it. “What good is a degree?” she asked, quite reasonably. “It won’t feed him, nor clothe him, nor keep him from being laughed at by those who don’t know him.” She went on to question why anyone would send a boy to Oxford when there was so much temptation, so little benefit, and so many places where he could be properly supervised—like, say, *home*. As for debts? “His father’s money was his!” she insisted. “And if he spent it, well—wasn’t that what money was for?”

So while Doctor Portman stood there, flustered and indignant, like a man who’d just discovered his favorite hat was made of paper, the widow simply smiled and said, “I’m glad he’s back.”

Meanwhile, Miss Laura Bell—now sixteen years old and about as tall as a maypole with roses growing around its top—had also been keeping an eye on her brother. Once upon a time, they’d shared affectionate embraces, kisses, and whispered secrets. But now, after college, Arthur had returned to find that the little girl he’d left behind had become a young lady—tall, graceful, and suddenly too dignified for cheek-kisses. Instead, she greeted him with a curtsey, a blush, and a hand offered like a peace offering. It was awkward. It was sweet. It was the kind of moment that makes you wish you’d stayed away.

Laura, though, had grown up fast. She’d developed opinions—especially about her brother’s behavior. His letters had become shorter, colder, more like postcards from a distant land where nobody cared about mothers. “It’s better he loses a prize,” she’d say, “than forgets his mother.” She didn’t understand why he couldn’t come home instead of spending vacations at the grand houses of his rich friends. “Nobody there loves him half as much as you do,” she’d tell her mother.

Helen would sigh. “But he must make his way in the world,” she’d murmur, trying to sound convincing even to herself.

Laura wouldn’t hear it. She thought Arthur had become too free with his talk—too sarcastic, too dismissive of women in general. He smoked pipes indoors. He refused to go to church. He yawned during novels while wearing a dressing-gown that looked like it had seen better days. Worst of all, he’d fallen for an actress—Miss Fotheringay, a woman whose name alone made Laura want to throw a teacup.

“A Pendennis!” she gasped. “Falling for *that* woman? Galloping off every day to kneel at her feet and drink with her horrid father? And then bringing them both into our house? I’d have run away, mamma—I’d have walked barefoot through snow!”

“Then you’d have left me,” Helen replied gently.

And so, with tears in her eyes, Laura withdrew her statement, and the two women embraced like sisters who’d just survived a hurricane.

Now, here’s the thing: Laura didn’t hate Arthur. Not really. She was angry—at his choices, at his pride, at his reckless ways. But when she heard he was miserable, ill, and ashamed, her anger vanished like smoke in the wind. In an instant, she became the most devoted sister imaginable. She took Pen’s side against Doctor Portman, who had the audacity to speak of “transgressions” and “moral failings.”

“Debts?” Laura scoffed. “They’re nothing! He was thrown into expensive society by his uncle—what was he supposed to do, eat bread and water while others dined on caviar? And as for the degree—poor boy, he was sick! Can you think of math when your mind is full of creditors?”

She glared at the doctor, seized her mother’s hand, and kissed her passionately in front of him. “How dare you speak against him?” she seemed to say with her eyes. “He’s my brother. My darling Pen.”

Doctor Portman left, slightly stunned, wondering if he’d just been outwitted by a pair of women who clearly didn’t believe in logic.

Once he was gone, Laura sprang into action. Fires were lit in Arthur’s room. His bedding was aired. And while Helen penned a tender letter to her son, Laura joined her, led her into the room, and sat beside her on the bed like two conspirators plotting a revolution.

Then, in a fit of inspiration, Laura added a postscript:
> “My dearest Pen—come home instantly! Two dashes under ‘Pen’ for emphasis. Be happy with your mother and your affectionate sister Laura.”

That night, as the two women lay awake—reading their Bibles and checking on Pen’s room—Laura suddenly sat up.
“Mamma,” she whispered, “are you awake?”

“Yes,” Helen murmured, though she hadn’t slept a wink. She’d been staring at the night-lamp, thinking of Pen.

Then Laura revealed her brilliant plan. “You know, mamma,” she said, “I’ve lived with you for ten years. You’ve never taken any of my money. That’s offended me. I’m proud. I don’t like being beholden.”

“And?” Helen asked, intrigued.

“Well,” Laura continued, “if I’d gone to school, it would’ve cost fifty pounds a year. Ten years times fifty—that’s five hundred pounds! I know you put it in the bank at Chatteris. So tomorrow—we’ll go to Chatteris. We’ll see Mr. Rowdy, the bald-headed banker, and ask for it. And I bet he’ll give us two more. We’ll save it, send it to Pen, and he can pay his debts without hurting anyone. Then we’ll live happily ever after.”

Helen’s response was a mix of laughter, tears, and frantic hugs. The plan was absurd—but somehow, perfectly logical.

And so, when Pen returned, the house was warm, welcoming, and full of love. He stepped down from the coach, shivering in the January cold, refusing an inside seat because he wanted to suffer for his sins. He met Old John, who had traded his livery for a fustian jacket and now worked as gardener and stable-man. “I’m in the lodge now,” he said proudly, grinning.

But as soon as Pen turned the corner, Helen appeared—beaming, forgiving, ready to embrace him as if he’d only been away for a week.

She’d already written him a letter detailing Laura’s noble offer, complete with praise and blessings for both children.

Pen blushed when he saw Laura, who waited in the hall. This time, she broke their long-standing rule and kissed him—just once. And then, wisely, stopped. There had been *too much* kissing already in this chapter.

The prodigal was home. The fatted calf was slaughtered (metaphorically). And he was made as happy as two simple women could possibly make him.

No one mentioned the Oxbridge disaster. No one asked what he’d do next. But Pen, restless and remorseful, couldn’t stop thinking about it.

A few days later, he rode to Chatteris, then came back on the top of the coach. He sold his horse and handed over the cheque. It was a gesture of virtue—or perhaps just guilt. Laura called it “strict justice.” Pen avoided talking about the loan, though he thanked her once or twice, stammering like a man who’d just been caught stealing.

He gave up wine, switched to whisky-and-water (with great moderation), quit cigars (though he’d always preferred pipes anyway), and fell asleep frequently after dinner. He watched coaches like a hawk, read newspapers obsessively, played cribbage with Captain Glanders, and avoided Doctor Portman like the plague.

But he went to church regularly. He read prayers for his mother. He kept the house humble—two maids, no silver dish-covers, John in his livery only on Sundays. It was a life of austerity, and it made him feel worse.

For a while, he swore he’d never return to Oxford. But then Laura, blushing furiously, suggested he go back—not for glory, but for redemption. “As punishment,” she said, “you should get your degree.”

And so, with a heavy heart, Pen returned.

At Oxford, he was a ghost. A plucked bird. No longer part of any set, no longer known by name. He went to chapel every morning, shut himself in his rooms at night, and studied like a man trying to win back something he’d lost.

There were no duns at his door—his debts were paid. No cards left. His classmates had graduated and moved on. He took a second examination—and passed easily.

When he returned in his bachelor’s gown, he felt a little lighter. He visited his uncle in London, but the old man barely acknowledged him. One visit, he got a cold nod. The second? Morgan the valet said, “Master’s not home.”

Pen returned to Fairoaks, to books, to silence, to loneliness.

He wrote tragedies. Poems about despair. He considered enlisting—Spain, the Legion, anything to escape. He cursed idleness, though it was idleness that had brought him home.

Helen worried. “He’s breaking his heart,” she said. “We must get him out of here.”

And so, when they could afford it, he would go abroad. Or to London. Anywhere but this quiet house with two women who loved him too much—and whose sadness began to deepen with each passing day.

For the first time, Laura noticed how pale her mother had become. How tired. How the light seemed to fade from her eyes.

And Pen? He was still there—repentant, penitent, and quietly dying of boredom.
