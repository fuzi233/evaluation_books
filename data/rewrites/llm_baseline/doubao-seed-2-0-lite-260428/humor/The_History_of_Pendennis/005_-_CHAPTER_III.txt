Arthur Pendennis was just turned sixteen, fresh off dethroning his schoolmasters to reign as unchallenged lord of Fairoaks estate, and to hear his doting mother tell it, he was the spitting image of a Greek god in miniature. Any neutral onlooker would have called him dumpy as a sack of new potatoes, but Helen’s golden-eyed assessment was “neat, compact, and distinguished.” His brown hair glinted like spun gold in the sun, his round rosy face was dotted with freckles, and he was so perpetually ready to laugh that his crinkly honest blue eyes made every stranger want to like him instantly. The faint reddish stubble he stared at in the mirror for 20 minutes every morning, praying it would turn into proper whiskers, was a family miracle as far as his mother was concerned. Between 16 and 18 he shot up three whole inches to 5 foot 8, a feat Helen refused to believe was even possible, given that his late revered father had been barely 5 foot 5. She was half-convinced he’d been sneaking growth tonic in his lemonade when no one was looking.

There was zero chance of him ever going back to Greyfriars School. The masters’ strict rules about Greek verb conjugations and cold porridge at 6 a.m. did not fit his new royal schedule of lazing on the lawn reading novels. His uncle the Major and the family doctor both insisted he needed to cram a full Greek tragedy under his belt if he ever wanted to get anywhere in life, but Pen very casually dropped a line to Helen over tea about how Greyfriars was full of unruly lads who swapped pocket pistols and stole pies off the headmaster’s windowsill, and Helen panicked so fast she agreed he could stay home indefinitely, no further questions asked.

Next, the Major offered to pull strings with the Commander-in-Chief to get Pen a fancy commission in the Foot Guards, and Pen’s heart leaped so high it nearly bounced out of his chest. He still remembered his old schoolmate Tom Ricketts, the sad little fourth-former who used to wear trousers so tight he could barely sit down without squeaking, who every older boy used as a human target for rubber erasers. Now he’d seen Tom parading in crimson and gold uniform, balancing a giant bearskin cap on his head, hefting regimental colours, and giving Pen a patronising little nod like he was a lowly stable boy. Pen would have traded every last one of his penny dreadfuls to have that uniform, to march at the centre of a crowd of bayonets and Waterloo medals, to have the band blast trumpets for him wherever he went.

But when he brought the news to Helen, she turned pale as a sheet. She said no proper Christian gentleman made the army his profession, and his dear departed dad would rise straight out of his grave to scold him if he even thought about it. Pen, who would sooner cut off his own nose than make his mom cry, immediately surrendered his imaginary red coat and epaulettes no questions asked. Helen thought he was the noblest creature to walk the face of the earth. The Major, meanwhile, fired off a huffy curt letter back calling his nephew a soft-headed spooney who’d thrown away the best possible career for no reason.

That Christmas, when the Major came down to visit, he softened a little when he saw Pen ride out hunting. Pen had a lovely bay mare named Rebecca, and he rode with such cool, unshowy pluck he cleared every fence in the shire without a single wobble. He immediately started bragging about his brand new top boots to all his old school friends, and even Helen had to admit he’d look devastatingly handsome in a hunting scarlet. The catch was, every time he rode out, she paced the parlour for four hours straight, fully expecting him to be carried home on a shutter after a bad fall.

Don’t go thinking he neglected his studies entirely, though. He devoured every single book in a 10 mile radius that wasn’t on the school’s required reading list: old plays, horse care manuals, a dusty crate of very racy French novels he dug out of the back of the Clavering bookshop, and all the old heavy folios in Dr Portman the vicar’s library, which he’d read perched on the top rung of the library step for hours like a very determined pirate scaling a mast. He and Dr Portman bonded hard over glasses of port after dinner, and the only thing Mrs Portman sighed over was that her daughter Minny was two full years older than Pen’s own mother, and weighed as much as Pen and Helen put together. No chance of that little romance ever taking off.

To keep him from turning into a total layabout, they hired Mr Smirke, the vicar’s curate, to tutor him daily for a very generous salary. Smirke was a perfectly nice man who never spilled tea on the tablecloth, wore one perfectly preened curl on his fair forehead, and tied his cravat with the somber grace of a man attending a funeral for a favourite goldfish. He rode his pony so badly his toes stuck out so far at the ankle you could hang a sheet over them. No teen boy with a working sense of humour could possibly respect that. Pen nearly gave Smirke a full heart attack when he plopped him on Rebecca and took him out across a common right where the local foxhounds were running. Smirke rode straight into the middle of the pack, lamed one of the hounds, and got yelled at by the huntsman so loud and so colourfully Smirke thought he’d be struck by lightning right there in the mud.

Smirke was an absolutely useless tutor, it turned out. Instead of drilling Pen on algebra, he shared all his terrible Latin and English poetry with him, and they galloped through Homer and the Greek tragedies at such a chaotic, breakneck pace Pen remembered almost nothing later except three random quotes he could drop at parties to sound well-read, exactly like every posh Oxford man of the era. Pen was a diehard Byron stan, knew all his lines by heart, and would drag little Laura, his mom’s tiny ward, over to the window to recite dramatic Corsair monologues at her until she stared so wide-eyed you’d think he was speaking alien. When he read Mrs Hemans and Bishop Heber out loud to Helen, she’d dissolve into tears so fast she was immediately sobbing into her linen handkerchief.

It wasn’t long before Pen started getting his terrible poetry published in the County Chronicle, all signed “NEP”: verses about a tear, a rant denouncing Popery on St Bartholomew’s Day, an ode to Madame Caradori singing at the assizes, and Helen kept every crumpled clipping tucked in her jewellery box right next to his baby socks and his first ever haircut. He wrote a full-length tragedy where 16 people died before the end of act two, and Helen laughed so hard at it Pen threw the whole manuscript in the fire in a huff. He even rode out electioneering for the local Blue Tory candidate, got a little too carried away with the election Madeira, and stumbled home at 10 p.m. with six yelling farmer voters trailing behind him, only for Helen to step out in her white nightgown holding a single candle. The voters scattered like naughty mice before she could even scold them.

By this point, 18-year-old Pen had caught the love bug full bore. He was dying for a grand, consuming, Byronic passion, a fair lady to write all his poems for, instead of wasting verses on fictional Zuleikas and Ianthes he’d read about in books. What he didn’t know was that Smirke the tutor was also madly in love – not with some pretty young lady his own age, but with Helen Pendennis, Pen’s mother. He was so smitten he gave Pen his favourite Elzevir Horace, a silver pencil case, and let him skip every single math lesson he wanted, just to hang around the estate near Helen. He never washed the glass Helen drank tea from once, and kept it on a special sacred shelf above his desk. He thought Pen was the luckiest boy alive, getting to live in the same house as the most beautiful woman in the whole shire.

Helen, meanwhile, was pinching pennies left and right, convinced Pen was going to grow up to be either Archbishop of Canterbury or Lord Chancellor, no less. There was just one tiny problem: there was literally no single girl Pen’s age within 15 miles. The dairymaid? Far too low for a Pendennis to court. The admiral’s daughters? Thought Pen was a snot-nosed kid. The three Glanders girls? Still in pinafores with their braids tied up in dirty pink ribbon. So Pen rode out every day, craning his neck to stare into every passing carriage, half-convinced his mysterious perfect Dulcinea would be sitting in the back of some random yellow post-chaise. Every single time, it was either a 70-year-old snuffy old dowager or three girls who were no prettier than the turnips growing by the side of the road. Helen, for her part, had already mapped out his whole romantic future: he’d marry little Laura, when he was 26 and she was 18, fresh back from his grand tour, settled in a rose-covered country parsonage preaching the finest sermons the shire had ever heard.

It was on one such trip, when Pen rode into Chatteris to drop off his new epic poem for the County Chronicle, that he was leaning against the stable wall chatting to the hostler about Rebecca, when a grand black tandem carriage with bright scarlet wheels rattled into the yard. A giant figure wrapped in three overcoats and a wide-brimmed hat leaned out the window and yelled, “Hallo, Pendennis! Is that you?” It was none other than his long-lost old schoolfellow, Mr Foker.
