“That doubt and trouble, fear and pain,
And anguish, all, are shadows vain,
That death itself shall not remain;
That weary deserts we may tread,
A dreary labyrinth may thread.
Thro’ dark ways underground be led;
Yet, if we will one Guide obey,
The dreariest path, the darkest way
Shall issue out in heavenly day;
And we, on divers shores now cast,
Shall meet, our perilous voyage past,
All in our Father’s house at last!”

Margaret hurried up the stairs as soon as the visitors had left, snatching her bonnet and shawl before rushing out to see how Betsy Higgins was faring. She meant to sit with her as long as she could before dinner, her heart drawn toward the girl by a quiet, growing tenderness. As she walked through the narrow, crowded lanes of Milton, the city seemed different—less alien, more alive—now that she had come to care for someone who lived among its soot-stained walls and echoing streets.

Mary Higgins, Betsy’s younger sister, had done what she could to make the house presentable for the visit. The central floor had been roughly scrubbed, though the corners beneath chairs and along the walls remained dark and grimy. Though the sun beat down fiercely outside, a large fire blazed in the grate, filling the room with an oppressive heat. Margaret did not understand why so much coal was burned—it seemed wasteful, even cruel—but Mary meant it as a gesture of welcome. Margaret thought perhaps it was needed for Betsy’s comfort, though she suspected it was more about hospitality than necessity.

Betsy lay on a short sofa under the window, weaker than the day before. Her strength had ebbed away, and she had grown tired from straining to see each passerby, hoping it might be Margaret. Now that Margaret was there, Betsy relaxed, her eyes softening as she studied the young woman’s face, her fingers brushing gently over the fine fabric of her dress with a childlike wonder.

“I never knew why folks in the Bible cared so much for soft clothes,” she murmured. “But it must feel good to wear such things. It’s not like common cloth. Most fine folk’s clothes blind me with their colors, but yours… they rest my eyes. Where did you get this gown?”

“London,” Margaret replied, smiling.

“London? You’ve been there?” Betsy asked, wide-eyed.

“Yes,” Margaret said. “I lived there for years. But my real home was in the country—in a forest.”

“Tell me about it,” Betsy whispered. “I love hearing of trees and open places.”

She leaned back, closed her eyes, and folded her hands across her chest, as if preparing to receive every word. Margaret had never spoken of Helstone since leaving it—only mentioned it in passing. Yet in dreams, the place came vividly to life: the deep shade of ancient oaks, the rustling leaves that made a constant whispering sound even when no wind stirred, the moss-covered ground soft as velvet, and the hidden brook that trickled just beyond sight, feeding the lush greenery.

“There are ferns too,” Margaret continued, “whole fields of them—some in shadow, others lit by golden sunlight, like waves on the sea.”

“I’ve never seen the sea,” Betsy murmured. “But go on.”

“And high above the trees, there are wide commons—open hills where you can see for miles.”

“I’m glad of that,” Betsy said softly. “Down here I always feel smothered. When I walk outside, I want to climb up high, to breathe deeply and see far. In Milton, I feel trapped. And that noise—the constant hum in the mill—it makes my head ache. Sometimes I think if I could just hear silence again, just once… I’d be healed.”

“You’ll find peace,” Margaret said gently. “Even in heaven, there is rest beyond anything we know.”

Betsy shifted uneasily. “I wish my father wouldn’t talk to me the way he does. He means well—I told you that yesterday, and I’ll tell you again. But when I’m feverish, half awake, his words come back to me—so strong, so terrible. I think, if this is all there is—if I’ve only been born to work myself to death in this stifling place, listening to those machines until I scream for them to stop, breathing cotton fluff until I thirst for clean air, with my mother gone and no chance to say how much I loved her—then I’d go mad. I’d kill you, I swear I would.”

She collapsed back, exhausted by the intensity of her emotion. Margaret knelt beside her, her voice calm and steady. “Bessy—we have a Father in Heaven.”

“I know,” Betsy moaned, turning her head from side to side. “I know. I’m wicked. I spoke wickedly. Don’t be afraid of me. I wouldn’t hurt a hair on your head. And…”—her eyes opened, meeting Margaret’s with sudden clarity—“I believe more than you do in what comes after. I’ve read Revelation till I know it by heart. When I’m清醒, I don’t doubt the glory that awaits us.”

“Then let’s not speak of the fears that come when you’re unwell,” Margaret said. “Tell me instead—what did you do when you were strong?”

“I was strong when Mother died,” Betsy said quietly. “But I’ve never been truly well since. I began working in the carding-room soon after, and the fluff got into my lungs. It poisoned me.”

“Fluff?” Margaret asked, puzzled.

“Tiny bits that fly off the cotton during carding,” Betsy explained. “They fill the air like white dust. They say it wraps around the lungs, tightens them. Many workers end up coughing blood, wasting away—poisoned by the very air they breathe.”

“But can’t it be stopped?” Margaret asked.

“I don’t know. Some factories have great wheels at one end to draw the dust away. But they cost hundreds of pounds—no profit, so few masters install them. And some men don’t like them either—they say the fresh air makes them hungry, after years of swallowing fluff. So they refuse to work where the wheels are. Between master and man, the wheels never get built. I wish ours had one.”

“Didn’t your father know?” Margaret asked.

“Yes. He was sorry. But our factory was steady, our people reliable. He feared moving me to another place. Back then, many called me a fine girl. I didn’t want to seem weak or delicate. And Mary needed schooling—Mother insisted—and Father liked books and lectures. All of it cost money. So I kept working, until I’ll never shake the hum from my ears or the fluff from my throat in this world.”

“How old are you?” Margaret asked.

“Nineteen, next July.”

“And I am nineteen too,” Margaret said softly. For a moment, she couldn’t speak—overcome by the contrast between their lives, her own sheltered youth against Betsy’s suffering and early decline.

“About Mary,” Betsy said, changing the subject. “I wanted to ask you to be kind to her. She’s seventeen, the youngest of us. I don’t want her to go to the mill. But I don’t know what else she’s fit for.”

“She could hardly take a servant’s place,” Margaret said, glancing unconsciously at the untidy corners of the room. “We have a faithful old servant—almost a friend—who needs help, but she’s particular. I couldn’t ask her to take someone who might cause irritation.”

“No,” Betsy agreed. “I see you’re right. Our Mary’s a good girl, but who taught her how to run a house? No mother, and me too sick to guide her properly. I scolded her for doing things wrong—when I didn’t know how to do them myself. Still, I wish she could have lived with you.”

“Even if she can’t come here,” Margaret said, “I’ll always be a friend to her—for your sake, Bessy. I must go now, but I’ll return as soon as I can. Even if it’s not tomorrow, or the next day, or even a week or two later—don’t think I’ve forgotten you. I may be busy.”

“I’ll know you won’t forget me,” Betsy said, squeezing her hand. “But remember—within a week or two, I may be dead and buried.”

“I’ll come again as soon as I can,” Margaret promised, holding her hand tightly. “And if you grow worse, tell me.”

“Ay,” Betsy whispered, returning the pressure.

From that day onward, Mrs. Hale grew increasingly frail. The anniversary of Edith’s marriage approached, and looking back over the year’s trials, Margaret marveled at how they had endured. If she had known then what lay ahead, she would have fled in terror. Yet each day passed with quiet resilience, small joys sparkling through the sorrow like drops of light in darkness.

A year ago, when she first came to Helstone and first sensed her mother’s irritability, she would have dreaded a long illness in a strange, noisy town, with comforts diminished. But now, despite the hardship, something new had taken root in her mother’s spirit—a deeper patience. Though physically suffering, she was gentle, quiet, almost peaceful, in a way she had never been when her troubles were merely imagined.

Mr. Hale, meanwhile, was caught in the trap of denial, a common flaw among men of his nature. He resented his daughter’s concern more than ever.

“Indeed, Margaret, you’re growing fanciful!” he declared. “God knows I’d be the first to act if your mother were truly ill. We always knew when she had headaches at Helstone—she turned pale. But now she has a healthy color, just as she did when I first met her.”

“But Papa,” Margaret hesitated, “I think that flush is pain.”

“Nonsense! You’re imagining things. You’re the one who’s not well. Send for the doctor tomorrow—for yourself. Then, if it will ease your mind, he can see your mother.”

“Thank you, dear Papa,” Margaret said, her voice soft. “It would make me happier.”

She went to him to kiss him, but he gently pushed her away—just enough to show discomfort, as if her presence carried unpleasant thoughts he wished to dismiss. He paced the room, restless.

“Poor Maria,” he muttered, half to himself. “I wish one could do right without hurting others. I’ll hate this town—and myself—if she…”

He paused, then turned to her. “Does your mother often talk of Helstone?”

“No, Papa,” Margaret said, her voice heavy.

“Then she’s not grieving for it, eh? That’s a relief. I always found comfort in knowing your mother was honest—she never hid her troubles from me. She would never lie about her health. So don’t bring these morbid ideas into our home. Come, give me a kiss and go to bed.”

She kissed him and retired slowly, undressing with languid movements. But long after she had lain down, she heard him pacing—back and forth, like a caged animal—his steps echoing in the silence, a rhythm of anxiety that refused to cease.
