The October morning came in upon Milton like an unbidden guest who had forgotten to wipe his boots. It was not the country’s October, with its tender pearl-grey veils that lift as the sun warms the hedgerows into colour; it was the town’s—heavy with fog that seemed to have learned its weight from the factory chimneys, a damp pressing on brick and skin alike, and turning every sound duller. Even the street-cries came as if muffled under wet cloth; the distant clank of iron and the faint, ceaseless breath of machinery threaded through the mist, a harsh counterpoint to the hush within the Hales’ little house.

Margaret moved from room to room with a languor that was not idleness, but the fatigue of a mind that must do duty for three. Dixon had the shutters half-opened, letting in a weak, colourless light; and Margaret helped as she could—straightening chair-backs, smoothing a coverlet, placing things with a kind of desperate exactness, as if the proper order of a breakfast-table might keep the world from further disorder. Her eyes smarted and blurred, not from smoke, but from tears that would come and go without asking leave. She had not the leisure for any full abandonment to grief. While her father and brother were lost in it, she must be the hand that measured and the voice that decided.

Dixon had been making the room as homely as she could: the fire bright and crackling, its resinous scent sharp against the damp; the kettle singing with that thin, domestic insistence which, on other mornings, had been comfort enough to summon everyone to life. The teacups stood ready. Toast was laid by the fender to keep warm, and its smell—brown crust and butter beginning to melt—rose up like an appeal to ordinary appetite; but nothing about this day had any right to be ordinary.

Margaret took one last look round, longing that cheerfulness might be possible, and hating it when it appeared. The contrast between the bright hearth and the dull weight within her made her throat ache. She dropped to her knees by the sofa and hid her face in the cushions so no one might hear the sound she could not keep back.

A hand, not gentle by nature yet softened now, touched her shoulder.

“Come, Miss Hale—come, my dear,” said Dixon. There was an unusual tremor under the primness. “You must not give way, or where shall we all be? There is not another person in the house fit to give a direction of any kind, and there is so much to be done. There’s who’s to manage the funeral; and who’s to come to it; and where it’s to be; and all to be settled. Master Frederick’s like one crazed with crying, and master never was a good one for settling; and, poor gentleman, he goes about now as if he was lost. It’s bad enough, my dear, I know; but death comes to us all; and you’re well off never to have lost any friend till now.”

Perhaps she was. Yet this loss seemed solitary, unmatched—an eclipse that blotted out other measures of sorrow. Dixon’s commonplaces gave no comfort; but the tenderness in her voice—so rare from her—went straight to Margaret’s heart. For that tenderness’ sake, as much as for any resolution of her own, she forced herself upright, managed a wan smile in answer to Dixon’s anxious gaze, and went to summon her father and Frederick.

Mr. Hale came as if called from a distance only he could see—moving with the unconscious obedience of a sleep-walker, whose eyes are open on another world. Frederick entered more briskly, with a cheerfulness strained into place; he grasped Margaret’s hand, looked once into her face, and the strain broke. Tears came as if they had been waiting behind his eyes for permission.

Through breakfast she talked of little nothings—of the fire, of the fog, of some trifling need in the house—because she could not bear that their thoughts should settle too heavily on the last meal taken with that perpetual listening for a sound from the sick-room. The kettle’s song seemed almost indecent now, too lively for so solemn a table; and outside, as if in rebuke, the town’s muffled roar went on—wheels, distant shouts, a faint metallic beat—Milton insisting on its own business while their private world fell in.

Afterwards, she tried to speak to her father of the funeral. He assented to all she suggested, and in his listlessness contradicted himself without knowing it; his “yes” had no more weight than a leaf. Margaret obtained no real decision; and she was turning away to consult Dixon when Mr. Hale motioned her back with a feeble urgency.

“Ask Mr. Bell,” he said, hollow-voiced.

“Mr. Bell?” She was startled. “Mr. Bell of Oxford?”

“Mr. Bell,” he repeated. “Yes. He was my groom’s-man.”

She understood in a moment how memory had leaped back to the old phrase and the old friend. “I will write to-day,” she answered; and he sank again into that stunned passivity, like a man who has used his last strength on a single word.

The hours went on in a whirl of mournful errands—messages, arrangements, the necessary commerce with strangers which death demands. It seemed to Margaret that the house had become a place where the living were only the hands of duty. Toward evening Dixon came to her with the air of one who has acted on her own authority.

“I’ve done it, miss,” she began, speaking quickly, as if she feared to lose her courage. “I was really afraid for master, that he’d have a stroke with grief. He’s been all this day with poor missus; and when I’ve listened at the door, I’ve heard him talking to her, and talking to her, as if she was alive. When I went in he would be quite quiet, but all in a maze like. So I thought to myself, he ought to be roused; and if it gives him a shock at first, it will, maybe, be the better for him afterwards. So I’ve been and told him, that I don’t think it’s safe for Master Frederick to be here. And I don’t.”

Margaret’s heart tightened. “Why? What has happened?”

“It was only on Tuesday, when I was out, that I met a Southampton man—the first I’ve seen since I came to Milton; they don’t make their way much up here, I think. Well, it was young Leonards, old Leonards the draper’s son, as great a scamp as ever lived—who plagued his father almost to death, and then ran off to sea. I never could abide him. He was in the Orion at the same time as Frederick, I know; though I don’t recollect if he was there at the mutiny.”

“Did he know you?” Margaret asked, suddenly eager, the question tasting bitter in her mouth.

“Why, that’s the worst of it. I don’t believe he would have known me but for my being such a fool as to call out his name. He were a Southampton man, in a strange place, or else I should never have been so ready to call cousins with him, a nasty, good-for-nothing-fellow. Says he, ‘Miss Dixon! who would ha’ thought of seeing you here? But perhaps I mistake, and you’re Miss Dixon no longer?’ So I told him he might still address me as an unmarried lady, though if I hadn’t been so particular, I’d had good chances of matrimony. He was polite enough: ‘He couldn’t look at me and doubt me.’ But I were not to be caught with such chaff from such a fellow as him, and so I told him; and, by way of being even, I asked him after his father (who I knew had turned him out of doors), as if they were the best friends as ever was.”

Dixon’s indignation made her voice ring sharper than she perhaps intended; then she lowered it, as if the very walls might carry sound out into the street.

“So then, to spite me—for you see we were getting savage, for all we were so civil to each other—he began to inquire after Master Frederick, and said, what a scrape he’d got into, and how he’d be hung for mutiny if ever he were caught, and how a hundred pound reward had been offered for catching him, and what a disgrace he had been to his family—all to spite me, you see, my dear, because before now I’ve helped old Mr. Leonards to give George a good rating, down in Southampton.”

Margaret felt as if the thick town fog had crept into the room and laid itself upon her chest. “What did you say? Did you—”

“So I said, there were other families as I knew, who had far more cause to blush for their sons, and to be thankful if they could think they were earning an honest living far away from home. To which he made answer, like the impudent chap he is, that he were in a confidential situation, and if I knew of any young man who had been so unfortunate as to lead vicious courses, and wanted to turn steady, he’d have no objection to lend him his patronage. He, indeed! Why, he’d corrupt a saint. I’ve not felt so bad myself for years as when I were standing talking to him the other day. I could have cried to think I couldn’t spite him better, for he kept smiling in my face, as if he took all my compliments for earnest; and I couldn’t see that he minded what I said in the least, while I was mad with all his speeches.”

“Did you tell him anything about us—about Frederick?” Margaret pressed, her voice low.

“Not I,” said Dixon, with fierce pride. “He had never the grace to ask where I was staying; and I shouldn’t have told him if he had asked. Nor did I ask him what his precious situation was. He was waiting for a bus, and just then it drove up, and he hailed it. But, to plague me to the last, he turned back before he got in, and said, ‘If you can help me to trap Lieutenant Hale, Miss Dixon, we’ll go partners in the reward. I know you’d like to be my partner, now wouldn’t you? Don’t be shy, but say yes.’ And he jumped on the bus, and I saw his ugly face leering at me with a wicked smile to think how he’d had the last word of plaguing.”

The words left a taste like iron on Margaret’s tongue. In the street beyond, a cart rattled over stones; a distant shout rose and fell; somewhere, a door banged—each sound now seemed an enemy, a signal that Milton was wide awake and watchful.

“Have you told Frederick?” she asked.

“No,” Dixon answered. “There was so much else to think about that I did not dwell on it all. But when I saw master sitting so stiff, and with his eyes so glazed and sad, I thought it might rouse him to have to think of Master Frederick in hiding, he would have to go, poor fellow, before Mr. Bell came.”

“Oh, I’m not afraid of Mr. Bell,” said Margaret quickly; “but I am afraid of this Leonards. I must tell Frederick. What did he look like?”

“A bad-looking fellow, I can assure you, miss. Whiskers such as I should be ashamed to wear—they are so red. And for all he said he’d got a confidential situation, he was dressed in fustian just like a working-man.”

It was enough. The necessity sprang up like a knife: Frederick must go.

Go—now, when he had vaulted back into his place among them as if he had never been absent; when his very care for the dying and his sorrow for the dead had bound him by a peculiar tie—those fierce, sacred attachments formed around loss. Go—when their grief had made them a small island in a loud, indifferent town, and he had been the strongest rock of it.

Margaret sat by the drawing-room fire, staring into the coals that crumbled and renewed, as if they could teach her how to endure. Her father moved restlessly, shifting as though the chair pricked him; this new fear was pressing on him too, though he had not yet spoken it aloud. The house held itself in that strained quiet where every footfall is heard, every small sound takes on meaning: the soft scrape of Dixon’s shoes in the passage; the faint clink of a spoon set down too carefully; the sullen far-off pulse of industry that never stopped.

Frederick came in at last, his brightness dimmed, though the wildest violence of grief had passed. He went straight to Margaret and kissed her forehead, as if the gesture were both blessing and apology.

“How wan you look, Margaret!” he said under his breath. “You have been thinking of everybody, and no one has thought of you. Lie on this sofa—there is nothing for you to do.”

“That is the worst,” she answered, in a sad whisper.

Still, she obeyed, letting her tired limbs sink into the cushions. He drew a shawl over her feet with an odd tenderness, then sat down on the floor beside her, close as when they were children. Their voices, when they spoke, were kept low, as if loud speech might break what little composure remained in the house.

Margaret told him Dixon’s story. As she described the red whiskers and the leering promise of partnership in betrayal, she watched her brother’s face harden. He gave a long, low whew—half dismay, half anger.

“I should just like to have it out with that young fellow. A worse sailor was never on board ship—nor a much worse man either,” Frederick said, his voice sharpened by old contempt. “I declare, Margaret—you know the circumstances of the whole affair?”

“Yes. Mamma told me.”

“Well, when all the sailors who were good for anything were indignant with our captain, this fellow to curry favour—pah! And to think of his being here! Oh, if he’d a notion I was within twenty miles of him, he’d ferret me out to pay all old grudges. I’d rather anybody had the hundred pounds they think I am worth than that rascal. What a pity poor old Dixon could not be persuaded to give me up, and make a provision for her old age!”

“Oh, Frederick, hush! Don’t talk so.”

Mr. Hale had come towards them; his face was eager and trembling. He had overheard enough. Taking Frederick’s hand in both of his, he spoke as a man who forces himself to pronounce sentence.

“My boy, you must go. It is very bad—but I see you must. You have done all you could—you have been a comfort to her.”

“Oh, papa, must he go?” Margaret pleaded, though the pleading fought against her own clear knowledge.

Frederick’s mouth twisted in a bitter smile. “I declare, I’ve a good mind to face it out, and stand my trial. If I could only pick up my evidence! I cannot endure the thought of being in the power of such a blackguard as Leonards. I could almost have enjoyed—in other circumstances—this stolen visit: it has had all the charm which the Frenchwoman attributed to forbidden pleasures.”

“One of the earliest things I can remember,” Margaret said, the memory rising strangely like a hearth-smell from far back, “was your being in some great disgrace, Fred, for stealing apples. We had plenty of our own—trees loaded with them; but some one had told you that stolen fruit tasted sweetest, which you took au pied de la lettre, and off you went a-robbing. You have not changed your feelings much since then.”

Frederick gave a short breath that might have been a laugh on any other day; but it died almost at once.

“Yes—you must go,” Mr. Hale repeated, answering the question Margaret had asked some time before, as if he had only just heard it. His mind clung to the one necessity; the turns of their talk seemed to cost him effort he could not afford.

Brother and sister looked at each other, and in that swift moment a whole understanding passed—quick as taste, like the first sharpness of grief on the tongue. Their sympathy could not be put into phrases; it lived only in a glance, and it would be theirs no longer when he went. The thought went round and round in both, like a refrain in a familiar old song, until it lost itself in sadness.

Frederick shook it off first, forcing the talk into safer channels.

“Do you know, Margaret, I was very nearly giving both Dixon and myself a good fright this afternoon. I was in my bedroom; I had heard a ring at the front door, but I thought the ringer must have done his business and gone away long ago; so I was on the point of making my appearance in the passage, when, as I opened my room door, I saw Dixon coming downstairs; and she frowned and kicked me into hiding again. I kept the door open, and heard a message given to some man that was in my father’s study, and that then went away. What could it have been? Some of the shopmen?”

“Very likely,” Margaret replied, indifferently; her mind was full of other footsteps, other rings, other men at doors.

“There was a little quiet man who came up for orders about two o’clock.”

“But this was not a little man—a great powerful fellow; and it was past four when he was here.”

“It was Mr. Thornton,” said Mr. Hale.

“Mr. Thornton!” Margaret repeated, surprised in spite of herself. “I thought—”

“Well, little one, what did you think?” Frederick asked, because she stopped.

“Oh—only,” she said, colouring and looking straight at him, “I fancied you meant some one of a different class, not a gentleman; somebody come on an errand.”

“He looked like someone of that kind,” Frederick answered carelessly. “I took him for a shopman, and he turns out a manufacturer.”

Margaret was silent. The remembrance of her own first mistake—how she had spoken and judged before she knew—came back with a sting. It was natural, she told herself; and yet she felt an unreasonable annoyance, an unwillingness to let Frederick keep that superficial picture.

Mr. Hale went on, glad perhaps to speak of any kindness that touched their grief. “He came to offer any assistance in his power, I believe. But I could not see him. I told Dixon to ask him if he would like to see you—I think I asked her to find you, and you would go to him. I don’t know what I said.”

“He has been a very agreeable acquaintance, has he not?” Frederick asked, tossing the question as if anyone might catch it.

“A very kind friend,” Margaret answered, when her father did not.

Frederick was quiet a moment, then spoke with a sudden gravity. “Margaret, it is painful to think I can never thank those who have shown you kindness. Your acquaintances and mine must be separate. Unless, indeed, I run the chances of a court-martial, or unless you and my father would come to Spain.”

The suggestion was thrown lightly; then, as if he could no longer keep the secret in, he plunged forward, colouring like a girl.

“You don’t know how I wish you would. I have a good position—the chance of a better. That Dolores Barbour that I was telling you of, Margaret—I only wish you knew her; I am sure you would like—no, love is the right word, like is so poor—you would love her, father, if you knew her. She is not eighteen; but if she is in the same mind another year, she is to be my wife. Mr. Barbour won’t let us call it an engagement. But if you would come you would find friends everywhere, besides Dolores. Think of it, father. Margaret be on my side.”

“No—no more removals for me,” Mr. Hale said, and his voice, though low, had an iron fixity. “One has cost me my wife. No more removals in this life. She will be here; and here will I stay out my appointed time.”

“Oh, Frederick,” Margaret said, turning eagerly to what could still be hopeful, “tell us more about her. I never thought of this; but I am so glad. You will have some one to love and care for you out there. Tell us all about it.”

“In the first place, she is a Roman Catholic,” Frederick replied. “That’s the only objection I anticipated. But my father’s change of opinion—nay, Margaret, don’t sigh.”

She could not help it. Before the talk ended she had reason to sigh more deeply. Frederick, in fact if not yet in open profession, was Roman Catholic too. It cast a backward light on his letters—on the faintness of his expressed sympathy when his father had left the Church of England. She had once attributed it to a sailor’s careless temper; now she saw it was the pull of his own tending away from the form into which he had been baptized, though toward a direction opposite to their father’s. How much love had shaped that change Frederick himself could not have said; but Margaret heard in his voice that mingling of conviction and passion which makes creed part of a man’s flesh and blood.

She let that branch of the subject drop at last; there were enough divisions already. Returning to the engagement, she looked at it with a different steadiness.

“But for her sake, Fred, you surely will try and clear yourself of the exaggerated charges brought against you, even if the charge of mutiny itself be true. If there were to be a court-martial, and you could find your witnesses, you might, at any rate, show your disobedience to authority was because that authority was unworthily exercised.”

Mr. Hale roused himself to listen, his eyes fixed on Frederick with anxious hunger for the answer.

“In the first place, Margaret,” Frederick said, “who is to hunt up my witnesses? All of them are sailors, drafted off to other ships, except those whose evidence would go for very little, as they took part or sympathised in the affair. In the next place allow me to tell you, you don’t know what a court-martial is, and consider it as an assembly where justice is administered, instead of what it really is—a court where authority weighs nine-tenths in the balance, and evidence forms only the other tenth. In such cases, evidence itself can hardly escape being influenced by the prestige of authority.”

“But is it not worth trying,” Margaret persisted, “to see how much evidence might be discovered and arrayed on your behalf? At present, all those who knew you formerly, believe you guilty without any shadow of excuse. You have never tried to justify yourself, and we have never known where to seek for proofs of your justification. Now, for Miss Barbour’s sake, make your conduct as clear as you can in the eye of the world. She may not care for it; she has, I am sure, that trust in you that we all have; but you ought not to let her ally herself to one under such a serious charge, without showing the world exactly how it is you stand. You disobeyed authority—that was bad; but to have stood by, without word or act, while the authority was brutally used, would have been infinitely worse. People know what you did; but not the motives that elevate it out of a crime into an heroic protection of the weak. For Dolores’ sake, they ought to know.”

“But how must I make them know?” Frederick demanded, almost impatient at the helplessness of it. “I am not sufficiently sure of the purity and justice of those who would be my judges, to give myself up to a court-martial, even if I could bring a whole array of truth-speaking witnesses. I can’t send a bell-man about, to cry aloud and proclaim in the streets what you are pleased to call my heroism. No one would read a pamphlet of self-justification so long after the deed, even if I put one out.”

“Will you consult a lawyer as to your chances of exculpation?” Margaret asked; and as she spoke she felt herself redden—how odd that this name should still have the power to colour her cheek.

“I must first catch my lawyer, and have a look at him, and see how I like him, before I make him into my confidant. Many a briefless barrister might twist his conscience into thinking, that he could earn a hundred pounds very easily by doing a good action—in giving me, a criminal, up to justice.”

“Nonsense, Frederick!” Margaret answered, forcing steadiness. “Because I know a lawyer on whose honour I can rely; of whose cleverness in his profession people speak very highly; and who would, I think, take a good deal of trouble for any of—Aunt Shaw’s relations. Mr. Henry Lennox, papa.”

“I think it is a good idea,” Mr. Hale said, slowly. “But don’t propose anything which will detain Frederick in England. Don’t, for your mother’s sake.”

“You could go to London by a night-train,” Margaret went on, warming to the plan as if it were a task like laying the breakfast-table—something that could be arranged into order. “He must go to-morrow, I’m afraid, papa,” she added, tenderly, “we fixed that, because of Mr. Bell, and Dixon’s disagreeable acquaintance.”

“Yes; I must go to-morrow,” Frederick said, decisively.

Mr. Hale gave a sound that was almost a groan. “I can’t bear to part with you, and yet I am miserable with anxiety as long as you stop here.”

“Well then,” Margaret said, “listen to my plan. He gets to London on Friday morning. I will—you might—no! it would be better to give him a note to Mr. Lennox. You will find him at his chambers in the Temple.”

“I will write down a list of all the names I can remember on board the Orion,” Frederick said, catching at the practical part. “I could leave it with him to ferret them out. He is Edith’s husband’s brother, isn’t he? I remember your naming him in your letters. I have money in Barbour’s hands. I can pay a pretty long bill, if there’s any chance of success. Money, dear father, that I had meant for a different purpose; so I shall only consider it as borrowed from you and Margaret.”

“Don’t do that,” Margaret said quickly. “You won’t risk it if you do. And it will be as risk; only it is worth trying. You can sail from London as well as from Liverpool?”

“To be sure, little goose,” Frederick replied, and for a moment his old playfulness came back, like the brief warmth of the fire when a new log catches. “Wherever I feel water heaving under a plank, there I feel at home. I’ll pick up some craft or other to take me off, never fear. I won’t stay twenty-four hours in London, away from you on the one hand, and from somebody else on the other.”

It was a strange relief to Margaret that Frederick chose to look over her shoulder while she wrote to Mr. Lennox. The steady movement of pen on paper—ink pooling and thinning, the scratch of the nib, the disciplined forming of words—kept her from hesitating, from being caught in the awkwardness of beginning again an intercourse that had last ended so uncomfortably. She wrote concisely, plainly; and before she could even read it over, Frederick took the note from her hand and put it away with care in his pocket-book.

As he did so, something slipped out—a long lock of black hair. The sight of it made his eyes glow, as if in an instant all the grey of Milton’s fog and sorrow were exchanged for sun.

“Now you would like to see that, wouldn’t you?” he said, holding it lightly.

“No! you must wait till you see her herself,” he went on, with a boy’s pride transfigured into a man’s devotion. “She is too perfect to be known by fragments. No mean brick shall be a specimen of the building of my palace.”
