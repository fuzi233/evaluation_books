The following morning was Palm Sunday, known locally in the olive-rich Valdarno as Olive Sunday. The sky, washed clean by the previous night's rain, held a brilliance of terrible purity. Once more Savonarola ascended the rostrum of San Marco, gazing upon a congregation whose faith remained unbroken, yet their eyes held a solemn, tragic gravity. In a voice calm and heavy with foreknowledge, he proclaimed his readiness to die, seeing clearly behind every vision the shadow of his own execution. As he spoke the final benediction, he looked into the upturned, veneration-filled faces of the crowd, knowing that he must turn away from them forever.

Before the sun had fully surrendered the sky, Florence erupted into chaos. The grievances kindled the day prior, which had simmered beneath a deceptive quiet, now boiled over into a frenzy aided by malice and tacit approval from within the city's power structures. The disturbance began at the Duomo, where certain *Compagnacci* attempted to disrupt the evening sermon preached by the *Piagnoni*. But once blood surged and the gathering swelled into a mob, the cry rose, "To San Marco! Fire to San Marco!"

Long before dusk, the church and its adjoining convent were besieged by a swelling tide of rage. Resistance was fierce. The friars, acutely aware of mounting hostility, kept arms hidden within their walls; clad in their long white habits, they fought with the vigor of knights, their Dominican serges offering little protection against the steel of their attackers. Even Savonarola's authority could not quell the instinct for self-preservation. Laymen who had refused to flee joined the fray, hurling stones and burning embers from the roof while sword-fights raged in the cloisters near the high altar. Despite the overwhelming numbers of the assailants, the assault lasted until the deepest hours of night.

Throughout these hours, the Government's stance remained singular: the convent was the enemy. Early in the breach, guards were dispatched not to disperse the rioters, but to order everyone inside the walls to lay down arms, to expel all laymen, and to command Savonarola himself to leave Florentine soil within twelve hours. Had Savonarola obeyed then, he likely would have been torn apart; he was willing to go, but his friends interceded. It was deemed far too dangerous for any man of high standing to exit via the garden gate, yet among those who did slip away was Francesco Valori, hoping to rally aid from without.

By the time the struggle neared its inevitable conclusion, when the *Compagnacci* stood poised to storm the library where Savonarola knelt in prayer with the brethren who had sheathed their weapons, a second contingent of guards arrived. Commissioned by the Signoria, they demanded the surrender of Fra Girolamo and his two lieutenants, Fra Domenico and Fra Salvestro.

A roar of triumphant hatred followed as lantern-light revealed the friar stepping from the convent doors, escorted by a guard offering him nothing but the grim sanctuary of a prison. Now the contest became a race for position in the surging stream that flowed up the narrow streets, eager to witness the Prophet led in shame back to the Piazza where he had dared them days prior. Who should stand nearest to tear at his ears with insults, or strike him with blows? This was easily achieved by many armed *Compagnacci*, who mingled freely with the royal guard.

As Savonarola felt himself jostled and dragged through the jeering throng, illuminated by lanterns reflecting mocking faces, spat upon and kicked with words of vile abuse, it appeared to him that life's bitterest cup had been drained. If men deemed him guilty and thirsted for his blood, death alone awaited. Yet the deepest bitterness cannot be forced from without; the lowest abyss of resignation is not found in martyrdom, but in the silent realization that one is unworthy to bear the cross, that truth may prosper, but not through oneself.

However, this fleeting, imperfect victory of humiliating the friar, who soon vanished beneath the archway of the Old Palace, proved as insubstantial as blood to a tiger. Did not the homes of the hypocrite's allies await sack? Already half the armed multitude, lagging too far behind to participate heavily in the siege, turned to the more lucrative business of ransacking wealthy residences. They did not strike at random; they selected with discrimination the houses of the chief *Piagnoni*, revealing that this riot was guided, and that the rabble with clubs and staves was led by sword-bearing officers of the *Compagnacci*.

Next in rank of crime after the Friar himself stood the ambitious Francesco Valori, suspected of plotting with the Friar to secure the office of Doge or Gonfaloniere for life. And there was the gray-haired man who, eight months prior, had raised his voice in a ferocious call for justice against five citizens, only to escape San Marco and experience what others termed justice. His house was surrounded by an angry, greedy horde; his wife was slain by an arrow, and he himself was murdered as he traveled to answer a summons to the Palazzo, falling to the swords of men named Ridolfi and Tornabuoni.

Thus played out this Masque of the Furies, labeled simply as Riot, throughout the hours of night and the breaking dawn.

Yet the chief director remained invisible, issuing orders from a private retreat, for his name was too illustrious to let his red plume wave openly amidst the carnage. This retreat was the same house and chamber in a quiet street between Santa Croce and San Marco where Tito had previously paid a secret visit to Dolfo Spini. Here sat the Captain of the *Compagnacci* through this memorable night, receiving a revolving cast of visitors, some disguised as armed *Compagnacci*, others shrouded in obscurity without visible arms. Abundant wine filled the table, cups offered to chance arrivals, and though Spini guarded against excess, he drank often enough to heighten the fever of news constantly brought to him.

Among the visitors clothed in shadows, Ser Ceccone was the most frequent, remaining as Spini's constant companion as the hours advanced toward morning twilight, alongside Francesco Cei, who hid carelessly within Florence, awaiting the revocation of his banishment upon the Friar's fall.

Candles burned down to low, shapeless heaps, and holes in the shutters marked the somber outer light, when Spini, rising from his seat and pacing with an angry flush on his face at some conversation between his two unmilitary guests, suddenly burst forth—"The devil spit him! He shall pay for it, though. Ha, ha! The claws shall come down on him when he least expects them. So he was to be the great man after all! Pretending to toss everything toward my cap, as if I were a blind beggar, while all the while he winked and filled his own purse! I should like to hang skins about him and set my hounds on him! And he has that fine ruby of mine, I was a fool enough to give him yesterday. Malediction! And he laughed at me in his sleeve two years ago, ruining the best plan that ever was laid. I was a fool to trust myself with a rascal who had long-twisted contrivances that no one could see to the end but himself."

"A Greek, too," observed Francesco Cei, a slight smile of amusement playing on his lips at Spini's fury, "who dropped into Florence with gems packed about him. You did not choose your confidant very wisely, my Dolfo."

"He is a cursed deal cleverer than you, Francesco, and handsomer too," Spini retorted, turning on his associate with a general desire to worry anything that presented itself.

"I humbly conceive," interjected Ser Ceccone, "that Messer Francesco's poetic genius will outweigh—"

"Yes, yes, rub your hands! I hate that notary's trick of yours," interrupted Spini, whose patronage relied largely on this frankness. "But here comes Taddeo, or someone else: now is the time! What news, eh?" he continued as two *Compagnacci* entered with heated looks.

"Bad!" said one. "The people have made up their minds they were going to sack Soderini's house, and now they have been balked; we shall have them turning on us if we do not take care. I suspect there are some Mediceans buzzing about among them, and we may see them attacking your palace over the bridge before long, unless we can find another bait for them."

"I have it!" cried Spini, seizing Taddeo by the belt and drawing him aside to whisper his instructions, while the other went on telling Cei how the Signoria had intervened regarding Soderini's home.

"Ecco!" exclaimed Spini, presently, giving Taddeo a slight push toward the door. "Go, and make quick work."
