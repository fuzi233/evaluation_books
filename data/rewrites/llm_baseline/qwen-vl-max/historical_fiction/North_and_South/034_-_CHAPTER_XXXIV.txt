“Truth will fail thee never, never!
Though thy bark be tempest-driven,
Though each plank be rent and riven,
Truth will bear thee on for ever!”

The strength Margaret had summoned to carry her through the days since her mother’s passing was now a fragile thread, stretched thin by grief and uncertainty. She moved through the house with a quiet dignity, yet beneath it all, a storm raged unspoken. At times, even in the midst of conversation with her father, a sudden pang would strike her—a sharp, silent cry of loss—reminding her that she no longer had a mother to turn to. Her heart was heavy, and her thoughts often wandered to Frederick, whose fate remained shrouded in silence. The Sunday post had passed without word from London, and by Tuesday, still no letter arrived. Margaret stood in the dim light of the drawing-room, her hands folded tightly in her lap, her mind racing with questions she dared not voice.

Her father, too, was restless. Once content to sit quietly in his armchair for hours, he now paced the room like a caged animal, his steps echoing against the walls. He would vanish into the hall, opening and closing doors as if searching for something unseen. Margaret tried to soothe him by reading aloud, but his mind was elsewhere—unreachable. It was then she felt immense relief that she had kept to herself the encounter with Leonards. To burden him further would have been unbearable.

A knock at the door brought a sudden shift in the air. Mr. Thornton had arrived. He entered directly to Mr. Hale, took his hands firmly in his own, and held them without speaking. In that silence, a deep understanding passed between them—one that words could not express. Then, turning to Margaret, he hesitated. He had meant to maintain the distance he had cultivated lately, but seeing her—pale, composed, yet marked by sorrow—his resolve faltered. She stood apart, her posture stiff with uncertainty, her eyes lowered. He stepped forward, his voice softening as he offered a few polite words. The moment touched her deeply; her eyes filled with tears, and she turned away quickly, clutching her work as if to anchor herself.

Mr. Thornton felt his pulse quicken. For a brief moment, the memory of Outwood Lane faded. He attempted to engage Mr. Hale in conversation, and though their exchange was measured, it brought a sense of stability to the older man—something Margaret noticed with quiet gratitude.

Then Dixon appeared at the doorway, breathless and pale. “Miss Hale, you are wanted.”

Margaret’s blood ran cold. Something had happened to Fred. There was no doubt in her mind. She glanced at her father and Mr. Thornton, who were still absorbed in their talk, and whispered, “Take care papa does not come down. Mr. Thornton is with him now.”

She followed Dixon silently, her hand trembling slightly as she reached the study door. Before entering, she turned and said, “Be sure he doesn’t come.”

Inside, the inspector stood waiting. His presence was abrupt, his manner formal. But Margaret met him with an icy calm. Her expression was unreadable—no fear, no curiosity, only a quiet defiance that seemed to challenge him to proceed.

“I beg your pardon, ma'am,” he began, “but my duty requires me to ask a few questions. A man died at the Infirmary after falling at Outwood station on Thursday evening, between five and six o’clock. At first, the fall seemed minor—but internal injuries, combined with alcohol, proved fatal.”

Margaret’s eyes widened slightly, but otherwise she did not move. Her lips pressed into a firm line, her jaw set. She watched him intently, as if expecting more.

“There is reason to believe the fall was caused by a push or scuffle,” he continued. “It appears the man had been behaving rudely—half-drunk—toward a young lady walking with a gentleman. That gentleman may have pushed him. One witness saw the altercation but thought little of it. Another observed the man, later identified as Leonards, running wildly after the train, shouting curses. He collapsed shortly afterward and was taken to the Infirmary. He never regained full consciousness. His last words were about a ‘Cornish trick’ and a curse on someone who’d made him lose money.”

The inspector paused. “There is some evidence linking the woman seen at the station to you, Miss Hale. You were seen near the time of the incident, described as remarkably handsome, and associated with a gentleman. A shop assistant claimed to recognize you—your family shops at his store.”

Margaret’s voice came low, steady: “I was not there.”

The inspector bowed slightly, but said nothing. He studied her closely. No tremor, no flinch. She stood like a statue, her gaze fixed, her face impassive.

“Then, madam,” he said, “you deny being the woman accompanying the gentleman who caused the fall?”

A flash of pain crossed her features—so swift, so deep, it might have escaped any but the most perceptive observer. But the inspector, though sharp, was not trained to read such hidden anguish. He saw only repetition—the same words, unchanged.

“I was not there,” she repeated, slowly, heavily.

He closed his notebook deliberately. “I hope you won’t think me impertinent, but I may need to call on you again. If the witness insists on identifying you, I may summon you to give testimony—perhaps to prove an alibi.”

She did not blink. Not once. Her face remained still, her posture rigid.

“It is unlikely,” he added, “that I’ll need to do so. I only act in my duty.”

Margaret inclined her head slightly. She could not speak. Her lips were dry, her throat tight. But then, without warning, she stepped forward, opened the study door, and walked ahead of him to the front entrance. She held the door open, watching him leave with that same distant, glassy stare—her eyes following him until he disappeared into the street.

Only when the door clicked shut behind him did she collapse. She staggered back into the study, swaying, her breath catching. Then, as if driven by some inner force, she turned and locked the door from within.

She stood still for a moment—then fell forward, unconscious, onto the floor.
