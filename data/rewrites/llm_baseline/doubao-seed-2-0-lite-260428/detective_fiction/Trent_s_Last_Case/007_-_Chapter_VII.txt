A stiff offshore wind drove the waves raging against the cliff foot, while bright sun poured over the land from a dappled blue English sky. Bad sleep had left John Trent restless, so before eight he hiked down to the hidden rock pool the inn staff had pointed him to, and dove deep into the crisp, clear water. He swam out between massive grey boulders into the choppy open sea, forced himself a short stretch against the coast-hugging current, then returned to the shelter of the rocks, winded but sharper than he’d felt in days.

Ten minutes later he was clambering back up the cliff, the heavy, gnawing disgust he’d carried for the Manderson murder case temporarily burned away by sea and wind. It was the day of the inquest, barely twenty four hours since he’d arrived in the small coastal town. He’d barely advanced his investigation at all after parting ways with the American, Bunner, on the road to Bishopsbridge the day before. That afternoon he’d wandered into town with Mr. Cupples, the dead millionaire’s lawyer, to make a quiet purchase at a chemist’s, confer in private with a local photographer, send a reply-paid telegram, and run a quick check at the town telephone exchange. He’d shared almost no details of his leads with the mild-mannered Cupples, who seemed perfectly happy to leave the detecting to him. Back from Bishopsbridge, he’d banged out a long, detailed dispatch for his paper *The Record* and sent it off to the local correspondent to wire through, then dined with Cupples before spending the rest of the evening alone on the inn veranda, turning the case over in his head until sleep eluded him entirely.

Halfway up the cliff now, Trent realized he’d never taken on a case he hated more, or one that had him so completely hooked. In the golden new sunlight, the whole affair felt darker, and more fiercely challenging, than ever. All his half-formed suspicions, the scraps of fact that had kept him awake for hours, glowed sharp and clear now, washed clean by the sea’s cold purity. He could almost see the shape of the guilt he was convinced was there, and the ugly motive he guessed at turned his stomach, but the thrill of the hunt was back, bright and hot. No holding back now, no unnecessary compunction. By the end of the day, he hoped, every thread of his net would be tied tight. He waited, half-excited, not quite daring to hope, for the answer to that telegram he’d sent the day before.

The path back to the inn wound along the cliff top, and when Trent drew near the spot he’d spotted from the water level – an old, long-ago landslip where the cliff face had fallen away – he leaned over the edge, expecting to watch the soft wash of small waves over broken rock below.

There was no broken rock. A few feet beneath him jutted a broad flat ledge, as big as a large sitting room, carpeted with wiry grass, walled in by steep stone on three sides. Right at the far edge, where the cliff dropped straight down to the sea, a woman sat, arms looped around her drawn-up knees, her gaze fixed on the thin trailing smoke of a distant liner, lost to the world in some private daydream.

To Trent, who had trained his eye to drink in every detail without staring, she was the most perfect thing he had ever seen. Southern pale skin, wind-kissed pink on the cheeks, a profile of delicate, unforgivingly regular lines softened by nothing but the full, gentle curve of her mouth. Dark thick brows slanted down to almost meet above her nose, giving her resting face a faint, severe look that vanished the second her lips curved. Thick dark hair escaped the two bandeaux meant to pin it back from her forehead, a mass of loose curls catching the wind at her nape. Everything she wore was matte, fine black – suede shoes, the hat pinned to the grass beside her, her dress that fell to her bare throat. The cut had the quiet elegance of French tailoring, and she looked every inch the sharp modern woman, until you looked at her face and caught that bright, unselfconscious glow of someone fully alive, drinking in sun and wind and sea. Trent thought she could not be English, certainly not American – that unapologetic, unthinking vigour of womanhood was something rarer.

He paused only a moment, then passed along the cliff path above her, unseen on the soft turf, every detail of her burned indelibly into his memory. Then she moved: uncurled her long hands from around her knees, stretched her whole body with the easy grace of a cat, then slowly lifted her head and spread her arms wide, fingers curved open, as if she meant to gather all the glory and unshakable sanity of the morning right into her chest. It was the gesture of a woman who had just claimed her own freedom, resolved to live, to move forward, to take what was coming to her.

Trent did not turn. He knew exactly who she was, and a thick curtain of gloom seemed to drop between him and the bright blue day.

Over breakfast at the inn, Cupples was bright as a sparrow, gushing about the coming inquest. He rambled on about the ancient history of the coroner’s court, that rare old tribunal unshackled by stuffy legal rules and precedents, before steering the conversation straight back to Manderson’s murder.
“Young Bunner was at my rooms last night,” he said, buttering his toast placidly. “He laid out his theory of the crime. A very sharp young man, Trent. He thinks this was an industrial vendetta – Manderson made so many enemies in the labour movement back in the States, it’s perfectly plausible a union hitman tracked him down. He showed me dozens of cases of exactly that sort of secret, violent revenge. Terrible times we live in, eh? All the fancy machinery science gives us, and we’ve no energy left for the old moral virtues. Agricultural machines killed the Harvest Home festival. Fast trains killed the old roadside inn. No wonder the US is falling apart at the seams.”
“Wait, I thought Puritanism was almost as strong there as the obsession with making money,” Trent said, half-listless. Cupples corrected him gently on the exact meaning of the word, then drifted back to his point, and Trent stood up to go.
“Right,” he said. “That philosophy needs a snappy slogan, you’d have crowds dying for it. But you were heading over to White Gables before the inquest, right? We can walk together, I’ll grab my camera first.”

The dull red roof of White Gables glowered through the dark trees, matching Trent’s heavy, troubled mood. If his clues all panned out, the first blow would land on that radiant, alive woman he’d just seen stretching in the sun on the cliff ledge. His mother had raised him to an old, chivalrous respect for women, and now the artist in him revolted at the thought of hurting something so perfectly, fiercely beautiful. But what was he supposed to do? Throw the whole case away? He was sure he was the only person within a hundred miles who was close to the truth. Today, he’d find out if he was chasing a ghost, or a killer. He’d trample down his compunction until he knew for certain it was deserved.

As they walked through the drive gate, they spotted Marlowe and Bunner chatting on the front porch steps. The lady in black stood in the shadow of the porch, and stepped forward to meet them, her walk straight, light, perfectly balanced – just as Trent had known it would be. When Cupples introduced them, her golden-flecked brown eyes regarded him kindly, her face a mask of pale, composed grief, no trace left of that wild, free gesture he’d seen on the cliff.
“I do hope you’ll succeed,” she said to him, earnest and quiet. “Do you think you will?”
Trent made up his mind in a split second. “I believe I will, Mrs. Manderson. When I have the case fully pieced together, I’d like to meet with you privately to go over the facts before we publish anything. I may need to consult you first.”
She looked puzzled, a flicker of distress crossing her face. “Of course, if it’s necessary.”
Trent hesitated. He remembered she’d been reluctant to talk to the police the day before, but there was a question that had been niggling at him, a loose thread that didn’t fit anywhere in the pattern of clues he’d built so far. “I wonder if I might ask you a small question, nothing that would distress you, I think.”
She sighed, tired. “There’s no point in me refusing. Ask, Mr. Trent.”
“It’s just this: your husband recently withdrew an unusually large sum of cash from his London bank, and was keeping it here in the house. Have you any idea why he would do that?”
She stared at him, completely shocked. “I had no idea. I can’t imagine why he would do that – my husband almost never kept cash in the house at all, he always carried a hundred pounds or so in his note case. On Sunday night, right before he left for that supposed moonlight motor ride with Mr. Marlowe, he came into the drawing-room where I was sitting, irritated about something, and asked me for every bit of cash I had on me until the next day. I unlocked my desk and gave him almost thirty pounds. He never explained why he needed it, just said Marlowe had talked him into the drive to help him sleep, since he’d had bad insomnia. I thought it odd he needed money that late, but I forgot all about it until this second.”
“Very odd, indeed,” Trent said, staring off into the distance. Cupples called his niece over to sort out inquest arrangements, and Trent wandered over to join Marlowe pacing the lawn. The young man laughed tiredly as he described the local police’s pomposity and Dr. Stock’s overinflated self-importance, until Trent steered the conversation back to the case.
“Bunner’s vendetta theory?” Marlowe said, shaking his head. “I’m not convinced. But I’ve lived in the US long enough to know that kind of over-the-top, dramatic revenge is exactly the sort of thing they love. Have you read *Huckleberry Finn*? Tom Sawyer wastes days cooking up this absurdly complicated, secret plan to free Jim, when he could have just unlocked the door and walked him out in twenty minutes. That’s the American brain. All the secret societies, the Ku Klux Klan, the Mormons’ penny-dreadful bloody tyranny in Utah – it’s all the same impulse.”
“Did it ever strike you that Manderson was paranoid, scared of that exact threat Bunner talks about?” Trent asked. “It was very strange to send you tearing off to London in the middle of the night.”
“10 PM, exactly,” Marlowe corrected. “And Manderson loved dramatic, unexpected moves. He told me he needed me to find a man called George Harris, collect a secret message from him that he couldn’t wire for. No one even knows who Harris is. Bunner’s never heard of him. I was told to book a deck cabin for him on the Monday steamer, that’s all.”
Trent leaned in, making sure no one was listening. “The butler Martin heard a snippet of your conversation with Manderson in the orchard before you drove off. He heard Manderson say, ‘If Harris is there, every moment is of importance.’ Are you sure you had no idea what that business was?”
“Absolutely none,” Marlowe said, calm. “He told me straight up he couldn’t tell me what it was about. I was just supposed to find Harris, bring back his note. That’s it.”
“Why did he lie to his wife about the moonlight drive, then?” Trent muttered, half to himself.
“Lie to the staff too,” Marlowe added. “He told the same story to Martin.”
Trent pulled two small, blank slips of fresh paper out of his breast pocket, holding them out. “Have you ever seen these before?”
Marlowe turned them over curiously. “They look like they were cut out of a 192X pocket diary, the October pages. No writing on them. I’ve never seen them around the house, anyway.”
Before Trent could reply, Mrs. Manderson walked over. “My uncle says we should leave for the inquest now,” she said.
“Cupples and Bunner have gone on ahead to sort out some business,” Trent said. “I’m not going to the inquest just yet – I wanted to poke around the house a little longer for some clues I think I might find.”
“Of course, do whatever you need to,” she said. “We’re all relying on you. I’ll just go get my things, Mr. Marlowe.” She stepped back into the house.
Trent nodded towards the distant road, where a small boy was trotting towards them, an unmistakable bright orange telegram envelope flapping in his hand. “That’s for me, I think.”
The boy panted to a stop. “Telegram for you, sir, just arrived.”
Trent tore the envelope open, and his face lit up so bright Marlowe’s tired face broke into a smile. “Good news?” Marlowe said.
“Not news,” Trent said, tucking the slip back into his pocket, his eyes sharp and bright. “Just confirmation that one more of my little guesses was dead right.”
