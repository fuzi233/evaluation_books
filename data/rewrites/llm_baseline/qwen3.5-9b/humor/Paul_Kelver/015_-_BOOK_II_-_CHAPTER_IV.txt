"Don't be nervous," advised the O'Kelly, adjusting my collar with the practiced touch of a man who knows exactly where the panic buttons are located. "And don't try to do too much. You have a very fair voice, but it lacks power. Just keep cool and open your mouth."

It was eleven o'clock in the morning, and we stood at the entrance of a narrow courtyard leading to the stage door. For the past two weeks, the O'Kelly had been coaching me. It had been nerve-wracking work for both of us, though perhaps doubly so for the O'Kelly himself. His wife, Mrs. O'Kelly—a thin, acid-faced lady whom I had glimpsed occasionally while loitering in Belsize Square awaiting his signal—was a woman of serious disposition with a conscientious objection to anything musical that wasn't strictly sacred. Hoping to win the O'Kelly away from at least one of his "sinful" habits, the piano had been banished from the drawing-room and replaced by an American organ of exceptionally lugubrious tone.

We made do with this mournful instrument. Although the O'Kelly—a veritable musical genius—managed to evolve an accompaniment to "Sally in Our Alley" that was less misleading than one might expect from such a device, the result didn't lighten our labors. Consequently, my rendition of the famous ballad acquired a dolefulness entirely unintended by the composer. Sung as I sang it, the theme became, to use a definition now sadly hackneyed in art criticism, a *problem ballad*. One couldn't help wondering if the marriage depicted would turn out as satisfactorily as the young man in the song anticipated. Was there not, after all, a certain ingrained melancholy and pessimism within the temperament of the complaining hero that clashed horribly with the frivolous instincts observable in the charming, albeit somewhat shallow, character of Sally?

"Lighter, lighter! Not so soulful!" the O'Kelly would demand, as the solemn notes rolled jerkily from the groaning instrument beneath his hands.

On one occasion, we were nearly caught. Mrs. O'Kelly had returned unexpectedly early from a district visitors' committee meeting. Hastily, I was hidden in a small conservatory adjoining the first-floor landing, where I crouched behind flower pots, listening in fear and trembling to the severe cross-examination of the O'Kelly.

"William, do not prevaricate. It was not a hymn."

"My dear, so much depends upon the time. Let me give you an example of what I mean."

"William, pray do not play tricks with sacred melodies in my presence. If you have no respect for religion, please remember that I do. Besides, why should you be playing hymns at ten o'clock in the morning? It is not like you, William, and I do not credit your explanation. And you were singing. I distinctly heard the word 'Sally' as I opened the door."

"Salvation, my dear," corrected the O'Kelly.

"Your enunciation, William, is not usually so much at fault."

"A little hoarseness, my dear," explained the O'Kelly.

"Your voice did not sound hoarse. Perhaps it will be better if we do not pursue the subject further."

With this, the O'Kelly appeared to agree.

"She's a lady a little difficult to get on with when you're feeling well and strong," the O'Kelly would explain later. "But if you happen to be ill, she becomes one of the kindest, most devoted of women. When I was down with typhoid three years ago, a tenderer nurse no man could have had. I shall never forget it. And so she would be again tomorrow, if there was anything seriously the matter with me."

I murmured the well-known quotation.

"Mrs. O'Kelly to a T," concurred the O'Kelly. "I sometimes wonder if Lady Scott may not have been the same sort of woman."

"The unfortunate part of it is," continued the O'Kelly, striking a chord, "that I'm such a healthy beggar; it doesn't give her a chance. If I were only a chronic invalid, now, there's nothing that woman wouldn't do to make me happy. As it is—"

We resumed our studies.

But let us return to our conversation at the stage door.

"Meet me at the Cheshire Cheese at one o'clock," said the O'Kelly, shaking my hand firmly. "If you don't get on here, we'll try something else; but I've spoken to Hodgson, and I think you will. Good luck to you!"

He departed, and I followed. Behind the door, inside a glass box, a curved-nosed, round-eyed little man resembling an angry bird in a cage demanded my business. I showed him my letter of appointment.

"Up the passage, across the stage, along the corridor, first floor, second door on the right," he instructed me in one breath before shutting the window with a snap.

I proceeded up the passage. It somewhat surprised me to discover that I was not in the least excited at the prospect of my first introduction to "behind the scenes." I recalled my father asking a young soldier on his return from the Crimea what his sensations had been at the commencement of his first charge.

"Well," the young fellow had replied, "I was worrying all the time, remembering I had rushed out leaving the beer tap running in the canteen, and I simply could not forget it."

As far as the stage was concerned, I found my way in safety. Pausing for a moment to glance around, my impression was not so much disillusionment concerning all things theatrical as the realization of my worst forebodings. In that single moment, all the glamour connected with the stage fell away, nor has it ever returned. From the tawdry decorations of the auditorium to the childish make-believe littered around on the stage, I saw the Theatre as a painted thing of shreds and patches—the grown child's dollhouse. The Drama may improve us, elevate us, interest, and teach us. I am sure it does; long may it flourish! But so likewise does the dressing and undressing of dolls, the opening of the front of the house, and the tenderly putting of them away to bed in rooms they completely fill, train our little dears to the duties and joys of motherhood. Toys! What wise child despises them? Are art, fiction, and musical glasses not merely preparing us for the time, however distant, when we shall finally grow up?

Lost in a maze of ways beyond the stage, I eventually found myself guided by voices into a large room furnished sparsely with many chairs and worn settees. Here I found some twenty to thirty ladies and gentlemen already seated. They varied in age, size, and appearance, but all shared an impossible-to-define yet unmistakable suggestion of theatricality. The men were chiefly remarkable for having no hair on their faces but plenty upon their heads; the ladies, one and all, were blessed with remarkably pink and white complexions and exceptionally bright eyes. The conversation, carried on in subdued but penetrating voices, was chiefly of "him" and "her." Everyone appeared to be on an affectionate footing with everyone else, the terms of address being "My dear," "My love," "Old girl," "Old chappie"; Christian names alone being used when a name was absolutely necessary.

I hesitated for a minute with the door in my hand, fearing I had stumbled upon a family gathering. However, as nobody seemed disconcerted by my entry, I ventured to take a vacant seat next to an extremely small and boyish-looking gentleman and asked if this was the room where applicants for a place in the chorus of the forthcoming comic opera ought to wait.

He had large, fishy eyes with which he looked me up and down. For such a length of time he remained thus regarding me in silence that a massive gentleman sitting nearby, who had overheard, took it upon himself to reply in the affirmative, adding that from what he knew of Butterworth, we would all of us be waiting here a damned sight longer than any gentleman should keep other ladies and gentlemen waiting for no reason at all.

"I think it exceedingly bad form," observed the fishy-eyed gentleman in deep contralto tones, "for any gentleman to take it upon himself to reply to a remark addressed to quite another gentleman."

"I beg your pardon," retorted the large gentleman. "I thought you were asleep."

"I think it very ill manners," remarked the small gentleman in the same slow and impressive tones, "for any gentleman to tell another gentleman, who happens to be wide awake, that he thought he was asleep."

"Sir," returned the massive gentleman, assuming with the help of a large umbrella a quite Johnsonian attitude, "I decline to alter my manners to suit your taste."

"If you are satisfied with them," replied the small gentleman, "I cannot help it. But I think you are making a mistake."

"Does anybody know what the opera is about?" asked a bright little woman at the other end of the room.

"Does anybody ever know what a comic opera is about?" asked another lady, whose appearance suggested experience.

"I once asked the author," observed a weary-looking gentleman speaking from a corner. "His reply was: 'Well, if you had asked me at the beginning of the rehearsals I might have been able to tell you, but damned if I could now!'"
