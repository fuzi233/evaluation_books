“The hollow oak our palace is,
Our heritage the sea.”

All who have wandered through the enchanting landscape of North Devon will know the little white town of Bideford, nestled along the broad tidal river, its banks lined with golden sands and crossed by an ancient stone bridge where salmon wait for autumn rains. The town climbs gently from the water’s edge toward the rolling uplands to the west. Above it, hills close in, draped in deep oak woods, broken here and there by jagged slate crags crowned with ferns; below, the land softens into rounded knolls and fertile fields of red and green, stretching out until they merge into vast salt marshes and dunes. Here, the rivers Torridge and Taw meet, flowing together toward the open sea, where waves crash against the bar and the endless thunder of the Atlantic rolls in.

Bideford stands there quietly, beneath a sky as soft as Italian art, cooled day and night by the fresh ocean breeze—breeze that keeps winter frosts at bay and summer heat at arm’s length. For perhaps eight hundred years it has stood thus, since the first Grenville, cousin to the Conqueror, returned from conquering South Wales and gathered around him Saxons, Norsemen with their golden curls, and dark Britons from Swansea—all mingling their blood, which still gives the people of the western counties their strength, intelligence, and even today, their distinctive beauty.

But in the year 1575, Bideford was more than just a peaceful market town with a few fishing boats bobbing at its quay. It was one of England’s most vital ports. It contributed seven ships to the fight against the Spanish Armada. A century later, chroniclers noted that “it sent more vessels to the northern trade than any port in England, save London and Topsham.” It was the heart of a thriving coastal culture—small by modern standards, yet how can we scorn the dawn of greatness? It was men like those of Devon—Drakes, Hawkinses, Gilberts, Raleighs, Grenvilles, Oxenhams, and countless others now forgotten—who laid the foundation of England’s naval might and global reach. Had they not first struck hard at Spain’s wealth in the West Indies, then shattered her final fleet at Britain’s Salamis—the glorious battle of 1588—we might now be no more than a Catholic dependency under a tyranny crueler than pagan Rome itself.

It is in honor of these men—their voyages, their battles, their courage, their faith, their lives lived boldly and often cut short—that I write this tale. If my words sometimes soar too high or grow grandly rhetorical, let me be forgiven: this story deserves song rather than speech, and should be proclaimed not as fiction but as epic—a true saga of heroism, like those of Troy or Marathon, meant for every true English heart.

One bright summer afternoon in 1575, a tall, fair-haired boy walked slowly along Bideford’s quay, his scholar’s gown trailing behind him, satchel slung over one shoulder, slate clutched in hand. His eyes were fixed on the ships and sailors, drawn to the sea as if by some invisible pull. Just past the foot of the High Street, he passed a tavern that opened onto the river. In its wide bay window sat merchants and gentlemen, sipping sack and debating the news. Outside, a group of sailors clustered around a man standing in their midst, speaking loudly and passionately.

The boy, eager for any word of adventure, slipped forward, joining the younger lads huddled near the men’s elbows. And so he heard the following account, delivered in a bold voice thick with Devon accent and punctuated with oaths:

“If you don’t believe me, go see for yourself—or stay here and rot with blue mold. I saw it with my own eyes, and so did Salvation Yeo, looking through a window in the lower room. We measured it ourselves—seventy feet long, ten feet wide, twelve feet high—stacked with silver bars, each weighing between thirty and forty pounds. And Captain Drake said: *‘There, my lads of Devon, I’ve brought you to the mouth of the world’s treasure-house. It’s your fault now if you don’t sweep it clean as a stockfish.’*”

“Why didn’t you bring some back, Mr. Oxenham?”

“Why weren’t you there to carry them? We could’ve taken it all—easily. Young Drake and I had already broken down the door, but Captain Drake fainted dead away. When we checked, he had a wound in his leg so deep you could fit three fingers inside. His boots were soaked in blood—had been for an hour or more—but he never knew it until he collapsed. Then his brother and I dragged him to the boats, kicking and screaming, begging us to let him keep fighting. Every step he took left a pool of blood in the sand. And tell me—wasn’t it worth saving him, even if it meant losing the silver? Silver we can get again. There’s more fish in the sea than ever came out of it, and more silver in Nombre de Dios than would pave every street in the west country. But a captain like Franky Drake? Heaven makes only one such man at a time. Lose him, and England’s luck dies with him. Who disagrees? Let him name his weapon—I’m ready.”

The speaker was a tall, broad-shouldered man with a ruddy face and a thick black beard. His eyes were sharp and restless. He leaned against the wall with legs crossed and arms akimbo, seeming to the schoolboy like some noble prince or duke. He wore a crimson velvet suit—outlawed by law, though clearly worn with pride—his side adorned with a long Spanish rapier and two daggers whose hilts sparkled with gold. Rings glittered on his fingers, gold chains hung about his neck, and large earrings dangled from his ears. Behind one ear, a red rose peeked from among his glossy black curls. On his head sat a wide-brimmed Spanish hat, not adorned with a feather, but with a whole Quezal bird—its feathers of iridescent green and gold shimmering like a single jewel.

As he finished, he removed the hat and looked at the bird.

“Look here, boys,” he said. “Have you ever seen such a creature? This is the bird the old kings of Mexico forbade anyone but themselves to wear. So I wear it—John Oxenham of South Tawton—as a sign to every brave lad in Devon: just as the Spaniards rule the Indians, we shall rule the Spaniards.”

A murmur of approval rose from the crowd. One man muttered, “I doubt the Spaniards are too many for us.”

“Too many?” Oxenham laughed. “We took Nombre de Dios with seventy-three men—no more when we left Plymouth Sound. Half were weakened by scurvy before we reached the Spanish Main. Then Captain Rawse of Cowes joined us with thirty more. With fifty-three men total, we cracked open the New World! Who died? Only the trumpeter—he stood braying like a donkey in the square instead of watching his neck. That’s why the Spaniards are cowards—like all bullies. They pray to a woman, those idol-worshipping rogues! No wonder they fight like women.”

“You’re right, captain!” cried a gaunt sailor beside him. “One westcountryman can beat two easterlings, and an easterling can take on three Dons any day. Eh, lads of Devon?”

“For O! it's the herrings and the good brown beef,
And the cider and the cream so white;
O! they are the making of the jolly Devon lads,
For to play, and eke to fight!”

“Come!” said Oxenham. “Who’ll join? Who’ll make his fortune?”

“Oh, who will join, jolly mariners all?
And who will join, says he, O!
To fill his pockets with the good red gold,
By sailing on the sea, O!”

“Who’ll join?” the gaunt man called again. “Now’s your chance! We’ve got forty men already waiting in Plymouth, ready to sail the moment we return. We need a dozen from Bideford—and a few boys. Then we’re off—make our fortunes or die trying.”

“Our bodies in the sea so deep,
Our souls in heaven to rest!
Where valiant seamen, one and all,
Hereafter shall be blest!”

“Now,” Oxenham declared, “you won’t let the Plymouth men say Bideford’s too scared to follow? North Devon versus South Devon—it’s settled. Who’ll join? It’s just a short voyage. Once past Cape Finisterre, the sea’s as calm as a duckpond. I’ll race a Clovelly herring boat there and back for twenty pounds—without taking a bucketful of water. Who’ll join? Don’t think you’re buying a pig in a poke. I know the route—Salvation Yeo here knows it too. He was gunner’s mate. Ask him to show you the chart—he’ll trace it better than Drake himself.”

At that, the gaunt man pulled from under his arm a great white buffalo horn carved with rough maps of lands and seas, islands, animals, and ships. He held it aloft.

“Look, boys,” he said. “Behold the picture of the place—drawn as real as life. I got it from a Portuguese down at the Azores. He marked every place he sailed, every thing he saw. Take it, Simon Evans—hold it in your hands. I’ll wager you’ll know the way in five minutes—better than any shark in the sea.”

The horn passed from hand to hand. Oxenham, seeing the crowd stirred, called through the window for a large tankard of sack, which he passed around after the horn.

The schoolboy, who had watched everything with wide eyes and ears, had managed to inch closer. Now he stood face to face with the man wearing the emerald bird, gazing at the marvel. As sailors turned the horn over and offered to join Oxenham, the boy’s heart burned with longing—to see that horn up close, as magical as Tristram’s horn or Ariosto’s enchanted instruments. When the group began to disperse and Oxenham stepped into the tavern with his recruits, the boy stepped forward boldly.

“Can I see it again?” he asked.

“It’s yours,” Oxenham said, handing it over. “If you want it.”

“I want it,” the boy replied. “But not your soul. That’s a stale sole, maybe. There are plenty of fresh ones in the bay.”

He fumbled in his pocket and pulled out a tester—the only coin he had.

“Will this buy it?”

“That? Not even twenty of them.”

The boy thought for a moment—what would a knight do in such a case?

“I’ll fight you for it,” he said.

“Thank you, sir,” Oxenham chuckled.

“Break the jackanapes’s head for him, Yeo,” he added.

“Call me jackanapes again, and I’ll break yours,” the boy snapped, raising his fist.

Oxenham studied him, smiling. “Tut, tut. Hit someone your own size, if you must. Spare little folk like me.”

“I may be a boy in age,” the lad said, “but I’ve a man’s fist. I turn fifteen this month, and I know how to answer anyone who insults me.”

“Fifteen, eh?” Oxenham said, admiring the boy’s broad shoulders, keen blue eyes, golden curls, and honest face. “You look more like twenty. If I had half a dozen lads like you, I’d make knights of them before I died. What do you say, Yeo?”

“He’ll do,” Yeo said. “In a year or two, he’ll make a fine gamecock—if he dares ruffle up so early at a tough old hen-master like the captain.”

Everyone laughed, including Oxenham, who then turned to the boy.

“So tell me, why are you so eager for that horn?”

“Because,” the boy said, lifting his head proudly, “I want to go to sea. I want to see the Indies. I want to fight the Spaniards. Though I’m a gentleman’s son, I’d far rather be a cabin-boy on your ship.”

“And you shall,” Oxenham declared, swearing fiercely. “You’ll eat galloon and dine on carbonadoed Dons. Who is your father, young gallant?”

“Mr. Leigh of Burrough Court.”

“Bless his soul! I know him as well as I know the Eddystone. Who’s dining with him tonight?”

“Sir Richard Grenville.”

“Dick Grenville? I didn’t know he was in town. Go home and tell your father John Oxenham will come to keep him company. Off with you! I’ll settle things with the good man, and you’ll have your place on my ship. And as for the horn—give it to him, Yeo. I’ll give you a noble for it.”

“No penny, noble captain,” Yeo said. “If the young master wants a poor sailor’s gift, here it is—for the love he shows the sea. May God bless him in it.” And with a sudden burst of generosity, he thrust the horn into the boy’s hands and walked away, avoiding thanks.

“And now,” Oxenham said, turning to his crew, “make up your minds what kind of men you want to be. I don’t want sneaky shore rats who take five pounds from one captain, ten from another, then vanish into tavern cellars or under women’s mufflers. If you’re that sort, better cut yourself up and salt yourself down like pork. If I catch you, seven years hence, I’ll slit your throat in the street—pity if I don’t! But if you’ll stand true to me, I’ll stand true to you—through wreck or prize, storm or calm, salt water or fresh, victuals or none—share and share alike. Here’s my hand on it, for every man and all!”

“Westward ho! with a rumbelow,
And hurra for the Spanish Main, O!”

With that, Oxenham strode into the tavern, followed by his new recruits. The boy walked home, cradling the horn, trembling with hope and fear, blushing with shame at having revealed his deepest dream to a stranger—one he’d hidden even from his parents since he was ten.

This youth, Amyas Leigh, though born of noble blood and raised in the finest circles of Devon, was not what modern readers might call “interesting” or “highly educated.” Beyond a smattering of Latin—drilled into him with blows like a nail—he knew only the Bible, the Prayer Book, Caxton’s *Mort d’Arthur*, and a translation of Las Casas’ *History of the West Indies*, titled *The Cruelties of the Spaniards*. He believed in pixies, thought they swapped babies and danced in mushroom rings, visited the white witch at Northam for warts and burns, and believed the sun moved around the earth and the moon resembled a Cheshire cheese. He thought swallows slept at the bottom of the horse-pond and spoke with a broad Devonshire accent, like Raleigh and Grenville. By today’s standards, he was ignorant—so much so that any schoolboy might laugh at him.

Yet this “ignorant savage,” untouched by modern science, literature, or history books that paint all pre-1688 England as hypocritical fools, had learned things no school today would teach: truthfulness, courage, endurance, and the dignity of being a gentleman—not as a title, but as a way of life. He had been trained to speak truth and draw the bow, to endure pain without complaint, and to value kindness above pride. For a year, he’d cared for a colt and a flock of young hawks from Lundy Isle, learning perseverance and patience. He read the clouds like a sailor, knew every bird, fish, and insect by name, and had become the undisputed champion of his school—fearless in fights, yet always defending the weak. He fought not for cruelty, but justice. He was feared by sailor boys, loved by town children, and felt he hadn’t done his duty unless he’d punished a bully.

He didn’t think about thinking. He didn’t feel about feeling. His ambition? To please his parents, earn red quarrenders and mazard cherries, and go to sea when he was big enough. He wasn’t what we’d call “pious”—he said his prayers, went to church, read Psalms with his mother—but beyond the catechism, he knew nothing of theology. Still, was he untrained in virtue? Was his narrow world devoid of depth?

Perhaps not. Perhaps the ignorance of his age was balanced by the strength of his character—by honesty, courage, loyalty, and a fierce sense of honor. So let us watch him now, climbing the hill with his precious horn, eager to tell his mother all that had happened—though he’d never hidden anything from her, except this sea fever, which he kept secret because he knew it would pain her, and because, wise beyond his years, he knew he wasn’t yet old enough. As he told her that afternoon, “There’s no use hollaing till I’m out of the wood.”
