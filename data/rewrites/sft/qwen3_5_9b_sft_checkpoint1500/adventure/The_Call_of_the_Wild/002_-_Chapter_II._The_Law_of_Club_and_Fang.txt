Buck's first day on the Dyea beach was a living nightmare, a jarring collision between the soft, sun-drenched world he had known and a brutal, primordial reality. He had been ripped from the heart of civilization and dumped into a chaos where peace, rest, and safety were non-existent concepts. Here, life was a constant, frantic motion, and every moment threatened the loss of limbs or life itself. There was no leisure, only a desperate, imperative need for vigilance. These were not the gentle dogs and men of town; they were savages, bound by a single, unforgiving code: the Law of Club and Fang.

Buck had never witnessed dog fights like those of these wolf-like creatures until that fateful morning. It was a vicarious tragedy, for if he had not been present, he might not have survived to learn its lesson. Near the log store, Curly, a friendly soul, attempted to approach a massive husky nearly twice her size. There was no warning—only a lightning-fast leap, a clatter of teeth, and a retreat. In seconds, Curly's face was torn open from eye to jaw. The fighter employed the wolf's method: strike and vanish, but the encounter held a darker secret. Within minutes, thirty or forty huskies converged on the pair, forming a silent, hungry circle. They licked their chops with predatory anticipation. When Curly charged, her opponent met her with a specific maneuver that sent her tumbling; she never rose. The waiting pack closed in, snarling and yelping, burying her beneath a bristling mountain of bodies.

The speed and cruelty of the slaughter stunned Buck. He saw Spitz panting, his scarlet tongue lolling in a mocking laugh, and François, swinging an axe, leaping into the melee alongside three men wielding clubs. Within two minutes, the attackers lay scattered or dead. Curly remained limp and lifeless in the trampled snow, a bloody ruin. François stood over her, cursing in a language Buck could not understand, while Spitz laughed again. From that moment, a bitter, deathless hatred took root in Buck's heart for Spitz. "No fair play," Buck realized, the horror settling deep. "Once down, you are finished. I must ensure I never go down."

Before the shock of Curly's death faded, François strapped Buck into a harness. It was the same gear used on the horses back home, and the job assigned was identical: haul François on a sled to the forest and return with firewood. Buck's human dignity was crushed, yet wisdom prevailed; he buckled down with determination. François was stern, demanding instant obedience enforced by a whip, while Dave, an experienced wheeler, nipped Buck's hindquarters whenever he faltered. Spitz, the leader, also served as a tutor, growling sharp reproaches or shifting his weight in the traces to physically correct Buck's errors. Under this harsh, combined discipline, Buck learned rapidly. Before returning to camp, he mastered the commands "ho" and "mush," learned to navigate bends, and knew how to stay clear of the sleigh on steep descents.

"T'ree vair' good dogs," François told Perrault, the team's driver. "Dat Buck, heem pool lak hell. I tich heem queek as anyt'ing."

By afternoon, Perrault arrived with two new additions: brothers named Billee and Joe. Though sons of the same mother, they were opposites. Billee was excessively good-natured, while Joe was sour, introspective, and perpetually snarling. Buck greeted them amicably, Dave ignored them, and Spitz immediately began his disciplinary rounds. Billee tried to appease Spitz with wags and whimpers, fleeing when that failed, crying softly as sharp teeth scored his flank. Joe, however, presented a terrifying visage. Whenever Spitz circled, Joe spun on his heels to face him, mane bristling, ears pinned back, lips writhing, jaws snapping with diabolical intensity. He was the embodiment of belligerent fear, so formidable that Spitz was forced to abandon his attack. Covering his humiliation, Spitz turned on the harmless, weeping Billee and drove him back toward camp.

Later that evening, Perrault brought in a ninth dog: Sol-leks, the Angry One. Long, lean, and gaunt, he bore the scars of battles and possessed a single eye that warned of prowess. Like Dave, he demanded nothing and expected nothing, marching into the camp with such deliberate authority that even Spitz left him alone. However, Sol-leks had a fatal flaw: he detested having anyone approach his blind side. Buck, unwittingly guilty, allowed himself to be caught on Sol-leks's vulnerable flank. The punishment was immediate—a slash from Sol-leks's claws that opened Buck's shoulder to the bone. For the rest of their journey, Buck kept a respectful distance from Sol-leks's rear, avoiding any further trouble.

That night, Buck faced the ultimate challenge of survival: sleep. The camp tent glowed warmly under a candle, but when Buck entered, Perrault and François bombarded him with curses and kitchen utensils, driving him back into the freezing cold. A biting wind chilled him, especially where his wound lay. Lying on the snow, he tried to rest, but the frost soon drove him upright. Miserable and shivering, he wandered among the tents, finding no warmth. Savage dogs lunged at him, but he bristled and snarled, honing his defensive instincts, and they retreated.

An idea struck him. Returning to the tent area, he found his teammates missing. Confused and forlorn, he circled the tent until the snow gave way beneath his paws, revealing something moving below. A small, friendly yelp reassured him. Investigating further, he discovered Billee curled up in a cozy burrow under the snow. A wave of warm air drifted from the hole. With a placating whine, Billee squirmed and offered a lick of his warm, wet tongue as a bribe for peace.

Another lesson learned. Buck selected a spot, dug a hole with frantic efficiency, and collapsed inside. Soon, his own body heat created a sanctuary, and he slept soundly through the long, arduous day, wrestling with nightmares in his slumber.

Morning brought a new terror. Roused by the noise of the waking camp, Buck blinked into a white world. Snow had fallen during the night, burying him completely. The walls of snow pressed in on every side, triggering a primal, ancestral fear—the fear of the wild beast trapped in a den. Muscles contracted spasmodically, fur stood on end, and with a ferocious snarl, he exploded upward, bounding free into the blinding daylight. Snow flew as he shook himself off, realizing instantly where he was and recalling the events of the previous twenty-four hours.

"Wot I say?" François shouted, spotting Buck emerging. "Dat Buck for sure learn queek as anyt'ing."

Perrault nodded gravely. As a government courier carrying vital dispatches, he needed the best possible team, and Buck's rapid adaptation pleased him immensely. Within an hour, three more huskies joined the team, bringing the total to nine. Harnessing and ready, they swung up the trail toward Dyea Canyon. Buck, relieved to be moving, found the hard labor surprisingly engaging. The entire team was animated by a fierce energy, a transformation wrought by the harness. Passivity vanished, replaced by alertness and a fierce irritation at any delay. For these dogs, the toil of the traces was the summit of existence, the sole purpose of their being.

The formation was set: Dave as wheeler, followed by Buck, then Sol-leks, with the remaining dogs strung out ahead, led by Spitz. Buck had been strategically placed between Dave and Sol-leks to receive direct instruction. Both were apt teachers, correcting his errors swiftly with sharp teeth. Dave was fair, nipping only when necessary, while Sol-leks's wrath was absolute. Once, during a halt, Buck tangled in the traces, causing a dangerous delay. Both Dave and Sol-leks flew at him, delivering a sound trouncing. The resulting tangle was severe, but Buck learned quickly to keep the lines clear. By day's end, the nagging had subsided, and Perrault even paused to examine Buck's hooves with care.

Their journey was grueling. They climbed the Canyon, traversed Sheep Camp, crossed the Scales and the timberline, and scaled glaciers and drifts hundreds of feet deep. They crossed the Great Chilcoot Divide, the barrier guarding the lonely North, before descending a chain of volcanic craters to the huge camp at Lake Bennett, where thousands of gold-seekers were preparing for the spring breakup. That night, Buck made his snow-hole and slept deeply, only to be roused in the dark to re-harness for another leg of the trip.

For days, they broke their own trail, working harder and slower through the deep powder. Perrault traveled ahead, packing the snow with webbed shoes, while François guided the sled at the gee-pole. They covered forty miles on the packed trail before hitting the broken ground, where progress slowed considerably. Day after day, the cycle repeated: breaking camp in the dark, hitting the trail at dawn, pitching camp after dusk.
