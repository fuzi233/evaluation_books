As the evening of June 1882 settled over London, casting a warm orange glow across the city's steel canyons, I found myself enveloped in the comforting aromas wafting from the kitchen of my flat on the tenth floor of a fortress-like building that loomed over Westminster. The air was alive with the scent of roasting meat and baking bread, a symphony of flavors that danced on my senses as I welcomed my guests to dinner. Captain Trench, a diminutive man with a balding head and piercing black eyes, had arrived earlier that afternoon, accompanied by Lieutenant Willoughby, a burly officer whose vacant stare and tangled moustache made him appear almost comical. Yet, there was something about him that belied his apparent foolishness – a stubborn determination that hinted at a depth beneath the surface. Durrance, a lieutenant of the East Surrey Regiment and my dear friend, had responded to a telegram and joined us mere minutes ago, his arrival marked by the faint tang of cigarette smoke on his coat.

As we gathered around the dinner table, the fire crackling in the hearth cast a golden light on our faces. Trench leaned forward, his eyes glinting with intensity, and began to speak of the unfolding events in Egypt. "There will be trouble," he said, his words hanging in the air like the promise of a storm. Willoughby, lost in thought, began to twist his moustache, his fingers moving in a slow, deliberate rhythm as he searched for a thread to cling to. I, too, found myself drawn into the conversation, my thoughts drifting to the far-off land where the Nile flowed like a lifeblood, where a young Dongolawi named Mohammed Ahmed was stirring the embers of rebellion. The year was 1882, and the world was poised on the brink of chaos, the air thick with anticipation and anxiety. As we ate and drank, our words wove a tapestry of speculation and concern, each of us trying to grasp the threads of a situation that seemed to be spinning rapidly out of control. And yet, amidst the uncertainty, there was a sense of excitement, a feeling that we were on the cusp of something momentous, something that would change the course of history forever.
