Major Pendennis, having that day engaged himself with nothing more pressing than his own importance, and Arthur, having engaged himself with an amiable vacancy of plan, arrived together at Sir Francis Clavering’s to dine; for there are houses in London where the very door-knocker seems to rap in the accent of “Society,” and the bell rings as if it had been at court.

In the drawing-room they found Sir Francis and Lady Clavering, and Captain Strong. Arthur’s face warmed at sight of the Captain, as a hearth warms at a familiar tread; but the Major’s countenance fell into that prim, pickled expression which he reserved for any man who had the misfortune to be useful for his living. A house-steward at table, thought Major Pendennis, was like a kitchen-ladle on the sideboard: necessary, no doubt, but not to be brought forward among decanters.

His spirits, however, were partly restored by the speedy appearance of Mr. Welbore Welbore, the country neighbour and brother senator; a gentleman of such solid respectability that if you had knocked on him, you might have expected to hear the ring of seven thousand a year. Welbore spoke little; he took no more part in the conversation than the footman behind his chair would have dared to do; but his dullness was ancestral and therefore comforting, and the Major always felt at ease when he could rest his eye upon landed income.

To these came the Dowager Lady Rockminster (who had her own good reasons for being pleasant to the Claverings), and Lady Agnes Foker with her son Harry, our old acquaintance, who looked as if he had been lately taken out of cotton-wool and did not yet know where to put his hands. Mr. Pynsent was detained at the House—those parliamentary duties which sit like lead on some men, and like a feather on others. Last of all, Miss Blanche Amory floated in, and instantly made the room look as if it had been dusted with moonlight.

She wore white silk—white of that insolent sort which seems to say it was born clean—and it displayed her pearly shoulders with the calm assurance of a cook who knows her sauce cannot curdle. Foker, having once whispered to Arthur that he considered her “a stunner,” proceeded to stare at her with the gratitude of a boy who has been suddenly promised pudding for life. Blanche, for her part, chose to be all cordiality and country recollection; she pressed Arthur’s hand, talked of dear Fairoaks, asked after Laura and Mrs. Pendennis, sighed prettily for fields and hedgerows, and presented herself, in short, as innocence with a good dressmaker.

Harry Foker, who ordinarily became dumb in the vicinity of petticoats, found himself miraculously gifted with speech before Blanche, as if her smile had uncorked him. By the time dinner was announced and the party moved down the broad stair, he was almost lively—indeed so much so that his mamma, watching him, looked as astonished as if her son had suddenly begun to speak Greek.

He longed, of course, to offer his arm to the fair Blanche; but she fell to Arthur’s lot, and Harry was allotted—by rank and family arithmetic—to Mrs. Welbore Welbore. He bore it as martyrs bear lesser flames, consoling himself with the hope of manoeuvring at table. Whether the move was his, or arranged by a hand more practised in such games, the event was this: Blanche sat with Arthur on one side and Harry on the other, like a dish placed artfully between two hungry gentlemen, each resolved to be the most attentive carver.

Harry, delighted to be near her, began talking with constant zeal and very little aim. “Seen Taglioni in the Sylphide, Miss Amory? Bring me that suprame of volaille again, if you please,”—this to the attendant, with the gravity of a man ordering cannon—“very good: can’t think where the suprames come from; what becomes of the legs of the fowls, I wonder?” And he fell to humming the air of that prettiest ballet, with the simple conviction that a tune is improved by being pursued and overtaken in public.

“Miss Amory is a sylph herself,” Arthur said.

“What a delightful tenor voice you have, Mr. Foker,” Blanche returned. “I am sure you have been well taught. I sing a little myself. I should like to sing with you.”

Arthur, who remembered having heard something astonishingly like that sentence before, wondered—without asking aloud—how many gentlemen had been honoured with duets since his time. Instead, he leaned on his own melancholy gallantry and said, very tenderly, that he should like to hear her sing again; he had never heard a voice he liked so well.

“I thought you liked Laura’s,” Blanche said, as innocently as a child mentioning a rival dish.

“Laura’s is a contralto,” Arthur replied; “and that voice is very often out.” He spoke bitterly, as if the contralto were a saucepan set aside and forgotten. He went on to complain, half languidly, half affectedly, of professional music—too loud, too showy—of London’s habit of making men old and blasé in a month. Like all old fellows, he said, he cared only for the songs of his youth.

“I like English music best,” Blanche cried. “I don’t care for foreign songs much.”

“Get me some saddle of mutton,” said Mr. Foker, as if he were supporting native art by native meat.

“I adore English ballads,” Blanche continued. “Sing me one of the old songs after dinner, will you?” Arthur asked, with an imploring air; and Blanche, turning to Harry, promised him an English song too—if he would come up soon—flashing him such a broadside of eyes that poor Harry felt himself struck amidships and made water instantly.

“I’ll come up after dinner, fast enough,” he said, simply. “I don’t care about much wine afterwards—I take my whack at dinner—I mean my share, you know; and when I have had as much as I want I toddle up to tea. I’m a domestic character, Miss Amory—my habits are simple—and when I’m pleased I’m generally in a good-humour, ain’t I, Pen?—that jelly, if you please—not that one, the other with the cherries inside. How the doose do they get those cherries inside the jellies?”

This last inquiry he delivered with the profound curiosity of a young food-historian confronting a lost technique. Indeed, a cherry imprisoned in clear jelly is a kind of culinary legend: the fruit lies there like a relic in amber, and you cannot help thinking of all the careful hands—hot syrup tempered, wine measured, the mould cooled just so, the cherries perhaps placed at the exact instant when the setting mass is neither fluid nor firm. Harry did not ask about the labour and timing, but his wonder was honest, and Blanche listened with inexhaustible good-humour, as if the mysteries of the kitchen were charming when explained by an earl’s grandson.

When the ladies rose, Blanche extracted solemn promises from both young men that they would quit the table early, and departed with kind glances that seemed to be rationed out with exact economy. She dropped her gloves on Harry’s side of the table and her handkerchief on Arthur’s, as if she had been feeding two tame creatures in a park and desired no biting.

Harry caught her last glance as she vanished; that bright look travelled across Strong’s broad white waistcoat and struck Harry full in the face. The door closed upon the charmer, and Harry, with a sigh, swallowed a bumper of claret, as if attempting to drown longing in Bordeaux.

The dinner, being one of the smaller sort, had been served at an hour when the day still clung to the windows. Lady Clavering—encouraged by Blanche’s impatience for her piano—made the signal early; the ladies rose while it was yet light enough for the outside world to look alive and unconcerned. From the flower-embroidered balconies above, one could see the two Parks: poor couples and children sauntering on, and equipages rolling through the arch, and horses clipping by with that neat, expensive sound of well-fed hooves.

Down below, the dining-room windows were opened; and there, like a framed picture in lamplight, sat six gentlemen in white waistcoats with decanters, fruit, and that complacent glow which wine puts on faces that have not earned it. Little boys passing would jump at the area-railings to peek, and whisper to one another about pineapple with a yearning as pure as prayer: “Hi hi, Jim, shouldn’t you like to be there and have a cut of that there pineapple?” Outside, too, the city’s soundscape shifted its gears: the clamping tread of the policeman on his beat; the rumble of wheels; the softened chatter of passers-by; then the gasman’s brief metallic business at the lamp, and the sudden little hiss and bloom of flame. Within, the butler lighted the antique chandelier, and the wax-candles made the table look like a shrine to appetite.

Jeames, with folded legs, leaned against the door-pillar and regarded the calm evening as if it were a moral lesson. He was busy attending to the quieter music of the world—the distant roll of carriages, the diminishing shrillness of boys, the polite hush that falls upon a rich street when daylight withdraws. His ear, trained by long service, could tell the footfall of a master from that of a stranger, the jangle of a cab-harness from the more shamefaced rattle of a needy cart.

While he was thus musing, an individual attached himself to the railings opposite and stared into the dining-room with the unabashed interest of a man inspecting his own supper. Policeman X, passing, gave attention to neither the moon nor the butler’s chandelier, but fixed instantly on the gawker—because the policeman’s business in life, like Mr. Strong’s in another line, was to keep certain things moving whether they wished to move or no.

The man at the railings was gorgeously attired in the vulgar splendour of one who has dressed for admiration rather than warmth. Chains, jewellery, a waistcoat that caught the lamplight like fish-scales; shiny boots; brass buttons; great white wristbands over his knuckles; whiskers dyed a Tyrian purple; and a head of black hair that looked like a borrowed thundercloud—everything about him declared “respectability” in a dialect the respectable do not speak. Yet he was grand enough that Policeman X, who had seen many sorts, took him at first for a member of Parliament or some other licensed nuisance.

He was, moreover, flushed with wine; he lurched and reeled with that unsteady gallantry which thinks itself superbly graceful. From the open window came Strong’s laughter—following on one of Strong’s own broad stories—and the stranger outside answered it with an answering snigger, slapping his thigh and winking at Jeames as much as to say, “Plush, my boy, isn’t that a good one?”

Jeames’s attention shifted, unwillingly, from the orderly heavens to this disorderly earth. He remarked later, in the servants’ hall, that a “holtercation” in the street was “never no good,” and he was not hired for any such purpose. But duty, like a kitchen timer, will not be ignored simply because it is inconvenient. So Jeames, after watching the glittering gentleman’s antics, leaned out and softly called, “Pleaceman,” and beckoned to the officer.

X marched up resolute, with one Berlin glove stuck in his belt-side; and Jeames, without another word, pointed calmly with his index finger at the laughing, reeling figure. It was a grand performance in pantomime: Jeames the silent pointer; X the moving authority; the stranger the errant note in the street’s composition.

X stepped up and said, “Now, sir, will you have the kindness to move hon?”

The stranger, being in perfect good-humour, did not seem to hear a syllable. He nodded and waggled his head toward Strong in the window, until his hat nearly toppled over the railings.

“Now, sir, move on, do you hear?” X repeated more sharply, and touched him gently with a gloved finger.

At once the ringed gentleman staggered back into what is called an attitude of self-defence, and began “squaring” at the policeman, brave as a warrior and steady as a jelly just turned from the mould.

“Hullo! keep your hands off a gentleman,” he said, with an oath which is best left to the street.

“Move on out of this,” said X, “and don’t be a blocking up the pavement, staring into gentlemen’s dining-rooms.”

“Not stare—ho, ho,—not stare—that is a good one,” cried the other, with a satiric laugh. “Who’s to prevent me from staring, looking at my friends, if I like? not you, old highlows.”

“Friends! I dessay. Move on,” answered X.

“If you touch me, I’ll pitch into you, I will,” roared the stranger. “I tell you I know ’em all—That’s Sir Francis Clavering, Baronet, M.P.—I know him, and he knows me—and that’s Strong, and that’s the young chap that made the row at the ball. I say, Strong, Strong!”

Within, Sir Francis started with a guilty look. “It’s that d—— Altamont,” he cried; and Strong, annoyed but prompt, rose and ran out.

A gentleman in a white waistcoat running bareheaded into the street; a policeman; an excited well-dressed fellow nearly coming to blows on the pavement—these things are a bell to a neighbourhood. A small crowd began to gather before Sir Francis’s door, for Londoners can resist a sermon, but not a scene.

“For God’s sake, come in,” Strong said, seizing the intruder’s arm. “Send for a cab, James, if you please,” he added under his breath; and, by main force of persuasion, he carried the excited gentleman into the hall and shut the door upon the mob, which immediately dispersed, disappointed to find that respectability, even in trouble, knows how to close its own front door.

Strong intended to usher him into Sir Francis’s private room, soothe him, and slip him away in a cab, like a hot pan removed from the fire before it scorches. But the newcomer, furious at the indignity of being asked to move on, insisted in a thick voice, “That ain’t the door—that’s the dining-room door—where the drink’s going on—and I’ll go and have some, by Jove; I’ll go and have some.”

The butler stood aghast and set himself before the dining-room door. But it opened; Sir Francis appeared, pale and anxious, as if the very house were reproaching him.

“I will have some,—by —— I will,” the intruder bellowed as Sir Francis came forward. “Hullo! Clavering, I say I’m come to have some wine with you; hay! old boy—hay, old corkscrew? Get us a bottle of the yellow seal, you old thief—the very best—a hundred rupees a dozen, and no mistake.”

Sir Francis’s eyes flickered with calculation. Only Welbore, Pendennis, and those two lads, he thought. With a forced laugh and a piteous look—like a man smiling while he hears his own crockery breaking—he said, “Well, Altamont, come in. I am very glad to see you, I’m sure.”

Colonel Altamont—for the intelligent reader will recognise in him His Excellency the Ambassador of the Nawaub of Lucknow—reeled into the dining-room with a triumphant glance at Jeames that said plainly, “There, sir: am I a gentleman or no?” and dropped into the first vacant chair.

Sir Francis timidly stammered the Colonel’s name to Welbore; and Altamont fell to drinking at once, surveying the company with alternating frowns and smiles, hiccupping praise of the liquor in the manner of a critic who has reviewed too many bottles.

“Very singular man,” Strong said gravely, his presence of mind never deserting him. “Has resided long in a native court in India. In those Indian courts they get very singular habits.”

“Very,” said Major Pendennis drily, wondering what infernal dinner-party lottery he had won.

Foker, delighted by the novelty, whispered to Arthur, “It’s the man who would sing the Malay song at the Back Kitchen.” Then, with an air of hospitality, he offered Altamont pineapple. “Try this pine, sir; it’s uncommonly fine.”

“Pines—I’ve seen ’em feed pigs on pines,” said the Colonel.

“All the Nawaub of Lucknow’s pigs are fed on pines,” Strong whispered to the Major.

“Oh, of course,” the Major replied, with a composure which suggested that pigs, pineapples, and Nawaubs were all equally part of the natural order, so long as they remained in India.

Meanwhile Sir Francis tried to explain the Colonel to Welbore, muttering that Altamont was eccentric, had Indian habits, didn’t understand English rules; to which Welbore, sipping steadily, remarked that it did indeed seem pretty clear.

Then Altamont’s attention settled on Arthur. He stared at him with such steadiness as his condition allowed, and said, “I know you, too, young fellow. I remember you. Baymouth ball, by Jingo. Wanted to fight the Frenchman. I remember you;” and he laughed, squared his fists, and acted the recollection with the enthusiasm of a man reenacting a battle in a drawing-room.

“Mr. Pendennis, you remember Colonel Altamont, at Baymouth?” Strong prompted.

Arthur bowed stiffly and said he remembered the circumstance perfectly.

“What’s his name?” cried the Colonel.

Strong repeated it.

“Pendennis!—Pendennis be hanged!” Altamont roared, thumping the table so that decanters trembled.

Major Pendennis drew himself up. “My name is Pendennis, sir,” he said, mortified beyond measure that he, Major Pendennis, should be exposed to this—this public seasoning of vulgarity. “My name is Pendennis, and I will be obliged to you not to curse it too loudly.”

Altamont turned and stared at the Major. And as he stared, something extraordinary happened: it was as if sobriety, like a cold cloth, were suddenly clapped upon his hot head. He put a hand to his forehead and displaced, in doing so, a portion of the black wig he wore; and his eyes fixed on the Major with a fierce, searching look. The Major, a resolute old campaigner in his own way, returned the stare as steadily as if they were measuring one another across a battlefield rather than a dinner-table.

After this silent inspection, Altamont began to button his brass-buttoned coat with a sudden precision, rose abruptly to the astonishment of all, reeled toward the door, and went out, followed instantly by Strong. All that Strong caught him muttering was, “Captain Beak! Captain Beak, by jingo!”

Not a quarter of an hour had passed between the Colonel’s strange entrance and his equally sudden exit. The baronet’s other guests sat blinking at the empty chair, as if the wine had conjured and dismissed a spirit. Sir Francis looked pale, agitated, and turned with almost terror toward Major Pendennis. The Major eyed his host keenly, as if tasting a sauce and detecting an ingredient that had no right to be there.

“Do you know him?” Sir Francis asked, breathless.

“I am sure I have seen the fellow,” the Major replied, puzzled. “Yes, I have it. He was a deserter from the Horse Artillery who got into the Nawaub’s service. I remember his face quite well.”

“Oh!” said Clavering, with a sigh so immense it was almost a confession; and the Major looked at him with a little twinkle, as if noting how quickly relief can rise in a man’s throat when a danger is renamed.

The cab Strong had ordered took away Strong and Colonel Altamont. Coffee was brought. The remaining gentlemen went upstairs to the ladies, and Harry Foker declared confidentially to Arthur that this was “the rummest go he ever saw,” which Arthur, laughing, said showed “great discrimination” on Mr. Foker’s part.

According to her promise, Blanche made music. Foker was enraptured, and joined in such airs as he knew; his big honest voice went blundering after the tune like a well-meaning dog. Arthur affected to talk aside with others; but Blanche drew him back to the piano by singing words of his own—verses we have met before—which she declared she had set to music herself. Whether the air was hers, or arranged for her by Signor Twankidillo, mattered little: Arthur was delighted, and turned her leaves with all the devotion of a man who believes his own syllables taste better when served in another’s voice.

“Gad! how I wish I could write verses like you, Pen,” Harry sighed later. “If I could do ’em, wouldn’t I, that’s all? But I never was a dab at writing, you see, and I’m sorry I was so idle when I was at school.”

No mention was made before the ladies of the queer scene below-stairs. Arthur was on the point of describing it to Blanche when she asked for Captain Strong to join her in a duet. Looking up, Arthur caught on Sir Francis’s ordinarily vacuous face a peculiar expression of alarm—an expression like a servant’s when a dish is in danger of being dropped. Arthur, for once discreet, held his tongue.

The evening, after the excitement, seemed rather dull. Welbore fell asleep as he always did after dinner and at music, as regularly as a clock that strikes only once and then forgets itself. Major Pendennis, who could ordinarily fill any room with anecdotes, scandal, and small poisonous bonbons of society, sat silent for the most part, listening—or pretending to listen—to Blanche, and watching her with an intentness which surprised Arthur almost as much as the compliments that presently followed.

When the hour for departure came, the Major rose and regretted aloud that so delightful an evening should have passed away so quickly. He addressed Blanche with a particularly fine compliment upon her singing. “Your daughter, Lady Clavering,” he said, “is a perfect nightingale—a perfect nightingale, begad! I have scarcely ever heard anything equal to her, and her pronunciation of every language—begad, of every language—seems to me to be perfect; and the best houses in London must open before a young lady who has such talents, and, allow an old fellow to say, Miss Amory, such a face.”

Blanche looked astonished. Arthur looked more astonished still; for the Major had lately spoken of the sylph in terms by no means so worshipful. It was as if, hearing her voice, he had heard also something else—some echo of an accent, some remembered cadence—though he said nothing of that kind, for the Major rarely translated his instincts into words.

The Major and the two young men walked home together after Harry had placed his mother in her carriage and procured a light for an enormous cigar. Harry’s company—or his tobacco—was not agreeable to Major Pendennis, who cast him several side-glances plainly meaning, “Go away, young sir, and smoke somewhere else.” But Harry hung on with the affectionate obstinacy of a man who has found a new subject and intends to wear it out in conversation.

They reached Bury Street. The Major bade the lads good night; and calling Arthur back, whispered confidentially, “Mind you make a point of calling in Grosvenor Place to-morrow. They’ve been uncommonly civil; mons’ously civil and kind.”

Arthur promised, wondering what civility had been cooked up here, and out of what ingredients. Morgan closed the door upon the Major. Harry took Arthur’s arm and walked with him some way in silence, puffing his cigar as if it were a steam-engine dragging his thoughts along the pavement. At last, near Charing Cross, Harry broke out again into his lament over poetry and his idle schooldays; and all the way along the Strand, and up to Arthur’s staircase in Lamb Court, Temple, he did not cease speaking about singing and Blanche Amory—as though her voice had lodged in his ear and would not be translated into any other language.
