Jorian Ketel’s heavy boots thudded across Margaret’s oak chamber planks, and he rounded the doorframe fully expecting to hunt his fugitive through every nook of the small house. What he found there stopped him dead, tallow candle shaking in his calloused fist so that wax dripped hot onto his jerkin: the very man he’d been chasing, Gerard, pale as a bleached funeral shroud, motionless, his head resting in Margaret’s lap. She knelt over him, frozen to stone, eyes blown wide yet glazed over, seeing neither the thin moonbeam slanting through the leaded glass pane nor Jorian’s sharp intake of breath, every thread of her being fixed solely on the white, still face pressed to her linen apron.

Jorian stood awestruck for a long minute, then crept over to the empty oak chest they had stashed the boy in, prodded the crumpled discarded cloak at its bottom, and the whole tale unspooled in his head. The girl’s dumb, frozen despair twisted something soft in the rough workman’s chest. “A sorry sight, this,” he muttered under his breath. “A black night’s work, all for a few miserable deerskins. Better he’d run off with us than end like this. She’s past hearing a word from me, poor wench—wait, though.”

He fished a tiny hand-mirror no bigger than his palm out of his pocket, held it to Gerard’s nostrils and mouth, and withdrew it. The polished glass was fogged, faint and sure, with breath. “There’s life in him!” he breathed to himself.

Though the words were no louder than a sigh, Margaret caught them instantly. It was as if a carved stone statue had burst open into warm, wild life. She sprang to her feet and flung her arms around Jorian’s neck, clinging to his broad shoulders almost fiercely, over and over, sobbing half-laughing blessings on the rough tongue that had brought her that single scrap of hope.

“There, there, lass, let’s lay him warm,” Jorian rumbled, and he hefted Gerard up gentle as a lamb, laid him on the piled wool blankets of the truckle bed. He pulled his flask of Schiedam gin from his belt, filled his cupped palm twice, and flung the sharp, pungent liquor hard across Gerard’s pale cheeks. The fumes bit through the fog of his unconsciousness, and he drew a faint, trembling sigh. No sound that ever fell on human ears was half so sweet to Margaret. She flew toward him, then skidded to a halt, quivering, too scared even to brush his hair back lest she snuff out his returning breath.

“That’s right, leave him be,” Jorian ordered, gruff but kind. “Don’t smother him the way you clung to me, or you’ll drive his breath right back out. He’s young, not some old grandad tottering at death’s door—he’ll come round of his own accord.”

Gerard sighed deeper still, a faint pink tinge stealing back to his colourless lips. Jorian turned for the door, and had barely crossed the threshold when something coiled tight around his ankles. It was Margaret, curled round his knees like a soft little eel, kissing his calloused work-worn hands, begging him not to drag Gerard back to the grave he had just pulled him from, not to undo the good he’d done.

“Never you fear, lass,” Jorian said. “This ain’t the first time I’ve done you two a good turn, remember? I slipped you the warning in the church before the chase ever began. Dierich Brower’s no friend of mine—I’d see him strung up before I breathe a word of this. Though truth be told, I’d be lying if I said the hundred crowns reward for those missing parchments don’t tempt me. That’d feed my Joan and our three bairns for years, no more scraping to make rent.”

“Those hundred crowns are yours,” Margaret swore at once. “I know exactly where they’re hidden. On my faith, and on St. Bavon’s bones, I’ll hand every last parchment over to you tomorrow—come alone, and no one else gets a penny of that gold.”

“Couldn’t pay me to share it with that weasel Brower,” Jorian grunted, and swore on his own bones he’d not breathe a soul that Gerard was under this roof, then slipped off fast, lest his long loitering rouse the rest of the bailiffs’ suspicions.

Margaret sank to her knees beside the bed then, shaking from head to toe, and prayed long and hard for Gerard, beside Gerard. When Dierich Brower demanded to know where on earth they’d got to, Jorian shrugged easy, told him the girl had worked herself into a dead faint, he’d roused her old doctor father Peter to tend to her, and the last thing they needed was a crowd of loud men tramping in to scare her straight to the churchyard. His offhand, unconcerned tone disarmed every last flicker of suspicion. The rest of the party quickly agreed the kitchen of the Three Kings tavern was far warmer than Peter’s cold, draughty parlour, trundled off, and untied the bound servant Martin on their way out. At the door, Dierich Brower even smirked at his men, muttering he’d told the burgomaster they’d be too late to catch the boy, and sure enough, he’d been right.

So in one single terrible night, Gerard had brushed the iron bars of a prison cell and the cold dark of the grave both. He owed his escape not to his cleverly conceived hiding spot, nor to Margaret’s quick wits, but to a small, unlooked-for spark of humanity in a rough, reckless man, tangled up with that same man’s quiet hunger for the gold that would feed his family. Human motives are always mixed, messy, seemingly contradictory; even the shrewdest, best laid plans go astray, and the paths out of disaster are rarely the ones we mapped out for ourselves.

Folks whose lives run gentle, unshaken by the fiercest heights of joy and depths of anguish the human heart can bear, are the luckiest of us all—but no quiet, untested soul could ever fathom the unnameable, overwhelming bliss that wrapped around Margaret and Gerard now. To sit and watch the face you love drift back from the edge of death, see roses bloom on his once death-pale cheek, light spark back in his eyes, words return to his lips, was a joy great enough to balance years of sorrow for Margaret. For Gerard, to wake from that trance and find his head pillowed on Margaret’s soft arm, to hear the woman he adored murmur words of love he’d never dared hope to hear, to feel her warm tears drop on his forehead and her gentle kisses brush his hands—he had never guessed just how fiercely, how tenderly she loved him. For half a second he almost thanked the men who had chased him to the grave’s edge, for gifting him this moment.

They wove their arms around each other, called each other husband and wife, and why not? They were solemnly betrothed, they had stood before the altar together, had received the Holy Church’s blessing on their union, and her curse on any soul who dared tear them apart.

But no woman’s nerves can bear a strain that sharp without breaking. Presently Margaret’s strength gave out, and she sank against Gerard’s shoulder, smiling faint and soft, completely unstrung. Gerard panicked and tried to rise to fetch help, but she held him with a gentle, unyielding grasp, begging him not to leave her for a single second. “While my hand is on you, I know you are safe, dearest. Nothing ails me—only that I am weak, and happy, so very happy.”

It was Gerard’s turn to cradle that dear head, her great waves of loose hair spilling over his chest, to soothe the trembling out of her, to whisper soft, quiet words of love. There is no charm in a woman’s heart sweeter to a man who loves her than that soft, unguarded vulnerability. They were poor, hunted, fugitives, but for those hours they were happy beyond measure. They knew they must part on the morrow, but that seemed nothing next to the grave they had just stood beside. A year or two apart, when they were so young? It was light as dandelion fluff. While there is life there is hope, they told themselves, and while there is hope there is joy. The future glowed bright as a sunrise, and the present was heaven itself.

Alas, their innocence held other risks they did not see, far greater than prison or the chase. Now that all visible danger had faded, they were in the gravest peril of all from their own full hearts, and their own inexperience.
