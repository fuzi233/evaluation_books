War, it seems to me—though I defer to those who have worn the uniform—was forever altered by the bloody lesson of Breed’s Hill. The slaughter our ranks endured behind those rebel barricades left even the boldest of our generals wary. From that day forward, a row of blankets might as well have been a fortress wall for the hesitation it inspired. At White Plains, when Washington’s ragged army again lay within our grasp, they halted before a breastwork of cornstalks, of all things! And then came Valley Forge—a starving, shivering host, left untouched while Howe’s army waltzed and dined in Philadelphia. The man was feted like a Roman conqueror, garlanded by ladies who might as well have been weaving his shroud. History, I suppose, will judge whether his mercy was wisdom or folly.

Yet how strange the turns of fate! Eighteen years before, we had sent Wolfe—a man who burned for victory like a furnace—to wrest Canada from France. He prowled the heights of Quebec like a wolf indeed, sleepless, relentless, until he seized his moment and died winning it. And now? Now we had Howe, fiddling while Burgoyne’s army bled out in the wilderness.

Ah, Burgoyne! His grand march from Quebec began like a parade: proclamations, trumpets, promises of swift triumph. Germans, Indians, artillery—enough to awe the colonies into submission, or so he thought. But the land did not rise for him. The Hudson did not open. And when the hour came for Clinton to strike north from New York, the winds, the tides, the very stars conspired to delay him. Always too late! That might be the epitaph of our war. Reinforcements too late, orders too late, rescue far too late for the thousands surrendered at Saratoga.

I confess, watching it unfold stirred something in me—some old fire my comfortable life in England had banked to embers. My brother Hal, content on his Virginia plantation with his dogs and his crops and his slow, sunlit days, would have scoffed at my restlessness. But then, Hal’s shoe fit him. Mine, though lined with every luxury, pinched.

Yet fate has a way of shaking a man awake. At Fort Clinton, as we stormed those rocky heights, a musket butt nearly dashed my brains out—until a blade flashed, and Joe Blake saved my life. (A debt repaid with a parish living, though Joe still grumbles that he’d rather have a commission.) That night, as the rebel ships burned on the river, lighting the sky like funeral pyres, I felt the strange exhilaration of war. Foolish, perhaps. But the heart beats louder when death whispers in your ear.

Then came the letter. A scrap of paper, delivered under flag of truce, that froze my blood: *Colonel H. Warrington, of the Virginia line, hopes that Sir George Warrington escaped unhurt...* My brother. My own flesh, there across the lines. That night, under a single blanket in a drafty hut, we spoke of home, of Mother—still a staunch Tory, though Hal swore she was safe—and of his Fanny, whom he praised as if she were Venus and Minerva combined. And I told him of my decision: to hang up my sword, to go home. His embrace was answer enough.

We parted at dawn. I carried with me the memory of his face, dimming as the boat pulled away. Would I ever see him again? In this world of strife and parting, nothing was certain.

Clinton’s victory, brilliant as it was, meant nothing in the end. Saratoga broke Burgoyne, and with him, our hopes. By the time the news reached our fleet, we were already bound for England—my wife weeping with relief, her prayers answered. Let others mourn the lost cause. I had my own to cherish.

*Satis pugnae.* Enough of battle. Let it be so.
