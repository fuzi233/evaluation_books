I had not taken ten steps when a grotesque shape halted my progress. Drawing closer, I discerned a carriage of archaic design, its frame rotting yet stubbornly upright upon massive wheels. Flanking the central pole lay the bleached skeletons of two horses, their grim white skulls pointing upward as shriveled reins connected to the tattered hammer-cloth where a skeleton coachman sat. Both doors hung open, revealing two skeletal figures slumped in their respective corners.

As I watched, they stirred. With a rattling crash of bone against bone, they leaped from the doors. One collapsed immediately; the other stood for a fleeting moment, its structure trembling violently, before creeping to the opposite side of the carriage. Its leg-bones, thin and brittle, seemed insufficient to bear its weight, yet it managed to kneel beside its fallen comrade, attempting to lift it, nearly toppling in the effort.

The prostrate figure rose with a sudden surge of effort, settling into a sitting posture. It turned its yellowed cranium this way and that, then, ignoring its neighbor, scrambled to its feet by gripping the spokes of the rear wheel. Standing half-erect, back to its companion, it held one knee-joint with both hands. With similar difficulty and several contortions, the kneeling figure followed suit and addressed its partner.

"Have you injured yourself, my lord?" the voice came, distant and ill-formed, as if blown away by a spectral gale.

"Yes, I have," the other replied in a rougher tone. "You offered no aid, and this cursed knee is dislocated!"

"I did my utmost, my lord."

"No doubt, my lady, for it was dire! I feared I'd never find my footing again!—But, bless my soul, madam! Are you also missing your bones?"

She glanced down at herself.

"I have nothing else to be missing," she retorted. "And YOU, at least, cannot complain! But what on earth is this? Am I dreaming?"

"You may be dreaming, madam—I cannot say; but this knee of mine forbids the grateful illusion.—Ha! I too perceive I have nothing but bones to walk in! Not unbecoming for a man, perhaps! I trust, by goodness, they are not MY bones! Every ache is worse than another, and this loose knee the worst. The bed must have been damp—and I too drunk to notice!"

"Probably, my lord of Cokayne!"

"What! What!—You make me think I'm dreaming too, with all these aches! How do YOU know the title my rowdy cronies give me? I don't recall you!—Anyhow, you have no right to take liberties! My name is—Lord—tut, tut! What do you call me when I'm—when you are sober? I can't—at the moment!—Why, WHAT IS my name?—I must have been VERY drunk when I went to bed! I often am!"

"You visit so rarely that I do not know, my lord; but I may take your word for THAT!"

"I hope so!"

"—if for nothing else!" "Hoity-toity! I never told you a lie in my life!"

"You never told me anything but lies."

"Upon my honour!—Why, I never saw the woman before!"

"You knew me well enough to lie to, my lord!"

"I seem to begin dreaming I've met you before, but, upon my oath, there is nothing to identify you! Without your clothes, who is to tell who you may not be?—One thing I MAY swear—that I never saw you so undressed before!—By heaven, I have no recollection of you!"

"I am glad to hear it: my memories of you are the less distasteful!—Good morning, my lord!"

She turned away, hobbling with a clacking rhythm, pausing a few paces later.

"You are just as heartless as—as any other woman, madam!—Where in this hellish place shall I find my valet?—What was the cursed name I used to call the fool?"

He twisted his bare head on its creaking pivot, still clutching his knee with both hands.

"I will serve as your valet for once, my lord," the lady said, turning back. "—What can I do for you? It is difficult to say!"

"Tie my leg on, of course, you fool! Can't you see it's almost off? Heigho, my dancing days!"

She scanned her surroundings with empty sockets, finding a piece of fibrous grass. She bound the adjoining parts that formed the knee together. Once finished, he took a few carefully tentative steps.

"You used to stamp rather differently, my lord!" she remarked as she rose.

"Eh? What!—Now that I look at you again, it seems I used to hate you!—Eh?"

"Naturally, my lord! You hated many people!—your wife, of course, among them!"

"Ah, I begin, I be-gin---- But—I must have been somewhere for a long time!—I really forget!—There! Your damned, miserable bit of grass is breaking!—We used to get on PRETTY well together—eh?"

"Not that I remember, my lord. The only happy moments I shared with you were scattered across the first week of our marriage."

"Was that the way of it? Ha! ha!—Well, it's over now, thank goodness!"

"I wish I could believe it! Why were we sitting in that carriage together? It wakes apprehension!"

"I think we were divorced, my lady!"

"Hardly enough: we are still together!"

"A sad truth, but capable of remedy: the forest seems extensive!"

"I doubt! I doubt!"

"I am sorry I cannot think of a compliment without lying. To judge by your figure and complexion, you have lived hard since I last saw you! I cannot surely be QUITE so naked as your ladyship!—I beg your pardon, madam! I trust you'll take it I jest in a dream! It matters not; dreaming or waking, all is appearance! You can't be certain of anything, and that's as good as knowing there is nothing! Life may teach any fool that!"

"It has taught me the fool I was to love you!"

"You were not the only fool! Women had a trick of falling in love with me:—I had forgotten you were one of them!" "I did love you, my lord—a little—at one time!"

"Ah, there was your mistake, my lady! You should have loved me much, loved me devotedly, loved me savagely—loved me eternally! Then I should have grown tired of you sooner, and not hated you so much afterward!—But let bygones be bygones!—WHERE are we? Locality is the question! To be or not to be, is NOT the question!"

"We are in the other world, I presume!"

"Granted!—but which or what sort of other world? This can't be hell!"

"It must: there's marriage in it! You and I are damned in each other."

"Then I'm not like Othello, damned in a fair wife!—Oh, I remember my Shakespeare, madam!"

She picked up a broken branch from a bush, steadying herself, and walked away, tossing her small skull.

"Give that stick to me," cried her late husband. "I need it more than you."

She gave no answer.

"You mean to make me beg for it?"

"Not at all, my lord. I mean to keep it," she replied, continuing her slow departure.

"Give it me at once; I mean to have it! I require it."

"Unfortunately, I think I require it myself!" returned the lady, walking faster, her joints cracking and bones clinking sharply.

He started to follow, but nearly fell; his grass binding had burst. With an oath, he stopped, grasping his leg again.

"Come and tie it up properly!" he would have thundered, but only piped and whistled!

She turned to look at him.

"Come and tie it up instantly!" he repeated.

She walked a step or two farther.

"I swear I will not touch you!" he cried.

"Swear on, my lord! There is no one here to believe you. But, pray, do not lose your temper, or you will shake yourself to pieces. Where to find string enough to tie up all your crazy joints is more than I can tell."

She returned, kneeling once more at his side—first, however, placing the disputed stick beyond his reach and within hers.

The instant she finished retying the joint, he lunged, seemingly to seize her by the hair, but his hard fingers slipped on the smooth poll.

"Disgusting!" he muttered, grabbing her upper arm-bone instead.

"You will break it!" she said, looking up from her knees.

"I will, then!" he answered, beginning to strain.

"I shall not tie your leg again the next time it comes loose!" she threatened.

He gave her arm a vicious twist, but luckily her bones were in better condition than his. She stretched her other hand toward the broken branch.

"That's right: reach me the stick!" he grinned.

She swung it around with such force that one of the bones of his sounder leg snapped. He fell, choking with curses. The lady laughed.

"Now you will have to wear splints always!" she said. "Such dry bones never mend!"

"You devil!" he cried.

"At your service, my lord! Shall I fetch you a couple of wheel-spokes? Neat—but heavy, I fear!"

He turned his bone-face aside, offering no answer, and lay groaning. I marveled he hadn't shattered completely upon falling. The lady rose and walked away—not ungracefully, I thought.

"What can come of it?" I murmured to myself. "These are too wretched for any world, and this cannot be hell, for the Little Ones are here, and the sleepers too! What can it all mean? Can things ever come right for skeletons?"

"There are words too big for you and me: ALL is one of them, and EVER is another," a voice near me said. It was a voice I knew.

I looked about but saw no speaker.

"You are not in hell," it resumed. "Neither am I. But those skeletons are in hell!"

Before he finished, I caught sight of a raven perched on a beech branch directly overhead. The moment he left it, he alighted on the ground, standing there as the thin old man of the library, with his long nose and long coat.

"The male was never a gentleman," he continued, "and in this bony stage of retrogression, with his skeleton showing through his skin and his character outside his manners, he does not look like one. The female is less vulgar and possesses a little heart. But, with society's restraints removed, you see them now just as they are and always were!"

"Tell me, Mr. Raven, what will become of them," I said.

"We shall see," he replied. "In their day they were the handsomest couple at court; and now, even in their dry bones, they seem to regard their former repute as an inalienable possession. To see their faces, however, may yet do something for them! They felt themselves rich too while they had pockets, but they have already begun to feel rather pinched! My lord used to regard my lady as a worthless encumbrance, for he was tired of her beauty and had spent her money; now he needs her to cobble his joints for him! These changes have roots of hope in them. Besides, they cannot now get far away from each other, and they see none else of their own kind: they must at last grow weary of their mutual repugnance, and begin to love one another! For love, not hate, is deepest in what Love 'loved into being.'"

"I saw many more of their kind an hour ago, in the hall close by!" I said.

"Of their kind, but not of their sort," he answered. "For many years these will see none such as you saw last night. Those are centuries in advance of these. You saw that those could even dress themselves a little! It is true they cannot yet retain their clothes so long as they would—only, at present, for a part of the night; but they are pretty steadily growing more capable, and will by and by develop faces; for every grain of truthfulness adds a fibre to the show of their humanity. Nothing but truth can appear; and whatever is must seem."

"Are they upheld by this hope?" I asked.

"They are upheld by hope, but they do not in the least know their hope; to understand it, is yet immeasurably beyond them," answered Mr. Raven.

His unexpected appearance caused me no astonishment. I was like a child, constantly wondering, and surprised at nothing.

"Did you come to find me, sir?" I asked.

"Not at all," he replied. "I have no anxiety about you. Such as you always come back to us."

"Tell me, please, who am I such as?" I said.

"I cannot make my friend the subject of conversation," he answered, with a smile.

"But when that friend is present!" I urged.

"I decline the more strongly," he rejoined.

"But when that friend asks you!" I persisted.

"Then most positively I refuse," he returned.

"Why?"

"Because he and I would be talking of two persons as if they were one and the same. Your consciousness of yourself and my knowledge of you are far apart!"

The lapels of his coat flew out, and the lappets lifted, and I thought the metamorphosis of HOMO to CORVUS was about to take place before my eyes. But the coat closed again in front of him, and he added, with seeming inconsequence,

"In this world never trust a person who has once deceived you. Above all, never do anything such a one may ask you to do."
