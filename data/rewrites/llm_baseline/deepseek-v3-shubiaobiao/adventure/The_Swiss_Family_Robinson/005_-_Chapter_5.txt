The morning sun had scarcely risen when I set to work preserving the kangaroo’s hide, determined to keep it intact. Meanwhile, Fritz prepared for our journey back to the wreck. I meant to give Ernest and Jack final instructions, but the two had vanished after breakfast. Their mother guessed they had gone to fetch potatoes—though leaving without permission was a habit I intended to correct. With Turk accompanying them, I trusted they’d be safe, though I regretted leaving my wife alone with little Franz.

We crossed Jackal River, only to be startled by Jack and Ernest leaping from a hiding place, delighted with their ambush. Their grins suggested they expected to join us, but I refused firmly—though their mischief softened my reproach. Instead, I sent them home with a message for their mother: we’d be working through the night on the wreck, constructing a raft to haul heavier cargo.

Fritz and I rowed out, the current swiftly carrying us to the shattered ship. Clambering aboard, we surveyed the wreckage and set to work. Empty casks became the raft’s foundation—twelve lashed together, topped with planks and a low bulwark. By dusk, exhaustion forced us to rest. We supped on the ship’s provisions and slept on spring mattresses, a luxury after our hammocks.

At dawn, we loaded the raft with everything precious: cabin furnishings, tools, chests of supplies, even young fruit trees—apple, pear, orange, and more. The cargo meant for a colony now became our treasure. Fishing gear, a compass, and harpoons were added, Fritz playfully mimicking a whaler. By afternoon, we set sail, the raft in tow.

Then—excitement. Fritz spotted a turtle asleep on the waves. Steering closer, he suddenly harpooned it. The creature surged forward, dragging our boats wildly. I shouted warnings, but Fritz, exhilarated, begged to hold on. The turtle hauled us toward an unfamiliar stretch of shore, grounding us abruptly. Wading out, I dispatched the beast, its strength astonishing even in death.

Fritz fired his gun to signal our return. The family came running, marveling at the turtle and our laden vessels. Supper was a feast of turtle meat—my wife skeptical of its green fat until I assured her it was a delicacy. Fritz dreamed of turning the shell into a water trough, while Jack boasted of discovering clay for its base.

Ernest, ever the naturalist, presented strange roots he’d found—possibly manioc, a staple if prepared carefully. Meanwhile, my wife revealed her secret: planted seeds from her "magic bag"—corn, melons, pumpkins—sprouting alongside our potatoes.

The next morning, I rose early to check the boats, hauling back more goods with the donkey’s help. Returning, I found the family still abed, lulled by the comfort of the ship’s mattresses—or perhaps the previous night’s indulgence in the captain’s canary sack.

Work resumed fiercely. Another trip to the wreck yielded wheelbarrows, tools, and—most thrilling—the disassembled parts of a pinnace, complete with brass guns. Jack, in his element, unearthed a wheelbarrow for potatoes, while penguins onshore provided comic relief, their waddling procession ending in chaos when the dogs bowled Jack over.

Back at Falconhurst, my wife’s industry shone—potatoes, manioc, and now her hidden garden promises. My tobacco-graters, mistaken for snuff mills, were actually destined for grinding flour. Skeptical but willing, she stitched a canvas bag as I planned our first attempt at bread.

As night fell, we rested, the day’s labor behind us, our fortunes ever expanding on this uncharted shore.
