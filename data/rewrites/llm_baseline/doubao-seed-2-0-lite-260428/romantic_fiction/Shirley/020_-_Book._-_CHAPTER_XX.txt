The two girls slipped through the greying dawn on their walk back to the rectory, not a single soul crossing their path. They turned the key in the latch as soft as breath, stole up the staircase without a single creak to betray them, the faint, honeyed light of breaking June guiding their steps. Shirley fell straight onto her bed the moment she stepped through the door; she had never spent a night away from Fieldhead before, and the wild, terror-charged drama of the riot they’d just witnessed was unlike any excitement she’d ever known, yet scarce had her head touched the pillow before deep, restoring sleep swept over her, smoothing every line of tension from her face.

Perfect, glowing health was Shirley’s gift: warm-hearted, fierce in her sympathies, she was never nervous. The strongest emotions could rouse her, sway her, shake her like a gale shakes an oak, but they never exhausted her. Her elasticity never bent, her bright freshness never faded. Where every day brought her some new, stirring joy, every night brought her the rest to meet it. Caroline sat beside her now, watching the soft serenity of her sleeping face, and felt the gulf between their two temperaments yawn wide.

She could not sleep. Even the mild, pleasant bustle of the previous day’s school tea would have kept her restive through the dark hours, and the raw, screaming terror of the mill attack replayed behind her eyelids on a loop, sharp and vivid. It was useless even to try to lie still; she sat upright beside Shirley, counting the dragging minutes, watching the pink edge of the sun climb higher over the thatched rooftops.

Caroline had kept too many of these lonely vigils of late: hours where the mind, starved of sweet hope, of warm, happy memories, tries to feed itself on nothing but thin, half-formed wishes, and when that fails, turns to philosophy, to resolution, to resignation, begging for aid that never seems to come. A devout Christian, she had poured out prayer after prayer through the dark, begging for patience, for strength, for a little relief from the quiet ache that had gnawed at her for months, but it felt as if her words had drifted unheard up to the empty sky. There were moments she sank into that cold dark of religious despair, convinced she was forsaken entirely, though she could not name the sin that had earned it.

Most people have known that hour: when hope after hope has been deferred, till the heart sickens in the chest. It is the darkest point before the dawn, the sharp January wind that carries both the dirge of dying winter and the secret promise of spring just over the horizon. The suffering soul cannot see that promise in the throes of its pain, but it is always there. Hold fast to love, hold fast to faith: He never deserts the souls He loves.

At last the rectory roused. The servants stumbled down to light the kitchen fire, the shutters were thrown open below. Caroline rose from the hard, thorny couch that had given her no rest, feeling that small, gentle lift of spirits that the return of day and the chance of small, kind actions gives to anyone not quite broken. She dressed with her usual careful neatness, taming her hair and smoothing the fabric of her gown till no trace of the forlorn, heavy languor in her heart showed on her face. When they both came down to breakfast, she looked as fresh as Shirley, save that Miss Keeldar’s eyes sparkled bright with anticipation, and Caroline’s held a soft, tired dimness.

“Today I am going to cross-examine Moore thoroughly,” Shirley declared as her first words, her face glowing with all the bright, busy interest that made every day feel like an adventure for her. “He thinks he outwitted us so cleverly, hiding all that danger away last night. That is always the way with men, is it not? They keep all sharp, hard truths from women, thinking they are sparing us pain. They had no idea where we were last night, just as we had no idea where they meant to hide. I do believe most men fancy women’s minds are no sharper than children’s. What a mistake that is.”

She spoke as she stood before the glass, twisting her naturally wavy hair into soft curls around her fingers, and picked up the theme again five minutes later while Caroline fastened the hooks of her gown and clasped her girdle snug.

“If men could see us exactly as we are, they would be quite shocked. The cleverest, sharpest of them all misread women, every time. They make our ‘good women’ into half doll, half angel, and our bad women into nothing but fiends, no shades in between. And then they gush over the heroines of poems and novels and plays, calling them divine! They may be pretty enough, but half the time they are as artificial as the silk rose pinned to the brim of my best bonnet. If I spoke all I thought on that subject, I would be stoned to death under a cairn of avenging stones before the hour was out.”

“Shirley, do hush, I cannot fasten these hooks if you fidget so,” Caroline laughed. “And for what it is worth, authors’ heroines are hardly more foolish than authoresses’ heroes.”

“Not a bit of it! Women read men far more truly than men read women. I shall write a magazine paper proving it one day, when I have time. Though I know perfectly well it will be ‘declined with thanks’ and left gathering dust at the publisher’s office.”

“Of course it will. You are not nearly learned enough to pull that off, Shirley.”

“God knows I cannot argue with you, Cary. I am as ignorant as a stone. The only comfort is, you are not much better.”

They settled down to their breakfast tea and toast.

“I do wonder how Mrs. Pryor and Hortense Moore fared through the night,” Caroline said as she poured out the coffee. “Selfish thing that I am, I did not think of either of them till this moment. They must have heard all the noise—Fieldhead and the cottage are so close to the mill, and Hortense is so timid of disturbances. I am sure Mrs. Pryor was frightened too.”

“Never fear, Moore will have made sure his sister was sent off to stay with Miss Mann last night, far away from the tumult,” Shirley assured her. “He will have stashed her there safely. Mrs. Pryor I am a little uneasy about, but we shall be back with her in half an hour.”

By that time, wild rumors of the night’s attack on Hollow’s Mill were spreading like wildfire across the whole parish. Fanny, who had run to Fieldhead to fetch the morning milk, came panting back with news that a full battle had broken out at the mill, and some folk were saying twenty men lay dead. Eliza, left at home, had been told by the butcher’s boy that the whole mill had burned to the ground. Both servants burst into the parlour breathless, spilling out their wild tales, swearing the rector must have run off last night to join Mr. Moore and the soldiers, that Mr. Malone had not been seen at his lodgings since the afternoon before, that Joe Scott’s wife and children were distraught wondering if their husband and father was hurt or dead.

Hardly had they finished the story when a knock came at the kitchen door: Fieldhead’s errand boy, red-faced and breathless, bearing a hurried scrawled note from Mrs. Pryor. She begged Shirley to come home at once, for the whole parish was in an uproar, and there were orders to be given that only the mistress of the hall could see to. A postscript added that Miss Helstone must on no account be left alone at the rectory, and that she ought to come with her.

“There is no question of that,” Shirley said, tying the ribbons of her own bonnet in a swift, neat bow before darting to fetch Caroline’s.

“But what of Fanny and Eliza? What if my uncle comes back?” Caroline protested.

“Your uncle will not be back for hours,” Shirley waved her off. “He will be galloping back and forth between Briarfield and Stilbro’ all day, rousing magistrates in the courthouse and officers at the barracks. Fanny and Eliza can ask Joe Scott’s wife and the clerk’s wife over to keep them company. Besides, there is no real danger now. The rioters will not dare rally for weeks, and I would wager Moore and your uncle will use last night’s outbreak to get the authorities to put a stop to the unrest entirely. I only hope they are not too cruel, not too relentless in hunting down the men who ran away defeated.”

“Robert will not be cruel,” Caroline said softly. “We saw that, last night.”

“But he will be hard,” Shirley corrected her. “And your uncle will be harder still.”

As they hurried along the path through the meadows and plantation to Fieldhead, they saw the distant highway already thronged with riders and pedestrians all streaming towards the usually quiet Hollow, every soul curious to gawk at the scene of the riot. When they reached the hall, the backyard gates stood wide open, the court and kitchen crowded with chattering neighbours who had come for their daily milk, Mrs. Gill the housekeeper vainly trying to shoo them off home.

Shirley stepped straight into the throng, her manner frank and easy, the quiet natural charm that always won over working folk far faster than any fine lady’s airs. She greeted them cheerfully, asked if everyone had got their milk measured out. When the men and women nodded, she raised an eyebrow.

“Then what on earth are you all lingering here for, gossiping?” she laughed, good-humoured. “The whole world loves to stand about talking over trouble, don’t they? You talk when someone dies sudden, you talk when there is a fire, you talk when a mill owner fails or gets hurt. What good does all that talk do?”

The Yorkshire crowd loved that plain, honest good-humoured chiding far better than any flattery, and they laughed back, unoffended. Shirley teased them gently for wasting their hard-earned free hours gossiping over tea and beer at their neighbours’ expense, then softened, asking them to do her the favour of heading home so the drive to the house would not be blocked for all the callers she expected that day. Stubborn against any force, Yorkshire folk yielded easily to fair, kind persuasion: the yard was empty and quiet in five minutes.

They stepped inside, and Mrs. Pryor came running down the broad oak staircase to meet them, her usual soft, placid face pale, her timid blue eyes wide and unsettled. She had not slept a wink all night, had heard every shot and shout, and had been too nervous to give any orders touching Shirley’s stores without her mistress there to approve.

“The soldiers sent up word asking for refreshments, and for linen and wine for the wounded,” she said tremulously, “but I did not dare take the liberty of giving away your property, my dear. I was afraid they would eat us out of house and home, I could not think how many men there were. I do fear there has been some delay, now.”

Shirley’s face flushed dark, her temper flaring hot. “Those men risked their lives to defend my mill, my property,” she said, sharp and clear as a bell, calling for Mrs. Gill so loud her voice rang through the whole house. She remembered the day Shirley had fought two huge carrier dogs single-handed to save her old hound Tartar, choking one of the brutes off his bleeding eye till help arrived, and everyone in the house knew that flash in her eye meant she would not be crossed.

“Send a man down to the mill at once, tell them everything in this house is at Mr. Moore’s service, the rector’s, the soldiers’ and the wounded’s,” she ordered. “Load all the bread, all the meat, every cask of wine in the cellar onto the hay cart, and send it all down to the Hollow. If we have not enough, send the butcher and the baker every scrap they have.”

Caroline slipped an arm around the flustered Mrs. Pryor, soothed her, walked her up to her room to rest, then came back down to help the overstretched servants. She was just hefting a heavy basket of linen up the cellar stairs when Shirley spotted her, darting over to take it from her hands at once. “This is nonsense, you will strain your soft arms,” she said, and the last of her sharp anger melted away, leaving only her usual warm, cheerful self.

She was still supervising the loading of the cart when a gentleman stepped into the yard, approaching her quiet and unannounced. It was Robert Moore, his face still smudged with ash, a faint band of court-plaster stuck to his temple.

“I hope Miss Keeldar is well this morning?” he said, a faint, teasing glint in his sharp eyes as he took in her still-flushed cheeks.

“Well enough, if I have not been too foolish,” Shirley laughed, not bothering to hide her smile. “And you are well too, I see. You deserve a little scolding for hiding all that danger from us last night, that is for certain. What brings you here?”

“Your messenger came down saying every single thing in Fieldhead was at our disposal,” he said, his quiet laugh rumbling in his chest. “We guessed you were overdoing it. There are only half a dozen soldiers and as many civilians down at the mill, not a full regiment. Let me help you cut this load down to size.”

He ordered half the baskets and casks unloaded, sent back to the cellar, teasing her all the while about what a wonderful over-generous army contractor she would have made. As the cart rumbled off towards the Hollow, Shirley leaned forward. “What of the wounded?” she asked.

“None of our side were hurt badly,” he answered.

“You were hurt yourself, on the temple,” came a soft, quiet voice from the shadow of the doorway, where Caroline had been lingering half-hidden behind Mrs. Gill. Moore’s eyes snapped to her at once.

“Lift your hair, let me see,” she said, her voice gentle. He took off his hat, pushed his dark curls back to show the small plaster, and Caroline nodded, satisfied, before stepping back into the dimness.

“How did she know I was hurt?” Moore asked, quiet.

“Rumour, no doubt,” Shirley said, then her tone hardened a little. “I was asking after your victims, the rioters, not your men. What harm came to them?”

“One of the rioters was killed,” Moore answered, his face growing serious. “Six others were wounded.”
