The small bright lawn ran like a green ribbon to the big bright sea.

It had been clipped and rolled until it looked edible—like some sugared confection laid flat for a fête—its edges stitched with scarlet geranium and dark coleus. Along the curving path that dipped toward the cliff, cast-iron vases—painted a glossy chocolate-brown—lifted looping garlands of petunia and ivy geranium. Even the gravel underfoot had been combed into obedient lines, as if a fastidious cook had dragged a fork through a tray of flour to make it ready for dough.

Halfway between the cliff’s edge and the square wooden house (also chocolate-coloured, with a verandah roof striped in yellow and brown to mimic an awning) two large targets stood against a thick screen of shrubs. Opposite them, facing the targets, a real tent had been pitched; benches and garden-seats were scattered around it like place-settings at an outdoor table. Ladies in summer dresses and gentlemen in grey frock-coats and tall hats stood about or sat and leaned in murmured conversation; and every now and then a slender girl in starched muslin would step out from the tent, bow in hand, and send her arrow singing toward the painted rings. Talk would pause, as it always does when a spoon hovers over a boiling pot; there would be that small, sharpened silence; then the soft sound of approval or disappointment would ripple across the lawn.

Newland Archer stood on the Beaufort verandah and looked down as if he had never seen such a scene before. On either side of the shiny painted steps, large blue china flower-pots perched on bright yellow stands; each pot held a spiky green plant arranged with the stiff emphasis of a centrepiece. Below the verandah ran a wide border of blue hydrangeas edged again with geraniums, as though the whole place were garnished in red to make the blue seem bluer. Behind him, through the French windows he had crossed, he caught glimpses—between swaying lace curtains—of parquet floors polished to a hard, glassy sheen; chintz poufs and dwarf armchairs sat like carefully placed dishes, and velvet tables were crowded with silver trifles whose only use was to glitter.

The Newport Archery Club always held its August meeting at the Beauforts’. The bow and arrow, once rivalled only by croquet, was beginning to be displaced by lawn-tennis; but tennis was still judged too rough for occasions meant to display delicate dresses and disciplined attitudes. For a social afternoon, the clean, controlled arc of an arrow—its brief whirr, its neat arrival—was preferable to the slap and scuffle of rackets.

Archer watched and wondered at the familiarity of it all. What unsettled him was not the spectacle but the fact that it continued unchanged while his own sense of it had altered so profoundly. Newport was where he felt the change most sharply. In New York, last winter, after he and May had settled into the new greenish-yellow house with the bow-window and Pompeian vestibule, he had dropped back into the routine of the office with an almost physical relief, as if the rhythm of his days had returned his body to its old posture. There had been the pleasing absorption of choosing a showy grey stepper for May’s brougham (the Wellands had given the carriage), and the steady satisfaction of arranging his new library as he had dreamed—dark embossed paper, Eastlake bookcases, and those “sincere” armchairs and tables which his family viewed with doubt. At the Century he had found Winsett again; at the Knickerbocker, the fashionable young men of his set; and between the hours given to law and those spent dining out or entertaining at home—with now and then an evening at the Opera or the theatre—his life had seemed, if not full, at least inevitable.

Newport, however, was an escape from duty into a holiday atmosphere so unmitigated that it left no corner for refuge. He had tried to persuade May to spend the summer on a remote island off the Maine coast—Mount Desert—where a few hardy Bostonians and Philadelphians were camped in “native” cottages, and where reports spoke of enchanting scenery and a wild, almost trapper-like existence amid woods and waters. He imagined quiet there: the true sounds of place—wind in firs, oars dipping, the hush after sunset—rather than the endless churn of wheels on gravel, the constant calling of names, the restless social din that Newport produced as naturally as a kitchen produces clatter when too many hands insist on stirring the same pot.

But the Wellands always went to Newport. They owned one of the square boxes on the cliffs, and their son-in-law could find no respectable reason to refuse joining them. Mrs. Welland, with her mild tartness, pointed out that it was hardly worth while for May to have worn herself out trying on summer clothes in Paris if she was not to be allowed to wear them; and Archer had not yet learned how to answer that kind of argument.

May herself could not understand his reluctance. She reminded him that he had always liked Newport as a bachelor. Since that was true, he could only reply that he was sure he would like it better than ever now that they were there together. Yet as he stood on the Beaufort verandah and looked over the brightly peopled lawn, it came home to him with a shiver that he was not going to like it at all.

It was not May’s fault. If, during their travels, they had once or twice fallen slightly out of step, harmony returned as soon as they were back among the conditions she understood. He had always foreseen that she would not disappoint him, and she had not. He had married—as most young men did—because he had met a perfectly charming girl at the moment when a series of rather aimless sentimental adventures were curdling into early disgust; and she had represented peace, stability, comradeship, and the steadying pressure of an unescapable duty.

He could not say he had been mistaken. It was gratifying to be the husband of one of the handsomest and most popular young married women in New York, when she was also one of the sweetest-tempered and most reasonable of wives; he was not insensible to those advantages. And that moment of madness on the eve of his marriage—he had trained himself to think of it as the last of his abandoned experiments. The idea that he could ever, in his senses, have dreamed of marrying the Countess Olenska had become almost unthinkable. In memory she had turned into the most plaintive and poignant of a line of ghosts.

But these neat eliminations left his mind emptier than he had expected, hollowed out by all the things it refused to name. Perhaps that was why the busy, animated people on the Beaufort lawn shocked him as if they were children playing in a graveyard, their laughter and murmurs rising and falling like the rattle of spoons on china when there was no hunger left.

A murmur of skirts brushed his side, and the Marchioness Manson fluttered out through the drawing-room window. As usual she was extravagantly festooned—her limp Leghorn hat anchored with many windings of faded gauze, and a small black velvet parasol with a carved ivory handle absurdly balanced above the larger brim.

“My dear Newland, I had no idea you and May had arrived! You yourself came only yesterday, you say? Ah, business—business—professional duties… I understand. Many husbands, I know, find it impossible to join their wives here except for the week-end.” She cocked her head and languished at him through screwed-up eyes. “But marriage is one long sacrifice, as I used often to remind my Ellen—”

Archer’s heart gave the queer jerk it had given once before, as if a door had slammed between him and the outer world; yet the break must have been no more than a blink, for he found himself listening to Medora answering a question he had apparently asked.

“No, I am not staying here, but with the Blenkers, in their delicious solitude at Portsmouth. Beaufort was kind enough to send his famous trotters for me this morning, so that I might have at least a glimpse of one of Regina’s garden-parties; but this evening I go back to rural life. The Blenkers, dear original beings, have hired a primitive old farm-house at Portsmouth where they gather about them representative people…” She drooped a little beneath her brim, and added with a faint blush: “This week Dr. Agathon Carver is holding a series of Inner Thought meetings there. A contrast indeed to this gay scene of worldly pleasure—but then I have always lived on contrasts! To me the only death is monotony. I always say to Ellen: Beware of monotony; it’s the mother of all the deadly sins. But my poor child is going through a phase of exaltation, of abhorrence of the world. You know, I suppose, that she has declined all invitations to stay at Newport, even with her grandmother Mingott? I could hardly persuade her to come with me to the Blenkers’, if you will believe it! The life she leads is morbid, unnatural. Ah, if she had only listened to me when it was still possible… When the door was still open…” She lifted her parasol an inch, as if to usher in a new subject. “But shall we go down and watch this absorbing match? I hear your May is one of the competitors.”

Beaufort himself came striding toward them from the tent—tall, heavy, too tightly buttoned into a London frock-coat, with one of his own orchids in his buttonhole. Archer, who had not seen him for two or three months, was struck by the change in him. In the hard summer light Beaufort’s floridness looked thick and bloated; but for his erect square-shouldered walk, he might have passed for an over-fed, over-dressed old man.

Rumours clung to Beaufort like the rich scent of his hothouse flowers. In spring he had gone on a long cruise to the West Indies in his new steam-yacht, and it was reported that at various ports a lady resembling Miss Fanny Ring had been seen in his company. The steam-yacht—built in the Clyde, fitted with tiled bathrooms and unheard-of luxuries—was said to have cost half a million; and the pearl necklace he brought back for his wife had the splendour of such expiatory gifts. His fortune was substantial enough, and yet the uneasy whispers persisted in Fifth Avenue drawing-rooms and in Wall Street offices. Some said he had speculated badly in railways; others that he was being bled by one of the most insatiable members of her profession. To every hint of threatened insolvency he answered with a fresh extravagance: new orchid-houses, a new string of racehorses, another Meissonnier or Cabanel for his gallery.

He greeted them with his usual half-sneer. “Hullo, Medora! Did the trotters do their business? Forty minutes, eh? … Well, that’s not so bad, considering your nerves had to be spared.” He shook hands with Archer and then, turning back with them, placed himself on Mrs. Manson’s other side and said a few low words which Medora did not catch.

She replied with one of her foreign jerks and a “_Que voulez-vous?_” that deepened Beaufort’s frown; but he produced a congratulatory smile as he glanced at Archer. “You know May’s going to carry off the first prize.”

“Ah, then it remains in the family,” Medora rippled; and at that moment they reached the tent, and Mrs. Beaufort met them in a girlish cloud of mauve muslin and floating veils.

May Welland was just coming out of the tent. In her white dress, with a pale green ribbon around her waist and a wreath of ivy on her hat, she had the same Diana-like aloofness Archer remembered from the Beaufort ballroom the night of their engagement. In the interval, it seemed, not a thought had passed behind her eyes nor a feeling through her heart; and though Archer knew she was capable of both, he marvelled again at the way experience slid off her, leaving no mark—as water slips from a waxed fruit.

She held bow and arrow, and stepping to the chalk-mark on the turf, lifted the bow to her shoulder and took aim. The attitude had such classic grace that a murmur of appreciation followed her. Archer felt again that warm glow of proprietorship that so often tricked him into momentary content. Her rivals—Mrs. Reggie Chivers, the Merry girls, and various rosy Thorleys, Dagonets, and Mingotts—stood behind in a lovely anxious cluster, brown heads and golden bent above the scores, pale muslins and flower-wreathed hats mingling like a soft-coloured salad of summer. All were young and pretty, all steeped in the season; but none had May’s nymph-like ease when, muscles tense and brow happily knit, she put her whole being into the small feat before her.

“Gad,” Archer heard Lawrence Lefferts say, “not one of the lot holds the bow as she does”; and Beaufort retorted: “Yes; but that’s the only kind of target she’ll ever hit.”

Archer felt irrationally angry. The contempt in Beaufort’s praise—his coarse tribute to May’s “niceness”—was precisely the sort of remark a husband might be expected to welcome. That such a man found her unalluring ought to have been proof of her quality. Yet the words sent a faint shiver through Archer. What if “niceness,” carried to the supreme degree, were only a negation—a curtain dropped before emptiness? As he watched May return, flushed and calm from her final bull’s-eye, he had the old, chilling sense that he had never once lifted that curtain.

She accepted congratulations with the simplicity that was her crowning grace. No one could be jealous of her triumphs, because she gave the impression she would have been as serene had she missed. But when her eyes met Archer’s, her face brightened with the pleasure she saw reflected there.

Mrs. Welland’s basket-work pony-carriage waited, and they drove off among dispersing vehicles—May taking the reins, Archer at her side.

The afternoon light still lingered over lawns and shrubberies; up and down Bellevue Avenue rolled a double line of victorias, dog-carts, landaus and “vis-à-vis,” carrying well-dressed people away from the Beaufort garden-party or home from their daily turn along the Ocean Drive. Newport’s soundscape was its own kind of tide: wheels crunching, hooves striking, voices calling, laughter lifted and dropped—an incessant social surf that left no room for the quieter notes Archer had once imagined he needed to hear himself think.

“Shall we go to see Granny?” May proposed suddenly. “I should like to tell her myself that I’ve won the prize. There’s lots of time before dinner.”

Archer agreed, and May turned the ponies down Narragansett Avenue, crossed Spring Street, and drove out toward the rocky moorland beyond. In this unfashionable region Catherine the Great—indifferent to precedent and thrifty of purse—had long ago built herself a many-peaked, cross-beamed _cottage-orné_ on cheap land overlooking the bay. In a thicket of stunted oaks, her verandahs spread above island-dotted waters. A winding drive rose between iron stags and blue glass balls embedded in mounds of geraniums to a front door of highly varnished walnut beneath a striped verandah-roof. Inside ran a narrow hall with a black-and-yellow star-patterned parquet floor. Four small square rooms opened off it, their heavy flock-papers pressing close under ceilings where an Italian painter had lavished all the divinities of Olympus.

One room had been made into a bedroom when the burden of flesh descended on Mrs. Mingott, and in the adjoining room she spent her days enthroned in a large armchair between door and window, perpetually waving a palm-leaf fan. Her prodigious bosom held the fan so far from the rest of her person that the air it made stirred only the fringe of the anti-macassars on the chair-arms, leaving the rest of the room to the warm, slow breaths of late summer and the faint saltiness that drifted up from the bay.

Since she had been the means of hastening his marriage, old Catherine had shown Archer the cordiality that a service rendered excites toward the person served. Convinced that irrepressible passion had driven his impatience, and being an ardent admirer of impulsiveness when it did not involve spending money, she always received him with a genial twinkle and a complicity of allusion to which May seemed, fortunately, impervious.

She examined the diamond-tipped arrow pinned on May’s bosom—the prize from the match—turning it with appreciative fingers. In her day, she remarked, a filigree brooch would have been thought enough; but there was no denying Beaufort did things handsomely.

“Quite an heirloom, in fact, my dear,” she chuckled. “You must leave it in fee to your eldest girl.” She pinched May’s white arm and watched the colour rise. “Well, well—what have I said to make you shake out the red flag? Ain’t there going to be any daughters—only boys, eh? Good gracious—look at her blushing again all over her blushes! What—can’t I say that either? Mercy me—when my children beg me to have all those gods and goddesses painted out overhead I always say I’m too thankful to have somebody about me that _nothing_ can shock!”

Archer laughed outright, and May echoed him, crimson to the eyes.

“Well, now tell me all about the party, please, my dears, for I shall never get a straight word about it out of that silly Medora,” the old lady continued; and when May said: “Cousin Medora? But I thought she was going back to Portsmouth?” Mrs. Mingott answered placidly: “So she is—but she’s got to come here first to pick up Ellen. Ah—you didn’t know Ellen had come to spend the day with me? Such fol-de-rol, her not coming for the summer; but I gave up arguing with young people about fifty years ago. Ellen—_Ellen_!” she called in her shrill old voice, trying to bend forward enough to glimpse the lawn beyond the verandah.

No answer came. Mrs. Mingott rapped her stick impatiently on the shiny floor. A mulatto maid in a bright turban appeared and told her mistress she had seen “Miss Ellen” going down the path to the shore.

Mrs. Mingott turned to Archer. “Run down and fetch her, like a good grandson; this pretty lady will describe the party to me,” she said; and Archer stood up as if in a dream.

He had heard the Countess Olenska’s name often enough in the year and a half since they last met; he knew the main incidents too. She had spent the previous summer at Newport and seemed to have gone a great deal into society; then in autumn she had abruptly sublet the “perfect house” Beaufort had found for her and decided to establish herself in Washington. During the winter Archer had heard of her—as one always heard of a pretty woman in Washington—as shining in the “brilliant diplomatic society” meant to compensate for the Administration’s social shortcomings. He had listened to these reports, and to contradictory accounts of her appearance, her conversation, her point of view and her choice of friends, with the detachment one gives to stories about someone long dead. Not until Medora spoke her name at the archery match had Ellen become living again. The Marchioness’s foolish lisp called up, with cruel precision, the small fire-lit drawing-room and the sound of carriage-wheels returning down a deserted street.

He thought of a story he had read—peasant children in Tuscany lighting a bunch of straw in a wayside cavern and revealing, on tomb walls, old painted faces that had waited in silence. So a name, spoken carelessly in the midst of a party, could flare up and show him what he had tried not to see.

The path to the shore descended from the bank where the cottage perched to a walk above the water planted with weeping willows. Through their veils Archer caught the glint of Lime Rock, with its whitewashed turret and the tiny house where the heroic lighthouse keeper Ida Lewis lived out her venerable years. Beyond lay the flat reaches and ugly government chimneys of Goat Island; the bay spread northward in a shimmer of gold to Prudence Island with its low growth of oaks, and the shores of Conanicut lay faint in sunset haze.

From the willow walk a slight wooden pier projected, ending in a pagoda-like summerhouse. In the pagoda a lady stood leaning against the rail, her back to the shore. Archer stopped as though waking from sleep. That vision of the past, he told himself, belonged to a dream; reality was the house on the bank above—Mrs. Welland’s pony-carriage circling the oval at the door; May sitting under shameless Olympians, glowing with secret hopes; the Welland villa at the far end of Bellevue Avenue; Mr. Welland already dressed for dinner, pacing the drawing-room with watch in hand and dyspeptic impatience. In such houses one always knew exactly what was happening at a given hour—each sound and movement measured like a recipe repeated until no one remembered it had ever been invented.

“What am I? A son-in-law—” Archer thought.

The figure at the end of the pier did not move. He stood halfway down the bank, staring at the bay furrowed by the coming and going of sailboats, yacht-launches, fishing-craft, and the trailing black coal-barges hauled by noisy tugs. The tugboats’ engines chopped the air and water into an industrial harshness that seemed to saw at his nerves; their noise made the gentler sounds—ripples, wind in willow leaves—retreat as if they were shy creatures driven back into cover. The lady in the summerhouse appeared held by the same sight, motionless as a fixed point in a shifting field.

Beyond the grey bastions of Fort Adams, a long-drawn sunset splintered into a thousand fires; the radiance caught the sail of a catboat as it beat out through the channel between Lime Rock and the shore. Watching it, Archer remembered a scene in _The Shaughraun_, Montague lifting Ada Dyas’s ribbon to his lips while she did not know he was in the room.

“She doesn’t know—she hasn’t guessed. Shouldn’t I know if she came up behind me, I wonder?” he thought; and suddenly he said to himself: “If she doesn’t turn before that sail crosses the Lime Rock light I’ll go back.”

The catboat slid on the receding tide. It passed before Lime Rock, blotted out Ida Lewis’s little house, and crossed the turret where the light was hung. Archer waited until a wide space of water sparkled between the last reef and the stern of the boat; still the figure in the summerhouse did not turn.

He turned and walked up the hill.

“I’m sorry you didn’t find Ellen—I should have liked to see her again,” May said as they drove home through the dusk. “But perhaps she wouldn’t have cared—she seems so changed.”

“Changed?” Archer repeated in a colourless voice, eyes fixed on the ponies’ twitching ears.

“So indifferent to her friends, I mean; giving up New York and her house, and spending her time with such queer people. Fancy how hideously uncomfortable she must be at the Blenkers’! She says she does it to keep cousin Medora out of mischief: to prevent her marrying dreadful people. But I sometimes think we’ve always bored her.”

Archer said nothing. The carriage wheels made a steady rustle over the road; somewhere a late cricket began its thin, persistent note, the one sincere sound in a world of polite noise.

May went on, and there was a tinge of hardness in her frank fresh voice that he had never noticed before. “After all, I wonder if she wouldn’t be happier with her husband.”

Archer burst into a laugh. “_Sancta simplicitas!_” he exclaimed; and when she turned a puzzled frown on him, he added: “I don’t think I ever heard you say a cruel thing before.”

“Cruel?”

“Well—watching the contortions of the damned is supposed to be a favourite sport of the angels; but I believe even they don’t think people happier in hell.”

“It’s a pity she ever married abroad then,” May said in the placid tone with which her mother met Mr. Welland’s vagaries; and Archer felt himself gently, firmly, put back among the unreasonable husbands.

They drove down Bellevue Avenue and turned in between the chamfered wooden gate-posts topped with cast-iron lamps that marked the approach to the Welland villa. Lights already shone through the windows, and as the carriage stopped Archer caught a glimpse of his father-in-law exactly as he had pictured him—pacing the drawing-room floor, watch in hand, wearing the pained expression he had long since found to be far more efficacious than anger.
