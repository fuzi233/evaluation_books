Do not rush to easy, generous conclusions, dear reader—do not assume out of hasty charity that from our last gentle shared moment, M. Paul Emanuel softened at once into an easy, placid man, free of the sharp, unruly sparks that could flare into discomfort, even quiet hurt, at a moment’s notice. That would be to misjudge him entirely. He was made of moods as unreasonable and sudden as summer thunderstorms: overworked as he so often was, he curdled into acute irritation at the smallest provocation, and threaded through the very blood in his veins ran the bitter, heady tincture of belladonna—no mere soft, tender jealousy of the heart, but a sterner, narrower, unforgiving sentiment that rooted itself in his very thoughts.

Sitting across from him at his desk, watching him knit his brow or thrust out his lip over an exercise of mine that bore far fewer errors than he’d hoped to find (for he loved a handful of my blunders as a child loves a cluster of sweet shelled nuts), I often thought he bore striking points of resemblance to Napoleon Bonaparte. I think so still. In his shameless disregard for performative magnanimity, he matched the great Emperor step for step. M. Paul would have quarrelled with twenty learned women, would have carried on a whole system of petty bickering and sniping with an entire capital’s worth of literary coteries, never once troubling himself over lost dignity or what others thought of him. He would have exiled fifty Madame de Staëls if they annoyed, offended, outrivalled, or dared oppose him.

I well remember one blazing, ridiculous episode with a certain Madame Panache, a lady Madame Beck had temporarily employed to teach history. She was clever—knew a great deal, in fact, and had perfected the art of making every scrap of her knowledge shine; she had unlimited command of words and easy, unshakable confidence. Her looks were far from unappealing; most would have called her a “fine woman,” yet there was something in her robust, ample charms and her bustling, demonstrative presence that the nice, capricious tastes of M. Paul could not abide. The echo of her voice through the common hall would fluster him into a strange, fidgety state; her long, free stride down the corridor would often make him snatch up his papers and decamp on the instant.

One day, moved by pure mischief, he barged unannounced into her classroom. In a flash he picked apart her teaching method, saw it conflicted with a pet system of his own, and pointed out what he called her “errors” with barely a scrap of ceremony, let alone courtesy. Whether he expected her to bow to his authority and listen, I know not—what he got was sharp, bitter opposition, and a full, round reprimand for his entirely unjustifiable interference. Instead of withdrawing with the dignity he still could have claimed, he threw down the gauntlet of defiance. Madame Panache, as warlike as the Amazon Penthesilea, picked it up at once. She snapped her fingers in his face, and unleashed a storm of words. M. Emanuel was eloquent, but Madame Panache was unstoppably voluble. A state of fierce, unrelenting antagonism followed. Instead of laughing at his prickly, loud, proud foe, M. Paul detested her with complete, deadly seriousness; he honoured her with his full, earnest fury, pursuing her vindictively and implacably, refusing to rest peacefully in his bed, enjoy his meals, or even smoke his cigar serenely until she was rooted out of the boarding house entirely.

The Professor won, but I cannot say the laurels of that victory sat gracefully on his temples. Once I dared hint as much. To my great surprise, he admitted I might be right, but swore that when he came face to face with people of Madame Panache’s coarse, self-satisfied breed, he had no control over his own passions; an unspeakable, driving aversion impelled him to wage a war of total extermination. Three months later, when he heard his vanquished foe had fallen on hard times and was at real risk of destitution for lack of work, he forgot all his hatred. Equal parts ferocious in goodness and ferocity in temper, he moved heaven and earth to find her a new post. When she came to visit him to make amends and thank him for his kindness, that old slightly loud voice, that old slightly forward manner, acted on him so strongly that within ten minutes he started up and bowed her—quite as good as chased himself—out of the room, in a fit of nervous irritation.

To press that bold Napoleon parallel a little further: M. Emanuel loved power, hungered for supremacy, just as the Emperor did. He was never a man to be submitted to unthinkingly. Sometimes resistance was necessary; it was right to stand your ground, look straight up into his eyes, and tell him his demands went past all reason—that his absolutism verged on tyranny.

The first glimmers, the first faint stirrings of unique talent that fell under his watchful eye never failed to excite him, even disturb him. He would watch that talent fight to be born, brow dark with a scowl, holding back his hand as if to say, “Come on if you have the strength,” but refusing to help the labour along. When the pang and peril of that first struggle were over, when the new talent drew its first breath, when he saw its metaphorical lungs expand and contract, felt its heart beat and discovered life glinting in its eye, he still refused to coddle it. “Prove you are true before I cherish you,” was his unyielding rule—and what a trial he made that proof to be! He strewed thorns and briars, sharp flints, across the path of feet unused to rough, unforgiving travel. He watched tearlessly as he exacted ordeals that had to be faced fearlessly, following footprints that grew sometimes faint, sometimes streaked with blood, as they neared their goal—following grimly, keeping the strictest, most unforgiving watch over the pain-wearied pilgrim. And when he finally granted them rest, before they could let slumber close their eyelids, he would pry those lids wide open with pitiless thumb and finger, gazing deep past the pupil and iris into the brain, into the heart, hunting to see if Vanity, or Pride, or Falsehood in any of its subtlest forms could be hidden in the furthest, darkest recess of their being. Only when every last severest test had been endured, when the most corrosive nitric acid had been used and failed to tarnish the ore, did he admit it was genuine—and, still wrapped in clouded silence, stamp it with his deep, unmistakeable brand of approval.

I do not speak of these trials from hearsay. Up to the close of our last chapter, M. Paul had never been my teacher, never given me lessons—but around that time, he heard me admit one day to an ignorance of arithmetic that, as he very truly pointed out, would have shamed a boy from the poorest charity school. He took me in hand at once, examined me thoroughly, found me abundantly deficient as you might guess, pressed some books into my hands, and set me my first tasks.

He did this at first with open, unconcealed exultation, condescending to say he believed I was “bonne et pas trop faible” — well enough disposed, and not wholly destitute of wit, but, he supposed thanks to all the hard, adverse circumstances of my life, trapped in a state of wretchedly imperfect mental development. The start of every new effort in my life has been marked by that strange, preternatural dim-wittedness. I never could, even when making the most ordinary new acquaintance, lay claim to average quickness. A depressing, difficult passage has prefaced every new page I have turned in life.

So long as that heavy, overcast passage lasted, M. Paul was so kind, so gentle, so forbearing. He saw the sharp pain of my own humiliation at my incapacity, and words can barely do justice to how tender and helpful he was. His own eyes would moisten when tears of shame and effort clouded mine; burdened as he was with endless work, he would steal half his own short recreation time to spend on me.

But strange, bitter grief! When that heavy, overcast dawn finally began to yield to sunlight, when my faculties began to struggle free and wake, when my time of energy and fulfilment came, when I voluntarily doubled, trebled, quadrupled the tasks he had set to please him, as I thought—his kindness curdled into sternness. The soft beam in his eyes turned to a sharp, restless spark. He fretted, he opposed me, he curbed me imperiously; the more I did, the harder I worked, the less content he seemed. Sarcasms whose severity amazed and puzzled me harassed my ears; then flowed out the bitterest insinuations against the “pride of intellect.” I was vaguely threatened with who knows what doom if I ever trespassed the proper limits of my sex, and conceived a contraband appetite for unfeminine knowledge. Alas, I had no such grand, abstract appetite! What I loved, it joyed me to work as hard as I could to please—but the noble hunger for science for its own sake, the godlike thirst after discovery, those feelings only touched me in the briefest, faintest flashes. Even so, when M. Paul sneered at me, I wanted to know them more fully; his injustice stirred ambitious wishes in me, gave my aspirations wings. At first, before I understood his strange motives, that uncomprehended sneer made my heart ache—but little by little, it only warmed the blood in my veins, and sent new, quick action to my pulses. Whatever powers God had given me, feminine or otherwise, I felt resolute not to be ashamed of any faculty He had bestowed on me.

The combat was sharp for a time. It felt as if I had lost M. Paul’s affection entirely; he treated me so strangely. In his most unjust moments, he would insinuate I had deceived him when I pretended, as he called it, “faible” — incompetent. He said I had feigned false stupidity on purpose. Then he would turn around and accuse me of the most far-fetched, impossible plagiarisms, swearing I had extracted all the pith from books I had never even heard of, and would have fallen asleep over as deep as Eutychus if I’d ever tried to read them.

Once, after he levelled that accusation, I turned on him, rose up against him. I gathered an armful of his books out of my desk, piled them in my apron, and emptied the whole heap in a pile at his feet on his little raised platform. “Take them away, M. Paul,” I said, “and teach me no more. I never asked to be made learned, and you compel me to feel very profoundly that learning is not happiness.” I went back to my desk, laid my head on my arms, and would not speak to him for two days afterwards. He had pained and chagrined me. His affection had been so sweet and dear, a new, incomparable pleasure—now that it seemed withdrawn, I did not care for his lessons at all.

He did not take the books away, though. He picked them all up carefully and restored them to their places, and came to teach me the next day as usual. He made his peace somehow—too readily, perhaps: I ought to have held out longer, but when he looked kind and good, and held out his hand in amity, my memory refused to hold the weight of his oppressive moments long enough to stay cross. And reconcilement, of course, is always sweet.

One particular morning, a message arrived from my godmother, inviting me to attend a notable lecture at the public rooms we had visited before. Dr. John had brought the message himself, and delivered it verbally to Rosine, who did not scruple to follow M. Emanuel down the hall as he walked to the first class, stand brazen-faced right before my desk with her hand in her apron pocket, and recite the whole announcement out loud, saucy as could be, finishing with: “Qu’il est vraiment beau, Mademoiselle, ce jeune docteur! Quels yeux—quel regard! Tenez! J’en ai le cœur tout ému!”

When she was gone, my professor demanded to know why I suffered “that impudent girl, that creature without an ounce of modesty” to speak to me in such terms. I had no pacifying answer to give. The terms were exactly what Rosine—whose skull held very little room for the organs of reverence and reserve—spoke every day. Besides, what she said about the young doctor was true enough. Graham was handsome; he had fine eyes and a thrilling, unforgettable glance. “Elle ne dit que la vérité,” I said. “Ah! Vous trouvez?” “Mais, sans doute.”

The lesson we had to sit through that day was so tense, we were all half-trembling and half-exultant with relief when it was over, and the rest of the pupils rushed out. I was heading for the door too, when a mandate to remain arrested me. I muttered that I desperately needed fresh air—the stove was glowing hot, the whole room overheated. An inexorable voice merely recommended silence. This salamander of a man, for whom no room ever seemed too hot, sat himself right between my desk and the stove—a spot where anyone else would have roasted alive, but which did not bother him a bit—and proceeded to confront me with a Greek quotation.

Deep in M. Emanuel’s soul festered a chronic suspicion that I already knew both Greek and Latin. Just as people say monkeys could speak if they dared, and hide the gift for fear it will be used against them, so I was accused of hoarding a fund of knowledge I was criminally, craftily concealing. It was insinuated I had enjoyed all the privileges of a classical education, revelled in the honey of Hymettus, and had a whole golden store of learning hived in my memory to secretly nourish my wits.

A hundred tricks M. Paul used to try to surprise my supposed “secret”: he wheedled, he threatened, he tried to startle it out of me. Sometimes he left Greek and Latin books lying in my path, then watched me the way Joan of Arc’s jailors tempted her with warrior’s armour, lying in wait for a reaction. Other times he would quote lines from who knows what ancient authors, rolling their sweet, sonorous verses off his tongue—for he had a wonderful voice, remarkable for its range, modulation, and matchless expression—while he fixed on me a vigilant, piercing, often malicious stare. He clearly expected grand reactions sometimes, but none ever came: not comprehending a single word, of course I could be neither charmed nor annoyed. Baffled, almost angry, he clung stubbornly to his fixed idea, pronounced my susceptibilities marble, my face a mask. It was as if he could not bring himself to accept the plain, homely truth, and take me for exactly what I was: men, and women too, must have some kind of delusion to hold to, and if none is ready to hand, they will invent exaggerations for themselves.

At moments I *did* wish his suspicions had been well-founded. There were times I would have given my right hand to possess all the treasures he ascribed to me. He deserved condign punishment for his testy crotchets. I could have gloried in proving his worst fears true, bursting upon his vision in a blaze of acquired erudition that would have confronted and confounded his little spectacles once and for all. Oh, why had no one tried to make me clever when I was young enough to learn, that I might by one grand, sudden, inhuman revelation—one cold, cruel, overwhelming triumph—crush that mocking spirit right out of Paul Carl David Emanuel!

Alas, no such feat was in my power. That day, as usual, his quotations fell completely flat. He soon shifted his ground. His next favourite theme was “women of intellect,” and here he was entirely in his element. A “woman of intellect,” it appeared, was a sort of lusus naturae, a luckless freak of nature, a thing with no place or use in creation, wanted neither as wife nor worker. Beauty far outstripped her in the first role. He believed in his very soul that lovely, placid, passive feminine mediocrity was the only pillow on which manly thought and sense could find rest for its aching temples; and as for work, male mind alone could produce any good practical result—hein?

That “hein?” was a sharp little interrogative intended to draw contradiction or objection out of me. But I only said, “Cela ne me regarde pas: je ne m’en soucie pas,” and presently added, “May I go, Monsieur? They have rung the bell for the second déjeuner, luncheon.”

“What of that? You are not hungry?”

“Indeed I am,” I said. “I have had nothing since breakfast at seven, and I will get nothing till dinner at five if I miss this bell.”

“Well, I am in the same plight,” he said, “but you may share with me.” He broke the brioche he had set aside for his own snack right in two, and gave me half. Truly his bark was far worse than his bite, but the really formidable attack was yet to come.

While eating my cake, I could not forbear expressing my secret wish that I really knew all the learning he accused me of hoarding. “Did I sincerely feel myself to be a total ignoramus?” he asked, in a softened tone. If I had replied meekly with an unqualified yes, I believe he would have stretched out his hand then and there, and we would have been friends on the spot—but instead I answered, “Not exactly. I am ignorant, Monsieur, of the knowledge you ascribe to me, but I sometimes, not always, feel a knowledge of my own.”

“What did I mean?” he asked, sharply.

Unable to answer that question in a breath, I evaded it by changing the subject. He had now finished his half of the brioche: certain that such a tiny fragment could not have satisfied his appetite, any more than mine had been sated, and breathing in the warm, sweet fragrance of baked apples drifting from the refectory far away, I dared ask if he did not smell that agreeable odour too. He confessed he did. I said if he would let me out by the garden door and let me run across the courtyard, I would fetch him a plateful; I added that I knew they were excellent, because Goton baked (or rather, stewed) the fruit perfectly, with a little spice, sugar, and a glass or two of white wine—might I go?
