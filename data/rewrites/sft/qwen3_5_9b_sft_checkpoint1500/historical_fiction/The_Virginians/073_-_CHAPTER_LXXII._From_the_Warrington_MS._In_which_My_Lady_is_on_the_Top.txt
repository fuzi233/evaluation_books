Gazing across the fire toward the armchair where my beloved companion has sat for the latter half of my existence, I frequently find myself asking—middle-aged gentlemen being permitted to recycle their jests, inquiries, and tales—whether any pair of youths ever displayed such folly and imprudence as we did upon our union, back in the year the old King passed? My son, who has leaped prodigiously far in the fervor of fox-hunting, remarks that he views the gaps and rivers he once crossed with terrifying hindsight, filled with astonishment at his own temerity in taking such desperate chances; yet, there remains no more ardent sportsman in either county than Miles. He loves his diversion so wholly that he cares for nothing else. He has fractured his collarbone and suffered a hundred tumbles (to his mother's horror), just as I, thinking perhaps of a poem or my address at Quarter Sessions, was cast over my old mare's head, who slipped on a stone as we dreamed along a park lane at four miles per hour. Miles's reckless play has been the joy of his life, just as my marriage has been the blessing of mine; and I never reflect upon it without giving thanks to Heaven. Mind, I do not set up my devotion as an exemplar. I tell no young folk, "Go forth and wed upon twopence a year," lest the vestry-men turn pale; yet my wife is notorious as a desperate matchmaker. When Hodge and Susan enter my justice-room discussing allowances, we urge them to spend their half-crown weekly at home, add a modest contribution of our own, and summon the vicar.

Now, when I pose a question to my dear oracle, I know precisely what the answer shall be; and thus, perhaps, the reason why I so constantly consult her. I need only wear a specific expression, and my Diana draws her reflection from it. If I say, "My dear, do you not think the moon was fashioned of cream cheese tonight?" she replies, "Well, Papa, it did look remarkably like cream cheese, indeed—you are unmatched in your droll similes." Or, if I remark, "My love, Mr. Pitt's speech was exquisite, though I fear he falls short of what I recall of his father," she answers, "No one equaled my Lord Chatham." Then one of the girls cries out, "But I've often heard our Papa say Lord Chatham was a charlatan!" Whereupon Mamma declares, "How like she is to her Aunt Hetty!"

As for Miles, *Tros Tyriusve* holds no sway over him. He attends solely to the sporting notices in the Norwich paper. Provided there is good scent, he cares not for the state of the realm. I believe the rascal has never read my poems, let alone my tragedies (for I mentioned *Pocahontas* to him recently, and the dunce mistook her for a river in Virginia). Regarding my Latin verses, how can he comprehend them when I know he cannot construe Corderius? Why, this notebook lies openly on the little table at my corner of the fireside, and anyone may read it who takes the trouble to lift my spectacles from their case; yet Miles never has. I insert caricatures of Miles therein, jokes directed at him; but he never learns nor heeds them. Only once, in lieu of a neat drawing of mine, executed in China ink, depicting Miles asleep post-prandium—a piece my friend Bunbury would not disavow—I discovered a crude sketch of myself vaulting over my mare Sultana's head, titled "The Squire on Horseback, or Fish Out of Water." The fellow roared with laughter, and all the girls tittered when I stumbled upon the page! My wife confessed she had never been so terrified as when I returned to my book; yet I can endure a joke against myself, having heard many, though (strange to relate, for one who dwelt among the chief wits of the age) I never heard a truly good one in my lifetime. Pray do not mind, Miles, though thou art no wit, I love thee none the less (there never was any love lost between two wits in a family); though thou lackst great beauty, thy mother deems thee as handsome as Apollo, or His Royal Highness the Prince of Wales, who was born in the very same year as thee. Indeed, she always finds Coates's portrait of the Prince strikingly similar to her eldest boy, keeping the print in her dressing-room to this very day.

In that same year, with what divergent prospects! did my Lord Esmond, son of Lord Castlewood, likewise appear to adorn the world. At that juncture, my Lord Castlewood and his humble servant had already arrived at a coolness, and heaven knows! my honest Miles's godmother brought no gold pap-boats to his christening! Matters have mended since, *laus Deo*—*laus Deo*, indeed! for I suspect neither Miles nor his father would ever have accomplished much for themselves, nor by their own wits.

Castlewood House bears quite a different visage now from that venerable countenance it wore in the days of my youth, when it was etched with the wrinkles of time, the scars of ancient wars, and the cracks and blemishes that years had marked upon its hoary features. I love best to remember it in its old shape, as I saw it when young Mr. George Warrington descended at the owner's invitation to attend his Lordship's marriage with Miss Lydia Van den Bosch—"an American lady of noble family of Holland," as the county paper announced her Ladyship to be. Then the towers stood as Colonel Warrington, the Marquis (as Madam Esmond would wish to term her father), had seen them. The woods (thinned not a little, it is true) stood, yea, some of the selfsame rooks may have cawed overhead which the Colonel had witnessed sixty years prior. His portrait hung in the hall which might have been his, had he not preferred love and gratitude to wealth and worldly honor; and Mr. George Esmond Warrington—that is, *Ipse Ego*, who writes this page down—as he traversed the old seat, pacing the long corridors, the smooth dew-spangled terraces, and the cool, shadowed avenues, felt momentarily as if he were one of Mr. Walpole's cavaliers, complete with ruff, rapier, buff-coat, and gorget, and as if an Old Pretender, or a Jesuit emissary in disguise, might emerge from behind any tall tree-trunk surrounding the mansion, or from within any antique carved cupboard inside it. I harbored the strangest, saddest, most pleasant, old-world fancies as I walked the grounds; I imagined tragedies, intrigues, serenades, escalades, Oliver Cromwell's Roundheads battering the towers, or bluff Hal's Beefeaters pricking over the plain before the castle. I was then courting a certain young lady (Madam, your Ladyship's eyes required no spectacles then, and above the brow thereof there was never a wrinkle or a silver hair), and I recall writing a ream of romantic description, under my Lord Castlewood's franks, to the lady who never tired of reading my letters then. She says I send her but three lines now, when I am away in London or elsewhere. 'Tis that I may not fatigue your old eyes, my dear!

Mr. Warrington deemed himself authorized to commission a genteel new suit of clothes for my Lord's marriage, and accompanied by Monsieur Gumbo, made his appearance at Castlewood a few days prior to the ceremony. I may mention that it had become expedient to dispatch my faithful Sady home aboard a Virginia vessel. A severe inflammation, attacking the throat and lungs, and proving fatal in countless instances in that year of Wolfe's expedition, had seized and nearly slain my poor lad, for whom his native air was pronounced the best remedy. We parted with an abundance of tears, and Gumbo shed as many when his master departed for Quebec: yet he possessed attractions in this country and none for the military life, so he remained attached to my service. We found Castlewood House brimming with friends, relations, and visitors. Lady Fanny was there under compulsion, a sulky bridesmaid. Some of the neighborhood virgins also attended the young Countess. A bishop's widow herself, the Baroness Beatrix, brought a holy brother-in-law of the bench from London to bind the holy knot of matrimony between Eugene, Earl of Castlewood, and Lydia Van den Bosch, spinster; and for some time before and after the nuptials, the old house in Hampshire wore an aspect of gaiety to which it had long been unaccustomed. The country families came gladly to pay their compliments to the newly wedded pair. The lady's wealth became the subject of everyone's talk, and no doubt did not diminish in the telling.
