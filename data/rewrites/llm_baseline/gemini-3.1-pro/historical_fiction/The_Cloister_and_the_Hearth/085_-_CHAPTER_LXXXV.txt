The sermon had already begun when Margaret crossed the threshold into the great church of St. Laurens. It was a vast, uncompleted edifice, functioning in its raw state as a monumental stone hearth for the city’s flock. Churches of such magnitude were not built in a single season; they grew like deep-rooted agrarian crops across generations. The side aisles were roofed, sealing in the scent of damp limestone and ancient timber, but the central aisle and chancel remained open to the heavens. Unfinished jagged openings in the outer walls stood in place of glazed windows, but on this glorious summer afternoon, the uncouth imperfections transformed the space into a vessel of profound sensory alchemy. 

Sunlight poured through the broken masonry not in sterile beams, but thick and golden, glazing the white pillars like clarified butter and scattering across the flagstones like crystalline shards of spun sugar. The central aisle was chequered with a cooling shade that felt as soothing as the damp loam of a root cellar, interspersed with patches of amber light animated by a heavenly, simmering fire. Above, the vivid blue sky vaulted the lofty aisle, pressing close against the stone. 

The mid-aisle, huge as it was, was crammed with worshippers, their white caps forming a sea of salt-white peaks beneath the blue vault. Yet, the space was suspended in a flawless acoustic ecology. The abrasive, industrial clatter of the burgeoning city outside was banished, replaced by the mellow, earnest cadence of the preacher’s voice. This sonic environment wrapped around the congregation, a critical balm for the psychological weariness of urban life. Margaret stood spellbound in the north aisle. Though she could hardly catch a specific word beneath the vast echoing arches, the sheer musicality of the preacher’s tone trickled over her agitated mind like warm, infused oil over rough waters, smoothing her frayed nerves. She leaned against a pillar, eyes half-closed, breathing in the faint, sweet memory of incense that clung to the stone like dried sage and roasted coriander.

Presently, she noticed a woman abandon an excellent vantage point in the south aisle to escape a shaft of sun that was beating down upon her head with the oppressive heat of a poorly vented oven. Margaret moved softly and swiftly through the crowd, securing the shadowed spot. She was now scarcely fifty feet from the preacher, positioned slightly behind him but within perfect auditory range of every syllable. 

Soon, however, her sensory immersion was disrupted by an acoustic and visual dissonance. A droll, bobbing shadow fell across the stone floor beside her, accompanied by the rhythmic, grating *thwack* of iron biting into soil. It was Jorian Ketel, the sexton, digging a grave outside the jagged window. The harsh, utilitarian noise threatened to pollute the fragile acoustic sanctuary of the church. 

Margaret turned her head toward the window to locate the source of the intrusion, but just as she did, a specific tone from the preacher's mouth struck her ear. It bypassed her intellect entirely, vibrating through her chest cavity with the resonance of a heavy stone mortar. Her hand flew to her bosom. It was not merely the timber of the voice; it was a phonetic contour, a deeply familiar inflection slipping through the dominant, polished lexicon of the ecclesiastical sermon. It was the subtle cadence of a dying regional dialect, one that tasted instantly of her native hearth. In that single, untranslatable sound, she smelled bruised fennel, slow-simmered bone broth, and the dark, nutty crust of rye bread baked on hot stones by the elders of her youth. 

She turned sharply and stared at the preacher. His back was to her, presenting only his tonsure. She sighed, the visual reality of the monk contradicting the visceral, agrarian memory his voice had evoked. She leaned forward, eyes downcast, desperate to catch that native accent again. It did not immediately return, but as the preacher warmed to his subject, the voice swelled, waking faint echoes of a thousand interwoven, multi-generational memories, domestic routines steeped in the profound sustenance of her past. She closed her eyes, unwilling to dispel the melancholy nourishment the sound provided.

Then, mid-sentence, the preacher stopped. 

The silence that followed was not peaceful; it was a sudden, violent rupture in the acoustic environment. Margaret almost sighed in disappointment as the soothing music ceased. She assumed the sermon had concluded, but the congregation remained frozen. Slowly, an uneasy rustling began. Startled countenances shifted, and hundreds of faces turned toward the pulpit. Margaret followed their gaze.

There, in the pulpit, stood the friar, wearing the face of a staring corpse. His naturally large eyes, exacerbated by the hollowness of his starved cheeks, were dilated to a supernatural size, glaring directly at her from a bloodless visage. Margaret cringed, terror spiking in her chest. She cast a fearful glance behind her, certain some monstrous anomaly had manifested at her back. But there was nothing. She was the outermost figure of the crowd. 

Instantly, the church’s acoustic harmony shattered into a chaotic cacophony. Figures sprang up, craning their necks. The turning of so many caps produced a loud, abrasive rustling, like the husks of dry wheat caught in a gale. Shrieks from nervous women and the panicked buzzing of men polluted the air. Seeing hundreds of eyes suddenly leveled upon her, Margaret shrank behind the pillar, casting one last, hurried glance at the preacher. Momentary as it was, she caught an expression on his stricken face that made her shiver to the marrow. Faint and overwhelmed, she collapsed onto a fragrant heap of pine chips left by the stonemasons, burying her face in her hands. 

The sermon resumed, but its resonance was dead. She heard the phonetic noise, but the semantic meaning was gone. Her mind whirled, snagging on a single, terrifying truth stamped upon the friar's ghastly face. It was *Recognition*. 

The sermon limped toward its conclusion in a strain of feverish, erratic eloquence that eventually dulled the crowd's memory of the preacher's supernatural pause. When it ended, Margaret hastily folded herself into the exiting tide of bodies. 

But at the threshold, she turned away from the streets and walked into the churchyard, seeking the grounding earth of her father Peter's grave. Despite her poverty, she had managed to procure a solid stone slab for him—a monument to a man who had understood the sacred, generational alchemy of turning raw harvest into life-giving sustenance. She sat on the cold stone, inhaling the scent of damp moss and turned soil, and threw her apron over her head to shroud her recognizable hair, enveloping herself in the faint smells of lye and flour dust.

"Father," she whispered, the old dialect slipping naturally from her tongue in a space where cultural memory refused to be erased. "Thou hast often heard me say I am wading in deep waters; but now I begin to think God only knows the bottom of them. I'll follow that friar round the world, but I'll see him at arm's length. And he shall tell me why he looked towards me like a dead man wakened, with not a soul behind me. Oh, father; you often praised me here: speak a word for me there. For I am wading in deep waters."

Her father’s tomb commanded a perfect side view of the church doors. And there upon the stone she sat, a silent sentinel wrapped in the culinary and linguistic ghosts of her ancestry, waiting to waylay the holy preacher who had shattered her world.
