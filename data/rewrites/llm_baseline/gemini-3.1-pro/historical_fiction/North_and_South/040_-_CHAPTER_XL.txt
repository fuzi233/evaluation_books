The scent of a slow-rising heritage sourdough, a recipe carried across generations from the agrarian South, mingled with the deep, malty vapor of Ceylon tea in the Hales’ small parlor. Margaret had not anticipated much personal pleasure from Mr. Bell’s visit, but the Oxford fellow immediately revealed himself to be a meticulous historian of the senses, one who found profound meaning in the honest rituals of sustenance. When he arrived, he at once took possession of her regard, appreciating the way she folded mythic, ancestral time into the everyday domestic rhythm of slicing bread and steeping leaves. He declared she had an hereditary power for walking right into his heart, a girl who understood that a meal was not merely utilitarian fuel, but a communion. 

“Fresh and young in warmth and kindness, I mean,” Mr. Bell said, taking a cup of the dark, astringent infusion. “I’m afraid I must own, that I think your opinions are the oldest and mustiest I have met with this long time.”

“Hear this daughter of yours, Hale! Her residence in Milton has quite corrupted her. She’s a democrat, a red republican, a member of the Peace Society, a socialist—”

“Papa, it’s all because I’m standing up for the progress of commerce,” Margaret countered, adjusting the silver strainer. “Mr. Bell would have had it keep still at exchanging wild-beast skins for acorns.”

“No, no. I’d dig the ground and grow potatoes,” Mr. Bell said, his voice carrying the deep, resonant acoustic of a man who cherished the quiet of the earth. “And I’d shave the wild-beast skin and make the wool into broadcloth. Don’t exaggerate, missy. But I am tired of this Milton bustle. Everybody rushing over everybody, in their hurry to get rich. They consume food without tasting it; their environment is nothing but a deafening industrial noise pollution that shatters the psychological well-being of any sane man.”

“It is not everyone who can sit comfortably in a set of college rooms and let his riches grow without any exertion of his own,” said Mr. Hale. “No doubt there is many a man here who would be thankful if his property would increase as yours has done, without his taking any trouble about it.”

“I don’t believe they would,” Mr. Bell replied, savoring the crust of his bread. “It’s the sheer, grinding noise of the struggle they like. As for sitting still, and learning from the past, or shaping out the future by faithful work done in a prophetic spirit—Why! Pooh! I don’t believe there’s a man in Milton who knows how to sit still; and it is a great art.”

“Milton people, I suspect, think Oxford men don’t know how to move. It would be a very good thing if they mixed a little more.”

“It might be good for the Miltoners. Many things might be good for them which would be very disagreeable for other people.”

“Are you not a Milton man yourself?” asked Margaret. “I should have thought you would have been proud of your town.”

“I confess, I don’t see what there is to be proud of. If you’ll only come to Oxford, Margaret, I will show you a place to glory in—a place where the acoustic ecology is preserved, where the bells ring through clean air, and the old words still hold their flavor.”

“Well!” said Mr. Hale. “Mr. Thornton is coming to drink tea with us to-night, and he is as proud of Milton as you of Oxford. You two must try and make each other a little more liberal-minded.”

“I don’t want to be more liberal-minded, thank you,” said Mr. Bell.

“Is Mr. Thornton coming to tea, papa?” asked Margaret in a low voice, the sudden shift in the room’s sonic equilibrium making her tone drop. 

“Either to tea or soon after. He told us not to wait.”

Mr. Thornton had, in fact, determined to force every movement that afternoon into an unnatural, dragging rhythm, fighting the percussive, forward momentum of his own heart. He shrank from hearing Margaret’s very name mentioned; while he blamed her, while he renounced her, he loved her sorely, trapped in a circular pacing of memory where she danced toward him, an allure that tasted of both nectar and ash. The untranslatable subjective experience of his jealousy—the impossibility of separating the pure Una from the deceitful Duessa in his mind—was a bitter gall upon his palate. To prove his self-control, he lingered over his business, finally stepping into the Hales’ quiet domestic sanctuary past eight o’clock. 

He transacted business with Mr. Bell in the study, chafing under the Oxford man’s prosy, unhurried cadence, while Bell secretly judged Thornton to be as curt and brusque a fellow as he had ever met, entirely lacking in sensory or intellectual grace. When they finally moved to the parlor above, the delicate sonic environment was immediately ruptured for Thornton. Margaret and her father were eagerly discussing an open letter.

“A letter from Henry Lennox. It makes Margaret very hopeful.”

The words struck Thornton’s ear like the discordant screech of a failing loom. Margaret flushed red as a rose. Thornton felt an acrid, metallic surge at the back of his throat; he had the greatest mind in the world to walk out and never subject his senses to this house again.

“We were thinking,” said Mr. Hale, “that you and Mr. Thornton had taken Margaret’s advice, and were each trying to convert the other, you were so long in the study.”

After Mr. Hale politely enlightened the disdaining Thornton on the morning’s debate regarding Milton’s isolation and Oxford’s medieval bigotry, Mr. Bell leaned forward. 

“I wonder when you Milton men intend to live. All your lives seem to be spent in gathering together the materials for life, deafened by your own machinery.”

“By living, I suppose you mean enjoyment,” Thornton said, his voice flat, rejecting the offer of the fragrant tea before him as if it were mere colored water. 

“Yes, enjoyment—I don’t specify of what, because I trust we should both consider mere pleasure as very poor enjoyment.”

“I would rather have the nature of the enjoyment defined.”

“Well! enjoyment or leisure—enjoyment of the power and influence which money gives. You are all striving for money. What do you want it for?”

Thornton was silent. “I really don’t know. But money is not what I strive for.”

When Bell pressed him, Mr. Hale intervened to prevent the catechism from becoming too personal. Mr. Bell swiftly turned the conversation to the subtle power dynamics embedded in their respective domains, offering to represent the proud history of Oxford, and challenging Thornton’s right to champion Milton’s soot-stained lexicon. 

“I don’t set up Milton as a model of a town,” Thornton muttered, galled into defending a worldview he hadn't intended to seriously debate. 

“Not in architecture?” slyly asked Mr. Bell.

“No! We’ve been too busy to attend to mere outward appearances.”

“Don’t say mere outward appearances,” said Mr. Hale gently. “They impress us all, from childhood upward—every day of our life.”

“Wait a little while,” said Mr. Thornton, his voice vibrating with the harsh, percussive consonants of the North. “Remember we are of a different race from the Greeks, to whom beauty was everything, and to whom Mr. Bell might speak of a life of leisure and serene enjoyment, much of which entered in through their outward senses. I belong to Teutonic blood. We retain much of their language; we retain more of their spirit; we do not look upon life as a time for enjoyment, but as a time for action and exertion. Our glory and our beauty arise out of our outward strength, which makes us victorious over material resistance. We hate to have laws made for us at a distance. We wish people would allow us to right ourselves, instead of continually meddling with their imperfect, centralized legislation.”

“In short, you would like the Heptarchy back again,” Bell chuckled. “You are regular worshippers of Thor.”

“If we do not reverence the past as you do in Oxford, it is because we want a lexicon which can apply to the present more directly,” Thornton argued, defending the muscular, working dialect of his life against the erosion of cultural memory. “It is fine when the study of the past leads to a prophecy of the future. But to men groping in new circumstances, the words of experience must direct us how to act in what concerns us most intimately.”

Thornton then mentioned the recent strikes, an acoustic reality of industrial disruption he was finding to his cost, noting with grim irony that the last one had been "respectable."

“A respectable strike!” said Mr. Bell. “That sounds as if you were far gone in the worship of Thor.”

Margaret felt the auditory dissonance in the room escalating. She tried to repair the fractured atmosphere by forcing a shift in the conversation, attempting to reintroduce a tactile, sensory element. “Edith says she finds the printed calicoes in Corfu better and cheaper than in London.”

“Does she?” said her father. “I think that must be one of Edith’s exaggerations.”

“Then I am sure of the fact,” said Mr. Bell lightly. “Margaret, I go so far in my idea of your truthfulness, that it shall cover your cousin’s character. I don’t believe a cousin of yours could exaggerate.”

“Is Miss Hale so remarkable for truth?” said Mr. Thornton bitterly. 

The moment the words tore through the quiet room, he could have bitten his tongue out. It was an acoustic violation, a violent imposition of his own internal poison into the shared space. He was possessed by ill-humor, irritated by the mention of a more successful lover, his palate soured by a jealousy he could not articulate. Margaret did not rise or flee as she once might have. After a momentary glance of grieved, dilated surprise—like a child encountering a sudden, deafening noise—her eyes fell to her lap. She bent over her heavy sewing, her round, taper fingers flying in and out of the fabric as steadily as if the domestic routine were the only anchor holding her against the tide of his cruelty. 

Thornton watched a sigh tremble over her body. He gave short, sharp answers to the others, his mind entirely consumed by the desperate, untranslatable need for a single look from her, before which he might prostrate himself in penitent humility. But the language of his remorse was dead on his lips. When the stifling silence became unbearable, he abruptly left the room, stepping out into the cold night to let the harsh, rhythmic roar of the Milton mills drown out his fierce experience of love—a sharp pang he resolved to bury until he emerged into the serenity of middle age.

As the three prepared for bed, Mr. Bell folded his napkin, muttering a condemnation of Thornton's inability to digest a simple jest. “Formerly, he was as simple and noble as the open day; you could not offend him, because he had no vanity.”

“He is not vain now,” Margaret said with quiet distinctness, her arms languid from the emotional fatigue. “To-night he has not been like himself. Something must have annoyed him before he came here.”

After she left the room, Mr. Bell turned to Mr. Hale. “Hale! did it ever strike you that Thornton and your daughter have what the French call a tendresse for each other?”

“Never!” Mr. Hale said, flurried. “If there is anything, it is all on Mr. Thornton’s side... why, Margaret would never think of him, I’m sure.”

“Entering her heart would do,” Bell mused, though he soon dismissed his untimely fancies for sleep.

The next day, before his departure, Mr. Bell urged Hale to leave the industrial noise of Milton. He proposed a return to a quiet Oxford parsonage—a sanctuary where they could tend to the old rhythms, where Margaret could be the village Lady Bountiful, and where the sonic environment would be cleansed of the commercialized soot that was choking them. 

“Never,” Mr. Hale said decidedly. “Here I stay out my life; and here will I be buried, and lost in the crowd.”

So they fell back into the circular, quiet monotony of their winter lives. The Higginses receded from immediate crisis, though Margaret went often to see Mary Higgins and the Boucher orphans. Nicholas Higgins himself had steadied into a sober, regulated method of thinking, returning to the agrarian reliability of his forebears. During these months, his rich Darkshire dialect—a stubborn monument against language death—became a bridge to an unlikely visitor. 

“To tell the truth,” Higgins told Mr. Hale one evening, “he fairly bamboozles me. He’s two chaps... Sometimes he says a rough thing or two, which is not agreeable to look at at first, but has a queer smack o’ truth in it when yo’ come to chew it. He’ll be coming to-night, I reckon, about them childer’s schooling.”

Margaret, hearing this, immediately checked her watch. The approaching auditory footprint of Mr. Thornton was more than she could bear. “It is nearly seven. Come, papa.” She hurried him away, only breathing freely when they were at a safe distance from the house. 

Indeed, Thornton came very seldom to the Hales now, even abandoning his Greek literature—the ancient language fading away, replaced by hurried notes canceling their lessons. Mr. Hale was depressed by the cessation of an intercourse that had become so dear to him, sitting in the quiet of his study, pondering the untranslatable reasons for the change.

He startled Margaret one evening, breaking the hushed acoustic peace of the room as she sat at her work. 

“Margaret! had you ever any reason for thinking that Mr. Thornton cared for you?”

He almost blushed as the words left his mouth, a sudden resurgence of Mr. Bell’s scouted idea. Margaret did not answer immediately. In the deep, resonant silence of the room, her head drooped, bending beneath a weight of subjective sorrow that no dominant lexicon could ever fully articulate. 

“Yes; I believe—oh, papa, I should have told you.” She dropped her work, hiding her face in her hands.

“No, dear; don’t think that I am impertinently curious... Did he speak to you about it?”

There was no answer at first; only the distant, mythic pulse of the town beyond the walls. Then, by-and-by, a little gentle, reluctant, “Yes.”

“And you refused him?”
