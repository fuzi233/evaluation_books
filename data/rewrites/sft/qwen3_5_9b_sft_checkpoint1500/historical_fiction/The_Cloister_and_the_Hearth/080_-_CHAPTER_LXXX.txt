For over a year, Brother Clement had labored in Basel, weaving his voice through the markets and chapels, when one day Jerome appeared. Dust clung to his robes, but his eyes burned with a triumphant light. "Glory be to God, Brother Clement," Jerome declared, "for now you may journey with me to England."

"I am prepared, Brother Jerome," Clement replied, his tone steady and warm. "In the quiet hours between my lessons and prayers, I have devoted myself to mastering the English tongue."

"A wise foresight," Jerome acknowledged. He explained that his delay had stemmed from securing special papal authority to gather funds for the English Church and to administer sacraments within the secular monasteries—a privilege denied to ordinary friars. "Now, gird up your loins," Jerome urged, "and let us strike a mighty blow for the Faith, and specifically against the Franciscans."

Thus, the two brothers descended the Rhine, a mobile beacon of Dominican order. In the great cities, they shared the single pulpit; in the lesser towns, they split apart, preaching from opposite banks before converging at a pre-arranged meeting point. Though both were men of considerable eloquence, their styles diverged sharply. Jerome spoke with noble gravity, his delivery impressive yet occasionally stiff, his topics somewhat narrow and his cadence monotonous.

Clement, however, possessed a fluidity that seemed to breathe life into his words. His speech was rich with variation, often dropping effortlessly into the vernacular of the common folk. Like a gentle current, his narrative curled around the listener's mind and heart, drawing them in. Yet, when his thoughts ascended to higher spiritual planes, Clement soared with a fervor that eclipsed Jerome. On such occasions, he became a tempest of prophecy—vehement as a storm, roused like a torrent, inflamed like lightning, thundering and flashing, sweeping away all resistance with the wild surges of his rhetoric.

Jerome, ever the pragmatist, believed it unnecessary to detail every nuance of Clement's genius here; suffice it to say that Clement had a habit of secretly investigating the local vices of every town before addressing them. Jerome, conversely, held to a rigid principle: he refused to tailor his message to specific sins unless they were matters of universal doctrine.

At a bustling city some leagues from the Rhine, they preached consecutively from the same platform. Jerome attacked the sin of vain attire, a subject dear to his heart. Satirical and sharp, he moved the crowd with a complacent ease, for vanity was not a prevalent vice among them.

Clement, however, chose a different path. He targeted drunkenness, a scourge that plagued the region and even infected the clergy themselves, shielded by deep-seated popular prejudice. Clement attacked the vice mercilessly, wielding Scripture and stark reality alike. He painted a grim picture: a recent parricide committed by a man lost to liquor, and a band of drunkards who had roasted one of their own comrades alive. He recalled the death of their last prince, misdiagnosed as apoplexy but truly the result of excess, and the miserable end of an alderman who had suffocated in a puddle while drunk.

"You feed your families with poison," Clement thundered, his voice rising. "You make your children bare-backed so you may gorge on beasts, leaving your souls to rot in hell. If your women drank as you do, this town—and indeed this nation—would sink beneath the mud of humanity. How long shall they hold back, defying nature and the examples of their husbands?"

The hall erupted. The medieval audience, unaccustomed to polite indifference, reacted with visceral horror. A shout of disbelief rose from the crowd. "It strikes home!" cried Clement, seizing the moment. "Do you imagine it shocks the All-Pure God less to see a man, His noblest creation, reduced to a swine, than to see a woman, pure and innocent, degraded to your level?"

He concluded with two vivid tableaux: the chaotic ruin of a drunkard's household versus the peace of a sober man's home. The descriptions were so precise and dramatic that the women in the gallery fell silent, overcome by tears and gasps of recognition. "Oh, that is true!" they whispered. "That is exactly what happens."

The effect was immediate and explosive. The sermon sparked a riot. Men gathered in knots, indignant and defensive. Their wives, sensing the danger to their honor, openly sided with the men, flattering their husbands and turning against the preacher. A husband, they argued, needed a drop to endure the labor of providing for his family. They refused to trade their sturdy, if flawed, mates for "milksops."

Yet, the reaction proved a double-edged sword. That same night, a group of men intercepted Clement, not to harm him, but to honor him. They filled his bag with coins, plucked fresh roses from their own heads to offer him, kissed his robe, and blessed him for undertaking to reform their "sots."

Jerome watched the scene with a furrowed brow. To him, the sermon felt too grounded, too earthly. "Drunkenness is not a heresy, Clement," he remarked quietly later. "Why preach an entire sermon against it?"

As their journey continued, Jerome found himself increasingly perplexed. He realized with growing surprise that Clement's words sank deeper into the hearts of the people than his own. While Jerome opened minds, Clement seemed to unlock them, making listeners weep, reflect, and occasionally repent. "He possesses the art of finding the peg upon which to hang his tale," Jerome mused, watching the crowds sway with emotion. "Yet, he can soar high enough when the spirit demands it."

On the whole, the situation troubled Jerome, who harbored a secret sense of superiority over his softer brother. After traveling nearly two hundred miles, this sentiment began to grate upon him, though he struggled to suppress it as petty and unworthy. "Souls differ like locks," he reasoned to himself, trying to reconcile the disparity. "Preachers must differ like keys; otherwise, the Church remains shut tight to God's entry. And certainly, this novice holds the key to these northern souls, for he himself is a man of the North."

Slowly, they drifted further down the Rhine, alternating between sailing a few miles downstream and walking the banks, preaching and hearing confessions in the scattered towns and villages. Eventually, they reached Düsseldorf.

They stepped onto the small quay where Gerard and Denys had once boarded their vessel upstream. The familiar streets stretched out before them, leading inevitably to "The Silver Lion." Everything remained unchanged—the stones, the buildings, the air—but Jerome himself was altered. He walked through the town barefoot, his heart a fortress of cold calm, his hands clasped tightly across his chest, his eyes bowed meekly to the earth. He was, in appearance and spirit, a true son of Saint Dominic and the Holy Church, standing apart from the world he sought to save.
