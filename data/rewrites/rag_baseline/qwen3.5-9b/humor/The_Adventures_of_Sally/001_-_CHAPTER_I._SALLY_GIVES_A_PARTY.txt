The air in Mrs. Meecher's boarding house tasted of stale tobacco and the specific, cloying sweetness of cheap perfume. Sally looked down the long table, her expression one of manufactured contentment. The initial tension—the brittle, white-waistcoated silence that had gripped the room since her brother Fillmore arrived—had evaporated. The patrons, transient souls and permanent residents alike, had returned to their natural state of noisy indifference.

Conversation swirled around the central axis of the evening: Sally's inheritance. To possess capital is one thing; to command the expenditure of another's capital is a different, intoxicating game. With rumors placing the sum at a dizzying height, the guests treated the budgeting of Sally's future with the casual brutality of gamblers planning a heist.

"Let me tell you," said Augustus Bartlett, cutting through the chatter with the sharp, clipped rhythm of a man who breathes stock prices. He sat low in his chair, a subordinate in Kahn, Morris and Brown who somehow projected the aura of a board member. "If I were you, I'd sink two hundred thousand into a safe bond issue. We just released one you should consider. Play with the rest. Have a flutter on anything promising. Multiple Steel is looking good; they say it'll hit one hundred and fifty by Saturday."

Elsa Doland, the girl with the large, dark eyes sitting to Bartlett's left, shook her head. "Buy a theatre, Sally. Put on good stuff."

"And lose every bean you've got," countered a mild young man with a deep, resonant voice. "If I had a few hundred thousand, I'd bet it all on Benny Whistler for the heavyweight title. Private intelligence suggests Battling Tuke has been bought out and plans to fold in the seventh..."

"Listen," another voice cut in. "Tell me what you'd do with four hundred thousand..."

"If I had four hundred thousand," Elsa Doland interjected smoothly, "the first thing I'd do is pay my bill from last week, which is due this morning."

Sally sprang up, moving with the fluid grace of a dancer correcting a partner. She looped her arm around Elsa's shoulder, whispering urgently. "Elsa darling, are you actually broke? If you are, I'll—"

Elsa laughed, a bright, clear sound that shattered the pretense. "You're an angel, Sally. No one compares to you. You'd give your last penny to anyone. But I'm not broke. I just returned from the road and saved a fortune. I said that only to draw you out."

Relieved, Sally sank back into her seat. The room had crystallized into factions. On one side, the prudent, led by Augustus Bartlett, advocated for three hundred thousand in Liberty Bonds and the remainder in safe real estate. On the other, the sporting minority, buoyed by the mild young man's insider whispers, had already allocated funds to Benny Whistler, distributing the bets cautiously to avoid spooking the market. The confidence in Mr. Tuke's reputation was absolute among the initiated; three to one odds were deemed achievable provided one acted with cunning and patience.

To Sally, this collective delusion was intolerable.

"I don't know where you're getting your figures," she said, her voice cutting through the hum. "They're wrong. I have twenty-five thousand dollars."

The statement hung in the air, a sudden drop in pressure. To these jugglers of half-millions, twenty-five thousand was negligible, a rounding error suitable for carfare. They adjusted their mental gears with a visible click, recalibrating their ambitions to fit the new, smaller scale.

"If I had twenty-five thousand," Augustus Bartlett rallied, recovering from the shock, "I'd buy Amalgamated..."

"If I had twenty-five thousand..." Elsa Doland began.

"If I'd had twenty-five thousand in the year nineteen hundred," observed a gloomy man with spectacles, staring into the middle distance, "I could have started a revolution in Paraguay."

He brooded on the phantom possibilities of a past that never existed.

"Well, I'll tell you exactly what I'm doing," Sally announced, her voice gaining strength. "Europe. Specifically France. As soon as I secure my passport, I'll loaf there for a few weeks. Then I'll return, scout for a cozy little business, invest, and live in luxury. Any objections?"

"Even a couple of thousand on Benny Whistler..." the mild young man persisted.

"I don't want your Benny Whistler," Sally snapped. "I wouldn't have him if you gave him to me. If I intend to lose money, I'll go to Monte Carlo and do it properly."

"Monte Carlo!" The gloomy man perked up at the magic syllables. "I was in Monte Carlo in '97. If I'd had another fifty dollars... just fifty... I'd have..."

At the far end of the table, a cough broke the spell, followed by the harsh scrape of a chair leg. Slowly, with the effortless dignity of an actor who knows his lines, Mr. Maxwell Faucitt, the boarding house's oldest inhabitant, rose.

"Ladies," Mr. Faucitt said, bowing with practiced courtesy, "...and gentlemen." He stopped bowing, lifting his white eyebrows to cast a silencing glare at the restless males of the younger generation. "I feel unable to let this occasion pass without speaking."

The audience showed no surprise. Life in New York was a generator of incidents, but Mr. Faucitt was a constant variable. Since the beginning of the meal, they had accepted that expecting him to remain silent on the night of Sally Nicholas's farewell dinner was madness. Braced for the performance, and warmed by the generosity of the feast, they settled to listen with feigned equanimity. Even the Marvellous Murphys, the new arrivals performing acrobatics in the Bushwick district, halted their preparations to heckle. Both lissome young men admired Sally immensely, and their admiration was not misplaced.

Sally was a small, trim creature, a wisp of a girl with tiny hands and feet, a smile that radiated warmth, and a dimple that danced in the curve of her chin. Her eyes, bright hazel, vanished whenever she laughed—a frequent occurrence. Her hair was a soft mass of brown. She possessed an air of distinction that the majority of Mrs. Meecher's guests lacked. She carried youth like a banner. The Murphys' approval was a testament to their high critical standards.

"I have been asked," Mr. Faucitt continued, smoothing his coat, "though I am aware that others here are far more worthy of such a task—Brutus-like figures compared to whom I, like Marc Antony, am no orator—I have been asked to propose the health of our charming hostess (applause), coupled with the name of her brother, our old friend Fillmore Nicholas."

The gentleman in question, seated at the speaker's end, nodded briefly. It was a nod of condescension, the gesture of a man surrounded by social inferiors trying desperately to appear benevolent. Sally saw it instantly. She debated, for a fleeting second, the utility of hurling an orange at her brother's glistening shirt-front. One lay ready in her hand; the target was perfect. But she held back. If a hostess yielded to primitive impulses, chaos ensued. She had just suppressed the rebellious Murphys; starting a fruit fight, even with noble motives, would weaken her moral authority.

She leaned back, sighing. The temptation had been fierce. She despised pomposity; it grated against her democratic soul. Yet she loved him, and she could not ignore the transformation. Affluence had not improved Fillmore Nicholas; it had calcified him. He regarded himself as a Man of Destiny. Conversing with him felt like receiving an audience from a standoffish monarch. It had taken her an hour to convince him to leave his Riverside Drive apartment and return to the boarding house. He had arrived in faultless evening dress, making the rest of the party resemble a gathering of tramp cyclists. His white waistcoat alone was a silent indictment of honest poverty, creating an awkward stiffness throughout the soup and fish courses. Those who had known Fill as an impecunious student, stretching a tweed suit beyond its limits and accepting small loans, now eyed the waistcoat with dumbfounded shame.

"Speaking," Mr. Faucitt intoned, "as an Englishman—for though I have long since acquired my papers, I first visited this great country as a subject of the island kingdom—I may say that the two factors in American life which have always made the deepest impression upon me are the lavishness of American hospitality and the charm of the American girl. Tonight we have witnessed the American girl as hostess. I commit myself to the statement that this has been a night none of us present will ever forget. Miss Nicholas has given us a banquet. I repeat, a banquet. There has been alcoholic refreshment. I do not know its source; I do not ask how it was procured, but we have had it. Miss Nicholas..."

Mr. Faucitt paused to puff at his cigar. Fillmore suppressed a yawn and checked his watch. Sally leaned forward, rapt. She knew how much the old gentleman relished delivering a formal speech. Though she wished the subject were different, she was prepared to listen indefinitely.

"Miss Nicholas," Mr. Faucitt resumed, lowering his cigar, "...But why," he demanded abruptly, "do I call her Miss Nicholas?"

"Because it's her name," hazarded the taller Murphy.
