In or near the Temple Garden

Fashion had long ago abandoned the verdant, charming Temple Garden. As Shakespeare imagined it, York and Lancaster once plucked innocent white and red roses there, which later became the emblems of their bloody wars; and the learned author of the *Handbook of London* informs us that "the commonest and hardiest kind of rose has long ceased to put forth a bud" in that smoky atmosphere. Few of the current residents of the surrounding buildings bother to notice whether roses still grow there or bloom past the ancient gate, save perhaps when hurrying to chambers. The attorneys' clerks certainly do not carry bouquets in their bags or posies tucked under their arms as they dash to the lawyers' offices—the handful of lawyers who take constitutional walks hardly ponder York and Lancaster anymore, especially since the railway business is concluded. Only antiquarians and literary dilettantes linger with genuine interest, conjuring up Sir Roger de Coverley and the sharp-faced Mr. Spectator pacing the path, or the beloved Oliver Goldsmith in the summer-house, perhaps contemplating his next 'Citizen of the World,' his new suit being fitted by Mr. Filby, or the collection letters from Mr. Newbery.

Treading heavily on the gravel, rolling majestically along in a snuff-colored suit and a wig desperately lacking the barber's powder and irons, one observes the Great Doctor approaching. Behind him follows his Scottish lackey, slightly tipsy from port wine consumed at the Mitre. Mr. Johnson invites Mr. Goldsmith to return home for a dish of tea with Miss Williams. Oh, kind fantasy! Sir Roger and Mr. Spectator feel as vivid to us now as the two physicians and the intoxicated, loyal Scotsman. These poetic figures inhabit our memory just as surely as the real characters. Since young Mr. Arthur Pendennis possessed a romantic and literary bent, entirely unaccustomed to the drab legal pursuits prevalent in the neighborhood, we may safely assume he was indulging in similar poetic reveries when, on the evening following the events of the last chapter, he selected the Temple Gardens for exercise and meditation.

On a Sunday evening, the Temple is typically tranquil. The chambers are mostly empty: the senior lawyers are hosting grand dinners in the Belgravia or Tyburn districts; the promising young barristers are absent, attending these parties or paying respects to Mr. Kewsy's excellent claret or Mr. Justice Ermine's accomplished daughters. Those not invited are enjoying the economical joint and modest half-pint of wine at the Club, amusing themselves and their fellows with circuit jokes and legal wit. No one occupies a chamber except poor Mr. Cockle, who is ill and subsisting on gruel prepared by his laundress; or Mr. Toodle, a flute enthusiast, whose solo piping drifts from his second-floor lodgings; or young Tiger, the student, whose open window belches clouds of cigar smoke, surrounded by discarded plates and covers marked with the insignia of Dicks' or the Cock. But wait! Where does Fancy lead us? It is vacation time, and with the sole exception of Pendennis, no one inhabits Chambers.

Perhaps it was solitude that drove Pen into the garden. Though he had never previously passed the gate, nor truly admired the neat flower beds or the groups of contented citizens strolling the trim lawns and broad gravel paths by the river, on this particular evening, after dining alone at a nearby tavern, the young gentleman took a whim to stroll through the gardens, breathe the fresh air, and gaze upon the gleaming Thames. After wandering briefly and observing various peaceful, happy clusters around him, weariness overtook him. He retired to one of the summer-houses flanking the ends of the main walk and sat down modestly. What were his thoughts? The evening was delightfully bright and still; the sky was cloudless; the chimneys on the opposite bank remained silent; the wharf warehouses glowed rosily in the sunlight, clear as if freshly scrubbed for the holiday. Steamers raced rapidly up and down the stream, loaded with holidaymakers. The bells of the countless city churches rang for evening prayers—such serene Sabbaths as Pen might have recalled in his youth, pacing with his arm around his mother's waist on the terrace before the lawn at home. The sun illuminated the little village of Brawl as well as the broad Thames, sinking majestically behind the Clavering elms and the tower of the familiar parish church. Were these thoughts, or merely the sunset, causing the flush on the young man's face? He tapped time on the bench to the choir of bells outside, flicked dust from his polished boots with his pocket-handkerchief, then sprang up, stamped his foot, and declared, "No, by Jove, I'll go home." With this resolve—which indicated an internal struggle regarding the propriety of staying or leaving—he stepped out of the summer-house.

He nearly collided with two small children who, standing no taller than his knees, trotted along the gravel path, casting long blue shadows toward the east. One cried out "Oh!" while the other burst into laughter, uttering a knowing infantile chuckle, "Missa Pendennis!" Arthur looked down and beheld his two little friends of the previous day, Mesdemoiselles Ameliar-Ann and Betsy-Jane. He blushed furiously at the sight. Seizing the child he had nearly knocked over, he lifted her into the air and kissed her. At this sudden assault, Ameliar-Ann began to weep in great alarm.

Her cry instantly summoned two ladies dressed in crisp collars, fresh ribbons, and magnificent shawls: Mrs. Bolton, adorned in a rich scarlet Caledonian Cashmere and a black silk gown, and Miss F. Bolton, wearing a yellow scarf and a sweet sprigged muslin, holding a parasol—truly the lady. Fanny uttered not a single word; however, her eyes flashed a welcome, shining as brightly as the most blazing windows in Paper Buildings. Yet Mrs. Bolton, after scolding Betsy-Jane, exclaimed, "Lor, sir—how very odd that we should meet you, yer! I ope you ave yer health well, sir.—Ain't it odd, Fanny, that we should meet Mr. Pendennis?"

"What do you mean by sniggering, Mesdames?" Arthur retorted. "When young Croesus stayed at a country house, have you never, by some singular coincidence, been walking with your Fanny in the shrubberies? Have you and your Fanny never happened to be listening to the heavies' band at Brighton when young De Boots and Captain Padmore clinked down the Pier? Have you and your darling Frances never chanced to visit old Widow Wheezy at the cottage on the common when the young curate arrived with a tract suitable for rheumatism? Do you suppose that if singular coincidences occur at the Hall, they don't also happen at the Lodge?"

"It was a coincidence, no doubt: that was all," Mrs. Bolton conceded. During the conversation the prior day, Mr. Pendennis had simply stated, in the simplest terms possible, in response to Miss Bolton's inquiry, that while some courts were gloomy, parts of the Temple were very cheerful and agreeable, particularly the chambers overlooking the river and surrounding the gardens, noting that the gardens made for a delightful Sunday evening promenade frequented by many. And here, by mere chance, all their acquaintances had reunited, just like so many folks in polite society. What could be more artless, good-natured, or natural?

Pen appeared extremely grave, pompous, and dandified. His attire was unusually smart and brilliant: white duck trousers, a white hat, a multi-colored neckcloth, a light waistcoat, gold chains, and shirt studs gave him the air of a prince of the blood. How splendidly his magnificence suited his figure! "Was anybody ever like him?" someone thought. "He blushed—how becomingly his blushes became him!" the same individual murmured. The children, having seen him the day before, had been so struck by him that after he departed, they played at being him. Ameliar-Ann, sticking her little chubby fingers into the armholes of her pinafore as Pen used to do with his waistcoat, had declared, "Now, Bessy-Jane, I'll be Missa Pendennis." Fanny had laughed until she cried, burying her sister in kisses for that feat. How joyful she seemed to see Arthur embrace the child!

If Arthur was crimson, Fanny, conversely, was very wan and pale. Arthur noticed and kindly inquired why she looked so fatigued.

"I was awake all night," Fanny replied, beginning to blush slightly.

"I put out her candle and ordered her to go to sleep and cease reading," the affectionate mother interjected.

"You were reading! And what captivated you so?" Pen asked, amused.

"Oh, it's so beautiful!" Fanny sighed. "'Walter Lorraine.' How I do hate that Neaera—I don't know the pronunciation. And I love Leonora, and Walter, oh, how dear he is!"

How had Fanny discovered the novel *Walter Lorraine*, and that Pen was the author? This little soul remembered every word Mr. Pendennis had spoken the night before, recalling how he wrote in books and newspapers. Which books? So eager to know, she almost considered being civil to old Bows, who suffered under her displeasure since yesterday, but resolved first to apply to Costigan.
