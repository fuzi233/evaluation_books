"At the court of the Tuileries," remarked Mr. Sillerton Jackson, his face lighting up with a nostalgic smile, "such matters were quite openly tolerated."

They stood in the imposing black walnut dining room of the van der Luydens on Madison Avenue. It was the evening following Newland Archer's visit to the Museum of Art. Mr. and Mrs. van der Luyden had recently arrived in the city from Skuytercliff, having fled precipitously upon news of Beaufort's financial collapse. Society had been advised that the current disarray demanded their visible presence; indeed, as Mrs. Archer had noted, they "owed it to society" to attend the Opera and even to open their own doors.

"It would never do, my dear Louisa, to allow people like Mrs. Lemuel Struthers to believe they can step into Regina's shoes," Mrs. van der Luyden explained, her tone sharp with social anxiety. "It is precisely at moments like these that newcomers push in and take root. Recall the winter of the chicken-pox epidemic; that was how Mrs. Struthers first gained a foothold—married men slipping away to her house while their wives tended the nursery. You and dear Henry, Louisa, must stand in the breach as you always have."

Reluctantly, yet heroically, the couple had answered the call. They had unveiled their home and issued invitations for two dinners and an evening reception. Tonight, they had extended an invitation to Mr. Jackson, Mrs. Archer, and Newland and his wife to accompany them to the Opera, where *Faust* was making its debut performance of the season.

Nothing occurred beneath the van der Luyden roof without strict ceremony. Although only four guests attended, dinner commenced promptly at seven, ensuring the proper sequence of courses could be served without haste before the gentlemen retired to their cigars. Archer had not seen his wife since the previous evening. He had departed early for the office, burying himself in a mountain of trivial business. Later, a senior partner had made an unscheduled demand on his time, leaving him to reach home so late that May had preceded him to the van der Luydens' residence and dispatched the carriage.

Now, viewed across the Skuytercliff carnations and heavy platters, May appeared pale and languid. Yet her eyes sparkled, and she spoke with exaggerated animation. The topic that inspired Mr. Jackson's favorite analogy had been introduced, Archer suspected not without design, by their hostess. The Beaufort scandal—or rather, Beaufort's demeanor following the disaster—remained a fertile ground for drawing-room morality. After the incident had been thoroughly dissected and condemned, Mrs. van der Luyden turned her scrutinizing gaze upon May Archer.

"Is it possible, dear, that what I hear is true?" she asked. "I was informed that Grandmother Mingott's carriage was seen standing at Mrs. Beaufort's door."

Notably, she no longer addressed the offending lady by her first name. May's cheeks flushed, and Mrs. Archer interjected hurriedly, "If it was, I am convinced it was there without Mrs. Mingott's knowledge."

"Oh, you think—" Mrs. van der Luyden paused, sighing, and glanced at her husband.

"I fear," Mr. van der Luyden offered, "that Madame Olenska's kind heart may have led her into the imprudence of calling on Mrs. Beaufort."

"Or perhaps her taste for peculiar individuals," Mrs. Archer added dryly, casting an innocent look toward her son.

"I am sorry to think it of Madame Olenska," Mrs. van der Luyden murmured. Mrs. Archer replied softly, "Ah, my dear—and after you had hosted her twice at Skuytercliff!"

It was then that Mr. Jackson seized the opportunity to deploy his preferred comparison. Seeing the expectant eyes of the company turn to him, he repeated, "At the Tuileries," he continued, "the standards were excessively lax in certain regards. And if you had asked where Morny's fortune originated—or who bore the debts of some of the Court beauties..."

"I hope, dear Sillerton," Mrs. Archer said gently, "you are not suggesting we should adopt such standards?"

"I never suggest," Mr. Jackson returned, perfectly impassive. "But Madame Olenska's foreign upbringing may render her less particular—"

"Ah," the two elder ladies sighed in unison.

"Still, to keep Grandmother's carriage at a defaulter's door!" Mr. van der Luyden protested. Archer surmised that he was recalling, with resentment, the hampers of carnations he had sent to the modest dwelling on Twenty-third Street.

"Of course, I have always maintained that she views things quite differently," Mrs. Archer summarized.

A flush rose to May's forehead. She looked across the table at her husband and spoke abruptly, "I am certain Ellen meant it kindly."

"Imprudent people are often kind," Mrs. Archer observed, as if the observation offered scant mitigation. Mrs. van der Luyden murmured, "If only she had consulted someone—"

"Ah, that she never did!" Mrs. Archer countered.

At this juncture, Mr. van der Luyden glanced at his wife, who bowed her head slightly toward Mrs. Archer. The shimmering trains of the three ladies swept out the door while the gentlemen settled down to their cigars. Mr. van der Luyden provided short ones for opera nights; they were so superior that they caused his guests to lament his relentless punctuality.

After the first act, Archer detached himself from the group and made his way to the rear of the club box. From there, peering over the shoulders of various Chiverses, Mingotts, and Rushworths, he watched the same scene he had observed two years prior on the night of his first encounter with Ellen Olenska. He had half-expected her to appear again in Old Mrs. Mingott's box, but it remained empty. He sat motionless, his eyes fixed upon it, until suddenly Madame Nilsson's pure soprano erupted into "_M'ama, non m'ama..._"

Archer turned to the stage. Within the familiar setting of giant roses and pen-wiper pansies, the same large blonde heroine succumbed to the same small brown suitor. His gaze drifted from the stage to the apex of the horseshoe-shaped seating area, where May sat between two older ladies. Just as on that former evening, she was dressed entirely in white. Archer, who had not initially noticed her attire, recognized the blue-white satin and antique lace of her wedding gown.

In old New York, it was customary for brides to wear this costly garment during the first year or two of marriage. His mother, he knew, preserved hers in tissue paper, hoping Janey might one day wear it, although poor Janey was approaching an age when pearl-gray poplin and the absence of bridesmaids would be deemed far more appropriate.

It struck Archer that May, since their return from Europe, had rarely worn her bridal satin. The sight of her in it prompted him to compare her present appearance with the young girl he had watched with such blissful anticipation two years ago. While May's silhouette was slightly fuller, as her godlike build had predicted, her athletic erectness of carriage and the girlish transparency of her expression remained unchanged. Save for the slight languor Archer had recently perceived in her, she was the exact image of the girl playing with the bouquet of lilies-of-the-valley on her betrothal evening.

This resemblance seemed an additional appeal to his pity; such innocence was as moving as the trusting grasp of a child. Then he remembered the passionate generosity latent beneath her indifferent calm. He recalled her look of understanding when he had urged that their engagement be announced at the Beaufort ball; he heard the voice in which she had declared in the Mission garden, "I couldn't have my happiness made out of a wrong—a wrong to someone else." An uncontrollable longing seized him to confess the truth, to throw himself upon her generosity, and to ask for the freedom he had once denied.

Newland Archer was a quiet, self-controlled young man. Conformity to the discipline of a small society had become almost his second nature. It was deeply distasteful to him to engage in anything melodramatic or conspicuous, anything Mr. van der Luyden would have deprecated and the club box would condemn as bad form. Yet, he had suddenly become unconscious of the box, of Mr. van der Luyden, of everything that had so long enclosed him within the warm shelter of habit.

He walked along the semi-circular passage behind the house and opened the door of Mrs. van der Luyden's box as if it were a gateway to the unknown.

"_M'ama!_" triumphed the singing Marguerite. The occupants of the box looked up in surprise at Archer's entrance. He had already broken one of the unwritten rules of his world, which forbade entering a box during a solo. Slipping between Mr. van der Luyden and Sillerton Jackson, he leaned over his wife.

"I've got a beastly headache; don't tell anyone, but come home, won't you?" he whispered.

May gave him a glance of comprehension. She whispered to his mother, who nodded sympathetically, then murmured an excuse to Mrs. van der Luyden and rose from her seat just as Marguerite fell into Faust's arms.
