Oleron understood, with a cold clarity that settled deep in his bones, what Elsie had meant when she said her next visit would come with a postcard. She hadn’t been hinting at convenience—she’d been warning him. And now, at last, he knew. He knew—and it was too late. The knowledge sat like a stone in his chest, heavy and unyielding. He didn’t want her. Not truly. Not the way she wanted to be wanted.

So when she appeared again within days—unannounced, knocking sharply on the door—he felt only a sickening pang of pity. From the landing, she called down, voice clipped and brittle: “I won’t stay long. Just came to tell you about an inquiry for short stories. You might want to apply.” Her words were polite, rehearsed. But there was no warmth in them. No trace of the woman who once laughed beside him in the garden, or whose hand he once held without thought.

He thanked her. He pressed her gently, but she refused even to step inside. Only after he insisted did she allow herself into the hallway, her face pale, her eyes darting around as if afraid the walls might swallow her whole.

Then, just as quickly, she turned to leave.

“Wait,” he said, though he didn’t mean to. “Let me see you out.”

She nodded, not looking at him. They descended the stairs together, silence thick between them. Halfway down, a sharp crack echoed through the house—a sound like wood splitting under pressure. Elsie gasped, then choked back a cry.

Oleron froze.

He knew the staircase was old. He knew it creaked. But he’d walked it countless times without incident. Yet now—now, beneath Elsie’s foot—the tread gave way. A jagged hole opened in the floorboards, and her boot tore through, sending splinters flying.

“Oh God—my poor girl!” he breathed, rushing forward.

She laughed—not a real laugh, but something broken, high-pitched, edged with hysteria. “It’s my weight… I know I’m getting fat…”

“Don’t move,” he snapped, hands trembling as he knelt. “Let me get this clear.”

But she wouldn’t stop laughing. Sobbing through it. “I’m too much… too heavy for everything… I’m not wanted…”

The words struck him like a blow. He looked up, heart pounding. “Elsie—”

“No,” she whispered, shaking her head violently. “Not that. Not *you*. Don’t say it. Not now. Please—just let me go.”

Her voice cracked. “I didn’t want to come. I tried—I really tried. But I couldn’t keep away. I *had* to see you one more time…”

Oleron felt the world tilt. He reached for her, but she pulled back, arms crossed tight over her chest.

“I’m not wanted,” she repeated, voice thin and raw. “Let me take what’s left of me and go.”

“Elsie—” he began, voice breaking. “You’re dear to me. You always have been.”

She flinched as if struck. “No. Not that. Not less than that. Don’t offer me half. Don’t make me feel like I’m begging. Let me keep my pride.”

He stared at her, helpless. He could offer nothing. Because he didn’t love her. Not in the way she needed. Not in the way that mattered.

“Let me get my coat,” he muttered. “Let me take you to a doctor.”

“No,” she said firmly. “And don’t touch me. I’ll be fine.”

She refused even his arm. As they reached the bottom, she smiled faintly, painfully. “Sorry I broke your stairs, Paul. You’ll look into those stories, won’t you?”

He groaned. “If you won’t go to a doctor, at least let Mrs. Barrett look at you. Look—there she is now.”

Barrett, tall and thin-faced, stood at the edge of the alley, staring up. But as Oleron opened his mouth to call, the man turned and vanished without a word.

Elsie didn’t wait. She stepped out into the street, shoulders hunched, and walked quickly away.

Oleron watched until she passed the “To Let” boards—those grim, hatchet-shaped signs that seemed to hang like tombstones over the doorway. He feared even they might fall upon her, crush her, end her.

That night, he did not eat.

He wandered from room to room, bare feet silent on the worn floorboards. His mind replayed her words: *"I'm not wanted... don't offer me anything less... let me take away what's left of me..."*

Each phrase echoed like a curse.

Poor Elsie. So fragile. So broken.

He thought of her face—pale, swollen with tears, drawn by pain. If tears were weapons, hers were self-inflicted. Weapons turned inward. And the thought came, unwanted: suicide.

Women had done such things before. Drowned themselves in dark rivers. Hung themselves from hooks on doors. Left behind only whispers and unanswered questions.

And then—what would happen? An inquest. Questions. “How did she get this wound on her hand? This abrasion on her ankle?” Barrett would testify: “I saw her leaving the house.” And Oleron—Oleron would be called to identify her body.

He shuddered.

He forced himself to stop. These thoughts were madness. Morbid. Unfair.

Yet still, the fear clung to him.

He sat in the dimming light, fire low, shadows stretching like fingers across the walls. He listened—to the creaks, the tickings, the soft raps from the panelling. The house was alive. Always had been.

And then—something else.

A sound.

Soft. Silent almost. Like silk brushing against silk. A slow, sweeping rustle, with a tiny, almost imperceptible crackle beneath it.

He frowned.

He’d heard it before. Earlier. When he’d first sat down.

But now—it came again.

Closer.

More insistent.

He stiffened.

His breath caught.

And then—like a switch flipping—he sprang to his feet, chair overturning, iron poker clattering to the hearth.

He turned slowly, hand reaching behind him, searching the air.

There was nothing.

But the sound—oh, the sound—was unmistakable.

Only one thing made that noise.

A woman brushing her hair.

He stood frozen, heart hammering against his ribs.

“God in Heaven,” he whispered.

The sound stopped.

For a moment—silence.

Then—scuttling. A mouse. He swallowed hard. It wasn’t that. That wasn’t what had made his blood freeze.

But the other sound—gone.

Still—his skin crawled.

He called out. “Who’s there? What is it?”

No answer.

Then—another scuttle. A mouse. He sank to his knees, trembling. He couldn’t breathe.

He couldn’t speak.

He moved toward the mantelpiece, keeping close to the wall, hands outstretched. Matches. There must be matches.

He found the box. It fell to the hearth.

He groped, fingers numb, until he cornered it in the fender.

One match. Struck. Flame flickered. Then died.

Second try. Lighter. The candle on the table—yes. He lit it.

The flame steadied. Grew bright.

He looked around.

Nothing.

The room was empty.

Just as it had always been.

But it wasn’t.

Because he had *heard* it.

He had *felt* it.

That soft, whispering brush of hair—long, wet, slow—moving through the air where no one stood.

And now—now he knew.

The house was not alone.

It had never been.

Something lived here.

Something that remembered.

Something that brushed its hair in the dark.

And it had been waiting.

Waiting for him.

Waiting for her.

Waiting—for someone to finally listen.
