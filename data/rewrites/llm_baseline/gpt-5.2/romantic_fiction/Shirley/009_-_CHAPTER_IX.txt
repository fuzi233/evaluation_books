Messrs. Helstone and Sykes met Mr. Moore again with a kind of hearty raillery, as if the whole morning’s sharp passage with the men had been but a bout at cudgels pleasantly concluded. They congratulated him—on firmness, on spirit, on the good fortune of being the man to say No when a crowd required Yes.

He listened as one listens to wind that does not cool. His face had the look of a still, dark day—no beam, no breeze; and Helstone, who could read an eye as readily as he could read a line of print, checked his own geniality as if it were an ill-timed joke over a dish left untouched. He buttoned up his compliments with his coat, turned to Sykes, and, with that decisive civility which is dismissal in a fine voice, said—

“Come, sir; your road and mine lie partly together. Had we not better bear each other company? We’ll bid Moore good-morning, and leave him to the happy fancies he seems disposed to indulge.”

“And where is Sugden?” Moore demanded, lifting his gaze at last, as if the name were a necessary ingredient in the day’s reckoning.

“Ah, ha!” cried Helstone, pleased to be useful. “I’ve not been quite idle while you were busy. I’ve been helping you a little; I flatter myself not injudiciously. I thought it better not to lose time; so, while you were parleying with that down-looking gentleman—Farren I think his name is—I opened the back window, shouted to Murgatroyd in the stable to bring Mr. Sykes’s gig round; then I smuggled Sugden and brother Moses—wooden leg and all—through the aperture, and saw them mount the gig (always with our good friend Sykes’s permission, of course). Sugden took the reins—he drives like Jehu—and in another quarter of an hour Barraclough will be safe in Stilbro’ jail.”

“Very good; thank you,” said Moore, in the same low, even tone, as if thanks were a coin he paid when due, not when he felt rich. “Good-morning, gentlemen.”

He conducted them to the door with punctilious politeness, and watched them clear his premises; then he turned back as a man might turn from a bright road into a passage where no sun falls.

All the rest of the day he remained taciturn and serious. He did not so much as bandy a repartee with Joe Scott. Joe, for his part, spoke to his master only what business demanded; yet his eyes kept sliding, sidelong and watchful, like a cook’s glance from the hearth to a guest who will not eat. He came more than once to poke the counting-house fire—raising and shifting the coals till they burned with a redder heart; and, when the mill was locked for the evening (work going but short time now, trade slack as a loaf that will not rise), he ventured, as if offering a simple restorative—

“It’s a grand evening, sir. I could wish you’d take a bit of a walk up th’ Hollow. It would do you good.”

At that, Moore gave a short laugh—sharp, unseasonable, like steel struck in a quiet room. He demanded of Joe what all this solicitude meant, and whether he took him for a woman or a child; then he snatched the keys from Joe’s hand and thrust him by the shoulders towards the door.

“Out of my way.”

Joe obeyed; but Moore called him back before he reached the yard-gate, as if some thought, buried all day under work and pride, had suddenly moved and knocked from within.

“Joe—do you know those Farrens? They are not well off, I suppose?”

“They cannot be well off, sir, when they’ve not had work as a three month. Ye’d see yoursel’ ‘at William’s sorely changed—fair paired. They’ve selled most o’ t’ stuff out o’ th’ house.”

“He was not a bad workman?”

“Ye never had a better, sir, sin’ ye began trade.”

“And decent people—the whole family?”

“Niver dacenter. Th’ wife’s a raight cant body, and as clean—ye mught eat your porridge off th’ house floor. They’re sorely comed down. I wish William could get a job as gardener or summat i’ that way; he understands gardening weel. He once lived wi’ a Scotchman that tached him the mysteries o’ that craft, as they say.”

Moore’s mouth tightened, not in anger, but in a restraint that was worse than anger—like hunger forced to sit still at a table spread for others.

“Now, then, you can go, Joe. You need not stand there staring at me.”

“Ye’ve no orders to give, sir?”

“None, but for you to take yourself off.”

Joe went.

Spring evenings can be treacherous. The day had been fine—warm even in the morning and in the noon sunshine; yet at sunset the air chilled as if some unseen door had opened to a cold pantry. The ground crisped; and before dusk a hoar frost crept, soft-footed, over grass and bud, laying its pale dust on what had dared to unfold. It whitened the pavement before Briarmains—Mr. Yorke’s house—doing its silent mischief among tender plants in his garden and on the mossy level of his lawn. The great tree by the gable nearest the road, strong in trunk and broad in arm, seemed to defy such a night; its boughs were bare still, and frost found little to bite. Behind, the grove of walnut-trees rose tall and leafless, stern as old tenants who have seen many seasons and do not flatter any.

It was dusk, moonless, though starry. Lights shone vividly from windows. The scene was neither dark nor lonely—nor even silent. Briarmains stood near the highway. It was an old place, built before that highway was cut, when a lane winding through fields had been the only approach. Briarfield lay scarce a mile off; its hum came on the wind, its glare was plain. Briar Chapel—a large, new, raw Wesleyan building—rose within a hundred yards; and a prayer-meeting was in full tumult inside, the chapel windows casting bright reflections on the road while the sound poured out: hymns that seemed less sung than shouted, as if zeal had become a kind of music and noise had been mistaken for spirit.

The words came in snatches between clamorous prayers and fearful groans—odd fragments, like crumbs from a table at which the diners ate too fast to taste.

“Oh! who can explain  
This struggle for life,  
This travail and pain,  
This trembling and strife?  
Plague, earthquake, and famine,  
And tumult and war,  
The wonderful coming  
Of Jesus declare!”

Then more: fierce images, hammered out on lungs already hoarse.

“For every fight  
Is dreadful and loud:  
The warrior’s delight  
Is slaughter and blood,  
His foes overturning,  
Till all shall expire:  
And this is with burning,  
And fuel, and fire!”

A pause—if pause it could be called—filled with groans and ejaculations. Then a cry of triumph rang from within: “I’ve found liberty!” “Doad o’ Bill’s has fun’ liberty!” and out broke the assembly again, louder, quicker, as if the building itself were an instrument played too hard.

“What a mercy is this! What a heaven of bliss!  
How unspeakably happy am I!  
Gathered into the fold,  
With Thy people enrolled,  
With Thy people to live and to die!”

And again, with the same buoyant ease of those who will not linger to savour one tune before leaping to the next:

“Oh, the goodness of God  
In employing a clod  
His tribute of glory to raise;  
His standard to bear,  
And with triumph declare  
His unspeakable riches of grace!”

They shouted of fire and flame; they clapped their hands amidst imagined burning; and the night-air carried it all, an acoustic storm rolling across hedge and stone. The roof did not fly off—which speaks volumes for the chapel’s solid slating; but the ears of any quiet creature within hearing might well have wished for the mercy of thick walls and deep rooms.

Briarmains was alive too—though with a different life. In some lower windows, candles glimmered behind curtains. Voices and laughter came muffled and domestic, not eruptive. We may pass the front door and find the heart of the house.

No company made it lively. There was none within save Mr. Yorke’s own family, assembled in their usual evening room—the back parlour, farthest to the right.

By day the windows would show stained glass: purple and amber predominating, glittering around grave medallions—Shakespeare’s suave head in one, Milton’s serene in another. On the walls hung Canadian views—green forest and blue water—between which blazed a painted night-eruption of Vesuvius, ardent and terrible, set against the cool foam and azure of cataracts and the shadowy depths of woods.

And on the hearth burned a fire such as a southerner seldom sees in a private room: a clear, hot coal fire, heaped high in an ample chimney. Mr. Yorke would have such heat even in summer, as if he believed warmth to be a necessary seasoning to thought. He sat beside it with a book in hand and a small round stand at his elbow bearing a candle; but he did not read. His eyes were on his children.

Opposite sat his wife. She might be described at length; yet one feels the description would be like setting down the measurements of a cloud. She was large, of gravest aspect; care sat on her brow and shoulders, but it was not the care that falls like necessity—it was the care that some take up as a duty and wear, exemplary, as a cloak. Mrs. Yorke held it profane to be mirthful and frivolous to be cheerful. She drew no distinctions. Yet she was a good wife, a careful mother, sincerely attached to her husband. Only, had she been able, she would have permitted him no friend but herself. His relations were insupportable to her; she kept them at arm’s length.

And still the pair agreed—perhaps because Mr. Yorke had a shadowy side as well as a sunny one, and his shadow found sympathy in his wife’s constant overcast. She was strong-minded, never weak, never trite; sternly democratic in view, cynically plain in estimate of human nature; sure of her own correctness and doubtful of everyone else’s. Her chief fault was suspicion—eternal, brooding, immitigable suspicion of men, creeds, parties, things; a mist before her eyes, a false guide at every turning.

Their children, then, were not likely to be commonplace. There were six.

The youngest was a baby on the mother’s knee—still all her own. That one she had not yet begun to doubt, suspect, condemn. It clung to her, drew its life from her, loved her with the whole blind fidelity of infancy; and because she was sure of that love, she returned it.

The two next were girls—Rose and Jessy—both now at their father’s knee. They seldom approached their mother except when duty drove them, as children are sometimes driven to bitter medicine. Rose, twelve years old, was like her father the most of all: a granite head copied in ivory. Yorke’s face was harsh; his daughter’s was softened in line and colour. It was not quite pretty; it was simple, childlike in feature; the round cheeks bloomed. But the grey eyes were not childlike—there lived in them a serious soul, young yet, but destined to mature if the body held. It held, too, something neither parent possessed in equal measure—purity, aspiration, a strength that might one day outgrow both father’s temper and mother’s gloom. Rose was still, sometimes stubborn. Her mother would have made of her a woman of dark and dreary duties; Rose had a mind thick-sown with ideas her mother had never known. To have those ideas trampled and repressed was agony. She had never rebelled; but if hard driven, she would one day rebel once for all. She loved her father; he did not rule her with iron. He was good to her, and sometimes feared she would not live—so keen and bright were the sparks that at moments flashed from her glance and lit her speech. That fear made him tender.

Jessy—little Jessy—was gay and chattering, arch, original; passionate when provoked, most affectionate when caressed; by turns gentle and rattling; exacting yet generous; fearless—of her mother, for instance, whose hard rule she had more than once defied—yet trusting to any who would help her. With her piquant face and winning ways she was made to be a pet; and her father’s pet she was. It was odd that she resembled her mother feature for feature, as Rose resembled her father, and yet the physiognomy—how different in expression, in promise.

If, in that warm back parlour with its coal-fire and stained glass, some magic mirror had been held up—if it had shown those two girls twenty years hence, you might have turned away from the picture as from a dish too bitter to finish.

First, Jessy: not here, not in England. Cypress and willow and yew; stone crosses; dim garlands of everlasting flowers. A gray marble headstone in green sod. Jessy sleeps below. She lived through an April day—much loved, much loving. Tears came often in her brief life, and sorrows; yet between she smiled and brightened whatever saw her. Her death was tranquil and happy in Rose’s guardian arms. They were alone then—two English girls watching and dying in a foreign country—and the soil of that country took Jessy’s small body and made it a grave.

And Rose, two years later: still farther. The crosses and garlands looked strange; but stranger still were the hills and woods. This was remote from England—some region of the southern hemisphere, wild and luxuriant, a virgin solitude. Unknown birds fluttered along the forest skirts. No European river ran there, and yet Rose sat by its bank thinking—quiet Yorkshire Rose, made lonely emigrant. Would she ever come back? The mirror shut, as mirrors do, when one most longs to press questions upon them.

The three eldest were boys—Matthew, Mark, and Martin—seated together in a corner, engaged in a game; and the noise of their play rose and fell like the clatter of knives on a board when impatient hands prepare a meal too quickly.

Their heads were alike at first glance, different at second, contrasted at third. Dark hair, dark eyes, red cheeks, small English features; each bore a blended resemblance to both parents; and yet each had his own stamp.

Matthew, the first-born, was no plain boy. Jet-black hair, white brow, high-coloured cheek, quick dark eyes—these were good points. And yet, as you looked, his face suggested—sinisterly and again and again—one object in the room more than any other: that painted eruption of Vesuvius. Flame and shadow seemed the elements of his soul—no daylight, no sunshine, no cool moonbeam. Cross him in the game and see the scowl. Mr. Yorke saw it and lowered his voice, pleading, “Mark and Martin, don’t anger your brother.”

This was the rule always. Both parents decried partiality in theory; no rights of primogeniture were to be allowed in their house. Yet Matthew was never to be vexed, never to be opposed. They averted provocation from him as they would avert fire from a barrel of gunpowder. “Concede, conciliate,” was their motto wherever he was concerned. Their republican household was fast making a tyrant of its own blood. The younger boys felt it, though they could not read the motive; they saw only injustice. Dragon’s teeth were sown among those olive-branches; discord would one day be the harvest.

Mark was bonny, the most regular-featured. He was calm—so calm his smile could be shrewd and his driest, cutting sayings came in the quietest tone. Yet a heavy brow spoke temper; smooth water is not always safe. He was too still to be happy. Life would bring little joy to Mark. At five-and-twenty he would wonder why people laughed, and think all fools who seemed merry. Poetry—whether in books or living—would sound to him rant and jargon. Enthusiasm he would despise. Though his body was fourteen, his soul was already thirty.

Martin, youngest of the three, promised another fate: brief or long, his life would be brilliant. He was not handsome now; there was a husk upon him, a dry shell he would wear till near twenty, then cast it off. He would make himself handsome. Uncouth manners and perhaps homely garments would cling to him for a time; but the chrysalis held its power of transfiguration, and in due season the butterfly would come. For a space he would be vain, eager for pleasure and admiration, athirst too for knowledge. He would drink deep at both fountains—enjoyment and lore. What then? The seer could not say. Martin might be remarkable; whether he would be, no open vision told.

Taken together, the six young heads held an unusual store of mental power, originality, vigour. Divided among half a dozen commonplace broods, it would have made each child above average. Mr. Yorke knew it and was proud. Yorkshire has such families among hill and wold—racy, vigorous, strong in brain and blood; turbulent in pride, intractable in native force; wanting polish, wanting docility, but sound and spirited and true-bred as eagle on cliff or steed on steppe.

A low tap came at the parlour door. The boys had been noisy over their game; and Jessy, besides, had been singing—sweetly—a Scotch song to her father. Mr. Yorke delighted in Scotch and Italian airs, and had taught his musical little daughter the best; so the ring at the outer door had gone unheard.

“Come in,” said Mrs. Yorke, in that conscientiously constrained voice of hers—solemn as if she were pronouncing sentence, though she might be but ordering pudding or calling children to sew.

And in came Robert Moore.

His habitual gravity, and his abstemiousness—for when he visited, no case of spirit decanters was ever ordered up—had so far recommended him to Mrs. Yorke that she had not yet made him the subject of private animadversion with her husband. She had not yet discovered that he was hampered by any secret intrigue, nor labelled him wolf in sheep’s clothing—discoveries she had made early in marriage about most of Mr. Yorke’s bachelor friends, and had thereafter excluded them from her board with a severity that had, perhaps, both harshness and sense.

“Well, is it you?” she said as he approached and offered his hand. “What are you roving about at this time of night for? You should be at home.”

“Can a single man be said to have a home, madam?” he asked.

“Pooh!” returned Mrs. Yorke, who despised conventional smoothness as much as her husband did, and practised it as little. “Pooh! you need not talk nonsense to me; a single man can have a home if he likes. Pray, does not your sister make a home for you?”

“Not she,” put in Mr. Yorke. “Hortense is an honest lass. But when I was Robert’s age I had five or six sisters, all as decent and proper as she is; but you see, Hesther, for all that it did not hinder me from looking out for a wife.”

“And sorely he has repented marrying me,” added Mrs. Yorke, who liked at times to crack a dry jest against matrimony, even at her own cost. “He has repented it in sackcloth and ashes, Robert Moore, as you may well believe when you see his punishment”—and she pointed to the children. “Who would burden themselves with such a set of great, rough lads as those, if they could help it? It is not only bringing them into the world, though that is bad enough, but they are all to feed, to clothe, to rear, to settle in life. Young sir, when you feel tempted to marry, think of our four sons and two daughters, and look twice before you leap.”

“I am not tempted now, at any rate,” said Moore. “I think these are not times for marrying or giving in marriage.”

This lugubrious sentiment obtained Mrs. Yorke’s approbation. She nodded and groaned assent, as if gloom were a broth that nourished her. Then, after a moment, she added—

“I make little account of the wisdom of a Solomon of your age; it will be upset by the first fancy that crosses you. Meantime, sit down, sir. You can talk, I suppose, as well sitting as standing?”

It was her way of inviting him to a chair. He had scarcely obeyed when little Jessy slipped from her father’s knee and ran straight into Mr. Moore’s arms. He held them out promptly, and lifted her lightly to his own knee, as if she were something warm and dear he did not fear to touch.

“You talk of marrying him,” she said to her mother, indignantly, with a child’s quick jealousy over what she counts her own, “and he is married now, or as good. He promised that I should be his wife last summer, the first time he saw me in my new white frock and blue sash. Didn’t he, father?” (These children were not accustomed to say papa and mamma; their mother allowed no such “namby-pamby.”)
