On a picturesque common near Tunbridge Wells, Lady Clavering had secured a charming villa, retreating there to escape the marital storms of the previous London season. Naturally, Miss Amory accompanied her mother, while Master Clavering returned for his holidays. For Blanche, the young schoolboy's presence was primarily an opportunity for fighting and quarreling—a delightful domestic pastime, though one the lad himself preferred to avoid. Instead, he discovered cricket, horses, and a legion of friends at Tunbridge.

The "good-natured Begum's" household had transformed into a chaotic sanctuary for a constant swarm of thirteen-year-old gentlemen. These youths consumed tarts and champagne with alarming frequency, raced across the lawns, terrified the fond mother, smoked until they felt ill, and generally rendered the dining room unbearable for Miss Blanche. She harbored no affection for this specific breed of young gentleman. Conversely, any change was inherently pleasant to the fair young creature; for a week or two, she would have traded her current comforts for poverty, a cottage, bread and cheese, and perhaps even a night in a dungeon with watered-down bread. Consequently, the move to Tunbridge was far from unwelcome to her.

Blanche wandered the woods sketching trees and farmhouses, devoured French novels, and frequently drove into Tunbridge Wells to attend plays, balls, conjurers, or musicians wherever they appeared. She slept excessively, quarreled with her mother and Frank during the mornings, attended the local village school with gusto—first coquettishly fondling the girls and thwarting the mistress, then scolding them and mocking the teacher—and remained dutifully present at church.

It was a lovely, ancient little church, an Anglo-Norman jewel seemingly constructed the day before yesterday, adorned with painted windows, carved saints' heads, gilded scripture texts, and open pews. Blanche immediately set about creating a perfectly correct high-church altar cover for it. To the clergyman, she soon passed for a saint. She charmed, coaxed, wheedled, and fondled him with such artful manipulation that poor Mrs. Smirke—initially captivated, then resigned, and finally barely speaking to her—was driven nearly mad with jealousy.

Mrs. Smirke was the wife of our old friend Smirke, Pen's former tutor and the suitor of poor Helen. Having consoled himself for Helen's rejection with a young lady from Clapham provided by his mother, his views hardened daily upon her death. He cut off his coat collar, allowed his hair to cascade over his shoulders, abandoned the curl atop his forehead and the necktie he once prized, opting instead for no tie whatsoever. He skipped Friday dinners, read the Roman Hours, and announced his readiness to hear confessions in the vestry.

The most harmless creature imaginable, he was denounced by Muffin of the Dissenting chapel and Mr. Simeon Knight of the old church as a black, dangerous Jesuit and Papist. Smirke had built his chapel-of-ease using the inheritance left by his mother in Clapham. Heaven help us if she heard him refer to a table as an altar, place candlesticks upon it, or sign letters on the Feast of Saint So-and-so! Yet, these were precisely the practices of the boy from Clapham, faithfully emulated by his equally zealous wife.

When Blanche held a conference lasting nearly two hours in the vestry with Mr. Smirke, Belinda paced anxiously on the grass, where only two small gravestones stood. She wished desperately for a third, fearing that her husband would soon offer himself to the creature who had infatuated him within a fortnight. No, she decided; she would retreat, enter a convent, make her profession, and leave him. Such dark thoughts plagued Smirke's wife and neighbors, who suspected him of direct correspondence with the Bishop of Rome and bewailing errors even more odious than hers. Yet, our friend meant no earthly harm. The post office never delivered a single letter from the Pope. Initially, he considered Blanche the most pious, gifted, and fascinating person he had ever met; her singing of the Chants delighted him. But gradually, he grew weary of Miss Amory. Her ways and graces seemed to lose their luster; doubt crept in. Then, she caused a disturbance in his school, lost her temper, and tapped the children's fingers.

Blanche possessed a peculiar power to inspire both admiration and satiety in many men. She attempted to please them, deploying all her charms simultaneously—jewels, smiles, cajolery, coaxing, and ogles. Once exhausted by the effort and realizing she never truly cared for them, she dropped them. In turn, the men grew tired of her and discarded her as well.

It was a blissful night for Belinda when Blanche departed. Her husband, blushing slightly and sighing, admitted he had been deceived. He claimed he thought her endowed with precious gifts, fearing they were merely tinsel; that she had been a right-thinking person, whereas she had simply made religion an amusement. He noted she had indeed lost her temper toward the schoolmistress and beaten Polly Rucker's knuckles cruelly. Belinda rushed into his arms; questions of graves and veils vanished instantly. He embraced her tenderly on the forehead. "There is none like thee, my Belinda," he declared, casting his fine eyes upward to the ceiling, "precious among women!"

As for Blanche, from the moment she lost sight of him and Belinda, she ceased to think or care about either of them. However, when Arthur descended to spend a few days at Tunbridge Wells with the Begum, this stage of indifference had not yet arrived for Miss Blanche or the simple clergyman. Smirke still regarded her as an angelic wonder. He had never witnessed such perfection; he sat listening to her music in summer evenings, mouth agape, rapt in wonder, forgetting tea and bread-and-butter entirely. Although he had once attended an opera exhibition (a memory he recalled with a blush and a sigh, recounting the day he accompanied Helen and her son to the play at Chatteris), he could conceive nothing more delicious or celestial than Miss Amory's music.

She was, in his eyes, a most gifted being with a precious soul, remarkable talents, and a heavenly disposition. Thus, while burning with his own fever and bewitchment for Blanche, Smirke discoursed to Arthur about her. Their reunion had been cordial. Arthur loved anyone who loved his mother, and Smirke could speak on that theme with genuine emotion. They exchanged hundreds of anecdotes about their respective lives.

"Arthur will perceive," Smirke remarked, "that your views on Church matters have developed significantly since our acquaintance." Mrs. Smirke, a model of exemplarity, seconded him with all her efforts. He explained that he had built the little church following his mother's demise, who had left him sufficient worldly means. Though living in a sort of cloister, he had heard of Arthur's reputation. Speaking in the kindest, most sorrowful tones, with lowered eyelids and a bowed head, he expressed his sentiments.

Arthur was immensely amused by him—by his airs, follies, simplicity, blank stocks, and long hair; by his true goodness, kindness, and friendly feelings. His praises of Blanche pleased and surprised our friend greatly, causing him to regard her with eyes of particular favor. Indeed, Blanche was very glad to see Arthur. Like anyone else, she rejoiced to encounter an agreeable man in the countryside who brought fresh news and stories from the great city, capable of speaking that darling London jargon so essential to Londoners and so little understood by provincials.

The first day Pen arrived, he kept Blanche laughing for hours after dinner. She sang her songs with renewed spirit, stopped scolding her mother, and instead fondled and kissed her to the honest Begum's surprise. At bedtime, she exclaimed, "Deja!" with the prettiest air of regret, genuinely sorry to sleep, and squeezing Arthur's hand affectionately. He pressed her palm warmly in return.

Our young gentleman was of a turn where moderately bright eyes could dazzle him. "She has improved remarkably," Pen thought, gazing out into the night. "Very much. I suppose the Begum won't mind my smoking with the window open. She's a jolly good old woman, and Blanche is immensely improved. I liked her manner with her mother tonight. I enjoyed her laughing way with that stupid young cub of a boy, whom they shouldn't allow to get tipsy. She sang those little verses very prettily; they were devilish pretty verses too, though I say it who shouldn't." He hummed a tune Blanche had composed for some verses of his own.

"Ah! What a fine night! How jolly a cigar is at night! How pretty that little Saxon church looks in the moonlight! I wonder what old Warrington's doing? Yes, she's a devilish nice little thing, as my uncle says."

"Oh, heavenly!"

Suddenly, a voice broke from a clematis-covered casement nearby—a girl's voice, the author of *Mes Larmes*. Pen burst into laughter.

"Don't tell about my smoking," he called out, leaning from his own window.
