CHAPTER XXXVIII. There are those who believe the truest tragedy lies not in Prometheus bound *after* he has stolen fire from the gods, but *before*—when he is already gripped by the invisible chains of circumstance, his soul aflame with purpose yet unable to act, trapped in a solitude where no one sees his suffering or hears his cry. Such men are not merely ignored; they are buried beneath the weight of poverty, illness, and silence, their dreams too fragile for the world’s notice. Yet there are others—few, but real—who do not merely dream, but *foresee*. Their minds shape visions so vivid they feel like prophecy: the deed they long to do appears whole and complete before it happens; the future they fear or desire rises up before them, rooted in countless impressions, growing like a seed in dark earth. These people are not less rational than others; often, they are more so—because their inner worlds are wider, more complex, like a city with many gates through which thoughts pour in from every direction. But as with all things, there are extremes: some such souls are diminished, broken, even grotesque—like a tiny creature that might fit into the palm of your hand. Still, what great idea or noble cause is free from its share of unworthy representatives? We dare not think how many claim the name “patriot” without honor, or how crowded heaven might be on Judgment Day with writers who brought their books—not just in hand, but on wagons.

This reflection is offered not as excuse, but as context—for Mordecai, whose presence had lodged itself in Daniel Deronda’s mind like a question waiting to be answered. But Deronda’s interest was only half-formed, a quiet curiosity. The man himself—thin, pale, with eyes that seemed too deep for his face—was a quiet craftsman, a scholar perhaps, living humbly like Spinoza once did. He did not match any image Deronda had held. Yet for Mordecai, the meeting was different. For years, as his body weakened and loneliness deepened, he had clung to one desperate hope: to find someone young, strong, and open-hearted enough to receive the life he could no longer live—to carry forward the wisdom, the passion, the very breath of his spirit. Not recovery—he had long abandoned that illusion—but transmission. His longing had grown into belief, then into faith—a faith sharpened, not dulled, by the certainty of his own dying.

He had spent years searching, not for a mirror of himself, but for someone *other*: a Jew, yes, but not like him—stronger, brighter, born into ease and grace. A man whose voice would flow freely, whose bearing would command respect, whose beauty would make the world see the greatness of their people. Mordecai had wandered through galleries, gazing at portraits of noble faces—those rare images of youth and strength, of sorrow that did not crumble into despair, but burned with quiet power. He had stood before one such painting, thin and worn in his fur-lined cap, unseen by others, perhaps mistaken for a beggar or a fool. But he knew the truth: that his ideas were mocked not only by prejudice, but by his own frailty—his voice, his body, his very appearance. And so he dreamed of another: not a second self, but a full life, vibrant and capable, ready to inherit all that was best in him.

His mind worked in images—so much so that his thoughts often resembled dreams: vivid, sudden, slipping from known to unknown. For years, he saw this imagined companion as a distant figure against a golden sky—dark and mysterious, moving toward him. Why golden? Because London, though grim, held moments of beauty. He loved the bridges at dawn, the river wide and calm, the city rising in dim grandeur, boats gliding into light. There, under the vast sky, his thoughts expanded, as if the air itself carried meaning. And so, when he pictured the one he waited for, the figure emerged against that luminous backdrop—no longer turned away, but advancing, face revealed: young, noble, Jewish, refined. A composite drawn from memory—faces seen in Holland, in Bohemia, from paintings that stirred his soul. It was not mere fantasy, but a spiritual need—like a child imagining love, but deeper, fiercer, shaped by loss and time.

And now, the urgency grew. Time was running out. Each breath was thinner. Was the moment near? Would the deliverer come? The one who would rescue his thoughts from oblivion, give them a place in the future of his people? Some might call it madness—this belief in a savior, this refusal to accept death as final. But Mordecai did not seek glory. He sought continuity. He wanted to be an agent, not just a witness. To love, not merely to observe. And while the sun still warms, there will always be those who say: *I am master of this moment—and I will fill it with my soul.*

Yet, despite his certainty, he did not wait passively. He tried, in small ways, to plant seeds. Two years earlier, he had moved into Ezra Cohen’s home—seen by the family as teacher, worker, oddity, saint, heretic. Little Jacob, now taller, quicker-witted, had grown close to him. Mordecai taught him English, arithmetic—yet something deeper passed between them. The boy, though unaware of it, became a vessel for Mordecai’s soul. At times, when work was done, Mordecai would invite Jacob to sit beside him, or let him jump onto his knees—small hands gripping worn limbs. Then, he would begin to recite a Hebrew poem, written long ago, pouring into it all the fire of his early dreams. “Repeat after me,” he’d say. “Let these words sink into you.”

Jacob, eager and clever, would mimic the sounds—though he didn’t understand a word. He’d roll his eyes, pull his cheeks, pinch noses—childish antics, but Mordecai endured them, breathing life into each syllable as if it were sacred. Once, the boy suddenly leapt away, landing on his hands, feet in the air, picking up a bright farthing from his pocket. Mordecai stopped mid-verse. A jolt went through him—as if the child had laughed at his prayer. “Child! Child!” he gasped, voice breaking. Jacob froze. Then, Mordecai leaned forward, seized the boy’s shoulders, whispering fiercely: “A curse upon your generation! They will dig up the mountain, tear down the golden wings, melt them into coins! They’ll break the solemn faces into earrings for wanton women! They’ll take a new name—but the angel of shame will know them, and their hearts will be tombs of dead desires!”

The words, spoken with such intensity, shook Jacob to his core. He stood trembling, convinced the world was ending. Then, as Mordecai relaxed, the fear turned to tears. Jacob wept openly, lifting his face like a little prophet in distress. Mordecai, softened by the child’s grief, drew him close, pressing his head to his chest. The danger passed. Jacob soon began to imitate the scene—turning it into play. The next day, he frightened Adelaide Rebekah in the same way, and succeeded well.

But Mordecai was shaken. He regretted the moment—the rawness, the madness of it. He was wise enough to know when he crossed the line. And so, he turned again to his hope—the coming friend, the one who would understand without needing proof. That was when he saw Daniel Deronda.

It was during his usual afternoon watch over the old bookshop. Deronda entered—tall, calm, with a face and frame that seemed to fulfill Mordecai’s vision. At first, the denial of Jewish birth struck him like a blow. But then, later, when Deronda sat at the Cohens’ table, the impression returned—stronger, confirmed by chance. Mordecai asked, “Do you know Hebrew?” He was already certain. But the answer—“No”—shattered everything. Again, he felt the weight of disappointment, like a shipwrecked man watching a distant sail vanish.

Yet the vision would not leave him. It came back—not as a ghost, but as a living presence. Deronda now appeared in his dreams, in his waking hours, against that golden sky—no longer distant, but near. The image had taken root. And though reason said no, emotion whispered yes. The longing had become a conviction. He felt the stirrings of something greater—something beyond words.

In January, the tension grew. He could no longer focus on teaching Jacob, or attending the weekly club. Something else was coming. He longed for the river—the wide sky, the bridges, the water that shimmered with life and sorrow. He needed the vastness, the breath of the world, to hold his spirit until the moment arrived.
