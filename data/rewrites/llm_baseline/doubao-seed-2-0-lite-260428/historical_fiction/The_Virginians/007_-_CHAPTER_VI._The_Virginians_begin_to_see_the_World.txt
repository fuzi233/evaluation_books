After their disgraced chaplain’s abrupt departure, Madam Esmond and her eldest son George appeared to have settled back into full, easy reconciliation—but the bitter quarrel between them weighed so heavily on the quiet boy’s mind that he fell into a raging, delirious fever soon after. For nights on end, the widow sat watch beside his bed, his thin frame tossing restlessly, his voice cracking out over and over in the dark: “Broken! Broken! It never, never can be mended!” Her usual stores of home remedies and folk nostrums, which she dispensed freely to every servant and tenant across Castlewood, did nothing to slow the fever’s advance.
Humility was forced upon her at last. One snowbound morning, her neighbors spotted her riding her pony through drifts up to the stirrups, arriving unannounced at the tiny cottage of Mr. Dempster, the little tutor she had once cast off in anger, to beg him to save her son. “I shall bury my resentment, madam, just as you have buried your pride,” the old scholar told her. “Please God, I may be in time to help my dear young pupil.” He packed his lancet and his small stock of medicines, called his only enslaved manservant after him, locked the door of his lonely hut, and rode back with her to Castlewood.
For three days, it looked certain that poor Harry would inherit the estate before the week was out—but Dempster’s steady care pulled George through. The fever broke, the intermittent chills faded, and when the boy was well enough to sit up for hours at a time, the doctor strongly prescribed a change of air, even a long sea voyage, to rebuild his shattered strength. The widow had long since quarreled with their Esmond kin in England, and was too contrite over her past hasty temper to write and beg for shelter there, so the party planned a grand journey north through the colonies instead.
The two young gentlemen, under Dempster’s charge, with two personal servants in attendance, boarded a sloop bound for New York, then sailed up the sun-dappled length of the Hudson to Albany, where the foremost gentry of the New York province welcomed them with open arms. From there they traveled north into French Canadian territory, bearing carefully sealed letters of introduction that opened every door of the seigneurial estates. Harry threw himself headlong into wilderness life: he camped for weeks at a time with local Indian bands, traded for furs, and tracked and shot bear deep in the pine forests. George, who cared nothing for rough field sports and still moved with the fragility of a man recovering from near death, became an immediate favorite of the French ladies. Few young English colonists spoke French half so fluently as he, and he perfected his accent until native Parisians at the Quebec court mistook him for one of their own. He carried himself with effortless, easy grace, danced the minuet to perfection, learned the newest Parisian court catches and arias, and played them beautifully on his violin—though he could not sing them, for his voice had just broken from treble to deep bass.
While Harry was away on a three-week bear hunt, George even fought an affair of honor with a hotheaded young ensign of the Auvergne regiment, the Chevalier de la Jabotiere, nicked him cleanly through the shoulder, and the two men swore a lifelong friendship immediately after the duel. Madame de Mouchy, the intendant’s wife, wrote a glowing, complimentary letter to Madam Esmond praising her eldest son’s courage and breeding, and the widow was so elated by the news that she gave no thought to how the pious preacher Whitfield would have frowned on her delight over her boy’s dueling prowess.
Ten months later, the lads rode back up Castlewood’s oak-lined drive, tanned, several inches taller, and so nearly identical that even their oldest servants could scarce tell them apart when their hair was powdered—though back in the rough Virginia countryside, they soon abandoned the fussy custom, George wearing his raven-black hair loose save for a simple ribbon, Harry his fair flaxen locks tied back the same way.
Any reader familiar with the boys’ characters will have marked that George Warrington was of a proud, jealous, slow-to-forgive disposition: generous, truthful, and gentle to a fault, he could never deliberately harm anyone, but he almost never let a slight or injury fade from his mind. He had left home nursing a deep, unspoken dislike for one of his mother’s closest friends, a gentleman whose name would one day become famous across the whole world, and ten months of travel had not softened his opinion an inch.
Mr. Washington, though barely twenty-one years old, looked and carried himself with a gravity that made him seem decades older. He had managed his own family’s tangled financial affairs from boyhood, and every magistrate and planter across the region trusted his judgment more than that of many men twice his age. Mrs. Mountain, Madam Esmond’s sharp-tongued, irrepressible companion, who doted on both boys even as she bickered daily with her mistress and threatened to quit Castlewood a dozen times a week, was an avid and wildly imaginative correspondent who sent the lads a steady stream of gossipy, amusing letters while they traveled. She was a born match-maker, convinced every unmarried man who crossed Castlewood’s threshold harbored designs on its wealthy widowed mistress. She was certain the odious Mr. Ward planned to court Madam Esmond, and doubly certain that the shrewd, ambitious young Major Washington was angling for her hand—never mind that he was fifteen years her junior. “Twas always the family way!” she cackled at George. “Her own mother was years older than Colonel Esmond when they wed, and she was so jealous she’d never let the poor man out of her sight! Great tall men like Mr. Washington, who stands six foot two, always marry tiny little women, and Madam’s scarce five feet tall even in her highest headdress and stacked heels! What could be plainer than that?”
She spilled every one of these absurd theories to George, who begged her for heaven’s sake to hold her tongue. She swore she would never breathe a word to Harry—Harry was his mother’s favorite, after all, and could not keep a secret to save his life. He would run straight to Madam, and there would be a screaming row that would get Mountain dismissed for good. George warned her in his most frigid, dignified tone that if she dared repeat such ridiculous suspicions to anyone else, they would quarrel too. Of course, Mountain could not hold her peace, and pointed out a hundred tiny seeming proofs to confirm her tale: Washington had his own permanent guest chamber at Castlewood, he left his portmanteau and spare riding clothes there for convenience, Madam never tired of singing his praises to her sons, and consulted him on every decision, from buying a new horse to selling a barrel of tobacco.
In response, George adopted a stance of stiff, formal courtesy towards his mother’s favorite, laced with sharp little sarcasms that stung the good-natured Washington, though he never complained, brushing them off as easily as he would brush brambles from his coat on a long hunt. While Harry rode and hunted side by side with Washington nearly every day, George retreated to his book room to pore over Latin, mathematics, and French texts, growing sullen and more isolated by the week.
When Harry was away across the county at a cockfight (to Madam Esmond’s great displeasure, his circle of sporting acquaintances was far from reputable), Washington arrived at Castlewood for a farewell visit. He was uncommonly tender to the mistress of the house, and she received him with such open, warm cordiality that George’s jealousy nearly boiled over into open confrontation—until he learned the major was bound on a long, brutal mission far west, to the very edge of the Virginia frontier and beyond.
For years, the French, holding colonies in Canada and Louisiana, had pushed steadily south and west, building a chain of forts to link their territories and block the British Atlantic colonies from expanding inland to the Mississippi. Though the two crowns had signed a formal peace treaty in 1748, the frontier dispute had never been settled, and by 1753, French troops were actively driving British settlers off their lands on the Ohio River. Virginia’s Lieutenant-Governor Dinwiddie resolved to send a formal envoy to the French commanding officer at Fort Le Boeuf, to demand they desist from all incursions on territory claimed by King George.
Young Washington jumped at the chance for distinction, volunteering for the dangerous post. He set out with a small party of guides, an interpreter, and a handful of attendants, following unmarked Indian trails 500 miles through frozen wilderness, navigating only by compass, camping in the snow through the dead of winter. He reached Fort Le Boeuf safely, only to receive a blunt refusal from the French commander: his orders were to hold every post, and drive every English settler out of the Ohio country by force. Washington made it back alive through every peril, his clothes frozen stiff, his horse dead under him, arriving back in Williamsburg a celebrated hero across the colony.
Harry raged that he had wasted his time at a stupid cockfight instead of getting to join such a noble adventure, and Madam Esmond held Washington up as the perfect example to both her sons, scolding George that all his book learning would never amount to half as much as the major’s service to his country. George retorted that he would be happy to sail for England to seek a royal commission, but he would never consent to serve under Washington in a ragtag provincial regiment, and Madam Esmond agreed hotly: an Esmond only served the king directly, and she would rather beg bread than ask Governor Dinwiddie for a favor.
Washington soon began recruiting a small provincial regiment to march west and drive the French out. But before his little force could reach the forks of the Ohio, a much larger French column overran the tiny half-built fort the advance party of Virginians had thrown up there, chased the 40-man garrison off without bloodshed, renamed the stronghold Fort Duquesne, and set up a permanent garrison. For months, no shot had been fired in open anger, even as the two sides eyed each other across the forest clearings. It was in that uncharted Pennsylvania wilderness that young Colonel Washington, marching west with his handful of raw recruits, stumbled on a small French scouting party and fired the first shot of a conflict that would stretch across three continents, cost France its entire North American empire, and eventually spark the revolution that would birth the great United States of America.
He had no idea then what history held in store for him. Outnumbered five to one by well-armed French regulars, he was forced to retreat to a crude, hastily built stockade he named Fort Necessity, and on July 4th, 1754, he surrendered his position and marched his half-starved, nearly ammunition-less men home in defeat.
Saddened, humbled, and sobered by the failure of his expedition, he soon presented himself at Castlewood. He held out his hand to George, admitting he had been arrogant before he left, had bragged too much of what he would accomplish, and had been hurt by George’s obvious scorn for his provincial regiment. George, his old jealousy melting in the face of the man’s obvious, unfeigned grief, took his hand warmly, telling him no fair person could blame him for the defeat. Then Madam Esmond stepped forward, curtsied deeply to the dejected young soldier, and told him no woman of her acquaintance would ever turn away a man who had done his best for his king and country. That one graceful, deferential curtsy rekindled every spark of George’s old jealous suspicion, burning hotter than ever as the chapter closed.
