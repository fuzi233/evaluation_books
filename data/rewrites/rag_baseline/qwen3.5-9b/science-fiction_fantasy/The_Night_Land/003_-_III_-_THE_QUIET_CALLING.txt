The air in the Tower of Observation tasted of recycled oxygen and the metallic tang of distant machinery. Below, the Great Redoubt sprawled like a fossilized insect, a cage built to keep the outside world out, but I knew the truth: the real danger was inside, in the silence between the gears.

For three years and two hundred and twenty-five days, I had walked the corridors of the Thousand Cities, visiting each once, a ritual of childhood training that stretched into adulthood. In every city, the same rumor hung heavy in the damp air: there was another refuge out there, in the desolation of the Night Lands, where the last millions of humanity hid to fight until the end. Some scholars in the libraries dismissed it as fiction, reading ancient records with skeptical eyes, treating the past as a puzzle to be solved rather than a truth to be lived. But I remembered differently. My memory was not a library of facts; it was a living archive of dreams.

My connection to this impossible truth began with Master Monstruwacan. We were friends, though he was a fully grown man and I was merely a boy when we met. When I turned twenty-one, he granted me a position in the Tower, the most coveted spot in the entire Redoubt. Why? Because of the Night-Hearing. It was a clumsy name for a rare gift, possessed by only one person in all the millions of the underground world. While others relied on recording instruments to catch the faintest tremors of the aether, I could hear the invisible vibrations directly. I could hear the messages coming through the eternal darkness, clearer than any machine could manage.

One afternoon, during the fifteen-hour Sleep-Time, I stood alone, contemplating a love that existed only in my mind. Mirdath the Beautiful. Her voice had sung in my memory-dreams for eons, a ghost of a woman from a lost age. As I communed with these phantom whispers, a thrill ran through me—a sensation distinct from the background hum of the tower. Out of the everlasting night, a whisper touched my subtile hearing.

Four years had passed since my awakening at seventeen. This time, the signal was different. I knew instantly what it was. Instead of shouting my name, which would invite chaos, I sent the Master-Word through the night, projecting it via my brain-elements. If it was truly Mirdath, she would hear it without instruments. If it was a trick of the Evil Forces or the House of Silence, they would fail to respond correctly.

The throb of the Master-Word circled my spirit, steady and undeniable. With a surge of emotion, I called out mentally: "Mirdath! Mirdath! Mirdath!"

At that exact moment, Master Monstruwacan entered the observation deck. He saw my face, pale and vibrating with tension, and fell silent. He did not possess the Night-Hearing, but he was wise enough to understand the gravity of the situation. He had just finished checking the receiving instruments, expecting nothing, yet he sensed the shift in the atmosphere.

"I heard it," I told him, recounting the dream, the longing, and the sudden contact. He listened with a troubled heart, his expression shifting from professional curiosity to genuine awe. In our age, sanity was defined by the ability to discuss the impossible with calm logic. To us, talking about the Days of Light and the Sun was not madness; it was science. To people in the future, our conversations would seem like the ravings of lunatics, yet we treated them as daily bread.

As I spoke, I listened with my spirit. Save for a faint, happy laughter wrapping around me, there was nothing else. Nothing more that day.

This pattern repeated itself. I argued with the learned men of the Redoubt, challenging their doubts about the old stories. They hungered for the tales, entranced by the romance of the Surface, even as their rational minds rejected the possibility. Master Monstruwacan was the exception. From the start, he believed. He understood. That belief earned him my loyalty.

Our discussions drew crowds. Sometimes, after hours of debate, I would drift back into the flow of my memories, and the entire staff would gather around me, forgetting their instruments and duties, captivated by the stories of the Earth-Current and the Gardens below. When Master Monstruwacan snapped back to the present, he would chide them gently, and they would scatter, looking guilty yet eager for more. Even the ploughboys in the Underground Fields, working a hundred miles beneath the Redoubt, knew the stories. They were the mightiest engineering feat of our world, a pyramid of land stretching a hundred miles deep, shielded by grey metal to protect the crops from the monsters outside. The Earth-Current lit the fields, nurturing the grain and poppies under a warm, artificial sun.

There was a law in the Pyramid: no one ventured into the Night Land before twenty-two. Men had to undergo lectures on mutilation and horror, proving their sanity and health. A capsule was implanted in the arm, a failsafe to release the spirit if trapped. It was a grim necessity, a reminder of the terror awaiting anyone who stepped out.

I had considered going. Twice, I sent the Master-Word solemnly into the void. But uncertainty lingered. Was it real? Or just a fading echo? So I settled for sending her name, "Mirdath," hoping for a response. Often, I thought I heard a faint thrumming, a weak answer from a spirit lacking strength, or perhaps a malfunctioning instrument. Anxiety replaced clarity.

Then, on a day marked by the thirteenth hour, the air changed. The beaten aether vibrated violently around me, disturbing the void itself. I signaled for silence. Every man in the tower froze, bowing over their breathing-bells to stop all noise.

Again came the gentle thrumming, breaking into a clear, low calling in my mind. It was my name—the old-earth name, not the designation used today. The sound struck me with the force of a fresh awakening, triggering terrified memories. Immediately, I sent the Master-Word back. The aether roared with activity. A silence followed, heavy and expectant, then a beat from afar, traveling through the void. Soon, the Master-Word throbbed with a sure, powerful rhythm.

Before this confirmation, I had suspected Mirdath; now, I knew.

"Mirdath," I said aloud, using the instruments to amplify the transmission.

Out of the darkness, a swift and beautiful reply emerged. She used the old love-name, a phrase only she had ever spoken to me.

I signaled the technicians to resume their logs; the records must remain intact. Standing nearby, Master Monstruwacan waited quietly, pen in hand, watching the others with a protective eye. For a brief, miraculous moment, I spoke across the abyss with a girl who knew my name, who knew the ghosts of my past, and who called herself Mirdath.
