I have already spoken of my feelings—now an old man—regarding that impetuous leap into marriage which I persuaded my dear wife to take with me when we were scarcely more than boys and girls. As a husband and father, with the weight of daily bills, the baker’s ledger, and a brood of spirited children who might at any moment decide to run off to Scotland, citing our own example as justification—I know I ought to approach this early chapter of George Warrington’s married life with caution. I should confess my sins, sit demurely in my armchair, and wear the white sheet of repentance, offering myself up as a warning to reckless youth.

Yet truth be told, that very marriage—about which my kin had prophesied so darkly—proved far kinder than they ever imagined. It had its trials, yes, but I recall them without bitterness. There were passionate sorrows, softened by time, which God has mercifully seen fit to console. There were days of true want, endured not in silence but with quiet dignity, to the astonishment of well-meaning relatives watching from afar. And then there were joys—so deep, so sacred—that I hesitate even to speak of them here, save with reverence and before One Ear alone, to whom all hearts turn in prayer and gratitude.

To marry without means is indeed unwise, perhaps even unjust to society. But how many thousands do it every year, trusting only in Heaven, health, and honest labour? Must young people wait until their cottage is fully furnished, their cellar stocked, their plate gleaming, and their strongbox brimming before they dare begin a home? If such rules governed all, the world would cease to grow. The gentlefolk tremble at the edge of poverty, clad in silk and fine shoes, waiting years for a bridge or a gilded barge to carry them across. The poor, barefoot, plunge straight into the stream, trusting their strength and fate to bear them through.

Who would wish his daughter into hardship? Who would urge his son to face the perils of a poor marriage—debt, sickness, loneliness, privation? I look at my own wife and feel shame for having placed such a burden upon one so tender-hearted. I remember her endurance, and I am grateful—for her love, her constancy, the grace with which she carried every trial. On the subject of marriage, I cannot claim impartiality: mine was rash, yet blessed beyond measure. So I will not counsel youth. I have known poverty, but never found it unbearable. Had I not known it, I could not have learned the kindness of friends, the sweetness of gratitude, nor the quiet pleasures that sometimes accompany a meagre meal and a small fire—the comfort that can warm even the longest day of toil. This much is certain: too often, the decent poor are pitied needlessly. Wealthy folk, stepping briefly into our narrow world, see shadows where we see light. They stumble over obstacles invisible to us. They marvel at our calm acceptance of thin beer and cold mutton—while we, though poor, ate with contentment and gave thanks.

My father-in-law, the good General, had married Molly when he was no more than a junior officer, his purse barely fuller than my own. Their lives had known both rise and fall. I suspect—though my wife denies it—they wedded young, without seeking parental leave. [The Editor searched Burn’s Registers of Fleet Marriages and found no record of Martin Lambert and Mary Benson.] Yet they were so pleased with their own union that they did not begrudge their children’s choices, nor fear the hardships we might face.

When I presented my financial state to Mr. Lambert, I may have exaggerated. Believing me to possess two thousand pounds, he left for Jamaica with ease, confident in his daughter’s future. After paying for the family’s journey, he returned little richer than I. My Theo’s dowry consisted of a few trinkets, some lace from Aunt Lambert, and twenty new guineas tucked into a purse stitched by her mother and sisters.

In valuing my own estate, I counted as a debt a sum my mother refused to acknowledge—even on her deathbed. She insisted the money I spent on her was for the upkeep of land destined for me. Any surplus, she claimed, belonged to my brother, who had nothing. He believed himself sole heir to the Virginian estate—until a mere half-hour’s difference in birth changed everything. He now served the king with distinction. To buy his promotion, she said, was surely his duty—and hers, too. When I finished my legal studies and my theatrical escapades, Madam Esmond informed me I was welcome to return to Virginia, to the place my birth entitled me to. She repeated this through Mountain, long before word of my marriage reached her.

Needless to say, her reaction upon learning of my marriage was fierce. When Harry, my brother, visited Virginia after the pacification of Canada, he described the reception he received. Castlewood, abandoned since our departure for Europe, reopened its doors. My mother hosted splendid feasts in honour of her son—the friend of Wolfe, the soldier of Quebec—honoured by all the colony.

But disputes arose. Harry remained steadfast in his friendship with Colonel Washington of Mount Vernon, whose virtues he praised endlessly. I admit the man had merit. In the later struggles that brought ruin to England, I can admire, as others do, Washington’s unwavering resolve and triumph.

If these arguments between Harry and our mother were frequent—as his letters suggested—I wondered why he stayed so long. A thought occurred to me, one I hesitated to share with Mrs. Warrington. We had discussed Hetty’s secret affection for him, marveling that he never noticed. Perhaps, I mused, he had found another woman in Virginia—one more suited to his taste.

Then came a letter—not a full confession, but a hint. A figure was described, unnamed—a creature of beauty and perfection, like any young lady under similar circumstances. My wife asked to see it. I handed it over, feeling sorrowful. To my surprise, she read it calmly, showing no sign of pain.

“I’ve thought about this before,” I said. “I feel for you, my love, regarding poor Hetty.”

“Ah, poor Hetty,” Theo murmured, eyes fixed on the carpet.

“It would never have worked,” I added.

“No,” she sighed. “They wouldn’t have been happy.”

“How strange he never discovered her secret!” I continued.

She looked at me sharply, a curious expression on her face. “What does that look mean?” I asked.

“Nothing, dear—nothing! Only I’m not surprised,” she said, blushing.

“What?” I asked, my mind stirred. “Do you mean to tell me… that you once cared for someone else?”

“Oh, George,” she whispered, “when I was at school, there was a boy—Dr. Backhouse’s school—sat in the loft next to ours. He had lovely eyes. I was so shocked when I saw him behind the counter at Mr. Grigg’s, buying a cloak for baby. I wanted to tell you, but didn’t know how!”

I went to find this boy with the lovely eyes. My wife described his clothes—blue camlet coat, red hair tied with a dirty ribbon. I saw him: a slight, bandy-legged fellow. I refrained from reproaching my wife—and I’ll never tell her I saw him, unless she reads this page. If wives saw us as we truly are, would they still love us? Are we not just as mistaken about them as they are about us? I gaze into one honest face, and believe I’ve never been deceived.

To prevent younger readers from imitating my folly, I won’t detail how little capital Mrs. Theo and I began with. Our tragedy brought us nothing—though recent reviewers have praised it, and Mr. Kemble himself commended it. Lord Wrotham offered to publish it by subscription, sending me a banknote for a hundred copies. But I disliked that method and locked away the manuscript, with my wife’s verses at the front. I don’t know why it offended court—perhaps because an actor who’d eloped with an earl’s daughter played a lead role. Or maybe because the Indian characters criticized British ambition and rule. Whatever the reason, royal favour slipped from my grasp.

What then? Months after the play failed, I counted what remained of my fortune (a brief calculation). I decided to leave my pleasant Bloomsbury lodgings. I gave notice to our landlady, telling her my wife needed country air. We moved only as far as Lambeth, Gumbo and Molly following. Though we were as poor as beggars, we still had a maid and a lackey in livery—like any household of standing. Naturally, relatives condemned our extravagance. Aren’t they always the ones to point out our faults?

One day, returning from London, I saw a grand gilt carriage near our lodgings—bearing the arms and livery of the Castlewoods. A crowd gathered, awed by the footmen in the sun, drinking ale. Inside, I found Lady Castlewood seated opposite my wife. Theo, with her lively way of recounting scenes, told me all.

“I’ve been here ages,” she said, “gossiping with cousin Theo while you were at the coffee-house, making merry. Guess she finds it lonely here, stitching caps and hemming frocks. Never mind, dear—you’ll soon have company to amuse you when cousin George is gone. What a nice lodging! Ours is twenty times bigger, covered in gold—but I like this better. Bless you, being rich isn’t better than being poor. When we lived at Albany, I did all the work—scoured rooms, boiled the kettle, helped wash. Just as happy as I am now. We had one old negro to keep store. Why don’t you sell Gumbo, cousin George? He’s idle, dawdling, making love to the maid. Fogh! These English people aren’t particular!”

She chattered cheerfully until it was time to go. Then she pulled out a fine repeating watch. “Time to call on Her Majesty at Buckingham House,” she declared. “And mind you come to us, George.” She waved from the coach. “Theo and I have settled all.”

“There,” I said, as the footmen climbed aboard, “is one who fears neither our poverty nor forgets her own.”

“Ashamed?” Theo resumed her needlework. “To do her justice, she’d make herself at home in any kitchen or palace. She taught me and Molly twenty lessons in housekeeping. She says she roasted, baked, swept, milked the cow.” (Madam Theo pronounced “cow” in our American fashion, mimicking the Countess delightfully.)

“And she has no pride,” I added. “It was kind of her to invite us to dine. When will Uncle Warrington ever offer us a crust—or a glass of his famous beer?”

“Yes, it was kind,” Theo replied, smiling. “But you don’t know the conditions!” She laughed, imitating the Countess. “She took out her book and told me her schedule. Monday: Duke and Duchess, several members of my lord’s household. Tuesday: earls, bishops, ambassador. ‘Of course you won’t come those days?’ she said. ‘You’re so poor—fine company isn’t good for you. Father doesn’t dine on company days—he eats cold meat.’ So I told her Mr. Warrington only cares for the best company, and suggested she invite us when the Archbishop dines. She agreed. And she’s economical—‘I thought of bringing my baby’s caps, but they might be needed again.’ So we lose that wardrobe addition.” Theo smiled. “‘When people are poor,’ she said, ‘they’re poor, and must get on.’ What shall we do for poor Maria? Goodness knows—we can’t ask her to visit as we can you, though you’re so poor. An earl’s daughter marrying a play-actor! La, my dear, it’s dreadful. His Majesty and the Princess have both spoken of it! Every noble family pities us. But I have a plan—to help them.’ She sent Simons, her groom, to tell Hagan to return to Dublin College, finish his terms, take orders, and become chaplain at home.”

This plan was fulfilled months later, when I could assist Hagan myself. Castlewood kept his word—Hagan became chaplain and received a colonial appointment. But not a penny did he spare for his sister or kinsman. I never asked him for aid—thank God. And though he expressed genuine joy when my poverty ended, I knew he meant it.

As for Uncle Warrington and his virtuous family—let me be fair. Throughout my trial, their sorrow for my condition was constant. I heard reports from acquaintances who visited them. Not once did they say a kind word about me or my wife. They called my tragedy a crime, me a reprobate, wandering Grub Street in rags. They offered no hand. My wife could cry in pain, but they had no twopence. They attended church six times a week, subscribed to charities. Their kind was known eighteen centuries ago and will endure. They thank Heaven they are not like others—and leave the suffering to fend for themselves.

I shudder to recall the doubts that haunted me—the plans I tried and failed. I turned to my friend Mr. Johnson. I remember his warmth, and blush at the sharp words I once spoke about his manners. I told him my story—my marriage, my prospects. He refused to see them as grim. “You have chances,” he said. “A position in England—slim, but possible. Your inheritance—natural, or soon to come. You have cash for now—though, to be frank, after your tragedy, I doubt nature gave you the gifts for literary fame.” And finally, “return to Virginia—plenty and a home await you.”

“Why, sir!” he exclaimed. “That sum would have been a fortune for me at the start. My friend Goldsmith would set up a coach-and-six on it. With youth, hope, and two hundred pounds—no young man need despair. You have a year. Who knows what may happen? Your relatives might help. You might inherit. You might win a fortune!”

I did not—though he did. Lord Bute granted Mr. Johnson a pension, setting Grub Street ablaze. Still, he did not abandon my literary efforts. He promised to secure work from booksellers—and kept his word. “But,” he warned, “you must not appear among them *in forma pauperis*. Have you no friend’s coach? Wear your best hat and waistcoat. We must seem to be doing *them* a favour.”

It worked—at first. Respect followed. But when the booksellers realized I expected payment, they stiffened. One, formerly a footman, told his boy: “Oh, it’s Pocahontas? Let him wait.” I stormed in. “Wait, sir?” I roared. “I’m not accustomed to waiting—but I’ve heard you are.” I marched out into Pall Mall, furious.

Yet Mr. D. was right. I came not to beg, but to bargain. It was my place to wait. Later, in better times, I apologized. The author of *The Muse in Livery* forgave me instantly.

So ends this chapter—of poverty, pride, and the quiet strength of love.
