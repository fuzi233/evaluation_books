Among the heirs of Art, as in the division of a promised land, each must fight hard to claim their share. The gift may be prophetic, but without effort, it remains mere title—no possession at all. Carrying a map of an estate you’ve never owned is little better than a hollow promise. And imagining you can cast your shoe over Eden doesn’t mean you’ll ever set foot on its soil.

The most stubborn beliefs we hold about ourselves often rest on no evidence beyond a constant, unexamined pulse of self-satisfaction—a hidden seed of madness, a quiet certainty that we can move the world, even when we don’t know where we stand or what lever we might use.

“Please go to church, mamma,” Gwendolen said the next morning. “I’d rather be alone with Herr Klesmer.” (He had replied to her note that he would arrive at eleven.)

“That’s hardly proper,” Mrs. Davilow said, uneasy.

“Our situation is too serious to worry about such trivial rules,” Gwendolen answered, dismissive. “They’re both absurd and insulting.”

“You wouldn’t mind if Isabel sat nearby? She could read quietly in a corner.”

“No,” Gwendolen insisted. “She’d fidget and stare. It would only distract me. Trust my judgment, mamma—I must be alone. Take everyone else to church.”

Gwendolen had her way, of course—though Miss Merry and two of the girls stayed behind, seated by the dining-room windows, to keep the house from looking abandoned.

It was a perfect Sunday morning. The soft, fading light of autumn rested gently on the half-mown grass, slanting through the windows in golden bands across the old furniture and the glass panel that reflected it all—the tapestried chairs with their faded flower patterns, the dark, mysterious paintings, the outdated organ where Gwendolen had once delighted in playing Saint Cecilia upon her joyful arrival, and the dusty collection of knick-knacks visible through the open doors of the antechamber, where she had once worn her Greek costume as Hermione. That memory stirred her now—hadn’t Klesmer been struck by her poise and expression then? Whatever he had said, whatever she imagined he thought, mattered deeply to her now. For the first time in her life, she felt truly dependent on another’s opinion, aware of needing validation from someone outside herself. A new flutter stirred within her, a fresh layer of self-awareness replacing the effortless confidence of intuition. Yet still, her inner voice kept repeating: Klesmer has seen so little of me. Any judgment he makes must be based on too narrow a foundation. Still, she believed herself clever enough for anything.

To pass the time, she gathered her music books and scores, placing them neatly atop the piano before beginning to sort them. Then, catching her reflection in the glass panel, she paused, drawn to her own image. Dressed simply in black, without ornament, her warm skin glowing between her light-brown hair and square-cut bodice, she might have inspired an artist to try again the ancient Roman trick of sculpting in black, white, and tawny marble. Watching herself approach slowly, she thought, *I am beautiful*—not with pride, but with quiet certainty. Beauty, after all, was the one thing she needed confirmation for. If anyone criticized the shape of her nose or the line of her neck and chin, she couldn’t imagine how she might prove herself capable of mastering those details.

There wasn’t much time left before the sound of wheels, the sharp ring of the doorbell, and the opening of the front doors confirmed that Klesmer was not going to disappoint her. This only heightened the flutter in her chest. Despite her confidence, she feared him—as part of a world beyond her control, something sharp and unyielding that burned regardless of whether you smiled or frowned. Poor girl! She stood at a higher turning point in her womanhood than she had during her encounter with Grandcourt. Then, her question had been whether to accept a man as husband. Now, the deeper question was whether she even needed a husband at all—whether she could achieve independence, fulfill her ambitions, and find satisfaction without being bound.

Klesmer entered with his most respectful bow in the wide doorway of the antechamber—his fine gray trousers and flawless gloves marking him as one who knew his place among the masters. Gwendolen met him with unusual seriousness, extending her hand. “Thank you so much for coming, Herr Klesmer. I hope I haven’t seemed presumptuous.”

“I took your request as a command—an honor,” he replied, equally grave. He had indeed rearranged his own affairs to give her full attention, though his nerves were still jangled from the events of the previous day, lending his words a sharper edge than usual.

For once, Gwendolen was too emotionally charged to remember etiquette. She remained near the piano, and Klesmer stood at the opposite end, backlit, his piercing eyes fixed on her. No pretense would serve. She began at once.

“I need your advice, Herr Klesmer. We’ve lost everything. We have no fortune. I must earn my own living—and I want to provide for my mother so she won’t suffer. The only path I can see—and the one I’d prefer above all—is acting. To go on the stage. But naturally, I’d like to reach a high position. So I thought—if you thought I could—that I might also study singing. Perhaps become a singer-actress, like Grisi.”

Klesmer placed his hat on the piano and folded his arms, as if gathering his thoughts.

“I know,” Gwendolen continued, her face shifting from pale to pink and back again, “that my singing technique is flawed. I’ve had poor instruction. But I could learn. I could train. And you understand my ambition—to sing and act, as Grisi does, is a far greater role. Of course, I’d aim as high as possible. And I trust your judgment. I’m sure you’ll tell me the truth.”

Somehow, she believed that now she made this earnest appeal, the truth would be favorable.

But Klesmer remained silent. He quickly removed his gloves, tossed them into his hat, placed his hands on his hips, and walked to the other side of the room. Compassion filled him—he wanted to guard his words carefully. When he turned again, he looked at her with a mild frown, speaking gently yet sharply: “You’ve never really seen artists’ lives, have you?—musicians, actors, performers of that kind?”

“Oh, no,” Gwendolen replied, untroubled by the obviousness of the fact—she had lived sheltered, well-provided for.

“You are—excuse me,” Klesmer said, pausing near the piano, “coming to such a decision without considering everything. You’re perhaps twenty?”

“I’m twenty-one,” she said, a flicker of unease rising. “Do you think I’m too old?”

Klesmer pursed his lips and raised his fingers upward in a gesture that meant nothing.

“Many begin later,” Gwendolen said, instinctively drawing on her usual habit of offering information as proof of her worth.

Klesmer ignored her, speaking with even more care: “You’ve probably never seriously considered an artistic life until now. You didn’t dream of it, desire it—what shall I say?—you didn’t long to be an actress until this crisis?”

“Not exactly,” she admitted, “but I’ve always enjoyed acting. I’ve performed—remember? You saw me here in charades, and as Hermione.”

“Yes, yes,” he said quickly, “I remember perfectly.” And again, he walked to the other end of the room. He found it hard to stay still when engaged in any argument, spoken or silent.

Gwendolen felt she was being weighed. The silence was uncomfortable. But she still didn’t believe the scale could tip against her. It seemed graceful to say, “I’ll be grateful for your honest advice, whatever it may be.”

“Miss Harleth,” Klesmer said, turning toward her, his accent slightly stronger, “I will not hide anything from you. I would feel guilty if I presented things falsely—making them too bright or too dark. The gods curse the man who willingly leads another down the wrong path. And if I misled someone so young, so beautiful—who, I hope, will find happiness on the right road—I would consider myself a _Bösewicht_.” His voice dropped to a low whisper on the last word.

Gwendolen felt a sudden pang beneath her chest. She stared at him, transfixed.

“You are a beautiful young lady,” he continued, “brought up in comfort. You’ve done as you pleased. You’ve never told yourself, ‘I must know this exactly,’ ‘I must understand this exactly,’ ‘I must do this exactly.’” As he spoke the three “musts,” he raised three fingers in succession. “In short, you’ve never been called upon to be anything but a charming young lady—someone it’s impolite to criticize.”

He paused. Then, hands on hips, chin thrust forward, he added:

“So now, with this background, you wish to enter the life of an artist—a life of relentless, unending work and uncertain praise. Your acclaim would have to be earned, like your bread; and both would come slowly, sparingly—perhaps not at all.”

This discouraging tone, which Klesmer hoped would suffice, stirred resistance in Gwendolen. With a slight turn of her head and a hint of irritation, she said:

“I thought, as an artist, you’d see this life as noble and fulfilling. And if I can do nothing better—well, I suppose I can endure the same risks as others.”

“Do nothing better?” Klesmer asked, slightly provoked. “No, my dear Miss Harleth—you could do nothing better—neither man nor woman could—*if* they could achieve excellence, if they could do what is best of its kind. I’m not denigrating the artist’s life. I’m exalting it. It belongs only to chosen souls—natures shaped to love perfection and labor for it. Ready, like true lovers, to endure, to wait, to say, ‘I am not yet worthy—but she—Art, my mistress—is worthy, and I will live to deserve her.’ An honorable life? Yes. But the honor comes from inner calling and hard-won achievement. There is no honor in wearing the life like a uniform.”

A spark from yesterday still burned in Klesmer, urging him into speech beyond his immediate purpose. He had wished only to gently awaken Gwendolen to her unpreparedness, but he could not resist condemning false pretensions. He caught himself and paused. But Gwendolen’s main impression was that he hadn’t yet denied her ability to achieve excellence. His passion seemed merely a glow he often cast over things. What she wanted to assure him was that she wasn’t afraid of hardship. The idea that stepping onto the stage would not bring the automatic admiration she’d always known—like shedding a piece of flesh—was painful, but not impossible. She said, with firmness:

“I’m ready to endure hardships at first. No one becomes famous overnight. And not everyone needs to be first-rate—actresses or singers alike. If you’d kindly tell me what steps to take, I’ll have the courage to follow them. I don’t mind climbing uphill. It’s easier than the flat emptiness of being a governess. I’ll take any path you recommend.”

Now Klesmer knew he must speak plainly.

“I’ll tell you the steps—not because I recommend them, but because they’ll be forced upon you. Whether your goal is excellence, fame, or mere mediocrity—it makes no difference. You must go to town under your mother’s care. You must submit to training—musical, dramatic, theatrical. Whatever you wish to do, you must learn.” Here, Gwendolen opened her mouth to speak, but Klesmer held up his hand. “I know. You’ve performed—recited, sung—from the drawing-room perspective. My dear Fräulein, you must unlearn all that. You’ve not grasped what excellence truly means. You must unlearn your mistaken ideals. You must know what you strive for, and then discipline your mind and body completely. Your mind, I say. Because you must stop thinking of fame. Extinguish that candle from your eyes and look only at perfection. You’ll earn nothing at first—no engagement for a long time. You’ll need money for yourself and your family. But that,” he added, frowning and flicking his fingers as if brushing aside a trifle, “could perhaps be arranged.”

Gwendolen flushed and paled through his words. Her pride had been cut with a sharp blade, and the final sentence only deepened the wound. She felt herself showing emotion and tried to regain composure by walking to a chair and gesturing to one for Klesmer. He refused, turning slightly to face her, leaning against the piano. At that moment, she regretted summoning him. This first experience of being judged not by her beauty or rank, but by her capacity—was becoming bitter.

Klesmer, focused on his purpose, continued without changing tone.

“And what might reasonably be expected from all this sacrifice? You’d ask that. It’s right that you see clearly. I’ll tell you honestly. The outcome would be uncertain—and likely, not worth much.”

At these words, Klesmer pursed his lips and looked through his spectacles with an expression that seemed almost monstrous, impenetrable by charm.

Gwendolen’s eyes began to sting, but fear of appearing weak drove her to tighten her control. She forced herself to speak, coldly:

“You think I lack talent—or that I’m too old to begin.”

Klesmer hummed softly, then declared emphatically: “Yes! The desire and training should have begun seven years ago—or much earlier. A child born to a mountebank, helping her father earn shillings at six, a girl inheriting a singing voice from generations of choristers, learning to sing as she learns to walk—such a start is far likelier. Every great accomplishment in music or acting grows with growth itself. Whenever an artist can say, ‘I came, I saw, I conquered,’ it’s after years of patient practice. Genius, at first, is little more than a vast capacity for discipline. Singing and acting—like the juggler’s dexterity with cups and balls—require shaping the body toward finer and finer precision. Your muscles, your entire frame, must function like a watch—true, true to a hair. That takes springtime, before habits harden.”

“I never claimed genius,” Gwendolen said, still clinging to the idea that she might achieve what he deemed impossible. “I only thought I might have some talent—enough to improve.”

“I don’t deny that,” Klesmer said. “If you’d been trained properly years ago and worked diligently, you might now have become a public singer—though I doubt your voice would carry much weight. On stage, your charm and intelligence might have helped, but now you lack experience, discipline, instruction.”

Klesmer seemed cruel, but his heart was not cruel. Even our most sincere speech carries echoes of other impulses. His words were shaped by compassion—for Gwendolen’s ignorance, her eagerness to leap into a world he saw in stark detail, with suffering and humiliation laid bare, yet unable to convey it fully to her.

But Gwendolen was not convinced. Her self-image rallied. Since the advisor she had summoned delivered such harsh judgment, she began to suspect his view was not only flawed, but biased. It occurred to her that a simpler, wiser step would have been to write directly to a London theatre manager, asking for an audition. She would no longer mention singing—Klesmer had already opposed it. But she felt confident she could argue her case for acting. She replied, defiantly:

“I understood, of course, that no one becomes a finished actress overnight. It may be impossible to predict success—but that seems reason enough to try. I thought I might secure an engagement while studying, earning money and learning simultaneously.”

“It can’t be done,” Klesmer said firmly. “I speak plainly—it can’t be done. I must clear your mind of these illusions, which resemble reality no more than a pantomime. People think that once they’ve dressed and put on gloves, they’re fit for the stage as they are for a drawing-room. No manager believes that. With all your grace, if you appeared as an applicant, a manager would either charge you as an amateur or send you away to be trained—taught to carry yourself on stage, like a horse must be trained for the circus. Not to mention the deeper study needed to embody a character convincingly, to animate it with natural expression, gesture, and voice. An immediate engagement suitable for you? Impossible.”

“I can’t understand that,” Gwendolen said, slightly haughty—then, correcting herself, softer: “Please explain how such poor actresses get hired. I’ve seen plays, and I’m certain there were performers who acted poorly and were plain.”

“Ah, my dear Miss Harleth,” Klesmer said, “that’s the easy criticism of the spectator. We who buy shoes discard pairs as clumsy, but each pair required apprenticeship. Excuse me—you couldn’t teach one of those actresses now. But she could teach you much. For example, she can project her voice so it reaches the back row. Ten times out of ten, you couldn’t do that without many trials. Merely standing and moving on stage is an art—it requires practice. We’re not discussing a minor role in a small theatre earning a needlewoman’s wages. That’s unthinkable for you.”

“Of course I must earn more than that,” Gwendolen said, wincing rather than refuted. “But I think I could soon master all these things you mention. I’m not so stupid. And even in Paris, I saw two actresses playing noblewomen who weren’t noble at all—ugly, even. I suppose I have no special talent, but surely being a lady, rather than a fright, is an advantage—even on stage.”

“Ah, let us clarify,” Klesmer said, a new intensity in his voice. “I’ve been speaking of what you’d face if you aimed at becoming a true artist—if you treated music and drama as a higher calling, striving for excellence. In that case, what I’ve said stands. After twenty-one years of doing things loosely, you’d face immense difficulty in study. You’d endure humiliations when presenting yourself as skilled. You’d undergo tests—people would no longer pretend not to see your flaws. You’d start on trial. You’d bear what I call glaring insignificance. Success would require the utmost patience. You’d have to hold your place in a crowd—and likely lose it, fade into obscurity. If you still choose this path, you’ll have the dignity of a noble purpose, even if you’ve made a mistake. You’ll have merit, even if you win no prize. You asked for my judgment on your chances. I don’t claim absolute knowledge, but judging probabilities: you’ll likely achieve no more than mediocrity.”

Klesmer spoke with forceful speed, then paused. Gwendolen sat motionless, staring at her hands folded in her lap, until the deep, lingering “_But_” startled her, making her look up.

“But—there are other ways, other dispositions, by which a young lady might enter the arts and appear before the public. She might rely on her beauty as a passport. She might seek admiration that demands no skill. That works, to a degree, on stage—but not in music. On stage, beauty is accepted when nothing stronger is available. But not without training: technical mastery is always required. Beyond that, we’re not dealing with art. Such a woman is not an artist—usually one seeking a luxurious life through a quick, easy route—perhaps marriage, her brightest chance, and rarest. Still, her path won’t be luxurious at first. She can barely earn her own livelihood immediately, and the indignities she faces… I won’t name them.”

“I want to be independent,” Gwendolen said, wounded and confused by what sounded like scorn in Klesmer’s tone. “That’s why I asked if I could get an immediate engagement. I don’t know how theatres work. But I thought I could make myself independent. I have no money, and I won’t accept help from anyone.”

Her pride demanded this declaration. It was unbearable to think Klesmer might have imagined she expected anything more from him than advice.
