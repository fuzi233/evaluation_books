Wandering the crowded streets of Florence in search of the cheapest possible shelter — a spare outhouse where he could bed down out of the wind and rain — Baldassarre’s steps drew him to the only patch of ground inside the city walls that broke the flat sprawl of terracotta roofs. From that small rise, a passerby lifted high enough could see past the clustered houses to the soft line of the protecting Tuscan hills and the wide, sun-dappled valley beyond, views otherwise hidden save for the narrow opening cut by the winding course of the Arno. This stretch of slope was not the quarry side of Bogoli Hill he had caught glimpses of before, but the quieter incline rising behind Via de’ Bardi, known locally as the hill of San Giorgio. Bratti the peddler had told him Tito’s home stood on Via de’ Bardi, and after scoping the narrow, shadowed thoroughfare, Baldassarre turned to trudge up the slope he had spotted from the bridge. A shelter on this hill would suit him perfectly: years of wandering under open skies had left him claustrophobic in the cramped medieval streets, where only a thin strip of sky peeked overhead between the towering walls, and the twisting labyrinth of alleys frayed his already spotty, faltering memory further still.

The hill was sparsely populated, mostly dotted with vegetable plots and untended overgrown gardens, save for one jagged stretch of rough, stone-strewn ground that had never been cultivated since a 13th century landslide had swallowed an entire row of houses there. Perched at the edge of that broken patch stood a strange little square building, like a truncated tower capped with fluted terracotta tiles, and tucked against a crumbled section of old stone wall right beside it was a small, low outhouse. Under a gnarled half-dead mulberry tree, shedding its last rustling autumn leaves through the open doorways, a shrivelled, weathered old woman named Monna Lisa was untying a nanny goat and her two frolicking kids. Half the outhouse was clearly given over to livestock, the other half empty save for a stack of fresh straw and a handful of rusted farm tools. It was exactly the shelter he was looking for.

He had to lean in and shout right into her ear to make the hard-of-hearing old woman understand he was a poor stranger from across the sea, could not afford a hostel, and would happily pay a quattrino or two a week just to sleep on that straw. At first she only shook her head, muttering “No — no lodging” in a muffled, uncomprehending tone. But after persistent pleading, she gestured to a large half-buried log in the corner, left rotting there since her husband died decades prior. If he chopped the entire log into kindling, she told him, he could stay one single night. He agreed. Monna Lisa stood arms akimbo watching him swing the axe, a sharp little cunning smile on her face as she whispered to herself: “That log’s sat there useless for years. I couldn’t have paid a local boy half a quattrino to split it. This odd-acting foreigner chops very well. And if he only wants a bed of straw, I can set him running errands up and down the hill, no one need be the wiser. Hidden sin is half forgiven, after all. He’s a stranger, he won’t take any notice of the girl up in the cottage, and I’ll tell her to keep her mouth shut tight.”

That very girl, Tessa, was pressed to a four-inch round hole cut in the closed upper shutter at that exact moment, craning to see him. The shutter was not fastened to punish her: the window on the opposite wall was the only one in the whole tiny cottage fitted with expensive glass, and the weather was far too cool to leave that shutter propped open. The hole was cut just a little too high, forcing her to stand on a rickety low stool to peer through, but Tessa would have endured far more discomfort for a glimpse of any new, different thing to break up the quiet monotony of her hidden life. She had darted to the shutter the moment she heard Baldassarre’s loud unfamiliar voice speaking to Monna Lisa, and stayed there until dusk fell, watching him finish chopping the wood, trudge back into the outhouse, and sink heavily down onto the straw, his whole frame heavy with exhaustion and loneliness.

A wild, giddy little temptation seized Tessa. She would sneak down and bring the old man a portion of her supper, talk to him for a while. He was not deaf like Monna Lisa, after all — she could prattle to him about all the silly little thoughts and small joys she had no one else to share them with. She knew it was naughty: obedience was the core of what Tessa considered duty, and her husband Naldo had strictly forbidden her from making any acquaintances, warning her that if anyone outside their tiny hidden circle learned of her life here, all her new comforts and happiness would vanish as quickly as hidden treasure that turns to lead the moment it is pulled into daylight. She had been perfectly obedient up to that point, even pulling her hood low over her face in church and pursing her lips tight to keep from greeting any passing stranger, half terrified her cruel brute stepfather Nofri would track her all the way from her old farm and drag her back to backbreaking work. But this old stranger was no proper acquaintance, right? He was a homeless wanderer with no ties anywhere, he would never tell anyone about her. She could even save the small trespass to confess to the Padre the next Easter, when she went to confession. It was hardly a real sin at all.

Her face flushed and excited, she sat on her low stool for a minute balancing on the edge of the small transgression, before jumping up to smooth down her festa-day contadina gown: a crisp clean dark green serge, cinched with a bright red girdle, red glass prayer beads strung around her neck, her curly brown hair knotted tight and pinned with the silver pin Naldo had gifted her, and the new fine gold ring on her finger she was still absurdly proud of. She tripped downstairs, feigned innocent curiosity to ask Monna Lisa what all the shouting outside had been about, then put on a fierce little scowl like a tiny kitten trying to look formidable, and ordered the old woman to go sit by the fire upstairs — she wanted to eat her supper down by the door. Monna Lisa huffed and obeyed, and three minutes later Tessa was knocking soft on the outhouse door, one hand clutching a tin lantern, the other hefting a wooden bowl piled high with steaming pecorino-flavoured macaroni.

Baldassarre, who had been gnawing on a crumb of stale dry bread he’d fished from his tunic pocket, thought he was trapped in a strange sad dream when he opened the door and found this round-faced, blue-eyed little girl beaming at him, bright with uncomplicated delight, breaking into his dark loneliness. “I’ve brought you supper!” she shouted, leaning right towards his ear, having forgotten he was not deaf like Monna Lisa. “Sit down and eat it, I’ll stay with you a while.”

Surprise and wariness surged in Baldassarre first, but he had no urge at all to turn her away. He sank passively back onto his straw bed, Tessa set the warm bowl on his lap, placed the lantern on the dirt floor between them, and crossed her hands over her lap, nodding at the food with a reassuring little smile as if to say there were no tricks here. He was ravenous, and the warm macaroni tasted like nothing he had eaten in months. Tessa watched him take two or three bites before she realized he could hear perfectly well. “Oh! I forgot!” she gasped, clapping her hands over her mouth, “I’ve spent so long shouting at Monna Lisa I do it to everyone!”

She prattled on happily, chattering that she lived in the little cottage with the old deaf woman, that they had plenty of nice things, that she could eat nuts whenever she wanted and never had to do hard farm work anymore. She said she missed her old mule Giannetta from the farm sometimes, and used to talk for hours to the goat because there was no one else to speak to. Then she teased him that she had a secret: she had a tiny, perfect bambino, born on Christmas Day, that she had been married one Christmas long ago and no one in the whole world knew. She clapped a hand over her mouth like she had accidentally revealed a great forbidden truth, and Baldassarre stared at her dreamily, as if this garrulous soft young thing was a half-forgotten memory drifting up out of his own lost youth.

He told her he only knew one man in all the world. Tessa’s face fell, and she asked anxiously if his name was Nofri, her cruel old stepfather. Baldassarre shook his head, and Tessa laughed, delighted, saying that was not her husband’s name at all — her husband was the most beautiful, gentle, perfect man in the whole world, only he did not come to visit often, he only ever arrived after dark, sometimes staying away for weeks at a time. She said she would bring the baby down to show him tomorrow, then picked up her empty bowl, wished him good night, and slipped back into the cottage. She immediately told Monna Lisa what she had done, and insisted the stranger be allowed to stay as long as he wanted, to help carry water up the hill and run small errands. Monna Lisa pretended to grumble that Naldo would be angry, but she had already been planning to turn the free laborer to her own use, and readily agreed as long as Tessa swore never to tell a single soul of his presence. Tessa promised, saying she would only ever tell the baby.

The next morning, Tessa hurried through all her tiny chores to bring Baldassarre a bowl of steaming hot broth and soft crusty bread, then ran back to grab her swaddled baby, all big dark eyes and barely a hint of a nose, that she swore looked exactly like the infant Jesus in the parish church painting. She handed the small warm bundle to Baldassarre to hold, and his rough deep voice rumbled out “Poor thing, poor thing,” so low it felt almost threatening under the soft apparent pity — as if the sight of this guileless unprotected girl and her child had linked them in his mind to all the cruelty that had been done to him, two innocent creatures wronged by the world, who deserved to be avenged. Tessa laughed and shook her head, saying she was not poor at all, she had almost everything she ever wanted, except to see her husband more often. When she asked him what his name was, Baldassarre rubbed at his furrowed forehead, and his memory went completely, helplessly blank. Tessa saw his confused, lost look, and patted his arm gently. “Never mind,” she said softly, “you are a stranger, you don’t need a name for us. We will be good to you.” She ran off to eat her own breakfast, and Baldassarre stared after her, muttering “Poor thing,” once more under his breath, as the first pale streaks of sun flooded over the slope of San Giorgio hill.
