“To tell you the truth,” the young stranger said, once the press of carts and sweating mules had loosened a little and the air was less thick with hoof-clatter, “I’m not sorry your patron has handed me over to someone with a less barbarous accent—and a less riddling errand. Is it common among Florentines for a hawker of cracked glass and old rags to speak of a shop where he sells lutes and swords?”

“Common? No. Our Bratti isn’t common,” Nello answered, and his words came out the way steam pours from a pot the moment you lift the lid. “He has a theory and lives up to it—which is more than I can say for any philosopher I’ve had the honor to shave. Bratti means to squeeze the utmost relish out of life—meaning, hard bargaining—then wind it up with a bargain for the easiest passage through purgatory by leaving Holy Church his winnings when the game is done. He’s had his will drawn for it at the cheapest terms a notary could be coaxed to. I’ve told him often, ‘Bratti, your bargain limps, and you’re the lame side of it. Doesn’t it make you a little melancholy to stare at the pictures of Paradise? You’ll never be able to chaffer there for rags and rusty nails. Saints and angels want neither pins nor tinder. And except with San Bartolommeo—who carries his skin in an inconvenient fashion—I see no chance of your striking a deal for second-hand clothing.’”

He checked himself, crossed his fingers to his brow, and made the sign of the cross as if to rinse his mouth of the irreverence. Even his voice changed, as though a bell had been struck in the morning air.

“But God pardon me—light talk ill suits a morning when Lorenzo lies dead and the Muses are tearing their hair—always a painful thought to a barber. And you yourself, Messere—when a man with your speech and presence takes up with so sorry a night’s lodging, it argues misfortune.”

“What Lorenzo is it whose death you speak of?” The stranger’s attention fastened there with a hungry quickness; the sidelong question about his own condition slipped past him as if unheard.

“What Lorenzo?” Nello’s eyes widened at the ignorance. “There’s but one Lorenzo whose death can throw the Mercato into an uproar, set the Duomo’s lantern leaping as if in despair, and make the lions of the Republic feel they must devour one another at once. I mean Lorenzo de’ Medici—our Pericles—if I may dare the comparison in the ear of a Greek.”

“Why not?” the other said, and laughed as lightly as if laughter were a habit of breathing. “I doubt Athens, even in Pericles’ day, could have produced so learned a barber.”

“Yes—yes,” Nello cried, gratified, as if he’d been served a cup of spiced wine exactly to his taste. “I thought I could not be mistaken—else I have shaved the venerable Demetrio Calcondila to little purpose. But pardon me: I’m lost in wonder. Your Italian is better than his, though he has been forty years in Italy—better even than accomplished Marullo’s, and he’s married the Italic Muse more ways than one, since he has married our learned and lovely Alessandra Scala.”

“It will ease your wonder,” said the stranger, “to know I come of Greek stock planted in Italian soil longer than your mulberry trees. I was born at Bari, and my—” he stopped, as if a word in his throat had edges, “—I was brought up by an Italian. In fact I’m a Greek much as your peaches are Persian: the dye subdued till I was dipped again by long abode and much travel in the land of gods and heroes. And to confess something private—this same Greek dye, and a few ancient gems I carry, are all shipwreck has left me. But when towers fall, it’s ill for the small nest-builders. The death of your Pericles makes me wish I had turned toward Rome, as I should have done but for a fallacious Minerva in the shape of an Augustinian monk. ‘At Rome,’ he said, ‘you will be lost in a crowd of hungry scholars; but at Florence every corner is penetrated by the sunshine of Lorenzo’s patronage. Florence is the best market in Italy for such commodities as yours.’”

“Gnaffè—and so it will remain, I hope,” Nello said, with the stubborn cheer of a man who refuses to let grief sour his morning. “Lorenzo wasn’t the only patron and judge of learning in our city—heaven forbid! Because he was a large melon, every Florentine besides isn’t a pumpkin. Have we not Bernardo Rucellai? Alamanno Rinuccini? Plenty more. And if you want to be informed on such matters, I, Nello, am your man. It seems to me a thousand years till I can be of service to a bel erudito like yourself. And first of all—in the matter of your hair.”

He tipped his chin toward the stranger’s beard as if it were a dish set on the table in need of trimming.

“That beard, my fine young man, must be parted with, were it as dear to you as the nymph of your dreams. Here at Florence we love not to see a man with his nose projecting over a cascade of hair. Remember—you will have passed the Rubicon when once you have been shaven. If you repent and let your beard grow after it has acquired stoutness by struggle with the razor, your mouth will by-and-by show no longer what Messer Angelo calls the divine prerogative of lips, but will look like a dark cavern fringed with horrent brambles.”

“That is a terrible prophecy,” the Greek said. “Especially if your Florentine maidens are many of them as pretty as the little Tessa I stole a kiss from this morning.”

“Tessa? She is a rough-handed contadina,” Nello returned, with the sniffing precision of a man who can sort scents in a crowded market. “You will rise into the favour of dames who bring no savour of mule-stables with them. But to that end you must not have the air of a sgherro—of an ill-reputed fellow. You must look like a courtier and a scholar of the polished sort, such as our Pietro Crinito—like one who sins among well-bred, well-fed people, not one who sucks down vile vino di sotto in a chance tavern.”

“With all my heart,” said the stranger. “If the Florentine Graces demand it, I’m willing to give up this small matter of my beard, but—”

“Yes, yes,” Nello broke in. “I know. It is the bella zazzera—the hyacinthine locks—you do not choose to part with. And there is no need. Just a little pruning—ecco!—and you will look not unlike the illustrious Pico di Mirandola in his prime. And here we are, in good time, in the Piazza San Giovanni, at the door of my shop. But you pause—you want to look at our wonder, our Duomo, our Santa Maria del Fiore. Well—well—one glance; but I beseech you to leave a closer survey till you have been shaved. I am quivering with the inspiration of my art even to the edge of my razor. Come round this way.”

He took the stranger’s arm, warm-blooded and proprietorial, and guided him to the south side of the piazza. There the whole great triad stood at once before them: the immense dark shell of the cupola rising like a stored thundercloud made stone; Giotto’s campanile, slender and sure as a reed that has learned to sing; and the quaint octagon of San Giovanni in front, its bronze gates still bearing, though dimmed, the gilded glory of their first blaze.

The inlaid marbles were fresher then—pink, white, and purple like salt and rose and crushed berry under a clean knife—before four centuries of winter would mellow the white toward ochre. The cathedral façade had not yet been smeared into stuccoed shame, but wore the magnificent promise of half-completed marble inlay and statued niches devised long ago by Giotto. And up the campanile the eye climbed, through harmonies of color and form, into the clear April air; the city’s morning noises—the clipped call of vendors, the wooden knock of carts, the metallic laughter of bridles—seemed for an instant to thin, as if the very soundscape had been rinsed by that upward beauty.

But the young Greek, though his eyes were drawn upward as by a string, stood with his arms folded and his curls falling back, and there was a slight scorn on his lip. When his gaze came down again it moved coolly, scanning, measuring—piquing, like a knife that tests bread with no intent to eat.

“Well, my fine young man,” Nello said, impatience pricking through his civic devotion, “you make as little of our cathedral as if you were the Angel Gabriel come straight from Paradise. Have you ever seen finer work than our Giotto’s tower? Any cupola that would not look a mere mushroom beside Brunelleschi’s? Any marbles finer, or more cunningly wrought than these our Signoria got from far-off quarries at a price that would buy a dukedom? Come now—have you ever seen anything to equal them?”

“If you asked me with a scimitar at my throat, after the Turkish fashion—or even with your own razor,” the Greek said, smiling, “you might wring a confession of the true faith from me. But with my throat free, I venture to tell you your buildings smack too much of Christian barbarism for my taste. I have a shuddering sense of what is inside—smoked Madonnas; fleshless saints in mosaic, staring down idiotic astonishment and rebuke; skin-clad skeletons on crosses, or stuck all over with arrows, or stretched on gridirons; women and monks with heads aside in perpetual lamentation. I have seen enough of those wry-necked favorites of heaven at Constantinople.”

He had wandered toward the Baptistery gates as he spoke, as if drawn by metal rather than by stone.

“But what is this bronze door rough with imagery? These women’s figures seem moulded in another spirit from the starved saints. These heads in high relief speak of a human mind within them, instead of being mere indexes to spasms and colic.”

“Yes—yes,” Nello said, triumph returning. “I think we shall show you by-and-by our Florentine art is not barbarous. These gates were moulded half a century ago by Lorenzo Ghiberti, when he counted hardly so many years as you do.”

“Ah—I remember,” the stranger said, turning away, his appetite for contemplation already sated like one who has tasted and moved on. “I have heard your Tuscan sculptors and painters have studied the antique a little. But with monks for models and legends of mad hermits and martyrs for subjects, the vision of Olympus itself would be small use to them.”

Nello gave a shrug that meant he understood too well.

“You are of the same mind as Michele Marullo—ay, and as Angelo Poliziano himself, in spite of his canonicate, when he relaxes a little in my shop after lectures and talks of the gods waking from their long sleep, making woods and streams vital once more. But he rails against the Roman scholars who would have us all talk Latin again. ‘My ears,’ he says, ‘are sufficiently flayed by the barbarisms of the learned; and if the vulgar are to talk Latin I would as soon have been in Florence the day they took to beating all the kettles in the city because the bells were not enough to stay the wrath of the saints.’”

As Nello spoke, the piazza’s sounds pressed and retreated—the long groan of a cart-axle, the splash of water from a bucket, the sudden shrill quarrel of a bird in the eaves—each noise taking its turn like instruments in a street ensemble. The barber seemed to savor it as other men savor broth: not merely hearing, but tasting the city through the ear.

“If you want the flavor of our scholarship,” he went on, “you must frequent my shop. It is the focus of Florentine intellect, and in that sense the navel of the earth—as my great predecessor Burchiello said of his shop, on the frivolous pretension that his street of the Calimara was the centre of the city. And here we are: the sign of ‘Apollo and the Razor.’ Apollo bestows the razor on the Triptolemus of our craft—the first reaper of beards—the sublime Anonimo, whose mysterious identity is indicated by a shadowy hand.”

At the threshold a solemn, dark-eyed youth moved aside.

“I see thou hast had custom already, Sandro,” Nello said. “Now make all clear for this signor. Prepare the finest-scented lather, for he has a learned and handsome chin.”

The stranger’s eyes travelled past the shop into a room divided by a latticed screen, and beyond that into a smaller walled enclosure where a few bays and laurels circled a stone Hermes. The air inside had its own composed odor—soap, warmed metal, the faint sourness of old oil in a lamp, and the greener breath of leaves from the little court—as distinct from the piazza’s mingled sweat and dust.

“You have a pleasant little adytum there,” he said. “I suppose your conclave of eruditi meets there?”

“There, and no less in my shop,” Nello answered, leading him inward. The inner room held benches, a table with one manuscript and one printed book lying open in solemn capitals, a lute, a few oil sketches, and a model or two of hands and ancient masks. “My shop is a fitting haunt of the Muses—as you will acknowledge when you feel the sudden illumination and serene vigour that come with a clear chin. Ah—you can make that lute discourse, I perceive. I too have skill that way, though a serenata is useless when daylight discloses a visage like mine—no fresher than an apple that has stood the winter.”

He pointed to a sketch.

“Look at that: a fancy of Piero di Cosimo’s—strange, freakish painter—who says he saw it by long looking at a mouldy wall.”

Three masks were drawn: a drunken laughing satyr; a sorrowing Magdalen; and between them a rigid, cold Stoic face. They rested obliquely on the lap of a child whose cherub features rose above them with a gaze that promised something beyond all masks.

“A symbol,” said the Greek, and as he spoke he touched the lute strings so they murmured like distant water. “The child perhaps is the Golden Age—wanting neither worship nor philosophy. And the Golden Age can always come back so long as men are born as babies, and not into the world in cassock or furred mantle. Or the child may mean the wise philosophy of Epicurus, removed alike from the gross, the sad, and the severe.”

“Ah—everybody has his own interpretation,” Nello said. “Ask Piero himself and he will tell you his pictures are an appendix Messer Domeneddio has been pleased to make to the universe, and if a man doubts the meaning he had better inquire of Holy Church. He has been asked to paint the picture after the sketch; he puts his fingers in his ears and shakes his head—the fancy is past, he says. A strange animal, our Piero. But now all is ready for your initiation into the mysteries of the razor.”

“Mysteries they may well be called,” he continued, his spirits rising as he wrapped the stranger in the shaving cloth like a shroud. “Mysteries of Minerva and the Graces. I get the flower of men’s thoughts because I seize them in the first moment after shaving. Ah—you wince at the lather. It tickles the outlying limits of the nose, I admit.”

Sandro had brought the bowl; the brush moved in circles, whipping soap to a foam that held air like whipped egg whites, and the scent rose—clean fat and herb, sharp as rosemary bruised between fingers. The razor, when Nello laid it open, gave off a thin metallic note, a dry whisper of iron against air.

“That is why a barber’s shop is fit for wit and learning,” Nello went on. “Look at a druggist’s shop—there is a dull conclave at the sign of ‘The Moor’ that pretends to rival mine. What inspiration comes from nauseous decoctions? And you no sooner enter than you see a doctor of physic like a gigantic spider disguised in fur and scarlet waiting for prey—or blocking the doorway on a bony hack, inspecting saliva. Your chin a little elevated, if it please you: contemplate that angel blowing the trumpet from the ceiling. I had it painted expressly for the regulation of clients’ chins.”

He set the blade to work. The sound was intimate and precise—soft rasp, soft rasp—as if a dry leaf were being pared into obedience. Each stroke left behind a strip of bare skin cooling under air, and the young man’s face, freed, began to seem younger, clearer, as if a fog had been wiped from a window.

“Besides,” Nello said, “a druggist who decocts is a man of prejudices: he has poisoned people according to a system and must defend his system. A barber can be dispassionate; the only thing he stands by is the razor—unless he is an author. That was the flaw in Burchiello: he was a poet and had a prejudice about his own poetry. I escaped that. I saw early authorship is narrowing, in conflict with the liberal art of the razor which demands impartial affection for all men’s chins. Ecco, Messer—the outline of your chin and lip is as clear as a maiden’s. Now fix your mind on a knotty question—whether you are bound to spell Virgil with an i or an e—and say if you do not feel unwonted clearness on the point. Only, if you decide for the i, keep it to yourself till your fortune is made: the e has the stronger following in Florence.”

The Greek’s eyes glittered with amusement beneath the lather’s edge.

“I have it on the authority of our young Niccolò Machiavelli,” Nello added, “keen enough to discern il pelo nell’ uovo, and a great lover of delicate shaving though his beard is hardly of two years’ date—that no sooner do the hairs begin to push themselves than he perceives a certain grossness of apprehension creeping over him.”

“Suppose you let me look at myself,” the stranger said, laughing. “The happy effect on my intellect is perhaps obstructed by doubt as to the effect on my appearance.”

“Behold yourself,” said Nello, and held up a Venetian mirror from Murano, gleaming like still water. “The true nosce teipsum—as I have named it—compared with which steel or silver is mere darkness. See now how diligent shaving preserves the human outline, instead of making no distinction between your physiognomy and that of a bearded owl or Barbary ape. I have seen men whose beards invaded their cheeks so that one might pity them as victims of a brutalising chastisement out of Dante’s Inferno—if they had not strutted with strange triumph in their extravagant hairiness.”

The Greek watched his own newly bared face. It did indeed seem as if time had been pared away with the stubble; the cheekbones were more evident, the mouth more mobile—made for speech, and, perhaps, for persuasion.

“It seems to me,” he said, “you have taken away some of my capital with your razor—I mean a year or two of age, which might win me readier credit for my learning. Under the inspection of a patron whose vision has grown dim, I shall have a perilous resemblance to a maiden of eighteen in the disguise of hose and jerkin.”

“Not at all,” Nello said, now clipping the too extravagant curls with shears that made a crisp, satisfying bite. “Your proportions are not those of a maiden. And for your age—I remember seeing Angelo Poliziano begin his lectures when he had a younger beard than yours; and between ourselves his juvenile ugliness was not less signal than his precocious scholarship. Whereas you—no, no—your age is not against you.”

Then, as if turning a spit to show the other side, he lowered his voice.

“But between ourselves—let me hint that your being a Greek, though only an Apulian Greek, is not in your favour. Certain scholars hold your Greek learning is but a wayside degenerate plant until transplanted into Italian brains; and now there is such a plentiful crop of superior quality, your native teachers are mere propagators of degeneracy.”

He stepped back, satisfied.

“Ecco! Your curls are now the right proportion to neck and shoulders. Rise, Messer, and I will free you from the encumbrance of this cloth. Gnaffè! I almost advise you to retain the faded jerkin and hose a little longer—they give you the air of a fallen prince.”

“But the question is,” the young Greek said, leaning against the high back of a chair, meeting Nello’s admiring scrutiny with an anxious, practical look, “in what quarter I am to carry my princely air so as to rise from the fallen condition. If your Florentine patrons share this scholarly hostility to Greeks, I see not how your city can be a hospitable refuge for me, as you seemed to say.”

“Pian piano—not so fast,” said Nello, planting his thumbs in his belt and nodding to Sandro to set things in order. “I will not hide there is prejudice among us. And though, as a barber unsnared by authorship, I share no prejudices, I must admit Greeks are not always such pretty youngsters as yourself. Their erudition is often uncombed and unmannerly, encrusted with a barbarous Italian that makes their converse hardly more euphonious than a Tedesco in vinous loquacity.”

He went on, warming to the city’s own self-knowledge.

“And again—excuse me—we Florentines have liberal ideas about speech. We consider the tongue, which can flatter and promise so cleverly, must have been partly made for those purposes; and truth is a riddle for eyes and wit to discover, which it were mere spoiling of sport for the tongue to betray. Still, we have our limits: beyond them we call dissimulation treachery. But it is said of Greeks that their honesty begins at what is the hanging point with us—and that since the old Furies went to sleep, your Christian Greek has so easy a conscience he would make a stepping-stone of his father’s corpse.”

The stranger’s face flushed—anger rising as naturally as blood rises to a cut—so that for a moment the barber saw not a supple client but a young man with pride like salt on a raw tongue.

“Be not offended, bel giovane,” Nello hastened, good-nature recovering his footing. “I repeat only what I hear; my eloquence is the cream I skim off clients’ talk. Heaven forbid I should fetter my impartiality by entertaining an opinion. And as for the scholarly objection—” he twisted his mouth into a grimace that mocked the mockers, “—the fact is, you are heretics, Messer. Jealousy has nothing to do with it. If you would just change your opinion about leaven and alter your Doxology a little, our Italian scholars would think it a thousand years till they could give up their chairs to you. Yes, yes—chiefly religious scruple; and partly the authority of a great classic—Juvenal, is it not? He, I gather, had his bile stirred by the swarm of Greeks, as our Messer Angelo is fond of quoting about your incorrigible impudence—audacia perdita.”

“Pooh!” said the Greek, and his lightness returned like a musician finding his key again. “The passage is a compliment.

‘Ingenium velox, audacia perdita, sermo
Promptus, et Isaeo torrentior.’

A rapid intellect and ready eloquence may carry off a little impudence.”

“Assuredly,” Nello said. “And since you know Latin literature as well as Greek, you will not fall into Giovanni Argiropulo’s mistake, who ran full tilt against Cicero and pronounced him all but a pumpkin-head. Let me give you one bit of advice—trust a barber who has shaved the best chins and kept eyes and ears open for twenty years—oil your tongue well when you talk of the ancient Latin writers, and give it an extra dip when you talk of the modern. A wise Greek may win favour among us—witness our excellent Demetrio, loved by many and not hated immoderately even by the most renowned scholars.”

“I discern the wisdom of your advice so clearly,” the Greek said, his bright smile lighting his face as if the razor had not only cleared his chin but polished his expression, “that I’ll ask for a little more. Who, for example, would be the most likely patron for me? Is there a son of Lorenzo who inherits his tastes? Or any other wealthy Florentine specially addicted to purchasing antique gems? I have a fine Cleopatra cut in sardonyx, and one or two other intaglios and cameos—curious and beautiful—worthy to be added to the cabinet of a prince. Happily I fastened them within the lining of my doublet before I set out on my voyage. Moreover, I should like to raise a small sum for my present need on this ring of mine”—he drew the ring out and slipped it back on his finger—“if you can recommend me to any honest trafficker.”
