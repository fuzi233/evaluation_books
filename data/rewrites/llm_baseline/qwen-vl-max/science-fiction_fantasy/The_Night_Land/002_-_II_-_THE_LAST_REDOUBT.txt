Since Mirdath, my beloved, passed from this world and left me alone, I have known a sorrow so deep, so endless, that no words can hold it. I who once had all life in her love—whose laughter lit the days, whose presence made the world whole—now wander through a silence that chokes the soul. The pain of absence has become a constant shadow, a weight upon my chest, a fire beneath my bones.

Yet I write again—not for fame, nor for record, but because something within me stirs. A strange hope has risen, born not in waking thought, but in dreams that were not dreams at all. For in sleep, I have awakened into the future—into a time long after the sun has died, when the earth is cloaked in eternal night. And there, I have seen wonders beyond imagining: landscapes of fire and shadow, ancient watchers that stand like gods, and places where time itself seems to fold back upon itself.

In those visions, I have glimpsed her again—Mirdath—not as memory, but as if she lived still, somewhere across the dark ages. We meet in dreams, only to part; then reunite after centuries, each reunion more terrible than the last, yet each one filled with a joy that burns brighter than any star. And so I must tell what I have seen, even if the tale breaks my heart to speak it. Perhaps, by writing it down, I may ease my own burden—and perhaps, in some distant way, offer comfort to another who walks alone in grief.

Some will call this madness. Some will say it is fantasy. But let them read. Let them see what I have seen. For in these words lies not just a story—but a doorway into eternity.

I do not dream when I enter that future. I *wake*. I wake into a world without light, where the sky is black as ink, and the stars are gone. The sun is dead. Time has ended. And yet, in that darkness, I am not lost. I know everything. Not as knowledge learned, but as truth felt in the marrow of my being.

When I first opened my eyes in that far future, I stood on a high ledge, gazing out over a land of endless night. Around me was a blur of grey—like mist or smoke—until it lifted, revealing the vastness below. And then I saw: a world lit not by suns, but by fires of strange color—red, blue, silver—burning in pits and valleys, glowing from unseen sources. There were shapes in the dark: towering monstrosities, silent roads winding through emptiness, and great beings that moved without sound.

But I knew this place. Not as a traveler might know a city, but as a man knows his home. This was the Last Redoubt—the final refuge of humanity, a pyramid of cold metal rising seven miles into the night sky. And I was not merely an observer. I was a boy of seventeen, standing in one of its high embrasures, looking out through a glass that showed the world in sharp, terrifying detail.

And though I was young, I carried the memories of a lifetime—of living in this place, of studying in its halls, of watching the horrors of the Night Land from its walls. Yet beneath that memory, deeper still, was a ghost—a half-remembered past where the sun shone, where trees grew green, and where a woman named Mirdath sang to me under a sky full of light. That life felt like a dream now—yet it was real. And I, in this future, remembered it with a longing so fierce it brought tears to my eyes.

To the north-west, I saw the Red Pit, blazing with fire that rose against the chin of the North-West Watcher—a colossal shape, immovable and ancient, said to have watched since time began. Its name echoed in my mind: *"That which hath watched from the beginning, until the opening of the Gateway of Eternity."* The words belonged to Aesworpth, a poet from that age, though he lived far beyond our own. And as I looked, I realized—I could remember the sunlight of my present life. I could feel the warmth of a summer day, hear the birdsong, see the golden glow of dawn. And in that moment, I understood: I was both men. The youth in the Redoubt *was* me. And I, here now, was him. Two lives, one soul, bridged across time.

This knowledge stunned me. For in that future, I had no memory of this world—until the moment I woke. Then, like a flood, the past rushed in: the love, the loss, the beauty of a world now vanished. And with it came hunger—not for food, but for her. I ached for Mirdath, not as a memory, but as a need. And so, even in that future, I listened. I waited. I searched the dark for any sign of her return.

Around me stretched the Night Land—a realm of horror and mystery. To the right of the Red Pit lay the Vale of Red Fire, a long river of flame cutting through the black. Beyond it, the Plain of Blue Fire cast a cold, eerie glow. Farther still, on the edge of the unknown, stood a range of low volcanoes, their peaks glowing faintly, lighting up the Black Hills where seven lights burned steady—neither flickering nor fading. No one had ever reached them. No explorer returned from that direction. They were called the Seven Lights, and they were said to be older than the Earth itself.

Below, in the Great Library of the Redoubt, countless books recorded the journeys of those who dared leave the safety of the Pyramid. Most never came back. Those who did spoke of things too terrible to describe—beings that walked without sound, lands where the air hummed with unseen power, and creatures that fed not on flesh, but on fear.

And among the most feared of all was the House of Silence—perched on a hill far to the north. It had lights, yes, but no sound. Never a whisper, never a breath. And around it wound the Road Where the Silent Ones Walk—a path that emerged from the Unknown Lands, passing near the Place of the Ab-humans, where a green mist always hovered. No one knew where it led. But it was believed to be the only road built by human hands in that age—constructed long before the fall, when men still worked together, not in terror, but in purpose.

I stepped away from the embrasure and walked along the central roadway of the One Thousandth Plateau—six miles above the Night Land, wide enough for a hundred men to walk side by side. At the southern wall, I looked toward the Three Silver-fire Holes, glowing before the Thing That Nods, a monstrous form in the south-east. Near it loomed the South-East Watcher—an immense, squat creature that seemed carved from stone and shadow. Torches flared beside it, casting light on its lumbering head, yet it never stirred.

To the east, I heard a sound—low, deep, like thunder echoing through caverns. A laugh. Not human. Not animal. Something ancient and terrible. We called it “The Country Whence Comes The Great Laughter,” though none had ever seen it. The sound sent shivers through my spine, reminding me how small we were—how fragile our survival.

Then I turned my gaze to the Giants’ Pit, south of the Kilns. The giants—huge, slow-moving figures—climbed from the depths, their forms blurred by dancing shadows. And beyond them, a black headland jutted between the Valley of the Hounds and the giants. From its edge, strange things peered out—shapes that darted forward into the light, then vanished back into darkness. We called it The Headland From Which Strange Things Peer. And it had been that way for ages.

I followed the Road Where the Silent Ones Walk. And there, alone in the vast grey expanse, I saw one—a cloaked figure, moving slowly, silently, eyes fixed ahead. It did not turn. It did not look. It simply walked. And I knew, as I had been told, that these beings were harmless—if you stayed far away. But no one approached them. Their silence was not peaceful. It was profound. Like the quiet before a storm.

The road curved southward, passing the Dark Palace, then bending west around the mountain bulk of the Watcher of the South—the largest monster in sight, a living hill of darkness brooding over the Glowing Dome. It had come from the South Unknown Lands a million years ago, growing slowly, relentlessly, until it reached the Dome—a pale, radiant sphere that rose from the ground before it. And there, it stopped. As if something held it back. Some said it was magic. Others, that it was fate. But I believe there are forces in the Night Land not of evil, but of balance—powers that oppose the monsters, though they act in ways we cannot understand.

There were men in that age called Monstruwacans—watchers, scholars, keepers of records. Their duty was to observe the beasts, measure their movements, record every shift in the dark. And they had done so for millions of years. They tracked the Watcher’s approach, noting its slow creep toward the Redoubt. It had moved barely an inch per century—yet over millennia, it had crossed continents.

And so the Redoubt stood—not just as a fortress, but as a beacon. A last island of life in a sea of death. It had been built when the sun was fading, when the world was turning cold. The builders—millions strong—had fled into its towers, sealing themselves behind walls of iron and steel. It had a thousand three hundred and twenty floors, each thickened to withstand the horrors outside. Above, a tower housed the watchmen. Below, the lower half-mile was sealed—no one could enter or exit.

For a time, peace reigned. The electric circle, powered by the Earth-Current, surrounded the pyramid, repelling the creatures. The outer lands teemed with fallen races—half-human, half-beast—some of whom attacked, only to be slaughtered in droves. But eventually, the war ceased. The Redoubt became a city of silence, of study, of waiting.

Yet the darkness grew stronger. New monsters emerged—not from the earth, but from dimensions beyond. Drawn to the last spark of humanity, they gathered like sharks around a dying ship. And the Night Land became not just a place, but a living nightmare.

All of this—the history, the horrors, the loneliness—I carry in my soul. And I write it not to impress, but to bear witness. For in that future, I was not just a watcher. I was a man who loved. Who mourned. Who dreamed of reunion. And in those dreams, I found a glimmer of hope—not that the world would end, but that love, even across time, may endure.

And so I continue. Not knowing if anyone will read. Not knowing if this will matter. But I must tell it. Because in the silence of the Night Land, even the smallest voice can echo through eternity.
