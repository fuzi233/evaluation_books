As the winter evenings descended upon me, like a shroud that shrouded the city, I found solace in the humble act of writing. Within the confines of my small, candlelit sanctuary, I sought to drown my sorrows, and perhaps, find a glimmer of meaning in the world beyond. I had always been drawn to the simple, rustic tales of village life – of cottage maidens and bucolic yeomen, of windmills and rolling hills. My stories were woven with the threads of sentiment, a fashion of the times, where heroines with curly hair and dancing eyes inevitably met their untimely demise, their graves marked by snowdrops and primroses in some forsaken churchyard by the coast.

I poured my heart into these creations, crafting them with meticulous care, yet, no matter how hard I tried, they failed to find a home. I dispatched them to various journals, each one a carefully crafted package, sometimes accompanied by a curt rejection letter, at others by a plea for understanding. I offered them for free, hoping to entice the editors, and even resorted to sarcasm, but none were accepted. They returned to me, battered and bruised, like the fragile petals of a flower that refuses to wilt.

In my isolation, I began to feel like a failed artisan, my artistry rejected by the very world I sought to describe. Yet, I persisted, driven by a stubborn desire to create. I poured over books on botany, learning about the delicate beauty of snowdrops and primroses, and wove their stories into my tales. I longed for someone to appreciate my work, to see the beauty in the simplicity of my creations. Alas, my manuscripts remained unsold, a testament to my failure.

As the days turned into weeks, and the weeks into months, I grew weary of the rejection. I yearned for a break from the monotony of it all, for a chance to prove that my writing was more than just a hobby. Little did I know, my chance was approaching, in the form of an unexpected encounter that would change the course of my life forever.
