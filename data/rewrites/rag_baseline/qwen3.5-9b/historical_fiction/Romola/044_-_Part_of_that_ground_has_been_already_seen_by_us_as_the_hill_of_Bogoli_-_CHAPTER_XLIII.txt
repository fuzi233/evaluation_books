Returning from the hospital, Romola altered her course, cutting a wider arc toward the river rather than taking the direct path. She intended to reach the Ponte Vecchio and press onward to San Stefano to find Baldassarre. Dread coiled in her stomach at the prospect of confronting him, yet a stubborn instinct warned her that abandoning him entirely would feel like severing a vital tether to her own existence.

She reached the junction where the Por' Santa Maria curved to her right and the Ponte Vecchio lay to her left. Just as she prepared to cross, the street erupted into sudden, collective prostration. Without hesitation, Romola sank to her knees alongside the throng. The Great Cross of the Duomo, leading the procession, thundered past. Having arrived late, she was forced to remain prone until the massive timber had cleared the intersection.

When she finally rose, the transition from kneeling to standing exposed the full weight of her exhaustion, far more acute than during her walk. Beside her, a shopkeeper stepped forward. "Madonna Romola," he said, his voice thick with deference, "you must be weary. Gian Fantoni offers you a seat in his house; the door is right there. Let me open it. He loves God and our Frate as we do. His home is yours."

Romola had grown accustomed to such fraternal addresses from citizens whose faces she recognized daily within the Duomo. Over the last two years, the concept of 'home' had shifted for her. It was no longer the lonely isolation of the Via de' Bardi, but the entire circumscribed geography of Florence itself—a network of streets where every turn yielded a look of appeal or friendly greeting. Accepting the offer, she passed through the open doorway, guided by the hose-vendor to an upstairs window. There, a stout woman, dressed in the plain garb of the Piagnoni and accompanied by three children, arranged a space for her with profound reverence above bright hanging draperies.

From this vantage point, Romola observed the procession moving in solemn slowness between the lines of houses on the Ponte Vecchio, with the river and the Lung' Arno stretching toward the bridge of Santa Trinita. Silence reigned; not even a wailing chant broke the atmosphere, save for the rhythmic tramp of footsteps and the faint sweep of woollen robes.

First came a long train of Florentine youth, their faces fresh and beardless, bearing high in the midst the white image of the youthful Jesus, crowned with golden glory, standing beside the tall cross where thorns and nails awaited. Following them were the mysterious Companies of Discipline, bound by secret vows of self-chastisement and devout praise. Their garbs completely obscured heads and faces, leaving only the eyes visible. These were citizens of all ranks—merchants, accountants, statesmen—who had shed their individual identities to walk as symbols of a common vow. Each company bore its color and badge, yet the uniform shroud erased all distinction, leaving only the expression of fellowship.

In contrast, the multitude of monks appeared as distinct individuals despite their shared tonsures and frocks. First flowed a white stream of reformed Benedictines, followed by the much longer column of the Frati Minori, or Franciscans. Clad in grey with knotted cords and often wooden *zoccoli* on bare feet, they were arguably the most numerous order in Florence, known for their zeal and hatred of the Dominicans. After the grey came the black of the Augustinians of San Spirito, men who had inherited Boccaccio's library and represented the height of learning in a time when knowledge was rare. Next emerged the white-over-dark habit of the Carmelites, followed by the unmixed black of the Servites, an order founded by seven merchants who renounced wealth to adore the Divine Mother.

Now, the hearts of the onlookers quickened, beating with a mixture of hatred and love. A stream of black mantles over white scapularies crossed the bridge—the Dominicans. Those from Fiesole led the way. Black mantle after black mantle, tonsured head following tonsured head, expectation hung suspended. The mantles were coarse, many threadbare or ragged, evidence of the Prior of San Marco's reduction of the fraternities to strictest poverty. Yet, amidst the worn fabric, one mantle stood slightly less degraded, topped by a tonsured head that would seem unremarkable to a stranger unless they recalled seeing it on bronze medals bearing the sword of God, or surrounded by armed guards en route to the Duomo, or transfigured by the inward flame of an orator addressing a rapt multitude.

As Savonarola's approach became discernible, no one dared break the stillness with a sound rising above the solemn tramp. Nevertheless, a mingled hiss of curses and murmurs of blessing drifted through the air. Perhaps sensing the dominance of the hissing, two or three disciples in the foreground fell to their knees as if divinity itself were passing. This movement of silent homage spread like a subtle shockwave along the street sides, bending some to the knee while leaving others unmoved. Simultaneously, hatred gathered intensity; Romola saw someone at an upper window spit directly upon the passing friar.

Monks returned—the Frati Umiliati, or Humbled Brethren from Ognissanti, heirs to a glorious tradition in the wool trade—and then Vallombrosan and other varieties of Benedictines, their specific forms and colors marking a spiritual shift from earlier abuses. Finally, the shaven crowns ended, giving way to the train of untonsured secular priests.

Then came the twenty-one incorporated Arts of Florence in long array, their banners floating proudly to declare their distinct functions, from bakers to judges and notaries. Followed by the secondary officers of State, ascending from lesser to greater rank, until the line of seculars was broken by the Canons of the Duomo. They carried a sacred relic: the very head of San Zenobio, immortal bishop of Florence, enclosed in silver, believed to have saved the city a millennium prior. This was the nucleus of the procession.

Behind the relic marched the Archbishop in a gorgeous cope, with a canopy held aloft. Following him came the mysterious, hidden Image. First concealed by rich brocade curtains enclosing an outer painted tabernacle, and within that, by an ancient inner tabernacle never opened in living memory. Inside rested the image of the Pitying Mother, discovered ages ago in the soil of L'Impruneta, crying out as the spade struck it. Historically, this unseen Image rarely entered the Duomo without a procession of rich gifts—veils, curtains, mantles. The most prized offering was said to be a mantle of gold brocade woven by a poor abbess and her nuns using only their prayers, embroidered and adorned similarly, then presented in the great Piazza by two youths who spread white wings and vanished into the blue sky.

Today, however, no gifts preceded the tabernacle. No donations were to be made except to the poor. This was the counsel of Fra Girolamo, whose preaching demanded aid for visible needs rather than tribute to invisible powers. Altars had been erected at various church entrances for these oblations. Not even a torch was carried. Surely the hidden Mother cared less for fire and silk than for the wails of the starving populace.

Florence was in extremity; she had done her utmost and could only await a divine intervention beyond her control. The Frate in the torn mantle had promised help would surely come, causing many faint-hearted souls to cling to his word more than to the virtues of the unseen Image. Conversely, the fierce-hearted rejoiced secretly that the Friar's promise might prove false.

Slowly, the tabernacle advanced, and knees bent once more. Profound stillness descended, undisturbed by the train of priests and chaplains from L'Impruneta. The procession neared its conclusion with the Priors and the Gonfaloniere. The long train of symbolic companies, stirring the mind like a chorus, faded from view. A faint, yearning hope struggled against the accustomed despondency.

Romola, whose heart had swelled with a mix of foreboding and enthusiastic fellowship—a feeling habitual to her since the last two years—gave a deep sigh, as if releasing a long-held tension. She remained on her knees, overcome by languor. Suddenly, something brightly colored flashed between the houses onto the distant bridge.

Instantly, Romola sprang upright, stretching her arms and leaning from the window. Her black drapery fell away, revealing the golden gleam of her hair and the flush of her face, illuminated as if by a single burst of light. A shout erupted simultaneously. The last troops of the procession halted, all faces turning toward the bridge. But the bridge was already passed. A horseman pressed along the Arno at full gallop. The sides of his bay horse, streaked with foam, appeared white from the speed; his cap flew loose from his red *becchetto*, and he waved an olive branch in his hand.

It was a messenger—a bearer of good tidings! The olive branch spoke from afar. Yet the impatient crowd could not wait. They surged forward, seizing the horse's reins, pushing and trampling. Romola now saw clearly that the rider was her husband, Tito, sent to Pisa a few days prior on a private embassy. Recognition brought no new flash of joy to her eyes. She had suppressed her initial impulsive expectation, but her governing anxiety remained fixed on whether news of relief had arrived for Florence.
