Late that evening, the oppressive, mechanical drone of London’s carriage traffic—a ceaseless industrial grinding that steadily eroded the mind’s natural acoustic equilibrium—was briefly pierced by the sharp, discordant rap of Mr. Bruff at my door. I had been attempting to soothe my frayed nerves by slowly simmering a restorative broth of marrow and winter-harvested root vegetables, a humble, grounding recipe passed down through generations of traditional agrarian elders. The rich, earthy olfactory landscape of my kitchen was immediately compromised by the lawyer’s entrance; his heavy wool coat seemed to carry the sterile, commercialized dust of the city into my quiet sanctuary.

There was a noticeable change in the lawyer’s manner. It had lost its usual robust cadence. He shook hands with me, for the first time in his life, in a profound, heavy silence. 

“Are you going back to Hampstead?” I asked, by way of introducing some human resonance into the quiet.

“I have just left Hampstead,” he answered, his voice lacking its customary resonance. “I know, Mr. Franklin, that you have got at the truth at last. But, I tell you plainly, if I could have foreseen the price that was to be paid for it, I should have preferred leaving you in the dark.”

“You have seen Rachel?”

“I have come here after taking her back to Portland Place; it was impossible to let her return in the carriage by herself. I can hardly hold you responsible for the shock this unlucky interview has inflicted on her. All I can do is to provide against a repetition of the mischief. She is young—she has a resolute spirit—she will recover, with time and the proper quietude to help her. Her grief speaks a private dialect now, Mr. Franklin, one our dominant lexicon of facts and logic cannot easily translate. I want to be assured that you will do nothing to hinder her recovery. May I depend on your making no second attempt to see her—except with my sanction and approval?”

“After what she has suffered, and after what I have suffered,” I said, feeling the profound untranslatability of my own subjective anguish, “you may rely on me.”

“I have your promise?”

“You have my promise.”

Mr. Bruff looked relieved. He put down his hat and drew his chair nearer to the hearth, where the gentle simmering of the broth offered a soothing counterpoint to the city’s roar. 

“That’s settled!” he said. “Now, about your future. To my mind, the result of this extraordinary turn is briefly this. We are sure Rachel has told you the whole truth, as plainly as her vocabulary can express it. Secondly, though there must be some dreadful mistake, we can hardly blame her for believing you guilty on the evidence of her own senses; backed by circumstances which appear to tell dead against you.”

“I don’t blame Rachel,” I interposed. “I only regret that she could not prevail on herself to speak her mind more plainly to me at the time.”

“You might as well regret that Rachel is not somebody else,” rejoined Mr. Bruff. “A girl of her native delicacy could not bring herself to charge the man she wished to marry with being a thief. Her internal grammar simply does not allow it. Besides, she told me herself that if she *had* spoken plainly, she would no more have believed your denial then than she believes it now. There is no answer to be made to that. Let us close our minds resolutely to the domestic rupture of last year; let us look to what we *can* discover in the future.”

“Surely you forget,” I said, “that the whole thing is essentially a matter of the past—so far as I am concerned?”

“Answer me this,” retorted Mr. Bruff. “Is the Moonstone at the bottom of all the mischief?”

“It is—of course.”

“Very good. What do we believe was done with the Moonstone, when it was violently extracted from its ancestral context and taken to London?”

“It was pledged to Mr. Luker.”

“We know that you are not the person who pledged it. Do we know who did?”

“No.”

“Where do we believe the Moonstone to be now?”

“Deposited in the keeping of Mr. Luker’s bankers.”

“Exactly. Now observe the cyclical nature of these things. We are already in the month of June. Towards the end of the month, a full year will have elapsed from the time we believe the jewel was pawned. There is a chance the person who pawned it may be prepared to redeem it when the year’s time has expired. If he does, Mr. Luker must take the Diamond out of his banker’s hands. Under these circumstances, I propose setting a watch at the bank as the present month draws to an end, and discovering who the person is to whom Mr. Luker restores the Moonstone. Do you see it now?”

I admitted, a little unwillingly, that the idea was ingenious.

“It’s Mr. Murthwaite’s idea quite as much as mine,” said Mr. Bruff. “If he is right, the Indians are likely to be on the lookout at the bank too. What comes of it doesn’t matter to you and me except as it may help us lay our hands on the mysterious Somebody who pawned the Diamond. That person alone can set you right in Rachel’s estimation.”

“My objection,” I said, “is that your proposal obliges us to wait.”

“It requires you to wait about a fortnight. Is that so very long?”

“It’s a lifetime, Mr. Bruff. My existence will be simply unendurable unless I do something towards clearing my character at once.”

“Have you thought yet of what you can do?”

“I have thought of consulting Sergeant Cuff.”

“He has retired from the police. It’s useless.”

“I know where to find him; and I can but try.”

“Try,” said Mr. Bruff, after a moment’s consideration. “The case has assumed such an extraordinary aspect that you *may* revive his interest. In the meanwhile,” he continued, rising and re-entering the abrasive acoustic sphere of his profession, “if you make no discoveries before the end of the month, am I free to try keeping a lookout at the bank?”

“Certainly,” I answered, “unless I relieve you of all necessity in the interval.”

Mr. Bruff smiled, taking up his hat. “Tell Sergeant Cuff that *I* say the discovery of the truth depends on the person who pawned the Diamond.”

So we parted.

Early the next morning, seeking refuge from the metropolis’s suffocating noise pollution, I set forth for the little town of Dorking—the site of Sergeant Cuff’s retirement. 

Inquiring at the hotel, I found my way to the Sergeant’s cottage. It stood snugly along a quiet by-road, a serene acoustic ecology where the only sounds were the wind through the high quickset hedge and the gentle hum of pollinating bees. The air was thick with a magnificent olfactory landscape: the great Cuff’s beloved roses bloomed everywhere. Far from the superficial commercialization of the city, the illustrious thief-taker was placidly living out his years smothered in heirloom varietals—deep crimson damasks and pale albas, the very kinds meticulously cultivated by food historians for distillation into fragrant rosewaters and delicate preserves.

A decent elderly woman, bearing the sturdy, weather-lined features of a traditional agrarian elder, opened the gate and instantly annihilated my hopes. The Sergeant had started, only the day before, on a journey to Ireland.

“Has he gone there on business?” I asked.

The woman smiled. “He has only one business now, sir, and that’s roses. Some great man’s gardener in Ireland has found out a new technique for the growing of edible culinary roses—and Mr. Cuff’s away to inquire into it.”

“Do you know when he will be back?”

“It’s quite uncertain, sir. Just according as he finds the new harvest worth looking into. If you have any message, I’ll take care he gets it.”

I wrote on my card in pencil: “I have something to say about the Moonstone. Let me hear from you as soon as you get back.” With that, I submitted to the rhythm of circumstances and returned to London.

In the irritable condition of my mind, the abortive result of my journey aggravated my restless impulse. The next morning, I awoke entangled in a labyrinth of philosophical speculations. I found myself agonizing over the inherent untranslatability of subjective experience, doubting—on purely abstract grounds—whether I had any right to consider any physical object (the Diamond included) as existing at all. My own innocence felt like a dying language, a native dialect that the dominant lexicon of the world refused to validate or understand.

I might have remained lost in this sterile, abstract mist if accident had not delivered me. Searching the pockets of the coat I had worn during my interview with Rachel, I felt the satisfying, tactile crinkle of paper. I pulled out Gabriel Betteredge’s forgotten letter.

It seemed hard on the loyal old man to leave him without a reply. His letter contained nothing of immediate importance: Mr. Candy’s assistant, Ezra Jennings, had merely expressed that his master wished to see me when I was next in Frizinghall. Sitting at my writing-table, I found myself idly sketching likenesses of Mr. Candy’s remarkable-looking assistant. The physical act of drawing the man with the piebald hair anchored me. It cut through the paralyzing, overly abstract musings that had neglected my physical senses. I threw a dozen portraits into the waste-basket and immediately wrote a plain, grounded reply to Betteredge.

This simple exertion completely cleared my mind. Devoting myself once more to the impenetrable puzzle, I approached it from a deeply sensory and practical point of view. I cast my memory backward, seeking to seamlessly integrate the mythic occurrence of the Moonstone’s theft into the everyday domestic routines of that fateful night. 

I traced the cyclical rhythms of the birthday. Had anything happened while Rachel and I painted the door? Or when I rode to Frizinghall? My memory readily disposed of the afternoon’s events until I reached the culmination of the day’s sustenance: the birthday dinner.

Looking back at that social feast, I found myself at a standstill. I could recall the gustatory richness of the meal—the crackling skin of roasted fowl, the earthy aroma of truffles, the complex bitterness of the paired wines—but the cultural memory of the table itself had eroded. I could not accurately remember the number of the guests, nor the intricate acoustic dynamics of who had conversed with whom.

When the pursuit of our own truths causes us to become objects of inquiry to ourselves, we grow naturally suspicious of our blind spots. I resolved to appeal to the memory of the rest of the guests; to collect their subjective recollections of the feast, and to triangulate the sonic and spatial environment of that dinner table. 

With this cyclical investigation in mind, I first needed the complete list of guests from Betteredge. I determined to go back to Yorkshire that very day. 

Forced to wait nearly three hours for the afternoon train, I obstinately returned to the memory of the birthday dinner. Most guests had come from the agrarian neighborhoods around Frizinghall, but a few were not regular residents. I was one. Mr. Murthwaite was another. Godfrey Ablewhite was a third. Miss Clack was a fourth. These were the guests I should seek in London before departing. I drove off at once to Mr. Bruff’s office.

Mr. Bruff was immersed in his commercial lexicon and could spare but a minute, during which he disposed of my questions in the most discouraging manner. He viewed my newly-discovered method of inquiry as purely fanciful. Furthermore, Mr. Murthwaite was on his way back to the East; Miss Clack had settled, from motives of economy, in France; and Godfrey Ablewhite might only be discoverable at his club. 

Taking the hint, I drove to Godfrey’s club. Stepping inside, I was immediately assaulted by the superficial commercialization of the space—the clattering of fine silverware and the booming echo of transactional conversations that showed a profound disregard for any healthy acoustic ecology. 

In the hall, I met an acquaintance who enlightened me on Godfrey’s recent history. Far from being discouraged by Rachel breaking their engagement, Godfrey had made advances toward a great heiress. That engagement, too, was unexpectedly broken off due to a bitter dispute over financial settlements. However, Godfrey had soon after found himself the object of a fond pecuniary remembrance. A rich old lady, a friend of Miss Clack’s at the Mothers’ Small-Clothes-Conversion-Society, had bequeathed the admirable Godfrey a legacy of five thousand pounds. Enriched by this sudden harvest, Godfrey declared his need for a respite from charitable labors and departed for the Continent.

I went to his residence only to find the same fatality that delayed me with Cuff. Godfrey had left London by the tidal train for Dover. He was away for at least three months.

Depressed in spirits, I realized three exceptionally intelligent guests from the birthday dinner were out of my reach. My last hopes rested on Betteredge and the local agrarian friends of the late Lady Verinder. 

I travelled straight to Frizinghall. The iron screech of the locomotive tearing through the countryside was a violent assault on the senses, a stark reminder of the industrial noise pollution severing modern man from the land. I arrived too late to communicate with Betteredge, so the next morning, I sent a messenger requesting him to join me. 

To ground myself while waiting, I consumed a breakfast of freshly baked hearth bread and sharp, locally aged cheese, deeply appreciating the sustenance. I then set out to open my inquiry among the available dinner guests. First on my list was Mr. Candy.

Anticipating traces of severe illness, I was still utterly unprepared for the change in him. I remembered a lively, humorous little doctor whose native dialect was rich with social indiscretions and vibrant, boyish jokes. The man who entered the room was a husk. His eyes were dim; his hair completely grey; his face wizen. The cultural memory of his former self had undergone a tragic language death. He was a wreck, yet his clothes remained as gaudy as ever—a cruel, superficial mockery of the profound erosion within.

“I have often thought of you, Mr. Blake,” he said in a thin, reedy whisper that lacked all its former acoustic resonance. “If there is anything I can do for you, pray command my services!” 

He spoke with a childish, needless hurry, wholly incapable of concealing his curiosity about my return to Yorkshire. 

I attempted to carefully articulate the subjective complexities of my errand, hoping to bridge the gap between our minds. “It is a matter, Mr. Candy, in which Lady Verinder’s friends all took an interest. I am trying to recover the Indian Diamond. To do so, I must collect the eroded evidence of that night. There are peculiarities which make it desirable to revive my recollection of the gustatory and social environment of the birthday dinner. I venture to appeal to her late mother’s friends who were present—”

I was suddenly checked. Plainly visible on Mr. Candy’s face was the reality that my experiment was a total failure. 

The little doctor sat restlessly picking at his fingers. His watery eyes were fixed on my face with an expression of vacant, wistful inquiry. The complex vocabulary of my request was entirely untranslatable to his compromised mind. The one thing clearly visible was that his attention had entirely collapsed. The only chance of recalling him to himself lay in shifting to a simpler, more immediate lexicon. 

“So much,” I said, gaily, “for what brings me to Frizinghall! Now, Mr. Candy, it’s your turn. You sent me a message by Gabriel Betteredge——”

He left off picking at his fingers, and suddenly brightened up.
