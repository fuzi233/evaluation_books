Bartolommeo Scala, the secretary of the Florentine Republic, resided in a stately palace near the Porta Pinti, now known as the Casa Gherardesca. His coat of arms, an azure ladder crossing a golden field with the motto *Gradatim* above the gate, declared to all who passed that the miller's son had climbed to his high station solely through his own exertions—a truth he wore with unashamed pride. The secretary was a man of vanity and pomp, yet fundamentally honest; he believed so deeply in his own worth that he saw no need for pretense. By this time, he had ascended to the very top rung of his ladder. For twenty years he had served as secretary, delivering his orations from the *ringhiera*, the balcony of the Old Palace, before princely guests while the Marzocco, the lion of the republic, wore a golden crown and the populace shouted, "Viva Messer Bartolommeo!" He had served as an ambassador to Rome, earning titles such as Titular Senator, Apostolical Secretary, and Knight of the Golden Spur. Eight years prior, he had become Gonfaloniere, the ultimate ambition of any Florentine citizen. Along with his honors, his wealth had grown, and so had his gout, a common affliction of successful mortals. Often, the Knight of the Golden Spur was forced to sit with his cushioned heel helpless beneath the magnificent loggia he had constructed, overlooking the vast gardens behind his home.

It was on such a day that he granted the requested audience to Tito Melema. The May sun warmed the flowers and grass beyond the pleasant shade of the loggia. Scala had discarded his heavy silk *lucco* for a light, loose mantle over his tunic. Seated beside him were his beautiful daughter, Alessandra, and her husband, the Greek soldier-poet Marullo. On the opposite side sat two friends, distinguished enough to be respectable but not so illustrious as to dominate the conversation, making them ideal listeners. Yet, despite the gout and the sunny afternoon, Messer Bartolommeo's felicity was far from complete. It was embittered by a pile of papers before him, mostly a correspondence between himself and the scholar Poliziano.

It was a peculiar foible of that era—incredible as it may seem today—to recite quarrels and offer scholarly visitors the full text of lengthy disputes. This was not the first time Scala had sought the candid opinions of his friends regarding the balance of right and wrong in a dozen Latin letters exchanged with Poliziano, all stemming from playful epigrams. It was the tale of a typical, petty quarrel, one that served precisely as the thorn necessary to prick the sluggish horse of friendship, as Nello had once noted.

Poliziano, having been a rejected suitor for the hand of Scala's daughter, kept a sharp, learned tooth ready against the prosperous secretary who had refused to marry the greatest scholar of the age to his daughter. Scala was a meritorious public servant and a lucky man—qualities naturally exasperating to an offended academic. However, Scala also possessed an itch for authorship, albeit a poor one. He was one of those delightful individuals who, sitting in gout-ridden slippers, penned poetic trifles purely for amusement, sending them to friends who acted as the literary periodicals of the fifteenth century. Scala had many friends eager to praise his work, such as Ficino and Landino, who found his Latin prose elegant and masculine. The terrifying Joseph Scaliger, who would later declare him totally ignorant of Latinity, was still a century away. But when does the coquetry of superfluous authorship ever find contentment with the ready praise of friends? That critical, haughty Poliziano—a fellow-browser who was far from amiable—had to be shown that the solid secretary possessed a fertile mind for verse, hinting at the potential greatness he might achieve if he were not so consumed by affairs.

What an ineffable moment it is when the man you secretly hate sends you a Latin epigram with a false gender, hendecasyllables with a questionable elision, or poetic figures that are manifest solecisms. That moment had arrived for Poliziano. The secretary had poked his soft head out of the official shell, and the terrible lurking crab descended upon him. Poliziano had used the freedom of a friend to correct Scala's mistake in gendering the *culex* (the mosquito, ubiquitous on the banks of the Arno) as feminine. Scala replied with a bad joke in suitable Latin verses, referencing Poliziano's unsuccessful suit. Poliziano found the verses charming and facetious, though he pitied the fact that they were seriously incorrect. Since Scala claimed to have written them imitating a Greek epigram, Poliziano, on such friendly terms, enclosed a Greek epigram of his own on the same insect. It contained a lively conceit about Venus, Cupid, and the mosquito, based on the zoological fact that the gnat, like Venus, was born from the waters.

In reply, Scala begged to state that his verses were never intended for a scholar with such delicate olfactory senses as Poliziano, the nearest living man to the perfection of the ancients, whose taste was so fastidious that even sturgeon would seem insipid to him. He defended his verses, noting they were written hastily without correction, intended merely as a distraction during the summer heat for himself and friends satisfied with mediocrity. Unlike others who courted publicity through booksellers, Scala was content to keep his work private. Furthermore, he admitted he barely had enough Greek to understand the sense of the epigram Poliziano had sent, let alone taste its elegance. Yet, the epigram was Poliziano's; what more needed to be said? Still, as a postscript, he feared Poliziano's comparison of the gnat to Venus, due to its watery origin, was problematic. Venus might be offended; on the other hand, unless the poet alluded to the doctrine of Thales, the cold, damp origin seemed doubtful for a creature so fond of warmth. Perhaps a fish was a better comparison, or, regarding flight, an eagle; considering the darkness, a bat or an owl would be a less obscure parallel.

Here was a golden opportunity for Poliziano. He wrote that he was unaware Scala had any question of sturgeon, but rather of frogs and gudgeons. He made short work of Scala's defense of his Latin and mangled him terribly over the stupid criticisms he had ventured on the Greek epigram kindly forwarded as a model. These were wretched cavils! Regarding the damp origin of the gnat, there was the authority of Virgil himself, who called it the "alumnus of the waters." As for Scala's comments on the fish, the eagle, and the rest, it was *nihil ad rem*; just because an eagle could fly higher did not mean the gnat could not fly at all. Poliziano was ashamed to dwell on such trivialities and swell a gnat into an elephant, but he added that he had nothing deceitful or double about him, nor was he to be caught by the false blandishments of those who slandered him in his absence. He agreed with a Homeric sentiment on this head, furnishing a Greek quotation to serve as powder for his bullet.

The quarrel could not end there. The logic grew harder to follow, but the secretary became more pompously self-asserting, while the scholarly poet's temper turned more venomous. Poliziano had been generously willing to hold up a mirror, hoping the too-inflated secretary, beholding his own likeness, might cease defending his ignorant Latin against ancient authorities confirmed by centuries of consent. Unless, indeed, he designed to sink in literature as he rose in honors, so that men of letters might feel themselves his equals. In return, Poliziano was begged to examine Scala's writings; nowhere would he find a more devout admiration of antiquity. The secretary was ashamed of the age in which he lived and blushed for it. Some wanted their works exalted to the level of divine monuments, but Scala could not oblige them. As for the honors offensive to the envious, they had been well earned, witnessed by his life since coming to Florence in penury. The elegant scholar replied that he was not surprised Scala found the Age distasteful, since he himself was so distasteful to the Age. Indeed, he had accurately called Scala a "branny monster," formed from the off-scourings of monsters, born amidst the refuse of a mill, and eminently worthy of the long-eared office of turning paternal millstones (*in pistrinis sordibus natus et quidem pistrino dignissimus*).

It was not without reference to Tito's appointed visit that these papers were brought out that day. Here was a new Greek scholar whose accomplishments were to be tested, and on nothing did Scala more desire a dispassionate opinion from persons of superior knowledge than on that Greek epigram of Poliziano's. After sufficient introductory talk concerning Tito's travels, a survey of the gems, and an easy transition from the lamented Lorenzo's eagerness for ancient art to classical tastes in general, it was inevitable to mention Poliziano. He was a man of eminent ability, indeed, but a little too arrogant—assuming to be a Hercules whose office was to destroy all literary monstrosities of the age, writing letters to his elders without signing them as if they were miraculous revelations. Were not his own criticisms often questionable and his tastes perverse? He was fond of saying pungent things about men who thought they wrote like Cicero because they ended every sentence with "esse videtur," yet while boasting of freedom from servile imitation, did he not fall into the other extreme, chasing strange words and affected phrases? Even in his much-lauded *Miscellanea*, was every point tenable? Tito, who had just looked into the *Miscellanea*, found so much agreeable to the secretary that he showed himself quite worthy to be made a judge in the notable correspondence concerning the *culex*. Here was the Greek epigram which Poliziano undoubtedly thought the finest in the world, though he had pretended to believe the "transmarini," the Greeks themselves, would make light of it. Had he not been unintentionally speaking the truth in his false modesty?

Tito was ready and scarified the epigram to Scala's content. Oh, wise young judge! He could doubtless appreciate satire even in the vulgar tongue. Scala, an excellent man who never sought publicity through booksellers, was never unprovided with "hasty uncorrected trifles" as a sort of sherbet for a visitor on a hot day, or a cordial if the weather were cold. He had a few little matters in the shape of Sonnets turning on well-known foibles of Poliziano's, which he would not like to pursue further but which might amuse the company.

Enough: Tito took his leave under an urgent invitation to return. His gems were interesting, especially the agate with the *lusus naturae* inside—a wonderful semblance of Cupid riding a lion—and the "Jew's stone," with a lion-headed serpent encased within. Scala agreed to buy both; the latter as a reinforcement of his preventives against the gout, which gave him such severe twinges that it was plain he needed rings of rare virtue and an amulet worn close under the right breast. But Tito was assured that he himself was more interesting than his gems. He had won his way to the Scala Palace by the recommendation of Bardo de' Bardi, who was indeed Scala's old acquaintance and a worthy scholar, despite his tendency to overvalue himself—a frequent foible in the secretary's friends. Yet, he must come again on the ground of his own manifest accomplishments.

The interview could hardly have ended more auspiciously for Tito. As he walked out at the Porta Pinti, laughing lightly at the affair of the *culex*, he felt that fortune could hardly mean to turn her back on him again at present, since she had taken him by the hand in this decided way.
