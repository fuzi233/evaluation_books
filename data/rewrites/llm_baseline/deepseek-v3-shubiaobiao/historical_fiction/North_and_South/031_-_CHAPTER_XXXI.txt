*"Show not that manner, and these features all,
The serpent’s cunning, and the sinner’s fall?"*

A damp October morning settled over Milton, its fog clinging to the streets like a shroud. There were no golden autumn hues here—only a dull haze that the sun, when it dared appear, could scarcely pierce. Margaret moved through the house with heavy steps, assisting Dixon in the grim task of setting things in order. Her eyes burned with unshed tears, but there was no time for surrender. Her father and brother leaned upon her; while they grieved, she must act. Even the funeral arrangements seemed to fall to her hands.

By the time the fire crackled to life and the kettle hummed on the hearth, the room was as warm and bright as she could make it. Yet the cheerfulness felt cruel against the weight in her chest. Kneeling by the sofa, she pressed her face into the cushions, stifling her sobs—until Dixon’s hand touched her shoulder.

"Come, Miss Hale—you mustn’t give way now. Who else is to manage things? There’s the funeral to see to, and who’s to come, and where it’s to be held—and Master Frederick’s beside himself, and your father wandering like a ghost. I know it’s hard, my dear, but death comes for us all."

Margaret swallowed the lump in her throat. No platitude could measure this loss. Still, Dixon’s rare tenderness moved her. She forced a smile and went to summon her father and brother.

Mr. Hale drifted into the room as if half-asleep, his gaze hollow. Frederick entered with false energy, but the moment he met Margaret’s eyes, his composure shattered. Through breakfast, she filled the silence with trivial chatter, desperate to distract them from the memory of their last meal—when every sound had been a potential summons from the sickroom.

Later, she approached her father about the funeral. He agreed to every suggestion, though his nods were vacant. Only when she turned to leave did he stir.

"Ask Mr. Bell," he murmured.

"Mr. Bell of Oxford?"

"He was my groomsman."

The association struck her. "I’ll write today," she promised.

The hours blurred into a haze of tasks. By evening, Dixon confessed her fears: Mr. Hale’s stunned grief was dangerous. She had roused him with a warning—Frederick was no longer safe in Milton.

"I met a man from Southampton," Dixon explained. "George Leonards, a scoundrel if ever there was one. Sailed with Frederick on the *Orion*—though whether he was there during the mutiny, I can’t say."

Margaret tensed. "Did he recognize you?"

"Only because I was fool enough to say his name first. He had the gall to mock Frederick’s troubles—claimed there’s a hundred-pound reward for his capture. As if that wretch could wash his own sins clean by betraying another!"

Margaret’s pulse quickened. "You didn’t tell him anything—about us?"

"Not a word. But he’s in Milton, and he’d sell his own mother for coin."

Frederick must go. The thought was a knife to Margaret’s heart. He had become their anchor—his laughter, his care for their mother, his shared sorrow binding them tighter. Now he would vanish again, leaving silence behind.

She found him by the fire, his usual brightness dimmed but his grief less raw. He kissed her forehead.

"You look exhausted," he said softly. "Rest awhile."

"The worst of it is having nothing to do but think," she whispered, but she let him tuck a shawl around her.

As they talked in hushed tones, she relayed Dixon’s news. Frederick’s lips thinned.

"I’d like to thrash that snake. He was a coward aboard ship—currying favor with the officers. If he knew I was here—"

"You must leave," Mr. Hale interrupted, his voice frayed. "It’s not safe."

Margaret bit her lip. "But is there no way to clear your name? For Miss Barbour’s sake, shouldn’t you try?"

Frederick scoffed. "Courts-martial weigh authority over truth. And who’d read a pamphlet defending a mutineer?"

"Then consult a lawyer," she pressed. "Someone honorable—Mr. Henry Lennox. He’d help."

Frederick considered it. "If I leave for London tomorrow, I could meet him before sailing."

Mr. Hale shuddered. "Every hour you stay risks discovery."

Margaret scribbled a note to Lennox, Frederick peering over her shoulder. When a lock of dark hair slipped from his pocketbook, his grin was boyish.

"You’ll see her whole someday," he said. "No snippet could do her justice."

As the firelight flickered, Margaret clung to the fragile hope that justice—for Frederick, for them all—might yet be found. But first, he must vanish into the shadows once more.
