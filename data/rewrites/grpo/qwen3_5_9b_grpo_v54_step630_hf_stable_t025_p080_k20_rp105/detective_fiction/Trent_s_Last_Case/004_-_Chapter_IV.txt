Philip Trent, a painter and the son of a painter, had secured a reputation within the English art world before reaching his twenties. His works sold, driven by a raw, forcible talent and a working rhythm of leisurely diligence punctuated by bursts of intense creative fervor. While his father's name and a substantial patrimony removed the stigma of struggle, Trent's true engine of success was an unconscious magnetism; a genial spirit and a lively, humorous fancy that commanded genuine affection rather than mere popularity. Even when applying himself vigorously to a task, his face retained an expression of contained vivacity. At thirty-two, he had not yet passed the age of laughter and adventure.

His ascent to a celebrity a hundred times greater than his artistic merit stemmed from a momentary impulse. One day, reading a newspaper dominated by a rare crime—a murder aboard a railway train—Trent, newly fascinated by such affairs, delved into the accounts. Intrigued, his imagination worked upon the facts with a strange intensity reminiscent of his artistic inspirations. By day's end, he had written and dispatched a lengthy letter to the editor of the *Record*, chosen for its comprehensive reporting. Mirroring Edgar Allan Poe's approach to the Mary Rogers case, Trent highlighted seemingly negligible details, arranging the evidence to cast grave suspicion on a self-proclaimed witness. Sir James Molloy, the editor, printed the letter in leaded type. That same evening, the *Sun* announced the arrest and full confession of the accused.

Sir James, a man who knew all corners of London, quickly befriended Trent. Their rapport was seamless, aided by Trent's native tact that bridged the gap between their ages. Trent found new enthusiasm in the rotary presses of the *Record*'s basement, painting there a "machinery-scape" in the manner of Heinrich Kley that Sir James purchased at sight. Months later, the Ilkley mystery arrived. Sir James hosted Trent at a dinner and offered him a fantastically large sum to serve as the *Record*'s special representative. "You could do it," the editor urged, praising Trent's ability to write, talk, and solve mysteries. "Think how it would feel if you pulled it off!" Trent admitted it would be a lark. Fear of the unfamiliar task, a habit he had long overcome, vanished, and he accepted.

He succeeded. For the second time, he gave the authorities a start, and his name became ubiquitous. He retreated to painting, and while Sir James honorably refrained from offering a permanent salary, Trent was summoned repeatedly for similar unravelings. Though sometimes forestalled or too busy to accept, his irregular connection with the *Record* made his name one of the best known in England. Characteristically, he imposed absolute silence regarding himself upon the Molloy papers; the public knew almost nothing of him save his name.

Walking rapidly up the sloping road to White Gables, Trent mused that the Manderson case might prove terribly simple. Cupples, the hotel manager, was wise, yet biased regarding his niece. However, the manager's description of Mrs. Manderson was telling: not merely kind-hearted, but possessing backbone, pluck, and a vitality that brightened the entire town of Marlstone. Trent was eager to meet her.

Beyond a spacious lawn and shrubbery stood the house: a modern, dull-red brick structure of two stories, named for its great gables. It possessed an air of opulent peace, beautifully kept, situated between rich meadows and a woody landscape stretching to the moors. That such a tranquil, disciplined setting could harbor violence seemed fantastic. Yet, near the boundary hedge separating the garden from the hot, white road, stood the gardener's toolshed. It was here, against the wooden wall, that the body had been found. Trent walked past the drive's gate, passing the shed where he found only the trodden grass of the uncut lawn. He searched the ground minutely, crouching with keen eyes and feeling fingers, but the search yielded nothing.

The solitude was broken by the sound of the front door closing. Trent stepped to the edge of the drive. A young man walked quickly away from the house toward the great gate. At the noise of Trent's footsteps, the man wheeled around with nervous swiftness. His face was almost terrible—white, worn, and haggard with strain, yet undeniably young. There were no wrinkles, only the tale of desperate fatigue in his blue eyes. He possessed breadth of shoulder and a lithe, strong figure. His carriage, despite weariness, and his handsome, regular features with short, smooth yellow hair, betrayed a special training.

"If you are Mr. Trent," the young man said pleasantly, "you are expected. Mr. Cupples telephoned from the hotel. My name is Marlowe."

"You were secretary to Mr. Manderson, I believe," Trent replied, feeling an immediate inclination to like the youth. Despite his near-breakdown, Marlowe radiated the clean living and inward health typical of his social type. Yet, there was a challenge in his tired eyes—an habitual expression of meditating on things unseen. "It is a terrible business for all of you," Trent said. "I fear it has upset you completely."

"A little limp, that's all," Marlowe replied wearily. "I drove the car all Sunday night and most of yesterday. I didn't sleep last night after hearing the news—who would? But I have an appointment now at the doctor's to arrange the inquest, likely tomorrow. If you go up to the house and ask for Mr. Bunner, you'll find him expecting you; he's the other secretary, an American and the best of fellows. There's also a detective here, Inspector Murch from Scotland Yard, who arrived yesterday."

"Murch!" Trent exclaimed. "But he and I are old friends. How under the sun did he get here so soon?"

"I have no idea," Marlowe answered. "He was here last evening before I returned from Southampton, interviewing everyone, and has been about since eight this morning. He's in the library now—that's where the open French window is at the end of the house. Perhaps you'd like to step down there."

"I think I will," Trent said. Marlowe nodded and departed. Trent moved silently across the thick turf, entering through the open leaves of the southward window. Inside, he considered a very broad back and a bent head covered with short grizzled hair. The man within, Inspector Murch, was stooping over papers on the table.

"'Twas ever thus," Trent murmured in a melancholy tone.

At the sound, the man turned with startling swiftness. "From childhood's hour I've seen my fondest hopes decay. I did think I was ahead of Scotland Yard this time, and now here is the hugest officer in the entire Metropolitan force already occupying the position."

The detective smiled grimly and approached the window. "I was expecting you, Mr. Trent. This is the sort of case that you like."

"Since my tastes were being considered," Trent replied, stepping into the room, "I wish they had followed up the idea by keeping my hated rival out of the business. You have got a long start, too—I know all about it." His eyes wandered the room. "How did you manage it? You are a quick mover, I know; the dun deer's hide on fleeter foot was never tied; but I don't see how you got here in time to be at work yesterday evening. Has Scotland Yard secretly started an aviation corps? Or is it in league with the infernal powers? In either case the Home Secretary should be called upon to make a statement."

"It's simpler than that," Mr. Murch said with professional stolidity. "I happened to be on leave with the missus at Havley, only twelve miles along the coast. As soon as our people there heard of the murder they told me. I wired to the Chief and was put in charge at once. I bicycled over yesterday evening and have been at it since then."

"Arising out of that reply," Trent said inattentively, "how is Mrs. Inspector Murch?"

"Never better, thank you," the inspector answered. "She frequently speaks of you and the games you used to have with our kids. But you'll excuse me saying, Mr. Trent, that you needn't trouble to talk your nonsense to me while you're using your eyes. I know your ways by now. I understand you've fallen on your feet as usual, and have the lady's permission to go over the place and make enquiries."

"Such is the fact," Trent said. "I am going to cut you out again, inspector. I owe you one for beating me over the Abinger case, you old fox. But if you really mean that you're not inclined for the social amenities just now, let us leave compliments and talk business." He stepped to the table, glanced through the papers, and turned to the open roll-top desk. He looked into the drawers swiftly. "I see this has been cleared out. Well now, inspector, I suppose we play the game as before."

Trent had found himself on numerous occasions in the company of Inspector Murch, a quiet, tactful, and very shrewd officer of great courage and vivid history with dangerous criminals. Through some obscure working of sympathy, they had appreciated one another from the beginning, forming a curious friendship. The inspector talked more freely to Trent than to anyone, discussing details and possibilities under the rose. There were rules, however: Trent made no journalistic use of official sources, and each reserved the right to withhold vital discoveries for the honor of their respective institutions. Trent had insisted on formulating these principles of "detective sportsmanship," and Mr. Murch, who loved a contest, entered heartily into the game.

The inspector replied with cordial agreement. Leaning on either side of the French window, with the deep peace and hazy splendor of the summer landscape before them, they reviewed the case. Trent took out a thin notebook and began to make a rough sketch plan of the room with light, secure touches—a habit often idle but occasionally vital.

This was a large, light apartment at the corner of the house, with generous window-space in two walls. A broad table stood in the middle. Entering by the window, the roll-top desk was just to the left against the wall. The inner door was in the wall to the left at the farther end, faced by a broad window divided into casement openings. A beautifully carved old corner-cupboard rose high against the wall beyond the door, and another filled a recess beside the fireplace. Colored prints of Harunobu hung on the unoccupied wall-space, looking uninspiring, as if bought by the yard and never removed. Bound with sober luxury, great English novelists, essayists, historians, and poets stood ranged like an army struck dead in its ranks. There were a few chairs of old carved oak, a modern armchair, and a swivel office-chair before the desk. The room looked costly but very bare. Almost the only portable objects were a great porcelain bowl of wonderful blue on the table, a clock and cigar boxes on the mantelshelf, and a movable telephone standard on the desk.

"Seen the body?" enquired the inspector.

Trent nodded. "And the place where it lay," he said.

"First impressions of this case rather puzzle me," said the inspector. "From what I heard at Halvey I guessed it might be common robbery and murder by some tramp, though such a thing is very far from common in these parts. But as soon as I began my enquiries I came on some curious points, which by this time I dare say you've noted for yourself. The man is shot in his own grounds, quite near the house, to begin with. Yet there's not the slightest trace of any attempt at burglary. And the body wasn't robbed. In fact, it would be as plain a case of suicide as you could wish to see, if it weren't for certain facts. Here's another thing: for a month or so past, they tell me, Manderson had been in a queer state of mind. I expect you know already that he and his wife had some trouble between them. The servants had noticed a change in his manner to her for a long time, and for the past week he had scarcely spoken to her. They say he was a changed man, moody and silent—whether on account of that or something else. The lady's maid says he looked as if something was going to arrive. It's always easy to remember that people looked like that, after something has happened to them. Still, that's what they say. There you are again, then: suicide! Now, why wasn't it suicide, Mr. Trent?"

"The facts so far as I know them are really all against it," Trent replied, sitting on the threshold of the window and clasping his knees. "First, of course, no weapon is to be found. I've searched, and you've searched, and there's no trace of any firearm anywhere within a stone's throw of where the body lay. Second, the marks on the wrists, fresh scratches and bruises, which we can only assume to have been done in a struggle with somebody. Third, who ever heard of anybody shooting himself in the eye? Then I heard from the manager of the hotel here another fact, which strikes me as the most curious detail in this affair. Manderson had dressed himself fully before going out there, but he forgot his false teeth. Now how could a suicide who dressed himself to make a decent appearance as a corpse forget his teeth?"

"That last argument hadn't struck me," admitted Mr. Murch. "There's something in it. But on the strength of the other points, which had occurred to me, I am not considering suicide. I have been looking about for ideas in this house, this morning. I expect you were thinking of doing the same."
