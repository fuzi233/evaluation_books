When little Gerard was barely three months old, a messenger arrived, breathless and hot from Tergou, bearing news for Catherine. "Go back immediately," she commanded him sharply. "Tell them I cannot come, and I will not. They have already taken Kate." With that dispatch sent, she turned her attention to her own plight. "There, child," she said, addressing Margaret, "I must depart. Everyone here is in a state of confusion; this is the third time they have asked. Tomorrow, my man will come himself to drag me home by the ear, flea and all."

She recounted her trials with infants, instructing Margaret meticulously on how to handle every potential emergency, and pressed money into her hands. Margaret declined with gratitude, yet Catherine insisted, her tone shifting to anger. Margaret offered reasons so logically sound that Catherine dismissed them with cold contempt, deeming them lacking in feminine spirit. "Come, out with your heart," Catherine urged, "as we part today—perhaps forever seeing one another's face again."

"Oh, Mother, do not speak thus!" Margaret pleaded.

"Alas, girl, I have witnessed this sorrow countless times," Catherine replied, her eyes clouding. "'Twill haunt me at every farewell. When I was your age, I harbored no such fears; we believed ourselves immortal then. Now, get it over with."

"Well, then, Mother... I would rather not have mentioned it—but your Cornelis must say to me, 'So you have come to share our table, eh, Mistress?' Those were his exact words. I told him I would be deeply sorry."

"Beshrew his sharp tongue! What does it signify?"

"He will surely find out eventually, but regardless, I will accept no begrudging bounty from any household. Unless I see my child starving—and Heaven alone knows what I might then be forced to do—"

"Nay, Mother, give me but your love. I value that above all silver. They do not withhold that from me, by everything I hold dear. Not a single stiver will I take from your house."

"You are a foolish lass," Catherine chided gently. "Were it me, I would take it simply to spite him."

"No, you would not. You and I are apples from the same tree."

Catherine relented with grace. When the moment of separation finally arrived, embraces and tears erupted from both women. Once Catherine was gone, the child wailed incessantly. All attempts to soothe him failed until Margaret suspected a pin pricking his spine. Searching between his clothes and skin, she discovered a gold angel causing him discomfort.

"There, now, Gerard," she whispered to the babe. "I thought Granny yielded too suddenly." Taking the coin, she wrapped it in linen and placed it at the bottom of her chest, telling the infant to observe that she, too, could be as resolute as his grandmother.

Later, Catherine confided in Eli about Margaret's proud defiance. Eli agreed that Margaret was right and Catherine wrong, tossing his head in disagreement. Catherine merely pondered the exchange.

Margaret, however, faced her own quiet anxieties. She still had two men to feed and could no longer labor as intensely as before. Keeping the house, caring for the child, and cooking for everyone consumed her days. Yet, she had saved a small sum, often telling her son that his father would return before their funds ran dry. Clinging to these bright hopes and the sanctuary of her boy, she endured several happy months.

But time marched on. No Gerard returned. Stranger still, no news reached them. Her mind, usually so practical, grew unsettled, drifting into sad reveries and silent sighs. As her heart troubled itself, her savings melted away. One day, she found the cupboard empty. Looking at the faces of her dependents, her bosom swelled with pity. Could she let Grandfather and poor old Martin go hungry? She retrieved Catherine's hidden angel. As she unwrapped the linen, a tear of gentle mortification fell upon the precious metal.

She sent Martin out to exchange it. While he was away, a Frenchman arrived with a dealer in illuminated manuscripts, who had previously offered her a pittance. This man claimed he was employed by his sovereign to collect masterpieces for a Book of Hours. Margaret showed him her finest works, including the shading of flowers, raspberries, and creeping vines created by her late husband, Margaret Van Eyck. The stranger was enchanted.

"Nay, mock not my desperate need, good stranger," she protested. "Three mouths await food in this house, and none to provide it but me."

A curious arithmetic indeed, leaving out number one.

"I shall not outbid you, fair mistress," he replied warmly. "My Princess charged me strictly to seek the best craftsmen, but I forbid hard bargains. Make them content with me, and I with them."

In the next instant, Margaret knelt beside the cradle, kissing little Gerard and showering him with four gold pieces, recounting the event in broken Dutch. "And oh, what a generous Princess! Was she not? We shall pray for her, won't we, my lambkin, when we are older?"

Martin entered, furious. "They will not change it! I fear they believe I stole it."

"I am indebted to you," Margaret hastened to say, snatching the coins from him, wrapping them once more, and returning them to their hiding place.

Before these unexpected funds vanished, she resumed her ironing and starching. Midway through, Martin fell ill and died after nine days. Nearly all her remaining money was spent on his decent burial. He was gone, leaving an empty chair by the fireside—a seat he had preferred to the sun since the Busy Body left. Margaret forbade anyone else to sit there. Yet, whenever her gaze lingered too long on the vacancy, it inevitably cost her a tear.

Now, with no one to carry her laundry home, she considered doing it herself, but that required leaving little Gerard in a neighbor's care. She dared not trust such a treasure to mortal hands for hours. Instead, she sought inquiries for a boy to transport her baskets on Saturdays and Mondays.

A plump, fresh-complexioned youth named Luke Peterson appeared. Though he looked fifteen, he was actually eighteen. Blushing and fidgeting with his cap, he asked if a man could not serve her better than a boy. Before she could answer, she thought, *This boy will suffice.* But taking his cue, she replied, "Nay; perhaps a man seeks more than I can afford."

"Not I," Luke declared warmly. "Mistress Margaret, I am your neighbor and work well at coopering. I can carry your basket before or after my daily work, gladly. You owe me nothing. We are neighbors, you know."

"Why, Master Luke, I recognize your face, though I cannot recall a word passing between us."

"Oh, yes, you did, Mistress Margaret. Have you forgotten? One day you struggled carrying your baby and a full pitcher of water. I said, 'Let me carry the baby.' You replied, 'Keep the bairn, I'll give you the pitcher.' I carried the pitcher home, and you took it from me at this door, saying, 'I am greatly obliged to you, young man,' in such a sweet voice—not like the harsh speech of people in this street."

"I remember now, Master Luke; and I think it was the least I could say."

"Well, Mistress Margaret, if you wish to say as much every time I carry your basket, I care not how often or how far."

"Nay, nay," Margaret murmured, flushing slightly. "I would not burden your kindness. You are young, Master Luke, and so good. How about giving you supper on Saturday night when you bring the linen home, and your morning meal on Monday? Would that balance us?"

"As you please; only do not claim I sought two meals for such a trifling favor."

With Luke Peterson's timely aid and the strength Heaven bestowed upon this poor woman to counterbalance her sorrows, the household ran smoothly for a while. Yet, her heart grew increasingly tormented by Gerard's long, now utterly mysterious silence. Suspense, that relentless tormentor, began to gnaw at her soul with hot pincers until she cried out often and desperately, "Oh, that I could know the worst!"

One day, hearing a heavy step ascend the stairs, she started and trembled. *That is no familiar step. Bad tidings?* The door opened, revealing an unexpected visitor: Eli, looking grave yet kind.

Margaret stared at him in silence, her agitation mounting. "Girl," he said softly, "the Skipper has returned."

"One word," Margaret gasped. "Is he alive?"

"Surely I hope so. No one has seen him dead."

"Then they must have seen him alive."

"No, girl. Neither dead nor alive has he been seen in Rome for many months. My daughter Kate believes he has gone to another city. She bid me tell you her suspicion."

"Ay, likely enough," Margaret muttered gloomily. "Likely enough. My poor babe!"

The old man, speaking in a faint voice, asked for a morsel to eat; he had come fasting. Filled with pity despite her agitated mind, Margaret cooked a meal for him, trembling and scarcely aware of her actions. Before departing, he laid his hand upon her head.
