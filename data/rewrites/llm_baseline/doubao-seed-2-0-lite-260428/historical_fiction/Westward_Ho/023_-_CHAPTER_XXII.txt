This chapter may bear a weight too heavy for some hearts to bear, so I will spin it as short as my pen may move, yet it cannot be omitted. No reader may judge fairly the grim, fire-forged foes that English seafarers faced in those rough days without seeing them unmasked, at their cruelest work.

Three weeks have slipped by since we last stood with our company on the open main, and the scene shifts: we are deep in the stony, fetid corridors of Cartagena’s fortress prison, lined end to end with low, narrow cells. One cell door stands ajar, and within stand two cloaked figures, one of whom we have met before: Eustace Leigh. The other is a lay familiar of the Holy Office, the hood of his robe pulled low to shadow his face, an iron lamp held tight in one hand. Its guttering yellow light spills across a pile of scratchy straw on the cell floor, and onto the man sleeping atop it. That high, pale brow, those fine, sharp, delicate features—we know that face too. It is Frank.

The savage rage of the enslaved Afro-Caribbean raiders left him half-dead, only to spare him for a far more refined cruelty at the hands of men who called themselves civilized, who called themselves Christian. He had been dragged to the torture rack only that afternoon, and now his own blood cousin, his betrayer, has come to his cell. Is Eustace here to persuade him? To trap him? Even he can scarce tell which urge tugs the harder at his fraying conscience.

He would lay down his own life this instant to save Frank, if it would serve. His life? He long ago ceased to value it. He did all he has done out of what he tells himself is sacred duty, and now he has been bidden to do one last act of that duty: wake the sleeping man, argue, coax, threaten him into recanting his Protestant faith while “his heart is still soft and raw from the torment,” as his Inquisitor masters phrased their order.

But Frank sleeps so peacefully. Is it only a trick of the wavering lamplight, or is there a faint, soft smile curved on his lips? Eustace takes the lamp from the familiar’s hand, leans in closer to see, and as he does, he catches Frank’s half-muttered dream speech: first his mother’s name, then a name far higher, far holier, breathed in a quiet, trusting whisper. Eustace cannot find it in his heart to wake him.
“Let him rest,” he whispers hoarsely to his companion. “I fear my words will do no good here in any case.”
“Nor do I hold out much hope, senor,” the familiar replies. “Never did I see a more stubborn heretic. He did not even flinch from scoffing openly at the Holy Father and our most reverend inquisitors.”
“Ah,” Eustace breathes, crossing himself automatically. “Great is the depravity of the human heart, and great is the power of Satan over souls that stray! Let us withdraw for now.”

Eustace turns to leave, but a cold curiosity pricks him, and he asks, “Where is she?”
“Which of the two sorceresses, the old one or the young?”
“The young one—the—”
“The Senora de Soto.” The familiar smirks a little, then adds, feigning regret, “Poor thing. One might almost feel pity for her, if she were not a heretic. She is with the notary just now, undergoing questioning that they say is for the good of her soul.”
Eustace staggers half a step back, a violent shudder wracking his frame. He has to fumble for a moment to catch his breath enough to gasp out a hollow, forced “Amen.”
“Through there,” the familiar says, nodding carelessly at a stout oak door further down the corridor as they walk. “We may pause to listen a moment, if you wish. But do not give me away to the superiors, senor.”

Eustace knows full well the man is watching him like a hawk, ready to report the flicker of even a spark of sympathy for a heretic to his masters, to ruin him on the spot if he shows the slightest sign of wavering. But a horrible, roiling curiosity drowns out his fear, and he presses himself close to that fateful door. His face burns like it is on fire, his knees knock so hard he can scarce stand, his ears ring with a high, thin hum, and his heart pounds so hard against his ribs he swears it will burst free. He presses one shoulder to the cold stone wall, hiding his twitching, convulsed face as best he can from the familiar at his side.
Inside, a man’s low, flat, distinct voice carries clear through the thick wood: the notary, reading that old, lazy charge of witchcraft the Inquisitors love to level against their victims, to justify their cruelty to their own consciences, to whitewash their villainy for the watching public of the city.
Then a woman’s voice cuts through the hum, sharp with indignation and the edge of fresh agony: “Witchcraft against Don Guzman? What need of that, oh God, what need?”
“You deny the charge then, senora?” the notary replies, in a tone of mock sorrow. “We grieve for you deeply, but—”
A ragged, choked murmur rises from the woman, words tangled and half-stifled that could mean anything, nothing at all.
“She confessed,” Eustace whispers, half wild, pressing a hand to his mouth. “Saints be praised—she—”

A wail bursts out, high, piercing, that cuts through Eustace’s ears, his brain, his very heart. He lurches forward, ready to tear the door off its hinges to get to her, but the familiar seizes his arm and drags him away. Another wail, then another, as the man pulls Eustace down the corridor, Eustace stuffing his fingers in his ears in a useless, vain effort to block the sound—cries that follow him like avenging angels, echoing off the stone vaults of the prison long after he is out of earshot.
He bursts out into the open air, fragrant with jasmine and tropical bloom, under the glow of a golden tropic moon, in a garden so lush and perfect it might have been Eden itself. But the hell of man’s cruelty has followed him into God’s own heaven: those wails still ring in his ears, no matter how hard he tries to shake them off.
“Misery, misery, endless misery,” he mutters to himself, grinding his teeth until his jaw aches. “I brought her to this. I had to bring her to this! What else could I do? Who dares blame me? What devil’s sin did I commit to deserve this punishment? Was there no other man in all the Indies they could have chosen for this? But it may save her soul. It may bring her to repentance!”
“Indeed it may,” the familiar says, calm as ever, falling into step beside him. “She is delicate, after all. She will not bear much more torment. You know as well as I do, senor, how merciful the Holy Office is to those under its care.”
“I know it, I know it,” Eustace stammers, now trembling for his own skin. “All done in love—all done out of Christian love. A fatherly chastisement.”
“And the proof of her heresy is plain to all, on top of the strong suspicion of enchantment, and the well-known wickedness of her old attendant, that English witch. You yourself told the Holy Office, senor, that the old woman was a notorious witch back in England, long before the senora brought her over here to serve her.”
“Of course she was. Of course,” Eustace babbles. “There was no other possible course to take. And though my flesh may be weak, sir, no man can say I have not proved to the Holy Office how utterly willing my spirit is to serve its cause.”

And so Eustace took his leave, and before the next sun had set, he had sought out the head of the Jesuit order in Cartagena, poured out all his fraying heart to him—or as much of it, poor wretch, as he dared admit even to himself—and begged leave to finish his novitiate and take his final vows as a Jesuit, on the condition that he be sent at once back to Europe, or anywhere, anywhere away from Cartagena. “Otherwise,” he told the superior frankly, “I shall go mad, even if I am not already half-mad now.”
The Jesuit, a man of no unkindness, went at once to the offices of the Holy Office, settled the whole matter with the inquisitors, and recited Eustace’s long record of faithful, valiant service to the Church to set his name above all possible suspicion. No one needed Eustace’s testimony any longer. He left Cartagena for the port of Nombre de Dios that very night, and sailed a week later for no destination I can name.

I cannot tell you where he went. Eustace Leigh vanishes entirely from the pages of this story. He may have risen in time to become General of the entire Jesuit Order. He may have worn out his days tramping through steaming tropical forests, “winning souls” for the Church—while seizing control of their bodies, too—among the native Indian tribes. He may have slipped back into England under a false name, and been the very Ballard who was hanged, drawn and quartered three years later for his part in the traitorous Babington Conspiracy. I do not know.
This book is the history of living men: of their virtues and their sins, their great victories and their crushing defeats. But Eustace is no longer a man. He has become a thing, a tool of the Jesuit order, sent wherever he is bidden, doing good or doing evil with equal indifference, as his superiors command. By an act of utter moral suicide, he has thrown away his own will, his own soul, in the desperate hope of saving it. He no longer has a will, no conscience, no sense of responsibility (or so he deludes himself) to God or to any man on earth, but only to the Society of Jesus. In a word, as he himself so plainly says, he is dead. Twice dead, I fear. Let the dead bury their dead. We have no more business with Eustace Leigh, for now or ever.
