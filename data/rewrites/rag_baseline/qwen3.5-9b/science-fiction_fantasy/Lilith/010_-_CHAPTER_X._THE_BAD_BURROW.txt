The twilight thickened into an oppressive void as winter tightened its grip, the dying embers of a small fire casting a frantic, luminous flare. Hovering in the stagnant air, a creature intercepted the fading heat—a bird-butterfly, its flight a mechanical, swallow-like oscillation. Its wings were vast, nearly square, flashing a spectrum of impossible colors that defied terrestrial physics. Mesmerized by this radiant absurdity, I stumbled over a protruding rock and collapsed, stunned. When consciousness returned, the entity hovered directly overhead, radiating a kaleidoscope of hues I had never witnessed, including shades that seemed to vibrate against the retina. Rising, I pursued it, my vision tunneling until I collided with another stone. Fear of a second fall pinned me to the ground, forcing me to watch the glowing spectacle. A desperate greed seized me; I wanted to possess this miracle. To my astonishment, it descended, slowing its approach until it appeared inches from my palm. It felt as though the universe itself was offering a gift. I grasped it. Instantly, the light extinguished. In my hand lay a cold, heavy slab—nothing more than a dead book with covers spread wide. Throwing it aside, I heard it crash into the heather. Buried in my hands, I sat paralyzed by misery. But the biting cold demanded action; the threat of freezing forced me to rise. The moment my feet touched the earth, a faint luminescence pierced the gloom. "Is it returning?" I cried, a surge of hope flooding my chest. No. It was merely the edge of a moon piercing the horizon, sharp and indifferent. She offered illumination, not salvation. Unlike the hovering phantom, the celestial body refused to linger, refusing to guide my stumbling steps. It provided data, not direction—an ignorant choice between paths equally fraught with peril. The moon ascended, filling the sky, and I finally discerned the landscape. To the west, a range of low hills broke the skyline. I moved toward them. What a night it proved to be. The moon seemed to possess a malevolent intelligence, staring back with an icy, scrutinizing gaze. It was not the familiar satellite of Earth; its face was alien, its light derived perhaps from a distant, unknown sun. Every upward glance met its unblinking scrutiny. Initially, I resented the rudeness of such a stare, but soon I perceived a strange, pitying curiosity in its depths. Why was I awake in this desolate expanse? This realization struck me with terrifying clarity: I *was*, and I could not cease existing. As I walked, the heather vanished, replaced by a bare, spongy substrate akin to dry, powdery peat. Under my weight, the ground heaved momentarily. Ahead, a shadowy ripple traversed the terrain like a seismic wave, visible only in the low moonlight. It receded into the distance, but as I watched, a solitary wave surged toward me. Two yards away, it erupted, and from the churned earth scrambled a beast resembling a tiger. Clumps of mold hung from its jaws and ears; its eyes flared with predatory fire as it lunged, teeth bared in a silent snarl. I stood transfixed, devoid of both courage and fear. The creature lowered its head and plunged back into the soil. "This moon affects my brain," I muttered, resuming my trek. "What life exists here but the phantasmagoria of dreams? I walk a vain show!" I struggled to keep my spirit above the rising tide of terror, unaware that the very entity I distrusted served as my shield against these hallucinations. Its light suppressed the monsters; without it, I would not have taken a second step on this hideous plain. "I shall not be appalled by mere appearances!" I declared internally, yet felt the sheer horror of treading a sea teeming with unseen horrors. Moments later, the head of a worm emerged from the earth, massive as a polar bear, sporting a white mane atop a crimson neck. The writhing motions required to extricate its colossal length were grotesque, yet I dared not look away. Once free, it lay spent, wallowing in a futile attempt to burrow again. "Does it feed on the dead?" I wondered. "If they scent prey, why do they ignore the living?" I now understood: the moon paralyzed them. Throughout the long night, hideous, unique creatures threatened me. Some combined breathtaking color with repulsive forms; one great serpent was draped from head to tail in feathers of glorious, unnatural hue. Eventually, I grew accustomed to their harmless threats, inventing monstrosities to distract myself, never suspecting that I owed every breath to the moon's relentless glare. Even a fraction of light is sufficient to halt the darkness, however dim. How quickly I would have perished had I realized that once her lamp faded from this cursed spot, I would instantly become prey to entities devoid of mercy—the center of a writhing mass of absolute terror. Ignorant fool that I was, I watched the weary, solemn moon descend across the widening vault above, troubled only by the fear of getting lost—a danger that did not yet exist. Drawing near the hills, my destination, the moon dipped toward the skyline. The soundless thrashing ceased; the burrow lay motionless and exposed. Then, I saw her. A woman walked the pale soil, enveloped in a white mist that constantly shifted, coalescing into garments only to dissipate in a wind that trailed her steps. She was beautiful, yet her expression blended arrogant pride with profound misery, making her appearance seem surreal. She paced aimlessly, trying to grasp the mist and wrap it around herself. Her eyes were dead, and a dark stain marred her left side, against which she periodically pressed her hand as if stifling agony. Her hair cascaded nearly to her feet, occasionally blending indistinguishably with the swirling mist, though it gleamed pale gold when it settled. Suddenly, clutching her heart with both hands, she collapsed. The mist rose from her and dissolved into the air. I ran to her aid, but she convulsed in such agony that I recoiled in horror. A moment later, her legs detached and sped away as serpents; her arms fled from her shoulders, transforming into snakes too. Something flew up from her torso like a bat. When I looked again, she was gone. The ground heaved violently, like a storm-tossed sea. Terror seized me. I turned toward the hills and ran. Already on the slope of their base, the moon sank behind a summit, plunging me into shadow. Behind me rose a wasting, sickening cry—a sound of frustrated desire, the first noise I had heard since the death of the butterfly. It shook my heart like a flag in a gale. I spun around, seeing dark shapes bounding in pursuit, and sprinted for the crest of a ridge where the moon still illuminated the path. It seemed to linger there specifically to allow me to see my attackers. Gaining speed, I crossed the shadow of a rock and heard the creatures panting hotly at my heels. Just as the lead beast threw itself upon me with a snarl of greedy hatred, we both rushed into the beam of the moon. It flashed with an angry brilliance, and the attacker fell, dissolving into a bodiless blotch. Strength surged through me, and I turned on the others. One by one, as they darted into the light, they dropped with a howl. Above me, the round face of the moon wore a strange, enigmatic smile. I climbed to the ridge's peak. Far away, the moon shone, sinking toward a low horizon. The air was pure and invigorating. Descending slightly, I found the temperature rising and sat to await the dawn. The moon slipped below the horizon, and the world returned to darkness.
