Still reeling from last night’s narrow brush with capture, Gerard roused long before the first streaks of grey gilded the cottage thatch, and shook old Martin awake. The veteran soldier blinked in utter astonishment, half-convinced the lad had already slipped out the window under cover of dark. When Gerard asked for the surest path to slip out of the country and shake off pursuit, Martin did not hesitate: there was only one safe road. “I’ll guide you through the great old forest to a hidden bridle track no traveler uses but me. It will carry you straight to a wayside hostelry where they’ll lend you a swift horse, and a full day’s gallop will put all of Holland behind you before any hue and cry can spread. But we must leave before the villagers drag themselves out of their beds at dawn.”
Peter’s cottage stood barely a furlong and a half from the forest edge. They set out at once: Martin slung his well-worn war bow over his shoulder and slipped three extra arrows in his belt, a old soldier’s habit he never broke on a Thursday, while Gerard carried nothing but the stout knotted oak staff Peter had pressed into his hands that morning, worn smooth from years of prodding turnip rows and fending off stray hounds. Margaret hitched her wool kirtle and farthingale up above her ankles, for the grass was still soaked and muddied from last night’s rain. Peter walked them all the way to his garden hedge, his usual stolid face softening with a rare surge of emotion, and pressed a father’s blessing to Gerard’s head.
The sun was just peeking its red rim over the low horizon as they tramped across the stony fallow fields, making straight for the wood. They were halfway across the open stretch when Margaret, who had been craning her neck backward every few steps, let out a sharp terrified cry. Her instinct took over before she could think: she sprinted for the trees, screaming all the way. Ghysbrecht and his men were hot on their heels.
To fight a whole band of pursuers would be madness, so Martin and Gerard ran after her. The men on foot gained ground little by little, but Martin shouted over the wind, “Only win the wood! Just reach the first trees, lads!” Their head start was still good, and hope swelled in their chests: the great oak boughs seemed to stretch out like open friendly arms, the thick underbrush a ready screen to hide them from sight.
But an unforeseen danger tore from the flank. Fiery old Burgomaster Ghysbrecht had hurled himself onto his sturdy mule, spurring the poor beast to a flat gallop, outpacing not just his own men but the fugitives themselves. He traced a wide semicircle, his only goal to cut Gerard off before he could reach cover. The old man came thundering around the curve, and suddenly he was right on the edge of the wood, dead in front of Gerard. The rest of the runaways he did not care about at all. Margaret shrieked, throwing her arms around Gerard to shield him, but he shoved her aside none too gently.
Ghysbrecht, drunk on rage, forgot that hunted animals turn on their hunter, that two men who have hated each other for months, who have both longed for the other’s ruin, will not run. Instead of ducking out of the way as the burgomaster fully expected, Gerard flew at him with a savage, wild cry, and swung that oak staff with every ounce of his fear, his fury, his pent-up strength. The stick crashed down on Ghysbrecht’s face with a sickening thunderous crack, and the old man went flying off his mule, landing in the mud under its tail, heels kicking wildly, blood streaming down his face and soaking his linen collar. The very next second, all three were diving into the shelter of the trees.
A roar of dismay and vengeance burst from Ghysbrecht’s men when they saw their leader felled, and the fugitives knew now it was a race for life or death.
“Why run?” Gerard panted, stopping short and shaking his bloodied staff. “You have your bow, I have this—we can stand and fight.”
“Boy, are you mad?” Martin roared back. “The gallows are waiting for us if they catch us! Follow me!” He bolted deeper into the undergrowth.
Soon they heard a sound like hounds baying when they catch sight of their quarry: the men were in the wood now, and had spotted their three figures darting between the trunks. Margaret whimpered and panted as she ran, Gerard clenched his teeth so hard his jaw ached, his grip on the staff so tight his knuckles blanched. A minute later they hit a thick dense hazel coppice. Martin burst right through it, shoving thin young saplings aside as if they were dry standing corn. They did not make fifty yards before they hit four overgrown blind paths snaking off in every direction. Martin dived down one, hissing “Bend low” and they half-crawled, half-scuttled along the soft leaf litter. Soon the path forked again into more tiny winding trails. They picked one that seemed to loop back on itself, then twisted sharply, leading them to a thick stand of old pines where the ground was hard and carpeted with pine needles, no paths at all. The young firs grew so close here you could not see three feet in front of your own face.
When they were well inside the pine grove, Martin dropped onto a fallen log. The old campaigner, long trained to shed all fear the second immediate danger passed, pulled a hunk of black bread and a slice of ham from his wallet and began calmly eating his breakfast, as if no hue and cry hunted them down. The two younger ones stared at him in dismay, and he met their looks with a dry grin.
“Not a soul in all Sevenbergen could find you lot here. And Gerard—you’re going to lose your purse long before you get to Italy, if you carry it hanging loose like that.”
Gerard glanced down, and there was a large triangular purse, tangled by its chain fast to the buckle of his wallet strap. “That’s none of mine,” he said, confused, fumbling to untangle it. “Wonder what’s inside?” It was stuck inextricably in the leather, so he had to pull his knife to cut it free. When they emptied it out, it was full of silver coins of every size, half its bloated bulk made of crumpled folded brown paper, stuffed in to make it look far fatter than it was.
Gerard laughed out loud. “Why, this must belong to that old thief Ghysbrecht! He stuffed it with paper to fool the world into thinking he carried twice the coin he did!”
They puzzled over how it got there, before the truth clicked: the purse had hung from Ghysbrecht’s saddle bow, and when Gerard charged at him, he had torn it free without even noticing, felling his enemy and robbing him in the exact same wild, single gesture. Gerard was giddy over the absurd lucky feat, but Margaret was uneasy.
“Throw it away, Gerard. Or let Martin take it back to him. They already call you a thief. I cannot bear them to have even more cause to speak ill of you.”
“Throw it away? Give it back? Not a single stiver!” Gerard crowed. “This is plunder fairly won in open fight against an enemy, isn’t it, Martin?”
“Aye, that’s the truth,” Martin said, chewing. “Send him his crumpled brown paper scraps back if you like, but the coin? Giving that back’d be a sin.”
“Oh Gerard,” Margaret said, soft and worried, “you are going to a far country. We need Heaven’s goodwill on our side. How can we hope for that if we take something that is not ours?”
But Gerard saw it differently, bright with pious delight. “Heaven handed it to me by a miracle, that’s what it is. The chosen people of Israel spoiled the Egyptians, did they not? And they were blessed for it.”
“Take your own way,” Margaret said humbly, bowing her head. “You are wiser than I. You are my husband,” she added in a low murmur, “it is not my place to gainsay you.”
Those soft meek words from Margaret, who had always led their little dynamic before that day, surprised Martin for a moment, and the thought tucked itself away in his head, to puzzle over later. Gerard kissed her tenderly in return, and they walked hand in hand deeper into the vast forest, Martin leading the way. The further they went, the safer they felt: no villager from Sevenbergen ever dared venture this far into the trackless heart of the wood.
Impetuous natures repent fast, and Gerard’s hot temper cooled as soon as the last echo of pursuit faded. His conscience pricked him sharp.
“Martin, I wish I had not struck quite so hard.”
“Whom? Oh, let that pass, he got off cheap.”
“I saw his grey hairs the second my stick fell. I doubt that sight will leave my eyes for many a day.”
Martin grunted with contempt. “Who spares a badger for his grey hairs? The greyer your enemy is, the older he is, the craftier he is. And the craftier the better for a little killing.”
“Killing? Killing, Martin? Speak not of killing!” Gerard shook all over. “I never meant that.”
“I am much mistaken if you have not done it,” Martin said cheerfully. “That old vagabond’s skull cracked like a ripe walnut, mark my words.”
“Heaven and the saints forbid it!”
“He rolled off his mule like a stone shot out of a cart. Said I to myself, there’s one old scoundrel wiped right off the map,” the iron old soldier grinned ruthlessly.
Gerard fell to his knees and began to pray for his enemy’s life. Martin lost his patience.
“What mummery is this? You that set up for learning, know you not a wise man never strikes his enemy but to kill him? And what is all this coil over killing an old man? If it had been a young fellow, with wine, women, and plage waiting for him, that’d be a tragedy. But an old greybeard tottering right on the edge of the grave—why not shove him in? Go he must, today or tomorrow. If I ever live to be as broken down as Ghysbrecht, riding a mule instead of walking on my own legs, with a back as stiff as my bow wood instead of as flexible as my bowstring? I’ll thank any young man that knocks me on the head, same as you did that old shopkeeper. Malison on his memory.”
“Oh, my fault! My great fault!” Gerard cried, smiting his breast.
“Look there!” Martin said to Margaret scornfully. “He is a priest at heart still—when he is not in a rage, what a milksop!”
“Tush, Martin!” Margaret chided him, then she wreathed her arms round Gerard, and comforted him with the soft dual magic of a woman’s gentle sense and a woman’s warm voice. “Sweetheart,” she murmured, “you forget: you went not a step out of your way to harm him, who hunted you to your death. You fled from him. He it was who spurred straight at you. Then did you strike, but in self-defence, a single blow, with that which was in your hand. If malice had ruled you, you would have drawn a knife, or struck again and again. Men are smitten with staves many blows over, and yet live! If your enemy has fallen, it is through his own malice, not yours, and by the will of God.”
“Bless you, Margaret, bless you for thinking so!”
“Aye, but beloved one, if you have had the misfortune to harm that wicked man, the more need there is that you fly with haste from Holland. Oh, let us on.”
“Nay, Margaret,” said Gerard. “I fear not man’s vengeance, thanks to Martin and this thick wood. Only Him I fear whose eye pierces the forest and reads the heart of man. If I but struck in self-defence, ’tis well. But if in hate, He may bid the avenger of blood follow me to Italy—ay, to earth’s remotest bounds.”
“Hush!” said Martin peevishly. “Your chatter’s drowning out the sound.”
“What is it?”
“Do you hear nothing, Margaret? My ears are getting old.”
Margaret listened, and presently she caught a tuneful sound, like a single stroke upon a deep ringing bell. She described it, and Martin nodded grimly.
“I heard it,” he said.
“So did I,” said Gerard. “It is beautiful. Ah, there it is again! How sweetly it blends with the air. It is a long way off. It is before us, is it not?”
“No, no! The echoes of this wood confound the ear of a stranger. It comes from the pine grove.”
“What! The one we passed? Martin, is this anything? You look pale.”
“Wonderful!” said Martin, with a sickly sneer. “He asks me is it anything? Come, on, on! At any rate, let us reach a better place than this.”
“A better place—for what?”
“To stand at bay, Gerard,” said Martin gravely. “And die like soldiers, killing three for one.”
“What’s that sound?”
“It is the Avenger of Blood.”
“Oh, Martin, save him! Oh Heaven be merciful! What new mysterious peril is this?”
“Girl,” Martin said, his voice tight with dread, “it’s a bloodhound.”
