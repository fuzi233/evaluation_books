But Gwendolen? She was thinking of Deronda much more than he was thinking of her. Often she wondered what his ideas were "about things," and how his life was truly occupied. Yet, a lapdog would be necessarily at a loss trying to frame the motives and adventures of doghood at large; and it was just as far from Gwendolen's conception that Deronda's life could be determined by the historical destiny of the Jews as it was for him to rise into the air on a brazen horse and vanish from her horizon as a twinkling star.

With all the sense of inferiority forced upon her, it was inevitable that she imagined a larger place for herself in his thoughts than she actually possessed. Only old and wise persons fail to see their own anxiety or elation reflected in other minds; and Gwendolen, with her youth and inward solitude, may be excused for dwelling on signs of special interest shown by the one person who had impressed her with a feeling of submission, and for mistaking the color and proportion of those signs in Deronda's mind.

Meanwhile, what would he tell her to do? *"He said I must get more interest in others, and more knowledge, and that I must care about the best things—but how am I to begin?"* She wondered what books he would recommend for her private study, recalling the famous writers she had either avoided or found utterly unreadable, with a half-smiling wish that she could mischievously ask Deronda if they weren't simply "medicine for the mind." Then she repented of her sauciness. When safely out of sight, she carried upstairs a miscellaneous selection—Descartes, Bacon, Locke, Butler, Burke, Guizot. As a clever young lady of education, she knew these authors were ornaments of mankind, feeling sure Deronda had read them, and hoping that by dipping into them all in succession, with her rapid understanding, she might reach a viewpoint nearer to his level.

But it was astonishing how little time she found for such vast mental excursions. Constantly she had to be on the scene as Mrs. Grandcourt, feeling herself watched by the exacting eyes of a husband who had found a motive to exercise his tenacity: making his marriage serve all the ends he chose, and with greater completeness the more he discerned any opposing will in her. Whatever rebellion might rage within her, she could not have made up her mind to failure in her performance. No feeling had yet reconciled her for a moment to any act, word, or look that would be a confession to the world. What she most dreaded in herself was any violent impulse that would make an involuntary confession. It was the will to remain silent in every other direction that had thrown such impetuosity into her confidences toward Deronda, to whom her thoughts continually turned as a help against herself.

Her riding, hunting, visiting, and receiving visitors were all performed in a spirit of achievement that served instead of zest and young gladness. Consequently, throughout Diplow during those weeks of the new year, Mrs. Grandcourt was regarded as wearing her honors with triumph.

"She disguises it under an air of taking everything as a matter of course," said Mrs. Arrowpoint. "A stranger might suppose that she had condescended rather than risen. I always noticed that doubleness in her."

To her mother above all, Gwendolen was bent on acting complete satisfaction. Poor Mrs. Davilow was so far deceived that she took the unexpected distance at which she was kept—as a result of Grandcourt's handsome provision for her—as a comparative indifference in her daughter, now that marriage had created new interests. Being fetched to lunch and then to dinner along with the Gascoignes, driven back soon after breakfast the next morning, and receiving brief calls where her husband waited for her outside on horseback or in the carriage, constituted all the intercourse allowed to her mother.

The truth was, the second time Gwendolen proposed inviting her mother with Mr. and Mrs. Gascoigne, Grandcourt had first remained silent, then drawled, *"We can't be having those people always. Gascoigne talks too much. Country clergy are always bores—with their confounded fuss about everything."* That speech was full of foreboding for Gwendolen. To have her mother classed under "those people" confirmed her previous dread of bringing her too near. Still, she could not give the true reasons; she could not say to her mother, *"Mr. Grandcourt wants to recognize you as little as possible; and besides, it is better you should not see much of my married life, else you might find out that I am miserable."*

So she waived every allusion to the subject as lightly as she could. When Mrs. Davilow again hinted at the possibility of having a house close to Ryelands, Gwendolen replied, *"It would not be so nice for you as being near the rectory here, mamma. We shall perhaps be very little at Ryelands. You would miss my aunt and uncle."*

All the while, this contemptuous veto of her husband regarding any intimacy with her family, making her proudly shrink from giving them the aspect of troublesome pensioners, was rousing more inward inclination toward them. She had never felt so kindly toward her uncle, so disposed to look back on his cheerful, complacent activity and spirit of kind management—even when mistaken—as more of a comfort than the neutral loftiness that was every day chilling her. Here, perhaps, she was unconsciously finding some of that mental enlargement which was hard to get from her occasional dashes into difficult authors, who instead of blending with her daily agitations required her to dismiss them.

It was a delightful surprise one day when Mr. and Mrs. Gascoigne were at Offendene to see Gwendolen ride up without her husband—accompanied only by the groom. All, including the four girls and Miss Merry, seated in the dining-room at lunch, could see the welcome approach. Even the elder ones were not without something of Isabel's romantic sense that the beautiful sister on the splendid chestnut—which held its head as if proud to bear her—was a sort of Harriet Byron or Miss Wardour reappearing out of her "happiness ever after."

Her uncle went to the door to give her his hand, and she sprang from her horse with an air of alacrity that encouraged that notion of guaranteed happiness. For Gwendolen was particularly bent today on setting her mother's heart at rest, and her unusual sense of freedom in being able to make this visit alone enabled her to bear up under the pressure of painful facts urging themselves anew. The seven family kisses were not so tiresome as they used to be.

*"Mr. Grandcourt is gone out, so I determined to fill up the time by coming to you, mamma,"* said Gwendolen, as she laid down her hat and seated herself next to her mother. Then, looking at her with a playfully monitory air, *"That is a punishment to you for not wearing better lace on your head. You didn't think I should come and detect you—you dreadfully careless-about-yourself, mamma!"* She gave a caressing touch to the dear head.

*"Scold me, dear,"* said Mrs. Davilow, her delicate, worn face flushing with delight. *"But I wish there was something you could eat after your ride—instead of these scraps. Let Jocosa make you a cup of chocolate in your old way. You used to like that."*

Miss Merry immediately rose to go out, though Gwendolen said, *"Oh, no, a piece of bread, or one of those hard biscuits. I can't think about eating. I am come to say good-bye."*

*"What! Going to Ryelands again?"* said Mr. Gascoigne.

*"No, we are going to town,"* said Gwendolen, beginning to break up a piece of bread but putting no morsel into her mouth.

*"It is rather early to go to town,"* said Mrs. Gascoigne, *"and Mr. Grandcourt not in Parliament."*

*"Oh, there is only one more day's hunting to be had, and Henleigh has some business in town with lawyers, I think,"* said Gwendolen. *"I am very glad. I shall like to go to town."*

*"You will see your house in Grosvenor Square,"* said Mrs. Davilow. She and the girls were devouring with their eyes every movement of their goddess, soon to vanish.

*"Yes,"* said Gwendolen, in a tone of assent to the interest of that expectation. *"And there is so much to be seen and done in town."*
