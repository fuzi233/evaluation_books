When Mr. Thornton departed from the house that morning, the road beneath his feet seemed to sway under the weight of a fury that threatened to blind him. His mind spun with a dizziness akin to vertigo, as if Margaret Hale, rather than appearing as the tender, graceful lady she was, had been a rough fish-wife striking him with heavy fists. He suffered a genuine, physical agony—a pounding headache and a pulse that hammered irregular beats against his ribs. The clamor of the streets, the glare of the sun, the endless rumble of traffic became intolerable to his nerves. He cursed himself for a fool, yet the source of his torment remained a foggy mystery, disproportionate in its aftermath to whatever cause had sparked it. A strange, irrational longing seized him: to sit on a doorstep beside a weeping child, letting his own passions rage outward in tearful storm against some imagined wrong.

"I hate Margaret!" he whispered to the empty air, shaping the words with a ferocity that betrayed nothing of his truth. Yet, as soon as the syllables formed, a jagged lightning bolt of love tore through his dark, thunderous resolve, cleaving it in twain. This was his solace, his torture: the knowledge that though she might scorn him, despise him, or treat him with the cold indifference of a queen, he remained immutable. She could not alter him. He loved her, and he would love her, defying both her and this miserable, throbbing body.

He paused, steeling this resolution until it burned bright and clear. An omnibus clattered past, bound for the country. The driver, mistaking his stillness for a desire for a seat, halted near the curb. Apologizing would have taken too much effort; so, he stepped aboard. The vehicle carried him swiftly away from the town, past rows of uniform housing, past manicured villas with their neat gardens, until the landscape softened into true country hedgerows and finally a small rural settlement. Here, the passengers alighted, and Mr. Thornton followed, unable to remain where the machine had deposited him.

He strode into the open fields, forcing a brisk pace to jolt the stagnant water of his mind. Now, the memories flooded back with cruel clarity. How ridiculous he must have appeared—the man who had done precisely what he had sworn never to do, meeting every disaster he had predicted for himself. Was he truly bewitched by those luminous eyes, that soft, sighing mouth pressed so intimately against his shoulder mere hours prior? He could not banish the recollection of her embrace, however brief, however fleeting the promise of "never again." He saw her only in flashes, unable to grasp her wholly. One moment she was brave, the next timid; today tender, tomorrow haughty and regal. He mentally reviewed every encounter, trying to find the version of her that fit his logic, but she eluded categorization. Even this morning, she had looked magnificent, her eyes blazing with indignation at the notion that sharing his danger yesterday implied any affection whatsoever.

If Mr. Thornton considered himself a fool in the morning, his afternoon offered no enlightenment. His sixpenny ride yielded only a sharper conviction: that no one existed like Margaret Hale, that she did not love him and never would, yet that neither she nor the entire world should stand between him and his devotion. Returning to the market square, he boarded the bus to return to Milton.

By late afternoon, the vehicle set him down near his warehouse. The familiar surroundings triggered the familiar rhythms of duty. He knew the mountain of tasks awaiting him: the commotion of the previous day had doubled his workload. He must meet with his fellow magistrates, finalize the precarious arrangements for his newly arrived Irish workers, and ensure they remained isolated from the disgruntled locals of Milton. Finally, he must return home to face his mother.

Inside the dining room, Mrs. Thornton had spent the entire day in a state of suspended animation, waiting for the news of her son's union with Miss Hale. She had braced herself against every creak of the floorboards, every footfall, catching up dropped needles with a trembling, dim-sighted hand. Time and again, the door had opened to admit some trivial visitor, causing her rigid countenance to slacken into a mask of despondency, alien to her usual sternness. She violently pulled her gaze away from the bleak future of widowhood, forcing her thoughts back into the safe channels of household management. She began counting out fresh linens for the prospective bride, sorting piles of tablecloths and napkins.

Confusion arose as she distinguished what belonged to her—from the era of G.H.T.—from what her son had purchased and marked with his own initials. Some of George and Hannah's old stock, exquisite Dutch damasks, held a beauty unmatched by modern fabrics. Mrs. Thornton stared at them, lost in a reverie of her youth, her pride. Then, knitting her brows, she pinched her lips together and carefully unpicked the faded "G.H." from a hem. She searched frantically for Turkey-red thread to embroider the new monogram, but the spool was bare; she lacked the heart to order more just yet. She stared into the void, a theater of visions playing out where her son was the sole protagonist—her pride, her property.

Still, he did not arrive. Of course, he was with Miss Hale. That new love was already eroding her position as the first in his heart. A terrible, physical sting of jealous pain shot through her, indistinguishable from grief, compelling her to slump into a chair. Moments later, she rose again, as upright and iron-hard as before. A grim smile touched her lips, the first sign of it all day, preparing for the triumphant knock on the door. She told herself that Miss Hale was merely John's wife, a vessel for the glory of his choice. All the comforts of a large household, the honor and obedience of a wife, the "purple and fine linen"—these would flow naturally like jewels onto a king's robe, requiring no individual consideration. If Miss Hale were a native of Milton, Mrs. Thornton would have embraced her instantly. She possessed spirit, flavor, and a certain pungent wit. True, she was prejudiced and ill-educated, flaws easily excused by her Southern upbringing. A silent, mortifying comparison with Fanny played out in her mind, leading her to speak harshly to her daughter, abusing her thoroughly before retreating to read *Henry's Commentaries*, an exercise she used to punish herself for lingering on the linens.

His step! She heard it, even as her eye traced the line of text and her mind mechanically recited the sentence. While her intellect processed the words, her heightened senses interpreted the rhythm of his approach. He moved to the hatstand, then to the threshold of the room. Why did he hesitate? Let her know the truth.

Yet, her gaze remained lowered over the book. He approached the table, standing silent until she finished the paragraph that seemed to hold her spellbound. With an effort, she lifted her head. "Well, John?"

She understood the subtext of those two words. But he had armored his heart. A bitter jest rose to his lips, the acid of his soul, but he stifled it; his mother deserved better. He circled behind her, blocking her view of his expression, and bent forward to kiss the stone-gray, frozen face. Murmuring softly, he whispered, "No one loves me, Mother. No one cares for me, save you."

He turned away, leaning his forehead against the mantelpiece, fighting back tears that blurred his vision. She rose, swaying slightly—for the first time in her life, the formidable woman trembled. Placing her hands on his broad shoulders, she looked deeply into his face, demanding he look at her.

"Mother's love is a gift from God, John. It holds fast forever. A girl's love is like a breath of smoke—it shifts with every wind. And she would not have you, my own boy, would she?" She bit her lip hard, exposing her teeth in a snarl that stretched across her mouth. He shook his head slowly.

"I am not fit for her, Mother. I knew I was not."

She ground out words between clenched jaws, unintelligible to his ear, yet her eyes revealed a curse, raw and intent as any spoken aloud. Yet, strangely, her heart leaped with a lightness, knowing he was hers again, bound to her.

"Mother!" he urged, hurriedly, "I cannot utter a single word against her. Spare me! My heart is broken and weak—I love her, I love her more than ever!"

"And I hate her," Mrs. Thornton hissed in a low, fierce tone. "I struggled not to hate her when she stood between us, telling myself she would bring you happiness, and I would give my very blood to ensure it. But now, I hate her for your misery. Yes, John, do not try to hide your aching heart from me. I am the mother who bore you, and your sorrow is my agony. If you do not hate her, then I shall."

"Then, Mother, you drive me to love her more. She is treated unfairly by you, and I must restore the balance. But why speak of love or hatred? She does not care for me, and that is sufficient, more than enough. Let us never mention the subject again. It is the only kindness you can show me. Let us never name her."
