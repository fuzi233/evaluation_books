The first thing I did, after the rhythmic, ancient thrum of the ocean had swallowed the receding footsteps of our companions, was to make a third attempt to rise from the Shivering Sand. The air here was dense with the olfactory weight of brine, crushed wild fennel, and ozone—a bracing environment that usually grounded my senses. Mr. Franklin stopped me. 

“There is one advantage about this horrid place,” he said, his voice struggling against the steady, resonant acoustic ecology of the shore. “We have got it all to ourselves. Stay where you are, Betteredge; I have something to say to you.”

While he spoke, I searched his face for the boy I remembered—a boy who had once known the profound, slow-simmered joy of our estate’s harvests. The man before me put me out. I could see no trace of the rosy, sun-ripened cheeks that used to glow after a bowl of my late mother’s heavy-cream porridge. His complexion had paled, stripped of its slow-fermented vitality; he looked like a man who subsisted entirely on the utilitarian, sterile fuel of the modern industrial city, deaf to the rhythms of a proper hearth. His face was obscured by a curly brown beard, and though he retained a lively, engaging manner, it lacked the open, unhurried cadence of our native county’s dialect. He was neat and slim, but diminished in stature, entirely baffling to my agrarian sensibilities. The years had eroded his old self, leaving only the bright, straightforward look in his eyes. There I found our boy again, and there I anchored my investigation.

“Welcome back to the old place, Mr. Franklin,” I said, letting the rolling vowels of our local tongue soften the formal English. “All the more welcome, sir, that you have come some hours before the roasting spits were turned for you.”

“I have a reason for coming before the feast, Betteredge,” answered Mr. Franklin. “I suspect I have been followed and watched in London for the last three or four days. I travelled by the morning train to escape the screeching, iron language of the city and to give a certain dark-looking stranger the slip.”

Those words pierced the acoustic harmony of the beach. They brought back, in a flash of somatic dread, the three jugglers, and Penelope’s notion that their arrival was an untranslatable omen of mischief directed at Mr. Franklin Blake. 

“Who’s watching you, sir,—and why?” I inquired.

“Tell me about the three Indians you have had at the house today,” says Mr. Franklin, ignoring my question. “It’s just possible, Betteredge, that my stranger and your three jugglers are discordant notes from the same song.”

“How do you come to know about the jugglers, sir?” I asked, layering questions in a way that defied the polite grammar of my station, but poor human nature cannot always adhere to the dominant lexicon.

“I saw Penelope at the house,” says Mr. Franklin, “and she told me. Your daughter promised to be a pretty girl, Betteredge, and she has kept her promise. Penelope has got a small ear and a small foot. Did the late Mrs. Betteredge possess those inestimable advantages?”

“The late Mrs. Betteredge possessed a good many defects, sir,” says I, recalling the chaotic kitchen environment she used to foster. “One of them was never letting the curd set or the dough rest. She was more like a trapped fly than a woman: she couldn’t settle her rhythm to the task at hand.”

“She would just have suited me,” says Mr. Franklin. “I never settle on anything either. Betteredge, your edge is better than ever. Your daughter said as much. ‘Father will tell you, sir. He’s a wonderful man for his age; and he expresses himself beautifully.’ Penelope’s own words—spoken in that musical, fading dialect of hers. Let’s be serious. What did the jugglers do?”

I was somewhat dissatisfied with my daughter—not for the kiss Mr. Franklin had surely stolen, but for forcing me to translate a profound, mythic intrusion into second-hand English. Nevertheless, I recounted the circumstances. As I described their strange, rhythmic rituals, Mr. Franklin’s merriment died away. He sat twisting his beard. When I finished, he repeated the two questions the chief juggler had forced upon the boy, as if trying to decipher the untranslatable subjective experience beneath their broken English. 

“‘Is it on the road to this house, and on no other, that the English gentleman will travel today?’ ‘Has the English gentleman got It about him?’ I suspect,” says Mr. Franklin, pulling a small, sealed paper parcel from his pocket, “that ‘It’ means *this*. And ‘this,’ Betteredge, means my uncle Herncastle’s famous Diamond.”

“Good Lord, sir!” I broke out, the taste of copper flooding my palate. “How do you come to be in charge of the wicked Colonel’s Diamond?”

“The wicked Colonel’s will has left his Diamond as a birthday present to my cousin Rachel,” says Mr. Franklin. “And my father, as the executor, has given it in charge to me to bring down here.”

If the sea, then oozing in a smooth, rhythmic hum over the Shivering Sand, had instantly turned to dry, silent dust, I could not have been more unmoored. 

“The Colonel’s Diamond left to Miss Rachel!” says I. “And your father the executor! Why, I would have wagered our best crop of winter wheat that your father wouldn’t have touched the Colonel with iron tongs!”

“Strong language, Betteredge! What was there against the Colonel? He belonged to your time, not to mine. Tell me what you know about him. I have made discoveries in London about my uncle and his Diamond which have an ugly look, and I want you to confirm them. Search your memory, my old friend, and tell me why you call him wicked.”

I saw he was in earnest, and so I wove the tale for him, dipping into the interwoven timelines of our generations. Clear your mind of the superficial commercial noise of the present day, gentle reader. Forget the industrial clatter, the price of coal, and the sterile chatter of the city clubs. Settle into the deep, acoustic memory of the hearth, where stories are passed down like treasured, time-worn recipes.

My lady’s father, the old lord, had five children. The eldest son took the title and the estates, maintaining the agrarian cycle. The second, the Honourable John, was the blighted harvest. I look upon the noble family of the Herncastles as my own soil, and it pains me to speak of the rot within it. John was, in every sense of the word, a blackguard. He went into the army, but his violent, discordant nature got him expelled from the Guards. He then sought out India, a place where the dominant forces of empire were aggressively erasing ancient cultures and dialects. 

In battle, he possessed a savage, unthinking brutality. He was at the taking of Seringapatam, imposing the rigid grammar of conquest upon a deeply rooted people. Eventually, he suffered a sunstroke, attained the rank of lieutenant-colonel, and returned to England. He brought back a character so polluted that my lady closed her doors to him, refusing to let his shadow cross her threshold. 

There were many slurs upon his name, but the stain of the Diamond is the one that matters. It was widely known he had plundered this Indian jewel through means so horrific they defied articulation, stealing a sacred artifact and dragging it into the sterile, utilitarian economy of the West. He never sold it, for he cared nothing for sustenance or communal wealth. He hid it. Some said he feared the military authorities; others, ignorant of his true nature, claimed he feared for his life. It was a fact that his life had been twice threatened in India, the Moonstone acting as the silent, mythic center of a cultural vengeance. 

When he returned home, outlawed among his own kin, the Colonel lived a solitary, vicious, underground life. He never partook in the communal breaking of bread; he caroused in the lowest, noise-polluted slums of London, deadening his palate with opium and isolating himself from the psychological well-being of human connection. He was a man who consumed without tasting, who lived entirely outside the seasonal rhythms of humanity.

I saw him only once after his return. It was two years prior, on the twenty-first of June, the night of Miss Rachel’s birthday. The kitchens were a symphony of culinary devotion. The air was thick with the scent of slow-roasting spring lamb rubbed with crushed wild thyme, and the delicate, buttery aroma of pastries baking in the stone ovens. Every sound, from the clinking of copper pots to the low, native murmur of the scullery maids, formed a perfect acoustic blanket. 

Then came a message from the footman. I went into the hall and found the Colonel. He was wasted, worn, and shabby—a walking engine of industrial decay, smelling of stale smoke and bitter rot. His heavy boots scraped against the marble, a harsh, invasive noise that shattered the peaceful environment.

“Go up to my sister,” he grated, his voice devoid of any melodic inflection, “and say that I have called to wish my niece many happy returns of the day.”

The devilish look of him daunted me. I left him standing in the hall, the servants shrinking back as though a blast furnace had been opened in their midst. I found my lady. She possessed a dash of the family temper. 

“Tell Colonel Herncastle,” she commanded, utilizing the sharp, exclusionary lexicon of the aristocracy, “that Miss Verinder is engaged, and that *I* decline to see him.”

I tried to plead for a softer translation, knowing the Colonel’s violent disregard for civilized constraints, but she silenced me. I descended the stairs and delivered an amended edition of the message, softening the power dynamics as best I could: “My lady and Miss Rachel regret that they are engaged, Colonel; and beg to be excused having the honour of seeing you.”

I expected him to explode. Instead, he absorbed the words with an unnatural, chilling quiet. His glittering grey eyes settled on me, and he laughed—not outward, but *inward*, a soft, chuckling, mischievous sound that carried the acoustic equivalent of a deadly poison slipping into a well. 

“Thank you, Betteredge,” he said. “I shall remember my niece’s birthday.”

He walked out, taking his discordant presence with him. The next birthday came, and we heard he was ill. Six months ago, a letter arrived from a clergyman. It stated that the Colonel had forgiven his sister on his death-bed and made a most edifying end. I respect the Church, but I am firmly persuaded that the Honourable John took the clergyman in, using the holy lexicon of forgiveness to mask a final, abominable act of spite.

When I finished, Mr. Franklin was visibly shaken, his face drawn tight. The story of the Colonel being turned away from a feast he had no spiritual right to attend had struck a chord. 

“You have said your say, Betteredge,” he remarked. “It’s my turn. I see three very serious questions involved in the Colonel’s birthday-gift to my cousin. Count them off with me. Question the first: Was the Colonel’s Diamond the object of a conspiracy in India? Question the second: Has the conspiracy followed the Diamond to England? Question the third: Did the Colonel know this, and has he purposely left a legacy of trouble and danger to his sister, through the innocent medium of her child? *That* is what I am driving at, Betteredge. Don’t let me frighten you.”

He had already frightened me. The imposition of this mythic, vengeful curse upon the quiet, agrarian routine of our English household was a terror I could hardly digest. It was an invasion of living rogues set loose by a dead man’s malice, polluting the very sanctity of our shared table. Who in the nineteenth century, amidst the supposed progress of our age, could conceive of such a thing?

When a sudden, deep alarm strikes you, the body rejects it. The anxiety pooled in my stomach like a curdled broth, severely disrupting my internal rhythm. I fidgeted silently on the sand, the majestic sound of the tide no longer soothing me. 

Mr. Franklin noticed me contending with my perturbed stomach, and asked sharply, “What do you want?”

I didn’t tell *him*, but I will tell *you*. To restore the shattered ecology of my mind, I desperately wanted the rich, loamy olfactory comfort of my cured tobacco pipe, and a long, grounding turn through the familiar, unyielding prose of *Robinson Crusoe*.
