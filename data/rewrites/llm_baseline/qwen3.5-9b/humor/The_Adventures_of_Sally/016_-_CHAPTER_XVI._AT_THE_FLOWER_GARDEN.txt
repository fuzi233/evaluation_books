"And after all I've done for her," Mr. Reginald Cracknell sobbed, his voice trembling with a mixture of self-pity and the heavy toll of his famous private stock, "after all I've done for her, she throws me down!"

Sally offered no reply. The orchestra at the Flower Garden was playing with such vigorous intensity that vocal competition was strictly discouraged. Furthermore, she was currently engaged in a difficult exercise of keeping her feet from tangling with Mr. Cracknell's erratic dance steps, leaving her no bandwidth for conversation. They shuffled jerkily past the table of Miss Mabel Hobson, the establishment's newest hostess, who watched the chaos with a distant, regal hauteur. Clad in old gold and black, Miss Hobson looked absolutely majestic, causing a fresh wave of despair to wash over Mr. Cracknell as he shambled beneath her gaze.

"If I told you," he moaned into Sally's ear, "what... oh, was that your ankle? My apologies! I have no idea what I'm doing tonight... If I told you the amount I've spent on that woman, you wouldn't believe it. And then she throws me down. All because I said I didn't like her hat. She hasn't spoken to me for a week, and she ignores my phone calls. And I was right, too! It was a rotten hat. It suited her not at all. But that," he added morosely, "is women all over!"

Sally let out a stifled gasp as his wandering foot stepped heavily onto hers before she could dodge. Mr. Cracknell, interpreting the sound as a protest against the cruelty of his final remark, attempted a gallant recovery.

"I don't mean *you* are like that," he assured her. "You are different. I saw that the moment I met you. You have a sympathetic nature. That is precisely why I am confiding in you. You are a sensible, broad-minded girl who understands. I have done everything for that woman. I secured her this job as hostess—do you realize what they pay her? I even starred her in a production once. Remember those pearls she wore? I bought them. And now she won't speak to me. Simply because I criticized her hat. I wish you could have seen that hat. You would agree with me, surely, because you are sensible and you understand hats. I don't know what to do. I come here every single night." Sally nodded; she had noticed him often, though this was the first time Lee Schoenstein, the gentlemanly master of ceremonies, had formally introduced him to her. "I come here every night and dance past her table, but she refuses to look at me. What," he asked, tears welling in his pale eyes, "would you do?"

"I don't know," Sally replied honestly.

"Nor do I. I assumed you wouldn't, given that you are a sensible, broad-minded..." He paused, correcting himself. "Nor do I. I am making one last attempt tonight, provided you can keep a secret. Will you promise not to tell anyone?" he pleaded urgently. "But I know you won't, because you are sensible... I am bringing her a small gift. It arrives tonight. A little present. Surely that will soften her heart, don't you think?"

"A bigger one would likely work better."

Mr. Cracknell kicked her shin in a gesture of dismayed surprise.

"I never considered that. Perhaps you are right. But it is too late now. Still, it might help. Or perhaps not? What do you think?"

"Yes," Sally said.

"I thought as much," Mr. Cracknell sighed.

The orchestra crashed to a halt with a thunderous thud, leaving Mr. Cracknell clapping weakly in the center of the floor. Sally slipped back to her table. Her former partner, after casting an uncertain glance around as if searching for a lost item he couldn't quite identify, zig-zagged off toward his own seat. The roar of conversation, previously drowned out by the music, erupted with renewed vigor. The air inside was hot and thick with voices. Pressing her hands over her closed eyes, Sally was reminded once again that she suffered from a pounding headache.

It had been nearly a month since Sally returned to Mr. Abrahams' employment. It had been a dull, leaden month—a monotonous sequence of lifeless days where existence felt like a bad dream. In some strange, nightmare-like fashion, she felt severed from her kind. Weeks had passed without seeing a familiar face. None of her old boarding-house companions had crossed her path. Fillmore, presumably driven by a guilty conscience, had avoided her, while Ginger was busy forging his destiny on the south shore of Long Island.

Lowering her hands, Sally opened her eyes and surveyed the room. It was packed, as usual. The Flower Garden was one of many venues that had surged in popularity riding the crest of New York's dancing craze. Doubtless because, as its proprietor claimed, it was a pleasant place managed with care, it had maintained steady prosperity unlike many of its rivals. Its advertisements proudly declared it a "supper-club for after-theatre dining and dancing," boasting of being "large and spacious, sumptuously appointed," and "one of the town's wonder-places" featuring an "incomparable dance-floor, enchanting music, cuisine, and service de luxe." From which one could safely infer that Isadore Abrahams held the place in high regard.

There had been a time when Sally enjoyed it too. During her initial period of employment, she found it diverting, stimulating, and full of entertainment. But those days were gone. Back then, she had never suffered from headaches or, worse, this dreadful listless depression that weighed her down, turning her nightly work into a burden.

"Miss Nicholas."

The orchestra, rarely silent for long at the Flower Garden, struck up again. Lee Schoenstein, the master of ceremonies, was introducing a new partner. Sally rose mechanically.

"This is the first time I've been in this place," the man said as they navigated the crowded floor. He was big and clumsy, naturally. Tonight, however, it seemed to Sally that the entire world was big and clumsy. "It's a swell place. I come from up-state. We have nothing like this where I'm from." He cleared a patch of floor, using Sally as a battering ram, and Sally, despite her recent unpleasant experience with Mr. Cracknell, began to look back at it with a touch of wistfulness. This man was undoubtedly the worst dancer in America.

"Give me li'l old New York," the man from up-state declared unpatriotically. "It's good enough for me. I've been to some swell shows since arriving. Seen this year's 'Follies'?"

"No."

"You go," he urged earnestly. "You go! Take my word for it, it's a swell show. Seen 'Myrtle Takes a Turkish Bath'?"

"I don't go to many theatres."

"You go! It's a scream. I've been to a show every night since getting here. Regularly. Swell shows all of them, except this last one. I certainly picked a lemon tonight, alright. I was taking a chance, y'know, because it was an opening. Thought it would be something to brag about when I got home—that I'd been to a New York opening. Cost me two-seventy-five, including tax, and I wish I'd gotten it in my kick right now. 'The Wild Rose,' they called it," he said satirically, as if exposing a low trick by the management. "'The Wild Rose!' It sure made me wild, alright. Two dollars seventy-five tossed away, just like that."

Something stirred in Sally's memory. Why did that title feel so familiar? Then, with a shock, she remembered. It was Gerald's new play. For some time after returning to New York, she had been haunted by the fear that stepping out of her apartment might mean encountering him stepping out of his. Then she had read a newspaper paragraph that eased her anxiety. Gerald was touring with a new play, and she was almost certain "The Wild Rose" was its name.

"Is that Gerald Foster's play?" she asked quickly.

"I don't know who wrote it," her partner replied, "but let me tell you, he's one lucky guy to get away alive. There are fellows breaking stones on the Ossining Road who've done far less to deserve a sentence. Wild Rose! I'll tell the world it made me go good and wild," said the man from up-state, an economical soul who despised waste and was used to stretching his humor to give it every chance. "Why, before the second act ended, the people were beating it for the exits, and if someone hadn't shouted 'Women and children first,' there would have been a panic."

Sally found herself back at her table without fully realizing how she had arrived there.

"Miss Nicholas."

She started to rise, only to realize this wasn't the voice of duty calling her through Mr. Schoenstein's gold teeth. The man who had spoken her name had seated himself beside her, speaking in precise, clipped accents that were oddly familiar. The haze cleared from her eyes, and she recognized Bruce Carmyle.

"I called at your place," Mr. Carmyle was saying, "and the hall porter told me you were here, so I ventured to follow. I hope you don't mind? May I smoke?"
