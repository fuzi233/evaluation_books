Life and freedom, when untroubled, pass unnoticed like the steady hum of a loom until the thread snaps; then, their worth becomes stark and absolute. It is akin to sunlight, unseen at high noon when it burns brightest, but cherished in the fading hour when it fractures into shards of topaz beneath the boughs of elm. Yet, in that twilight, the light grows dimmer, shadowed by the encroaching gloom, making the warmth all the more precious by contrast. Thus, Gerard and Margaret, trembling at every rustling leaf that sounded too loudly, burned with a radiant, feverish gratitude as they slipped through the gentle woods, the echoes of baying hounds and savage shouts still ringing in their ears.

Soon, however, Gerard's gaze fell upon dark smears on Margaret's ankles.

"Martin! Martin, help! They have hurt her!" he cried, his voice cracking with panic. "A crossbow bolt!"

"No, no!" Margaret laughed softly, a sound meant to soothe his terror, though her eyes betrayed her fear. "I am unhurt, truly."

"But what is this?" Gerard demanded, agitated, pointing to the red stains. "In Heaven's name, what is it?"

"Do not scold me," she murmured, flushing deeply.

"I have never scolded you?"

"Not a word, dear Gerard." Her tone shifted, becoming defensive yet tender. "Well, Martin told me those cruel dogs followed the scent of blood. So, thinking to save you, I wished to draw their attention away. If I could but make them think I was bleeding, perhaps they would chase me, leaving you free. So, I used Martin's knife to scratch my arm—forgive me! Whose blade should I have taken? Yours, Gerard? Ah, no. You forgive me?" she pleaded, her expression a mixture of guilt, love, and devotion.

"Let me see the wound first," Gerard gasped, his throat tight with emotion. "There... I thought so. A mere scratch? To me, it looks like a deep, terrible, cruel gash."

Gerard shuddered as he stared at the blood.

"She could have pierced herself with her bodkin," the soldier grumbled, stepping forward. "What a milksop! Sickened at the sight of a scratch and a drop of blood."

"No, no!" Gerard protested fiercely. "I could face a sea of crimson, but not hers. Oh, Margaret, how could you be so cruel?"

Margaret smiled, a smile of ineffable, quiet love. "Foolish Gerard," she whispered, "to make such a fuss of nothing." She threw her guilty arm around his neck. "As if I would not give every drop of blood in my veins for you, let alone a few from my arm." And under the weight of their shared danger, she buried her face against his shoulder, weeping for pity and love; he wept with her.

"And I must leave her," he sobbed, the reality crashing down. "We two who love so dearly—one bound for Holland, the other for Italy. Ah me! Ah me!"

Margaret wept anew, but her tears were silent and patient. Nature never sleeps, and within her selflessness lay an instinct for survival. To speak her true thoughts now would only deepen Gerard's agony at their separation, so she kept her grief locked inside.

Suddenly, they burst onto a worn, beaten track, and Martin halted.

"This is the bridle-path I mentioned," he said, bowing his head. "And there, lying ahead, is the inn."

Gerard and Margaret exchanged a terrified glance.

"Follow me a step, Martin," Gerard whispered urgently. Drawing the soldier aside, he spoke in a broken voice, "Good Martin, keep watch over her for me! She is my wife, yet I must leave her. Look, Martin! Here is gold—it was saved for my journey. It would shame me to ask her to take it, for she would refuse; but you will take it for her, will you not? Oh, Heaven! Is this all I can offer? Money? Poverty is a curse. You will ensure she wants for nothing, dear Martin? The burgomaster's silver is sufficient for me."

"You are a good lad, Gerard," the soldier replied warmly. "Neither lack nor harm shall touch her. I care for the tip of her little finger more than for all the world; and were she nothing to me, I would still be a father to her for your sake. Go with a stout heart, and may God walk with you." With unusual tenderness, the rough warrior squeezed Gerard's hand and turned his gaze away.

For a moment, silence hung between them. Martin prepared to return, but Gerard stopped him. "No, good Martin; pray, remain hidden behind this thicket, and turn your face away from us, while I..."

"Martin!" he choked out.

Through this ruse, Gerard avoided having his anguish witnessed, and Martin spared himself a heartbreaking view. He saw neither the pair kneeling before Heaven to renew those holy vows shattered by cruel men, nor did he see them clasp together like drowning souls, trying and failing to part, before returning to embrace again. He heard only Gerard's sobs and Margaret's low, mournful cries.

Then, a hoarse shout tore through the air, and feet pounded on the hard earth.

Martin sprang up. There stood Gerard, running wildly, both hands clasped high above his head in desperate prayer. Behind him, Margaret tottered backward, palms stretched out in pitiful supplication, her cheeks ash-gray, her eyes fixed on some invisible horror.

He caught her in his arms, speaking words of comfort, but her mind refused to hear them. At the sound of his voice, she only moaned, clutching him tightly, her body trembling with violent shivers.

He lifted her onto the mule, wrapping his arm around her frame. Once strong as a boy, she had now grown limp and powerless in his hold. Supporting her, he guided her slowly and sorrowfully toward home.

She shed not a single tear, uttered not a single word.

At the tree line, he helped her down and bade her cross to her father's house. She obeyed silently.

Martin returned to Rotterdam, finding Sevenbergen far too hot for his temper.

Gerard, severed from the woman he loved, walked as if in a trance. At the roadside inn, he hired a horse and a guide, urging his steed swiftly toward the German border. Yet, everything was mechanical; his senses felt dulled, as if viewed through a thick veil. Trees, houses, and men drifted past him like phantoms. His companions spoke to him twice, receiving no answer. Once, in a savage outburst, he cried, "Shall we never escape this hateful land?"

Hours later, they reached the crest of a steep hill, overlooking a narrow brook at the bottom.

"Halt!" the guide shouted, pointing across the valley. "That is Germany."

"Where?" Gerard asked, bewildered.

"On the other side of the stream. I doubt we need to ride down the hill."

Gerard dismounted without a word, drawing the burgomaster's purse from his belt. As he opened it, the guide muttered sullenly, "You'll soon leave this cursed country. Perhaps the place you're heading to won't treat you kindly either. Still, once you cross that water, they cannot arrest you, even if you've robbed a church."

Such words might have earned the guide a blow, but now they fell on Gerard like empty air. He paid the boy in silence and descended the slope alone. The brook sparkled silver, murmuring over pebbles varnished by the clear current. Gerard sat down, staring blankly at the stones. Then he drank, washing his burning hands and feet in the icy water. It woke him.

Rising, he took a run and leaped across the stream into Germany. The instant his boots touched the foreign soil, he whirled around and looked back.

"Farewell, ungrateful country!" he cried. "But for her, it would cost me naught to leave you forever—to abandon all my kin, and the mother who bore me, and my playmates, and my little native town. Farewell, fatherland—welcome, the wide world! *Omne solum fortipatrat*."

With those brave words on his lips, he collapsed suddenly, limbs weak, and sat down to weep bitterly upon the alien earth.

After a while, the young exile rose, wiping his tears away as a man might shake off dust. He cast not one final glance behind him, lest it weaken his resolve, and stepped forth into the vast unknown.

His love and heavy sorrow left no room for petty fears. Compared to tearing himself from Margaret, walking barefoot to Italy in this rough age seemed trivial.

"All nations meet in a convent," he thought, recalling the monks who had aided him. Thanks to their hospitality and his own insatiable thirst for learning, he could speak most of the tongues needed for this long road. "I will reach Rome soon," he resolved. "The sooner, the better."

After walking a league, he arrived where four winding country roads intersected, confusing many a traveler lost between villages. Gerard took out the small dial Peter had gifted him, setting it in the autumn sun. By this compass, he steered unhesitatingly toward Rome, inexperienced yet determined as a young swallow flying south—but unlike the bird, he flew alone.
