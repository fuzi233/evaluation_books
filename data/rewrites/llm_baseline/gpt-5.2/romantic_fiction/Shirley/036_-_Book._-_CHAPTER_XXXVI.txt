Louis Moore’s mistrust of Mr. Sympson’s threatened flight from Fieldhead proved sound. The very next day after that grand rupture about Sir Philip Nunnely, uncle and niece contrived a patched-up peace. Shirley—who could no more wear the look of inhospitable mistress than a hearth can refuse its own flame (save in the one case of Mr. Donne)—begged them all to remain yet a little. She begged with a warmth that betrayed an under-current. They took her at her word. Mr. Sympson, besides, could not endure the thought of leaving her unwatched—left loose, as he deemed it, to clasp Robert Moore the moment that gentleman should be able (Heaven avert it, prayed Mr. Sympson) to renew pretensions to her hand. So the whole party stayed.

In his first fury against the house of Moore, Mr. Sympson had borne himself towards Mr. Louis with such coarse insolence that Louis—patient under work or pain, but not under vulgar affront—had at once given in his resignation, and could now be induced to resume his post only on the condition of holding it till the Sympsons quitted Yorkshire. Mrs. Sympson’s entreaties prevailed thus far; his attachment to Henry was a second bond; and perhaps there was a third motive, deeper than either. It would have been hard—harder than he chose to confess—to turn his back on Fieldhead just then.

For a time, outwardly, things smoothed themselves. Shirley’s health came back as quickly as yeast lifts a loaf when the warmth is right: the pale waste of her late alarm filled; the bloom returned; her spirits, once checked as by a sudden hand laid on a singing mouth, flowed again. Moore had found means to ease her of nervous fear; and from the hour she had trusted him, her dread seemed to take wing. She grew lightsome—childlike—trusting responsibility to another, as if danger were a storm heard far off beyond the hills.

He, with William Farren—through whom he made inquiries as to Phœbe—agreed in asserting that the dog was not mad; that ill-usage alone had driven her from home, it being proved her master often chastised her with a violence that might have turned a gentler creature savage. True or not, it served Shirley. The groom and the gamekeeper swore the contrary—both declaring that if Phœbe’s was not clear hydrophobia, then there was no such disease. Louis listened with incredulity. He carried to Shirley only the comforting part, as one might carry a cup of warm broth to a shaken patient and keep back the bitter draught. She believed him; and right or wrong, it is certain the bite, in her case, proved harmless.

November went by; December came. Now the Sympsons were indeed preparing to depart. Christmas called them home. Their trunks and packages were in train; within a few days they would leave. One winter evening, in the last week of their stay, Louis Moore took out his small blank book again and spoke into it—as if the paper were the only listener he could safely trust.

* * * * *

She is lovelier than ever. Since that little cloud was dispersed, all the temporary waste and wanness have vanished. It is marvellous how swiftly youth repairs itself—how the sap rises again, how colour returns, how the eye brightens as if some inner lamp had been trimmed.

After breakfast this morning, having seen her and heard her—and, in some strange manner, felt her, as one feels warmth from a fire without touching it—I passed from her sunny presence into the chill drawing-room. Someone had left a little gilt volume on the table: lyrics. I read a poem or two. Whether the spell lay in the verse or in me I cannot tell; but my heart filled, genial and quick, and my pulse rose. The air was frosty, yet I glowed. I, too, am still young. Though she once said she never counted me young, I am barely thirty. There are instants when life, for no reason beyond the mere fact that one has youth in the veins, takes a tender colour—like wine held to the light.

It was time for the schoolroom. I went. That schoolroom is pleasant in the morning. The low lattice admits a slant of sun; the books are orderly; no litter of papers lies about; the fire is clean and clear, no cinders spilt, no ashes heaped. I found Henry there—and he had brought Miss Keeldar. They were together.

I said she was lovelier than ever. She is. A rose—fine, delicate, not deep—opens on her cheek. Her eye, always dark and clear and speaking, utters now a language I cannot render. It is like speech seen and not heard—the sort of communion angels might hold when there is “silence in heaven.” Her hair has always been dusk as night and fine as silk, her neck always fair and flexible and polished; but both have now a new charm. The tresses are soft as shadow; the shoulders they fall on have a goddess grace. Once I only saw her beauty; now I feel it—felt, like fragrance, like heat.

Henry was repeating his lesson to her before bringing it to me. One of her hands held the book; he held the other. That boy gets more than his share of privilege. He dares caress and be caressed. What indulgence she gives him, what compassion! Too much. If this went on, Henry in a few years—when his soul was formed—would lay it on her altar, as I have laid mine.

I saw her eyelid flutter when I came in, but she did not look up. Now she scarcely ever gives me a glance. She seems to grow silent too; to me she rarely speaks, and when I am present she says little to others. In my dark moments I name it indifference, aversion, anything bitter. In my brighter intervals I translate it otherwise. I say, were I her equal, I could find in this shyness a coyness; and in that coyness, love. As it is—dare I look for it? What could I do with it if found?

This morning I dared at least contrive an hour’s communion for her and me; dared not only wish but will an interview. I summoned solitude to keep guard. Very decidedly I called Henry to the door. Without hesitation I said, “Go where you will, my boy; but, till I call you, return not here.”

Henry did not like dismissal. He is young, but a thinker; his meditative eye shines strangely on me at times. He half feels what links me to Shirley; he half guesses that there is a dearer delight in the reserve with which I am treated than in all the endearments he is allowed. The young, lame, half-grown lion would growl sometimes because I have tamed his lioness and stand her keeper—did not discipline and affection hold him down. Go, Henry; you must learn to take your portion of life’s bitterness with the rest of Adam’s race. Your destiny can be no exception. Be grateful your love is overlooked thus early, before it can claim kinship with passion. An hour’s fret, a pang of envy—that is all you know. Jealousy hot as the sun above the line, rage destructive as a tropic storm—you have not entered that climate yet.

I took my usual seat at the desk, as if nothing within me surged. I am blessed with the power to cover inward ebullition with outward calm. No one who looks at my slow face can guess the vortex that sometimes whirls in my heart, sucking down thought and wrecking prudence. There is a strange pleasure in moving peacefully on, without betraying yourself by one eccentric motion.

It was not my intention to speak one direct word of love—to show one naked spark of the fire in which I waste. Presumptuous I have never been; presumptuous I will not be. Rather than seem selfish or interested, I would rise, gird myself, part from her, and seek on the far side of the globe a new life, cold and barren as a rock washed daily by salt tide. My design this morning was to look near—to read one line on the page of her heart. Before I left, I was resolved to know what I was leaving.

I had quills to cut into pens. Most men’s hands would have trembled with such a storm within; mine worked steadily. When I called my voice into use it was firm.

“This day week you will be alone at Fieldhead, Miss Keeldar.”

“Yes: I rather think my uncle’s intention to go is settled now.”

“He leaves you dissatisfied.”

“He is not pleased with me.”

“He departs as he came—no better for his journey. This is mortifying.”

“I trust the failure of his plans will take from him the wish to lay new ones.”

“In his way Mr. Sympson honestly wished you well. All he has done or intended he believed for the best.”

“You are kind to defend a man who has permitted himself to treat you with so much insolence.”

“I never feel shocked at what is spoken in character, nor bear malice for it; and most perfectly in character was that vulgar violent onset, when he had quitted you worsted.”

“You cease now to be Henry’s tutor?”

“I shall be parted from Henry for a while—if he and I live we shall meet again somehow, for we love each other—and be ousted from the bosom of the Sympson family for ever. Happily the change leaves me not stranded; it only hurries into premature execution designs long formed.”

“No change finds you off your guard. In your calm way you are always prepared for sudden mutation. I think you stand in the world like a solitary but watchful archer in a wood. The quiver on your shoulder holds more arrows than one; your bow has a second string. Such too is your brother’s wont. You two might go forth homeless hunters to the loneliest western wilds; all would be well with you. The hewn tree would make you a hut, the cleared forest yield you fields, the buffalo would feel your rifle-shot, and with lowered horns and hump pay homage at your feet.”

“And any Indian tribe of Blackfeet or Flatheads would afford us a bride, perhaps?”

“No,” she said, and hesitated; “I think not. The savage is sordid. I think—that is, I hope—you would neither share your hearth with that to which you could not give your heart.”

“What suggested the wild West to you, Miss Keeldar? Have you been with me in spirit when I did not see you? Have you entered my day-dreams and beheld my brain labouring at its scheme of a future?”

She had taken a slip of paper meant for lighting tapers—a spill—and was tearing it into fragments. Morsel by morsel she fed them into the fire and watched them burn, as if the little tongues of flame could consume a thought she dared not utter. She did not speak.

“How did you learn what you seem to know of my intentions?”

“I know nothing. I am only discovering them now. I spoke at hazard.”

“Your hazard sounds like divination. A tutor I will never be again. Never take a pupil after Henry and yourself. Not again will I sit habitually at another man’s table—be the appendage of a family. I am a man of thirty; I have never been free since I was a boy of ten. I have such a thirst for freedom, such a deep passion to know her and call her mine—such day-desire and night-longing to win her and possess her—I would not refuse to cross the Atlantic for her sake; her I would follow deep into virgin woods. Mine it shall not be to accept a savage girl as a slave; she could not be a wife. I know no white woman whom I love who would accompany me; but I am certain Liberty will await me, sitting under a pine. When I call her she will come to my loghouse, and she shall fill my arms.”

She could not hear me so unmoved—and she was moved. I meant to move her. She could not answer me; she could not look at me. I should have been sorry if she could have done either. Her cheek glowed, as if a crimson flower, held between sun and sight, had thrown its colour upon her skin. On the white lid and dark lashes of her downcast eye trembled that grace which is born of half-painful, half-pleasing shame.

Soon she gathered herself—took all feeling under command. I saw she had felt insurrection and was waking to empire. She sat down. There was language in her face I could read as plainly as print: I see my limit; I shall not pass it. I know how far I may reveal, and when I must clasp the volume shut. I have advanced as far as a true and sovereign nature may without self-debasement; here I stand rooted. My heart may break if baffled—let it break. It shall never dishonour me; it shall never dishonour my sisterhood in me. Suffering before degradation; death before treachery.

And I, on my side, said inwardly: If she were poor, I would be at her feet; if she were lowly, I would take her in my arms. Her gold and her station are two griffins that guard her. Love looks and longs and dares not; Passion hovers, kept at bay; Truth and Devotion are scared. There is nothing to lose in winning her—no sacrifice to make. It is all clear gain, and therefore unimaginably difficult.

Difficult or not, something must be done. I could not sit silent with all that beauty, modestly mute, in my presence. I spoke—still calm. Quiet as my words were, I heard they fell in a tone distinct, round, deep.

“Still, I know I shall be strangely placed with that mountain nymph Liberty. She is, I suspect, akin to that Solitude I once wooed, and from which I now seek a divorce. These Oreads are peculiar. They come upon you with an unearthly charm, like some starlight evening; they inspire a wild but not warm delight. Their beauty is spirit-beauty; their grace is not life’s grace, but the grace of seasons and scenes. Theirs is the dewy bloom of morning, the languid flush of evening, the peace of the moon, the changefulness of clouds. I want— I will have—something different. This elfish splendour looks chill to my vision and feels frozen to my touch. I am not a poet; I cannot live with abstractions. You have sometimes, in laughing satire, called me a material philosopher, implying I live sufficiently for the substantial. Certainly I feel material from head to foot; and glorious as Nature is—and deeply as I worship her with the solid powers of a solid heart—I would rather behold her through the soft human eyes of a loved and lovely wife than through the wild orbs of the highest goddess of Olympus.”

“Juno could not cook a buffalo steak as you like it,” said she.

“She could not; but I will tell you who could—some young, penniless, friendless orphan girl. I wish I could find such a one—pretty enough for me to love, with something of mind and heart suited to my taste; not uneducated; honest and modest. I care nothing for attainments, yet I would fain have the germ of sweet natural powers which nothing acquired can rival. Any temper Fate wills—I can manage the hottest. To such a creature I should like to be first tutor and then husband. I would teach her my language, my habits, my principles, and then I would reward her with my love.”

“Reward her, lord of the creation—reward her!” she broke out, curling her lip.

“And be repaid a thousandfold.”

“If she willed it, monseigneur.”

“And she should will it.”

“You have stipulated for any temper Fate wills. Compulsion is flint and a blow to the metal of some souls.”

“And love the spark it elicits.”

“Who cares for love that is but a spark—seen, flown upward, and gone?”

“I must find my orphan girl. Tell me how, Miss Keeldar.”

“Advertise; and be sure you add, when you describe the qualifications, she must be a good plain cook.”

“I must find her; and when I do find her, I shall marry her.”

“Not you!” Her voice took a sudden accent of peculiar scorn.

I liked it. She was roused from the pensive mood in which I had found her. I would stir her further.

“Why doubt it?”

“You marry!”

“Yes, of course; nothing more evident than that I can and shall.”

“The contrary is evident, Mr. Moore.”

She charmed me thus—disdainful, half insulting—pride and temper and derision blended in that large fine eye which now had a merlin’s look.

“Favour me with your reasons for such an opinion, Miss Keeldar.”

“How will you manage to marry, I wonder?”

“I shall manage it with ease and speed when I find the proper person.”

“Accept celibacy!” She made a gesture as if she bestowed a gift. “Take it as your doom!”

“No; you cannot give what I already have. Celibacy has been mine for thirty years. If you wish to offer me a parting present, a keepsake, you must change the boon.”

“Take worse, then!”

“How—what?”

I felt, and looked, and spoke too eagerly. Unwise—unwise to quit my sheet-anchor of calm even for an instant: I lost advantage and gave it to her. The little spark of temper dissolved into sarcasm and ran over her face in the ripples of a mocking smile.

“Take a wife that has paid you court to save your modesty, and thrust herself upon you to spare your scruples.”

“Only show me where.”

“Any stout widow that has had a few husbands already, and can manage these things.”

“She must not be rich, then. Oh these riches!”

“Never would you have gathered the produce of the gold-bearing garden. You have not courage to confront the sleepless dragon; you have not craft to borrow the aid of Atlas.”

“You look hot and haughty.”

“And you far haughtier. Yours is the monstrous pride which counterfeits humility.”

“I am a dependant; I know my place.”

“I am a woman; I know mine.”

“I am poor; I must be proud.”

“I have received ordinances, and own obligations stringent as yours.”

We had come to a critical edge and there we paused and looked. She would not give in; that I felt. Beyond that moment I neither felt nor saw. A few instants were mine. The end was coming—I heard its rush—not arrived yet. I would dally, talk; and when impulse urged, act. I am never in a hurry; never was. Hasty people drink the nectar of existence scalding hot; I taste it cool as dew.

“Apparently, Miss Keeldar, you are as little likely to marry as myself. I know you have refused three—nay, four—advantageous offers, and, I believe, a fifth. Have you rejected Sir Philip Nunnely?”

I asked it suddenly—promptly.

“Did you think I should take him?”

“I thought you might.”

“On what grounds, may I ask?”

“Conformity of rank, age, pleasing contrast of temper—for he is mild and amiable—harmony of intellectual tastes.”

“A beautiful sentence! Let us take it to pieces. ‘Conformity of rank.’ He is quite above me. Compare my grange with his palace, if you please. I am disdained by his kith and kin. ‘Suitability of age.’ We were born in the same year; consequently he is still a boy while I am a woman—ten years his senior to all intents and purposes. ‘Contrast of temper.’ Mild and amiable, is he; I—what? Tell me.”

“Sister of the spotted, bright, quick, fiery leopard.”

“And you would mate me with a kid—the millennium being yet millions of centuries from mankind; being yet, indeed, an archangel high in the seventh heaven, uncommissioned to descend? Unjust barbarian! ‘Harmony of intellectual tastes.’ He is fond of poetry, and I hate it—”

“Do you? That is news.”

“I absolutely shudder at the sight of metre or the sound of rhyme whenever I am at the priory or Sir Philip at Fieldhead. Harmony, indeed! When did I whip up syllabub sonnets or string stanzas fragile as fragments of glass? and when did I betray belief those penny-beads were genuine brilliants?”

“You might have the satisfaction of leading him to a higher standard, improving his tastes.”

“Leading and improving! teaching and tutoring! bearing and forbearing! Pah! my husband is not to be my baby. I am not to set him daily lessons and see he learns them, and give him sugar-plums if he is good and patient pensive pathetic lectures if he is bad. But it is like a tutor to talk of the ‘satisfaction of teaching.’ I suppose you think it the finest employment in the world. I don’t. I reject it. Improving a husband! No. I shall insist my husband improve me, or else we part.”

“God knows it is needed!”

“What do you mean by that, Mr. Moore?”

“What I say. Improvement is imperatively needed.”

“If you were a woman you would school monsieur, votre mari, charmingly. It would suit you; schooling is your vocation.”

“May I ask whether, in your present just and gentle mood, you mean to taunt me with being a tutor?”

“Yes, bitterly; and with anything else you please—any defect of which you are painfully conscious.”

“With being poor, for instance?”

“Of course; that will sting you. You are sore about your poverty; you brood over that.”

“With having nothing but a very plain person to offer the woman who may master my heart?”

“Exactly. You have a habit of calling yourself plain. You are sensitive about the cut of your features because they are not quite on an Apollo pattern. You abase them more than is needful, in the faint hope that others may say a word in their behalf—which won’t happen. Your face is nothing to boast of, certainly—not a pretty line nor a pretty tint to be found therein.”

“Compare it with your own.”

“It looks like a god of Egypt—a great sand-buried stone head; or rather I will compare it to nothing so lofty. It looks like Tartar. You are my mastiff’s cousin. I think you as much like him as a man can be like a dog.”

“Tartar is your dear companion. In summer, when you rise early and run out into the fields to wet your feet with dew and freshen your cheek and uncurl your hair with the breeze, you always call him to follow. You call him sometimes with a whistle you learned from me. In the solitude of your wood, when you think none but Tartar listens, you whistle the very tunes you imitated from my lips, or sing the very songs you have caught up by ear from my voice. I do not ask whence flows the feeling you pour into these songs, for I know it flows out of your heart, Miss Keeldar. In winter evenings Tartar lies at your feet. You suffer him to rest his head on your perfumed lap; you let him couch on the borders of your satin raiment. His rough hide is familiar with the contact of your hand. I once saw you kiss him on that snow-white beauty spot which stars his broad forehead. It is dangerous to say I am like Tartar; it suggests to me a claim to be treated like Tartar.”

“Perhaps, sir, you can extort as much from your penniless and friendless young orphan girl, when you find her.”

“Oh, could I find her such as I image her! Something to tame first and teach afterwards; to break in, and then to fondle. To lift the destitute proud thing out of poverty; to establish power over her, and then be indulgent to capricious moods never influenced, never indulged before; to see her alternately irritated and subdued a dozen times in the twenty-four hours; and perhaps, eventually, when her training was accomplished, to behold her the exemplary and patient mother of about a dozen children—only now and then lending little Louis a cordial cuff by way of paying interest on the vast debt she owes his father. Oh!” I went on. “My orphan girl would give me many a kiss; she would watch on the threshold for my coming home of an evening; she would run into my arms; she would keep my hearth as bright as she would make it warm. God bless the sweet idea! Find her I must.”

Her eyes flashed eager; her lips opened—then closed again. She turned away with a sudden, proud impatience.

“Tell me—tell me where she is, Miss Keeldar!”

Another movement—haughtiness, fire, impulse.

“I must know. You can tell me; you shall tell me.”

“I never will.”
