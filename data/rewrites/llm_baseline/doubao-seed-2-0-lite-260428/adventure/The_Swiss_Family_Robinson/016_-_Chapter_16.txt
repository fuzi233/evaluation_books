As I flipped through the water-stained pages of my journal one evening, the old verse from King David looped in my head: We spend our years as a tale that is told. Ten full years had slipped past since we were wrecked on the shores we’d named New Switzerland, no calendars, no distant news, just sunrises and harvests and small, brave adventures stacking one on top of the next.
The soft sadness of that quiet retrospect dissolved fast in a rush of gratitude. Not one of my family had come to harm in all that time, and our four boys had grown straight and strong, tempered by hard work, long walks, and the steady affection of our little household. My greatest hope, that any young person reading our story would see how a peaceful, loving, industrious family shapes good, brave men, felt closer than ever to coming true.
I didn’t see any point in boring our readers with ten years’ worth of trivial hunting trips, clunky homemade tool experiments, or encyclopedia-style lists of every plant and animal we’d discovered. But before we moved on to the biggest, most life-altering stretch of our story, it was worth pausing to lay out exactly how far we’d come.
Rockburg, our cliffside winter home, was almost unrecognizable from the sweltering, bare hollow my wife Elizabeth had once hated so much. Fountains splashed in its central courtyard, trellised verandahs draped in flowering creepers wrapped around its balconies, and native and European saplings we’d planted had grown into shady groves heavy with jasmine and orange blossom. Out in Safety Bay, Shark Island stood guard, its slopes thick with graceful palms, a small battery and flagstaff visible atop its highest rock.
The swamp we’d spent months clearing and draining had turned into a wide, glassy lake, with just enough reedy marsh at its far end to make perfect cover for waterfowl. Black swans sailed its surface alongside white geese and iridescent wild ducks; flamingos waded in the shallows alongside soft blue-grey cranes and crested herons, none of them afraid of us at all. Jack’s two giant pet frogs, Grace and Beauty, had grown as big as small rabbits, and would waddle all the way from the marsh at his call to gobble up a juicy grasshopper. Old Hurry, our stately pet ostrich, paced the paths under the shade trees like he was king of the whole island, and every kind of bright, soft-cooing pigeon nested in our cliffs and dovecots.
Rockburg was only accessible by one narrow bridge: the stretch of river above the waterfall that had once been fordable was now completely blocked by a dense, impenetrable thicket of thorn trees, lemon bushes, and prickly pears we’d planted ourselves. Shark Island’s rabbit warren supplied us with all the meat and soft fur we needed, and when we found antelopes didn’t thrive on nearby Whale Isle, we moved them there too, turning Whale Isle over to all our messy, smelly work: candle making, tanning hides, cleaning wool. The Woodlands farm flourished, our flocks and herds gave us all the mutton, beef, and milk we could use, and Elizabeth’s dairy grew so busy she could barely keep up.
The boys still loved naming every animal they owned: they had a creamy white cow named Blanche, a bull with a roar so loud you could hear it half a mile away named Stentor, two fleet young wild donagers called Arrow and Dart, and Jack kept a descendent of his old favorite jackal Fangs, which he named Coco, and babbled about how every good name needed an “o” in it until our ears ached.
We’d all stayed in excellent health those ten years, only suffering from occasional mild bouts of fever for Elizabeth, and tiny scrapes and tumbles for the boys, who were all now fine, strapping young men:
Fritz, 24, was average height, broad-shouldered, absurdly strong, and full of high spirit. Ernest, two years younger, was tall and slight, gentle, quiet, and studious, the old lazy streak he’d had as a boy almost entirely gone, with a sharp, curious intellect and refined taste for all things beautiful. Jack, 20, looked almost as tall as Fritz but was lighter built, famous for being impossibly graceful and quick rather than bulkily strong. Franz, the youngest at 17, had a little bit of every one of his brothers in him – sharp, witty, and kind, if not quite as silly and prankish as Jack. All four were honorable, God-fearing boys, unfailingly dutiful to Elizabeth and me, and fiercely loyal to each other.
Even after ten full years of total seclusion, I still clung to the quiet hope that one day we’d be reunited with other human beings. But time that made our boys men was also creeping up on Elizabeth and me, and I sometimes lay awake at night with a cold, heavy dread of what would happen to them if we died and left them all alone. I never told the family about those dark spells, though. I would just pray it out, and the strength and hope would always come back.
The older boys often took off on all-day expeditions without a heads up, and my unease always melted away the second they came tramping back, grinning and loaded down with treasure. One day, Fritz didn’t come home at all. By sunset, we realized his cajack – his small, fast sailing skiff – was gone from the harbor, and he’d headed out to open sea.
Worried he’d get lost after dark, I took Ernest and Jack out to Shark Island to climb the watchtower, hoist our signal flag, and load the old cannon we kept there to warn him we were watching. We stared out across the glittering, sunlit water for ages before we spotted a tiny black dot on the horizon, which our telescope confirmed was Fritz’s skiff. He was moving far slower than usual, and I could see he was towing a huge bulging sack behind the cajack, on top of all the gear piled on his deck. We fired the cannon, then hurried back to the harbor to meet him.
“Welcome home, Fritz!” I called as he pulled up to the dock. “Looks like you’ve got a whole cargo there!”
“More than cargo – I made discoveries that’ll make all of us want to head straight back out there,” he laughed. “C’mon boys, haul the stuff up, I’ll tell you the whole story while I rest.”
We all clustered around the campfire while he apologized for leaving without permission, and launched into his tale. He’d been plotting a full coastal exploration voyage for ages, ever since he’d killed the walrus years before, mapping stretches of shore we’d never seen. He’d pre-packed the cajack with provisions, a compass, his rifle, harpoon, axe, boat hook, net, and brought his pet eagle Pounce along for the trip. The morning had dawned calm and bright, and he’d slipped out of the harbor before anyone woke, letting the current carry him out past the point where our old wreck had gone down. He could even see the rusted cannons and iron debris scattered across the flat, clear seabed under the water.
He sailed past cliffs crowded with thousands of seabirds, and low rock shelves where walruses and seals lazed in the sun, lumbering over the rocks on their tusks. He hurried past that stretch, nervous of the huge tusked beasts, and an hour and a half later he rounded a tall, steep headland jutting far out to sea – and spotted a massive natural arch carved straight into the rock face, like the grand entrance to a giant stone cathedral.
He sailed right under it, and found the huge cavern inside was full of tiny swallows barely bigger than wrens, their nests plastered all over the walls. The nests weren’t made of twigs – they looked fibrous, gelatinous, almost like sponges or fungi, and Fritz had stuffed a whole net full of them.
I laughed when I saw them. “Those are edible bird nests! The Chinese pay their weight in silver for the fresh white ones, they make a strengthening soup out of them. Java has whole industries built around harvesting these things from caves just like this, people risk their lives climbing for them. Why don’t you try boiling a few, Elizabeth, just for curiosity?”
“Ugh, they look disgusting,” she said, grinning. “But I’ll try, if you all really want to.”
“Please please please!” Jack cackled. “I want to taste bird soup even if it feels like I’m swallowing a mouthful of feathers!”
Fritz kept going with his story. He’d sailed all the way through the long, tunnel-like cavern, and emerged into a completely hidden, almost entirely landlocked bay so calm it looked like a lake. The shores beyond the rocks stretched out into fertile, grassy plains, leading to a river mouth and a dense cedar forest. He’d looked down through the crystal clear water and spotted huge clusters of giant oysters stuck to the rocks by hairy little threads. He hooked a few up with his boat hook, left them on the sand while he explored a little, and when he came back the hot sun had made all the shells gape open. He cut one open with his knife – and found three tiny, round, pearly balls rolling around inside.
The boys whooped when he pulled the little box of gleaming pearls out for them to see. “Treasure!” I exclaimed. “Right now they’re just pretty trinkets, but if we ever meet another civilized ship again, that oyster bed will make us all rich. We have to go explore that Pearl Bay as soon as possible.”
Fritz told us he’d sailed all the way across the new bay, found it fully sheltered from the open sea by a line of reefs with only one narrow channel out. When the tide came in strong, trapping him on the wrong side of the channel, he’d climbed the headland cliffs – and hundreds of seabirds, from gulls to giant albatrosses, had mobbed him, screaming and diving at his head. He’d swung his boat hook at them in frustration, and knocked one huge albatross clean unconscious into the water. He’d hauled it onto his cajack, nursed it back to life, and then sailed home through the channel once the tide calmed.
That night, after the rest of the boys had run off to start pripping open more oysters, Fritz pulled me aside, his face tight with excitement. “I didn’t tell everyone the whole truth about that albatross, father. Tied around its leg was a scrap of old rag, with English writing scrawled on it. It said: Save an unfortunate Englishwoman from the smoking rock. I was so shocked I could barely breathe. I tore a strip off my handkerchief, wrote Do not despair! Help is near! tied it to the bird’s other leg, and let it fly west once it woke up. We have to find her. We have to rescue her.”
I was stunned. I told him it was wisest not to tell the rest of the family yet – the note could have been written years ago, on some distant shore, and the poor woman could be long dead. The “smoking rock” he referenced had to be an active volcano, and there were none on our island. But Fritz was sure the rag wasn’t that old, and that the woman was still alive somewhere nearby. After a long quiet talk, we agreed we’d use the planned trip to Pearl Bay as cover. We’d modify Fritz’s cajack to fit two people, and load it with enough provisions for a long, multi-day search voyage to look for her.
Back at the house, the boys were half-crazy with excitement prying oysters open, yelling every time they found a new pearl. They begged to start a full pearl fishery right away, building a hut on the bay shore to harvest the oysters full time. I announced to the whole family that Fritz was now old enough to take full control of his own voyages, that none of the rest of us would panic if he stayed away for a few days, and that we trusted his judgement completely. His mother hugged him tight, teary, and blessed him.
It took us a little while to build a set of special scraping tools I’d designed to pry the oysters off the bay rocks, which gave Fritz time to refit the cajack with a second extra seat for a passenger. His brothers assumed he’d take one of them as his first mate, and he let them think that.
Finally, the day of our big excursion came. We loaded up our main yacht, took the dogs and Jack’s jackal Coco along, and set out. Jack rode in the new second seat in Fritz’s cajack, proudly leading the way as our pilot. We sailed past the walrus rookery, through a stretch of calm mirror-smooth water where tiny, fairy-like paper nautilus shells bobbed on the waves. The boys chased them down and caught half a dozen for our natural history collection, and we named that spot Nautilus Creek. Further on we rounded a flat little point with a sharp rock at the tip, named Cape Pug-Nose, and soon spotted the huge stone headland Fritz had told us about. We sailed right through that grand archway, which was so tall and intricately shaped with stone pillars and spires it looked exactly like the entrance to a gothic cathedral, so we named the whole place Cape Minster.
A whole cloud of the tiny edible swallows burst out of the arch as we sailed in, their nests strung all along the walls of the long, light-dappled stone tunnel like decorations. The tide carried us smoothly all the way through, past stone pillars that looked like cathedral columns and shadowed side caves that looked like side chapels, until we burst out into the bright sun, right in the middle of Pearl Bay. It was even more beautiful than Fritz had described, dotted with tiny green islets, its shores lined with grassy meadows and murmuring brooks and shady groves.
We found a perfect landing spot next to a sparkling little stream, built a big watch fire as the sun went down, ate a quick supper, left the dogs and Coco on shore to keep watch, and anchored the yacht a short way out from the bank for the night. The boys were chattering nonstop about diving for oysters and hunting for pearls at dawn, and no one but Fritz and I knew that somewhere out west, past the bay, past the cliffs, a stranger was waiting, hopefully, for the help we’d promised.
