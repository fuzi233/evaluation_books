At this point, I must pause briefly to gather my thoughts and set the record straight.

By reviewing my own memories—and with the help of Penelope, who has carefully kept a journal—I can now move swiftly through the time between Mr. Franklin Blake’s arrival and Miss Rachel’s birthday. For most of that period, nothing of real consequence occurred. Days passed quietly, without incident or alarm. With your permission, and aided by Penelope’s notes, I shall focus only on key dates for now, saving a full account of each day for later—once we reach the moment when the Moonstone becomes the central concern of every person in the house.

With that said, let us proceed—beginning, naturally, with the bottle of sweet-smelling ink I discovered on the gravel path one night.

The following morning—the twenty-sixth—I showed this curious object to Mr. Franklin and recounted what had happened. His reaction was immediate and clear: not only had the Indians been lurking near the house in search of the Diamond, but they actually believed in their own superstitions. He meant the ritual of marking a boy’s head, pouring ink into his hand, and expecting him to see things invisible to ordinary eyes. Such practices, he explained, are not uncommon—even here in England—though usually without the ink. These so-called “clairvoyants” call it *lucidité*, a French term meaning something like “brightness of sight.” “There’s no doubt about it,” he declared, “the Indians assumed we’d keep the Diamond here. They brought their boy to reveal its location—if they managed to get inside last night.”

“Do you think they’ll try again, sir?” I asked.

“It depends,” he replied, “on whether the boy truly sees anything. If he can spot the Diamond even through the iron safe at Frizinghall Bank, then we may be free from further visits. But if he can’t, then we’ll have another chance to catch them before long.”

I waited with some confidence for that second attempt. Yet, strangely enough, it never came.

Whether the Indians heard rumors in town about Mr. Franklin visiting the bank and concluded he was onto them; or whether the boy really did see the Diamond where it was hidden (a notion I personally reject); or whether it was simply luck—none of these matters is certain. What is certain is this: not a single Indian approached the house again during the weeks leading up to Miss Rachel’s birthday. The performers remained in town, entertaining crowds as usual. Meanwhile, Mr. Franklin and I stayed vigilant, determined not to give away our suspicions too soon. That, then, concludes what I have to say about the Indians—at least for now.

On the twenty-ninth, Miss Rachel and Mr. Franklin found a new way to pass the time—a pastime that, though seemingly harmless, would prove significant later.

Many well-born people face a peculiar challenge in life: the burden of idleness. Their days are often spent searching for something to occupy themselves, and when their tastes lean toward intellectual pursuits, they frequently drift into strange, unseemly habits. Too often, they end up harming living creatures or destroying beautiful things—all under the pretense of improving their minds. I’ve seen ladies and gentlemen alike collect frogs, spiders, beetles, and newts, only to pin them down or cut them open without a second thought. One finds a young master peering at a spider’s innards through a magnifying glass, or a frog walking down the stairs headless. When questioned, the explanation is always the same: “It’s natural history.” Others spend hours dismantling flowers with sharp tools, driven by a morbid curiosity. Does knowing the structure of a bloom make it more fragrant or more lovely? Not at all. But they must fill the hours somehow. Childhood games of mud pies become adult hobbies of dissecting insects and ruining plants. The root cause is always the same: empty heads and idle hands. So they paint canvases, create foul odors, raise tadpoles in murky water, chip stones, stain fingers with chemicals, and ruin photographs—each act born of boredom and a lack of purpose.

But Mr. Franklin and Miss Rachel were spared such excesses. They didn’t harm anything. They merely made a mess—and the only thing they ruined was the paneling of a door.

Mr. Franklin, ever eager to experiment, took up what he called “decorative painting.” He claimed to have invented a new liquid medium—what he termed a “vehicle”—to mix with paint. I don’t know what it was made of, but I do know one thing: it smelled terrible. Miss Rachel, eager to try her hand at the technique, prompted Mr. Franklin to order supplies from London. He mixed the concoction, filling the room with an odor so strong it made the dogs sneeze. Then, after dressing her in apron and bib, he set her to work decorating her small sitting-room—her “boudoir,” as she called it. They began with the inside of the door. Mr. Franklin scraped off the varnish with pumice stone, preparing a surface for painting. Miss Rachel then applied intricate designs—griffins, birds, flowers, cupids—copied from sketches by a famous Italian artist whose name escapes me. The man who painted countless Madonnas and had a sweetheart at the baker’s shop, I believe. The work was slow and messy, yet the two of them never tired. When not riding, socializing, eating, or singing, they were bent over the door, heads close together, working like bees. Who wrote that “Satan finds mischief still for idle hands to do”? If that poet had watched Miss Rachel with her brush and Mr. Franklin with his stinking vehicle, he could not have written a truer line.

Next, a date worth noting: Sunday, June 4th.

That evening, in the servants’ hall, we discussed a matter of household interest—one that, like the door’s decoration, would later prove relevant.

Given the evident affection between Mr. Franklin and Miss Rachel, and their striking compatibility in appearance and temperament, we couldn’t help but wonder about their future. Some speculated that a wedding would take place before summer ended. Others—myself among them—thought Miss Rachel might marry, but doubted whether her husband would be Mr. Franklin Blake. Why? Reasons will become clear soon enough.

That Mr. Franklin was in love was obvious to anyone who observed him. The question was whether Miss Rachel returned his feelings. Allow me to introduce her properly—after which, you may judge for yourself.

Miss Rachel’s eighteenth birthday was approaching—June 21st. If you favor dark-haired women (though they seem out of fashion lately), and if size doesn’t matter to you, then I assure you, Miss Rachel was one of the most beautiful girls you could ever hope to meet. She was petite and slender, yet perfectly proportioned. Watching her sit, rise, or walk was enough to convince any sensible man that her grace came from within—not from her clothes. Her hair was blacker than any I’ve ever seen. Her eyes matched it. Her nose was slightly small, but her mouth and chin—according to Mr. Franklin—were “morsels for the gods.” Her complexion, he added, was warm as sunlight, with the advantage of always being fresh and radiant. Add to that her upright carriage, clear voice with a rich tone, and a smile that started in her eyes before reaching her lips—and you have a portrait of her, as vivid as life itself.

What of her character? Was this charming girl without flaws? No more than any of us.

To speak plainly, Miss Rachel possessed many gifts—but one flaw stood out. Unlike most girls her age, she had firm opinions and refused to bow to fashion if it clashed with her beliefs. In minor matters, this independence was admirable. But in serious affairs, it went too far. She made decisions alone, rarely sought advice, never shared her plans, and kept secrets from everyone—from her mother down. Whether dealing with friends or foes (and she felt both deeply), she followed her own path. Over and over, I’ve heard my lady say, “Rachel’s best friend and worst enemy are both—Rachel herself.”

Yet despite her secrecy and stubbornness, there was no deceit in her. I never saw her break a promise or say “no” while meaning “yes.” As a child, she once took blame for a friend’s misdeed—suffering punishment without confessing. She never lied, but neither would she tell the truth when pressed. She’d look you straight in the eye, shake her head firmly, and declare, “I won’t tell you!” Even when punished with bread and water, she held fast. Stubborn? Yes—sometimes fiercely so. But she was, without question, the finest young woman I’ve ever known. You may think this contradictory? Then consider this: observe your wife closely for twenty-four hours. If she shows no contradictions, Heaven help you—you’ve married a monster.

Now that you know Miss Rachel, we come to the next question: what were her views on marriage?

On June 12th, my mistress sent an invitation to a gentleman in London—Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite—to join us for Miss Rachel’s birthday. This was the man I suspected had captured her heart!

Like Mr. Franklin, he was a cousin. His father, Mr. Ablewhite, was a banker in Frizinghall and had married my lady’s younger sister—against family wishes. Though he was wealthy and respectable, he had risen from humble beginnings, which caused outrage at the time. But time and changing attitudes softened the scandal. Today, we’re all more tolerant—provided we scratch each other’s backs, who cares if you’re a dustman or a duke? The Ablewhites lived just outside Frizinghall, in a fine house and estate. Respectable people, though we’ll hear little of them except for Mr. Godfrey, the second son—whose presence must be acknowledged for Miss Rachel’s sake.

Mr. Franklin, bright and clever as he was, stood little chance against Mr. Godfrey in Miss Rachel’s estimation.

First, Mr. Godfrey was physically imposing—over six feet tall, with a striking red-and-white complexion, a smooth-shaven face, and long, golden hair falling loosely over his neck. But why describe him? If you’ve ever supported a Ladies’ Charity in London, you’ve seen him. A barrister by trade, a charmer by nature, and a philanthropist by choice. He served on every committee—maternal societies, Magdalen homes, feminist groups—always vice-president, always guiding the ladies with patience and charm. Wherever there was a table with women debating, there he sat at the bottom, calming tempers and steering them forward. He was a master speaker—capable of moving audiences to tears and donations alike. I once went to Exeter Hall to hear him speak. The crowd roared with applause. And beneath it all, he was gentle, simple, and easygoing. Everyone loved him—and he loved everyone. How could Mr. Franklin—or any ordinary man—compete with such a figure?

On the fourteenth, Mr. Godfrey responded.

He accepted the invitation, arriving Wednesday and leaving Friday evening—his duties to the charities calling him back to London. He also included a poem celebrating Miss Rachel’s “natal day.” At dinner, Miss Rachel and Mr. Franklin laughed at the verses. Penelope, loyal to Mr. Franklin, triumphantly asked me what I thought. “Miss Rachel has misled you,” I replied. “But my instincts aren’t so easily fooled. Wait until Mr. Ablewhite arrives in person.”

Penelope retorted that Mr. Franklin might act first—before the poet arrived. And indeed, Mr. Franklin left no stone unturned to win Miss Rachel’s favor.

Though a devoted smoker, he gave up cigars when she complained about the smell. He suffered terribly—sleepless nights, haggard looks—yet refused to relapse. Miss Rachel begged him to resume, but he insisted he’d endure. Such devotion, some said, should surely win her heart—especially when combined with their daily painting project. But then there was the photograph in her bedroom: Mr. Godfrey speaking at a rally, hair flying, eyes glowing, commanding attention and money. Every morning, as Penelope confessed, he watched her comb her hair—silent, but present. Soon, he’d be there in person—that was my belief.

Then, on June 16th, a new development cast doubt on Mr. Franklin’s chances.

A foreign-looking gentleman arrived, speaking English with an accent, and requested to see Mr. Franklin on business. It couldn’t have been about the Diamond—for two reasons: Mr. Franklin told me nothing, and he reported the matter to my lady afterward. My lady likely mentioned it to her daughter. That evening, Miss Rachel spoke sharply to Mr. Franklin at the piano, criticizing the people he’d lived among and the principles he’d adopted abroad. The next day, the door-painting stopped. I suspect he’d done something reckless overseas—perhaps involving a woman or debt—but I can’t be sure. In this case, both Mr. Franklin and my lady kept me in the dark.

On the seventeenth, the tension eased. They resumed painting, and seemed reconciled. According to Penelope, Mr. Franklin had made a proposal—neither accepted nor refused. She believed Miss Rachel had rejected him by pretending he wasn’t serious, then regretted it afterward. But I knew Miss Rachel too well to trust such gossip. Penelope’s report was likely wishful thinking, not fact.

Then, on the nineteenth, another event unfolded.

We summoned the doctor to attend Rosanna Spearman, our second housemaid.

This poor girl—already a mystery at the Shivering Sand—continued to puzzle me. Penelope’s idea that she was in love with Mr. Franklin seemed absurd. Yet her behavior grew increasingly strange.

She constantly placed herself near Mr. Franklin—quietly, subtly, but unmistakably. He paid her no more attention than he did the cat. Her appetite dwindled, and her eyes bore the marks of sleepless nights. One day, Penelope caught her at Mr. Franklin’s dressing-table, removing a rose Miss Rachel had given him and replacing it with one of her own. After that, she became insolent when I gave her general warnings—and worse, she grew disrespectful when Miss Rachel spoke to her.

My lady noticed the change and asked for my opinion. I tried to protect the girl by saying she was unwell. That led to the doctor’s visit. He diagnosed nerves and doubted she could continue in service. My lady offered to send her to a farm for fresh air. Rosanna pleaded desperately to stay—and, in a moment of weakness, I advised my lady to keep her for a short while longer. As events proved, this was the worst decision I ever made. If I had foreseen what would happen, I would have removed her myself—then and there.
