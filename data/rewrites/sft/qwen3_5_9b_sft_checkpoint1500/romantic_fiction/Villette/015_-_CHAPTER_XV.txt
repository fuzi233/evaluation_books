In the wake of Madame Beck's fête, with its three weeks of uneasy respite, its fleeting twelve-hour explosion of gaiety and indulgence, and the single, droning day of exhaustion that followed, a profound reaction set in. Two months of rigorous application, of intense and unyielding study, descended upon the school. These final months of the "school year" were the only authentic working season of the calendar. Into them, driven by professors, mistresses, and pupils alike, the entire burden of preparation for the upcoming examinations—and consequently the distribution of prizes—was funneled. Those seeking accolades were compelled to labor with genuine earnestness; masters and teachers had to brace themselves, pushing the lagging students forward and cultivating the promising ones. A grand display, a compelling exhibition, was demanded for public view, and every available means was deemed legitimate for this purpose.

I paid scant attention to how the other teachers embarked upon their tasks; I had my own business to attend to. My undertaking was not the least onerous: it was to imbue approximately ninety sets of minds with what they considered a highly complex and difficult science—the English language—and to drill ninety tongues in an almost impossible pronunciation, specifically the lisping and hissing dentals of the Islands.

The day of examination finally arrived. An awful day! It had been prepared for with anxious deliberation and dressed for with silent dispatch; there was nothing vaporous or fluttering now, no white gauze or azure streamers. The order of attire was grave, close, and compact. To me, it seemed I was especially doomed this day—the main burden and trial falling solely upon me among all the female teachers. The others were not expected to examine in the subjects they taught; the Professor of Literature, M. Paul, undertook this duty upon himself. He, the school autocrat, gathered all and sundry reins into the hollow of his single hand; he fiercely rejected any colleague; he would not have assistance. Madame herself, who evidently wished to conduct the examination in geography—her favorite subject, which she taught well—was forced to submit and remain subordinate to her despotic kinsman's direction. He set aside the entire staff of instructors, male and female, and stood alone upon the examiner's estrade. It irked him that he was compelled to make one exception to this rule. He could not manage English; he was obliged to leave that branch of education in the hands of the English teacher, which he did, not without a flash of naive jealousy.

A constant crusade against the "amour-propre" of every human being save himself was the peculiar obsession of this able, yet fiery and grasping little man. He possessed a strong relish for public representation in his own person, coupled with an extreme abhorrence of any similar display in anyone else. He quelled, suppressed, and stifled whenever possible; and when he could not, he fumed like a bottled storm.

On the eve of the examination-day, I was walking in the garden, as were the other teachers and all the boarders. M. Emanuel joined me in the "defended alley;" his cigar hovered at his lips; his paletôt—a garment of most characteristic, shapeless menace—hung dark around him; the tassel of his Greek bonnet sternly shadowed his left temple; his black whiskers curled like those of a wrathful cat; his blue eye held a cloud within its glitter.

"Ainsi," he began abruptly, confronting and arresting me, "you are going to reign like a queen; tomorrow—to reign beside me? No doubt you will savor beforehand the delights of authority. I perceive in you some radiant, petty ambition!"

And the truth was, he happened to be entirely mistaken. I did not—and could not—estimate the admiration or the high opinion of tomorrow's audience at the same rate he did. Had that audience numbered as many personal friends and acquaintances for me as for him, I know not how matters might stand; I speak of the case as it existed. Upon me, school triumphs cast but a cold lustre. I had wondered—and I wondered still—how it was that for him they seemed to shine with the warmth of a hearth-fire. *He* cared for them perhaps too much; *I*, probably, too little. Nevertheless, I had my own fancies as well as he. For instance, I liked to see M. Emanuel jealous; it illuminated his nature and woke his spirit. It threw all sorts of queer lights and shadows over his dun face and into his violet-azure eyes (he used to declare that his black hair and blue eyes were "une de ses beautés"). There was a relish in his anger; it was artless, earnest, quite unreasonable, yet never hypocritical. I offered no disclaimer regarding the complacency he attributed to me; I merely asked where the English examination fitted in—whether at the commencement or conclusion of the day?

"I hesitate," he said, "between placing it at the very beginning, before many persons have arrived, and when your aspiring nature will not be gratified by a large audience, or quite at the close, when everybody is tired, and only a jaded, worn-out attention will be at your service."

"How hard you are, Monsieur!" I said, affecting dejection.

"One ought to be 'hard' with you. You are one of those beings who must be *kept down*. I know you! I know you! Other people in this house see you pass, and think that a colorless shadow has gone by. As for me, I scrutinized your face once, and it sufficed."

"Are you satisfied that you understand me?"

Without answering directly, he continued, "Were you not gratified when you succeeded in that vaudeville? I watched you and saw a passionate ardour for triumph in your physiognomy. What fire shot into the glance! Not mere light, but flame: I consider myself warned."

"What feeling I had on that occasion, Monsieur—and pardon me if I say—you immensely exaggerate both its quality and quantity—was quite abstract. I did not care for the vaudeville. I hated the part you assigned me. I had not the slightest sympathy with the audience below the stage. They are good people, doubtless, but do I know them? Are they anything to me? Can I care for being brought before their view again tomorrow? Will the examination be anything but a task to me—a task I wish well over?"

"Shall I take it out of your hands?"

"With all my heart; if you do not fear failure."

"But I should fail. I only know three phrases of English, and a few words: *par exemple*, de sonn, de mone, de stare—est-ce bien dit? My opinion is that it would be better to give up the thing altogether: to have no English examination, eh?"

"If Madame consents, I consent."

"Heartily?"

"Very heartily."

He smoked his cigar in silence. He turned suddenly.

"Give me your hand," he said, and the spite and jealousy melted from his face, replaced by a generous kindliness.

"Come, we will not be rivals, we will be friends," he pursued. "The examination shall take place, and I will choose a good moment; and instead of vexing and hindering, as I felt half-inclined ten minutes ago—for I have my malevolent moods: I always had from childhood—I will aid you sincerely. After all, you are solitary and a stranger, and have your way to make and your bread to earn; it may be well that you should become known. We will be friends: do you agree?"

"Out of my heart, Monsieur. I am glad of a friend. I like that better than a triumph."

"Pauvrette!" said he, and turned away, leaving the alley.

The examination passed over well; M. Paul was as good as his word, and did his best to make my part easy. The following day came the distribution of prizes; that, too, passed successfully. The school broke up; the pupils went home, and now began the long vacation.

That vacation! Shall I ever forget it? I think not. Madame Beck went, on the first day of the holidays, to join her children at the seaside; all the three teachers had parents or friends with whom they took refuge; every professor quit the city; some went to Paris, some to Boue-Marine; M. Paul set forth on a pilgrimage to Rome. The house was left quite empty, save for me, a servant, and a poor, deformed, and imbecile pupil, a sort of cretin, whom her stepmother in a distant province would not allow to return home.

My heart almost died within me; miserable longings strained its chords. How long were the September days! How silent, how lifeless! How vast and void seemed the desolate premises! How gloomy the forsaken garden—grey now with the dust of a town summer departed. Looking forward at the commencement of those eight weeks, I hardly knew how I was to live until the end. My spirits had long been gradually sinking; now that the prop of employment was withdrawn, they went down fast. Even to look forward was not to hope: the dumb future spoke no comfort, offered no promise, gave no inducement to bear present evil in reliance on future good. A sorrowful indifference to existence often pressed upon me—a despairing resignation to reach betimes the end of all earthly things. Alas!
