Gerard walked beside Teresa in silence, his thoughts turning inward as artists often do—wondering what she might intend for him, rather than asking outright. And so, at last, she spoke of her own accord.

“A friend told me,” she said, “that a goldsmith’s wife is seeking a writer. Her shop is nearby—you’ll not have far to go.”

Soon they reached the woman’s door. Teresa stepped forward with quiet dignity.

“Madama,” she said, “Leonora informs me you need a scribe. I have brought you one—handsome and true, who saved my child from the sea. Pray look upon him kindly.”

The goldsmith’s wife, Olympia, obeyed in part. She fixed her gaze on Gerard’s face, drawn by its noble features, and could not easily tear her eyes away. Yet her reply was dismissive.

“No,” she said, “I have no use for a writer. Ah—now I recall! It is my neighbor Claelia, the sausage-maker, who seeks one. She told me, and I passed it on to Leonora.”

Teresa bowed slightly and withdrew without haste or complaint.

Claelia lived farther off. When they arrived, she was out. Teresa sat down calmly on the step, composed and still as a statue, her presence commanding the air around her. Gerard, unable to resist, began sketching her in secret when suddenly Claelia returned.

“Madama,” Teresa said, stepping forward, “I’ve heard from Olympia that you require a writer.” She took Gerard by the hand and led him forward. “Here is one—beautiful and brave. He rescued my child from the cruel waves. For the sake of our Lady, look upon him with favor.”

Claelia blinked. “My good lady, my fair sir,” she replied, “I have no need of a writer. But now you remind me—it was my friend Appia Claudia who asked me for one just the other day. She’s a tailor, lives in the Via Lepida.”

Teresa nodded once and turned away.

“Madama,” Gerard said gently, “this may prove wearying for you.”

She raised her eyes. “What has ever been accomplished without patience?”

She added softly, “We shall knock at every door in Rome—but you shall have justice.”

“But madama,” he insisted, “I believe we are being followed. A man trails us—sometimes near, sometimes distant.”

“I have noticed,” she said coolly, though her cheeks flushed faintly. “It is my poor Lodovico.”

She paused, then turned and beckoned with her finger. A figure approached reluctantly. When he came close, she looked him full in the face. He shifted uncomfortably.

“Lodovico mio,” she said, “know this young gentleman—of whom I have spoken to you so often. Know him and love him, for it was he who saved your wife and child.”

At those words, Lodovico’s forced smile melted into genuine emotion. He stepped forward and embraced Gerard warmly.

Yet something about his earlier stealth, his silent pursuit, made Gerard uneasy beneath the embrace.

“Shall we have your company, Ser Lodovico?” he asked.

“No, signor,” Lodovico replied. “I go not that side of the Tiber.”

“Addio, then,” Teresa said quietly.

“When shall you return home, Teresa mia?” he called after her.

“When I have finished my errand,” she answered.

They walked on in silence. Teresa now wore a solemn expression, almost sorrowful.

Appia Claudia, the tailor, proved merciful. She did not send them across the Tiber, but only a hundred yards down the street—to Lucretia, who ran the glove shop. It was she who wanted a writer, though even Appia could not say why.

Lucretia was a lively little woman, warm and welcoming. She greeted them cheerfully and confessed she needed no writer—she kept all her accounts in her head.

“It was for my confessor, Father Colonna,” she explained. “He is mad for writers.”

“I’ve heard of him,” said Teresa.

“Who hasn’t?” Lucretia laughed.

“But good dame,” Gerard interjected, “he is a friar—a man of vows. I cannot let him write without payment. He saved my child at sea.”

“Did he now?” Lucretia said, casting a kind glance at Gerard. “Well, rest easy. A Colonna never wants for money. The good father has only to speak, and princes of his blood will pour thousands into his hands. And such a confessor! The best in Rome. His mind wanders leagues away while you confess—he pays no heed. Once I tested him: I confessed I had killed my lodger’s little girl and baked her in a pie. When I finished, he woke from his dream and said, ‘My erring sister, recite three Paternosters and three Ave Marias kneeling, abstain from butter and eggs next Wednesday, and pax vobiscum!’ Then off he went, hands behind his back, as if I were nothing.”

Teresa waited patiently, then drew the conversation back. “Would you be so kind as to accompany this youth to the friar and speak for him?”

“Oh, I can’t leave my shop!” Lucretia protested. “And why should I? His door is always open to writers, painters, scholars—anyone who knows their craft. One day he refused the Duke d’Urbino because a learned Greek was with him. Their heads were so close over a parchment from Greece, you could have placed one cowl over both. Onesta told me—she comes here often to chat.”

“This is the man for you,” Teresa said firmly.

“All you need do,” Lucretia continued, “is go to his lodgings—my boy will show you—and tell Onesta you come from me. Say you are a writer, and she will take you to him. If you give the girl a piece of silver, it won’t hurt.”

“I have silver,” Teresa said warmly.

“But remember,” Lucretia warned, “if the young man claims he can do something, he must truly be able to do it—or avoid the friar like poison. He hates bunglers. Only the other day, someone brought him a poorly carved crucifix. He roared, ‘Is this how you present “Salvator Mundi,” who died in agony for you? You begrudge Him careful work? This slovenly trifle—a crucifix? Were it not a crucifix and I a holy man, I’d dust your jacket with it!’ Onesta heard every word through the keyhole. So mind!”

“Have no fear,” Teresa said proudly. “I stand by his ability. He saved my child.”

Gerard, less perceptive, did not grasp the weight of her confidence. In fact, he begged for delay.

“I would rather not see the friar today,” he said. “Would tomorrow not serve as well?”

“Here is a coward for you,” Lucretia chuckled.

“No, he is not a coward,” Teresa snapped, defending him. “He is modest.”

“I am afraid,” Gerard admitted, “of this high-born, fastidious friar. He has seen the finest writing in Italy. If you would allow me to prepare better work, I will face him tomorrow with it.”

“I consent,” Teresa said.

They walked home together. Near Gerard’s lodging stood a shop selling vellum. In the window lay a perfect white skin—pure and radiant. Gerard stared longingly, but knew he could not afford it. He hurried on.

But soon he decided where to get vellum. Parting from Teresa at his door, he rushed upstairs and took out the bond he had carried from Sevenbergen. With a sigh, he laid it on the table.

Then, with chemicals in hand, he prepared to erase the old writing. But this was his final chance to read it. Overcoming his loathing for bad handwriting, he began to decipher the deed despite its clumsy contractions.

It revealed that Ghysbrecht Van Swieten was to lend Floris Brandt money secured by land, repaying himself from the rent.

Gerard felt it would be wrong to destroy such a document. Instead, he vowed to transcribe every word at his leisure.

Descending, he resolved to buy a small piece of vellum with his half of the card-money.

At the foot of the stairs, he found the landlady and Teresa talking. Seeing him, the landlady exclaimed, “Here he is! You’re caught, donna mia. See what she bought you?”

She pulled from under her apron—the very vellum Gerard had longed for.

“Why, dame! Why, Donna Teresa!” Gerard stammered, speechless with joy and astonishment.

“Dear boy,” Teresa said, “there is no skin in all Rome like it. Did not your eyes linger on it with longing? Did you not sigh as you turned away? Could I let you want it after that?”

“What wisdom! What kindness, madama! Oh, I never thought I’d possess this. How much did you pay?”

“I forget,” she smiled. “Addio, Fiammina. Addio, Ser Gerard. Be happy, be prosperous—as you are good.”

And with that, the Roman matron glided away, leaving Gerard stunned, wondering how to repay such generosity.

The next afternoon, Gerard visited Lucretia. Her boy guided him to Fra Colonna’s lodgings. He announced himself, gave Onesta a piece of silver, and was led up.

Gerard entered with a trembling heart.

The room was vast, filled with art, antiquities, and learning—manuscripts, carvings, musical instruments—all scattered in glorious chaos. At its center sat the friar, deeply absorbed in an Arabian text.

He glanced up, annoyed by the interruption.

Onesta whispered in his ear.

“Very well,” the friar said. “Let him sit. Wait—young man, show me how you write.”

He tossed Gerard a sheet of paper and pointed to an inkhorn.

“Reverend father,” Gerard said, “my hand trembles too much now. But last night I wrote a page of Greek, with the Latin translation beside it—showing the different scripts.”

“Show it to me.”

Gerard brought it forward, heart pounding. He stood, breathless, awaiting judgment.

When it came, it struck him like thunder.

For the friar fell upon him, embracing him with a Dominican’s fervor.

Meanwhile, in Holland, an event unfolded whose consequences none could foresee—not even the reader.

A magnificent procession marched to Eli’s door—brave to the eye, noble in appearance, yet pitiable upon closer inspection. It seemed one creature—a centaur—but analysis revealed two.

The human half was dressed in finery, bedizened with gold and silver, metals that men have sold their souls to possess. But the horse was worse adorned—its ears hidden in linen sheaths tipped with silver and blue; its body swaddled in stiff, gorgeous cloths descending to the ground, save for a narrow space to mince forward; its tail concealed heaven knows how. Only its eyes gleamed through holes in the fabric, and its hooves peeped in and out like rats.

Yet this grotesque creature represented absolute power—sent from the Duke’s court, returning from a tournament. Its mission: to claim a dwarf.

“What ho!” the rider cried, as Eli emerged with his wife behind him. “Peace be with you, good people. Rejoice! I come for your dwarf.”

Eli stared, speechless.

But Catherine shrieked, “You’ve mistaken your way, good man! We have no dwarf here.”

“Nay, wife,” Eli said, “he means our Giles—he is small of stature. Why deny what cannot be denied?”

“Ay!” the rider boomed. “That is he—and speaks like a big fellow.”

“His breast is sound,” Catherine snapped. “And quick with fists, even at odds.”

“Else how would the poor thing keep his head in such a world?”

“Well said, dame. You’re as ready with your weapon as he. Likely his mother. Bring him forth, and quickly. See—they lead a stunted mule for him. The Duke needs him sorely—we’re clean out of dwarves and tiger-cats. Our last thumb-hopper tumbled down the well yesterday.”

“And think you I’ll let my darling go to such a reckless house?” Catherine cried. “Where servants leave wells open to trap innocents—like wolves?”

The official lost patience. “Beware,” he warned, voice stern. “Choose wisely: either let your abortion be fed like a bishop and clothed like a prince at court—or have all your heads cut off and borne on poles, with the bellman crying, ‘Behold the heads of hardy rebels, who, having by luck a misbegotten son, traitorously grudged him to the Duke, who is the true father of all his folk, great or small.’”

“Nay,” Eli said sadly, “do not call us rebels. We are loyal folk. But it is sudden—and the lad is our flesh and blood. He has lately shown more sense than before.”

“Threats avail not,” Catherine whimpered. “We don’t grudge him to the Duke—but truly, he cannot go. His linen is all in holes. That ends it.”

But the male mind resisted. “Think you the Duke won’t find linen—and cloth of gold besides? None are so brave, none so favored at court, as our monsters—big or small.”

How long the debate might have lasted, I know not. But it ended abruptly—by the bone of contention himself.

The dwarf stepped out of the house and declared, “If my folk are mad, I am not. I’ll go with you—this instant.”

Catherine wailed. Another child slipping from her care.

Giles, though, felt her pain. “Do not mourn, mother! It’s a blessing. I’ve grown tired of this place since Gerard left.”

“Ah, cruel Giles! Should you not say she is bereaved of Gerard? You must stay and comfort her.”

“Oh, I’m not going to Rome. Not such a fool. I’ll never go beyond Rotterdam. I’ll visit often. And if I don’t like it, who shall keep me there? Not all the dukes in Christendom.”

“Good sense lies in little bulk,” the emissary approved. “Therefore, Master Giles, kiss your parents and thank them for misbegetting you. And you—bring the mule here.”

One servant led forward the tiny mule.

Giles scorned it. “Why would you throw all into one scale? Put large with large, small with small. Besides, I hate small things. I’ll ride the highest horse here—or not at all.”

The official studied him a moment, then adopted a courteous tone. “I shall honor your will in all reasonable things. (Dismount, Eric—your horse is the highest.) And if you wish to wait an hour to bid farewell, say the word. Your pleasure shall be mine.”

Giles reflected. “Master,” he said, “if we wait a month, it will be the same. My mother is good-hearted, but her body is bigger than her spirit. We’ll part with tears—better done quickly. Bring me that horse.”

Catherine threw her apron over her face and sobbed.

The tall horse was brought. Giles tried to climb by the tail—like a rope.

“Wait!” a servant cried. “He kicks.”

“I’ll kick him,” Giles said. “Bring him beneath the window. I’ll show you how to mount a horse that kicks and refuses the tail.”

He dashed inside and reappeared at an upper window, rope in hand. He tied one end and descended swiftly—smooth as oiled lightning—landing astride the horse unperceived.

The official lifted his hands in admiration. “I have found a pearl,” he thought. “This will be a fine day’s work.”

“Come, father, mother—kiss me, bless me, and off I go.”

Eli blessed him, bidding him be honest and true, a credit to his family.

Catherine could not speak—only clung to him, sobbing. Through her tears, she saw the tear in his sleeve from climbing out the window. Without a word, she pulled out her needle and mended it—her tears falling on his arm, unnoticed—except perhaps by the unseen eyes said to fill the air.

And so the dwarf mounted the high horse and rode away, jocund and bold, the court’s butter spread thick upon his back.

Perpusillus cared little for the two women left behind, rocking and weeping by the hearth, praising his virtues, blind to his faults.

Arrived at court, he became a favorite. One strange habit electrified the palace—but due to his size, and for variety’s sake, he was indulged.

He was allowed to speak the truth.

An unpopular thing.

He made it unbearable.

He bawled it.
