"Dearest Blanche," Arthur began his letter, "you are perpetually engaged in reading and dreaming up pretty dramas and exciting romances in real life. Are you now prepared to star in one? Not the delightful role where the heroine inherits her father's palace and wealth, welcoming her husband amidst loyal retainers to declare, 'All of this is ours,' but rather the tragic part of the unfortunate lady who suddenly realizes she is not the Princess's wife, but Claude Melnotte's—the beggar. Think of Alnaschar's wife arriving just as her husband has kicked over the tray of porcelain destined to make his fortune. Wait—Alnaschar wasn't actually married; he'd merely caught the eye of the Vizier's daughter, and his hopes crashed along with the shattered teacups.

Shall you be the Vizier's daughter, refusing and mocking Alnaschar? Or shall you be the Lady of Lyons, falling for penniless Claude Melnotte? I am willing to play whichever part you prefer. I will love you with my utmost devotion. I will strive to make your humble life happy—for humble it must be. The odds are heavily stacked against anything else; we shall simply live and die in a poor, prosaic, humdrum existence. No stars, no epaulettes for the hero of our tale. I shall write a couple more stories that will quickly fade into obscurity. I will call to the Bar and try to establish myself professionally; perhaps, if I am exceptionally lucky and work incredibly hard—which is absurd—I might secure a colonial appointment, and you could become the lady of an Indian judge. In the meantime, I intend to buy back *The Pall Mall Gazette*. The publishers are weary of it following the death of poor Shandon and will sell it cheaply. Warrington will serve as my right hand, editing it to a respectable standard. I will introduce you to Mr. Finucane, the sub-editor, and I know who will eventually become Mrs. Finucane—a very nice, gentle creature who has endured a sad life sweetly. We shall jog along, hoping for better times and earning our living decently. You shall oversee the opera boxes and manage the fashionable gossip, breaking your little heart in the poet's corner. Shall we live over the offices? There are four excellent rooms, a kitchen, and a garret for Laura in Catherine Street, Strand; or would you prefer a house in Waterloo Road? It would be charming, except for that halfpenny toll at the bridge. Can the boys attend King's College? Does all this sound like a joke to you?"

"Oh, dear Blanche, it is no joke, and I am sober while telling the truth. Our grand daydreams have vanished. Our carriage has whirled out of sight like Cinderella's; our Belgravia mansion has been whisked away by a malevolent genie, and I am no longer a Member of Parliament—not even remotely close to being a Bishop on the Lords' bench or a Duke with a garter. You know quite well what my assets are, and your modest fortune. With those two combined, we might achieve decent comfort: occasional cabs for outings, and certainly no shame in taking an omnibus when tired. But that is all. Is that sufficient for you, my little dainty lady? I often wonder if you can endure the life I offer. At least, it is fair that you know exactly what awaits. If you say, 'Yes, Arthur, I will follow your fate regardless, and be a loyal, loving wife to support and cheer you,' then come to me, dear Blanche, and may God grant me the strength to fulfill my duties. However, if you aspire to a higher station, I must not bar Blanche's fortune. I will stand in the crowd and watch your ladyship depart to Court upon your presentation, and you may offer me a smile from your chariot window. I recall seeing Lady Mirabel last season; her happy husband glittered with stars and cords, and all the garden's flowers bloomed in the coachman's bosom. Will you have the carriages and the glory, or shall we walk and mend my stockings?"

"I cannot reveal now—though I might later, when secrets are no longer necessary—what transpired in the last few hours to alter my life's prospects entirely. Yet, the fact remains: I have discovered something forcing me to abandon my plans and crush many vain ambitions. I have written to Sir Francis Clavering stating I cannot accept his parliamentary seat until after my marriage. Similarly, I cannot and will not accept any greater fortune from you than that which has remained yours since your grandfather's death and the birth of your half-brother. Your good mother is unaware, and I pray she never learns, the reasons behind this peculiar decision. They stem from a painful circumstance attributable to neither of our faults, yet once occurred, it is as fatal and irrepairable as the shock that overturned honest Alnaschar's porcelain, shattering all his hopes beyond repair. I write cheerfully, for there is no point in lamenting such a hopeless misfortune. We have not drawn the grand prize in the lottery, dear Blanche; but I shall be content without it, provided you are too. Once again, with all my heart, I pledge to do my best to make you happy."

"Now, what news? My uncle is gravely ill and takes my refusal of the seat in terrible dudgeon; the scheme was his, and he mourns its failure bitterly. But Warrington, Laura, and I held a council of war. They know this dreadful secret and fully back my decision. You must love George as you love what is generous, upright, and noble. As for Laura—she must be our sister, Blanche, our saint, our guardian angel. With two such friends at home, who cares for the world outside? Who matters if one is elected for Clavering or invited to the season's great balls?"

To this frank confession arrived Blanche's reply to Laura and one to Pen himself, a response perhaps justified by his own letter. "You are spoiled by the world," Blanche wrote. "You do not love your poor Blanche as she deserves, nor would you offer so lightly to take her or leave her, no, Arthur, you do not love me—a man of the world, you have pledged your troth and are ready to redeem it; but where is that entire affection, that love whole and abiding, the vision of my youth? I am but a pastime of your life, whereas I would be its all—a fleeting thought versus your whole soul. I would merge our two hearts into one; but alas, my Arthur, how lonely yours is! How little you give me of it! You speak of our parting with a smile on your lips; of our reunion, and you seem indifferent to hastening it! Is life merely a disillusionment, then? Have the flowers of our garden already faded? I have wept, prayed, spent sleepless hours, shedding bitter tears over your letter! To you I bring the gushing poetry of my being—the yearnings of a soul longing to be loved—that pines endlessly for love, love, love! That throws itself at your feet crying, 'Love me, Arthur!' Your heart beats no faster at my kneeling appeal; your proud eye dims with no tear of sympathy! You accept my soul's treasure as though it were dross—not pearls from the unfathomable depths of affection, not diamonds from the heart's caverns. You treat me like a slave, bidding me bow to my master! Is this the reward of a free maiden? Is this the price of a lifetime's passion? Ah me! When was it otherwise? When did love meet with anything but disappointment? Could I hope (fond fool!) to be the exception to my race's lot, laying my fevered brow on a heart that comprehends my own? Foolish girl that I was! One by one, all the flowers of my young life have faded; and this, the last, the sweetest, the dearest, the fondly and madly loved, the wildly cherished—where is it? But no more of this. Heed not my bleeding heart. Bless you, bless you always, Arthur!"

"The words of this letter were perfectly clear, penned in Blanche's neatest hand on her scented paper; yet the meaning puzzled Pen considerably. Did Blanche intend to accept or refuse his polite offer? Her phrasing suggested either that Pen did not love her and she declined, or that she accepted him despite his coldness, sacrificing herself to him. He laughed sardonically over the letter and the transaction that prompted it. He chuckled at how Fortune had jilted him and how he deserved his precarious position. He turned the musky, gilt-edged riddle over and over. It amused his humor; he enjoyed it as if it were a funny story.

Seated thus, twirling the strange manuscript and joking grimly to himself, his servant entered with a card from a gentleman wishing to speak particularly. Had Pen stepped into the passage, he would have seen his old acquaintance, Mr. Samuel Huxter, sucking his stick, rolling his eyes, and displaying great signs of anxiety.

"'Mr. Huxter on particular business! Pray, ask Mr. Huxter to come in,'" said Pen, amused. His amusement grew when poor Sam appeared.

"'Pray take a chair, Mr. Huxter,'" said Pen in his most superior tone. "'In what way can I assist you?'"

"'I'd rather not speak before the flunky—before the man, Mr. Pendennis,'" Huxter replied, prompting Arthur's attendant to withdraw.
