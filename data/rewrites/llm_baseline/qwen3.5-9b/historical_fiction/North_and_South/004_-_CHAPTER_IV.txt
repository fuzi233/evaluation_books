"Cast me upon some naked shore,
Where I may tracke
Only the print of some sad wracke,
If thou be there though the seas roare,
I shall no gentler calm implore."

He was gone. The house was locked up for the evening, shutting out the deep blues and crimson ambers of the sky. Margaret ascended to prepare for the early tea, finding Dixon in a foul temper, irritated by the interruption a visitor had inevitably caused on such a busy day. He displayed his irritation by brushing away at Margaret's hair with vicious haste, claiming he was rushing to Mrs. Hale. Yet, despite his urgency, Margaret was forced to wait a considerable time in the drawing-room before her mother descended.

She sat alone by the fire, unlit candles standing sentinel on the table behind her, reviewing the day. She recalled the happy walk, the delightful sketching, the cheerful dinner, followed by that uncomfortable, miserable stroll in the garden. *How different men are to women!* she thought. Here she was, disturbed and unhappy, her instincts making a refusal impossible; while he, mere minutes after meeting with a rejection of what ought to have been the deepest, holiest proposal of his life, could speak as if briefs, success, and the superficial trappings of a good house and agreeable society were the sole objects of his desire.

"Oh dear!" she lamented internally. "How she could have loved him if he had but been different—a difference she felt, on reflection, went low, deep down." Then, a fleeting thought crossed her mind: perhaps his lightness was merely assumed, a mask covering a bitterness of disappointment that would have branded her own heart had she loved and been rejected.

Her mother entered before this whirlwind of thoughts could organize itself. Margaret had to shake off the recollections of the day's events and turn into a sympathetic listener to Dixon's complaints about the ironing-iron burning the blanket again, and to reports of Susan Lightfoot wearing artificial flowers in her bonnet, evidence of a vain and giddy character. Mr. Hale sipped his tea in abstracted silence; Margaret bore the brunt of the conversation. She wondered how her parents could be so forgetful, so careless of their companion throughout the day, failing to mention his name. She momentarily forgot that he had not made them an offer.

After tea, Mr. Hale rose, leaning his elbow on the chimney-piece, his head resting in his hand, lost in musing, sighing deeply from time to time. Mrs. Hale departed to consult with Dixon regarding winter clothing for the poor. Margaret prepared her mother's worsted work, shrinking from the prospect of the long evening ahead, wishing only for bedtime so she could revisit the day's events in solitude.

"Margaret!" Mr. Hale called out suddenly, in a tone of desperate urgency that made her startle. "Is that tapestry work of immediate consequence? Can you leave it and come into my study? I wish to speak to you about something very serious to us all."

*Very serious to us all.* Mr. Lennox had never had the chance for private conversation with her father following her refusal; otherwise, this would indeed be a grave matter. First, Margaret felt guilty and ashamed of growing into a woman enough to be considered for marriage. Second, she feared her father might be displeased that she had taken upon herself to decline Mr. Lennox's proposal. However, she quickly realized this was not about recent complications. He gestured for her to sit beside him, stirred the fire, snuffed the candles, and sighed twice before summoning the courage to speak. It came out abruptly: "Margaret! I am going to leave Helstone."

"Leave Helstone, papa! But why?"

Mr. Hale remained silent for a minute or two, playing nervously with papers on the table, opening his mouth to speak several times only to close it again without uttering a word. Margaret could not endure the suspense, which distressed her father even more than herself. "But why, dear papa? Do tell me!"

He looked up suddenly, then spoke with a slow, enforced calmness: "Because I must no longer be a minister in the Church of England."

Margaret had imagined nothing less than that some preferment her mother had coveted had finally fallen to her father—something forcing him to leave beautiful, beloved Helstone for some stately, silent closes in cathedral towns. Those places were grand and imposing, but to abandon Helstone forever for them would have been a sad, lingering pain. Yet, nothing compared to the shock of her father's announcement. What could he mean? It was worse for being so mysterious. The piteous distress on his face, almost imploring a merciful judgment from his child, gave her a sudden sickness. Could he be implicated in something Frederick had done? Frederick was an outlaw. Had her father, driven by natural love for his son, connived at anything?

"Oh! what is it? Speak, papa! Tell me all! Why can you no longer be a clergyman? Surely, if the Bishop were told all we know about Frederick, and the hard injustice—"

"It is nothing about Frederick; the Bishop would have nothing to do with that. It is all myself. Margaret, I will tell you about it. I will answer any questions this once, but after tonight, let us never speak of it again. I can meet the consequences of my painful, miserable doubts; but it is an effort beyond me to speak of what has caused so much suffering."

"Doubts, papa! Doubts as to religion?" Margaret asked, more shocked than ever.

"No! Not doubts as to religion; not the slightest injury to that." He paused. Margaret sighed as if standing on the verge of some new horror.

"I have been reading today of the two thousand who were ejected from their churches," Mr. Hale continued, smiling faintly, "trying to steal some of their bravery; but it is of no use—no use—I cannot help feeling it acutely."

"But, papa, have you well considered? Oh! it seems so terrible, so shocking," Margaret said, suddenly bursting into tears. The one staid foundation of her home, her idea of her beloved father, seemed to reel and rock. What could she say? What was to be done? Seeing her distress, Mr. Hale gathered his nerves to comfort her. Swallowing down dry, choking sobs that had risen in his heart, he went to his bookcase, retrieved a volume he had recently been reading, and found within it the strength to embark on his course.

"Listen, dear Margaret," he said, putting one arm around her waist. She took his hand tightly but could not lift her head, nor could she attend to his words due to her internal agitation. "This is the soliloquy of one who was once a clergyman in a country parish, like me. It was written by Mr. Oldfield, minister of Carsington in Derbyshire, a hundred and sixty years ago or more. His trials are over. He fought the good fight." He spoke the last two sentences low, as if to himself.

Then he read aloud: *"When thou canst no longer continue in thy work without dishonour to God, discredit to religion, foregoing thy integrity, wounding conscience, spoiling thy peace, and hazarding the loss of thy salvation; in a word, when the conditions upon which thou must continue (if thou wilt continue) in thy employments are sinful, and unwarranted by the word of God, thou mayest, yea, thou must believe that God will turn thy very silence, suspension, deprivation, and laying aside, to His glory, and the advancement of the Gospel's interest. When God will not use thee in one kind, yet He will in another. A soul that desires to serve and honour Him shall never want opportunity to do it; nor must thou so limit the Holy One of Israel, as to think He hath but one way in which He can glorify Himself by thee. He can do it by thy silence as well as by thy preaching; the laying aside as well as thy continuance in thy work. It is not pretence of doing God the greatest service, or performing the weightiest duty, that will excuse the least sin, though that sin capacitated or gave us the opportunity for doing that duty. Thou wilt have little thanks, O my soul! if, when thou art charged with corrupting God's worship, falsifying thy vows, thou pretendest a necessity for it in order to a continuance in the ministry."*

As he read, glancing at passages he chose not to voice, he gained resolution, feeling as if he too could be brave and firm in doing what he believed right. But as he ceased, he heard Margaret's low, convulsive sob, and his courage sank under the keen sense of suffering.

"Margaret, dear!" he said, drawing her closer. "Think of the early martyrs; think of the thousands who have suffered."

"But, father," she said, suddenly lifting her flushed, tear-wet face, "the early martyrs suffered for the truth, while you—oh! dear, dear papa!"

"I suffer for conscience' sake, my child," he replied with a dignity trembling only from the acute sensitivity of his nature. "I must do what my conscience bids. I have borne long with self-reproach that would have roused any mind less torpid and cowardly than mine." He shook his head.
