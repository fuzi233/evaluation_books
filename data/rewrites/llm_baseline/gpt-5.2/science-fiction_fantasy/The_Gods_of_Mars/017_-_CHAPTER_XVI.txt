Carthoris, Xodar, Tars Tarkas, and I stood where the low hills broke the wind, watching the great gray battleship slide above us with the tranquil assurance of a gull riding warm air. Its hull caught the thin Barsoomian sun and returned it in hard, metallic glints, yet what struck me more keenly than the light was the sound: the disciplined hush of well-tuned engines, the faint, steady purr of lifting fans—nothing like the rasping industrial shriek of lesser craft that scour men’s nerves raw. This was Helium’s voice in the sky, measured and confident, a music the streets below had not heard in too long a time.

A second vessel topped the hills, then a third, ghosting after their sister. From the nearer ship, one-man scouts sprang in a score from the upper decks, diving in long, swift arcs that made the air itself seem to bow before them. Their approach stitched new notes into the soundscape—whistling descents, the soft cough of jets correcting angle, the crisp clap of vanes biting into thinner air—until, in the space of a few breaths, armed sailors ringed us.

An officer stepped forward to speak, but the moment his eyes fell on Carthoris his whole body answered before his tongue could. Surprise broke into joy; he strode in and gripped the boy by the shoulders as though to make certain the flesh was real.

“Carthoris, my Prince!” he cried. “Kaor! Kaor! Hor Vastus greets the son of Dejah Thoris, Princess of Helium, and of her husband, John Carter. Where have you been, O my Prince? All Helium has been plunged in sorrow. Terrible have been the calamities that have befallen your great-grandsire’s mighty nation since the fatal day that saw you leave our midst.”

“Grieve not, my good Hor Vastus,” Carthoris answered, and though he spoke with a prince’s poise I heard the tremor beneath it—the same tremor a young cook gets when he carries the first dish out to an unforgiving hall. “Since I bring not back myself alone to cheer my mother’s heart and the hearts of my beloved people, but also one whom all Barsoom loved best—her greatest warrior and her saviour—John Carter, Prince of Helium!”

Hor Vastus turned to me, and for an instant the wind seemed to pause with him. His face lost its color, as though he had bitten into something too bitter to swallow.

“John Carter!” he breathed. Then trouble shadowed his eyes. “My Prince,” he began, “where hast thou—” He stopped, the question dying behind his teeth. He would not be the one to force the confession from me, not here, not in front of men whose ears were already tuned to the old doctrine of Dor as surely as a temple chorus is trained to certain notes.

“Ah, my Prince,” he continued, as if no fracture had opened in his greeting, “that you are back is sufficient, and let Hor Vastus’ sword have the high honour of being first at thy feet.”

He unbuckled his scabbard and cast his blade upon the ground before me.

There are oaths that require grand words, and there are oaths that need only the weight of metal on stone. In that clean clink of steel I heard every vow Helium makes to its own memory: the allegiance not to a crown alone, but to a way of being, to names spoken in old dialects that carry meanings no conqueror’s tongue can fully steal. It was surrender and trust together—“My sword, my life, my soul.” A single act, yet it filled the air like the aroma of a familiar hearth bread after years of exile.

I stooped, lifted the sword, raised the hilt to my lips, and stepped close enough that Hor Vastus could see the truth in my eyes if he wished.

“Hor Vastus,” I said, my hand upon his shoulder, “you know best the promptings of your own heart. That I shall need your sword I have little doubt; but accept from John Carter upon his sacred honour the assurance that he will never call upon you to draw this sword other than in the cause of truth, justice, and righteousness.”

“That I knew, my Prince,” he replied, “ere ever I threw my beloved blade at thy feet.”

While we spoke, other craft came and went between ground and battleship like bees between hive and flowering scarlet fields. Presently a larger boat dropped from above—room for a dozen, perhaps—and settled near us with scarcely more sound than a lid set gently upon a pot so as not to lose the steam. An officer sprang down and saluted Hor Vastus.

“Kantos Kan desires that this party whom we have rescued be brought immediately to the deck of the Xavarian,” he said.

As we moved toward the boat, I looked about for those who had stood with us in the dust and danger, and for the first time my eyes understood what my mind had missed: Thuvia was not there. I asked; answers came too quickly and all the same—none had seen her since Carthoris had sent her thoat galloping madly toward the hills, hoping speed and distance would be her shield.

Hor Vastus’ face tightened. Without flourish he snapped orders, and a dozen air scouts sprang away in as many directions, their engines whispering through the air with the searching insistence of hounds. It could not be possible she had gone far. Yet absence is sometimes louder than any engine: it rang in my skull.

We stepped aboard. A moment later we were upon the Xavarian.

The first to greet me was Kantos Kan.

Age and office had set new lines around his eyes, but they were the same eyes that had watched me in a Warhoon dungeon, that had endured the horrors of the Great Games, that had followed me through Zodanga when I was a stranger to Barsoom and he a simple padwar. Now he commanded Helium’s terrors of the skies. Now I bore the title of Prince in the House of Tardos Mors. Yet when his hands gripped mine, I felt not rank but history—like the taste of an old family dish prepared the same way each generation, a continuity that survives the ruin of cities.

He did not ask where I had been. His silence had the careful courtesy of one who knows a man cannot be forced to taste grief before he is ready, and that some bitterness, once on the tongue, will not leave.

He greeted Carthoris and Tars Tarkas with open delight; he could scarcely keep his hands off my son.

“You do not know, John Carter,” he said to me, “how we of Helium love this son of yours. It is as though all the great love we bore his noble father and his poor mother had been centred in him. When it became known that he was lost, ten million people wept.”

“What mean you,” I whispered, my throat suddenly dry, “by ‘his poor mother’?”

Kantos Kan drew me aside, away from the ears of sailors and the softer, treacherous ears of superstition. The ship around us murmured with contained power; below, the city’s distant hush pressed up like a held breath.

“For a year,” he said, “ever since Carthoris disappeared, Dejah Thoris has grieved and mourned for her lost boy. The blow of years ago, when you did not return from the atmosphere plant, was lessened to some extent by the duties of motherhood, for your son broke his white shell that very night.

“That she suffered terribly then, all Helium knew, for did not all Helium suffer with her the loss of her lord! But with the boy gone there was nothing left, and after expedition upon expedition returned with the same hopeless tale of no clue as to his whereabouts, our beloved Princess drooped lower and lower, until all who saw her felt that it could be but a matter of days ere she went to join her loved ones within the precincts of the Valley Dor.

“As a last resort, Mors Kajak, her father, and Tardos Mors, her grandfather, took command of two mighty expeditions, and a month ago sailed away to explore every inch of ground in the northern hemisphere of Barsoom. For two weeks no word has come back from them, but rumours were rife that they had met with a terrible disaster and that all were dead.

“About this time Zat Arras renewed his importunities for her hand in marriage. He has been for ever after her since you disappeared. She hated him and feared him, but with both her father and grandfather gone, Zat Arras was very powerful, for he is still Jed of Zodanga, to which position, you will remember, Tardos Mors appointed him after you had refused the honour.

“He had a secret audience with her six days ago. What took place none knows, but the next day Dejah Thoris had disappeared, and with her had gone a dozen of her household guard and body servants, including Sola the green woman—Tars Tarkas’ daughter, you recall. No word left they of their intentions, but it is always thus with those who go upon the voluntary pilgrimage from which none returns. We cannot think aught than that Dejah Thoris has sought the icy bosom of Iss, and that her devoted servants have chosen to accompany her.

“Zat Arras was at Helium when she disappeared. He commands this fleet which has been searching for her since. No trace of her have we found, and I fear that it be a futile quest.”

His words fell one upon another like stones into a pot already boiling over. I tasted metal at the back of my mouth. The names—Iss, Dor—were not merely places; they were the old lexicon’s weapons, terms loaded with centuries of fear and obedience. To speak them was to invoke a doctrine that had eaten the language of truth until men could no longer tell hunger from holiness.

While I stood there, the scouts Hor Vastus had dispatched returned to the Xavarian, one by one. Not one brought word of Thuvia. Each arrival was a brief surge of engine-song, each report another silence.

The weight of Dejah Thoris’ disappearance pressed me toward the deck. And beside it, the new, sharp worry for Thuvia—whom I believed the daughter of some proud Barsoomian house, and whom I had meant to return with honor to her people—added itself like a second blade laid across the first.

I was about to ask Kantos Kan to continue the search when a flier from the flagship arrived, bearing an officer with a message from Arras. Kantos Kan read the dispatch. His jaw tightened.

“Zat Arras commands me to bring our ‘prisoners’ before him,” he said. “There is naught else to do. He is supreme in Helium, yet it would be far more in keeping with chivalry and good taste were he to come hither and greet the saviour of Barsoom with the honours that are his due.”

“You know full well,” I said, and I heard my own voice find a thin edge of irony as if it were something I could chew to keep from choking, “that Zat Arras has good cause to hate me. Nothing would please him better than to humiliate me and then to kill me. Now that he has so excellent an excuse, let us go and see if he has the courage to take advantage of it.”

Summoning Carthoris, Tars Tarkas, and Xodar, we entered the small flier with Kantos Kan and Arras’ officer. The craft lifted; the sound of its engines, sharp and insistent compared to the battleship’s disciplined hum, set my teeth on edge. We crossed to the flagship and stepped upon its deck.

Zat Arras awaited us.

No greeting softened his face. Not even to Carthoris did he give a kind word. He stood cold and haughty, his silence a calculated noise—like a gong struck once, meant to command a hall.

“Kaor, Zat Arras,” I said.

He did not respond. His gaze slid over us as if measuring not men but liabilities.

“Why were these prisoners not disarmed?” he demanded of Kantos Kan.

“They are not prisoners, Zat Arras,” Kantos Kan replied, and there was steel in him now beneath the courtesy. “Two of them are of Helium’s noblest family. Tars Tarkas, Jeddak of Thark, is Tardos Mors’ best beloved ally. The other is a friend and companion of the Prince of Helium—that is enough for me to know.”

“It is not enough for me, however,” Zat Arras snapped. “More must I hear from those who have taken the pilgrimage than their names. Where have you been, John Carter?”

I did not lower my eyes. “I have just come from the Valley Dor and the Land of the First Born, Zat Arras,” I said.

“Ah!” He allowed pleasure to show itself, quick as a knife-flash. “You do not deny it, then? You have returned from the bosom of Iss?”

“I have come back from a land of false hope,” I answered, “from a valley of torture and death; with my companions I have escaped from the hideous clutches of lying fiends. I have come back to the Barsoom that I saved from a painless death to again save her, but this time from death in its most frightful form.”

“Cease, blasphemer!” Zat Arras cried. “Hope not to save thy cowardly carcass by inventing horrid lies to—”

He did not finish.

Some words are thrown as easily as scraps to a hungry animal. “Coward.” “Liar.” He spoke them as though the old creed gave him license, as though the dominant lexicon could rewrite a man’s life by naming it.

Before a hand could rise to stop me, I was at his side. My fingers closed on his throat.

“Come I from heaven or hell, Zat Arras,” I said, and my voice was very quiet, the way a man speaks when he has put all unnecessary sound away, “you will find me still the same John Carter that I have always been; nor did ever man call me such names and live—without apologizing.”

I bent him back across my knee and tightened my grip.

“Seize him!” he choked out.

A dozen officers sprang forward. The air around us filled with the scuffle of sandals on deck plates, the strained squeak of leather, the sudden raggedness of men breathing too fast—noise that tore at the order of the ship like a careless hand tearing cloth.

Kantos Kan came close, his lips to my ear. “Desist,” he whispered. “I beg of you. It will but involve us all, for I cannot see these men lay hands upon you without aiding you. My officers and men will join me and we shall have a mutiny then that may lead to revolution. For the sake of Tardos Mors and Helium, desist.”

For Helium. For the fragile continuity that held a nation together when its old words were being used as chains. I released Zat Arras.

He staggered, white and trembling amid his officers, his dignity hanging by a thread he pretended was iron. I turned my back upon him and walked toward the ship’s rail.

“Come, Kantos Kan,” I said. “The Prince of Helium would return to the Xavarian.”

No one interfered. Some faces watched Zat Arras with scorn; some shifted toward me as though pulled by an older allegiance than fear. One man—long in the service and confidence of Tardos Mors—spoke in a low voice as I passed.

“You may count my metal among your fighting-men, John Carter,” he said.

I thanked him and went on.

In silence we embarked, and shortly after stepped once more upon the deck of the Xavarian. Fifteen minutes later orders came from the flagship to proceed toward Helium.

The journey was uneventful in event, but not in mind. Carthoris and I moved through the hours as men move through a room where a pot has boiled dry: everything smells faintly scorched, and the smallest sound seems like accusation. Kantos Kan grew sombre, considering what further calamity Zat Arras might invite by clinging to the age-old precedent that demanded a terrible death for fugitives from the Valley Dor. Tars Tarkas grieved for Sola, his daughter, and the grief sat on him like a cloak too heavy for the season. Xodar alone wore a kind of lightness—a fugitive and outlaw, he could be no worse off in Helium than elsewhere.

“Let us hope that we may at least go out with good red blood upon our blades,” he said, as though discussing the only honest seasoning left in a tasteless world.

Among the officers of the Xavarian I felt division begin to form, quiet but unmistakable—like two dialects emerging in the same household, one spoken openly at the table, the other whispered in back rooms. There were those who gathered about Carthoris and myself whenever they could, their loyalty a warmth they tried to hide beneath formality; and about an equal number held aloof, courteous yet distant, their eyes carrying the old fear of Dor and Iss and Korus. I could not blame them. A creed, however ridiculous to the outsider, can root itself in a people’s daily habits the way a staple grain roots itself in soil; it becomes sustenance and identity, and to doubt it is to threaten starvation of the spirit.

By returning from Dor we had committed sacrilege. By speaking of what we had seen we had, in their minds, tried to murder the religion of their fathers. We were blasphemers—lying heretics.

Even those who still clung to us out of love and loyalty did so, I think, with an ache behind their eyes—the ache of being asked to translate experience that will not translate, to accept a truth that has no words in the language they were given. It is hard enough to exchange one faith for another; harder still to be told the old is false and offered nothing familiar in its place.

Kantos Kan would not listen to my account.

“It is enough,” he said, “that I jeopardize my life here and hereafter by countenancing you at all—do not ask me to add still further to my sins by listening to what I have always been taught was the rankest heresy.”

Sooner or later, I knew, friends and enemies would be forced to declare themselves openly. In Helium there must be an accounting. If Tardos Mors had not returned, Zat Arras’ enmity might weigh heavy against us, for he represented the government; to oppose him was treason. The majority of troops would follow their officers, and I knew many powerful men of the land and air forces would cleave to John Carter in the face of god, man, or devil. Yet the majority of the populace, steeped in the old words, might demand our blood to cleanse the sacrilege.

The outlook was dark from whatever angle I turned it. But my mind was torn open by the thought of Dejah Thoris, and in that pain all other calculations tasted thin. Day and night I saw a nightmare of what she might even then be enduring—plant men, white apes, the machinery of terror disguised as sanctity. At times I covered my face with my hands as though I could shut out images by closing a door.

It was in the forenoon that we arrived above the mile-high scarlet tower that marks greater Helium from her twin city. As we descended in great circles toward the navy docks, a mighty multitude surged in the streets beneath. Helium had been notified by radio-aerogram of our approach, and the city’s sound rose to meet us—not a roar, but a broad, breathing murmur like wind moving through a dry harvest. From the deck of the Xavarian we four—Carthoris, Tars Tarkas, Xodar, and I—were transferred to a lesser flier for transport to quarters within the Temple of Reward.

It is there that Martian justice is meted to benefactor and malefactor alike. There the hero is decorated. There the felon is condemned. The place itself is a kind of public memory, a stone repository of praise and punishment, deciding what names will be sung in the old tongue and what names will be spat out as warning.

We were taken in from the landing stage upon the roof, so we did not pass among the people at all, as is customary. Always before, prisoners of note or wanderers returned from distant ventures had been paraded from the Gate of Jeddaks to the Temple of Reward up the broad Avenue of Ancestors through dense crowds—jeering or cheering, blessing or cursing. Now we were kept from them.

Zat Arras dared not trust the people near to us. He feared their love for Carthoris and myself might flare into a demonstration strong enough to burn away, even for a moment, their superstitious horror at the charge he meant to lay upon us. Only his most trusted servitors accompanied us to the temple.

We were lodged in a room on the south side overlooking the Avenue of Ancestors. From there we could see the full length down to the Gate of Jeddaks, five miles away. The temple plaza and the streets for a mile were packed so tight with bodies that the city looked paved with living color.

And yet there was no disorder. Neither scoffs nor plaudits rose—only a vast, contained quiet, broken here and there by sobbing. When they saw us at the window above them, many buried their faces in their arms and wept. That sound—human, untrained, uncommercialized—cut deeper than any shouted hatred could have. It was the sound of a people whose old words had left them only one way to mourn.

Late in the afternoon a messenger arrived from Zat Arras. He informed us that we would be tried by an impartial body of nobles in the great hall of the temple at the first zode on the following day.
