Sir William Moore, having demonstrated his fierce resolve in the defense of the mill, turned his gaze with equal ferocity toward those who had instigated the unrest. While he spared the common mob—the misguided followers driven by hunger and false counsel—he pursued the ringleaders with an indefatigable, relentless vigor. To him, punishing the suffering masses would have been the act of a tyrant, not a judge; thus, he let them pass unseen on the roads and streets. But the leaders were different. They were strangers, emissaries from the great cities, mostly "down-draughts"—bankrupts, debt-ridden drunkards, and men with nothing to lose and everything to gain in terms of character and cash. These were the targets Moore hunted with the enthusiasm of a sleuth-hound, finding the chase infinitely more thrilling than the mundane labor of weaving cloth.

His horse seemed to share his master's disdain for these times, enduring a regimen of hard riding that left them both breathless. Moore lived his life on the road, breathing in the crisp, invigorating air of the countryside rather than the choking steam of the dye-houses. The local magistrates, men of timidity and slowness, likely dreaded him. Moore relished the power to frighten them, to force a tremor of fear into their resolve—a simple, primal terror of assassination. Indeed, this shadow hung over every manufacturer and public figure in the district save for the old Cossack himself. Sir William knew the peril; he knew he could be shot. Yet, for a man of his steel nerves, such a death held no terror. If given a choice, it would have been his preferred end.

Conversely, the knowledge that he hunted assassins acted as a spur to his high spirits. Fear was foreign to his proud, hard-natured constitution. Countless nights, he rode alone over the moors, whether moonlit or moonless, feeling more elated and refreshed than when trapped within the stifling walls of his counting-house. There were four leaders to account for. Within two weeks, two were cornered near Stilbro', while the final pair required a journey further west, rumored to haunt the outskirts of Birmingham.

Yet, amidst this storm of stern justice and exhausting business, did Moore ever spare a moment for gentler fires? Did he nurture anything softer than the cold embers of Nemesis? It was difficult to discern. He rarely ventured near Fieldhead, and his visits to the rectory were strictly confined to formal conferences with the rector in his study. His course remained rigid, unmoving. Meanwhile, the wider world remained embroiled in turmoil. The war's hurricane still swept across the continent, bringing no respite, no serene skies, and no gentle rains to soothe the land. Beneath Moore's feet, the specter of Ruin worked tirelessly, sapping the foundations of the region. Whether crossing his hearth or galloping over the sullen moors, he felt the earth vibrate with a hollow echo, shaking beneath his tread.

But how did the season pass for Shirley and Caroline? Let us first turn our attention to the heiress. Was she a love-lorn maiden, pale and pining for a neglectful lover? Did she spend her days bent over sedentary tasks, eyes fixed on books or sewing, silent and withdrawn?

Far from it. Shirley was vibrant and whole. Though a wistfulness lingered in her gaze, her careless smiles remained intact. She kept her dark, ancient manor house aglow with her cheerful presence. The galleries and low-ceiled chambers echoed with her lively voice; the dim entrance hall grew accustomed to the rhythmic rustle of silk dresses as she swept from room to room. Now she carried flowers to the barbarous peach-bloom salon, now entered the dining room to open casements and let in the scents of mignonette and sweet-briar, and anon brought plants from the staircase window to the sun-drenched porch.

Sewing occupied her hands, but fate seemed to doom her to abandon it before five minutes elapsed. Hardly would her thimble be fitted or her needle threaded when a sudden impulse would whisk her upstairs—perhaps to retrieve an unnecessary ivory-backed needle-book or a confused drawer, or merely to peer from a specific window at the view of Briarfield church and rectory nestled in the trees. Before she returned, Tartar's bold scrape and strangled whistle would herald his arrival at the porch. Panting from the heat, he must be ushered to the kitchen to ensure his water-bowl was full.

Through the open kitchen door lay the courtyard, a riot of sunshine and gaiety, teeming with turkeys, poults, peahens, Guinea-fowls, and a dazzling array of pigeons in white, purple, blue, and cinnamon plumage. An irresistible spectacle, Shirley ran to the pantry for a roll and stood on the doorstep scattering crumbs. Soon, her eager, plump, happy feathered vassals thronged around her. John was about the stables, and he demanded conversation; her mare needed admiration. Still petting the animal, she was interrupted by the cows returning to be milked. This was of paramount importance; Shirley stayed to inspect them all. Perhaps there were tiny calves or newly yeaned lambs, maybe twins rejected by their mothers. Miss Keeldar had to be introduced to them by John, permitted the treat of feeding them by hand under the watchful eye of her careful foreman. Meanwhile, John raised doubts about the farming of certain "crofts," "ings," and "holmes," necessitating that his mistress fetch her gypsy-straw hat and accompany him over stiles and along hedgerows to witness the agricultural debate on the spot itself. Thus, the bright afternoon wore into soft evening, leading to a late tea, after which she never sat to sew again.

After tea, Shirley read. She possessed the same tenacity for a book as she lacked for a needle. Her study was the rug, her seat a footstool, or perhaps merely the carpet at Mrs. Pryor's feet—the very place where she had learned her lessons as a child, a habit that held strong sway. Beside her sprawled the tawny, lion-like bulk of Tartar, his negro muzzle resting on his forepaws, straight, strong, and shapely like the limbs of an Alpine wolf. Often, one of Shirley's hands rested on the loyal beast's rough head; remove it, and he would groan in discontent. Shirley's mind was wholly given to her book. She lifted not her eyes, stirred not, or spoke—not unless to offer a brief, respectful reply to Mrs. Pryor's occasional admonishments.

"My dear, you had better not have that great dog so near you; he is crushing the border of your dress."

"Oh, it is only muslin. I can put a clean one on to-morrow."

"My dear, I wish you could acquire the habit of sitting to a table when you read."

"I will try, ma'am, some time; but it is so comfortable to do as one has always been accustomed to do."

"My dear, let me beg of you to put that book down. You are trying your eyes by the doubtful firelight."

"No, ma'am, not at all; my eyes are never tired."

Eventually, however, a pale light spilled onto the page from the window. She looked up; the moon had risen. Closing the volume, she rose and walked through the room. The book, perhaps a good one, had refreshed, refilled, and rewarmed her heart, setting her brain afire with images and ideas. The still parlour, the clean hearth, the window opening onto the twilight sky and revealing its "sweet regent," new-throned and glorious, sufficed to transform earth into an Eden and life into a poem for Shirley. A deep, inborn delight glowed in her young veins, unmingled and untarnished, incapable of being seized or spoiled by human agency, for it was a divine gift, the free dower of Nature bestowed upon her child. This joy granted her a glimpse of a *genii*-life. Buoyant, ascending by green steps and glad hills, all verdance and light, she reached a station scarcely lower than that from which angels looked down upon the dreamer of Bethel. Her eye sought, and her soul possessed, the vision of life as she wished it. Alas, not as she wished it; she had no time to wish. The swift glory spread out, sweeping and kindling, multiplying its splendors faster than Thought could combine them or Aspiration articulate her longings. While the trance held her, Shirley said nothing, quite mute; but if Mrs. Pryor spoke to her now, Shirley would depart quietly, continuing her walk upstairs in the dim gallery.

Had Shirley not been indolent, reckless, and ignorant, she would have seized a pen at such moments, while the memory burned fresh on her spirit. She would have fixed the apparition, told the vision revealed. Had she possessed a greater measure of the organ of acquisitiveness, a stronger love of property, she would have taken a large sheet of paper and written plainly, in her own queer yet clear hand, the story narrated, the song sung to her, thereby possessing what she had created. But indolent she was, reckless she was, and most ignorant; for she did not know that her dreams were rare, her feelings peculiar. She did not know, nor would she know until her dying day, the full value of that spring whose bright, fresh bubblings kept her heart green.

Shirley took life easily.
