Elinor's curiosity regarding Mrs. Ferrars was finally sated. She had discovered everything necessary to confirm that any further alliance between their families would be disastrous. Having witnessed firsthand the depth of her aunt-in-law's pride, her pettiness, and her resolute prejudice against herself, Elinor comprehended fully the obstacles that had delayed Edward's union with her. Yet, viewing these barriers through the lens of self-preservation, she felt a profound gratitude. While she did not truly rejoice that Edward remained bound to Lucy Steele, she convinced herself that, had Lucy possessed any genuine amiability, she *ought* to have celebrated the match. Instead, the reality was far less charming.

What puzzled Elinor most, however, was the elevation of Lucy Steele's spirits following the civilities of Mrs. Ferrars. How could such trivial attention, offered merely because Lucy was not Elinor, suffice to blind Lucy's judgment and vanity? How could she interpret a mere lack of hostility as a sign of affection? Elinor suspected the truth was evident to anyone observing Lucy's eyes at the time, yet the matter became undeniably clear the following morning. At Lucy's specific request, Lady Middleton had arranged a private audience in Berkeley Street, ostensibly to ensure Elinor received news of the visit alone. This arrangement proved fortuitous, as a note from Mrs. Palmer shortly after Lucy's arrival summoned Mrs. Jennings away, leaving the two sisters completely isolated.

"My dear friend," Lucy exclaimed the moment they were alone, "I must discuss my happiness. Was there ever anything so flattering as Mrs. Ferrars's demeanor yesterday? She was so excessively affable! You know how dreadfully I feared encountering her, yet the instant I was introduced, her behavior suggested a sudden, overwhelming fondness. Did I not read it correctly? You saw it, too, did you not?"

"She was certainly civil to you," Elinor replied cautiously.

"Civil!" Lucy protested, indignant. "Did you perceive nothing beyond mere politeness? I saw far more. A kindness reserved exclusively for me! No pride, no hauteur—unlike your sister, who remains unchanged in her sweetness, though perhaps less demonstrative."

Elinor attempted to steer the conversation toward safer ground, but Lucy persisted, demanding acknowledgment of her reasons for joy.

"Undoubtedly," Lucy pressed, "if they knew of your engagement, their treatment of me would seem equally flattering. But since they do not know—"

"I anticipated you would say that," Lucy interrupted quickly. "There was no earthly reason for Mrs. Ferrars to favor me unless she actually did, and her favor is everything. Do not try to dissuade me from my satisfaction. I am certain it will conclude happily; there will be no difficulties whatsoever compared to what I once imagined. Mrs. Ferrars is a charming woman, and so is your sister. Both are delightful! I wonder why I have never heard you describe how agreeable Mrs. Dashwood is!"

Elinor, having no suitable response, remained silent.

"You appear unwell, Miss Dashwood," Lucy observed, feigning concern. "You seem listless, quiet... surely you are not well?"

"I have never been in better health," Elinor lied smoothly.

"I am delighted to hear it," Lucy said, her tone shifting instantly from suspicion to adoration. "Though you did not look it. I would be grieved indeed if *you* were ill; you have been the greatest comfort in the world to me. Heaven knows what I should have endured without your friendship."

Elinor offered a polite, albeit strained, reassurance. To her surprise, this seemed to satisfy Lucy, who immediately returned to her previous topic.

"I am absolutely convinced of your deep regard for me," Lucy declared. "Next to Edward's love, it is my greatest solace. Oh, poor Edward! But now, consider the bright side: we shall be able to meet frequently. Lady Middleton delights in Mrs. Dashwood, so we shall likely spend considerable time in Harley Street. Besides, Edward spends half his time with his sister, and both Lady Middleton and Mrs. Ferrars have repeatedly expressed their eagerness to see me. They are such charming women! If you ever mention to your sister what I think of her, you must praise her highly."

However, Elinor refused to provide any encouragement for Lucy's hopes that she would eventually reveal the secret to her sister. Undeterred, Lucy continued her monologue.

"I am certain I would have detected any disfavor instantly. Had Mrs. Ferrars merely offered a perfunctory courtesy, then ignored me thereafter, never casting a pleasant glance in my direction—I know what you mean—if I had been subjected to such cold indifference, I would have despaired immediately. I could not endure it. Wherever she dislikes someone, I understand, it is with the utmost violence."

Before Elinor could respond to this triumphant assessment of her aunt-in-law's affections, the front door burst open. An announcement revealed Mr. Ferrars's approach, and moments later, Edward entered the room.

An awkward silence descended upon the trio. Every countenance reflected the gravity of the situation; they all appeared absurdly exposed. Edward seemed torn between fleeing the room and advancing further, paralyzed by the circumstances they had both desperately sought to avoid. Not only were they all together, but they were trapped together without the buffer of any third party. The ladies recovered composure first. It was not Lucy's role to draw attention, and the appearance of secrecy required maintenance. She could offer only a fleeting, tender gaze and a barely audible greeting before falling silent.

Elinor, however, faced a far more arduous task. Driven by anxiety for Edward's feelings and her own reputation, she compelled herself to summon a demeanor of near-ease and openness. After a brief internal struggle, she succeeded in projecting a warm welcome. She deliberately avoided letting Lucy's watchful eyes or her own sense of injustice inhibit her from expressing joy at seeing him and offering apologies for her absence during his previous call at Berkeley Street. She resolved to pay him the attentions due to a friend and distant relative, regardless of Lucy's scrutinizing gaze, which she noted was fixed intently upon her.

Her efforts yielded partial results. Edward, reassured by her composure, found the courage to sit, though his embarrassment surpassed theirs, a disparity that the nature of his entanglement justified. His heart lacked Lucy's indifference, and his conscience, unlike hers, held no peace. Meanwhile, Lucy maintained a demure, settled air, refusing to utter a single word that might alleviate the tension. Consequently, nearly every sentence spoken originated from Elinor, who felt compelled to volunteer details concerning her mother's health and their journey to town—information Edward ought to have inquired about but neglected to ask.

Her exertions did not end there. Feeling a heroic resolve, Elinor decided, under the pretext of fetching Marianne, to withdraw the others to privacy. With remarkable dignity, she lingered on the staircase for several minutes, displaying the highest fortitude, before descending to join her sister. Once separated, however, Edward's rapturous mood began to fade. Marianne, whose joy was as intense and vocal as any emotion she harbored, greeted him with a hand extended and a voice brimming with familial affection.

"Dear Edward!" she cried. "This is a moment of great happiness! This would almost compensate for everything!"

Edward endeavored to reciprocate her kindness, yet before such witnesses, he dared not confess half of what he truly felt. As they resumed their seats, a heavy silence fell over the group. Marianne gazed alternately at Edward and Elinor with profound tenderness, silently lamenting that their shared delight was marred by Lucy's unwanted presence. It was Edward who broke the silence, commenting on Marianne's altered complexion and voicing his concern that London might prove disagreeable to her constitution.

"Oh, do not think of me!" Marianne replied, her eyes filling with tears despite her spirited tone. "Do not consider *my* health. Look at Elinor; she is well. That must suffice for us both."

This observation served to unsettle neither Edward nor Elinor, nor did it win Lucy's goodwill, whose upward glance at Marianne held no benevolence.

"Do you find London agreeable?" Edward asked, attempting to shift the subject.

"Not at all," Marianne answered. "I expected immense pleasure, yet I have found none. The sight of you, Edward, is the sole comfort it offers. Thank Heaven, you remain exactly as you always were!"

She paused, allowing the unspoken weight of her words to hang in the air.

"Elinor," Marianne continued after a moment, "we must entrust Edward with our care upon returning to Barton. Within a week or two, I presume, we shall depart. I trust Edward will not be overly reluctant to accept this charge."

Edward murmured vaguely, the content of his words lost to everyone, including himself. Marianne, perceiving his agitation and interpreting it according to her own inclinations, was perfectly satisfied and swiftly changed the subject.

"We spent such a tedious day in Harley Street yesterday!" Marianne exclaimed. "So dull, so wretchedly dull! Yet I have much to relate to you regarding this, which I cannot discuss here."

With admirable discretion, she deferred her candid account of finding their mutual relatives increasingly disagreeable and her particular disgust for his mother until they were in private.

"But why were you not there, Edward?" Marianne demanded, turning her attention back to him. "Why did you not come?"

"I was engaged elsewhere," Edward replied evasively.
