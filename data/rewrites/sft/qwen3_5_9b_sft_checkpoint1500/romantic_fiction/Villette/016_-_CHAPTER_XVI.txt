My soul fled in that swoon, though I cannot trace its path. Whatever visions haunted her trance on that strange night, or whatever realm she traversed, she kept her counsel absolute, never whispering a word to Memory, baffling imagination with an indissoluble silence. Perhaps she soared upward, glimpsing her eternal home, hoping for respite from this painful union with matter, believing herself finally freed. Yet, as she deemed, an unseen guardian warned her back from heaven's threshold, guiding her weeping spirit downward, binding her once more, shuddering and unwilling, to that frail, cold, and wasted frame. From which companionship she had grown weary.

She returned to her prison with pain, reluctance, a moan, and a long shiver. The divorced mates, Spirit and Substance, were hard to reunite; they greeted each other not in an embrace, but in a racking struggle. Sight returned red, as if swimming in blood; hearing rushed back loud like thunder; consciousness revived in terror. I sat up, appalled, wondering into what strange region, among what bizarre beings, I was waking. At first, I knew nothing I looked upon: a wall was not a wall, a lamp not a lamp. I should have understood the concept of a ghost as easily as I did the commonest object, which implies that everything my eyes rested upon struck me as spectral. But gradually, the faculties settled into their places; the life-machine resumed its wonted working.

Yet I knew not where I was, until slowly I realized I had been moved from the spot where I fell. I lay on no portico-step; night and tempest were barred by walls, windows, and a ceiling. I had been carried into some house—but what house?

Thinking of the pensionnat in the Rue Fossette, I tried to discern in what room I lay: the great dormitory or one of the little ones? Puzzled, I could not reconcile the glimpses of furniture with my knowledge of those apartments. The empty white beds were missing, along with the long line of large windows. "Surely," I thought, "it is not to Madame Beck's own chamber they have carried me!" Then my eye fell on an easy-chair covered in blue damask. Other seats, cushioned to match, appeared by degrees, revealing the complete fact of a pleasant parlor. A wood fire burned on a clear-hearth, a carpet where arabesques of bright blue relieved a ground of shaded fawn. Pale walls bore a slight but endless garland of azure forget-me-nots, tangled and bewildered amongst myriad gold leaves and tendrils. A gilded mirror filled the space between two windows, curtained amply with blue damask. In this mirror, I saw myself laid, not in bed, but on a sofa. I looked spectral; my eyes larger and more hollow, my hair darker than natural, contrasting with my thin, ashen face. It was obvious, from the furniture and the layout of windows and doors, that this was an unknown room in an unknown house.

Hardly less plain was it that my brain was unsettled. As I gazed at the blue arm-chair, it appeared to grow familiar, as did a certain scroll-couch, the round centre-table with its blue covering bordered with autumn-tinted foliage, and especially two little footstools with worked covers and a small ebony-framed chair, whose seat and back were worked with groups of brilliant flowers on a dark ground.

Struck by these things, I explored further. Strange to say, old acquaintances were everywhere, and "auld lang syne" smiled out of every nook. Two oval miniatures hung over the mantel-piece, of which I knew by heart the pearls around the high, powdered heads, the velvets circling the white throats, the swell of the full muslin kerchiefs, and the pattern of the lace sleeve-ruffles. Upon the shelf stood two china vases, relics of a diminutive tea-service smooth as enamel and thin as egg-shell, alongside a white centre ornament, a classic group in alabaster preserved under glass. Of all these, I could have told the peculiarities, numbering flaws and cracks like any clairvoyante. Above all, there were a pair of handscreens with elaborate pencil drawings, finished like line engravings. These ached my eyes to behold again, recalling hours when they had followed, stroke by stroke, the tedious, feeble, finical school-girl pencil held in those fingers, now so skeleton-like.

Where was I? Not only in what spot of the world, but in what year of our Lord? For all these objects belonged to past days and a distant country. Ten years ago I had bid them farewell; since my fourteenth year, they and I had never met. I gasped audibly, "Where am I?"

A shape, hitherto unnoticed, stirred, rose, and came forward—a shape inharmonious with the environment, complicating the riddle. It was merely a native *bonne* in a commonplace cap and print-dress. She spoke neither French nor English, and I gained no intelligence from her dialect. Yet she bathed my temples and forehead with cool, perfumed water, raised the cushion beneath me, signaled that I should not speak, and resumed her post at the foot of the sofa.

Busy knitting, her eyes drawn away from me, I gazed upon her uninterrupted. I wondered how she had come there, what she could possibly do among the scenes or days of my girlhood. Even more, I marveled what those scenes and days could now have to do with me.

Too weak to scrutinize the mystery, I tried to dismiss it as a mistake, a dream, a fever-fit. Yet I knew there could be no mistake, that I was not sleeping, and that I believed I was sane. I wished the room were less brightly lit, so I need not see so clearly the little pictures, the ornaments, the screens, the worked chair. All these objects, as well as the blue-damask furniture, were precisely the same, in every minutest detail, with those I so well remembered in the drawing-room of my godmother's house at Bretton. It seemed only the apartment itself had changed, differing in proportions and dimensions.

I thought of Bedreddin Hassan, transported in his sleep from Cairo to Damascus. Had a Genius stooped his dark wing down the storm to whose stress I had succumbed, gathering me from the church-steps, "rising high into the air," bearing me over land and ocean, and laid me quietly down beside a hearth of Old England? But no; I knew the fire of that hearth burned before its *Lares* no more—it had gone out long ago, and the household gods had been carried elsewhere.

The *bonne* turned again to survey me. Seeing my eyes wide open, she presumed them perturbed and put down her knitting. I saw her busy at a little stand, pouring out water and measuring drops from a phial. Glass in hand, she approached me. What dark-tinged draught might she offer? What Genii-elixir or Magi-distillation?

It was too late to inquire. I had swallowed it passively, and at once. A tide of quiet thought gently caressed my brain; softer and softer rose the flow, with tepid undulations smoother than balm. The pain of weakness left my limbs; my muscles slept. I lost power to move, but losing the wish simultaneously, it was no privation. The kind *bonne* placed a screen between me and the lamp. I saw her rise to do this, but do not remember seeing her resume her place. In the interval between the two acts, I "fell on sleep."

At waking, lo! all was changed again. High daylight surrounded me—not a warm, summer light, but the leaden gloom of raw, blustering autumn. I felt sure now that I was in the pensionnat, sure by the beating rain on the casement, sure by the "wuther" of wind amongst trees denoting a garden outside, sure by the chill, the whiteness, and the solitude amidst which I lay. I say _whiteness_—for the dimity curtains, dropped before a French bed, bounded my view.

I lifted them and looked out. My eye, prepared to take in the range of a long, large, whitewashed chamber, blinked baffled, encountering the limited area of a small cabinet. It was a cabinet with sea-green walls. Instead of five wide, naked windows, there was one high lattice shaded with muslin festoons. Instead of two dozen little stands of painted wood, each holding a basin and ewer, there was a toilette-table dressed, like a lady for a ball, in a white robe over a pink skirt, crowned by a polished, large glass and a pretty pin-cushion frilled with lace. This toilette, together with a small, low, green and white chintz arm-chair, a washstand topped with a marble slab, and supplied with utensils of pale-green ware, sufficiently furnished the tiny chamber.

Reader, I felt alarmed! Why? What was there in this simple, somewhat pretty sleeping-closet to startle the most timid? Merely this: These articles of furniture could not be real, solid arm-chairs, looking-glasses, and washstands—they must be ghosts of such articles. Or, if this were denied as too wild a hypothesis—and, confounded as I was, I _did_ deny it—there remained but to conclude that I had passed into an abnormal state of mind; in short, that I was very ill and delirious.
