To you, fortunate dwellers of the Third Dimension, I designate our realm "Flatland." This nomenclature serves not as a local custom, but as a necessary tool to clarify the nature of existence here for minds accustomed to the boundless depth of Space.

Conceive, if you will, an immense plane—a two-dimensional expanse where straight lines, triangles, squares, pentagons, hexagons, and myriad other geometric forms wander freely. Yet, unlike shadows cast upon a screen, these entities possess substance; however, they are utterly incapable of rising above or sinking below the surface. In doing so, you shall grasp the essence of my nation and its citizens. A few years past, I might have termed this entire construct "my universe," but my understanding has since expanded to embrace higher, more profound truths.

Within such a realm, the concept of "solidity"—that three-dimensional bulk you take for granted—is physically impossible. One might reasonably assume, despite this limitation, that we could visually differentiate between a triangle, a square, or a pentagon as they moved across the plain. Such an assumption, however, is fatal to the logic of our vision. To us, no distinct shapes exist save for straight lines. Why? Let me prove this through a simple experiment.

Take a coin and place it squarely in the center of a table in Space. Lean forward and gaze down upon it. To your eyes, suspended above the surface, it appears a perfect circle. Now, draw backward until your eye rests near the table's edge, gradually lowering your perspective to mimic the state of a Flatlander. Observe the transformation: the coin shifts from circular to increasingly oblong, eventually stretching into an ellipse. But the moment your eye aligns perfectly with the table's surface—truly inhabiting the two-dimensional plane—the illusion shatters. The coin ceases to be an oval entirely; it resolves into nothing more than a single, rigid straight line.

Apply this same principle to a triangle, a square, or any polygon cut from pasteboard. As soon as your viewpoint flattens against the surface, the figure vanishes. It loses all angular identity and becomes indistinguishable from a mere line. Consider, for instance, an equilateral triangle, which among us denotes a tradesman of the respectable standing. Finally, when the eye lies flush with the surface—as is the case for every soul in Flatland—all that remains is a straight line.

A parallel phenomenon occurs in Spaceland for your seafaring explorers. When traversing the oceans, distant islands or coasts often lie upon the horizon. Though those lands may harbor countless bays, forelands, and intricate indentations, to the distant sailor they appear merely as a grey, unbroken strip against the water. Only when illuminated sharply by your sun, casting shadows that reveal projection and recession, do details emerge. Without such relief, distance reduces complexity to a linear silhouette.

Precisely this optical constraint governs our interactions within Flatland. There is no sun here, nor any light capable of generating shadow to betray depth. When a companion approaches, whether they be a triangle, a square, a pentagon, or a circle, their form does not expand in volume or shape. They simply grow larger, their perimeter extending outward, yet they remain, to the naked eye, nothing more than a straight line. Conversely, as they recede, they shrink, retaining their linear appearance. To us, a Circle and a Triangle, when viewed face-to-face, are identical in visual form: both are lines.

You may rightly wonder how, under these severe visual disadvantages, we manage to distinguish friend from foe or neighbor from stranger. The mechanism by which we achieve this discrimination is complex and deeply tied to the physiology of our people. I shall defer the explanation of this vital subject until I describe the inhabitants of Flatland in greater detail. For now, allow me to turn briefly to the climate and architecture of our world.
