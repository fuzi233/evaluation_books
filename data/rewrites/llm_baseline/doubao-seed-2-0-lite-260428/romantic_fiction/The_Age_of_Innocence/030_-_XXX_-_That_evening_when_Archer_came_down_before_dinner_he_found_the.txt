The gas lamps in their brownstone drawing room glowed soft and amber over empty velvet armchairs the evening Archer drifted down before dinner, the whole house wrapped in the quiet hush that had settled over their household since Mrs. Manson Mingott fell ill. Every family social engagement had been put on hold for weeks, so he and May were dining alone. He knew May was somewhere overhead, he’d heard the faint rustle of her skirts through the floorboards as he dressed, and since she was always the punctual one, her absence struck him as odd. Lately he’d taken to clinging to these tiny, trivial little puzzles of domestic life as a lifeline, something to tie his whirling, half-dreaming thoughts firmly back to the real world. He understood his father-in-law now, the strange, endless absorption Mr. Welland had for sorting stamps or polishing ivory cane handles; long ago, he suspected, even the mild Mr. Welland had had his own escapes, his own visions of roads not taken, and had built a whole army of small, boring, familiar domestic rituals to defend himself from them.

When May finally stepped into the room, he thought she looked almost wan. She was wearing the tight-laced, low-necked dinner dress even the most informal Mingott gatherings required, her fair hair coiled high in its usual neat stacked braids, but her face looked faded, drained of its usual glow. Still, she turned that soft, tender smile on him, her bright blue eyes still holding that same luminous warmth he’d fallen in love with years before.
“Where did you vanish to, dear?” she asked. “I was waiting for you at Granny’s, and Ellen turned up alone, said she’d dropped you on the walk home because you had to rush off for urgent business. Nothing’s wrong, is there?”
“Only a handful of letters I’d forgotten, needed to send them out before the post went at dinner time,” he said, fumbling the easy lie.
“Ah,” she said, quiet, a beat later. “I wish you could have stopped by Granny’s, that is, if the letters weren’t too pressing.”
“They were,” he said, surprised by her quiet insistence. “Besides, I had no idea you were going over there this afternoon.”

She turned to the gilded mantel mirror, lifting her long arm to tuck a stray lock of hair back into its intricate coiling, and Archer was struck by how languid, how almost lifeless her movement felt, as if the slow, unchanging monotony of their days had seeped all the bounce right out of her. Then he remembered: that morning, as he’d left the house, she’d called down the stairs that she would meet him at her grandmother’s so they could drive home together. He’d called back a cheery yes, and then gotten so wrapped up in thoughts of Ellen he’d forgotten the promise entirely. Guilt pricked at him, but irritation nipped right at its heels. They’d been married nearly two years now, and he was tired of living in this tepid, endless half-honeymoon—no fire, no passion, but all the quiet unspoken expectations of a newlywed pair, no room for any deviation from the script. If May would just say out loud whatever small grievances she was tucking away, he could laugh them off, but she’d been raised to tuck every imagined slight, every quiet hurt, behind that Spartan, perfect social smile.

He asked after Mrs. Mingott instead, to distract himself, and May said the old woman was steadily recovering, but she’d been very upset by the latest news about the Beauforts.
“What news?” Archer asked.
“They’re not leaving New York at all. I hear he’s taken some kind of insurance job, and they’re hunting for a tiny little house to rent somewhere on the edge of the city.”

There was no point even commenting on how absurd the whole situation was, and they drifted into the dining room where the silver candelabra glowed over the white linen. Their conversation stayed within all their usual narrow, safe boundaries, but Archer noticed May never once mentioned Madame Olenska, never alluded to Ellen being at Granny’s that afternoon, and he was half grateful, half uneasy, like there was a silent secret hanging between the two of them that neither would ever dare name.

They retired to the library for coffee after dinner, Archer lit a cigar and pulled a volume of Michelet down from the shelf. He’d gotten into history lately, ever since he noticed May would always ask him to read aloud if she caught him with a book of poetry. It wasn’t that he disliked the sound of his own voice, but her soft, earnest, surface-level little comments would strip all the warmth and romance right out of any verse he tried to read. Back in their engagement days, he realized, she’d just echoed every opinion he gave her; now that he’d stopped feeding her lines to parrot, she was making up her own safe, unchallenging thoughts, and they killed every bit of joy he might have gotten out of a book.

Seeing he’d chosen history, she dragged her workbasket over, pulled an armchair right up to the green-shaded student lamp, and pulled out the half-finished embroidered cushion she was stitching for his sofa. She was no great needlewoman—her big, capable hands were made for riding horses, rowing boats, spending hours out in the fresh air—but every other wife he knew stitched cushions for their husbands, and she didn’t want to leave out even that tiny little gesture of devotion. She sat bent over the work frame, her ruffled elbow sleeves slipping down to show her firm, round arms, her sapphire engagement ring glinting over the thick wide gold of her wedding band as she slowly, carefully stabbed her needle through the canvas. The lamplight fell full on her clear, unlined brow, and a cold quiet dread settled in Archer’s chest: he would never be surprised by her, not in all the decades ahead. No sudden new idea, no unplanned wild emotion, no messy unkindness or unexpected vulnerability. She’d spent every last scrap of her romance and poetry on their short courtship, and now she was just slowly, steadily ripening into an exact copy of her mother, and by some quiet invisible magic, she was trying to turn him into a copy of mild, contented Mr. Welland too.

He stood up so fast he nearly knocked over his cigar ashtray. “What’s wrong?” May asked, lifting her head at once.
“The room is stifling. I need a little air.”

He’d insisted the library curtains run on a sliding rod, not nailed permanently to a gilt cornice and looped up under layers of lace the way they were in the drawing room, so he wrenched them both back and pushed the sash all the way up, leaning out into the sharp, icy November night. He could see the glow of a hundred other gas lamps from the windows of the brownstones stretching out in every direction, the dark slant of roofs and chimneys, and for a second he could almost feel all the other lives unfolding outside their four walls, other cities, other worlds far beyond the tight little bubble of New York high society he’d been trapped in. His head cleared instantly, and he could finally breathe.

After a few minutes he heard May’s soft voice behind him. “Newland! Do shut that window. You’ll catch your death.”

He pulled the sash down and turned back, and the words almost slipped out of his mouth: I already have caught my death. I’ve been dead for months. A wild, terrible thought flashed across his mind, sharp and bright: what if it was her who was dead? Young, healthy women her age died all the time, didn’t they? If she died tomorrow, he would be free, no chains, no unspoken promises, no endless soft obligations. The thought was so overwhelming, so fiercely fascinating, that he didn’t even register how monstrous it was at first. It was just a new possibility his starved, sick soul could cling to.
May glanced up, and her eyes went wide when she saw the wild, distant look on his face. “Newland! Are you ill?”

He shook his head and stumbled back to his armchair. As he passed her, he laid a light hand on her soft hair. “Poor May,” he said.
“Poor? Why poor?” she laughed, the sound tight and strained.
“Because I’ll never be able to open a window without you worrying yourself sick over me,” he said, laughing back.

She was quiet for a long moment, head bowed over her stitching. “I won’t ever worry,” she said very low, “as long as you’re happy.”
“Ah, my dear,” he sighed, “and I’ll never be happy unless I can open the windows when I want to.”
“In this weather?” she chided softly, and he buried his head back in his history book with a sigh.

Six or seven days drifted by. Archer heard not a single word from Ellen Olenska, and he noticed no one in the family ever spoke her name in front of him, as if she’d been deliberately erased from all their conversations. He didn’t try to seek her out; it would have been impossible, with her camped out at Mrs. Mingott’s guarded sick bedside, every servant and relative keeping watch. Somewhere deep under all his drifting, half-formed thoughts, that quiet resolve he’d made leaning out the library window stayed firm, and it made waiting easy, no need to rush.

Then May came to him one afternoon, bright and pleased, to say Mrs. Manson Mingott had asked to see him. The request was no surprise, the old woman was recovering steadily, and she’d always openly said he was her favorite out of all her grandsons-in-law. May gave him the news with obvious pride, happy that the old matriarch thought so highly of her husband.
“Shall I come with you this afternoon?” Archer offered, automatically.
May’s face lit up, but she shook her head at once. “Oh no, you’d much better go alone. Granny gets bored seeing the same people back to back, it tires her out.”

His heart was hammering so hard he could hear it in his ears when he rang the bell at the Mingott house. This was exactly what he’d wanted: an afternoon alone at the house, no one else there, a chance to steal a minute alone with Ellen. All he wanted to ask her was the date of her return to Washington, and once he had that answer he would know exactly what to do next. But when he stepped into the yellow damask parlor, it was only the mulatto maid waiting for him, her white teeth gleaming, who pushed open the sliding doors and ushered him straight into Mrs. Mingott’s bedchamber.

The old woman was propped up in a vast, throne-like armchair next to her bed, no books or workbasket anywhere in reach—conversation had always been her only hobby, and she’d never bothered feigning interest in needlework. There was barely a trace left of the slight distortion her stroke had left, only a paler face, darker shadows tucked into the folds of her multiple chins. She held out one of her tiny soft hands, nestled in the wide curve of her lap like a pampered little pet, and ordered the maid not to let anyone else in. If any of her daughters called, they were to tell her she was asleep.
“Am I perfectly hideous, at my age?” she asked gaily, patting at the muslin kerchief crossed over her voluminous purple dressing gown.
“My dear, you’re handsomer than you’ve ever been,” Archer laughed back.
She threw her head back and cackled. “Ah, but not as handsome as Ellen, eh?” she winked at him, mischievous. “Was she so very pretty the day you drove her up from the ferry that she had to kick you out of the carriage halfway home? Back in my day, young men didn’t abandon pretty women unless they were forced to!” She chuckled, then added, half querulous, “It’s a pity you didn’t marry her, you know. I always told her so. It would have spared me all this endless family drama. But when did children ever bother to spare their grandmothers worry?”

Archer wondered if her illness had blurred her wits, but then she leaned forward, bright and sharp as ever. “Well, it’s all settled now, no matter what the rest of the family says. Ellen’s staying here with me. She hadn’t been in the house five minutes before I would have crawled on my knees to keep her, if I could just still see where the floor was under all these chins.” She told him how the whole family had talked her over before Ellen arrived, convinced her to cut off her granddaughter’s allowance, pressure her to go back to her cruel husband Count Olenski, even when Olenski’s lawyer came over with very generous settlement offers. “Marriage is marriage, money is money, both are useful things,” she said, “but the second I laid eyes on my sweet bird again, I said no. I’m not shutting her back in that cage. She’s staying here to nurse her old granny as long as I’m around, and I’ve already told Letterblair to give her every cent of her proper allowance, no strings attached.”

Archer’s veins were thrumming so hard he could barely think, confused if the news was joy or a sharp new ache. But slowly, a warm, giddy relief spread through him. Ellen had chosen to stay, not to run back to Washington, not to run back to Olenski. She must have realized it was impossible to give him up. The old woman said the family was going to fight them, claim that a sick weak old woman had been manipulated by Ellen, and she couldn’t fight all of them one by one. So Archer would have to do it for her.
“Me?” he stammered.
“Of course you,” she said, her round eyes suddenly sharp as penknives, her tiny pale nails clutching at his wrist like bird claws. “You’re Letterblair’s new partner. You’re the only one in this whole family who never ranted on about how it was Ellen’s duty to go back to her husband. I knew I could count on you.”

He reassured her he would handle it, and the old lady sighed, settling back into her cushions, satisfied. Then Archer asked when he would finally get to see Ellen.
Mrs. Mingott cackled, her eyes crinkling shut. “Not today. One visitor at a time, thank you. Ellen’s out. She took my carriage to go call on Regina Beaufort.” She paused to let the news sink in. “Walked straight up to me the day after she got here, put on her best bonnet, and told me cool as you please she was going to see the wife of that scoundrel Beaufort. I told her as much, and she looked right back at me and said ‘Well, I’m married to a scoundrel too, and all the family wants me to go back to him.’ Floored me, that line. And I let her have the carriage, even when I looked out the window and saw it wasn’t raining a drop. I’ve always admired courage more than anything else, and both those women have plenty of it.”

Archer bent down and pressed a kiss to the tiny soft hand still resting on his.
“Eh—eh—eh!” the old lady cackled, mocking, “Whose hand do you think you’re kissing there, young man? I hope it’s not my granddaughter’s behind my back!” He was nearly out the door when she called after him, grinning, “Give Ellen her granny’s love, but don’t you dare tell her a single word about our little conversation.”
