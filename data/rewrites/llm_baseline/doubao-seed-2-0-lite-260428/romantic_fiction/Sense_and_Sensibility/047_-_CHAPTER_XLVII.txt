Mrs. Dashwood could not listen unmoved as Elinor laid out the full, halting vindication of Willoughby, the man who had once seemed so bright and dear to their little Barton cottage circle. She pressed a hand to her heart, relieved to know he was not entirely the unrepentant monster they had presumed for a time, sorry for the ruin his own foolish choices had wrought, and sincerely wished him some small measure of happiness. But the magic of those early days at Allenham, the perfect version of him Marianne had once loved, could never be brought back. No apology, no explanation, could wipe away the pain Marianne had suffered at his hands, nor the unforgivable harm he had done to the helpless Eliza Williams. There was no path that could restore him to the family’s good graces, nor any chance he might ever stand between Marianne and the quiet, steady affection of Colonel Brandon.

Had Mrs. Dashwood heard Willoughby’s plea in person, as Elinor had, had she witnessed his haggard distress and the raw desperation in his voice, her compassion might well have overridden all her good sense. But Elinor had no wish to stir that soft, misplaced pity in her mother now. Time and quiet reflection had steadied her own judgment, tempered the fleeting rush of sympathy she had felt for him in his London study, and she had chosen only to share the unvarnished truth, no soft embellishments of tenderness to twist anyone’s view of his real character.

That evening, the three of them sat huddled around the cottage parlour fire, the October rain tapping soft against the leaded windows, when Marianne spoke of Willoughby of her own accord. The effort cost her: she had been twisting the fringe of her wool shawl, lost in restless, unspoken thought for nearly an hour before she spoke, her cheeks flushing bright pink, her voice trembling so thin it was almost lost in the crackle of the flames.
“I want you both to know,” she said, lifting her eyes to theirs, “that I see everything, exactly as you have all been hoping I would.”
Mrs. Dashwood would have burst in with soft, soothing endearments at once, but Elinor, who ached to hear her sister’s unfiltered, honest thoughts, shot her a quick, eager glance to beg her for silence.
Marianne continued slowly. “What Elinor told me this morning is such a relief. I have heard every last detail I have been desperate to know.” For a long moment her voice broke, and she had to press a handkerchief to her eyes before she could go on, calmer now than she had been in weeks. “I am perfectly content with how things are. I would never have been happy with him, not once I learned the truth—one way or another, I would have found out. I would never have trusted him, never esteemed him. Nothing in the world could have made that right for me.”
“I know it, my love!” Mrs. Dashwood cried, leaning forward. “Happy with a man who lives so recklessly, who broke the heart of one of the kindest, most honourable men who ever lived? No. My Marianne’s soft, tender conscience would never have let her rest easy, even if he had never done you wrong.”
Marianne sighed, a long, quiet breath, and repeated, “I would not change a single thing that has happened now.”
“You are seeing this exactly as any good, sound heart must see it,” Elinor said gently. “I am sure you know, as well as I do, that a marriage to him would have brought nothing but steady, certain disappointment. His affection for you was never half as steady as yours for him. You would have lived in constant, pressing poverty—he admits himself he is wildly reckless with money, that the very concept of self-denial is foreign to him. On that tiny, insufficient income you would have shared, his endless demands and your total inexperience with frugality would have brought distresses you never once could have imagined. You, with your strict sense of honour and fairness, would have worked to pinch every penny for his sake, skimping on your own books, your own walks, your own small comforts, and he would never have noticed. If you had ever dared to try to cut back on his amusements, his hunting trips, his fancy dinners, do you not think he would have grown resentful of you, come to see you as a burden that had spoiled his easy, happy life?”
Marianne’s lips trembled. “Selfish?” she whispered, the word half broken, as if she still could barely bring herself to believe it of the man who had once recited poetry to her beneath the Barton trees.
“Every part of this affair, from start to finish, was rooted in his selfishness,” Elinor said softly. “It was selfishness that made him toy with your heart when he had no intention of marrying you. It was selfishness that made him hide his engagement to Lucy, selfishness that ran him off from Barton the moment he risked losing his fortune. His own ease, his own pleasure, that was always the first thing he cared about.”
“It is true,” Marianne said, a tear rolling down her cheek. “My happiness was never his priority.”
“He regrets what he has done now,” Elinor went on. “But not for you. Not for the pain he caused. He regrets it because it did not work out for him. He is free of debt now, no financial troubles hounding him, and all he can think is that his wife Lucy has a far less gentle temper than yours. But do you really think if he had married you, he would be happy? No. The troubles would just be different. He would be the one buried under debt, yearning for the large estate and easy income he gave up to marry you, and before long he would be resenting you for robbing him of it.”
“I have no doubt of that,” Marianne said, shaking her head. “I have nothing to grieve for but my own foolishness.”
“No, no, that is my fault, my dear,” Mrs. Dashwood cut in quickly. “I was the foolish one, to let you two grow so close so fast, to never think to warn you.”
Marianne protested, and Elinor, glad they had both acknowledged their own small missteps without lingering on the pain of the past, steered the conversation gently forward again. “There is one lesson we can draw from all this, is there not? Every bit of Willoughby’s ruin traces back to that first cruel choice he made, to hurt Eliza Williams. That first crime spawned every other bad choice, every bit of unhappiness he suffers now.”
Marianne nodded, her throat tight, and Mrs. Dashwood launched into a warm, lengthy speech about Colonel Brandon’s many kindnesses, his noble heart, all he had done for Eliza and for their family, but Marianne hardly seemed to hear a word of it, lost in her own quiet thoughts.

For the next two or three days, Elinor noticed Marianne was not regaining her strength as quickly as she had before. But her sister never faltered in her resolve to be cheerful, to sit with them at meals, to pick up her sketchbook now and then, and Elinor trusted that time would heal the last of the hurt. When Margaret came home from her visit, the whole family was finally gathered safe again in the little cottage. They did not throw themselves back into their old studies and walks with the same wild energy they had when they first moved to Barton, but they made soft, gentle plans for all the things they would do, come spring.

All the while, Elinor waited impatiently for news of Edward. She had not heard a single word from him since they left London, no hint of his plans, not even confirmation of where he was staying. A few letters had passed between her and her brother John while Marianne was ill, and in the very first one, he had written, “We know nothing of unfortunate Edward, and dare not make enquiries after a subject so forbidden, but we presume he is still at Oxford.” That was all the news she got—John never even mentioned Edward’s name in any of his later letters.

But she did not have to wait long for answers. One morning, their man-servant Thomas was sent into Exeter on errands. When he waited on them at dinner, and had answered all his mistress’s questions about how his errand had gone, he added a piece of news entirely of his own accord.
“I suppose you have heard, ma’am, that Mr. Ferrars is married?”

Marianne jolted so hard her teacup clattered against its saucer. She stared at Elinor, saw her turn deathly pale, and then promptly fainted back in her chair, wracked with hysterics. Mrs. Dashwood, who had been glancing at Elinor at the exact moment Thomas spoke, was horror-struck to see how deep her daughter’s distress ran. A heartbeat later, she was equally distraught over Marianne, and had no idea which of her two girls to tend to first.

Thomas, who only saw that Marianne had fallen ill, had the good sense to run and fetch the parlour maid. Between them, they half-carried Marianne into the next bedroom. By the time they laid her on the bed, she was already coming to a little. Mrs. Dashwood left her in Margaret and the maid’s care, and hurried back to Elinor, who was still shaking badly, but had recovered enough of her wits to start asking Thomas exactly where he had heard that news. Mrs. Dashwood took the burden of asking all the questions on herself, so Elinor would not have to exert herself.
“Where did you see Mr. Ferrars, Thomas?”
“I saw him myself in Exeter this morning, ma’am,” Thomas said. “And his wife too—what used to be Miss Steele. Their chaise was stopped right outside the New London Inn, as I went in with a message from Sally at the Park to her brother, who’s one of the post boys. I tipped my hat as I passed the chaise, and she spotted me right off, called me over, asked after all of you—especially Miss Marianne. She bid me give you all and Mr. Ferrars’s best compliments, said they were so sorry they didn’t have time to stop by on their way, but they’re in a great hurry to travel further west for a little while. They promised they’d come call the very second they’re back this way.”
“Did she tell you she was married?” Mrs. Dashwood asked.
“Of course, ma’am. She smiled right bright and said she’d changed her name since she was last staying in these parts. She’s always such a friendly, pleasant young lady, so I wished her joy, of course.”
“Was Mr. Ferrars in the chaise with her?” Elinor managed to ask, her voice thin.
“Yes, miss. I saw him leaning back against the seat, but he never lifted his head. He never was much of a talkative gentleman, that one.”

Elinor knew exactly why he had hidden his face, why he had not wanted to speak to anyone who knew her.
“Was no one else in the carriage?” Mrs. Dashwood asked.
“Just the two of them, ma’am.”
“Where did they come from, then?”
“Straight from London, Mrs. Ferrars told me.”
“And they’re travelling west?”
“Yes, ma’am, but they won’t be long. They’ll be back before you know it, and they’ll be sure to call.”

Mrs. Dashwood glanced at Elinor, but Elinor knew full well they would never darken their door. Every word of Lucy’s gushing message was exactly the sort of petty, triumphant little gesture Elinor would have expected from her. She leaned over to murmur to her mother that they were almost certainly heading to visit Lucy’s uncle, Mr. Pratt, near Plymouth.

Thomas had no more news to share. Elinor longed to hear some small detail that would tell her Edward was not utterly miserable, but there was nothing more. He said the horses were being harnessed when he left, that Lucy looked very well, very pleased with herself, and that was all. Soon enough, Thomas was dismissed, and Marianne sent word that she did not want any dinner. Mrs. Dashwood and Elinor had no appetite at all, either. Only Margaret, who had never yet been forced to go without a meal in all the weeks of her sisters’ shared grief, counted herself lucky.

When the dessert and wine were set out, and the two of them were left alone by the fire, they sat in long, heavy silence, both lost to their own thoughts. Mrs. Dashwood dared not say a word, dared not offer empty comfort to the daughter who had hidden her pain so bravely for months. She saw now that Elinor had deliberately softened every detail of her attachment to Edward, had played down how deep her feelings ran, all to spare her the extra grief at a time when she had been so wrapped up in tending to Marianne’s suffering. She had been so blinded by Marianne’s loud, unmissable heartbreak that she had almost entirely forgotten that Elinor was hurting just as badly, had carried that pain for months with no one to share it, all for the sake of the people she loved. She reached across the table, laced her fingers through Elinor’s cold, trembling hand, and for a long time, neither of them said a word.
