After leaving Bretton—just a few weeks after Paulina’s departure, unaware that I would never return to its quiet streets or walk again through its familiar lanes—I made my way home, having been away for six months. One might suppose that I was overjoyed to be back among family and the comfort of familiar surroundings. And perhaps that assumption does no harm. Let it stand unchallenged. After all, who is to say what lies beneath the surface? Still, I must confess: though I could pretend contentment, I did not feel it. The years that followed were not ones of peace, but of quiet endurance—like a ship anchored in still waters, lulled into silence by calm skies and gentle breezes. Yet even such serenity can hide a storm beneath the surface.

I had imagined myself drifting through life like a vessel at rest, sails furled, drifting on a glassy sea. But fate has a way of stirring the depths. Though I longed for stillness, I was destined for upheaval. I remember now—vividly—the cold, the terror, the struggle. In dreams, I still taste salt on my lips, feel the icy grip of waves pressing against my chest. There was a storm—not one night, nor one day, but many. Days without sun or stars. We threw ropes overboard with our own hands. The wind howled like a wounded beast. Hope vanished. The ship went down. The crew perished. And yet, I said nothing. To whom could I speak? Mrs. Bretton was gone from my life—cut off by circumstances beyond my control. Her fortune, once substantial, had dwindled; her son Graham had taken up a profession, and both mother and son had moved to London. No one remained to turn to. I was alone.

And so, when Miss Marchmont sent for me, I went—not with joy, but with resignation. She lived near us, a woman of means, but crippled by rheumatism for twenty years. She had not walked in decades. Her world was confined to a single room, upstairs, where her drawing-room adjoined her bedroom. I had heard whispers about her—her eccentricities, her sharp tongue, her solitary nature—but I had never met her until now.

She was old, lined by time and pain, her hair silvered, her eyes deep-set and watchful. There was a gravity in her presence, born not just of age, but of long suffering. She sat stiffly in her chair, wrapped in shawls, her hands clenched tightly as if holding onto something unseen. When I entered, she looked at me with a gaze that seemed to pierce through my mourning dress, my tired face, my hollow eyes. She knew I had lost much.

Her companion—a woman who had cared for her for years—was soon to marry. Miss Marchmont had heard of my loneliness, my displacement, and decided I might take her place. After tea, we sat together by the fire. She spoke plainly.

“It will not be an easy life,” she said. “I require constant attention. You will be confined. But compared to what you’ve known lately, it may seem bearable.”

I thought of it. It should have seemed bearable. But it didn’t. The idea of spending my youth in this close room, watching her suffer, enduring her moods—while the world outside continued, full of color and motion—filled me with a quiet dread. My heart sank. Then rose again. For though I feared the burden, I refused to imagine it worse than it was. I wasn’t given to fantasy. I saw things as they were.

“I doubt I’ll have the strength,” I said softly.

“That’s your concern,” she replied. “You look worn out.”

She was right. In the mirror, I saw a ghost—pale, thin, dressed in black, a woman who had already begun to fade. But inside, I felt something still alive. Not hope, perhaps, but a stubborn pulse of existence. I believed the blight was only skin-deep.

“What else do you plan?” she asked.

“Nothing clear. But perhaps something will come.”

“Then try your way first. If it fails, mine is open to you—for three months.”

That kindness surprised me. I thanked her. As I spoke, a spasm seized her. I moved quickly, applying compresses, doing exactly as she instructed. By the time she calmed, a strange closeness had formed between us. I saw in her endurance a strength I admired. She saw in my care a quiet devotion. She called for me again the next day—and then again, and again.

We grew acquainted. Her temper flared sometimes—sharp, sudden, like lightning—but never cruel. It was more like a mother scolding a child than a mistress reprimanding a servant. And beneath the anger, there was reason. Even in fury, she was logical.

Gradually, I began to understand her. Her sternness masked a depth of feeling. Her solitude had shaped her, but not broken her. I found myself drawn to her—not because I loved her, but because she needed me, and I, in my own way, needed to be needed.

One evening, she spoke of the past. Her voice softened, and for the first time, I glimpsed the woman she had once been—young, passionate, full of dreams. She told me of Frank—her lover, her soul’s companion, the man she had expected to marry on Christmas Eve thirty years before.

“I waited,” she said, her eyes distant. “The snow fell gently. The moon was bright. I saw him ride up the path—then… something wrong. A horse, trembling. A man holding it. I ran out. On the lawn lay a body—dragged, bruised, breathing faintly. It was Frank. He was dying.”

She described it with chilling clarity. How she carried him in. How he whispered, “Maria, I am dying in Paradise.” How he died in her arms at dawn, on Christmas morning.

“And that,” she said, “was thirty years ago. I have lived since in sorrow. I am not good. I am not kind. But I have loved—truly loved—once. And that love, though gone, remains my deepest truth.”

She paused. Then turned to me.

“Lucy,” she said, “what do you think? Can I be forgiven for loving him so much, so long? Have I failed God?”

I could not answer. Words failed me.

She smiled faintly. “Very well. We must accept what we cannot change. We must strive to bring joy to others. Tomorrow, I shall begin by trying to make *you* happy. I will do something for you—something lasting.”

She closed her eyes. Her head ached from speaking too much. “Go to bed. It’s two o’clock. You’ve stayed late. I’ve kept you awake selfishly. But go now. I shall sleep well.”

I left her, retiring to my small closet within her room. The night passed quietly. In the morning, she was found still—peaceful, calm, her face serene. No pain. No struggle. Only silence.

Her sudden cheerfulness, her renewed spirit, had been the sign. A final surge of life before the end. The stroke came swiftly, cutting short a life long burdened by suffering.

And so, Miss Marchmont passed. Not in grief, but in quiet release. And I, who had come seeking refuge, found myself standing at the edge of a new chapter—one I had not chosen, but which now claimed me.
