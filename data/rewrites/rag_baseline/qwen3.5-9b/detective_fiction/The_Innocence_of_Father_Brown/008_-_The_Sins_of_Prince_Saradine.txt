"No room. Full up."

The door slammed with the dull, decisive thud of bureaucratic finality.

I stood in the freezing drizzle of the Hammersmith night, calculating the precise rate at which a man's core temperature drops when he has been walking for fourteen hours on a deficit of three thousand calories. To have scoured the grime-choked streets of London all day for labor was an expected hardship; to have stooped to petition a casual ward for a night on a plank bed was a tactical compromise. To be denied even that miserable sanctuary was merely a lesson in the unreliability of institutional safety nets.

As I mentally mapped the grim reality of my situation, a figure detached itself from the shadows of the brick wall.

" Won't let you in, eh?"

I turned to assess the man. His shoulders were hunched against the damp, his hands buried deep in the pockets of ruined trousers. He was a veteran of the gutters, worn down to the sinew, reeking of wet wool and cheap gin.

"He claims it's at capacity," I replied, my voice steady despite the shivering that had begun to seize my chest.

The man offered a jagged, cynical grin. "That's the standard deterrent at Fulham. They always say it's full. Keeps the ledger clean and the workload light."

I studied him. "You're suggesting it's a calculated falsehood? An administrative bluff to filter the weak?"

"I'm saying the bloke's having a laugh at your expense. If there's square footage, they're legally bound to take you. If I were you, I'd ring the bell again and make him do his job."

There was a certain dark logic to testing the defenses twice. I approached the heavy door and yanked the iron bell-pull. The door swung open almost immediately, revealing the same grizzled pauper who acted as gatekeeper. If he had been the Prime Minister himself, he could not have worn a heavier mantle of unearned authority.

"Here again?" he sneered. "What's the tactical objective here? Think I've got nothing better to do than wait on the likes of you?"

Flambeau took his monthly leave from Westminster not in a grand estate, but aboard a vessel so diminutive it spent more time rowing than sailing. He navigated the narrow arteries of the Eastern counties, where the craft resembled a toy drifting across fields of corn and meadow. Designed for two souls, the boat carried only the absolute essentials according to Flambeau's pragmatic philosophy: tinned salmon for sustenance, loaded revolvers for conflict, a flask of brandy for collapse, and a priest for the inevitable end. With this minimal loadout, he drifted down the Norfolk waterways, drawn toward the Broads, yet finding equal pleasure in the overhanging gardens, the reflection of manor houses in the still water, and the quiet act of fishing in secluded pools.

Like a true philosopher, Flambeau possessed no rigid itinerary, yet he maintained a half-purpose—a goal serious enough to validate the journey, yet light enough that its failure would not ruin the escape. Decades prior, during his reign as a notorious thief in Paris, he had received countless missives of admiration, condemnation, and infatuation. One, however, remained etched in his memory: a visiting card bearing an English postmark. Inscribed in French with green ink on the reverse, it read: *"If you ever retire and become respectable, come and see me. I want to meet you, for I have met all the other great men of my time. That trick of yours of getting one detective to arrest the other was the most splendid scene in French history."* The front bore the formal engraving: *Prince Saradine, Reed House, Reed Island, Norfolk.*

At the time, Flambeau had dismissed the prince as a footnote—a brilliant, fashionable figure from Southern Italy who had scandalized society by eloping with a married noblewoman, an act compounded by the alleged suicide of the cuckolded husband in Sicily. Later, the prince vanished into Vienna and a life of restless wandering. Yet, upon settling in England, the coincidence struck Flambeau: why not pay a surprise call to this eminent exile in the forgotten marshes of Norfolk? He had no idea if the location existed anymore, given how small and obscure it had become, but fate proved efficient.

One night, they moored the boat beneath a bank draped in high grasses and pollarded trees. Exhaustion from heavy sculling brought sleep quickly, and they awoke to a pre-dawn hour illuminated by a large, setting lemon moon. The sky burned with a vivid violet-blue, casting the tall weeds into the shape of a dense forest. Standing amidst the gloom, the flora seemed magnified; daisies loomed like giants, dandelions like towering sentries, evoking the whimsical wallpaper of a nursery. The riverbed dropped sharply, forcing them to look upward at the canopy of grass.

"By Jove!" Flambeau exclaimed. "It's like being in fairyland."

Father Brown sat bolt upright, crossing himself with sudden vigor. His companion stared mildly, asking what had disturbed him.

"The authors of medieval ballads," the priest corrected, "understood fairies far better than you. It isn't only pleasant things that occur in fairyland."

"Oh, bosh!" Flambeau scoffed. "Under such an innocent moon, only good things can happen. I intend to press on and see what truly awaits us. We may die and rot before witnessing such a moon or such a mood again."

"All right," Father Brown conceded. "I never claimed it was wrong to enter fairyland. Only that it is invariably dangerous."

They propelled the boat slowly upstream as the violet sky and golden moonlight faded into the colorless void preceding dawn. Soon, the horizon fractured with streaks of red, gold, and grey, interrupted by the black silhouette of a town sitting on the riverbank. As the mist lifted into working daylight, they passed under the hanging roofs and bridges of a riverside hamlet. The houses, with their long, stooping lines, appeared to lean down to drink from the river, resembling massive grey and red cattle. Before spotting a single living soul on the wharves, they encountered a prosperous man in his shirt sleeves, his face round and smooth like a newly set moon, framed by a halo of red whiskers. Leaning against a post above the sluggish tide, he smiled expansively at their shout regarding Reed Island and simply pointed upstream toward the next bend.

Flambeau moved ahead without further comment. The boat navigated numerous grassy bends and silent, reedy stretches until they rounded a particularly sharp angle to reveal a pool of unnatural stillness. Dominating the center of this wider expanse, fringed by rushes, lay a long, low islet. Upon it stood a bungalow constructed of bamboo or similar tropical cane. The vertical rods forming the walls were pale yellow, while the sloping roof rods were a darker reddish-brown. The structure was a study in repetitive monotony, its interior echoing with the wind as the early morning breeze rustled the surrounding reeds, singing through the ribbed walls like a giant panpipe.

"By George!" Flambeau cried. "Here it is, finally! Reed Island, if such a place exists. Reed House, wherever it may be. I suspect that fat man with the whiskers was a fairy."

"Perhaps," Father Brown noted neutrally. "If he was, he was a malevolent one."

Before the priest could elaborate, Flambeau had already driven the boat onto the rattling reeds. They stepped onto the long, quaint islet beside the silent house. The building faced away from the river, its rear aligned with the sole landing stage, while the main entrance overlooked the long garden stretching down the island. Approaching via a narrow path circling nearly three sides of the structure, they peered through three separate windows on different facades, all opening into the same long, brightly lit room. Panelled in light wood and lined with numerous mirrors, the space was arranged for an elegant luncheon.

Reaching the front door, flanked by two turquoise-blue flowerpots, they were greeted by a butler of the dreariest variety—tall, lean, grey, and listless. He murmured that Prince Saradine was currently absent but expected within the hour, noting the house was prepared for him and his guests. Seeing the visiting card with its green scrawl, the butler's parchment-like face flickered with renewed animation. With a shaky courtesy, he suggested they wait. "His Highness may arrive any moment," he warned, "and would be distressed to have missed a guest he had invited. We have orders to keep a cold lunch ready, and I am certain he would wish it served."

Curiosity overriding caution, Flambeau agreed gracefully and followed the elderly servant into the long, lightly panelled room. Nothing remarkable marked the space save for the alternating rhythm of long, low windows and equally long, low rectangular mirrors, creating an illusion of weightlessness, as if dining outdoors. A few quiet portraits hung in the corners: a large grey photograph of a young man in uniform, and a red chalk sketch of two lanky boys. When Flambeau asked if the soldier was the prince, the butler curtly denied it, identifying the subject as the prince's younger brother, Captain Stephen Saradine. Afterward, the old man seemed to drain of all conversational ability.
