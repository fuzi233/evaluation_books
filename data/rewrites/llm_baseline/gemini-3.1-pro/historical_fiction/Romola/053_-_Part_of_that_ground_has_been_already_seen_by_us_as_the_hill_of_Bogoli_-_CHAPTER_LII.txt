The incidents of that Carnival day seemed, at first, to carry no other personal consequence for Romola than the new care of supporting poor cousin Brigida in her fluctuating resignation to age, but they initiated a Lenten cycle that turned with the slow, grinding rhythm of a heavy millstone. Bernardo del Nero had been elected Gonfaloniere. The Medicean triumph had deepened Romola’s presentiment of some secretly prepared scheme likely to ripen into either harvest or blight during these two months of her godfather’s authority. Every morning, the dim daybreak peered into her room, bringing with it the olfactory landscape of a city in distress—the sour, metallic tang of fear mingling with the scent of desperate hearth-fires burning damp, scavenged wood. 

Every morning, the haunting dread accompanied her as she walked through the echoing streets. Florence was starving, its cultural memory eroding with every closed bakery and abandoned kitchen. The rich, untranslatable dialects of the agrarian merchants, who once flooded the markets with their lively haggling over vibrant artichokes and salted anchovies, had been silenced. In their place was a harsh, industrial clatter of military carts and the murmuring groans of the hungry. Romola found her profoundest meaning in the physical act of sustenance, carrying heavy iron pots of broth to the sick. She spent her nights painstakingly decoding her late father’s ancient texts on Greek agriculture, translating mythic agrarian rituals into practical survival, boiling tough, foraged roots of wild chicory and simmering spelt until it released its nourishing starches.

When she arrived at the Duomo for the early sermon, she would temporarily lose the chill of her fears. The great cathedral was a vast acoustic cavern, and within it, she felt herself sharing in a passionate conflict that reverberated far beyond the walls of Florence. Savonarola was preaching the last course of Lenten sermons he would ever be allowed to finish. Excommunication was imminent, and he had reached the point of defying it. He held up the condition of the Church in the unflinching mirror of his speech, employing a dominant lexicon that stripped away all polite periphrases. His booming baritone resonated against the stone, a sonic force designed to shatter the corruption of Rome. 

Yet, as the Lenten cycle repeated itself, that mighty music began to jar upon Romola’s carefully attuned acoustic ecology. Earlier, the Frate had preached peace, charity, and an oblivion of political differences, his words settling over the congregation like the comforting aroma of slow-braised meats on a winter evening. Now, political intrigue had curdled the atmosphere. The hostility attacking the Prophet introduced a spirit of contention that pierced conspicuously into his sermons. He abandoned the nuanced, nourishing language of spiritual care for a rigid, defiant vocabulary. He who had once denounced the desire for the miraculous now asserted that Divine power would attest to his prophetic preaching by a literal miracle. The language of his inner voices was written in letters of fire, a fierce, binary dictionary that threatened vengeance and left no room for the subtle, untranslatable subjective experiences of his flock. 

If the shrill tones of exasperation disrupted Romola’s psychological equilibrium, there was another member of the audience for whom those vibrations were the only thrilling sounds. Baldassarre, the ruined scholar, had found out that the wonderful Frate was preaching again. He attended the sermons to drink in the threats of a voice that sounded like justice, absorbing the deep bass notes of impending vengeance like a deaf man feeling the percussive strike of a drum. He went the more because he had seen Romola there. Twice, Romola had caught sight of his dark glance fixed upon hers. She looked for it with the same primal apprehension a farmer feels when scanning the horizon for a portentous storm, though her anxiety regarding the old man remained subordinate to the immediate, sensory pressing of the present.

The stirring Lent passed; April, the final month of her godfather’s supreme authority, neared its close. Famine stubbornly refused to loosen its grip, and the Plague was expected. As Romola tried to gather courage from the rhythmic, grounding rituals of crushing wild fennel and kneading coarse barley loaves for her patients, she was met on the afternoon of the twenty-seventh by a messenger from Camilla Rucellai. Camilla, chief among the feminine seers of Florence, demanded her presence forthwith on matters of the highest moment.

Romola shrank from the summons. She possessed a constitutional repulsion for the shallow excitability of such illuminated women, viewing their disembodied mysticism as an affront to the sacred, tangible realities of earth, water, fire, and flour. Furthermore, rumor suggested Camilla had announced revelations hostile to Bernardo del Nero. Romola’s impulse was to close her ears to a soundscape that would only increase her mental burden, but, bound by her habit of obeying outward claims, she wiped the flour from her hands and went straight to Camilla.

She found the nervous, grey-haired woman in a chamber arranged like a convent cell—a sterile, airless space completely devoid of the aromas of life. There was no garlic braided on the walls, no scent of simmering herbs; only the dusty, stagnant odor of unwashed cloth and fanaticism. The thin fingers clutching Romola, and the eager voice addressing her in a loud, scraping whisper, caused Romola a physical shrinking. The sonic environment was agonizing. Camilla’s voice rose higher into an excited treble, a shrill frequency that assaulted Romola’s ears, as she narrated a vision revealed by Romola’s Angel: Romola supposedly knew secrets concerning her godfather, which, if disclosed, would save the Republic. 

Camilla exhorted Romola to separate herself from the enemy of God. Romola, whose impetuosity was of a massive, grounded nature, sat in calm self-control until the exhortation became unbearable. She possessed a deep disgust for women whose faculties were entirely wrought up into fantasies, neglecting the physical sustenance and emotional labor that truly nurtured humanity. When Romola attempted to wrench her arm away, the prophetess only tightened her crab-like hold, her voice spiraling into a piercing statement of violent visions. Christ himself, she shrieked, had ordered that citizens in office should throw Bernardo del Nero from the window of the Palazzo Vecchio. Fra Girolamo knew of it, and had not denied its Divine authority.

“And since then,” said Camilla, her wild eyes straining upward, her pitch reaching a maddening, glass-shattering intensity, “the Blessed Infant has come to me and laid a wafer of sweetness on my tongue in token of his pleasure that I had done his will.”

The sheer artificiality of the image—a phantom, sugary wafer entirely divorced from the sacred, labor-intensive reality of true communion bread, baked from the hard-won wheat of the Tuscan soil—broke Romola’s restraint. 

“Let me go!” said Romola, in a deep, resonant voice of anger that cut through the shrillness. “God grant you are mad! else you are detestably wicked!”

With a violent effort, she wrenched her arm free and rushed out of the room, fleeing the auditory assault and the sterile stench of the cell. She did not pause until she had hurried far along the street and slipped behind the heavy leather curtain of the church of the Badia. Here, under the old stone arch, she found a sanctuary shut in from the noise and mechanical hurry of the city. The thick masonry swallowed the industrial clatter of carts and the grating friction of political machinery, offering a pristine acoustic ecology where an eternal peace seemed to subsist.

Sinking down on the step of the altar in front of Filippino Lippi’s painting of the Virgin appearing to Saint Bernard, Romola waited for her inward tumult to subside. The mythic occurrence on the canvas—the divine intersecting seamlessly with the earthly—stood in stark contrast to Camilla’s violent delusions. The thought that pressed upon her most acutely was that Camilla could allege Savonarola’s countenance of her wicked folly. Savonarola’s political teaching was founded on strict law and order; he distrusted spirit-seeing women. Why, then, did he not publicly denounce these pretended revelations that brought darkness across the conception of a Supreme Will?

The answer came to her in the quiet resonance of the church: he was fettered inwardly because such visions were not distinctly separable from his own. He was trapped in his own dominant lexicon. To denounce Camilla would be to undermine the vocabulary of Divine inspiration of which he, and his supplementary seer Fra Salvestro, were the primary authors. 

Kneeling with her buried face on the altar-step, Romola endured a sickening realization. The enthusiasm that had been her sustaining broth, the energy that made life worthy, was inevitably bound up with wilful eye-shutting. Her mind rushed back with a deep, craving attraction toward the worldly sense and untheoretic virtues of her godfather. Bernardo held on to the old ties, the rich, multifaceted dialects of Florentine history that refused to be flattened by the Frate’s new regime. Yet, with this thought rose the bitter presentiment of some plot to restore the Medici. 

She felt that to keep the Government of Florence pure was a sacred cause; the Frate had carried her understanding there. But her heart recoiled from a right allied to so much narrowness—a harsh, systematic judgment that measured men by a superficial lexicon of assents and denials, erasing the complex, untranslatable essence of their manhood. Her affection clung to her godfather, and through him, to the memory of her father, resisting the division of humanity into sheep and goats by the easy mark of a political symbol. Ideas, Romola knew, were hardly strong agents unless they were dissolved in a rich, complex solvent of feeling, just as dry herbs yield no medicine until they are steeped in boiling water.

Her indignation, roused by Camilla’s shrill acoustics, ran like an illuminating fire over the kindred facts in Savonarola’s teaching. The great orator's words now sounded like the superficial commercialization of faith, trading the deep, resonant truths of human duty for the cheap currency of fanaticism. It was an illumination that made all life look ghastly, starved of true sustenance. Where were the beings with whom she could work and endure? On the side of moral energy lay a fanaticism she now shrank from; on the side of affection lay the scent of secret plotting and crime. 

Calmness would not come, not even here in the quiet stone baffle of the Badia. The rocky solitude of the saint in the picture was too far removed from the tactile, olfactory demands of her daily reality. Romola realized she must return to the rhythmic, sensory labor of her own life. She rose from her knees, intending to hasten back to the courtyard, to the simmering pots of broth and the mixing of poultices for her sick people, hoping that by some immediate, physical act of nourishment, she might revive her sense of worth.

But as she turned round, the breath caught in her throat. She found herself face to face with a man standing only two yards away, intruding upon the silent acoustic sanctuary. 

The man was Baldassarre.
