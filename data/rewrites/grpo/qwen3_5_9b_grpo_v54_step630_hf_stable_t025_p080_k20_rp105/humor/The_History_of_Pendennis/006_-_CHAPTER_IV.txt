Mrs. Haller

Having retreated to the George, Mr. Foker and his guest sat down to a repast of such grandeur that the coffee-room itself seemed to lean in for a closer look. Mr. Rincer entered with the first dish, bowing with the solemn gravity of a Lord-Lieutenant serving royalty. Mr. Foker attacked the turtle and venison with a gusto that belied his previous feasts on ginger-beer and smuggled polonies. Pen, witnessing this, could not help but respect his connoisseurship, especially when Foker pronounced the champagne "condemned gooseberry" and winked knowingly at the port. The latter, Foker declared, was of the right sort; he knew the waiters by their Christian names, cared deeply for their families, and greeted the arriving London coaches with theatrical flair. Flinging the window open, he called out to the guards and coachmen, imitating the tooting of horns with such lively accuracy that the ostler, Jem, seemed to whip the horses' cloths off in sympathy as the carriages drove gaily away.

"A bottle of sherry, a bottle of sham, a bottle of port and a shass caffy, it ain't so bad, hay, Pen?" Foker asked, declaring it time to "toddle" after a mountain of nuts and fruit. Pen sprang up with bright, flushed eyes, and they moved toward the theatre, paying their money to a wheezy old lady slumbering in the box office. "Mrs. Dropsicum, Bingley's mother-in-law, great in Lady Macbeth," Foker noted, knowing her well.

They secured seats in the boxes, a sparse affair despite the playbill's promises of "galvanic thrills." A score of people dotted the pit, a few kicked and whistled in the galleries, and a dozen free-admission guests sat nearby. Lieutenants Rodgers and Podgers, along with young Cornet Tidmus of the Dragoons, occupied a private box, conversing with the players and applauding them by name.

The manager, Bingley, assumed all chief roles except when London stars descended upon Chatteris. Dressed in tight pantaloons, Hessian boots, a large cloak, and a drooping hearse feather that barely concealed his buckled brown wig, he played the 'Stranger.' He adorned himself with stage jewelry, selecting the largest, shiniest rings, allowing his little finger to quiver with a sham diamond that twiddled in the faces of the pit. Bingley treated this ring as a family heirloom, passed down from George Frederick Cooke via Mr. Quin, and delighted the young men of his company with its history.

Reading from a stage-book rouged and tawdry like the heroes it held, Bingley pointed passages ominously, lifting his eyes to the ceiling as if deriving intense consolation from the text's affinity with heaven. When the Stranger spotted the young men, he acted directly at them, calculating the effect of every ornament to fascinate his paying victims. His servant, Francis, remarked on his master's gloom, noting that nature held no beauty for him and that he hadn't smiled in three years.

"He is but a child in the leading-string of hope," Bingley observed, looking steadily at Foker, who remained unconcernedly sucking the top of his stick. "And her cradle—is the grave," Bingley added with a moan of a bassoon in agony, fixing his eyes so steadily on Pendennis that the poor lad felt the whole house staring at him. Throughout the scene, Bingley played at him, marking pages carefully to continue his perusal off-stage, determined to subjugate Pendennis. When the scene ended, Foker cried "Bravo, Bingley!" and the good-natured young gentleman, laughing Pendennis, and the Dragoons began clapping hands to their utmost ability.

As the play shifted to Wintersen Castle, servants bustled about. "That's Hicks and Miss Thackthwaite," Foker whispered. "Pretty girl, ain't she, Pendennis? But stop—hurray—bravo! Here's the Fotheringay."

The pit thrilled, umbrellas thumped, and a volley of applause erupted from the gallery. The Dragoon officers and Foker clapped furiously. Amidst the red face and ragged whiskers of Mr. Costigan peering from the side-scene, Pen's eyes opened wide. Mrs. Haller entered with a downcast look, then rallied at the applause, sweeping the house with a grateful glance before sinking into a magnificent curtsey. More applause, more umbrellas; Pen, flaming with wine and enthusiasm, clapped and sang "bravo" louder than all. Mrs. Haller saw him, and so did old Mr. Bows, the little first fiddler, who looked up from his desk with a crutch beside him and smiled at the lad's enthusiasm.

Those who have only seen Miss Fotheringay in later London life cannot imagine her beauty at that time. She was tall, twenty-six though she vowed she was nineteen, in the prime of her existence. Her vast forehead was crowned with black hair waving in natural ripples, confined in shining braids at the back of a neck reminiscent of the Louvre Venus. Her eyes, when lifted, shone with tenderness and mystery; Love and Genius seemed to peer out before retiring coyly. She never laughed, for her teeth were not good, but a smile of endless sweetness played around her lips and dimples. Her nose defied description, and her ears resembled little pearl shells, which the finest earrings only insulted. Dressed in long flowing black robes, she moved with wonderful grace, revealing only her large sandals occasionally, which Pen found ravishing.

But it was her hand and arm that this magnificent creature most excelled in. They surrounded her. Whether folded over her bosom in resignation, dropped in mute agony, or raised in superb command, they beckoned, repelled, entreated, and embraced the whole house. Thus she stood, complete and beautiful, as Pen stared. "I say, Pen, isn't she a stunner?" Foker asked.

"Hush!" Pen whispered, "she's speaking."

In a deep, sweet voice, Mrs. Haller began. The play of the 'Stranger' was notoriously stupid, filled with absurdly fine words that no idiot would use in real life. Yet, amidst the balderdash, ran a reality of love, children, and forgiveness that set the world sympathizing. With smothered sorrow and gushing pathos, she delivered her part. First, giving orders with the calm agony of despair, she then overflowed to the pit and house, crying out her griefs as if to confidants. When she reached the favorite passage about her William and his little sisters, little Bows buried his face in his blue cotton handkerchief after crying "Bravo."

All the house was affected. Foker wept piteously with a large yellow bandanna. Pen, however, was gone too far for tears. He followed the woman about the stage; when she left, the house and lights reeled wildly before his sight. Watching her at the side-scene, where her father removed her shawl, he saw only Fotheringay, Fotheringay, when the reconciliation arrived and she flung herself onto Mr. Bingley's shoulders. The curtain fell upon him like a pall. He did not hear Bingley announce the next play, nor did the manager comprehend that anyone else but himself had caused the success. Finally understanding, Bingley stepped back with a grin and appeared with Mrs. Haller on his arm. How beautiful she looked! Her hair had fallen down, officers threw her flowers, and she clutched them to her heart. Her eyes met Pen's. Down went the curtain again, and she was gone. Not one note of the overture could he hear.

"She is a crusher, ain't she now!" Foker asked.

Pen answered vaguely, unable to speak to any mortal. He felt an overwhelming, maddening, delicious fever of wild joy and undefined longing. When Rowkins and Miss Thackthwaite danced the double hornpipe, Foker abandoned himself to the ballet, but Pen cared little, remembering only that the woman had acted with him in the scene where she first came in. It was a mist before his eyes. At the end of the dance, he checked his watch and said it was time to go.

"Stay to see The Bravo of the Battle-Axe," Foker urged. "Bingley's splendid in it... It's great fun, do stop."

Pen glanced at the bill, hoping Miss Fotheringay's name might be hidden in the after-piece list, but there was no such name. He must go. He had a long ride home. Squeezing Foker's hand, he choked to speak but couldn't. He quitted the theatre, walked frantically about the town, then mounted at the George and rode homewards. As he came into the yard at Fairoaks, the Clavering clock sang out one. The lady of the house might have been awake, but she only heard him from the passage as he dashed into bed and pulled the clothes over his head.

Pen, unused to wakeful nights, fell instantly into a sound sleep. Even in later days, a man from practice or fatigue begins by sleeping as usual, getting a nap in advance of anxiety. But she soon comes up with him, jogs his shoulder, and says, "Come, my man, no more of this laziness, you must wake up and have a talk with me." Then they fall together in the midnight. Well, whatever might afterwards happen to him, poor little Pen was not come to this state yet; he tumbled into a sound sleep, not waking until an early hour when the rooks began to caw from the wood beyond his bedroom windows.

At that very instant, as his eyes started open, the beloved image was in his mind. "My dear boy," he heard her say, "you were in a sound sleep and I would not disturb you: but I have been close by your pillow all this while: and I don't intend that you shall leave me. I am Love! I bring with me fever and passion: wild longing, maddening desire; restless craving and seeking. Many a long day ere this I heard you calling out for me; and behold now I am come."

Was Pen frightened? Not he. It was all wild pleasure and delight. Just as three years previously, on entering the fifth form at the Cistercians, his father had given him a gold watch which the boy examined on waking, rubbing and polishing it in private to listen to its ticking, so the young man exulted over his new delight. He felt in his waistcoat pocket to ensure it was safe, wound it up at nights, and at the first moment of waking hugged and looked at it. By the way, that first watch of Pen's was a showy, ill-manufactured piece that never went well and was eventually swapped for a more useful time-keeper.

Pen felt himself many years older since yesterday. There was no mistake; he was as much in love as the best hero in the best romance he had ever read. He told John to bring his shaving water with utmost confidence. He dressed in some of his finest clothes and came splendidly down to breakfast, patronizing his mother and little Laura, who had been strumming her music lesson for hours. After he read the prayers—which he did not heed a single syllable of—she wondered at his grand appearance and asked him to tell her what the play was about.

Pen laughed and declined. It was quite as well that she should not know. Then she asked why he had put on his fine pin and beautiful new waistcoat. Pen blushed and told his mother that the old schoolfellow with whom he had dined at Chatteris was reading with a tutor at Baymouth, a very learned man. Since he was himself to go to College, and several young men pursued their studies there, he was anxious to ride over and see what the course of their reading was.

Laura made a long face. Helen Pendennis looked hard at her son, troubled more than ever with the vague doubt and terror haunting her since the last night when Farmer Gurnett brought news that Pen would not return home to dinner. Arthur's eyes defied her. She tried to console herself, driving off her fears. The boy had never told her an untruth. Pen conducted himself during breakfast in a very haughty and supercilious manner. Taking leave of the elder and younger lady, he was presently heard riding out of the stablecourt. He went gently at first, but galloped like a madman as soon as he thought he was out of hearing.

Smirke, thinking of his own affairs and softly riding with his toes out to give Pen his three hours' reading at Fairoaks, met his pupil, who shot by him like the wind. Smirke's pony shied as the other thundered past; the gentle curate went over his head among the stinging-nettles in the hedge. Pen laughed as they met, pointed toward the Baymouth road, and was gone half a mile before poor Smirke had picked himself up.

Pen had resolved to see Foker that morning; he must hear about her, know about her, be with somebody who knew her. Honest Smirke, sitting up among the stinging-nettles as his pony cropped quietly in the hedge, thought dismally to himself whether he ought to go to Fairoaks now that his pupil was evidently gone away for the day. Yes, he thought he might go, too. He might ask Mrs. Pendennis when Arthur would be back and hear Miss Laura recite Watts's Catechism. He got up on the little pony—both were used to his slipping off—and advanced upon the house from which his scholar had just rushed away in a whirlwind.

Thus love makes fools of all of us, big and little; and the curate had tumbled over head and heels in pursuit of it, and Pen had started in the first heat of the mad race.
