I moved forward, my eyes fixed ahead, refusing to look back. My heart was steady, though the weight of what lay before me pressed like a stone—either salvation for the Maid in the dark, or death itself, if fate so willed. I had no choice but to go on.

And so I walked, every step echoing with the memory of parting from the great Pyramid, my home, the last refuge of mankind. The warmth of that place—the care, the wisdom beneath its rigid laws—lingered in my soul. And I carried with me the pride of knowing the Master Monstruwacan had come down in full armor to stand beside the Full Watch, to see me off, to strengthen me in spirit. That honor meant more than words could say.

As I walked, the night air clung to me—cold, sharp, and thick as though it bore more substance than the breath of the plateau where I’d lived. It stung my lips, filled my lungs with a strange clarity. I knew now why those who lived lower down breathed easier; the air here, deep in the Fields, was different—thicker, more alive. Each city had its own atmosphere, shaped by height and distance from the dying sun. Some peoples migrated seeking health, guided by science and decree. But not all were healthy. Some thrived in what others called poison.

And yet, even this small difference—the taste of the air—spoke of a world far greater than we ever dreamed. Beneath the silence of the Night Land, there was life, movement, hidden currents in the ether. I felt them—like whispers carried on the wind—echoes of the millions I left behind, their thoughts and hopes swirling around me like unseen hands.

For a time, I let myself be lost in memory. The vastness of the Pyramid, the watchful towers, the glassed embrasures through which we once peered at the monstrous shapes beyond—those images flooded back. As a child, we’d take turns watching, hoping to be the first to spot a Beast-Monster leaning over the glowing Circle, its face half-lost in shadow. We’d cheer when one appeared, then shiver as it vanished into the darkness, repelled by the light.

Now, standing just beyond the Circle, I saw it again—smaller than I expected. A thin tube of light, barely two inches wide, yet blazing bright enough to seem enormous from afar. It was the barrier between us and the outer world. And now I stepped across it.

I turned back once, only once, to look upon the Pyramid. And what I saw took my breath away.

It rose like a mountain forged from steel and fire, stretching up into the endless night. Its sides were covered in rows upon rows of embrasures—three hundred thousand on the nearest slope, twelve hundred thousand in total. Lights flickered from each, climbing upward in a spiral of brilliance until they blurred into a single, shimmering peak that pierced the heavens. From below, the people looked like insects clustered against the glow. I knew they watched me—through their spy-glasses, their hearts beating with fear and hope.

And then I saw it—the tiny star at the very top, the Observation Tower. That was where the Master Monstrudes had stood, where I had spent my days scanning the void. He was there now, looking down. I raised my Diskos—a salute, a farewell—though I could not see him.

A murmur rose from the lower tiers. A wave of voices, waves of hands. They were sending me off. Calling for me to return. Or perhaps, only wishing me well.

But I could not linger. I lowered the Diskos, reversed it—symbol of respect from a young man to the millions—and stepped fully into the Night Land.

The air resisted me, thick and heavy, like swimming through cold syrup. I pushed forward, not looking back. To gaze upon the Pyramid again would break my resolve.

Yet even as I walked, I felt the presence of those I’d left behind—not just in memory, but in the ether. Their thoughts clung to me, prayers, wishes, silent vigilance. It was comforting, almost like companionship. But then came the fear: such a disturbance might draw attention. Might alert something ancient and terrible that lurked in the dark.

Could I stop it? No. Even if I had warned them, they could not have ceased thinking. The mind of a million is a storm—one that cannot be silenced.

At first, I walked blindly, driven only by the need to put distance between myself and the Pyramid. But soon, I slowed. I began to think.

I could not follow the path of the Youths—they were watched, hunted. So I changed course. Instead of heading due north, I veered northwest, planning to circle around the North-West Watcher, then cut toward the Plain of Blue Fire. From there, I would aim true north, avoiding the House of Silence—the place that chilled me more than any beast.

It was longer, yes. But better to walk slowly and live, than rush toward death.

Why did I choose north? Not just instinct. I had studied. In the Great Library, I found an old metal book, forgotten for millennia. Its pages spoke of a time long before the Sun dimmed—a cataclysmic earthquake that split the world open, swallowing oceans, creating a chasm so deep no eye could see its bottom. Steam burst upward, shaking the continents. Rain fell for days. The sky turned red.

This was not myth. Ancient records confirmed it. But this book—this one—was different. It didn’t speak in legends. It quoted witnesses. It described the aftermath: a valley formed by the rift, stretching a thousand miles, with walls a hundred miles high. At its base, seas and forests, mountains and fires, beasts both ancient and new.

And in that valley, a people emerged—hardened, fierce, born of necessity. They called themselves the Road Makers. They climbed down the cliffs, generation after generation, building paths, cities, forging a way through the darkness. They fought monsters, mastered the heat, the steam, the poisons. They built roads from city to city, winding through the Valley of Shadow, where the Sun never reached.

But the Valley bred horror. Evil forces stirred. The Road Makers fought, won, and retreated to the western end, where the sun still glowed faintly. There, they built cities, grew wise—but also reckless. They experimented with forbidden powers, opened doors they should not have touched. And the world paid the price.

Then came the twilight. The Sun faded. Madness spread. Cities fell. Beasts from the west attacked. Chaos reigned.

And from that chaos rose a leader. He rallied the survivors. He declared: *Build a refuge.*

They built a Great House. But it was not enough. So they wandered, finally reaching the Bight—the curve where the valley turned north. There, they built the Pyramid.

That was the story. And I had read it recently, discussed it with the Master Monstruwacan. We both believed it true.

And if it was true—if the Valley existed—then the Lesser Redoubt must lie within it. Perhaps near the western edge, where the Ab-humans dwelled. Or farther north. But I had no map, no guide. Only a feeling—that it was north.

So I would search northward first. If I found nothing, then I would turn west. But I would not wander blindly. I would follow the logic of the book.

And now, as I walked, I understood something deeper: the cliffs that surrounded us were not just rock. They were the edges of a world-long wound. The Pyramid sat in a crater, not a sea bed. The land rose sharply around us, hidden in darkness. We had always thought we lived in a basin. But the truth was worse—we lived in a tomb.

Most of the people didn’t know. The schools taught fragments, theories, myths. Some laughed. Others believed wildly. Children loved the stories. The wise remained cautious. But none truly grasped the scale of what lay beyond the light.

They accepted the night, the monsters, the fires. They lived in comfort, content with what they saw.

But I was no longer content.

I walked on, into the blackness, carrying the weight of a world’s past, and the hope of a future that might never be.
