The air of Milton Street possessed a peculiar alchemy; it was heavy with the scent of coal dust and wet wool, yet within its narrow confines, it carried a strange, electric vitality that lifted Margaret's spirits. She stepped out of the mill gates with a heaviness in her heart, but as the cobblestones gave way to the bustling thoroughfare, her step lightened, her lips curving upward. Here, the world was not confined to the silent rows of machines and the stoic faces of workers. She observed a tableau of human energy: men with hands buried deep in pockets, striding with a swagger that suggested ownership of the pavement; clusters of girls, their voices rising in raucous laughter, radiating a boisterous independence that felt both thrilling and dangerous. Yet, lurking in the shadows of the beer-houses and gin-pubs were the discreditable minority—men whose faces bore the marks of excess, smoking and casting judgmental glances at every passerby. Margaret recoiled slightly from this underbelly of the city, eager to escape the long walk through these streets and reach the fields she had envisioned, but a sudden impulse redirected her path. She would not seek the solitude of the countryside today; instead, she would find Bessy Higgins. It lacked the serene refreshment of a rural stroll, but it promised a kinship of shared burden.

Inside the Higgins household, the atmosphere was thick with the haze of tobacco smoke. Nicholas Higgins sat by the fire, the ember glowing steadily in his pipe, while Bessy rocked rhythmically on the opposite side of the room, her movements a physical manifestation of her unrest. When Margaret entered, Nicholas extinguished his pipe and stood, pushing his chair aside to lean casually against the chimney-piece. His posture was relaxed, yet his eyes held a steeliness that spoke of suppressed tension.

"How fares the spirit?" Margaret asked gently, turning her attention to Bessy.

"Hoo's rather down in the mouth regarding spirits," Bessy replied, her voice flat, "but hoo's better in health. Hoo doesn't like this strike. Hoo's a deal too much set on peace and quietness at any price."

"This is the third strike I've seen," she continued, letting out a weary sigh that seemed to carry the weight of years.

"Well, third time pays for all," Nicholas declared, his voice dropping to a conspiratorial rumble. "See if we don't damn the masters this time. See if they don't come begging us to return at our own terms. That's the game. We missed our mark before, I grant you; but this time, we've laid our plans desperate deep."

"Why do you strike?" Margaret pressed, her curiosity sharpening. "Striking is simply ceasing work until you secure your desired wage, isn't it? I must confess my ignorance; in my homeland, such a concept was unknown to me."

"I wish I were there," Bessy murmured, her gaze drifting to the window. "But it is not for me to grow sick and tired of strikes. This shall be the final one I witness. Before it concludes, I shall be in the Great City—the Holy Jerusalem."

"Hoo's so consumed with the life to come, hoo cannot comprehend the present," Nicholas countered, resuming his pipe. "Now, you see, I am bound to do the best I can here. A bird in the hand is worth two in the bush. Thus, our differing perspectives on the strike question arise."

"But," Margaret interjected, "if the people struck, as they do in my land among the field laborers, the seeds would rot unsown, the hay remain uncut, and the corn unreaped."

"Well?" Nicholas challenged, puffing a cloud of gray smoke.

"Why," Margaret persisted, "what becomes of the farmers?"

He exhaled slowly. "I reckon they'd either abandon their farms or offer a fair rate of wages."

"Suppose neither option presents itself?" Margaret argued. "They cannot relinquish their lands instantly, regardless of their desire. Without hay or corn to sell that year, where would the capital originate to pay the laborers' wages the following season?"

Still puffing, Nicholas finally spoke. "I know naught of your customs down South. I've heard they are a breed of spiritless, downtrodden men, utterly cowed, too numbed by oppression to recognize when they are being exploited. Here, it is not so. We know precisely when we are put upon; and we possess enough blood in our veins to endure no more. We simply remove our hands from our looms and declare, 'You may cower us, but you shall not oppress us, my masters!' And be damned to them, they shall not succeed this time!"

"I wished I lived down South," Bessy whispered.

"There is much to endure there," Margaret admitted. "Sorrows abound everywhere. There is grueling physical toil performed with scant sustenance to fuel the body."

"But it is outdoors," Bessy insisted. "Away from the endless, deafening noise and the sickening heat of the factories."

"It is sometimes in torrential rain, and sometimes in biting cold," Margaret corrected. "A young person might withstand it, but an old man is ravaged by rheumatism, bent and withered prematurely. Yet, he must continue working, or face the grim alternative of the workhouse."

"I thought you were so enamored with the ways of the Southern country," Nicholas remarked abruptly.

"So I am," Margaret smiled faintly, realizing she had walked into a trap of her own making. "I only mean, Bessy, that there is good and evil in all things. Since you perceive the darkness here, I deemed it fair that you should also understand the shadow cast by the South."

"And you claim they never strike down there?" Nicholas asked sharply.

"No!" Margaret answered firmly. "I believe they possess too much sense."

"And I contend," Nicholas retorted, dashing the ashes from his pipe with such violent force that the stem snapped, "that it is not that they lack sense, but that they lack spirit."

"Oh, father!" Bessy cried out. "What have you gained by striking? Recall that first strike when Mother passed—how we were forced to cease work, you suffering most of all. Yet, countless others returned weekly at the same wage until employment vanished entirely, leaving some destitute for the remainder of their lives."

"Aye," Nicholas conceded. "That strike was poorly managed. Fools and traitors seized control. You shall see; this time shall be different."

"But throughout this discourse, you have not revealed what you are striking for," Margaret demanded again.

"Why, you see," Nicholas explained, his voice gaining intensity, "there are five or six masters who have doubled down on the wages they paid over the past two years, prospering and growing wealthier. Now, they approach us demanding a reduction. We refuse. We shall grind them to a halt first, and observe who remains willing to employ us. They intend to kill the goose that lays the golden eggs, I wager."

"And so you plan to perish in order to exact revenge upon them!" Margaret exclaimed, horrified.

"No," he denied vehemently. "I merely anticipate the possibility of dying at my post sooner than yielding. That is what men call noble and honorable in a soldier, and why not in a poor weaver? But listen closely."

"But," Margaret protested, "a soldier dies for the Nation—for the sake of others."

He laughed, a grim, dry sound. "My lass," he said, "you are but a young woman, yet do you not realize I must sustain three lives—Bessy, Mary, and myself—on sixteen shillings a week? Do you think I strike for my own sake? It is just as much for the cause of others as that soldier you praise. Perhaps the difference lies in the fact that the soldier perishes for strangers he has never met, whereas I champion the cause of John Boucher, who resides next door, burdened by a sickly wife and eight children, none of whom are of factory age. I do not advocate solely for him, though he is a poor soul capable of managing only two looms simultaneously; I fight for the cause of justice. Why should we accept lower wages now than we did two years ago?"

"Do not ask me," Margaret deflected, sensing the danger of further inquiry. "I am profoundly ignorant. Inquire of your masters. Surely they will provide a rationale. It cannot be merely an arbitrary decree issued without cause."

"You are merely a foreigner, and nothing more," Nicholas sneered, his contempt palpable. "What wisdom do you possess? Ask the masters! They would bid us mind our own business while they mind theirs. Our duty is to accept the reduced wage and express gratitude; their business is to drive us down to the brink of starvation to inflate their profits. That is the essence of it."

"But," Margaret insisted, refusing to retreat despite seeing the irritation flare in his eyes, "the state of trade may be such that they are unable to maintain previous remuneration levels."

"State of trade!" Nicholas spat the words. "That is mere humbug concocted by the masters. I was speaking of the rate of wages. The masters monopolize the state of trade; they wield it like a specter, a black bug-a-boo, to frighten naughty children into submission. Let me enlighten you: it is their role, their cue, to crush us and swell their fortunes; it is ours to rise and fight hard—not solely for ourselves, but for those surrounding us, for justice and fair play.
