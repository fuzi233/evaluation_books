For the present, it seemed, my lodging was to be within the royal pyramid itself.

The glittering procession drew up in the paved square before that enormous pile of stone. Groups broke and re-formed like schools of bright fish; gold flashed; the skin of the mammoth steamed under its harness; the air rang with trumpets. A stair was brought; the canopy of snakes above our golden half-castle shivered in the light; and we three who had ridden beneath it descended into the heat of the open day.

I was passing from sky into stone, from sun into the cool entrails of the palace. Without thinking—because habit and reverence had been kneaded into me since childhood like salt into dough—I turned toward the Sacred Mountain, where the towering rocks frowned above the city, and I made the customary obeisance. I touched my forehead to the ground, and in silence I offered the prescribed prayer of thanks for the good air our Lord the Sun permitted us to breathe before we entered a dwelling.

When I rose, I could have sworn I heard a titter—thin, tinkling laughter somewhere among the fanciful crowd.

I glared toward the scoffers, ready to demand their punishment. Then I turned to Phorenice, expecting to see her rising too from the same reverence.

She stood upright. She had never bent.

She was watching me with a look I could not name—amusement, perhaps; perhaps something sharper under it, as when a cook tastes a sauce and decides whether to sweeten or scorch.

Whatever she thought, she had no mind to shout it in the square. “Come,” she said. She faced the doorway and gave the secret word appointed for the day.

The ponderous stone blocks that barred the porch swung back on their hinges. Phorenice passed from the glare into the pyramid’s cool gloom, her fan-girl following decorously. With a heaviness beginning at my heart like over-fermented bread, I followed. The stone doors shut behind us with a sullen thud, and the outdoor sound—hoof, trumpet, chatter—was clipped off as cleanly as a thread.

We did not go far. Phorenice halted in the hall of waiting.

I knew the place well: red walls painted with kings; the bronze jet in the center that breathed burning earth-breath and gave light. Twenty years earlier the old King—now gone—had brought me thus far when he bade me farewell for my vice-royalty in Yucatan. Then the air had been pure, almost sweet, as though the stone itself had been washed and aired in sun. Now it lay heavy with perfume, thick and languid, an oppressive sweetness that made my lungs work as if I were breathing through cloth steeped in syrup.

“My minister,” said the Empress, “I acquit you of intentional insult; but I think the colonial air has made you very simple. Such an obeisance as you made to that mountain has not been made since I was sent to reign over this kingdom.”

“Your Majesty,” I said, “I am of the Priests’ Clan. I was reared in their tenets. Before entering a house I have been taught to thank the Gods—and more especially our Lord the Sun—for the good air He and They provide. More than once I have been chased by streams of fire and stinking vapours among the mountains when they boil suddenly, and so I can say the prescribed prayer straight from my heart.”

“Circumstances have changed since you left Atlantis,” Phorenice said, and her voice carried easily in that high chamber. “When thanks are given now, they are not thrown at those old Gods.”

I understood. The meaning struck like sourness in milk: unmistakable, immediate.

If this was now the rule, I would have no hand in it. I could serve a reigning monarch faithfully. But to make sacrilege of my faith, to accept a swineherd’s daughter as a God to receive prayers and obeisance, revolted every part of me that had ever knelt in discipline before the Sun.

So I chose the crisis.

“Phorenice,” I said, “I have been a priest from childhood, revering the Gods and growing intimate with their mysteries. Until I find for myself those old things are false, I must stand by that allegiance. If there is a cost for faithfulness, I must pay it.”

She looked at me with a slow smile. “You are a strong man, Deucalion.”

I bowed.

“I have heard others as stubborn,” she said. “They were converted.” She shook out the ruddy bunches of her hair and shifted so the glow of the earth-breath struck full upon her face and form. In that light she was almost unearthly—beauty sharpened into a weapon. “I have found it as easy to convert the stubborn as to burn them. Indeed, there has been little talk of burning. They have all rushed to conversion, whether I would or no. But it seems my poor looks and tongue are wanting in charm to-day.”

“Phorenice is Empress,” I said stolidly, “and I am her servant. To-morrow, if she gives me leave, I will clear away this rabble clamouring outside the walls. I must begin to prove my uses.”

“I am told you are a pretty fighter,” she said. “Well, I hold some small skill in arms myself, and have a conceit that I am something of a judge. To-morrow we will take a taste of battle together. But to-day I must carry through the honourable reception I have planned for you. The feast will be set soon, and you will wish to make ready for it. Chambers have been chosen for your use, and stored with what is needful. Ylga will show you.”

We waited until Phorenice had passed out of the circle of the light-jet and vanished through a doorway in the far shadow. Then the girl took up a lamp and led me into the narrow mazes of the pyramid.

Everywhere perfume lay in the air—oily sweetness, resin, crushed blossoms, something musky beneath as if the stone sweated it. The passages turned and twisted and doubled in cunning fashion through the solid mass, as though the builders had planned not merely to shelter kings but to confuse murder. A stranger could have wandered hours—days—before finding the chamber he desired. There was craft in those forgotten hands. And men say the king of that time, to make doubly sure, killed all who had built it, or had even learned the lay of its inner stones.

But the fan-girl went as one bred to it. She doubled here, turned there, stopped at a blank wall and pressed a stone that swung inward. Once she set her foot to the corner of a flag, and the slab lifted, showing a stair steep and narrow. We descended, crossed the foot of an inclined way that led upward again, and by degrees reached the chamber assigned to me.

“There is raiment in those chests,” she said, “and jewels and gauds in that bronze coffer. They are Phorenice’s first presents—she bade me say—and only a small earnest of what is to come. My Lord Deucalion can drop his simplicity now and fig himself in finery to suit the fashion.”

“Girl,” I said sharply, “be more decorous, and spare me such advice.”

“If my lord calls it rudeness, he can give a word to Phorenice and I shall be whipped,” she retorted, eyes sullen under black brows. “If he asks it, I can be stripped and scourged before him. The Empress will do much for Deucalion just now.”

“You are nearer to that whipping than you think.”

“I have a name,” she snapped. “They call me Ylga. You might have heard it as we rode on the mammoth if you had not been so wrapped up in Phorenice.”

I studied her. “You have never seen me before,” I said, “yet you speak as though you meant to provoke trouble. There is some object in this.”

Instead of answering at once, she pushed the massive stone of the doorway shut. Then she took my garment with her jewelled fingers and drew me away from the airshaft into the farther corner, as though even the moving air might carry words to some hidden ear.

“I am the daughter of Zaemon,” she said, “whom you knew.”

Zaemon. The name fell into me with the weight of an old loaf set on a table—solid, remembered, homely in a way this perfumed stone was not. A man of the Mountain. A friend from before my exile into the colonies.

“You bring me a message from him?”

“How could I? He lives in the priests’ dwellings on the Mountain you obeised. I have not put eyes on him these two years.” Her voice tightened. “But when I saw you step out from that red pavilion they pitched by the harbour, I—I felt pity for you, Deucalion. I remembered you were my father’s friend, and I knew what Phorenice had in store. She has been plotting it these two months.”

“I cannot hear words against the Empress.”

“And yet—”

“What?”

She stamped her sandal on the stone as though to beat sense into it. “You must be blind, Deucalion—or daring. I shall not interfere further; not now. Still, I shall watch. If ever you seem to want a friend, I will try and serve you.”

“I thank you for your friendship.”

“You take it lightly.” A flush rose under her skin. “Even now you do not know my power, any more than you guess my motive. You may be first man in this kingdom, but I rank as second lady. And women stand high in Atlantis now. My friendship is a commodity sought with frequence and industry.”

“And I am grateful,” I said. “You think little of my gratitude, Ylga; but I have never bestowed it on a woman before. Treasure it for its rarity.”

She looked as though she did not know whether to laugh or strike. “My lord, there is an education before you.”

She showed me how to call slaves when I wished for their help and left me.

For a full minute I stood in that chamber, hearing my own words as though another had spoken them. Who was the daughter of Zaemon that she should draw from me a thing I had withheld as carefully as a priest guards a sacred name?

At my bidding the slaves came. They were eager to deck me in a thousand foolishnesses—robes, belts, chains, trinkets—and, in what seemed to be the modern fashion of their class, to press perfumes and unguents upon me. They spoke of scents as though they were sauces: this one “sharp,” that one “round,” this one “warming,” that one “cool,” each to be layered upon the skin in measured touches. But their pressing attentions irritated me. I was already clean and shaved; my hair was trimmed; my robe unsoiled. Their insistence felt less service than impertinence. I set them to beat one another as punishment, promising that if they did not do it thoroughly I would send them to the brander for stripes that would endure.

It is strange, but a common menial can often ruffle one more deeply than a rebellious general.

I had seen many strange sights that day and borne many new sensations, but nothing surprised me so much as the manner by which Phorenice summoned her guests to feast.

I sat waiting in my chamber when, growing out of nothing, a sound touched my ear—first a faint tremor, then increasing by infinitely small steps until it became a clanging din that hurt like a hammer on the bone. Then the whole massive fabric of the pyramid trembled and groaned and shook, as though it were a child’s wooden toy brushed by a strong man’s sandal.

The earthquake-cry.

It was the portent served out yearly by the chiefs of the Priests’ Clan on the Sacred Mountain when they bade all the world take count of sin: the sacred reminder that from roaring fire and monstrous tremors man had been born, and by those same agencies he would be swallowed up—he and the sins within him.

Here the Empress was prostituting that solemnity into a call to gluttony, a signal for ribald laughter and sensuous display.

Yet how had she authority to do it? Who was Phorenice that she should tamper with forces that dwell within the liquid heart of our mother earth? Had there been treachery—some priest who forgot his vow and babbled holy mystery into her ear? Or had she, with her agile brain, discovered a key herself?

If she had found it herself, I could serve her with silent conscience. She might not be of my making, but she was Empress, and obedience was duty. But if she had suborned a weaker member of the Clan, the matter was different. Our constitution demanded that secrets and mysteries be kept inviolate, and that we pursue with undying hatred both the betrayer and the receiver of his confidence.

With these undecided feelings working in me, I obeyed the summons. Slaves led me through the windings to the great banqueting-hall.

The scene was dazzling.

The vast chamber with its marvellous carvings blazed under jets of earth-breath bright as midday. The company filled it—gauds and colours beyond counting, like a living tapestry. They had been shaken by the sacred quake-call, and yet no chastening fell upon them. They recked nothing of death and misery stalking through the city wards without, of rebels beyond the walls, of neglected Gods and their priests on the Mountain. They were gluttonous for the passions of the moment; it was their fashion to look at nothing beyond.

When I stepped onto the pavement, trumpets blared to proclaim my arrival. There was no roar of welcome; only mincing voices and ridiculous bows, ornaments jangling and silks swishing to show themselves. When Phorenice appeared, cries rose and lawful obeisance was made—but even that seemed staged, polished, airless. Heartiness, once a virtue, had come to be counted barbarism.

Two chamberlains—posturing, smirking—took me in charge and ushered me with flimsy golden wands to the dais. I was to sit on Phorenice’s divan and eat from her dish.

“There is no stint to the honour the Empress puts upon me,” I said, kneeling and taking my seat.

She gave me that queer sidelong look. “Deucalion may have more beside, if he asks prettily. He may have what all other men have sighed for, and what none will ever get. But I have given enough of my own accord; he must ask warmly for further favours.”

“I ask first,” I said, “that I may sweep the boundaries clear of the rabble clamouring against the city walls.”

“Pah.” She frowned, as though I had spoken of stale bread at a dessert table. “Have you appetite only for sterner pleasures? They must have been rustic folk in your colony. You shall give me news now of the toothsomeness of this feast.”

Dishes and goblets were set before us. I ate, though I had little appetite for victual so broken and so highly spiced—meat minced fine and drowned in sauces that stung the tongue; sweets heavy with syrups; wines too lush, as though they had been cooked down to a perfume. The smell of it all rose and mingled with the hall’s incense until my senses were cloyed, as if I had been made to dine in a perfumer’s shop.

But if the food did not please me, it pleased them. Men and women alike lingered over each titillation of the palate as though it were a caress from the Gods. They moaned and sighed and praised the cookery with extravagance, turning eating into display. They did not take sustenance as one takes strength for labour; they took it as one takes a drug.

Phorenice watched with quick bright eyes, throwing a word here and there to the groups while she spoke with me. At times she called some favoured creature up to receive a scrap from the royal dish. The honoured one ate it with exaggerated gesture—or, twice, tucked it away into the folds of his clothes as though human lips were too common to profane it.

To me, the flattery was gross. Phorenice accepted it as due. There had to be a weakness in her somewhere, though none showed plainly. Her face was strong and subtle and wondrous comely; the Gods had favoured her in body beyond mortal fairness. The courtiers raved about her, and even I, no lover of such matters, could not deny the groundwork for their admiration.

Yet lovely as she was, my eyes strayed more willingly to Ylga, who by rank sat behind us on the divan as immediate attendant. There was an honesty in her face which Phorenice’s lacked.

The feast rolled on in new absurdities. Some manners touched closely upon disrespect. At the halfway, a gorgeous popinjay—a governor of an out-province driven into the capital by rebellion—lurched forward with flushed face and unsteady gait. He made obeisance before the divan.

“Most astounding Empress,” he cried, “fairest among Goddesses, Queen regnant of my adoring heart, hail!”

Phorenice smiled and stretched him her cup. I expected respectful libation poured out. He drank it all, draining it to the final drop.

“May all your troubles,” he cried, “pass from you as easily, and leave as pleasant a flavour.”

Phorenice turned to me. “You do not like this new habit?”

I said bluntly that pouring liquor at a person’s feet had through custom become respect, but drinking it was mere self-indulgence which could be practised anywhere.

“You still keep to old austere teachings,” she said. “Our newer code bids us enjoy life first, and order other things so as not to meddle with immediate pleasure.”

So the guests pursued gluttony and display beneath the roaring light-jets, while guards stood round the walls motionless and stern as the stone kings carved outside. The contrast—orgy within, discipline without—made the hall feel like a drum: taut hide held by rigid pegs, beaten at the center for noise.

But suddenly the noise changed.

There was a stir at the far doorway; a clash as two guards crossed spears. The man they tried to stop—perhaps to pin—passed unharmed. He walked across the pavement between the lights and the groups of feasters.

All turned to look. A few flung ribald words. None stepped to bar him. Mostly women shuddered as he passed, as though a breath of mountain cold had entered and laid a chill along their skin.

He halted before Phorenice’s divan and stared at her without obeisance.

He was a frail old man. White hair tumbled to his shoulders; a ragged beard hung like torn wool. Mud clotted his feet and legs as though he had come far through rough country and would not pause even to wash. His wizened body was bare save for a single cloth wound about shoulders and loins. In his hand he carried a wand, and at its tip the symbol of our Lord the Sun glowed.

That wand declared his caste. Yet in no other way could I recognize him.

I took him for one of those ascetics of the Priests’ Clan who forswear the nurtured life of the Sacred Mountain and dwell among the burning hills, where the ground splits, rocks fall, fire streams run, evil vapours rise, and monstrous beasts prowl. Such sojourners keep the might of the Holy Gods ever before the mind, and through that constant peril become chastened—and sometimes gain power over mysteries even the learned cannot reach.

Silence filled the hall when he stopped. Phorenice broke it first, her voice clear as a blade.

“Those two guards who held the door are not equal to their work. I cannot have imperfect servants; remove them.”

The soldiers next in rank lifted spears and drove them home. The two men who had admitted the old ascetic fell. One shrieked once; the other made no sound. Both thrusts were clean.

The old man’s voice rose thin and high, broken as wind through dry reeds. “Another crime added to your tally, Phorenice. Not half your army could have hindered my entrance had I wished to come. I am here to bring you your last warning. The Gods have shown you much favour; they gave you merit by which you rose above your fellows, till at last only the throne stood above you. It was seen good by those on the Sacred Mountain to let you have this last ambition and sit upon the throne long honourably filled by the ancient kings of Atlantis.”

Phorenice leaned back on her cushions, smiling as though at an amusing singer. “I seemed to get these things as I chose, and in spite of your friends’ teeth. I may owe you, old man, a small parcel of thanks—which I offered to repay. But as for my lords the priests, their permission was small value when it came. Remember I was firm upon the throne as this pyramid on its base when your worn-out priests crawled up with their tottering benediction.”

He waved aside her interruption. “Hear me out. I am here with no trivial message. There is nothing paltry in the threat I bring. With your fire-tubes, your handling of troops, your other fiendish clevernesses, you may not be easy to overthrow by mere human means—though these poor rebels who yap at your walls have held their ground long enough. It may be you are becoming enervated. It may be you are too wrapped up in feasting, dressing, pomps, and debaucheries to find leisure for war. It may the man’s spirit has gone out from your arm and brain and you are a woman once more—weak and pleasure-loving. I do not know.

“But this must happen: you must undo the evil you have done. You must give bread to the starving people, even if you take it from these gluttons in this hall. You must restore Atlantis to the state in which it was entrusted to you—or you must be removed. It cannot be permitted that the country sink back into lawlessness and barbarism from which its ancient kings have dug it. You hear, Phorenice. Now give me true answer.”

From behind us, Ylga’s voice came in a hurried whisper, quick as a knife drawn in panic. “Speak him fair. Oh—for the sake of your fortune, speak him fair.”

Phorenice did not so much as turn her head. She leaned forward, brow knit, eyes bright with anger and interest.

“Do you dare threaten me, old man,” she said, “knowing what I am?”
