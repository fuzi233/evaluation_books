My Dear Molloy—

If this reaches you in my absence, know that I have uncovered the truth behind Manderson’s murder. The enclosed dispatch names the culprit—an unsuspected figure whose guilt is laid bare in these pages. I leave it to your discretion whether to publish before his arrest, though I warn you: the law may frown upon it. Share this with Scotland Yard at once. As for me, I wash my hands of the affair. Would to God I had never touched it.

***

This, my final account of the Manderson case, is written with no small measure of relief. Until now, I withheld crucial facts—facts that, if revealed prematurely, might have allowed the killer to slip through our fingers. But the time for secrecy has passed. What follows is a tale of cold treachery, a labyrinth of deceit so cunningly constructed that even now, with the truth laid bare, it leaves a bitter taste.

Recall my earlier reports: the body discovered beside the shed, the eerie silence of the household despite the violent struggle, the missing whisky, the abandoned dental plate—trifles that hinted at something darker lurking beneath the surface. At first glance, they seemed mere oddities. But oddities, in my line of work, are often the threads that unravel the whole tapestry.

Take the shoes.

Manderson’s patent leather pair, worn the night of his death, bore minute but damning signs of strain—splits in the vamp, stretched stitching. These were not the marks of a man who prized his elegant, narrow footwear. No, they had been forced onto feet too large for them. And the edges of the tears were fresh. Someone else had worn them—recently.

Why? Because footprints, my dear reader, are the alphabet of the detective’s craft. The killer needed to leave Manderson’s prints, not his own. But it wasn’t just the shoes. The dental plate, left behind though Manderson never went without it? A prop, planted to cement the illusion. The whisky, gulped down not by the abstemious Manderson but by a man steeling himself for an unholy charade.

And then, the pièce de résistance: the clothes. The jacket Martin glimpsed in the library—unmistakably Manderson’s—worn by a man who kept his back turned, his face hidden beneath the broad-brimmed hat. A performance so brazen it bordered on genius.

But performances, no matter how polished, leave cracks.

Why did "Manderson" spin an elaborate tale to his half-asleep wife about sending Marlowe to Southampton? Why mention Marlowe at all, when the real Manderson scarcely spoke to her? Because the killer *needed* that alibi—for Marlowe, and for himself.

Here, then, is the truth:

Between ten and eleven that night, Manderson was shot—far from the house, where no one would hear. His body was brought back, stripped, and staged. Then the impostor entered—through the window, to avoid Martin’s sharp ears—dressed in Manderson’s clothes, his voice a flawless mimicry. He planted the revolver (Marlowe’s, of course) back in its case. He arranged the bedroom to perfection: shoes by the door, clothes strewn carelessly, the denture placed just so.

And who was this phantom Manderson?

A man who knew the household intimately.
A man of similar build, though with slightly larger feet.
A man with a talent for acting—*Marlowe’s* talent, honed in Oxford’s theatrical circles.
A man who *had* to sustain the illusion of Manderson’s survival until past midnight.

Every fact fits. Every shadow lifts.

The rest, Molloy, is in your hands.
