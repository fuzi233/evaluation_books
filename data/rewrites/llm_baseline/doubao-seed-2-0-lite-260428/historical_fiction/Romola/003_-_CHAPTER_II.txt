After Bratti the peddler wove his way into the huddled, chattering knot of Florentine townsfolk, the young foreign stranger quickly gave up on making sense of the fevered public agitation rippling through the piazza. He had no stake in whatever local scandal or political upheaval was sending every bystander craning their neck to shout over their neighbors, and he soon grew tired of lingering to wait for Bratti to escort him onward. He set off to stroll the sun-warmed flagstones of the market square, keeping an eye out for any food vendor who might not be half-distracted chasing the latest gossip.

A sudden thought struck him, and he fumbled at the leather wallet slung from his waist, rummaging through its pockets again and again until his face fell in clear frustration. “Not an obolus left, by Jupiter,” he muttered under his breath, in a language that was neither Tuscan nor any dialect of Italian the local folk would recognize. “I swore I had one poor copper piece left. Looks like I’ll have to earn my breakfast for the kindness of strangers, then—for love, not coin.”

He had barely taken a dozen more steps when it seemed he had stumbled on just the quiet corner of the market where that odd form of payment might not be turned away. Tucked out of earshot of every gossiping group stood two mules, draped with bright red wool tassels and stitched leather collars. One carried a stack of hewn wooden milk vessels, the other straddled by woven panniers heaped high with fresh wild herbs and crisp, dewy salad greens. Leaning her elbow on the neck of the milk-carrying mule was a young girl, no more than sixteen, a soft red hood pulled up to frame her face so that not a strand of her hair showed. Her features were round and baby-soft, prettier than any market vendor he had seen so far. Likely worn out from tramping three or four miles from her family’s small hill farm before dawn to get to the square, she had drifted asleep half-standing, half-propped against the mule’s warm neck.

The stranger felt no guilt at rousing her, for he chose the gentlest possible way: he plucked a tiny sprig of thyme from the top of her herb pannier and brushed it light as a breath across her lips. In her half-dream, it felt like she was still out in the meadows, stooping to pluck herbs for market herself, and the spell broke softly. She blinked open wide, bright blue baby eyes, and jolted upright in startled confusion to find the tall dark stranger standing inches from her. His voice was soft, but lilted with such a strange foreign accent that she would have assumed he was speaking total nonsense if she had been fully awake, and her first instinct was to twist her face away and lift the edge of her heavy green serge mantle to hide behind.

He spoke again, slow and clear, picking out the simplest Tuscan phrase he knew. “Forgive me, pretty one, for waking you. I’m dying with hunger, and the smell of your warm milk makes breakfast feel more necessary than ever.”

The words *muoio di fame* were familiar to her, and the edge of the mantle fell from her face. In seconds, she had dipped a large earthen cup full of frothy, fragrant warm milk and held it out to him. He did not waste time on flowery compliments before lifting it to his lips, and as he drank, the little maiden found the courage to peer up at his long, loose dark curls. He spoke like a beggar, but even though his fine tunic was scuffed and worn at the cuffs, he looked nothing like the ragged street beggars she saw lingering at the church doors. Some quiet, impulsive kindness made her reach into the canvas bag slung at the mule’s side, and when he set down the empty cup, he found a thick hunk of crusty sourdough bread held out to him, her blue eyes peeking up to encourage him to take it.

“But that is your own breakfast, is it not?” he said, softening his tone. “I have no coin to pay you for this, gentle one. Thank you a thousand times over.”

She did not say a word in reply, just pushed the bread a little closer to him, impatient that he would refuse her gift. Their gazes tangled, the stranger’s dark eyes holding hers, and Tessa’s shy little face grew bolder and bolder, no longer rushing to hide behind her mantle.

“Ah, if you insist I take the bread,” he said, laughing softly as he curled his fingers around it, “I shall grow even more bold, and beg a small kiss to make it taste all the sweeter.”

His strange accent was growing far more understandable by the minute, even if it had made her half-think of crossing herself when she first heard it. She blushed bright pink, and lifted the corner of her mantle up to cover her mouth again. But just as he leaned in, his fingers brushing the soft arm that held the mantle edge up between them, a harsh, rough voice boomed right next to his ear.

“Who do you think you are—may the murrain take you? No honest buyer, that’s for certain, just a loiterer hanging around the dice dens, or something worse. Off with you! Dance along to find more suitable company, or I’ll give you a beating that’ll make you move faster than you like.”

The stranger stepped back, no flicker of fear or deference in his gaze, just a faint, amused lift of his eyebrows that bloomed into a bright, warm grin as he looked over the woman who had threatened him. She was broad, brawny, sunburned, wearing a man’s thick leather jerkin slung over her faded green serge gown, an old peaked hood from a worn-out mantle tied tight around her lined, prematurely wrinkled face. Beneath the coarseness and the sharp set of her jaw, you could see the same soft round features that made Tessa’s face so sweet—a maternal resemblance that felt almost jarring, like a grim little foreshadowing of what the soft young girl might look like after decades of backbreaking work.

That bright young smile of his was disarmingly charming, but Monna Ghita was not the sort of woman to let softness slip, and she only grew more exasperated. “Yes, yes, you can grin just like every other capering monkey on the street. You’re some wandering minstrel or mountebank, I’ll swear it—you look just as silly as a tumbler who’s been standing on his head so long he’s forgotten how to walk rightside up.” She rounded on Tessa, her temper snapping at the easier target. “And what fool’s tricks have you been up to, you little stupid thing? Giving away our milk and our food for free? Aye, you’d carry water in your ears for any idle vagabond too lazy to bend over and help himself, you silly wide-eyed rabbit! Turn around now, haul those herbs out of the panniers, or I’ll make you recite a dozen Aves without pausing to count them.”

“Nay, good Madonna,” the stranger said, still smiling that pleading, charming smile. “Don’t be cross with your sweet Tessa for taking pity on a hungry traveller who found himself suddenly without a single copper quattrino. Your fine face looks so striking when you frown, I can only imagine how lovely it would be lit up with a smile.”

“Get along with you!” Monna Ghita snapped. “I know exactly what sort of trickster you are. You can tickle me with that pretty talk all day long, I’m not going to laugh for you. Be off with you, and a bad Easter to you, or I’ll scuff that pretty face of yours up so bad none of your kisses will be welcome anywhere before Advent.”

She lifted her calloused, claw-like hands to follow through on her threat, just as Bratti stepped up beside his friend Nello, the town barber, who had been observing the whole scene for a minute or two. “What do you make of this pretty trickster, eh, Nello?” Bratti murmured. “Doesn’t his lilt sound like he’s fresh from Venice?”

“Nay, Bratti,” Nello the barber whispered back, clapping him on the shoulder. “Your wits are as thick as a donkey’s, like I told you only a minute ago, and not just about people’s ears. That stranger’s Greek, or my name isn’t Nello the barber who’s shaved the great learned Demetrio himself, and pulled more than one rotten aching tooth out of his scholarly jaw. That lad looks like he stepped straight off Mount Olympus—at least he would, once I got my razor to his overgrown curls.”

“Hold on there, Monna Ghita!” Nello called, stepping forward to lean against the mule’s harness, delighted to stir up a little fun. “What’s got you thundering like a summer storm? Has this young gentleman misbehaved himself?”

“By San Giovanni, the patron of our city!” Bratti put in, loud enough for everyone to hear, still wary of the shabbily dressed stranger he had found earlier with a fine gemmed ring on his finger that looked like the sort of thing Bernardo Rucellai wore. “He was right to run off from me if he was planning to get up to mischief! I swear I found him loitering under the Loggia de’ Cerchi earlier, with a ring on his finger finer than any I’ve ever laid eyes on on a common traveller. I don’t know a single other thing about him, not even if his name’s not a lie.”

“The truth of it is,” Nello said, grinning at the stranger as he clapped him on the back, “this handsome young man grew too bold admiring your charms, Monna Ghita, and tried to steal a kiss while Tessa’s back was turned—seeing as she’s far too busy sorting herbs to pay attention, right?” He turned to the stranger, tone polite and teasing. “Isn’t that right, Messer?”

“You’ve guessed my crime perfectly, like a soothsayer,” the stranger laughed, playing along. “Only the misfortune was I didn’t find the lovely Monna Ghita here at first. I only begged a cup of milk from her daughter, accepted this fine loaf of bread, and was offering my humble gratitude before I was lucky enough to catch sight of her far more mature charms—though I daresay I was too bold to admire them out loud.”

“Go on, get out of here, all of you!” Monna Ghita snapped, elbowing Nello out of the way and tugging her mule’s bridle to force the stranger to step back out of the path. “I hope you all rot in purgatory until I scrape together enough coin to buy your way out, you hear? Tessa, you little simpleton, bring your mule forward a pace—there’s a market cart coming right for us, it’ll run us all over.”

As Tessa turned to grab the mule’s bridle, she darted one shy, quick glance back at the stranger, who was moving off beside Nello to get out of the cart’s path. The glance lasted just long enough for her to catch the tiny, secret wave of his hand, a quiet little adieu he’d been waiting for the chance to give her.

“Well then!” Bratti called out, loud enough to carry over the rattle of the approaching cart. “I’m leaving you with Nello, young man, there’s no way I can push my heavy sack and basket any further, I’ve got errands to run back home. But you remember our bargain, all right? It’s not my fault you found that pretty little Tessa without me tagging along. Nello’ll show you my shop down in the Ferravecchi district, I won’t leave you hanging.”

“A thousand thanks, friend!” the stranger laughed, as he followed Nello up the narrow, sun-dappled side street that led straight to the marble facade of the Duomo looming over the city.
