The flurry of the first shock and greeting had barely settled when the sharp little maiden launched straight into her rebuke. “So you have finally come round to ask after Theo? And you deign to feel sorry that your cruel neglect has laid her low these past six weeks, while you never sent so much as a single word of inquiry? Very fine behaviour indeed, Mr. George, I am sure!”
“Kind?” I gasped, my own pale, fever-worn hands trembling where I clutched the half-empty vial of lavender cordial the doctor had left on my mantel that morning. “Do you call it kind to sit at her side every hour of every day for a full year, then vanish without a single farewell?”
“My dear girl, you know full well I gave your father my solemn promise—”
“Promise!” Miss Hetty tossed her head, shrugging her small shoulders so hard the mob cap perched on her curls slid askew. “A very grand sort of promise, that, to make my darling waste away, then out of the blue bid her ‘good-bye’ and walk off for ever. I do suppose gentlemen keep those promises only when it suits them. If I were a man, I would never trifle with a poor girl’s heart then abandon her. What harm did Theo ever do you, but be foolish enough to love you far too well? By what right do you steal her away from all the quiet happiness we had before you ever stepped through our door, then cast her off just because some old lady across the Atlantic does not think her good enough for her precious son? She was the sun of our whole house before you came! I was the whole world to her, until you turned her head. And now, forsooth, because your mamma fancies you might catch a richer bride, you desert her!”
“Great heavens, child!” I cried, stung to exasperation by her warped, unseeing version of the truth. “It was not I who drew back! It is not I who am forbidden your threshold! Your father demanded my honour on the spot that I would never attempt to see her again—”
“Honour!” she laughed, bitter as winter frost, flinging the half-embroidered handkerchief stitched with Theo’s initials onto my table. “You men preen yourselves as our superiors, and expect us to bow down and admire you! Upon my word, George Warrington, you ought to be sent back to your Virginia schoolroom, let your black nurse tuck you up in bed, and beg your mamma’s permission before you dare step out for a walk. Oh George, little did I think my sister would give her whole heart to a man with no more spirit than to flee at the very first sign of trouble! When Dr. Heberden told me he was attending you these past weeks, I resolved to come and see you myself—and you do look wretched, thank goodness, so I know you are not quite as hard-hearted as you seem. I will not tell Theo you are ill, though. She has not forgotten you, you see. She cannot simply walk out of a room, break every solemn vow she ever made, and step gaily back into society the next day as if nothing ever happened. That privilege is reserved for men, our betters in courage and wisdom—men who will desert an angel, yes, an angel ten thousand times too good for you, the angel who was the blessing of all our lives until she laid eyes on you! That is what you call honour? I despise the lot of you! Your clever plays, your verses, your fine wit—half the time they are dreadfully stupid. I will not stay up half the night copying your manuscripts, nor loiter for hours at the window wasting my time just to catch a glimpse of your worship strutting down the street with your hat tilted at that absurd cock. If you are set on running off, then give me back my sister! Give me back the old Theo who loved every one of us, before you stole her away and left her to die here alone while your mother hunts you a wealthier wife. O brave, fine gentleman! Go marry whoever your mother picks out for you, and leave my dearest to waste away!”
“Hetty, for the love of all that is good!” I cried, reeling under the force of her passion. “It is not I who wish to leave her! I offered to keep my promise to marry her, and your father turned me away, and bound me on my honour never to seek her out. What have I in this world, but my word and my good name?”
“Good name, indeed! You keep your word to him, and break it to her! A very pretty sort of honour, that! If I were a man, I would soon show you what I think of your fine honour—oh, George, George! Do you not see how distracted I am? I hardly know what words I am saying, I am so distraught. They do not guess the truth back at our lodgings. They do not see what I see, that she will surely die if you leave her. Say you will not leave her! Have pity on me, Mr. Warrington, and give me back my dearest!”
The warm-hearted, overwrought creature careened from blazing anger to wild entreaty, from sharp scorn to scalding tears, her whole small frame shaking with sobs. Was my fiery little self-appointed doctor right about her patient’s case? Was there no other cure but the one she begged for? Have not thousands of other souls endured that same cruel amputation of a heart, wasted away through the same fever and despair, lain convinced there was no life left worth living, only to recover after all, and limp on through the rest of their days? Once, Theo and I had sat snug by our fireside, listening to Hetty grieve over her own long-ago hopeless attachment, and we had smiled complacently, wrapped in our own perfect happiness, as you might huddle warm indoors and hear the winter wind howl outside the panes, or stand safe on the shore and watch a ship toss through a storm at sea. But in that moment, I did not care a farthing for that old proof that hearts could heal after separation. I was all too ready to share Hetty’s terrors, and fully convinced that the life I held dearest on earth would be snuffed out if we were kept apart.
I was half ready to pour all this out to Dr. Heberden when the good little man clamped a hand over my mouth, his round eyes wide with fright. “Hush! Not a word of this to me, sir! If two people who happen to be acquainted chance to meet and talk in my patient’s rooms, that is no fault of mine. But match-making? Love plots? I will have no part of it! General Lambert will challenge me to a duel behind Montagu House the second he returns to London, that I swear, and I am far too fond of my own skin to meet him there!” He grabbed his gold-headed cane, his wig askew and his silk stockings rolled down at the ankle, and scurried out to his waiting chariot before I could protest. “And no more secret meetings with Miss Hetty, mind you!” he called over his shoulder as the footman shut the carriage door.
Oh, of course! We were men of honour, after all, our word our bond. I had been granted that one precious, unexpected glimpse of Hetty, and I told myself I could be content to hold out for weeks more. I walked with her all the way to the edge of Soho, never once dreaming of arranging another meeting—but the clever little emissary had thought of that for me.
“Still go up to the British Museum to read your old histories, don’t you?” she asked, not looking at me.
“Oh yes, sometimes,” I said. “Though I am far too wretched to read now. The words blur right off the page. Even my beloved Pocahontas manuscript feels wearisome to me these days. I—”
“Nonsense!” she stamped her tiny kid slipper on the cobblestones. “You are more stupid than Harry, I do declare! You leave the Museum at three o’clock. You cut across the road to Tottenham Court, walk through the village, and come back down that Green Lane that runs north of the new Foundling Hospital. You know you do! Walk there for a week, it will do you no harm at all. Good day, sir!” She dropped me a sharp little curtsey, pulled her veil down over her face, and vanished down the street.
That Green Lane is all built over with neat brick terraces now, but back in the days of good old King George the Second, it was a rough, half-rural track skirting London’s outskirts, so dangerous that parties of folk out for junkets in Hampstead and Highgate would only return after dark escorted by tavern waiters bearing lanthorns, to ward off the footpads that lurked in the hedgerows. The two hilltop churches of Hampstead and Highgate rose clear against the blue sky at the end of the lane, and for three days straight I walked that path, lingering to look out over the open fields toward the hills. Halfway down the lane there was a little roadside alehouse with a sign for The Protestant Hero, selling spiced cake and cool bitter beer. On my third pass by, the landlady, a plump decent woman in a clean starched apron, smiled and curtseyed at me over her threshold. “The lady has not come by yet, sir,” she called out. “You are welcome to step in and take a can of my best cool beer while you wait.”
At last, on the 25th of May—a day I will mark with the whitest stone of my memory for as long as I live—just past Mr. Whitefield’s Tabernacle on Tottenham Road, I spied a landau bobbing toward me. Perched on the box-seat beside the driver was young Charley Lambert, who waved his hat wildly and shouted my name. I ran to the carriage so fast my knees knocked together, I thought I would stumble and fall under the wheel. Inside, behind the open window, sat Hetty—and beside her, propped up on a stitched linen pillow, was my Theo.
The little hand that slipped into mine was so thin and light it felt like holding a sparrow. Her cheeks were flushed and hollow, her eyes bright with that strange, burning light illness brings, and the thrill of her soft voice as she spoke my name wrapped around me so tight I could not tell if the pain in my chest was grief or joy, the two tangled up so close.
“I am taking her out for an airing to Hampstead,” Hetty said, demure as a lamb, though I caught the wicked little twitch at the corner of her mouth. “The doctor says the open country air will do her a world of good.”
“I have been ill, but I am better now, George,” Theo whispered.
Just then, a great swell of psalm music burst from the congregation pouring out of the Tabernacle beside us. I held her hand fast in mine, and her eyes met mine, and it felt as if we had never been parted at all. I have heard that psalm a thousand times since then: my wife has played it on her harpsichord, our little ones have warbled it at the nursery fire. Do you understand now, my dears, why I have always loved it so? It was the music that played when our love was given back to us, the music that sang hope to me in the very darkest, most miserable stretch of my whole life. Even the long harsh confinement at Fort Duquesne had its soft little memories, after all: kind old Biche, my drunken jailor, the trill of the forest birds at reveille, the distant military bugles of the garrison.
Young Charley leaned down from his perch on the box, grinning at the sight of Theo and I lost in our own little world, Hetty half smiling beside us listening to the psalm. “I say, I think I would like to go inside and hear Mr. Whitefield preach right now! Come along with me, Charley,” Hetty said, tugging at his coat sleeve. “George can drive on ahead for half an hour with Theo, up toward Hampstead and back, and we will meet them here when the service is done.”
Charley made a face, no great desire to sit through a two-hour sermon, and suggested George might go into the chapel with him instead—but Hetty was not to be swayed. “I will never help you sneak out to cock-fights or horse races ever again, sir, if you do not come along this instant!” she threatened, and the poor boy was forced to clamber down off the box, and the two of them vanished through the chapel doors together.
Let any priggish moralist, who remembers all my old promises to General Lambert, judge me now. Could any man alive blame me for climbing straight up into that landau and sitting down beside my dearest Theo? Suppose I did break my word? You are welcome to your fine, spotless virtue, reverend sir, if you would have done any different. I do not suppose any of my own children would ever be so bold as to follow their own hearts and choose the person they love, after all. No no, my dears, you will all let papa pick your spouses for you, be hungry exactly when I tell you to be, and arrange your own children’s marriages just as soon as you are grown.
I looked over at the sweet lady beside me now, her needles plying soft wool by the fireside, and laughed. “There we are, my dear. I have reached that part of the story at last.”
“Which part is that?” Theo said, lifting her head, her cheeks flushing pink. “Do skip all that silly love nonsense, and get on to the grand battles, and your heroic defence of—”
“Of Fort Fiddlededee in 1778!” I teased. “When I pulled General Washington’s epaulet right off his shoulder, gouged General Gates’s eye, cut off Charles Lee’s head and pasted it back on again!”
“Tell us the fighting bits, papa!” the boys clamoured, and even our old friend the Captain, home on leave, nodded gravely and said he would deign to listen to any military details, even from a militia officer.
“Softly now, young gentlemen, everything in its proper turn. I have not reached the war yet. I am still just a foolish young fellow, climbing into a landau beside the girl I swore I would never speak to again, holding her small, hot hand that was thumping a hundred and twenty beats a minute, do you remember that, my love? And as the carriage trotted slowly up the Hampstead road, I said to Miss Lambert—”
“Ah! Ah! Now we listen!” the two little girls cried in unison, and their French governess Mademoiselle Eleonore laughed, shrugging her pretty shoulders. “La parole est a vous, Monsieur le Chevalier!”
We were all circled round the fire that night: Theo in her usual chair on one side, me on the other, Mademoiselle de Blois over in the corner (the Captain was looking very sweet at her, and I suspected our friend the Chevalier de Florac had a hand in bringing her to us), the two girls listening as attentively as little nymphs learning song from Apollo, and John and Thomas trundling in the tea tray and the silver urn. I pulled my crumpled manuscript from my pocket and waved it at them all. “Very well. Now I shall tell you all your mother’s secrets, and mine.”
“There is nothing there to be ashamed of, papa,” my sweet wife said, the blush rising all the way over her kind old face.
“But before I begin, young folks, allow me two or three little questions.”
“Allons! Always questions!” Mademoiselle laughed, and tossed her head.
