Lunch passed in a slow, meditative rhythm, punctuated by long silences where words seemed unnecessary, and brief rushes of speech when the spell was finally broken. Between these quiet intervals, there was so much unsaid that conversation often felt like a mere accessory to the deeper dialogue of unspoken thoughts. Archer deliberately avoided mentioning his own affairs, not out of conscious strategy, but because he feared missing a single detail of her history. Leaning forward, chin resting on her clasped hands, she spoke to him of the year and a half since they had crossed paths.

She confessed a weariness with what the world called "society." New York, she admitted, had been kind, almost oppressively hospitable in its welcome; she would never forget it. Yet, past the initial thrill of novelty, she realized she was somehow "too different" to truly care for the things society valued. That realization had driven her decision to attempt life in Washington, a place supposedly brimming with varied opinions and people. On balance, she likely intended to stay there, establishing a home for poor Medora, whose patience had finally worn thin among all her relatives at the very moment Medora needed protection from matrimonial pitfalls.

"But Dr. Carver—aren't you afraid of him? I heard he has been staying with you at the Blenkers'," Archer asked, his voice betraying a flicker of concern.

A smile softened her features. "Oh, the danger posed by Dr. Carver has passed. He is a remarkably clever man, driven by the ambition to find a wealthy wife to fund his grand designs. As for Medora, she serves merely as a convenient advertisement for his conversion."

"To what exactly?" Archer pressed.

"To a multitude of radical and chaotic social experiments. But tell me, do you know how strangely fascinating they become compared to the blind adherence to tradition—the *other* people's traditions—that plagues our circle here? It seems absurd to discover America only to turn it into a carbon copy of Europe." She looked across the table, her eyes sparkling. "Do you imagine Christopher Columbus sailed the ocean blue simply to attend an opera with the Selfridge Merrys?"

Archer flushed. "And Beaufort... do you say such things to Beaufort?" he demanded, his tone abrupt.

"I haven't seen him in ages," she replied gently. "Though I used to. And he understands."

"It is exactly what I have always suspected," Archer murmured, glancing around the sparsely furnished room, out toward the barren beach and the row of stark white houses lining the shore. "You dislike us. You prefer Beaufort precisely because he is so unlike us." He gazed at the desolate landscape. "We are dreadfully dull. Lacking character, lacking color, lacking variety. Tell me," he broke out, "why do you not return?"

Her eyes darkened, and he braced himself for an indignant retort. Instead, she sat in silence, contemplating his question, and a fresh fear seized him lest she admit that she, too, wondered why she stayed.

Finally, she spoke softly. "I believe it is because of you."

It was impossible to phrase the confession more calmly, or in a tone less flattering to the vanity of the listener. Archer blushed violently to his temples, frozen as if a rare butterfly hovered above him; the slightest movement might scatter it, yet leaving it undisturbed allowed it to gather a following.

"At least," she continued, "it was you who showed me that beneath this dullness lie things so fine, so sensitive, so delicate that even the people I cherished most in my former life appear cheap by comparison. I struggle to explain..." Her brows furrowed in distress. "...but it feels as though I had never previously grasped how deeply hard, shabby, and base the price must be for the most exquisite pleasures."

"Exquisite pleasures—it is a blessing to have experienced them!" he felt compelled to retort, yet the pleading quality in her gaze held him silent.

"I wish," she persisted, "to be entirely honest with you—and with myself. For some time now, I have hoped this moment would arrive: the chance to tell you how you have helped me, what you have made of me."

Archer stared beneath furrowed brows, then cut her short with a laugh. "And what do you claim you have made of me?"

She paled slightly. "Of *you*?"

"Yes," he insisted. "For I am the product of your making far more than you ever were of mine. I am the man who married one woman solely because another commanded him to."

Her pallor shifted into a fleeting blush. "I thought—you promised—you were not to speak of such things today."

"Ah—how like a woman! None of you ever sees a bad bargain through!"

She lowered her voice. "_Is_ it a bad bargain—for May?"

He stepped to the window, tapping his fingers against the raised glass, acutely aware of the wistful tenderness with which she had invoked his cousin's name.

"That is the burden we must always carry, is it not?" she insisted, pressing her point home.

"My own burden?" he echoed, his vacant eyes fixed on the sea.

"If not," she continued, her logic cutting painfully deep, "if it is not worthwhile to sacrifice, to miss out, so that others might be spared disillusionment and misery—then everything I returned home for, everything that rendered my previous life so barren and impoverished by contrast because no one there acknowledged it—all these things amount to a sham, a dream."

Turning from the window, he faced her. "In that case, there is no earthly reason why you cannot go back," he concluded for her.

Her eyes clung to him with desperate intensity. "Oh, _is_ there no reason?"

"Not if you staked your entire existence on the success of my marriage. My marriage," he said with savage sharpness, "will not be a spectacle worthy of keeping you here." She offered no reply, and he pressed on. "What is the point? You gave me my first glimpse of a real life, and simultaneously asked me to continue living a counterfeit existence. It is beyond human endurance—that is all."

"Oh, do not say that; I am enduring it!" she cried, her eyes filling with tears.

Her arms dropped to the table, and she sat with her face abandoned to his gaze, as if in the reckless midst of a perilous gamble. The exposure of her countenance revealed her soul as completely as if her entire being were laid bare. Archer stood dumbstruck, overwhelmed by the sudden revelation.

"You too... oh, all this time, you too?"

As an answer, she let the tears spill over her lashes, tracing a slow path downward.

Half the room remained between them, and neither made a show of bridging the gap. Archer felt a strange indifference to her physical presence; he would scarcely have noticed it had it not been for the hand she had flung onto the table, which captured his attention as it had in the small house on Twenty-third Street, drawing his eye away from her face. Now, his imagination swirled around that hand as if it were the edge of a whirlpool, yet he made no effort to draw near. He knew the love sustained by caresses and fed by them; but this passion, intimate as bone, refused superficial satisfaction. His greatest terror was doing anything that might erase the sound and impact of her words; his singular thought was that he should never again feel quite alone.

Yet soon, a sense of waste and ruin overtook him. There they sat, close together, safe and enclosed, yet bound by separate destinies so firmly that they might as well have been worlds apart.

"What is the use—" he exploded, "when you will go back?" A cry of despair, *How on earth shall I keep you?*, rose within him beneath the words.

She sat motionless, her lids lowered. "Oh—I shall not go yet!"

"Not yet? Then sometime later? A time you already foresee?"

At that, she lifted her clear, luminous eyes. "I promise you: not as long as you hold out. Not as long as we can look straight at each other like this."

He sank into his chair. The subtext of her answer was clear: *If you lift a finger, you will drive me back—to all the horrors you know of, and all the temptations you half suspect.* He understood it as plainly as if she had spoken it aloud, anchoring him to his side of the table in a kind of moved, sacred submission.

"What a life for you—" he groaned.

"Oh—as long as it is a part of yours."

"And mine a part of yours?"

She nodded.

"And that is to be all—for either of us?"

"Well, it _is_ all, isn't it?"

At those words, he sprang up, forgetting everything save the sweetness of her face. She rose as well—not to meet him nor to flee—but quietly, as though the hardest part of the ordeal were done and she merely waited. So quietly that, as he approached, her outstretched hands acted not as a barrier but as a guide. They fell into his, while her arms, extended but not stiff, kept him at a distance sufficient to let her surrendered face speak the remainder.

They may have stood thus for a long time, or perhaps only a few moments; but it was long enough for her silence to convey everything she wished to say, and for him to realize that only one thing mattered. He must do nothing to make this meeting their final encounter; he must leave their future in her care
