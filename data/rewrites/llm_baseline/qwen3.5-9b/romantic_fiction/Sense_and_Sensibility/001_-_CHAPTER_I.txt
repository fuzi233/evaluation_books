The Dashwood family had long called Sussex home, residing at Norland Park, a sprawling estate situated squarely within their ancestral lands. For generations, they had maintained a reputation of such high standing that they commanded the warmest regard of everyone in the neighborhood. The late proprietor of Norland was a bachelor who lived to a remarkable age, spending his later years in the devoted company of his sister, who served as both his companion and housekeeper. However, her passing a decade prior had fundamentally altered his domestic life. To fill the void she left, he welcomed into his household the family of his nephew, Mr. Henry Dashwood—the rightful heir to Norland and the beneficiary of his will. Surrounded by his nephew, niece, and grandchildren, the elderly gentleman found his days filled with genuine contentment. The unwavering attentiveness of Mr. and Mrs. Henry Dashwood to his needs, born of kindness rather than mere duty, provided him with profound comfort, while the lively spirits of the children brought a special joy to his existence.

Mr. Henry Dashwood's lineage included one son from a previous marriage and three daughters from his current union. The son, a steady and respectable youth, had already been generously provided for by his mother's substantial fortune, receiving half upon reaching maturity and further augmenting his wealth through his own recent marriage. Consequently, the inheritance of Norland held significantly less weight for him than it did for his sisters. Their financial prospects, aside from whatever portion their father might inherit, were meager; their mother possessed nothing, and their father held only seven thousand pounds personally. Moreover, the remainder of his first wife's fortune was legally secured to her son, leaving him with only a life interest in that capital.

When the old gentleman finally passed away, the reading of his will delivered a mixture of relief and crushing disappointment. Though he was neither unjust nor ungrateful enough to disinherit his nephew entirely, he attached conditions that effectively halved the value of the bequest. Mr. Dashwood had coveted the estate primarily for the sake of his wife and daughters, yet the will stipulated that the property pass directly to his son and grandson—a toddler of four—to prevent any encroachment on the boy's security. This arrangement stripped Mr. Dashwood of the power to provide for those dearest to him, prohibiting any charges against the estate or the sale of its valuable timberlands. The entire property was thus locked away for the benefit of the child, who, during occasional visits to Norland, had managed to charm his uncle with the irresistible allure typical of toddlers aged two or three: broken speech, stubborn demands, mischievous antics, and endless noise. These traits ultimately outweighed the years of devotion shown to him by his aunt and cousins.

Despite these constraints, Mr. Dashwood intended no cruelty. As a token of his affection for the three girls, he bequeathed them one thousand pounds apiece. Initially, Mr. Dashwood's despair was acute, but his naturally cheerful and optimistic temperament allowed him to hope that, by living frugally, he could accumulate a significant sum from the estate's existing resources and potential improvements. Tragically, this fortune proved fleeting. He survived his uncle for barely twelve months, dying just as his health began to fail. Upon his deathbed, summoning his son, Mr. John Dashwood, he urged him with all the urgency his failing strength permitted to ensure the welfare of his mother-in-law and sisters.

Mr. John Dashwood lacked the deep emotional resonance felt by the rest of the family, yet the gravity of his father's plea moved him. He promised to use every means at his disposal to secure their comfort. Satisfied by this assurance, Mr. Dashwood passed peacefully, leaving behind only ten thousand pounds—including the legacies—for his widow and daughters. Meanwhile, Mr. John Dashwood retreated into quiet contemplation regarding the extent of his ability to assist them. Not inherently malicious, though perhaps somewhat cold-hearted and self-regarding, he was a man of propriety and respectability. Had he married a woman of greater warmth, he might have become more endearing; instead, he wedded a woman who was a stark mirror of his own limitations—narrow-minded and equally selfish.

Mrs. John Dashwood had harbored no particular affection for her husband's family until this crisis revealed her true character. While Mr. John had envisioned presenting his widowed mother and sisters with three thousand pounds—a sum he believed he could easily spare given his projected annual income of four thousand plus his share of his mother's fortune—he found his resolve crumbling under the reality of the situation. Yet, before his father's funeral concluded, Mrs. John Dashwood arrived at Norland with her child and retinue, making no prior announcement of her intent. Though legally entitled to enter the house now that it belonged to her husband, her abrupt appearance was a breach of etiquette that would have caused offense to anyone with ordinary feelings. For Mrs. Dashwood, however, possessing a romanticized sense of honor and a fierce, albeit misguided, generosity, such rudeness was an insurmountable affront.

Though never a favorite among her in-laws, Mrs. John Dashwood had previously lacked the opportunity to demonstrate her lack of consideration. Her behavior stung Mrs. Dashwood profoundly, sparking a desire to abandon the house forever. Only the gentle persuasion of her eldest daughter, Elinor, tempered this impulse. Elinor, despite being only nineteen, possessed a sharp intellect and calm judgment that guided her mother wisely. Possessing a noble heart and strong affections, Elinor learned to govern her emotions—a lesson her mother had yet to master, and which her youngest sister, Marianne, explicitly rejected.

Marianne matched Elinor in intelligence and capability but differed radically in temperament. Eager in all things, her sorrows and joys knew no bounds. Generous, charming, and captivating, she lacked prudence entirely, bearing a striking resemblance to her mother. Elinor observed her sister's overwhelming sensitivity with concern, whereas Mrs. Dashwood cherished it. Together, mother and daughter indulged their grief, voluntarily renewing their anguish and refusing any form of consolation. They immersed themselves in their sorrow, finding new depths of misery in every reflection, vowing never to accept comfort again.

Elinor, though deeply afflicted, retained the strength to endure. She consulted with her brother, greeted his wife with dignity, and strove to encourage her mother toward similar restraint. The third sister, Margaret, was a cheerful and well-meaning girl of thirteen, though she had absorbed much of Marianne's romanticism without acquiring her sister's sense, suggesting she would not match her older siblings' maturity in the years ahead.
