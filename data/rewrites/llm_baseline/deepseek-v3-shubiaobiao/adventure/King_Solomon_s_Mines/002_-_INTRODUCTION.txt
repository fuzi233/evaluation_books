Now that these pages are bound and ready to meet the eyes of the world, I feel the weight of their imperfections—both in style and substance. Let me confess at once: this is no exhaustive chronicle. Much of what we witnessed in Kukuanaland remains untold, pared down to the bones of the tale.

There were stories I longed to linger over—legends of the ancient chainmail that turned death away from us in the battle of Loo, or the silent, towering sentinels at the mouth of the cave. A scholar might have filled volumes with the distinctions between Zulu and Kukuana speech, each nuance a clue to their histories. The land itself, teeming with strange beasts and plants (I counted eight antelope unknown to me, and bulbs of shapes no botanist had sketched), deserved more than a passing note.

And then there was their army—a machine of such precision it would humble even Chaka’s Zulu regiments. No forced celibacy weakened their ranks; their mobilization swifter than any I’d seen. Their craftsmen, too, shaped iron and steel with a skill that bordered on artistry—those *tollas*, heavy throwing knives with edges of flawless steel fused to iron backs, were marvels no European smith could disdain.

Yet Sir Henry and Captain Good argued rightly: some tales are best told plain. So I’ve spared you the tangents, though I’ll gladly speak of them to any who ask. Forgive the roughness of my writing—a rifle fits my hand better than a pen. I leave the flourishes to novelists; a sharp spear needs no polish, and neither, I hope, does a true story.
