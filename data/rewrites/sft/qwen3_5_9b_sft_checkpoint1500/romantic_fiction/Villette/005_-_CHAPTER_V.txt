With my mistress gone, I found myself standing alone once more, forced to seek shelter elsewhere. My nerves, perhaps, were a trifle shaken—not enough to ruin me, but enough to make me feel pale, thin, and hollow-eyed, like someone who has stayed up all night or lost their footing in a storm. Yet, I was not truly in debt, nor entirely destitute. Though Miss Marchmont could not fulfill her promise of a gift on that final night, her second cousin, the heir, eventually paid me my wages. He was a man of sharp features and a pinched nose, an avaricious soul who, I learned later, lived a life of stingy miserliness—a stark, ironic contrast to the generosity of his kin, whose memory remains a blessing to those who need it.

Possessing fifteen pounds, a body weary but unbroken, and a spirit that mirrored my physical state, I realized that compared to many, I held an enviable position. Yet, it was an awkward spot. On a specific afternoon, I felt the weight of this ambiguity acutely; soon, I would leave my current home, knowing full well I had nowhere else prepared for me.

Desperate for guidance, I visited an old servant from my family, now the housekeeper of a grand estate nearby. Once my nurse, she offered comfort but lacked wisdom for my particular plight. As twilight fell, leaving me with a heavy heart and a two-mile walk ahead, I stepped out into a clear, frosty night. Alone, poor, and perplexed, I walked through silent fields under a moonless sky, guided only by stars. My heart, however, beat with a vigor born of youth, uncounted twenty-three winters yet. It did not falter. Indeed, the eerie, moving lights of the Aurora Borealis dancing in the northern sky did not frighten me; instead, they seemed to summon a new strength. Drawing breath from the keen, low wind, a bold thought took root in my mind, hardening it to accept whatever fate awaited.

*"Leave this wilderness,"* it whispered, *"and go forth."*

*"Whither?"* I asked silently.

I needed not look far. Gazing upon the flat, fertile lands of central England, I imagined something I had never seen with my own eyes: London.

The following day, I returned to the hall to speak with the housekeeper, Mrs. Barrett. A grave and sensible woman, though she knew little of the wider world, she did not dismiss my plan as madness. I carried myself with a staid dignity that often served as a shield, allowing me to pursue ambitious ideas that might have branded me a dreamer if expressed with excitement.

As Mrs. Barrett pondered the difficulties of my move, preparing orange rinds for marmalade, a small child bounded past the window and into the room. A lovely, laughing boy, he danced straight to me, and I lifted him onto my knees. His mother, young Mrs. Leigh, entered shortly after. Time had transformed her from a dull, lower-class schoolgirl into a beautiful, kind-hearted wife and mother, though intellect seemed to have yielded to domestic grace. She had forgotten me, and I made no effort to remind her; why should I? She wished to take her son for a walk, accompanied by a nurse carrying an infant.

It was the nurse who caught my attention. Addressing her, Mrs. Leigh spoke French—bad French, with an accent so thick it transported me instantly back to our school days. The little boy chattered back in the same tongue. When the visitors departed, Mrs. Barrett revealed that the nurse was a foreigner, brought home two years prior on a continental trip, treated almost like a governess, and tasked mostly with walking with the baby and speaking French. "She claims," Mrs. Barrett noted, "that many Englishwomen find themselves similarly situated in foreign households."

I tucked away this fragment of news, saving it for a future moment of need. Before I left, she kindly provided the address of a respectable, old-fashioned inn in the City, a haunt of my uncles in days past.

Venturing to London required less audacity than one might assume; it was merely fifty miles. My funds covered the journey, a few days' lodging, and the return ticket if necessary. I viewed this not as a perilous adventure, but as a brief respite for my wearied faculties, a chance to breathe. One must always estimate one's actions moderately; grandiose schemes tend to induce fever, whereas calm calculation preserves the mind.

What was once a day's journey became a reality by nine on a wet February night. Upon reaching London, I found myself in a Babylon and a wilderness, a vast, strange city that tested the limits of my clear thinking and self-possession. Leaving the coach, the dialect of the cabmen sounded like a foreign tongue to me, a jumbled chaos of sounds I had never heard before. Yet, I managed to navigate, understanding enough to secure transport to the inn I had been given.

How oppressive and confusing this flight felt! For the first time, I stood in London, at an inn, exhausted by travel, confused by the darkness, numbed by the chill, and utterly lacking in experience or counsel. And yet, I was compelled to act.

I entrusted the situation to common sense. Alas, it was as chilled and bewildered as the rest of me, acting only under the spur of an inexorable necessity. With spasmodic effort, it directed me to pay the porter, to inquire for a room, and timidly to summon the chambermaid. This young lady, a model of town smartness, greeted me with a mincing, glib accent that seemed to rebuke my own speech, her spruce attire flaunting an easy scorn against my plain country garb.

"Well, it can't be helped," I told myself, steeling my resolve. "The scene is new; I shall gain from it."

I maintained a quiet demeanor toward her arrogance and the black-coated, white-neckclothed waiter, and soon, civility replaced suspicion. They likely mistook me for a servant at first, but quickly shifted to a hovering uncertainty between patronage and politeness.

I held my ground until I had eaten, warmed myself by a fire, and locked myself safely into my room. Only then, as I sat by the bed resting my head and arms upon the pillow, did a terrible oppression seize me. Suddenly, my position loomed large, ghost-like and anomalous. Desolate, almost devoid of hope, it stood before me. What was I doing, alone in great London? What would I do tomorrow? What prospects lay in store? Who were my friends? Where did I come from, and whither should I go?

Tears rushed over me, wetting the pillow, my arms, and my hair. A dark interval of bitter thought followed this outburst, yet I felt no regret for the step I had taken, nor any desire to turn back. A strong, vague persuasion dominated my spirit: it was better to move forward than backward, and I *could* move forward. A way, however narrow and difficult, would surely open in time. Its influence soothed my turmoil, bringing me enough tranquility to pray and prepare for sleep.

I had just blown out my candle and lain down when a deep, low, mighty tone swelled through the night. At first, I knew not what it was, but it rang twelve times. On the twelfth colossal hum and trembling knell, I whispered to the silence, "I lie in the shadow of St. Paul's."
