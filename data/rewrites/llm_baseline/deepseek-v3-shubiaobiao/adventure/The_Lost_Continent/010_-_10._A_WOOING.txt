The murmurs around me swelled into shouts. "Kneel," someone hissed, "kneel, or they'll see you!" Another voice, rougher, threatened, "Bow to the Goddess, or I'll carve you open myself!" The clamor rose like a storm.

But then my name—Deucalion—began to pass through the crowd. The fervor faltered. They might not know much of the man who had ruled Yucatan twenty years ago, but they remembered the one who had ridden beside the Empress beneath the golden canopy, the serpent standard writhing above us. The thought of vengeance cooled their zeal, and the uproar died into uneasy silence.

When Phorenice turned and saw me standing alone among the prostrate worshippers, I stepped forward, passing between the great stones of the circle until I stood before her at the altar. I did not kneel. Instead, I gave the salute she herself had decreed for her chief minister.

"Deucalion," she said, her voice a blade wrapped in silk, "man of ice."

"I remain faithful to the old Gods."

Her lips curled. "I wasn’t speaking of gods."

Before I could reply, Ylga rushed forward, pale and trembling, pleading for my life. "If he won’t forsake the old ways yet, grant him time! He’s strong-willed, not easily swayed. You could mold him later. But think—if you kill him now, who else in Atlantis could serve you as he would?"

Phorenice’s eyes flashed. "You forget your place, little maid. You’re here to tend my wardrobe and fan me, not meddle in my affairs. Back, unless you want to be whipped—or worse. Shall I point at you and watch your pretty flesh rot like Tarca’s?"

Ylga shrank away, cowed. Phorenice turned back to me. "I’ve only just found my wings, Deucalion. I’m eager to test how far they can carry me."

"May every flight serve Atlantis."

She laughed, sharp and bright. "Oh, I know what you truly care for. But we’ll discuss that later, in private." She extended her hand. "Escort me back to my mount."

I took her fingers—warm, delicate, yet calloused from gripping weapons—and led her from the defiled circle. The Sun had not struck her down for her sacrilege, but I knew His judgment would come in His own time.

We reached the mammoth, its red hide dwarfing the golden howdah on its back. She climbed the steps, then gestured for me to join her. The seat beside her was empty; the fan-girl’s place remained unfilled.

"You vanished after the gate fell," she said. "Tell me what happened."

I recounted my escape, careful to praise Nais’s courage, hinting that she might yet be reclaimed for Atlantis. Only Zaemon’s final commands—spoken in the sacred tongue—did I withhold.

"You seem fond of this rebel," Phorenice mused.

"She proved herself more than common rabble."

She sighed, a sound almost genuine. "An Empress loses much that lesser women take for granted."

"Such as?"

"Honest courtship."

"If you claim godhood—"

"Pah! I’ve no wish to be a goddess to *you*, Deucalion. That’s for the rabble—it gives me power. You priests know the trick of calling fire; I merely borrowed it. Must you always be so stiff? Can’t you see the woman beside you, not the crown?"

I forced a smile. "Perhaps I’ve been too patient."

"Patient? I’ve played the maiden long enough. I’m a woman now, and I won’t wait forever."

I clasped her hand. "Then take me as your husband. But know this—I’ll be no puppet."

Her eyes gleamed. "I’ve ruled alone too long. It might be... refreshing to yield sometimes."

A distant explosion rocked the air—rebels hurling fire-pots into the city. The mammoth’s ears twitched.

"You’ll teach me what love is," she murmured.

"If I knew myself."

She leaned closer. "Then we’ll learn together."

But the beast’s patience frayed. Smoke curled into the street. The mammoth trumpeted, its terror of fire overriding training. It bolted, crushing guards and scattering crowds, the golden howdah lurching wildly.

I braced, gripping a pillar, pulling Phorenice against me. "Hold tight!"

She laughed, exhilarated. "You’re a better shield than I expected!"

The mammoth charged through the city, past pyramids and slums, until the harbor glittered ahead. It plunged into the shallows, sinking into the sludge. The howdah pitched; we were thrown clear.

We swam for the galley that had raced to intercept us. The port-captain groveled, offering prisoners to appease the beast.

Phorenice waved him off. "Bring the woman. Let’s see if she’s a lovelorn fool or a traitor."

The captain dragged forth a prisoner—the girl from the circus, the one I’d saved from the tigers.

Phorenice’s smile turned knife-sharp. "Ah. Nais."

The girl lifted her chin, defiant even in chains.

The Empress tilted her head. "Tell me, little rebel, was it worth it? Risking everything for a man who’ll never be yours?"

Nais said nothing.

Phorenice sighed. "How tedious." She turned to me. "Shall I be merciful today, Deucalion? Or shall we see how deep her loyalty runs?"

The choice, I knew, was another test.

And the game had only just begun.
