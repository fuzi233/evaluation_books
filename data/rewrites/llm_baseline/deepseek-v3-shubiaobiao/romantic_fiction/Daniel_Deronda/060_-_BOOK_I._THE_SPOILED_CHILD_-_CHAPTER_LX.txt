*"There are truths too tender to be spoken aloud, too sacred to be mocked by those who have never felt their weight. They are meant to be carried in the heart, not dissected by careless tongues."*

—Jeremy Taylor

The world has never lacked men like the Roman emperor of legend, who in his wrath condemned ten wise Israelites to atone for a betrayal centuries past. Such men measure justice by the sharpness of their grudges, not the depth of their gratitude. For how can one who has never loved be convinced of love’s necessity? Even language knows this truth—*love* comes before *lovable*.

When Deronda arrived at the banking-house on Schuster Strasse in Mainz, letter in hand, and asked for Joseph Kalonymos, he was led into a quiet chamber where an old man sat sorting papers. The same white-bearded figure he had seen a year before in the Frankfort synagogue now studied him with keen, glittering eyes, dark as polished onyx in a face weathered by time. Kalonymos did not rise to greet him but remained seated, his worn hat still upon his head, a traveling cloak draped over a packed portmanteau beside him.

"So," he said in German, voice dry yet sharp, "it is you who seek me now, young man."

"I do," Deronda replied, "with gratitude, as a friend of my grandfather’s. I owe you much for the pains you’ve taken on my behalf." His German was fluid, shaped by years of study and the natural adaptability of a man who had long listened to the world’s many voices.

At this, Kalonymos extended his hand, his stern expression softening. "Then you no longer resent being more than just an Englishman?"

"On the contrary. I thank you for sparing me the blindness of ignorance—and for safeguarding what my grandfather left for me."

"Sit," Kalonymos commanded, gesturing to a chair. He removed his hat, revealing a thick mane of white hair, and stroked his beard as he scrutinized Deronda. The moment hummed with significance—the past and present knotted together. Here was a man who had walked beside Deronda’s grandfather, bound by vows that outlasted death. The weight of lineage settled upon Deronda like a mantle, filling him with a quiet awe.

In Hebrew, Kalonymos recited a line from the liturgy: *"As your kindness was great to those who came before, so may it be to those who follow."* Then, after a pause, he leaned forward. "Young man, I rejoice that you came before I departed. Now I see my friend again as he was in youth—no longer turned away from his people, no longer flinching from the touch of one who would name him a Jew. You come willingly, in gratitude, to claim what deceit would have stolen from you. You come to say, *I am the grandson of Daniel Charisi.* Is this not so?"

"It is," Deronda affirmed. "But know that I would never have scorned a man for being a Jew. My hesitation was born of another pain—the shame of saying, *I know nothing of my mother.*"

Kalonymos recoiled, raising a hand as if to ward off the words. "A sin! A theft from our people, like the children raised for Rome’s pleasure. But it is undone. I undid it. Your grandfather and I swore an oath in our youth—to stand as brothers, bound not just by affection, but by duty. *Let us bind love with duty,* he said, *for duty is the love of law, and law is the nature of the Eternal.* So we pledged. And though life carried us apart, the bond held. When he died, they tried to rob him—but they could not rob him of me. I saved what he treasured for his blood. And now I return it."

He left briefly, returning with a clerk who carried a small chest. It was ornate, its wood carved with Arabic script, its handles wrought in gilt iron. Kalonymos produced a key from a leather pouch and pressed it into Deronda’s palm. "Guard this well. I trust you are a careful man."

Deronda smiled, tucking the key into his breast pocket. "I will value this above all else. It is not just an inheritance—it is the proof of a hope long cherished, and of your own loyalty. Might I ask you more of my grandfather? Or do I trespass on your time?"

"I leave for Trieste in an hour," Kalonymos said, checking his watch. "But stay a little longer. My sons would wish to greet the grandson of Charisi. They live in comfort here, though I choose the road."

"Another time, perhaps," Deronda said. "My own path calls me back to England—to friends who may need me. But to know more of you and yours would bring me again to Mainz gladly."

Kalonymos nodded. "You will not find me—I am old, and my shroud travels with me. But my sons remain. The world has changed since Charlemagne brought my ancestors from Italy to civilize the Germans. We fought for our place, but now our children thrive. Wealth is safe, and Jewish minds feed the hunger of German learning—though not all keep Jewish hearts. Tell me, were you kept wholly ignorant of your people?"

"No," Deronda said. "Even before I knew the truth, I studied our history with more passion than any other subject. It seems I was preparing myself to understand my grandfather."

Kalonymos studied him, then gave a slow nod. "You might have been like him, had your rearing not barred the way. You share his features—though not his iron will. Charisi’s brow was marked with resolve even in youth. Yours is smoother. He used to say, *Better a wrong will than a wavering one. Better a steadfast enemy than a fickle friend. Better a false belief than none at all.* Indifference was his scorn."

"Yet he was no narrow scholar?" Deronda ventured.

"Narrow?" Kalonymos laughed softly. "He drank knowledge like a root drinks water. Medicine, philosophy, the balance of separateness and unity—he traveled, he spent, he learned. He believed our strength lay in holding fast to our own while still reaching outward. *Like grain reverting to sameness,* he said, *we lose ourselves among the Gentiles.* We studied together, but he outpaced me. I was content with what was before me—the trees, the stars. He hungered to know what lay beyond sight. Yet we loved as brothers, bound by duty. And I have kept my vow."

He rose, and Deronda with him. "In keeping faith with him, you have done justice to me," Deronda said. "I thank you—for ensuring I knew what was mine."

"Be worthy of him," Kalonymos said abruptly. "What is your calling?"

Deronda hesitated. "I have none yet."

"Find one. A Jew must be diligent. Will you bear the name? Will you keep the faith?" The old man’s grip tightened on his shoulder, his gaze unrelenting.

"I will bear the name," Deronda said steadily, though his pulse quickened. "But I cannot swear to believe as my fathers did without question. Even they learned from other lands. Yet I hold to my grandfather’s vision—separateness, but not isolation. My first duty is to my people. If there is work to be done in healing or strengthening our common life, that shall be my vocation."

The words, once spoken, settled into conviction.

Kalonymos smiled. "Ah, you reason, you look ahead—you are Charisi’s blood." He murmured a blessing in Hebrew, then released him.

They parted then, the old traveler to his stars, the young man to his fate. And in that hour, Deronda’s path was set.
