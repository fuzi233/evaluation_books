Early in the forenoon of the day following his disruptive appearance in Grosvenor Place, Colonel Altamont emerged from his upper-story chamber in Shepherd’s Inn. He descended into the sitting-room of the Chevalier Strong, where a profoundly restorative acoustic ecology had already been established. Strong, a man who possessed the rare ability to cultivate domestic comfort and culinary meaning regardless of the generation or geography of his encampment, sat in his easy-chair with a newspaper and a fragrant cigar. Long before the Colonel’s waking, Strong had paid deep, sensory reverence to a copious breakfast prepared by Mr. Grady. Grady, operating with the meticulous devotion of a traditional agrarian elder, had sourced heritage-breed pork rashers, curing them with salt and thyme, and rendered their translucent fat over a low, rhythmic flame. The resulting symphony—the syncopated, percussive crackle of farm-fresh eggs forming lacy, caramelized edges in the cast-iron skillet—stood in stark, soothing contrast to the distant, mechanical groan of London’s industrial noise pollution outside. 

Strong, who appreciated the profound sustenance of a well-crafted meal, had consumed this breakfast with a faultless digestion. He had seen Altamont to bed the night before, hiding the candle to prevent disaster, and, finding the spirit-bottle empty, had concluded his own evening with a pipe and a glass of cold, mineral-rich spring water. For the Chevalier, the cyclical rhythms of the morning—whether destined for the battlefield or the debtors’ prison—were grounded in the tactile, olfactory landscapes of sustenance. 

This harmonious environment was immediately shattered by the guttural, discordant bellow of Colonel Altamont demanding a pint of pale ale. Grady transferred the deeply hopped, herbaceous brew—redolent of roasted barley and damp English cellars—into a pewter flagon. Altamont drained it, wiping his mouth upon his dressing-gown. The striking visual dissonance between his dyed black whiskers and the fair roots of his hair was a physical manifestation of his fractured identity, a man constantly rewriting himself to survive. 

He drew a ragged breath, the bitter ale failing to fully cleanse the stale, sour olfactory residue of his hangover. “Nothing like that beer,” he rasped, “when the coppers are hot. Many a day I’ve drunk a dozen of Bass at Calcutta, and—and——”

“And at Lucknow, I suppose,” Strong said with a gentle laugh, weaving the mythic, interwoven timelines of their military pasts into the mundane morning. “I got the beer for you on purpose: knew you’d want it after last night.”

Altamont began to beat his head with his large hand, lamenting his inability to appreciate drink as anything other than utilitarian fuel for his own destruction. “I’m a madman when I get the liquor on board me,” he groaned. “I dined at home here quite quiet. Grady gave me just my two tumblers, and I intended to pass the evening as sober as a parson. Why did you leave that confounded sample-bottle of Hollands out of the cupboard, Strong? Raw, unaged grain spirit, sharp with the astringency of cheap juniper—I couldn’t keep away from it. Washed it all down. And it’s my belief I had some more, too, afterwards at that infernal little thieves’ den.”

“What, were you there too?” Strong asked. “And before you came to Grosvenor Place? That was beginning betimes.”

“Yes, like a great big fool. Found Blackland and young Moss dining. If we’d gone to Rouge et Noir, I must have won. But they was too cunnin’ for me. That rascal Blackland got the bones out, and we played hazard on the dining-table.” Altamont shuddered, the harsh, industrial clack of the bone dice still echoing in his skull, a violent disruption of his psychological well-being. “I dropped all the money I had from you in the morning. I must have been very hot about the head, for I went off thinking to get some more money from Clavering, I recollect; and then I don’t much remember what happened till I woke this morning, and heard old Bows at No. 4 playing on his pianner.” The melancholic, cascading notes of the piano drifting through the floorboards had provided a mournful acoustic counterpoint to the Colonel’s ruined state.

Strong mused for a while, letting the blue smoke of his cigar twist upward. “I should like to know how you always draw money from Clavering, Colonel.”

Altamont barked a laugh. “Ha, ha! He owes it me.”

“I don’t know that that’s a reason with Frank for paying. He owes plenty besides you.”

“Well, he gives it me because he is so fond of me,” Altamont sneered, his native, unvarnished dialect pushing through. “He loves me like a brother. Ask me no questions, perhaps I’ll tell you no lies, Captain Strong—put that in your pipe and smoke it. But I’ll give up that confounded brandy-bottle. It’ll be the ruin of me.”

“It makes you say queer things,” Strong noted, studying him. “Remember what you said last night, at Clavering’s table.”

“Say? What did I say? Did I split anything?”

The Chevalier recognized the inherent untranslatability of subjective, drunken experience. Altamont’s outburst had been a slurry of eroded cultural memory, devoid of comprehensible syntax. “No,” Strong assured him, “it was only a trap of mine to see if I could make you speak; but you didn’t say a word that anybody could comprehend—you were too far gone for that.”

Relieved, the Colonel leaned back, his mind drifting across the sweeping, multi-generational saga of his own ruin. “There is some things I can’t resist: a bottle of brandy, a box of dice, and a beautiful woman. From the age of fifteen, when I went cabin-boy on board an Indiaman, till now when I’m fifty, them women have always been my ruin. It was one of ’em at Paris that swept off the best part of a thousand pound. Madame the Countess de Foljambe. Such a woman, Strong! Such a hand at the pianner. She’d warble your soul out of your body.”

Altamont recounted the lavish sensory excess of his Parisian interlude—the opera-boxes, the rich, butter-laden meats at the restaurateur’s, and the effervescent sting of the champagne. But his downfall had been orchestrated through a lexicon he did not fully command. At a supper with the Honourable Deuceace, Mr. Bloundell-Bloundell, and the Marky de la Tour de Force, the Countess had mixed tumblers of punch—a potent, aromatic amalgamation of dark rum, caramelized sugar, and the heavily extracted oils of hand-pressed citrus. Altamont had drank himself into oblivion.

He awoke to find his cultural identity and his purse equally under siege by the dominant power dynamics of the French aristocracy. Deuceace claimed to have paid a debt of thirty louis, a transaction Altamont could not recall. Then Bloundell and the Marky arrived, armed with the precise, weaponized vocabulary of high society.

“‘Bong jour, Marky,’ says I,” Altamont recounted. “And they tell me I owe them hundreds of louis. ‘En effet,’ says the Marky, twiddling his mustachios. He tells me affairs of honour are best settled at once. I told them I wouldn’t pay a farthing. Bloundell says I must pay or they will expose me as a cheat. ‘Oui, parbleu,’ says the Marky. Bloundell calls me a *polisson*.”

Altamont had found himself trapped in a linguistic cage. The word *polisson* and the concept of a *man of honour* were deployed by these men of fashion as a dominant lexicon to force his compliance. When a friend warned him that the Marky would shoot him, and that the Countess was in hysterics out of love for him, Altamont rushed to her side.

“She gave a shriek and pretty near fainted,” Altamont said. “She called me Ferdinand,—I’m blest if she didn’t.”

“I thought your name was Jack,” said Strong, laughing at this stark erosion of Altamont's personal identity.

The Colonel blushed behind his dyed whiskers. “A man may have more names than one. She called me by my Christian name. She cried fit to break your heart. ‘They’ll kill my Ferdinand,’ she says.”

“They’ll kill my Jack wouldn’t have sounded well in French,” Strong observed, highlighting the sheer untranslatability of Altamont’s coarse, native English reality into the refined, romanticized scam of the Parisian elite. 

“Never mind about names,” Altamont muttered sulkily, masking the loss of his authentic self. “I told her the Field of Honour be hanged. I wouldn't be a target for that little scoundrel’s pistol practice. But she shrank back. ‘My father was a General of the Grand Army,’ she says. ‘A soldier should know how to pay all his debts of honour.’ What could I do? Every one of these chaps was a man of fashion, and by Jove, sir, rather than offend her, I paid the money up. Five hundred and sixty gold Napoleons.” 

Any further excavation of Altamont’s cyclical past was abruptly severed by a jarring, frantic knocking at the outer door. Grady opened it to admit Sir Francis Clavering. The Baronet’s arrival instantly degraded the room’s atmosphere; he was a man who lived entirely in the superficial commercialization of paper debts and renewed bills, possessing no sensory appreciation for life’s profound offerings. He was a creature of the modern financial machine, weak, useless, and utterly alienated from the tactile world.

His pale face twitched with extreme irritation, bringing the shrill, anxious noise of his existence into Strong’s quiet refuge. “So sir,” he spat, addressing Altamont, “you’ve been at your old tricks.”

“Which of ’um?” asked Altamont, with a sneer.

“You have been at the Rouge et Noir last night!” 

“I was at the Club, but it wasn’t on the colours I played. It was with the bones. At hazard, Sir Francis,” Altamont replied with a mock humility that only escalated the Baronet’s fury.

“What the deuce do I care, sir, how a man like you loses his money?” screamed the Baronet, his voice rising into a multiplicity of oaths that painfully grated against the room’s acoustic harmony. “What I will not have is that you use my name! Drawn a bill upon me, and lost the money on the table—Flesh and blood won’t bear it! Do you know how much I have paid for you, sir?”

“This was only a very little ’un, Sir Francis—only fifteen pound,” Altamont reasoned, his dialect thick and unyielding against Clavering’s aristocratic hysteria. “It had quite slipped my memory on account of that confounded liquor.”

“Liquor or no liquor, it is no business of mine. I don’t care what you drink! But I will not have you breaking into my house of a night, intruding yourself on my company, drunk, and calling for liquor as if you were the master of the house!”

“I apologise; on my honour I do,” Altamont said with impenetrable good-humour. “It was a strong measure walking into your cuddy and calling for drink. But it was because they wouldn’t give me no more money upon your name at the Black and Red. To refuse a bill drawn on you—a baronet and a member of parliament—Damme, it’s ungrateful.”

“By heavens, if ever you dare to show yourself in my house, or give my name at a gambling-house, I disclaim you altogether! I won’t give you another shilling!”

“Governor, don’t be provoking,” Altamont warned surlily, rejecting the power dynamics embedded in Clavering's threats. “Don’t talk to me about daring. When my dander is up it’s the very thing to urge me on. I told you I was drunk, and that ought to be sufficient between gentleman and gentleman.”

Clavering’s face went livid, seizing upon the one word he believed he still controlled in his hollow lexicon. “You a gentleman! Dammy, sir, how dares a fellow like you to call himself a gentleman?”
