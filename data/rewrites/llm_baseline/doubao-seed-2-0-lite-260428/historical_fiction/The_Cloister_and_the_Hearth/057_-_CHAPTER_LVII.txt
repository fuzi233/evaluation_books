Barely seven days had slipped by, and the two friends sat side by side at their workbench in their cramped, sun-warmed Roman garret, though their spirits could not have been more unalike. Pietro dashed at his half-finished fine art panel in fitful bursts, turning out strokes of breathtaking genius in the space of a few breathless minutes, only to fling down his brush a moment later and curse the very canvas he had just blessed. Then, white-hot with creative fury, he would snatch up his tools again, labour like one possessed for an hour, and finally collapse back in his chair with a guttural groan of despair. All the while, the steady, canny Dutchman beside him worked on, calm as a millpond, a faint, constant smile playing about his lips.

To put it plainly, Gerard – who had never yet made a friend he could not gently steer to shared purpose – had harnessed Pietro’s wild, unruly talent to a far more practical end. The two were painting custom playing cards to put bread on the table and keep their landlord from the door.

When the last of the stack was finished, the indignant Pietro swept up his own masterpiece to set out on his daily, fruitless round of calls on wealthy Roman patrons. Gerard pressed him to take the newly finished cards along too, and try to sell them for whatever they could get. Pietro’s face tightened, coiled as a rattlesnake ready to strike, but after a long, hot moment he softened, embraced Gerard in the warm, quick Italian fashion, and tucked the deck under his arm – first laying the still slightly damp top cards out to crisp in the strong, golden Roman sun that gilded the window sills that season.

Left to his own devices, Gerard scribbled a pair of elegant Greek calligraphy strokes for a small commission he was picking at, then fetched a needle and thread to mend a tiny rent in his wool hose. His landlady found him thus occupied, and leant in the doorway to tease him, irony lacing her tone: “Have we no women left in the whole house, that a fine young man must stoop to mending his own clothes? Once you’re done patching that, come down and speak to Teresa – the friend I told you of a while back, the one married to a good-for-nothing lout who never stops boasting of his acquaintance with every great lord in the city.”

Gerard trundled down the stairs after her, and when he stepped into the parlour, who should be sitting there waiting but the Roman matron whose small son he had saved from drowning not long before. “Ah, madama!” he exclaimed, delight flooding his face. “It is you! The good dame never told me it was you I was to meet. And the little fair-haired lad – is he well? Did he take no lasting harm from that dreadful adventure in the drifting boat?”

“He is perfectly well,” Teresa said, her throat tight with unspoken gratitude.

“What in the world are you two on about?” the landlady cut in, staring from one to the other in frank bewilderment. “And why are you trembling so hard, my dear Teresa mia?”

“He saved my child’s life,” Teresa said, forcing herself to steady her voice.

“What? My quiet little lodger? And he never breathed a single word of it to me?” The landlady rounded on Gerard, half mock-indignant. “Art thou not ashamed to look me in the face and keep such a grand, kind deed hidden?”

“Oh, do not speak harshly to him,” Teresa pleaded, turning to her friend and pouring out a glowing, vivid account of the day Gerard had dragged her boy from the Tiber. Gerard stood blushing like a shy schoolgirl the whole time, hardly able to recognise his own small act of kindness when the matron’s fond gratitude painted it in such glowing colours.

“To think I once asked you to put in a good word for this young man,” the landlady laughed, turning to Gerard. “I knew nothing of him save that you spoke well of him, Fiammina – and that was enough for me. Dear boy, helping you succeed will feel like paying back a debt I never even knew I owed.”

For the next hour, the two women chattered eagerly, plotting every possible way to help Gerard break through the thick, tangled wall of bribes, hidden fees, and petty chicanery that stood between unknown young artists and the wealthy art patrons of the Eternal City. Teresa smiled a sad, knowing smile when Gerard told her he had left samples of his work propped on the doorsteps of great men’s palaces. “What, no promise to share a cut of the sale with the doormen? Not even a single coin to slip the servants so they will actually show your work to the masters of the house?” she said, shaking her head. “You might as well have tossed every sketch straight into the Tiber. They will use them to stoke the kitchen fires before their masters ever lay eyes on them.”

“Alas, what is a poor artist to do then?” Gerard sighed, dismayed. “We are not rich men, we cannot afford to scatter bribes at every gate.”

“Then you ought to go to a noble city that is not half so greedy as this one,” Teresa said. “The Roman court will not take a sheep that does not come already shorn of every last coin it has.” She sank into quiet thought, then told him she would come back to see him again at the same time the next day.

The moment Teresa had stepped out the door, Pietro stormed back up the steps, his face black as a thunderstorm. Gerard exchanged a quick, worried glance with his landlady, then hurried upstairs to comfort his friend. “What, did none of them buy your masterpiece?” he asked gently.

“Same as every other day,” Pietro growled.

“Fools, all of them,” Gerard said.

“That is not the worst of it,” Pietro snarled.

“What on earth has gone wrong, then?”

“They bought the cards!” Pietro yelled, flailing his fists wildly at the empty air.

“All the better!” Gerard said, brightening.

“They practically pounced on me the second I laid them out,” Pietro ranted. “They were utterly enraptured, could not hide how badly they wanted them even when they tried. I saw the longing in their eyes, played the hard-to-get fool, and picked their pockets clean – curse those witless boobies!” He flung a dozen small silver coins clattering across the floor, then danced on them, his eyes blazing like a serpent’s, kicking them across the garret until they skittered under every last stick of furniture.

Gerard dropped straight to his knees, chasing the poor maltreated coins across the floor, tucking every last one tenderly into his purse. “You ought to smile at their foolishness, my friend, and turn it to good profit,” he said.

“Oh, I will turn it to profit,” Pietro said, his voice thick with simmering indignation. “The brutes! We will paint a full pack of cards every single day, we will have the whole city gambling and ruining their households, and we will live like princes off their vices and their stupidity. Though there was one queen in that last pack I almost could not bear to sell. You painted her, brother – the one with the red-brown hair like ripe chestnuts, and eyes as blue as sapphires, and more than that, a soul you could see shining right through the paint.”

“Pietro,” Gerard said softly, “I painted that one straight from my heart.” The quick-witted Italian nodded, his eyes twinkling with understanding. “You love her that much, and yet you left her behind?”

“Pietro, it is because I love her more than anything in the world that I have wandered all this long, weary road so far from home.”

Their quiet conversation was cut short by the landlady shouting up the stairs from the bottom of the landing: “Gerard! Come down at once, you are wanted!” He hurried down the steps, to find Teresa waiting for him by the door, holding her shawl. “Come with me, Ser Gerard,” she said, and held out her hand.
