The morning at Roville-sur-Mer unfolded with the predictable rhythm of a seaside resort: a slow assembly of visitors onto the golden sand, where whiskered fathers formed cheerful clusters and their female companions sought shade beneath colorful parasols. Children dug industriously, pausing only to strike one another with their spades, while dogs roamed aimlessly. Among them, a poodle of distinctly military bearing approached Sally, noting her possession of a sweet box and deciding to wait.

Sally sat with her back against a hillock, watching this familiar tableau with half-closed eyes. Her vacation had been a month of lazy, drifting happiness, carried from town to town until the glitter of Roville—the blue sky, the Casino, the snow-white hotels along the promenade—anchored her. Yet the anchor held only temporarily. America called. Gerald's letter, arriving with the urgency of a busy man, announced that "The Primrose Way" would open in Detroit, requiring her immediate return. It was a scrappy note, unsatisfactory in tone, but impossible to ignore. Tomorrow, she would leave. Today, however, belonged to the stillness of the beach, to the warmth of the sun and the faint murmur of waves.

This tranquility, usually refreshing, carried a specific danger: the tendency to drift into sleep. To doze so soon after breakfast, even on holiday, felt undignified. Sally fought the urge, but the heat was insistent, the whispers of the sea persuasive. Just as consciousness threatened to fade, voices pierced the haze. They spoke English—a rarity in Roville—and the sound of a familiar tongue snapped her awake. A few feet away, two men sat on the sand.

Sally had long enjoyed the game of assigning characters to strangers, a pastime marked by consistent failure. She glanced at the first man. Tall, dark, with a tight mouth and high cheekbones that suggested a perpetual struggle with facial hair, he possessed a vaguely sinister air. He shaved meticulously, maintaining a self-control that bordered on the theatrical.

"Hard," Sally diagnosed. "A lawyer, perhaps. Unpleasant."

She turned to the second man, who had been staring at her with an intensity that suggested he had finally found something worth observing after years of disappointment. Sturdy, thick-set, with a freckled face and the reddest hair she had ever seen, he wore a square chin marked by a fresh cut.

"A temper," she noted mentally. "Quick, but fleeting. Not brilliant, but agreeable."

Her assessment was interrupted by the dark man, who, in a display of competence that defined his entire demeanor, managed to light a cigarette despite the wind. He resumed a conversation with his companion.

"And how is Scrymgeour?"

"Oh, fine," the redhead replied absently, though his eyes remained fixed on Sally.

"I was surprised to see him here. He intended to stay in Paris."

A brief silence followed. Sally offered a piece of nougat to the attentive poodle.

"Sally," the redhead said, his voice vibrating with sudden, intense conviction, "that is the prettiest girl I have ever seen!"

Startled, yet not displeased, Sally regarded him. Broad-minded, she accepted this as a valid observation of public interest. His companion, however, recoiled in shock.

"My dear fellow!"

"It's all right," the redhead insisted, unmoved. "She doesn't understand. No one in this blasted place speaks English. Without my scraps of French, I'd be starving. That girl..." He paused, unable to suppress his enthusiasm. "She's a topper! I swear, I've never seen anyone compare. Look at those hands, those feet. You don't find them anywhere else. Granted, her mouth is a bit wide," he admitted reluctantly.

Sally's stillness, combined with the dark man's concern over the locals' linguistic limitations, reassured him. He exhaled, relieved not to be implicated in impropriety.

"You must be careful," he warned austerely.

He shifted his gaze to Sally, who was now divided between the poodle and a new arrival, a ragged mongrel, before turning back to Scrymgeour.

"How is Scrymgeour's digestion?"

"Do you notice how her hair curls over her ears?" the redhead asked, distracted. "Pretty much the same, I suppose."

"What hotel are you staying at?"

"The Normandie."

Sally dipped into her box for another chocolate cream, giving an imperceptible start. She also stayed at the Normandie. Presuming he was a recent arrival, she wondered why she hadn't noticed him.

"The Normandie?" The dark man frowned. "I know Roville by reputation, but I've never heard of a Hotel Normandie. Where is it?"

"It's a shack near the station. Not much of a place. Cheap, though. Cooking is decent."

His companion's confusion deepened.

"What on earth is a man like Scrymgeour doing there?" he exclaimed, sensing an urgent need to know more about the absent figure whose name had grown strangely familiar. "If there's one thing he's fussy about..."

"There are at least eleven thousand things he's fussy about," the redhead interrupted, sounding disapproving. "That jumpy old fool!"

"If there's one thing he's particular about, it's the quality of his lodging. Since I've known him, he's always demanded the best. I expected the Splendide." He mused briefly, accepting the necessity of accommodating a wealthy eccentric. "I'd like to see him again. Tell him to dine with me at the Splendide tonight. Eight o'clock sharp."

Sally, occupied with the growing pack of dogs—including a white terrier with a black patch over its left eye—could not see the redhead's expression. But his voice betrayed him; it carried a false lightness.

"Oh, Scrymgeour isn't in Roville."

"No? Where is he?"

"Paris, I believe."

"What!" The dark man's tone sharpened, resembling a cross-examination. "Then why aren't you there? What are you doing here? Did he send you on holiday?"

"Yes, he did."

"When do you rejoin him?"

"I don't."

"What!"

The redhead's demeanor was not particularly stubborn.

"Well, if you want to know," he said, "the old blighter fired me the day before yesterday."

Sand shifted as the dark man sprang up. Sally, absorbed in the unfolding drama, absent-mindedly fed the poodle a treat that rightfully belonged to the terrier. She glanced sideways to see the dark man standing rigid, evoking the stern father of melodrama preparing to cast his erring son out into the snow. The redhead, outwardly stoic, stared down the beach at a fat bather in an orange suit who, after six failed attempts, finally entered the water, floating with the dignified helplessness of a deflated balloon.

"Do you mean to tell me," the dark man demanded, "that after all the trouble the family took to secure you a position with endless possibilities, provided you behaved, you have deliberately thrown it away?" A despairing gesture completed his sentence. "Good God, you're hopeless!"

The redhead made no reply, continuing to watch the bather. Few outdoor spectacles rival the stimulation of watching middle-aged Frenchmen bathe. From the tentative testing of the water to the final, seal-like plunge, the drama is relentless. Yet the former employee of Mr. Scrymgeour viewed this free circus with zero enjoyment.

"It's maddening! What are you going to do? What do you expect us to do? Are we to spend our lives securing positions you refuse to keep? I can tell you we're... it's monstrous! It's sickening! Good God!"

With these words, the dark man, feeling the futility of language as Sally sometimes did in her brother Fillmore's company, turned sharply and stalked away up the beach. His exit was momentarily compromised when his straw hat blew off and was promptly trodden upon by a passing child.

Behind him lingered an electric calm, the stunned silence following a thunderclap. This tranquility might have persisted, but within a minute, it shattered into terrestrial uproar. With an abruptness heralded by a single, low gurgling snarl, the prettiest dogfight of the season erupted.

The terrier with the black patch initiated it. His loyal companion, eager to support his case, could not deny firing the first shot. But justice demands acknowledgment of Sally's role. Absorbed in the drama and acutely curious about Scrymgeour's decision to dismiss the redhead, she had inadvertently aided the poodle three times in succession. The third instance was too much for the terrier.

Any dogfight possesses a wild, gusty fury that induces a sense of helpless awe in observers, a feeling of being outside one's jurisdiction. This was no ordinary skirmish. It was a stunning mêlée that would have impressed even the most cynical critics. Dogs of every size, breed, and color raced to the scene; some barked from the sidelines, while others engaged in general combat, content to fight without regard for origin. The terrier gripped the poodle by the left hind leg, restating his war aims. The mongrel attempted to engage a stranger of the Sealyham family.
