Within a few minutes the regiments appointed for the flanking march had slipped away, their feet falling with a practised softness along the lee of the rising ground, so that even the sharp-eared scouts of Twala might mistake their going for the ordinary hushes and stirrings of a camp. The pause that followed had in it a strange domestic quiet, as though the whole army, for half an hour, had drawn the same careful breath a cook draws while waiting for a stubborn pot to come to the boil—knowing that the smallest disturbance might spoil the work.

Only after that interval did the Greys and their supporting regiment, the Buffaloes—the chest—bestir themselves. Both were almost fresh: the Greys had been held back in the morning and had lost little save in that successful sweep when I had charged with them and paid for it by being stunned; and the Buffaloes, third line of defence on the left, had scarcely been forced into the press at all. They stood now as full as granaries after harvest, and just as grimly purposeful.

Old Infadoos, wary as an elder who has counted seasons and knows how spirits must be tended before hard labour, used the delay to speak to the Greys. His words came in their poetical fashion—praise, promise, and the old songs of duty—telling them what honour it was to stand in the forefront, telling them, too, that the great white warrior from the Stars would fight in their ranks, and that cattle and promotion would be given to those who lived to taste tomorrow.

I looked along the long black lines—waving plumes, faces cut hard as dried hides—and my throat tightened. Most of them were men past forty, veterans with the patience of old fields that have borne crop after crop, and within the space of an hour the greater part must lie down in the dust and never rise. It was not cruelty for cruelty’s sake; it was that cold, wise recklessness by which a general saves the body by sacrificing the ribs. The Greys were being sent like a well-fed ox to the slaughter-yard so the herd might pass the winter. They understood it. Not a face flinched. There they stood—about to quit the light of day—and looked upon it as if it were simply another task set by custom and king. I confess my own mind was far less composed, and my envy of their steadiness had in it something bitter, like chewing a root one has not learned to like.

“Behold your king!” Infadoos ended, pointing at Ignosi. “Go fight and fall for him, as is the duty of brave men; cursed and shameful for ever be the name of him who shrinks from death for his king, or who turns his back to the foe. Do your homage to the sacred Snake, and then follow on, that Incubu and I may show you a road to the heart of Twala’s host.”

A pause—then the army answered, not in speech, but in sound that moved through bone. From the serried phalanxes rose a murmur like the far whisper of sea on a night without wind: six thousand spear-handles tapping gently against shield rims, one to one, as if a vast household were beating time before a song. The whisper swelled and deepened into a rolling roar that struck the mountain faces and came back, wave upon wave, until the air itself seemed thick with it. Then it ebbed away by degrees, and in the sudden emptiness the royal salute crashed out.

Ignosi lifted his battle-axe in acknowledgement, and the Greys began to file off in triple line—about a thousand fighting men to each, not counting officers. When the last companies had marched some five hundred yards, Ignosi took his place at the head of the Buffaloes, also drawn in threefold order, and gave the word. Off we went. I, as may be imagined, put up the most sincere prayers that I might come out of it with my skin untorn. I have been in queer corners; never in one quite so full of the certainty that my body might be turned from a vessel for breath into mere meat.

When we reached the edge of the plateau the Greys were already half-way down the slope toward the green tongue of land that thrust itself into the mountain bend like the frog of a horse’s foot into the shoe. Beyond, on the plain, Twala’s camp boiled with movement. Regiment after regiment started forward at that long swinging trot of theirs, eager to seize the root of the tongue before our force could spill out into the open of Loo.

The tongue ran some four hundred yards deep. Even at its root, its widest, it was scarcely six hundred and fifty paces across; at the tip, not ninety. The Greys, descending and narrowing into column, reached the place where the strip broadened again, spread back into their triple line, and halted—dead, silent.

We—the Buffaloes—moved down behind them and took our place in reserve, a hundred yards back and a little higher. From there we could see Twala’s whole host, reinforced since the morning and still, for all their losses, not less than forty thousand. They came on swiftly—then hesitated at the root when they understood the throat of the pass: only one regiment could go in at a time, and there, seventy yards from the mouth, unassailable except from the front because of the boulder-strewn walls to either side, stood the Greys—famous, dreaded—ready to hold the way as a man might bar a doorway with his own body.

They stopped. There was no hunger in them for this meal.

Presently a tall general in the usual nodding ostrich plumes came forward with chiefs and orderlies—Twala, I thought. He gave an order. The first regiment raised a shout and charged up at the Greys. The Greys did not stir or answer until the attackers were within forty yards and a volley of tollas rattled among their ranks like stones flung on a roof.

Then, with a bound and a roar, the Greys sprang forward. The meeting was instant and terrible. The roll of shields struck our ears like thunder; in the clear air, with no smoke of firearms to veil it, the plain flickered with spear-points catching light. The mass swayed and stabbed—humanity kneading itself into slaughter—until suddenly the attacking line thinned, and with a slow, long heave the Greys passed over it, as a great wave passes over a hidden ridge. That regiment was destroyed. But the Greys had only two lines left: a third of their number lay down and did not rise.

They closed up shoulder to shoulder and halted again in silence. My heart lifted to catch sight of Sir Henry’s yellow beard moving to and fro as he set men in place. He lived.

We moved forward onto ground carpeted with bodies—dead, dying, wounded—stained red, thick as fallen fruit under a tree after a storm. Ignosi sent the order down that none of the enemy’s wounded were to be killed. So far as I saw, it was obeyed. The cries and groans, if one listened, were like a village after a fire—so many voices that the mind, for sheer self-defence, refuses to translate them into sense.

A second regiment, marked by white plumes, kilts, and shields, advanced against the remaining two thousand Greys. Again the Greys waited in that dreadful stillness until the foe was within forty yards, then hurled themselves in. Again the clash came—awful, heavy, unrelenting.

This time the issue hung longer. The attackers were young men, fighting with a fury that was like green wood flaring hot. By weight and rage they seemed to drive the veterans back. Hundreds fell every minute. Above the shouting and the groans, and woven through the music of clashing spear on shield, ran that thin hissing undertone—“S’gee, s’gee”—the small triumph each victor breathed as his assegai went through flesh and out again.

But discipline, steady as a hand that never forgets the true measure of salt, began to tell. A veteran is worth two young ones when the press is close and breath is short. Just when we thought the Greys must be finished and were readying ourselves to take their place, Sir Henry’s deep voice rang through the din. I saw his battle-axe circling high above his plumes. The Greys ceased to give. They held, rock-still, and the young regiment broke against them like surf, recoiling, returning, recoiling again. Then the Greys began to move—forward.

Ignosi stood beside me, teeth grinding with excitement. “Ah, these are men indeed; they will conquer again. See, it is done!”

Suddenly the white-plumed regiment broke away in flying groups, their head-dresses streaming like torn cloth in the wind. The Greys were victors—yet no longer a regiment. Of the triple line that had entered three thousand strong forty minutes before, there remained perhaps six hundred, blood-spattered and swaying with fatigue. They cheered, waved their spears, and instead of falling back upon us, ran forward a hundred yards after the fugitives, took a rising knoll, and formed again—this time a threefold ring round its base. On the top of the mound I saw Sir Henry, apparently unhurt, and Infadoos with him.

Then Twala’s regiments rolled down upon that doomed ring, and the fight closed in again.

I am, if truth be told, no hero by inclination. I have always disliked fighting and have preserved my own blood, when possible, by the judicious use of my heels. Yet at that moment something in me—perhaps the long tension, perhaps the music of the spears, perhaps the sheer sight of men making a wall with their bodies—caught fire. Verses and savage scraps of old tales leapt up in my head. My horror thawed into a desire to kill. I looked at the Buffaloes behind us—hands twitching, lips apart, faces hungry—and wondered, absurdly, whether my own expression had begun to resemble theirs, as if a man’s features can be rewritten by the language of a crowd.

Ignosi, for all his grinding teeth, seemed the calmest among us, like a chief who can still taste what is in the pot while others only smell smoke.

“Are we to stand here till we put out roots, Umbopa—Ignosi, I mean—while Twala swallows our brothers yonder?” I cried.

“Nay, Macumazahn,” he answered. “See—now is the ripe moment: let us pluck it.”

As he spoke a fresh enemy regiment rushed past the ring on the mound, wheeled, and attacked it from our side.

Ignosi lifted his battle-axe. The signal went. Screaming the wild war-cry, the Buffaloes charged—like the rush of the sea when the tide turns and nothing can argue it back.

What followed immediately I cannot set in order. I remember an advance that was irregular yet somehow kept its shape, as though the ground itself were moving; a sudden forming up of the regiment we struck; then an awful shock—dull roar—spears flashing, seen through a red mist that seemed to rise from the world and hang before the eyes.

When my mind cleared I found myself inside the remnant of the Greys near the top of the mound, just behind Sir Henry. How I came there I did not know then. Sir Henry afterwards told me the first furious rush of the Buffaloes carried me almost to his feet, and when we were pressed back he dashed out, seized me, and dragged me into the shelter of the ring.

The fighting that followed—who can truly describe it? Again and again the multitudes surged against our circle, which shrank as men fell; again and again we beat them back. Each time a comrade dropped, another stepped into his place without thought, as naturally as a hand moves to pinch the next bit of mealie-cake when the first is gone.

It was a splendid horror to see Twala’s battalions come on over barriers of their dead. Some lifted corpses before them to take our spear-thrusts—then left their own bodies to swell the rising heaps. Infadoos was everywhere, cool as if on parade, shouting orders, taunts, and even jests, tending courage as a household tends a shared fire. Sir Henry was more gallant still. A spear had shorn away his ostrich plumes, and his long yellow hair streamed behind him. Hands, axe, and armour were red. None could live before his stroke. Time after time I saw his axe sweep down as a warrior ventured close; and with each blow he shouted “O-hoy! O-hoy!” like some old northern forefather, and the blow went through shield and spear, through head-dress, hair, and skull—until men of their own will ceased to come near the great white umtagati, the wizard who killed and did not miss.

Then a cry rose: “Twala, y’ Twala!” Out of the press sprang the gigantic one-eyed king himself, armed with battle-axe and shield, clothed in chain armour. He was like a bull that has broken its pen, maddened and magnificent.

“Where art thou, Incubu, thou white man, who slewest Scragga my son—see if thou canst slay me!” he shouted, and flung a tolla straight at Sir Henry. Sir Henry saw it, caught it on his shield; it pierced through and stuck wedged in the iron plate behind the hide.

With a cry Twala sprang forward and struck Sir Henry’s shield so terrible a blow that the shock brought Sir Henry—strong as he is—down upon his knees.

But the matter went no further. In that very instant a shout rose from the regiments pressing us—something like dismay. I looked up and saw why.

To right and left the plain was suddenly alive with charging plumes. Our outflanking squadrons had come. Their approach had been masked by the lay of the ground and by the greater sound of the struggle itself; Twala’s men had listened only to the loudest language—the clang and roaring at the mound—and had not heard the quieter sentence being spoken on their flanks until it was too late. Believing the horns still hidden in reserve on the crescent hill, they were caught before they could form properly, and the outflanking impis leapt on them like greyhounds.

Within five minutes the fate of the battle was decided. Taken on both sides and shaken by the slaughter the Greys and Buffaloes had worked among them, Twala’s regiments broke. The whole plain between us and Loo scattered into running groups retreating as best they might. The hosts that had surrounded us seemed to melt away like mist when sun takes hold of it; suddenly we stood there almost alone, like a rock from which the sea has retreated.

But what a shore it left behind.

Around us lay the dead and dying in heaped masses. Of the gallant Greys there remained only ninety-five men on their feet. More than three thousand four hundred had fallen in that one regiment alone, most never to rise.

Infadoos, binding a wound on his arm with the calm precision of one who has dressed many cuts in many seasons, surveyed what was left to him. “Men,” he said quietly, “ye have kept up the reputation of your regiment, and this day’s fighting will be well spoken of by your children’s children.”

Then he turned and shook Sir Henry Curtis by the hand. “Thou art a great captain, Incubu,” he said simply. “I have lived a long life among warriors and have known many a brave one, yet have I never seen a man like unto thee.”

At that moment the Buffaloes began to march past our position on the road to Loo, and a message came from Ignosi asking Infadoos, Sir Henry, and myself to join him. We told the remaining ninety of the Greys to gather the wounded, and went on. Ignosi meant to press to Loo and complete the victory by capturing Twala if possible.

We had not gone far when we saw Good sitting on an ant-heap about a hundred paces away. A Kukuana lay close beside him, apparently dead.

“He must be wounded,” Sir Henry said anxiously.

As he spoke, the “dead” man sprang up, knocked Good head over heels off the ant-heap, and began to spear him. We rushed forward. The brawny warrior drove thrust after thrust at the prostrate Good, who jerked all his limbs up at each prod like a puppet yanked by strings. Seeing us, the fellow gave one final, vicious dig and shouted, “Take that, wizard!” and bolted.

Good did not move. We thought him finished. But when we came close, he was pale and faint yet wore a serene smile, and his eyeglass was still fixed in his eye, absurdly neat amid the chaos.

“Capital armour this,” he murmured, as our faces bent over him. “How sold that beggar must have been,” and then he fainted.

He had, it seemed, been seriously wounded in the leg by a tolla during the pursuit, but his chain armour had saved him from the spear, which did little more than bruise him. It was a merciful escape. Since nothing could be done at once, we placed him on one of the wicker shields used as stretchers and had him carried along.

Before the nearest gate of Loo we found one of our regiments watching it by Ignosi’s orders. Others guarded the remaining exits. The officer in command saluted Ignosi as king and reported that Twala’s army had taken refuge within the town and that Twala himself had escaped there too; but they were demoralised and would likely surrender.

Ignosi took counsel with us, then sent heralds to each gate. They were to order the defenders to open and, on Ignosi’s royal word, promise life and forgiveness to every soldier who laid down arms; but if they did not submit before nightfall he would burn the town and all within its gates. The message worked. Half an hour later, amid the shouts and cheers of the Buffaloes, the bridge dropped across the fosse and the gates beyond were flung wide.

Taking precautions against treachery, we marched into the town. Along the roadways stood thousands of dejected warriors, heads drooping, shields and spears at their feet. Officers at their front saluted Ignosi as king as he passed. We went straight on to Twala’s kraal. When we reached the great open space where, a day or two before, we had watched the review and the witch-hunt, we found it deserted.

Not quite deserted.

There, on the far side, in front of his hut, sat Twala himself with only one attendant—Gagool.

It was a melancholy thing to see him thus: battle-axe and shield beside him, chin resting on his mailed breast, a king reduced to a man alone with an old crone. For all his crimes, a pang of compassion went through me at the sight of him fallen from his high estate. Not one soldier remained of the armies that had roared his name; not one courtier from the hundreds who had crawled about his feet; not even a wife. Poor savage—he was tasting the lesson Fate gives so many: that the eyes of mankind turn blind toward the discredited, and that the defenceless fallen find few friends and little mercy. In his case, indeed, he had earned none.

We filed through the kraal gate and crossed the space toward him. At fifty yards the regiment halted, and with only a small guard we advanced. Gagool reviled us bitterly in that shrill, rasping tongue of hers, flinging words like hot ash.

As we drew near, Twala lifted his plumed head at last and fixed his one eye—flashing with fury as bright as the great diamond on his forehead—upon Ignosi.

“Hail, O king!” he said with bitter mockery. “Thou who hast eaten of my bread, and now by the aid of the white man’s magic hast seduced my regiments and defeated mine army, hail! What fate hast thou in store for me, O king?”

“The fate thou gavest to my father, whose throne thou hast sat on these many years!” Ignosi answered sternly.
