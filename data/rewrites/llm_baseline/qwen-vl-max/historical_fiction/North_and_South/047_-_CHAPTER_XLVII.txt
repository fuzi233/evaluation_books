“Experience, like a pale musician, holds
A dulcimer of patience in his hand;
Whence harmonies we cannot understand,
Of God’s will in His worlds, the strain unfolds
In sad, perplexed minors.”

It was around this time that Dixon returned from Milton, resuming her place as Margaret’s maid. She arrived laden with news—endless, vivid tales of life in the industrial town. She spoke of Martha, who had moved into Miss Thornton’s home following her marriage, recounting every detail of the wedding: the bridesmaids’ gowns, the breakfasts, the guests, and how some thought Mr. Thornton had gone too far in splendour, especially after the losses from the strike and the costly failure of several contracts. Then came stories of the auction—how certain cherished household items, once prized by Dixon herself, fetched paltry sums, which she found deeply unjust given the wealth of Milton’s elite. Mrs. Thornton had appeared one day, securing a few fine bargains; the next, Mr. Thornton himself came, so eager for certain pieces he ended up outbidding himself—a scene that delighted onlookers. “That,” Dixon declared with satisfaction, “made things even. If Mrs. Thornton paid too little, Mr. Thornton paid too much.”

Mr. Bell, meanwhile, had sent a flurry of orders regarding the books—so particular and exacting that it was nearly impossible to follow. “If he’d come himself,” Dixon sighed, “it would have been plain enough. But letters—ah! they’re always more trouble than they’re worth.”

She had little to say about the Higginses. Her memory, though sharp when it came to matters of status and society, failed her entirely when it touched upon those beneath her notice. Nicholas, she said, was well enough—had called several times to inquire after Miss Margaret, the only person who ever did, save once, Mr. Thornton. As for Mary? “Oh, she’s very well,” Dixon declared, “a great, stout, slatternly thing!” She then added, hesitating, perhaps remembering a dream or a half-heard rumour, that Mary might have taken work at Mr. Thornton’s mill—because, she supposed, her father wanted her to learn how to cook. But what sense that made, Dixon couldn’t say. Margaret, hearing this, found it no less absurd—yet not entirely unbelievable. Still, it was comforting to have someone now with whom she could speak of Milton, its people, its ways.

Dixon, however, was not particularly fond of such talk. She preferred to dwell instead on the speeches of Mr. Bell, from which she had formed a theory: that he meant to make Margaret his heir. She hinted at this often, cloaked in suspicion or quiet assertion, but Margaret gave her no encouragement. No matter how subtly she probed, Margaret remained silent, leaving Dixon’s hopes unfulfilled.

All this while, Margaret carried within her a strange, nameless longing—to hear that Mr. Bell had gone to Milton. It had been agreed between them, during their conversation at Helstone, that any explanation she desired should be delivered personally to Mr. Thornton, and never forced upon him. Yet Mr. Bell had not yet made that journey. He wrote occasionally—sometimes long letters, sometimes brief—but none mentioned Milton. Though Margaret did not admit it even to herself, each letter brought a quiet disappointment. He was not going. At least, he said nothing of it.

His letters lately seemed unlike him—shorter, more weary, tinged with a bitterness unfamiliar to his usual tone. He spoke little of the future, more often lamenting the past, as if tired of the present. Margaret began to suspect he was unwell. When she asked about his health, he replied with a short note: “An old-fashioned complaint called the spleen. I’m suffering from it—whether mental or physical, you may decide. But I shall indulge myself in grumbling without sending bulletins every time.” After that, she said no more.

Then one day, Edith let slip a fragment of a conversation she’d had with Mr. Bell during his last visit to London. It sparked in Margaret a sudden, vivid idea: he had considered taking her to visit her brother Frederick and his new wife in Cadiz during the autumn. She questioned Edith relentlessly until the younger woman grew exhausted, insisting there was nothing more to recall. All he had said, she repeated, was that he half-thought he might go—to hear Frederick’s own account of the mutiny—and that it would be a good chance for Margaret to meet her new sister-in-law. “He always travels somewhere during the holidays,” Edith added, “and why not Spain?” That was all.

But Margaret could not contain her excitement. The mere possibility of Spain—of seeing Frederick again, of meeting his wife, of escaping the quiet monotony of her days—filled her with a quiet joy. She tried to hide it, but when she dressed for dinner, she could not resist asking Dixon, “Wouldn’t you like to see Master Frederick and his new wife very much indeed?”

“Oh, Miss,” Dixon replied, “she’s a Papist, isn’t she?”

“I believe—yes, certainly,” Margaret answered, her enthusiasm dimmed slightly by the thought.

“And they live in a Popish country?”

“Yes.”

“Well then,” Dixon declared, “I must say my soul is dearer to me than even Master Frederick himself. I should be in constant terror of being converted.”

“Oh,” Margaret said gently, “I don’t know that I’m going. And if I do, I’m not so delicate a lady as to need you by my side. No, dear old Dixon—you’ll have a long holiday, if we go. But I’m afraid it’s a long ‘if.’”

Dixon did not like this. First, she disliked the affectionate “dear old Dixon”—a term Miss Hale used freely for anyone she liked, but which stung Dixon, who felt she was still in the prime of life, barely fifty. Second, she resented being so easily convinced. For all her piety, she harboured a secret curiosity about Spain—the Inquisition, the mysteries of Catholicism. So, after clearing her throat as if to signal resolve, she asked, “Do you think, Miss, if I were careful never to see a priest or enter one of their churches, I’d be safe from conversion?”

Master Frederick, after all, had changed without warning.

“I fancy it was love that first turned him,” Margaret said, sighing.

“Indeed, Miss!” Dixon exclaimed. “Well, I can avoid priests and churches—but love sneaks in unawares! I think it’s best I stay behind.”

Margaret avoided dwelling too long on the idea of Spain. It was a bright distraction, a château en Espagne, perhaps—but it lifted her mind from the restless ache of wanting clarity from Mr. Thornton. Mr. Bell remained in Oxford, showing no signs of moving to Milton. A quiet restraint held Margaret back—she could not ask, could not hint, could not even mention Edith’s revelation. He had never spoken of Spain at Helstone, not during that golden afternoon. It might have been a fleeting whim. Yet if true, what a relief it would be—from the slow weight of waiting, the silence, the uncertainty.

One of the greatest joys in Margaret’s life at present was Edith’s son. He was a delight to both parents when well-behaved, but when his temper flared, Edith would collapse in despair, calling out, “Oh dear, what shall I do with him? Do, Margaret, please ring for Hanley.”

But Margaret loved him most in those storms. She would carry him away to a private room, where they wrestled not with force, but with quiet strength. She held firm, using every charm, every gentle persuasion, until he calmed—then rubbed his hot, tear-streaked face against hers, kissing her, clinging to her, often falling asleep in her arms. These moments were precious—tasting, as she believed, of a love she would never know.

And then there was Mr. Henry Lennox. His frequent presence added a new dimension to the household. Margaret found him colder than before, yet sharper, more brilliant. His intellect sparkled, his knowledge wide-ranging, enriching conversations that might otherwise have been dull. She noticed, too, a faint disdain in his manner toward his brother and sister-in-law—what he saw as frivolity, lack of purpose. Once or twice, in her presence, he questioned Captain Lennox sharply: “Do you mean to give up your profession entirely?” When the captain replied that he had enough to live on, Mr. Lennox curled his lip. “And is that all you live for?”

Yet the brothers were close—bound in that way two men are when one leads and the other willingly follows. Mr. Lennox advanced steadily in his career, cultivating connections with cold precision, keen-eyed, ambitious, proud, and sarcastic. Since their long talk about Frederick, the only real conversation they’d had, Margaret had seen little of him beyond the routine of shared family life. But that was enough to ease the awkwardness between them—her shyness, his pride, both softened by time and proximity.

They met often, of course, but she sensed he avoided being alone with her. They had drifted apart—no longer aligned in opinion or taste. Yet when he made a particularly clever remark, she noticed his glance seeking hers—brief, fleeting, but unmistakable. And in the quiet exchanges of family life, she knew he listened to her—not out of affection, but with a reluctant respect, hidden beneath a mask of indifference.
