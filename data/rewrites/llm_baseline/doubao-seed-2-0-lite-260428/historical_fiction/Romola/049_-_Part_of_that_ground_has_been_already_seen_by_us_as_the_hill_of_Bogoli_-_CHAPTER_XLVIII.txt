Late golden afternoon light slanted thin through the leaded panes of Romola’s narrow workroom, gilding the edges of the half-transcribed scholarly texts stacked on the oak cabinet before her. She was just lifting her ink-stained fingers to set her pen aside, the dimness making the script blur, when the door swung open and Tito stepped across the threshold.

He had come straight to seek her, a clear, unshakable purpose hardening every line of his face—something new and unrecognizable to Romola, who had spent the whole day reassured of Fra Girolamo’s safety, softening into penitence for the public, sharp confrontation she had forced on her husband that morning. She had chided herself for the impulsive indignation that had led her to air a grievance he so fiercely wished kept private, telling herself that the unavoidable scheming of Florentine party politics, the very sort her godfather had once alluded to, could easily explain his ties to the unsavory Dolfo Spini without any deeper treachery on Tito’s part. For hours she had turned over the quiet hope that confessing her hastiness might finally break the two years of cold, unspoken silence between them. That silence had hung so thick neither had ever dared name the rift: Romola did not even know Tito was unaware she had fled him in the aftermath of their great falling out, then chosen to return, the two of them tiptoeing around the wreckage of their young love like guests avoiding the site of a death at a banquet.

She lifted her face to him now, soft with self-reproof, but the stark, new set of his features froze the apology on her tongue. He had not even doffed his velvet cap or damp travel-stained mantle, leaning one elbow on the carved cabinet directly in front of her, his dark eyes holding hers without a flicker of his usual easy charm. A crisis had arrived for their marriage: the quiet drive to mastery he had always hidden under his soft, winning demeanor had risen permanently to the surface, altering his face the way a man’s features twist tight when he is secretly, deliberately throttling something small and weak that still threatens to destroy him.

“Romola,” he began, in that cool, smooth, unhurried tone that sent a shiver down her spine, “it is time we understood each other.”
“That is all I desire, Tito,” she said faintly. Her pale, sweet face, stripped of all the morning’s anger, held only the timidity of self-doubt, making his dark, unyielding strength feel overwhelming in the small room.
“The step you took this morning,” he went on, “you must see for yourself now was useless. It drew eyes to you, and threatens to land me in very serious practical danger.”
“I know I was too hasty. I am sorry for any injustice I may have done you,” Romola said, her tone fuller, firmer, hoping her regret would soften that hard look, open the door for all the honest words she had been turning over in her head.
“I want you to understand once and for all,” he said, his tone unchanging, “that these sorts of public collisions are incompatible with our life as husband and wife. I want you to reflect on exactly how you came to take that foolish step, so it never happens again.”
“That depends far more on you than on me, Tito,” Romola flared, unexpectedly. It was nothing like the gentle overture she had planned, but words between two people who have hidden from each other for years never go the way you anticipate.
“You would say, I suppose,” Tito answered, “that nothing must ever happen again that sparks your unreasonable suspicions. You were frank enough last night to tell me you no longer trust me. I do not blame you for drawing wild, exaggerated conclusions from the smallest scraps of evidence, but I must show you what will happen if you act on those conclusions, meddling in affairs you know nothing of. Are you listening closely?”
“Yes,” Romola said, flushing hot with irrepressible resentment at his cold, patronizing air of superiority.
“Very well. It will not be long before some chance word or trivial incident sets your imagination running wild, inventing crimes to pin on me, and you will go rushing off to the Palazzo Vecchio to alarm the Signoria, set the whole city in an uproar. Do you know what the result would be? It would not just be your husband’s disgrace, that heroic sacrifice you think you are so brave to face. It would be the arrest, the total ruin, of half the most prominent, respected men in Florence – including Messer Bernardo del Nero, your own godfather’s oldest friend.”

He had made his decisive, carefully planned move. The color drained from Romola’s face, leaving even her lips pale, a thing that almost never happened to her, so little used to personal fear. She could see the terrible, unavoidable truth of it all, rising sharp and clear before her. Tito saw he had succeeded.
“You might flatter yourself you were doing some great heroic act of deliverance,” he went on, “but you might as well try to turn iron locks with pretty poetry as apply your simple, unworldly moral rules to Florentine politics. The question now is not whether you choose to trust me. It is whether, now that I have warned you, you will dare to walk like a blind man holding a lit torch through a labyrinth of tangled affairs you cannot even begin to map.”
“I was too rash,” Romola said, quiet. “I will try not to be rash again.”
“Remember,” Tito pressed, unsparing, “that your act of distrust this morning could, for all you knew, have brought far more fatal harm than any brave sacrifice of your husband you think you are ready to make.”
“Tito, that is not true!” Romola burst out, tears stinging her eyes, rising to step closer to him, desperate now to speak every hidden word. “It is a lie that I would willingly sacrifice you. The hardest thing I ever did was cling to you, even when I was furious. I ran away two years ago, that day I left the house, but I came back. Because you were more to me than anything else on this earth. But it is useless. You shut me out of every corner of your mind. You pretend I am too unreasonable to be allowed to know anything of your affairs, and you will not be honest with me about anything.”

She looked like a good angel pleading with him, her eyes wide and bright as she leaned toward him, laying a light hand on his arm. But that touch, that gaze, stirred no flicker of tenderness left in Tito. The easy, good-natured man who had never before felt anything but gentle indifference to most people, found himself turning inexplicably, unyieldingly hard to the wife who had once been the whole center of his world. For all his soft, easy disposition, he had a sharp, driving edge to his intellect and purpose, and Romola’s own stubborn, principled energy had begun to thwart him at every turn. No man who is not weak will long endure being thwarted by his own wife. Marriage, for Tito, could only ever be a relationship of easy sympathy – or total conquest.

No emotion flickered across his face at her confession that she had run away two years prior. His lips only hardened a fraction, and he smiled a thin, cold smile.
“My Romola, when certain facts are settled, we have no choice but to adapt to them. Wishes will not fill the Arno, as the Florentines say, no more than they will turn a plum into an orange. Even prayers, I have noticed, have little power that way. You are made with a set of strong, fixed impressions that no reason can shift. I cannot share those impressions, and so you have withdrawn all your trust from me. You changed first toward me, and so I changed toward you. There is no use digging up the past. We only have to make the best of the conditions we have now.”
“Tito, speaking openly would not be useless!” Romola said, stung to exasperation, the way you are when you push with all your strength against a heavy, immovable stone wall. “The deception I sensed in you is what changed me, what has kept us apart. And you changed first. You changed to me the very first night you wore that chain armor, hiding a secret from me about that old man – the one I saw yesterday in the streets. Tito,” she begged, voice breaking, “if you would only tell me everything, no matter how painful, there would be no more walls between us. Could we not start a new life, together?”

For a split second, a flash of raw emotion crossed Tito’s face, and he went perfectly still, the color draining from his cheeks. He ignored her plea entirely, pausing a long beat before speaking in a light, cutting tone.
“Your impetuous obsession with trivial nonsense, Romola, has a coldness that could freeze even the baths of Nero.”
Romola flinched as if struck, drawing back to stand tall, wrapped again in her usual quiet self-poise.
“If by ‘that old man’ you mean that half-mad Jacopo di Nola who once tried to kill me, making wild, absurd accusations I never told you of for fear of scaring you for no reason – that poor wretch died in prison, not long ago. I saw his name on the list of dead inmates.”
“I do not know anything about his accusations,” Romola said. “But I know he is the man I saw in the Duomo, with a rope around his neck. The man Piero di Cosimo painted, his hand clamped tight around your arm, the exact way he grabbed you the day the French entered Florence, the very day you first put on that armor.”
“And where did you find him, pray?” Tito said, still pale, but holding tight control over his voice.
“He was lying in the street, half dead of starvation,” Romola said. “I revived him with bread and wine, brought him back to our door, but he refused to cross the threshold. I gave him money, and he left without telling me a single thing, though he learned I was your wife. Who is he, Tito?”
“Half a witless fool, half a madman. He was my father’s servant in Greece, once, and he has hated me ever since I had him dismissed for stealing. There is your whole mystery. Now you also know I face constant risk of assassination, thanks to him. That armor you obsessed over? You must have guessed I was in danger from this man – was that why you chose to tend to him, to invite the enemy of your own husband close to our doors?”

Romola had no words left. To speak would be nothing but throwing herself bare-breasted against a polished, unbreakable shield. Tito pushed off from the cabinet, slowly taking off his cap and mantle, running a hand back through his dark hair, gathering his thoughts for his final, decisive words. Romola stood straight before him, looking at him the way you would look at an oncoming, unstoppable force of destruction, ready to meet nothing but silence and endurance.
“We need never speak of these things again, Romola,” he said, his tone as level and unyielding as it had been at the start. “That is all. Only remember: the next time your generous, ardent heart leads you to meddle in political affairs, you will not save anyone from danger. You will only light scaffolds, and burn innocent houses down. You are not yet so devout a Piagnone that you believe Messer Bernardo del Nero is the devil incarnate and Messer Francesco Valori is the archangel Michael. I do not think I need to ask you for a promise.”
“I have understood you perfectly, Tito.”
“It is enough,” he said, and left the room.

Romola turned, despair written deep on her face, and sank back into her chair. The last of the daylight had faded completely, leaving the room shadowed. *Oh God, I have tried. I cannot reach him. We will always be divided*, the words ran silent through her mind. Then she spoke out loud, as if a sudden unbidden vision had wrenched the words from her: “Unless shared misery should come, strong enough to join us.”

Tito, too, carried a new, sharp thought with him as he pulled the door shut behind him. He had long planned to leave Florence, once his position there was high enough to lift him to far greater fortune at Rome, or Milan – and for the first time, he knew he wanted to leave Romola behind. She was no longer a pleasant, desirable piece of the comfortable life he had built for himself. There was no way to have an easy, peaceful marriage with her unless he was fully honest, and full honesty would mean admitting every secret of his past, destroying all the careful plans he had laid. He was no more inclined to do that than a full-grown leopard is to lap milk from a child’s bowl. He shrank from every difficulty, every discomfort – and Romola had made his life harder in far more ways than just their private strife. He had already had a vicious, unpleasant meeting with Dolfo Spini that day, who had returned furious after spending hours soaked through in the rain, waiting in vain for an ambush that never came. Word of Romola’s public confrontation with Tito outside Nello’s shop had already reached Spini’s ears, and that small incident could easily spiral into a danger he could not control. He thought, for the moment, that he had mastered her: he had stirred her affection and her conscience with the shadow of innocent lives ruined, he had dazzled her intellect with the idea that Florentine politics were too tangled, too complex for any simple moral judgment to untangle.

But Tito was not entirely at ease. The world around him was not yet cushioned soft with velvet. Before he stepped out into the dark, lamp-lit streets of Florence that evening, he reached into his chest, and pulled on his fine hidden coat of chain armor, the very armor Romola had first seen him wear the day the French marched through the city gates.
