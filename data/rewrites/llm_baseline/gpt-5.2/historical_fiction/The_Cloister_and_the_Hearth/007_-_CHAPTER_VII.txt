The morning broke as if some lord had spilled a casket of bright stuffs across the street: velvet that drank the sun and gave it back softened, plumes that nodded like wheat heads in a light wind, and horses going by in such sleek order that even their hoofs seemed to flash in time. Tergou ran to its windows and balconies the way a kitchen runs to its door when a new scent comes up the lane—curiosity first, then appetite.

The French ambassador rode through to hunt in the neighbouring forest, with his own train and, for Burgundy’s honour, a borrowed handful of the Duke’s servants. Before them all went the Duke’s tumbler—solemn as a church beadle one moment, rolling off his horse the next with such artless ignominy that the gravest faces cracked. He would spill himself into the dust, legs and arms all askew, then climb back up by the beast’s tail as if it were the most natural stirrup in Christendom. The crowd’s laughter followed him like a ripple follows a dropped pebble.

But the eye was drawn, too, to the leopard.

A huntsman sat a Flemish horse of giant build—broad as a threshing-board, muscles shifting beneath its hide with every step. Strapped to the rider’s loins was a long wooden box, contrived like a traveling bench, and upon it crouched the leopard: bright, glossy, dappled with ink-dark rosettes, her body coiled as if she could spring clean through the air and land on the far side of the street. A chain lay along her foreleg to the huntsman’s hand.

People pressed near. One or two, emboldened by the morning’s gaiety, stretched fingers as if the spotted coat were some rare fur at market.

The huntsman’s shout cracked across the lane like a cleaver on a block. “Beware! At Antwerp one did but fling a handful of dust at her, and the Duke made dust of him.”

“Gramercy!” someone breathed.

“I speak sooth. The good Duke shut him in a cell under ground, and the rats cleaned the flesh off his bones in a night. Served him right, for molesting the poor thing.”

Fear ran through the crowd and cooled their hands. It was as if the street’s music changed key: less laughter, more murmuring; the very clop of the hooves sounded sharper now that men had been reminded what power could do when it chose to be offended.

Then came a sound to mend their spirits: a thin, fluty voice, piping from a body that should have thundered.

The Duke’s giant brought up the rear—a Hungarian seven feet four, a wall of a man on long legs, yet speaking as if a bird had learned words and could not quite push them out. He was vain, and so unaware of his own absurdity that he fed on it. His sharp little voice travelled up, and caught Giles—Giles the dwarf—perched atop a balcony like a watchful cat.

“Hallo, brother!” squeaked the giant. “I had nearly passed without seeing thee.”

Giles answered from his small chest with a great bell of a bellow. “You are plain enough to see.”

“Come on my shoulder, brother,” piped Titan, offering an arm the size of a hunk of mutton, fist like a mallet.

“If I do I’ll cuff your ears,” roared Giles, and the town burst into such laughter that it seemed the street itself had learned to sing.

Yet the laughter was not for wit alone. It was for that mad duet the air carried: the mountain giving birth to a squeak, the mole-hill belching thunder. In a world where the ear reads a man before the eye has finished its glance, the voices made a farce of nature’s expectations. Even the horses’ snorts and the jangling harness seemed to join in, each sound finding its place in the morning’s rough music.

The giant, thinking himself applauded for cleverness, played on Giles the more—until Giles, who had little fear and less patience, lost the last thread of command.

Like a stone dropped from a roof, the dwarf let himself fall upon the giant’s neck. One hand knotted in the man’s hair, the other drummed blows upon his head with astonishing quickness. At first the giant laughed, his fluty he-he rising. Then the weight and the stinging rain of punches changed laughter to yelps.

“He! he! Ah! ha!—hallo!—oh! oh! Holy saints! Help, or I must throttle the imp. I can’t—I'll split your skull against the—”

In blind fury he ran backward, aiming to dash Giles against the balcony. Giles felt the danger as a cook feels the first curl of smoke from a neglected pan: in an instant, without pausing to wonder, he seized the balcony rail with both hands and whipped himself over, just as the giant’s head struck the wood with a stunning crack.

The crowd roared—relief, triumph, and that hungry delight people take in any nimble escape. Giles had become, in a blink, their champion, their own small thunder resisting ducal bulk.

The giant, crimson with rage, grabbed two of the laughers. He knocked them together like dumb-bells, shook them as if to beat sense into their bones, and flung them down. Catherine shrieked and threw her apron over Giles as if cloth could ward off wrath. The giant strode away after the party, still boiling, his thin voice squealing curses that sounded the more impotent for his vastness.

No one in that moment guessed what a seed had been dropped into the day—how a small scene, all noise and sport, might sprout consequences later. For now the effect was immediate and, to Giles, profitable. Tergou swelled with pride in him; their smiles softened. When he went begging parchment for Gerard, they were more affable than before. He drove a steady trade: begged the scraps and leaves gratis, and Gerard paid him coppers for each—because ink and prayer and learning must have something to bite on, even when money is thin.

That afternoon, inside the little house where leather and glue and family breath mingled, Catherine and her daughter sat and spoke of what warmed them most: Gerard—his goodness, his benefice, and the brightened prospects that now seemed, like a well-risen loaf, to promise substance and comfort.

“He will marry Cornelis and Sybrandt,” Catherine said, counting practical joys as women do while their hands keep busy in thought. “They can wed, good housewives, now if they will. Gerard will take care of you and Giles when we are gone.”

“Yes, mother—and we can confess to him instead of to a stranger,” said Kate.

“Ay, girl! and he can give the sacred oil to your father and me, and close our eyes when our time comes.”

“Oh, mother, not for many, many years,” Kate pleaded. “Pray speak not of that; it always makes me sad. I hope to go before you, mother dear. No; let us be gay to-day. I am out of pain, mother—quite out of all pain. It seems so strange; and I feel so bright and happy that—mother, can you keep a secret?”

“Nobody better, child. Why, you know I can.”

“Then I will show you something so beautiful. You never saw the like, I trow. Only Gerard must never know; for sure he means to surprise us with it. He covers it up so, and sometimes he carries it away altogether.”

Kate took her crutches and moved slowly away. Her mother watched, curiosity rising like yeast. She returned with something wrapped in cloth. When she uncovered it, a presence seemed to enter the room: a painted Virgin, with her insignia, her tiara, and a wealth of hair flowing loose over her shoulders—gold like sunlit flax, set down in strokes that held light.

Catherine drew in her breath as if incense had been lit. “It is herself,” she whispered. “It is the Queen of Heaven. I never saw one like her to my mind before.”

“And her eyes, mother—lifted to the sky as if they belonged there and not to a mortal creature. And her beautiful hair of burning gold.”

“And to think I have a son that can make the saints live again upon a piece of wood!”

“The reason is, he is a young saint himself, mother. He is too good for this world; he is here to portray the blessed, and then to go away and be with them for ever.”

They had not half exhausted their wonder when a voice—unwelcome as a cold draught across a hearth—sounded at the door. By one of those quick, silent instincts, they hid the picture again, though no one yet had asked to see it.

The man who entered had not crossed their threshold in ten years: Ghysbrecht Van Swieten.

He came in casting his eyes around, not openly, but with a furtive measuring. The two women stared—at him, then at each other—as if some old wound had walked back into the room. “The burgomaster,” they said, and in their tone lay memory enough to make a man defensive.

“Yes,” he answered, stiff. “I own the last time I came here was not on a friendly errand. Men love their own interest—Eli’s and mine were contrary. Well, let this visit atone the last. To-day I come on your business and none of mine.”

Catherine and Kate exchanged a glance of contempt that was swift as a knife’s flash.

“It is about your son Gerard.”

“Ay, ay,” Catherine snapped, “you want him to work for the town all for nothing. He told us.”

“I come on no such errand,” said Ghysbrecht, too quickly. “It is to let you know he has fallen into bad hands.”

“Now Heaven and the saints forbid!” Catherine cried, and the mother in her rose like a flame. “Man, torture not a mother. Speak out—and quickly. Speak ere you have time to coin falsehood: we know thee.”

The burgomaster’s face paled at the affront, and spite came and sat beside whatever other motives had brought him.

“Thus it is, then,” he said, teeth grinding as the words ran fast. “Your son Gerard is more like to be father of a family than a priest. He is for ever with Margaret—Peter Brandt’s red-haired girl—and loves her like a cow her calf.”

Mother and daughter both burst out laughing.

Ghysbrecht stared, thrown off balance as a man is when his expected chord is met with a different tune. “What! you knew it?”

“Carry this tale to those who know not my son, Gerard,” Catherine said. “Women are nought to him.”

“Other women, mayhap. But this one is the apple of his eye, or will be, if you part them not, and soon. Come, dame—make me not waste time and friendly counsel. My servant has seen them together a score times, handed, and reading babies in one another’s eyes like—you know, dame—you have been young, too.”

Catherine’s laughter died. Her hand went to her breast as if he had struck there. “Girl, I am ill at ease. Yea, I have been young, and know how blind and foolish the young are. My heart—he has turned me sick in a moment. Kate, if it should be true—”

“Nay, nay!” Kate cried, quick as a pan snatched from the fire. “Gerard might love a young woman; all young men do. I can’t find what they see in them to love so; but if he did, he would let us know. He would not deceive us. You wicked man! No, dear mother, look not so. Gerard is too good to love a creature of earth. His love is for our Lady and the saints. Ah! I will show you the picture—if his heart was earthly, could he paint the Queen of Heaven like that? Look! look!”

She whipped the cloth away and thrust the panel forward. In that moment Kate herself, lit from within by certainty, seemed to outshine the painted face—so that Ghysbrecht, confronted by two sorts of fervour, stood with mouth and eyes open, turning as if on a pivot between the women and the picture.

“Why,” he gasped, “it is herself.”

“Isn’t it!” Kate cried, and for the first time her hostility softened. “You admire it? I forgive you for frightening us.”

“Am I in a mad-house?” said Ghysbrecht, bewildered into bluntness. “You show me a picture of the girl, and you say he painted it, and that is a proof he cannot love her. Why, they all paint their sweethearts, painters do.”

“A picture of the girl?” Kate echoed, shocked as if he had spat on the floor. “Fie! this is no girl. This is our blessed Lady.”

“No, no. It is Margaret Brandt.”

“Oh blind! It is the Queen of Heaven.”

“No; only of Sevenbergen village.”

“Profane man! behold her crown!”

“Silly child—look at her red hair! Would the Virgin be seen in red hair? She who had the pick of all the colours ten thousand years before the world began.”

Their voices rose; the house filled with quarrel the way a larder fills with sharp smells when something has turned. And as loud words carry farther than gentle ones, an anxious face appeared at the edge of the open door: their neighbour Peter Buyskens, drawn by the clamour as surely as a man is drawn by the sound of a market bell.

“What is to do?” he asked in a cautious whisper. “We can hear you all across the street. What on earth is to do?”

“Oh, neighbour,” Catherine cried, “what is to do? Why, here is the burgomaster blackening our Gerard.”

“Stop!” Ghysbrecht snapped, pouncing on Peter as on a witness. “Peter Buyskens is come in the nick of time. He knows father and daughter both. They cast their glamour on him.”

“What! is she a witch too?” asked Peter, half in jest, half uneasy at the burgomaster’s heat.

“Else the egg takes not after the bird. Why is her father called the magician? I tell you they bewitched this very Peter; they cast unholy spells on him, and cured him of the colic. Now, Peter—look, and tell me who is that? And you be silent, women, for a moment, if you can. Who is it, Peter?”

Peter’s eye went to the picture and stayed there, caught as if by a hook. “Well, to be sure!” he said slowly.

“Who is it?” Ghysbrecht repeated, stamping impatience into the floor.

Peter smiled, faintly. “Why, you know as well as I do. But what have they put a crown on her for? I never saw her in a crown, for my part.”

“Man alive!” the burgomaster burst out. “Can’t you open your great jaws and speak a wench’s name plain out to oblige three people?”

“I’d do a great deal more to oblige one of you than that, burgomaster,” Peter retorted, nettled. “If it isn’t as natural as life!”

“Curse the man! he won’t, he won’t—curse him!”

“Why, what have I done now?”

Kate, turning pale, leaned forward on her crutches as if her very bones needed the answer. “Oh, sir—for pity’s sake tell us. Are these the features of a living woman, of—of—Margaret Brandt?”

“A mirror is not truer, my little maid.”

“But is it she, sir, for very certain?”

“Why, who else should it be?”

“Now, why couldn’t you say so at once?” Ghysbrecht snarled, triumphant.

“I did say so, as plain as I could speak,” Peter snapped back.

They bristled at each other over this small bone of contention, so taken with their own disputing that they did not see Catherine and Kate had drawn their aprons over their heads and begun to rock to and fro, stricken as if a blow had fallen.

Then Elias came in from the shop, drawn by the raised voices the way a man is drawn by the wrong sound in his own house—like a kettle screaming when it ought to be singing low.

He stood aghast. Though Catherine’s face was covered, she knew his footstep, the weight and rhythm of it learned over years like the sound of one’s own hearth-lid. “That is my poor man,” she sobbed. “Tell him, good Peter Buyskens, for I have not the courage.”

Elias went pale. The burgomaster’s presence after so many years, and the women’s distress, made his mind leap to calamity.

“Richart! Jacob!” he gasped—naming their sons as if death must have chosen one.

“No, no,” Ghysbrecht said. “It is nearer home, and nobody is dead or dying, old friend.”

“God bless you, burgomaster!” Elias breathed, as if a band had been cut from his chest. “Ah! something has gone off my breast that was like to choke me. Now, what is the matter?”

Ghysbrecht told him all, and set the picture forward as proof. Elias looked, listened, and then—strangely—his first feeling was relief, rough and practical.

“Is that all?” he said. “What are ye roaring and bellowing for? It is vexing—it is angering, but it is not like death, not even sickness. Boys will be boys. He will outgrow that disease: ’tis but skin-deep.”

But when Ghysbrecht added that Margaret was a girl of good character, and no such intimacy was likely unless marriage had been spoken of, Elias’s brow darkened, and the easy tolerance baked into hardness.

“Marriage! that shall never be,” he said, stern as a man laying down a rule at the table. “I’ll stay that—ay, by force, if need be—as I would his hand lifted to cut his throat. I’d do what old John Koestein did t’other day.”

“And what is that, in Heaven’s name?” Catherine cried, snatching her apron away so her face showed, wet and shaken.

It was the burgomaster who answered, almost with satisfaction, for the law gives some men a relish as salt gives broth. “He made me shut young Albert Koestein up in the prison of the Stadthouse till he knocked under. It was not long. Forty-eight hours all alone, on bread and water, cooled his hot stomach. ‘Tell my father I am his humble servant,’ says he, ‘and let me into the sun once more—the sun is worth all the wenches in the world.’”

“Oh, the cruelty of men!” Catherine sighed.

“As to that,” Elias returned, “the burgomaster has no choice: it is the law. And if a father says, ‘Burgomaster, lock up my son,’ he must do it. A fine thing it would be if a father might not lock up his own son.”

“Well, well! it won’t come to that with me and my son. He never disobeyed me in his life; he never shall. Where is he? It is past supper-time. Where is he, Kate?”

“Alas! I know not, father.”

“I know,” said Ghysbrecht. “He is at Sevenbergen. My servant met him on the road.”

Supper was set and eaten without comfort. The familiar sounds of the house—bowls touched, bread broken, a cup set down—came muffled, as if grief had padded the room. Evening deepened, and still no Gerard. Eight o’clock came—no Gerard. At last Elias sent all to bed but Catherine.

“You and I will walk abroad, wife, and talk over this new care.”

“Abroad, my man, at this time? Whither?”

“Why, on the road to Sevenbergen.”

“Oh no,” Kate had cried earlier. “No hasty words, father. Poor Gerard! he never vexed you before.”

“Fear me not,” Elias had answered. “But it must end; and I am not one that trusts to-morrow with to-day’s work.”

So the old pair went out hand in hand, not with elbows linked—folk had not yet learned that fashion—but with fingers twined in the older way, as if the hand were the true rope between two lives. They walked in silence awhile. The night was clear and balmy, and it had its own hush: the soft lay of air on leaves, the far-off bark of a dog, a cart somewhere creaking like an old song. In such quiet, memory rises readily, as stew rises when the pot has been left undisturbed and the flavours have had time to marry.

“It is a many years since we walked so late, my man,” Catherine said softly.

“Ay, sweetheart—more than we shall see again,” Elias murmured, and then, like a thorn, “Is he never coming, I wonder?”

“Not since our courting days, Eli.”

“No. Ay, you were a buxom lass then.”

“And you were a comely lad, as ever a girl’s eye stole a look at. I do suppose Gerard is with her now, as you used to be with me. Nature is strong, and the same in all our generations.”

“Nay, I hope he has left her by now—confound her—or we shall be here all night.”

“Eli!”

“Well, Kate?”

“I have been happy with you, sweetheart, for all our rubs—much happier, I trow, than if I had—been—a—a—nun. You won’t speak harshly to the poor child? One can be firm without being harsh.”

“Surely.”

“Have you been happy with me, my poor Eli?”

“Why, you know I have. Friends I have known, but none like thee. Buss me, wife!”

“A heart to share joy and grief with is a great comfort to man or woman. Isn’t it, Eli?”

“It is so, my lass. ‘It doth joy double, and halveth trouble,’ runs the byword. And so I have found it, sweetheart. Ah! here comes the young fool.”

Catherine’s fingers tightened on his hand. They were in the shadow of trees, though the moon rode bright; and their son did not see them. Gerard came down the road singing in the moonlight, his face shining as if the night itself had seasoned him with happiness.
