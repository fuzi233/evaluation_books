It was too late and too dark last night to discern the old house at Stow. Let us, however, survey the scene this bright October morning. About eleven o'clock, Sir Richard and Amyas pace up and down the terraced gardens to the south. Amyas has slept until luncheon—meaning until an hour ago—but Sir Richard, despite the turmoil of the previous night, rose at dawn. By six o'clock he was already in the valley, reviving the spirits of himself and two terrier dogs with the vigorous pursuit of several badgers.

Old Stow House stands—or rather stood—some four miles beyond the Cornish border, perched on the northern slope of the largest and most lovely of those combes mentioned in the preceding chapter. Eighty years after Sir Richard's era, a massive Palladian mansion arose there, adorned with every monstrosity of bad taste. Legend holds it was built by King Charles II for Sir Richard's great-grandson, the heir of that famous Sir Bevil who routed Parliamentary troops at Stratton and died shortly thereafter, fighting bravely at Lansdowne near Bath. Yet, like most creations of the Stuarts, it rose only to fall. An elderly man who witnessed the laying of the new foundation as a boy lived to see it demolished; the very bricks and timber were sold on the spot. Since then, the stables have become a farmhouse, the tennis court a sheepfold, and the great quadrangle a rickyard. Civilization, surging wave upon wave across the land, has receded from this lonely corner—for now, at least.

My concern lies with a simpler age and a sterner generation; with the ancient house that had stood there, in part, since gray and mythic times. This was the home of the first Sir Richard, son of Hamon Dentatus, Lord of Carboyle—the grandson of Duke Robert, son of Rou—who settled at Bideford after slaying the Prince of South-Galis and the Lord of Glamorgan, donating all his Welsh conquests to the Cistercian monks of Neath. It was a vast, rambling structure, half castle, half dwelling, akin to the almost unique specimen remaining at Compton Castle near Torquay, the residence of Humphrey Gilbert, Walter Raleigh's half-brother, and Richard Grenville's bosom friend.

On three sides—to the north, west, and south—the lofty walls of the old *ballium* still stood, complete with machicolated turrets, loopholes, and dark chutes for raining stones and fire upon besiegers, relics of a turbulent age. However, the southern courtyard had transformed into a flower garden, featuring quaint terraces, statues, floral knots, clipped yews and hollies, and all the pedantic arts of topiary. To the east, where the valley vista opened, the old walls had vanished. The frowning Norman keep, ruined during the Wars of the Roses, had been replaced by the rich, stately architecture of the Tudors. Altogether, the house mirrored the times: in a transitional state, faithfully representing the passage from the old Middle Ages into the new life blossoming across Europe—a springtime we pray will never see its autumn or winter.

From the house, the hill sloped steeply on three sides. The garden where Sir Richard and Amyas walked offered a truly English prospect. At one turn, peering over the western walls, they caught a glimpse of the blue ocean flecked with passing sails. At the next, spread far below, lay range upon range of fertile parkland, stately avenues, yellow autumn woods, and purple heather moors, layering up the valley to the old British earthworks standing black and bramble-covered on its conical peak. Against the sky, atop the highest bank closing the valley to the east, rose the lofty tower of Kilkhampton Church, rich with the monuments and offerings of five centuries of Grenvilles.

A yellow eastern haze softened the park, wood, and moor. Red cattle lowed to one another as they brushed flies from their hides in the rivulet far below. Colts in the horse-park whinnied as they played, answered by their sires from the Queen's Park on the opposite hill in fuller, though fainter, voices. A rutting stag caused the still woodland to tremble with his hoarse thunder, while a rival far up the valley returned a trumpet note of defiance, itself challenged from heathery brows quivering in the distant mist. Close at home, upon the terrace before the house, amidst romping spaniels and golden-haired children, sat Lady Grenville herself—the beautiful St. Leger of Annery, the central jewel of that glorious place. She looked down at her noble children, then up at her more noble husband, and around at that broad paradise of the West, until life seemed too full of happiness, and heaven too radiant.

All the while, Amyas and Sir Richard paced up and down, speaking long, earnestly, and slowly, for both knew the turning point of the boy's life had arrived.

"Yes," said Sir Richard, after Amyas, in his blunt, simple manner, recounted the entire story concerning Rose Salterne and his brother—"Yes, sweet lad, thou hast chosen the better part, thou and thy brother also, and it shall not be taken from you. Only be strong, lad, and trust in God that He will make a man of you."

"I do trust," replied Amyas.

"Thank God," said Sir Richard, "that you have removed from my heart that which was my greatest anxiety for you, since the day your good father, who sleeps in peace, entrusted you to my care. For all best things, Amyas, become, when misused, the very worst. The love of woman, because it is capable of lifting a man's soul to the heavens, is equally capable of dragging him down to hell. But you have learned better, Amyas. Know, with our old German forefathers, that as Tacitus says, *Sera juvenum Venus, ideoque inexhausta pubertas*. And not only that, Amyas; but trust me, that silly fashion of the French and Italians, to dangle perpetually at some woman's apron strings, so that no boy counts himself a man unless he can *vagghezziare le donne*, whether maids or wives—alas, it matters little—that fashion, I say, is little less harmful to the soul than open sin. Through it are bred vanity, expense, envy, heartburning, yes, hatred and murder often. Even if one escapes that, the rich treasure of a manly worship, reserved for one alone, is squandered and divided among many, and the bride finally receives nothing but the very last remnants and *caput mortuum* of her bridegroom's heart, becoming merely an ornament for his table and a means to secure progeny. May God, who has saved me from that death-in-life, save you also!"

As he spoke, he looked down toward his wife upon the terrace below. She, as if guessing instinctively that he spoke of her, looked up with such a sweet smile that Sir Richard's stern face melted into a glory of spiritual sunshine.

Amyas looked at them both and sighed. Then, abruptly changing the subject:

"And may I go to Ireland tomorrow?"

"You shall sail in the *Mary* for Milford Haven, carrying these letters to Winter. If the wind permits, you may order the master to drop down the river tonight and depart; for we must lose no time."

"Winter?" said Amyas. "He is no friend of mine, since he abandoned Drake and us so cowardly at the Straits of Magellan."

"Duty must not wait for private quarrels, even though they be just ones, lad: but he will not be your general. When you reach the Marshal or the Lord Deputy, present either of them with this letter, and they will set you to work—and hard work, I warrant."

"I want nothing better."

"Right, lad. The best reward for having worked well already is to have more to do; and he who has been faithful over a few things must find his account in ruling over many. That is the true and heroic rest, worthy only of gentlemen and sons of God. As for those who, in this world or the next, seek idleness and hope God will feed them with pleasant things, as with a spoon, Amyas, I count them cowards and base, even though they call themselves saints and the elect."

"I wish you could persuade my poor cousin of that."

"He has yet to learn what losing one's life to save it means, Amyas. Bad men have taught him—and I fear these Anabaptists and Puritans at home teach little else—that the one great business of everyone is to save his own soul after death; everyone for himself; and that this, not divine self-sacrifice, is the one thing needful and the better part Mary chose."
