"I ask Thee for a thoughtful love,
Through constant watching wise,
To meet the glad with joyful smiles,
And to wipe the weeping eyes;
And a heart at leisure from itself
To soothe and sympathise."

Margaret Hale listened with a patience that bordered on adoration to her mother's schemes for alleviating the hardships of the parish poor. Yet, beneath the surface of her compliance, a quiet grief churned. Every charitable plan her mother devised felt like a final farewell, a prelude to their departure from Helstone. The frost was already biting; soon, the winter would seal them away from the people they loved. Old Simon's rheumatism would ache in the cold, and his failing eyes would search in vain for the familiar face of the girl who brought him broth and red flannel. Mary Domville's crippled boy would crawl to the door, hoping to catch a glimpse of Margaret walking through the forest, but she would be gone. These neighbors would never understand the sudden severance, nor the many others whose lives had been woven into the fabric of the vicarage. "Father has always spent his stipend entirely on the parish," Mrs. Hale explained, her voice tinged with a sense of duty that Margaret found heavy. "I may be encroaching on the future, but the winter is cruel, and the poor must be fed."

"Oh, Mother, let us do all we can," Margaret urged, her eagerness masking the dread that this might be their last chance to serve. "We may not be here long."

"Do you feel unwell, my darling?" Mrs. Hale asked, her anxiety sharpening as she noted her daughter's pallor. "You look exhausted. Perhaps this damp, unhealthy air of Helstone is affecting you."

"No, Mother, it is not that. It is the finest air imaginable, smelling of purity after the smog of Harley Street. But I am weary; surely it is near bedtime."

"It is half-past nine. You should retire immediately. Ask Dixon for some gruel. I shall check on you once you are settled. I fear you have caught a chill, or perhaps the stagnant ponds..."

"Oh, Mother," Margaret murmured, offering a faint smile as she kissed her mother's cheek. "I am perfectly well. I am simply tired."

Retiring upstairs, Margaret submitted to a bowl of gruel to soothe her mother's fears. Languidly, she lay in bed as Mrs. Hale made her final inquiries before locking her own door. The moment the latch clicked, Margaret sprang up. Throwing on her dressing-gown, she paced the room until the floorboards groaned under her feet, forcing her to stop. She curled herself onto the window seat, a small, deeply recessed nook. Earlier that morning, the bright lights on the church tower had promised a sunny day. Now, sixteen hours later, she sat there, too consumed by sorrow to weep, feeling a cold, dull ache that had drained the vitality from her young heart.

Mr. Henry Lennox's visit, his bold proposal, seemed like a distant dream, severed from her reality. The harsh truth was that her father had allowed doubts to fester until he became a schismatic, an outcast. All the changes looming over them stemmed from this single, blighting fact. She gazed out at the dark grey silhouettes of the church towers, square and rigid against the deep blue void of the night sky. Into those depths she looked, searching for a sign of God, finding none. It seemed to her that the earth was more desolate than if enclosed by an iron dome; the infinite, serene spaces of the universe mocked her suffering, shutting in the cries of the afflicted so they might ascend to a throne that would never hear them.

In this mood, her father entered silently. The moonlight revealed his daughter in her unusual posture. He approached and touched her shoulder.

"Margaret, I heard you were awake. I could not resist coming in to pray with you—to recite the Lord's Prayer. It will benefit us both."

Mr. Hale and Margaret knelt by the window. He looked upward; she bowed her head in humble shame. God was present, close and listening to her father's whispered words. Her father might be a heretic, but had not she, in her despairing doubts moments before, revealed herself as a skeptic even more profound? She said nothing, retreating to bed after her father left, like a child fleeing from a discovered fault. If the world was filled with perplexing problems, she would trust, asking only for the one necessary step for the hour. Mr. Lennox haunted her dreams that night. He climbed a tree of impossible height to retrieve her bonnet; he fell, and she struggled to save him, held back by an invisible, powerful hand. Then, the scene shifted: she was back in the Harley Street drawing-room, talking to him as of old, yet acutely aware that she had witnessed his death by that terrible fall.

It was a miserable, unresting night, a poor preparation for the day ahead. She awoke with a start, unrefreshed, sensing a reality far worse than her feverish dreams. The sorrow returned, accompanied by a terrible discord. Where had her father wandered, led by doubts that were temptations of the Evil One? She longed to ask, yet dreaded the answer.

The crisp morning brought a false cheer to Mrs. Hale at breakfast. She chattered happily about village kindness, ignoring her husband's silence and Margaret's monosyllabic responses. Before the meal was cleared, Mr. Hale rose, leaning heavily on the table.

"I shall not return until evening. I am going to Bracy Common and will ask Farmer Dobson for dinner. I will be back for tea at seven."

He did not look at either of them, but Margaret understood the subtext. By seven, the announcement must be made. Mr. Hale would have delayed, but Margaret possessed a different constitution. She could not bear the weight of the secret all day; it was better to confront the worst immediately. Standing by the window, she waited for the servant to leave, planning how to begin. Her mother ascended the stairs to prepare for the school, returning shortly in a brisker mood.

"Mother, come round the garden with me this morning. Just one turn," Margaret said, linking her arm through her mother's waist.

They stepped through the open window. Mrs. Hale spoke, but Margaret could not decipher the words. Her eye caught a bee entering a deep-bellied flower. When the insect emerged with its spoil, she would begin—that was the signal. Out flew the bee.

"Mother! Father is leaving Helstone!" Margaret blurted out. "He is leaving the Church and moving to Milton-Northern." The three hard facts hung in the air.

"What makes you say that?" Mrs. Hale asked, her voice incredulous. "Who has been telling you such nonsense?"

"Father himself," Margaret replied, wishing she could speak gently but lacking the words. They were near a garden bench. Mrs. Hale sat down and began to weep.

"I do not understand," she said. "Either you have made a grave mistake, or I do not comprehend you."

"No, Mother, I have made no mistake. Father has written to the Bishop stating that his doubts prevent him from remaining a priest of the Church of England, and that he must resign Helstone. He has also consulted Mr. Bell—Frederick's godfather—and it is arranged that we move to Milton-Northern." Mrs. Hale looked up, the shadow on her face confirming she believed the truth.

"I do not think it can be true," Mrs. Hale said finally. "He would surely have told me before it came to this."

Margaret realized her mother ought to have been informed sooner. Whatever her own faults of discontent might have been, it was an error for her father to leave her to learn his change of heart from a better-informed child. She sat beside her mother, resting her head on her breast, caressing her face.

"Dear, darling Mother! We were so afraid of causing you pain. Father felt it acutely. You are not strong, and the suspense must have been terrible."

"When did he tell you, Margaret?"

"Only yesterday," Margaret replied, detecting the jealousy in the question. "Poor Father!" She tried to steer her mother's thoughts toward compassion for her father's struggle. Mrs. Hale raised her head.

"What does he mean by having doubts?" she asked. "Does he not mean that he thinks differently—that he knows better than the Church?"

Margaret shook her head, tears filling her eyes as her mother touched the raw nerve of her own regret.

"Can the Bishop not set him right?" Mrs. Hale asked, half impatient.

"I am afraid not," Margaret said. "But I did not ask. I could not bear to hear his answer. It is settled anyway. He is leaving Helstone in a fortnight. I am not sure if he did not say he sent in his resignation."

"In a fortnight!" Mrs. Hale exclaimed. "I think this is very strange—not at all right. I call it unfeeling." Tears began to flow as she sought relief in lamentation. "He has doubts and gives up his living without consulting me. If he had told me at the first, I could have nipped it in the bud."

Though Margaret felt her father's conduct had been flawed, she could not bear to hear it blamed by her mother. She knew his reserve sprang from a tenderness that might seem cowardly but was not unfeeling.

"I almost hoped you might be glad to leave Helstone, Mother," she said after a pause. "You have never been well in this air."

"You cannot imagine the smoky air of a manufacturing town like Milton-Northern, full of chimneys and dirt. How could it compare to this pure, sweet air, however soft? Imagine living amidst factories and factory people! Though, of course, if Father leaves the Church, we shall be excluded from society everywhere. It will be a disgrace! Poor dear Sir John! It is well he is not alive to see what your father has come to! Every day after dinner, when I lived with your Aunt Shaw at Beresford Court, Sir John would give the first toast: 'Church and King, and down with the Rump.'"

Margaret was relieved her mother's thoughts had shifted from her husband's silence to social status. Next to the vital anxiety of her father's doubts, this was the circumstance that caused her the most pain.

"You know, we have very little society here, Mother. The Gormans, our nearest neighbors, have been in trade just as much as these Milton-Northern people."

"Yes," Mrs. Hale said indignantly, "but at least the Gormans made carriages for half the county's gentlemen and were part of society. These factory people—who on earth wears cotton when they can afford linen?"

"Well, Mother, I give up the cotton-spinners. I am not defending them or any tradespeople. We shall have little enough to do with them."

"Why on earth has Father chosen Milton-Northern?"

"Partly," Margaret sighed, "because it is so different from Helstone. Partly because Mr. Bell says there is an opening for a private tutor."

"A private tutor in Milton? Why not go to Oxford and tutor gentlemen?"

"You forget, Mother! He is leaving the Church because of his opinions. His doubts would do him no good at Oxford."

Mrs. Hale sat in silence, quietly weeping. Finally, she said, "And the furniture? How in the world shall we manage the removal? I have never removed in my life, and we have only a fortnight!"

Margaret felt immense relief that her mother's distress had narrowed to this practical point, on which she could exert influence. She planned, promised, and guided her mother through arrangements that could be fixed before they knew more about Mr. Hale's intentions. Throughout the day, Margaret never left her mother's side, bending her entire soul to sympathize with her shifting emotions. Towards evening, as anxiety grew that her father would find no welcoming home after his day of fatigue, she dwelt on what he must have endured in secret. Her mother replied coldly that he ought to have told her, that then she would have had an adviser. Margaret felt faint at heart when she heard her father's step in the hall. She dared not go to meet him, fearing her mother's jealousy. She heard him linger, waiting for a sign. Seeing her mother's twitching lips and changing color, she knew her husband had returned.

Presently, he opened the room door and stood there, uncertain whether to enter. His face was grey and pale; his eyes held a timid, fearful look, something almost pitiful in a man's visage. That look of despondent uncertainty, of mental and bodily languor, touched his wife's heart. She went to him and threw herself on his breast, crying out:

"Oh! Richard, Richard, you should have told me sooner!"

Then, in tears, Margaret left her, rushing upstairs to throw herself on her bed and hide her face in the pillows to stifle the hysterical sobs that threatened to break free after the rigid self-control of the day.

How long she lay there she could not tell. She heard no noise, though the housemaid entered to arrange the room. The frightened girl stole out on tiptoe and told Mrs. Dixon that Miss Hale was crying as if her heart would break, certain she would become ill if she continued. Consequently, Margaret felt compelled to rise and sit up. She saw the familiar room, the figure of Dixon in shadow holding a candle behind her, fearing the effect on Miss Hale's swollen, blinded eyes.

"Oh, Dixon! I did not hear you come in!" Margaret said, resuming her trembling self-restraint. "Is it very late?"
