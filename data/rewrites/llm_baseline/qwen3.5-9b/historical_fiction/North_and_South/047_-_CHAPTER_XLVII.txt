"Experience, like a pale musician, holds / A dulcimer of patience in his hand; / Whence harmonies we cannot understand, / Of God's will in His worlds, the strain unfolds / In sad, perplexed minors." — *Mrs. Browning*

Just as these lines found their echo in the quiet corners of the Hale household, Dixon returned from Milton, bringing with her the fresh air of the town and assuming her station once more as Margaret's maid. Her arrival was accompanied by an endless stream of local gossip, delivered with the enthusiasm of one who believes the world revolves around the latest tidings. She recounted how Martha had departed to reside with Miss Thornton following the latter's marriage, detailing the bridesmaids, the gowns, and the elaborate breakfasts of that memorable ceremony. There were whispers that Mr. Thornton had thrown a wedding far too grand for his circumstances, especially considering the financial blow dealt by the recent strike and the heavy costs incurred from failed contracts.

Dixon also shared her dismay over the auction of household goods. She marveled at the paltry sums fetched for items she had long cherished, deeming it a shame that such treasures yielded so little, particularly given the apparent wealth of Milton's inhabitants. Yet, she noted with amusement how Mrs. Thornton had managed to secure a few good bargains before Mr. Thornton arrived the very next day. Driven by a singular desire to acquire just one or two specific pieces, he had ended up bidding against himself, providing ample entertainment for the onlookers. As Dixon observed, this maneuver served to "make things even"; if Mrs. Thornton had paid too little previously, Mr. Thornton ensured he paid too much now.

The narrative of Mr. Bell was equally complex. He had issued various orders regarding the books, displaying a fastidiousness that left Dixon utterly bewildered. "There is no understanding him," she remarked, noting that if he had visited personally, matters might have been clearer, but letters remained perpetually more puzzling than worthwhile. When pressed for details concerning the Higginses, Dixon's memory betrayed her. Possessed of an aristocratic bias, her recollections faltered whenever she attempted to recall anything pertaining to those beneath her social standing. She asserted that Nicholas was doing well, citing his repeated visits to inquire after Miss Margaret—as had Mr. Thornton, though rarely. As for Mary, she dismissed her as a "great, stout, slatternly thing." However, a rumor persisted, perhaps merely a dream born of her imagination, that Mary had secured employment at Mr. Thornton's mill under the instruction of her father to learn the art of cooking. The logic behind such a command escaped Dixon, and Margaret, finding the tale incoherent, agreed it sounded suspiciously like a dream. Nevertheless, it was a relief to have someone with whom she could discuss Milton and its people.

While Dixon preferred to dwell on the flattering implications of Mr. Bell's speeches—which hinted at a design to make Margaret his heiress—Margaret offered no encouragement. She deflected his insinuating inquiries, regardless of how they were disguised as suspicion or assertion. Throughout this period, Margaret harbored a strange, undefined yearning to hear that Mr. Bell had traveled to Milton for business. It was a mutual understanding established during their conversation at Helstone that the explanation she craved should be delivered orally to Mr. Thornton, and even then, it should never be forced upon him. Though Mr. Bell corresponded occasionally, his letters lacked substance. Margaret received them with a lingering sense of disappointment, for he showed no sign of heading to Milton. Patience, she decided, was the only recourse; sooner or later, the mist would clear.

His correspondence had changed, becoming shorter, more complaining, and tinged with an unusual bitterness. He seemed less focused on the future and more weighed down by regrets of the past and weariness of the present. Margaret feared he was ill, prompting her to ask about his health. His response was a brief note mentioning an "old-fashioned complaint called the spleen," leaving it to her to determine whether it was mental or physical, while graciously declining to issue regular bulletins on his condition. Consequently, Margaret ceased her inquiries.

One day, Edith inadvertently revealed fragments of a conversation with Mr. Bell from his last stay in London. These words sparked a vivid hope in Margaret: that he intended to take her to visit her brother and new sister-in-law in Cadiz during the autumn. She interrogated Edith relentlessly until the girl grew weary and confessed that Mr. Bell had only half-thought of the trip. He viewed it as an opportunity to hear Frederick's firsthand account of the mutiny and for Margaret to meet her new relative. After all, he traveled annually during the long vacation; why not Spain?

Edith, hoping Margaret did not wish to depart, expressed anxiety, finally breaking down in tears to declare that she cared for Margaret far more than the reverse. Margaret comforted her gently, though she struggled to convey how this potential journey to Spain—a mere *Château en Espagne*—charmed her spirit. Edith, however, interpreted any joy experienced away from her as a tacit insult or proof of indifference. Thus, Margaret was forced to suppress her delight, venting it instead through Dixon during dressing preparations.

"She's a Papist, Miss, isn't she?" Dixon asked.
"I believe—oh, yes, certainly!" Margaret replied, momentarily dampened by the thought.
"And they live in a Popish country?"
"Yes."
"Then I'm afraid I must say that my soul is dearer to me than even Master Frederick, his own dear self. I should be in a perpetual terror, Miss, lest I should be converted."
"Oh," said Margaret, "I do not know that I am going. And if I do, I am not such a fine lady as to be unable to travel without you. No! Dear old Dixon, you shall have a long holiday if we go. But I'm afraid it is a long 'if.'"

Dixon bristled at this sentiment. First, she disliked the affectation of "Dear old Dixon," a term of endearment Margaret reserved for those she favored. To Dixon, nearing fifty and in the prime of her life, the label felt diminutive. Second, she resented being so readily convinced; despite her professed terror, a latent curiosity about Spain, the Inquisition, and Catholic mysteries lingered within her. Clearing her throat to dispel the tension, she queried whether avoiding priests and church entrances would suffice to prevent conversion.

"Master Frederick, to be sure, went over unaccountably," Margaret sighed.
"I fancy it was love that first predisposed him to conversion," Margaret continued.
"Indeed, Miss!" Dixon exclaimed. "Well! I can preserve myself from priests and churches; but love steals in unawares! I think it's as well I should not go."

Margaret feared indulging too deeply in this Spanish fantasy, as it distracted her from her impatience regarding the need for clarity with Mr. Thornton. For the moment, Mr. Bell remained stationary at Oxford, showing no immediate intent to visit Milton. A secret restraint held Margaret back from even alluding to the possibility of his departure or the fleeting mention of Spain Edith had heard. Had Mr. Bell truly considered it at Helstone? Likely just a passing fancy—but if true, it represented a radiant escape from the monotonous stagnation of her current existence.

A distinct pleasure in Margaret's life at this juncture was Edith's son. He was the pride and plaything of both parents when compliant, but his strong will often erupted into stormy passions. In such moments, Edith would collapse in despair, sighing, "Oh dear, what shall I do with him! Do, Margaret, please ring the bell for Hanley." Paradoxically, Margaret found the child more engaging in these displays of character than in his placid, blue-sashed moods. She would whisk him away to a private room where the two of them engaged in a silent battle. With a firm power that subdued him into peace, she countered every charm and wile he employed, guiding him toward righteousness until he would bury his hot, tear-streaked face against hers, kissing and caressing until sleep claimed him in her arms. These were Margaret's sweetest moments, offering a foretaste of affection she feared would forever elude her.

Mr. Henry Lennox introduced a new, albeit cool, element to the household dynamic. Margaret found him more brilliant but colder than before, yet his intellect and vast knowledge seasoned the otherwise insipid conversations. She detected in him a subtle contempt for his brother and sister-in-law, viewing their lifestyle as frivolous and aimless. On occasion, he addressed his brother sharply in Margaret's presence, questioning if he truly intended to abandon his profession. When Captain Lennox replied that his income sufficed, Mr. Lennox curled his lip, remarking, "And is that all you live for?" Despite this friction, the brothers remained attached, bound by the dynamic of the clever leading the patient follower.

Mr. Lennox advanced steadily in his career, calculatingly cultivating connections for future utility. Keen-sighted, far-seeing, intelligent, sarcastic, and proud, he maintained a certain distance from Margaret. Following their initial deep conversation regarding Frederick's affairs, their interactions had diminished to the necessities of shared domestic life. This proximity, however, had worn down her initial shyness and his signs of mortified pride. They met frequently, yet she sensed he actively avoided solitude with her. Both seemed aware they had drifted apart from their former alignment of opinions and tastes.
