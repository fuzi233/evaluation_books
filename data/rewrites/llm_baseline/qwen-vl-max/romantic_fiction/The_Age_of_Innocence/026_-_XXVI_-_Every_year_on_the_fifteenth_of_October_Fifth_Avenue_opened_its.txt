XXVI. Every year on the fifteenth of October, Fifth Avenue awoke from its autumn slumber. Shutters flew open, carpets unfurled across polished floors, and heavy drapes were drawn back to reveal windows gleaming like eyes in the crisp morning light. By the first of November, the rituals of high society had settled into their familiar rhythm—dinner invitations piled up, dance calendars filled in, and the opera season burst forth with new productions and glittering premieres. And just as reliably, around this time, Mrs. Archer would declare, with a sigh both weary and wistful, that New York had changed once again.

From her elevated vantage point of quiet disengagement, she watched the city’s social fabric with the keen eye of a historian. With Mr. Sillerton Jackson and Miss Sophy at her side, she traced each subtle fracture in tradition, each new bloom of behavior pushing through the carefully cultivated rows of propriety. It had long been one of young Newland Archer’s amusements to anticipate his mother’s annual lamentations—the way she could spot signs of decay invisible to others. To Mrs. Archer, change was never neutral; it was always a decline. And Miss Sophy Jackson, ever the dutiful companion, echoed her sentiments without hesitation. Mr. Sillerton Jackson, however, remained aloof, listening with amused detachment, though he never denied that something had shifted. And now, in the winter of his second year of marriage, even Newland felt it: New York wasn’t just changing—it was transforming.

These reflections came to the table during Mrs. Archer’s Thanksgiving dinner. At a time when gratitude was expected, she instead took stock of what had been lost. Society, if it could still be called such, seemed less a refuge than a spectacle—one fit only for biblical condemnation. Indeed, everyone knew exactly what Reverend Dr. Ashmore meant when he chose Jeremiah 2:25 for his sermon. The new rector of St. Matthew’s was considered “advanced”—his sermons bold, his language daring. When he spoke against fashionable life, he always referred to its “trend.” And to Mrs. Archer, it was both terrifying and thrilling to feel herself part of a world that was actively trending—like a crack spreading across stone.

“There’s no doubt Dr. Ashmore is right,” she said, as if the trend were something measurable, something visible. “But it was odd to preach about it on Thanksgiving.”

Miss Jackson nodded thoughtfully. “He means us to give thanks for what’s left.”

Newland had once smiled at these yearly pronouncements. But this year, he found himself nodding along. The changes were undeniable.

“The extravagance in dress,” Miss Jackson began, “is staggering. Sillerton took me to the first night of the Opera, and I can only say that Jane Merry’s gown was the only one I recognized from last season—and even that had been altered. Yet I know she brought it out of Worth two years ago. My seamstress always goes in to refashion her Paris gowns before they’re worn.”

“Ah, Jane Merry is one of *us*,” Mrs. Archer sighed, as if belonging to a fading caste were no longer an honor.

“Yes,” Miss Jackson replied. “In my youth, it was considered vulgar to wear the newest fashions. Amy Sillerton told me Bostonians kept their Paris dresses locked away for two years. Old Mrs. Baxter Pennilow imported twelve annually—two velvet, two satin, two silk, six in poplin and cashmere. A standing order. She was ill for two years before she died, so forty-eight Worth dresses were found untouched in tissue paper. When her daughters emerged from mourning, they wore the first batch at Symphony concerts—without looking ahead of the times.”

“Well, Boston is more conservative,” Mrs. Archer conceded. “But I think it’s wise for any lady to wait at least one season before wearing a French gown.”

“It was Beaufort who started this new fashion,” she continued. “Making his wife parade her clothes the moment they arrived. At times, it takes all Regina’s dignity not to look… well…”

Miss Jackson glanced at Janey, who was staring too intently, and murmured something indistinct.

“Like her rivals,” Mr. Sillerton Jackson offered, with the air of delivering a clever remark.

“Oh,” the ladies murmured, shifting their attention.

Mrs. Archer turned to her son. “Poor Regina. Her Thanksgiving hasn’t been cheerful, I fear. Have you heard the rumors about Beaufort’s speculations, Sillerton?”

Mr. Jackson shrugged. Everyone had heard them. He saw no need to confirm what was already common knowledge.

A silence fell over the room. No one truly liked Beaufort, but the idea of him bringing financial ruin upon his wife’s family was deeply unsettling. New York tolerated moral lapses in private life—but in business, honesty was sacred. It had been decades since a prominent banker failed disgracefully, but the memory of the social annihilation that followed such an event lingered. If Beaufort did fall, no amount of Dallas influence would save Regina. Not even her charm or her beauty.

The conversation drifted to safer ground, but every topic seemed to reinforce Mrs. Archer’s sense of inevitable change.

“Of course, Newland,” she said, “I know you let dear May go to Mrs. Struthers’s Sunday evenings.”

May interjected cheerfully, “Oh, everyone does now. And she was invited to Granny’s last reception.”

Archer reflected on this—how New York managed transitions: ignoring them until they were complete, then pretending they’d happened long ago. There was always someone who opened the gates. Once people tasted Mrs. Struthers’s easy hospitality, they couldn’t return to their old ways—even if they remembered her champagne had once been rumored to be transmuted shoe polish.

“I know, dear,” Mrs. Archer sighed. “Such things must happen, I suppose, as long as amusement is the goal. But I’ve never forgiven your cousin Madame Olenska for being the first to support Mrs. Struthers.”

A sudden flush rose in May’s cheeks. Her husband noticed it as much as anyone else. “Oh, *Ellen*,” she murmured, using the same tone her parents might have used when speaking of the Blenkers—accusing yet sorrowful.

It was the tone the family had adopted whenever Ellen Olenska was mentioned, since she refused to return to her husband. But coming from May, it carried a deeper weight. Archer looked at her with a strange sense of distance—this woman who belonged so completely to her world, yet sometimes seemed to exist outside it.

Mrs. Archer pressed on, unaware of the tension. “I always thought people like the Countess Olenska, who’ve lived among aristocrats, should help preserve our standards—instead of abandoning them.”

May’s blush didn’t fade. It seemed to carry a meaning beyond mere social disapproval.

“I suppose we all seem alike to foreigners,” Miss Jackson remarked tartly.

“I don’t think Ellen cares for society,” May said, searching for neutrality. “But nobody knows exactly what she *does* care for.”

Mrs. Archer sighed again. Everyone knew Ellen was no longer welcome in her family’s circle. Even old Mrs. Manson Mingott, her fiercest defender, had given up trying to persuade her to return. The Mingotts hadn’t declared their disapproval aloud—their loyalty ran too deep. Instead, they simply let Ellen “find her own level”—and that level, tragically, was somewhere near the Blenkers, among writers and artists who celebrated their messy lives.

It was incredible, but true: Ellen, despite her privilege, had become “Bohemian.” This confirmed, in many minds, that she had made a fatal mistake by refusing her husband. After all, a young woman’s place was under her husband’s roof—especially when she had left it under circumstances… well, if one cared to examine them…

“Madame Olenska is very popular with the gentlemen,” Miss Sophy observed, as if offering comfort while planting a thorn.

“Ah, yes,” Mrs. Archer agreed gravely. “That’s the danger a woman like her faces.”

And so the ladies retired to the drawing-room, leaving Archer and Mr. Jackson in the Gothic library.

Settling before the fire, Mr. Jackson lit a cigar and leaned back, suddenly expansive. “If Beaufort fails,” he said, “there’ll be disclosures.”

Archer looked up sharply. The image of Beaufort, thick-set and opulent, walking through snow at Skuytercliff, flashed in his mind.

“There’ll be a nasty cleanup,” Jackson continued. “He hasn’t spent all his money on Regina.”

“Oh, well—that’s discounted, isn’t it? I believe he’ll pull through,” Archer said, eager to shift the subject.

“Perhaps,” Jackson allowed. “He was supposed to meet some influential men today. Of course, we hope he can get through this—this time. I wouldn’t want poor Regina ending up in some shabby foreign spa for bankrupts.”

Archer said nothing. It seemed natural—however tragic—that ill-gotten wealth should be paid for in suffering. His thoughts, however, turned elsewhere: to May’s blush, to the memory of the day he and Ellen had spent together four months ago. Since then, he hadn’t seen her. He knew she was in Washington, living in a small house with Medora Manson. He had written once—briefly, asking when they might meet. Her reply: “Not yet.”

No more words followed. And in the silence between them, a sanctuary had formed in his mind—a quiet chamber where Ellen reigned, where his real thoughts and feelings lived. Outside, his life felt hollow, a performance. He moved through it with growing unease, bumping into old beliefs as if he weren’t truly present.

He became aware that Jackson was clearing his throat.

“I don’t know how far your wife’s family knows what people are saying about… well, about Madame Olenska’s refusal to accept her husband’s latest offer.”

Archer remained silent.

Jackson continued, obliquely: “It’s a pity—certainly a pity—that she refused.”

“A pity? In God’s name, why?” Archer asked.

Jackson looked down his leg, adjusting his sock. “Well—on the lowest ground—what’s she going to live on now?”

“Now?”

“If Beaufort—”

Archer stood abruptly, slamming his fist on the writing desk. The inkwell trembled.

“What the devil do you mean, sir?”

Jackson shifted in his chair, his gaze steady. “I have it on good authority—old Catherine herself—that the family reduced her allowance when she refused to return. And by refusing, she forfeits the money settled on her at marriage—money Olenski was ready to transfer if she came back. So, what *do* you mean by asking me what *I* mean?”

Archer walked to the fireplace, knocking ashes into the grate.

“I don’t know her affairs,” he said, voice tight. “But I don’t need to know to be certain that what you suggest—”

“Oh, I don’t,” Jackson interrupted. “Lefferts, for one, thinks it’s a shame.”

“Lefferts—who made love to her and got rejected!” Archer snapped.

“Ah—did he?” Jackson replied, as if catching a trap.

They stared at each other across the fire.

“Well,” Jackson said slowly, “it’s a pity she didn’t go back before Beaufort’s downfall. If she goes *now*, and he fails, it will only confirm the impression—though it’s not just Lefferts who feels that way.”

“She won’t go back now—less than ever!”

Archer had spoken without thinking. And instantly, he sensed Jackson had been waiting for that.

The old man studied him. “That’s your opinion, eh? Well, perhaps you do know best. But everyone says Medora Manson’s few remaining funds are in Beaufort’s hands. How the two women will survive unless he pulls through, I can’t imagine. Of course, Madame Olenska might still win over old Catherine—she’s been the most opposed. But we all know she hates spending money. And the rest of the family has little interest in keeping her here.”

Archer burned with frustration. He knew he was acting rashly—but he couldn’t stop. He realized Jackson had noted his ignorance of Ellen’s strained relations with her family, and had drawn conclusions about why he wasn’t privy to those secrets.

Yet he remembered: Jackson was a guest. Under the rules of old New York, no argument could take place beneath a host’s roof.

“Shall we go up and join my mother?” he asked curtly.

On the drive home, May remained silent. Through the darkness, he felt her blush like a warning.

They reached the house. He went to the library. She usually followed—but this time, he heard her turn toward her bedroom.

“May!” he called.

She returned, surprised by his tone.

“This lamp is smoking again,” he muttered, irritated. “The servants should see to it.”

“I’m sorry. It won’t happen again,” she said, bright and firm—just like her mother.

It angered him. Already, she was treating him like a younger Mr. Welland.

She bent to adjust the flame. The light caught her shoulders, her face—so young, so pure.

“How young she is,” he thought. “For how many endless years will this life go on?”

He felt the heat of his own blood, the strength of his youth.

“Look here,” he said suddenly. “I may have to go to Washington—soon. Next week, perhaps.”

Her hand stayed on the lamp’s key as she turned slowly.

“On business?” she asked, as if it were the only possible reason.

“On business, naturally. There’s a patent case before the Supreme Court—” He named the inventor, gave details, speaking with the smooth confidence of Lawrence Lefferts.

She listened, nodding. “Yes, I see.”

“When you go,” she said quietly, “you must be sure to visit Ellen.”

She looked at him, smiling—clear-eyed, calm.

It was the only word they exchanged. But in their world, it meant everything:

*Of course I know what people say about Ellen. I understand your role in her defiance. I know you advised her not to return—against the wishes of the whole family, including Grandmother. I know you encouraged her. I know you plan to see her. I know you may be going to Washington for that purpose. And I tell you—because I cannot speak plainly—this is not approved. But I also tell you: I approve of your seeing her. I wish you to go. And I wish you to make her understand the consequences of her choices.*

Her hand remained on the lamp’s key.

She lowered the wick, removed the globe, and blew gently on the flame.

“They smell less if you blow them out,” she said, practical, serene.

At the doorway, she paused. “Goodnight.”

He kissed her—once, briefly—before stepping into the darkened hall.
