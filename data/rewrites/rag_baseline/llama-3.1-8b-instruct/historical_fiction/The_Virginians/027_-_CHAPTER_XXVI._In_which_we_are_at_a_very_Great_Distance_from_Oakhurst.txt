The room was dimly lit, the scent of old books and dust hanging heavy in the air. I sat amidst stacks of papers, the faint scratching of quills on parchment a soothing background hum. My mind wandered to the conversation I'd overheard at the White Horse Tavern, the words of the three gentlemen lingering like a melody. Mr. Morris's proposal to adjourn to the bowling green, the banter between Lord Chesterfield and Lord March, the wager over the value of Castlewood – it all seemed like a distant memory, a pleasant, trivial thing.

I recalled the specifics, though: the three gentlemen, their laughter and jests, the clinking of glasses, the dice-box and bottle-screw. And Mr. Warrington, the young man from Virginia, his blush when Lord March teased him about playing cards with his cousin and Will Esmond. The way he deftly navigated the conversation, his smile a subtle rebuke to the mocking tone.

My thoughts drifted to the music of the tavern, the sound of strings and woodwinds carried on the breeze through the open window. A lone violinist played a melancholy air, the notes weaving in and out of the murmur of conversation. I felt a pang of longing, a sense of disconnection from the world outside these walls. The silence was almost palpable, a physical presence that wrapped itself around me like a shroud.

As I returned to my work, the sounds of the tavern receded, leaving only the scratch of quill on parchment, the soft creak of leather-bound books. The world outside was vast and unknowable, a realm of uncertainty and noise. But in this quiet, dimly lit room, I found a fragile peace, a moment's respite from the chaos that lay beyond the walls.
