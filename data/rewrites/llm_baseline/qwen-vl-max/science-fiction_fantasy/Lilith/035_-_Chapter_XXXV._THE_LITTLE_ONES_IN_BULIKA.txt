The morning dawned clear and bright as we set forth across the wide plain, a vibrant procession beneath a sky of endless blue and grass that shimmered like emerald silk. We moved with purpose—marching through the morning light, resting in the afternoon heat, then pressing on under the starlit night. Our rhythm was deliberate: travel by day, pause by sun, journey again at twilight. By the time we reached the city’s edge, we aimed to arrive just as the first golden rays touched the horizon, slipping through the gates before anyone could raise alarm.

It seemed the entire forest had come with us. Birds surged ahead in flocks, mistaken for vanguards; butterflies and dragonflies danced around our heads like living sparks; and four-legged creatures trailed behind, drawn by curiosity or instinct. As dusk fell, most of the beasts turned back, but the birds and insects stayed, fluttering and humming all the way to the city walls.

We halted mid-afternoon and slept soundly—a true march, yet no one felt weariness. At night, the cold urged us forward. Some drifted off atop their mounts, waking refreshed with the morning light. Not a single rider tumbled from their beast. Bears, shaggy and slow-moving, carried some, yet kept pace with the elephants. Others rode deer, eager to race, until I stopped them. Those perched high above on hay-laden elephants, unable to see below, chatted endlessly with their unseen companions. Once, during a rest, I heard a small voice whisper into the bundle of straw: “Nosy dear, I’m digging you out of the mountain now—just a little more! Soon you’ll lift your nose up, and we’ll kiss like good elephants, won’t we?”

That night, a storm of noise erupted—elephant roars, horse whinnies, children’s cries echoing far across the still land. Fearing we might be too close to the city, I silenced the clamor swiftly, lest it betray our presence.

Then, one morning, the sun rose—and so did the city, rising from the earth like a dream made stone. To the children, the walls were merely a great rock formation. But when I told them it held countless stone nests within, fear flickered in their eyes for the first time—perhaps the first real fear in their short lives. How could they find mothers in such a place? Yet they pressed on, guided by faith in Lona, and in me, though I knew I scarcely deserved it.

We rode through the archway. The thunder of hooves, the pad of paws and feet, shook the ancient stones. Horses startled at their own echoes, some reared, others veered wildly—but soon calmed. The Little Ones stood frozen, silent as shadows. The girls clutched their infants tighter. Only bears and butterflies remained unafraid. The woman beside me wore a mask of dread, and I, too, felt its weight—this army was mine, and I bore its fate. I had led them to danger, and now its shadow stretched over us.

But I found strength in vision—the kingdom I dreamed of, where the Little Ones ruled, the giants served, and animals bowed in loyalty. Alas, I who spoke of freedom had not learned obedience myself. My pride had placed me at the head of this innocent host. I was no king—I was a slave, like any ruler in the world I’d left behind, doing only what pleased me.

Yet beside me rode Lona—truly a child, truly free. Calm, watchful, fearless. She needed no words, no reassurance. Her presence steadied me.

We neared the heart of the city before any inhabitant noticed us. Then windows flew open, sleepy faces peered out. At first, blank astonishment spread across each face. But when they saw the animals—bears, deer, elephants—their expressions twisted into horror. Still, upon seeing that our force was almost entirely children, women poured into the streets, followed by men. They kept to the edges, leaving the center clear—afraid to approach the beasts.

Then, a boy—no older than five, his mind fixed on finding his mother—spied a woman in the crowd whose face stirred recognition. He leapt from his antelope and clung to her neck. She embraced him instantly, kissing him fiercely. But a man’s hand seized the boy by the throat. A girl nearby drove her spear into the man’s arm. He howled, and in seconds, several others struck him down. He fled, screaming.

“They are just bad giants,” Lona said, her voice sharp as steel, as she spurred her horse toward a towering figure blocking her path. The giant flinched, stepped aside—and moments later crumpled under a hail of stones.

Another brute stepped between me and the boy riding behind me, speaking in a language harsh and guttural. The boy told him to speak to the king. The giant swung a hammer, striking the horse’s head. It collapsed. Before he could strike again, an elephant charged, knocking him flat and trampling him underfoot. He didn’t rise until long after we passed.

But when the women came into view, Lona’s face darkened. Not one looked kind, not one seemed worthy. Could her darlings find mothers among such faces?

No sooner had we entered the central square than two girls galloped up, breathless. Two boys had been taken by women. We turned—only to realize the woman we had trusted had vanished with her baby.

Then, from a narrow lane leading to the palace, a white leopardess bounded toward us. The Little Ones remembered the battle in the forest—some recoiled, their ranks wavering. But I had given orders. They stood firm.

We watched. Then Odu, a swift and brave boy, leapt from his bear and ran to meet her. The leopardess braked so sharply she rolled over twice. When she regained her footing, the child was already on her back.

Who could doubt the power of transformation when a child of the enemy rode the creature they feared most?

Confident now, we continued. At the house our guides pointed to, a scream tore through the air. I dismounted and pounded on the door. My horse pushed me aside, turned, and began battering the wood with his heels.

Then—Odu arrived, riding the leopardess. He froze, trembling. But the animal had heard the cry. She forgot the child on her back and lunged at the door. The boy was thrown against it, knocked unconscious.

Before I could move, Lona had him in her arms. When he woke, she placed him back on his bear, which had followed faithfully.

The leopardess charged the door a third time—and it gave way. She darted inside.

We rushed after her. The house was empty. Upstairs, nothing. Downstairs, we found a hidden door beneath the stairs, leading into a maze of tunnels.

Not far in, we met the leopardess again—carrying the boy we sought.

He told us the woman he thought was his mother threw him into a pit, saying she would give him to the leopardess. But the leopardess was good. She pulled him out.

We searched for the other boy. Found him too late. One of the savages had killed him.

Lona sighed—not with grief, but relief. “At least,” she said softly, “he won’t grow into a bad giant.” Death had spared him the worst.

The leopardess leapt onto the murderer, seized him by the throat, dragged him into the street, and carried him like a cat dragging a rat.

“Let us leave this place,” Lona said. “There are no mothers here. This people is not worth saving.”

She dropped the body. The leopardess charged into the crowd, slashing through the throng, scattering slaves like leaves in a gale.

Back at the army, all stood in order, waiting. But I was uneasy. The princess had not shown herself. What secrets might she hide? We would keep watch through the night.

The Little Ones needed no beds. We told them to lie down with their animals where they stood. In an instant, they were asleep—breathing in soft, rhythmic waves, like water flowing over grass, or wind through leaves.

Their beasts slept lightly, ever alert. Older children wandered quietly among the sleeping mass. Silence fell. The whole city seemed to breathe with us—still, quiet, and heavy with unease.
