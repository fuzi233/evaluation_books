Ten days after that fateful morning, we found ourselves back in our old quarters at Loo—safe, sound, and remarkably unscathed by the horrors we had endured, save for a few telltale signs. My once-thick hair had turned three shades grayer from the ordeal, and Good, though still as sharp as ever, carried a quiet sorrow in his eyes ever since Foulata’s death. It was a loss that deeply moved him, and I could not help but feel it was better this way. From my perspective as a seasoned traveler of the world, her passing spared us future entanglements. She was no ordinary girl—she was strikingly beautiful, with a dignity and grace rare among her people, and possessed a mind far beyond the usual. Yet, as she herself once said, “Can the sun mate with darkness? Can white unite with black?” No amount of beauty or refinement could have made such a union wise—or desirable.

We never returned to Solomon’s treasure chamber. After two days of rest to recover from our exhaustion, we descended into the great pit where we’d escaped, hoping to locate the narrow passage that had led us out. But rain had washed away our tracks, and the sheer cliffs were riddled with ant-bear burrows and other crevices—so many that we couldn’t be sure which one had saved our lives. The mystery remained.

On the eve of our departure, we revisited the stalactite cave, drawn by a restless curiosity. Once more, we stepped into the Chamber of the Dead, passing beneath the spear of the White Death. There, before us, lay the massive rock that had sealed our escape route—impenetrable now, like a tombstone over an ancient secret. We stared at it, transfixed, thinking of the treasures beyond, of Gagool’s mangled remains crushed beneath it, and of Foulata, whose final resting place it guarded. Yet despite our efforts, no trace of the sliding door remained visible. No mechanism could be found—no lever, no pressure plate, no hidden switch. The device was gone, lost to time, its operation forever forgotten. It was a marvel of engineering—massive, silent, and utterly mysterious—a relic of a civilization long vanished. Even if the stone had risen before our eyes, I doubt any of us would have dared step across the bones of the witch to re-enter the chamber, even knowing it held diamonds beyond measure.

And yet… I could have wept at the thought of leaving behind what may well be the greatest hoard of wealth ever gathered in one place. But there was nothing to be done. Only dynamite could break through five feet of solid rock. So we left it. Perhaps one day, in some distant age, another explorer will discover the key—the “Open Sesame”—and unleash those gems upon the world. But I suspect not. Something in me feels that those tens of millions in jewels, locked in their stone coffers, are destined never to shine on human necks. They will remain cold companions to Foulata’s bones until the end of time.

With heavy hearts, we turned back toward Loo. And yet, our disappointment was misplaced. For I had taken care—before fleeing the cave—to fill every pocket of my old shooting coat and trousers with gems. And Foulata’s basket, though partly occupied by a water bottle, still held twice as many more. Some stones tumbled out during our descent down the pit—several large ones, too, which I’d stuffed into my coat pockets—but the majority remained. Among them were ninety-three magnificent diamonds, ranging from seventy to over two hundred carats. Between my coat, my trousers, and the basket, we carried enough treasure to make us rich beyond imagination—not millionaires by American standards, perhaps, but wealthy beyond dreams, with enough gemstones left for each of us to possess the finest collections in Europe. We hadn’t come out empty-handed.

At Loo, Ignosi received us warmly. He was well, busy rebuilding his kingdom, reorganizing his regiments after the great war with Twala. He listened intently to our tale, but when we spoke of Gagool’s death, he fell silent. Then he called to an ancient Induna, seated with others in a circle near him.

“Come hither,” he said.

The old man rose, bowed, and sat before the king.

“You are aged,” Ignosi said.

“Ay, my lord,” replied the Induna. “Thy father’s father and I were born on the same day.”

“Tell me—when you were young, did you know Gagaoola, the witch-doctress?”

“I did, my lord.”

“What was she like? Young, as you?”

“No, my lord. She was as she is now—as she was in the days of my great-grandfather: old, shriveled, ugly, full of evil.”

“She is dead.”

“So, O king. Then the ancient curse is lifted from the land.”

“Go.”

“_Koom!_ I go, Black Puppy, who tore out the old dog’s throat. _Koom!_”

“See, brothers,” said Ignosi, “this woman was strange indeed. I am glad she is gone. She would have let you die in the dark place—and perhaps later have killed me, as she killed my father and raised Twala, whom her black heart loved, to take his place. Now, continue your tale. Never has there been anything like it!”

After I recounted our escape—just as we had agreed—I turned to Ignosi with a request.

“And now, Ignosi,” I said, “the time has come for us to bid thee farewell and return to our own land. You came to us as a servant; now we leave you a mighty king. If you are grateful, remember your promise: rule justly, honor the law, and spare life without cause. In doing so, you shall prosper. Tomorrow, at dawn, send us an escort to guide us across the mountains. Is it not so, O king?”

Ignosi covered his face with his hands and remained silent for a long while.

“My heart is torn,” he said at last. “Your words split it in two. What have I done to you—Incubu, Macumazahn, Bougwan—that you should leave me so desolate? You stood by me in rebellion, in battle—will you abandon me now, in peace and victory? Would you not stay? Wives? Choose from my maidens. A home? Take all the land you can see. Houses? I will learn my people to build them. Cattle? Every married man shall bring you an ox or cow. Game? The elephant walks through my forests, the river-horse sleeps in the reeds. War? My Impis await your command. If there is anything else you desire, I will give it.”

“We want none of these things,” I replied. “We seek only our own place.”

Then Ignosi spoke bitterly, his eyes flashing.

“Now I understand,” he said. “You love the bright stones more than me, your friend. You have the stones. Now you wish to go to Natal, cross the black waters, sell them, and grow rich—just as a white man desires. Cursed be the white stones! Cursed be the man who seeks them! Death to anyone who sets foot in the Place of Death to find them. I have spoken. Go, white men. You may leave.”

I placed my hand on his arm.

“Ignosi,” I said, “tell me—when you wandered in Zululand and among the white people of Natal, did your heart not turn toward your mother’s land, the place where you first saw light, where you played as a child? That was your true home.”

“It was so, Macumazahn.”

“In the same way, Ignosi, our hearts turn toward our own lands. Our homes call to us.”

A silence followed. Then Ignosi spoke again, softer now.

“I see now, as ever, that your words are wise and full of reason. The bird that flies does not wish to run on the ground. The white man does not wish to live among the kraals of the black. Well, you must go. And I shall be left alone, for you will be as dead to me—since no word from you can reach me.”

“But listen,” he continued, “and let all your brothers hear. No other white man shall cross these mountains—no trader with guns and gin. My people shall fight with spears and drink water, as their ancestors did. I will not allow praying-men to frighten men’s hearts, to stir up rebellion against the king, or open the way for white folk to follow. If one white man comes to my gates, I will send him back. If a hundred come, I will push them back. If armies come, I will wage war with all my strength, and they shall not prevail. None shall seek the shining stones—not even an army. If they come, I will send a regiment to fill the pit, collapse the white columns in the caves, and bury them under rocks, so that no one may reach even the door you speak of—whose secret is now lost.

“But for you three—Incubu, Macumazahn, Bougwan—the path is always open. For you are dearer to me than anything alive.”

“You would go,” he said. “Infadoos, my uncle and my Induna, shall take you by the hand and lead you with a regiment. There is another way across the mountains—he shall show you. Farewell, my brothers, brave white men. Do not come back. I cannot bear to see you go.”

He rose, looked at us earnestly for a moment, then threw the corner of his karross over his head, hiding his face from us. We left in silence.

At dawn the next day, we departed from Loo, escorted by Infadoos—our old friend, broken-hearted at our leaving—and the regiment of Buffaloes. Though early, the main street was packed with people. As we passed at the head of the regiment, they gave us the royal salute. Women threw flowers at our feet, blessing us for freeing their land from Twala. It was deeply moving—an honor rarely given by natives.

One incident broke the solemnity, and we welcomed it. Just before we left the town, a young girl ran forward, holding lovely lilies, and offered them to Good—everyone seemed to favor him, perhaps because of his eye-glass and solitary whisker, which gave him a curious charm. Then she asked for a favor.

“Let my lord show his servant his beautiful white legs,” she said. “So that I may remember them always, and tell my children of them. I have walked four days to see them—for their fame has spread across the land.”

Good recoiled. “I’ll be hanged if I do!” he exclaimed.

“Come now,” said Sir Henry, “you can’t refuse a lady.”

“I won’t,” insisted Good. “It’s positively indecent.”

But eventually, he relented. With the crowd watching, he rolled up his trousers to the knee. Gasps of admiration rose from the women—especially the young girl—and he had to walk like that until we cleared the town. I fear Good’s legs will never be admired again. His teeth, his “transparent eye”—these the Kukuanas grew tired of. But his legs? Never.

As we traveled, Infadoou told us of a second pass north of Solomon’s Great Road—a path where one could descend the cliff wall separating Kukuanaland from the desert, broken by the towering shapes of Sheba’s Breasts. He also spoke of hunters who, two years prior, had descended this route in search of ostriches. Thirst drove them far from the mountains, until they saw trees on the horizon. Following them, they discovered a vast oasis—fertile, well-watered, miles across. Infadoos suggested we take this route, and it seemed wise: it would spare us the harsh mountain pass. Moreover, several of the hunters were with us to guide us, and they claimed they could see other fertile patches far off in the desert.

It puzzled us all—how Ignosi’s mother, carrying a child, could have crossed these dangers and survived, when we nearly died. But I’ve since wondered: perhaps she took this very path, wandering into the wilderness like Hagar. If so, everything makes sense. She might have been found by ostrich hunters, brought to the oasis, then slowly guided southward to Zululand—just as Ignosi himself described.

We journeyed easily, and on the fourth night, we stood once more atop the mountain ridge overlooking the desert, stretching endlessly below, twenty-five miles north of Sheba’s Breasts. At dawn the next day, we reached the edge of a deep chasm, where we would descend to the plain two thousand feet below.

There, we bid farewell to Infadoos, our steadfast friend and warrior. He blessed us solemnly, nearly weeping.

“Never,” he said, “shall my old eyes see the like of you again. Ah! the way Incubu cut down his men in battle! Ah! the stroke that severed Twala’s head! It was beautiful—beautiful! I may never see such a sight again—unless in dreams.”

We were deeply saddened. Good was so overcome that he gave Infadoos a souvenir—what do you think? An *eye-glass*. Later we realized it was a spare one. Infadoos was thrilled, convinced it would greatly enhance his status. After several attempts, he actually managed to screw it into his own eye. The sight of the old warrior in a leopard-skin cloak, black ostrich plumes, and a European eye-glass was absurd—yet somehow fitting.

After ensuring our guides were well-supplied, and receiving a thunderous farewell salute from the Buffaloes, we shook Infadoos’s hand and began our descent. It was grueling, but by evening we reached the bottom safely.

That night, as we sat by the fire and looked up at the towering cliffs above, Sir Henry said:

“I think there are worse places than Kukuanaland in the world. I’ve known unhappier times than these past weeks—though never stranger ones. Eh, you fellows?”

“I almost wish I were back,” sighed Good.

For my part, I reflected that all’s well that ends well. But in a lifetime of narrow escapes, none had matched the ones we’d just survived. Thinking of the battle sent a chill through me. And the treasure chamber—!

Next morning, we set out across the desert, with our five guides carrying ample water. We camped in the open, marching again at dawn. By noon on the third day, we could see the trees of the oasis. By sunset, we walked once more upon grass, hearing the gentle sound of running water.
