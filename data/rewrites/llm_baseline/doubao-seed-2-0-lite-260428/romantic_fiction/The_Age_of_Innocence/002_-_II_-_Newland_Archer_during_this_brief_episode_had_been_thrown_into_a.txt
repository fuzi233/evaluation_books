Newland Archer had been twisted into a strange, hot, flustered state of embarrassment by the scene playing out before him at the opera. It was infuriating that the box drawing every unapologetic stare from New York’s entire masculine elite was the one holding his betrothed May Welland, seated primly between her mother and aunt. For a long, confused moment he couldn’t place the lady in the soft, clinging Empire gown at the center of the fuss, nor work out why her presence had every seasoned society regular whispering behind their gloves. Then realization dawned, and a hot rush of indignation pricked at his cheeks. No, really—no one would have imagined the Mingotts would dare pull this stunt!

But they had. There was no denying it; the low, knowing murmurs rippling behind him left no room for doubt: the young woman was May’s cousin, the one the family only ever called “poor Ellen Olenska.” Archer knew she’d slipped back into New York from Europe just a day or two prior, had even heard from May (who hadn’t sounded the least bit disapproving) that she’d stopped by to visit the bereaved, ill-fated countess where she was staying with old Mrs. Manson Mingott.

Archer had always stood fully by the idea of family solidarity, and one of the traits he most adored about the Mingott clan was their stubborn, unapologetic defense of the very few black sheep their otherwise spotless bloodline had ever produced. There was nothing petty or mean-spirited in the young man’s heart, and he was glad his future wife would never be held back by silly, false prudery from showing kindness, in private, to her unhappy cousin. But welcoming Countess Olenska into a quiet family drawing room was one thing. Parading her out in public, of all places at the Opera, and in the very same box as May—whose engagement to him was due to be formally announced in a matter of weeks? That was crossing every unspoken line Fifth Avenue had ever drawn. He felt exactly as old Sillerton Jackson did: he never thought the Mingotts would have dared try it.

Of course he knew that whatever man dared to do within the unwritten laws of Fifth Avenue, old Catherine Manson Mingott, the clan’s fierce matriarch, dared first. He’d long admired the towering, unyielding old woman: she’d started life as plain Catherine Spicer of Staten Island, with a father whose reputation was shadowed by scandal, no money, no connections to make the world forget her humble roots, and still she’d married the heir to the vast Mingott fortune, wed two of her daughters to “foreigners” (an Italian marquis and a wealthy English banker), and topped every one of her audacious feats by building a sprawling pale cream stone house in what was then an uninhabited wilderness right next to Central Park—at a time when brown sandstone was as universal a home choice for respectable families as a frock coat for afternoon calls.

Old Mrs. Mingott’s foreign-raised daughters had become local legend. They never came home to visit, and their mother, like many sharp-witted, strong-willed people, was already too sedentary and stout to travel far, so she’d stayed put at home, unapologetic. That cream house, supposedly modeled on the private aristocratic hotels of Paris, stood as a public monument to her unshakable nerve. She held court inside it, surrounded by pre-Revolutionary antiques and trinkets from Louis Napoleon’s Tuileries (where she’d shone in her middle age), as placid as if there was nothing odd at all about living above 34th Street, or having French windows that swung open like doors instead of the stuffy old sash panes everyone else used.

Everyone, including Sillerton Jackson, agreed old Catherine had never been conventionally beautiful—a gift New York society believed justified every success, and excused a whole host of small failings. Crueler gossips said she’d carved out her success the way her namesake the Russian empress had: by sheer force of will, a hardiness of heart, and a kind of haughty, unashamed effrontery that was somehow made acceptable by the spotless decency of her private life. Her husband Manson Mingott had died when she was only twenty-eight, and had tied up his fortune tight with extra clauses born of the whole family’s inherent distrust of the Spicer line, but his bold young widow had moved through the world fearlessly anyway: mixed freely in high European society, married her daughters off in glamorous, slightly disreputable foreign circles, dined with dukes and ambassadors, counted opera singers and Madame Taglioni herself as close friends, and through it all, as Sillerton Jackson was the first to point out, there had never been a single whisper against her own reputation. The only way, he never failed to add, that she differed from the original Catherine the Great.

Old Mrs. Mingott had long since untied the strictures on her husband’s fortune, and lived in easy affluence for half a century, but the sting of her early poverty had made her stubbornly thrifty. Though every dress and piece of furniture she bought was of the finest possible quality, she could never bring herself to spend much on the fleeting, trivial pleasures of a good meal. For entirely different reasons than Archer’s own mother, her table was famously poor, and her cellar wines even worse. Her relatives complained that the stingy, pre-made dishes she served discredited the Mingott name, which had always been synonymous with generous, luxurious hospitality, but people still flocked to her parties anyway, no matter the flat champagne. When her son Lovell, who kept the best chef in New York to salvage the family name, remonstrated with her over it, she’d only laugh and say “What’s the use of two good cooks in one family, now that I’ve married the girls off and can’t even eat rich sauces anymore?”

Lost in these thoughts, Archer lifted his eyes back to the Mingott box. He saw that Mrs. Welland and her sister-in-law were facing their semicircle of staring, judgmental onlookers with that signature Mingott poise old Catherine had drilled into every member of the family, and only May Welland showed a flush high on her cheeks (likely because she knew Archer was watching her) that betrayed she understood exactly how high the stakes were. As for the cause of all the commotion: Ellen Olenska sat gracefully in the far corner of the box, her eyes fixed intently on the stage, leaning forward just far enough to show a little more bare shoulder and décolletage than respectable New York ladies ever dared to wear, especially women who supposedly had good reason to keep a low profile.

To Archer, few sins felt more unforgivable than an offense against Taste—that distant, untouchable divinity for whom Form was the only visible, unchallenged representative. Countess Olenska’s pale, serious face struck him as perfectly suited to the moment, suited to her sad, unhappy history, but the way her dress, unadorned by any lacy neck tucker, sloped away from her thin shoulders shocked him, unsettled him. He hated the thought of his sweet, unworldly May being exposed to the influence of a young woman so careless of society’s unspoken rules.

“After all,” he heard one of the younger men behind him begin—everyone always talked noisily through the Mephistopheles and Martha scenes of the opera—“what actually happened?”
“She left him, no one’s pretending that’s not true,” someone replied.
“He’s an absolute brute, isn’t he?” the young inquirer pressed, a earnest boy named Thorley who was already gearing up to leap to the countess’s defense.
“The worst sort,” Lawrence Lefferts cut in, with his usual authority on all matters of scandal and taste. “I knew him in Nice. Half-paralyzed, white, sneering creature—handsome enough head, but eyes with a ridiculous amount of dark lashes. Let me tell you what he’s like: when he wasn’t chasing women, he was hoarding fancy china. Paid absurd prices for both, from what I hear.”
A ripple of laughter went around the group, and young Thorley pushed on: “Well, then—?”
“Well then, she ran off with his secretary.”
“Oh.” The young would-be champion’s face fell.
“It didn’t last long, though. A few months later she was found living all alone, half-starved in Venice. Lovell Mingott went over to bring her home. Said she was utterly miserable. That’s all fine and good, but parading her out at the Opera? That’s something else entirely.”
“Maybe,” Thorley ventured, flushing bright red, “she’s too unhappy to be stuck alone at home.”
The line was met with a burst of irreverent snickering, and the boy blushed even harder, trying to look like he’d meant to imply some racy double entendre rather than simple sympathy.
“Well, it’s odd they brought Miss Welland along, no matter what,” someone muttered, with a pointed side-glance straight at Archer.
“Oh, that’s all part of the plan. Granny’s orders, no doubt,” Lefferts laughed. “When the old lady sets her mind to something, she sees it through to the end.”

The act drew to a close, and the whole opera house stirred. Suddenly Archer felt a wild, unshakable impulse to act. The urge to be the very first man to cross the threshold of the Mingott box, to announce to every staring gossip in the room that he stood firmly on the family’s side, to make their engagement public before the gossips could taint it further, to stand beside May and help her weather whatever awkwardness her cousin’s odd, anomalous position might drag her into: that impulse swept away every last one of his scruples and hesitations. He was hurrying down the plush red opera house corridors before he even thought twice, heading for the far side of the auditorium.

The moment he stepped into the box his eyes locked with May’s, and he saw instantly that she understood exactly why he’d come—even if the fierce family dignity they both considered a core virtue kept her from saying a single word out loud. Everyone in their world lived surrounded by faint, half-spoken implications and pale, delicate courtesies, and the quiet, wordless understanding that passed between them felt to Archer far more intimate than any grand, flowery declaration ever could. Her eyes said *You see exactly why Mamma brought me here*, and his answered *I would never, for anything in the world, have let you face this alone*.

“You know my niece, Countess Olenska?” Mrs. Welland asked as she shook hands with her future son-in-law. Archer bowed, as custom dictated for a new introduction to a lady, without offering his hand, and Ellen Olenska tilted her head in a small, quiet nod, her pale gloved hands clasped tight around a massive fan made of eagle feathers. After greeting Mrs. Lovell Mingott, the large, blonde matron whose heavy satin dress creaked softly when she moved, Archer sank down into the seat beside his betrothed, and leaned in to speak low in her ear.
“I hope you’ve told Madame Olenska that we’re engaged? I want everyone to know. I want you to let me announce it formally tonight, at the ball after the opera.”

May Welland’s face flushed pink as dawn breaking over the park, and she looked up at him with radiant, sparkling eyes. “If you can persuade Mamma,” she said, soft and bright. “But why should we change the plans we already settled?”
He didn’t answer with words, only with the look he gave her, and she smiled, more confident now, and added: “Go tell my cousin yourself. I give you leave. She says she used to play with you, when you were both very small children.”

She pushed her chair back to make space for him, and Archer, deliberately, a little ostentatiously, making sure every single person in the surrounding boxes saw exactly what he was doing, sat down right beside Countess Olenska.
“We did use to play together, didn’t we?” she asked, turning her grave, soft eyes up to meet his. “You were a horrid little boy, who once kissed me once behind a drawing room door. But it was your cousin Vandie Newland, who never once even looked at me, that I was truly in love with back then.” Her gaze drifted out, sweeping the whole horseshoe curve of boxes packed with New York’s elite. “Ah, how this place brings it all flooding back. I keep seeing every last one of them as small children, in knickerbockers and pantalettes,” she said, in that soft, slightly lilted accent she’d picked up living abroad, her eyes drifting back to his face.

Pleasant as her expression was, Archer was shocked that she could paint such an unseemly, flippant picture of the august, unforgiving society tribunal that was judging her every move at that very moment. Nothing could be worse taste than that kind of misplaced, careless lightness. He answered a little stiffly: “Yes. You have been away a very long time.”
“Oh, centuries and centuries. So long,” she said, “that I am quite sure I am dead and buried, and this dear, familiar old place is heaven.” For reasons he could not name, the line struck Newland Archer as an even more shockingly irreverent way to describe the stuffy, rule-bound New York society he’d lived in his whole life.
