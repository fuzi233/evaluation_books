The following morning, Willoughby arrived at the cottage with a gracefulness that Margaret, in her description of him, termed "elegance" rather than mere precision. He paid his respects early, driven by a desire to inquire personally into the welfare of those he had aided. Mrs. Dashwood welcomed him with a warmth born of both her own gratitude and Sir John's glowing endorsement; indeed, the entire visit served to reassure him of the family's charm, mutual affection, and domestic harmony. Yet, regarding their physical allure, he needed no second glance to be convinced.

Elinor possessed a delicate complexion, regular features, and a remarkably pleasing figure. Marianne, however, surpassed her sister entirely. Though her stature lacked the strict correctness of Elinor's, her height lent her form a striking presence. Her face was so radiant that calling her "beautiful" felt like a lesser offense than usual. Her skin, though tanned, glowed with an unusual brilliance due to its transparency. Every feature was excellent; her smile was sweet and inviting, while her deep, dark eyes burned with a vitality, spirit, and eagerness that was impossible not to admire.

Initially, Marianne kept her gaze lowered, embarrassed by the memory of his rescue. But as this initial shyness faded, replaced by a recognition of his frankness and vivacity—and especially when he confessed his passionate love for music and dancing—she bestowed upon him a look of approval that instantly claimed the majority of his conversation. Any mention of favorite pastimes immediately unlocked her tongue; she spoke with neither reserve nor hesitation. Soon, they discovered a profound alignment in their tastes for dance and song. When Marianne turned her inquiry toward literature, her enthusiasm was so infectious that any man of twenty-five would have been hard-pressed not to agree with her judgments, regardless of prior opinions. Their minds mirrored one another perfectly; whether agreeing on a passage or debating a point, Willoughby yielded to the force of her arguments and the sparkle in her eyes. Before his departure, they conversed with the intimacy of old friends.

"Well, Marianne," Elinor remarked as soon as he left, "for a single morning, I believe you have accomplished quite enough. You have already gauged Mr. Willoughby's views on nearly every important subject. You know his take on Cowper and Scott, are sure he appreciates Pope appropriately, and have confirmed his admiration for the beauties of poetry. But how long can such rapid progress sustain an acquaintance? You will soon exhaust every favorite topic. A future meeting will merely reveal his thoughts on picturesque scenery and remarriage, leaving you with little else to discuss."

"Elinor!" Marianne exclaimed, indignant. "Is this fair? Is this just? Are my ideas so limited?" She quickly realized her error. "Ah, I see! I have been too comfortable, too happy, too open. I have violated every convention of decorum; I have been sincere where I should have been reserved, spirited where I should have been dull, and honest where I should have been deceitful. Had I spoken only of the weather and the roads, limiting myself to speech once every ten minutes, this rebuke would have been avoided."

"My dear," their mother interjected gently, "do not take offense at Elinor's jest. I would scold her myself if she wished to dampen the joy of your conversation with our new friend."

Marianne's anger melted instantly. On his part, Willoughby offered every sign of delight in their growing friendship, eager to deepen the bond. He visited daily. Initially, checking on Marianne's health was his pretext, but as her welcome grew warmer each day, the need for such an excuse vanished entirely, especially since she had fully recovered and was no longer confined to the house.

Willoughby proved to be a youth of considerable ability, quick imagination, lively spirits, and open, affectionate manners. He was ideally suited to capture Marianne's heart. Beyond these qualities, he possessed a natural ardor of mind that was ignited and amplified by Marianne's own example, making him irresistible to her. Their society became her greatest delight. They read, talked, and sang together; his musical talent was significant, and he approached reading with a sensitivity and spirit that Edward sadly lacked. In Mrs. Dashwood's eyes, he was flawless. Elinor, however, found fault only with a tendency shared by her sister: speaking too freely of his opinions without regard for the listener or the situation. By hastily judging others, prioritizing the attention of those he loved over general politeness, and lightly dismissing social conventions, he displayed a lack of caution that Elinor could not endorse, despite Marianne's vigorous defense.

Marianne began to realize that her despair at sixteen—a fear that no man could ever meet her ideal of perfection—had been rash and unjustified. Willoughby embodied everything her youthful imagination had conjured in that tragic hour and in all subsequent dreams. His behavior confirmed his desires were as intense as his talents were strong. Even Mrs. Dashwood, who had initially harbored no romantic hopes for her daughters' futures based on financial prospects, found herself hoping and expecting a match within a week, secretly congratulating herself on securing two such sons-in-law: Edward and Willoughby.

Meanwhile, Colonel Brandon's affection for Marianne, previously noted only by acquaintances, finally drew Elinor's full attention. With everyone's focus diverted to the more fortunate rival, the mockery Brandon had endured earlier faded as his genuine feelings emerged. Elinor reluctantly accepted that the sentiments Mrs. Jennings had attributed to him were real, and that while Willoughby's attraction stemmed from a similarity of temperament, Brandon's regard thrived precisely because of their differences. She viewed the situation with concern: what hope did a quiet man of fifty-three have against a vibrant one of twenty? Unable to wish him success, she fervently prayed for his indifference. Yet, she liked him. Despite his gravity and reserve, she saw in him an object of interest. His serious manner was mild, and his reserve seemed born of sorrow rather than innate gloom. Having heard hints from Sir John of past injuries and disappointments, she regarded him with respect and pity, perhaps feeling even more sympathy because he was overlooked by Willoughby and Marianne, who, biased against his lack of liveliness and youth, seemed determined to undervalue his worth.

One day, while discussing him, Willoughby remarked, "Brandon is exactly the sort of man everyone praises yet ignores; whom all are pleased to see, yet no one bothers to speak to."

"That is precisely my view of him," Marianne agreed eagerly.

"Do not boast of it," Elinor chided. "It is unfair to both of you. He is highly esteemed by the entire household at the Park, and I never fail to make an effort to converse with him whenever I see him."

"He is patronized by *you*," Willoughby retorted, "which is certainly to his credit. But as for the esteem of the others, it is an insult in itself. Who would endure the indignity of being approved by women like Lady Middleton and Mrs. Jennings if they could command the indifference of anyone else?"

"But perhaps the disdain of men like yourselves makes up for the regard of Lady Middleton and her mother. If their praise is criticism, then your criticism might be praise, for they are no less discerning than you are prejudiced and unjust."

"In defense of your protégé, you can even be impertinent," Willoughby teased.

"My protégé, as you call him, is a sensible man," Marianne countered. "And sense always holds appeal for me. Yes, even in a man between thirty and forty. He has traveled widely, read extensively, and possesses a thoughtful mind. I have found him capable of providing vast information on many subjects, answering my questions with readiness, good breeding, and good nature."

"To put it plainly," Marianne sneered, "he has simply told you that the climate in the East Indies is hot and the mosquitoes are annoying."

"He *would* have told me so, I suspect, had I asked, but fortunately, I was already informed on those points."

"Perhaps," Willoughby suggested, "his observations extended to the existence of nabobs, gold mohurs, and palanquins."

"I dare say his observations went far beyond your candour. But why do you dislike him?"

"I do not dislike him," Willoughby insisted. "On the contrary, I consider him a very respectable man, universally praised yet ignored; a man with more wealth than he spends and more leisure than he knows how to use, sporting two new coats annually."

"And furthermore," Marianne added scornfully, "he lacks genius, taste, and spirit. His understanding shines with no brilliance, his feelings possess no ardor, and his voice lacks expression."

"You judge his flaws so broadly," Elinor observed calmly, "relying so heavily on your own imagination that my own praise seems cold and uninspired by comparison. I can only state that he is a sensible man, well-bred, well-informed, of gentle address, and, I believe, possessing an amiable heart."

"Miss Dashwood," Willoughby cried out, "you are treating me unkindly now. You attempt to disarm me with logic and convince me against my will. But it will not work. You shall find me as stubborn as you are cunning.
