On the second Sunday following Harry's arrival at Castlewood, the family made their way to the chapel, a charming village church attached to the castle. A discreet door from the house led directly into a spacious state pew, where they settled in for the service. Below them, a sizable congregation from the village filled the pews, their soft murmurs and rustling of pages creating a soothing background hum. The church's ancient banners, bearing the coats of arms of long-forgotten ancestors, hung from the rafters, and Harry found himself lost in thought, imagining the battles they must have witnessed during the tumultuous Commonwealth era. His family's loyalty and distinction in those times were well-documented, and he felt a sense of pride knowing that he was a part of this storied lineage.

As they knelt in prayer, Harry's gaze wandered to the effigy of Esmond, the patriarch of their family line, situated within the altar rails. The stern figure, dressed in the attire of King James the First's era, seemed to embody the gravity and dignity that defined their family's history. Madame de Bernstein, a pillar of the community in her capacity as Bishop's widow, conducted her devotions with a quiet solemnity, her movements a testament to the reverence she held for the traditions of the past.

The clergyman, a tall, affable young man with a rich, resonant voice, began the service, his words weaving a gentle tapestry of scripture and reflection. The music was beautiful, with one of the younger ladies of the family expertly playing the organ, her notes floating above the congregation like a misty dawn. However, their serenity was briefly disrupted by a burst of laughter from the servants' pew, where Gumbo, Mr. Warrington's devoted servant, had begun to sing the psalm with a voice that soared like a bird in flight. The congregation turned towards him in surprise, and even the parson, usually unflappable, covered his mouth with his handkerchief, while the liveried gentlemen from London looked on in consternation.

Pleasingly, Gumbo's impromptu performance continued, his voice growing stronger and more confident, until it threatened to overshadow the formal hymn. It transpired that Gumbo, though unable to read the words, had committed the psalm to memory, and his rendition was both heartfelt and authentic.
