With victory secured, the woman of Bulika turned her gaze toward the city itself. She spoke passionately of its vulnerability, denouncing the wickedness of its princess and the cowardice of its people. Within days, the chatter of the children filled the air with nothing but talk of Bulika, though they possessed no true concept of what a city entailed. It was then that I discerned the woman's design, even if the deeper motive remained hidden.

The prospect of seizing the place appealed strongly to Lona—and to me as well. The children were developing faculties with startling rapidity; I saw no insurmountable obstacle to our enterprise. I knew the terrible Lilith, whether woman or leopardess, possessed one fatal flaw: her doom lay through her daughter. Given the weight of the ancient prophecy upon the citizens' minds, any risk undertaken in this cause seemed justified. If successful—who could doubt our success?—the Little Ones, rising from a mere crowd of children, would swiftly become a youthful people. Their governance and influence would be rooted entirely in righteousness. Could they not rule the wicked with a rod of iron, thereby redeeming the nation?

Yet, I must confess, I harbored ambitions of personal gain. It seemed fitting that Lona should reclaim the throne of her mother, and natural that I serve as her consort and minister. I envisioned spending my life in her service; between us, with the Little Ones as our foundation, what might we not achieve in building a noble state? I even entertained a foolish dream of establishing a gem trade between the two worlds—a venture doomed to fail and harmful to both realms. Recalling Adam's plea, I suggested finding water to hasten the children's growth. Lona deemed it prudent to postpone this, fearing unknown consequences, noting that eventually, they would likely face a new necessity regardless. "They are what they are without it!" she declared. "Once we hold the city, we will seek water!"

Thus, we pressed forward with preparations, constantly reviewing our merry troops. Lona focused on logistics, while I drilled the young soldiers, teaching them to throw stones and wield various weapons. Our greatest challenge was ensuring they rallied instantly to the flag upon the signal. Most carried slings; the older boys wielded bows and arrows. The larger girls bore aloe-spikes, hardened as steel and sharp as needles, mounted on sturdy shafts—formidable tools. Their sole duty was to protect the smallest fighters.

Lona had grown significantly, yet she seemed unaware, remaining perpetually the tallest. Her hair had lengthened, and she had matured nearly into a woman, yet none of childhood's beauty had faded. When we reunited after our long separation, she laid down her infant, embraced me silently with a face glowing with joy, while the child whimpered. She snatched the toddler into her boson instantly. To witness her with any thoughtless, obstinate, or irritable child was to behold a tender grandmother. I felt as though I had known her forever—from before time began. I barely remembered my own mother, but in my mind's eye, she resembled Lona; whenever I imagined a sister or child, her face was Lona's. Every thought drifted to her; she was my heart's wife. Though she rarely sought me, she was always near, within earshot. Whatever I did or thought, I referred to her, rejoicing that while working independently, she thrived by my side. She never neglected a child, and my love sharpened my sense of duty. To love her and fulfill my duty felt inseparable. She might suggest tasks or ask counsel, yet she never assumed I cared for anything other than what *had* to be done. Her love flowed onto me—not in caresses, but in a profound recognition akin to the devotion of a divine beast.

I never told her of her mother. The forest teemed with birds, their plumage so splendid it compensated for the absence of flowers, which could not grow without water. Seeing their glorious feathers scattered throughout the woods, I conceived a garment for Lona. Gathering them, I bound them in overlapping rows. As I worked, she watched with evident appreciation, saying nothing, merely waiting expectantly. Within a week or two, the creation was complete: a long, loose mantle to fasten at the throat and waist, with armholes. I rose and draped it over her. She stood, removed it, and placed it at my feet, seemingly out of propriety. I placed it back on her shoulders, guiding her arms through the sleeves. She smiled, touched the feathers briefly, then removed it again, setting it aside. When she departed, she carried it, and I saw it no more for several days.

Finally, one morning, she approached me wearing the feather mantle, accompanied by another garment she had crafted from the dried leaves of a tough evergreen. These leaves possessed the strength of leather and the appearance of scale armor. I donned mine immediately, and henceforth, we wore these outfits whenever riding. For, on the forest's edge, a troop of full-grown horses had appeared. Unalarmed by our strange shapes, I quickly befriended them, training two of the finest for Lona and myself. Accustomed to small mounts, her delight was immense when she first looked down from the back of a giant steed, which prided itself on bearing her. We exercised them daily until obedience became instinctual and fear vanished. From then on, we rode them during parades and marches.

The undertaking sometimes seemed foolhardy, but the woman of Bulika's confidence—real or feigned—overwhelmed my hesitation. She insisted the princess's magic would be powerless against the children, and that our animal allies ensured military superiority. She claimed she was ready to face any two men of Bulika with a simple stick, though she admitted fear of the leopardess. I, however, felt prepared for her. Yet, I recoiled from bringing *all* the children. "Would it not be better," I asked, "if you stayed in the forest with your baby and the youngest?"

She replied that she relied heavily on the impact of seeing them on the women, especially the mothers. "When they see the darlings," she said, "their hearts will be seized. I must be there to encourage them to stand firm! If there is any courage left in that place, it lies among the women!"

"You must not encumber yourself," I urged Lona, "with any children; you will be needed everywhere!" There were two additional babies besides hers, and even on horseback, she often cradled one.

"I do not recall ever being without a child to care for," she answered. "But when we reach the city, it shall be as you wish!" Her confidence in someone who had failed so unworthily shamed me. Neither had I initiated the movement, nor grounds to oppose it. With no choice but to support her, I committed fully to her purpose. I was ready to live or die alongside Lona. Her humility and trust humbled me, driving me to dedicate myself heartily to her aims.

Our path crossed a grassy plain requiring no feed for the horses or the two cows accompanying us for the infants, but the elephants required provision. Although the grass suited them, it was short, and with their one-fingered trunks, they could not gather enough for a single meal. Consequently, we tasked the entire colony with gathering grass and making hay, allowing the elephants to carry quantities lasting several days, supplemented by fresh harvests at every halt. For the bears, we stored nuts; for ourselves, we dried abundant fruit. Having captured and tamed several more large horses, we loaded the beasts and elephants with supplies, preparing to depart.

Then, Lona and I conducted a general review, and I addressed the troops. I began by explaining that I had learned much about them and knew their origins. "We did not come from anywhere," they interrupted eagerly. "We are here!"

I told them that each had a mother, similar to the mother of the last baby, and that they had all been brought from Bulika when too young to remember. I explained that the wicked princess there feared babies so intensely she sought to destroy them, forcing their mothers to flee and hide them. Now, we marched to Bulika to find their mothers and liberate them from the evil giantess.

"But I must warn you," I continued, "there is danger ahead. We may have to fight hard to seize the city."

"We can fight! We are ready!" cried the boys.

"Yes, you can," I affirmed. "And I know you will: mothers are worth fighting for! Just remember, you must stay together."

"Yes, yes; we'll look out for each other," they responded. "No one shall touch one of us but his own mother!"

"You must heed every command given by your officers immediately!"

"We will, we will! Now we're ready! Let us go!"

"One final thing," I added. "When you strike, ensure a full, swinging blow. When shooting an arrow, pull it to the head. When slinging a stone, hurl it strong and straight."

"That we will!" they shouted with jubilant, fearless cries.

"Perhaps you will be injured!"

"We don't mind that! Do we, boys?"

"Not a bit!"

"Some of you may very possibly be killed!" I warned solemnly.

"I don't mind being killed!" cried one of the finest younger boys, riding a beautiful miniature bull that galloped and leaped like a horse.

"I don't either! I don't either!" echoed voices from all sides.
