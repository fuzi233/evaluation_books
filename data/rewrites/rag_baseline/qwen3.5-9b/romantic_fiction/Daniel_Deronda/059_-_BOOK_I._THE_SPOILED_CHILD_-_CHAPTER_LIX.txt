"I count myself in nothing else so happy as in a soul remembering my good friends." —Shakespeare.

Sir Hugo Mallinger did not depart for Genoa with the alacrity Mr. Gascoigne had displayed, nor would Daniel Deronda leave until he had secured a final audience with the baronet. The shadow of Grandcourt's drowning loomed large, compounded by the recent, shattering revelation of Deronda's own origins; these twin tragedies demanded a face-to-face communion that letters, clumsy and distant, could never convey. It was not until the fifth evening that Deronda stood at the station platform, waiting for Sir Hugo's arrival between eight and nine. While he gazed toward the tracks, anticipating the familiar, kind visage that formed part of his earliest memories, a faint, tentative smile curved his lips. It was a smile born of gratitude for Sir Hugo's newfound mastery over his estates, his ability to provide for his daughters, or perhaps, according to a newly crystallized vision of inheritance, to adopt makeshift feminine offspring as intermediaries to secure a male heir. We are churlish indeed if we cannot share in our fellows' joys, provided they do not clash violently with our rigid theories of righteousness; otherwise, our mouths twist sour, our eyes freeze, while our own possessive desires remain stubbornly misaligned with our ideals. Yet, we must allow ourselves companionship with imperfection. It is possible, and fortunate, to harbor gratitude even when we perceive a mistake that may have caused harm, recognizing that the vehicle of that mistake was an affectionate intention pursued through a lifetime of kindly service.

Deronda's heart and judgment recoiled from Sir Hugo's role as the architect of a falsehood—a deception under which he had been reared—but the baronet likely lacked clear knowledge of the mother's breach of trust. With his characteristic lightness of touch regarding life's complexities, Sir Hugo had deemed it a reasonable preference that her son become an English gentleman, especially given the mother's eccentric refusal to sever ties with her child, effectively choosing to be absent in spirit while present in name. Daniel's affectionate gratitude drove him to seek grounds for excuse rather than condemnation; for it is as easy to be rigid in principle yet tender in blame as it is to suffer from the sight of things askew while remaining patient with the hanger-on who sees clearly. Had Sir Hugo, in his bachelorhood, been beguiled into viewing children primarily as products designed to make life agreeable for the fully grown, whose convenience dictated their disposal, he had merely shared an assumption massively enacted during that era of history. Deronda, armed with the keen memory of his own painful internal struggle, recalled how utterly excluded Sir Hugo's conception had been from such experiences. Ignorant kindness can inflict cruelty, but to rage against it as if it were direct malice would be an ignorance of its own, a *un_kindness* furthest removed from Deronda's expansive imaginative leniency. Perhaps, after the searching, harrowing scenes of the last ten days, where the curtain had been forcibly lifted on secrets unlike his own, he was more disposed than ever to curb that rashness of indignation or resentment which bears an unpleasant resemblance to the love of punishment.

When Sir Hugo's familiar figure descended from the railway carriage, the lifelong affection that had always been adept at finding excuses surged forth, submerging all newer knowledge that might have served as fresh soil for blame. "Well, Dan," said Sir Hugo, seizing Deronda's hand with a serious fervor. He uttered no further greeting; the current of mutual consciousness ran too strong for words. Next came instructions for the courier, followed by a proposal to walk slowly in the mild evening, for there was no urgency to reach the hotel. "I have taken my journey easily and am in excellent condition," Sir Hugo remarked as they emerged under the starlight, still faintly tinged with the lingering sheen of day. "I did not hasten my departure, wishing to inquire into matters a little; thus, I caught sight of your letter to Lady Mallinger before I started. But now, how fares the widow?"

"Growing calmer," Deronda replied. "She appears to be escaping the physical illness one might have feared following her plunge and terrible excitement. Her uncle and mother arrived two days ago, and she is being well cared for."

"Is there any prospect of an heir being born?"

"From what Mr. Gascoigne told me, I conclude not," Deronda said. "He spoke as if the question were merely whether the widow would retain the estates for her life."

"It will not be much of a wrench to her affections, I fancy, this loss of the husband?" Sir Hugo asked, casting a look around at Deronda.

"The suddenness of the death has been a great blow to her," Deronda answered quietly, deftly sidestepping the deeper implication.

"I wonder whether Grandcourt gave her any notion of the provisions of his will?"

"Do you know what they are, sir?" Deronda parried.

"Yes, I do," the baronet replied promptly. "Good heavens! If there is no prospect of a legitimate heir, he has left everything to a boy he had by a Mrs. Glasher—you know nothing of this affair, I presume? She was, in a manner, his wife for many years, and there are three older children, girls. The boy is to take his father's name; he is already named Henleigh, and henceforth he shall be Henleigh Mallinger Grandcourt. The Mallinger fortune will be of no use to him—I am happy to say—but the young dog will have more than enough to sustain him through his fourteen years of minority. No need to have plugged the holes with my fifty thousand for Diplow that he had no right to claim. Meanwhile, my beauty, the young widow, is to endure a paltry two thousand a year and the house at Gadsmere—a nice sort of banishment for her if she chooses to lock herself away there, which I doubt she will. The boy's mother has lived there for recent years. I am perfectly disgusted with Grandcourt. Though, so far as my affairs are concerned, nothing in his life suited him better than leaving it behind."

"In my opinion, he did wrong when he married this wife—not necessarily in leaving his estates to the son," Deronda observed, his tone somewhat dry.

"I say nothing against his leaving the land to the lad," Sir Hugo countered. "But since he had married this girl, he ought to have granted her a handsome provision, sufficient for her to live in a style commensurate with the rank he had raised her to. She deserved four or five thousand a year and the London house for her life; that is what I would have done. I suppose, as she was penniless, her friends could not press for a settlement beforehand; otherwise, it is ill-trusting a man's will made after marriage. Even a wise man generally allows some folly to ooze out of him in his testament—my father did, I know. And if a fellow harbors spite or tyranny, he is likely to bottle up a good deal within such a document. It is quite clear Grandcourt intended his death to serve as an extinguisher for his wife, should she fail to bear him an heir."

"And in the other case, I suppose everything would have been reversed—illegitimacy receiving the extinguisher?" Deronda asked, with a note of scorn.

"Precisely—Gadsmere and the two thousand. It is queer. One annoyance is that Grandcourt has made me an executor; but seeing he was the son of my only brother, I cannot refuse to act. And I shall mind it less if I can be of any use to the widow. Lush suspects she was not in total ignorance regarding the family arrangements under the rose, and the purport of the will. He hints that there was no very good understanding between the couple. But I fancy you are the man who knew most about what Mrs. Grandcourt felt or did not feel—eh, Dan?"

Sir Hugo did not pose this question with his usual jocularity, but rather with a lowered tone of earnest inquiry. Deronda sensed that any evasion would be misconstrued. He answered gravely, "She was certainly not happy. They were unsuited to each other. But as to the disposal of the property—from all I have observed of her, I should predict that she will be quite contented with it."

"Then she is not much like the rest of her sex; that is all I can say," Sir Hugo remarked, giving a slight shrug. "However, she ought to be something extraordinary, for there must be an entanglement between your horoscope and hers—eh? When that tremendous telegram arrived, the first thing Lady Mallinger said was, 'How very strange that it should be Daniel who sends it!' But I have experienced something of the same sort in my own life. Once, at a foreign hotel, I encountered a lady abandoned by her husband without means. Upon hearing of her plight and stepping forward to assist, who should appear but an early flame of mine, foolish enough to marry an Austrian baron with a long mustache and short affection? But it was an affair of my own that brought me there—nothing akin to knight-errantry, any more than your coming to Genoa had to do with the Grandcourts."

A silence fell for a moment. Sir Hugo had begun discussing the Grandcourts as the less perilous topic between them, yet both wished to overcome a reluctance toward absolute frankness regarding the events touching their relationship. Deronda felt that his letter, following the first interview with his mother, had acted more as a thickening of the ice than a breaker, and that he ought to await the first opening from Sir Hugo.
