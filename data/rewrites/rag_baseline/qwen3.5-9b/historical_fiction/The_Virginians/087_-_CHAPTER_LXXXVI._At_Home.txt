The letter from our mother possessed a gravity that would have melted iron, yet it hardened our resolve instead. We discussed the immediate journey to Virginia, the logistics of securing the entire cabin of the *Young Rachel*, and the mechanics of departure. But the calculus of travel demanded a variable we could not remove: our child. Illness was a certainty for her on the high seas, and the mother could not sever the tether. Thus, the *Young Rachel* sailed that year without us.

Another year brought another obstruction. My worship suffered his first assault of the gout—a condition that occupied me significantly, though it subsequently clarified my wits and enlivened my spirits. Then came a sorrow deeper than either: news from Jamaica. Two years after establishing ourselves at the Manor, our General returned from his government. He carried a modest increase in worldly goods, yet he bore a loss for which no accumulation of wealth could provide solace. Afterward, he ceased to wish to remain in the West Indies. My Theo's mother—the most tender and affectionate friend I have ever known, barring one exception—had died abroad of the fever. Her final regret was that she would not live to witness our children and ourselves in prosperity.

"She sees us, though we do not see her," her husband said, his voice flat. "And she thanks you, George, for having been good to her children."

We believed he would not long join her. His love for her had constituted the entirety of his happiness and business; to exist away from her was to cease living. Watching the good man sit with us was pitiable. My wife, through her manner and countless gestures, constantly resurrected the memory of the bereaved widower's mother in his heart. Whatever cheer we could muster, we offered. Yet, above all, little Hetty became his primary support and consolation. She had refused more than one advantageous match in the island, according to the General, and upon returning to England, the heir of Lord Wrotham threw himself at her feet. But Hetty preferred to remain with her father. "As long as he is not tired of me," she stated, "I care for no husband."

"Nay," we replied when the proposal of this final great match arose. "Let the General stay six months with us at the Manor, and you may have him at Oakhurst for the remaining six."

Hetty declared her father could never endure Oakhurst again now that her mother was gone. She would marry no man for his coronet or his money—not she! The General, discussing the matter gravely, suggested the child's lack of desire for marriage stemmed from some early-life disappointment she never articulated. Respecting her feelings, we fell equally silent. By this time, my brother Lambert held a college living near Winchester and a wife to adorn his parsonage. We professed a moderate liking for this lady, welcoming her when she visited, though her opinion of Hetty's determined celibacy differed sharply from mine. Mrs. Jack was a chatterbox, accustomed to speaking her mind freely and taking pride in her ability to inflict pain upon her friends.

"My dear Sir George," she announced, "I have often told our dear Theo that I would not have a pretty sister in my house to make tea for Jack when I am upstairs, always available when summoned to the kitchen or nursery, and perpetually dressed neatly in her best while I bake pies or tend to the children. I have every confidence in Jack, of course. I should like to see him look at another woman, indeed! And so I feel about Jemima, but they do not converge in my house when I am upstairs—that I promise you! And so I informed my sister Warrington."

"Am I to understand," the General asked, "that you have done my Lady Warrington the favor of warning her against her sister, my daughter Miss Hester?"

"Yes, pa, of course I have. A duty is a duty, and a woman is a woman, and a man is a man, as I know very well. Do not tell me! He is a man. Every man is a man, with all his sanctified airs!"

"You yourself possess a married sister, with whom you were staying when my son Jack first had the happiness of making your acquaintance?" the General remarked.

"Yes, of course I have a married sister; everyone knows that, and I have been as good as a mother to her children, that I have!"

"And am I to gather from your conversation that your attractions proved a powerful temptation for your sister's husband?"

"Law, General! I don't know how you can go for to say I ever said any such a thing!" cried Mrs. Jack, turning red and voluble.

"Do you not perceive, my dear madam, that it is you who have insinuated as much, not only regarding yourself, but regarding my own two daughters?"

"Never, never, never, as I'm a Christian woman! And it's most cruel of you to say so, sir. And I do say a sister is best out of the house, that I do! And as Theo's time is coming, I warn her, that's all."

"Have you discovered, my good madam, whether my poor Hetty has stolen any of the spoons? When I came to breakfast this morning, my daughter was alone, and there must have been a score of pieces of silver on the table."

"Law, sir! Who ever said a word about spoons? Did I ever accuse the poor dear? If I did, may I drop down dead at this moment on this hearth-rug! And I ain't used to be spoke to in this way. And me and Jack have both remarked it; and I've done my duty, that I have." With this, Mrs. Jack flounced from the room, in tears.

"And has the woman had the impudence to tell you this, my child?" the General asked when Theo, who was somewhat delicate, approached the tea-table.

"She has told me every day since she has been here. She enters my dressing-room to inform me. She visits my nursery and declares, 'Ah, I wouldn't have a sister prowling about my nursery, that I wouldn't.' Ah, how pleasant it is to have amiable and well-bred relatives, say I."

"Thy poor mother has been spared this woman," the General groaned.

"Our mother would have made her better, papa," Theo said, kissing him.

"Yes, dear." I observed that both of them were already at prayer.

Yet it must be acknowledged that loving one's relatives is not always an easy task; living with one's neighbors is sometimes not amusing. From Jack Lambert's demeanor the following day, I saw that his wife had delivered her version of the conversation. Jack was sulky, though not dignified. He was angry, yet his anger did not suppress his appetite. He preached a sermon to us that was entirely stupid. Little Miles, clad once more in sable, sat at his grandfather's side, his small hand placed in that of the kind old man.

Would he stay to keep house during our Virginian trip? The housekeeper should be placed under the full domination of Hetty. The butler's keys should be handed over to him; for Gumbo, I thought with excessive grace, was to accompany us to Virginia: having, it must be noted, united himself with Mrs. Molly in the bonds of matrimony and populated a cottage in my park with sundry tawny Gumbos. Under the care of our good General and his daughter, we left our house. We traveled to London, and thence to Bristol, where our obsequious agent declared he would offer up prayers for our prosperity and vowed that children so beautiful as ours—we had an infant by this time to accompany Miles—were never seen on any ship before. We made a voyage without accident.

How strange the feeling was as we landed from our boat at Richmond! A coach and a host of negroes waited to receive us; nearby, a gentleman on horseback, accompanied by negroes in our livery, dismounted and rushed forward to embrace us. Both of us were greatly charmed to see our dearest Hal. He rode with us to our mother's door. There she stood on the steps to welcome us; Theo knelt to ask her blessing.

Harry rode in the coach with us as far as our mother's house but declined to enter further, claiming he would spoil the sport. "She sees me," he admitted, "and we are pretty good friends; but Fanny and she are best apart, and there is no love lost between them, I can promise you. Come over to me at the Tavern, George, when thou art free. And tomorrow I shall have the honor to present her sister to Theo. 'Twas only from happening to be in town yesterday that I heard the ship was signaled, and I waited to see you. I have sent a negro boy home to my wife, and she will be here to pay her respects to my Lady Warrington." After this brief greeting, Harry jumped from the carriage, leaving us to meet our mother alone.

Since parting from her, I had seen much fine company, and Theo and I had paid our respects to the King and Queen at St. James's; yet we had seen no stater person than this individual who welcomed us. Raising my wife from her knee, he embraced her and led her into the house. It was a plain, wood-built place with a gallery around it, typical of our Virginian houses; but even if it had been a palace housing a little empress, our reception could not have been more courteous.
