“Bear up, brave heart! we will be calm and strong;
Sure, we can master eyes, or cheek, or tongue,
Nor let the smallest tell-tale sign appear
She ever was, and is, and will be dear.”

The July evening hung heavy and golden over the London house, the air thick with the scent of roses from the square garden below, no breeze stirring to lift the edge of a curtain or cool the flush of heat on unprotected skin. Edith burst into Margaret’s bedchamber first, breeches and riding habit still dusted with lane mud from her afternoon ride, and half an hour later returned, fully laced into her dinner gown, ribbons fluttering at her wrists. The room stood empty at her first arrival; on her second, she found Dixon laying Margaret’s evening gown across the counterpane, smoothing out the folds of soft straw-coloured silk, but no sign of Margaret herself. Edith fretted about the room, picking at trinkets on the dressing table until her restlessness boiled over.

“Oh, Dixon—do not pair that dreary dead gold gown with those horrid pale blue flowers! What a terrible taste. Wait one moment, I shall fetch my pomegranate blossoms, the bright scarlet ones that look like embers.”

“It is not dead gold, ma’am,” Dixon huffed, her fingers going still on the gown fabric. “It is proper straw-colour, and blue has gone with straw-colour since good taste was invented.” But Edith was already out the door, and returned with her armful of brilliant scarlet blooms before Dixon could finish her remonstrance. She arranged the stems at Margaret’s breast and in the pins of her dark hair, stepping back to admire the effect before she spoke again.

“Where on earth is Miss Hale?” she asked, petulant. “I do declare, Aunt allowed her to pick up the most dreadful rambling habits while we were all in Milton! I live in constant fear of hearing she has stumbled into some horrid trouble wandering those wretched, narrow lanes down there. I would never dare set foot in half those streets without a footman at my elbow. They are no place for a lady.”

Dixon was still prickling at her judgement of her taste, so she replied shortly, her tone sharp as a pin: “It is no wonder to me, when I hear fine delicate dainty ladies clucking on about what is and is not fit for their kind, that there are no saints left walking the earth anymore—”

“Margaret! There you are at last, I have been looking everywhere for you. Poor child, your cheeks are so flushed with the heat, you look like you have run all the way home. But you will never guess what tiresome Henry has gone and done now—he quite exceeds the limits of what a brother-in-law ought to be allowed to do. I had my dinner party arranged perfectly, every number fitting exactly to suit Mr Colthurst, and then Henry turns up, apologising all over the place and using your name as an excuse, to ask if he might bring that Mr Thornton from Milton—your tenant, you know, the mill owner—who is in London on some law business. It will quite spoil my carefully counted party.”

“I do not mind joining dinner at all,” Margaret said, her voice soft and low, avoiding Edith’s eyes. “I would much rather Dixon bring me a cup of tea up here, and lie down for a little while. I shall join you all in the drawing-room as soon as you finish dinner, I am quite worn out with the heat.”

“No, no, that will never do, we cannot manage without you. You do look terribly pale, I grant, but that is only the heat, and you know we planned for you to talk to Mr Colthurst about Milton life, to give him all those little details he wants for his new speech. Oh! What a happy accident! This Mr Thornton is from Milton, himself! It will be capital! Mr Colthurst can pump him full of every fact he needs, and we can trace all your old experiences and Mr Thornton’s good sense straight into his next speech in the House. I was cross at first, but this is a perfect stroke. I asked Henry if he was the sort of man one would be ashamed to introduce, and he said ‘You will not be, if you have an ounce of good sense in you, little sister.’ So I suppose he can sound his aitches properly, which is not half as common in Darkshire as you might hope, eh Margaret?”

“Did Mr Lennox say why Mr Thornton has come to town?” Margaret asked, her voice constrained, her fingers twisting in the edge of the counterpane. “Is the law business connected to the mill property?”

“Oh, he has failed in business, or something of that sort, Henry told me the day you had that terrible headache—don’t you remember?” Edith paused, stepping back to adjust the pomegranate blossoms a little lower in Margaret’s dark hair. “There! You look splendid, Dixon, does she not do us credit? I wish I was as tall as a queen and as brown as a gipsy, Margaret. But about Mr Thornton—oh, I have a dreadful head for all this law business. Henry will be only too happy to tell you every detail of it. All I gathered is that he is in very reduced circumstances now, but a very respectable man, and I am to be extra civil to him. I had no notion how to go about that, so I came to find you to help me. Come on now, lean on my arm, we will go and rest on the sofa for a quarter of an hour before the guests arrive.”

The privileged brother-in-law Henry Lennox arrived early, and Margaret, colouring high on her cheeks as she spoke, leaned over to ask him all the questions she had been burning to answer about Mr Thornton.

“He has come up to arrange the sub-let of Marlborough Mills, the house and all the surrounding premises,” Henry told her, keeping his voice low for her ear alone. “He cannot afford to keep the mill running any longer, you see, and there are stacks of deeds and leases to review, new agreements to draw up. I do hope Edith receives him properly. I could tell she was quite put out at first that I took the liberty of begging an invitation for him. But I thought you would wish him to be shown every proper attention. And after all, one owes a special measure of respect to a man who is going down in the world, and bears it as well as he does.”

He stood up abruptly as the front door sounded, and crossed the room to introduce Mr Thornton, who had just stepped inside, to Edith and Captain Lennox. Margaret watched him with sharp, anxious eyes as he made his greetings. More than a full year had passed since she had seen him last, and all the bitter events of that time had carved new lines into his face. His broad, tall frame still stood head and shoulders above most men, carrying with it that natural, easy dignity that had always marked him out; but his face was lined, careworn, with a quiet noble composure settled over all his features that struck every person in the room who had just heard of his fallen fortunes, conveying a sense of inherent manly strength no loss could shake.

He had spotted Margaret the very first second he glanced around the room. He had seen her intent, earnest face as she listened to Henry Lennox, and he walked over to her with the perfectly steady, unforced manner of an old, trusted friend. The moment he spoke his first calm greeting, a vivid rose flushed up into Margaret’s cheeks, and never left them for the rest of the evening. She seemed to have hardly anything to say to him, answering only the most necessary polite questions about their old mutual acquaintances in Milton, and he was quietly disappointed by her reserve. But more guests soon arrived, people far more intimate with the family than he was, and he slipped gently into the background, talking now and then with Henry Lennox at the edge of the group.

“You will agree Miss Hale is looking very well, don’t you?” Henry said to him. “Milton never seemed to agree with her, you know. When she first came back to London, I thought I had never seen anyone so changed, so thin and worn. Tonight she looks absolutely radiant. And she is so much stronger now. Last autumn she would be worn out after a walk of two miles, but last Friday evening we walked all the way up to Hampstead and back, and she looked as well as she does now the very next day.”

We. They two, alone? The quiet question hung unspoken in Thornton’s chest, a faint, dull sting he had no time to dwell on before they were called in to dinner.

Mr Colthurst was a sharp, clever rising Member of Parliament, and he picked up immediately on the depth and good sense of a remark Thornton made over the dinner table. He leaned over to ask Edith who the gentleman was, and to her great surprise, his “Indeed!” carried a tone of genuine recognition—Mr Thornton of Milton was far from the unknown name she had assumed he was. The dinner party ran perfectly smooth. Henry was in excellent humour, his dry caustic wit drawing out peals of quiet laughter from around the table. Mr Thornton and Mr Colthurst found a dozen shared interests, touching lightly on them over dinner and agreeing to discuss them in full after the ladies had retired. Margaret, the scarlet pomegranate blossoms glowing bright in her dark hair, looked more beautiful than any of them had ever seen her. She leaned back in her chair, spoke very little, and Edith was not the least annoyed, for the conversation flowed on perfectly smoothly without needing her contributions. All the while, Margaret watched Thornton’s face. He never once looked directly at her, so she could study him unobserved, noting every small change the last hard year had wrought. Only when Henry Lennox made a sharp, unexpected witticism did his face break out into the old bright look of unguarded enjoyment: the merry light flashed back into his eyes, his lips parted to show that brilliant, full smile she remembered so well, and for a split second his glance flew instinctively to hers, as if he wanted to share that spark of amusement with her alone. The moment their eyes met, his whole face hardened back into gravity, and he resolutely avoided looking anywhere near her for the rest of the meal.

There were only two other ladies besides their party, and when they returned to the drawing-room, the aunt and Edith kept them occupied in their own corner, so Margaret sat languidly over a piece of needlework, half listening to the conversation around her. Presently the gentlemen joined them, Mr Colthurst and Mr Thornton walking side by side in deep, close discussion. Henry Lennox wandered over to Margaret, leaning down to speak low in her ear.

“Edith owes me a world of thanks for adding your Mr Thornton to the party,” he said. “You have no idea what an agreeable, sensible fellow he is. He has given Colthurst every single fact he was searching for to flesh out his new speech. I cannot for the life of me imagine how a man with his gifts and sense could have mismanaged his affairs so badly.”

“With your advantages and his power, you would have succeeded far better,” Margaret said quietly. Henry did not quite relish the cool, detached tone of her reply, even though the words echoed a thought he had already thought himself. He fell silent, and they caught the drift of the conversation by the fireplace, where Colthurst and Thornton were talking.

“I assure you, his name was mentioned everywhere in your part of the country when I visited last year, everyone was waiting with great interest, even curiosity, to see how your experiment would turn out,” Colthurst was saying. Some words slipped away from them, and the next thing they heard was Thornton’s low, steady voice.

“I never had the qualities to win popularity. If people were talking of me that way, they were mistaken. I am slow to warm to new projects, slow to let people know me, even the people I wish to be open and frank with. But even with all those flaws, I knew I was on the right path. I started with one single friendship, and I began to know dozens of my workers. The good ran both ways: we were all teaching each other, consciously and not.”

“You say ‘were.’ I trust you mean to pick that work back up again?”

Henry Lennox started, and jumped in with a sudden well-timed question, turning the flow of the conversation away before Thornton had to admit he had lost his mill, his position as a master, all the power to carry on that work. But the moment the new topic they had started came to a natural close, Thornton picked up the old thread right where he left off, and answered Colthurst’s unfinished question directly, no evasion in his voice.

“My business failed. I have had to give up my position as a master manufacturer. I am now looking for employment in Milton, working under another owner who will let me carry on with the work I care about, in my own way. I no longer have any rash new theories I would rush to put into practice. My only wish is for the chance to build real, personal connection with the workers that goes far beyond the mere cash nexus of wages paid for work done. To hear some of the other manufacturers talk, you would think that sort of arrangement was the lever Archimedes asked for to move the whole world: they shake their heads and look grave the moment I mention the small experiments I want to try.”

“I notice you call them experiments,” Colthurst said, his tone softening with a deep new respect.

“Because that is what they are. I do not know for certain what the end result will be. But I am certain they ought to be tried. I have come to the conclusion that no set of rules, no matter how clever, no matter how much thought went into writing them, can ever bind the different classes to each other the way they ought to be bound, unless the people behind those rules meet each other face to face, as individuals. That personal intercourse is the very breath of life. A worker can never understand how many hours his employer stayed up late, poring over plans to make his life easier, if he only ever sees him once a quarter to pay his wages. A perfect finished plan looks like a piece of fine machinery, built to survive every emergency, but the workers will accept it just the way they accept machinery: they take it for granted, and never see the weeks of thought and labour that went into making it run. But if you build a plan that needs people to work together, side by side, to make it work—then every hitch will make more and more people care about its success, until in the end everyone wants it to do well, because every single one of them had a hand in building it. And even then, it will stop being alive, stop having any purpose, the moment that shared interest fades away, the moment they stop seeing each other, learning each other’s characters, their little tempers, their ways of speaking. The more we know each other, the better we will understand each other, and I will stake my reputation that we will like each other far more.”

“And you think that will put an end to strikes for good?”

“Not at all. I do not hope for so much. My utmost expectation is only this: that it will make strikes no longer the bitter, venomous things that leave decades of hatred behind them. A very hopeful man might imagine that kind of close, warm connection between classes could do away with strikes entirely. But I am not a hopeful man.”

Suddenly, as if a new thought had struck him, he crossed the room directly over to Margaret where she sat over her needlework, and spoke without any polite preamble, as if he knew full well she had been listening to every word he said.

“Miss Hale, I had a round-robin note sent to me, signed by dozens of my old workers. I suspect John Higgins wrote the whole thing out by hand. They all said they would come back and work for me any day, if ever I was in a position to hire men on my own behalf again. That was good, was it not?”

“Yes. That was just right. I am so glad for it,” Margaret said, lifting her face to look straight at him, her eyes bright and speaking, before she dropped them again, unable to bear the weight of his steady, earnest gaze. He stared back at her for a long quiet minute, as if he had forgotten exactly where he was for a moment. Then he sighed, soft and low, and said, “I knew you would be glad to hear it.” He turned away, and never spoke a single word to her again for the rest of the evening, until he bade her a formal, distant good night at the door.

As the rest of the guests took their leave, Margaret stopped Henry Lennox at the door, her cheeks burning bright with a blush she could not hide, her fingers twisting nervously at the edge of her shawl.

“May I speak to you tomorrow?” she said, hesitating. “I need your help about—something.”

“Of course,” he said instantly, his face lighting up with bright, unmistakeable joy. “Name whatever time you please. There is no greater pleasure you could give me than to let me be of use to you. Shall I come at eleven? Very well.” His eyes shone with exultant hope. She was learning to depend on him now, he thought. Surely any day now he would have the answer he had spent so many months waiting for, the answer that he had sworn he would never ask for until he was certain it would be yes.
