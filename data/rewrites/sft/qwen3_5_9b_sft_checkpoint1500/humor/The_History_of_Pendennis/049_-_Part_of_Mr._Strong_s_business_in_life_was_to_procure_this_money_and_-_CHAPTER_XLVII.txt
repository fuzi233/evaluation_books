Major Pendennis, having pushed himself to the absolute limit the previous night at Gaunt House, found his weary bones demanding immediate relief. With a groan that echoed the exhaustion of a long campaign, he dragged himself to Buxton, seeking solace in the region's famous healing waters. By the time Parliament adjourned and Sir Francis Clavering's family had departed town, the affairs of the moment had not progressed a single step. Yet, the city itself had undergone a dramatic transformation. The season had drawn to a close; the lawyers had vanished onto the circuits, and the fashionable set had packed their trunks for the Continent or fled to the Scottish Highlands for health and thrills. The bow-windows of the clubs stood empty, and Pall Mall was a ghostly street. The scarlet coats of the Guards had melted away from the Palace gates, and the tradesmen of St. James's wandered the countryside. Even the tailors, sporting sudden mustaches, had traveled up the Rhine, while the bootmakers were found at Ems or Baden, flushing crimson whenever they encountered their former clients amidst their debts and dice tables. The clergy of St. James's preached to a mere handful of souls, devoid of any sinners of consequence. The brass bands of Kensington Gardens had silenced their silver trumpets, leaving the Serpentine banked with only a few straggling carriages. Clarence Bulbul, trapped in town by his tedious Treasury duties, rode Rotten Row and lamented the silence, comparing the scene to the vast, dusty solitude of an Arabian desert where he was the lone Bedouin. Meanwhile, Warrington stuffed his carpet bag with Cavendish tobacco and retreated to his brother's estate in Norfolk.

Left behind, Arthur Pendennis found himself the sole occupant of his chambers. Though he styled himself a man *blasé* and worn out by life, the truth was a different matter entirely. Pen was a robust young fellow with a voracious appetite and an insatiable hunger for company. He was far from a misanthrope; a mediocre dinner satisfied him as readily as a feast, and the lack of wit or beauty in a gathering mattered little if conversation flowed freely. Whether in a tavern parlour, aboard a Greenwich steamboat, on a Hampstead excursion with his colleague Finucane, or at the summer theaters across the river, Pen found joy. At Vauxhall, he rubbed shoulders with the great Simpson, shaking hands with the principal comic singer of the arena with a satirical humor that never lost its thread of genuine sympathy. He watched the grimaces and graces of the performers with a keen eye, equally appreciative of the roystering youth, the delighted parents, and the poor outcasts who danced away their shame and cares until dawn. To Pen, anthropology—the study of human variety—was his true passion. From the coquetting dowagers to the innocent children feeding ducks, he observed humanity with an unfailing delight, finding deep, secret pleasure in watching the endless tapestry of human nature, a pursuit that required a heart both clean and lonely.

"You see," Pen would boast to Warrington, "I loved fiercely in my youth and burned that flame out forever. Any future marriage will be purely of reason, with a sensible, good-looking woman with a bit of money to cushion our carriage ride through life. Romance is exhausted; I'm old before my time!"

"Balderdash!" Warrington would grunt. "You thought you were going bald yesterday and bragged about it. But you started using bear grease the moment the hairdresser warned you, and you smell like a fresh shave."

"You're a modern-day Diogenes," Pen retorted, blushing despite himself, "demanding everyone live in a tub. Violets smell better than stale tobacco, you grizzled cynic." Yet, Pen cared deeply for his appearance, ornamenting himself with careful attention to ensure he remained agreeable to the world, wearing tight boots and bright varnish like a weary pilgrim on a pilgrimage to vanity.

It was on a shining Friday night in late autumn, following a brilliant editorial piece penned by Pen for the *Pall Mall Gazette*, that he decided to cross the water to Vauxhall. Having secured his admittance ticket and paid the necessary fare, he strolled across Waterloo Bridge under a canopy of stars, heading toward the Royal Gardens. Before entering the enchanted grounds, illuminated by twenty thousand additional lamps, visitors traversed a dark, dreary passage guarded by ticket collectors. Pen presented his order, only to find himself blocked by a gentleman in a short, shabby cloak and a lady accompanied by her daughter.

The gentleman, whose hat leaned precariously to one side, was Captain Costigan, a man of distinct Hibernian dignity who spoke with a heavy, rolling accent. He was loudly arguing with the check-taker, insisting that a "most unlucky contraction"—a lost ticket obtained from Mr. Hodgen, a renowned singer of "The Body Snatcher"—should allow entry for three instead of two. The check-taker refused, leading to a theatrical display of distress from the Captain regarding the fair ladies, particularly the younger one, who had never been to Vauxhall.

"My poor child!" Costigan cried, feigning outrage. "Sir, surely you cannot deny this innocent creature?"

"It isn't my business," the peevish doorkeeper grumbled.

Just then, Arthur Pendennis appeared. Recognizing the Captain, he bowed politely to the ladies and extended a warm hand to Costigan. "Don't you know me, Captain? Pendennis!"

"My dear boy! My dear friend!" Costigan exclaimed, rushing forward to embrace him. "Mr. Pendennis, you've been my preserver! I lost the order, and now these ladies are doomed to disappointment. Especially this young miss; she's never been inside."

Pen, possessing a heart full of good nature, immediately offered assistance. His first impulse was to pay for the entire party, but he quickly realized that doing so would make him appear like a grand *seigneur*. Instead, he handed his purse to Costigan and, with a charming smile, produced his own ticket, inviting the elder lady to follow.

"Fanny, take the gentleman's arm," the lady instructed. "Since you'll be so kind..."

Fanny curtsied, placing her hand, clad in a shabby little glove, under Arthur's arm. She was hardly a woman yet, but her cheeks flushed with youthful blushes and her eyes glistened with gratitude. Arthur, protective and kind, placed his other hand over hers. "Fanny's a very pretty little name," he said. "Do you know me?"

"We keep the lodge at Shepherd's Inn," Fanny replied, shrinking back slightly as she took in the dazzling spectacle of the gardens. "I've never been here, sir... Oh, law, how beautiful!" She stared at the hundreds of lamps, awestruck. Pen felt a surge of pleasure at seeing her delight, pressing her small hand to his side. "What would I not give for a little of this pleasure?" he murmured, feeling suddenly less *blasé*.

Behind them, Captain Costigan's voice boomed, "Pendennis, my dear boy, you've been my preserver! I've known him since childhood, Mrs. Bolton; he's the proprietor of Fairoaks Castle, where I've drunk copious quantities of claret with the nobility!"

While Pen displayed magnificence in his courtesy, Costigan performed a different sort of splendor, bowing and calling "Bravo" to the singers with the dignity of a king descending from a line of Hibernian monarchs. However, unlike Pen, Costigid felt compelled to maintain a distance from the ladies, viewing them as his housekeepers rather than equals. Pen, conversely, had no such scruples. He walked closely beside Miss Fanny Bolton, who, comparing him to the other gentlemen around her, declared him the handsomest and grandest.

They were soon joined by the eccentric party of Lord Viscount Colchicum and his companion, Mademoiselle Caracoline, a French rider who spoke with idiomatic force. Nearby, young Tom Tufthunt, a guard, acted as a loyal lapdog to the elderly viscount, carving poultry and making salads. When Pen and Fanny approached, the viscount merely leered, but Tufthunt greeted Pen with a knowing nod.

"How beautiful they are, sir!" Fanny cried later, as they paused near a concert stage. Her eyes turned toward a pale, gloomy figure standing nearby, Henry Foker Esq., who looked utterly miserable.

"I think he's crossed in love," Pen suggested, looking down at Fanny with the protective air of Egmont guarding Clara. "Isn't that enough to make any man dismal, Fanny?"

"Poor gentleman," Fanny sighed, her eyes filled with kindness. But Harry Foker, lost in his melancholy, failed to notice the beautiful dark eyes fixed upon him.

Suddenly, a voice interrupted the quiet. "How dy do, Mr. Pendennis!" It was Mr. Samuel Huxter, a young surgeon dressed in a flamboyant white coat, a dingy shirt collar, and trousers that seemed to scream "Look at me!" He held a lady in pink satin on his arm and produced a card-case with his teeth.

"Saw your name in the Morning Post," Huxter chirped, handing Pen a card. "Supper tomorrow night at Charterhouse Lane? Some devilish good fellows from Bartholomew's. Stunning gin-punch. Come along, Mary."
