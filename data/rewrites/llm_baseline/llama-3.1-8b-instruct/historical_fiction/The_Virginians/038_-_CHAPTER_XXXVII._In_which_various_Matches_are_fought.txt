As Mr. Warrington perused the London Advertiser over his breakfast, an advertisement caught his eye – an invitation to witness a match between the esteemed pugilists Sutton and Figg. Intrigued, he decided to attend, and after a leisurely drive through the streets of London in his newly acquired carriage, he arrived at the Wooden House in Marybone Fields. Though unfamiliar with the route, he navigated the winding roads, pausing to admire the architecture of Mr. Whitfield's round Tabernacle along Tottenham Road.

Upon arriving at the venue, he found a sizable crowd assembled to watch the impending contest. A sea of faces, both familiar and unknown, stretched before him, with the fashionable elite mingling alongside rougher patrons. Begging hands extended towards him, while shoeblacks vied for the privilege of polishing his boots. Flower sellers and street vendors hawked their wares, drawing his attention away from the commotion. Amidst the hubbub, a flag fluttered above the building, and the manager's voice boomed through the air, announcing the imminent commencement of the event.

Mr. Warrington paid his admission fee and claimed a seat in a gallery offering a clear view of the performance area. His valet, Gumbo, opted for a spot closer to the action, periodically rising to take a stroll outside or engage in a game of cards with the other servants and coachmen gathered in the surrounding carriages. The abundance of liveried attendants was striking – a relic of a bygone era, when every gentleman and lady seemed to require an entourage of vassals in attendance. Though their numbers had dwindled, remnants of this proud class still lingered, donning their finest attire and sporting ornate accessories on levee days.

As the main event drew near, a series of preliminary matches and exhibitions took place. A sparring match commenced, but the participants showed little spirit, and the crowd grew restless. A bout of cudgel-playing followed, though the blows struck were feeble and the wounds incurred negligible, prompting the audience to express their discontent with hushed murmurs and disapproving glances. "The masters, the masters!" someone in the crowd cried, and the renowned Sutton and Figg finally emerged onto the stage, armed and ready to face each other in the highly anticipated contest.
