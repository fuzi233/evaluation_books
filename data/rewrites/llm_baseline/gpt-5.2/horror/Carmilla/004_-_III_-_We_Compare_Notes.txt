We watched the little procession of lanterns and black velvet melt into the timbered fog until even the hoof-beats and the complaint of wheels were swallowed by the night. The forest, that moment before so full of moving sound, settled into a hush so complete it felt manufactured—an oppressive quiet in which one begins to hear one’s own blood and mistrust it. Had the whole thing been only a flare of imagination—an accident invented by mist and candlelight—there remained, on the stones of our bridge, the proof of weight and warmth: the young lady, stirring at last.

Her face was turned from me, hair unbound in a dark spill, but she raised her head as if listening for a familiar step. In a voice so sweet it was almost plaintive music, she asked, with a child’s injured complaint, “Where is mamma?”

Madame Perrodon answered her with the tenderness she reserved for the sick and the frightened, smoothing the strangeness of our walls into softer words. The girl’s gaze wandered; she tried to make our castle fit to her memory.

“Where am I? What is this place? I don’t see the carriage; and Matska—where is she?”

Madame explained what she could, and by degrees the young lady pieced together the misadventure, like one reconstructing a dropped recipe from scattered scraps: the jolt, the overturn, the sudden press of hands, the forest’s wet breath. She was relieved—almost fiercely relieved—that no one had been hurt. But when she understood that her mother had gone on and left her with us until her return “in about three months,” the composure that had held like a thin crust broke, and she wept.

I stepped forward, meaning to offer my own poor consolations; but Mademoiselle De Lafontaine caught my arm, her fingers light yet decisive.

“Not yet,” she murmured. “One at a time. A little excitement might overpower her.”

A bedroom was being made ready above—fires laid, sheets aired, water heated—those calm domestic rites that try to convince calamity it has wandered into the wrong house. My father, practical in emergencies, had already sent a servant on horseback for the physician who lived two leagues off.

The stranger rose at length. Leaning on Madame’s arm, she crossed the drawbridge slowly, as though each step had to be argued for. The servants waited in the hall to receive her. She was taken at once to one of our best rooms, and scarcely had she lain down before she sank into a sleep so deep it seemed to claim the whole house’s sound for itself. Madame Perrodon and Mademoiselle De Lafontaine left her with a maid and came down to us.

Our drawing-room—long, with four windows looking over moat and bridge to the darkened forest—held its familiar carved oak and crimson Utrecht velvet, the old cabinets and the thick tapestry that showed hunting and hawking and feasts in a hundred faded threads. Candles were lit; their wicks hissed and steadied, and the small noises of comfort began: the shifting of chairs, the minute clink of porcelain, the pour of hot tea.

My father, with his patriotic fondness for the national beverage, would always have tea appear alongside coffee and chocolate, as if the day could not close without that particular bitterness and fragrance. The steam rose with its faint leaf-scent, and for a moment it seemed absurd that such homely perfume could share air with the memory of overturned wheels and a woman in black velvet disappearing into fog.

We sat together, tasting the night again in words.

“How do you like our guest?” I asked Madame Perrodon as soon as she entered. “Tell me everything.”

“I like her extremely,” Madame answered. “She is, I almost think, the prettiest creature I ever saw. About your age—and so gentle, so nice.”

“She is absolutely beautiful,” Mademoiselle added; she had peeped in for a moment at the sleeping girl.

“And such a sweet voice,” said Madame Perrodon, as if that, too, were a kind of beauty you could carry in a bowl.

“Did you remark a woman in the carriage,” Mademoiselle asked, “after it was set up again—one who did not get out, but only looked from the window?”

We had not. Mademoiselle described her then: a hideous black woman with a colored turban, gazing fixedly out, nodding and grinning as though in mockery. Her eyes, Mademoiselle said, shone with a peculiar gleam; the whites looked large; her teeth were set as if in fury. The description fell into the room like a cold draft, and even the little crackle of candlewick seemed to hesitate.

“And what an ill-looking pack the servants were,” Madame Perrodon said. “Did you notice them?”

My father came in at that moment, and took up the remark at once.

“Ugly, hang-dog fellows as ever I beheld. I hope they won’t rob the poor lady in the forest. Clever rogues, though—they got everything to rights in a minute.”

“I dare say they are worn out with too long travelling,” Madame said, always charitable, though her voice betrayed that she had also seen those lean, dark, sullen faces and had not liked the look of them.

“I’m curious,” she confessed. “But the young lady will tell you tomorrow, if she is sufficiently recovered.”

“I don’t think she will,” said my father, and smiled oddly, with a little nod as though he had locked away a key.

That half-smile sharpened our curiosity. We all remembered his brief, earnest interview with the lady in black velvet before the carriage vanished. When at last we were alone, I begged him to tell me what had been said. He needed little urging.

“There’s no particular reason I should not. She expressed reluctance to trouble us with the care of her daughter—said she was delicate, nervous, but not subject to any kind of seizure.” He laughed a little at the detail. “She volunteered that. Nor to any illusion. Perfectly sane, in fact.”

“How very odd to insist on that,” I said. “So unnecessary.”

“Necessary to her, apparently,” he replied. “Then she said: ‘I am making a long journey of vital importance’—she emphasized the word—‘rapid and secret. I shall return for my child in three months. In the meantime she will be silent as to who we are, whence we come, and whither we are travelling.’ That was all.”

He paused, remembering.

“She spoke very pure French. When she said ‘secret’ she stopped and looked at me—sternly. She makes a great point of it. You saw how quickly she was gone. I hope I have not done a foolish thing taking charge of the young lady.”

For my part, foolish or not, I was thrilled. In our solitude, a new companion was like a sudden harvest after a long thin season. It promised talk, laughter, a second heartbeat in the house. I longed to see her and to hear her speak again, to learn the particular rhythm of her voice the way one learns the particular simmer of a sauce—by patience and proximity.

The doctor did not arrive until nearly one o’clock, and while we waited, the castle seemed to hold its breath. In the countryside, night is not merely the absence of light; it is a whole soundscape of its own, a hush patterned by distant owls and the soft interruptions of settling wood. Tonight, even those usual small proofs of life felt muted, as if the forest were listening too, and the silence itself had weight.

When the physician at last came down to the drawing-room, he reported very favorably. She was sitting up; her pulse regular; no injury; the nervous shock had passed without mischief. He saw no harm in my visiting her for a few minutes, if she wished it.

With that permission I sent at once to know whether she would receive me. The servant returned immediately: she desired it above all things.

I went without delay.

She lay in one of the handsomest rooms in the schloss—handsome, yet a little stately in its old gravity. Opposite the foot of the bed hung a sombre tapestry of Cleopatra with the asps at her breast; other classic scenes, faded into mournful colors, looked down from the walls. Yet the room was rich with gold carving and deep hues that caught candlelight warmly, as if trying, vainly, to outshine the gloom of those ancient stories.

Candles stood at her bedside. She sat propped among pillows, her slender figure wrapped in a soft silk dressing-gown embroidered with flowers and lined with thick quilted silk—the same covering her mother had thrown over her feet as she lay upon the ground. The silk seemed to remember the outside cold and resist it; its sheen moved when she breathed.

I had reached the bedside and begun my greeting when something struck me speechless—so sudden I recoiled a step, as if the air between us had flashed.

It was her face.

I had seen it before—twelve years ago—at night, in my childhood. It had fixed itself in my mind with the tenacity of a nightmare you cannot translate into daylight without losing its teeth, and yet cannot forget. Pretty—yes, even beautiful—and then, as now, wearing a melancholy expression. And now that expression lit instantly into a strange, fixed smile of recognition.

We were silent for a full minute. The candle flames made small sounds—tiny ticks in their own language—while my heart hammered out a louder dialect in my ears.

At last she spoke, and her voice, so gentle, seemed almost to brush the air.

“How wonderful,” she said. “Twelve years ago I saw your face in a dream, and it has haunted me ever since.”

“Wonderful indeed,” I managed, forcing my voice past the horror that had seized it. “Twelve years ago—in vision or reality—I certainly saw you. I could not forget your face. It has remained before my eyes ever since.”

Her smile softened. Whatever had looked strange in it fell away, and the dimpling of her cheeks became simply charming, intelligent. The terror in me, so sharp a moment before, began to loosen like a hand unclenching. I did what hospitality demanded; I welcomed her, told her how much pleasure her arrival had given us all, and—truly—how happy it made me.

As I spoke I took her hand. I was shy, as lonely people are, but the sudden intimacy of the moment made me bold. She pressed my hand; she laid her other hand upon it too; her eyes glowed. She glanced quickly into mine, smiled again, and blushed.

She answered my welcome prettily, and I sat beside her, still dazed by the circularity of it—past stepping cleanly into present, as if the years between were only mist.

“I must tell you my vision about you,” she said. “It is so strange that you and I should have had—each of the other—so vivid a dream, and that each should have seen, I you and you me, looking as we do now, when of course we both were mere children.”

She spoke with a seriousness that made the room feel smaller, more intimate, as though the tapestries leaned in to hear.

“I was a child, about six years old. I awoke from a confused and troubled dream and found myself in a room unlike my nursery—wainscoted clumsily in some dark wood, with cupboards and bedsteads and chairs and benches placed about. The beds were empty, I thought, and the room itself without anyone but myself.

“I looked about for some time, and I remember especially an iron candlestick with two branches—I should know it again. I crept under one of the beds to reach the window. But as I got from under it I heard someone crying, and looking up—while I was still upon my knees—I saw you. Most assuredly you, as I see you now: a beautiful young lady with golden hair and large blue eyes—and lips—your lips—you as you are here.

“Your looks won me. I climbed on the bed and put my arms about you, and I think we both fell asleep.

“I was aroused by a scream. You were sitting up screaming. I was frightened and slipped down upon the ground, and it seemed to me I lost consciousness for a moment; and when I came to myself I was again in my nursery at home. Your face I have never forgotten since. I could not be misled by mere resemblance. You are the lady whom I saw then.”

Now it was my turn, and I told her my own corresponding vision, as faithfully as my shaken memory allowed. She listened with undisguised wonder, her eyes fixed on mine as if trying to hear the exact accent of my recollection—what I could name and what I could not.

When I finished she smiled again, and there was something in the smile that flirted with dread and dismissed it in the same breath.

“I don’t know which should be most afraid of the other,” she said. “If you were less pretty I think I should be very much afraid of you. But being as you are—and you and I both so young—I feel only that I made your acquaintance twelve years ago, and have already a right to your intimacy. At all events it does seem as if we were destined, from our earliest childhood, to be friends.”

Her voice lowered on the last word, as if speaking it changed it.

“I wonder whether you feel as strangely drawn towards me as I do to you. I have never had a friend—shall I find one now?”

She sighed, and her dark eyes rested on me with a passionate, hungry tenderness that made me glance away and then back again, as if caught. The truth—shameful to admit even to myself—was that I did feel drawn to her, strongly and without good reason. Yet in that attraction there was also something like a faint recoil, a minute inward protest—like the body’s instinct when it hears a wrong sound in the night, too low to be explained, too clear to be ignored. But the attraction greatly prevailed. She interested me, she won me, she was so beautiful and so engaging that I felt almost grateful to be overcome.

Her eyelids drooped; a languor crept over her features, as if the effort of feeling so much had tired her. I rose, anxious not to exhaust her.

“The doctor thinks you ought to have a maid to sit up with you to-night,” I said. “One of ours is waiting. You’ll find her quiet and useful.”

“How kind of you,” she replied, “but I could not sleep. I never could with an attendant in the room. I shan’t require any assistance—and shall I confess my weakness? I am haunted with a terror of robbers. Our house was robbed once, and two servants murdered, so I always lock my door. It has become a habit—and you look so kind I know you will forgive me. I see there is a key in the lock.”

She drew me close for a moment in her pretty arms and whispered at my ear, her breath warm and intimate against my skin.

“Good night, darling. It is very hard to part with you, but good night. To-morrow—but not early—I shall see you again.”

She sank back with a sigh. Her eyes followed me with a fond, melancholy gaze, and she murmured once more, “Good night, dear friend.”

Young people love quickly; loneliness makes the heart greedy. I was flattered by her evident, though undeserved, fondness; I liked the confidence with which she received me, as if we were resuming a conversation interrupted only yesterday and not by twelve years.

The next day came, and we met again. In daylight she lost nothing. She was still, beyond doubt, the most beautiful creature I had ever seen, and the unpleasant remembrance of that childhood visitation had dulled and retreated, like a nightmare pushed back by morning duties. She confessed she had felt the same shock on seeing me, and the same faint antipathy mixed into admiration.

And so—astonishingly—we laughed together over our momentary horrors, as if dread itself were only another story that could be told in a calm room, over familiar cups, while outside the forest held its breath and kept its secrets in a language no one in the house could quite speak.
