"My desolation does begin to make A better life." —Shakespeare: *Antony and Cleopatra.*

Before Daniel Deronda was summoned to a second audience with his mother, a full day had elapsed during which she had merely dispatched a message stating she was not yet well enough to receive him. However, on the third morning, a note arrived commanding his immediate presence: "I leave to-day. Come and see me at once."

He was ushered into the same chamber as before, yet the atmosphere had shifted dramatically. The room was now heavily darkened by drawn blinds and thick curtains, casting the space in a gloomy twilight. The Princess was initially absent, but she soon appeared, draped in a loose wrap of soft, dusky-orange silk. Black lace floated loosely around her head, and her arms were bare beneath the wide sleeves of her gown. In the somber light, her face seemed even more striking; her eyes appeared larger, and the lines etched upon her visage carried a newfound vigor. One might have imagined her a sorceress, stretching forth a powerful hand to brew elixirs of youth for others, yet scorning to mix them for herself, having already drunk her fill of that fleeting season.

She placed her arms around her son's shoulders instantly, kissing him on both cheeks, before settling onto her cushions with an air of assured firmness and dignity—a stark contrast to the erratic volatility of their first meeting. She gestured for Deronda to sit beside her. He complied, asking softly, "You are quite relieved now, I trust?"

"Yes, I am at ease again," she replied, her tone possessing the matter-of-fact authority of a queen rather than the warmth of a mother. "Is there anything further you wish to ask?"

"May I inquire about the house in Genoa where you once lived with my grandfather?" Deronda asked.

"No," she answered, making a dismissive gesture with her arm. "It has been demolished—not to be found. As for our family history and where my father dwelt at various times, you will discover all of that among the papers in the chest; it is better served there than by my tongue. My father was a physician; my mother was a Morteira. I used to listen to these stories without truly hearing them. You will find them all recorded. I was born amidst them against my will, but I banished them the moment I could."

Deronda attempted to mask the pain swelling in his chest. "Is there anything else I should desire to know from you that would offer you personal satisfaction in telling me?"

"I believe I have disclosed everything that could reasonably be demanded of me," the Princess said, her expression turning cold and meditative. It seemed she had exhausted her emotional reserves during their previous encounter. In truth, she had reasoned to herself, *"I have done it all. I have confessed every secret. I will not relive this agony. I will spare myself further agitation."* She was meticulously enacting this plan of withdrawal. Yet, for Deronda's sensitive nature, the moment was cruel; it transformed his lifelong filial longing into a pilgrim's journey to a shrine stripped of its sacred symbols. It felt as though all the womanhood missing from her was concentrated entirely within him. With a tremor in his voice, he asked, "Then are we to part, and I never be anything to you?"

"It is better so," the Princess replied, her voice suddenly softer, mellower. "Even if it were possible for you to assume the role of my son, there could be nothing but hard duty involved. You would not love me. Do not deny it," she interrupted sharply, raising her hand. "I know the truth. You dislike what I have done. You are angry with me. You feel I have robbed you of something. You stand on your grandfather's side, and you will harbor a condemnation of me in your heart forever."

Deronda felt himself under a silent banishment. Rising from his seat, he preferred to stand if obedience required him to withhold any tenderness. But his mother looked up at him, her gaze filled with a fresh, intense admiration. "You are wrong to be angry with me," she said. "You are the better for what I did."

After a brief pause, she added abruptly, "And now, tell me what you intend to do?"

"Do you mean immediately?" Deronda clarified. "Or regarding the course of my future life?"

"I mean in the future. How does it affect you that I have revealed your birth?"

"A very great difference," Deronda stated emphatically. "I can hardly conceive of anything that would alter my existence more profoundly."

"What will you do, then?" the Princess pressed, her tone sharpening. "Will you mold yourself exactly like your grandfather—become what he wished, turn yourself into a Jew as he did?"

"That is impossible," Deronda insisted, his voice gaining tenacity. "The effects of my upbringing can never be erased. The Christian sympathies in which my mind was nurtured can never die out of me. However, I consider it my duty—and it is the impulse of my feelings—to identify myself, as far as possible, with my hereditary people. If I can discern any work to be done for them to which I can devote my soul and hand, I shall choose to do it."

His mother fixed her eyes upon him, speculating with wonder, examining his face as if trying to decipher a complex language through close scrutiny. He met her gaze firmly, sustained by a resolute opposition that expressed his most authentic self. She leaned forward slightly, emphasizing her next words decisively. "You are in love with a Jewess."

Deronda blushed. "My reasons would be independent of any such fact."

"I know better. I have observed men throughout my life," the Princess declared peremptorily. "Tell me the truth. She is a Jewess who will accept no one but a Jew. There are a few such," she added with a hint of scorn.

Deronda faced the difficulty everyone encounters when speaking to those whose minds are rigidly fixed on their own interpretations, refusing enlightenment. Beyond this, the issue at hand stirred a repugnance in him to either deny or affirm. He remained silent. Presently, she spoke again. "You love her as your father loved me, and she draws you after her as I drew him."

These words struck a chord in Deronda's filial imagination, and a tenderness in his glance was interpreted by his mother as an assent. Her passion rose as she continued, "But I was leading him the other way. And now your grandfather is getting his revenge."

"Mother," Deronda remonstrated gently, "let us not view it in that manner. I admit that some benefit may arise from the education you chose for me. I prefer to cherish that benefit with gratitude rather than dwell with resentment on the perceived injury. I believe it would have been right for me to grow up conscious of being a Jew, yet it must always have been a good to possess such broad instruction and sympathy. Now, you have restored me my inheritance—circumstances have brought a fuller restitution than you could have engineered alone. You have been spared from robbing my people of my service and me of my duty. Can you not bring your whole soul to consent to this?"

Deronda paused in his plea; his mother listened intently, as if the rhythm of his voice captivated her ear, yet she shook her head slowly. He began again, with even greater urgency. "You have told me you sought what you deemed best for me. Open your heart to relenting and love toward my grandfather, who sought what he held best for you."

"Not for me, no," she replied, shaking her head with absolute denial and crossing her arms tightly. "I tell you, he never thought of his daughter except as an instrument. Because I had needs outside his purpose, I was to be framed and tortured. If that is the law of the world, I will not claim to love it. If my actions were wrong—if it is God demanding that I surrender what I withheld—who is punishing me for deceiving my father and failing to warn him that I would contradict his trust? Well, I have told everything. I have done what I could. And *your* soul consents. That is sufficient. I have, after all, been the instrument my father desired. 'I desire a grandson who shall have a true Jewish heart. Every Jew should rear his family as if he hoped that a Deliverer might spring from it.'"

As she uttered these final sentences, the Princess narrowed her eyes, nodded her head up and down, and spoke slowly with a strange, resonant chest-voice, as if reciting words she did not willingly endorse.

"Were those my grandfather's words?" Deronda asked.

"Yes, yes; and you will find them written," the Princess confirmed. "I wanted to thwart him," she burst out suddenly, reigniting the passion she had displayed earlier.
