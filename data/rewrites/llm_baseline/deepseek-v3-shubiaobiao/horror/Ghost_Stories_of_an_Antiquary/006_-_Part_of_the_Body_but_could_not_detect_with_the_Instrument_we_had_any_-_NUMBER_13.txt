Viborg, that quiet jewel of Jutland, wears its history like a shroud. A bishop’s seat, a cathedral of pale new stone, gardens where storks stalk like sentinels—it is a place of whispers. Just beyond its edges lie Hald, with its postcard prettiness, and Finderup, where King Erik Glipping met his bloody end in 1286. Fifty-six wounds from iron maces marked his skull when they pried open his tomb centuries later. But this is no guidebook.

My cousin—let us call him Anderson—chose the Golden Lion for his stay, though he never returned after that first visit. The reason, I think, will soon become clear.

The inn had survived the great fire of 1726, which devoured half the town. Its red-brick façade loomed, gabled and grim, a scripture etched above the door. The courtyard within was a patchwork of black beams and white plaster, the sort of place that ought to creak with ghosts. Anderson, arriving in the slanting light of dusk, found it charming. He had come to study church records—old papers salvaged from the flames, fragments of a lost world.

The landlord, a practical man, showed him the available rooms. Number 17 was dismissed for its gloom; Numbers 12 and 14 overlooked the street, bright and airy. Anderson chose 12.

It was a long, narrow chamber, three windows along one wall. The stove bore a faded scene of Abraham’s sacrifice, the iron worn smooth by time. A print of old Viborg hung crookedly, but nothing else stirred the imagination.

Yet that first night, as Anderson settled in, he noticed something odd. The hotel’s blackboard listed every guest—lawyers, merchants, a German traveler—but no Number 13. A common superstition, he thought. But when he fetched his book from the dining room and mistook his door in the passage, he found himself rattling the handle of Number 13. From within came a sudden movement, a hiss of breath. He withdrew, embarrassed, and found his true room.

Sleep did not come easily. The walls seemed closer than before, the ceiling higher. And when he woke, his missing portmanteau had returned to its place, untouched. A cigarette left on the right-hand sill now rested on the middle window. Small things, but unsettling.

Then there was the matter of the shadows.

Across the street, the blank wall caught the glow of his lamp—and others. The occupant of Number 11 (a lawyer, Anderson recalled) passed his window in shirtsleeves, then a nightshirt. But Number 13…

A figure stood there, tall and thin, draped in something pale. A red light flickered behind it, casting a dull pulse on the wall. Then, at the sound of footsteps below, it vanished. The light snuffed out.

Morning brought no clarity. His boots sat outside his door—but the number was 14. Where was 13? He retraced his steps. Next to 14 was 12. There was no door between them.

At supper, the landlord laughed when Anderson asked about the missing number.

“Superstition,” he said. “Travelers won’t take it. Bad luck.”

“But I saw it,” Anderson insisted.

The man’s smile faded. “There is no Number 13.”

That night, Anderson invited the landlord to his room, under the pretext of showing him English photographs. But as he wrote letters beforehand, his nerves prickled. He entered through Number 11 to avoid passing the place where 13 should be.

His neighbor, the lawyer, was oddly restless. Through the wall came no sound, but the shadow on the street told another story—a gaunt figure dancing, barefoot, in furious silence.

And Anderson, watching, wondered if the missing room had always been there, waiting.
