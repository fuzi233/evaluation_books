Mr. Tartar's chambers were the neatest, cleanest, and best-ordered rooms ever seen under heaven. The floors were scrubbed so thoroughly that one might have imagined the London cobblestones had been liberated forever and exiled from the city. Every inch of brasswork possessed by Mr. Tartar was polished and burnished until it reflected light like a brazen mirror. Not a single speck, spot, or smudge marred the purity of his household gods, regardless of their size. His sitting-room resembled an admiral's cabin; his bathroom, a pristine dairy; and his sleeping chamber, lined with lockers and drawers, a seed merchant's shop. Even his neatly balanced cot seemed to stir gently in the center, as if breathing.

Everything belonging to Mr. Tartar had its designated quarter. Maps and charts had their space; books, brushes, boots, clothes, case-bottles, telescopes, and instruments all had their specific homes. Nothing was left to chance. Shelves, brackets, lockers, hooks, and drawers were arranged to maximize utility and eliminate wasted space, ensuring every item had a snug niche where it belonged. His gleaming service of plate was arranged on the sideboard so precisely that a missing salt-spoon would betray itself instantly. Similarly, his toiletries on the dressing table were organized so that a toothpick of sloppy behavior could be spotted at a glance. Curio cabinets displayed stuffed birds, dried fish, repolished reptiles, weapons, garments, shells, seaweed, grasses, and coral memorabilia, each preserved according to its nature and placed exactly where it should be. Paint and varnish were kept hidden but ready to erase any stray fingerprints. No warship was ever kept cleaner from careless touch.

On this bright summer day, a neat awning was rigged over Mr. Tartar's flower garden with the skill of a seasoned sailor, giving the entire scene a maritime air so complete that the garden might have belonged to a ship's stern windows. The whole establishment looked ready to sail gallantly, provided Mr. Tartar had only raised the speaking trumpet hanging in the corner and issued hoarse orders to "heave the anchor up" and "set all sails!"

Mr. Tartar, presenting this magnificent vessel, matched the perfection of his surroundings. When a man pursues an amiable hobby that neither shies nor kicks, it is delightful to see him enjoy the humor of his own creation. Being a cordial, earnest, and genuinely fresh man, he appeared at his best while showing off his contrivances. Rosa, treated with the homage due to the First Lady of the Admiralty, naturally found it charming to watch Mr. Tartar laugh and rejoice in his inventions. After the tour concluded, the sunburnt sailor delicately withdrew, inviting her to consider herself the Queen of his admiral's cabin and waving her free to explore the flower garden with the hand that had once held Mr. Crisparkle's life.

"Helena! Helena Landless! Are you there?"

"Who speaks to me? Is it not Rosa?" Another handsome face appeared.

"Yes, my darling!"

"Why, how did you come here, dearest?"

"I—I don't quite know," Rosa blushed, "unless I am dreaming!"

Why the blush? Their faces were alone amidst the other flowers. Were blushes a fruit of the magic beanstalk country?

"I am not dreaming," Helena smiled. "I would expect less if I were. How did we come together—or so near together—so unexpectedly?"

Indeed unexpected, among the dingy gables of P.J.T.'s domain and the salt-tolerant blooms. Rosa quickly explained how they met, detailing the why and wherefore.

"And Mr. Crisparkle is here," Rosa concluded rapidly. "And, could you believe it? Long ago he saved his life!"

"I could believe anything of Mr. Crisparkle," Helena replied, her face flushing.

"Yes, but it wasn't Crisparkle," Rosa corrected swiftly.

"I don't understand, love."

"It was very kind of Mr. Crisparkle to be saved," Rosa hastened to add, "and he could not show higher regard for Mr. Tartar. But it was Mr. Tartar who saved him."

Helena's dark eyes fixed earnestly on the bright face among the leaves. "Is Mr. Tartar with you now, dear?"

"No; for he has given up his rooms to me—to us, I mean. It is such a beautiful place!"

"Is it?"

"It is like the interior of the most exquisite ship ever built. It is like—"

"A dream?" Helena suggested.

Rosa nodded slightly and smelled the flowers.

After a brief silence, during which Helena seemed to pity someone (perhaps it was only Rosa's imagination), she continued: "My poor Neville is reading in his room; the sun is too bright here. I think he would prefer not to know you are so close."

"Oh, I think so too!" Rosa agreed readily.

"I suppose," Helena pursued doubtfully, "that he must eventually learn everything you've told me, but I'm unsure. Ask Mr. Crisparkle's advice, my darling. Ask him whether I should reveal to Neville as much or as little of your story as seems best."

Rosa retreated to her state-cabin to consult the Minor Canon. He advised the free exercise of Helena's judgment.

"I thank him," Helena said when Rosa returned. "Ask him whether it is better to wait for further evidence of Neville's persecution by this villain, or to try to anticipate it by discovering if any such plotting occurs secretly around us?"

The Minor Canon struggled to offer a confident opinion. After several failed attempts, he suggested consulting Mr. Grewgious. Helena agreed. With a feigned air of indifference, Mr. Crisparkle crossed the quadrangle to P.J.T.'s office. Mr. Grewgious held the firm principle that one should strike first against a brigand or wild beast, especially since John Jasper combined both traits.

Thus advised, Mr. Crisparkle returned to report to Rosa, who relayed the news to Helena. Standing by her window, Helena pondered.

"We can rely on Mr. Tartar's willingness to help, Rosa?" she asked.

Oh yes! Rosa shyly believed so, almost vouching for it. Should she ask Mr. Crisparkle? "Your authority on this point equals his, my dear," Helena said calmly, "so you need not vanish again for that."

"You see, Neville knows no one else here," Helena continued after reflection. "He hasn't spoken to anyone. If Mr. Tartar visited him openly and often, perhaps even daily, something might happen."

"Something might?" Rosa repeated, looking at her friend's beauty with perplexity.

"If Neville's movements are truly watched, and the goal is to isolate him and grind his daily life away grain by grain—as that seems to threaten you—is it not likely," Helena reasoned, "that his enemy would somehow contact Mr. Tartar to warn him off? In that case, we could not only confirm the plot but learn its terms through Mr. Tartar."

"I see!" Rosa cried, darting back into the cabin.

Soon, her flushed face reappeared. She had informed Mr. Crisparkle, who had summoned Mr. Tartar. Mr. Tartar, currently waiting outside, had declared his immediate readiness to act as suggested.

"I thank him from my heart," Helena said. "Please tell him so."

Confused between the flower garden and the cabin, Rosa delivered the message and received further assurances from Mr. Tartar, standing wavering between Helena and him—a confusion that, rather than being awkward, presented a pleasant appearance.

"Now, darling," Helena said, "let us heed the caution restricting us to this brief meeting. I hear Neville moving. Are you returning?"

"To Miss Twinkleton's?" Rosa asked.

"Yes."

"Oh, I could never go there again. That dreadful interview changed everything!"

"Then where *are* you going, pretty one?"

"Now that I think of it, I don't know," Rosa admitted. "I haven't decided anything; my guardian will manage me. Don't worry, dear. I'll surely be somewhere."

"And I shall hear from my Rosebud via Mr. Tartar?" Helena inquired.

"Yes, I suppose so—from—" Rosa glanced back in a flutter, unable to supply the name. "But tell me one thing before we part, dearest Helena. Be absolutely sure—you're sure I couldn't help it."

"Help it, love?"

"Help making him malicious and revengeful. I couldn't bargain with him, could I?"

"You know how I love you, darling," Helena answered indignant. "But I would sooner see you dead at his wicked feet."

"That comforts me greatly! Will you tell your poor brother so? Give him my remembrance and sympathy? And ask him not to hate me?"

Helena shook her head mournfully, as if such an entreaty were unnecessary. She lovingly kissed her two hands to her friend, who returned the gesture, before seeing a third hand—a brown one—appear among the foliage to escort her friend away.
