The mundane, rhythmic dripping of a kitchen faucet held a peculiar resonance for Oleron, echoing with uncanny precision against the backdrop of the real world. This acoustic mirroring triggered a strange, hyper-awareness within him, sharpening his ears to the hidden symphony of the old house. Silence, he realized, was a lie; the place was never truly quiet. Perhaps the gentle warmth of the spring air had loosened the joints of ancient timber, causing the house to stretch its weary bones. Insects drilled through beams and burrowed beneath floorboards, creating a subterranean chatter that vibrated through the soles of his feet. To Oleron, the house was not dead matter. It breathed. A beam groaned softly as it settled deeper into its mortise; the walls hummed with the ticking of unseen tenants; windows shifted with a soft, metallic click, rearranging themselves without wind.

As Oleron sat, lost in these musings, a fantastical notion took root. If his own soul resonated with his physical form, why not the house? He imagined a symbiotic relationship where the dwelling was an extension of his very self, a sentient entity that might one day be regarded by a future occupant as haunted—not by a ghost, but by the lingering spirit of its creator. It was a ludicrous thought for a man whose only ambition was to restart a novel, yet the idea amused him. He felt a growing, almost parasitic attachment to the decaying fabric around him.

Across from him, Elsie Bengough watched with increasing distaste. Where she had once been indifferent, she now radiated a sharp, defensive hostility toward the setting.

"This place isn't suited for today, and certainly not for you," she declared, her voice cutting through the atmospheric hum. "You're drifting away from reality, slipping into a dangerous apathy. You need concrete floors, a gas meter, the clatter of a tradesman's lift. You need a job that forces you to scrape shoulders with living people. If I could find you something, just a few days a week, leaving time for your writing, would you take it?"

A flicker of annoyance crossed Oleron's face. Being dissected like a specimen irritated him. "Thank you, Miss Bengough," he said, his tone flat. "But I don't think so. We all have our own lives to live."

"Your own life to live!" She leaned forward, her brow furrowing. "How long has it been since you stepped outside, Paul?"

"About two hours."

"Not buying stamps or mailing letters. How long since you had a real stretch?"

"Some time, I suppose. I don't know exactly."

"Since I was last here?"

"I haven't ventured out much."

"And has *Romilly* improved under this confinement?"

"She seems to be progressing. I'm laying the foundations. I'll start the actual writing soon."

Elsie frowned, turning slightly away before snapping back. "Ah! So you still harbor that ridiculous obsession?"

"If you mean," Oleron said slowly, "that I've abandoned the original *Romilly* for a new version, then yes, I do."

There was a cold edge in his voice that stung her. A fighter by nature, she bristled. "Pshaw!" she exclaimed impatiently. "Where is the old manuscript?"

"Why?"

"I want to see it. I want to show you parts of it. I want to snap you out of whatever trance you're in."

For the first time, Oleron turned his back. When he faced her again, his gentleness was laced with resolve. "It's futile, Elsie. I am responsible for my own path, and I must walk it—even if it looks wrong to you. I am thinking clearly. Regarding the manuscript, I considered burning it, but I didn't. It sits in the window seat, if you really need to see it."

She moved swiftly to the bay window, lifting the lid. Suddenly, she gasped, pressing the back of her hand to her mouth. Over her shoulder, she pointed. "Paul, you must hammer those nails down!"

He scrambled to her side. "What? What is it? Did they come loose?"

"You left enough sticking out to cause serious injury," she whispered, revealing a deep, angry red gash running from her wrist to her knuckles.

"Oh, goodness!" Oleron cried. "Come to the bathroom immediately. We must wash it—"

He ushered her into the small bathroom, turned the tap to warm water, and gently cleansed the raw wound. Holding her hand, he switched to cold water, his voice breaking with shock. "Lord, how did this happen? I swear I checked every box! I opened them a dozen times! I can't believe..."

"Don't stop," she murmured, her eyes squeezed shut, though she bore the pain stoically.

Returning to the sitting room, Oleron wrapped her hand tightly in a handkerchief. Yet, perplexity clouded his features. He had spent half the day organizing the flower boxes, meticulously checking every corner, yet he could not fathom how a single inch-and-a-half of rusted nail had remained embedded in the wood. He had handled the boxes repeatedly, searching for pests, yet missed the danger entirely.

"It must be removed now," he muttered, retrieving a pair of pliers. With steady hands, he extracted the offending fragment, wincing at the sight of the blood.

Meanwhile, Elsie sank into a chair, her complexion pale. Clutched in her uninjured hand was the manuscript of *Romilly*. She hadn't relinquished her grip on it. Soon, she launched into her plea again. "Oh, Paul, if you proceed with this, it will be the gravest mistake of your life!"

He bowed his head, genuine distress etching lines into his forehead. The image of the bloody nail haunted him, pushing *Romilly* to the background of his thoughts. "What can I say, Elsie? I can only hope that when you read the new draft, you'll understand. And if, despite everything, you still dislike her..." He made a helpless gesture. "Do you realize I must follow my own inner light?"

Silence fell between them.

"Elsie," he said softly, trying to bridge the gap. "We've worked together well so far. Let's not break over this."

The words died on his lips before he could finish them. Regret washed over him instantly. She had been cradling her bandaged hand, her eyelids fluttering, but her lips trembled. A shiver ran through her as she spoke. "I cannot help but say it, Paul, but you have changed so profoundly."

"Hush, Elsie," he cooed, soothingly. "You've been shocked. Rest for a moment. How could I possibly change?"

"I don't know, but you have. You aren't yourself since we arrived here. It has stalled your work, transformed you into a stranger, and filled me with terror for your safety. Oh, listen to my hand throbbing!"

"Poor child," he sighed. "Shall I call a doctor to dress this properly?"

"No," she interrupted, rising. "I'll manage. I'll keep it elevated."

She placed her elbow on the back of the chair, resting the swaddled hand lightly on his shoulder.

At that contact, a jolt of terrifying clarity struck Oleron. Hundreds of times before, during their walks and outings, she had slipped her hand into his arm with the casual intimacy of a sister, and he had accepted it as a platonic bond. But now, a storm of questions tore through his mind. Her eyes remained closed, her head tilted back in a posture of exhausted surrender, and a lost, ineffable smile played on her parted lips. The truth crashed down upon him with the force of a revelation. God above! Had he not seen it before?

Stranger still was what followed. Seeing her lost in love for him did not summon the expected feelings of sorrow, humility, or abasement. Instead, a chaotic, alien emotion surged within him—something he fought desperately to suppress. It was petulance, irritation, resentment, and a brutal ungentleness. A selfish impulse, primal and sudden, seized control before his conscious mind could grasp it. *Why was she here?* he thought, the words forming in his throat. *Why wasn't she working on her own affairs? Who had granted her the authority to guard him in this manner?*

"Changed?" he thought, the accusation burning hot. *It was she who had changed...*

By the time she opened her eyes, he had wrestled the resentment down, masking it with a reserved politeness. "Please, let me take you to a doctor."

She stood up. "No, thank you, Paul. I'll go now. If I need a dressing, I'll arrange it. Take the other hand, please. Goodbye."

He did not try to stop her. They walked to the foot of the stairs. Halfway down a narrow alley, she paused. "If you're not home, it will be a long journey. I'll send you a postcard next time."

At the gate, she turned back one final time, her gaze heavy with sorrow. "Leave this place, Paul. Everything here is wrong."

And then she vanished.

Oleron retreated to his room, crossing directly to the window box. He lifted the lid and stared into the darkness for a long time. Finally, he lowered the cover and turned away.
