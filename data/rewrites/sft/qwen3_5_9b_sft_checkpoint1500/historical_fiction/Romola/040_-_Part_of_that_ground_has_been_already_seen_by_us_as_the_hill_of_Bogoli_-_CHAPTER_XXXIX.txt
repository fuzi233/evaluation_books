A Supper in the Rucellai Gardens.

As Tito stepped into the elegant pavilion, his keen observation instantly confirmed his suspicion: this gathering was a political assembly, disguised merely as a celebration of camaraderie. In the era of Florence, where wealth had spawned a peculiar reverence for *Madonna della Gozzoviglia* and Saint Buonvino, it was widely believed that fine food and vintage wine sharpened the mind's grasp on political truths. Only those with "invited stomachs" could truly discern their loyalties with the clarity afforded by post-prandial leisure. While the Florentines were typically sober, the Rucellais, among the city's few magnates who maintained a magnificent household, spared no expense tonight. High philosophical treatises were out of place; the atmosphere demanded the bust of Plato, recently transferred from Lorenzo's villa to gaze down approvingly on the scholars, and the frescoes of the cardinal virtues adorning the walls.

Plato's marble visage, crowned with laurel, had long supervised the Academies' gatherings, particularly on the thirteenth of November, the saint's reputed birthday. Tonight, however, the company was not merely discussing the Master's doctrines with moderation; they were indulging in them with a fervor that bordered on excess. Among them sat Pico della Mirandola, a former Quixotic genius now humbled by a consuming quest for inner perfection; Marsilio Ficino, the diligent reared-up philosopher whose mind, fed exclusively on Platonism, seemed softened by the diet; Angelo Poliziano, the age's supreme literary spirit, whose verses flowed with living blood; and Leon Battista Alberti, the venerable patriarch, a robust mind of universal scope, both practical and theoretical. These valiant workers, whose names might fade from daily records, formed the bedrock of an intellectual inheritance that, like the ploughing of past generations, sustained the soil beneath their feet.

Bernardo Rucellai, the host and patron, stood in the prime of life, barely forty, radiating a cautiously dignified hauteur. He possessed a purity of Latinity that Erasmus deemed uncatchable, a tongue that refused to yield a single syllable to foreign scrutiny. He greeted Tito with particular favor, seating him between Lorenzo Tornabuoni and Giannozzo Pucci, two accomplished young lions of the Medicean faction.

While the silver basin of scented water circulated, allowing the company to cleanse their hands, the air filled with the perfume of fresh white damask from France, and rings glittered on slender, white fingers under the flickering wax-lamps. Conversation drifted to the familiar, harmless topics of the day. Someone mused what Dante, if resurrected, would make of silver forks replacing iron ones, and the consensus was unanimous: posterity's habits would shock the ancients if the latter could witness them. As silver spoons teased delicacies designed to precede serious discourse—morsels of liver cooked to a melting point—the eye lingered on the enamel centers of the brass service. Attention turned to the silver dish for *confetti*, a masterpiece attributed to Antonio Pollajuolo, whom papal patrons had enticed away from Florence to the opulence of Rome.

"I recall," interjected Niccolò Ridolfi, a middle-aged gentleman whose negligent ease masked a lifelong conviction of superior rank, "how our Antonio grew bitter regarding the chiseling and enameling of these metal trinkets. He declared a furious shift to painting, reasoning that 'the artist who puts his work into gold and silver puts his brains into the melting-pot.'"

"And surely that was a prophetic warning," Giannozzo Pucci remarked. "If this war with Pisa continues, spreading to our other towns, it will not only be our silver dishes that disappear. I suspect Antonio's silver saints around the altar of San Giovanni will one day vanish from the faithful's eyes to be worshipped more devoutly as coin."

"The Friar prepares us for this," Lorenzo Tornabuoni observed dryly. "He tells the populace that God rejects silver crucifixes alongside starving bellies, insisting the Church is best adorned with the gems of holiness and the golden hue of brotherly love."

"A sound doctrine of war finance," Bernardo Rucellai replied with a crisp wit. "Yet politics wait for the wine to wash them down; they are too heavy for roast and boiled meat."

"Yes," Niccolò Ridolfi agreed. "Luigi Pulci would insist this boiled kid requires an impartial palate. I remember a day at Careggi when Luigi, in his rattling vein, claimed nothing corrupted the tongue like opinion. 'Opinion,' he cried, 'spoils the saliva—that is why men embraced pepper. Scepticism alone is a philosophy that tastes nothing.' 'Nay,' poor Lorenzo de' Medici countered, 'you are mistaken, Luigi. Here sits Matteo Franco, an untainted sceptic craving hotter sauce than anyone.' 'Because he holds a strong opinion of himself!' flashed Luigi. 'That is the primal egg of all other opinion. Is he a sceptic? He believes in the immortality of his own verses. Such a logician as that preaching friar who mapped the pavement of Hell! Poor Luigi, his mind was steel, sharp enough to cut whatever it touched.'"

"And yet a tender soul," Giannozzo Pucci murmured. "His words sounded like soap bubbles, yet he was as temperate as a butterfly."

For a time, the light chatter and solid victuals held sway. After the roasts, the indispensable capon and game appeared, capped by the crown of a well-laid table: a peacock prepared à la Apicius. Cooked with feathers intact, it was presented so artfully that it resembled a live bird resting peacefully. Great skill was required of the official carver to rotate the classical, albeit insipid, bird on its back, exposing the plucked breast for slicing, unless a guest possessed the rare independence to decline the "expensive toughness" for the "vulgar digestibility" of the capon. Few dared refuse. Tito, quoting Horace, scattered his slice in minute particles; Bernardo Rucellai offered a learned digression on the ancient price of peacock eggs but feigned non-eating; and Niccolò Ridolfi balanced a mouthful on his fork while recounting Luigi Pulci's tale of a Siennese man who deceived guests by boiling a wild goose in its feathers to mimic a pheasant.

Little peacock was consumed, yet the mere spectacle conferred a profound satisfaction, a marker of status accessible only to the wealthy. To slight the bird's flesh or question the institution at a time when Fra Girolamo preached that luxury was not the duty of the rich would have been rash.

Beyond the circle of warmth, light, and savory aromas, the chill obscurity of the garden hid a solitary figure. Baldassarre walked in narrowing circles, pausing to peer through the windows, which glowed like brilliant jewels against the gloom. He heard the laughter, saw Tito gesticulate with careless grace, and heard his voice weaving through the merry confusion. Baldassarre's mind was a harp string stretched to breaking. He prepared himself for the moment of entry, deriving a savage pleasure from watching Tito's unconscious gaiety prepare its victim for torture. Yet, the men seated among the branching tapers and flashing cups knew nothing of the pale, fierce face watching from without. Light could serve as effectively as darkness as a curtain.

As the conversation deepened, shedding its triviality, the sense of citizenship pressed heavily upon the minds of the guests. The disturbing influence of Fra Girolamo dominated the air. Before the stewed fish was removed, his name surfaced, and despite Bernardo Rucellai's prior restraint, the talk turned openly political. Gossip rippled through the room regarding the first day's voting in the Palazzo: the fiery, domineering nature of Francesco Valori, who seemed to believe his austere virtue entitled him to absolute rule, and the evident alignment between Soderini's speeches for the Great Council and the Friar's sermons.

"My view," Niccolò Ridolfi asserted, "is that the Friar possesses a longer head for public affairs than Soderini or any of the Piagnones. Rest assured, Soderini speaks as his mouthpiece more than the Friar acts as his."

"No, Niccolò," Bernardo Rucellai corrected gently. "I disagree. The Friar has an acute mind, seeing clearly what serves his ends, but it is unlikely that Pagolantonio Soderini, a veteran of affairs who has studied the Venetian Council, owes his ideas to a monk. No, Soderini loads the cannon; I grant you, the Friar provides the powder and strikes the match. He masters the people, and the people are beginning to master us. Behold!"

"Well," Lorenzo Tornabuoni ventured, now that the servants had departed and only wine circled, "regardless of Soderini's debt, *we* owe the Friar the general amnesty accompanying the Council scheme. We might have survived without the terror of God or the moral reforms passed by a majority of black beans. That proposal allowing Medicean heads to remain safely on our shoulders, sparing us the confiscation of our properties in fines, earns my warm approval. I am convinced nothing short of the Friar's dominance could secure it. He stands as firm as a rock on promoting peace. I have spoken privately with him."

A murmur of surprise rippled through the far end of the table, but Bernardo Rucellai nodded silently, inviting Tornabuoni to proceed.
