Our readers, who have followed Harry Warrington’s journey with honesty and care, know well the truth of his feelings for Cattarina—and how that tender connection was shaped by circumstance, not desire. Yet in the gossipy circles of Tunbridge Wells, where tongues wagged like windmills and reputations were as fragile as glass, such truths were buried beneath a mountain of rumor. Many of the older ladies there, particularly Madame de Bernstein, believed the young Virginian to be no better than any other reckless English nobleman—dissipated, impetuous, and given to every kind of scandalous indulgence. To her, Harry’s behavior was proof enough: he danced, he smiled, he offered tea and music at the Wells, and he spoke with charm to every woman who crossed his path. But these were not signs of vice, as she imagined—they were marks of a gentleman raised in a culture where manners mattered more than whispers.

Madame Bernstein, a woman of strong Roman pride and even stronger prejudice, had long held the belief that her nephew should live as Rome did—boldly, proudly, without restraint. So when stories began to circulate about Harry’s supposed affairs—his “Lovelace adventures,” as they were called—she swallowed them whole. She listened eagerly, nodded gravely, and repeated them with relish to anyone who would listen. And yet, though she once watched Maria closely, fearing her niece might fall under Harry’s spell, she now relaxed. Her attention drifted, distracted by her own concerns—her dinners, her rheumatism, her endless round of social duties. The fire in her eyes dimmed; her vigilance cooled. Perhaps she truly believed the danger had passed. Or perhaps, like so many elders before her, she simply stopped caring. And then there was Maria herself—so clever, so composed, so skilled in the art of quiet influence—that she may well have lulled the old Baroness into a false sense of security. At Maria’s age, girls learn quickly how to hide their hearts. And at Madame Bernstein’s age, dragons grow weary. Their claws grow blunt, their breath less fierce, their watchfulness slack. I’ve known several such women in my time—once fearsome, now gentle, their sharp edges softened by years. They no longer threaten the world; they merely sigh over it.

If the folks at Oakhurst could believe every slander whispered about Harry, why should Madame Bernstein, with her long experience of human frailty, be any wiser? Of course, every woman in her circle accepted the tales of Harry’s wild ways as gospel. When the little French dancer returned to London, it was said she’d been cast aside—abandoned by the heartless Virginian for another, whose name was guessed at, whispered, and repeated until it became fact. In Tunbridge Wells, all ranks mingled—ladies of fashion, ladies of questionable reputation, those seeking health, pleasure, or escape—all drinking from the same glasses, dancing to the same fiddles, and exchanging secrets like currency. A century ago, our ancestors gathered at Epsom, Bath, Harrogate, just as we do now at Homburg and Baden. Society never changes its nature—it only changes its dress.

So Harry’s bad name brought comfort to his aunt. It eased her mind about his feelings for Lady Maria. She was so reassured, in fact, that when the chaplain arrived to escort her home, she barely blinked. She preferred to keep Maria close, to talk of Harry’s “wild extravagances,” to whisper that boys were boys, and to confide her plans for marrying Harry off—to someone suitable, someone rich, someone respectable. All this poor Maria had to endure with patience, though her heart beat otherwise.

There lived in the last century a certain French duke and marquis, famed across Europe and America alike, who left behind a memoir so bold and candid that no decent reader should dare open it. He had played the role of Don Juan throughout his life, and in his book named countless court beauties who had fallen to his charm. What must it be like, one wonders, for descendants of those women to find their ancestresses immortalized in such a volume—flaws exposed, virtues mocked?

This nobleman once traveled to North America, where, as he had done in Europe, he expected to conquer hearts. But here, among the simple folk of the colonies, he failed entirely. Had he succeeded, he surely would have recorded it with pride. Instead, silence. Travelers often complain of American rudeness, but let me say from personal experience: the conversation of American gentlemen is modest, and the lives of their women are pure.

Harry Warrington brought that same colonial modesty with him to England. Though he heard the free talk of his new companions—men of the world, seasoned in pleasure—he remained silent, reserved. He avoided double meanings, never boasted of conquests, and blushed when others told bawdy stories. Mr. Sampson, the chaplain, had noticed this during their time at Castlewood. Once, when cousin Will shared a particularly risqué tale, Harry looked uncomfortable. Lord Castlewood had rebuked his brother sharply, telling him to save his jokes for the usher’s table at Kensington. That moment gave rise to the chaplain’s first remark upon seeing Harry: “Reverentia pueris”—respect for youth. For though Sampson lacked strength in virtue, he had grace enough not to offend an innocent boy with cynicism.

The chaplain felt genuine warmth toward Harry after the young man gave him the horse. “You see, sir,” he said, “I am of the world. I’ve led a rough life, and can’t afford to be more particular than my neighbors. Video meliora, deteriora sequor—‘I see what is better, but follow what is worse.’ That was our motto at college. I have a sister, Patty, at boarding school nearby. I keep my tongue clean when speaking to her, and expect others to do the same. So I’ll try to do the same by you.”

He praised Harry often to Madame Bernstein. She delighted in hearing it. She liked Harry—his good looks, his courage, his easy blushes, his bright eyes, his deep voice. His simplicity amused her; his shrewdness charmed her. If he had been witty or learned, she might have grown bored. But he was exactly as he was—natural, unpretentious, and full of quiet dignity. “We must find a good wife for him,” she said to Sampson. “I have a few in mind. We must set him up here. He’ll never return to his savages—or to that little Methodist mother of his.”

And here, too, Sampson had his own designs. He, too, wished to see Harry married—and not just for the sake of propriety, but because he saw a way to serve both his lord and his own interests. There had been talks at Castlewood—perhaps between Sampson and the Earl—about a marriage for Lady Maria, and a reward for the chaplain if he could help bring it about. If he could assist that anxious lady in finding a husband, Sampson was ready to act. He waited now, watching, planning, waiting to see how best to apply his influence.

Sampson proved a most agreeable companion. Within hours, he and Harry were close. The parson was cheerful, hearty, fond of good food and drink, and utterly devoid of sanctimony. He spoke plainly, laughed freely, and never forced piety on his young friend. Yet he took care not to shock him—never revealing the deeper secrets he himself knew too well. With Harry, Sampson was simply a lively, jolly companion—ready for a drinking bout, a cockfight, a game of cards, or a gallop across the common. But his talk remained decent. He aimed to amuse, not corrupt. And he succeeded.

Sampson had animal spirits and natural wit, and a lifetime of experience in the art of flattery—what some might call “toad-eating.” From the moment he entered college as a servitor, he had learned how to make his way in the world by serving others. That was satire, of course—when we say there are no toad-eaters today. But they exist still. Young men at public schools are taught early to flatter, to please, to stand beside greatness. Only now, they do it subtly—no longer bowing low or calling their patrons “sir” in public. They walk arm-in-arm, call men by their first names, borrow money, and swallow their toads in private.

But in Harry’s time, things were different. A gentleman’s toad-eater wore no mask. He openly served, called his patron “sir,” stood when addressed, and flattered by habit. Sampson took no shame in calling Harry his young patron—a Virginian nobleman recommended by the Earl of Castlewood. He was proud to walk beside him, to speak of him in public, to give orders to Harry’s tradesmen (and perhaps receive a percentage in return). He performed every duty of an aide-de-camp—though no one would have called him such. He had played the role of *ami du prince* for many young men of fashion since entering society. And Harry, despite his good intentions, had not always found trustworthy friends.

“Look at the reputation they’ve made for you here!” Sampson exclaimed, returning from the coffee-house. “Monsieur de Richelieu was nothing compared to you!”

“What do you mean?” asked Harry, puzzled. “I’ve never been to Minorca.”

Sampson explained. The widow Patcham, newly arrived, was clearly smitten. Her behavior at the rooms the night before proved it. Mrs. Hooper was another matter—her husband had already fetched her back to London. The whole place was talking.

“Who says so?” Harry demanded, furious. “I’d like to meet the man who dares say it—and confound him!”

“I wouldn’t show him to you,” Sampson laughed. “It might be the worse for him.”

“It’s a shame,” Harry continued, pacing the room. “To speak so lightly of a man’s or a woman’s character. Do you know, in our country, a fellow’s ears wouldn’t be safe? Just before I left home, three brothers shot a man for speaking ill of their sister.”

“Serve the villain right!” Sampson declared.

“They’ve already started this calumny about me—about me and that poor little French dancer.”

“I’ve heard,” Sampson said, shaking powder from his wig.

“Wicked, wasn’t it?”

“Abominable.”

“They said the same thing about my Lord March. Isn’t it shameful?”

“Indeed it is,” Sampson replied, keeping a solemn face—even though he hadnᵗt truly minded the rumors.

“I don’t know what I’d do if my mother heard these stories. It would break her heart. A military friend of mine, Mr. Wolfe, told me how terrible lies were spread about me. Good heavens! What do they think a gentleman of my name and country could possibly do? That I’m a seducer? They might as well accuse me of stealing horses or breaking houses. If I hear one man say it, I’ll have his ears!”

“I’ve read,” Sampson said, laughing, “that the Grand Seignior of Turkey sometimes receives bushels of ears. If you collected all those who’ve spoken ill of you or others, you’d fill baskets.”

“And so I would,” Harry declared. “Any man who speaks against a lady or a gentleman of honor—I’d punish him. Mark that.”

“I shall be sorry for his sake if you do,” Sampson said. “You’ll mark him in a way he won’t like. And I know you’re a man of your word.”

“You may be sure of that,” Harry said. “Now, shall we go to dinner, and then to Lady Trumpington’s tea?”

“You know, sir,” Sampson replied, “I can’t resist a card or a bottle. Let’s have the last first, and then the first shall come last.” And off they went.

That was an age when wine was drunk freely, especially since General Braddock’s army had returned to England. Harry, having heard that it was a point of honor among officers never to refuse a toast or a challenge, had grown fond of claret and bumpers. So he and Sampson drank deeply, naming a favorite lady with each glass, as was the custom.

Sampson had his reasons for wanting to know how far Harry’s feelings for Lady Maria had gone. And as the claret warmed them, their conversation grew frank, open, sincere—just as good wine often inspires. O noble vines of Aquitaine! O sunlit banks of Garonne! O dark caves of Gledstane and Morol, where the dusky flasks sleep! May we not offer thanks for the joy you bring? Can the Temperance men shout in the streets? Can the Vegetarians cry “Cabbage forever”? Must we not, as modest Enophilists, sing your praises?

For after a generous draught of good Bordeaux, there comes a moment—though not quite a pint—when the soul awakens. Wit sparkles, intellect sharpens, thoughts burst forth like birds from a cage. Kindness floods the heart, and truth stands naked before the world. We rush to aid the poor, defend the wronged. We feel ourselves capable of great deeds. But then—the next glass spoils it. The morning brings a headache. We do not enter Parliament. We do not shoot French officers who insult our country. And Jeremy Diddler knocks at eleven, asking for half a sovereign, while we lie ill in bed, unable to answer.

Yet in that golden hour, as they sat over their cups, the company gone, the bottle brought in by Monsieur Barbeau, Sampson found himself eloquent, eager to impart wisdom. And Harry, moved by emotion and wine, longed to confess everything—to tell his new friend all he felt.

“I am glad to hear how you’ve behaved with Cattarina,” Sampson said. “Truly glad. The wine is with you. You’ve shown you can resist temptation, bear down calumny. Ah, my dear sir, not all men are so fortunate. What a fine wine this is!” He sipped deeply. “A toast from you, if you please?”

“Miss Fanny Mountain, of Virginia,” Harry said, filling a bumper. His thoughts flew straight to home.

“One of your American conquests, I suppose?” Sampson teased.

“Nay,” Harry replied. “She’s ten years old. And I’ve never made any conquests in Virginia, Mr. Sampson.”

“You are like a true gentleman,” Sampson said. “You don’t kiss and tell.”

“I neither kiss nor tell. It isn’t our custom to ruin girls or consort with low women. We Virginian gentlemen honor women. We don’t wish to bring them to shame,” Harry declared, proud and handsome. “That girl I mentioned has lived in our family since childhood. I’d shoot any man who harmed her—by Heaven, I would!”

“Your sentiments do you honor!” Sampson cried, grasping his hand. “Let me shake hands with you—not as a retainer to a patron, but as a true friend. With wine like this, all men are equal. Faith, all men are rich—for a time! And Tom Sampson is as wealthy with his bottle as you are with your acres!”

“Let us have another bottle of riches,” Harry laughed. “Encore du cachet jaune, mon bon Monsieur Barbeau!” And out went Monsieur Barbeau.

“Another bottle of riches! Capital! How beautifully you speak French, Mr. Harry!”

“I do speak it well,” Harry said. “At least, Monsieur Barbeau understands me.”

“You do everything well,” Sampson said. “That’s why they say you’ve won so many hearts.”

“There you go again with the women!” Harry snapped. “I hate these stories. Confound me, Sampson, why must a gentleman’s character be blackened so?”

“Well, at any rate,” Sampson said, “there is one—unless my eyes deceive me—whose heart seems to belong to you.”

“Whom do you mean?” Harry asked, flushing.

“Nay, I name no names. It’s not for a poor chaplain to meddle with his betters’ affairs.”

“Thoughts! What thoughts, Sampson?”

“I fancied I saw a preference from a certain lovely lady at Castlewood. And a strong liking from a certain distinguished young gentleman. But I may be wrong. Pardon me.”

“Oh, Sampson!” Harry broke out. “I tell you I’m miserable. I’ve longed to confide in someone. You know, then, that something has been going on—between me and—help, Monsieur Barbeau—and—someone else?”

“I’ve watched it this past month,” Sampson said quietly.

“Confound you, sir! Are you saying you’ve been spying on me?”

“A spy? You made no effort to hide it, Mr. Warrington. Nor did she. You were always together—in the shrubberies, the walks, the village, the galleries. Plenty of eyes saw you.”

“Gracious powers! What did you see, Sampson?”

“Nay, sir,” the chaplain said, smiling. “It’s forbidden to kiss and tell. I say so again.”

Harry turned crimson. “Oh, Sampson,” he whispered. “Can I—can I confide in you?”

“Dearest sir,” Sampson said, squeezing his hand, “you know I would shed my heart’s blood for you.”
