“I will not interrupt you,” Ethne said, and Captain Willoughby—settling beside her on the bench where the hedge made a green alcove against the lawn—had scarcely begun before she broke her vow as naturally as one breaks bread.

“I am Deputy-Governor of Suakin,” he started.

“How did he look?” she cut in at once, as if the name had been a spice thrown on the coals and the smoke had stung her eyes.

Willoughby’s brows pinched together. He stared, hunting for a detail as a man hunts for a lost coin. “Really, I do not know. Much like other men, I suppose, who have been a year or two in the Soudan—overtrained a little, that sort of thing.”

Never mind, she told herself—and the disappointment tasted like cold tea. For five years she had lived on scraps: a rumour, a name half-mispronounced, the silence between letters. She wanted the smallest homely particulars—whether the sun had bitten his skin darker, whether his mouth still set itself in that old obstinate line, whether his hands, which once had been idle at a dance, had grown rough with work. But she glanced at Willoughby’s honest, obtuse face and knew she would get none of it.

“I beg your pardon,” she said evenly. “Go on.”

“I was sitting in the verandah on the first floor of the palace about ten o’clock at night,” Willoughby resumed, “looking out over the harbour and the distillation works, and wondering whether it was worth while to go to bed at all. You are fortunate enough not to know Suakin in May, Miss Eustace. No white woman can live in that town. It has a sodden, intolerable heat peculiar to itself. The air is heavy with brine; you can’t sleep for its oppression. Well—then a servant told me that a man, who refused to give his name, wished particularly to see me. The man was Feversham. There was only a lamp burning in the verandah, and the night was dark, so that I did not recognise him until he was close to me.”

Ethne’s fingers tightened around the white feather in her lap. It felt different in daylight than it must have under that lamp at Suakin: here, soft and almost frivolous; there, a small pale thing that might catch the lamplight like a knife-edge. She waited, listening not only to Willoughby’s words but to the garden’s own sound—wind through leaves, a far clink from the house, a thrush’s note dropped cleanly into the hush. The quiet made the story clearer, as though silence were a broth in which meaning steeped.

“I asked him what he wanted,” Willoughby went on, “and why he had not sent in his name. ‘You would not have seen me if I had,’ he replied, and he drew a packet of letters out of his pocket. Those letters had been written a long while ago by General Gordon in Khartum. They had been carried down the Nile as far as Berber. But the day after they reached Berber, that town surrendered to the Mahdists. Abou Fatma, the messenger, hid them in the wall of the house of an Arab called Yusef, who sold rock-salt in the market-place. Abou was thrown into prison on suspicion, and escaped to Suakin. The letters remained hidden in that wall until Feversham recovered them. I looked over them and saw they were of no value, and I asked him bluntly why he—who had not dared to accompany his regiment—had risked death and torture to get them back.”

Ethne did not miss the harshness. She heard it as one hears a dish judged by a palate that has never learnt the taste of hunger. Yet she sat still, because what mattered was not Willoughby’s tone but the chain of acts—step after step, as methodical as kneading, as stubborn as tending a slow fire.

On that hot verandah with the Red Sea breathing salt into the night, Harry Feversham had told his story “quietly and without exaggeration.” Willoughby, who could not paint a man’s face, could at least remember the skeleton of deeds.

Feversham had fallen in with Abou Fatma at Suakin. Together they planned the recovery of the letters. They travelled as far as Obak; then, since Abou dared not go farther, Feversham drove his grey donkey on alone to Berber.

“He did not even conceal an access of panic,” Willoughby said, “when he first saw the low brown walls and the towering date palms beyond. He told me how it loosened his joints—set him running and leaping across the empty desert like a marrowless thing of fear.”

Ethne could supply what Willoughby did not know he was leaving out: how a man might crouch on hot sand with his coat drawn over his head, and in that suffocating darkness summon—by sheer will—some remembered face, some domestic softness, like drawing water from a deep well with a rope that cuts the hands. She could imagine him doing it, because she had done the same, only with different deserts.

“He went down into Berber at the setting of the sun,” Willoughby said.

Ethne breathed out, and relief loosened her mouth into a smile before she could stop it. “He went down into Berber,” she repeated under her breath, as if repeating it kept it true.

“And found the old town destroyed by order of the Emir,” Willoughby continued, “and a new one building on its southern confines. All the landmarks by which he was to know the house where the letters were hidden were gone. The roofs torn off, houses dismantled, front walls carried away—narrow alleys of crumbling fives-courts, crossing this way and that and gaping to the stars. Here and there a broken tower—the remnant of a rich man’s house. But of any sign that could tell a man where the hut of Yusef, once a seller of rock-salt in the market-place, had stood—no hope in those acres of mud. The foxes had already made their burrows there.”

Her smile died; then she looked down at the feather again. It lay dusty, yellowed with desert grit, a ridiculous little thing to carry such weight. And yet because it existed—because it had travelled back—it promised there would be no word of failure. She laughed softly, a laugh of pure relief, as if tasting water after long thirst.

“Go on,” she said.

Willoughby told how Feversham despatched the negro with the donkey back to Abou Fatma at the Wells of Obak.

“He stayed for a fortnight in Berber,” Willoughby said. “A week during which he came every morning to the well and waited for the return of his negro from Obak, and a week during which the negro searched for Yusef. I doubt, Miss Eustace, if you can realise what that fortnight must have meant—the anxiety, the continued expectation that a voice would bid him halt and a hand fall upon his shoulder. And if the hand fell—death would be the least part of his penalty.”

Willoughby tried to sketch the town: low houses, broad streets of sand, pits dug for mud to build again, overhead a blistering, shadowless sky. A crowd all day, jostling and shouting—crowded deliberately so all might be watched and each man become his neighbour’s spy. Feversham buying food at booths and fearing talk; refusing any roof at night because he dared not trust his tongue; sleeping in the ruined old town among roofless courts. He could not even loiter to listen for a name, lest idle men draw him into speech. In daylight he could not prowl the ruins. From sunrise to sunset he must walk quickly up and down the public streets like a man with urgent business.

Ethne, listening, heard more than he said: the constant rasp of sandals on sand, the bark of voices, the far thump of drums, the thin metallic clatter of some new tool in the new town. Even now, in this English garden where leaves whispered properly and birds made music for no pay, the imagined noise of that other place seemed to press on her skull. She thought—bitterly—of what modern towns were becoming: engines and wheels and endless clamour, and how sound itself could be used like a lash. In Berber the noise had been policy, not accident.

Willoughby paused, struggling with his memory. “In front of us was the quiet harbour and the Red Sea; above us the African stars. Feversham spoke in the quietest manner possible, but with a peculiar deliberation—and with his eyes fixed upon my face, as though forcing me to feel with him. Even when he lighted his cigar he did not avert his eyes. By that time I had given him a cigar and offered him a chair. I had, I assure you. It was the first time in four years he had sat with one of his equals—indeed with any of his countrymen—on a footing of equality. He told me so.”

Ethne felt a pang at that: the chair, the cigar, the small ceremonial of fellowship—as intimate, in its way, as being offered bread. How much of a man’s life could be measured by such small permissions.

“Well,” Willoughby went on at last, “after he had skulked for a fortnight, the negro discovered Yusef—no longer selling salt, but tending a small plantation of dhurra on the river’s edge. From Yusef, Feversham obtained particulars enough to guide him to the house where the letters were concealed in the inner wall. But Yusef was no longer to be trusted. Possibly Feversham’s accent betrayed him. More likely Yusef took him for a spy, and thought it wise to confess to Mohammed-el-Kheir, the Emir, his own share in the concealment. That is conjecture. The important fact is this: on the same night Feversham went alone to old Berber.”

“Alone,” Ethne repeated, and the word came out sharper than she intended, like a knife struck on bone.

“Yes. He found the house fronting a narrow alley, sixth of the row. The front wall was destroyed, but the side walls and the back still stood. Three feet from the floor and two feet from the right-hand corner the letters were hidden in the inner wall. Feversham dug into the mud bricks with his knife—made a hole where he could slip his hand. The wall was thick; he dug deep, stopping now and again to feel for the packet. At last his fingers clasped it and drew it out; and as he hid it in a fold of his jibbeh, the light of a lantern shone upon him from behind.”

Ethne started as if the lantern had flared in the hedge-gap before her. She saw it all—the roofless alleys, the dead silence beneath the stars, the distant glow and noise of the new town. Harry with the packet in his hand, the taste of dust in his mouth, the scent of old mud warmed by day’s sun turning sharp and sour in the night air. And then that sudden spear of light.

“Well?” she forced out.

“He was standing with his face to the wall,” Willoughby said. “The light came from the alley behind him. He did not turn, but out of the corner of his eye he could see a fold of a white robe hanging motionless. He secured the packet with a care and composure which astonished him even then. The shock strung him to a concentration he had never known. His fingers trembled, he said—trembled with excitement, not fear; his mind worked rapidly but coolly. He came to a definite conclusion of what he must do. Every faculty was extraordinarily clear and extraordinarily still.

“He had his knife in his hand, faced about suddenly, and ran. Two men were waiting. He ran at the man with the lantern. He was aware of the point of a spear—ducked and beat it aside with his left arm—leaped forward and struck with his right. The Arab fell at his feet; the lantern was extinguished. Feversham sprang across the white-robed body and ran eastward toward the open desert. But not in panic—he had never been so collected. He was followed by the second soldier. He had foreseen it. If he was to escape, it was necessary. He turned a corner, crouched behind a wall, and as the Arab came running by he leaped out upon his shoulders. And again, as he leaped, he struck.”

Willoughby stopped and turned toward Ethne with a puzzled earnestness, as though he had found a spice in a dish he could not name.

“The strangest feature of those few fierce minutes,” he said, “was that Feversham felt no fear. From the first moment when the lantern shone, to the last when he ran through the ruins toward the desert and the Wells of Obak—he felt no fear. I don’t understand that. Do you?”

Ethne looked down at the feather until its barbs blurred. Willoughby, practical and stubborn, could not imagine the kind of terror that lives for years in a man’s blood—not the sharp terror of a spear-point in an alley, but the slow fear of disgrace, the dread of what one might do, of what one might be, of how one’s own name might taste in another man’s mouth. That fear was like a language learned in childhood and never forgotten; it shaped the world, cut its edges, made certain sounds unbearable.

“Yes,” she said, and her voice came out low. “I understand.”

She saw him again in the little room off the hall at Lennon House, speaking of his mother, of his father’s cold gulf, of the way fear had haunted his nights and disfigured the day. She had been too sure of her own words then, too proud of the clean, dominant meanings of them—coward, honour—as though those words translated perfectly from one soul to another. They did not. They never had.

“It was the possibility of cowardice he shrank from,” she said, “not the possibility of hurt.” Her throat tightened. “If only one had been a little older—less narrow. I should have listened. I should have understood. At all events I should not, I think, have been cruel.”

The fourth feather—her own—burned against her palm as though it had a hidden ember.

Willoughby, however, would not bend even here. “Yes, but these fine distinctions are too elusive for practical purposes,” he argued. “You can’t run the world on fine distinctions. I cannot bring myself to believe that we three men were at all to blame—and if we were not, you of all people can have no reason for self-reproach.”

The complacency in him, after what she had just heard, roused anger like a sudden bitter taste. She looked him over, from his clean boots to his stubborn jaw, and saw stamped on him an obstinate stupidity. How dare he sit in judgment on the meanest of his fellows, let alone Harry Feversham? And in the same instant she remembered that she herself had endorsed that judgment. Shame tingled through her blood. She kept her mouth shut, watching him from the corners of her eyes, waiting almost eagerly for him to say anything that would justify a savage retort.

But he said nothing. Ethne broke the silence herself.

“Which of you three first thought of sending the feathers?” she asked.

“Not you?”

“No. I think it was Trench.”

“Trench,” Ethne repeated, striking her clenched hand—feather and all—into the palm of the other. “I will remember that name.”

“But I share his responsibility,” Willoughby said sturdily. “I do not shrink from it. I regret we caused you pain and annoyance, but I do not acknowledge any mistake. I take my feather back now, and I annul my accusation. But that is your doing.”

“Mine?” She turned her head. “What do you mean?”

“A man may live in the Soudan and yet not be wholly ignorant of women,” he said, and smiled as if he were paying her a compliment. “And of their great quality of forgiveness. You gave the feathers back to Feversham in order that he might redeem his honour. That is evident.”

Ethne sprang to her feet before he had finished. She stood very still a little in front of him, her face turned away. In his ignorance he had struck true, not by thought but by chance: he had pointed out a way she might have taken and had not. Remorse, sharp as vinegar, told her it was the way she ought to have taken.

But she would not accept the comfort of that misconception. She had failed him then; she would not steal credit now.

“Repeat what you said,” she asked without turning. “And ever so slowly, please.”

“You gave the feathers back into Feversham’s hand—” Willoughby began.

“He told you that himself?”

“Yes—” Willoughby answered, surprised, and continued, “in order that he might by subsequent bravery compel the men who sent them to take them back, and so redeem his honour.”

“He did not tell you that?”

“No. I guessed it.” Willoughby leaned forward, pleased with his own penetration. “His disgrace was—on the face of it—impossible to retrieve. The opportunity might never have occurred. As it happened, he waited three years in the bazaar at Suakin before it did. No, Miss Eustace, it needed a woman’s faith to conceive that plan—a woman’s encouragement to keep the man who undertook it to his work.”

Ethne laughed and turned back, and for the first time since he arrived Willoughby saw something in her face that made him forget himself. Her pride—strangely—did not harden her; it made her tender, lit her eyes, warmed her mouth, as though the knowledge of another’s courage had fed her like bread.

“Yes,” he cried, carried away, “you were the woman to plan this redemption.”

She laughed again, more happily than he could understand. “Did he tell you of a fourth white feather?”

“No.”

“I shall tell you the truth.” She sat down again, very straight. “The plan was of his devising from first to last. Nor did I encourage him, for until to-day I never heard a word of it. Since the night of that dance in Donegal I have had no message from Mr. Feversham, and no news of him.”

Willoughby’s face altered a little; he had not expected that.

“I told him to take up those three feathers because they were his,” she continued, and each sentence came as if she were placing a dish on the table and naming it plainly, without garnish, because garnish would be a lie. “And I wished to show him that I agreed with the accusations of which they were the symbols. That seems cruel? But I did more. I snapped a fourth white feather from my fan and gave him that to carry away too.”

Her voice thinned on the edge of old pain, but she held it steady.

“I wanted to make an end for ever and ever—not only of my acquaintanceship with him, but of every kindly thought he might keep of me, of every kindly thought I might keep of him. I wanted to be sure myself, and I wanted him to be sure, that we should always be strangers now and—and afterwards,” and the last words fell into a whisper, as if she spoke them in a dialect too private for the air.

Willoughby did not understand. Perhaps only Harry Feversham, and Lieutenant Sutch, would have known what “afterwards” meant in her mouth.

“I was sad and sorry enough when I had done it,” she said. “Indeed, I think I have always been sorry since. I think I have never at any minute during these five years quite forgotten that fourth feather and the quiet dignity with which he took it. But to-day I am glad.”

Her voice, though low, was rich with pride.

“Oh, very glad. For this was his thought—his deed. They are all his, as I would have them be. I had no share, and of that I am proud. He needed no woman’s faith, no woman’s encouragement.”

“Yet he sent this back to you,” Willoughby said, pointing, perplexed, to the feather in her hand.

“Yes,” she answered simply. “He knew that I should be glad to know.”

And suddenly she drew the feather to her breast, pressing it there as though it were not a token but a living thing whose warmth might be returned. For a while she sat so, eyes shining, the garden’s soft sounds gathering around her like a shawl.

Then Willoughby rose abruptly and pointed toward the gap in the hedge by which they had entered the enclosure.

“By Jove! Jack Durrance,” he exclaimed.

Durrance was standing in the gap, which was the only way in or out.
