The winter of '59 hung heavy over Castlewood, its chill seeping into the bones of those who dwelled within its walls. In the great hall, a fire crackled, casting flickering shadows on the faces of the assembled guests. Will Esmond stood apart, nursing a mug of ale, his eyes fixed on the newlyweds. His brother's union with the colonial heiress had been a bitter pill to swallow, and Will's resentment simmered like the embers of the fire.

As the evening wore on, Will's discontent grew. He spoke little, his words laced with venom, as he toasted the young bride in a glass raised high. His gaze drifted to the Dissenting clergyman, who had once spoken words of caution to his brother, warning him of the dangers of this marriage. Now, the old man's eyes seemed to hold a mixture of disappointment and understanding, a recognition that Will's anger was rooted in more than mere disappointment.

The lawyer, seated to one side, spoke in hushed tones, his words a litany of complaint against the young American woman. He spoke of her supposed deceit, of the fabricated tales she had spun to ensnare his brother. Will listened intently, his expression a mask of calm, yet his mind racing with thoughts of betrayal and loss.

As the night wore on, the conversation turned to the young couple's future. George Warrington, Will's cousin and confidant, offered words of counsel, urging Will to let go of his anger and accept the situation. But Will's wounds ran too deep, and he found himself drawn back to the memory of what could have been. He recalled the words of the little Cerbera, the way she had teased and tantalized him, leading him further into the web of deceit that had ensnared his brother.

In the silence that followed, Will's thoughts turned to the dark corners of his own heart. He saw himself reflected in the mirror of his brother's actions, a reflection he did not care to acknowledge. The fire crackled on, casting its warm glow over the assembly, but Will felt the coldness of his own isolation, a chill that seemed to seep from the very marrow of his bones.
