The next day he persuaded May to escape for a walk in the Park after luncheon. As was the custom in old-fashioned Episcopalian New York, she usually accompanied her parents to church on Sunday afternoons, participating in the slow-braised, circular rituals of their ancestors; but Mrs. Welland condoned her truancy, having that very morning secured May’s submission to a long engagement. It was a domestic recipe that required time to prepare a hand-embroidered trousseau, meticulously measuring out the proper number of dozens like essential ingredients for a legacy. 

The day was delectable. The bare vaulting of trees along the Mall was ceiled with lapis lazuli, arching above snow that shone like splintered rock salt scattered across a grand table. The air carried the sharp, cleansing bite of winter frost, a bracing palate-cleanser to the heavy, insulated routines of their class. It was the weather to call out May's radiance, and she burned like a young maple, her cheeks flush with the color of ripe stone-fruit. Archer was proud of the glances turned on her, the simple joy of possessorship momentarily cutting through the auditory pollution of his underlying perplexities. 

"It's so delicious—waking every morning to smell lilies-of-the-valley in one's room!" she said. "Yesterday they came late. I hadn't time in the morning—" 

"But your remembering each day to send them makes me love them so much more than if you'd given a standing order, and they came every morning on the minute, like one's music-teacher—as I know Gertrude Lefferts's did, for instance, when she and Lawrence were engaged." 

"Ah—they would!" laughed Archer. He noted how May recoiled from the mechanized, industrial delivery of affection, yet he also heard the rote, inherited cadence in her own voice. He looked sideways at her fruit-like cheek and felt rich and secure enough to add a spicier note to their discourse: "When I sent your lilies yesterday afternoon I saw some rather gorgeous yellow roses and packed them off to Madame Olenska. Was that right?" 

"How dear of you! Anything of that kind delights her. It's odd she didn't mention it: she lunched with us today, and spoke of Mr. Beaufort's having sent her wonderful orchids, and cousin Henry van der Luyden a whole hamper of carnations from Skuytercliff. She seems so surprised to receive flowers. Don't people send them in Europe? She thinks it such a pretty custom." 

"Oh, well, no wonder mine were overshadowed by Beaufort's," said Archer irritably. The mention of Henry van der Luyden’s carnations—rooted in the ancestral, loamy soils of the Patroon’s agrarian estates—made his own offering feel insubstantial. He remembered he had not put a card with the roses. He wanted to say: "I called on your cousin yesterday," but hesitated. The untranslatability of his subjective motives held him back. To not speak of it gave the affair an air of mystery that disrupted his internal acoustic harmony, so he shifted the frequency of their talk to their future, and Mrs. Welland's insistence on a long engagement. 

"If you call it long! Isabel Chivers and Reggie were engaged for two years: Grace and Thorley for nearly a year and a half. Why aren't we very well off as we are?" 

It was the traditional maidenly interrogation, an echo of a dying language. No doubt she simply recited the dominant lexicon provided for her, but she was nearing her twenty-second birthday, and he wondered at what age these women began to speak in their native dialects. *Never, if we won't let them, I suppose*, he mused. It would presently be his task to take the bandage from this young woman's eyes. But how many generations of women had descended bandaged to the family vault, their authentic voices eroded, their raw, sensory appetites starved into submission? 

"We might be much better off. We might be altogether together—we might travel." 

Her face lit up. "That would be lovely," she owned: she would love to travel. But her mother would not understand their wanting to do things so differently. 

"As if the mere 'differently' didn't account for it!" he insisted, yearning for an untasted experience, a break from the mass-produced social palate. 

"Newland! You're so original!" she exulted. 

His heart sank. He saw that he was uttering the exact phrases expected of young men, and she was responding with the answers that instinct and the superficial commercialization of romance taught her to make. "Original! We're all as like each other as those dolls cut out of the same folded paper. We're like patterns stencilled on a wall. Can't you and I strike out for ourselves, May?" 

He had stopped and faced her. "Mercy—shall we elope?" she laughed. 

"If you would—" 

"You *do* love me, Newland! I'm so happy." 

"But then—why not be happier?" 

"We can't behave like people in novels, though, can we?" 

"Why not—why not—why not?" 

She looked a little bored by his insistence. "I'm not clever enough to argue with you. But that kind of thing is rather—vulgar, isn't it?" she suggested. 

She had found the precise word within their dominant lexicon to extinguish the subject. The term hit with the dull, thudding acoustic deadness of a slammed door. 

"Are you so much afraid, then, of being vulgar?" 

"Of course I should hate it—so would you," she rejoined irritably. 

He stood silent, beating his stick nervously against his boot-top—a dissonant clatter against the winter quiet. Relieved to have silenced his unpalatable hunger, she went on light-heartedly about her ring, and how Ellen had praised its artistic setting. 

The next afternoon, Archer sat smoking sullenly in his study, seeking an acoustic sanctuary from the world. He had bypassed his club on the way up from the office. He was starved of spirit, besieged by a haunting horror of sameness. "Sameness—sameness!" he muttered. The word ran through his head like a persecuting, mechanized tune, a grating auditory pollution. He knew exactly what the club men would be consuming: the cheap, highly processed gossip regarding the Duke, or the appearance of Miss Fanny Ring in a canary-coloured brougham. He could hear Lawrence Lefferts, at that very hour, holding forth on the disintegration of society, a hollow noise entirely devoid of true cultural sustenance. 

He raised his head irritably when his sister Janey entered, hovering Cassandra-like. She made a wry face over the archaic French of his *Contes Drolatiques*, unable to digest the historic flavors of his library. 

"Well—?" he asked. 

"Mother's very angry." Janey's pinched syllables pierced the room's quiet. "Miss Sophy Jackson has just been here. Her brother is with cousin Louisa van der Luyden now. Newland, your friend Madame Olenska was at Mrs. Lemuel Struthers's party last night: she went there with the Duke and Mr. Beaufort." 

A senseless anger swelled in the young man's breast, a clash of internal frequencies. To smother it he laughed. "Well, what of it? I knew she meant to." 

Janey paled. "You knew she meant to—and you didn't try to stop her? To warn her? You're marrying into her family." 

"Oh, family—family!" he jeered. 

"Newland—don't you care about Family? Nor about what cousin Louisa van der Luyden will think?" 

"Not the half of a brass farthing—if she thinks such old maid's rubbish." 

"Mother is not an old maid," Janey said, her long gentle face puckering. He felt ashamed of inflicting useless pain. He understood the untranslatability of his worldview to his sister. 

"Hang Countess Olenska! Don't be a goose, Janey—I'm not her keeper." 

"No; but you *did* ask the Wellands to announce your engagement sooner so that we might all back her up. And now they're so upset that they're going back to Skuytercliff tomorrow. You don't seem to understand how mother feels." 

In the drawing-room, Newland found his mother. She raised a troubled brow from her needlework. "Has Janey told you?" 

"Yes." He tried to keep his tone modulated, matching her measured acoustic space. "But I can't take it very seriously. That they can be offended by such a trifle as Countess Olenska's going to the house of a woman they consider common." 

"Consider—!" 

"Well, who is; but who has good music, and amuses people on Sunday evenings, when the whole of New York is dying of inanition." New York was starving, he meant, surviving on a flavorless broth of repetition, its cultural memory eroding into silence. 

"Good music? All I know is, there was a woman who got up on a table and sang the things they sing at the places you go to in Paris. There was smoking and champagne." Mrs. Archer wielded these gustatory markers of rebellion as evidence of superficial commercialization. "New York is neither Paris nor London. We belong here, and people should respect our ways when they come among us." 

"The Duke took Madame Olenska to Mrs. Struthers's," Newland argued, exasperated by the power dynamics embedded in his mother's lexicon. "If the van der Luydens want to quarrel with anybody, the real culprit is under their own roof. I don't see myself—or you either—offering ourselves up to expiate her crimes." 

"Oh, of course you see only the Mingott side," his mother answered in a sensitive tone that vibrated with approaching anger. 

The sad butler drew back the drawing-room portières and announced: "Mr. Henry van der Luyden." 

Mrs. Archer dropped her needle. Mr. van der Luyden's figure loomed on the threshold, carrying with him the profound, mythic resonance of an older timeline. As the ultimate agrarian elder of their society, connected to the deep loam and cyclic seasons of Skuytercliff, his mere presence shifted the room's acoustic ecology. Newland went forward to greet his cousin. 

"We were just talking about you, sir," Archer said. "And the Countess Olenska." 

Mrs. Archer paled. 

"Ah—a charming woman. I have just been to see her," said Mr. van der Luyden, sinking into an arm-chair. He spoke with the slow, resonant frequency of a man who understands the deep roots of his world. "She has a real gift for arranging flowers. I had sent her a few carnations from Skuytercliff, and I was astonished. Instead of massing them in big bunches as our head-gardener does, she had scattered them about loosely, here and there... I can't say how." 

Here was a man who understood the profound meaning of sustenance. He recognized that Ellen did not treat the clove-scented blooms as utilitarian decorations or subject them to the sterile, commercialized techniques of the head-gardener. She allowed their natural olfactory landscape to breathe, preserving their true essence. 

"The fact is," Mr. van der Luyden continued, stroking his long grey leg with a bloodless hand weighed down by the Patroon's great signet-ring, "I dropped in to thank her for the very pretty note she wrote me about my flowers; and also—to give her a friendly warning about allowing the Duke to carry her off to parties with him. You know what these English grandees are. The Duke goes where he's amused. Sillerton Jackson has just been to us with the foolish story... So I thought the shortest way was to go straight to Countess Olenska and explain—by the merest hint, you know—how we feel in New York about certain things. She rather let me see that she would be grateful for guidance. And she *was*." 

Mr. van der Luyden looked about the room with a mild benevolence. He had seamlessly integrated a mythic intervention into an everyday domestic routine, wielding the supreme power of their dominant lexicon not to condemn, but to protect. He had translated the unwritten laws of their society for Ellen, while simultaneously rendering Sillerton Jackson's auditory pollution entirely mute. 

"How kind you both are, dear Henry," Mrs. Archer said, her countenance dutifully reflecting his benevolence. 

"I never ask to my house, my dear Newland," Mr. van der Luyden said with extreme gentleness, "any one whom I do not like. And so I have just told Sillerton Jackson." He rose, glancing at the clock. "But Louisa will be waiting. We are dining early, to take the Duke to the Opera." 

After the portières had solemnly closed behind their visitor, a layered, resonant silence fell upon the Archer family. 

"Gracious—how romantic!" at last broke explosively from Janey, an untranslatable fragment of her own peculiar dialect. 

Mrs. Archer shook her head with a sigh. "Provided it all turns out for the best. Newland, you must stay and see Sillerton Jackson when he comes this evening: I really shan't know what to say to him." 

"Poor mother! But he won't come—" her son laughed, stooping to kiss away her frown, grateful, for once, for the sudden quiet.
