The letter Sir Hugo placed in Deronda’s hands bore a weight of unspoken history, its words sparse yet heavy with implication:

> *To my son, Daniel Deronda,*
>
> *Sir Hugo Mallinger, a friend to us both, will have informed you of my wish to see you. My health falters, and I desire no delay in delivering what I have long withheld. Come to the Albergo dell’Italia in Genoa by the fourteenth of this month. Await me there. My arrival from Spezia is uncertain—circumstances dictate it. Wait for me.*
>
> *—The Princess Halm-Eberstein.*
>
> *P.S. Bring the diamond ring Sir Hugo gave you. I should like to see it again.*

The letter offered no clarity, only a summons. Deronda, accustomed to the shadows of his own origins, felt neither eagerness nor dread, only resolve. Speculation was futile; he would meet the truth as it came.

Yet his thoughts could not help but drift—toward Mordecai, whose fervent hopes might soon be dashed or exalted, and toward Mirah, whose gentle presence had already woven itself into the fabric of his life. He spoke vaguely of his departure, masking its significance behind Sir Hugo’s supposed wishes.

*"I will ask for young Jacob to stay with me,"* Mordecai said, seeking solace in the child’s company.

*"Mrs. Cohen will not refuse,"* Mirah replied warmly.

Deronda smiled. *"You were both wrong—she welcomed you after all."*

*"I underestimated her heart,"* Mordecai admitted. *"She finds joy in another’s bloom, even as her own fades."*

Mirah’s laughter was light, but a shadow crossed her face when Deronda teased, *"What if Ezra had been your brother?"*

*"He is not unkind,"* she murmured, though her blush betrayed deeper wounds—the specter of a father lost, a past still haunting.

Deronda understood. His own heart was divided. The letter had made his mother real, yet distant. The tender yearning he had nurtured for an unknown parent now cooled into wary neutrality. Who was this woman who had withheld herself so long?

Before leaving, Sir Hugo clasped his hand. *"Whatever comes, you’ve been like a son to me. I’d have preferred you master of the Abbey, but fate had other plans."* The baronet’s voice wavered—rare for him.

Genoa greeted Deronda with sun and salt air, but no princess. Days stretched into weeks, marked only by a second letter: *"Wait. My arrival is uncertain."*

Restlessness took hold. He wandered the city, its history pressing upon him—the exiled Jews of centuries past, starving on these very quays. Their sorrows felt intertwined with his own unanswered questions. Was Mordecai right? Did his blood tie him to that suffering lineage? He dared not hope, yet could not stifle the quiet pull of kinship.

And always, Gwendolen lingered in his thoughts—not with passion, but with a protective ache. *"I should have loved her, if—"* The *if* was a barrier, a law written in regret. Her pleas—*"Do not forsake me"*—echoed like a cry in the dark.

The days blurred. The city thrived around him—mule bells, evening songs, moonlight on palace walls—while he remained suspended, a man awaiting judgment.

Then, at last, the summons came. A servant in livery announced: *"The Princess Halm-Eberstein has arrived. She will receive you at seven."*

The hour was upon him.
