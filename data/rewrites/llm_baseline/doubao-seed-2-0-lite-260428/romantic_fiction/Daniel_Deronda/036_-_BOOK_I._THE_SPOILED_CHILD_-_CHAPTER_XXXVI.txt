*“Nothing weighs so heavy as a secret: the carrying of it far beyond reach is a hardship few ladies can bear, and for that matter, I know full well a great many men who turn as soft and yielding as women in the face of it.” — LA FONTAINE*

Deronda had been tugged away from the others by Mr. Vandernoodt, who craved a brisker walk, a lit cigar, and the kind of unguarded gossip no one could say to a man whose secrets they might accidentally brush against. When they were far enough from the house party’s clusters of guests, Vandernoodt huffed out a laugh around his cigar.
“Grandcourt’s the palest, most washed-out scrap of cambric I’ve ever laid eyes on. If he’s a favorite of yours, I’ll take the remark back at once.”
“Not the slightest,” Deronda assured him.
“I thought not. One can’t help wondering how he worked himself up to a great passion all over again—he must have felt something fierce, to marry a girl out of the blue like that. Though Lush, his old college chum, hints he did it purely out of stubborn obstinacy. By George, that obstinacy is far more accountable than people think. No man would need anyone egging him on to decide he wanted to marry her. But he must have drained his purse pretty dry, eh?”
“I know nothing of his affairs,” Deronda said evenly.
“What! Not about his other establishment?”
“Diplow? Of course. He took that of Sir Hugo’s, but only for the year.”
“No, no—Gadsmere. Sir Hugo knows all about it, I’d stake my life on that.”
Deronda said nothing. He was already curious, and knew full well Vandernoodt would spill the whole story without him stooping to ask.
“Lush won’t admit it outright, of course—he’s Grandcourt’s go-between, his confidant. But I’ve it on the best authority. There’s a lady living at Gadsmere with four children. She’s had Grandcourt under her thumb for ten years and more. Left her own husband for him, traveled everywhere at his side, and her husband’s dead now. I met a man who served in the same regiment as him, who knew this Mrs. Glasher back before she ran off—fiery, dark-eyed, a famous beauty in her day. He thought she was long dead. They say she still has him wrapped around her finger. It’s a wonder he never married her, what with a fine young son to carry his name, and full control over his estates to do whatever he pleases. Lush told me as much himself.”
“What right had he to marry this girl?” Deronda said, sharp with disgust.
Vandernoodt puffed his cigar, shrugged, and pursed his lips.
“She can know nothing of it,” Deronda said firmly—then immediately asked himself, quiet and cold: Could she have known?
“It’s a delightfully piquant little scene,” Vandernoodt went on. “Grandcourt caught between two fiery women. Depend on it, that fair-haired new bride of his has got plenty of devil in her. I thought as much back at Leubronn. It’s a Medea and Creusa sort of story. Fancy the two of them meeting! Grandcourt’s a new kind of Jason, and I can’t wait to see what fool’s part he stumbles into. I can almost hear Ristori wailing out ‘Jasone! Jasone!’ These fierce, brilliant women always get hold of a stick to beat a man like that with.”
“Grandcourt can bite, though. He’s no stick,” Deronda said.
“No, no, I meant Jason. I can’t quite puzzle Grandcourt out, but he’s sharp as a tack, well-built too. If he comes into all that property, the estates will stretch far enough for both households. This girl, whose family was down to begging, I hear, should think herself lucky to land a man like him. I don’t mean to be hard on a man tangled in an old affair of that sort—but he might make himself a little more agreeable. I was telling him a wonderful story last night, and he just got up and walked away mid-sentence. I nearly kicked him. D’you think that’s inattention, or pure insolence?”
“Both, likely. He goes through the motions of politeness, but he never really listens,” Deronda said. He paused a beat, then added, “I’d wager there’s some exaggeration in that story about the lady at Gadsmere.”
“Not a bit, depend on it. It’s all lain quiet for years, people forgot all about it. But the nest is there, and the birds are all settled inside. Grandcourt drives out there regularly, I’ve got solid proof of it. Still, it’s nobody’s business but his own. The whole affair sank under the surface long ago.”
“I wonder how you dug up so much of it,” Deronda said, a little drily.
“Oh, there are dozens of people who know every detail. But old gossip gets packed away like forgotten letters, doesn’t it? I love that stuff. I don’t care for the dusty old scandals of Semiramis or Nitocris that all the stuffy historians dig up, the ones poets write a thousand verses about. What do I care about the faux pas of mummies? You’re the one who likes all that old history, aren’t you—more interested in a lady when she’s got rags for a face and skeleton toes peeking out? Flatter your imagination, does it?”
“Well, if she suffered over love, at least you can take comfort in knowing she’s well out of it now,” Deronda said.
“Ah, you’re thinking of Medea, I see.”
Deronda turned to point out a stand of gnarled, bare giant oaks worth stopping to look at. He could not remember the last time he had turned his thoughts so fiercely to unpacking the hidden truths of a stranger’s private life—not since he was a boy, picking apart every scrap of rumor he could find about the secret of his own birth. Now his mind raced through every possible thread of Gwendolen’s marriage. Could she have learned of Mrs. Glasher and the children, before the wedding? Could that be the reason she shrank from the match, until poverty and desperation pushed her over the edge? He could recall every single word she had ever said to him, and in half of them, he now saw the glimmer of a confession: that she knew she had done something wrong, inflicted some unspoken harm. His own lifelong, quiet experience of being one of the forgotten, unacknowledged children stung sharp in his chest. Was Gwendolen, beneath that bright, proud mask of satisfaction, being eaten alive by a three-headed grief: remorse, disappointment, and the gnaw of jealousy? He had no more patience for teasing himself that she was just a pretty flirt laying traps for him. From that moment on, he decided, he would never evade a chance to speak to her again.

He shook off Vandernoodt soon after, wandering into a dim, empty corner as twilight fell, and spent half an hour turning all these possibilities over in his head. When he resolved to seek her out, he remembered she would be in the drawing room for tea with the other ladies. He was right: Gwendolen had locked herself in her room after lunch, swearing she would not come down for four hours, but by the end of one, she was wild with restlessness, furious to be wasting the last two days of her visit shut away from all chance of seeing or hearing something that might distract her. She straightened her gown, set her face in its usual mask of easy self-possession, and went down to make herself agreeable to the other ladies. Lady Pentreath was in the middle of describing a Regency drawing room, the ridiculous figures all the lords and ladies cut in 1819, the year she was presented, when Deronda walked in.
“Am I intruding?” he asked. “I ought to go back and find the rest of the gentlemen—they’re probably in the billiard room.”
“No, no, stay,” Lady Pentreath waved him in. “They were all getting sick of hearing me talk. Let’s hear what you have to say for yourself.”
“That’s an embarrassing order to fill,” Deronda laughed, pulling a chair up next to Lady Mallinger at the tea table. “I came to mention our songstress, actually—unless you’ve already told everyone about her.”
“Oh, the little Jewess!” Lady Mallinger said. “No, it never even crossed my mind anyone here wanted singing lessons.”
“Every lady knows at least one other person who does,” Deronda said, turning to Lady Pentreath. “I stumbled on an exquisite singer, recently. She’s staying with some friends of mine, the mother and sisters of an old Cambridge chum of mine. She was on the stage in Vienna, but she wants to leave that life and make her living teaching.”
“Swaths of girls do that, don’t they?” the old lady said. “Are her lessons absurdly cheap or absurdly expensive? Those are the only two lures I know of.”
“There’s a third lure for anyone who hears her sing,” Deronda said. “Her voice is exceptional. She’s had perfect training, but it sounds as if every note comes straight from her nature, no effort at all.”
“Why would she leave the stage then?” Lady Pentreath asked. “I’m too old to believe people with first-rate chances ever give them up for nothing.”
“Her voice is too slight for the opera house. It’s a perfect, delicious sound for a drawing room. You who put up with my terrible Schubert singing would be enchanted by hers,” he added, smiling at Mrs. Raymond. “She’d also be happy to sing at small private parties or charity concerts, her voice is more than strong enough for that.”
“I’ve invited her to my drawing room when we go up to London,” Lady Mallinger put in. “You’ll all hear her then. I haven’t heard her sing myself, but I trust Daniel’s recommendation. My girls are going to take lessons from her.”
“Is this a charity project?” Lady Pentreath frowned. “I can’t stand charity music, all thin and wobbly and full of pathos.”
Lady Mallinger, who had promised not to tell anyone Mirah’s sad story, smiled awkwardly and glanced over at Deronda for help.
“It’s a kindness to everyone who wants to hear what good, pure singing sounds like,” Deronda said smoothly. “Everyone with ears would benefit from hearing something better than the overwrought style most people sing in these days. If you heard Miss Lapidoth,” he turned to Gwendolen then, “I think you might even revoke that resolution you made to give up singing forever.”
“I’m far more likely to decide I was right to stop,” Gwendolen said. “I don’t take comfort in the idea of enjoying my own mediocrity, I’m afraid.”
“For my part,” Deronda said, “anyone who does something beautifully makes me want to try, too. Not because I think I can do it half as well, but because they make the thing itself seem worth doing. I don’t mind if my own music isn’t much good—what a dismal world it would be if I thought music itself wasn’t worth caring about. Excellence makes you feel hopeful about life, you know? It shows you how much spiritual wealth the world holds.”
“But if we can never reach that level, it just makes our own small lives feel all the tamer, doesn’t it?” Gwendolen said, sharp, resenting the idea that she was supposed to be happy being second-best.
“That depends entirely on how you look at it,” Deronda said softly. “We’d have a miserable time if we could only take pleasure in the things we do ourselves. A little fumbling, private effort to copy something beautiful is a kind of quiet devotion to it. Most of us should only practice art as a way to learn how to understand and enjoy the things the rare few can do for us. And Miss Lapidoth is definitely one of the rare few.”
“Then she must be perfectly happy, isn’t she?” Gwendolen said, a faint, sharp edge of sarcasm in her tone as she turned to Mrs. Raymond.
“I wouldn’t say that until I know more of her,” Mrs. Raymond said, steady.
“It must have been a terrible disappointment to her, to lose the stage career she’d worked for,” Juliet Fenn said sympathetically.
“I suppose she’s past her prime, though,” Lady Pentreath rumbled in her deep voice.
“On the contrary, she’s barely twenty, and hasn’t even reached it yet,” Deronda said.
“And she’s very pretty, too,” Lady Mallinger added kindly, eager to back Deronda up. “Perfect manners. It’s a pity she’s a strict Jewess, of course, but that doesn’t matter at all when it comes to singing.”
“Well, since her voice isn’t strong enough to scream across an opera house, I’ll send her to my nine granddaughters,” Lady Pentreath declared. “I hope she convinces eight of them they’ve got no business singing anywhere but in church. My theory is half the girls these days need lessons on how not to sing.”
“I’ve already had that lesson, as it happens,” Gwendolen said, looking straight at Deronda. “You see Lady Pentreath is on my side.”
Just then Sir Hugo walked in with the rest of the gentlemen, Grandcourt among them. He leaned against the edge of the tea table, smiling good-humoredly. “What trickery is Deronda pulling on you all, slipping in here alone without the rest of us?”
“Trying to pass off some unknown girl as better than any famous opera singer,” Lady Pentreath said. “A pretty Jewish songstress who’s supposed to wow all these young people. You and I, who heard Catalani in her prime, aren’t so easily dazzled.”
Sir Hugo took the cup of tea his wife handed him, still smiling. “Well, a liberal man has to believe there have been good singers since Catalani’s time, you know.”
“Ah, you’re younger than me. I expect you were one of the men chasing after Alcharisi. Then she went and married and left all of you high and dry.”
“Shameful, that sort of thing—great singers silenced by marriage before their voices even crack. Her husband’s a public thief, that’s what he is. I remember Leroux saying ‘A man might as well pull down a whole peal of church bells and cart them off to the steppes,’” Sir Hugo laughed, setting down his cup and wandering off. Deronda, who had moved to make room for the new arrivals, sat apart from the group for a minute, until he noticed Gwendolen slipping away from Vandernoodt’s attempts at small talk, and walking over to the piano, where she stood looking down at the sheet music stacked on the stand.
No one could blame Deronda for guessing she wanted him to join her. He had noticed before that her first impulse was always to snap and say something sharp, then immediately regret it—and he thought she might be trying to make amends for her cold, prickly tone when he’d spoken of Mirah earlier. He walked to her side.
“Are you relenting, and looking for something to play or sing?”
“I’m not looking for anything at all—but I am relenting,” Gwendolen said, her voice soft, submissive, nothing like her usual bold self.
“May I ask why?”
“I want to hear Miss Lapidoth, and take lessons from her—since you admire her so much. That is, when we go to London. Lessons on how to rejoice in how brilliant she is, and how mediocre I am,” she added, turning to him with a sweet, open smile, the kind he remembered from their first meeting at Leubronn.
“I will be genuinely glad for you to meet her,” Deronda said, smiling back.
“Is she as perfect in every other way as she is in her singing?”
“I can’t swear to that, not quite. I haven’t known her long. But I’ve seen nothing in her that I would change. She’s had a terrible life, troubles starting when she was a tiny child, growing up around nothing but pain. But I think you’ll agree no amount of privilege could have given her more grace, or truer refinement.”
“I wonder what sort of troubles she suffered through?”
“I don’t know all the exact details. But I do know she was on the very edge of drowning herself, in despair.”
“What stopped her?” Gwendolen asked quickly, her wide eyes fixed on his face.
“Some small ray of hope, that made her feel she ought to go on living—that living was worth doing,” he said quietly. “She’s full of piety, and she’ll submit to anything, as long as it feels like duty.”
“People like that don’t deserve pity,” Gwendolen said, impatient. “I’ve no sympathy for women who never do anything wrong. I don’t believe they ever really suffer.” Her fingers twitched, moving fast over the edges of the sheet music at her feet.
“That’s true,” Deronda said. “The weight of knowing you have done wrong cuts deeper, tastes more bitter. We flawed, stumbling creatures will always feel more for the people bruised and scraped raw from fighting their own mistakes than we ever will for the irreproachable ones. It’s the oldest story there is—the lost sheep—but it feels new every single day.”
“That’s just talk, though. No one acts like that, not really,” Gwendolen said, bitter. “You admire Miss Lapidoth because you think she’s blameless, perfect. And you’d despise a woman who’d done something you thought was very wrong.”
“That would depend entirely on how she herself felt about what she’d done,” Deronda said.
“I suppose you’d be satisfied if she was utterly miserable, then,” Gwendolen burst out, impetuous.
“No—never satisfied. I’d be full of sorrow for her. That wasn’t just empty talk. I didn’t mean that a pure, uncomplicated nature isn’t lovely, but the people who would never have caught your interest before can become far more worthy of sympathy once they’ve done something that wakes in them a sharp, burning remorse. Lives get opened up in all kinds of ways. I daresay plenty of people would never have their eyes opened at all, if they didn’t get a hard, violent shock from the consequences of their own actions.
