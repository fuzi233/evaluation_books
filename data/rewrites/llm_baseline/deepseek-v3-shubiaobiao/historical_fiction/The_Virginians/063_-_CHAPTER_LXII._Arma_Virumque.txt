The drums of war echoed across Europe, and in London’s coffee-houses and drawing rooms, Harry Warrington found his restless spirit stirred by the clash of empires. While Britain’s own king had tasted defeat, Frederick of Prussia carved his name into history with victories that set the world aflame—Rossbach, Leuthen—battlefields where Harry might have sought glory had fate spun a different thread. But no, his musings remained just that: idle longings, for his pen was not destined to chronicle such deeds. That task belonged to fiercer hands, mightier quills.

Harry was no scholar. Books bored him; novels struck him as trifles, and the ladies who wept over Richardson’s pages seemed to him absurd. His laughter came slow but hearty, rumbling after the jest had passed. Must every gentleman be a wit or a poet? Surely not. Yet idleness gnawed at him—ruined, dependent, his strong hands empty of purpose. A man bred for action, left to dawdle in drawing rooms, was a man at war with himself.

The Lambert women pitied him—all but one. Theo and her mother softened his gloom with kindness, but Hetty’s tongue was sharp as a saber’s edge. Why should he linger, useless, when the world was in arms? "If I were a man," she declared, pacing like a caged officer, "I would not waste my days sighing over misfortunes. I would make my own fate!"

Harry winced. "You think I lack courage?"

"I think you lack nothing but the will to use what you have," she shot back. "Must you wait for permission to be a man?"

Her words struck deeper than she knew. Pride and shame warred in him. He was no coward—had he not faced worse than battlefield perils? Yet here he stood, shackled by debt, by duty, by his mother’s unyielding will.

"I would volunteer tomorrow if I could," he muttered. "But commissions aren’t bought with empty pockets, and I’ll not beg from George again."

Hetty scoffed. "Must it be bought? Can you not earn it?"

A silence fell between them. Outside, London carried on—coaches rattling, hawkers calling, the distant pulse of a military march. Somewhere, men were rising, striving. And Harry Warrington, with his broad shoulders and idle hands, felt the weight of his own inertia like a leaden coat.

Mrs. Lambert’s entrance broke the tension. "What troubles you, my dear?" she asked, eyeing Harry’s stormy brow.

"Nothing new," Hetty said lightly. "Only Harry fretting over his lack of occupation."

But the truth hung unspoken: a man who aches for purpose and finds none is a man half-alive. And Harry, for all his strength, had never felt weaker.
