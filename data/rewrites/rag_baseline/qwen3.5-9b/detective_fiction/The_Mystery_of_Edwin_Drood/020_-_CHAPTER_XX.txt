The air in the room was still, thick with the dust of unspoken things. Rosa opened her eyes, and the memory of the interview pressed against her, refusing to fade even as consciousness returned. It had followed her into the darkness, a persistent weight. Her mind offered no path forward, only a single, sharp command: flight. She must leave this man.

But where? How?

Helena was the only confidante, yet mentioning Jasper's threat to Helena would be an invitation to disaster. Jasper possessed the power, and worse, the will, to enact the harm he threatened. To speak of it was to invite the blow. Every hesitation, every slip in judgment, could unleash his malice upon Helena's brother.

For six months, Rosa's mind had been a storm of conflicting currents. A suspicion, half-formed and unnamed, rose and sank like a tide. Jasper's obsession with his nephew, his relentless questioning of the boy's fate, had become the local gossip, rendering the notion of foul play impossible for others to entertain. Had she imagined it? Was her revulsion a projection of her own fear, a sign of baselessness? Or was there a motive? She flinched at the thought: the desire to possess *her*. To build a crime on such trivial vanity felt like a sin greater than the act itself.

She replayed his words by the sundial. He treated the disappearance as murder, consistent with his public persona since the discovery of the watch and shirt-pin. If he feared the truth, would he not encourage the lie of voluntary absence? He had spoken of sweeping "even him" away. Was that a threat or a boast? He claimed to offer his six months of labor as a sacrifice of justice. Could he truly pour his wasted life and despair onto her altar if the foundation was a lie? His first offering was fidelity to the dead boy. These facts argued against her fear, yet he was a terrible man. The girl, unable to comprehend the criminal intellect which scholars failed to read because they sought to reconcile it with the average mind, found only one conclusion: flee.

She had been Helena's shield, assuring her of her brother's innocence and sharing in his misery. She had never seen him since the disappearance, nor had Helena mentioned his confession to Mr. Crisparkle. To her, he was simply Helena's unfortunate brother. The promise she gave her suitor was true, though now she regretted the clarity of it. Fear of him coexisted with a swelling pride that he knew it from her own lips.

Where could she go? Anywhere beyond his reach. She decided on her guardian, Miss Twinkleton, and acted immediately. The feeling of unsafety, the realization that the convent walls could not stop his spectral pursuit, had calcified into terror. The fascination of repulsion had bound her. Even glancing at the sundial through the window sent a chill through her; it seemed invested with his awful nature.

She scribbled a hurried note to Miss Twinkleton, claiming urgent need to see her guardian, and left it in plain sight. Packing a few useless items into a tiny bag, she slipped out, closing the gate softly.

It was the first time she had traversed Cloisterham High Street alone. Knowing its winding paths, she hurried to the corner where the omnibus waited. It was departing.

"Stop and take me, if you please, Joe. I am obliged to go to London."

Within a minute, she was on the road to the railway, protected by Joe. He waited until she reached the station, placing her safely inside the carriage and handing in her small bag as if it were a hundredweight trunk she could not possibly lift.

"Can you go round when you get back, and tell Miss Twinkleton that you saw me safely off, Joe?"

"It shall be done, Miss."

"With my love, please, Joe."

"Yes, Miss—and I wouldn't mind having it myself!" Joe thought the last clause, keeping it silent.

Now, whirring toward London, Rosa allowed her thoughts to return. The indignation that his declaration soiled her, cleansing only through appeal to the honest and true, bolstered her resolve. But as dusk deepened and the great city loomed closer, doubt crept in. Was this rash? How would Mr. Grewgious react? Would he be there? What if he wasn't? Alone in a strange, crowded place, she wondered if waiting and seeking counsel first would have been wiser. A multitude of uneasy questions swirled as the train crossed rooftops, revealing the gritty streets below, lit by lamps unnecessary for the heat of the summer night.

"Hiram Grewgious, Esquire, Staple Inn, London." That was her destination. Enough to send her rattling away again in a cab through deserts of grit, where crowds gathered for air and others shuffled with monotonous noise on hot stones. Everything was gritty, shabby. Music played here and there—a barrel-organ, a big drum—but it offered no relief. Only echoes from brick surfaces and dust. The wind instruments seemed broken-hearted, pining for the country.

The cab stopped at a fast-closed gateway, belonging to someone afraid of burglars. Rosa paid the driver and knocked timidly. A watchman let her in, bag and all.

"Does Mr. Grewgious live here?"

"He lives there, Miss," the watchman pointed deeper into the shadows.

Rosa proceeded, stopping on P.J.T.'s doorstep at ten o'clock, wondering what had become of the street door. Guided by the painted name, she ascended and tapped softly. No answer. The handle yielded. She entered to find her guardian sitting on a window-seat at an open window, a shaded lamp distant on a corner table.

Rosa approached in the twilight. He saw her and whispered, "Good Heaven!"

She threw herself upon his neck, weeping. He embraced her, soothing.

"My child, my child! I thought you were your mother!—But what, what, what," he added gently, "has happened? My dear, what has brought you here? Who has brought you here?"

"No one. I came alone."

"Lord bless me!" he exclaimed. "Came alone! Why didn't you write to me to come and fetch you?"

"I had no time. I took a sudden resolution. Poor, poor Eddy!"

"Ah, poor fellow, poor fellow!"

"His uncle has made love to me. I cannot bear it," Rosa burst out, stamping her little foot, "I shudder with horror of him, and I have come to you to protect me and all of us from him, if you will?"

"I will," cried Mr. Grewgious, with a sudden surge of energy. "Damn him! 'Confound his politics! Frustrate his knavish tricks! On Thee his hopes to fix? Damn him again!'"

This outburst, alternating between loyal enthusiasm and combative denunciation, left him beside himself. He wiped his face, composing himself.

"I beg your pardon, my dear, but you will be glad to know I feel better. Tell me no more just now, or I might do it again. You must be refreshed and cheered. What did you take last? Was it breakfast, lunch, dinner, tea, or supper? And what will you take next? Shall it be breakfast, lunch, dinner, tea, or supper?"

Kneeling before her, he removed her hat and disentangled her hair with respectful tenderness, a chivalrous sight unexpected from him.

"Your rest too must be provided for," he continued. "You shall have the prettiest chamber in Furnival's. Your toilet must be attended to, and you shall have everything an unlimited head chambermaid can procure. Is that a bag?" He scrutinized it closely. "And is it your property, my dear?"

"Yes, sir. I brought it with me."

"It is not an extensive bag," he admitted candidly, "though admirably calculated to contain a day's provision for a canary-bird. Perhaps you brought a canary-bird?"

Rosa smiled and shook her head.

"If you had, he should have been made welcome," Mr. Grewgious said. "I think he would have been pleased to be hung upon a nail outside and pit himself against our Staple sparrows; whose execution must be admitted to be not quite equal to their intention. Which is the case with so many of us! You didn't say what meal, my dear. Have a nice jumble of all meals."

Rosa thanked him but said she could only take tea. Mr. Grewgious ran out and in several times to order marmalade, eggs, watercress, salted fish, and frizzled ham. Soon the board was set.

"Lord bless my soul," he cried, placing the lamp on the table and sitting opposite Rosa, "what a new sensation for a poor old Angular bachelor, to be sure!"

Rosa's eyebrows raised in query.

"The sensation of having a sweet young presence in the place, that whitewashes it, paints it, papers it, decorates it with gilding, and makes it Glorious!" Mr. Grewgious sighed mournfully.

Touching him with her tea-cup, Rosa touched him with her small hand.

"Thank you, my dear," he said. "Ahem! Let's talk!"

"Do you always live here, sir?" Rosa asked.

"Yes, my dear."
