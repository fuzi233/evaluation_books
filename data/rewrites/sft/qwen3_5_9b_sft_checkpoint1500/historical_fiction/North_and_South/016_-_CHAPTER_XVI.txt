None by the path that he would go;
And always be for change prepared,
For the world's law is ebb and flow."*

The following afternoon, Dr. Donaldson arrived to pay his first professional visit to Mrs. Hale. The veil of mystery that Margaret had hoped their past intimacy had lifted fell back down, sealing her out of the room while the doctor entered with Dixon. Margaret was not naturally inclined toward love, yet where affection took root within her, it burned with a fierce, consuming passion, accompanied by a jealous intensity that knew no bounds.

Retreating to her mother's bedchamber, situated directly behind the drawing-room, Margaret paced the floor, her footsteps echoing softly against the silence. Every few moments, she froze, straining her ears. A faint, guttural sound drifted to her—a moan. Her hands clenched until her knuckles turned white, and she held her breath, terrified lest her inhaling disturb the fragile peace of the chamber. Was it a sigh? A groan? Or merely the settling of dust? Minutes passed in agonizing stillness, followed by the chaotic symphony of leaving guests: the scraping of chairs, the raised voices of departure, the rustle of fabric. When the front door finally clicked open, signaling the doctor's exit, Margaret sprang from her hiding place.

"My father is away, Doctor," she announced, her voice steady despite the tremor in her heart. "He has a pupil attending to matters at this hour. Will you kindly step into his study downstairs?"

She watched Dixon, noting the subtle shift in the old servant's posture as Margaret asserted her authority. With a deliberate grace, she adopted the demeanor of the senior daughter, a role that silenced Dixon's usual officious interjections. A flash of amusement crossed Margaret's mind amidst her turmoil; she could almost see the ridiculous gravity of her own performance reflected in Dixon's startled gaze. This brief illusion of dignity transported her down the stairs, offering a fleeting respite from the sharp, stinging reality of her mission. But the moment the illusion faded, the weight of the truth crashed back down, stealing her breath.

Standing before the doctor, she demanded the truth with an air of imperious command. "What is the matter with Mother? Speak plainly." Seeing a fraction of hesitation in his eyes, she pressed further. "I am her only child living here. I fear Father is not sufficiently alarmed, and if there is grave danger, it must be revealed to him gently. I can endure this burden. I can care for Mother. Please, sir, do not leave me in suspense. To see your face unreadable is a terror far worse than whatever words you might utter."

"My dear young lady," the doctor replied, his tone gentle but firm, "your mother is attended by a servant of remarkable efficiency and devotion, one who has become something akin to a confidante—"

"I am her daughter, sir!" Margaret interrupted, her voice rising.

"But when I told you she expressly wished to spare you this knowledge—"

"I am neither virtuous nor patient enough to submit to such a prohibition. Moreover, I am certain you possess too much wisdom and experience to have promised a lie. You would not betray her trust."

"Well," the doctor said, a faint, sad smile touching his lips, "in that, you are correct. I made no promise. Indeed, I fear the truth will surface inevitably without my intervention."

He paused, allowing the heavy silence to settle. Margaret's complexion drained of color, turning a stark, ghastly white, and her lips pressed together tightly. Yet, beneath the surface, her features remained frozen. Through his profound understanding of human nature, Dr. Donaldson recognized that she would extract every detail; that any omission would inflict a torment far sharper than the knowledge itself. Speaking in a low, measured voice, he delivered two short, devastating sentences, keeping his gaze fixed on her. He observed the widening dilation of her pupils, transforming into pools of black horror, and the rapid, livid pallor of her cheeks. He fell silent, waiting for the shock to subside, for the ragged intake of breath to return.

Finally, she spoke. "Thank you, sir, for your confidence. That dread has haunted me for weeks. It is a genuine, crushing agony. My poor, poor mother!" Her lips quivered, and he allowed her the release of tears, confident in her ability to regain control swiftly.

Only a few tears fell before she remembered the questions burning within her. "Will there be much suffering?"

He shook his head slowly. "We cannot predict that. It depends entirely on constitution and countless variables. However, recent advancements in medicine have granted us significant power to alleviate pain."

"Father!" Margaret whispered, her body shaking.

"I do not know Mr. Hale personally. Giving advice in such cases is fraught with difficulty. I suggest you proceed, armed with the knowledge I have reluctantly shared, until the reality becomes somewhat familiar to you. Then, you may offer the comfort you can, without excessive strain. Until then, my visits will continue periodically, though I fear I can do little more than ease symptoms. A myriad of circumstances will inevitably arouse his alarm and deepen his fears, preparing him gradually. Nay, my dear—young lady, nay, my dear friend—I saw Mr. Thornton, and I hold your father in high regard for the sacrifices he makes, however misguided I may believe them to be. On this occasion, if it pleases you..." He hesitated, then continued warmly, "remember, when I return, I come as a friend. And you must learn to view me as such. For meeting in times of trial, growing to understand one another, is worth a lifetime of polite morning calls."

Margaret found her voice choked by emotion, unable to speak, but she squeezed his hand with a grip that nearly bruised him.

"That," Dr. Donaldson mused later, seated safely in his carriage, examining his hand, "is what I call a formidable young lady. Who would have guessed that delicate frame could generate such a vice-like grasp? The bones are strong, well-set. She is quite the queen! First, her head thrown back to force me into truth, then bent forward with desperate eagerness to listen. Poor soul! I must ensure she does not overexert herself. It is astonishing what these bred-for-stature creatures can endure. That girl is tough as oak. Another, reduced to that deadly hue, would likely collapse into hysterics or faint immediately. But she did not falter—not she! Her sheer willpower brought her back. A woman like that would win my heart, if I were thirty years younger. Alas, it is too late now. Here, at the Archers', we are."

Leaping from the carriage, he approached the residence, ready to serve the family with the same devoted attention he offered anyone else.

Meanwhile, Margaret returned to her father's study, seeking a moment to steady herself before ascending to her mother's bedside. "Oh, my God, my God! How shall I bear this? A fatal illness, no hope! Oh, Mamma, Mamma, I curse the day I went to Aunt Shaw's, those precious years away from you! Poor Mamma! Think of what she must have endured! Oh, Lord, let her sufferings not be too acute, too dreadful. How can I witness them? How can I survive seeing Papa's agony? He must not know yet, not all at once. It would kill him. But I cannot waste another second of my time away from Mother."

She rushed upstairs. Dixon was absent. Mrs. Hale reclined in an easy chair, draped in a soft white shawl and wearing a modest cap, having anticipated the doctor's arrival. A faint flush colored her cheeks, and the exhaustion following the examination lent her countenance a strangely peaceful quality. Margaret was taken aback by her mother's composure.

"Why, Margaret, you look strangely agitated! What troubles you?" Mrs. Hale asked. As suspicion dawned in her mind regarding the true state of affairs, her tone shifted slightly to one of mild disapproval. "Have you not seen Dr. Donaldson, and asked him questions, child?"

Margaret offered no verbal reply, only casting a pleading, wistful glance toward her mother. Displeasure deepened in Mrs. Hale's eyes. "He would not surely break his word to me, and—"

"Oh yes, Mamma, he did! I compelled him. It was I—blame me." Kneeling beside her mother, Margaret seized her hand, refusing to let go even as Mrs. Hale attempted to withdraw. She kissed the hand repeatedly, her hot tears soaking the skin.

"Margaret, it was very wrong of you. You knew I wished to spare you." Yet, worn down by the struggle, she left her hand in Margaret's clasp, returning the pressure feebly. This quiet acceptance encouraged Margaret to speak.

"Oh, Mamma! Let me be your nurse. I will learn whatever Dixon teaches me. But you know I am your child, and I feel I have a right to do everything for you."

"You do not realize what you are undertaking," Mrs. Hale murmured, shuddering.

"Yes, I do. I know far more than you suspect. Let me tend to you. At the very least, let me try. No one will strive harder than I will. It will bring such comfort, Mamma."
