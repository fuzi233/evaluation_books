Before the sun had fully claimed the sky, Lieutenant Sutch was already awake, vibrating with a restlessness that defied his habitual slowness. He roused the household, a frantic conductor of chaos, packing and unpacking his trunks with a boyish fervor that doubled the time required for every mundane task. For thirty years, his life had been a slow drift on the half-pay list, a quiet shadow behind the scenes of empire; but now, miraculously, the tide had turned. Something important, demanding energy, tact, and decisive action, had finally fallen into his lap. Sutch was to be employed again, and the thought set his blood racing. He dreaded the brief interlude between breakfast and departure, terrified that some unforeseen obstacle might snuff out this sudden purpose and return him to the stagnant waters of idleness.

"I shall be ready this afternoon," Sutch announced briskly to Durrance over breakfast, the silverware clinking softly against the china. "I intend to catch the night mail to the Continent. Perhaps we could ride up to London together? London lies conveniently on your path to Wiesbaden."

"No," Durrance replied, his gaze steady. "I have but one remaining errand in England. It occurred to me only last night, prompted by your urgency."

"And who, pray, do you propose to visit?" Sutch asked, leaning forward.

"General Feversham," Durrance said simply.

Sutch dropped his knife and fork, staring at his companion with genuine astonishment. "Why, in the name of heaven, would you seek an audience with him?"

"To tell him how Harry has redeemed his honor," Durrance explained, his voice low but firm. "Last night you warned me not to reveal Harry's intentions or successes to the General until the son returned. Yet I am bound by no such oath. Consider the burden it places upon the General: the idea that his son was a coward. A father might prefer a murderer, a thief, a rogue above a man who fled from battle. I must go to Surrey today and tell him that Harry Feversham was never a coward."

Sutch shook his head slowly, a grim line etching itself between his brows. "He will not comprehend, sir. Of course, he will be grateful. He will rejoice that the stain is washed away, but he will never grasp *why* the disgrace befell him in the first place. In the end, he will celebrate only because the family name is whole again."

"My sentiments differ," Durrance countered gently. "I suspect the old man loves his son deeply, though he would deny it to his dying breath. I, too, hold a measure of affection for General Feversham."

Sutch had seen little of the General in the five years since the disaster. He could never forgive Feversham for his role in Harry's ruin. If the General had possessed the capacity to sympathize with the boy's nature, if he had truly understood the spirit of the lad, the four white feathers would never have been pinned to Harry's tunic at Broad Place. Sutch imagined the old man sitting sternly upon his terrace, oblivious to his own culpability, posing instead as a tragic martyr who had been betrayed by a shameful child. Sutch resolved that he could never bear to sit patiently with the stubborn old fool, convinced that no argument could bend Feversham from his conviction. He had not given the matter a single thought prior to Durrance's declaration.

"You are remarkably considerate for others," Sutch remarked dryly.

"It is not a virtue for which I boast," Durrance smiled faintly. "I practice consideration solely as an instinct of self-preservation. Selfishness is the natural flaw of the blind; knowing this, I guard myself carefully against it."

And so, Durrance traveled by branch lines from Hampshire into Surrey, arriving at Broad Place bathed in the golden light of the afternoon. General Feversham, nearing his eightieth year, stood tall and erect, his spine as rigid as it had been twenty years prior when he first presented his son to his Crimean comrades. Yet, time had shrunk his stature, and his face seemed smaller, drawn tight. Though blind, the General's presence was palpable. Two years ago, when Durrance had strolled with him along the terrace, the old man had walked with a lighter step; now, his gait was heavy, and a peculiar, childish timbre had entered his speech.

"You have joined the veterans before your time, Durrance," the General said, his voice rasping slightly. "I read of it in a paper. I would have written, had I known your address."

Whether he suspected the purpose of Durrance's visit, the General showed no sign. He rang the bell, and tea was served in the vast hall where the portraits of the dead Fevershams hung in somber silence. The General chatted of officers from the Sudan, grumbled about the iniquities of the War Office, and expressed a deep fear that the nation was sliding into ruin.

"Every misfortune stems from bad luck or poor management," he exclaimed irritably. "Even you, Colonel Durrance, you are not the same man who walked with me on my terrace two years past."

Feversham lacked tact, and the solitude of his life had offered no remedy. Durrance could have retorted with a *tu quoque*, noting the General's own failings, but he held his tongue.

"But I arrive with business in hand," Durrance said quietly.

The General sat up straight in his chair, his movements stiff. "And I give you the same answer. I have nothing to say regarding Harry Feversham. I will not discuss him."

His voice remained hard and unemotional, devoid of any sorrow, as if speaking of a stranger. Durrance found himself wondering if the springs of love had completely dried up within the General's heart.

"It would not bring you pleasure, then, to know where Harry Feversham has been, nor how he has lived these last five years?"

A pause stretched between them—not long, but heavy enough to weigh down the room.

"Not in the least, Colonel Durrance," the General replied.

The answer was absolute, yet Durrance leaned on the silence that preceded it. "Nor on what business he has been engaged?"

"I am not interested in the smallest degree. My solicitor informs me that he draws his allowance; I am content with that knowledge."

"I shall risk your wrath, General," Durrance said, his tone calm but resolute. "There are moments when it is wise to disobey a superior officer. This is one of them. Naturally, you may eject me from your house. Otherwise, I shall recount the history of your son and my friend since he vanished from England."

Feversham laughed, a sharp, brittle sound. "Of course, I cannot turn you out of the house," he conceded, adding severely, "but I warn you that you are abusing your position as my guest."

"Yes, there is no doubt of that," Durrance agreed, settling into his narrative. He spoke of the recovery of the Gordon letters from Berber, his meeting with Harry at Wadi Halfa, and Harry's subsequent imprisonment at Omdurman. He carried the tale down to the present day, ending with the news of Lieutenant Sutch's imminent departure for Suakin. Feversham listened without interruption, unmoving in his chair. Durrance could not decipher the spirit in which the story was received, but he drew solace from the fact that the General listened at all, offering no immediate rebuttal.

For some time after Durrance concluded, the General sat in silence. He raised a hand to shade his eyes, as if trying to see the speaker, though his vision failed. He remained thus, the gesture stiff with pride forbidding him to show even the natural weakness of joy at his son's reconquest of honor before the watching ghosts on the walls.

"What I do not understand," the General said slowly, "is why Harry ever resigned his commission. I could not fathom it before; I understand it even less now, despite your account of his great bravery. It is one of those queer, inexplicable things. They happen, and there is naught else to be said. Nevertheless, I am very glad you compelled me to listen, Durrance."

"I acted with a specific objective," Durrance noted. "Naturally, it is for you to decide, but for my part, I see no reason why Harry should not return home and reclaim all that he lost."

"He cannot regain everything," Feversham murmured, his voice trembling slightly. "It is not right. He committed the sin, and he must pay the price. He cannot restore his career, for that is gone forever."

"No, that is true," Durrance admitted. "But he may find another path. He is not yet so aged that opportunity has fled him. That is the entirety of his loss."

Slowly, the General lowered his hand and shifted in his chair. He glanced quickly at Durrance, opening his mouth as if to speak, then closing it again.

"Well," he said briskly, as though dismissing a trivial matter, "if Sutch can arrange Harry's escape from Omdurman, I see no cause why he should not return home."

Durrance rose. "Thank you, General. If you will see me driven to the station, I can catch the six o'clock train to town."
