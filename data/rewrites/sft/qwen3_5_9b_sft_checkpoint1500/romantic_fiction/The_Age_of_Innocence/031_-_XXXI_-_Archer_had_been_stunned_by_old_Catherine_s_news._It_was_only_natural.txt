The shock of Old Catherine's news settled upon Archer like a heavy, suffocating stone. It was perfectly understandable, given the gravity of the summons, that Madame Olenska would hasten back from Washington. However, the decision to remain under the roof of her grandmother—particularly now that Mrs. Mingott was recovering her strength—defied simple logic.

Archer knew the exact arithmetic of Madame Olenska's finances. He possessed the precise figures of the meager allowance her husband permitted following their separation. Without the substantial sum from her grandmother, it would have been impossible for either woman to survive within the lavish standards of the Mingott household. With Medora Manson ruined, that pittance would scarcely suffice to clothe or feed them both. Yet, Archer was certain that Madame Olenska had not accepted her grandmother's offer out of necessity or greed.

She possessed a reckless generosity and a spasmodic extravagance born of those accustomed to vast wealth, rendering her indifferent to monetary constraints. Her relatives, particularly Mrs. Lovell Mingott and Mrs. Welland, had frequently lamented that anyone who had basked in the cosmopolitan luxuries of Count Olenski's estates cared so little for propriety. Furthermore, several months had passed since her allowance was cut off, yet she had made no concerted effort to win back her grandmother's favor. If her course had truly shifted, it must have been driven by something other than survival.

He need not search far for the catalyst. Upon the ferry ride, she had told him that they must remain apart. She had spoken the words with her head resting upon his chest. There was no calculated coquetry in her tone; she was fighting a desperate battle against her fate, just as he had fought his own, clinging fiercely to their shared resolve to honor the trust placed in them. Yet, during the ten days since her return to New York, she might have sensed from his silence, from his failure to visit, that he was contemplating a decisive, irreversible step. At the thought, a sudden terror of her own weakness might have seized her—a realization that it was better to accept the conventional compromise, the path of least resistance, than to risk further ruin.

An hour prior, when he had rung the bell at Mrs. Mingott's, Archer had imagined his path laid out clearly. He had intended to speak privately with Madame Olenska, and failing that, to ascertain from her grandmother the date and time of her return to Washington. In that train, he planned to join her, traveling as far as she would allow, perhaps even as far as Japan. He intended to leave a note for May that would sever all other alternatives. He had felt nerved for this plunge, eager to take it.

Yet, the first emotion upon learning that the circumstances had altered had been a strange sense of relief. Now, walking home from Mrs. Mingott's, he felt a growing distaste for what lay ahead. The path he was treading was not unfamiliar; he had traversed it before. But previously, he had done so as a free man, accountable to no one, able to lend himself with amused detachment to the intricate game of precautions, prevarication, concealment, and compliance. This procedure was known as "protecting a woman's honor." The best fiction, combined with the post-prandial musings of his elders, had long since initiated him into every nuance of its code.

Now, however, the matter appeared in a stark new light, and his role within it seemed singularly diminished. It was, in fact, the same performance he had secretly observed Mrs. Thorley Rushworth enact toward a devoted and unsuspecting husband: a smiling, bantering, humor-infused, watchful, and ceaseless fabrication. A lie by day, a lie by night, woven into every touch, every glance, every caress, every quarrel, every word, and every silence.

It was arguably easier, and less dastardly overall, for a wife to play such a part. A woman's standard of veracity was tacitly regarded as lower; she was the subject creature, versed in the arts of the enslaved. She could always plead with moods and nerves, invoking the right not to be held to strict account. Even in the most rigid societies, the laughter invariably fell upon the husband.

But in Archer's little world, no one laughed at a deceived wife, and a certain measure of contempt adhered to men who continued their philandering after marriage. In the agricultural metaphor of the social order, there was a recognized season for wild oats, but they were not to be sown more than once.

Archer had always shared this sentiment; in his heart, he had deemed Lefferts despicable. But to love Ellen Olenska did not necessarily transform a man into a Leffert. For the first time, Archer found himself face-to-face with the dreadful argument of the unique individual case. Ellen Olenska was unlike any other woman, and he was unlike any other man; consequently, their situation resembled no precedent, and they were answerable to no tribunal save their own judgment.

Yes, but in ten minutes, he would be mounting his own doorstep. There were May, and habit, and honor, and all the ancient decencies that he and his people had always revered...

At the corner, he hesitated, then walked on down Fifth Avenue.

Ahead of him, looming in the winter darkness, stood a massive, unlit house. As he drew near, memories surfaced: how often he had seen it ablaze with lights, its steps awninged and carpeted, carriages lined up in double rows to draw up at the curb. It was in the conservatory, stretching its dead-black bulk down the side street, that he had taken his first kiss from May; it was beneath the myriad candles of the ballroom that he had first seen her appear, tall and silver-shining as a young Diana.

Now the house was as dark as a grave, save for a faint flare of gas in the basement and a solitary light in one upstairs room where the blinds remained down. As Archer reached the corner, he spotted the carriage standing at the door—it was Mrs. Manson Mingott's. What an opportunity for Sillerton Jackson, if he should happen to pass! Archer had been deeply moved by Old Catherine's account of Madame Olenska's attitude toward Mrs. Beaufort; it made the righteous reprobation of New York seem like a fleeting shadow. But he knew well enough what construction the clubs and drawing-rooms would place upon Ellen Olenska's visits to her cousin.

He paused and gazed upward at the lit window. Undoubtedly, the two women were sitting together within. Mrs. Beaufort had likely sought consolation elsewhere. There were even rumors that she had departed New York with Fanny Ring, though Mrs. Beaufort's demeanor rendered that report improbable.

Archer possessed the nocturnal solitude of Fifth Avenue almost entirely to himself. At this hour, most people were indoors, dressing for dinner, and he was secretly glad that Ellen's departure was likely to go unnoticed. As the thought crossed his mind, the door swung open, and she emerged. Behind her trailed a faint light, perhaps carried down the stairs to guide her way. She turned briefly to speak to someone inside, then the door closed, and she descended the steps.

"Ellen," he called softly as she reached the pavement.

She stopped with a slight start. Just then, two young men of fashionable cut approached. There was a familiar air about their overcoats and the way their smart silk mufflers were folded over their white ties. He wondered how youths of their quality happened to dine out so early. Then he recalled that the Reggie Chiverses, whose residence was a few doors above, were hosting a large party that evening to see Adelaide Neilson in *Romeo and Juliet*, and surmised that these two belonged to the number. They passed beneath a lamp, and he recognized Lawrence Lefferts and a young Mr. Chivers.

A mean desire not to have Madame Olenska seen leaving the Beauforts' vanished as he felt the penetrating warmth of her hand.

"I shall see you now—we shall be together," he burst out, hardly knowing what he said.

"Ah," she answered, "Granny has told you?"

While watching her, he became aware that Lefferts and Chivers, having reached the far side of the street corner, had discreetly struck away across Fifth Avenue. It was the kind of masculine solidarity that he himself often practiced; now, he sickened at their connivance. Did she truly imagine that he and she could live like this? And if not, what else did she imagine?

"Tomorrow I must see you—somewhere where we can be alone," he said, in a voice that sounded almost angry to his own ears.

She wavered, moving toward the carriage.

"But I shall be at Granny's—for the present, that is," she added, as if conscious that her change of plans required explanation.

"Somewhere where we can be alone," he insisted.

She gave a faint laugh that grated on him.

"In New York? But there are no churches... no monuments."

"There's the Art Museum—in the Park," he explained, as she looked puzzled. "At half-past two. I shall be at the door..."

She turned away without answering and climbed quickly into the carriage. As it drove off, she leaned forward, and he thought she waved her hand in the obscurity. He stared after her in a turmoil of contradictory feelings.
