CHAPTER LXXIV. How Arthur Should Have Bought a Return Ticket (And Other Regrets)

The train, that merciless express of modern life, deposited Arthur Pendennis at Tunbridge with all the efficiency of a well-oiled machine—too efficiently, in fact. He had barely time to reflect on his entire existence before arriving at his destination, which was already, in his mind, the end of the line. As he rattled along through the countryside, he reviewed his past with the solemnity of a man who’d just been handed a bill for a lifetime of bad decisions.

“Here it ends,” he mused, staring out the window as if expecting to see a signpost reading *Regret Ahead*. “Romance? Ambition? Dreams? All gone. I refuse an angel—my mother begs, and I say no! And yet, if I had taken her, would she have been an angel? Probably not. She’d have been a wife forced upon me by my uncle’s matchmaking, like some prepackaged emotional package. I couldn’t have loved her honestly. I needed to discover her myself, not through someone else’s eyes. But now look—here I am, accepting Blanche, a seat in Parliament, riches, and a career—all guaranteed by Uncle Pendennis, and what do I get? Fortune comes, drops off the dowry, and leaves me holding only the woman. The heart without the money. What a bargain!”

He sighed, then turned to the priest sitting across from him, who hadn’t blinked since Reigate Tunnel. “I wish I were him,” Arthur thought. “At least he has a breviary. A clear path. No doubts. No guilt. Just pages of prayer and a quiet soul.” Then he glanced at the old gentleman next to the priest, scowling at him over a newspaper like a man who’d just discovered the world was built on lies. “And I wish I were him too,” Arthur added, “at least he has something to hate.”

Just then, the stout gentleman beside him leaned forward. “Would you care to read the paper, sir?” he asked, offering a copy with a headline screaming about the evils of black-coated men (a group clearly responsible for all national sins).

Arthur took it, nodded politely, and then promptly forgot he was holding it. His mind wandered back to deeper matters: faith, truth, destiny. “Who could understand another’s inner chaos?” he pondered. “Who can measure the weight of a childhood fear, a missed chance, a single misstep? A grain of sand might shift the course of a river—or a man’s life. Who but God knows the full account?”

He bowed his head, not in prayer exactly, but in surrender. “If I marry Blanche,” he whispered to himself, “may I be true. May I make her happy. If I have children, may I teach them honesty. May they inherit a name that isn’t stained by my cowardice or my vanity.”

Then the train stopped. Tunbridge. His moment had arrived.

He handed the newspaper back to the stout gentleman and stepped out, carpet-bag in hand, determined to face whatever awaited him. A fly whisked him to Lady Clavering’s house, where he composed a little speech—a noble, sincere, almost poetic declaration of love and honor, though slightly undermined by the fact that he was delivering it to a woman he no longer wanted to marry.

The gist:
“Blanche, I don’t know what your last letter meant. Is my proposal acceptable? I’ve given up the wealth and status because I couldn’t accept them without feeling dishonored. If you doubt my affection, let’s marry right now. I’ll keep my vow. I’ll cherish you. I’ll be a loving husband.”

He sprang from the fly, marched to the door, and was met by a servant who looked utterly baffled.
“Her Ladyship’s not at home, sir,” the man said.
“I’m Mr. Pendennis,” Arthur replied.
“Lightfoot is gone,” the man continued. “My Lady is out, and my orders were——”
“I hear Miss Amory singing,” Arthur interrupted. “Take my bag to a dressing room.”

He strode into the drawing room, where Blanche sat at the piano, belting out a melodramatic tune so passionate it made the air crackle. Master Clavering slept on the sofa, blissfully unaware. But near Blanche sat a gentleman—Foker—who looked like he’d just won the lottery, a gold medal, and the keys to heaven.

As Arthur entered, Foker jumped. The music cut off mid-note. Blanche shrieked. Frank Clavering woke up, yawned, and muttered, “Here’s a jolly go!” before vanishing in a snigger.

Arthur froze. Then he saw it.

On the piano, open and glowing like a jewel thief’s dream, was a purple-leather box. Inside, curled around white satin, lay a serpentine bracelet—diamond tail, ruby head—so magnificent it seemed to hum with power. The same kind Harry had shown him three days earlier in Waterloo Place.

“Hullo!” Foker said, trying to sound casual.
“How de-do, Pendennis?”
Blanche trembled, fumbled with her handkerchief, and tried to cover the bracelet. Then she stepped forward, hands shaking, and greeted Arthur.
“How is dearest Laura?” she asked.

Foker, meanwhile, looked like a man caught red-handed in a crime he didn’t know was illegal. His ears turned crimson. His cheeks flushed. Arthur stared. Then—*BAM!*—he erupted into laughter. Not polite chuckles. Not a smirk. A full-blown, thunderous, “Oh dear heavens, this is absurd!” fit that startled Blanche more than any funeral procession.

“And this,” Arthur gasped between laughs, “was the secret? You’re a marvel, Foker! A model of loyalty! Could I possibly stand between Blanche and such unwavering devotion? Could I stand between Miss Amory and fifteen thousand pounds a year?”

“It is not that!” Blanche declared, puffing up with dignity. “It is not money. It is not rank. It is constancy. It is fidelity. It is a whole, trusting, loving heart offered to me—that I treasure!”

She reached for her handkerchief—but paused. Then slowly, deliberately, she put it down. “I do not disown,” she said, “that I once thought I loved you. I believed I was beloved. I clung to that hope. I prayed. I longed. But your words—your coldness—your indifference—undid me. You trifled with my heart. You flung back my troth with scorn. I’ve told Mr. Foker everything.”

“That you have,” Foker said, eyes shining with devotion.

“Everything?” Arthur asked, raising an eyebrow.
“It is I who am in fault,” Blanche said. “I won’t appeal. I bear it in silence.”
Arthur smiled sadly. “I came here expecting one thing. Heaven knows what I expected. But not this. I hope you’ll be happy with him. Truly. He deserves you.”

Foker, overwhelmed, burst into tears. “She don’t know—she can’t know how much I love her. I’m just a poor little beggar. And she takes me—says she’ll try—and I—I—love her. I ain’t worthy. Give us your hand, old boy. She forgives you. Let’s be friends.”

They shook hands. Arthur, half-laughing, half-sighing, felt oddly relieved.
“Only one person here needs pity,” he said. “You, Blanche.”

“You’re an angel!” Foker cried, wiping his eyes.
Blanche looked up, radiant, like a saint in a Victorian painting. “In spite of everything,” she said, “I must always regard Arthur as a brother. We’ve known each other years. We’ve walked the same paths. Plucked the same flowers. Arthur! Henry! Take hands and be friends!”

They did. Foker kissed Blanche’s hand, weeping. She placed her other hand gently on his head. “Foolish boy,” she murmured. “It shall be loved as it deserves. Who could resist such a silly creature?”

Frank Clavering returned, bursting in. “Pendennis! The man wants to be paid and go back. He’s had some beer.”
“I’ll go back with him,” Arthur said. “Good-bye, Blanche. God bless you, Foker. You two don’t need me here.”

But Blanche stopped him. “Wait. One word. In private.”

Foker, thrilled by the mention of “Henry,” practically danced away. “Trust you? Oh, who wouldn’t trust you?”

Alone, Blanche spoke calmly. “You never loved me, Mr. Pendennis.”
“I told you how much,” Arthur said. “I never deceived you.”
“You’re going to Laura tonight, aren’t you?”
“Was that what you had to say?”
“You never cared for me.”
“Et vous?”
“Et moi, c’est différent. I’ve been spoiled early. I cannot live without excitement. Without emotion. You offered neither. You’re bored with everything—even ambition. You had a career. You gave it up—for what? For a betise? For a scruple? Why not take the seat? Be a puritan? Why refuse what is mine by right?”

Arthur blinked. “You know all this?”
“Only within a month. But I suspected since Baymouth. N’importe. It’s not too late. He’s as if he never existed. There’s still a place in the world for you. Why not sit in Parliament? Use your talent? Give yourself a future? I take celui-là. Il est bon. Il est riche. Il est—vous le connaissez autant que moi enfin.”

She paused. “Think you that I would not prefer un homme qui fera parler de moi? If the secret comes out, I’m rich beyond millions. How does it affect me? It’s not my fault. It will never come out.”

Arthur stared. “You’ll tell Harry everything?”
“Je comprends. Vous refusez.” She said it with a sneer. “I’ll tell Harry when we’re married. You won’t betray me, will you? You, who hold a defenseless girl’s secret—will you use it against her? S’il me plait de le cacher, mon secret; pourquoi le donnerai-je? Je l’aime, mon pauvre père, voyez-vous? I’d rather live with that man than with you, your fades intriguers of the world. I must have emotions—il m’en donne. Il m’écrit. Il écrit très bien—comme un pirate—comme un Bohémien—comme un homme.”

Arthur sighed. “The pirate would tire you, like the rest.”
“Eh! Il me faut des émotions,” she insisted.

And there it was—the truth of Blanche Amory. Arthur had never seen her so clearly, nor so completely. But he also saw more than she was. Because beneath the passion, the drama, the fire—there was only performance. A sham enthusiasm. A fake grief. A counterfeit love. Each emotion blazed for a moment, then fizzled out, replaced by the next theatrical spark.

And as Arthur walked away, carrying his carpet-bag and his broken dreams, he realized something:
He should have bought a return ticket.
