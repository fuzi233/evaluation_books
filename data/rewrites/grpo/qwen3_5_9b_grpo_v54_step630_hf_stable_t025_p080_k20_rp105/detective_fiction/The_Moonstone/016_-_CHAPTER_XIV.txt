The most direct route from my lady's sitting-room to the garden lay through the shrubbery path, a thoroughfare familiar to all who knew the estate. It was, however, the particular haunt of Mr. Franklin Blake; whenever he wandered the grounds and could not be located elsewhere, he invariably sought refuge there.

I confess, with some shame, that I am a stubborn old man. The more resolutely Sergeant Cuff attempted to shield his thoughts from my prying gaze, the more determinedly I endeavored to peer behind his back. As we turned onto the shaded path, I tried a different tack.

"As matters stand," I remarked, "if I were in your shoes, I should be utterly at a loss."

"If you were in my position," the Sergeant replied, "you would have already formed a conclusion. Any lingering doubt you might harbor regarding your own deductions would now be dispelled. For the present, Mr. Betteredge, let us not concern ourselves with the nature of those conclusions. I have not summoned you here to corner me like a badger; I have brought you out to solicit information. You could have offered it within the house, yet doors and eavesdroppers have a peculiar talent for converging. In my profession, we cultivate a distinct appreciation for the open air."

Who was capable of outwitting *this* man? I surrendered my resistance and waited with as much patience as I could muster for his next move.

"We shall not dwell on the motives of your young lady," the Sergeant continued. "It is merely a pity that she refuses to assist me, for her reluctance renders this inquiry more arduous than it need be. We must now attempt to unravel the mystery of the smear upon the door—which, I assure you, is synonymous with the mystery of the Diamond itself—through alternative means. I have resolved to interrogate the servants, to probe their minds and actions, Mr. Betteredge, rather than rummage through their wardrobes. Before I commence, however, I require your assistance. You are an observant man; did you detect anything unusual among the staff following the discovery of the Diamond's loss? Allow for the natural panic, of course. Was there any specific discord? Did anyone appear out of sorts, unexpectedly irritable, or strangely indisposed?"

I barely had time to recall Rosanna Spearman's sudden illness at yesterday's dinner before I noticed Sergeant Cuff's eyes dart sharply toward the shrubbery. A soft exclamation escaped him: "Hullo!"

"What is the matter?" I asked.

"A touch of the rheumatics in my back," the Sergeant announced in a volume loud enough to invite a third party to our conversation. "We shall likely see a change in the weather soon."

A few paces further brought us to the corner of the house. Turning sharply to the right, we ascended the terrace and descended the central steps into the garden below. The Sergeant halted in the open space, granting us a view of the surroundings on all sides.

"Regarding that young person, Rosanna Spearman?" he inquired. "Given her appearance, it is unlikely she possesses a lover. Yet, for her own sake, I must ask you immediately: has she, poor wretch, secured a sweetheart like the others?"

What on earth did he mean by such a question under the current circumstances? I stared at him, unable to formulate an answer.

"I observed Rosanna Spearman concealing herself in the shrubbery as we passed," the Sergeant stated.

"When you uttered 'Hullo'?"

"Yes—precisely when I said 'Hullo!' If a sweetheart is involved, her hiding is of little consequence. If there is none, as appears to be the case in this household, then her concealment is a highly suspicious circumstance, and it will be my painful duty to act upon it."

What, in God's name, was I to say? I knew the shrubbery was Mr. Franklin's favorite promenade; I knew he would likely return from the station via that route; and I knew that Penelope had repeatedly caught her fellow-servant lingering there, always declaring to me that Rosanna's intent was to attract Mr. Franklin's attention. If my daughter was correct, she might well have been lying in wait for Mr. Franklin's return when the Sergeant spotted her. I was trapped between the dilemma of presenting Penelope's fanciful notion as my own truth, or allowing an unfortunate creature to suffer the grave consequences of arousing Sergeant Cuff's suspicion. Driven by pure pity for the girl—on my soul and my reputation, driven by pure pity—I provided the necessary explanations, informing the Sergeant that Rosanna had been foolish enough to set her heart on Mr. Franklin Blake.

Sergeant Cuff never laughed. On the rare occasions when amusement struck him, he merely curled the corners of his lips upward, nothing more. He curled them now.

"Would it not be wiser to claim she is mad enough to be an ugly girl and merely a servant?" he asked. "Falling in love with a gentleman of Mr. Franklin Blake's manners and appearance does not seem to me to be the most insane aspect of her conduct by any means. Nevertheless, I am glad the matter is clarified; it relieves the mind to have things settled. Yes, I shall keep this secret, Mr. Betteredge. I like to be tender toward human infirmity—though I rarely get opportunities to exercise that virtue in my line of work. Do you suppose Mr. Franklin Blake harbors no suspicion of the girl's fancy for him? Ah! He would have discovered it swiftly enough had she been beautiful. Ugly women endure a harsh existence in this world; let us hope it is compensated for them in the next. You possess a lovely garden here, and a meticulously kept lawn. Observe for yourself how much better the flowers appear with grass surrounding them rather than gravel. No, thank you. I shall not take a rose. It breaks my heart to sever them from the stem. Just as it breaks yours, you know, when there is wrongdoing in the servants' hall. Did you notice anything you could not explain regarding the servants when the loss of the Diamond was first discovered?"

I had managed quite well with Sergeant Cuff thus far. However, the sly manner in which he slipped that final question into the conversation placed me on guard. In plain English, I did not relish the prospect of aiding his inquiries when they led him, like a snake in the grass, among my fellow-servants.

"I noticed nothing," I replied, "save that we all lost our heads simultaneously, myself included."

"Oh," the Sergeant said, "is that all you have to report?"

I answered, with (as I flattered myself) an impassive countenance, "That is all."

Sergeant Cuff's dismal eyes fixed themselves hard upon my face.

"Mr. Betteredge," he said, "do you have any objection to oblige me by shaking hands? I have developed an extraordinary liking for you."

(Why he should have chosen the precise moment when I was deceiving him to offer me this proof of his good opinion is beyond all comprehension! I felt a surge of pride—I truly did feel a little proud of having bested the celebrated Cuff at last!)

We returned to the house. The Sergeant requested a private room, instructing me to summon the indoor servants, one by one, in order of rank, from highest to lowest.

I escorted Sergeant Cuff into my own chamber and then gathered the servants in the hall. Rosanna Spearman appeared among them, much as usual. She moved with the same swiftness as the Sergeant, and I suspect she had overheard his remarks concerning the servants in general just prior to his discovery of her. There she stood, looking as though she had never heard of a place called the shrubbery in her entire life.

I sent them in individually as directed. The cook was the first to enter the Court of Justice, otherwise known as my room. She remained but a brief moment. Her report upon exiting: "Sergeant Cuff is depressed in his spirits; yet, Sergeant Cuff is a perfect gentleman." My lady's own maid followed. She remained considerably longer. Her report: "If Sergeant Cuff does not believe a respectable woman, he might keep his opinion to himself, at any rate!" Penelope went next. She stayed only a moment or two. Her report: "Sergeant Cuff is much to be pitied. He must have been crossed in love, father, when he was a young man." The first housemaid followed Penelope. Like my lady's maid, she lingered for a long time. Her report: "I did not enter her ladyship's service, Mr. Betteredge, to be doubted to my face by a low police-officer!" Rosanna Spearman went next. She remained longer than any of the others. There was no report upon her exit—only dead silence and lips as pale as ashes. Samuel, the footman, followed Rosanna. He stayed a minute or two. His report: "Whoever blacks Sergeant Cuff's boots ought to be ashamed of himself." Nancy, the kitchen-maid, went last. She remained a minute or two. Her report: "Sergeant Cuff has a heart; _he_ doesn't cut jokes, Mr. Betteredge, with a poor hard-working girl."

Upon returning to the Court of Justice to inquire if there were further commands, I found the Sergeant at his old trick—gazing out the window and whistling "The Last Rose of Summer" to himself.

"Any discoveries, sir?" I inquired.

"If Rosanna Spearman requests leave to go out," said the Sergeant, "let the poor thing depart; but inform me beforehand."

I might as well have held my tongue regarding Rosanna and Mr. Franklin! It was plain enough; the unfortunate girl had fallen under Sergeant Cuff's suspicions, despite all my efforts to prevent it.

"I hope you do not think Rosanna is implicated in the loss of the Diamond?" I ventured to say.

The corners of the Sergeant's melancholy mouth curled upward, and he looked hard into my face, just as he had done in the garden.

"I think I had better not tell you, Mr. Betteredge," he said. "You might lose your head, you know, for the second time."

I began to doubt whether I had truly been one too many for the celebrated Cuff, after all! It was a relief that our conversation was interrupted by a knock at the door and a message from the cook. Rosanna Spearman _had_ requested to go out, for the usual reason: her head was bad, and she craved a breath of fresh air. At a signal from the Sergeant, I agreed. "Which is the servants' way out?" he asked when the messenger had departed. I showed him the path. "Lock the door of your room," the Sergeant commanded. "And if anyone asks for me, say I am in there, composing my mind." He curled the corners of his lips once more and vanished.

Left alone under these circumstances, a devouring curiosity compelled me to make some discoveries of my own.

It was evident that Sergeant Cuff's suspicions of Rosanna had been aroused by something he uncovered during his examination of the servants in my room. Now, the only two servants (excluding Rosanna herself) who had remained under scrutiny for any significant duration were my lady's own maid and the first housemaid; these were also the two women who had taken the lead in persecuting their unfortunate fellow-servant from the outset. Reaching this conclusion, I casually visited them in the servants' hall. Finding tea in progress, I instantly invited myself to join the meal. (For, _nota bene_, a drop of tea is to a woman's tongue what a drop of oil is to a wasting lamp.)

My reliance on the tea-pot as an ally was not unrewarded. In less than half an hour, I knew as much as the Sergeant himself.

It appeared that my lady's maid and the housemaid had neither believed in Rosanna's illness of the previous day. These two devils—I ask your pardon, but how else can one describe a pair of spiteful women?—had stolen upstairs at intervals during the Thursday afternoon. They had tried Rosanna's door and found it locked; they had knocked and received no answer; they had listened and heard no sound within. When the girl descended to tea and was subsequently sent back to bed, still out of sorts, the two aforementioned devils tried her door once more, found it locked, looked at the keyhole and discovered it stopped up, saw a light beneath the door at midnight, and heard the crackling of a fire (a fire in a servant's bedroom in the month of June!) at four in the morning. All this they had reported to Sergeant Cuff. In return for their eagerness to enlighten him, he had eyed them with sour and suspicious glances, making it plain that he did not believe either of them. Hence, the unfavorable reports they carried from the examination. Hence, also (without reckoning the influence of the tea-pot), their readiness to let their tongues run wild on the subject of the Sergeant's ungracious behavior.

Having gained some experience of the great Cuff's roundabout methods, and having last seen him evidently bent on following Rosanna privately when she departed for her walk, it seemed clear to me that he deemed it inadvisable to let the lady's maid and the housemaid know how materially they had assisted him. They were precisely the sort of women who, if he had treated their evidence as trustworthy, would have been puffed up by it and said or done something that would have put Rosanna Spearman on her guard.

I walked out into the fine summer afternoon, feeling profound sorrow for the poor girl and deep unease regarding the turn events had taken. Drifting toward the shrubbery some time later, I encountered Mr. Franklin. After returning from seeing his cousin off at the station, he had been with my lady, engaging in a long conversation with her. She had informed him of Miss Rachel's inexplicable refusal to allow her wardrobe to be examined, and had put him in such low spirits concerning my young lady that he seemed to shrink from speaking on the subject. The family temper appeared in his face that evening for the first time in my experience of him.
