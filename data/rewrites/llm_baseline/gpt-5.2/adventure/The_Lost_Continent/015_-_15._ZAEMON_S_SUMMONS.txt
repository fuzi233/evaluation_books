In the first mornings of the world—so the old stories say, in the tongue that my mother’s mother still mouthed over her bread—when the Gods shaped man and stepped back to watch what would become of him, They left corners of the earth where a human life could not easily take root. Of all such places in Atlantis none was so stubbornly ill-suited as that steaming margin which faces the savage continents of Europe and Africa.

The common folk would not go near it. In their market-speech, in the half-remembered prayers they muttered while kneading dough, they called it cursed ground: a place where the evil dead walked in daylight and finished what bog and beast began. Men who fled there from justice—so the gossip ran from hearth to hearth—came back of their own choosing, begging for chains rather than those bright, fabulous terrors.

I had been taught to weigh such tales. Much of it was myth, yes—myth as a cook’s “pinch” is myth, a measure that changes with the hand—yet enough of the old dread proved real that every hour demanded caution. The mountains kept their bellies on the boil. Tremors were as common as the tick of a pestle in a mortar. Invisible vapours, sour as spoiled broth, slid low through the fern-water and struck without warning; more than once I only knew them by the way birds fell silent and insects stopped their thin fiddling, as if the whole air held its breath, before the stench seized my throat and I stumbled away choking. Rivers ran clear and innocent-looking, yet carried poisons that burned the belly. The ground split into sudden cracks that drank whole streams; later, those stolen waters were spat out again in explosions of steam and mud that could flay a man to the bone. At any moment the land might belch boiling mire or a tongue of molten rock.

And still—beneath that violence—stood forests so old their age felt like a word that had no translation. Their black trunks rose out of warm mist; their crowns knitted the sky to a dim green ceiling. Fires were rare under those trees. Everything below was water: ankle-deep, knee-deep, sometimes to the waist, always warm, always wet. Steam wrapped the air and made each breath a swallow of bathhouse heat. It mortified the strength, and strength was the first ingredient required there.

The second was cunning, for these dangerous lands were the kingdom of lizards—endless families of them, some small enough to face with a spear and a steady nerve, others so huge that the mind refused their shape until the eye had no choice. I saw one rise out of a lake after feeding, and the wet earth shook and pulsed with its tread as if the whole swamp had a heart. It could have taken Phorenice’s mammoth into its belly as a man swallows a fig. Horny plates armoured its head and body, and along its back and tail and limbs ran spines that tore slivers from the trees as it pushed through them.

Now and again such monsters were caught in some great fissure of the ground, but not often. They moved quickly, and their sagacity was keen. They seemed to know when the worst boilings of the mountains would come; then they found safety in deeper lakes or buried themselves in mud wallows. One danger, which drove men frantic, did them no harm at all: heat. They lay in pools where sudden steam-bursts made the water leap into boiling fountains. I saw one run across a flow of molten rock that threatened to cut it off and pass without so much as a singe.

Among such neighbours my new life was thrown, and from the outset it was hard and perilous. I came close to knowing what Fear truly is, and only a fervent trust in the most High Gods—only the belief that my life remained under Their fostering care—kept me from that black knowledge.

For long enough, until I learned something of the ways of this steaming, sweltering land, I was as miserable as even Phorenice could have wished to see me. Constant wet rotted my clothes from my back until I went near naked, like a savage from beyond the seas. Agues racked my limbs, and I could find no proper herbs for their easing; the plants I tried tasted of tar and resin so rank I could not force them down without gagging. For days my best food was tree-grubs—fat, pale, and sweet in a sickly way when you crushed them between the teeth—and bitter leaves that promised nourishment and delivered only more weakness. When I did kill beasts, I knew too little of their qualities; I ate flesh that turned my stomach and made my guts knot like bad twine. Hunger taught quickly, and pain taught more.

Yet a man is born adaptable. As the months dragged on, I learned the limits of this new life and gathered some knowledge of its resources. I found a great black tree with a hollow core and a hole near the roots that led into its middle. There I harboured—at first with gratitude, like a traveller finding a warm oven-mouth in winter—until one night a monstrous lizard came nosing at the trunk. Its sheer weight made the tree rock like a sapling. It set its mouth to the opening and tried to suck me out as a bird draws a worm from rotten wood. The tongue that probed for me was hard as horn and slick as boiled hide. I could no more have wounded it than I could have harmed a mountain. By the will of the Gods I escaped; by my own will I took warning.

So I cut myself a ladder of footholds up the inside of the trunk until I reached a point ten man-heights from the ground, and there I notched more steps. With branches I made a floor to lie upon. Later, for luxury, I carved arrow-slit windows, narrow as a knife cut in bread, through which I could watch the steaming world below without being seen. I carried up sand for a hearth, grain by grain in my hands and in a skin, and there I learned to cook my victuals above the dangers of the open ground.

Those first attempts were crude, but even in exile the body demands its small ceremonies. I discovered which woods smoked sweetly and which filled the mouth with bitterness. I learned to split fish along the spine and pin the flesh flat on green sticks, so that the heat would take it evenly. The large-scaled river fish, when you scraped them clean with a sharpened shell and rubbed their bellies with crushed sour berries, gave a savour that—if I closed my eyes—almost recalled the salted dishes of home. The lesser turtles were easier captured if you waited at dusk when the air cooled and their movements grew sluggish; their flesh, stewed with a handful of aromatic bark, made a broth that strengthened me. I could not find grains for gruel, and every green thing fit to chew reeked of tar, but at least I no longer felt my ribs threaten to break through my skin.

Still the peril remained: the greater lizards, the sudden scalding floods, the treacherous ground. And as months mounted into years and the brute part of my nature grew more satisfied, other longings rose—hungers no turtle soup could answer.

From the ivory of a river-horse’s tooth I tried to carve a likeness of Nais as last I had seen her. My fingers were loving, my will earnest, but my art was dull. Time and again the face came out clumsy and pitiful: a curve too heavy, a mouth too stiff, eyes without her living softness. Yet even in that rude work there were hints—an outline here, a tilt there—that set my heart burning anew. I would sit in my tree-house and hold the ivory by the hearth’s low glow, “eating” it with my eyes as if looking could nourish me. It did not satisfy; it sharpened the ache to know her fate, to be with her again, or at least to see the place where she lay.

Other men of the Priests’ Clan had come into these Dangerous Lands. By mortifying the flesh, by fasting and cold vigils and the hard disciplines, they won intimacy with the Higher Mysteries that carried them beyond mere learning and repetition. Some returned to the abodes of men with knowledge that the vulgar called magic and miracles. The common people, hungry for wonders and easily bribed by spectacle, chased such arts with increased ardour, and the priests—since all arts must be allowed that serve a holy cause—did not rebuke them overmuch.

But I could not free my mind for those abstruse studies. On the voyage home from Yucatan I had communed with the Mysteries with growing insight; here, in exile, my mind was not my own. Nais held it with a lien that could not be foreclosed. Her sweet trespass was my chief solace, and it made my thoughts circle always back to her name, a name whose sound in our old dialect carried shades the court-speech could never hold. In the common tongue she was merely “Nais.” In the older word she was—no, there is no clean rendering: the syllables meant tenderness braided with light, a vow spoken low at dawn. That untranslatable richness lived in me like a flavour only my own mouth remembered.

At last my longing could no further be denied. Through one of my narrow windows I could see, far away, a mountain-top whitened with perpetual snow. Each night our Lord the Sun dyed that snow with blood as He set. Night after night I watched that ruddy light until my eyes watered. Night after night I remembered how, in my days of entering the priesthood, it had been my duty to adore our Lord as He rose behind those same snows. Always the thought followed: from that distant crest I could see across the continent to the Sacred Mount—the place with the city below it, where I had buried my love alive.

So I gave way and set out.

It was a perilous journey. In heavy mists that clung to the lower ground, my way lay blind before me. I lost it continually. To say I traversed three times the direct distance is modest. In those swamps the great lizards hunted, and because the country was new to me I did not know the safe hollows, the deadfalls, the places where a man might crouch and become no more than another stump. A hundred times I was within an ace of being seen and devoured at a mouthful. Yet the High Gods still desired me for Their own purposes; more than once I saw a huge head swing my way and then, as if the beast’s eyes had filmed, pass on. I did not call it luck. I did not call it skill. I called it the will of the Gods.

Twice rivers of scalding water roared boiling across my path, sudden and furious. I could not ford them without being cooked alive. I waited, shivering in steam, until I had gathered enough black timber to lash together rafts that would give me dry ferriage. The timber stank of resin; the rope I twisted from grasses cut my palms; my arms burned with labour. In another life I would have judged such work by the neatness of the knot, the soundness of the binding—like judging a well-trussed bird. Here I judged it by whether it held.

The journey was infinitely tedious, yet to me—after years of waiting—time ran on winged feet. I had been separated from my love until the strain was unbearable. Let me but see from a distance the place where she lay. Let me fill my eyes with it for a while. Then I could go back to my tree and remain patiently, awaiting whatever the Gods willed.

As I rose above the region of trees, the air grew chill. The steam thinned; sound changed. Below, the swamp had been loud with constant dripping and the hiss of vapours, the ceaseless rasp of insects, the sudden plop of unseen bodies sliding into water. Up here, those wet noises fell away. The wind took their place, a clean, sharp voice that made the mind feel bare. I made buskins and a coat of woven grasses to protect my body. The grasses were harsh; I softened them by rubbing them between stones and warming them by my skin before weaving, as one softens stubborn fibre before it can be made into something serviceable.

Higher still, where snow lay eternal, blown into gullies and frozen into solid banks and bergs of ice, progress became a labour in itself. The chill numbed me and stole my strength. My breath rasped; my chest ached; each step sank and slid. Overhead a huge man-eating bird followed me with forbidding swoops, sometimes coming so close that I had to threaten it with my spear. It lived by pulling down creatures whose weakness it scented, and up here weakness had a scent as plain as blood.

Yet the lure ahead made these difficulties seem small. Though the mountain air agreed with me ill, bringing sickness and panting, I pressed on with what speed I could muster. Time after time I thought the next spurt would bring me to the view my soul yearned for; time after time another bank of snow and ice rose before me.

At last I reached the crest.

I leaned on my spear and gave thanks to the most High Gods for Their protection and favour. The world spread below me in hard clarity. Far, far away I could see the Sacred Mountain, its ring of fires burning pale under the day. The splendid city at its foot was too distant to be seen, but I knew its position as a man knows the place of a scar beneath cloth. I knew its plan, and my soul went out to that throne of granite in the square before the royal pyramid where, years before, I had buried Nais alive.

Had Phorenice left the tomb unviolated?

I stood and filled my eye with the prospect, warming even to the smoke of mountains that I recognised like old acquaintances. My love for Nais burned so fiercely it seemed to push everything else out of me. It was as if my whole being leaned forward toward her, leaving room for nothing beside.

For long enough a voice seemed dimly to be calling my name, but I gave it no regard. I had come to that hoary summit for communion with Nais alone, and I wanted no interruption. The wind’s clean howl, the thin squeak of snow under my feet, the distant crack of ice settling—these were the only sounds I would accept. Below, the swamp’s constant hiss and drip was gone; up here even my own heartbeat sounded loud, like a drum in an empty room.

But the voice grew louder, insistent, and at last I started as if struck. Since parting with Tob and his company, I had seen no other human being. I gripped my weapon and called out, asking who spoke.

The reply came clearly from up the slopes. I saw a man coming toward me over the snows.

He was old and feeble. His body bent; his hair and beard were white as the ground he trod. Yet he moved with incredible speed for such years and seeming frailty. In his hand he carried the glowing Symbol of our Lord the Sun, and I knew that holy strength could lend power beyond flesh.

As he came closer, recognition settled on me like a cloak. Zaemon.

He made the sign of the Seven. I returned it, completing the form with due ceremony. Then he saluted me as messenger appointed by the High Council of the Priests seated before the Ark of the Mysteries. I bowed in humble obeisance.

“In all things I will obey the orders that you put before me,” I said.

“Such is your duty, my brother.” His voice cut through the wind as if it owned the air. “The command is that you return immediately to the Sacred Mountain, so that if human means may still prevail, you—as the most skilful general Atlantis owns within her borders—may yet save the country from final wreck and punishment. The woman Phorenice persists in her infamies. The poor land groans under her heel. And now she has laid siege to our Sacred Mountain itself, and swears that not one soul shall be left alive in all Atlantis who does not bend humbly to her will.”

“It is a command and I obey it.” The words came readily—oath-trained words, the old liturgy of obedience. But another hunger rose behind them, stronger than discipline. “Yet let me ask of another matter that is intimate to both of us. What of Nais?”

“Nais rests where you left her, untouched. Phorenice knows by her arts—she has stolen nearly all the ancient knowledge now—that still you live. She keeps Nais unharmed beneath the granite throne in the hope that some time she may use her as a weapon against you. Little she knows the sternness of our Priests’ creed, my brother. Why, even I, who am the girl’s father, would sacrifice her blithely if her death or ruin might do a tittle of good to Atlantis.”

“You go beyond me with your devotion,” I said, and heard how my own voice had changed: the old cadences still there, but roughened by years of swamp and solitude.

Zaemon leaned toward me, brow glowering. “What!”

“Or my old blind adherence to the ancient dogma has been sapped and weakened by events.” I spoke plainly, tasting each word as if it were a foreign spice, measuring whether it would burn the tongue. “You must buy my full obedience, Zaemon, if you want it. Promise me Nais—and your arts, I know, can snatch her—and I will be true servant to the High Council of the Priests, and will die in the last ditch if need be for the carrying out of order. But let me see Nais given over to the fury of that wanton woman, and I shall have no inwards left except to take my vengeance and to see Atlantis piled up in ruins as her funeral-stone.”

Zaemon’s look turned bitter, as if I had served him rancid wine in a sacred cup.

“And you are the man the High Council thought to trust as they would trust one of themselves? Truly we are in an age of weak men and faithless now. But, my lord—nay, I must call you brother still: we cannot be too nice in our choosing to-day—you are the best there is, and we must have you. We little thought you would ask a price for your generalship, having once taken oath on the walls of the Ark of the Mysteries itself that always, come what might, you would be servant of the High Council of the Clan without fee and without hope of advancement. But this is the age of broken vows, and you are going no more than trim with the fashion. Indeed, brother, perhaps I should thank you for being no more greedy in your demands.”

“You may spare me your taunts.” The wind pressed cold against my teeth; my mouth was dry as old bread. “You, by self-denial and profound search into the highest of the Higher Mysteries, have made yourself something wiser than human. I have preserved my humanity, and with it its powers and frailties. It seems each of us has his proper uses, or you would not be come now here to me. Rather you would have done the generalling yourself.”

“You make a warm defence,” Zaemon said. “But I have no leisure to stand before you with argument. Come to the Sacred Mountain, fight me this wanton upstart Empress, and by my beard you shall have your Nais as you left her as a reward.”

“It is a command of the High Council which shall be obeyed,” I said. “I will come with my brother now, as soon as he is rested.”

“Nay,” said Zaemon, and there was something in the word—an edge like a knife slid free of its sheath. “I have no tiredness. And as for coming with me, there you will not be able. But follow at what pace you may.”

He turned and set off down the snowy slopes.

I followed.

Gradually he distanced me. His speed increased, though the incline was treacherous and the snow deep. Soon he passed out of my sight around the spur of an ice-cliff, and I found myself alone on the mountain side.

Alone—yes, truly alone. For his footmarks, once deep, grew shallower, less noticeable. I had to stoop to find them, as if searching for a lost word in a dead language. Presently even those faint impressions vanished entirely. The great flank of the mountain lay before me trackless, untrodden by the foot of man since time began.

I was not shaken by amazement. It was beyond my art to compass such a thing—I had spent my exile more with memories of Nais than with study of the uppermost recesses of the Higher Mysteries in which Zaemon was so prodigiously wise—but I had some inkling of his powers. Zaemon, I knew, would be back again in his dwelling on the Sacred Mountain, shaken and breathless, even before I had found the end of his tracks.

It behoved me to join him there in the quickest possible time.

I had his promise now for my reward, and I knew he would carry it into effect. Before, I had made an error. I had valued Atlantis most, and Nais—my private love—second. Now I meant to be honest with others even as with myself. Though all the world were hanging on my choice, I could but love my Nais most, and serve her first and foremost of all.
