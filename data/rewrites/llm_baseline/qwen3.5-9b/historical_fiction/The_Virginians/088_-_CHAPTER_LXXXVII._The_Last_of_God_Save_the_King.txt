What a perverse law of fate seems to dictate that I should forever find myself in the minority? If a motion were brought forward to surrender this realm to the Pretender of Rome or the Grand Turk, submitting it to a new sovereign's religion, it would surely pass—except for my vote, which I would undoubtedly cast against it. Back home in Virginia, I found myself disagreeing with everyone, as was my custom. To the Patriots, I was branded a Tory; to the Tories, I was immediately labeled a dangerous Republican. The times were utterly out of joint. Oh, cursed spite! Within a year of arriving in Virginia, I longed to be back by the banks of the Waveney. Yet, the situation was so fraught with peril that I could not abandon my mother—a solitary woman—to face potential war and disaster. Neither would she leave the country at such a critical juncture, nor should a man of spirit desert his post.

At His Excellency's table, amidst the generous flow of claret, many of the well-disposed agreed on a specific course of action, raising countless brimming bumpers in a toast. No, it was *statue signum, signifer*! We Cavaliers would rally around it. At these moments, our Governor spoke with the courage of the bravest. Among all my acquaintances in Virginia, Madam Esmond stood out as the most consistent. Many of our gentlefolk had gathered in Williamsburg, proposing to treat Her Excellency, the Governor's lady, to a ball when news of the Boston Port Bill reached us. Immediately, the House of Burgesses adopted an indignant protest against Parliament's measure, decreeing a solemn day of fasting, humiliation, and prayer across the country to avert the calamity of civil war.

Meanwhile, the invitations to my Lady Dunmore had already been issued and accepted. The gentlemen resolved that their ball would proceed on the appointed evening, with sackcloth and ashes reserved for several days afterward.

"A ball!" exclaimed Madam Esmond. "I am to attend a gathering hosted by rebels who intend to publicly insult His Majesty a week hence! I would rather die!" She wrote to the stewards stating that, given the dangerous state of the country, she could not possibly attend.

Her surprise was immense the following time she rode abroad in her chair. She was greeted by a hundred voices, white and black alike, shouting, "Huzzah, Madam!" and "Heaven bless your ladyship!" They clearly interpreted her refusal to go to the ball as an act of supreme patriotism. To ensure there was no misunderstanding, she leaned out of the chair and cried out, "God save the King" as loudly as she could. The crowd echoed her, crying "God save the King." Indeed, everyone shouted "God save the King" in those days.

On the night of the entertainment, my poor Harry, serving as a Burgess and one of the hosts, donned his scarlet uniform of Wolfe's regiment—which he would so soon exchange for another color—and departed with Madam Fanny to the ball. My Lady Warrington and her humble servant, being strangers in the land and essentially English, were permitted by Madam to attend the assembly from which she, of course, absented herself. I had the honor of dancing a country dance with the lady of Mount Vernon, whom I found a most lively, pretty, and amiable partner. However, I must note that my wife's praises of her were received with a very grim acceptance by my mother when Lady Warrington recounted the evening's events. Could not Sir George Warrington have danced with My Lady Dunmore or her daughters, or anyone else but Mrs. Washington? To be sure, the Colonel thought so highly of himself and his wife that he undoubtedly considered her the grandest lady in the room, despite remembering him as a road surveyor earning a mere guinea a day! Well, indeed! There was no measuring the pride of these provincial upstarts. As for this gentleman, my Lord Dunmore's partiality for him had evidently turned his head.

Whether Mr. Washington possessed similar pride remains unknown to me; I only know that my good mother never could be induced to love him or anything associated with him. She was no less displeased with him for attending the ball than she was for his conduct three days later, when the day of fasting and humiliation arrived. He attended the service performed by our new clergyman. She invited Mr. Belman to dinner that day along with various colonial authorities. The clergyman excused himself. Madam Esmond tossed her head and said he might do as he pleased. She staged a magnificent dinner, lighting her house brightly while the rest of the city remained shrouded in darkness and gloom. She requested Mr. Hardy, one of His Excellency's aides-de-camp, to sing "God Save the King." The people listening from the street outside believed it was part of a religious service Madam was celebrating. Then, however, she called for "Britons, Strike Home!" The simple young gentleman, fresh from Europe, began to perform it, when suddenly a great yell erupted from the street. A large stone, hurled by a rebellious hand, crashed into the punch bowl before me, scattering its contents across our dining room.

My mother walked to the window, undaunted. I can still see her rigid little figure standing there, head thrown high, frilled arms outstretched, with the twinkling stars as her backdrop, singing in chorus, "Britons, strike home! Strike home!" The crowd before the palings shouted and roared, "Silence! For shame! Go back!" but she refused to retreat. "Fling more stones, if you dare!" cried the brave little lady. More might have followed, but some gentlemen emerging from the Raley Tavern intervened between her and the mob. "You mustn't insult a lady," said a voice I believe I recognized. "Hurrah, Colonel! Hurrah, Captain! God bless your honour!" cried the people in the street. Thus, the enemies were pacified.

My mother, insisting the disturbance was entirely over, attempted to have Mr. Hardy sing another song. He gave a sickly grin and replied, "I really did not like to sing to such accompaniments," and the concert for that evening concluded. Though I must admit, some scoundrels returned that night, frightening my poor wife almost out of her wits and smashing every single window in the front of our tenement. "Britons, Strike Home!" proved to be too much; Madam should have contented herself with "God Save the King."

Militia was drilled, bullets cast, and supplies of ammunition prepared. Cunning plans were devised to thwart royal ordinances. Yet, to be sure, "God Save the King" was the cry everywhere. In response to my objections against the gentleman-Patriots—"Why, you are scheming for separation; you are inviting the inevitable wrath of the greatest power on earth!"—the answer was invariably, "We mean no separation at all! We yield to no men in loyalty; we glory in the name of Britons," and so on, ad infinitum. Powder barrels were stacked in the cellar, the fuse laid, but Mr. Fawkes persisted in his dutiful petitions to King and Parliament, intending no harm whatsoever.

It is true that when I spoke of our country's power, I imagined she would exert it fully. I thought she would not expect to conquer three million fellow-Britons on their own soil with merely a few battalions, six generals from Bond Street, and a handful of mercenaries hired from Germany. As if we needed to insult the thirteen colonies alongside subduing them, we must attack them with these hordes of Hessians and murderers from Indian wigwams. Was our great quarrel not to be fought without foreign aid and these defenders? Ah! It is easy now, since we are worsted, to glance over the map of the vast empire torn from us and declare how we ought not to have lost it. Long Island should have annihilated Washington's army; he should never have emerged from Valley Forge except as a prisoner. The South was ours after the Battle of Camden, but for the inconceivable meddling of the Commander-in-Chief at New York, who paralyzed the efforts of the only capable British general who appeared during the war, sending him into that miserable dead-end at Yorktown, from which he could only emerge defeated and captured. Oh, for just one more week! One more day! One more hour of darkness or light!

Reading over our American campaigns from their unfortunate beginning to their inglorious end, now that we can see the enemy's movements and conditions as clearly as our own, I imagine we can discern how an advance or a march might have placed enemies in our power who lacked the means to resist, thereby changing the entire outcome of the struggle. But it was ordained by Heaven, and for the good, as we can now have no doubt, of both empires, that the great Western Republic should separate from us. The gallant soldiers who fought on her side, their indomitable and heroic chief above all, earned the glory of facing and overcoming not only veteran soldiers well-provisioned and hardened to war, but also wretchedness, cold, hunger, internal dissension, and treason within their own ranks. All must have collapsed under such pressure but for the pure, unquenchable flame of patriotism that burned eternally in the bosom of that heroic leader.
