It is a truth seldom disputed that women possess an uncanny ability to discern the character of men—an intuition so innate it defies logic, scorns evidence, and pronounces judgment with unshakable certainty. Yet what is less often acknowledged is how rarely this faculty permits revision. Once a verdict is rendered, no appeal may overturn it. Even when proven wrong by all mortal standards, such judgment clings to its decree with the obstinacy of prejudice, for the very possibility of contradiction taints it from the outset. A woman’s divination is not impartial testimony; it is a personal creed, fiercely defended.

“Tell me, Mother,” the Minor Canon ventured one evening as she sat knitting in his book-lined study, “do you not think you judge Mr. Neville too harshly?”

The needles clicked. “I do not, Septimus.”

“Shall we discuss it?”

“By all means,” she replied, her cap trembling with unspoken defiance. “I am always open to discussion.”

“Excellent,” said her son, ever the peacemaker. “There is no virtue greater than openness.”

“I should hope not,” she returned, though her tone suggested the gates of debate were already barred.

“Very well. On that regrettable night, Mr. Neville acted under provocation—”

“And mulled wine,” she interposed.

“The wine, I grant you. Though both young men were equally at fault in that regard.”

“I disagree.”

“How so?”

“Because I *do*,” said the old lady. “Still, I remain open to discussion.”

Septimus sighed. “Mother, we cannot discuss if you refuse to yield an inch.”

“Blame Mr. Neville for that, not I,” she said with regal finality.

“Mr. Neville? Why?”

“Because,” she declared, retreating to bedrock principle, “he returned to this house in disgrace, intoxicated, bringing shame upon us all. And for that, he has yet to atone.”

“No one denies his error. But he has expressed remorse—”

“Had it not been for Mr. Jasper’s kindness—coming to me after service, still in his vestments, to inquire if I had been disturbed—I might never have known of the affair at all,” she said pointedly.

“In truth, I had hoped to spare you the knowledge,” Crisparkle admitted. “I meant to consult Jasper about discretion, but he reached you first.”

“Too late, then. Jasper himself was still pale as ashes the next morning, horrified by what had transpired under his roof.”

“Had I concealed it, Mother, it would have been for your peace—and theirs.”

At this, she rose, crossed the room, and kissed his forehead. “I know your heart, my dear.”

“Yet the tale spread beyond my control,” he said ruefully.

“And I said then, as I say now, that I hold no faith in Mr. Neville’s reformation.” Her cap quivered with conviction.

“I grieve to hear it—”

“As I grieve to say it,” she interrupted, needles flashing. “But my mind is fixed.”

“Yet he is diligent in his studies, eager to improve, and—dare I say—has formed some attachment to me.”

“That last merits no praise,” she sniffed. “If he boasts of it, I think the worse of him.”

“He does not boast.”

“No matter. The fact remains.”

Mr. Crisparkle observed her with affectionate amusement, knowing argument was futile.

“Consider, Septimus,” she pressed, “what would he be without his sister? Her influence steadies him; her mind sharpens his. If you praise her, what is left for him?”

The question sent the Minor Canon into brief reflection. He recalled the pair walking the riverbank at dawn, heads bent over a shared text, or in twilight beneath the skeletal arches of the ruin. He had come to realize that in teaching one, he taught both—adjusting his words for twin minds. Gossip from the Nuns’ House whispered that Helena, once so fierce, now deferred to Rosa’s gentler wisdom. How swiftly these threads had woven into his life!

As was her habit when he grew pensive, his mother bustled to the dining-room closet—a marvel of orderly indulgence—and returned with a glass of Constantia and a biscuit. Thus fortified, he resumed his duties, and the day waned into vespers.

The Cathedral’s chill drove him to a brisk walk after service, ending at his favorite vantage among the ruins. Below, the river churned with seaweed, gulls wheeled, and the gathering dusk promised storm. It was there he spotted Neville and Helena.

“A bitter evening for a walk,” he called, descending with practiced ease.

“We favor solitude,” Helena replied.

“Then let me speak plainly,” Crisparkle said, joining them. “Neville, your sister knows of my urging you to apologize for that unfortunate night?”

“She does,” said Neville.

“And yet the stain lingers. Men avoid you as hot-tempered, dangerous.”

Helena’s eyes flashed. “I hear it daily—whispers he does not deserve.”

“Then let us mend it,” Crisparkle pressed. “Neville was not blameless.”

“He was provoked,” Helena countered.

“He struck first.”

They walked in silence until Helena met his gaze. “Would you have him grovel before Drood—or Jasper, who slanders him? In your heart, you could not.”

“I have told Mr. Crisparkle,” Neville said quietly, “that to feign remorse would be false. I am still angry.”

The Minor Canon frowned. “Your hands betray you.”

“I confess it. Time may soften me, but not yet.”

Helena watched Crisparkle’s face. “It is so,” she murmured.

Neville drew a breath. “There is more. I admire Miss Bud—deeply. Were it not for the insult to myself, I would still resent how Drood treats her.”

Crisparkle stiffened. “She is pledged to another. This fancy is reckless.”

“It is not fancy! He is unworthy of her. I love her—and despise him!” The words burst forth, raw and unchecked, until Helena seized his arm.

Shamefaced, Neville covered his eyes.

Crisparkle weighed his next words carefully. “This feud ends now. I will see Drood meet you halfway. Swear to me, as a gentleman, that once he does, the matter is closed. As for this… infatuation—” He fixed Neville with a grave look. “It goes no further. Bury it.”

Neville struggled before answering hoarsely, “I swear.”

Helena, moved, pressed Crisparkle’s hand to her lips.

“Tut,” he said softly. “I am overpaid.”

As he walked away, the weight of his promise settled upon him. The path ahead was fraught—but one thing was certain: this storm would not abate without intervention.
