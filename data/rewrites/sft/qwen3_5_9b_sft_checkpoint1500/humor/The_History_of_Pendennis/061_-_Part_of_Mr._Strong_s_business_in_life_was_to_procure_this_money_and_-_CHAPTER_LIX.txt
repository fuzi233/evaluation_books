The Great Festival of Epsom Downs, where London takes its annual holiday, proved to be a veritable circus of the elite. Amidst the throngs, a comfortable open carriage, drawn by a pair of spirited horses, carried Mrs. Bungay of Paternoster Row, resplendent in attire that suggested she was a modern Solomon in all her glory. Beside her sat modest Mrs. Shandon, a steadfast friend since the dawn of their acquaintance, while the worthy publisher, Mr. Bungay, recovered from a copious luncheon by furiously shying at nearby sticks until sweat poured down his bald pate. Nearby, Finucane attended the ladies with dutiful vigilance, intercepting gentlemen connected to the publishing house who sought to pay their respects.

Mr. Archer, a man of boundless gossip, approached to pay his compliments. With the enthusiasm of a tour guide, he regaled Mrs. Bungay with the location of the Prime Minister and urged her to back "Borax," though Archer privately favored "Munmeer." He then launched into a tale of international diplomacy involving the Chinese Ambassador. Archer described how, despite bringing his own cook, the Ambassador had once suffered a culinary tragedy at Archer's own table. The cook, having seized the opportunity while Archer was absent, had slaughtered Mrs. Archer's beloved Blenheim spaniel—a dog inherited from the Duke of Marlborough and whose lineage traced back to the Battle of Malplaquet—to stuff him with forcemeat for the second course.

"Law!" gasped Mrs. Bungay, horrified.

Archer detailed the aftermath: the cook screaming up the stairs, the discovery of Fido's skin in the scullery, and the immediate severing of ties between the Archers and the Ambassador. He noted that the Lord Mayor, however, had enjoyed the dish immensely, remarking that it tasted remarkably like duck when paired with green peas. The conversation drifted to the tragic fate of Lady Flamingo, who Archer claimed was squeezing her feet down for marriage to the Ambassador, a prospect he deemed destined to cripple her permanently. Comparing her plight to Mrs. Archer's own tiny feet, Archer concluded with a sigh regarding the absurdities of cross-cultural matrimonial customs.

Suddenly, Mrs. Bungay spotted a carriage containing Mr. Pendennis and Mr. Warrington. She noted Pendennis's somber demeanor and his association with "tip-top company." Archer revealed that Pendennis possessed an annual income of eighteen hundred from land and twenty-two thousand five hundred in consols, explaining that his Uncle Major had wisely tied up the assets to prevent squandering. As they identified Lord Rochester, Lord John, and Sir James, Archer proudly declared them his "intimate friends." He singled out the Duke of Hampshire as a model of antiquarian virtue, recounting how the Duke had attended sixty-five Derbies, having first ridden a piebald pony alongside the future Prince of Wales at age seven.

Further down the line, Mrs. Bungay identified a yellow carriage surrounded by parasols. Archer explained this was Lady Clavering, accompanied by her tipsy son and Miss Amory, Lady Clavering's daughter from a first marriage. Archer hinted that Miss Amory held a secret affection for Pendennis, though he suspected her heart was elsewhere, citing the recent suicide attempt of the brewer's son, young Mr. Foker, who had been rescued from hanging by his valet due to a fatal infatuation for Miss Amory.

While the aristocracy marveled, an hour prior, a battered stagecoach known as Wheeler's "Harlequin's Head" drag had arrived from Bow Street, carrying a motley crew of "choice spirits" and shabby swashbucklers. As the Derby race thundered past, the passengers shouted wild predictions—"The Cornet!" "Muffineer!" "Blue Sleeves!"—until the signal announced the winner: Podasokus. One of the passengers, a Colonel, leaped onto the roof in jubilation, only to crash back down, cracking the wood under his weight. He roared for supper, declaring that everyone should join him regardless of cost, prompting whispers among the "dusky dandies" that he stood to win fifteen hundred pounds and urging caution lest anyone get him into debt.

In stark contrast to the Coach of the Spirits, Cab No. 2002 rolled gently along, transporting a gentleman and two ladies. Inside, a repast of lobster salad and bitter ale was enjoyed by a young lady, Fanny Bolton, and her mother. Fanny, fresh and rosy, attracted the gaze of many a dandy strolling near the "Sticks," the gambling tables where sticks were thrown for amusement. Her escort, Mr. Sam Huxter, a young surgeon, was a whirlwind of activity, filling the cab with trophies from the Sticks—including pincushions, wooden apples, and jack-in-the-boxes. He even brought a gypsy to tell fortunes, though the prophecy that Fanny must beware of a "fair man" who would prove false caused a momentary chill in the otherwise sunny atmosphere.

Then, the scene shifted. Arthur Pendennis, looking dandified in black crape and jet buttons, suddenly encountered Cab No. 2002. His mood instantly soured, replaced by a "severe demeanour" born of alarmed morality. He viewed the scene through the lens of a prude, suspecting impropriety between the ladies and the rough-looking Mr. Sam Huxter, whose bare hands and heavy boots seemed worlds apart from Pendennis's refined lavender gloves.

Inside the cab, chaos erupted. Mrs. Bolton spoke with frantic volubility, listing her knowledge of Mr. Pendennis and praising Mr. Warrington's height, nearly breaking his head on gate posts. Fanny's heart pounded as she compared Pendennis's immaculate appearance to Huxter's disheveled state, noting the blackened glove mark on Pendennis's hand versus Huxter's dirty fist. Yet, when Huxter extended his hand, the gesture softened the air. Pendennis, seeing the offer of refreshment, melted his stern features into a genial smile. He accepted Huxter's handshake, leaving a black stain on his pristine glove, and toasted the party with frothy, lukewarm beer, declaring it "delicious."

As they parted ways, the dynamic shifted again. A young lady in a dove-colored dress, Miss Amory, leaned on the arm of a stalwart gentleman with a military mustache, Captain Strong. She exchanged playful French insults with Pendennis, calling him a "monster," which he dismissed with a laugh, pointing out her safety between her mother and her guardian. However, the mention of lobsters and laughter haunted Pendennis later, as Miss Amory warned that if Huxter attended to him when sick, he would kill him.

By evening, the carriage of Lady Clavering departed, leaving behind a trail of complications. The "Begum" (Lady Clavering) was in high spirits, boasting of a lottery win, but her husband, Sir Francis, had been secretly gambling. Young Hopeful Clavering, the heir, confessed to having backed both Borax and Podasokus, revealing that his father had lost seven thousand pounds. The revelation shattered the mood; Lady Clavering wept, vowing to separate from her "blackleg" husband, while Captain Strong, trapped by his own tongue, confirmed the betrayal.

As the carriage rolled toward Grosvenor Place, the scene transformed from festive hilarity to grim domestic reality. The Major, observing the trajectory of the Clavering fortune, feared the inevitable bankruptcy that plagued the family line. Meanwhile, inside the carriage, the young heirs slept off their excesses, while Miss Amory plotted future balls and dances, oblivious to the financial abyss opening beneath her family's wheels. The day ended not with the triumph of the race, but with the sobering calculations of debt, the tears of a betrayed wife, and the silent, heavy burden of inheritance falling upon the shoulders of the innocent.
