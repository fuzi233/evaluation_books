*"To win a lady’s faith, be noble in thy aim;
Speak as befits a sacred thing—
Not lightly, nor for game.
Lead her from hollow mirth,
Point to the steadfast stars above,
And prove thy love not only worth
Her smile, but her respect and love."*

—After Mrs. Browning

Margaret was not surprised when Sarah announced Mr. Henry Lennox. She had just been thinking of him—how odd it was that the mind could summon a presence so effortlessly. The sunlight seemed to brighten as she rose, setting aside her sketching board to greet him.

"Tell Mamma, Sarah," she said, then turned to Lennox with a smile. "We’ve so many questions about Edith. It’s kind of you to come."

"Did you doubt I would?" His voice was lower now, almost intimate. "Though I confess, after chasing your cousins through the Highlands, I scarcely imagined Hampshire would be my next destination."

He laughed then, the lightness returning. "Those two were incorrigible—climbing cliffs, sailing lochs, sending their poor father into apoplexies. I couldn’t in good conscience abandon them until I saw their ship safely off at Plymouth."

"Plymouth! Edith never mentioned—but then, her letters have been so hurried. Did they truly sail on Tuesday?"

"Gone, and left me to my own devices at last." He produced a small, folded note. "Edith tasked me with delivering this."

Margaret took it, fingers brushing his, and excused herself—ostensibly to alert her mother again (Sarah must have forgotten), but in truth to read Edith’s words without his observant eyes upon her.

Left alone, Lennox surveyed the room. The morning sun gilded the worn chintz and faded carpet, revealing shabbiness at odds with Margaret’s quiet elegance. On the table lay Dante’s *Paradiso* in gilt-edged vellum, beside vocabulary lists in her precise hand. The contrast struck him: this was no frivolous girl.

When Margaret returned, her mother fluttered in after, full of apologies about their meager dinner—cold meat, owing to the servants’ ironing—though secretly pleased by the attention. Mr. Hale, she fretted, was out of spirits, having buried his face in his hands earlier when she criticized Helstone’s damp air.

Margaret felt a chill at the words. *One place he loved on earth.* She steered the conversation back to practicalities: "Papa likes Mr. Lennox. They talked for hours at the wedding. And cold meat will do perfectly—he’ll think it a luncheon."

To spare her mother, she proposed sketching. Lennox agreed with alacrity, selecting brushes with care. They wandered to the forest’s edge, where cottages sagged under ivy.

"These haunted me all last rain," Margaret said. "I ought to have drawn them sooner, before they crumble."

"Then we must seize the moment." He gestured to a felled oak. "Your throne, my queen—if you don’t mind a puddle for a footstool."

An old man hobbled from one cottage, and Margaret went to him, speaking gently while Lennox captured them both in quick strokes—she, tall and graceful; the man, bent like a wind-twisted tree.

"You’ve made us part of your landscape!" she accused later, laughing.

"I couldn’t resist. You’ve no idea how this sketch will torment me." The words slipped out, uncalculated. She pretended not to hear, rinsing her palette in the brook with pink-tipped ears.

Dinner was salvaged by a neighbor’s gift of carp and Mr. Hale’s return. Though threadbare, his bearing was unmistakably gentlemanly. Margaret watched him anxiously; something lurked beneath his courteous interest in their sketches.

"Pears!" he exclaimed suddenly. "Margaret, fetch the beurrés from the south wall."

Lennox proposed eating them outdoors, and they adjourned to the garden. As Mr. Hale meticulously peeled a pear, Margaret and Lennox strolled the terrace.

"You live an idyll here," he murmured. "I begin to scorn my own city-bound life."

"You mocked Helstone once—called it a ‘village in a tale.’"

"Scorned? No. Though I might have been... dismissive."

They rounded a bend, bees humming in the wall’s warmth. Abruptly, he stopped.

"Margaret." He seized her hand. "I wish you didn’t love this place so much—that you missed London just enough to—" She tried to pull away, but he held firm. "I’ve wanted to say this for months. I love you. Have I startled you too much?"

Her lips trembled. "I *am* startled. I’ve only ever thought of you as a friend. Please, let’s forget this."

His grip slackened. "Of course. If it’s unpleasant, by all means, forget." The bitterness leaked through. "Though that’s easier said than done."

She winced. "I don’t mean to hurt you."

"A struggling barrister dreaming of marriage—how absurd." His tone turned flippant, mocking himself. Margaret’s mouth curled faintly in disdain.

They rejoined Mr. Hale, who was still savoring his pear, oblivious. The conversation limped along until Lennox took his leave. At the door, his mask cracked.

"Margaret, don’t despise me. However I prattle, my heart is in earnest. Goodbye."

Then he was gone, leaving Margaret to the quiet and the weight of what had passed.
