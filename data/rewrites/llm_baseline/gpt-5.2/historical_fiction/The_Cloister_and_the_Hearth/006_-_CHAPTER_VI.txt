“Look into your own heart and write!” cried Herr Cant; and all the earth’s cuckoos took up the note, thin and tireless, as if a single call could make a man truthful. Look into the Rhine where it runs darkest, and the Thames where it runs thickest, and swear you have painted the bottom. Drop a bucket into a well of self-deceit, and because the water comes up cold and shining, call it immortal truth.

Yet no child of Adam reads his own heart plain, not at first. He must learn the alphabet of feeling by poring over other hearts—listening to their confessions as one listens to a market square at dawn, when every sound has its place: the clop of hooves, the hush of a broom, the soft chime of a spoon against a bowl. Only then, with borrowed light, can he spell out his own desires; and even then he stumbles over the words.

Halfway to Sevenbergen Gerard tried it. The road had its own music: larks above the wet fields; a cart far off, squealing in its axle like a bad fiddle; the steady, comforting crunch of his soles on grit. He asked his heart why he was going.

It answered him at once, bold as a drummer.

“We go out of curiosity—to learn why she jilted us; to show her it has not broken our hearts; to prove we are content with our honours and our benefice in prospectu; and we want neither her nor any ally of her fickle sex.”

That was what it said. Gerard almost believed it, because pride makes a tidy story, like a loaf squared off to seem larger than it is. But another truth ran beneath, as water runs under ice.

He found Peter Brandt’s cottage without much asking. The place sat low and honest to the earth, thatch smelling of sun-warmed straw and last week’s smoke. In the doorway a girl sat sewing. The click of the needle through linen was small, exact—one of those domestic sounds that can quiet a mind or unhinge it, depending on what the mind has lost. A stalwart figure leaned beside her with a longbow, talking in the plain, unhurried way of a man who has stood in ranks and learned to waste no breath.

At the sight of that man, a sharp, unreasonable pang went through Gerard, as if a string inside him had been plucked too hard. Then the fellow turned his head; the face was weathered, the hair grizzled, the shoulders still broad though the years were past fifty. An old soldier. Gerard knew him—Martin, who had shot at the butts with such clean force that even boys stopped their boasting to watch.

Another moment, and Gerard stood before them.

Margaret looked up. Her work fell in her lap. A faint cry escaped her, almost swallowed at once; and her face went white and then red, as if blood and breath were arguing over who owned her. The show of it was swift; she put it away too quickly, and her manner turned cool—cooler than it would have been if she had felt nothing.

“What! is it you, Master Gerard?” she said, as though the name had no taste in her mouth. “What on earth brings you here, I wonder?”

“I was passing by and saw you,” said he. “So I thought I would give you good day, and ask after your father.”

“My father is well. He will be here anon.”

“Then I may as well stay till he comes.”

“As you will.” Her fingers gathered up the fallen sewing, too composed, too careful. Then, without looking at him, she spoke to the bowman. “Good Martin, step into the village and tell my father here is a friend of his.”

“And not of yours?” Gerard’s words slipped out before he could catch them.

She lifted her eyes then, and they were steady. “My father’s friends are mine.”

“That is doubtful,” said Gerard, bitterness rising like over-salted broth. “It was not like a friend to promise to wait for me, and then make off the moment my back was turned. Cruel Margaret, you little know how I searched the town for you; how, for want of you, nothing was pleasant to me.”

She held her needle still. The air between them had changed; even the cottage seemed to listen. “These are idle words,” she answered. “If you had desired my father’s company—or mine—you would have come back. There I had a bed laid for you, sir, at my cousin’s, and he would have made much of you; and, who knows, I might have made much of you too. I was in the humour that day.” Her lip trembled, and she punished it into stillness. “You will not catch me in the same mind again—neither you nor any young man, I warrant me.”

“Margaret,” cried Gerard, “I came back the moment the Countess let me go; but you were not there.”

“Nay, you did not, or you had seen Hans Cloterman at our table; we left him to bring you on.”

“I saw no one there,” said Gerard, baffled, “but only a drunken man, that had just tumbled down.”

“At our table?” She leaned forward a little, the way a cook leans to judge whether the scent is true or deceptive. “How was he clad?”

“Nay, I took little heed,” said Gerard; “in sad-coloured garb.”

At that, warmth began to creep back into her face, as if some buried ember had been found. Yet she would not surrender at once; she put on incredulity like a stiff cap and pinned it tight. Question after question she asked, sharp and shrewd—where he had stood, what he had seen, how long he had waited, who had spoken. Gerard answered each as loyally as if he were under oath. He had no art in lying, and no desire to learn it now.

Slowly the cloud thinned. Their two accounts fitted together at last, not like rival tales but like two halves of a broken platter: a misunderstanding, simple as spilt ale on a crowded bench, yet bitter enough to sour two hearts for months. Hans Cloterman. A drunken stranger. A moment’s absence and a wrong face, and all their hopes had been driven off like geese.

When they both saw it, silence fell—a silence with its own sound, louder than speech: the breath they took at the same time; the tiny rustle of linen in Margaret’s lap; a distant cock crowing as if it were any morning in the world.

Then came the revulsion—tenderness surging back all the more fiercely because they had wounded each other. And after tenderness, more perilous still, came confession: neither had been happy since; neither had found the days sweet; and without this chance meeting, neither believed they ever would.

Gerard, seeking shelter from the sudden blaze of feeling, let his eyes rove—only to fasten on a treasure that lay open on the table: a manuscript Vulgate. The sight of it struck him like the smell of fresh bread to a hungry man. Manuscripts were his delight; he moved toward it as a hawk drops to prey.

But before his fingers could touch the page, two white hands fell flat upon it, swift as wings. Above them bent Margaret’s face, and it was red now for shame and for laughter half-choked.

“Nay,” said Gerard softly, the heat of joy in his voice, “take away your hands, Margaret, that I may see where you are reading; and I will read there too at home. So shall my soul meet yours in the sacred page.”

She shook her head, stubborn and smiling, guarding it like a pot on the hearth no one must taste yet.

“You will not?” said he. “Nay, then I must kiss them away.”

And he did—once, and again, and again—until the very persistence of his kisses made her fingers retreat in surrender, for sheer shame. The book lay open, and the words showed clear:

“An apple of gold in a network of silver.”

“There, now,” she cried, half laughing, half vexed with herself, “I had been hunting for it ever so long, and found it but even now—and to be caught!” And, inconsistent as a girl can be when her heart is too full to keep its own rules, she pointed it out with her white finger, as proud as if she had cooked a perfect dish and been teased at the first bite.

“Ay,” said Gerard, eyes on her more than on the Latin, “but to-day it is all hidden in that great cap.”

“It is a comely cap,” she retorted, quick as a sparrow, “I’m told by some.”

“Maybe; but what it hides is beautiful.”

“It is not,” she said, turning her head away though the corner of her mouth betrayed her. “It is hideous.”

“Well, it was beautiful at Rotterdam.”

“Ay,” she breathed, and the sigh carried more truth than her sharpness had done. “Everything was beautiful that day.”

At that moment the latch lifted and Peter Brandt came in with the smell of outdoors on him—damp earth, wind, and the faint tang of stable. His entrance broke the spell without destroying it, the way a spoon breaks the skin on warm milk without cooling it. He welcomed Gerard with a hearty face and a good grip, and would have him stay supper.

Margaret vanished at once, as if a daughter’s duty had been waiting just behind the door. Gerard sat with Peter and talked learnedly—of books, of letters, of the old tongues that carry a people’s memory like seed in a pouch. Words in one mouth, he found, did not always mean the same thing in another; a phrase could be harmless in a city and an insult in a village. Even the way men named bread and barley, ale and cheese, marked who they were and what they held sacred. Gerard listened, and in the background he heard the cottage’s homely orchestra: the scrape of a pot, the rinse of water, the soft thud of a loaf set down, and the steady crackle of the hearth that made all speech gentler.

Then Margaret returned.

Her hair was no longer hidden; it lay gathered in a silver net that caught the lamplight like dew in a spider’s web. She shot Gerard a glance—half arch, half coy—and went about the board with a light step, setting things down with that quiet authority women have when they feed a house. The scent of supper rose: warm bread, a simple fare honestly cooked, and something green and sharp that cleared the head. She moved between father and guest, beaming now with gaiety and happiness as if the day had been remade.

When the meal was done and the evening cooled, Gerard coaxed her out. She objected—and came. He coaxed her down the road toward Tergou; she declined—and came. The air held the day’s last sweetness; hedges murmured with hidden life; somewhere a stream talked to itself over stones, and the far-off village carried its small sounds as a shell carries the sea.

They strolled up and down, hand in hand, as if the simple act could mend what had been torn. And when at last he must go, they pledged each other never to quarrel nor misunderstand again. The promise was earnest as prayer and as fragile as a thin wafer.

They sealed it with a long, loving kiss.

Gerard went home on wings, the road’s noises turned to music under his feet.

From that day he spent most evenings with Margaret, and their attachment deepened and deepened on both sides, until the hours they spent together were the hours they lived; the rest they counted and underwent. At the outset all went smoothly. Obstacles there were, but they seemed distant and small to the eyes of hope, youth, and love. The feelings and passions of so many persons—whom this attachment would thwart—gave no warning smoke to show their volcanic nature and power. The course of true love ran smoothly, placidly, until it had drawn these two young hearts into its current for ever.
