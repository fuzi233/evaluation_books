November 17, 1494. Eighteen months had passed since Tito and Romola were wedded under the Easter sun, a union blessed with a shower of comfits that promised sweetness for their double life. Yet, since that spring, the air of Florence had shifted, thickening with the weight of history. Just as every blossom depends on the sap flowing from the root, the private fortunes of the couple were now tethered to the grand, trembling currents of Italian politics.

Only a week prior, the ancient spirit of the Republic had surged back into the hearts of the Florentines. The great bell in the palace tower had shattered the silence with an alarm, summoning the populace to arms. With rusted weapons and improvised clubs, they had driven the Medici from their throne. The gate of San Gallo slammed shut on Piero, fleeing toward Bologna with his mercenaries in panic, and barred the path of his younger brother, the Cardinal, who escaped disguised as a Franciscan monk. A price was placed on their heads, houses were sacked in accordance with old precedents, and the ignominious portraits of past conspirators were scrubbed from public walls. The exiles were welcomed home, and the half-grown tyrants were ejected from their splendid nest in the Via Larga. The Republic had, for the moment, reclaimed its will.

Now, however, the great palace in the Via Larga was being dressed for a different occupant. Drapery draped the streets in unfamiliar colors; banners spilled from windows; carpets and tapestries covered every step where exceptional feet might tread. This was the visible proof of a city brimming with anticipation. The stream of color flowed from the palace, past the Cathedral, through the Piazza della Signoria, and across the Ponte Vecchio to the Porta San Frediano. There, near the gate facing Pisa, a platform and canopy awaited the Signoria. Messer Luca Corsini, a doctor of law, felt his heart flutter with the anticipation of delivering a Latin oration. Every chief elder prepared himself, smoothing their chins and adjusting their silk *luccos*, while the well-born youths inspected their new tunics, styled after the French fashion, hoping to impress the stranger with a peculiar grace. A vast procession of clergy, from the Archbishop in his radiance to trains of black, white, and grey monks, consulted early in the morning on how to marshal their relics and sacred banners, ready to greet the illustrious visitor at three o'clock in the afternoon.

This was an unprecedented guest. He had crossed the Alpine passes with an army Italy had never witnessed: thousands of terrible Swiss, hardened to fight for love, hate, or coin; a host of gallant cavaliers; and an infantry where every hundredth man carried an arquebus. More terrifying still were the bronze cannons, hurling iron balls instead of stones, drawn by horses rather than oxen, capable of firing twice before a city could repair the breach. Some compared this newcomer to Charlemagne, the rebuilder of Florence and conqueror of degenerate kings; others saw in him the likeness of Cyrus, the liberator of the chosen people. He arrived with glorious projects: to march through Italy amid jubilation, to satisfy conflicting complaints at Rome, to seize the Kingdom of Naples by hereditary right and sword, and from there to launch a crusade against the Turks, cutting them down or converting them to Christ. This was the scheme of the Most Christian King, Charles the Eighth of France, son of the subtle Louis XI, whose shadow loomed over the strongest Christian monarchy of the age.

On a general level, the spectacle seemed grandiose, fit to revive memories of divine dispensations. A widespread conviction held that the arrival of the French king and his host was an event beyond the ordinary order of Providence—a moment when marble statues might sweat, phantom warriors fight in the air, and beasts bear monstrous offspring. This belief rested less on the sheer magnitude of the invasion than on the moral emotions of the times, finding its most remarkable voice in a single man.

That man was Fra Girolamo Savonarola, Prior of the Dominican convent of San Marco. On a September morning, as news of the French army's entry rang in men's ears, he preached in the Cathedral from the text, "Behold I, even I, do bring a flood of waters upon the earth." He believed this was supreme guidance, interpreting the "flood" as a symbol of both avenging wrath and purifying mercy. His audience, comprising the most cultivated spirits of the age, listened with shuddering awe. Savonarola possessed a rare power to imprint his beliefs upon others, swaying varied minds. Four years prior, he had proclaimed from Florence's chief pulpit that a scourge was descending to purify the Church. He appeared to hold a mission akin to the Hebrew prophets, viewing the Florentines as a second chosen people. While prophecy in that age was often like a farthing candle illuminating small corners of destiny, Savonarola's vision was a mighty beacon shining far out to warn and guide. To the soberest minds, the supernatural character of his insight was attested by the peculiar conditions of the age.

At the close of 1492, following the death of Lorenzo de' Medici, Italy had enjoyed a peace unthreatened by near danger. There was no fear of famine, for the seasons had been bountiful; new palaces rose in fair cities, and villas dotted pleasant slopes. Those who had more than their share showed no fear for those who had less. Citizens' armor grew rusty, and populations seemed tame, licking the hands of masters who paid for ready-made armies as easily as goods from Smyrna. Even the fear of the Turk had faded, replaced by the Pope's willingness to accept bribes for prospective poisoning rather than plan conquest. Altogether, the world seemed a handsome establishment for the lucky and wise, a realm where lust, obscenity, lying, and treachery were pleasant and useful when properly managed. Patronage of learning and fine arts adorned this substantial delight of tyranny, allowing flattery to be spoken in the choicest Latin and sublime artists to paint the holy and the unclean with equal skill. The Church, though disgraced in its head and lacking vital belief in its lower members, was more prosperous than in past days. The heavens smiled above, and below, there were no signs of earthquake.

Yet, even then, Savonarola had been preaching for two years that a scourge was at hand, that the world was not framed for hypocrites, libertines, and oppressors. From amidst those smiling heavens, he saw a sword hanging—the sword of God's justice, soon to descend with purifying punishment. In brilliant Ferrara, seventeen years prior, the contradiction between men's lives and their professed beliefs had destroyed his appetite for the world, driving him into the cloister at twenty-three. He believed God had entrusted the Church with the sacred lamp of truth, but saw that corruption had turned it into a sepulcher. As scandals multiplied and hypocrisy gave way to impudence, he asked if the world had ceased to have a righteous Ruler. Reason itself declared vengeance imminent; unless the Church were reclaimed, how could the promises of converting heathens and subjecting the world to the one true law be fulfilled? He had seen his belief reflected in visions, frequent since his youth.

The real force of Savonarola's demonstration lay in his burning indignation at wrong, his fervent belief in an Unseen Justice that would end it, and an Unseen Purity that abhorred uncleanness. To his ardent, power-loving soul, the faith in a supreme Ruler merged with the faith in speedy divine interposition to punish and reclaim.

Meanwhile, beneath the splendid masquerade of dignities, conditions worked slowly to disturb the general festivity. Ludovico Sforza, holding the ducal crown of Milan and desiring to wear it himself rather than let it rest on a feeble nephew, feared the Spanish-born King Ferdinand and Crown Prince Alfonso of Naples. These Neapolitans, disliking the cruelty and treachery useless to themselves, objected to poisoning a near relative for a Lombard usurper. The Neapolitan royalties feared their suzerain, Pope Alexander Borgia. All three anxiously watched Florence, lest its midway territory determine the game by underhand backing. All four, along with every small state in Italy, feared Venice—the cautious, stable, and strong power stretching its arms across the Adriatic to the western ports.

It was thought that Lorenzo de' Medici had prevented the fatal outbreak of these jealousies by maintaining the old Florentine alliance with Naples and the Pope while persuading Milan of its general advantage. But young Piero de' Medici's rash vanity quickly nullified his father's wary policy. Ludovico Sforza, roused to suspicion of a league against him, decided to invite the French king to march into Italy and, as heir of the house of Anjou, take possession of Naples. Ambassadors, orators as they were called in those haranguing times, went and came. A recusant cardinal, determined not to acknowledge a Pope elected by bribery and his own particular enemy, also traveled, seconding the invitation with hot rhetoric. The young king seemed to lend a willing ear. By 1493, the rumor spread that Charles the Eighth of France was about to cross the Alps with a mighty army. The Italian populations, accustomed since the fall of the Roman Empire to look for an arbitrator from afar, began to vaguely regard his coming as a means of avenging wrongs and redressing grievances.

In that rumor, Savonarola heard the assurance that his prophecy was being verified. What filled the ears of ancient prophets but the distant tread of foreign armies coming to do the work of justice? He no longer looked vaguely to the horizon for the coming storm; he pointed to the rising cloud. The French army was the new deluge to purify the earth from iniquity; Charles the Eighth was the instrument elected by God, as Cyrus had been of old. All men who desired good rather than evil were to rejoice in his coming, for the scourge would fall destructively on the impenitent alone. Let any city of Italy, let Florence above all—Florence beloved of God, to whose ear the warning voice had been specially sent—repent and turn from its ways like Nineveh, and the storm-cloud would roll over it leaving only refreshing raindrops.

Fra Girolamo's word was powerful, yet now that the new Cyrus had been three months in Italy and was not far from Florence's gates, his presence was expected with mixed feelings, where fear and distrust certainly predominated. It was not understood that he had redressed any grievances; the Florentines clearly had nothing to thank him for. He held their strong frontier fortresses, which Piero de' Medici had surrendered without securing honorable terms. He had done nothing to quell the alarming revolt of Pisa, encouraged by his presence to throw off the Florentine yoke. Orators, even with a prophet at their head, could win no assurance from him except that he would settle everything once within the walls of Florence. Still, there was satisfaction in knowing that the exasperating Piero had been pelted out for the ignominious surrender of the fortresses, and in that act of energy, the spirit of the Republic had recovered some of its old fire.

The preparations for this equivocal guest were not entirely those of a city resigned to submission. Behind the bright drapery and banners symbolic of joy, preparations of another sort were made with common accord by government and people. Hidden within the walls were hired soldiers of the Republic, hastily called from surrounding districts. Old arms were furbished, sharp tools and heavy cudgels laid carefully at hand to be snatched up on short notice. Excellent boards and stakes formed potential barricades, and a good supply of stones waited to make a surprising hail from upper windows. Above all, the people were in a humor for fighting any personage supposed to have designs of hectoring over them, having lately tasted that new pleasure with much relish. This mood was not diminished by the sight of occasional parties of Frenchmen arriving beforehand to choose their quarters, some with a hawk on their left wrist and metaphorically a piece of chalk in their right hand to mark Italian doors. Especially as credible historians imply that many sons of France were characterized by a swagger that must have whetted the Florentine appetite for a little stone-throwing.

This was the temper of Florence on the morning of November 17, 1494.
