“Oh, where be these gay Spaniards,
Which make so great a boast O?
Oh, they shall eat the gray-goose feather,
And we shall eat the roast O!”

—Cornish Song.

Imagine, if you will, that those who this summer stood with pride upon the shores of Plymouth—gazing at its grand breakwater, its bustling docks, its towering arsenals, and its mighty screw-driven steamers gliding effortlessly through the crowded harbor—were suddenly transported back three hundred years, to the golden summer of 1588. The modern world vanished in an instant: no railroads, no telegraphs, no engines of steel and fire. Instead, they found themselves in a time when ships were sails and men were made of iron.

Mount Edgecombe still rose above the sea, as green and fair as ever. But where were the vast dry-docks of Devonport? Where the naval yards that now stretch along the shore? Gone. Drake’s Island held only a modest battery, newly built by the man whose name it bore. Mount Wise was little more than a gentleman’s estate surrounded by fields. The citadel, once a fortress, was now a paltry fort, barely strong enough to withstand a cannonade from a single modern steamer. And the ships? The great floating castles of the Hamoaze had shrunk to mere lime-hoys crawling across the water. The Catwater, once filled with merchant vessels, now teemed with warships—ships that would soon set sail into the greatest naval battle the world had yet known.

They lay there, humble in the eyes of today’s men: none larger than a frigate of thirty-six guns, carrying less metal than a modern gunboat, their cannons firing only a fraction of the distance our own can reach. Would the visitors from the nineteenth century—just arrived by train, eager to see the troops embark before returning home for dinner—have looked down on this modest fleet with scorn? Would they have smiled, sneered, and muttered about progress, science, and how foolish their ancestors must have been?

Some surely would have. Those who treat marvels as curiosities, who use inventions without understanding them—like oxen chewing clover without knowing where it came from—would have laughed. But others—the thinkers, the builders, the men who know what effort lies behind every breakthrough—would have seen something deeper. They would not have mocked, but marveled. For in those small, weathered ships, they would have glimpsed the seed of the mighty navy that now ruled the Black Sea, the Atlantic, and beyond. They would have understood that to build such power from such meager means required courage, intellect, and vision no less remarkable than any we claim today.

Yet if these travelers had been students not just of machines, but of men, they might have found few nobler company than gathered on the terrace bowling-green behind the Pelican Inn on the afternoon of July 19th. There, beneath the shade of roses, sat nearly every notable captain of England’s fleet—“England’s forgotten worthies,” as history would later call them. The Spanish Armada had scattered in a storm. Lord Howard had sailed out to hunt for it, as far as the Spanish coast, but the wind had shifted southward. Fearing the Spaniards might slip past him, he had returned to Plymouth, uncertain whether the fleet would come at all.

Now, let us slip into the tavern, like Prince Hal, bearing a tray of long-necked Dutch glasses and a silver tankard of wine. Step through the rose-clad door and look upon the gallant captains waiting—like lions in their den, watching for the herd of deer to pass.

At the center of a silent ring stands a group of five men speaking earnestly. You recognize the soft eyes and pointed chin—Walter Raleigh. Beside him, a young man in a flame-colored doublet, his arm around Raleigh’s neck—Lord Sheffield. Opposite them, standing beside Sir Richard Grenville, is a noble figure: Lord Charles Howard of Effingham, lord high admiral of England. Next to him, his son-in-law, Sir Robert Southwell, captain of the *Elizabeth Jonas*. But who is that short, sturdy man, plainly dressed, hands clasped behind his back, legs slightly apart, gazing intently at each speaker? His cap rests in his hand, revealing a bullet-shaped head of crisp brown hair, a wrinkled brow, high cheekbones, a broad forehead, thick lips—set firm as stone. A rough, plebeian face—but the whole posture speaks of iron will, calm strength, and unshakable resolve. When he finally speaks, even the most seasoned officers turn to listen. His name is Francis Drake.

Then comes a grizzled elder, broad-shouldered and sea-stained, wearing garments soaked in salt and oil, yet adorned with a massive gold chain that seems out of place. He waddles forward as if born to the gale. His face is split between leather-red skin above and coarse fur below. Clapping Drake on the back, he bellows in thick Devon dialect:

“Be you a coming to drink your wine, Francis Drake, or be you not?—saving your presence, my lord!”

Lord Howard laughs, bidding Drake go ahead. For John Hawkins, admiral of the port, is the patriarch of Plymouth seamen. If Drake is their hero, Hawkins is their father. He does as he pleases, says what he likes—and today, the prospect of battle has loosened his usual grim reserve, filling him with boisterous cheer—even toward his rival, Drake.

So they push through the crowd, where many other names linger in legend. Martin Frobisher and John Davis sit on a bench, smoking tobacco from long silver pipes. By them are Fenton and Withrington, both men who had tried to follow Drake’s path around the world and failed—not through fault of their own. The one who raises a toast to them is George Fenner, known among “the seven Portugals” and favored by Leicester, captain of the galleon Elizabeth bought from him. The short, prim man in a huge yellow ruff, sharp chin, tiny mustache, and self-satisfied smile is Richard Hawkins—son of Admiral John, already famed and doomed. Talking with him is his uncle William Hawkins, whose monument still stands—or should stand—in Deptford Church. It reads: “A worshipper of the true religion, an especial benefactor of poor sailors, a most just arbiter in difficult causes, and of singular faith, piety, and prudence.” That, and his service at Porto Rico, is all I know of him—but if any of us could hope for such words after our own lives, we would have no reason to complain.

There is John Drake, brother of Sir Francis, ancestor of the Drakes of today. There is George, his nephew, not overwise, who sailed with Amyas. And there is Amyas himself, talking fiercely with Captain Barker of Bristol—brother of Andrew Barker, who found John Oxenham’s guns and died by Spanish hands in Honduras twelve years prior. Barker now commands the *Victory*, one of the queen’s finest ships, and like Amyas, he has scores to settle with the Spaniards. So they growl together in a corner, while the rest laugh and joke like flies on a summer vine.

But who is the old man seated against the southern wall of the inn? His long white beard flows almost to his waist, his hands rest on his knees, his head moves slowly from side to side, catching fragments of conversation. At his feet, a little girl of six lays her curly head upon his lap. Beside him stands a buxom woman of thirty, tending him—half nurse, half showman, for he draws curious glances from every captain. She whispers again and again: “Bless you, sir, please don’t give him liquor, poor old soul—the doctor says.”

It is old Martin Cockrem, father of the innkeeper, aged beyond reckoning. He remembers the bells of Plymouth ringing for Henry VIII’s coronation. He may well have been the first Englishman to step onto the soil of the New World. He sits there like an ancient Druid stone amid a modern field of wheat and clover. He has seen the death of old Europe and the birth of a new world. Approach him—he is still sharp in mind. Just now, he seems restless, peering through the crowd, listening for a familiar voice.

“There 'a be again! Why don’t 'a come, then?”

“Quiet, gramfer, don’t trouble his worship.”

“Here an hour, and never speak to poor old Martin! I say, sir—” he feebly pulls at Amyas’s cloak. “I say, captain, do ‘e tell young master old Martin’s looking for him.”

“Marcy, gramfer, where’s your manners? Don’t vex him, sir—he’s almost a babe, and tejous at times.”

“Young master who?” asks Amyas, bending down, smiling at the woman to let the old man have his way.

“Master Hawkins; he’m never been near me all day.”

Amyas goes off, finds young Richard Hawkins, but as he reaches to speak, the woman stops him, laughing and blushing.

“No, sir, not Mr. Richard—Admiral John, sir, his father! He always calls him young master, poor old soul!” She points to the grizzled beard and weathered face, scarred by fifty years of storms and battles.

Amyas hurries to the admiral and delivers the message.

“Mercy on me! Where be my wits? Iss, I’m a-coming,” says the old hero in his broadest Devon accent. He waddles over, rummaging in his pockets. “Here, Martin, I’ve got mun, I’ve got mun, man alive! But his Lordship kept me so. Lookee here—why, I do get so lusty of late, Martin, I can’t get to my pockets!”

Out tumble a tarred string, papers, a thimble, a lump of pudding tobacco—and finally, a small paper of Muscovado sugar, a rare delicacy then, as precious as French bonbons today. He thrusts it into the old man’s trembling hand.

Old Martin dips his finger in, rubs it on his toothless gums, smiles, nods thanks. The little girl at his knee takes her share too.

“There, Admiral Leigh—both ends meet—gramfers and babies! You and I shall be like that one day, young Samson!”

“We shall have slain a good many Philistines first, I hope.”

“Amen! So be it. But look to mun! So fine a sailor as ever drank liquor—and now greedy after a hit of sweet trade! Piteous like. But I bring mun a hit whenever I come, and he looks for it. He’s one of my own flesh, is old Martin. He sailed with my father, Captain Will, when they were two little cracks aboard a trawler. My father went up—here I am. He didn’t—there he is. We’m up now, we Hawkinses. We may be down again some day.”

“Never, I trust,” said Amyas.

“‘Tain’t no use trusting, young man—you go and do. I hear too much of that from my lad. Let they ministers preach till they’re black in the face—works is the trade!” He nudges Amyas. “Faith can’t save, nor charity nether. There, you tell with him, while I go play bowls with Drake. He’ll tell you a sight of stories. Ask him about good King Hal, now—”

And off waddles the Port Admiral.

“You’ve seen good King Henry, then, father?” asked Amyas, intrigued.

The old man’s eyes sparkled. He stopped chewing his sugar.

“Seed mun? Iss, I reckon. I was with Captain Will when he went to meet the Frenchman at Calais—the Field, the Field—”

“The Field of the Cloth of Gold, gramfer,” the woman suggested.

“That’s it. Seed mun? Iss, fegs. Oh, he was a king! The face o’ mun like a rising sun, and the back o’ mun so broad as that there” (he spreads his arms). “And the voice o’ mun! Oh, to hear mun swear if he was merry—oh, ‘tas royal! Seed mun? Iss, fegs! And I’ve seed mun do what few has—I’ve seed mun christle like any child.”

“Cry?” asked Amyas. “I shouldn’t have thought there was much cry in him.”

“You think what you like—”

“Gramfer, don’t be rude—”

“Let him go on,” said Amyas.

“I seed mun christle. Oh dear, how he did put hands on mun’s face! ‘Oh, my gentlemen,’ says he, ‘my gentlemen! Oh, my gallant men!’ Them was his very words.”

“But when?”

“Why, Captain Will had just come to the Hard—that’s Portsmouth—to speak with mun. The barge Royal lay against the Hard, and our boat alongside. The king stood above my head on the quay edge. The ship came in near abreast of us, looking most royal to behold, poor dear! And went to cast about. And Captain Will says, ‘Them lower ports is cruel near the water.’ She had not more than sixteen inches to spare. Says he, ‘That won’t do for going to windward in a say, Martin.’ And as the words left his mouth, a bit of a flaw came from the west, sharp like—and overboard goeth my cap, hits the wall, and as I stooped to pick it up, I heard a cry—and it was all over!”

“He’s telling of the Mary Rose, sir.”

“I guessed so.”

“All over—and the cry of mun, and the screech of mun! Oh, sir, up to the heavens! And the king he screeched right out like any maid, ‘Oh my gentlemen, oh my gallant men!’ And as she lay on her beam-ends, just settling, the last souls I seen were that man’s father, and that man’s. I knew mun by their armor.”

He pointed to Sir George Carew and Sir Richard Grenville.

“Iss! Iss! Drowned like rattens. Drowned like rattens!”

“Now, you mustn’t trouble his worship any more.”

“Trouble? Let him tell till midnight—I shall be well pleased,” said Amyas, sitting down beside him. “Drawer! ale—and a parcel of tobacco.”

And Amyas settled in to listen, while the old man purred to himself:

“Iss. They likes to hear old Martin. All the captains look upon old Martin.”

“Hillo, Amyas!” said Cary. “Who’s your friend? Here’s a man been telling me wonders about the River Plate. We should go thither for luck next time.”

“River Plate?” said old Martin. “It’s I knows about the River Plate—none so well. Who’d ever been there, nor heard of it, before Captain Will and me went? And I lived among the savages a whole year. Audacious civil I found ‘em—if they’d had shirts to their backs. And so was the prince o’ mun, that Captain Will brought home to King Henry—leastwise he died on the voyage. But the wild folk took it cruel well, for you see, we was always as civil with them as Christians. If we hadn’t been, I should not be here now.”

“What year was that?”

“In the fifteen thirty. But I was there afore, and learnt the speech o’ mun. That’s why Captain Will left me as a hostage when he took their prince.”

“Before that?” said Cary. “Why, the country was hardly known before that.”

The old man’s eyes flashed.

“Knowed? Iss, and you may well say that! Look ye here! Look to mun!” He waved his hand. “There’s captains! And I’m the father of ‘em all now—poor Captain Will’s in glory. I, Martin Cockrem! . . . Iss, I’ve seen a change. I mind when Tavistock Abbey was full o’ friars and golden idols—such noxious trade as ever was a wheat-rick of rats. I mind the fight off Brest in the French wars—Oh, that was a fight, surely!—when the Regent and the French Carack burned side by side, fast grappled, because of Sir Thomas Knivet. And Captain Will gave him warning as he ran past us, saying—”

“But,” said Amyas, seeing the old man drift, “what do you mind about America?”

“America? I should think so! But I was a-going to tell you of the Regent—and seven hundred Englishmen burnt and drowned in her, nine hundred French in the Brest ship, besides what we picked up. Oh dear! But about America.”

“Yes, about America. How are you the father of all the captains?”

“How? You ask my young master! Why, before the fifteen thirty, I was up the Plate with Cabot—and a cruel fractious ontrustful fellow he was, like all they Portingals—and bid there a year and more, and up the Paraguaio with him, discovering no end. Whereby, gentles, I was the first Englishman, I hold, that ever set foot on the New World!”

“Then here’s your health, sir!” said Amyas and Cary.

“Long life? Iss, fegs, I reckon—long enough already! Why, I mind the beginning of it all. I mind when there wasn’t a master mariner to Plymouth that thought there was aught west of the Land’s End except herrings. They held them, pure wratches, that if you sailed west far enough, you’d fall off the edge. Iss—‘Twas dark parts round here, till Captain Will arose. And the first of it I mind was inside the bar of San Lucar. He and I were boys, ten year old, aboard a Dartmouth ship, going for wine. And there came in over the bar he that was the beginning of it all.”

“Columbus?”

“Iss, fegs, he did—no pistol-shot from us. And I saw mun stand on the poop, so plain as I see you. No great shakes of a man to look at. There’s a sight better here, to please me. And we was disappointed, we lads, for we expected to see mun with a golden crown and sceptre. And the ship o’ mun all over like Solomon’s temple. And I mind that same year, seeing Vasco da Gama, going out over the bar, when he found the Cape of Good Hope and sailed to the Indies. Ah, that was the making of they rascally Portingals! . . . Our crew told what they seen and heerd—but nobody minded sich things. ‘Twas dark parts, and Popish, then. Nobody knowed nothing, nor got no schooling, nor cared for nothing but scrattling up and down like prawns in a pule. Iss, sitting in darkness, we was, and the shadow of death—till the day-spring from on high arose, and shined upon us poor out-o'-the-way folk—The Lord be praised! And now, look to mun!” He swept his hand wide. “Look to the works of the Lord! Look to the captains! Oh blessed sight! One’s been to the Brazils, one to the Indies, the Spanish Main, the North-West, the Rooshias, the Chinas, up the Straits, round the Cape, round the world of God—bless His holy name! And I seed the beginning of it. And I’ll see the end of it too. I was born into the old times—but I’ll see the wondrous works of the new, yet, I will! I’ll see they bloody Spaniards swept off the seas before I die, if my old eyes can reach so far as outside the Sound. I shall, I knows it. I says my prayers for it every night—don’t I, Mary? You’ll bate mun, sure as Judgment, you’ll bate mun! The Lord’ll fight for ye. Nothing’ll stand against ye. I’ve seed it all along—ever since I was with young master to the Honduras. They can’t bide the push of us! You’ll bate mun off the face of the seas, and be masters of the round world, and all that therein is. And then, I’ll just turn my old face to the wall, and depart in peace, according to his word.”

“Deary me, now—while I’ve been telling with you, here’ve this little maid been and ate up all my sugar!”

“I’ll bring you some more,” said Amyas, moved by the childish bathos.

“Will ye, then? There’s a good soul—and come and tell with old Martin. He likes to see the brave young gentlemen, a-going to and fro in their ships, like Leviathan, taking of their pastime therein. We had no such ships to our days. Ah, ‘tis grand times, beautiful times surely—and you’ll bring me a bit sugar?”

“You were up the Plate with Cabot?” said Cary, after a pause.

“Do you mind the fair lady Miranda, Sebastian de Hurtado’s wife?”

“What! Her that was burnt by the Indians? Mind her? Do you mind the sun in heaven? Oh, the beauty! Oh, the ways of her! Oh, the speech of her! Never was, nor never will be! And she to die by they villains—for the goodness of her! Mind her? I minded naught else when she was on deck.”

“Who was she?” asked Amyas.

“A Spanish angel, Amyas.”

“Humph!” said Amyas. “So much the worse for her, to be born into a nation of devils.”

“They’m not all so bad as that, yer honor. Her husband was a proper gallant gentleman, kind as a maid, couldn’t abide De Solis’s murderous doings.”

“His wife must have taught it him, then,” said Amyas, rising.

“Where did you hear of these black swans, Cary?”

“I have heard of them, and that’s enough,” answered he, unwilling to stir sad memories.

“And little enough,” said Amyas. “Will, don’t talk to me. The devil is not grown white because he has trod in a lime-heap.”

“Or an angel black because she came down a chimney,” said Cary.

And so the talk ended—cut short by a sudden roar from Admiral John Hawkins.

“Fail? Fail? What a murrain do you here, to talk of failing? Who made you a prophet, you scurvy, hang-in-the-wind, croaking, white-livered son of a corby-crow?”

“Heaven help us, Admiral Hawkins, who has put fire to your culverins in this fashion?” said Lord Howard.

“Who? my lord! Croakers! my lord! Here’s a fellow calls himself the captain of a ship, and her majesty’s servant, and talks about failing—as if he were a Barbican loose-kirtle trying to keep her apple-squire ashore! Blurt for him, sneak-up! Say I.”

“Admiral John Hawkins,” quoth the offender, “you shall answer this language with your sword.”

“I’ll answer it with my foot—and buy me a pair of horn-tips to my shoes, like a wraxling man. Fight a croaker? Fight a frog, an owl! I fight those that dare fight, sir!”

“Sir, sir, moderate yourself. I am sure this gentleman will show himself as brave as any when it comes to blows. But who can blame mortal man for trembling before so fearful a chance as this?”

Hawkins turned, his eyes blazing.

“Trembling? Is that all you’ve learned? You think fear is wisdom? Then you’re no soldier, no sailor, no Englishman! I’ll not have such croaking here. When the fleet sails, I’ll not have a man who doubts the cause. This is not a game—we are not playing at soldiers. This is war. And war is not for cowards or prophets of doom. If you cannot stand with courage, then stay ashore. But if you sail with me, you sail with the belief that England shall prevail!”

And with that, he strode away, leaving silence in his wake.
