October 30, 1496. The air was crisp, carrying the gentle promise of autumn, yet beneath the surface of the clear sky lay a storm far more terrifying than any wind. The Florentines paid little heed to the land breezes; their minds were consumed by the gales at sea, tempests that seemed to conspire against the Friar's assurance that Providence watched over their city. From the shores of Leghorn, ships laden with Marseilles grain and soldiers had been driven back, leaving Florence in a stranglehold of starvation and military vulnerability. Hunger stalked the streets like a ghost, and the borders of the state were exposed to invasion.

France, under a King who had swept through Italy like a new Charlemagne, had recently withdrawn, reportedly broken by the grief of a lost child. In response, a "Holy League" had formed, led by Pope Borgia, ostensibly to expel the French "barbarians" from Naples. Yet, to the sharp-eyed observers, this coalition resembled nothing so much as a pack of wolves agreeing to drive out rivals only to tear each other apart over the carcass. And Florence, standing alone against this tide due to her stubborn adherence to the French alliance, was viewed not as a partner, but as the most desirable meal. While Pisa fought savagely for liberty, bolstered by Venice, Milan, and the German Emperor Maximilian, Florence sat isolated, cut off from the sea and surrounded by enemies.

The government, influenced heavily by the followers of Savonarola, responded with a mixture of vigor and compassion. They raised funds and recruits, refusing the traditional cruelty of shutting out the starving peasants and beggars who flocked to the city like birds fleeing a frozen north. Instead, they opened their gates wide, inviting the destitute into the belly of the beast. Yet this benevolence drew fire from the disaffected, who argued that Florence should join the League to secure peace rather than invite chaos, and that feeding the alien poor weakened the state against foreign foes.

Distress grew with every passing day, fueling louder murmurs in the streets. To compound the crisis, Fra Girolamo, obeying a mandate from Rome, had remained silent at the pulpit for over a month. Only after the news of the failed grain shipment reached the Signoria did they summon him back. Two days prior, Savonarola had returned to the Duomo, delivering a bold sermon wherein he wagered his own habit—if God did not save Florence for their piety, he would strip it off. Yet, as the morning broke on the thirtieth, no sign of divine rescue had appeared.

Desperation gnawed at the city's resolve. Some whispered that the miraculous *Tabernacle* of the Madonna dell'Impruneta, a powerful intercessor known to deliver Florence from drought, plague, and war, needed to be brought in. For centuries, the city had trusted in this "Mother rich in sorrow," pouring wealth and devotion upon her shrine. Though the Tabernacle had already been escorted from Impruneta and placed in the church of San Gaggio outside the walls, it had not yet entered the city proper. The morning sun rose on unchanged misery, where hospitals overflowed, courtyards served as infirmaries, and the bodies of the dead, buried by the various fraternities, lined the roadsides.

Among the many women moving through the streets in plain garb, attending to the sick and feeding the hungry, one figure stood out unmistakably: Romola de' Bardi. Cloaked in simple black serge, with a veil hiding her hair save for bands of gold rippling across her brow, she walked from the Ponte Vecchio toward the Porta Santa Maria. Her path was suddenly blocked by a bier carried by the Company of San Jacopo del Popolo. The brothers paused to inspect a load, while a cluster of idle workers, their faces gaunt and sharpened by hunger, gathered around, speaking in a confused murmur.

"He's dead!" one cried. "Messere Domeneddio has taken him!"
"It would be kind to stretch out our legs and walk ahead with our heads," another joked grimly. "Standing upright with hunger is no support."
"A beggar turned out of Siena," someone noted. "Or perhaps a prisoner from the Stinche?"

There was speculation whether the man was truly dead or merely collapsed from lack of food. One brother suggested giving him wine. Before the decision could be fully acted upon, Romola stepped forward. With a deft motion, she loosened a small flask at her belt and used a tiny ivory implement to pour a few drops of wine into the prostrate man's mouth. The liquid worked quickly; the man gasped, swallowing eagerly. More followed, and slowly, the old man's head turned toward her, his eyes opening with a vague, returning consciousness.

A jolt of profound recognition struck Romola. Those wild, dark eyes peering from a sallow, deep-lined face, framed by a long, white beard, were unmistakable. They were the same eyes she had seen in the Duomo years ago, belonging to the escaped prisoner who had terrified Tito and haunted the sketches in Piero's studio. A tremor seized her heart. Here, finally, was the secret that might be more bitter than anything she had endured: the man her husband had wronged. An impulse to flee surged within her, a visceral reaction to the horror of the encounter, yet a deeper, imperious need to stay rooted her feet. She knew, with the intuition of keen feeling, that this man was connected to her husband's crimes. Her hands trembled, but the muscle memory of her charitable work guided them as she supported his neck with her left arm and prepared to offer more wine with her right.

Baldassarre, the prisoner, turned his gaze onto her for the first time. The seclusion of his imprisonment and her recent flight had kept them apart, erasing any specific memory he might have held of the fair, golden-haired wife described in rumors. Now, seeing her clearly, those descriptions vanished like mist.

"The steps of San Stefano," Romola suggested gently. "We must move him there so we do not block the street. You may proceed with the bier."

They moved but thirty yards, and by the time they reached the stone steps of the church, Baldassarre managed to sit up, propping himself against the doorway. The charitable brethren continued on their rounds, but the crowd of onlookers swelled. The atmosphere shifted from casual curiosity to a tense, watching silence as the reality of his survival settled.

Romola sat beside him, offering a piece of soft bread from her basket. "Can you eat this now? Perhaps later you will be able, if I leave it for you. I must go to the hospital, but I will return if you wait. Do you understand? Wait for me."

Her words echoed in his enfeebled mind. "Come back," he repeated dreamily.

As she offered him the bread, a rough-looking man in a red night-cap shoved past the others to elbow his way closer. "Is this bread for those who cannot swallow, Madam?" he sneered. Another voice chimed in, mocking the idea of feeding thieves. "If every criminal is to be revived with wine and bread, the Ciompi had better go fill themselves in the Arno while the waters are plenty."

The circle tightened, the gaunt, starving men pressing in, their coarseness cutting through the polite distance Romola usually maintained. She held out her basket, containing the bread and a small horn-cup of wine, soaked with the liquid. Until now, she had ignored their taunts, focusing solely on the dying man. But now, rising to her feet, she looked around at the crowd. Instinctively, the men nearest to her stepped back, as if sensing a boundary they dared not cross.

"Hunger is hard to bear," Romola said, her voice steady despite the strain on her face, which bore the marks of prolonged fasting. "You have the power to take this bread if you wish. It was saved for sick women and children. You are strong men; if you do not choose to suffer because you are strong, you have the power to take everything from the weak. Take the bread from this basket if you will, but I shall watch by this old man. I shall resist anyone who tries to take bread from *him*."

Silence fell over the group. The man in the red night-cap looked foolish, backing away and jabbing his elbow into his neighbor's ribs in a gesture of mock rebuke. The young man in the fine cloth tunic melted away into the crowd. Just as the tension began to dissipate, the armed servitors of the Signoria arrived to break up the obstruction blocking the street.

The man called Cecco, who had retreated up the church steps, turned back. Seeing the threat of a mace, he approached Romola with a sudden shift in demeanor. "Madam, if you wish to continue your errands, I will take charge of the old man."

Cecco was a wild-looking figure, clad in a ragged, variegated tunic, his arms bare and sinewy, his face bruised by hardship. He spoke quickly, trying to assure her. "Do not distrust me, Madam. I am not handsome, but I have an old mother who eats my porridge. I buy candles for the Virgin. Look, he is eating his sop. He is hale enough; soon he will stand as well as the best of us."
