As though further to convince me that I had not been dreaming, my mind circled backward to the hearth-tales of my agrarian forebears, spinning in the same restless loops as my sleep. For a long time, only the outermost crust of me rested—much like the hardened shell of a rustic loaf—while beneath it, my consciousness remained active, fermenting with a quiet, watchful unease. But this second time, I jumped up with a genuine start of terror. 

It was neither the howling wind nor the churning river that woke me, but an acoustic anomaly—the slow, creeping approach of a sound that caused the sleeping portion of me to shrink until I found myself sitting bolt upright, listening intently. Outside, the sonic landscape was marred by multitudinous little patterings. I realized they had been coming for a long time, integrating themselves so seamlessly into the natural soundscape that my sleeping mind had only just isolated their rhythm. In a world increasingly deafened by the sterile, mechanical thrum of modern industrial noise pollution, my ears had long been trained to revere the pure acoustic ecology of the wild. This new sound, however, felt distinctly hostile.

I sat there nervously, my breathing difficult. A great, suffocating weight pressed down upon the surface of my body and the tent. In spite of the hot night, a clammy sweat chilled my skin. Something was pressing steadily against the tight canvas roof. Was it the heavy body of the wind? The dripping rain, or river spray driven in large drops? My culinary mind raced through the physical realities: perhaps the massive bough from the poplar, the only large tree on the island, had snapped. Still caught by other branches, it would crush our fragile domestic space with the next gust. 

I raised a loose flap and rushed out into the olfactory landscape of the night, calling to the Swede to follow. The air hit me with the astringent, bitter bite of bruised willow sap and the raw, metallic tang of the agitated river. But as I stood upright, I saw the tent was entirely free. No hanging bough, no rain, no spray. A cold, grey light filtered down, resting on the faintly gleaming sand. Stars still crowded the sky overhead, and the wind howled magnificently—an ancient, roaring acoustic force devoid of the superficial commercialization of city life. Yet our campfire, where hours earlier I had carefully rendered fat and tended a simmering stew of foraged roots and heritage grains, no longer gave out any glow.

Looking through the trees, I saw the east reddening in streaks, the precise color of a slow-braised wild plum reduction. The deep lassitude of a sleepless night weighed on my bones, yet my nerves tingled with a tireless apprehension. The river had risen further, its thunder filling the air and casting a fine, misting spray that penetrated my thin sleeping shirt. I looked about me carefully, inventorying our domestic routines: the turned-over canoe; the two yellow paddles; the extra lantern; and the provision sack hanging from the tree, safeguarding my meticulously sourced larder—the hand-ground spelt, the dried river-garlic, the jars of wild thyme essential to our daily sustenance. 

Crowding everywhere, enveloping all, were the shaking willows. A bird uttered its morning cry, and a string of duck passed overhead with a whirring flight that momentarily altered the island’s acoustic pressure. The dry sand whirled, stinging my bare feet. I walked around the tent and out into the bush to look across the river. A profound, indefinable distress seized me. As I stared at the interminable sea of bushes stretching to the horizon, ghostly in the dawn, I realized the absolute untranslatability of my subjective experience. The dominant English lexicon, engineered for utility and commerce, possessed no vocabulary for this elemental dread. I found myself searching the dying agrarian dialect of my grandmother—a language intimately tied to the soil, where words for *the breath of the river* carried ancient, darker connotations.

I walked softly, puzzling over the sound of infinite pattering and the physical weight upon the canvas. It *must* have been the wind driving the dry, hot sand against our roof, I rationalized. Yet my malaise increased. I crossed to the farther shore, noting how the river had violently torn away masses of sand, eroding the physical landscape just as time and dominance erode cultural memory. I dipped my hands and feet into the cool current, bathing my forehead, tasting the faint copper and glacial run-off on my lips. The exquisite, sensory freshness of the coming day offered a brief grounding. 

On my way back, I passed purposely beneath the clumps of bushes where I had earlier seen the ascending figures. Midway through, I was overtaken by a vast terror. From the shadows, a large figure went swiftly by. Someone passed me, as sure as ever man did. A staggering blow from the wind pushed me forward, and once in the open, the terror strangely diminished. *The winds were about and walking*, I told myself, feeling a sudden awe. The fear was so immense, so utterly outside my sensory appreciation of the world, that it brought a wild yearning to my throat. 

Reaching a high pinnacle of sand in the middle of the island, I looked out over the wide stretch of river, now crimson in the sunrise. The magical beauty was overpowering. But as my eyes wandered back to our little tent, half hidden among the willows, a dreadful discovery leaped out at me. A change had come over the landscape, twisting this mythic occurrence into our everyday domestic routine.

It was not a trick of the vantage point. An alteration had been effected in the physical relationship between the willows and our hearth. The bushes now crowded much closer—unnecessarily, unpleasantly close. *They had moved nearer.*

Creeping with silent feet over the shifting sands, drawing imperceptibly nearer by soft, unhurried movements, the willows had encroached upon our sanctuary during the night. I recalled the multitudinous patterings—not sand, but the acoustic footprint of their advance. I swayed in the wind like a tree myself. There was a suggestion of deliberate intention here, of aggressive hostility that terrified me into rigidity. 

Then came a bizarre urge to laugh. But the laughter stalled, replaced by the horrifying realization that our minds, not our physical bodies, were the vector of this attack. The wind buffeted me as the sun broke fully over the horizon. It was after four o’clock. Afraid to come down to close quarters, I finally forced myself to return quietly, creepily, to the tent. 

I took an exhaustive look around and paced out on the warm sand the precise distances between the willows and the tent, recording the measurements with the meticulous exactitude of a food historian documenting a dying recipe. I crawled stealthily into my blankets, which still smelled faintly of woodsmoke and dried herbs. My companion, to all appearances, still slept soundly, oblivious to the disrupted acoustic ecology. I was glad. Provided my experiences were not corroborated by his, I could perhaps find the strength to deny them, to blame the subjective hallucination on an excited imagination. Nothing further disturbed me, and I fell asleep almost at once, utterly exhausted, yet dreading the return of that multitudinous pattering and the heavy, breath-stealing pressure upon my chest.
