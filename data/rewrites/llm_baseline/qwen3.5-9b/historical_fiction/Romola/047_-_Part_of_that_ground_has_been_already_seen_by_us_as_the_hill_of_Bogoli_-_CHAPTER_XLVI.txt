That evening, the sky was heavy with the promise of rain, dark and foreboding. Romola returned from the Hospital of San Matteo, accompanied by Maso and a lantern, having visited the sick after vespers. Just as she emerged onto the street, she met her husband, Tito, stepping out of the Monastery of San Marco. Tito had ventured forth shortly after his arrival in the Via de' Bardi earlier that day and had seen little of Romola since. Seeing her, he immediately proposed to escort her home, dismissing Maso, whose short, hurried steps irritated him. Such official attentions were customary for Tito only when explicitly demanded; otherwise, he maintained a cool distance.

Between Tito and Romola, there existed no friction, no open quarrel. They were too hopelessly alienated in their inner lives to engage in the kind of conflict that seeks reconciliation. Instead, they navigated their shared existence with a careful adherence to a tacit truce. If Tito desired supper prepared in the old library, now transformed into a pleasant banqueting hall, Romola assented and ensured everything was arranged. Conversely, Tito left her entirely free in her daily habits, accepting her assistance with transcription and digesting texts, while in return providing the supplies she needed for her charitable works. Yet, despite this functional harmony, he constantly avoided exchanging glances with her. He pretended to believe she was elsewhere to avoid seeking her in her own rooms, and he playfully attributed to her a perpetual preference for solitude over his company.

In the early days following her decision to remain rather than flee, Romola had made timid attempts to restore a frank relationship between them. However, she realized that true reconciliation required open speech regarding their differences and a striving toward a moral understanding. Tito, conversely, could only be saved from alienation by a return to her former effusive tenderness—a sentiment that would require forgetting their differences. He cared for no explanation; he deemed a thorough accounting impossible. He wished for Romola to love him again, but for her, love was impossible. She could be submissive and gentle, capable of suppressing any sign of repulsion, but tenderness could not be feigned. Helpless before this reality, she recognized that her husband was indeed alienated from her. This realization reinforced her resolve to keep herself strictly outside any secrets he would never voluntarily share. Regarding his political actions, he sought to convince her that he viewed the Medici cause as hopeless and that he sincerely served the popular government, in which she now held a warm interest. Nevertheless, subtle impressions, like faint odors, unsettled her concerning his dealings with San Marco. She was torn between the dread of witnessing evidence that would confirm her suspicions and the urgent impulse to watch closely, fearing harm she might otherwise have prevented.

As they walked together that evening, Tito remarked, "The business of the day is not yet concluded for me. I shall conduct you to our door, my Romola, and then I must fulfill another commission. It may take an hour, perhaps longer, before I can return to rest, which I sorely need." He then spoke amusingly of events in Pisa until they approached a loggia near which a lamp burned before a picture of the Virgin. The street was usually quiet, and few people had passed them thus far, but now the sound of many approaching footsteps and confused voices grew louder.

"We shall not reach home dry unless we take shelter under this convenient loggia," Tito said hastily, urging Romola up the steps with a slightly startled movement. "Surely it is useless to wait for this light drizzle," Romola observed in surprise. "No," Tito replied, "I felt it growing heavier. Let us wait a moment."

With that keen awareness of the slightest indications characteristic of a mind habitually in a state of caution, Tito had noticed through the lamplight that the leader of the approaching group wore a red feather and a glittering sword-hilt. In fact, he was almost the last person in the world he wished to meet at that hour beside Romola. Earlier that day, he had already conducted one momentous interview with Dolfo Spini; the task he mentioned to Romola was a second meeting with that same man, a sequel to his visit at San Marco.

By a long-prearranged plan, Tito had acted as the bearer of letters to Savonarola—carefully forged documents. One bore, through a clever stratagem, the very signature and seal of the Cardinal of Naples, who among the Sacred College had exerted the greatest influence at Rome in favor of the Friar. These letters stated that the Cardinal, on his journey from Pisa and unwilling for strong reasons to enter Florence directly, yet eager to consult Savonarola at this critical juncture, intended to stop that very day at San Casciano, ten miles from the city. From there, disguised in the simple garb of a priest, he planned to ride out the next morning and meet Savonarola seemingly by chance, five miles along the road to Florence, two hours after sunrise.

The plot, initiated by these forged letters, was for Dolfo Spini, leading a band of his *Compagnacci*, to lie in ambush at a lonely spot five miles from the city gates. There, they were to seize Savonarola and the Dominican friar accompanying him according to monastic rule, delivering them to a small detachment of Milanese cavalry stationed near San Casciano, who would carry them into Roman territory. There was a significant chance that the penetrating Friar would suspect the trap and refuse to incur the risk of traveling beyond the city walls alone. Even when preaching, his friends insisted he be attended by an armed guard; here, however, he was asked to commit himself to a solitary road with no other companion than a fellow monk. To maximize the likelihood of his acceptance, minimal time was allotted for decision-making. The strategy relied on the assumption that the Friar's intense desire to unite internal and external forces toward a General Council, coupled with his expectation of immediate support from the Cardinal in his struggle with the Pope, would overcome his natural shrewdness and caution within the brief window provided.

Tito had previously received an audience with Savonarola, declining to entrust the letters to anyone else. With consummate art, he had led the Friar to infer their meaning, suggesting they referred to a rendezvous outside the gates. Consequently, he urged that Savonarola seek an armed guard from the Signoria, offering his own services to convey the request with utmost privacy. Savonarola had replied briefly that this was impossible; an armed guard was incompatible with the privacy required. Speaking with a flashing eye, he indicated his intent to accept the risk. Tito himself cared little for the outcome. He managed his affairs so skillfully that, regardless of the result, he expected to benefit. Whether Savonarola fell into the snare, yielding diamonds and papal patronage, or refused, Tito's adroit agency would strengthen his standing with both the Friar and Spini, making him more valuable as an agent of the Mediceans.

However, Spini was an inconvenient colleague. Though cunning enough to enjoy plots, he lacked the ability or self-command necessary for such a complex operation requiring secrecy. He often became intoxicated, for even sober Florence had its "beoni," or drunkards, both lay and clerical, who grew loud in taverns and private banquets. Despite agreements between him and Tito to maintain a cool demeanor in public, there was always the danger that an evening encounter might provoke Spini to burst forth with sudden affability. The delicate signal of casting the *becchetto* over the left shoulder was understood in the morning, but a mere hint might fail to deter a fraternal grasp of the shoulder in the evening.

Tito's chief hope was that Dolfo Spini had not spotted him. This hope would have been well-founded had Spini possessed no clearer view of Tito than Tito had of Spini. Yet, hidden in the shadows, Tito had seen himself illuminated for an instant by the direct rays of the lamp. In his own way, Tito was as distinct a figure as the captain of the *Compagnacci*. Romola's black-shrouded figure had escaped notice, and she now stood behind her husband's shoulder in the corner of the loggia.

Tito was not left to hope long. "Ha! My carrier-pigeon!" grated Spini's harsh voice, intended as an undertone, as his hand seized Tito's shoulder. "What did you run into hiding for? You didn't know it was comrades arriving. It's well I caught sight of you; it saves time. How fares the chase tomorrow morning? Will the bald-headed game rise? Are the falcons ready?"

Had Tito possessed a nature prone to rage, he would have felt it against this bull-faced accomplice, unfit to be either a leader or a tool. His lips turned white, but his agitation stemmed from the pressing difficulty of choosing a safe response. If he tried to hush Spini, it would only deepen Romola's suspicion; she knew him well enough to understand that if a strong alarm were raised in her, she could neither be silenced nor deceived. On the other hand, if he repelled Spini angrily, the wine-soaked *Compagnaccio* might become savage, more ready for retaliation than for reading motives.

He adopted a third course, proving that Romola still held a certain power over him—the power of dread. He pressed her hand, as if intending a signal to her, and said in a good-humored tone of camaraderie, "Yes, my Dolfo, you may prepare in all security. But take no trumpets with you."
