As I sit gazing across my hearth at the familiar armchair where the partner of my last fifty years of life rests, I find myself repeating the same old question again and again — a privilege no middle-aged man can be fairly denied, after all, for we hoard our jokes, our anecdotes, and our favourite little queries as carefully as we hoard our old port. “Did any two young fools ever make a rasher, more imprudent choice than we did,” I ask her, “when we married the very year the old King breathed his last?”

My son Miles, that most fearless of fox-hunters, answers the same way when he is home from the chase: he says that when he stops to look back at the gaps and flooded rivers he cleared at full gallop, his blood runs cold at the thought of the risk he ran, and he cannot believe his own foolhardiness. Yet there is no keener sportsman across the whole two counties. He will break his collarbone, tumble ten times in a day (to his mother’s constant terror), and be back in the saddle by sunrise next morning. I have had my own share of tumbles, too: thrown head-first over my old mare Sultana’s head when she slipped on a park path stone, both of us half-asleep while my mind wandered off to a new verse or a speech I was to give at Quarter Sessions. Miles’s reckless hunting has been the joy of his life, and my reckless marriage has been the blessing of mine — and I never think of it without giving thanks to Heaven.

Mind you, I do not hold our example up to all the young folk of the parish. If I stood up in vestry meeting and bade every lad marry on twopence a year, the faces round the table would turn black enough to sour the ale. But my wife is a desperate match-maker, Heaven bless her: when Hodge and Susan trudge into my justice room, shyly mentioning they have a half-crown a week saved to set up house, we add a little purse of our own money to their store, send for the vicar, and make a wedding of it.

I know the answer I will get when I tease my dear oracle, of course — that is half the fun of asking. If I say, “My dear, did not the moon look exactly like cream cheese tonight?” she will smile back and say, “Well, papa, it did, to be sure — there is no one alive with such droll similes as you.” If I grumble that Mr. Pitt’s speech was fine enough, but never half as grand as what I remember his father Lord Chatham delivering, she will agree at once that no man ever lived to match the old Earl. Then one of the girls will cry out, “But papa has said a hundred times Chatham was nothing but a charlatan!” and mamma will only laugh and say the girl takes after her wild Aunt Hetty.

As for Miles, none of this matters a whit to him. He only reads the hunting notices in the Norwich paper, and cares no more for politics than he does for Greek. The rascal has never read a line of my poems, much less my tragedies — when I mentioned Pocahontas to him the other day, he thought it was a tributary of the James River in Virginia. He cannot construe a line of Corderius, so how could he possibly understand my Latin verses? My little memoir notebook sits on the corner of the fireside table, with my spectacles resting on the cover, for anyone to lift and read if they please — and Miles has never touched it. I fill its loose pages with caricatures of him, little jokes at his expense, and he never notices a thing. Only once, I came back to find my fine China-ink sketch of Miles snoring after dinner (a drawing my friend Bunbury himself would have been proud of) crossed out, and a rude scrawl of a picture of me flying over Sultana’s head, titled *The Squire on Horseback, or A Fish Out of Water*. Miles roared so loud with laughter that the girls all tittered in corners, and my wife swore she thought I would fly into a passion when I opened the book. But I can take a joke against myself, even if — strange to say, for a man who has counted many of the great wits of the age as his friends — I have never heard a truly good original witticism in my whole life. No matter, Miles, I love you none the worse for being no wit. There never was any friendship between two wits under one family roof, after all. His mother swears he is as handsome as Apollo, or as the Prince of Wales, who was born the very same year he was. She has a print of the Prince by Coates hanging in her dressing room to this day, and will point out the resemblance to anyone who will listen. [Note in a neat, slanted female hand: “My son is no spendthrift, no breaker of ladies’ hearts, as some young gentlemen are. But that he was the very image of His Royal Highness when both were babes is past all doubt: the Duchess of Ancaster herself said so, when Gumbo and my poor Molly used to wheel him round St. James’s Park for his daily airing. Th. W.”]

What different prospects lay before the other boy born that very same year: Lord Esmond, the son of the Earl of Castlewood. Even then, my lord and I had already settled into a cool, distant acquaintance, and Heaven knows no fairy godmother laid gold christening cups in honest Miles’s cradle when he was born. But things have mended since then — Laus Deo, indeed! — for I suspect neither Miles nor his father would ever have gotten half so far in the world relying only on their own wits.

Castlewood House bears a very different face now than the venerable, weathered old place I knew in my youth. Back then, its walls were lined with the wrinkles of hundreds of years, the scars of old civil wars, the cracks and blemishes time had marked on its hoary stone features. I love best to remember it as it was when young George Warrington (that is, your humble narrator, writing these lines) was invited down by its owner, to attend his lordship’s wedding to Miss Lydia Van den Bosch, “an American lady of noble Dutch family,” as the county papers announced her. The towers stood just as my grandfather Colonel Esmond — the Marquis, as Madam Esmond will insist on calling him — had seen them. The old woods had been thinned a little, to be sure, but many of the same rooks that cawed over the Colonel’s head threescore years before still wheeled and cawed above the treetops. His portrait hung in the hall, the same house that would have been his, had he not chosen love and loyalty over wealth and a noble title. As I paced the long corridors, the dew-damp terraces, and the cool, dark avenues, I half fancied myself one of Mr. Walpole’s cavaliers, ruff at my throat, rapier at my hip, buff coat and gorget on my back, ready at any moment to spot a Jacobite spy slipping out from behind an oak tree, or a Roundhead raider peering from a carved oak cupboard. I dreamed up a hundred old-world romances there: secret intrigues, serenades under balcony windows, scaling ladders, Oliver’s men battering at the gates, bluff King Hal’s beefeaters pricking across the plain before the castle walls.

I was courting my dear wife at the time, of course — your eyes had no need of spectacles back then, my love, and not a single wrinkle or silver thread marked your brow. I wrote ream upon ream of flowery, romantic descriptions of the old house to her, all franked under Lord Castlewood’s name, and she never tired of reading a single line. Now she teases me that when I go up to London, I only send her three lines of note. That is not out of laziness, my dear — it is only that I will not fatigue your old eyes.

I spared no expense on a new, fine suit of clothes for the wedding, and arrived at Castlewood a few days before the ceremony, attended by my faithful servant Gumbo. I ought to mention that we had sent Sady, our other lad, home to Virginia on a merchant ship not long before. A vicious inflammation of the throat and lungs, which killed hundreds of people the year of General Wolfe’s expedition to Quebec, had nearly carried him off, and the doctors swore only the air of his native colony would cure him. We parted in floods of tears, though Gumbo had shed just as many when I myself left for the army at Quebec. But he had made too many friends in England to care for a military life, so he stayed on in my service.

Castlewood House was packed full of guests, relations, and well-wishers. sulky Lady Fanny was there, dragged there against her will to act as a bridesmaid, along with a bevy of other young ladies from the neighbouring county families. The Baroness Beatrix, that sharp old bishop’s widow, had brought her brother-in-law, a London bishop, down to Hampshire to officiate at the wedding of Eugene, Earl of Castlewood, and Lydia Van den Bosch, spinster. For weeks before and after the ceremony, the old house hummed with a gaiety it had not known for generations. The county gentry flocked over to pay their respects to the new couple. Everyone gossiped endlessly about the lady’s fortune, of course, and no two versions of the sum ever agreed. The naughty rumours that had spread through London, of her jilting half a dozen disappointed suitors, took months to drift down to Hampshire — and when they did, no one cared to listen. The Hampshire folk were far too glad to see Castlewood House open again, beer flowing in the cellars, horses stabled in the stalls, and spits turning over the kitchen fires to be overly curious about her behaviour in town. If she had laughed at a few suitors and turned them away? So had half the heiresses in England, and she was no more mercenary than the rest of them. Her marriage settlement was advertised as seventy thousand pounds, and the county folks whispered that was but a small fraction of her wealth — when her rich old grandfather died, they said, the plums would fall thick from the tree.

That sharp, quiet old grandfather of hers set to work at once untangling Castlewood’s accounts, which decades of neglect, poverty, and careless extravagance had left in total ruin. Every hour of his days, for months and years afterwards, was spent patching up Lord Castlewood’s finances: raising rents where it was fair to do so, paring down the household expenses to the bone, so that he could track every yard of worsted lace on the footmen’s coats, and every pound of beef sent up to their tables. His old sharp eye counted every flagon of beer drawn from the buttery, and made sure no food went to waste in the larder. The household staff was smaller, but every soul was paid on the dot, no more overdue wages; the footmen’s liveries were neat and new, and no tailor was ever left to dun for his money. The gardeners and grooms grumbled a little, even as their pay came on time — but the horses grew fat on less corn, and the fruit and vegetable harvests were larger than they had been in living memory, all overseen by that old man from his tiny chamber in the house turret.

These improvements took years, not days, to come to pass. Slowly, with steady thrift and careful spending, the estate and its household grew prosperous again. No one could deny how well the house ran — not even the old dowager Countess and her two children, who now rarely set foot inside Castlewood’s gates. The new young Countess had declared war on them the moment she took possession, and routed them completely. Though she had been raised in the colonies, and knew nothing of the fine nuances of noble London society in her youth, Lydia had a sharp tongue and an iron will that no one who quarrelled with her could stand against. The dowager and Lady Fanny fled all the way back to their jointure house in Kensington, and no one missed them — least of all Lord Castlewood, who was never one to grieve over the absence of anyone who had left his household.

As for me, who had known my lord for years, I never for a moment imagined he would grieve if I fell ill, or met with an accident, or died. He was too well-bred, too witty, too polished, to show any of that petty sentiment. I rather enjoyed watching his peculiar ways, even if he had no courage and no heart at all. What are those things, after all, for a man of the world? You do not demand that every gentleman be brave and loyal, any more than you demand he have half a million pounds in the funds. All society asks is that he be pleasant company, keep a decent household, and pay his way. My grandfather, Colonel Esmond, who had turned down the very title and estate my lord now owned, my lord once tapped his own placid, high forehead and said, “That old fellow must have been a little touched here.” There are some acts of unselfishness that selfish men can never understand, which they dismiss as outright madness or lies. “I do you the justice to say, cousin,” I told him once, “that you would never give up even the smallest advantage for any friend alive.” He shrugged his shoulders in the French fashion, pinched a pinch of snuff from his box, and laughed. “Eh! I am selfish, but am I any more selfish than the rest of the world?”

Once, when we were walking the fields, my lord was wearing a fine scarlet coat, and a cow took a sudden run at him. The ordinarily languid, slow-moving nobleman cleared a five-barred stile faster than any schoolboy I ever saw. He did not even try to pretend he was not trembling afterwards, or hide that he was no brave man. “I dare say you respect me no more than I respect myself, George,” he said, candid as ever, and then launched into a very witty, sardonic little speech about the fall of man, and all our faults and shortcomings, and wondered aloud why Heaven had not made every one of us tall, handsome, brave, and rich. I could not help but like him, for all his weaknesses. There was something in his look that reminded me of the poor ass in Mr. Sterne’s famous story: *Do not beat me, he seemed to say, but if you must, you may*. No man who is not a bully and a coward himself will ever be cruel to a creature that makes no pretence of spirit at all.
