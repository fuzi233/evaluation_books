My fair friend, Miss Clack, having laid down her pen, there are two compelling reasons for me to take up the quill in my turn.

First, I stand in a unique position to shed light on certain shadows that have lingered over this affair. I was privy to the private reason behind Miss Verinder's sudden dissolution of her marriage engagement, and I was at the very bottom of it. Furthermore, I uncovered the hidden motive driving Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite to abandon his claim to his charming cousin.

Second, fate—or perhaps misfortune—cast me directly into the vortex of the Indian Diamond mystery at the very moment I am writing. I was honored to host an interview in my own office with an Oriental gentleman of distinguished bearing, who was none other than the chief of the three Indians. To this, I must add my subsequent meeting with the celebrated traveler, Mr. Murthwaite, and the critical conversation we held regarding the Moonstone, a dialogue that bears profound weight on the events to come. Thus, I lay claim to the narrative role I now occupy.

The chronology of the broken engagement demands precedence, so we shall begin there. Tracing the chain of events backward, I find it necessary to open this scene, though it may seem odd, at the bedside of my late client and friend, Sir John Verinder.

Sir John possessed a generous share of the harmless, amiable weaknesses common to mankind. Among these, relevant to our tale, was an invincible reluctance to confront the solemnity of making his will, provided he enjoyed his customary robust health. Lady Verinder exerted her influence to rouse him to a sense of duty, and I did likewise. He acknowledged the justice of our views but took no action until illness finally claimed him. Only then was I summoned to draft his testament. The instructions were the simplest I have ever received in my entire professional career.

Sir John was dozing when I entered. He roused himself at my approach. "How do you do, Mr. Bruff?" he said. "I sha'n't be very long about this. And then I'll go to sleep again." He watched with keen interest as I gathered pens, ink, and paper. "Are you ready?" he asked. I bowed, dipped my pen, and waited.

"I leave everything to my wife," Sir John declared simply. "That's all." Turning on his pillow, he settled back to rest.

I was compelled to interrupt his slumber. "Am I to understand," I asked, "that you leave the entirety of your property, of every description, absolutely to Lady Verinder?"

"Yes," said Sir John. "Only, *I* put it shorter. Why can't *you* put it shorter and let me sleep? Everything to my wife. That's my Will."

His estate comprised land and money. In most cases, I would have urged reconsideration, but regarding Sir John, I knew Lady Verinder to be not only worthy of such unreserved trust but also uniquely capable of administering it—a rare talent among women. Within ten minutes, the Will was drawn, executed, and Sir John resumed his nap.

Lady Verinder fully justified her husband's confidence. In the early days of her widowhood, she called upon me to make her own Will. Her perspective was so sound and sensible that I required no advice beyond shaping her instructions into legal form. Before Sir John had been in his grave a fortnight, the future of his daughter was secured with wisdom and affection.

This Will remained safely stored in my fireproof box for many years. It was not until the summer of 1848, under melancholy circumstances, that I revisited it. The doctors had pronounced a sentence of death upon poor Lady Verinder. As the first to be informed, I found her anxious to review her Will once more.

The provisions for her daughter were immutable. However, her wishes regarding minor legacies to other relatives had shifted over time, necessitating several Codicils. To avoid confusion and repetition, I obtained her permission to draft a second Will incorporating these changes. As Miss Clack, who witnessed the signing, noted, the second Will was identical to the first regarding Rachel Verinder's financial interests. The only alterations concerned the appointment of a guardian, made under my advice. Upon Lady Verinder's passing, the document was submitted to my proctor for probate.

Approximately three weeks later, a warning sign emerged. While visiting my friend the proctor, I noticed an unusual air of agitation. "I have news for you," he said. "What do you think I heard at Doctors' Commons this morning? Lady Verinder's Will has been requested and examined!"

This was startling. The Will was indisputable, and no one seemed to have a legitimate interest in scrutinizing it. (For those unaware, the law permits anyone to examine a Will at Doctors' Commons for a shilling fee.)

"Did you hear who requested it?" I asked.

"Yes," he replied. "The clerk told me immediately. Mr. Smalley, of the firm of Skipp and Smalley, asked for it. Since it hadn't yet been copied into the Great Folio Registers, he was granted access to the original. He studied it carefully and made notes in his pocket-book. Do you have any idea why he wanted it?"

I shook my head. "I shall find out," I answered, "before I am a day older."

Returning to my office, I utilized a leverage I held over the firm of Skipp and Smalley. My common-law clerk was Mr. Smalley's brother, and for years, my office had served as a feeder for their practice. I intended to remind them of this patronage if necessary. I dispatched my clerk to Mr. Smalley's office with a formal inquiry regarding the examination of the Will.

Mr. Smalley returned promptly, accompanied by his brother. He admitted acting on client instructions but initially claimed professional confidentiality. We engaged in a sharp discussion; he was technically correct, but I was driven by anger and suspicion. I insisted on knowing more, refusing to accept any information as a secret, and claiming the freedom to use my own discretion. Pushing further, I threatened the risk of losing their client's business against the risk of losing mine. An act of tyranny, I admit, but effective nonetheless. Mr. Smalley chose his alternative without hesitation.

He smiled resignedly and revealed the name of his client: Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite.

That was sufficient for me.

Armed with this knowledge, I must now place the reader on equal footing with myself regarding Lady Verinder's Will. Let it be stated clearly: Rachel Verinder held only a life-interest in the property. Her mother's foresight and my experience had shielded her from the dangers of managing wealth, ensuring she could not touch the principal assets—land or money—nor could she raise capital against them. She would enjoy the London and Yorkshire residences and a handsome income, but nothing more.

Reflecting on this discovery left me perplexed. Hardly a week had passed since I learned of Miss Verinder's proposed marriage to Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite. I held her in the highest esteem and was grieved to see her cast herself upon a man I had long suspected as a smooth-tongued impostor. Now, his lawyer's findings confirmed my worst suspicions, revealing the mercenary nature of his intent. Yet, one must ask: would you judge so lightly if the matter involved your own sister?

My primary concern was whether Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite would honor his engagement after learning the truth of the Will. This depended entirely on his financial desperation. If he was not in dire need of funds, marrying Miss Verinder for her income would be worthwhile. However, if he required a large sum urgently, the Will would provide exactly that, effectively saving his daughter from a scoundrel.

If he needed the money, I could delay revealing the truth until after her mother's funeral. If he did not, remaining silent would be complicity in a lifetime of misery for her.

My doubts led me to the London hotel where Mrs. Ablewhite and Miss Verinder were staying. They informed me they were departing for Brighton the next day, but an unexpected obstacle prevented Mr. Godfrey from joining them. I immediately offered to take his place. Though I hesitated initially, thinking only of Rachel, the moment I saw her, my resolve hardened. Come what might, I would tell her the truth.

I found my opportunity during a walk the day after my arrival.

"May I speak to you," I asked, "about your marriage engagement?"

"Yes," she replied indifferently, "if you have nothing more interesting to talk about."

"Will you forgive an old friend and servant of your family, Miss Rachel, if I venture to ask whether your heart is truly set on this marriage?"

"I am marrying in despair, Mr. Bruff—on the chance of dropping into some sort of stagnant happiness which may reconcile me to my life."

Such strong language! It hinted at a deeper romance or turmoil beneath the surface. Yet, I declined to pursue side issues.

"Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite can hardly share your sentiments," I countered. "His heart must be set on the marriage at any rate?"

"He says so, and I suppose I ought to believe him. He would hardly marry me, after what I have confessed to him, unless he was fond of me."

Poor soul! The notion that a man might marry her solely for selfish, mercenary ends had never crossed her mind. The task ahead seemed far harder than anticipated.

"It sounds strangely," I continued, "in my old-fashioned ears..."

"What sounds strangely?" she asked.

"To hear you speak of your future husband as if you were not quite sure of the sincerity of his attachment. Are you conscious of any reason in your own mind for doubting him?"

Her keen perception detected a shift in my tone, warning her that I had ulterior motives. She stopped, withdrew her arm from mine, and searched my face intently.

"Mr. Bruff," she said, "you have something to tell me about Godfrey Ablewhite. Tell it."

Knowing her well, I took her at her word. I spoke the truth.

She put her arm back into mine, walking slowly beside me. I felt her hand tighten mechanically on my arm and saw her growing paler with every word, yet she remained silent. When I finished, she still said nothing. Her head drooped slightly, and she walked by my side, lost in her own thoughts, oblivious to her surroundings.

I made no attempt to disturb her. My experience taught me that she required time to process such revelations.

Generally, girls react to news by asking endless questions and running to a friend. Rachel Verinder's instinct was to retreat inward, to think it over alone. This self-reliance is a great virtue in men but often isolates women, exposing them to misunderstanding. I suspect I share the world's view on this, except in Rachel's case. Her self-dependence was a virtue in my eyes, born partly of my admiration for her and partly of my belief that she had done nothing unworthy regarding the Diamond. Even though it was shocking to associate her with an undiscovered theft, I was convinced she had taken no steps without first shutting herself away to deliberate.

We walked nearly a mile before she roused herself. Suddenly, she looked up, wearing a faint reflection of her happier smile—the most irresistible expression I have ever seen on a woman's face.

"I owe much already to your kindness," she said. "And I feel more deeply indebted to it now than ever. If you hear any rumors of my marriage when you return to London, contradict them at once, on my authority."

"Have you resolved to break your engagement?" I asked.

"Can you doubt it?" she returned proudly, "after what you have told me!"

"My dear Miss Rachel, you are very young—and you may find more difficulty in withdrawing from your present position than you anticipate. Have you no one—a lady, of course—with whom you could consult?"

"No one," she answered.

It distressed me greatly to hear this. She was so young and so lonely, yet she bore it so well! The impulse to help her overcame any sense of my own unfitness. I offered advice on the spur of the moment. I recommended that she invite Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite to a private interview and inform him that, to her certain knowledge, he had betrayed the mercenary nature of his motives. She was to declare their marriage impossible under such circumstances and challenge him: would he secure her silence by agreeing to part, or force her to expose his motives publicly? If he defended himself or denied the facts, she was to refer him to me.

Miss Verinder listened attentively. She then thanked me prettily but informed me it was impossible for her to follow this plan.

"May I ask," I said, "what objection you see to following it?"

She hesitated, then met me with a question. "Suppose you were asked to express your opinion of Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite's conduct?"

"Yes?"

"What would you call it?"

"I should call it the conduct of a meanly deceitful man."

"Mr. Bruff! I have believed in that man. I have promised to marry that man. How can I tell him he is mean, how can I tell him he has deceived me, how can I disgrace him in the eyes of the world after that? I have degraded myself by ever thinking of him as my husband. If I say what you tell me to say to him—I am owning that I have degraded myself to his face. I can't do that. After what has passed between us, I can't do that! The shame of it would be nothing to *him*. But the shame of it would be unendurable to *me*."

Here was another marked peculiarity of her character revealing itself without reserve. Her sensitive horror of anything mean blinded her to what she owed herself, hurrying her into a false position that might compromise her in the eyes of her friends. Up to this point, I had been diffident about the propriety of my advice. But after her words, I had no doubt it was the best course; I pressed it upon her again.

She only shook her head, repeating her objection. "He has been intimate enough with me to ask me to be his wife. He has stood high enough in my estimation to obtain my consent. I can't tell him to his face that he is the most contemptible of living creatures, after that!"
