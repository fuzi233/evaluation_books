When the household gathered for their next meal, not a trace of yesterday’s frayed temper lingered on Madame de Bernstein’s face. She was soft, cordial, and gracious to every soul at the table, Harry Warrington included. She lavished praise on the cook that night, declared the rabbit fricassee was perfectly seasoned, and swore no eels in all of England could compare to the plump, tender ones pulled from Castlewood’s own moats. She waved off protests that the wine might be corked, refusing to hear of opening a fresh bottle “for a useless old woman like me,” and when the new vintage was brought round, she raised her glass in toast to Madam Esmond Warrington of far-off Virginia, before leaning across to wink at Harry: “I do hope your mama gave you leave to bring home a proper English wife when you sail back, my dear.”

Had he no memory of his grandmother? Her own beloved mother, of course! The Baroness soon had the whole table laughing at her string of lively anecdotes, recounting her mother’s legendary beauty and gentle goodness, her long blissful marriage to Colonel Esmond despite the great gap in their ages. Everyone for miles round had celebrated the couple’s tender attachment, she said—what did disparity in years or station matter to true love, after all? Lady Castlewood and her two children sat quiet, listening, while Harry hung on every word and Maria blinked in quiet surprise. Lord Castlewood, for his part, turned over the sudden shift in his aunt’s mood in his head, wondering what scheme or whim had sparked this unprecedented display of amiability, but never let a flicker of doubt cross his composed, perfectly satisfied face.

The Baroness’s good humour soon infected the whole room. Not a soul at table escaped a kind word from her. When the sweet, unworldly Will blurted out how shocked and pleased he was to see her so cheerful, she laughed outright. “Complimentary, my dear? Of course I am! I am making amends for being shockingly rude to every single one of you this morning. When I was a little girl of seven, back when my own mother and father lived here at Castlewood, I had exactly the same habit. If I’d been cross and naughty all day, I’d coax and pout and charm them into forgiving me by supper time. Why, this very room, this very table—hundreds and hundreds of years ago, it feels!—I sat right here begging my father, my mother, and your grandfather, Harry, to pardon my tantrums. We had collared eels for supper that night, just as we do now. That’s what brought the memory back! I was as wayward then as I am peevish at seventy today, and so I confess my sins here, and ask you all to forgive me, like a good little girl.”

“I absolve your ladyship!” the household chaplain cried, raising his glass.

“Ah, but you don’t know how abominably cross I was,” she laughed. “I scolded my sister Castlewood. I snapped at her children. I even boxed Harry Warrington’s ears—all because the poor boy would not agree to ride with me to Tunbridge Wells!”

“And I will ride with you now, madam!” Harry burst out at once. “Nothing would give me greater pleasure.”

“There, chaplain, you see how dutiful they all are?” she smiled. “I was the only ill-tempered creature in the house. It was cruel of me to treat you all so. Maria, my dear, I beg your pardon, too.”

“Madam, you have never done me any wrong,” Maria said, bewildered by such a humble plea.

“But I have, a great wrong, child!” the Baroness insisted. “I was so bored with my own bad mood that I told you your company would only weary me. You offered to come to Tunbridge with me, and I turned you away rudely. But it will not weary me now! It was so kind of you to offer. I beg, pray, entreat, nay command you to come along.”

Lord Castlewood sipped his wine, looking utterly unconcerned. So that was the end game of this little performance, he thought. “Anything that brings my aunt pleasure will make me happy too,” Maria said, forcing her face bright with joy.

“You shall stay with me as my guest, my dear, and I promise to keep in a good humour the whole while,” Madame de Bernstein went on. Turning to Lord Castlewood, she added, “You will lend me your sister for the trip, won’t you?”

“Lady Maria Esmond is of full age to make her own choices in such matters,” he replied, with a polite bow. “If any of our household can be of service to you, madam, you have but to command us.” The unspoken subtext was clear enough: plague take the old witch, she is stealing Maria away to separate her from this young Virginian heir.

“Tunbridge Wells will be perfectly delightful!” Maria sighed.

Harry traced idle patterns on the oak table with his finger. All the lovely, secret delights he had been daydreaming of—long walks, slow rides, endless whispered conversations, hidden sun-dappled shrubberies and quiet summer houses, hours poring over music books together, moonlight trysts and soft sweet words—he had been certain he was to be left all alone with his adored Maria, when the rest of the household scattered! To sit at her feet, to brush his lips against the hem of her gown, all that unutterable rapture was his for the taking? The thought made his head spin. And then the crash came. He knew something would spoil it, poor Harry thought. He knew some mischance would take her away from the quiet time he’d imagined.

“Of course you will ride with us to Tunbridge, nephew Warrington,” the Baroness said, turning to him. “You will keep us safe from highwaymen, won’t you?”

Harry could feel the heat rush up to his cheeks, and prayed no one noticed how flustered he was. He steadied his voice as best he could, and promised he would ride at their side, and they need fear no danger. Danger? He would welcome it. He would strike down a dozen highwaymen single-handed if any dared approach Maria’s coach. He would not even need to speak to her for days—only ride beside her carriage, catch glimpses of her face at the window, press her gloved hand warmly at the inn at night, and lead her out to the carriage step at dawn. They would have two full days on the road to Tunbridge, and he could stay a day or two after that. Even a man condemned to hang at Newgate would count himself lucky for two or three extra days of life, wouldn’t he?

We will not dwell at length on the full foolishness of Harry Warrington’s lovesick state. Some young men fall gently in love, while others are struck by the fever so fierce it nearly kills them, and carry the scars of that first wild passion all the way to old age, even to the grave. It is no kindness to mock a young man raving in the delirium of that fever. Even if he is head over heels for a woman twice his age—have we not all read the tale of the young French gentleman who killed himself out of passion for Mademoiselle Ninon de l’Enclos, only to learn she was his own grandmother? If you are never guilty of playing the ass for love, you may kick and jeer at poor Harry now. But all you good, honest fellow sinners who have been that silly, all long-eared donkeys who have brayed over a cruel beauty’s face, recognise a brother!

“You will stay a day or two at the Wells with us,” Madame de Bernstein continued, “see us settled in our lodgings, and then you can ride back to Castlewood for the partridge shooting, and all the studies you and my lord have planned together.” Harry bowed in quiet acquiescence. A whole week of seeing her, near her—life was not all empty, after all. “There will be plenty of company at the Wells, too, and I shall be happy to introduce you to everyone,” she added graciously.

“Company?” Harry sighed, before he could catch himself. “Oh, I shall not need any other company. I shall be perfectly content just with the two of you ladies.” His cousin Will stared, bewildered at Harry’s odd taste in entertainment.

This was, by unspoken agreement, to be Harry’s last night of his current visit to Castlewood. So cousin Will suggested that he, the chaplain, and Harry gather in Harry’s private quarters after the ladies retired, to settle their outstanding wagers from their previous evenings of play. Harry, who had won a tidy sum off both of them at cards, was happy enough to agree. Once the ladies had gone up to their chambers, and Lord Castlewood had retreated to his own private apartments as was his custom, the three men gathered in Harry’s little parlour, where a steaming bowl of punch—Will’s favourite midnight companion—sat waiting on the table.

But Will’s idea of settling accounts was pulling out two fresh packs of cards, and proposing they play to double Harry’s winnings, or wipe the slate entirely. The chaplain had no more ready cash on hand than Will, for that matter. Harry had no desire to win either of their money. How could he cause pain to the brother of his adored Maria? How could he let any of her kinfolk accuse him of being ungenerous, or grasping? He was happy enough to give them their chance to win it all back, as they asked. He agreed to play until midnight, for any stakes they named. Soon the dice box rattled, and cards were shuffled and dealt, over and over.

Harry was not paying the slightest attention to the cards, of course. His mind was far away, wandering: At this very moment, my beloved is sitting at her dressing table, her golden hair spilling loose under her maid’s gentle fingers. What a lucky maid that is! Now she is on her knees, my sainted angel, saying her prayers to the same Heaven that holds all the angels. Now she has climbed into bed, behind her soft damask curtains. Bless her, bless her forever.

“A pair of natural vingt-et-uns!” Will was roaring across the table. “You doubled us, so you owe me—wait no, hang it, you have the ten, then the queen—so I owe you another twenty, blast it.”

Will spat oaths by the dozen, and the chaplain grumbled and cursed right alongside him, astonished at Harry’s impossible luck. He won because he did not care a farthing for the cards. Fortune, that flighty, coquettish jade, had a habit of fawn over men who gave her no mind, especially when their hearts were fixed on another far fairer nymph. Will and the chaplain kept raising the stakes, certain the wealthy Virginian would go easy on them and let them win all their losses back. But Harry knew better than to fling his luck away. He had played hundreds of games just like this back on his Virginia plantation, many of which he had kept carefully hidden from his straitlaced mother. He played fair, and he expected fair play in return.

“The luck does seem to be sticking with me, cousin,” he said, when Will’s grumbling grew too loud to ignore, “and I’ve no wish to press my advantage too hard. But you must not think I’m fool enough to throw all my winnings away. I’ve got a stack of your promissory notes already. If we keep playing, let’s have the value of your wagers on the table, please, in hard cash or goods that are worth the sum named.”

“Typical rich man,” Will growled. “Never bet without security, always win because you’ve got the money to throw around.”

“Faith, cousin, you’ve been harping on my wealth for weeks now,” Harry laughed. “I’ve got enough for my own wants, and enough to pay every creditor I have.”

“Ah, if only we could all say that!” the chaplain moaned. “How happy we would be, and how happy all our creditors would be! What have I got to wager against our conqueror here? My new clerical gown, Mr. Warrington—will you lay me five pieces against it? If I lose I can just preach in the old stuff. Wait! I’ve got a fine Chrysostom, a copy of Foxe’s Martyrs, Baker’s Chronicle, and a cow and her calf out in the pasture. What do you say to that lot against your twenty pounds?”

“I will wager one of Will’s twenty-pound notes,” Harry said, pulling the scrap of paper out of his pocket. “Or I’ll bet my brown mare against both your notes of hand, if you haven’t the ready cash.”

“I’ll stake my bay hunter against you for fifty pounds!” Will bawled.

Harry took both wagers. Ten minutes later, the bay hunter, the brown mare, the chaplain’s gown and his whole stack of precious books were all his. Will swore so loud the rafters rattled. The chaplain tore his wig off and threw it on the floor, cursing as loud as his pupil. Harry, meanwhile, was perfectly calm, not a bit elated by his run of luck. They had asked him to play, and he had played. He knew he would win. My beloved is sleeping safe in her room, he thought, staring out his window. How could I lose, when she is kind to me? His gaze fixed on the lit casement across the courtyard, the one he knew belonged to Maria. He had already forgotten all about his two unlucky friends, their groans and their ill fortune, before they even crossed the courtyard to their own rooms. Behind that faint flickering lamp, beneath that bright star hanging low over the roof, was all his joy, his whole heart, all his treasure.
