The rhythmic, percussive *thwack-stamp-thwack* of the fencing match had entirely shattered the delicate acoustic ecology of Captain Costigan’s sitting room. To the little crooked old man in the window-seat, Mr. Bows, the violent vibrations of the gentlemen’s boots were an intolerable industrial intrusion upon the subtle, scratching cadence of his quill as he attempted to transcribe theatre music. Yet, as Major Pendennis crossed the threshold, his polished leather boots stepping with the measured, cyclical grace of an elder who understood the turning wheels of generations, the chaotic sonic environment abruptly shifted. 

The Major distributed the most graceful bows to the flurried assembly. Miss Fotheringay’s cheek flushed with a sudden, healthy bloom, a visceral reaction that set her slow heart beating a new rhythm beneath her unripped satin garments. 

“Me daughter—me friend, Mr. Bows—me gallant young pupil and friend, I may call ’um, Sir Derby Oaks,” Costigan announced, his rich Hiberno-English baritone rolling through the room, preserving in its resonant vowels the fading cultural memory of a deeply rooted, agrarian Ireland. With a splendid wave of his hand, Costigan vanished into the adjoining chamber to twist his lank hair with an ancient brush and to assume a clean collar and his new coat, his 'Shoot.' Sir Derby followed suit, presently emerging cased tightly in his shell-jacket.

When Costigan re-entered, the ambient olfactory landscape of the room—a mingling of wool, rosin, and the faint, sweet memory of morning oats simmered with salted butter—was momentarily overtaken by the sharp scent of his fresh linen. “I needn’t apologoise to ye, Meejor, for receiving ye in me shirt-sleeves,” Costigan said courteously. 

“An old soldier can’t be better employed than in teaching a young one the use of his sword,” the Major replied, his own metropolitan English crisp and dominant. “I perfectly well recollect meeting Mr. Costigan, and hearing him sing at Sir Richard Strachan’s table at Walcheren.” It was a masterstroke of cyclical pacing, seamlessly folding a mythic, martial past into the everyday domesticity of the Chatteris lodgings. Bows looked up, thoroughly puzzled by the untranslatable nuances of this cordiality.

The Major turned his courtly attention to the actress. “Permit me to say your performance delighted and astonished me. You are as majestic as Mrs. Siddons.”

“Faith, I always said so,” Costigan winked, signaling his daughter. Milly rose, gracefully clearing a chair for the elder gentleman. 

“Your manner reminded me of Mars,” the Major continued, bowing. “Did you ever see the Mars, Miss Fotheringay?”

“There was two Mahers in Crow Street,” Miss Emily remarked placidly. “Fanny was well enough, but Biddy was no great things.” 

Here lay the profound untranslatability of subjective experience. The dominant, classical lexicon of the Major’s Roman mythology failed entirely to map onto the localized, lived reality of the Irish provincial theatre circuit. The intersection of her personal identity with her native dialect rendered his metropolitan flattery utterly foreign. 

“Sure, the Major means the god of war, Milly, my dear,” interposed the parent.

“It is not that Mars I meant, though Venus, I suppose, may be pardoned for thinking about him,” the Major smiled, directing his gaze at Sir Derby Oaks. The young Dragoon, entirely lacking in any sensory appreciation or linguistic agility, received the compliment with dense sulkiness. He was a creature of utilitarian force, blind to the subtle power dynamics embedded in the Major’s lexicon. 

When the Major noted that Sir Derby’s father, the Apothecary, had once saved his life, the young officer looked as if he wished the medical intervention had failed. “I’m not in that line myself,” Sir Derby muttered, turning his back on the room's rich interplay of histories. He marched out, and the jarring, metallic clatter of his sabre against the creaking stairs acted as a brutal noise pollution. His angry voice cursed little Tom Creed in the passage, culminating in a heavy, discordant oath as he kicked the boy’s wooden peg-top into the street—a violent erasure of childhood acoustics.

“He’s very well, in spite of his weight,” Milly noted, “but he’s no conversation.” 

With the industrial clatter of the Dragoon gone, Costigan’s inherent agrarian hospitality bloomed. He pressed refreshment upon his guest. The Major, understanding the profound meaning woven into shared sustenance, requested a biscuit and a glass of wine. Milly was dispatched with a piece of silver to source these provisions, returning shortly via little Tommy Creed with a pint of Madeira from the Grapes and sixpennyworth of sorted biscuits from the baker’s. 

When Bows and Miss Hebe finally departed for rehearsal, leaving the two elders to their negotiation, Costigan immediately poured the wine. The Major took his glass and drained it with a jovial, resonant smack of his lips. The Madeira was a revelation—an evocative, liquid time capsule. It carried the sun-baked warmth of volcanic slopes, deeply gustatory notes of oxidized walnut, caramelized figs, and the briny breath of the sea. The sorted biscuits, rough-hewn and aromatic with caraway and dark molasses, spoke of traditional stone-ground milling and the meticulous care of local bakers. The Major, no stranger to the sensory landscapes of fine dining, savored the public-house vintage as if it were Lord Steyne’s own cellar.

“Capital Madeira, Captain Costigan,” the Major declared, allowing the complex finish to linger on his palate. “I drink the health of that charming creature in a bumper. The man who gets her will have a jewel to a wife.”

“And I drink his health, sir, and ye know who I mean, Major,” Costigan replied, his eyes watering with a sentimental, almost mythic emotion. He poured another glass of the dark amber sustenance. “When she leaves me humble home for your own more splendid mansion, I hope she’ll keep a place for her poor old father, poor old Jack Costigan.”

The acoustic warmth of the room suddenly chilled as the Major began to dismantle Costigan’s constructed reality. “You were speaking about our splendid mansion—I mean the park and mansion of Arthur Pendennis, Esquire. Do you know our history? My eldest brother was a country apothecary who made every shilling he died possessed of out of his pestle and mortar.”

“I have consented to waive that objection, sir,” Costigan replied majestically, his dialect wrapping around his fading aristocratic pride. “The Costigans, too, have met with misfortunes; and our house of Castle Costigan is by no manes what it was.”

The Major’s voice grew measured, utilizing the cold, precise lexicon of legal dominance. “My good sir, I tell you the boy is the son of a country apothecary, and that when he comes of age he won’t have a shilling. My brother left little more than five hundred a year behind him.”

“And with aconomy, a handsome sum of money too, sir,” the Captain answered, clinging to the native rhythms of his speech. “Faith, I’ve known a man drink his clar’t, and drive his coach-and-four on five hundred a year and strict aconomy, in Ireland, sir.”

“Every shilling my brother had, he left to his widow,” the Major insisted, his words acting as an aggressive colonizer over Costigan's dreams. “What allowance can my sister make to her son out of five hundred a year to maintain your daughter?”

The realization struck Costigan with the force of a physical blow. His wrath erupted, an operatic, booming resonance that shook the teacups and vibrated through the floorboards. “Am I to understand, sir, that the young gentleman is an imposther? Have you yourself been working upon the feelings of the young man’s susceptible nature to injuice him to break off an engagement?” Costigan pointed a trembling finger toward a chest of drawers. “Look at that chest of dthrawers, it contains heaps of letthers! And when I have dragged the scoundthrel before the Courts of Law, I have another remedy in yondther mahogany case, sir, which shall set me right with any individual who has counselled your nephew to insult a soldier and a gentleman!”

“I am to understand then that you threaten in the first place to publish the letters of a boy of eighteen to a woman of eight-and-twenty: and afterwards to do me the honour of calling me out,” the Major summarized, his cool, metropolitan detachment refusing to engage with the passionate acoustic fury of the Irishman.

“You have described my intentions with perfect accuracy, Meejor Pendennis.”

The Major stood, placing his hat upon his head. The clash of their lexicons was absolute, a tragic testament to the erosion of shared understanding. “To tell lies has not been a habit in our family, Mr. Costigan. I come here as the boy’s guardian to protest against a marriage that cannot but bring poverty and misery with it: and prevent it I will, by every means in my power.”

“Ye said, in the coorse of conversation, that ye were at the George Hotel,” Costigan said, his voice dropping to a stately, dangerous timbre that resonated in the chest. “A friend shall wait upon ye there before ye leave town, sir.”

“Let him make haste, Mr. Costigan,” the Major cried. He turned and retreated down the creaking stairs. Above him, Captain Costigan delivered a magnificent bow of defiance over the landing, leaving the echoing stairwell to absorb the heavy, ringing silence of their severed worlds.
