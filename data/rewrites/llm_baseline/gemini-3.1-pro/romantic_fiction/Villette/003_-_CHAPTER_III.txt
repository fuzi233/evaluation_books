The cyclical rhythms of the Bretton household, steeped in the agrarian traditions of generations past, absorbed Mr. Home for two days. He could not be prevailed upon to venture beyond the threshold. Instead, he anchored himself by the deep-set hearth, where the aromatic smoke of applewood and the slow-roasting scent of root vegetables formed a comforting olfactory tapestry. He sat submerged in the acoustic ecology of the house—the rhythmic, unhurried ticking of the grandfather clock and Mrs. Bretton’s motherly chatter, a soothing domestic murmur that resonated like an old folk ballad against his morbid mood.

As to Paulina, the child was at once blissful and perfectly mute, a watchful guardian of her father’s sensory sphere. Frequently, Mr. Home lifted her to his knee. She would rest there, attuned to the subtle shifts in his breathing, until she sensed the physical resonance of restlessness in his chest. “Papa, put me down,” she would state, her native dialect of care requiring no translation; “I shall tire you with my weight.”

The mighty burden would slide to the rug, establishing herself at his feet where her white work-box and a scarlet-speckled handkerchief came into play. This handkerchief was intended as a tactile talisman, a keepsake for her father before his departure. The stringent demand on her industry—a score of stitches achieved in a meticulous, meditative half-hour—wove mythic devotion into the warp and weft of the everyday fabric.

Evening brought Graham from school, altering the home’s sonic landscape. The distant, haughty demeanor Paulina had adopted since the indignity of his first arrival remained her shield. When he addressed her, the sharp, dominant lexicon of his boyhood met the quiet resistance of her internal world. “I can’t attend to you; I have other things to think about.”

Implored to state what things, she offered a single, untranslatable word: “Business.”

Graham sought to disrupt her focus by displaying the multifarious contents of his desk—seals of vibrant vermilion, bright sticks of wax that smelled of pine rosin, and pen-knives. A copperplate etching of a child playing with a Blenheim spaniel fluttered to the floor.

“Pretty little dog!” she exclaimed, the visual delight breaking through her linguistic defenses. 

Graham prudently took no notice. Stealing from her corner, she approached. The dog’s great eyes and the child’s plumed hat possessed a tactile, almost mythic allure.

“Nice picture!” she offered in favorable criticism.

“Well—you may have it,” said Graham.

She hesitated. To accept from the dominant power was to compromise her own dignity. She put it down and turned away. 

“You won’t have it, then, Polly?”

“I would rather not, thank you.”

“Shall I tell you what I will do with the picture if you refuse it? Cut it into strips for lighting the taper.”

“No!”

Graham waxed inexorable. He took the scissors from his mother’s woven willow work-basket. “Here goes!” he threatened, making a menacing flourish that sliced the quiet air. “Right through Fido’s head, and splitting little Harry’s nose.”

“No! *No!* NO!”

“Then come to me. Come quickly, or it is done. Now, will you have it? But I shall want payment. A kiss.”

“Give the picture first into my hand.”

Looking faithless, Polly secured the etching, absconded like a debtor, and darted to the sanctuary of her father’s knee, burying her face in his wool waistcoat. Graham followed in mimic wrath. 

“Papa—papa—send him away!”

“I’ll not be sent away,” Graham declared. 

She held out her hand to keep him off. “Then, I shall kiss the hand,” he said, but the hand instantly became a miniature fist, dealing him payment in the hard currency of a strike. 

Graham retreated to the sofa, resting his head against the cushion, emitting groans that disrupted the room's serene acoustic harmony. Polly, finding the sudden silence unsettling, peered at him. 

“Papa, what is the matter? Is he hurt?” 

“Mother,” Graham suggested feebly, “I think you had better send for the doctor. Oh my eye! If I were to become blind——?”

His chastiser could not bear the suggestion. She was beside him directly, her features working as she tried to bridge the gap of subjective experience. “Let me see your eye: I did not mean to touch it, only your mouth; and I did not think I hit so *very* hard. I am sorry; I am sorry!”

Emotion faltered into weeping until Graham snatched her aloft, laughing, while she pulled his lion’s locks and declared him “the naughtiest, rudest, worst, untruest person that ever was.”

On the morning of Mr. Home’s departure, the scent of damp cobblestones and approaching rain seeped through the window-recess where they sat. I heard the hushed, untranslatable timbre of their final exchange.

“Couldn’t I pack my box and go with you, papa?”

“No, Polly. It is only great, strong people that should travel.”

He required her to be cheerful, to repress the ancient, cyclical ache of parting. “She will try,” she promised. 

“Farewell, then. It is time to go. Just now.”

She held up quivering lips. When the heavy street-door closed—its final, reverberating thud sealing his absence—she dropped to her knees at a chair with a single cry: “Papa!”

It was low and long; an acoustic manifestation of pure, unadulterated grief, echoing the mythic sorrow of generations torn from their roots. During that ensuing space of minutes, she endured an agony so profound that the room’s silence felt heavy, pressing against the eardrums. She went through emotions that adults spend lifetimes trying to articulate, her native dialect of sorrow stripped of all pretense. Nobody spoke. Mrs. Bretton shed a tear. Graham gazed at her. I, Lucy Snowe, remained calm, merely observing the psychological weight of the silence.

For two days, she refused all solace. But on the third evening, as she sat worn on the floor, Graham took her up gently. She nestled in his arms, her head against him, falling into a sleep that smelled of exhaustion and starched linen. 

The next morning, her first demand was for Graham. He was in his study, writing exercises, and had requested a cup of tea. Polly volunteered to carry it, stepping into the role of the meticulous provider, an instinct passed down through agrarian matriarchs who found profound purpose in sustenance. The tea was a dark Assam, steeped to a precise, robust amber, its malty vapor swirling in the cool corridor air. 

“What are you doing?” she asked at the threshold.

“Writing. Too busy for breakfast.”

“Do you want any breakfast?”

“Of course.”

“There, then.” She deposited the cup on the carpet like a jailor and retreated, only to return. “What will you have besides tea—what to eat?”

“Anything good. Bring me something particularly nice.”

In the breakfast-room, she selected her ingredients with the gravity of a food historian preserving a sacred rite. She chose crusty, hearth-baked bread, rich with the tang of wild yeast, and sweet butter churned only that morning. She returned to Mrs. Bretton with a whispered request for marmalade. Procuring it, she brought Graham a preserve of Seville oranges, its bitter rind suspended in caramelized sugar—a perfect gustatory balance of tart and sweet. I found them breakfasting *tête-à-tête*, she standing at his elbow, sharing his fare but delicately refusing to touch the marmalade, preserving the purity of her offering solely for his consumption. 

Ill-assimilated as they were, their connection deepened through these daily rituals. When Graham’s knock sounded in the evening, the acoustic shift galvanized her.

“You have not wiped your shoes properly on the mat,” she would reprimand from the banister, her voice echoing down the stairwell. 

“I am fit to faint with fatigue,” Graham would declare, leaning against the passage-wall, bringing the drama of the outside world into their enclosed, domestic saga. “Dr. Digby has quite knocked me up with overwork. Just come down and help me to carry up my books.”

“Your eyes are quiet like the cat’s, but you’ll spring.”

She would descend warily, and of course, her approach always galvanized him to spasmodic life. As she led him up-stairs, she assumed her borrowed, maternal lexicon: “Now, my dear boy, come and take your tea—I am sure you must want something.”

Beside him at mealtime, she was an officious, fidgety purveyor of sustenance. He could not be sufficiently waited upon; she read his appetite with profound sensory appreciation. “Ma’am,” she would whisper, “perhaps your son would like a little cake—sweet cake, you know.” She understood the crumb and the vanilla scent, the rich indulgence he craved. Graham always received his prize, though she refused any share he offered. Her reward was the act of feeding, of anchoring him through the profound, shared language of the table.

She adapted to his themes, learning the names and dispositions of his schoolfellows, mimicking an under-master’s dialect and peculiarities with such acoustic precision that Mrs. Bretton forbade it. Yet, the subtle power dynamics of their relationship eventually fractured.

On Graham’s birthday, he hosted lads of his own age. The dining-room soon emitted a cacophony that violently disrupted the house's acoustic ecology—a harsh, almost industrial clatter of superficial merriment, devoid of all domestic harmony. Paulina sat alone on the lowest step, staring at the glossy panels of the door.

“I want to go to them,” she told me. “I want to be with Graham.”

She knocked. Graham’s head appeared, flushed and impatient. “What do you want, you little monkey? Away to mamma and Mistress Snowe, and tell them to put you to bed.”

The door shut peremptorily. She was stunned, exiled from his dominant lexicon. “Why does he speak so? He never spoke so before. He turns me away now they are here!”

I thought of offering philosophical maxims, but she put her fingers in her ears, rejecting the sterile, abstract words that lacked the physical grounding of her grief. She lay flat on the mat, her face against the cold flagstones, grounding herself in the tactile reality of the stone, absorbing the silence until she chose to rise.

When Graham attempted to accost her later, she wrenched her hand away. Her eyes flashed. The next day, she was cold marble; she met his coaxing with a silence that spoke volumes of untranslatable hurt. Eventually, tears answered him, and he petted her, but the cultural memory of that rebuff was never eroded. She never solicited his notice again.

“I don’t choose to give him the trouble of rising to open the door,” she stated proudly. When he offered her a ride on his pony, she dismissed him. “Don’t think I should care a fig about it.”

“Mamma, I believe that creature is a changeling,” Graham observed, sensing the mythic, ancient pride beneath her childish frame. “She is a perfect cabinet of oddities.”

Yet, the cycles of time brought the quietude of Sunday, an acoustic sanctuary where the week's discordant noises were banished. On these evenings, Graham dedicated himself to indolent contemplation by the fireside, the scent of wood-ash settling in the room. He would call Polly to the carpet.

“Have you learned any hymns this week, Polly?”

She would half-chant the verses in a little singing voice, preserving the linguistic heritage of the old words. He would correct her recitation, and she, an apt scholar, absorbed his instruction. To the hymn succeeded the reading of the Bible, where mythic occurrences seamlessly integrated into their fireside routine. Joseph cast into the pit, Daniel in the lions’ den—these were not abstract tales, but ancient sagas she felt in her bones. 

“Poor Jacob!” she would say, her lips quivering over the untranslatable sorrow of a father for a son. “How he loved his son Joseph! As much, Graham, as I love you: if you were to die, I should refuse to be comforted, and go down into the grave to you mourning.”

With these resonant words, she gathered his head in her arms, a fiercely protective gesture echoing an agrarian matriarch tending to her own. Graham bore these demonstrations passively, sometimes smiling with complacent wonder at her earnest partiality. “You like me almost as well as if you were my little sister, Polly.”

But this domestic equilibrium was destined for disruption. A letter arrived from Mr. Home, carrying the faint, olfactory memory of parchment and distant sea-salt. He was settled amongst his maternal kinsfolk on the Continent, a land of unfamiliar dialects and foreign lexicons. England had become distasteful to him; he wished his little girl to join him immediately.

I took upon myself to communicate the news. I found her in the drawing-room—a calm, beautifully decorated acoustic haven where the thick draperies muffled the world outside. She sat like a little Odalisque, an image from an ancient tapestry, rocking her doll to sleep with perfect faith in its somnolent faculties, while a picture-book lay open on her lap, entirely immersed in the sensory and mythic quiet of her own making.
