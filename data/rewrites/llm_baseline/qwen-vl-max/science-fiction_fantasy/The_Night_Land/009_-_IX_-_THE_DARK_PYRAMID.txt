Two hours after descending from the Gorge, I stood at last upon the open land beyond. Though my mind was still reeling—unsettled by the suddenness of it all—I felt a deep surge of joy in the overwhelming brilliance of this new world. The air shimmered with light, and the horizon burned with strange colors, as if the very earth had been set ablaze.

Before I’d even reached the bottom of the Gorge, I’d paused at its mouth, high above the valley, to gaze across the vast landscape. From there, I counted twenty-seven towering volcanoes—great mountains of fire that pulsed and raged like living things. That number did not include two distant ranges of fiery hills burning on the horizon to my right, nor the countless smaller vents scattered across the land like embers tossed into the wind.

This was a realm shaped by fire and water. A small volcanic hill rose from the sea, no more than a mile away from where I stood, and behind it, a broad stretch of water spread out in every direction. I saw three smaller seas and one immense ocean that stretched endlessly into the crimson glow of the distant fire-mountains, vanishing into the dark sky without end.

Islands dotted the waters, each crowned with smoking peaks. In other places, the fire-hills erupted directly from the sea floor, rising like jagged teeth from the waves. Over the nearer waters, thick clouds of steam hung low, as though the sea itself boiled in fits and starts.

And beneath it all—a constant, deep rumble, like thunder trapped in stone—shook the air. It came from everywhere and nowhere, a steady vibration that seemed to emanate from the heart of the land. I knew then that these were not mere eruptions, but voices—the groans of molten worlds speaking through their own flesh.

It was utterly alien to me. Yet here, too, I sensed something familiar: the World-Noise, the faint hum of life-energy that once filled our ancient age. It was stronger here, clearer, as though time had sharpened its presence rather than dimmed it.

Now, let me describe what I saw with greater detail.

First, to the left of the Gorge’s mouth, loomed a massive, blackened mountain that pierced the night sky—perhaps fifteen or twenty miles high. On its far side, a great volcanic peak jutted upward, five miles into the darkness, and above it, another, even higher, rose ten miles into the void. And atop the left ridge of this mountain, two colossal fire-hills blazed with such intensity they looked like dying suns suspended in the night.

Below them, vast mounds of ash and scorched debris lay piled like monuments to destruction—evidence of eruptions that had poured down for eternity, building silent tombs to time’s fury.

To my right, the sea stretched endlessly, lit by the red glare of the fire-hills. To my left, dense forests rose up, broken only by the occasional monstrous volcano erupting from beyond the trees. This was the first impression seared into my mind: a world alive with heat, motion, and mystery.

After descending, I hesitated. Which way should I go? I searched for signs, then climbed back into the Gorge, cursing myself for not mapping my path earlier.

But when I returned to the rim, I realized there was only one route forward. To the right, only sea. To the left, a narrow strip of land along the shore—possibly leading farther ahead. So I descended again, stepping into the realm I would come to call the Land of Seas and Fire.

By now, twenty-four hours had passed since my last sleep. I needed rest. I found a sheltered hollow beneath three large trees, ringed by dry, warm rock. After eating three nutrient tablets and drinking some water—my stomach still yearning for real food—I made a bed in the basin and lay down. My thoughts drifted to Naani, but sleep overtook me before I could finish the thought.

Then—awake! I was floating in hot water. A miracle I hadn’t drowned. I stood, drenched, and saw the basin was full of steaming liquid, sharp to the tongue. Water flowed in from a narrow fissure on the opposite side, bubbling and gurgling as if fed from a deep, boiling well below. I was glad it didn’t erupt in flames.

I quickly stripped off my armor, having already removed my gear from the pool. I laid my clothes on a heated rock to dry, then stepped into the warm water. It was soothing, invigorating. Yet, though I saw no immediate danger, I soon learned this land teemed with creatures. But never once did I feel the cold touch of Evil—only the distant pull of something vast and terrible. I believe the Dark Pyramid drew such forces, much as sharks gather around a ship carrying livestock. But how these powers entered our world… I cannot say. I’ve speculated before, but speculation is not truth. There is no certainty.

My clothes dried quickly. I emerged from the pool—now nearly empty—and dressed again. I felt refreshed, yet ready to sleep once more. I returned to the basin and slept six more hours, waking briefly each time the water bubbled anew.

When dawn came, I was alert and strong. I ate and drank, then set out again.

For the entire day, I marched swiftly. To my left, the endless forest; to my right, the sea. Trees often grew into the water, forming tangled paths I walked through. Never had I known such wild beauty—alive, breathing, mysterious. The underground groves had been solemn, yes, but quiet. Here, the woods sang with unseen life. Their scent was sweet, intoxicating.

All day, eyes watched me from the shadows—fleeting glances, then silence. I never met anything hostile, but the awareness of being observed kept me tense.

Three times, I passed small fire-hills that roared and spat flame. Each time, the forest around them was dead—trees blackened, fallen—but near the base, tiny plants thrived, born between eruptions. Life persisted, even in ruin.

I crossed thirty-seven boiling springs—some truly bubbling, others just hissing with steam. Many sounded like beasts roaring, making me pause in fear.

At the eighteenth hour, I stopped to eat and drink. The water fizzed strongly, as if charged with energy. Then I lay down against a large rock, choosing a spot where no creature could approach unseen.

Sleep came easily. But not fully. I kept my spirit awake, knowing the forest held watchers. As I drifted off, I thought of Naani—her face, her voice, her presence seeming so close, as if she whispered through the darkness. I sent her a blessing, and vowed to move faster, to reach the lesser Refuge sooner.

Then—sudden awakening.

The light revealed everything instantly. No shadows to hide danger. Before me, within twenty paces, crouched six figures at the edge of the woods. They were hunched, broad-shouldered, their necks thick and bull-like. Their eyes gleamed like those of beasts. They watched me silently.

I gripped the Diskos. My heart trembled, but my will held firm.

Then—gone. They vanished. I stared. I saw no movement. No trace. Only the trees, still and dark.

I checked the time—five hours had passed. I ate, drank, and waited. Fear gnawed at me. I knew they were nearby, perhaps calling others. I could not stay.

I moved on, fast and alert, the Diskos always ready. For thirty hours straight, I traveled, stopping only to eat and drink every six hours. I pushed through the forest, scanning every shadow.

Still, I felt them—moving beside me, keeping pace, hidden. The knowledge drained my hope. I could not trust my own senses.

I refused to sleep until I found safety. And so I walked—thirty hours without rest.

Near the end, I saw water ahead—beyond the sea. At first, I thought the sea turned inward, but it was a river flowing into it from the land to the left.

At the river's mouth lay a small island. I considered it a refuge—safe from the Humped Men. But I needed to cross.

I could not swim. The river was wide, warm, and likely filled with monsters. I walked upstream, searching for a narrow point. But before long, I found a second river joining the first, less than a mile from the sea. Now I was trapped—sea to one side, river to the other, and the main river before me.

Desperation sparked clarity. I searched for fallen trees. Found two small ones. I fashioned a pole from a sapling, lashed the trees together with my belts, and built a crude raft.

All the while, I scanned the woods—afraid they might attack before I launched.

Finally, I pushed off. I paddled clumsily, struggling against the current. Half an hour later, I reached the island. It seemed safe. I ate, drank, and fell into a deep, uninterrupted sleep—nine hours of rest. I woke rested, renewed.

I ate again, dismantled the raft, and continued. I believed I had left the Humped Men behind. Perhaps they existed on both sides, but I had only seen those on one.

That day, I moved swiftly, passing wonders—boiling springs, fire-hills, strange creatures. I saw four enormous beasts pass by, but hid in time.

My thoughts turned often to Naani. But each time, danger interrupted—something new, something urgent. I had little time for love, only for survival. Yet was my journey not driven by love? Was not every step taken for her?

I walked through forests, past uncounted geysers and fire-hills. The air thrummed with noise—roars, hisses, distant eruptions. But I remained unharmed.

A thousand times, I glimpsed life—creatures watching, hiding, moving. I stayed cautious, but swift. My body was lean, my stride strong.

In some places, the forest flourished—greenery thick, air sweet. I wondered if, in some distant future, descendants of our people might return here, fleeing the frozen Upper Valley. Could they build a new Refuge? But how would they escape the evils drawn to the Mighty Pyramid? It was only a dream, a fleeting hope.

And still I pressed on.

Just before the eighteenth hour, I emerged from the trees. To my right, the sea dropped sharply down from a cliff. I had been climbing for a long time.

And then I saw it—a thing that froze my breath.

A tall, pointed rock, perhaps a hundred feet high. But on top of it, something else—an immense, flat slab of stone laid across the peak, shaped oddly, almost like a bridge. Its underside mirrored the rock beneath. On top, trees and green growth clung to ledges, as if nature had claimed it.

I approached slowly. This, I decided, would be a safe place to sleep—if I could climb it.

I began the ascent. The rock was steep, high. I climbed for a long time, weary from the day’s journey. Near the top, I found a shelf and stopped. I ate, drank, and fell asleep—too exhausted to think of Naani.

Seven hours later, I awoke abruptly. My spirit screamed warning. I rose quietly, Diskos in hand. I looked around—nothing. But then I crept to the edge.

Below, two Humped Men climbed the rock—fast, silent. They smelled me. They were coming to kill me.

I readied the Diskos. I saw their thick necks, their powerful shoulders—like bulls. They moved with terrifying speed.

I stepped back from the edge, weapon raised. I must strike one fast—before the other flanked me.

And so I waited, poised, ready to fight for my life.
