The winter following the initial excursion brought a repetition of strangeness, though the scale shifted from travel to obsession. Every correspondence sent to the Widener Library, the Bibliothèque Nationale, the British Museum, the University of Buenos Aires, and the Library of Miskatonic University ended in silence or refusal. The desired volume remained inaccessible through bureaucratic channels. Consequently, Wilbur traveled personally. He arrived in Arkham shabby, dirty, bearded, and speaking with a dialect that marked him as uncouth. Carrying a cheap new valise from Osborn's general store, this creature—almost eight feet tall, dark, and possessing the angular, goatish profile of a gargoyle—presented himself at the college library. His sole purpose was the acquisition of the forbidden text: the *Necronomicon* of the mad Arab Alhazred, specifically the Latin edition printed in Spain during the seventeenth century, held under lock and key.

Wilbur had never witnessed a city before, yet he navigated the streets with singular intent, passing heedlessly by the great white-fanged watchdog that barked with unnatural fury and tugged frantically at its chain. In his possession was the imperfect English copy of Dr. Dee's translation, bequeathed by his grandfather. Upon gaining access to the Latin original, he immediately began collating the two texts, searching for a specific passage located on the 751st page of his defective volume. He could not help but mention this discrepancy to the librarian, Henry Armitage—a man of considerable erudition holding degrees from Miskatonic, Princeton, and Johns Hopkins—who had previously visited the farm and now questioned him politely.

Armitage watched as Wilbur copied the chosen formula. The left-hand page of the Latin text contained monstrous threats regarding the peace and sanity of the world. The words, translated mentally by the librarian, spoke of a reality where humanity was neither the oldest nor the last of Earth's masters. "Not in the spaces we know, but between them," the text declared. "They walk serene and primal, undimensioned and to us unseen. Yog-Sothoth knows the gate. Yog-Sothoth is the gate. Yog-Sothoth is the key and guardian of the gate. Past, present, future, all are one in Yog-Sothoth." It detailed how These entities knew where the Old Ones had broken through and where they would break through again, noting that while their scent could sometimes reveal their proximity, their true semblance was invisible except in the distorted features of those they had begotten upon mankind. The text described a realm where the wind gibbered with their voices and the earth muttered with their consciousness, bending forests and crushing cities without being beheld. It mentioned Kadath in the cold waste, the ice deserts of the South, and the sunken isles of the ocean, all bearing seals of these powers. Even Great Cthulhu, their cousin, could perceive them only dimly. The prophecy concluded with a chilling assertion: "Man rules now where They ruled once; They shall soon rule where man rules now."

Dr. Armitage, associating these lines with the brooding presences of Dunwich and the dim, hideous aura radiating from Wilbur Whateley—from a dubious birth to a cloud of probable matricide—felt a wave of fright as tangible as a draft from a tomb. The bent, goatish giant before him seemed like the spawn of another planet or dimension, linked to black gulfs of essence stretching beyond all spheres of force, matter, space, and time.

Presently, Wilbur raised his head and began speaking in a strange, resonant fashion, hinting at vocal organs unlike any run-of-the-mill human anatomy. "Mr. Armitage," he said, "I calc'late I've got to take that book home. They's things in it I've got to try under sarten conditions that I can't git here, an' it 'ud be a mortal sin to let a red-tape rule hold me up. Let me take it along, sir, an' I'll swar they wun't nobody know the difference. I dun't need to tell ye I'll take good keer of it. It wa'n't me that put this Dee copy in the shape it is...."

He paused, sensing the firm denial on the librarian's face, his own goatish features shifting into a crafty expression. Armitage, half-ready to suggest copying necessary passages, suddenly recoiled. The responsibility of handing such a being the key to blasphemous outer spheres was too great. Whateley perceived the rejection and attempted to deflect it lightly. "Wal, all right, ef ye feel that way abaout it. Maybe Harvard wun't be so fussy as yew be." Without further comment, he rose and strode out of the building, stooping awkwardly at each doorway.

From the window, Armitage heard the savage yelping of the watchdog and studied Whateley's gorilla-like lope across the campus. He recalled the wild tales and old Sunday stories from the *Advertiser*, the lore gathered from Dunwich rustics, and the certainty that unseen, non-earthly horrors rushed fetid through New England's glens. Now, he sensed the close presence of a terrible fragment of the intruding horror, glimpsing a hellish advance in the black dominion of the ancient nightmare. With a shudder of disgust, Armitage locked away the *Necronomicon*, though the room still reeked with an unholy, unidentifiable stench—the same odor that had sickened him at the Whateley farmhouse less than three years prior.

"Inbreeding?" Armitage muttered half-aloud, laughing mockingly at the village rumors. "Great God, what simpletons! Show them Arthur Machen's *Great God Pan* and they'll think it a common Dunwich scandal!" Yet the question persisted: what cursed, shapeless influence had fastened itself on the world in half-human flesh nine months after May Eve of 1912? What walked on the mountains that night?

During the ensuing weeks, Dr. Armitage collected all possible data on Wilbur Whateley and the formless presences surrounding Dunwich. Communication with Dr. Houghton of Aylesbury revealed much to ponder in the grandfather's final words. A visit to Dunwich Village yielded little new information, but a close survey of the *Necronomicon* in the sections Wilbur had sought provided terrible clues to the nature and desires of the strange evil threatening the planet. Talks with students of archaic lore in Boston and letters to others abroad fueled a growing amazement that escalated from alarm to acute spiritual fear. As summer approached, Armitage felt dimly that something must be done about the lurking terrors of the upper Miskatonic valley and the monstrous being known to the human world as Wilbur Whateley.
