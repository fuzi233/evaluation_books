"So on those happy days of yore
Oft as I dare to dwell once more,
Still must I miss the friends so tried,
Whom Death has severed from my side.

But ever when true friendship binds,
Spirit it is that spirit finds;
In spirit then our bliss we found,
In spirit yet to them I'm bound."

Margaret had risen before the dawn, allowing herself a solitary moment of quiet grief unseen by others, followed by a bright composure for the journey ahead. Her only lingering fear was missing the train, yet she arrived with the party just in time, breathing a sigh of relief as the carriage swept past the familiar stations of the south. The landscape unfolded in warm, golden hues, the tiled roofs glowing under a sun that seemed to bathe the countryside in a ruddier light than the cold slates of the North. Pigeons circled the quaint gables, settling softly to enjoy the heat, while the stations themselves were surprisingly still, devoid of the bustling trade that characterized the northern lines. Spectators lounged idly, hands in pockets, seemingly content to watch the trains depart and stare blankly at the empty tracks left behind. The air danced over the golden stillness of the fields, evoking images of German idylls and ancient legends, until the carriage halted at the point where the fly would take them to Helstone.

As the vehicle slowed, a sharp pang pierced Margaret's heart, a mixture of pain and pleasure she could scarcely define. Every mile was steeped in memory, each turn and familiar tree identical to those of her childhood summers, yet now flooded with sunlight that felt alien to her orphaned state. Nature remained eternally young, indifferent to the profound loss she carried; her parents, once so present, had vanished from the earth, leaving her alone on a road that seemed to mock her solitude with its radiant beauty.

Mr. Bell, sensing her turmoil, wisely maintained a respectful silence. They arrived at the Lennard Arms, a sturdy establishment standing apart from the road, its front facing the village green where an ancient lime tree sheltered the Lennard escutcheon. The inn door stood open, but there was no frantic hospitality. When the landlady, Mrs. Purkis, finally appeared, she offered a warm, motherly welcome, apologizing profusely for her delay due to the demands of hay-making. Her eyes widened in recognition when she spotted Margaret, calling loudly for her daughter Jenny before rushing to shake Margaret's hand with affectionate familiarity.

"How are you all?" Mrs. Purkis asked, her voice trembling slightly. "How's the Vicar and Miss Dixon? God bless the Vicar! We've never stopped grieving for his departure."

Margaret attempted to speak of her father's death and her mother's passing, but the effort choked her. She could only touch her deep mourning and whisper the single word, "Papa."

"Never!" Mrs. Purkis exclaimed, turning to Mr. Bell for confirmation. "A gentleman visited us in the spring, mentioning Mrs. Hale's passing, but never a word of the Vicar's illness!"

"It is true," Mr. Bell confirmed gently. "He died suddenly while visiting me in Oxford. He was a good man, Mrs. Purkis, and I am grateful for such a calm end. Come, Margaret, my dear. Your father was my oldest friend, and she is my goddaughter. We came to see the old place, and I know you can provide us with comfortable rooms and a fine dinner. You may not remember me clearly, but my name is Bell; I have slept here often when the parsonage was full and tasted your excellent ale."

"To be sure! I ask your pardon," Mrs. Purkis stammered, distracted by her focus on Margaret. "Let me show you to a room where you can remove your bonnet and wash your face. This morning I placed fresh roses head-downward in the water-jug, hoping someone might arrive, for nothing scents spring water like a musk rose. To think the Vicar is dead! Well, we must all die; that gentleman said he was recovering from his sorrow over Mrs. Hale."

"Come down to me later, Mrs. Purkis," Mr. Bell instructed, taking charge. "I wish to consult with you regarding dinner."

In Margaret's chamber, the casement window was nearly obscured by climbing vines and roses. Pushing them aside, she caught a glimpse of the parsonage chimneys above the trees, their outlines familiar yet distant. Mrs. Purkis smoothed the bed and sent Jenny for lavender-scented towels, lamenting the changes in the village. "Times have changed, miss," she noted. "Our new Vicar has seven children and is building a nursery where the old arbour stood. He has installed new grates and plate-glass windows. He and his wife are energetic, doing much 'good,' though I suspect they merely turn things upside down for little purpose. He is a teetotaller and magistrate, and his wife makes yeastless bread and talks incessantly. They are so loud one cannot think until they leave. He will inspect the hay-fields and peep in, causing a fuss if they drink ginger-beer instead of malt liquor. My ancestors sent good ale to haymakers and used salts for ailments, though Mrs. Hepworth wants to replace medicine with comfits. I must go now, miss, though I wish to hear much more."

Mr. Bell prepared a rustic luncheon of strawberries, cream, brown bread, milk, Stilton, and port for Margaret. After this simple meal, they set out for a walk, unsure of which direction to take amidst the familiar scenery.

"Shall we pass the vicarage?" Mr. Bell suggested.

"No, not yet," Margaret replied. "We will make a round and return by it."

As they walked, Margaret noted the disappearance of old trees felled the previous autumn and the replacement of a decaying squatter's cottage with a tidy, respectable new one. She grieved for these losses as if they were old friends. They passed the spot where she and Mr. Lennox had once sketched; the lightning-scarred beech tree was gone, replaced by a small garden. The old man who inhabited the ruined cottage was dead.

"I did not think I had grown so old," Margaret murmured, turning away with a sigh.

"Yes," Mr. Bell agreed. "It is the first changes among familiar things that confuse the young. Later, we accept instability as a matter of course. For you, it is new and oppressive."

"Let us visit little Susan," Margaret said, leading him up a grassy path under a forest glade.

"With all my heart," Mr. Bell replied, adding, "I have a kindness for all Susans, for simple Susan's sake."

"My little Susan was disappointed when I left without saying goodbye," Margaret confessed, feeling a pang of guilt. "It weighs on my conscience. But it is a long way; are you sure you won't tire?"

"Quite sure," Mr. Bell assured her, slowing his pace. "Unless I am Hamlet, Prince of Denmark, walking with someone 'fat and scant o' breath' sounds romantic. Have compassion on my infirmities for his sake."

"I will walk slower for your own sake. I like you twenty times better than Hamlet."

"On the principle that a living ass is better than a dead lion?"

"Perhaps so. I do not analyze my feelings."

"I am content to accept your liking for me without scrutinizing its origins. Only, let us not walk at a snail's pace."

"Very well. Walk at your own speed, and I will follow. Or stop and meditate like your Hamlet if I go too fast."

"Thank you. But since my mother did not murder my father and marry my uncle, I have little to ponder except the prospect of a well-cooked dinner. What do you think?"

"I am hopeful. She was renowned as a cook in Helstone."

"But have you considered the distraction of mind caused by all this haymaking?"

Margaret felt Mr. Bell's kindness in attempting to lift her spirits with trivial talk, preventing her from dwelling too deeply on the past. Yet, she would have preferred silence, or even solitude, over his cheerful distractions.

They reached the cottage of Susan's widowed mother, only to find Susan absent, having gone to the parochial school. Margaret felt a disappointment the mother sensed, prompting an apologetic explanation.

"Oh! it is quite right," Margaret said, relieved. "I am glad to hear it. Only she used to stay home with you."

"Yes, she did. I miss her sadly. I used to teach her what little I knew at night. She was becoming such a handy girl, I miss her sore. But she is far above me in learning now," the mother sighed.

"I am all wrong," Mr. Bell grumbled. "I am a century behind the world. But I believe the child received a better, simpler education staying home, helping her mother, and reading the New Testament nightly, than from all the schooling under the sun."

Margaret, wishing to avoid prolonging the discussion before the mother, turned the conversation to old Betty Barnes.

"How is old Betty Barnes?" she asked.

"I don't know," the woman replied shortly. "We are not friends."

"Why not?" Margaret queried, recalling her former role as the village peacemaker.

"She stole my cat."

"Did she know it was yours?"

"I don't know. I reckon not."

"Well, could you not retrieve it when you told her?"

"No! For she had burnt it."

"Burnt it!" Margaret and Mr. Bell exclaimed in unison.

"Roasted it!" the woman clarified.

It was no clarification. Through questioning, Margaret learned the horrifying truth: Betty Barnes, having lent her husband's Sunday clothes to a gypsy fortune-teller on the promise of their return before Goodman Barnes missed them, grew terrified when they failed to appear. Driven by fear of her husband's anger and fueled by savage country superstitions, she believed the cries of a cat being boiled or roasted could compel the powers of darkness to fulfill the gypsy's wishes. The poor woman genuinely believed in the charm's efficacy, feeling only indignation that her cat had been chosen for sacrifice. Margaret listened in horror, unable to enlighten the woman, who insisted that while the act was cruel, it was unmatched in granting wishes. Margaret walked away sick at heart.

"You are a good girl not to triumph over me," Mr. Bell remarked.

"How? What do you mean?"

"I admit I was wrong about schooling. Anything rather than have that child raised in such practical paganism."

"Oh! I remember. Poor little Susan! I must see her. Would you mind calling at the school?"

"Not a bit. I am curious to see the teaching she receives."

Emerging from the forest, the buzz of voices from the schoolhouse filled the air like a hive of bees. The door stood open, and a brisk lady in black greeted them with the hostess-like air Margaret remembered from her mother, though softer and more languid. Recognizing the current Vicar's wife, Margaret felt a urge to retreat but quickly conquered it, advancing modestly as she exchanged glances of recognition with old acquaintances. The Vicar's lady heard the name "Miss Hale" and her manner shifted instantly to one of kindness, bordering on patronization. She extended her hand to Mr. Bell, assuming he was the Vicar's father based on a resemblance, and expressed her gladness to see him.

Margaret corrected her, stammering about her father's death, wondering how Mr. Hale could revisit Helstone if the Vicar's wife held such assumptions. She did not hear Mrs. Hepworth's words, leaving Mr. Bell to respond while she scanned the room for familiar faces.

"Ah! I see you wish to take a class, Miss Hale," Mrs. Hepworth announced. "First class, stand up for a parsing lesson with Miss Hale."

Margaret, whose visit was sentimental rather than academic, felt a sense of intrusion. Yet, seeing the eager faces of girls who had been baptized by her father, she sat down, tracing their changing features. She held Susan's hand briefly, unnoticed, as Mrs. Hepworth explained the Phonetic system to Mr. Bell. Margaret bent over her book, hearing only the children's voices, until old memories surged forth and her eyes filled with tears. Suddenly, a pause occurred as a girl stumbled over the simple word "a."

"A, an indefinite article," Margaret said softly.

"I beg your pardon," Mrs. Hepworth interrupted, alert and eager. "But we are taught by Mr. Milsome to call 'a' an—who can remember?"

"An adjective absolute," half a dozen voices chorused. Margaret sat abashed; the children knew more than she did. Mr. Bell turned away, smiling.

Margaret said nothing during the remainder of the lesson. Afterward, she quietly spoke with a few old favorites, noting how they were growing into young women, slipping from her memory as she faded from theirs. Though sad, she was glad to have seen them again. When school ended, Mrs. Hepworth invited Margaret and Mr. Bell to the parsonage to see the "alterations" the Vicar was making. Margaret cared little for the improvements, which jarred against her cherished memories, but she longed to see the old place one last time, bracing herself for the pain she knew awaited.

The parsonage had been transformed, both inside and out. The garden, once meticulously trimmed, was strewn with children's toys and a straw hat crushed onto a rose tree, destroying a delicate branch that would have been treasured in her time. The hall bore the marks of rough, healthy childhood.

"You must excuse this untidiness, Miss Hale," Mrs. Hepworth said. "When the nursery is finished, I shall insist on order. We are converting your room into a nursery, I believe. How did you manage without one?"

"We were but two," Margaret replied. "You have many children, I presume?"
