The first breath of the unimagined snowstorm hung in the air like a translucent veil, setting off the azure clarity of the sky. Inside the house, Anna stood in the shadow of Rex's silence. For the first time, the boy she loved had withheld his innermost thoughts, assuming she already knew the weight of his affection, while simultaneously rendering her own confessions impossible. A strange, heavy ache settled in her chest—not merely the pain of seeing him look elsewhere, but a terrifying realization that Gwendolen, her cousin whom she revered with simple, dutiful loyalty, viewed Rex with indifference. To Anna, Gwendolen was a magnificent creature, a wondrous animal of beauty whose nature remained a locked mystery; she feared that this creature might one day devour the small, cherished pets of her own heart. Now, Anna's spirit sank beneath the crushing conviction she dared not voice: that Gwendolen would never return Rex's love. Where Anna found tenderness, Gwendolen found only boredom; where Anna saw a soul to cherish, Gwendolen saw a project to dismiss. Worse still, Gwendolen was forever chasing the extraordinary, while poor Rex, terrified of his father's wrath and deemed too young for such passions by Anna's own reckoning, remained trapped in the role of a future housekeeper. In the prospect of his inevitable suffering, Anna found herself developing a fierce distaste for her cousin's captivating arrogance.

Time seemed to stretch and compress, filled with a tumultuous life visible to all but understood by none. If asked, Rex would claim he harbored no secrets regarding an impending engagement he intended to announce to his father immediately. Yet, for the first time, he was guarded—not just about his feelings, but about his very movements, a shift that struck Anna as profoundly significant. Meanwhile, the elders moved through their days oblivious to the pantomime unfolding around them. They watched the young people with the detached curiosity one reserves for observing lively ants, missing the dramatic tension that played out clearly only in the minds of those living it.

On a gray morning, as her father departed for the sessions with Mrs. Gascoigne, Anna caught sight of Rex donning his antigropelos, the closest approximation to hunting gear he owned.

"Where are you going, Rex?" she asked.

"To see the hounds throw off at the Three Barns," he replied.

"Are you going to take Gwendolen?" she whispered, her voice trembling.

"She told you, did she?"

"No, but I thought... Does papa know?"

"Not that I'm aware of. I shouldn't suppose he'd trouble himself."

"You're going to use his horse?"

"He knows I do that whenever I can."

"Don't let Gwendolen ride after the hounds, Rex," Anna pleaded, her fears granting her a sudden, piercing insight.

"Why not?" Rex smiled, the expression tinged with provocation.

"Papa and Mamma and Aunt Davilow all wish her not to. They think it isn't right for her."

"Why should you suppose she intends to do what is not right?"

"Gwendolen minds nobody sometimes," Anna retorted, emboldened by a flash of anger.

"Then she wouldn't mind me," Rex said, turning her anxiety into a perverse jest.

"Oh, Rex, I cannot bear it. You will make yourself very unhappy." The dam broke, and Anna burst into tears.

"Nannie, Nannie, what on earth is the matter with you?" Rex exclaimed, impatient at being detained, hat in hand and whip poised.

"She will not care for you one bit—I know she never will!" the poor child sobbed, losing all control.

Rex flushed crimson and hurried from the hall, leaving Anna alone with the misery of having spoken in vain. As he rode away, he replayed her words, finding them unwelcome, akin to a bad prophecy even when laughed at. Yet, he quickly rationalized them as mere expressions of little Anna's tender protectiveness, regretting only that he could not soothe her before departing. However, every other emotion on the subject rapidly dissolved into a stubborn resistance to her fears, fueled by a new determination to prove her wrong. This certainty carried just enough kinship to doubt and unease to hasten a confession that safety might have delayed.

When Rex reached the gate, Gwendolen was already mounted, pacing the avenue. Anticipating his delay, she had prepared the groom to wait behind her, unwilling to linger beyond reason. But now, the groom was dismissed, and the pair rode away in a blaze of delightful freedom. Gwendolen was in high spirits, appearing more lovely than Rex had ever seen her; her figure, long white throat, and the curve of her cheek were framed perfectly by the compact simplicity of her riding dress. To Rex, a youthful lover, she seemed the embodiment of the good, the true, and the beautiful—a fundamental identity already manifest in the object of his adoration. Most observers would surely find it equally plausible that a girl should hold such reverent impressions of Rex, for in his handsome face lay nothing to counteract the indefinable, stinging quality—a trace of demon ancestry—that caused some to hesitate in their admiration of Gwendolen.

It was an exquisite January morning, devoid of rain, with a gray sky providing the calmest backdrop for a mild winter scene. The grassy borders of the lanes, hedgerows dotted with red berries and filled with low twitterings, the purple bareness of the elms, and the rich brown of the furrows created a tapestry of peace. The horses' hooves chimed musically, echoing their young voices. Gwendolen laughed at his equipment, noting how he was the reverse of a dandy, and he reveled in her laughter. The freshness of the morning mingled with the freshness of their youth; every sound from their clear throats, every glance exchanged, bubbled forth from a spring of pure joy. It was all morning to them, inside and out. One is tempted, looking back on such moments, to indulge in that futile wishing: *if only things could have been slightly otherwise, so greatly otherwise afterwards*—if only these two beautiful young creatures could have pledged themselves then and there, never to swerve from that vow throughout their lives. Some of the goodness Rex believed in was indeed present. Goodness is a vast, often prospective word, like harvest. At one stage, it lies buried underground, an indeterminate future; is the germ prospering in the darkness? At another, it pushes forth delicate green blades, and soon the trembling blossoms stand ready to be shattered by an hour of rough wind or rain. Each stage carries its peculiar blight, vulnerable to the choking action of the foul soil that rears it or the damage brought from distant corruption.

"Anna had got it into her head that you wanted to ride after the hounds this morning," Rex said, his secret association with Anna's earlier words making the topic feel perilously close to the most momentous of subjects.

"Did she?" Gwendolen laughed. "What a little clairvoyant she is!"

"Shall you?" Rex asked. He hadn't believed she would actually do it if the elders objected, but he trusted she had good reasons.

"I don't know. I can't tell what I'll do until I get there. Clairvoyants are often wrong; they foresee what is likely. I'm not fond of what is likely; it's always dull. I do what is unlikely."

"There, you've told me a secret," Rex remarked. "Once I knew what people generally would likely do, I'd know you'd do the opposite. So you'd come round to a likelihood of your own sort. I'll be able to calculate on you. You couldn't surprise me."

"Yes, I could. I'd turn round and do what was likely for people in general," Gwendolen replied with a musical laugh.

"You see, you can't escape some sort of likelihood. And contradictoriness makes the strongest likelihood of all. You must give up a plan."

"No, I won't. My plan is to do what pleases me." (Should any young lady wish to emulate Gwendolen, let her observe the set of her head and neck: had the angle been different, the chin more protrusive, the cervical vertebrae slightly more curved, ten to one Gwendolen's words would have jarred the sweet-natured Rex. But everything odd in her speech was merely humor and pretty banter, which he eagerly sought to steer toward a single point.)

"Can you manage to feel only what pleases you?" he asked.

"Of course not; that comes from what other people do. But if the world were pleasanter, one would only feel what was pleasant. Girls' lives are so stupid; they never do what they like."

"I thought that was more the case of men. They're forced to do hard things, often dreadfully bored, and knocked to pieces too. And then, if we love a girl very dearly, we want to do as she likes, so after all, you have your own way."

"I don't believe it. I never saw a married woman who had her own way."

"What should you like to do?" Rex asked, quite guileless and genuinely anxious.
