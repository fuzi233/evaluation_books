In the mosaic of human existence, the seasons do not march in rigid succession but mingle as they did in a mythical golden age: fruit hangs beside blossom, and in the same breath, the sickle cuts the grain while seeds are scattered for future growth. One hand tends the green vine, another crushes the grape; harvest and spring are eternally fused within the pulse of a single life, until the Great Gatherer calls us home to sow us anew in His unseen fields. Among the myriad gifts of love, none surpasses the exquisite privilege of binding another's destiny to our own—to stand as guardian over their joy, to weave comfort into the fabric of their hardship, and to turn the bitter wells of past suffering into springs of radiant delight. Deronda's love for Mirah burned with this sacred protectiveness. Though she had begun her journey with infantine steps upon a path strewn with thorns, the first sight of her face had struck him as the pale, girlish visage of utter despair. Now, however, she glowed like a flower of delicate ivory tipped with dark petals, bathed in the warm sunlight of contentment. She viewed every potential grief as merely a shadow cast by the immense light of her union with Deronda, a bond she could name only *good*. He watched the sober gladness that animated her movements, the habitual poise of her repose, with a delight so profound he whispered to himself that saving her from pain was sufficient recompense for his own personal joy.

Mirah remained ignorant of Hans's internal struggle or the sharp pang Gwendolen suffered; having accepted the truth that Deronda's hidden heart belonged solely to her, she interpreted Gwendolen's anxious concern as a natural expression of gratitude and dependent affection, a sentiment she herself had once known. Every word Deronda spoke regarding Mrs. Grandcourt reinforced this comforting narrative, though he carefully avoided direct confrontation, speaking only in the most distant terms. Mirah was prepared to believe he had been a rescuing angel to countless souls, and the only marvel was that she alone possessed the supreme bliss of walking constantly by his side. Thus, when the bridal veil descended upon Mirah, it concealed no trembling doubt, only a thrilling awe at receiving a magnificent gift that demanded equally magnificent stewardship. No more beautiful pair ever stood beneath a velvet canopy, nor did any couple deserve the prayers of their people for progeny more truly. Their lips, sealed with unerring honesty, touched the sacramental wine; their mutual pledge gathered a promise of fulfillment rooted in absolute integrity. Naturally, they wed according to the ancient Jewish rite. Since no faith demands that a feast exclude all but the highest ranks of acquaintance, few will likely take offense to learn that the entire Cohen family attended Deronda's modest nuptials, save for the infant daughter, who continued her intelligent battle with teething comforts at home. How could Mordecai bear the thought of shutting out those who had shared his adversity from the joy of his triumph? Mrs. Meyrick, understanding this perfectly, had fully reconciled herself to meeting the Jewish pawnbroker, arriving with her three daughters. Each woman basked in the consciousness that Mirah's marriage to Deronda crowned a romance destined to remain a sweet memory for them all. Which of them—mother or daughters—had not played a generous role, giving their best feelings and actions to the girl who needed them? If Hans had been present, it would have been preferable, but Mab had already noted that men must sometimes suffer for their inconvenience; surely, if she, Kate, and Amy had all fallen in love with Mr. Deronda, they would not be so ridiculous as men, for they were women. The Meyricks were rewarded for conquering their prejudices by hearing a speech from Mr. Cohen, possessing a rare quality among public addresses: it defied the usual mold. Jacob ate beyond his years, contributing several small, whinnying laughs as a free accompaniment to his father's words—not irreverently, but from a lively sense that his family was distinguishing itself. Meanwhile, Adelaide Rebekah, clad in a new Sabbath frock, maintained a grave air of solemn responsibility throughout. Mordecai's brilliant eyes, sunken deep in their large sockets, surveyed the scene with the cherishing benignity of a spirit already lifted into an aloofness that nullified only selfish requirements, leaving pure sympathy alive. Yet, continually, after his gaze traveled over the others, it returned to dwell on Deronda with a fresh gleam of trusting affection.

The wedding feast was humble, yet Mirah was not without splendid gifts. Upon the news of the betrothal, friends devised graceful tributes. Sir Hugo and Lady Mallinger provided a complete equipment for Eastern travel, alongside a precious locket inscribed: *"To the bride of our dear Daniel Deronda all blessings. H. and L. M."* The Klesmers sent a flawless watch, also bearing a tender inscription. But something far more precious than gold or gems arrived for Deronda from the neighborhood of Diplow on the morning of his marriage. It was a letter containing these words: *Do not think of me sorrowfully on your wedding-day. I have remembered your words—that I may live to be one of the best of women, who make others glad that they were born. I do not yet see how that can be, but you know better than I. If it ever comes true, it will be because you helped me. I only thought of myself, and I made you grieve. It hurts me now to think of your grief. You must not grieve any more for me. It is better—it shall be better with me because I have known you. GWENDOLEN GRANDCOURT.*

Preparations for the departure of the trio to the East commenced immediately, for Deronda could not deny Ezra's wish that they set sail forthwith, allowing him to accompany them rather than detain them to watch over him. Deronda held no belief that Ezra's life would endure the voyage, for symptoms indicated the final stage of his malady had set in. Yet Ezra himself had declared, "Never mind where I die, so that I am with you." He did not depart with them. One early morning, he told Deronda, "Do not quit me today. I shall die before it is ended." He chose to be dressed and sit upright in his easy chair as usual, with Deronda and Mirah on either side. For several hours, he remained unusually silent, making no effort to speak, yet looking at them occasionally with eyes full of restful meaning, as if to assure them that while this remnant of breathing time was difficult, he felt an ocean of peace beneath him. It was not until late afternoon, as the light began to fall, that he took hold of each of their hands, looking at Deronda. "Death is coming to me as the divine kiss which is both parting and reunion—which takes me from your bodily eyes and gives me full presence in your soul. Where thou goest, Daniel, I shall go. Is it not begun? Have I not breathed my soul into you? We shall live together." He paused, and Deronda waited, expecting further words. But slowly, with evident effort, Ezra pressed on their hands, raised himself, and uttered in Hebrew the confession of the Divine Unity, a prayer long spoken on the lips of the dying Israelite. He sank gently back into his chair and spoke no more. It was some hours before he ceased to breathe, enveloped in the arms of Mirah and Deronda. "Nothing is here for tears, nothing to wail Or knock the breast; no weakness, no contempt, Dispraise or blame; nothing but well and fair, And what may quiet us in a death so noble."
