In the realm of Flatland, where the intricacies of society were as delicate as the angles of its inhabitants, the women held a mystique that bordered on reverence. Their shape, a slender needle, seemed to embody the very essence of pointedness, a trait that struck awe in the hearts of even the most stalwart triangles. It was said that a woman could become almost invisible at will, a phenomenon that only added to their enigmatic allure.

Some might wonder how this feat was possible, and indeed, it was a question that had puzzled many a mind. To illustrate the principle, imagine a needle laid flat upon a table. Seen from the side, its length is evident, but when viewed end-on, it appears as a mere point, all but invisible. So too, a woman of Flatland could shift her orientation to render herself nearly imperceptible, her presence discernible only by the subtle sheen of her tip.

As a result, the women of Flatland posed a danger that was both real and intangible. Their presence was a constant threat to the well-being of the men, who risked collision and potentially catastrophic injury should they encounter a woman in their midst. The more acute the angle of the man, the greater the peril, and it was not uncommon for a collision with a soldier to result in grievous harm. A woman, with her needle-like shape and ability to vanish at will, was a force to be reckoned with, and the consequences of encountering her were dire.

To mitigate this risk, the various states of Flatland had enacted numerous laws and regulations governing the behavior of women. In the southern regions, where the force of gravity was stronger and the population more prone to random movements, the rules were particularly strict. Everywhere, however, women were required to enter homes through designated eastern entrances, to maintain a constant peace-cry while in public, and to display their presence to others by shifting their backs from right to left.

In some jurisdictions, additional measures were taken to ensure the safety of the population. Women suffering from certain afflictions were summarily executed, lest they pose a risk to others. Others were forbidden from venturing out without a chaperone, while in still other places, they were confined to their homes except during sacred festivals. The lawmakers of Flatland had come to realize, however, that excessive restriction only served to weaken the very fabric of society, leading to an increase in domestic strife and, ultimately, a loss of overall well-being.
