Mrs. Jennings burst into the room the moment she returned, her usual briskness replaced by a look of genuine distress. Without waiting for permission, she pushed open the door and stepped inside, her voice thick with sympathy. “How are you, my dear?” she asked, turning to Marianne, who turned her face away, unable even to speak.

“How is she, Miss Dashwood?” Mrs. Jennings continued, her eyes fixed on the pale figure lying still on the bed. “Poor thing! She looks dreadful. No wonder—poor girl. It’s true, I’m afraid. He’s marrying very soon—a shameless fellow, if ever there was one! I can’t bear it. Mrs. Taylor told me just half an hour ago, and she heard it from a close friend of Miss Grey herself. Otherwise, I wouldn’t have believed it. I nearly fainted when I heard. Well,” she said, shaking her head, “all I can say is, if this is true, he’s behaved abominably to a young lady I know well—and I hope with all my heart his wife gives him no peace. That’s what I’ll always say, my dear—you may count on it. I’ve no patience for men who act like this. If I ever see him again, I’ll give him such a piece of my mind as he hasn’t had in years. But there’s one comfort, Miss Marianne: he’s not the only man in the world worth having. And with your beauty, you’ll never lack admirers. Poor soul! I won’t stay longer—I’ll let her cry it out now and be done with it. The Parrys and Sandersons are coming tonight, you know. That’ll cheer her up.”

She left quietly, tiptoeing out as though every sound might deepen Marianne’s sorrow.

To Elinor’s surprise, Marianne insisted on dining with them. Though Elinor urged her not to, saying it might be too much, Marianne replied firmly, “No, I’ll go down. I can manage. The noise will distract me more than hurt me.”

Elinor, touched that her sister could summon such strength—even if fleeting—said nothing further. She helped Marianne dress, adjusting her gown as gently as she could while Marianne remained seated on the bed. When they were summoned, Elinor supported her sister into the dining room.

There, despite her drawn features and hollow eyes, Marianne ate more than expected and seemed calmer than Elinor had dared hope. Had she been aware of Mrs. Jennings’s constant, well-meaning but clumsy efforts to soothe her—offering delicacies, telling news, coaxing her to eat—she might have broken down. But Marianne was lost in thought, untouched by the world around her. Her silence was absolute.

Elinor, understanding Mrs. Jennings’s intentions, though often overbearing, offered polite thanks and returned courteous nods where her sister could not. Their kind-hearted hostess saw Marianne’s pain and felt bound to ease it however she could. She treated her like a child on the last day of summer holidays—giving her the best seat by the fire, tempting her with every treat in the house, and regaling her with stories of the day’s events.

Had Elinor not seen the sorrow in her sister’s face, she might have found amusement in Mrs. Jennings’s attempts to cure heartbreak with olives, sweets, and warmth. But when the repetition of these gestures finally registered with Marianne, she could endure no more. With a sharp cry—“Misery!”—and a gesture for Elinor not to follow, she rose and fled from the table.

“Oh, poor thing!” Mrs. Jennings exclaimed once she was gone. “It breaks my heart to see her! And she didn’t even finish her wine! Or the dried cherries! Lord, nothing seems to help. If I knew what she’d enjoy, I’d send across the whole town for it. I just don’t understand how a man can treat such a lovely girl so cruelly! But when one side has plenty of money and the other next to none—well, bless you, they don’t care at all!”

“The woman—Miss Grey, I think you said—is very wealthy?”

“Fifty thousand pounds, my dear. Did you ever see her? They say she’s stylish, smart, but not particularly handsome. I remember her aunt well—Biddy Henshawe, married a rich man. The whole family’s well-off. Fifty thousand pounds! And from what I hear, it won’t come until it’s needed; because he’s practically ruined. No wonder—racing about with his curricle and hunters! Still, it doesn’t matter what we say. When a young man comes to court a pretty girl and promises marriage, he shouldn’t run off just because he’s poor and a richer girl is ready to take him. Why doesn’t he sell his horses, rent out his house, dismiss his servants, and start fresh? I’m sure Miss Marianne would have waited. But that’s not how things are anymore. Young men today won’t give up any pleasure.”

“What sort of girl is Miss Grey? Is she kind?”

“I’ve never heard a bad word about her. In fact, I barely know her—except that Mrs. Taylor mentioned earlier that Miss Walker hinted Mr. and Mrs. Ellison wouldn’t mind seeing Miss Grey married, since she and Mrs. Ellison never got along.”

“And who are the Ellisons?”

“Her guardians, my dear. But now she’s of age and free to choose. And what a choice she’s made!—Well, now,” she paused, “your poor sister’s gone back to her room, I suppose, to grieve alone. Is there anything we could bring her? Poor dear—it’s cruel to leave her alone. Soon we’ll have friends over. That’ll help. What shall we play? She hates whist, I know—but is there any round game she likes?”

“Dear ma’am,” Elinor replied softly, “your kindness is truly unnecessary. Marianne likely won’t come out again tonight. I’ll do my best to persuade her to go to bed early. She needs rest.”

“Aye, that’s probably best,” Mrs. Jennings agreed. “Let her name her supper and go straight to bed. Lord, no wonder she’s looked so low these past days. This must have weighed on her for weeks. And the letter today—well, that finished it. Poor soul! If I’d known, I’d never have joked her about it. But how could I guess? I thought it was just another love letter—everyone laughs about those. Oh, Sir John and my girls will be so upset when they hear. I should have stopped by Conduit Street on my way home. But I’ll tell them tomorrow.”

“It wouldn’t be necessary,” Elinor said gently, “for you to warn Mrs. Palmer or Sir John against mentioning Mr. Willoughby or alluding to what’s happened before my sister. Their good nature will naturally prevent them from speaking of it. And the less said about it, the better—for myself, I assure you, it would spare me much pain.”

“Oh, indeed I understand,” Mrs. Jennings responded. “It must be terrible to hear it discussed. And your sister—well, I wouldn’t mention a word to her for the world. You saw I didn’t at dinner. Nor would Sir John or my girls—they’re all thoughtful. Especially if I remind them. Honestly, I believe the less we talk about such things, the sooner they’re forgotten. What good does talking ever do?”

“In this case,” Elinor said, “it can only harm. More so than in many similar situations, because of certain circumstances that make it unfit for public discussion. I must do this justice to Mr. Willoughby—he has not broken a formal engagement with my sister.”

“Law, my dear!” Mrs. Jennings exclaimed. “Don’t try to defend him! No formal engagement? After showing her every room in Allenham House, picking out their future bedroom? That’s more than enough!”

Elinor, mindful of her sister’s feelings, did not press further. She hoped it wasn’t required of her for Willoughby’s sake—though Marianne might lose much, he stood to gain little from truth being spoken.

After a quiet pause, Mrs. Jennings brightened again. “Well, my dear, it’s true what they say—an ill wind blows someone good. This will be excellent news for Colonel Brandon. He’ll get her at last. Mark my words—he’ll marry her by midsummer. Lord, how he’ll rejoice! I hope he comes tonight. It’ll be a far better match for your sister. Two thousand a year—no debts, no worries—except that little love-child, of course. Ah, I forgot her. But she can be apprenticed cheaply, and then what does it matter? Delaford is a lovely place—just the sort I like: old-fashioned, comfortable, full of conveniences. Surrounded by high garden walls covered in the finest fruit trees. And that mulberry tree in the corner! Charlotte and I stuffed ourselves the last time we were there. Then there’s a dove-cote, beautiful stew-ponds, a little canal—everything one could want. And it’s near the church, only a quarter-mile from the turnpike road, so it’s never dull. Sit in the yew arbour behind the house, and you can watch every carriage pass by. Oh, it’s a charming place! A butcher nearby, the parsonage just a stone’s throw away. To me, a thousand times prettier than Barton Park, where they have to send three miles for meat and no neighbor closer than your mother. I’ll rally the Colonel as soon as I can. One shoulder of mutton drives another down. If we can just get Willoughby out of her thoughts!”

“If we can do that,” Elinor said quietly, “we’ll be doing very well—whether Colonel Brandon comes or not.”

Then rising, she went to find Marianne, whom she discovered in her own room, leaning over the dying embers of a small fire—the only light in the dim chamber.

“You’d better leave me,” Marianne murmured without looking up.

“I will,” Elinor replied, “if you’ll go to bed.”

At first, Marianne refused, caught in the stubborn pride of grief. But Elinor’s gentle persistence softened her, and soon she lay down, resting her aching head on the pillow. Elinor watched, hoping she might sleep peacefully before she left.

In the drawing-room, Mrs. Jennings soon joined her, holding a glass of wine. “My dear,” she said, “I just remembered—I have the finest old Constantia wine in the house. I brought a glass for your sister. My poor husband—how he loved it! Whenever his old colicky gout flared up, he said nothing helped like this. Take it to her.”

“Dear ma’am,” Elinor smiled, “you’re so kind. But I’ve just left Marianne in bed—almost asleep. And I believe nothing would help her more than rest. If you’ll allow it, I’ll drink it myself.”

Mrs. Jennings, disappointed she hadn’t arrived five minutes sooner, accepted the compromise. As Elinor sipped the wine, she reflected that while its healing powers for colicky gout were irrelevant now, its soothing effect on a broken heart might be just as real—for herself as for her sister.

Colonel Brandon arrived while tea was being served. From the way he glanced around the room, Elinor sensed he neither expected nor wished to see Marianne. He already knew why she was absent.

Mrs. Jennings, however, missed the sign. Shortly after he entered, she walked over to Elinor and whispered, “The Colonel looks as grave as ever. He knows nothing of it—do tell him, my dear.”

A few moments later, he pulled a chair close to hers and, with a glance that confirmed his knowledge, asked, “How is your sister?”

“Marianne is unwell,” Elinor said. “She’s been indisposed all day. We’ve persuaded her to go to bed.”

“Then perhaps,” he hesitated, “what I heard this morning… may be true. More true than I could have believed at first.”

“What did you hear?”

“That a gentleman—someone I had reason to believe was engaged—someone I _knew_ was engaged—but how shall I say it? If you already know, I needn’t tell you.”

“You mean,” Elinor said, forcing calmness, “Mr. Willoughby’s marriage to Miss Grey. Yes, we know everything. Today has been a day of revelations. This morning was when it all came to light. Mr. Willoughby is inscrutable. Where did you hear it?”

“In a stationer’s shop in Pall Mall. I had business there. Two ladies were waiting for their carriage, and one was telling the other about the wedding plans—in a voice so loud, I couldn’t help hearing. The name ‘Willoughby’—John Willoughby—kept repeating itself. Then came a clear statement: everything was settled. It wouldn’t be a secret anymore. It would happen within weeks. Details of preparations, travel plans—all laid out. One thing I remember especially—it identified him: after the wedding, they were going to Combe Magna, his estate in Somersetshire. My astonishment—I can’t describe it. The woman who spoke, I learned later, was Mrs. Ellison. And as I’ve since been told, that’s the name of Miss Grey’s guardian.”

“Yes. But have you heard that Miss Grey has fifty thousand pounds? That might explain it.”

“It may. But Willoughby is capable—well, I think—” he paused, then added, almost to himself, “And your sister—how did she take it?”

“Her suffering has been severe. I can only hope it’s brief. It’s been a cruel blow. Until yesterday, I believe she never doubted his affection. Even now, perhaps—but I’m almost convinced he never truly loved her. He’s been deceitful. And in some ways, there’s a hardness in him.”

“Ah,” Colonel Brandon said softly, “there is indeed. But your sister—does she see it as you do?”

“You know her nature. You can imagine how eager she is to justify him, if she can.”

He said nothing. Moments later, the tea things were cleared, and card games began—ending the conversation naturally.

Mrs. Jennings, who had watched them closely, expecting to see joy spark in Colonel Brandon’s eyes upon hearing the news, was stunned. Instead, he remained more serious and thoughtful than ever throughout the evening.
