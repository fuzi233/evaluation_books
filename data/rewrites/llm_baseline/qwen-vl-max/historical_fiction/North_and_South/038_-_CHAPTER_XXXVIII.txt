“Then proudly, proudly up she rose, Tho’ the tear was in her e’e, Whate’er ye say, think what ye may, Ye’s get na word frae me!” SCOTCH BALLAD.

Mr. Thornton could not shake off the belief that Margaret had lied to him—though he thought it was only because of that lie that she had fallen so deeply from his regard. But the falsehood itself carried with it, in his mind, a shadow of something more: a secret attachment, an unseen lover. He could not forget the look she had exchanged with another man—the quiet intimacy, the closeness that seemed almost tender. That image haunted him, sharp and vivid, no matter where he went or what he did. And then there was the time—dusk falling—and the place—so far from home, so seldom trodden. At first, his better nature had argued that such a walk might have been innocent, even accidental. But once he allowed her the right to love, as she plainly had refused his own, why should he deny her that? She had spoken clearly enough when she turned away his affection. So if she loved another, she might well have lingered longer than intended, drawn by emotion into silence and solitude. Yet the lie—oh, the lie!—that spoke of guilt, of shame hidden beneath the surface. It was unlike her. And yet, in this very unlikeness, he found proof of how deeply she was bound to someone else—a man slight and elegant, refined and handsome—while he himself was coarse, stern, and built like a man who lived by force and will. The jealousy burned within him, fierce and relentless. He remembered that look—that moment of softness and surrender—how he would have laid down his life for such glances, such gentle attention. Now he saw how she looked when truly loved, and it mocked him. He recalled her words, sharp and cold: “There was not a man in all that crowd for whom she would not have done as much, far more readily than for him.” He shared her desire to spare bloodshed, yes—but this other man, this hidden one, shared nothing. He held her gaze, her words, her hand, her secrets—all to himself.

Mr. Thornton knew he had never been so irritable in his life. He felt the urge to snap at every question, to reply in short, clipped tones like a growl. This awareness stung his pride—he had always prided himself on self-control. Yet control he would keep. So his manner remained calm, measured; but beneath it, his spirit was harder, colder than ever before. He grew quieter at home, spending evenings pacing back and forth across the room, a habit that would have annoyed his mother greatly if anyone else had shown it. It did little to soften her temper toward him, even though he was her beloved son.

“Can you stop—can you sit down for a moment?” she asked, stepping forward. “I have something to say to you—if you’d only cease that endless walking.”

He sat immediately, on a chair against the wall.

“I want to speak about Betsy. She says she must leave us—her lover’s death has broken her spirits, and she can’t give her heart to her work.”

“Very well. I suppose we can find another cook.”

“That’s just like a man,” she replied. “It’s not just the cooking—it’s that she knows every corner of this house. Besides, she told me something about your friend Miss Hale.”

“Miss Hale is no friend of mine. Mr. Hale is my friend.”

“I’m glad to hear you say that. For if she were your friend, what Betsy says would have troubled you.”

“Let me hear it,” he said, his voice quiet, subdued—as it had been for days now.

“Betsy says that the night Leonards was last seen at the station—when he was last on duty—Miss Hale was there, walking with a young man. Bessy believes he killed Leonards with a blow or a push.”

“Leonards was not killed by a blow or push.”

“How do you know?”

“Because I asked the surgeon at the Infirmary. He told me Leonards had suffered from a long-standing internal illness caused by heavy drinking. The rapid decline while intoxicated confirmed that the final attack was due to alcohol, not the fall.”

“The fall? What fall?”

“The fall caused by the blow or push Betsy mentions.”

“So there *was* a blow or push?”

“I believe so.”

“And who did it?”

“There was no inquest, because of the doctor’s opinion. So I cannot say.”

“But Miss Hale was there?”

No answer.

“And with a young man?”

Still no answer. Then, after a pause: “Mother, there was no inquest—no inquiry. No judicial one, I mean.”

“Betsy says Woolmer—some man she knows, working at a grocer’s shop in Crampton—can swear Miss Hale was at the station at that hour, walking with a young man.”

“I don’t see what concern it is of ours. Miss Hale is free to act as she chooses.”

“I’m glad you say that,” Mrs. Thornton replied eagerly. “It matters little to us—not at all to you, after what has passed! But I—I made a promise to Mrs. Hale. I vowed I would not let her daughter go astray without advising her. I shall certainly tell her what I think of such behavior.”

“I do not see any harm in what she did that evening,” said Mr. Thornton, rising and stepping near his mother. He stood by the fireplace, his face turned away from her.

“You wouldn’t have approved of Fanny being seen out after dark, in such a lonely spot, walking with a young man. I say nothing of the taste in choosing that time—when her mother lay unburied—to take such a walk. Would you have liked your sister noticed by a grocer’s assistant doing so?”

“In the first place,” he said, “since I myself was once a draper’s assistant, the fact that a grocer’s clerk sees something doesn’t change its nature. And secondly, I see a great difference between Miss Hale and Fanny. I can imagine Miss Hale has reasons—weighty ones—that make her overlook appearances. I never knew Fanny to have weighty reasons for anything. Others must protect her. I believe Miss Hale protects herself.”

“A fine character for your sister, indeed! Really, John, one would have thought Miss Hale had done enough to make you clear-sighted. She led you on to propose, with a show of interest—only to play you off against this young man, I suspect. Her whole conduct is plain to me now. You believe he is her lover, I suppose—you agree to that.”

He turned sharply to face her. His face was pale and grim. “Yes, mother. I do believe he is her lover.” Then he turned away again, twisting as though in pain. He leaned his head into his hand. Before she could speak, he turned back:

“Mother. He is her lover, whoever he is. But she may need help—womanly counsel. There may be difficulties or temptations I don’t know. I fear there are. I don’t want to know what they are. But since you’ve always been a good—yes, a kind—mother to me, go to her. Win her confidence. Tell her what must be done. I know something is wrong—some dread, some agony she carries. It must be torture.”

“For God’s sake, John!” she said, genuinely shocked. “What do you mean? What do you know?”

He did not answer.

“John! I don’t know what to think unless you speak. You have no right to say what you’ve said about her.”

“Not against her, mother! I *could* not speak against her.”

“Well, then, you have no right to say half-truths unless you finish them. These half-expressions destroy a woman’s reputation.”

“Her reputation!” he snapped, turning to face her, eyes blazing. “You dare not—” He straightened himself, composed, dignified. “I will say no more than this—what I say is simple truth, and I am sure you believe me. I have reason to believe Miss Hale is in some difficulty connected with an attachment. From what I know of her character, that attachment is innocent and proper. What my reason is, I refuse to say. But never let me hear anyone suggest anything worse than that she needs the guidance of a kind, gentle woman. You promised Mrs. Hale to be that woman!”

“No,” said Mrs. Thornton firmly. “I did not promise kindness. I felt at the time I might not be able to offer it to someone like Miss Hale. I promised counsel—advice, as I would give to my own daughter. I shall speak to her as I would to Fanny if she had gone gallivanting with a young man at dusk. I’ll speak based on what I know, without being swayed by the ‘strong reasons’ you won’t share. Then I’ll have kept my word—and done my duty.”

“She’ll never bear it,” he said, passionately.

“She will have to, if I speak in her mother’s name.”

“Well,” he broke away, “don’t tell me more. I can’t endure thinking of it. It will be better for you to speak to her—any way—than not to speak at all.” He muttered under his breath, “Oh, that look of love! And that cursed lie—showing some terrible shame behind it, hidden from the light in which I thought she lived! Oh, Margaret, Margaret! Mother, how you’ve tortured me! Oh, Margaret—couldn’t you have loved me? I am rough and hard, but I would never have led you into a lie.”

Mrs. Thornton reflected on her son’s words—his plea for mercy toward Margaret—and the more she thought, the more bitter she became. She took pleasure in imagining herself speaking plainly to her, fulfilling her duty with no hesitation. She relished the idea of showing herself untouched by the charm Margaret cast over others. She sneered at the picture of her beauty—black hair, fair skin, bright eyes—thinking they would do nothing to save her from the stern rebuke she had already rehearsed in her mind.

“Is Miss Hale within?” she asked, knowing full well she was. She had seen her at the window and was already inside the hall before Martha could answer.

Margaret sat alone, writing to Edith, recounting details of her mother’s final days. The task softened her heart, and she brushed away tears as Mrs. Thornton was announced.

She greeted her visitor with gentle grace, so ladylike that Mrs. Thornton felt momentarily disarmed. The speech she had prepared seemed ill-suited to this soft presence. Margaret’s voice was low and rich, softer than usual—because in her heart she was grateful for the visit. She made efforts to engage in conversation, praised Martha, the servant Mrs. Thornton had found for them, and mentioned asking Edith for a Greek tune she had discussed with Miss Thornton. Mrs. Thornton was thrown off balance. Her sharp tongue felt misplaced among roses. She remained silent, gathering courage. Then, a suspicion flickered—perhaps this sweetness was merely a ploy to win back Mr. Thornton, now that the other man had failed. Poor Margaret—there may have been truth in that: Mrs. Thornton was the mother of the man whose regard she valued, and feared losing. This thought, unconsciously, added to her desire to please.

Mrs. Thornton rose to leave, but paused. She cleared her throat.

“Miss Hale, I have a duty to perform. Your mother asked me to advise you if you acted wrongly—or inadvertently—without warning. I promised I would offer counsel, whether you accepted it or not.”

Margaret stood before her, blushing, eyes wide. She thought Mrs. Thornton had come to speak of the lie—perhaps Mr. Thornton had sent her to explain the danger of being caught in falsehood. Though her heart sank that he hadn’t come himself, she was humbled enough to accept blame patiently.

Mrs. Thornton continued:
“At first, when I heard from a servant that you were seen walking with a gentleman at Outwood station, so late, I hardly believed it. But my son confirmed it. It was indiscreet, to say the least. Many a young woman has lost her reputation through such actions—”

Margaret’s eyes flashed fire. This was new—this was insult. If Mrs. Thornton had spoken of the lie, she would have confessed, humbled herself. But to question her conduct, to touch on her character—by a stranger! It was unbearable. She said nothing.

Mrs. Thornton saw the defiance in her eyes—and it stirred her own.

“For your mother’s sake, I warn you. Such improprieties will degrade you in the world’s eyes—even if they don’t lead to ruin.”

“For my mother’s sake,” Margaret said, voice trembling, “I will endure much—but not this. She never meant me to be insulted.”

“Insult, Miss Hale?”

“Yes, madam,” she said steadily. “It is insult. What do you know of me to suspect—Oh!” she broke down, covering her face with her hands. “Now I understand—Mr. Thornton has told you—”

“No, Miss Hale,” Mrs. Thornton interrupted, stopping her confession. “He has told me nothing. You do not know my son. You are not worthy to know him. He said only this: ‘Go to her. I have good reason to believe she is in some strait arising from an attachment. She needs womanly counsel.’ I believe those were his exact words. Beyond confirming you were at the station with a gentleman on the twenty-sixth, he said nothing—nothing against you. If he knows anything that makes you weep, he keeps it to himself.”

Margaret’s face remained hidden, fingers wet with tears. Mrs. Thornton softened slightly.

“Come, Miss Hale. There may be circumstances—explained—that would lessen the appearance of impropriety.”

No answer. Margaret weighed her words. She wished to stand well with Mrs. Thornton—but she could not explain.

Mrs. Thornton grew impatient.
“I shall regret ending our acquaintance. But for Fanny’s sake—had she done this, we’d consider it a disgrace. Fanny might be led astray—”

“I can give no explanation,” Margaret said quietly. “I have done wrong—but not in the way you think. I believe Mr. Thornton judges me more mercifully than you—” She fought to hold back her sobs. “But I believe, madam, you mean to do what is right.”

“Thank you,” Mrs. Thornton said, lifting her chin. “I wasn’t aware my intentions were doubted. This will be the last time I interfere. I didn’t want to when your mother asked. I disapproved of my son’s feelings for you—when I only suspected them. You didn’t seem worthy of him. But when you compromised yourself during the riot, exposed to servants’ talk—then I felt it was no longer right to oppose his wish to propose—though he denied wanting to until that day.”

Margaret flinched, drawing in her breath sharply. Mrs. Thornton ignored it.

“He came. You seemed to have changed your mind. Yesterday I told him it was possible, in such a short time, you might have learned something of this other lover—”

“What must you think of me, madam?” Margaret asked, throwing her head back with proud disdain, her throat curving like a swan’s. “You can say nothing more. I refuse to justify myself. I ask you to allow me to leave.”

And she swept from the room with silent, regal grace. Mrs. Thornton, possessing enough humor, couldn’t help but see the absurdity of her situation. She left, not annoyed—she cared too little for Margaret to feel that. But she was pleased. Margaret’s passion had proven the effect of her words. “My young lady,” she thought, “you’ve a fine temper. If John and you had married, he’d have needed a tight rein to keep you in place. But I doubt you’ll walk with your beau again at dusk. You’ve too much pride. I like a girl who flies at being talked about. It shows she’s not giddy. That girl might be bold—but never giddy. I’ll give her that. As for Fanny—she’d be giddy, not bold. No courage in her, poor thing.”

Mr. Thornton spent his morning less productively than his mother. While she fulfilled her purpose, he wrestled with the damage the strike had wrought. Much of his capital was tied up in expensive new machinery. He had bought large quantities of cotton for major orders—orders now delayed beyond measure. Even with his skilled workforce, fulfilling them would be difficult. With the Irish laborers—untrained, slow—every day brought fresh frustration.

It was not a good time for Higgins to approach him. But he had promised Margaret he would try. So, despite growing irritation, pride, and sullenness, he stood leaning against the dead wall, shifting from foot to foot, hour after hour. Finally, the latch clicked, and Mr. Thornton emerged.

“I want to speak to you, sir.”

“Can’t stay now, my man. I’m too late as it is.”

“Well, sir, I reckon I can wait till you come back.”

Mr. Thornton walked halfway down the street. Higgins sighed. But there was no choice. To catch him in the street was his only chance. If he’d gone to the lodge or the house, he’d have been sent to the overlooker. So he waited, nodding curtly to familiar faces as the millhands streamed out for dinner, scowling at the newly arrived Irish workers.

At last, Mr. Thornton returned.

“What! You still here?”

“Ay, sir. I must speak to you.”

“Come in then. Wait—we’ll cross the yard. The men aren’t back. We’ll have it to ourselves. These good people,” he said, closing the lodge door, “are at dinner.”

He paused to speak to the overlooker, who whispered:
“I suppose you know, sir, that man is Higgins—one of the Union leaders. The one who gave that speech in Hurstfield.”

“No, I didn’t,” Mr. Thornton said, turning sharply. He recognized Higgins by name—known as a troublemaker.

“Come along,” he said, tone rougher. “Men like this,” he thought, “who disrupt commerce and harm their own town—mere demagogues, craving power at others’ expense.”

“Well, sir,” Mr. Thornton said, facing him in the counting-house, “what do you want?”

“My name is Higgins—”

“I know that,” he cut in. “What do you want, Mr. Higgins?”

“I want work.”

“Work! You’re a bold fellow to ask me for work. You don’t lack impudence, that’s clear.”

“I’ve got enemies and backbiters, like my betters,” Higgins replied, roused by the tone. “But I never heard one of them called over-modest.”

Mr. Thornton saw a letter on the table. He read it through. Then he looked up.

“What are you waiting for?”

“An answer to my question.”

“I gave it. Don’t waste more of your time.”

“You remarked on my impudence,” Higgins said. “But I was taught it’s manners to answer ‘yes’ or ‘no’ to a civil question. I’d be grateful if you’d give me work. Hamper can vouch for me.”

“I’ve a notion you’d better not send me to Hamper for a character, my man. I might hear things you’d prefer I didn’t.”

“I’d take the risk. The worst they could say is that I did what I thought best—even if it hurt me.”

Mr. Thornton stared at him, unreadable.
