Meanwhile, at Fairoaks, everyone was beginning to worry that the Major hadn't returned. Just then, Dr. Portman and his wife, heading home to Clavering, pulled up at Helen's lodge gate. They delivered a short note from Major Pendennis. In it, he explained he'd stay another day at Chatteris to discuss legal matters with Messrs. Tatham, whom he planned to meet that afternoon. Notably, he didn't mention the morning's dramatic encounter. The note was penned during a lull in the chaos, precisely when the Major had decisively lost the argument.

Young Pen, however, wasn't eager to venture into town while his uncle was around. He disliked the idea of his guardian potentially watching him from the Dean's lawn while he tried to woo Miss Costigan inside. The simple joy of a solitary walk—a rare luxury for him—would have been ruined by bumping into the "polished boot" of authority. Pen's affection was a secret thing, visible only in his eyes, which he gazed at Miss Costigan with violent intensity whenever possible. But in front of others, it remained silent. And honestly, that was better. Of all the chatter in the world, the ramblings of love-struck fools sound the silliest to anyone who hasn't lived through it. It's a vocabulary without a key, a lamp without a flame. Try reading some old love letters you've long forgotten; they seem utterly blank and meaningless now. What magical infatuation made that nonsense beautiful back then? One wonders how such wailing and trash could ever bring happiness. Yet, there were times when you kissed those silly pages with rapture, living on six absurd lines for a week, until the inevitable crash came, leaving you restless and miserable until you got a fresh supply of folly.

That is exactly why we refuse to publish the letters and verses Mr. Pen wrote during this phase of his life. Out of pure respect for the young man's character, we'll keep them bundled up with Miss Fotheringay's string and sealed with Captain Costigan's great silver seal. They are simply too *spooney* and wild for young ladies to read in cold blood. Bide your time, young women; perhaps you'll compose similar masterpieces yourselves soon. For now, let us respect Mr. Pen's first outpourings.

The Major emerged from his meeting with Captain Costigan in a state of concentrated fury that made him terrifying to approach. *"The impudent bog-trotting scamp!"* he fumed internally. *"Dares to threaten me! Dares to suggest his damned Costigans marry the Pendennises! Send me a challenge! If he can find a gentleman to carry it, I'm game. Psha! What would people say if I fought a tipsy mountebank over a row with an actress in a barn?"*

When he encountered Dr. Portman, who anxiously asked about the outcome of his battle with the "dragon," the Major decided not to reveal the General's insolence. Instead, he claimed the affair was ugly, disagreeable, and far from over. He strictly enjoined the doctor and his wife to say nothing about the business at Fairoaks, sending them off with the note we previously mentioned. Then, he retreated to his hotel, where he vented his wrath on Mr. Morgan, his valet, cursing upstairs and downstairs, according to Mr. Morgan's report to Mr. Foker's man.

The servant relayed the news to his master. Mr. Foker, finishing his breakfast at two in the afternoon, recalled his eagerness to learn the results of the interview between his two friends. Donning his brocade dressing-gown, he marched to the Major's sitting room and knocked.

Inside, the Major was indeed occupied with business regarding a lease for the widow's estate, consulting old Mr. Tatham, the lawyer who had managed his late brother's affairs. Mr. Tatham and his client were deep in discussion when Mr. Foker appeared, displaying his magnificent dressing gown and embroidered skull cap. Seeing the Major engrossed in papers and red tape alongside an elderly gentleman with a white head, the modest youth initially hesitated to interrupt. *"Oh, you're busy—call again another time,"* he murmured.

But Mr. Pendennis waved him in with a smile. Mr. Foker promptly removed his embroidered tarboosh (worked by the fondest of mothers) and advanced, bowing gracefully. Mr. Tatham had never seen such a splendid apparition before. The brocaded youth seated himself in an armchair, spreading out his crimson skirts, radiating kindness and frankness.

*"You seem to like my dressing-gown, sir,"* Foker remarked to Mr. Tatham. *"A pretty thing, isn't it? Neat, but not in the least gaudy. And how do you do, Major Pendennis, sir, and how does the world treat you?"*

There was something in Foker's manner that would have softened even an Inquisitor, smoothing the wrinkles beneath the Major's hair. *"I've had an interview with that Irishman—you may speak freely before my friend, Mr. Tatham, who knows all our family affairs—and it hasn't been very satisfactory. He refuses to believe my nephew is poor; claims we're both liars. He even hinted I was a coward as I took my leave. And I thought when you knocked, you might be the gentleman bringing a challenge from Mr. Costigan—that's how the world treats me, Mr. Foker."*

*"You don't mean that Irishman, the actress's father?"* cried Mr. Tatham, a Dissenter who held no patience for the drama.

*"That Irishman, the actress's father—the very man. Haven't you heard what a fool my nephew has made of himself about the girl?"* Mr. Tatham, who never stepped foot in a theater, knew nothing of the details. The Major recounted the story of his nephew's loves, with Mr. Foker adding appropriate commentary in his familiar style. Mr. Tathom was astounded. Why hadn't Mrs. Pendennis married a serious man? Why keep this unfortunate boy from perdition? As for Miss Costigan, he said nothing; her profession characterized her sufficiently.

Mr. Foker interjected that he had known some uncommonly good people in the "booths," as he called the Temple of the Muses. *"Well, it might be so,"* Mr. Tatham conceded hopefully. *"But the father—I know him personally—a man of the worst character, a wine-bibber, an idler in taverns and billiard rooms, and a notorious insolvent."*

*"I can understand the reason, Major,"* Tatham said. *"Why the fellow wouldn't come to my office to verify your statements. We have a writ out against him and another disreputable fellow, a play-actor, for a bill given to Mr. Skinner, a most respectable grocer and Quaker merchant. This Costigan came crying into Mr. Skinner's shop, and we haven't proceeded against either of them because neither was worth the powder and shot."*

Just as Mr. Tatham was weaving this tale, a third knock echoed at the door. An athletic gentleman in a shabby braided frock entered, holding a letter with a large, blotched red seal.

*"May I have the honor of speaking with Major Pendennis in private?"* he began. *"I bear a message from my friend, Captain Costigan."*

But the man with the bass voice paused, faltered, and turned pale. He caught sight of the red-faced, well-known visage of Mr. Tatham.

*"Hullo, Garbetts, speak up!"* cried Mr. Foker, delighted.

*"Why, bless my soul, it is the other party to the bill!"* exclaimed Mr. Tatham. *"I say, sir; stop!"*

Garbetts, his face as blank as Macbeth's upon seeing Banquo's ghost, gasped inarticulate words and fled the room. The Major's gravity shattered instantly, replaced by laughter. So did Mr. Foker. *"By Jove, it was a good 'un,"* he chuckled. Even the serious attorney laughed.

*"I don't think there'll be any fight, Major,"* young Foker predicted, beginning to mimic the tragedian. *"If there is, the old gentleman—your name Tatham?—will be happy to make your acquaintance, Mr. Tatham, and may send the bailiffs to separate the men."* Mr. Tatham readily agreed. The Major was not sorry for the ludicrous conclusion to the quarrel.

*"It seems to me, sir,"* he told Foker, *"that you always arrive to put me into good humor."*

Nor was this the only service Mr. Foker performed that day. Knowing Captain Costigan's habits, he decided to visit the General to hear firsthand what had transpired in the morning's conversation. Captain Costigan was absent, having received encouragement from his daughter to frequent the convivial club at the Magpie Hotel, where he was undoubtedly bragging about his desire to murder a certain ruffian. He was brave, knew it, and loved to air his courage among peers.

Miss Fotheringay, however, was at home, washing tea cups while Mr. Bows sat opposite.
