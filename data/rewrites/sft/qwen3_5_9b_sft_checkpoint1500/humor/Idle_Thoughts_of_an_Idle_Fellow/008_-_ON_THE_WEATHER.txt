My life, it seems, is a study in contradictions. I set out with the noblest intentions: to craft an article on a topic so utterly novel, so completely uncharted, that the world would gasp in surprise. "I shall write about something entirely new," I declared to myself, "something no human soul has ever touched upon; then I shall hold the keys to this kingdom alone." For days, I pored over the mental map, searching for this pristine territory. Nothing. Not a single inch of blank canvas.

Then, yesterday, Mrs. Cutting arrived—the charwoman, whom I shall name freely, knowing her piety renders her immune to this frivolous nonsense. She consumes only the Bible and *Lloyd's Weekly News*; all other literature is, in her view, a sin against order.

"Lor', sir," she observed, regarding me with mild concern, "you do look worried."

"I am, Mrs. Cutting," I explained, adopting a tone of grave seriousness. "I am attempting to conceive of a subject so startling, so unprecedented, that it will shock the foundations of human discourse. A topic untouched by pen or tongue before."

She chuckled, a sound like dry leaves skittering on pavement, and labeled me a "funny gentleman."

And there it is, my curse: when I speak earnestly, the room fills with guffaws; when I attempt wit, the silence is deafening. Last week, I concocted a masterpiece of humor. I had been discussing Shakespeare's stance on the Reformation at a dinner party, and I dropped a line, followed immediately by, "Ah, that recalls a truly hilarious incident from Whitechapel." "Oh?" they asked, eyes gleaming. "It will bring you to your knees!" I promised, already giggling. I delivered the punchline.

Silence. Absolute, vacuum-sealed silence. Then, slowly, someone whispered, "And... that was the joke?"

I insisted it was, and the others, being polite, accepted it. Except for one elderly gentleman at the far end, who demanded to know the setup—what was said to whom—and we spent twenty minutes arguing it out. Some people are simply wired wrong. I recall a friend whose brain functioned solely on the principle of "everything is a joke." If you wished to discuss the state of the economy, you had to preface it with, "Please, do not laugh, this is serious." Otherwise, he would stop mid-sentence, slap his knee, and erupt into a cacophony of mirth over the mere mention of a tax rate. One could never tell him a genuinely funny story; it would likely kill him instantly.

Back to the present. I vigorously denied being frivolous and pressed Mrs. Cutting for concrete suggestions. After a prolonged period of contemplation, holding a tea-tray like a sacred relic, she ventured, "Samplers?" She claimed they were the vogue in her youth.

I waved away samplers with a dismissive flick of the wrist. "Think harder," I urged. Finally, she blinked and offered, "The weather."

And thus, I am trapped. Since that idiotic recommendation, the weather has occupied my mind like a persistent tenant refusing to leave.

Indeed, it is wretched weather. At the moment of this writing, it is miserable. And if it remains so when you read this, it will quickly turn foul anyway. To us, the weather is perpetually wrong, much like the government. In summer, it stifles; in winter, it kills; in spring and autumn, it refuses to commit, leaving us to scream for consistency. If it is sunny, we mourn the lack of rain to save the crops; if it rains, we beg for sunshine. If December passes without snow, we act as if we've been defrauded of a guaranteed product, demanding our "good old-fashioned winters"; and when snow finally arrives, our outrage is a national disgrace. We shall never be satisfied until every man controls his own local climate.

Or, failing that, we'd prefer none at all.

Yet, I suspect this hatred is unique to city dwellers. Out in the countryside, Nature is a benevolent mistress in every mood. Who can resist the beauty of snow falling in mysterious silence, draping the fields and trees in a bridal white? Or the joy of tramping on frozen earth that rings like a bell, with the sharp, invigorating air biting one's cheeks, the distant bark of sheepdogs, and children's laughter echoing like Alpine chimes? And skating! Gliding on wings of steel across the swaying ice, creating a whirl of music. Oh, and Spring! Nature at eighteen!

Those hopeful buds peeking out, green and shy, like young souls entering the world; the blossom-laden trees, pink and white, hiding cottages in a cloud of fragile splendor, while the cuckoo's cry floats on the breeze. Summer, with its deep greens and sleepy hums, where raindrops whisper secrets to the leaves. Autumn! Ah, how tragically beautiful, with golden glows and dying grandeur, blood-red sunsets, ghostly mists, the murmur of reapers, and the festivals of praise!

Even the rain and hail are merely faithful servants when tending the soil, and the East Wind is nothing more than a boisterous old friend between the hedges.

But in the city, where painted stucco blistered by smoke and sooty rain turns streets to slurry, where snow piles in filthy heaps and chilling blasts whistle down dingy alleys, Nature loses all charm. Urban weather is like a skylark trapped in an accountant's office—it is out of place and in the way. Cities should be sealed tight, heated by hot water, and illuminated by electricity. The weather is a country lass who looks terrible when forced to mingle in Pall Mall. We enjoyed flirting with her in the hayfields, but she becomes unbearable when she meets us in town. There is too much of her here. Her free, hearty laugh clashes violently with artificial urban life, and her ways become a torment.

Lately, she has favored us with relentless rain for three weeks. I am, as Mr. Mantalini described, a "damned damp, moist, unpleasant body."

Our neighbor emerges into his back garden occasionally to declare that the weather is doing the country a "world of good"—though he refers to the weather, not his gardening. Having started a cucumber frame last summer, he deludes himself into thinking he is an agriculturist, speaking in absurdities to impress the terrace residents. I can only hope he is correct, for the weather is currently inflicting significant damage on me. It ruins both my clothing and my temper. While I have ample supply of the latter, seeing my beloved hats and trousers succumbing to the cold world's blasts is a wound to the soul.

Consider my new spring suit. It was beautiful, until it hung there today, a dripping monument to mud.

Blame Jim. I should never have worn it that night without his interference. I was admiring it in the mirror when he burst in, throwing up his arms and yelping, "Got 'em again!"

"Does it fit all right behind?" I asked, confused.

"Spiffin, old man," he replied. Then he asked if I intended to venture out.

"No," I said.

He overruled me. "A man with a suit like that has no right to stay inside!" he declared. "Every citizen owes a duty to the public. Contribute to general happiness. Go out and treat the girls."

Jim speaks in slang I cannot trace; it certainly didn't rub off from me.

"Do you think it'll please them?" I hesitated.

"It'll be like a trip to the country for them," he assured me.

Decided. It was a lovely evening, so I went.

Upon returning, I engaged in a rigorous self-care regimen: undressing, rubbing whisky into my skin, soaking my feet in hot water, applying a mustard plaster to my chest, consuming a basin of gruel and a glass of hot brandy-and-water, tallowing my nose, and crawling into bed.

These vigorous measures, bolstered by my robust constitution, saved my life. But as for the suit? Well, it was never a suit. It was a splashboard.

I fancied that suit. That is the tragedy of existence: I rarely grow attached to anything before disaster strikes. As a boy, I adored a tame rat, loving it as a child loves a creature of the wild. One day, it fell into a large bowl of cooling gooseberry fool. No one noticed it missing until the second serving appeared.

I despise wet weather in town. Specifically, I detest the mud. I possess an irresistible, magnetic allure for it. Step onto a muddy street, and I am half-smothered by it. An old lady once remarked, upon being struck by lightning, that it was all due to my attractiveness. Others can wander the filth for hours clean as a whistle; if I cross the road, I return a disgrace—a fact my mother, in her boyhood, frequently reminded me of. If there were even a single speck of mud in all of London, I would undoubtedly collect it, beating every competitor to the prize.

I wish to return the affection, but I fear it is impossible. I have a profound horror of the "London particular." On a dirty day, I feel miserable and muggy, relieved only by stripping off my clothes and retreating to bed, escaping the grime. Everything malfunctions in wet weather.
