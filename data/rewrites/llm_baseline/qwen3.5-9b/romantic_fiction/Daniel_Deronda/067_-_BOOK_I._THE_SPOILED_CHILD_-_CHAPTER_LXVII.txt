The divine spark within us often twists our noblest actions into struggles against our own reluctance. For Daniel Deronda, returning from the Abbey to his lodgings in Brompton proved to be an unpleasant surprise: the "undesirable" father had already settled into their home.

Mirah had deemed it necessary to inform her father of Deronda's existence. She had carefully constructed her narrative, detailing the origins of her friendship with Ezra and the deep sympathy that bound them, yet she skirted the specifics of Deronda's aid to her. She omitted entirely the dramatic rescue from drowning and minimized her refuge in the Meyrick household, leading her father to assume Deronda had met her merely through Ezra's circle. Unable to expose the full depth of her connection to Deronda, she shielded her relationship from her father's judgment. Consequently, Lapidoth, though curious about her flight and arrival in England, showed little eagerness to probe further. However, he was deeply intrigued by the news that his children possessed a benevolent friend seemingly of high station.

It was Ezra's brother who informed Deronda of this new development. "I have grown calm observing him now," Ezra concluded. "I strive to believe that my sister's tenderness and her daily taste of peace may eventually win him to distance himself from temptation. I have instructed her, and she has promised, to entrust him with no money. I have convinced her that he would purchase his own ruin with it."

Deronda arrived three days after Lapidoth's installation. His newly tailored clothes were not yet ready, and wishing to make a favorable impression, he declined to appear in his old attire. Watching Deronda depart from the window, Lapidoth was struck by the young man's vitality—a quality Mirah had not emphasized. Having imagined Deronda to be a grave figure engaged in hoary studies alongside the somber Ezra, Lapidoth was surprised by his youth. He quickly surmised that Deronda's true motivation must be love for Mirah. To Lapidoth, this was advantageous; a bond with Mirah offered more promise of leniency for her father than one with Ezra alone. Hoping to recommend himself to Deronda and soften any initial prejudice, Lapidoth behaved with marked amiability. He sought easy domestication, engaging with Mirah's music, submitting to restrictions on smoking in Mrs. Adam's parlors, and strolling the square with his German pipe, using tobacco kindly supplied by Mirah. Acute enough to accept the refusal of funds—which Mirah insisted upon as a solemn duty owed to her brother—he remained comfortably patient.

On his next visit, Lapidoth, freshly dressed and pleased with his reflection, joined Ezra and Deronda. Ezra, performing a severe duty of self-discipline, tolerated his father's presence whenever required. Deronda, however, felt cold and distant. The sight of the man who had blighted the lives of his wife and children created in him a repulsion bordering on physical discomfort. Undeterred, Lapidoth requested permission to stay, listening to papers read from an old chest. He even proved useful, assisting in deciphering a difficult German manuscript. This led him to suggest transcribing the document, offering his services to copy manuscripts and documents in Roman characters. Although Ezra noted his own eyesight failing, he observed that Lapidoth's vision remained sharp. Deronda accepted the offer, interpreting it as a sign of grace in Lapidoth's willingness to be employable. He noticed a gratified expression on Ezra's face, though Ezra soon cautioned, "Let all the writing be done here; I cannot trust the papers out of my sight, lest there be an accident by fire or otherwise."

Poor Ezra felt akin to guarding a convict on leave. Unless he saw his father working, he could not believe he would labor in good faith. Yet, this arrangement fastened the heavy burden of his father's presence upon him. The atmosphere became painful, tainted not only by their deepest historical associations but also by Lapidoth's restless temperament. As Lapidoth grew accustomed to his situation, he shed any lingering awe for his son. In reality, Ezra was placing a strenuous constraint on himself, confining his attention solely to win Deronda's favor. Like a man trapped in an ill-fitting garment, he sought relief at every opportunity: stepping out to smoke, pacing and chatting, or slumping silently in his chair while conducting a mute dialogue of facial expressions and gestures. When Mirah was present, he reverted to his old habits, gossiping with her about past adventures, companions, play adaptations, and plotlines, believing he could summon the vivacity of his younger days. This was a mortal torment to Ezra. Whenever Mirah was home, she attempted to alleviate his suffering by escorting her father downstairs to the parlor, keeping watch over him there.

What duty arises from a single difficult resolve? Its challenge lies in the daily, unflinching endurance of consequences that mar the dawn with prospects of irritation to suppress or shame to endure. Such burdens were borne by these individuals, as by many other heroic children of unworthy fathers, with the hope—at least for Mirah—that their endurance would stretch through the solid years of life.

Meanwhile, Lapidoth's presence erected a new, intangible barrier between Deronda and Mirah. Both dreaded the potentially defiling inferences of his mind, each misinterpreting the heightened reserve and diffidence of the other.

However, clarity soon broke through for Deronda. Shortly after his return from the Abbey, he visited Hans Meyrick's rooms, feeling it a debt of friendship that Hans should be informed of the reasons for his recent journey and the shifts in intention it had wrought. Hans was absent, reportedly in the countryside for a few days. Leaving a note, Deronda waited a week, anticipating a reply. Receiving none, and fearing some erratic mood in the capricious Hans—who had postponed his stay at the Abbey—Deronda made a second call. He was admitted to the painting room, where he found his friend in a light coat without a waistcoat, his long hair still damp from a bath, yet with a face that looked worn and wizened—the antithesis of a country retreat. Hans held his palette and brushes before an easel, but the setup appeared hastily arranged.

As they shook hands, Deronda remarked, "You don't look much as if you'd been in the country, old fellow. Was it Cambridge?"

"No," Hans replied curtly, tossing down his palette with the air of someone who had feigned by mistake. Pushing forward a chair, he leaned back with his hands behind his head. "I've been to 'I-don't-know-where'—No Man's Land—and a mortally unpleasant country it is."

"You don't mean to say you've been drinking, Hans?" Deronda asked, seating himself opposite with an anxious gaze.

"Nothing so good. I've been smoking opium. I always intended to try it sometime to measure the bliss it could yield; finding myself recently devoid of other bliss, I judged it prudent to seize the chance. But I pledge you my word I shall never touch that cask of bliss again. It disagrees with my constitution."

"What has been the matter? You were in good spirits when you wrote."

"Oh, nothing in particular. The world began to look seedy—a sort of cabbage garden with all the heads cut. A malady of genius, you may be sure," Hans smiled grimly. "In fact, I was tired of being virtuous without reward, especially in this stifling London heat."

"Nothing else? No real vexation?"

Hans shook his head. "I came to tell you of my own affairs, but I lack the grace to do so if you intend to hide yours."

"Haven't an affair in the world," Hans said flippantly, "except a quarrel with a bric-à-brac man. Besides, since it's the first time in our lives you've spoken of your own matters, you're only beginning to repay a rather long debt."

Deronda sensed Hans was acting artificially but trusted that old frankness would return if he shared his own confidence. "You laughed at the mystery of my journey to Italy, Hans," he began. "It was for an object touching my happiness at its roots. I knew nothing of my parents until I went to Genoa to meet my mother. My father died when I was an infant. My mother was the daughter of an eminent Jew; my father was her cousin. Many signs had led me to suspect this origin before I left. So prepared was I that I was glad of the confirmation—glad to find myself a Jew."

"You need not expect surprise from me, Deronda," Hans said, shifting his posture to cross one leg over the other and inspecting the heel of his slipper. "You knew it?"

"My mother told me. She visited the house the morning after you were there—both brother and sister informed her. Imagine we cannot rejoice as they do. But whatever you are glad of, I shall come to be glad of in the end—though precisely *when* that end arrives, I cannot predict," Hans murmured in a low, habitual tone, out of humor with his lot yet determined to avoid a scene.

"I quite understand you cannot share my feelings," Deronda said. "Yet I could not allow silence to divide us on what casts such a new light upon my future. I have adopted some of Mordecai's ideas and intend to pursue them as far as one man's effort can go. I daresay I shall soon travel to the East and be gone for years."
