Rosa Bud had known no home but the Nuns’ House, no mother but Miss Twinkleton. Her own mother was a ghost of memory—a fragile, drowned thing, cradled in her father’s arms, her summer dress clinging with wet petals, her hair a dark river across the pillow. That image, sharp as a blade, had never left her. Nor had the sight of her father’s broken heart, following his wife to the grave a year later.

It was his grief that had birthed her betrothal. His old friend Drood—himself a widower—had offered comfort, and in time, the promise of union between their children. But death had taken the elder Drood too, leaving only Edwin, and Rosa, and the weight of an arrangement made in another lifetime.

The air around Rosa had always been thick with pity. As a child, it had swaddled her; as a girl, it gilded her. She was petted, indulged, fought over in gentle, venomless skirmishes—who would gift her the prettiest trinket, who would claim her holidays, who would earn her brightest smile. Well for the Nuns’ House that their rivalries were so soft.

Rosa had grown winsome and willful, trusting in kindness as her birthright, but never cold in its receipt. Her heart was a deep well, untroubled yet by love’s stirrings—but what storms might come when its waters were finally disturbed?

The news of the quarrel arrived before breakfast, as if borne on the wind itself. By what means? The birds? The baker’s loaf? The housemaids beating dust from their mats? No matter. By the time Miss Twinkleton descended, it had seeped into every crevice of the old house.

No—**a knife.**

No—**a fork.** (The cook insisted it was all three, and a decanter besides.)

The tale swelled with each retelling. Neville had admired Rosa; Edwin had objected; words had flown, then objects. By mid-morning, it was whispered that the very cutlery had risen in revolt.

Rosa pressed her fingers to her ears, retreating to a corner. **"Don’t tell me any more!"** But Helena Landless, with a glance that dared refusal, left to seek the truth from Mr. Crisparkle.

She returned with a pared-down version: Neville had been provoked—grievously so—but for Rosa’s sake, she skimmed over Edwin’s part in it. There was a plea for forgiveness, delivered stiffly, and then the matter was buried.

Miss Twinkleton, ever the diplomat, addressed her pupils with solemn grace. **"Rumour, ladies—as painted by the immortal Bard (Miss Jennings, cease slouching)—is a beast of many tongues. Yet let us not be swayed by whispers. A youthful altercation, magnified by gossip, is beneath our notice."**

But it was noticed. At dinner, Miss Ferdinand donned a paper mustache and pantomimed hurling a water-jug, earning herself a reproachful spoon from Miss Giggles.

Rosa could not shake the unease coiled in her chest. The quarrel clung to her, sticky as guilt. Her engagement had always been a fragile thing—a promise made over graves. With Edwin, she felt it like a ill-fitting glove; apart, it gnawed at her. Today, even Helena’s silence was a wall.

And then her guardian arrived.

Mr. Grewgious was a man of angles—sharp elbows, a throat too long, ankles too prominent. His face was a half-carved block, as if Nature had abandoned him mid-chisel. Yet there was kindness in him, awkward but genuine.

**"My dear,"** he began, floundering under Miss Twinkleton’s gaze. **"I come on matters of—ah—pounds, shillings, and pence. And… marriage."**

The word dropped like a stone.

He fumbled through his notes, smoothing his sparse hair as if it might help. **"Edwin visits, you write, you like one another—good, good. At Christmas, formal notice must be given to Miss Twinkleton. A proxy might give you away—the dancing-master, perhaps? I am… angular. Unsuitable."**

Rosa laughed despite herself.

Then came the will. A copy for her, another for—

**"Jasper?"** Rosa interrupted. **"Not Eddy himself?"**

Grewgious blinked. **"As his uncle is his trustee—"**

**"I’d rather it went to Eddy,"** she insisted, a flicker of unease in her voice. **"I don’t like Mr. Jasper… between us."**

Grewgious noted it, then laid out the terms: the betrothal was no shackle. If it dissolved, she would remain his ward until twenty-one; Edwin would inherit his due regardless.

**"It was a wish,"** he said, **"not a chain. Young hearts must choose freely."**

Rosa pleated her skirt, thoughtful. **"I’d like to speak with Eddy first. He’s away until Christmas."**

**"Then then it shall be."** Grewgious rose, a stork of a man. **"Unless—is there anything else?"**

She hesitated. **"If I needed you… would you come? At Christmas?"**

He bowed. **"Without fail."**

And with that, he took his leave—a man of business, stepping lightly around the edges of a girl’s heart.
