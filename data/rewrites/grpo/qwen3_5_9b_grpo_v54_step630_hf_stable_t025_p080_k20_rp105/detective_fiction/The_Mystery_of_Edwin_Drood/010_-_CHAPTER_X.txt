It is a well-known truth that women possess an innate, instinctive faculty for reading the male character—a power arrived at without reasoning, offering no self-account, and pronouncing with absolute confidence even against the accumulated observations of men. Yet, this power, fallible as any human trait, is rarely capable of self-correction. When it delivers a verdict later proven false, it indistinguishably becomes prejudice, refusing amendment. Indeed, the mere possibility of contradiction seems to strengthen this feminine judgment, connecting the observer so personally to her intuition that she views any challenge as the testimony of an interested party.

"Now, don't you think, Ma dear," the Minor Canon asked his mother one afternoon as she knitted in his small study, "that you are rather hard on Mr. Neville?"

"No, I do *not*, Sept," the old lady replied.

"Let us discuss it, Ma."

"I have no objection to discussion, Sept. I trust I am always open to it." A vibration ran through her cap, suggesting an internal thought: *And I should like to see the discussion that would change my mind!*

"Very good, Ma," said her conciliatory son. "There is nothing like openness."

"I hope not, my dear," the old lady returned, effectively shutting the door.

"Well! Mr. Neville, on that unfortunate occasion, committed himself under provocation."

"And under mulled wine," added the old lady.

"I must admit the wine. Though I believe the two young men were alike in that regard."

"I don't," said the old lady.

"Why not, Ma?"

"Because I *don't*," she insisted. "Still, I am quite open to discussion."

"But, my dear Ma, how can we discuss if you take that line?"

"Blame Mr. Neville for it, Sept, and not me," she said with stately severity.

"My dear Ma! Why Mr. Neville?"

"Because," Mrs. Crisparkle retreated to first principles, "he came home intoxicated, brought great discredit to this house, and showed great disrespect to this family."

"That is not to be denied, Ma. He was then, and is now, very sorry for it."

"But for Mr. Jasper's well-bred consideration in coming to me the next day, after service, in the Nave itself, with his gown still on, to express hope that I had not been greatly alarmed or had my rest broken, I believe I might never have heard of that disgraceful transaction," the old lady noted.

"To be candid, Ma, I think I should have kept it from you if I could: though I had not decidedly made up my mind. I was following Jasper out to confer with him on hushing the thing up, when I found him speaking to you. Then it was too late."

"Too late, indeed, Sept. He was still as pale as gentlemanly ashes at what had taken place in his rooms overnight."

"If I *had* kept it from you, Ma, you may be sure it would have been for your peace and quiet, and for the good of the young men, and in my best discharge of duty."

The old lady immediately crossed the room to kiss him, saying, "Of course, my dear Sept, I am sure of that."

"However, it became town-talk," Mr. Crisparkle rubbed his ear as his mother resumed her seat, "and passed out of my power."

"And I said then, Sept," the old lady returned, "that I thought ill of Mr. Neville. And I say now, that I think ill of Mr. Neville. And I said then, and I say now, that I hope Mr. Neville may come to good, but I don't believe he will." Her cap vibrated again.

"I am sorry to hear you say so, Ma—"

"I am sorry to say so, my dear," the old lady interposed, knitting firmly, "but I can't help it."

"—For," pursued the Minor Canon, "it is undeniable that Mr. Neville is exceedingly industrious, attentive, and improving apace, and that he has—I hope I may say—an attachment to me."

"There is no merit in the last article, my dear," the old lady said quickly; "and if he says there is, I think the worse of him for the boast."

"But, my dear Ma, he never said there was."

"Perhaps not," returned the old lady; "still, I don't see that it greatly signifies."

Mr. Crisparkle contemplated the pretty old piece of china she held with a pleasant, humorous sense of its being a poor opponent for close argument.

"Besides, Sept, ask yourself what he would be without his sister. You know her influence; you know her capacity; you know that whatever he reads with you, he reads with her. Give her her fair share of your praise, and how much do you leave for him?"

At these words, Mr. Crisparkle fell into a reverie. He thought of the times he had seen the brother and sister in deep converse over his old college books: in the rimy mornings of his pilgrimages to Cloisterham Weir; in the sombre evenings when he faced the sunset from his favorite lookout, a beetling fragment of monastery ruin, watching the two studious figures pass below him along the river's margin, where town fires made the landscape bleaker. He thought how the consciousness had stolen upon him that in teaching one, he was teaching two; how he had insensibly adapted his explanations to both minds. He recalled the gossip from the Nuns' House that Helena, whom he had mistrusted as proud and fierce, had submitted to the fairy-bride he called her, learning from her what she knew. He pondered the picturesque alliance between those two, externally so different. He wondered—perhaps most of all—if these things, though but weeks old, had already become an integral part of his life.

Whenever the Reverend Septimus fell a-musing, his good mother took it as an infallible sign that he "wanted support." The blooming old lady hastened to the dining-room closet to produce the support embodied in a glass of Constantia and a home-made biscuit. It was a wonderful closet, worthy of Cloisterham and Minor Canon Corner. Above it, a portrait of Handel in a flowing wig beamed down with a knowing air of understanding the contents and a musical air of intending to combine all its harmonies in one delicious fugue. This was no common closet with a vulgar door; it had a lock in mid-air where two perpendicular slides met. The upper slide, pulled down, revealed deep shelves of pickle-jars, jam-pots, tin canisters, and agreeably outlandish vessels of blue and white, the luscious lodgings of preserved tamarinds and ginger. Every benevolent inhabitant had his name inscribed upon his stomach. The pickles, in a uniform of rich brown double-breasted coats, announced their portly forms in printed capitals as Walnut, Gherkin, Onion, Cabbage, Cauliflower, and Mixed. The jams, of a less masculine temperament and wearing curlpapers, whispered in feminine caligraphy of Raspberry, Gooseberry, Apricot, Plum, Damson, Apple, and Peach. Closing the scene, the lower slide ascending revealed oranges attended by a mighty japanned sugar-box to temper their acerbity. Home-made biscuits waited at the Court of these Powers, accompanied by plum-cake and slender ladies' fingers to be dipped into sweet wine. Lowest of all, a compact leaden vault enshrined the sweet wine and stock of cordials: whence issued whispers of Seville Orange, Lemon, Almond, and Caraway-seed. There was a crowning air upon this closet of closets of having been hummed through by the Cathedral bell and organ for ages, until those venerable bees had made sublimated honey of everything in store; and every dipper emerged mellow-faced, seeming to have undergone a saccharine transfiguration.

The Reverend Septimus yielded himself quite as willing a victim to a nauseous medicinal herb-closet, also presided over by the china shepherdess, as to this glorious cupboard. To what amazing infusions of gentian, peppermint, gilliflower, sage, parsley, thyme, rue, rosemary, and dandelion did his courageous stomach submit! In what wonderful wrappers, enclosing layers of dried leaves, would he swathe his rosy face if his mother suspected a toothache! What botanical blotches would he cheerfully stick upon his cheek if she convicted him of a pimple! Into this herbaceous penitentiary, situated on an upper staircase-landing—a low, narrow whitewashed cell where bunches of dried leaves hung from rusty hooks—he would be led submissively, like a popular lamb led to slaughter, and there he would, unlike that lamb, bear nobody but himself. Not even doing that much, so that the old lady were busy and pleased, he would quietly swallow what was given him, merely taking a corrective dip of hands and face into the great bowls of dried rose-leaves and lavender, then go out as confident in the sweetening powers of Cloisterham Weir and a wholesome mind as Lady Macbeth was hopeless of the seas that roll.

In the present instance, the good Minor Canon took his glass of Constantia with excellent grace, and, so supported to his mother's satisfaction, applied himself to the remaining duties of the day. Their orderly progress brought round Vesper Service and twilight. The Cathedral being very cold, he set off for a brisk trot after service, ending in a charge at his favorite fragment of ruin, to be carried by storm without a pause for breath.

He carried it in a masterly manner, standing breathless looking down upon the river. The river at Cloisterham is near enough to the sea to throw up seaweed. An unusual quantity had come in with the last tide, and this, the confusion of the water, the restless dipping of noisy gulls, and an angry light out seaward beyond the brown-sailed barges turning black, foreshadowed a stormy night. In his mind, he contrasted the wild, noisy sea with the quiet harbor of Minor Canon Corner, when Helena and Neville Landless passed below him. He had had the two in his thoughts all day and climbed down to speak to them together. The footing was rough in the uncertain light, but the Minor Canon was as good a climber as most men and stood beside them before many good climbers would be half-way down.

"A wild evening, Miss Landless! Do you not find your usual walk with your brother too exposed and cold for the time of year? Or at all events, when the sun is down and the weather is driving in from the sea?"

Helena thought not. It was their favorite walk. It was very retired.

"It is very retired," assented Mr. Crisparkle, seizing his opportunity and walking on with them. "It is a place of all others where one can speak without interruption, as I wish to do. Mr. Neville, I believe you tell your sister everything that passes between us?"

"Everything, sir."

"Consequently," said Mr. Crisparkle, "your sister is aware that I have repeatedly urged you to make some kind of apology for that unfortunate occurrence which befell on the night of your arrival here." He looked to her, and not to him; therefore it was she, and not he, who replied:

"Yes."

"I call it unfortunate, Miss Helena," resumed Mr. Crisparkle, "forasmuch as it certainly has engendered a prejudice against Neville. There is a notion about that he is a dangerously passionate fellow, of an uncontrollable and furious temper: he is really avoided as such."

"I have no doubt he is, poor fellow," said Helena, with a look of proud compassion at her brother, expressing a deep sense of his being ungenerously treated. "I should be quite sure of it, from your saying so; but what you tell me is confirmed by suppressed hints and references that I meet with every day."

"Now," Mr. Crisparkle resumed, in a tone of mild though firm persuasion, "is not this to be regretted, and ought it not to be amended? These are early days of Neville's in Cloisterham, and I have no fear of his outliving such a prejudice, and proving himself to have been misunderstood. But how much wiser to take action at once, than to trust to uncertain time! Besides, apart from its being politic, it is right. For there can be no question that Neville was wrong."

"He was provoked," Helena submitted.

"He was the assailant," Mr. Crisparkle submitted.

They walked on in silence, until Helena raised her eyes to the Minor Canon's face and said, almost reproachfully: "O Mr. Crisparkle, would you have Neville throw himself at young Drood's feet, or at Mr. Jasper's, who maligns him every day? In your heart you cannot mean it. From your heart you could not do it, if his case were yours."

"I have represented to Mr. Crisparkle, Helena," said Neville, with a glance of deference towards his tutor, "that if I could do it from my heart, I would. But I cannot, and I revolt from the pretence. You forget however, that to put the case to Mr. Crisparkle as his own, is to suppose to have done what I did."

"I ask his pardon," said Helena.

"You see," remarked Mr. Crisparkle, again laying hold of his opportunity, though with a moderate and delicate touch, "you both instinctively acknowledge that Neville did wrong. Then why stop short, and not otherwise acknowledge it?"

"Is there no difference," asked Helena, with a little faltering in her manner; "between submission to a generous spirit, and submission to a base or trivial one?"

Before the worthy Minor Canon was quite ready with his argument regarding this nice distinction, Neville struck in:

"Help me to clear myself with Mr. Crisparkle, Helena. Help me to convince him that I cannot be the first to make concessions without mockery and falsehood.
