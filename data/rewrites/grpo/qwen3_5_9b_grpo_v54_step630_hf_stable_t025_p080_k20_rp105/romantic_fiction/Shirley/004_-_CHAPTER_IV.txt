Mr. Yorke was the very essence of a Yorkshire gentleman, a man of fifty-five who looked older, his hair a stark, silver-white. His face was broad rather than high, fresh and hale, yet carved with the harshness of the north that echoed in his voice. Every line of his countenance was thoroughly English—strikingly devoid of any Norman lineage. It was an inelegant, unclassic visage, lacking aristocratic polish; fine folk might have called it vulgar, but sensible ones would recognize it as characteristic. To the shrewd, it was a map of pith, sagacity, and rude, real originality. Yet, it was also the face of a man indocile, scornful, and sarcastic—a man difficult to lead and impossible to drive. Tall and wiry, with a stately integrity in his posture, he bore no trace of the clown.

To sketch his person was easy; to define his mind was far harder. Do not expect a benevolent philanthropist here. While he could speak with sense and good feeling to Mr. Moore, do not assume he always thought or spoke justly.

First, Mr. Yorke lacked the organ of veneration. This deficiency threw him askew wherever reverence was due. Kings, nobles, priests, dynasties, and parliaments were to him mere rubbish, an abomination. He found no pleasure in their forms or rights, believing the world would be clearer if their high places were razed and their occupants crushed. This lack of awe dried up the electric delight of admiration, withering a thousand pure sources of enjoyment. He was not irreligious—he believed in God and heaven—but his deity was one stripped of imagination, tenderness, and awe.

Secondly, he lacked the organ of comparison. This stripped him of sympathy, rendering him inconsistent. While professing doctrines of mutual toleration, he harbored a bigoted antipathy toward certain classes. He spoke of "parsons" and "lords" with a harshness and insolence that was unjust and insufferable. Unable to place himself in their shoes or weigh their temptations against their defects, he expressed ferocious wishes regarding those he deemed tyrannical. By his own threats, he would have employed cruel means to advance freedom. Equality was a word he spoke, yet at heart he was a proud man, haughty as Beelzebub toward anyone he deemed superior. Revolt ran in his blood; he could not bear control, nor could his ancestors or descendants.

His lack of general benevolence made him impatient of imbecility and faults that grated on his shrewd nature, leaving no check to his cutting sarcasm. He was not merciful; he would wound repeatedly, caring little for the depth of his thrusts.

As for his lack of ideality, it was hardly a fault. He possessed a fine ear for music and a correct eye for color, granting him taste. Who cares for imagination? Many deem it a dangerous, senseless attribute, akin to frenzy—a disease rather than a gift. To hear the fanatics speak, one believes their hearts are cold without that elixir, their eyes dim without that flame. It is an illusion, yet they cling to it for gold. Since Mr. Yorke possessed no poetic imagination, he considered it superfluous in others. He could tolerate painters and musicians because he relished their results, but a quiet poet, burning with fire yet unable to play the counting-house game, would live despised and die scorned under Hiram Yorke's gaze.

Yet, the true poet, though quiet externally, often harbors a truculent spirit and shrewdness beneath his placidity. He can measure the stature of those who look down on him and maintain a festal brightness in his bosom, independent of the world's cold judgment. He is not to be pitied; he laughs in his sleeve at misguided sympathizers. These, however, were not Mr. Yorke's reflections.

Mr. Yorke was one of the most honorable and capable men in Yorkshire, beloved by the poor for his fatherly kindness. To his workmen, he was considerate. When dismissing them, he tried to find them new employment or aid their removal. If insubordination arose, he crushed it in the germ, eradicating it like a bad weed. Secure in his own affairs, he felt free to speak severely of others, blaming their misfortunes entirely on their own faults while advocating for the operatives.

His family was the oldest in the district, and though not the wealthiest, he was highly influential. Educated well and having traveled the Continent before the French Revolution, he was adept in French and Italian. His home was adorned with paintings and rarities collected during his two-year sojourn in Italy. When he chose, his manners were those of a finished gentleman of the old school, and his conversation singularly interesting. Yet, he preferred his native Doric, asserting a Yorkshire burr was as superior to a Cockney lisp as a bull's bellow to a rat's squeak.

Known by everyone for miles, his intimate acquaintances were few. Original himself, he had no taste for the ordinary. He spent hours with a shrewd workman or a sagacious old cottager, grudging a moment to a commonplace fine gentleman or a frivolous lady. He made exceptions, however, for a certain order of mind: plain, ingenuous, devoid of intellectuality, yet incapable of feeling disgust at his rudeness or analyzing his sarcasm. With such people, he was at ease, and they, submitting implicitly without reflecting on his superiority, were perfectly tractable. Their unthinking insensibility was as convenient to him as the chair he sat upon.

He was not entirely uncordial with Mr. Moore, holding a faint partiality for three reasons. First, Moore's foreign accent and anti-Yorkshire appearance recalled Mr. Yorke's youthful travels in Italy and Paris, bringing back memories of his younger self. Second, he had known Moore's father, with whom his firm had been connected and implicated in losses. Third, he found Robert a sharp businessman, respecting his resolution and acuteness. Finally, as a guardian of the minor on whose estate Hollow's Mill stood, they consulted frequently on improvements.

With Mr. Helstone, however, existed a double antipathy: that of nature and circumstance. The free-thinker hated the formalist; the lover of liberty detested the disciplinarian. Rumor also whispered that in former years, they had been rival suitors of the same lady.

Mr. Yorke, once noted for preferring sprightly, dashing women with lively wits, had suddenly fallen in love with a girl who presented a complete contrast: Mary Cave, a girl of living marble, stillness personified. She answered in monosyllables, returned no glances, and rarely smiled. Yet, for him, she was perfect. He loved her for some reason he could not articulate.

Mr. Helstone, then curate of Briarfield, also fancied Mary. She was beautiful as a monumental angel, but the clergyman was preferred for his office, which invested him with an illusion Miss Cave did not find in the wool-staplers. Unlike Yorke's absorbing passion, Helstone saw her more as she really was. He was master of her and himself. She accepted him at the first offer, and they were married.

Nature never intended Mr. Helstone to be a good husband to a quiet wife. He believed silence meant contentment. If she did not complain of solitude, it could not be irksome. He made no pretense of understanding women, viewing them as an inferior order. After a year or two, his wife became of little importance to him. When she died, leaving only a still, beautiful-featured mold of clay in the conjugal couch, he felt his bereavement, though he shed no tears. His dry-eyed, sober mourning scandalized an old housekeeper and a female attendant who knew her capacity for feeling better than her husband. They gossiped over the corpse, working themselves up to indignation against the austere little man examining papers in the next room.

Hardly under the sod, rumors rife that she died of a broken heart magnified into reports of hard usage and harsh treatment. Grossly untrue, these stories were eagerly received. Mr. Yorke heard them and partly believed them. Having no friendly feeling for his successful rival, and remembering his own great disappointment, he conceived a rooted, bitter animosity toward Helstone for neglecting what would have been precious to him.

Mr. Helstone was half-aware of this animosity, unaware of how much Yorke had loved Mary or the calumnies concerning his treatment of her. He believed only political and religious differences separated them. Had he known the truth, he would not have crossed Yorke's threshold.

Mr. Yorke did not resume his lecture to Robert Moore. Soon, the conversation turned general, yet disputative, fueled by the unquiet state of the country and recent depredations on mill-property. The three gentlemen differed in their views. Mr. Helstone thought the masters aggrieved and the workpeople unreasonable, condemning the spirit of disaffection. He prescribed vigorous government interference, strict magisterial vigilance, and prompt military coercion.

Mr. Yorke asked if such measures would feed the hungry or give work to the unemployed. He scouted the idea of inevitable evils, declaring public patience a camel whose back was already laden to the limit; resistance was now a duty. He viewed the spirit of disaffection as the most promising sign of the times. The masters were aggrieved, but their grievances were heaped upon them by a "corrupt, base, and bloody" government. Madmen like Pitt, demons like Castlereagh, and mischievous idiots like Perceval were the tyrants whose ruinous war and oppressive taxation hung a millstone about England's neck.

"But where was the use of talking?" Yorke demanded. "What chance was there of reason being heard in a land king-ridden, priest-ridden, peer-ridden? Where a lunatic was nominal monarch, an unprincipled debauchee the real ruler? Where hereditary legislators and a bench of bishops were endured and venerated? Where a standing army was maintained to keep lazy parsons and their pauper families on the fat of the land?"

Mr. Helstone, rising to put on his shovel-hat, replied that he had seen sentiments like Yorke's bravely maintained only when allied with health and prosperity. But there came a time when the keepers of the house trembled. That was the test of the advocate of anarchy. He had read prayers for the dying bed of one of her most rancorous foes, stricken with remorse and unable to find a place for repentance. He warned Yorke that blasphemy against God and the king was a deadly sin and that there was judgment to come.

Yorke agreed fully that judgment to come existed. Otherwise, how could the scoundrels who broke innocent hearts with impunity, abused privileges, and took bread from the poor be properly paid off? But whenever he grew low-spirited about the seeming success of such evil in this mucky lump of a planet, he reached down to the old book, opened it at random, and found a verse blazing with blue brimstone that set all straight. He knew where some folk were bound, just as well as if an angel with great white wings had come over the door-stone to tell him.

"Sir," said Mr. Helstone, collecting his dignity, "the great knowledge of man is to know himself, and the bourne whither his own steps tend."

"Ay, ay. You'll recollect, Mr. Helstone, that Ignorance was carried away from the very gates of heaven, borne through the air, and thrust in at a door in the side of the hill which led down to hell."

"Nor have I forgotten, Mr. Yorke, that Vain-Confidence, not seeing the way before him, fell into a deep pit, which was on purpose there made by the prince of the grounds, to catch vainglorious fools withal, and was dashed to pieces with his fall."

"Now," interposed Mr. Moore, who had sat a silent but amused spectator, indifferent to the party politics and neighborhood gossip, making him an impartial, if apathetic, judge, "you have both sufficiently blackballed each other, and proved how cordially you detest each other, and how wicked you think each other. For my part, my hate is still running in such a strong current against the fellows who have broken my frames that I have none to spare for my private acquaintance, and still less for such a vague thing as a sect or a government. But really, gentlemen, you both seem very bad by your own showing—worse than ever I suspected you to be. I dare not stay all night with a rebel and blasphemer like you, Yorke; and I hardly dare ride home with a cruel and tyrannical ecclesiastic like Mr. Helstone."

"I am going, however, Mr. Moore," said the rector sternly. "Come with me or not, as you please."

"Nay, he shall not have the choice; he *shall* go with you," responded Yorke. "It's midnight, and past; and I'll have nob'dy staying up i' my house any longer. Ye mun all go."

He rang the bell.

"Deb," he said to the servant who answered, "clear them folk out o' t' kitchen, and lock t' doors, and be off to bed." Turning to his guests, he continued, "Here is your way, gentlemen," and, lighting them through the passage, he fairly put them out at his front door.

They met their party hurrying out pell-mell by the back way. Their horses stood at the gate; they mounted, and rode off, Moore laughing at their abrupt dismissal, Helstone deeply indignant thereat.
