Darkness snapped back into light as if someone had lifted a lid from my eyes. I could not have been insensible more than a handful of heartbeats, yet the certainty of that blank interval lay in the next thing I knew: the staring eyes that had held me were gone, and a widening radiance now washed the corridor stones with torch-flare yellow.

My forehead throbbed where it had kissed the flagging in my fall. I sprang up, tasting dust, and turned toward the light.

Four green warriors were coming fast, the foremost carrying the torch high so its smoke smeared the air with the sharp, resinous scent of burning fat and dried reed. They had not yet seen me. I slid into the first intersecting passage like a shadow slipping behind a curtain, but this time I did not risk losing the spine of the place. I kept near the main artery of the pits, close enough that their footfalls and the faint clank of metal could be read like a familiar dialect.

They swept past the opening where I crouched, their gait steady, their talk low. Relief came and went in a single breath, for I recognized them at once: Tars Tarkas and his three guards—the same party I had followed down into the darkness.

I fell in behind them when the last had passed, silent as the air itself, and soon we stood before the cell where the great Thark had been chained.

Two warriors remained without while the one with the keys entered with the Thark to fasten his irons again. The others, bored and confident, began to stroll toward the spiral runway that led upward. In a moment they vanished beyond a bend.

The torch was set into an iron socket beside the door, and its wavering light illuminated the corridor and the cell at once. The flame hissed softly each time it licked at the fat-soaked wrapping; the sound reminded me bitterly of how even a small noise can rule a place—how the pits were built not merely of stone but of echoes and the obedience those echoes enforced.

When the two guards were out of sight I moved toward the doorway, a plan already hardened in me. I had no love for what I meant to do, but I saw no other path if Tars Tarkas and I were to stand again in the open air together—if we were to find my little camp in the hills and the others depending upon me.

Keeping to the wall, I came close to the cell entrance and raised my longsword over my head, both hands on the grip, so that one swift blow would end it the instant the jailer stepped out.

I will not dress what followed in any fine words. There are moments a man carries like a stone under his tongue—always there, never spoken. It is enough that within another minute Tars Tarkas, now wearing the metal of a Warhoon chief, was hurrying down the corridor toward the spiral runway with the torch in his hand to light the way, and a dozen paces behind him followed John Carter, Prince of Helium.

The two companions of the man who lay now beside the cell door had just started to ascend when the Thark came into view.

“Why so long, Tan Gama?” called one.

“I had trouble with a lock,” replied Tars Tarkas without hesitation, the borrowed voice rough but steady. “And now I find that I have left my short-sword in the Thark’s cell. Go you on; I’ll return and fetch it.”

“As you will, Tan Gama,” the other answered. “We shall see you above directly.”

“Yes,” said Tars Tarkas, and turned as if to go back. But he waited only until they had disappeared above.

Then I joined him. We extinguished the torch—pinching the life from it so the corridor’s smell of smoke died into cold stone—and together we crept toward the spiral incline.

At the first floor we found the hall ran only half the building’s length, forcing anyone who wished to cross to the inner courtyard to pass through a rear room crowded with green folk. There was no thread of safety through that knot. We climbed to the second floor instead, where I had earlier traversed the long hallway.

Cautiously we ascended. Voices drifted down, laughter and argument, the layered noise of a people who believed themselves secure. Yet the hall above was unlit, and when we reached the top of the runway no one was in sight. We threaded the long corridor and reached the balcony overlooking the courtyard without being detected.

To our right was a window admitting to the room where I had seen Tan Gama and his companions at the beginning of the night. They had returned, and their talk slid out into the hall like steam from a pot.

“What can be detaining Tan Gama?” asked one.

“He certainly could not be all this time fetching his short-sword from the Thark’s cell,” said another.

“His short-sword?” a woman’s voice cut in. “What mean you?”

“Tan Gama left his short-sword in the Thark’s cell,” the first explained, “and left us at the runway to return and get it.”

“Tan Gama wore no short-sword this night,” the woman replied. “It was broken in to-day’s battle with the Thark, and Tan Gama gave it to me to repair. See, I have it here.”

There was the soft rasp of metal against fabric, the unmistakable whisper of a blade drawn from beneath silks and furs.

The warriors sprang to their feet; we could hear it in the scrape of their ornaments, the sudden sharpness of their breathing.

“There is something amiss here,” cried one.

“’Tis even what I myself thought when Tan Gama left us at the runway,” said another. “Methought then that his voice sounded strangely.”

“Come! Let us hasten to the pits.”

We waited to hear no more. I slung my harness into a long single strap and lowered Tars Tarkas to the courtyard beneath; an instant later I dropped to his side.

We had spoken scarcely a dozen words since I had felled Tan Gama at the cell door and seen, by torch-light, the bewilderment on the great Thark’s face.

“By this time,” he had said then, “I should have learned to wonder at nothing which John Carter accomplishes.”

That was all. He did not need to speak gratitude; in him it would have been a speech too large for the mouth. Yet I knew. This fierce warrior had been the first to greet me on that day—now twenty years gone—when I first stood upon Mars. He had met me with leveled spear and hatred as he charged down upon me beside the incubator of his horde on the dead sea bottom beyond Korad. Now, among two worlds, I counted none a better friend than Tars Tarkas, Jeddak of the Tharks.

In the shadows beneath the balcony we halted a moment to shape our next moves.

“There be five now in the party, Tars Tarkas,” I whispered. “Thuvia, Xodar, Carthoris, and ourselves. We shall need five thoats to bear us.”

“Carthoris!” he exclaimed softly. “Your son?”

“Yes. I found him in the prison of Shador, on the Sea of Omean, in the land of the First Born.”

“I know not any of these places, John Carter. Be they upon Barsoom?”

“Upon and below, my friend; but wait until we have made good our escape, and you shall hear the strangest narrative that ever a Barsoomian of the outer world gave ear to. Now we must steal our thoats and be well away to the north before these fellows discover how we have tricked them.”

We reached the great gates at the far end of the courtyard, where the mounts were kept. Five thoats. Five tempers. Five savage stomachs and the feral pride of beasts that were broken not by kindness but by force.

Even before we touched them they lifted their heads and tested us. They sniffed our unfamiliar scent and squealed—a tearing, rusty sound that set my teeth on edge—and circled, necks upreared so their gaping mouths hung high above us, the heat of their breath carrying the sour tang of ochre moss and old blood. Their hides were sleek and hairless, dark slate along back and sides, shading down eight legs to vivid yellow at the huge padded, nailless feet; their bellies were white as salt. A broad flat tail, heavier at the tip than the root, slapped and twitched with impatience. Ten feet at the shoulder, all muscle and malice—fit war-steeds for warlike men.

There were no reins or bridle; the thoat is guided by thought alone, by that strange telepathic communion Barsoom has never lost the way it has lost so much else. But communion requires recognition, and we were strangers to these brutes. Our task was to seize two that would accept our unspoken commands long enough for us to herd the rest.

As they charged about us we kept them from combining, turning aside sudden lunges, dodging the snap of jaws. Their squealing rose and fell in ugly waves that promised to carry straight to any listening warrior’s ears. The courtyard’s soundscape, usually a hollow bowl of echoes, became a beacon.

At last I managed to reach the flank of one great brute. Before he could twist away I was astride his glossy back, knees clamped, mind pressing hard against his will. A heartbeat later Tars Tarkas had mounted another. Between us we drove three more toward the gates.

Tars Tarkas rode ahead, leaned down, and flung the barriers open while I held the loose thoats from bolting back into the herd. Then we rode through into the avenue, mounts stolen, and did not waste the time to close the gates. We hurried toward the southern boundary of the dead city.

Thus far our escape had been little short of marvelous—and fortune did not desert us. We passed the outer purlieus of the silent city and came to our camp without hearing even the faintest sound of pursuit.

A low whistle—the signal agreed upon—slipped out into the thin air. The others answered at once, and we were met by Thuvia, Xodar, and Carthoris with a joy that needed no words.

There was no leisure for full telling. Tars Tarkas and Carthoris exchanged the formal greetings common upon Barsoom, yet I could read in the Thark’s eyes that he loved my boy, and in Carthoris’s carriage that the feeling was returned. Xodar and the green Jeddak were presented to one another with due ceremony. Then we set to the simple domestic labor that underlies all flights and all wars: getting bodies and beasts arranged, burdens balanced, tempers soothed, the living made ready to move.

Thuvia was lifted to the least fractious thoat. Xodar and Carthoris mounted two others. Tars Tarkas and I took the remaining pair, and we set out at a rapid pace toward the east. At the far extremity of the city we circled toward the north, and under the glorious rays of the two moons we sped noiselessly across the dead sea bottom—away from Warhoons and First Born, but toward what new dangers we could not know.

Toward noon the following day we halted to rest ourselves and the thoats. We hobbled the beasts so they might move slowly about, cropping the ochre, moss-like vegetation that serves them as both food and drink on the march. The dry stalks crackled faintly under their padded feet, a small, steady sound—almost comforting in its natural honesty after the metallic clamor of the pits.

Thuvia volunteered to stand watch while the rest of us slept an hour.

It seemed to me I had scarcely closed my eyes when I felt her hand upon my shoulder.

“Arise, O Prince,” she whispered. “There be that behind us which has the appearance of a great body of pursuers.”

She pointed back along our trail. On the far horizon a thin dark line lay like a seam in the world.

I roused the others. Tars Tarkas, towering above us all, stared longest.

“It is a great body of mounted men,” he said at last, “and they are travelling at high speed.”

There was no time to measure fear. We sprang to the hobbled thoats, freed them, mounted, and turned north again. We took flight at the highest speed of our slowest beast.

For the balance of that day and all the following night we raced across the ochre wilderness with the pursuers at our backs, ever gaining. Slowly but surely they lessened the distance. Just before dark they were near enough for us to distinguish their color—green—and all through the long night we heard behind us the clanking of their accoutrements, a hard, industrial music that invaded the open world and made even the stars seem farther away.

When the sun rose on the second day of our flight it showed the pursuing horde not a half-mile in our rear. A fiendish shout of triumph rose from their ranks.

Ahead lay a range of hills—the farther shore of the dead sea. Could we but reach those slopes our chances would improve. But Thuvia’s mount, though carrying the lightest burden, already showed exhaustion: his gait uneven, breath coming in harsh pulls that carried the stale odor of strain.

I rode beside her when her thoat staggered and lurched against mine. I saw he was going down. Before he fell I snatched Thuvia from his back and swung her behind me. She clung with her arms about my waist, her breath warm through my harness.

The double burden soon proved too much for my already overtaxed beast. Our speed faltered, and the others would proceed no faster than we could go. In that small party there was not one who would desert another—though we were of different countries, different colors, different races, different religions, and one of us of a different world.

We were close to the hills, yet the Warhoons gained so rapidly that hope thinned to a thread.

Thuvia and I were in the rear now, our thoat lagging more with every hundred yards. Suddenly I felt the girl’s warm lips press a kiss upon my shoulder.

“For thy sake, O my Prince,” she murmured.

Then her arms slipped from about me, and she was gone.

I wheeled and saw she had deliberately dropped to the ground in the very path of the demons behind us, thinking that by lightening my mount she might save me. Poor child—she did not know John Carter as she thought she did.

I turned my thoat after her at once, urging him back, hoping to reach her side and lift her again into the saddle. Carthoris must have looked behind him at the same instant, for by the time I reached her he was there too. He sprang from his mount, caught Thuvia, flung her across the beast’s back, and, turning its head toward the hills, struck it sharply with the flat of his sword to send it on.

Then he tried to do the same with mine.

The boy’s chivalrous self-sacrifice filled me with pride. Nor did I begrudge that it tore away our last frail chance for escape. The Warhoons were close now. Tars Tarkas and Xodar, seeing our absence, wheeled and charged back toward us.

Everything pointed toward the end of my second journey to Barsoom.

I hated to go out without having held my divine Princess once more, yet if Fate had written otherwise, then I would take what was coming to me. In these last moments I could at least leave behind such an account of myself that the Warhoons of the South would have food for discourse for twenty generations.

Carthoris stood unmounted beside me. I slipped from my thoat and took my place at his side. A moment later Tars Tarkas and Xodar ranged themselves on either hand, turning their mounts loose so that we might all meet the charge on equal footing.

The Warhoons were perhaps a hundred yards away when a loud explosion sounded from above and behind us. Almost at the same instant a shell burst among their advancing ranks.

Confusion tore through them like fire through dry moss. A hundred warriors toppled. Riderless thoats plunged among the dead and dying. Dismounted men were trampled in the stampede. Order dissolved into panic. As they stared upward, trying to trace the origin of the attack, retreat became a wild flight.

In another moment they were racing away from us as madly as they had been charging down.

We turned toward the sound, and there, just clearing the nearer hilltops, swung a great battleship through the air, majestic as a moving citadel. Her bow gun spoke again even as we looked, and another shell burst among the fleeing Warhoons.

As she drew nearer I could not repress a cry of elation, for upon her bows I saw the device of Helium.
