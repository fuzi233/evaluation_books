The pale warm sand of St. Augustine’s main street swallowed the soles of Archer’s leather boots as he strode toward the house the local livery hand had pointed out as the Wellands’ winter rental, and the second he spotted May Welland lingering under a broad-leafed magnolia, sun tangled thick in her loose fair hair, he kicked himself for wasting so many weeks cooped up behind his Manhattan desk. This was it: the unvarnished, warm reality he’d been craving, the life that was meant to be his, and he’d hidden away out of a stupid, cowardly fear of what his stuffy social set might mutter about him stealing an unplanned holiday.

Her first exclamation was soft, surprised: “Newland—has anything happened?” He half-joked to himself that a more “naturally feminine” girl would have read the raw, aching longing in his eyes the second he rounded the corner, but the words slipped out before he could catch them: “Yes—I found I had to see you.” The pink flush that bloomed across her sun-kissed cheeks chased the chill right off her surprise, and he knew he was already forgiven. Even mild, proper Mr. Letterblair’s faint disapproval would melt away fast under the Wellands’ gentle, tolerant roof.

It was still far too early for formal public displays of affection on the busy main street, where linen-clad tourists trundled past in mule-drawn buggies, and Archer ached to pull May aside somewhere private to pour out every last scrap of his pent-up tenderness and impatience. They still had an hour before the Wellands kept their famously late breakfast, so instead of inviting him inside, May suggested they walk out to the abandoned old orange grove past the town limits. She’d just come back from a row on the river, and the golden sun that stitched little sparks across the wave tops seemed to have wrapped her in its light too. Strands of wind-tousled hair across her warm brown cheek glinted like fine silver wire, and her eyes looked softer, paler, bright with the limpid clarity of youth. She walked beside him with that loose, long, unhurried stride of hers, her face wearing the calm, uncomplicated serenity of a young marble statue of an athlete, and to Archer’s frayed, overstretched nerves the sight was as soothing as the wide blue Florida sky and the slow, lazy drift of the river.

They settled on a weathered slat bench under the orange trees, heavy with tiny green unripe fruit that scented the whole air with soft blossom, and he looped an arm around her waist to kiss her. It felt like drinking from a cold, clear spring dappled with sunlight—but his hold was more desperate, more vehement than he’d meant it to be, and the blood rushed to her face so fast she drew back like he’d startled her. “What is it?” he laughed, and she blinked at him, confused, and said “Nothing.” A faint awkwardness settled between them, her hand slipping out of his, and he realized this was only the second time they’d kissed on the lips, after that furtive, quick embrace in the Beaufort’s conservatory weeks before. She was flustered, knocked clean out of that cool, boyish composure she usually carried so easily.

“Tell me what you do all day out here,” he said, tipping his head back and pushing his hat down over his eyes to block the sun’s glare. Letting her ramble about small, familiar things let his own tangled thoughts unspool unhurriedly, and he sat half-listening as she gushed about swims in the shallow clear inlet, bareback pony rides, and the occasional small dance at the rickety waterfront inn when a navy man-of-war docked in the harbor. A cluster of pleasant families from Philadelphia and Baltimore were staying at the inn for the winter, and the Selfridge Merrys had come down for three weeks because Kate Merry had come down with bronchitis. They were trying to lay a lawn tennis court out on the sand, but only Kate and May owned racquets, and most of their new friends had never even heard of the game. She’d been so busy, she said, she’d barely had time to flip through the little vellum copy of *Sonnets from the Portuguese* he’d sent her the week before—but she’d memorized every line of “How They Brought the Good News from Ghent to Aix,” because it was one of the first poems he’d ever read to her, and she laughed as she told him Kate Merry had never even heard of Robert Browning.

Presently she jumped up, exclaiming they’d be late for breakfast, and they hurried back to the tumble-down little rental house, with its lopsided pointless porch and the unpruned hedge of deep blue plumbago and bright pink geraniums spilling over the front walk. Mr. Welland hated the slovenly discomfort of the local southern hotels, so every winter Mrs. Welland went to immense trouble and expense to cobble together a household half made up of discontented New York servants, half hired from local Black families, to make sure her husband never felt like he was away from his own home. “The doctors say he’ll never get better if he doesn’t feel settled,” she explained to every sympathetic new winter visitor, and Mr. Welland, beaming across a breakfast table piled high with every fancy delicacy he could have ordered from New York, was soon teasing Archer: “You see, my dear fellow, we’re camping—literally camping. I keep telling the girls I’m trying to teach them how to rough it.”

The Wellands were just as surprised as May by his sudden unannounced arrival, but Archer had explained he’d woken up feeling like he was coming down with a terrible nasty cold, and that was more than enough excuse for Mr. Welland, who believed no duty was worth risking a winter cold turning into something worse. “You can’t be too careful this close to spring,” he said, heaping his plate high with golden straw-colored griddle cakes and drowning them in thick amber syrup. “If I’d been half as prudent at your age, May would be dancing at the New York Assemblies right now instead of spending her winters out in this wilderness with an old invalid.”

“Oh, but I love it here, Papa,” May protested. “If only Newland could stay I’d like it a thousand times better than New York.”

“Newland must stay until he’s completely shaken off that cold,” Mrs. Welland smiled indulgently, and Archer laughed, saying he supposed he couldn’t abandon his work forever. But after a quick flurry of telegrams back to the firm, he managed to stretch his “cold” out for a full week, and he smiled to himself at the irony of it all: Mr. Letterblair’s generosity was half down to the fact that his brilliant young junior partner had wrapped up the messy Countess Olenska divorce matter perfectly, to the entire family’s satisfaction. Mr. Letterblair had even written to tell Mrs. Welland that Archer had “rendered an invaluable service” to the whole clan, and old Mrs. Manson Mingott had been especially thrilled with how things had turned out.

One afternoon, when May had driven off with her father in the town’s only rickety one-horse buggy, Mrs. Welland took the chance to bring up a topic she always carefully avoided in her daughter’s hearing. “I’m afraid Ellen’s ideas are not at all like ours,” she said gently, rocking on the porch swing beside him. “She was barely eighteen when Medora Manson dragged her back to Europe—do you remember that awful excitement when she showed up in full black at her own coming-out ball? Another of Medora’s silly fads, but it feels almost prophetic now, doesn’t it? That was at least twelve years ago, and she’s never set foot in America since. No wonder she’s completely Europeanized.”

“But European society hardly favors divorce,” Archer heard himself say, surprised the words had slipped out. “Countess Olenska thought she’d be following American ideas when she asked for her freedom.” It was the first time he’d spoken Ellen’s name out loud since he’d left Skuytercliff, and he felt heat rise up to his cheeks.

Mrs. Welland smiled compassionately. “That’s exactly the sort of ridiculous thing foreigners make up about us! They think we dine at two o’clock and openly approve of divorce. That’s why I’ve always thought it so foolish to entertain them when they come to New York. They eat our food, accept our hospitality, and then go home and spread those silly made-up stories about us.”

Archer said nothing, and Mrs. Welland went on. “But we all do so thoroughly appreciate you persuading Ellen to give the whole idea up. Her grandmother and her uncle Lovell couldn’t get through to her at all—they both wrote to say her changing her mind was entirely down to your influence, in fact Ellen told her grandmother that herself. She has an unbounded admiration for you. Poor Ellen—she was always a wayward, wild little child. I do wonder what will become of her.”

He bit his tongue hard to stop himself from snapping back what he was thinking: If you’d all rather she ended up as Beaufort’s mistress than marry a decent man who loved her, you’ve certainly gone the right way about making that happen. He wondered what Mrs. Welland would do if he said that out loud, how that soft, placid face of hers would crumple, the face shaped by a whole lifetime of worrying over nothing but tiny trivial social rules to give her a false air of unassailable authority. She still had faint traces of the same fresh, bright beauty May had, and he found himself wondering if May’s face would thicken into that same middle-aged mask of deliberate, invincible innocence, the kind that seals your mind shut against imagination and your heart shut against any messy real experience. He hated that thought more than he could say.

“I truly believe,” Mrs. Welland continued, “that if that horrible business had leaked to the newspapers it would have been my husband’s death blow. I don’t want to know a single detail of it, I told poor Ellen that when she tried to talk to me. With an invalid to care for, I have to keep my mind bright and happy, no dark ugly things to worry over. But Mr. Welland was terribly upset—he ran a slight fever every single morning the whole time we were waiting to hear what you’d decided. It was the horror of the thought of his daughter learning that such ugly things existed in the world—though of course, dear Newland, you felt that same horror too. We all knew you were thinking of May the whole time.”

“I’m always thinking of May,” Archer said, standing up abruptly to cut the conversation short. He’d planned to use this quiet private talk with Mrs. Welland to beg her to move their wedding date forward, but he couldn’t think of a single argument that would budge her, and he sighed in quiet relief when he spotted Mr. Welland and May driving up the drive.

His only remaining hope was to plead his case to May herself, and on the day before he was due to leave, he walked with her out to the crumbling, overgrown ruins of the old Spanish Mission. The weathered stone walls draped in wild jasmine felt exactly like the European scenes he’d been daydreaming of, and May, looking softer than ever under a wide-brimmed hat that cast a soft mysterious shadow over her usually too-clear eyes, lit up with excitement as he told her stories of Granada and the Alhambra. “We could see all of it this spring,” he urged, exaggerating his plans a little to win a bigger concession. “Even the Easter ceremonies in Seville.”

“Easter in Seville?” She laughed. “But Lent starts next week! You don’t have weddings in Lent, Newland.”

He backtracked fast. “Of course I don’t mean that, dearest—but soon after Easter, so we can sail at the end of April. I know I can arrange it at the office, I promise.”

She smiled dreamily at the idea, but he could tell the fantasy was enough for her, like the beautiful stories he read her out of his poetry books that never actually happened in real ordinary life. “Oh do go on, Newland, I love hearing you describe it all.”

“But why should they only be stories? Why can’t we make them real right now?”

“We will, dearest, of course—next year,” she said, her voice soft and slow over the words.

“Don’t you want them to be real sooner? Can’t I persuade you to break away from all the silly waiting rules now?”

She bowed her head, hiding her face under the wide brim of her hat. “Why waste another whole year?” he pressed. “Look at me, dear! Don’t you understand how badly I want you to be my wife?”

For a second she was perfectly still, then she lifted her eyes to his, so clear and earnest and full of a quiet sadness that he loosened his hold on her waist like he’d been caught doing something wrong. Then her expression shifted, deepening into something he couldn’t read. “I’m not sure if I do understand,” she said. “Is it—Newland, is it because you aren’t sure you’ll keep caring for me? Is there someone else?”

Archer jumped up from the bench like he’d been burned. “My God—maybe—I don’t know,” he broke out, angry and bewildered.

May stood up too, and as they faced each other she seemed to grow taller, steeper, full of a quiet womanly dignity neither of them had ever seen in her before. They stood silent for a long moment, both stunned by the turn their conversation had taken, until May spoke in a soft low voice. “Let’s talk frankly, Newland. Sometimes I’ve felt a difference in you—especially since our engagement was announced.”

“Dear, that’s utter madness!” he protested, scrambling to recover.

She gave him a faint, gentle smile. “If it is, it can’t hurt us to talk about it. Even if it’s true—why shouldn’t we speak of it? You could so easily have made a mistake.”

He dropped his head, staring at the pattern of black oak leaves scattered across the sun-warmed path at their feet. “Mistakes are always easy to make—but if I’d made the kind of mistake you’re thinking of, is it even likely I’d be begging you to rush our marriage?”

She looked down too, nudging the leaf pattern apart with the tip of her sunshade, struggling to find the right words. “Yes,” she said at last, very quiet. “You might want to settle the question once and for all. It’s one way to make yourself do the right thing.”

Her quiet lucidity startled him, but he could see the faint pallor of her profile under the hat brim, the tiny tremor in her nostril above her resolute, steady lips, and he knew she wasn’t as unaffected as she seemed. “Well?” he asked, sitting back down on the bench and looking up at her, forcing a playful frown to his face.

She sank back down beside him. “You mustn’t think a girl knows as little as her parents all pretend we do. We hear things, we notice things, we have our own feelings and ideas. Long before you ever told me you cared for me, I knew there was someone else you’d cared for—everyone was gossiping about it two years ago at Newport. I even saw you two sitting together on the verandah at a dance once, and when she walked back into the house her face was so sad I felt sorry for her. I remembered that moment, later, when we got engaged.”

Her voice had dropped almost to a whisper, and she sat twisting her hands around the handle of her sunshade, and Archer laid his hand gently over hers. His chest swelled with a huge, unnameable relief. She was thinking of that old, long-dead fling he’d had with Mrs. Thorley Rushworth, not Ellen Olenska.

“My dear child—was that all it was? If you only knew the truth!”

She lifted her head fast. “Then there’s a truth I don’t know?”

“I meant the truth about that old story you’re talking about,” he said, keeping his hand over hers. “There’s no pledge, no obligation at all of the kind you’re imagining. Those situations don’t always play out quite as simply as people think—but that doesn’t matter. I love how generous you are, because I feel the same way you do about those things—every case should be judged on its own, no matter what stupid public opinion says. Every woman deserves her own liberty, no matter what the rules say.” He pulled himself up short, startled by where his thoughts had wandered, and smiled at her. “Since you understand all that so well, dearest, can’t you go just a little further and see how silly it is for us to submit to this other stupid old convention, and wait another whole year to marry? If there’s no one and nothing standing between us, that’s all the more reason to marry quickly, not drag our feet.”

A bright, happy flush spread over her face, and she lifted her mouth to his, happy tears brimming in her eyes. But the next second, that brave, mature, wise version of her vanished, and she shrank back into the soft, timid, sheltered girl everyone had always known May Welland to be. The effort of speaking so frankly had drained every last scrap of her courage, and she sank back into the safety of all the old familiar rules like a child running to hide in her mother’s arms.

Archer didn’t have the heart to keep pleading with her. He was too soft, sad, and disappointed to see that strange, bright, deep glimpse of the real May slip away again, the May who had stared at him with those clear, unflinching eyes just a few minutes before. May seemed to sense his quiet disappointment, but she had no idea how to fix it, so they stood up in silence, and walked slowly home together, the sun dipping low over the river to paint the orange groves pink and gold all around them.
