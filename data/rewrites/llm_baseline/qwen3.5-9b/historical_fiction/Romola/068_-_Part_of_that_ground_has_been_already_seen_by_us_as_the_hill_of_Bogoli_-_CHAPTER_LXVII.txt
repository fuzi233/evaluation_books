On the opposite bank of the Arno, amidst the chill grey twilight, another figure emerged from the shadows. While the Compagnacci marched toward their appointed violence, this solitary man sought the river's edge at a secluded spot within the city walls. Here, high warehouses and granaries turned their backs to the water, leaving a stretch of damp, sloping grass and rushes, crisscrossed by gutters that fed the swift current below. This desolation was his sanctuary. Drawn by the memory of kindness—a crust of bread, a raw carrot, apple parings offered by a friendly "runlet"—Baldassarre returned to this hidden place to wait. It was worth the vigil in a world unseen by others, preferable to the silent, humiliating begging on the steps beyond Porta San Frediano. Baldassarre loathed the act of begging; he would sooner choose death than make that appeal, yet one desperate possibility anchored him to life.

It was no longer hope, but a haunting probability that seized his mind entirely. Like a woman watching a headland for a ship long deemed lost, he clung to the faint chance that fate might intervene. Having escaped the convent hospital of San Miniato only to find the wife who was to aid him dead to the plague, he had realized his own impotence. His knife was gone, his body broken, his mind too weary to devise a plan for revenge. A shattered, bewildered old man, he nonetheless waited for something formless and dim that stirred violent pulses within him, much like the instinctual dread we feel waking from sleep. Because he desired to live, he crept out into the grey light, sat in the long grass, and watched the waters, seeking a faint promise in their flow.

Meanwhile, the Compagnacci executed their orders. Left largely unchecked by a distracted Signoria, the armed bands split into two streams converging on the Arno—one heading for the Ponte Rubaconte, the other for the Ponte Vecchio. Their destination was unanimous: the Via de' Bardi, specifically a house near its eastern end where the master was expected to be asleep. But Tito was not sleeping. The events of the previous day had spurred his flight; investigations loomed, and delay bred danger. Though the specific fear of Baldassarre had died with the man's apparent demise, the *habit* of fear remained undying. He felt unsafe in Florence's turbulent air and prepared to depart. His household was packed: Maso handed over the keys, horses awaited in San Gallo, and Tessa and the children rested in the Borgo, ready to join him.

Tito descended the stone steps and crossed the threshold, no longer the man who had once loved Romola, but a figure casting off his old life. He intended to take the private route via the Ponte Rubaconte, but sounds approaching from the opposite direction forced a change of course. Was the mob coming into Oltrarno? Unpleasant sensations tightened his mantle as he hurried toward the Ponte Vecchio. Soon, louder noises confirmed his dread: the crowd was crossing the bridge. Driven by an impulse stronger than reason, he turned to flee, only to realize the mob was already flooding the street from the other end. Refusing to return to his house, fearing the chaos, he pushed onward toward the bridge. The roar of trampling feet and confused shouts grew deafening until he reached the end of the street, where the crowd poured out to hem him in.

Before he could process the sudden shout rising around him, he found himself surrounded—not by unarmed rabble, but by armed Compagnacci. His cap was knocked off, and he was shoved violently into the throng along the narrow bridge passage. Shouts erupted: *"Piagnone! Medicean! Throw him over!"* Strong hands tore at his mantle, threatening to throttle him, while others lunged for his money bag (*scarsella*). Amidst the hustle and drag, the bag slipped free, swinging at his side. The cacophony of execrations stunned him. Tito's worst nightmare was being trampled before reaching the central arches. His only salvation lay in being thrown overboard before he was beaten senseless.

In that critical moment, Tito summoned a desperate reserve of strength. Mastering the clasp of his belt, he flung it and the *scarsella* toward a patch of clear space against the parapet, screaming in a ringing voice, **"There are diamonds! There is gold!"**

The hold on him loosened instantly as the mob rushed for the valuables. Seizing the opening, Tito threw himself onto the parapet and leaped. He plunged with a great splash into the dark river below. It was his chance of survival. Rising to the surface, he felt a surge of deliverance. Striking out with the energy of his youth, aided by the current, he aimed to swim past the Ponte alla Carrara to a remote landing near San Gallo. Life still beckoned. Above, the idiot mob continued to shout, convinced he was drowned. Peering over the parapet, they saw only the drifting blackness of his hair and velvet sleeves. Only from the downstream perspective could a pale olive face be seen, white against the dark water—a face marked by a square forehead, low arched brows, and lustrous, agate-like eyes.

Driven by terror of his fellow men more than the uncertainty of the river, Tito swam on, panting and straining. He passed the bridge of Santa Trinita, then the final bridge. Exhaustion began to blur his senses, dragging him toward unconsciousness, but suddenly, distant eyes caught his movement.

Downriver, Baldassarre sat motionless among the rank green stems, searching the runlet for scraps. When a white object drifted into view, he stared, his eyes filling with a new light. As the form gathered clarity, he leaned forward, a start of recognition seizing him. Suddenly, a body was cast violently onto the grass mere yards from him. Baldassarre sprang forward like a panther, clutching the velvet tunic as he fell atop the stranger.

"Dead?" he wondered, staring into rigid eyes. But no—justice often brings a semblance of death. Hope surged in his heart, mixed with trembling tremors. All the slow-burning embers of his life seemed to leap into flame. The eyes remained locked, unblinking. Could he truly be dead? Time seemed to stretch, freezing hope into despair. Then, a vibration broke the stillness; the eyelids quivered, and the eyes opened wide.

Tito recognized him, yet the nature of their reunion terrified him. Was this life or the chill gloom of the past forever hanging over him? Now, Baldassarre's sole dread was losing his grip on the young limbs. Pressing his knuckles against the round throat, he knelt upon the chest with the full force of his aged frame. *Let death come now!* He watched the face, refusing to trust the fleeting signs of life. When the eyes stiffened again, he held fast, vowing never to release his hold until witnesses arrived. He declared internally that he had killed this traitor, his former son-in-law, and hoped justice would validate his claim. He wished to die clutching the body, following the traitor to hell to seize him there.

Thus he knelt, pressing his knuckles against the throat, ignoring the passing hours and the lack of witnesses. Florence busied itself with greater tragedies nearby; shortly after, Savonarola cried out under torture, "I will confess!" It was not until late afternoon that a wagon drawn by a mild grey ox approached the grassy margin. The driver, gathering stones, noticed the startling sight: the aged man had collapsed forward, his dead hand clutching the garment of the younger. Unable to separate them, the driver loaded both bodies into the wagon to transport them to the Piazza for the Eight.

As the wagon rolled into the crowded streets, a growing escort followed the strange burden. At first, the identities were obscured, the old man's face hidden beneath the younger. But recognition spread quickly.

"I know that old man," testified Piero di Cosimo. "I painted his likeness once. He is the prisoner who clutched Melema on the steps of the Duomo."

"He is perhaps the same old man who appeared at supper in my gardens," added Bernardo Rucellai, one of the Eight. "I had forgotten him. I thought he had died in prison. But the truth is unknowable now."

Who can point to the work of justice and declare, "It is done"? Justice resembles the Kingdom of God: it exists not merely as an external fact, but as a profound yearning within us.
