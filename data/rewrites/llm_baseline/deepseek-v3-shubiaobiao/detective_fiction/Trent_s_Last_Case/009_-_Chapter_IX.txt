The knock at the door came just as Trent was tilting the photographic dish toward the fading evening light. His fingers, usually so steady, trembled slightly as he adjusted the negative.

"Come in," he called, not glancing up.

The door creaked open, and Mr. Cupples stepped inside, his round face alight with curiosity. The room was a familiar chaos—chemical bottles strewn across the table, a faint acrid scent of developer lingering in the air. Trent’s hotel sanctuary looked, as always, as if a whirlwind had passed through it.

"Sit anywhere," Trent muttered, nodding toward the sagging sofa. "Mind the stain on the arm—that’s from a particularly spirited debate with a bottle of ink in Leeds."

Cupples chuckled, picking up a small vial and examining it. "And this?"

"Hypo-eliminator. Excellent for stripping silver from negatives. Less excellent for the human digestive system." Trent finally set the negative aside, rubbing his temples. "You’re just in time for a lesson in criminal science."

Cupples perked up. "Oh?"

Trent reached into his bedroom and returned with a drawing board laden with objects—a paper knife, a walnut case, a small glass bowl. "Exhibits," he said. "All borrowed without leave from White Gables this morning."

Cupples blinked. "You stole them?"

"Borrowed," Trent corrected, grinning. "Temporarily reallocated in the name of justice." He tapped the glass bowl. "Now, observe."

From a squat bottle, he poured a fine grey powder—mercury and chalk—across the bowl’s surface. With a soft brush, he dusted it lightly, then held the bowl up to the light.

Fingerprints emerged like ghosts.

"Ah," Cupples breathed.

"Precisely." Trent’s voice was taut. "Now, compare." He produced a photographic negative, pointing to the ridges. "Same hand. Same man."

Cupples frowned. "But where—?"

"The bedroom window. Mrs. Manderson’s." Trent’s jaw tightened. "And this bowl? Manderson’s. Held nightly by the man who supposedly never left his room alive."

The air between them thickened. Cupples opened his mouth, but Trent cut him off with a sharp shake of his head.

"I can’t say more. Not yet." He turned to the window, his back rigid. "There’s only one way to be certain now."

Cupples hesitated. "And that is?"

Trent didn’t answer. Instead, he reached for the lamp-black, his movements precise, deliberate. The truth, it seemed, was written in the dust—and Trent was the only one who could read it.
