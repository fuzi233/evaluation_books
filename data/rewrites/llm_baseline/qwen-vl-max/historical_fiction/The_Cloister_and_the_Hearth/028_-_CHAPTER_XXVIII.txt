In life, good and evil often balance each other in ways both subtle and profound. The very steel bow strapped to the back of the arbalestrier had nearly drowned him—yet it was that same weapon, held aloft by his own hands, that now saved him. Gerard seized the bow with one hand, and with the other slipped between the bow and its cord, securing a firm grip. In an instant, Denys was hauled upward with such force that half his body broke the surface of the river.

“Don’t touch me! Don’t touch me!” Gerard shouted, trembling at the thought of a fatal mistake.

“Pas si bete,” Denys gasped, choking on water.

Seeing the kind of man he faced, Gerard’s fear melted into calm confidence. “On your back,” he commanded sharply. Seizing the crossbow, he pushed forward, aiding the motion. “Hand on my shoulder! Slap the water with the other—no, downward, like this. Do nothing but what I say.”

He grabbed hold of Denys’s long hair, twisted it tightly, and clamped the end between his teeth. With the strength of youth and the sinews of his neck, he kept the soldier’s head above water, striking out powerfully against the current. For a moment, Gerard hesitated—should he swim toward the west bank or the east? He didn’t know how vital that choice would be. Then, seeing the western shore slightly nearer, he turned toward it, abandoning the safer path to jail for a daring detour—one that would become legend.

The current pulled them sideways, and after swimming nearly a hundred yards, Denys murmured uneasily, “How much farther?”

“Courage,” Gerard mumbled. “Whatever a duck knows, a Dutchman knows. You’re as safe as if you were in bed.”

Then, to their surprise, they found themselves wading through shallow water and stepped ashore.

Once on solid ground, they stared at each other from head to toe, as though their eyes could consume the other’s soul. Then, instinctively, they threw their arms around one another’s necks, breathing heavily, hearts too full for words. At that sacred moment, life felt sweeter than heaven—especially for the exiled lover who had just rescued his friend.

Oh, what poet has ever reached the height of such joy? To save a life—especially the life of one you love. Such moments are worth living for, yes, even three score years and ten.

After a while, they calmed down, took each other’s hands, and walked along the riverbank side by side, like lovers lost in bliss, caring little where they went.

Meanwhile, the boatmen had managed to right their craft—the once concave vessel now convex—and all were safely aboard. Herr Turnip-face, the self-styled “Inverter of Things,” stood calmly in the center, seemingly unshaken by the chaos. He greeted them when they fell into the Rhine, and greeted them again when they emerged.

“Shall we wait until they fix the boat?” asked Gerard.

“No,” replied Denys. “Our fare is paid. We owe them nothing. Let us go—quickly.”

Denys agreed, noting they could walk all the way to Cologne along the riverbank.

“I’m not going to Cologne,” said Gerard quietly.

“What? Then where?”

“To Burgundy.”

“To Burgundy? Ah, no—that’s too good to be true.”

“It is true, and sensible besides. What does it matter how I reach Rome?”

“Nay, nay—you only say that to please me. This change is too sudden. And do not think me so cruel as to believe you without hesitation. Did I not see your eyes light up at the wonders of Cologne—the churches, the statues, the relics?”

“How dull you are, Denys. That was when we were meant to share them together. Churches? I’ll see plenty, wherever I go. The bones of saints and martyrs—alas, the world is full of them. But a friend like you—where else on earth can I find another? No, I will not turn you from the road to your home, nor from the woman who waits for you. Nor will I take myself from you by leaving. Since I pulled you from the Rhine, I love you more than ever. You are my pearl—I fished you out, and I must keep you. So do not argue. Or you’ll bring back my fever. Instead, cry courage and lead on—hey for Burgundy!”

Denys leapt with joy. “Courage! Va pour la Bourgogne! Oh, soyez tranquille! Cette fois il est bien decidément mort, ce coquin-là.”

And they turned their backs on the Rhine.

As soon as their decision became clear, a commotion erupted across the river among those who had watched the scene unfold. Before the friends had taken many steps, a voice called out from the opposite bank: “HALT!”

Gerard turned and saw one of the four men holding a badge of office and a parchment scroll. His heart sank—he was a dutiful citizen, used to obeying authority, especially when it summoned him back to Düsseldorf under the law.

But Denys felt no such scruples. He was French, and scorned foreign lands, laws, customs, and people alike. A soldier, he viewed the situation militarily: superior force ahead, a river between, their rear open. Retreat was simple. He saw the boat still drifting midstream and knew no ferry lay closer than Düsseldorf.

“I shall make a quick retreat to that hill,” he declared. “Then, out of sight, we’ll march swiftly.”

They walked off leisurely.

“Halt! In the bailiff’s name!” came a cry from the shore.

Denys turned, snapped his fingers mockingly at the bailiff, and continued.

“Halt! In the archbishop’s name!”

Again, he snapped his fingers and moved on.

“Halt! In the emperor’s name!”

A third time, he flicked his fingers at the emperor himself and kept walking.

Gerard watched this display with quiet dismay. Once they passed the hilltop, he said, “Now there’s only one course—we must run to Burgundy instead of walking.”

And he began to run, covering nearly a league before stopping.

Denys was breathless. “What on earth happened to your fever?”

“I begin to miss it terribly,” Gerard replied dryly.

“I dropped it in the Rhine, I suppose,” Denys answered.

Soon they reached a small village. There, Denys bought a loaf of bread and a large bottle of Rhenish wine.

“We must sleep somewhere hidden,” he said. “If we stay at an inn, they’ll arrest us in our beds.”

It was sound judgment. In that age, the web of law enforcement was tight, especially for common folk. Though public cooperation was scarce—particularly on the Continent—inns were closely monitored. Innkeepers were held partly responsible for their guests’ actions, sometimes even more than for their safety.

Both men were glad when night fell. And when, after a long journey beneath the stars (the moon, if memory serves, rising only near dawn), they reached a large barn attached to a distant house, they felt relief.

The barn had recently been used for threshing barley. One pile of straw rose almost as high as the untouched corn on the other side.

“Here are two royal beds,” said Denys. “Which shall we choose—the mow or the straw?”

“The straw for me,” Gerard replied.

They sat on the heap, ate their bread, drank their wine, and then Denys covered Gerard with straw, piling it high and leaving only a small opening for air.

“Water kills fevered men,” he muttered. “I’ll make warm water on him, anyway.”

Gerard smiled faintly. “These few drops from the Rhine won’t chill me. I feel hot enough to parch a kennel—or boil a cloud, if I were in one.”

With that, he drifted into unconsciousness.

Denys stayed awake for a time, listening. Then he heard it—the thunder of hooves coming from Düsseldorf. The wooden barn trembled as riders galloped past, their shouts echoing with a sound familiar in the 15th century, yet strange today—like the war-whoop of a Native American warrior.

Denys shivered where he lay. But Gerard slept on, undisturbed.

The troop passed, and the noise faded into silence. The soldier drew a deep breath, whistled softly, closed his eyes—and slept like a top.

At dawn, Denys sat up and reached out to wake Gerard. His hand touched the young man’s forehead—and found it drenched in sweat.

As a nurse, Denys refrained from waking him. “It’s ill to disturb sleep or sweat in a sick man,” he said. “I know that well, though I’ve never minced an ape or a gallows-bird.”

After waiting an hour, hunger overcame him. He turned over and, in self-defense, fell asleep again—a trick he’d learned from years of hardship.

At noon, he awoke to find Gerard sitting upright, surrounded by smoke rising from the straw like a dung heap. Heat versus moisture.

Gerard called him “a lazy loon.”

Denys grinned silently.

They set out again. The first thing Denys did was return the crossbow to Gerard and climb a tall tree beside the road.

“Coast clear to the next village,” he announced, and they continued.

As they neared the settlement, Denys stopped suddenly and asked, “How do you feel?”

“What? Can’t you see? I feel as if Rome were no farther than that hamlet.”

“But your body—your skin?”

“Neither hot nor cold. Yesterday it was hot one moment, cold the next. But this cursed leg—it won’t leave me alone.”

“Le grand malheur!” Denys sighed. “Many of my comrades faced no such trouble.”

“Oh, here it goes again—itching terribly.”

“Unhappy youth,” Denys said solemnly, “the sum of your troubles is this: your fever is gone, and your wound is healing.”

“And so,” he added gently, “I shall tell you something I might have kept silent.”

“What is it?” Gerard asked, eyes bright with curiosity.

“Oh!”
