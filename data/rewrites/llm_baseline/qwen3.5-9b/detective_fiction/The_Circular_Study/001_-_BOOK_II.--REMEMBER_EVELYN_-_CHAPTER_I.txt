Detective Mr. Gryce was in a state of profound melancholy. He had reached that particular season of life when the spirit flags and enthusiasm requires a constant, artificial spur. Lately, however, there had been a distinct lack of special excitement, leaving him feeling dull and hopelessly superannuated. In fact, he was seriously contemplating resigning his post on the force to retire to the small farm he had purchased in Westchester. Such a prospect did little to lift his mood; for a man of action, the mere exercise of his mental faculties was far more inspiring than any material comfort retirement might offer. Yet, fate had other plans.

Just as his secret dissatisfaction peaked, a telephone message crackled through Headquarters, instantly rousing the old man to a vigor he hadn't felt in years. It cast a new, unexpected interest over the gray afternoon. The call originated from Carter's famous drugstore. A young boy had been dispatched by a woman to report a bizarre crime committed in the mansion located on ---- Street, just around the corner. The boy claimed he didn't know the woman, though it was obvious she had paid him handsomely. He was terrified, lending credibility to his tale. If the police needed him, he could be found waiting at the drugstore until released.

A *strange* crime. The single word struck Gryce with the force of a gavel, silencing his thoughts on age and retirement. While the officers nearby murmured about the sudden spotlight falling on the property—a historic landmark hemmed in by modern shops and clubs—Gryce seized the opportunity. With a glance at the inspector in charge, he received his orders.

He moved immediately to the drugstore, retrieving the trembling boy. En route to the scene, Gryce tried to draw out details, but the poor waif added nothing to the initial report. His story was simple: a stunningly beautiful woman in flashy clothes and heavy jewelry had approached him while he admired toys in a window. She handed him a coin, dragged him down the block, and then produced another, promising more if he would deliver a specific message to the phone operator. *"Tell him a strange crime has been committed in the old house on ---- Street,"* she had commanded. Fear had gripped the child, causing him to slide away, but the woman had shaken him back to his senses before he could flee.

When questioned about her appearance, the boy's observations were limited to clichés: she was a "stunner," wearing shiny things and lots of bling. Recognizing the limits of the witness, Gryce stopped probing and focused entirely on the looming brick edifice.

Externally, the house offered no clues. A closed door, a pristine stoop, and windows draped in heavy curtains suggested a home of quiet domestic respectability. Its dignified brownstone facade stood in stark, peaceful contrast to the chaotic commercial noise of the neighboring buildings.

"Some mistake," Gryce muttered, struck by the eerie perfection of the silence.

Before he could conclude he was being hoaxed, a movement broke the stillness beneath the stoop. An officer emerged, his face etched with confusion.

"Nothing wrong? No bloodshed? Everything looks perfectly quiet here," Gryce asked urgently.

The officer tipped his hat. "We can't get in. We've rung every bell. I thought the place empty until I spotted some activity in an upper window. Should I try forcing entry through the rear yard via Knapp & Co.'s store next door?"

"Yes," Gryce ordered, handing the boy over. "Lock the kid in one of their offices. Then find a way in from the backyard. It should be straightforward."

As the officer vanished into the adjacent store with the child, Gryce was immediately surrounded by a throng of curious onlookers, eager to share in the drama. However, the swift opening of the front door silenced the crowd. Gryce slipped inside, finding himself in a basement hall leading to a kitchen that appeared immaculate, devoid of the chaos often associated with tragedy. Finding no evidence of violence or disruption, he followed the officer upward.

Upon reaching the parlor floor, the officer shared local gossip: "Mr. Raffner from the shop next door says the resident is an odd fellow, known only as a 'bookworm.' He's lived here six months, yet no one has ever seen anyone else but himself and a peculiar, silent old servant."

"I know," Gryce replied quietly. Everyone knew the truth: the house, once the domain of New York's aristocracy, was currently occupied by Mr. Adams. Adams was a man of immense wealth and striking personal charm, but his temperament was so uncongenial that he avoided all human contact. Entering the home of such a recluse was rare; entering it on a matter of life and death was a thrill that reawakened the dormant passions of an aging detective.

They entered a wide, old-fashioned hall, sparsely furnished in antique style. Two doors stood open. They passed through the nearest, stepping into a room as bare as a doctor's waiting room. There was nothing of note here, and they would have departed without ceremony had they not caught a glimpse of opulence behind a partially drawn portière at the far end.

Pushing through, they froze.

Nothing in their vast experience had prepared them for the solemn, terrifying spectacle before them. Though daylight still filled the room, it was dominated by a jet of rose-colored light. This illumination did not fall upon the endless rows of books lining the lower walls or the grand painting that usually commanded attention. Instead, the light converged solely on the upturned face of a man lying on a bearskin rug.

In his heart protruded a dagger. Across his chest lay a cross, its golden lines sharply defined against the long, dark garment that swathed his body. He resembled a saint prepared for burial in some holy sanctuary, except for the dagger speaking of brutal murder and the expression on his face.

It was an anguish for which Mr. Gryce, despite a lifetime of witnessing death, could find no name. It bore no resemblance to fear, pain, or surprise—the standard reactions of a victim struck down by an assassin. It was something far deeper, far stranger, hanging in the red-lit air of the circular study.
