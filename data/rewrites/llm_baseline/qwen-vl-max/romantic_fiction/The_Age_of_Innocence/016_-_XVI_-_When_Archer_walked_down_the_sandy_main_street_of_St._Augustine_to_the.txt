XVI. When Archer walked down the sandy main street of St. Augustine toward the house that had been pointed out to him as Mr. Welland’s, he felt a quiet certainty settle over him. The air was warm and still, thick with the scent of magnolias and sea salt, and when he saw May standing beneath one of the great trees—her hair catching the sunlight like spun gold—he understood why he had waited so long to come. This was not just a visit; it was home. This was where he belonged. And yet, he thought, how foolish he’d been, afraid to leave his desk, to break free from duty, all because of what people might say. He had called himself bold, unbound by convention—but in truth, he had trembled at the idea of stepping outside the rules.

She turned at his approach, her face brightening. “Newland—has anything happened?” she asked, her voice light but edged with curiosity.

He smiled. It would have been more natural, perhaps, if she had read the truth in his eyes—the urgency, the longing—but instead, she looked surprised, then delighted, when he replied simply: “Yes. I found I had to see you.”

Her cheeks flushed, soft and warm, and for a moment the tension between them melted into something tender, almost familiar. He knew then that forgiveness would come easily, that even Mr. Letterblair’s mild disapproval would fade before the kindness of her family.

But the street was too public, too full of polite glances and idle gossip. He longed to be alone with her, to speak freely, to tell her everything—the ache in his chest, the way her presence made the world feel alive again. Still, it was early, and breakfast was not due for another hour. Instead of inviting him inside, she suggested they walk to an old orange grove beyond the town. She had just returned from rowing on the river, and the sun, dancing across the water like scattered coins, seemed to have caught her in its glow.

As they walked beside each other, her stride long and easy, her hair fluttering in the breeze, she looked like a statue carved from youth and light. Her face held a calm serenity, untouched by worry or desire—a purity that calmed Archer’s restless soul. They sat on a wooden bench beneath the citrus trees, and he reached for her, pulling her close, kissing her gently.

It was like drinking from a spring touched by sunlight—refreshing, pure, intoxicating. But perhaps he held her too tightly, for her breath hitched and she pulled back, startled.

“What is it?” he asked, smiling softly.

“Nothing,” she whispered, though her eyes held a flicker of confusion.

A silence fell between them, fragile and sudden. Her hand slipped from his, and he realized this was only the second time he’d kissed her on the lips—the first being their brief embrace in the Beaufort conservatory. And now, he saw, she was shaken, pulled from her usual composure.

To ease the moment, he leaned back, crossing his arms behind his head, shielding his eyes from the glare. “Tell me what you do all day,” he said, letting his voice fall into a relaxed rhythm.

She began to speak—of swimming, sailing, riding, of dances at the inn when a warship docked. A few guests from Philadelphia and Baltimore were staying nearby, and the Selfridge Merrys had come down because Kate Merry had fallen ill. They were planning a tennis court on the beach, though only Kate and May owned racquets, and most of the others hadn’t even heard of the game.

All of it kept her busy, and she confessed she hadn’t had time to read the little vellum book he’d sent her—the *Sonnets from the Portuguese*. But she had memorized “How they brought the Good News from Ghent to Aix,” because it was one of the first poems he’d ever read aloud to her. And she found amusement in telling him that Kate Merry had never heard of Robert Browning.

Then she glanced at her watch and gasped. “We’ll be late for breakfast!” she exclaimed, jumping up.

They hurried back to the modest house with its crooked porch and overgrown garden of plumbago and geraniums. The place was a makeshift refuge, arranged each winter by Mrs. Welland with painstaking care. Mr. Welland, delicate and sensitive, could not endure the discomforts of southern hotels, so year after year, at great expense and effort, his wife created a semblance of home—using disgruntled New York servants and local help alike.

“The doctors insist my husband must feel he’s at home,” Mrs. Welland explained each season to visiting guests, “or the climate won’t help him at all.” And sure enough, at breakfast, Mr. Welland sat beaming, surrounded by delicacies that seemed impossible in such a remote place. “We’re camping,” he declared, piling his plate with golden griddle-cakes drowned in syrup. “I want to teach my wife and May how to rough it.”

They were surprised by Archer’s sudden arrival, but he had cleverly claimed he was recovering from a cold—just beginning to take hold. That excuse, Mr. Welland declared, was perfectly reasonable. “You can’t be too careful, especially in spring,” he said. “If I’d been as prudent at your age, May would be dancing at Assemblies now, instead of spending winters in this wilderness with an old invalid.”

“Oh, but I love it here, Papa,” May replied, her voice warm. “If only Newland could stay, I’d like it a thousand times better than New York.”

“And he shall,” Mrs. Welland said kindly. “Until he’s completely recovered.”

Archer laughed, though he already knew he would manage to extend his illness for a week—long enough to delay his return. And it was ironic, he thought, that Mr. Letterblair’s indulgence was partly due to the fact that Archer had recently resolved the Olenski divorce case with skill and discretion. The firm had praised him highly, and even old Mrs. Manson Mingott had expressed approval. One day, while May was out with her father, Mrs. Welland took the chance to speak of Ellen Olenska.

“I’m afraid Ellen’s ideas are very different from ours,” she said. “She was barely eighteen when Medora Manson took her to Europe—do you remember the stir when she appeared in black at her coming-out ball? Another of Medora’s whims, really. And since then, Ellen has never returned to America. No wonder she’s become so European.”

“But European society doesn’t approve of divorce,” Archer said quietly. “Countess Olenska believed she was conforming to American ideals by seeking freedom.”

It was the first time he had spoken her name since leaving Skuytercliff, and he felt heat rise to his cheeks.

Mrs. Welland smiled gently. “That’s just the kind of thing foreigners imagine about us. They think we dine at two o’clock and accept divorce! That’s why I don’t understand why we invite them to New York. They accept our hospitality—and then go home and repeat these silly stories.”

Archer said nothing. He knew she meant well, but the words stung. He wanted to say that Ellen wasn’t merely a foreigner, but someone who had chosen a different path—one that required courage, not scandal. Yet he remained silent.

Mrs. Welland continued. “But we are truly grateful for your influence in persuading Ellen to abandon her plans. Her grandmother and Uncle Lovell wrote that her change of heart was entirely due to you. In fact, she told her grandmother so herself. She has great admiration for you. Poor Ellen—she was always a difficult child. I wonder what her future holds?”

Archer nearly answered: “What we’ve all made it.” He imagined saying, “If you’d rather she become Beaufort’s mistress than a decent man’s wife, you’ve done exactly the right thing.” But he didn’t speak. He wondered what Mrs. Welland would do if he did—how her composed face would crumble under the weight of truth. There were traces of beauty still in her features, like May’s, and he couldn’t help thinking: Would May grow into that same image of perfect, unyielding innocence?

No, he thought fiercely. Not May. He didn’t want her to carry that kind of innocence—the kind that shuts out imagination, that seals the heart against real feeling.

Later, he hoped to talk to Mrs. Welland about advancing their wedding date. But when the moment came, he found no words strong enough to move her. Relief washed over him when he saw May and her father returning. His only hope now was to plead with May directly.

On the eve of his departure, he walked with her to the ruins of the Spanish Mission. The crumbling walls and arches evoked visions of distant lands—Granada, the Alhambra. May, wearing a wide-brimmed hat that shadowed her clear eyes, lit up at the mention of Spain.

“We might see it all this spring—even the Easter ceremonies in Seville,” he urged, exaggerating his hopes in the hope of gaining more.

“Easter in Seville?” she laughed. “And it’s Lent next week!”

“Why shouldn’t we marry in Lent?” he replied.

She looked shocked. “Oh, Newland—I didn’t mean that. But soon after Easter—so we could sail at the end of April. I know I could arrange it at the office.”

She smiled dreamily, as if picturing a future that was already written in poetry. But he saw it clearly—she was content to dream, not to act. It was like reading beautiful verses that could never come true.

“Oh, go on,” she said. “I love your descriptions.”

“But why should they be only descriptions?” he asked. “Why can’t they be real?”

“We shall, dearest,” she murmured. “Next year.”

Her voice lingered on the word, as if it carried the weight of a promise.

“Don’t you want them to be real sooner?” he pressed. “Can’t I persuade you to break away now?”

She bowed her head, disappearing beneath the brim of her hat. “Why should we waste another year? Look at me, dear. Don’t you understand how much I want you?”

For a moment, she stood motionless. Then she raised her eyes—clear, desperate, unguarded—and he felt his grip loosen slightly.

Suddenly, her expression shifted. “I’m not sure if I _do_ understand,” she said softly. “Is it… is it because you’re not certain of continuing to care for me?”

Archer stood up abruptly. “My God—perhaps,” he said, anger flashing in his voice. “I don’t know.”

May rose too, and as they faced each other, she seemed to grow taller, more dignified. Silence stretched between them—uncomfortable, charged.

“If that’s it,” she said quietly, “is there someone else?”

“Someone else—between you and me?” he echoed, as if the words were unfamiliar.

She nodded. “Let’s talk frankly, Newland. Sometimes I’ve noticed a difference in you—especially since our engagement was announced.”

“Dear—what madness!” he protested.

She smiled faintly. “If it is, it won’t hurt us to talk about it. Or even if it’s true—why shouldn’t we speak of it? You might have made a mistake.”

He stared at the pattern of leaves on the path beneath their feet. “Mistakes are easy to make,” he said. “But if I had made one like the kind you suggest, do you think I’d be begging you to hurry our marriage?”

She looked down, her sunshade trembling slightly in her hands. “Yes,” she said slowly. “You might want—to settle it once and for all. That’s one way.”

The clarity of her voice startled him, but he saw the fear beneath it—the pallor of her profile, the slight quiver of her nostril.

“Well?” he asked, sitting down, trying to lighten the mood with a frown.

She joined him. “You mustn’t think girls know nothing,” she said. “We hear things. We notice. We have feelings. And long before you told me you cared for me, I knew there was someone else you were interested in. Everyone talked about it two years ago at Newport. Once I saw you sitting together on a verandah at a dance. When she came back into the house, her face was sad. I felt sorry for her. I remembered it later—when we were engaged.”

Her voice dropped to a whisper. She clasped and unclasped her hands around her sunshade.

Archer laid his hand over hers. “My dear child—was that it? If you only knew the truth.”

She lifted her head. “Then there’s a truth I don’t know?”

He kept his hand on hers. “I meant—the truth about that old story.”

“But that’s what I want to know,” she said. “What I ought to know. I couldn’t build my happiness on something unfair to someone else. And I believe you’d feel the same. What kind of life could we have on such foundations?”

Her face filled with a tragic courage that humbled him. He felt as if he should kneel before her.

“I’ve wanted to say this for a long time,” she went on. “When two people truly love each other, I understand there may be situations where it’s right to go against public opinion. And if you feel pledged—somehow—to the woman we’ve spoken of—if there’s any way you can fulfill that pledge—even through divorce—Newland, don’t give her up because of me.”

He was stunned. That she feared his past—his fleeting affair with Mrs. Thorley Rushworth—was shocking. But her generosity, her willingness to let him choose his own path, was almost unbearable. It was a kind of heroism he had never expected.

He swallowed hard. “There is no pledge,” he said. “No obligation. Such cases aren’t always simple. But that’s not important. I admire your generosity—because I feel the same way. Each situation must be judged on its own merits—without stupid conventions. I mean—each woman’s right to her liberty—”

He stopped himself, startled by the direction of his thoughts. Then he smiled. “Since you understand so much, dearest—can’t you understand the uselessness of submitting to another form of the same foolish conventions? If there’s no one and nothing between us—doesn’t that argue for marrying quickly, rather than waiting longer?”

She flushed, joy lighting her face. She looked up at him, tears glistening in her eyes.

But then, as if the moment had passed, she sank back into her usual timidity. The courage she showed for others vanished, replaced by the helpless girlhood he knew so well. He realized her strength was reserved for others—not for herself.

He had no heart left to press her further. The woman who had looked at him with such depth and honesty was gone, replaced by the May he had always known—sweet, obedient, afraid to step beyond the lines.

She seemed to sense his disappointment, but didn’t know how to mend it. They stood up and walked silently home, the warmth of the afternoon fading into the quiet of separation.
