Two weeks later, a pale, bowed figure made its way into the Dominican convent situated in the suburbs of Gouda. Seeking audience with Brother Ambrose—who governed the house as deputy following the recent death of the prior, whose successor had yet to arrive—the visitor was none other than Gerard. He came to end his life as he had begun it. Though he had entered initially as a novice upon probation, the truth was that he was a failing man, acutely aware of his decline. He sought only to pass away in peace, surrounded by the kindness of Ambrose, his dear friend, and the other monks whose doors and hearts had always remained open to him.

His own home had become unbearable; it was too saturated with painful memories of her. Ambrose, recognizing both the worth of his soul and the depth of his grief, harbored a gentle hope that he might be cured and restored to his parish duties. To this end, he assigned Gerard a comfortable cell directly above the gateway and strictly forbade him from fasting or engaging in any acts of austerity. Yet, before these remedies could take hold, the newly appointed prior arrived. He proved to be a stern disciplinarian, a "very Tartar" indeed. Initially consumed with rectifying abuses and tightening the monastic rules, his demeanor shifted abruptly when he learned that the Vicar of Gouda had also entered the order as a novice.

"'Tis well," the new prior declared sharply. "Let him first resign his vicarage, then remain; otherwise, depart. I will have no fat parsons in my house."

Soon thereafter, the prior summoned Gerard. The younger monk obeyed and presented himself before the superior. The moment their eyes met, both men recoiled in startled recognition.

"Clement!" cried the prior.

"Jerome!" replied the novice.
