An old oak desk, deep-bodied and sturdy, stood by the window of a room commanding a view of St. James's Park from a considerable height. The chamber was spacious, furnished and adorned with evident taste, yet the atmosphere was dominated by the solitary weight of a bachelor's habitation. John Marlowe unlocked the desk and extracted a long, stout envelope from its recesses.

"I understand," he addressed Mr. Cupples, "that you have already read this."

"I read it for the first time two days ago," replied Mr. Cupples, seated comfortably on a sofa and surveying the room with a benevolent expression. "We have discussed it fully."

Marlowe turned to Trent. "There is your manuscript," he said, placing the envelope upon the table. "I have scrutinized it three times. I do not believe there is another man who could have uncovered as much of the truth as you have recorded therein."

Trent dismissed the compliment. Sitting beside the table, he stared stonily into the fire, his long legs twisted awkwardly beneath his chair. "You mean, of course," he said, pulling the envelope toward him, "that there remains more of the truth to be revealed now. We are ready to hear you whenever you wish. I anticipate a lengthy narrative, and the longer the better, so far as I am concerned; I require thorough understanding. What we should both desire, I suspect, is a preliminary account of Manderson and your relationship with him. From the outset, it appeared to me that the character of the deceased man must be an integral element in this affair."

"You were correct," Marlowe answered grimly. He crossed the room and settled onto a corner of the high, cushioned fender. "I shall commence as you suggest."

"I ought to inform you beforehand," said Trent, fixing Marlowe with a steady gaze, "that while I am here to listen, I possess no reason as yet to doubt the conclusions I have outlined here." He tapped the envelope. "It represents a defense you intend to mount—you comprehend that?"

"Perfectly." Marlowe remained cool and composed, a stark contrast to the worn, nervous figure Trent had recalled from Marlstone eighteen months prior. His tall, lithe frame was held with perfect muscular tension. His brow was open, his blue eyes clear, though they retained, as he paused to gather his thoughts, the unsettling quality that had troubled Trent at their initial encounter. Only the lines around his mouth betrayed his awareness of a difficult position he intended to face squarely.

"Sigsbee Manderson was not a man of normal mind," Marlowe began in a quiet voice. "Most of the exceedingly wealthy individuals I encountered in America had achieved their status through abnormal greed, abnormal industry, abnormal personal force, or abnormal luck. None possessed remarkable intellects. Manderson delighted in accumulating wealth; he labored incessantly at it; he was a man of dominant will; he possessed ample fortune; yet what rendered him singular was his cerebral power. In his own country, they might tell you that his ruthlessness in pursuing his objectives was his most striking trait; however, hundreds of others would have executed his plans with equal disregard for human consequence had they been capable of devising them.

"I am not suggesting Americans lack cleverness; as a nation, they are ten times more astute than we are. Yet, I never met another individual displaying such a degree of sagacity and foresight, such gifts of memory and mental tenacity, such sheer force of intelligence, as underpinned everything Manderson accomplished in his financial career. They frequently dubbed him the 'Napoleon of Wall Street' in the press; few realized, as I did, the profound accuracy of that epithet. He seemingly never forgot a fact that might prove useful. Systematically, regarding business matters, he applied the same methodology Napoleon employed with military data. He studied them in specialized digests prepared at regular intervals, always within reach, enabling him to consult reports on coal, wheat, railways, or whatever commodity interested him at any unoccupied moment. Consequently, he could formulate bolder and wiser strategies than any competitor. The Street learned that Manderson would never perform the obvious act, yet they failed to grasp the full extent; the action he invariably took was almost always a surprise, and much of his success stemmed from this unpredictability. The market would tremble when it was rumored the old man was out with his 'gun,' and often his opponents surrendered as readily as Colonel Crockett's raccoon in the tale. The scheme I am about to describe would have occupied most men for weeks. Manderson could have plotted it down to the final detail while shaving."

"I used to speculate that his distant Indian ancestry might contribute to the man's cunning and ruthlessness. Strangely, this lineage was unknown to anyone save himself and me. It was when he requested I apply my aptitude for genealogical research to his obscure family history that I discovered he possessed a fraction of the blood of the Iroquois chief Montour and his French wife—a formidable woman who governed the savage politics of the wilderness tribes two centuries prior. The Mandersons were active in the fur trade along the Pennsylvania border during that era, and numerous members married Indian women. Other strains of Indian blood may have descended to Manderson through preceding and subsequent unions; some wives' antecedents were entirely untraceable, and so many generations of pioneering preceded the full civilization of the region. My investigations led me to conclude that there is a vast amount of aboriginal blood present in the genetic makeup of the American populace, widely dispersed. Newer families constantly intermarried with older ones, and so many possessed a native strain—and were often quite proud of it in those days. However, Manderson harbored a belief in the disgracefulness of mixed blood, a sentiment that likely intensified with the rise of the Negro question following the War. He was thunderstruck by what I revealed and eager to conceal it from everyone. Naturally, I never divulged it while he lived, nor did I believe he expected me to; yet, I have since concluded that his mind turned against me from that moment onward. It occurred approximately a year before his demise."

"Did Manderson," Mr. Cupples asked abruptly, causing the others to startle, "possess any definable religious attitude?"

Marlowe pondered for a moment. "None that I ever heard of," he replied. "Worship and prayer were utterly foreign to him, as far as I could discern, and I never heard him mention religion. I doubt if he possessed any genuine sense of God, or if he was capable of perceiving Him through emotion. Nevertheless, I understood that as a child he received a religious upbringing with a strong moral component. His private life was, in the conventional limited sense, blameless. He was almost ascetic in his habits, aside from his smoking. I lived with him for four years without ever witnessing him utter a direct verbal falsehood, despite his constant practice of deceit in other forms. Can you conceive the soul of a man who never hesitated to undertake actions designed to hoodwink others, who utilized every market trick to mislead, yet remained scrupulous in never telling a direct lie on the most trivial matter? Manderson was precisely that, and he was not unique. One might compare his state of mind to that of a soldier who is personally truthful but will stop at nothing to deceive the enemy. The rules of engagement permit it; the same applies to business, as many businessmen regard it. Only with them, it is perpetually wartime."

"It is a sorrowful world," Mr. Cupples observed.

"As you say," Marlowe agreed. "Now, I was stating that one could always accept Manderson's word if delivered in a definitive form. The first instance I ever heard him utter a outright lie was on the night he died; hearing it, I believe, saved me from execution as his murderer."

Marlowe stared upward at the light fixture, and Trent shifted impatiently in his seat. "Before we address that," he interjected, "will you explain exactly on what footing you stood with Manderson during the years of your association?"

"We maintained excellent terms from beginning to end," Marlowe answered. "Not akin to friendship—he was not a man for cultivating friends—but the finest rapport between a trusted subordinate and his chief. I joined him as private secretary shortly after obtaining my degree at Oxford. I was originally destined to enter my father's business, where I currently reside, but my father suggested I travel for a year or two. Thus, I accepted this secretaryship, which promised diverse experiences, allowing the provisional period to extend to four years before the tragedy unfolded. The offer arrived via the most unlikely channel imaginable for securing a salaried post: chess."

At the word, Trent clapped his hands together with a muttered exclamation. The others regarded him in surprise.

"Chess!" Trent repeated, rising to approach Marlowe. "Tell me," he said, "what was the first thing I noticed about you at our initial meeting? It was your eye, Mr. Marlowe. I could not place it then, but I now recall where I had seen those eyes before. They belonged to no lesser man than the great Nikolay Korchagin, with whom I once shared a railway carriage for two days. I thought I would never forget the 'chess eye' after that encounter, yet I could not identify the owner when I saw it in you. I apologize," he concluded abruptly, returning to his statue-like posture in the chair.
