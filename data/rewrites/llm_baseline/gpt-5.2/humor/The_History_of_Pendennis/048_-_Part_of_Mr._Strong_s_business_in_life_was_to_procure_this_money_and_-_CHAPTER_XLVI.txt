The noble Henry Foker—whom we had the impoliteness to mislay for a little—had, in the interval, been doing what steady men do with a steady passion: feeding it, tending it, turning it on the spit, basting it hourly with hope, and then burning his fingers when it spluttered.

If a few of my youthful readers, who dabble in that cookery, would but keep accounts for a week, they would discover love to be one of the dearest articles in the market. It begins by robbing you of sleep, which is the first of household provisions. You lie awake, listening to the whole night—coach-wheels grumbling in the street like distant thunder, a drunken voice spoiling a tune that once belonged to somebody’s village, a watchman’s call going by like a melancholy refrain—until you come down to breakfast when the toast is cold and the family has long since gone to work. Then, when at last you sit to your occupation, you stir it without looking, like a man who spoils a sauce because his nose is elsewhere. The day’s labour is slurred over, friends neglected, home becomes insipid, and the honest dinner, which your poor mother has contrived with affectionate skill—cutlets properly turned, gravy reduced to the right gloss, a pudding that should have made any Christian grateful—is sent away untasted. The whole table of life has no flavour, except one single dish; and even that one you are forever forbidden to eat in peace.

Such is the condition of ill-regulated youth; and such, precisely, was Mr. H. Foker’s. Having been reared in the comfortable belief that whatever his fancy asked for was a thing Providence meant him to have, he gave himself to this longing with his usual good-natured selfishness. Nor let any man of the world be astonished that one who had preached prudence to Arthur Pendennis should himself become a captive. Who among us has not been a solemn food-historian at another man’s table—explaining, with learned gravity, the virtues of moderation—only to go home and devour the very pie he condemned? You, madam, perhaps, are a creature of perfect digestion and perfect virtue; you, sir, so strong-minded that no foolish appetite ever interrupts your attendance at ’Change. We will not trouble such lofty persons with sympathy. We keep ours for the fallible, who burn their hands, taste again, and carry on cooking with the rest of mankind. Angels are very admirable, but intolerable companions; they sit above us, pristine as untouched sugar, and we cannot reach up to talk to them over the pot.

There are no perfect characters here—save perhaps one little one, and she, poor soul, does not know it, but suspects herself of being as wicked as need be. At present, however, she is not in London; and it is by no means for the like of her that Harry’s heart was simmering and boiling over.

Besides, what does a few failings matter to worship? Need we be saints to be treated like them? The world’s taste is a mighty miscellany. There is no aged booby, no pompous offender, no stupid tyrant, no figure cut from the coarsest joint, that may not, some day, find himself preferred to virtue—hugged, clung to, defended, as though sin were a family recipe and repentance an exotic condiment. History and the newspapers can furnish love-stories between persons as unpromising as yesterday’s bones and beer; yet the passion has been as grand, to the sufferers, as Cleopatra’s banquets.

So Henry Foker longed after his love and cursed the fate that kept him from her. When Lord Gravesend’s family retired to the country—his lordship leaving his proxy behind with the venerable Lord Bagwig—Harry still lingered in London, not to the smallest sorrow of Lady Ann, his affianced cousin, who missed him no more than she would miss an extra footman at dinner. Wherever Miss Clavering went, the infatuated youth contrived to hover. But because his engagement to Lady Ann was known, he was forced to keep his fever under his coat; and so it raged inwardly, like a pot too tightly covered, until one wondered it did not blow him up and scatter him, lid and all, into the street.

A grand entertainment took place at Gaunt House one beautiful June evening; and next day the journals printed, in close-set columns, two yards of nobility and gentry who had been honoured with invitations. Among the guests were Sir Francis and Lady Clavering and Miss Amory—whom the indefatigable Major Pendennis had thrust, by patient engineering, into the blaze of fashion—together with our two young friends, Arthur and Harry. Each danced a great deal with Miss Blanche.

As for the worthy Major, he assumed the charge of Lady Clavering, and, knowing her ladyship’s brightest talent lay not in conversation but in consumption, conducted her into the refreshment-room: that sacred chapel of cold chicken and hot flattery, where, under Titians and Giorgiones, beneath Vandyke’s cavaliers and Reynolds’s ladies, and amongst gold and silver salvers that might have served a regiment, supper went on all night with the reckless splendour of people who never paid for it. There were creams that trembled like pale custard clouds, jellies shining as if a ruby had learned to wobble, white soups exhaling pepper and marrow, salads crisp with that sharp green scent that wakes the tongue, peaches that seemed to have stolen their bloom from dawn, grapes that burst with a faint crack like tiny drums. Tea steamed in bright urns; champagne whispered and hissed, like fashionable applause.

Of how many of these articles Lady Clavering partook does not become us to say. The Major followed her with admirable patience, signalling to solemn footmen and lovely servant-maids, bringing plate after plate to the Begum, ministering to her palate as though it were the public service. What he suffered—what heroic pangs in boots, what rheumatism politely ignored, what silent prayer for bed—no man can compute. He never confessed; he only smiled.

Mr. Wagg, who was present, counted Lady Clavering’s dishes as long as arithmetic remained possible; but as he himself drank champagne with the zeal of a patriot, his calculations grew untrustworthy. Still, he recommended Lady Steyne’s medical man, Mr. Honeyman, to look carefully after the Begum, and to call next day for tidings of her health—as one might inquire after a ship that had taken in too much cargo.

Sir Francis Clavering showed himself too; he lurked about the magnificent rooms with the air of a man who has strayed into church by mistake. The splendour and the company were not to the Baronet’s taste; after tossing off a tumbler of wine or two at the buffet, he escaped to the region of Jermyn Street, where his true worship awaited him: Loder, Punter, little Moss Abramns, Captain Skewball, and the familiar green table. There, in the rattle of the box and the agreeable din of gaming talk—coins clinking, oaths popping like corks, the harsh music of London pleasure—Sir Francis’s spirits rose to their accustomed pitch of feeble hilarity.

Meanwhile in the dancing-room Mr. Pynsent, having engaged Miss Amory, came up to claim her hand. But scowls of recognition had already passed between him and Arthur Pendennis; and Arthur, rising abruptly, claimed Miss Amory for the present dance. Mr. Pynsent, biting his lips, scowled yet more savagely, bowed profoundly, and withdrew, saying he gave up his claim. There are certain men whom life keeps throwing across one’s path as persistently as a bad tune in the street. Pynsent and Pen had that fate with each other; and each judged the other accordingly.

“What a confounded conceited provincial fool!” thought the one. “Because he has written a twopenny novel, his head’s turned. A kicking would cure him.”

“What an impertinent idiot!” thought the other, and said aloud to his partner, “His soul is in Downing Street; his neckcloth is foolscap; his hair is sand; his legs are rulers; his vitals are tape and sealing-wax. He was a prig in his cradle and has only laughed three times—always at the same joke of his chief. I have the same liking for that man, Miss Amory, that I have for cold boiled veal.”

Blanche, of course, pronounced him wicked—méchant—perfectly abominable; and wondered, with charming malice, what he would say when her back was turned.

“Say?” cried Pen. “Say you have the most beautiful figure and the slimmest waist in the world, Blanche—Miss Amory, I mean. I beg your pardon. Another turn: this music would make an alderman dance.”

“And you have left off tumbling when you waltz now?” she asked, looking up at him with that little air of mock concern which is more provoking than scorn.

“One falls and one gets up again in life, Blanche,” he said. “I used to call you so in old times—and it is the prettiest name in the world. Besides, I have practised since then.”

“And with a great number of partners, I’m afraid,” she sighed—sham sigh—and shrugged her shoulders. And indeed Mr. Pen had practised a good deal in this life and had become a better dancer.

If Pendennis was impertinent, Foker—usually bland and talkative—became wholly mum and melancholy when he danced with Miss Amory. To clasp her slender waist was rapture, to whirl with her was delirium; but to speak—what could he say worth the hearing of such a queen? His tongue, which could discourse at length upon horses, coats, bets, and the private history of half the club, now stuck like dough that will not rise.

Blanche did the talking. She asked after that dear little pony; she thanked him with a tender kindness and regret, and refused the dear little pony with a sigh so delicately administered it might have been whipped syllabub.

“I have nobody to ride with in London,” she said. “Mamma is timid, and her figure is not pretty on horseback. Sir Francis never goes out with me. He loves me like—like a stepdaughter. Oh, how delightful it must be to have a father—a father, Mr. Foker!”

“Oh—uncommon,” said Mr. Harry, who enjoyed that blessing with calmness. At which—forgetting her sentiment in a flash—Blanche’s grey eyes twinkled so archly that both burst out laughing; and Harry, enchanted and suddenly at ease, began to chatter with a rush of innocent prattle—kind, simple Foker talk—seasoned with expressions not to be found in dictionaries, and full of little personal histories of himself and horses, with artless freedom about people passing in the ballroom, whose looks and motives he sketched with a humorous ease that would have made him, in a different world, an excellent kitchen gossip.

When his modesty returned and the talk flagged, Blanche knew how to stir the sauce again. She asked of Logwood: Was it pretty? Was he a hunting man? Did he like women to hunt?—in which case she was prepared to adore hunting. But Mr. Foker, expressing his dislike of sporting females, pointed out Lady Bullfinch passing by—whom he had seen at cover with a cigar in her face. Immediately Blanche too detested field sports, and declared it made her shudder to think of a dear sweet little fox being killed; on which Harry laughed and waltzed with renewed vigour.

At the end of the waltz—the last they danced together that night—Blanche questioned him about Drummington: was it a fine house? His cousins, she heard, were accomplished. Lord Erith she had met; which cousin was his favourite? Was it not Lady Ann? Yes—she was sure it was she—sure by his looks and blushes. She was tired; it was very late; she must go to mamma; and, without another word, she sprang away from Harry’s arm and seized upon Pen’s, who was swaggering about the room, and cried, “Mamma, mamma!—take me to mamma, dear Mr. Pendennis!”—shooting Harry through with a Parthian glance as she fled.

Lord Steyne, with garter and ribbon, bald head and shining eyes, and that collar of red whiskers about his face, looked grand upon an occasion of state; and he made a prodigious impression on Lady Clavering when he introduced himself at the request of the obsequious Major. With his own white and royal hand he gave her a glass of wine; said he had heard of her charming daughter; begged to be presented; and at that instant Arthur Pendennis came up with Blanche upon his arm.

The peer bowed profoundly; Blanche made the deepest curtsy ever executed by young person with a tired foot. His lordship gave Arthur his hand; said he had read his book—very wicked and clever—asked Miss Blanche if she had read it; at which Pen blushed and winced, because Blanche, in black ringlets and a little altered, was one of his heroines. She had read it. Her eyes did the praising with rapture enough to make an author feel at once crowned and ashamed. The little scene played off, the Marquis made two more bows to mother and daughter and moved on through his guests, spreading grandeur as a stove spreads heat.

Mamma and daughter were loud in their admiration of the noble Marquis the moment his broad back was turned. “He said they make a very nice couple,” whispered Major Pendennis to Lady Clavering. Did he, indeed? Mamma thought they would. Mamma was so flustered—by the honour, by the lights, by the compliments, by the supper, by all the sweet froth of the night—that her good-humour overflowed like punch in a crowded bowl. She laughed; she winked; she nodded knowingly at Pen; she tapped him with her fan; she tapped Blanche; she tapped the Major. Her contentment was boundless, and she expressed it upon everything within reach.

When the party at last went down the great staircase of Gaunt House, morning had risen stark and clear over the black trees of the square. The sky was tinged with pink, like the inside of a shell. The faces of some of the dancers—ah, how ghastly they looked in that honest light!—were like dishes that have stood out too long. And the admirable Major above all—who had for hours been by Lady Clavering’s side, feeding her body with everything nice and her ear with everything flattering—what an object he was now! The rings round his eyes were the colour of bistre; those orbs themselves resembled the plovers’ eggs of which Lady Clavering and Blanche had tasted; the wrinkles in his old face were cut deeper than usual; and a silver stubble, like chilly dew, glittered on his chin beside the dyed whiskers now limp and out of curl.

There he stood, enduring without complaint a silent agony; knowing everybody could see his collapse (as he could see theirs), longing for bed for hours, aware that suppers disagreed with him, yet having eaten a little to keep Lady Clavering in good-humour; with twinges in back and knees; with weary feet burning in varnished boots. If the gods admire fortitude, surely that Power in whose chapels Major Pendennis worshipped must have looked approvingly upon this martyrdom. There are sufferers in this cause as in the other; priests have gashed themselves for their idols, and men will torture themselves to please what they take for divinity. Smash the idols if you can—but don’t be too savage upon the idolaters; they adore the best thing they know.

The elder and younger Pendennis waited with Lady Clavering and her daughter until her carriage was announced; and then the Major’s martyrdom ended, for the good-natured Begum insisted upon leaving him at his door in Bury Street. So he took the back seat after a feeble bow or two and a speech of thanks—polite to the last, resolute in duty. The Begum waved her dumpy hand in farewell to Arthur and Foker; Blanche smiled languidly at the young men and wondered whether she looked wan and green under her rose-coloured hood, and whether it was the mirrors at Gaunt House or the fatigue and fever in her eyes that made her seem so pale.

Arthur, perhaps, saw well enough how yellow Blanche looked, and did not attribute it to glass or fancy. Our young man of the world could use his eyes keenly and saw Blanche’s face pretty much as nature and late hours had made it. But poor Foker—dazzled and blinded—found no more fault in it than in the sun now flaring over the rooftops.

Among the wicked London habits Pen had acquired, the moralist may observe he kept abominable hours, and often went to bed when sober country people were thinking of getting up. Men grow used to any hour: editors, Covent Garden folk, night cabmen and coffee-sellers, chimney-sweeps, and the ladies and gentlemen of fashion who frequent balls are often lively at three or four in the morning when honest mortals are snoring. Pen, as we have shown, was brisk at this period, inclined to smoke his cigar and speak freely.

So Foker and Pen walked away from Gaunt House indulging in both amusements—or rather, Pen talked and smoked, and Foker looked as if he wanted to say something and couldn’t find the words. Pen, after being amongst great folks, grew sarcastic and dandified; he imitated their tones without knowing, and, having a lively imagination, mistook himself for a person of importance with extraordinary ease. He rattled on, attacking this person and that; sneering at Lady John Turnbull’s bad French—introduced into all conversation in spite of everybody’s groans—at Mrs. Slack Roper’s extraordinary costume and sham jewels; at old dandies and young ones; at whom didn’t he sneer?

“You fire at everybody, Pen—you’re grown awful, that you are,” Foker said. “Now you’ve pulled about Blondel’s yellow wig, and Colchicum’s black one, why don’t you have a shy at a brown one—hay? you know whose I mean. It got into Lady Clavering’s carriage.”

“Under my uncle’s hat? My uncle is a martyr, Foker, my boy,” Pen cried. “My uncle has been doing excruciating duties all night. He likes to go to bed early. He gets a dreadful headache if he sits up and touches supper. He has the gout if he walks or stands much at a ball. He has been sitting up, and standing up, and supping—and he has gone home to the gout and the headache for my sake. Shall I make fun of the old boy? No—not for Venice!”

“How do you mean—for your sake?” Foker asked, stopping short with a look as if someone had suddenly clapped a cold plate into his hands.

“Boy! canst thou keep a secret if I impart it to thee?” Pen cried, in high spirits. “Art thou of good counsel? Wilt thou swear? Wilt thou be mum, or wilt thou preach? Wilt thou be silent and hear—or wilt thou speak and die?” And as he flung himself into a ridiculous theatrical attitude, the cabmen at the stand in Piccadilly looked up from their dozing and grinned at the antics of the two young swells, while the city’s morning noises—wheels, hoofs, a hawker’s cry—began their daily overture.

“What the doose are you driving at?” Foker asked, very much agitated.

Pen, scarcely noting the agitation, kept his bantering, excited tone. “Henry, friend of my youth,” he declaimed, “and witness of my early follies—though dull at thy books, yet thou art not altogether deprived of sense—nay, blush not, Henrico; thou hast a good portion of that, and of courage and kindness too, at the service of thy friends. Were I in a strait of poverty, I would come to my Foker’s purse. Were I in grief, I would discharge my grief upon his sympathising bosom—”

“Gammon, Pen—go on,” Foker muttered, uneasy as a man who hears his own name called in a kitchen where creditors are waiting.

“I would, Henrico, upon thy studs, and upon thy cambric worked by the hands of beauty to adorn the breast of valour! Know then, friend of my boyhood’s days, that Arthur Pendennis of the Upper Temple, student-at-law, feels he is growing lonely and old. Care is furrowing his temples, and Baldness is busy with his crown. Shall we stop and have a drop of coffee at this stall? it looks very hot and nice. Look how that cabman is blowing at his saucer. No, you won’t? Aristocrat! I resume my tale. I am getting on in life. I have got devilish little money. I want some. I am thinking of getting some, and settling in life. I’m thinking of settling. I’m thinking of marrying, old boy. I’m thinking of becoming a moral man; a steady port-and-sherry character: with a good reputation in my quartier, and a moderate establishment of two maids and a man—with an occasional brougham to drive out Mrs. Pendennis, and a house near the Parks for the accommodation of the children. Ha! what sayest thou? Answer thy friend, thou worthy child of beer. Speak, I adjure thee by all thy vats.”

“But you ain’t got any money, Pen,” said the other, still looking alarmed.
