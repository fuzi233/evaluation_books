Barely a week later, on the seventh of April, Florence’s great Piazza della Signoria played host to a stranger spectacle even than the famous Bonfire of Vanities. A greater throng had crammed into its wide expanse than had ever gathered there, even for the feast day of San Giovanni.
It was nearing midday, and since the first light of dawn the people had swarmed to claim every possible vantage point—good or ill—clinging to the facades and roofs of palaces, and staking out every free patch of pavement. Men perched on sharp iron brackets angled out from rising walls, wrapped arms and legs around slim marble pillars, straddled the necks of rough stone statuary that crested grand entranceways, balanced on a palm’s breadth of architrave, and braced their feet on the jagged projections of rustic masonry, clinging tight to the heavy iron rings and staples driven into the walls beside them. They had come to see a miracle. Cramped limbs and scraped skin felt like trivial inconveniences with that prospect so close at hand. Most of the world only hears tales of miracles, and believes them in some small measure; but the Florentines were about to witness one for themselves. At the very worst, they would see half a miracle: if the monk did not walk unharmed from the flames, they would at least watch him step into them, and know he was consumed at the heart of the fire.
No reasonable soul could doubt the pyre would be lit, and the monks would enter it. There, before their eyes, stretched a platform eight feet broad and twenty yards long, heaped high with fuel in a terrible grove of flame: great dry oak boughs formed the foundation, layered with crackling thorns, and topped with well-anointed tow and rags known to burn bright and fierce in Florentine festival illuminations. The platform began at the corner of the marble terrace before the Old Palace, close to Marzocco, the ancient stone lion whose weathered, frowning face looked down the line of fuel stretching diagonally across the piazza.
Three large armed formations were deployed across the open space: five hundred hired Signoria soldiers stationed before the palace, five hundred of Dolfo Spini’s Compagnacci far across on the opposite side of the piazza, and three hundred armed loyalist citizens under Marco Salviati, Savonarola’s friend, posted in front of Orgagna’s Loggia, where the Franciscans and Dominicans would gather with their two champions. The event had cost vast sums of money and labor, and drawn the attention of the city’s highest dignitaries. There could be no doubt something monumental was about to unfold. Even if both monks were simply burned to ash, that would be a great thing in its own right: God would have spoken plain and clear, that Fra Girolamo was no prophet.
The waiting was almost over. Half the monks had already taken their posts, the half of the Loggia facing the palace filled with their grey cowled mantles, while the far half, partitioned off by rough planks, held nothing but a small altar. The Franciscans had entered and taken their places in utter silence.
Then, from the far side of the piazza, two hundred voices rose in loud, rolling chanting, and the crowd stirred with general satisfaction if not at the hymn itself, then at the proof that the Dominicans had arrived. That thunderous repetition of the prayer *“Let God arise, and let His enemies be scattered”* struck many impartial onlookers as an unseemly boast of confidence, a deliberate attempt to strike dread in their rivals. So too did the flame-colored velvet cope worn by Fra Domenico, who led the procession with a cross in his hand, his simple heart truly exalted in faith, fully sincere in his intention to walk through the flames for the glory of God and Fra Girolamo. Behind him walked Savonarola in a white priest’s vestment, carrying a vessel holding the consecrated Host. He too chanted loudly, his bearing firm and unshakable, and as every eye turned to him—some in anxious hope, some in sharp curiosity, some in raw malice—the second he stepped into the piazza and mounted the Loggia steps to set the Sacrament on the small altar, his face glowed with a bright, unyielding energy that answered every stare.
Few of us escape this quirk of human nature: the false show of confidence that once made us shrink in lonely private dread, when we imagined we would have to perform it, will seize our muscles and move our lips as if it were the easiest thing in the world, once we stand under the pressure of hundreds of expectant eyes and ears. The force of that stimulus on Savonarola can hardly be measured by the experience of ordinary men. Few men ever wield great influence over their fellows without an innate hunger to dominate, a hunger that grows all the more imperious when the tangled compromises of public life twist self-interest into something that looks, even to the man himself, like an unselfish cause. On this day of the Trial by Fire, that doubleness—the core temptation of every public career, for priest, orator, or statesman—burned sharper in Savonarola’s consciousness than it ever had before. He was not fighting impending martyrdom: he was fighting impending ruin.
Beneath his unshakable public front, foreboding pressed on his heart like lead, not only for the uncertain outcome of the trial, but for a secret disaster already done. Two evenings prior, news had arrived that the Florentine courier carrying the Ten’s despatches had been arrested and robbed of all his mail. Savonarola’s sensitive letters were already in the Duke of Milan’s hands, and would soon reach the Pope’s, stoking their rage and giving them full justification to move against him with every extreme measure. That fact was the source of soft, unmalicious satisfaction for Tito Melema, leaning from a window of the Old Palace and looking down at the worn, passion-driven prophet far below. He felt no spite, only the quiet self-congratulation of a man who has just won a game of clever strategy, no effort of brawn or heated temper required. To a brilliant, worldly scholar who understood all the turns of power, the tonsured Fra Girolamo—high-nosed, heavy-lipped, half prophet half opportunist—was no tragic figure. He was an immensely clever friar, who mixed absurd superstitions and deliberate fabrications with strikingly radical ideas about government, a man who never babbled secrets. Tito bore him no more ill will than he bore Saint Dominic. On the contrary, Fra Girolamo’s rise had been a perfect ladder for Tito’s own ascent, one he was about to step off of onto a smooth, stable new footing that suited him perfectly. All his preparations were almost done: one more sun would rise and set, and Tito planned to leave Florence for good. He had worked so hard for months that he felt he had earned the leisure to amuse himself with today’s farce—one the thick-headed Dolfo Spini never could have arranged, not without his quiet scheming.
The chanting did not fade, it swelled to a deafening roar, taken up across every corner of the piazza by the Piagnoni, their red cross badges pinned to their chests, most of them chanting the prayer for the confusion of God’s enemies fully expecting a public, miraculous answer through Fra Domenico. The good friar in his flame-colored cope knelt before the small altar holding the Sacrament, waiting for the call to step toward the pyre.
On the Franciscan side of the Loggia there was no chanting, no bright flame color—only silence, and a sea of grey cowls. They had one advantage: they fielded two champions, Fra Giuliano to face Fra Domenico, while their original challenger, Fra Francesco, reserved his demand for Savonarola himself.
The crowd perched uneasily on their brackets and pillars thought all must be ready now. The chanting could stop, and they would get a clear view of the friars moving toward the platform. But the friars did not move. Pale Franciscan faces peered uneasily over the plank partition at that bright flame-colored cope. It looked evil. It might be enchanted, woven with devil’s magic to produce a false miracle. Their monk might walk out of the fire unharmed, and it would be no work of God at all.
Men began passing back and forth between the Loggia and the Palazzo’s marble terrace, the roar of chanting softened a little as everyone in the far corners of the piazza leaned in to watch. It soon became clear the new movement was not the start of the trial, but a new obstacle to it. The appointed moderators, leading Florentine dignitaries picked from both factions, went in and out of the palace, locked in long debate with the Franciscans. At last, the bright figure of Fra Domenico, conspicuous in his flame-colored robes, was led away toward the palace. The crowd assumed the pyre must already be lit, hidden from their view, and the miracle was about to begin.
They were wrong. The flame-colored cope vanished inside the palace, then another Dominican was fetched away. For a long while, everything dragged on as before: the tiresome, non-miraculous chanting, and Fra Girolamo standing in his white vestment, not moving an inch. At last Fra Domenico emerged again, and returned to his brethren. He had swapped every last piece of clothing with another Dominican, and two Franciscans walked flanking him on either side, to keep him from straying near Savonarola and being bewitched again.
The distant spectators, half forgetting their cramped limbs and empty stomachs, exhaled in anticipation. Fra Domenico would not be entering the fire. It was Fra Girolamo who would offer himself, after all. They would see him move any moment, and if he walked out unharmed, they would have a perfect view of the miracle.
But Fra Girolamo did not step toward the pyre. He only spoke, bold and firm, his tone echoing Elijah’s rebuke to the priests of Baal, demanding the trivial delays cease. Speech, though, is the most infuriating kind of argument for a crowd that cannot hear a word of it, cramped in their posts and empty in their bellies. What need was there for words? If the miracle never started, it must be Fra Girolamo’s fault, who could end all their quarrels by walking into the ready pyre himself, as confidently as he had boasted he would when there was no fuel stacked in sight.
More back and forth, more unheard debate. The afternoon slipped away faster as dark clouds gathered, dimming the light over everything, sending a cold chill through the spectators, hungry in body and restless in spirit. Now the Franciscans declared Fra Domenico could not carry a crucifix into the fire, that he would profane the holy symbol by exposing it to the flames. Savonarola conceded the point after short resistance, scoring one more point for reasonableness, but immediately placed the vessel holding the consecrated Host in Fra Domenico’s hands. The thought that the divine Mystery of the Eucharist might, at the last, avert the fire’s ordinary effects hovered in his mind as a faint possibility, but he counted on a far more certain outcome. As he set the Host in the friar’s palms, he spoke quietly, as if the arrangement had been agreed on from the first:
“Since they will not suffer you to enter with the crucifix, my brother, you will enter carrying the Sacrament alone.”
Fresh horror swept the Franciscan ranks, and fresh unyielding firmness from Savonarola. It was impious presumption, they protested, to carry the Sacrament into fire. If it burned, the weak and unlearned would be scandalized beyond repair.
“Not at all,” Savonarola countered. “Even if it burns, only the outward accidents of bread will be consumed. The divine Substance will remain untouched.”
It was an argument that could be argued until sunset and still remain as elastic as ever. No one could propose settling it by proceeding with the trial, for it was a purely preliminary quarrel. All the two sides had to do was stay firm: the Franciscans refuse to let the Host enter the fire, and Fra Domenico refuse to enter without it.
The clouds grew darker, the air colder. The crowd missed the distraction of the chanting now that it had given way to silent, unseen debate, and the confused murmur of talk rippling across the piazza showed that expectation was fraying fast, curdling into a prickling irritation that nothing decisive would ever happen. Individual shouts broke out, then more frequent, rising in a crescendo of scorn.
“Light the fire and drive them in!”
“Let’s get a smell of roast friar—we want our dinner!”
“Come on, Prophet! Show us something before the twenty-four hours are over!”
“Tell us your last vision, eh? I bet you’ve got a dozen little ones stuffed inside your tunic, small change for a miracle!”
“Hey, Frate, where’d you go? Don’t waste all that good fuel!”
The same back and forth movement between the Loggia and the palace, the same slow debate as unintelligible to the crowd as ants touching antennae for no visible purpose but to wander back and forth. The crowd had no trouble guessing what the unheard arguments meant: it was Fra Girolamo who was holding the trial back, hanging back while everyone else begged him to step forward.
The shouts blurred into a great uproar, mixed with the clash of metal and the thunder of trampling feet. The crowd’s taunts had stoked old ambitions in Dolfo Spini and his Compagnacci. This was a perfect chance, they thought, to end all Florence’s troubles once and for all by seizing the arch-hypocrite himself. The band of armed men charged hard for the Loggia, shoving ordinary citizens aside or driving them back against the line of Signoria soldiers posted in front of the palace.
The monks and flustered magistrates froze, all other business forgotten, waiting to see how the clash would end. But Salviati’s loyal citizen guards held the Loggia, and the Signoria soldiers rushed to help repulse the charge, driving the Compagnacci back toward the Tetto de’ Pisani. In that same moment of utter chaos, the blackness of the sky deepened, and the scattered raindrops that had been spattering down moments earlier burst into a torrential downpour, dousing the stacked fuel on the platform, sending streams of water running off its planks, soaking every last weary, hungry spectator through to the skin, driving their disgust and rage inwards to ferment in the damp chill.
Everyone knew at once the Trial by Fire would never happen. The Signoria seized on the rain as a perfect, obvious excuse, no flimsy pretext needed, to declare both factions free to go home. It was the exact outcome Savonarola had planned for, the one he had counted on—but no one could call him glad. As the rain splashed at the edge of the Loggia, sending spray over the altar, over every cowl and face, the friar knew the demand that he enter the fire was gone for good. But he knew it with a certainty as unshakable as the damp chill that had settled in his bones: his enemies had won. His honor was not saved. He would have to make his way back to San Marco through a crowd raging against him, and many of the friends who would once have defended him with their lives would now turn away.
He had said he was willing to die for the work of his life, and he had not lied. But to die in dishonor, to be held up to scorn as a hypocrite and a false prophet? O God, that is no martyrdom. That is blotting out a whole life spent in protest against wrongdoing. Let him die for the good he has done, not for his failure.
The rain slowed and stopped, and light broke through the clouds to fall on Savonarola as he left the Loggia surrounded by his requested Signoria guard, still walking as he had come, the Sacrament in his hands. But there was no glory in that light, no smile of heaven. It was the plain, impartial light that shines on all things patiently, revealing every turn of slow ripening history without judging, only showing what is. He heard no blessings, no tones of pity, only taunts and threats. He knew this was only a foretaste of the bitterness to come, but his courage rose against the storm of moral attack, and he showed no sign of dismay.
“Very well parried, Frate,” Tito murmured, watching him descend the Loggia steps from his palace window. “But I fear your time in Florence is at an end. What say you, my Niccolò?”
Machiavelli shrugged, a faint melancholy on his face. “It is a pity his falsehoods were not all wise ones. The times were so far on his side on matters of Church reform, he might have done something truly great.”
