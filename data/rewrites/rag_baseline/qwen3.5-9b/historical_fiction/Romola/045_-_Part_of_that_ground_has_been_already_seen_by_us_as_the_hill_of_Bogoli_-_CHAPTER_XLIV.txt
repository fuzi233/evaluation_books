The crowd surged past, dissolving into the throng, leaving Romola to descend swiftly onto the street. She hurried toward the steps of San Stefano, her movements driven by a desperate need for order amidst the chaos. There, in the shadow of the church portal, stood Baldassarre. Cecco had been swept up with the general flow, but Baldassarre remained solitary, the silver horn-cup clutched firmly in his hand.

A profound transformation marked his visage. The vacant, dreamlike stare of a mind returning from the abyss had vanished, replaced by a ferocity that struck Romola with the intensity of lightning. As she approached and addressed him, the gaze locked onto her—a look of caged fury fixated on its potential prey, seeing her pass safely beyond the invisible bars of his rage. Romola flinched instinctively at the intensity, yet her mind raced instantly to a singular conclusion: he had seen Tito. The hatred radiating from him grated against her nerves, yet it sparked a flickering ember of hope. Perhaps this man *was* the criminal; perhaps Tito had been innocent of the charge that haunted her. If she could force a confrontation, if she could drive Tito face-to-face with this accuser, she might finally find peace.

"If you will come with me," she said, her voice steady despite the turmoil, "I can offer you shelter and sustenance until you regain your strength. Will you come?"

"Yes," Baldassarre replied, his voice rough. "I shall be glad to get my strength." He repeated the phrase, muttering it as if rehearsing a mantra for himself rather than addressing her directly.

"Come!" she commanded, stepping beside him. She guided him away from the main thoroughfare, choosing the route along the Arno toward the Ponte Rubaconte, a quieter passage suited to their current state.

"I suspect you are not a Florentine," she remarked as they crossed onto the bridge.

He scanned the surroundings, silent and wary. The fog of confusion and physical debilitation had thickened around him, clouding his judgment. Yet, Romola watched him intently, her gentle eyes holding a quiet authority that eventually compelled a response.

"No," he answered cautiously. "I am no Florentine. I am a lonely man."

She noted his reticence, fearing that further questioning might trigger a desire to flee. Glancing at him periodically, her mind worked frantically, extinguishing the faint hope that her husband was blameless. If this stranger harbored grievances, where was the source of such dread and secrecy? They walked in silence until they reached the entrance of the Via de' Bardi. Suddenly, Baldassarre halted, turning his head with a jerky motion as if an electric shock had coursed through him. Moments later, Romola stopped at the threshold of the courtyard, facing him.

"Ah!" he exclaimed, cutting her off before she could speak. "You are his wife."

"Whose wife?" Romola asked, her voice trembling slightly.

The name died in Baldassarre's throat. The overwhelming image of Tito seemed to paralyze his tongue, expelling any verbal sign. He offered no reply, staring at her with a fixed, unnerving intensity.

Romola pushed the door wide, revealing a courtyard strewn with straw. Upon it lay four or five sick individuals, while tiny, pale children crawled or sat nearby, gnawing on straws and emitting soft, gurgling sounds.

"If you will come in," Romola pleaded, her voice breaking, "I will find you a comfortable spot and bring you more food."

"No," Baldassarre refused flatly. "I will not come in."

Yet he remained frozen, paralyzed by the crushing weight of conflicting impressions, unable to chart a course through the storm of his mind.

"Is there nothing I can do for you?" Romola insisted. "Let me give you coin so you may purchase food. Supplies will be more abundant shortly."

She reached into her *scarsella*, withdrawing her palm to reveal several *grossi*. Deliberately, she offered more than she would have to any other beggar in his condition.

Baldassarre stared at the coins for a long moment, then nodded. "Yes, I will take them."

She poured the coins into his hand; he gripped them tightly, as if anchoring himself to the earth.

"Tell me," Romola urged, her tone nearly supplicating. "What shall you—"

Baldassarre turned away abruptly, resuming his march toward the bridge. Crossing it, he proceeded straight up the Via del Fosso, stopping abruptly before the shop of Niccolò Caparra. Without hesitation, he stepped inside, as though the weapon rack had been his sole destination.

Niccolò was absent, engaged elsewhere in the armorers' procession, leaving only a single apprentice behind. The shop bristled with weaponry, and Baldassarre's eyes zeroed in on what he craved far more than bread. Though Niccolò would surely refuse to sell arms to a man bearing the marks of imprisonment, the apprentice—unobservant and lacking scruple—sold a sharp hunting-knife for three *grossi* without a second thought.

It was a compact blade, easily concealed within the folds of Baldassarre's tunic. Clutching it, he felt a surge of renewed vigor. That sharp edge could lend lethality to the thrust of an aging arm; at the very least, it was a companion, a power allied with his cause. It might shatter against plate mail, but was the armor guaranteed to remain forever? During those long months of incarceration, baseness had perhaps grown complacent and secure. This knife had been purchased with the traitor's own money—that was justice served.

Before accepting the funds, he had known exactly what he intended: to buy a weapon. Yes, and ideally, food as well. Food to fuel the arm that would wield the steel, food to sustain the body that served as the temple of vengeance. Once he had eaten enough, he would possess the clarity to think and act—to devise a method of concealment, ensuring Tito did not drag him back into custody once more.

With this plan forming in his mind, Baldassarre navigated the narrowest alleys, purchasing meat and bread. He settled beneath the first available loggia to eat. The bells began to toll, their joyful peals swelling louder and louder, vibrating through the air and seemingly against him. To Baldassarre, they represented nothing but the roar of a hostile world arrayed against his quest.

Romola watched him until he vanished around the corner into the Piazza de' Mozzi. Half relieved by his departure, half chastised for failing to pursue the truth with greater resolve, she wrestled with her emotions. Could she have done otherwise if the truth involved a painful secret regarding her husband? Was a wife not permitted to desire ignorance of her spouse's transgressions, especially when she alone bore the duty of remaining silent and non-confrontational? These thoughts tangled too deeply within the intricate fibers of her grief to be untangled now, in her exhaustion.

It was a time for rejoicing, for aid had arrived in Florence. Turning back into the courtyard, she prepared to share the news with her patients lying on their straw beds. Closing the door to muffle the clamor of the bells, she threw back the black drapery covering her head so the women might see her clearly. Standing amidst them, she announced that grain was arriving and that the bells rang in jubilation.

The sick propped themselves up to listen. Children trotted and crawled toward her, tugging at her black skirts with impatient eagerness to be close to her face. Weary as she was, Romola yielded to them, sitting down on the straw. The pale faces peered into her basket, pulling at her hair, while the feeble voices around her chorused, "The Holy Virgin be praised!"

"It was the procession!" someone cried. "The Mother of God has shown pity to us!"

Finally, rising from the heap of straw, too drained to muster a smile, she turned up the stone steps. "I will return by-and-by to bring you your dinner."

"Bless you, Madonna! Bless you!" the faint chorus responded, echoing the praise they had offered moments earlier to the unseen divine presence.

Romola cherished that music profoundly. Unlike some women who found comfort in the mere mechanics of nursing the sick and clothing the destitute, her early training had kept her aloof from such domestic labors. Had she not infused them with the inspiration of her deepest convictions, they would have been tedious burdens. Yet, they had become the only unshakable anchor for her mind, the narrow path where light pierced through the darkness.

Even as the chasm between herself and Tito widened, fueled by her futile attempts to bridge it through submission, doubts gnawed at her. Did the bond she strove to honor prove false? When she withdrew from her confessor, Fra Salvestro, or from the circle of Savonarola's disciples, she often felt a sickness at their narrow-mindedness, reacting with an impulsive resurgence of her old contempt for their superstitions. Yet, in her acts of compassion toward her fellow citizens, she found a solid footing. Whatever else cast doubt, her service to others confirmed Fra Girolamo's wisdom in calling her back.

According to his unforgettable words, her place had not been empty; it had been filled with her love and her labor. Florence needed her. The heavier her personal sorrow pressed down, the greater her gladness in recalling those two long years spent lightening the burdens of others.
