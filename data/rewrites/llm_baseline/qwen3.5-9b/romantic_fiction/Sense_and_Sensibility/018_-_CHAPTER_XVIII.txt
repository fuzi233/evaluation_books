Elinor watched her brother-in-law with deep concern, noting the heavy cloud that had settled over his spirits. His recent visit offered her only a fleeting comfort, especially since his own apparent enjoyment seemed so muted. It was painfully clear that he was unhappy. She desperately wished it were just as obvious that he still held for her the same affection she had once believed she inspired; yet, up until now, the persistence of his favor had remained dangerously uncertain. Furthermore, his reserved demeanor toward her constantly contradicted any hint of animation he might have shown moments prior.

The following morning, Edward joined Elinor and Marianne in the breakfast room before the rest of the household had risen. Marianne, ever eager to foster their happiness, quickly excused herself to leave them alone. However, barely halfway up the stairs, she heard the parlor door open. Turning around, she was startled to see Edward emerging. "I'm off to the village to check on my horses," he explained, "since you aren't ready for breakfast yet. I'll be right back."

When Edward returned, he spoke with renewed enthusiasm about the surrounding countryside. During his walk to the village, he had viewed various parts of the valley from advantageous angles, and the village itself, situated much higher than their cottage, provided a sweeping panorama that delighted him immensely. This topic naturally captured Marianne's attention. She began to describe her own awe of the scenery and questioned him in detail about the specific features that had struck him most.

Edward, however, cut her short. "You mustn't inquire too closely, Marianne," he warned. "Remember, I possess no knowledge of the picturesque. If we delve into specifics, I shall surely offend you with my ignorance and lack of taste. I tend to call hills 'steep' when they ought to be described as 'bold'; I label surfaces 'strange and uncouth' when they should be 'irregular and rugged'; and I note distant objects as merely 'out of sight,' whereas they should appear indistinct through the soft haze of a misty atmosphere. Please accept whatever admiration I can honestly offer. To me, it is simply a very fine country—the hills are steep, the woods brim with excellent timber, and the valley looks cozy and secure, dotted with rich meadows and neat farmhouses. It perfectly matches my definition of a beautiful place because it combines aesthetic appeal with practicality. And I daresay it is picturesque too, simply because you admire it. I can easily imagine it teeming with rocks, promontories, grey moss, and brushwood, though none of that registers with me. I know nothing of the picturesque."

"I fear it is indeed too true," Marianne admitted. "But why boast of such limitations?"

"Perhaps," Elinor interjected gently, "to avoid one form of pretension, Edward has fallen into another. Believing that many people feign a deeper appreciation for nature's beauties than they truly feel, and disgusted by such hypocrisy, he adopts an attitude of greater indifference and less discernment than he actually possesses. He is fastidious and has cultivated his own affectation."

"That is entirely accurate," Marianne agreed. "Admiration of landscape has devolved into mere jargon. Everyone pretends to feel and attempts to describe things with the refined taste of the man who first defined 'picturesque beauty.' I detest jargon of every sort, and often I keep my feelings to myself because I cannot find a language to express them that isn't worn, hackneyed, and devoid of meaning."

"I am convinced," Edward stated firmly, "that you genuinely feel all the delight in a fine prospect that you claim to feel. In return, I ask your sister to admit that I feel no more than I profess. I enjoy a fine view, but not according to picturesque principles. I dislike crooked, twisted, or blasted trees; I prefer them tall, straight, and flourishing. I have no fondness for ruined, tattered cottages. I am not enamored with nettles, thistles, or heather blossoms. A snug farmhouse brings me far more pleasure than a watchtower, and a group of tidy, happy villagers pleases me infinitely more than the finest bandits in the world."

Marianne stared at Edward with astonishment, while looking at her sister with profound pity. Elinor, meanwhile, only smiled. They did not pursue the subject further, and Marianne fell into a thoughtful silence until a new object caught her eye. Sitting beside Edward, she took her tea from Mrs. Dashwood. As Edward reached across to pass the cup, his hand brushed hers, revealing a ring on one of his fingers. At its center lay a small plait of hair, making it conspicuously visible.

"I've never seen you wearing a ring before, Edward!" Marianne exclaimed. "Is that Fanny's hair? I recall her promising to give you some. Though I suppose I expected her hair to be darker."

Marianne spoke impulsively, voicing exactly what she felt. Yet, upon seeing the pain her thoughtlessness caused Edward, her own regret was matched only by his distress. He flushed deeply, cast a quick, meaningful glance at Elinor, and replied, "Yes, it is my sister's hair. The setting tends to alter the shade slightly, you know."

Elinor had met his gaze and appeared equally conscious. Instantly, she realized the hair belonged to her. The only divergence between their thoughts was that where Marianne assumed it was a voluntary gift from her sister, Elinor knew it must have been obtained through some theft or deception unknown to her. Nevertheless, she was not in the mood to consider it an insult. Pretending to ignore the incident, she immediately changed the subject, but inwardly she resolved to seize every opportunity to examine the hair and confirm beyond doubt that its color matched her own exactly.

Edward's embarrassment lingered for some time, eventually settling into a profound preoccupation. He remained unusually grave throughout the entire morning. Marianne chided herself harshly for her remark, though her self-forgiveness might have been quicker had she understood how little offense it had truly caused her sister.

Before midday, they were visited by Sir John and Mrs. Jennings. Having heard news of a gentleman's arrival at the cottage, they came to inspect the guest. With the aid of his mother-in-law, Sir John was not long in deducing that the name Ferrars began with an "F." This discovery laid the groundwork for a future mine of teasing directed at the devoted Elinor—a jest that, had they known her longer, they might have launched immediately. For now, however, she gleaned only the extent of their insight from significant glances exchanged between them.

Sir John never failed to invite the Dashwoods either to dinner at the Park the following day or for tea that evening. On this occasion, wishing to ensure his guest's amusement, he proposed engaging them for both events.

"You *must* join us for tea tonight," he insisted, "for we shall be quite alone. And tomorrow, you must absolutely dine with us, as we shall be hosting a large party."

Mrs. Jennings reinforced the invitation. "And who knows but you might raise a dance," she added. "That would certainly tempt *you*, Miss Marianne."

"A dance!" Marianne cried out. "Impossible! Who is there to dance?"

"Why, yourself, of course, along with the Careys and the Whitakers!" Sir John replied cheerfully. "What! Did you think nobody could dance because a certain person, let's say nameless, has departed?"

"I wish with all my soul," Sir John declared, "that Willoughby were among us again."

These words, coupled with Marianne's sudden blush, sparked fresh suspicions in Edward's mind. "And who is Willoughby?" he asked quietly to Miss Dashwood, to whom he happened to be seated. She offered him a brief answer, but Marianne's face was far more expressive. Edward observed enough to understand not only the implications of the others' remarks but also the meanings behind Marianne's earlier puzzling expressions. Once their visitors departed, he moved immediately to stand beside her and whispered, "I've been guessing. Shall I share my theory?"

"What do you mean?"

"Shall I tell you?"

"Certainly."

"Well then," he said, "I guess Mr. Willoughby hunts."

Marianne was taken aback and flustered, yet she couldn't help but smile at the quiet wit of his manner. After a brief pause, she said, "Oh, Edward! How can you?—But perhaps the time will come, I hope... I am sure you will like him."

"I do not doubt it," he replied, somewhat surprised by her earnestness and warmth. Had he not assumed it was merely a lighthearted joke intended to entertain her acquaintances generally, based on nothing more substantial than a vague rumor regarding Mr. Willoughby and herself, he would never have dared to mention it.
