Edith moved through the house as if her slippers were made of felt. Even Sholto, all limbs and bright voice, was checked with a finger to his lip, for the doors of the drawing-room had been shut since morning, and the very hush of the place seemed pressed into service, as though a sudden clatter might break whatever was being tried—patiently, stubbornly—within.

It was the kind of London quiet that came only when one tried to manufacture it: the faint tick of a clock, the soft scuff of a servant’s step held carefully back, and, beyond all, the distant, ceaseless grind and rumour of the city that could never wholly be barred out. Edith listened as one listens for milk to rise—half tender, half anxious—measuring time by sound.

Two o’clock chimed; still the doors were closed.

Then, at last, a man’s footstep—quick, decisive—ran down the stairs. Edith, who had been poised like a cook waiting for the exact instant to lift a lid, peeped out.

“Well, Henry?” she asked, with the look of a woman trying to read a pudding by its steam.

“Well!” he returned, rather shortly, as if the word were a snapped thread.

“Come in to lunch!”

“No, thank you, I can’t. I’ve lost too much time here already.”

“Then it’s not all settled?” Edith’s voice sank with an almost childish disappointment; she had pictured the whole thing as neatly arranged as a nursery tray—Margaret close at hand, the children safe under her eye, life made smooth with a single domestic adjustment.

“No! not at all. It never will be settled, if the ‘it’ is what I conjecture you mean. That will never be, Edith, so give up thinking about it.”

“But it would be so nice for us all,” she pleaded. “I should always feel comfortable about the children, if I had Margaret settled down near to me. As it is, I am always afraid of her going off to Cadiz.”

“I will try, when I marry, to look out for a young lady who has a knowledge of the management of children. That is all I can do. Miss Hale will not have me. And I shall not ask her.”

Edith’s mouth puckered, as if the air had turned sour. “Then what have you been talking about?”

“A thousand things you would not understand: investments, and leases, and value of land.”

“Oh, go away if that’s all. You and she will be unbearably stupid, if you’ve been talking all this time about such weary things.”

“Very well. I’m coming again to-morrow, and bringing Mr. Thornton with me, to have some more talk with Miss Hale.”

“Mr. Thornton! What has he to do with it?”

“He is Miss Hale’s tenant,” said Mr. Lennox, already turning away, “and he wishes to give up his lease.”

“Oh! very well. I can’t understand details, so don’t give them me.”

“The only detail I want you to understand is, to let us have the back drawing-room undisturbed, as it was to-day. In general, the children and servants are so in and out, that I can never get any business satisfactorily explained; and the arrangements we have to make to-morrow are of importance.”

Edith, who could marshal a household dinner and time the serving of a joint to the minute, yielded with a little shrug; yet she resented business as she resented soot—an intrusion that dirtied the edges of what ought to be pleasant.

No one ever knew why Mr. Lennox did not keep to his appointment the following day. His absence lay on the house like a draught under a door—unaccountable, and making everyone aware of an unseen gap.

Mr. Thornton came true to his time.

He was shown in and made to wait; the very hour seemed to stretch. In the front rooms there were the muffled stirrings of the children—sound held back, as promised—yet even restraint had its own noise, like a spoon kept from clinking against china. From outside, the city pressed on: carriage wheels, a distant call, the low, incessant undertone of London breathing through brick and glass. One could not escape the world’s din; one could only choose, for a while, what one admitted close to the heart.

At length Margaret came in.

She looked very white and anxious, as if she had walked from one climate into another without time to put on the proper clothing. Her hands held papers—law papers, statements of accounts—things that had no scent of garden or kitchen, no comforting warmth; they rustled dryly, like husks.

She began hurriedly, as if speed might carry her past the painful place. “I am so sorry Mr. Lennox is not here,—he could have done it so much better than I can. He is my adviser in this—”

“I am sorry that I came, if it troubles you,” said Mr. Thornton. There was something subdued and careful in him—like a man speaking low for fear of waking someone sick. “Shall I go to Mr. Lennox’s chambers and try and find him?”

“No, thank you.” Margaret drew her breath, as if making herself steady. “I wanted to tell you, how grieved I was to find that I am to lose you as a tenant. But, Mr. Lennox says, things are sure to brighten—”

“Mr. Lennox knows little about it,” said Mr. Thornton quietly.

His quiet had weight; it fell into the room like a heavy knife set down on wood. In it Margaret heard not only his words, but all the other sounds that had made him—looms, engine-throb, the harsh, wearing clamour of a mill yard. That noise—so different from Helstone’s bees and church bells—had become part of his very speech. It was as if the industrial world had taught men a new dialect of endurance, sharp-edged, economical, hard to translate into the softer idioms of drawing-rooms.

“Happy and fortunate in all a man cares for,” he went on, “he does not understand what it is to find oneself no longer young—yet thrown back to the starting-point which requires the hopeful energy of youth—to feel one half of life gone, and nothing done—nothing remaining of wasted opportunity, but the bitter recollection that it has been. Miss Hale, I would rather not hear Mr. Lennox’s opinion of my affairs. Those who are happy and successful themselves are too apt to make light of the misfortunes of others.”

“You are unjust,” Margaret said gently, and the gentleness was not weakness; it was like warm bread laid beside a bitter dish, not denying the bitterness, but offering something that might let one bear it. “Mr. Lennox has only spoken of the great probability which he believes there to be of your redeeming—your more than redeeming what you have lost—don’t speak till I have ended—pray don’t.”

She collected herself, and with that quick, practical movement—so unlike her old dreamy Helstone pauses—she began turning over the papers she had brought, searching as if the right sheet might serve as a shield. Her fingers trembled; the dry pages rasped softly, a small and nervous sound in the large quiet room.

“Oh! here it is!” she said at last; and the words came out in a rush, like a confession forced through. “And—he drew me out a proposal—I wish he was here to explain it—showing that if you would take some money of mine, eighteen hundred and fifty-seven pounds, lying just at this moment unused in the bank, and bringing me in only two and a half per cent.—you could pay me much better interest, and might go on working Marlborough Mills.”

As she spoke, her voice cleared and steadied, as if the very act of naming figures and terms—interest, lease, security—gave her something firm beneath her feet. She was eager—desperately eager—to keep this in the language of business, to make it sound like a sensible transaction, a careful recipe with measured quantities, no more.

Mr. Thornton did not speak.

Margaret bent again to the papers, hunting for another sheet on which Mr. Lennox had written down the proposals for security, anxious to set it all forth properly, as if exactness might protect them both from the dangerous heat that was gathering unseen.

And then, when he spoke, her heart seemed to stop, arrested not by his words alone but by the tone—hoarse, trembling, thick with feeling held too long under restraint.

“Margaret!”

For an instant she looked up; then, as if light were too much, she tried to veil her luminous eyes by dropping her forehead into her hands.

Again he stepped nearer. “Margaret!”

Still lower went the head; more closely hidden was the face, nearly resting on the table, as if she sought refuge in the plain wooden certainty of it. The rustle of paper ceased; the room listened.

He came close. He knelt by her side to bring his face level with her ear, and whispered—panted the words, as if they had to be forced through some narrow passage in him.

“Take care.—If you do not speak—I shall claim you as my own in some strange presumptuous way.—Send me away at once, if I must go;—Margaret!—”

At that third utterance of her name she turned her face—still covered with her small white hands—towards him, and laid it on his shoulder, hiding it there. The contact was answer enough. It was too delicious to feel her soft cheek against his to make him wish, just then, to see blushes or the truth of her eyes.

He clasped her close.

They kept silence—an intimate quiet, not the strained hush Edith had tried to impose, but the profound pause in which two lives find their own measure. Outside, London continued its ceaseless sound; inside, there was only the faint stir of breath, the softest creak of cloth as he held her, the beating that both of them felt and yet could not name in any common vocabulary. Some experiences refused translation: they could be lived, but not rendered into the tidy words one used for ordinary arrangements.

At length Margaret murmured, brokenly, as if her humility were the only safe speech. “Oh, Mr. Thornton, I am not good enough!”

“Not good enough!” His voice shook. “Don’t mock my own deep feeling of unworthiness.”

After a minute or two he gently disengaged her hands from her face, as tenderly as if he were unwrapping something precious, and then—quietly, reverently—he laid her arms as they had once before been placed: up, around him, as if to protect him.

“Do you remember, love?” he murmured.

“And how I requited you with my insolence the next day?” His words carried the old sting; he did not seek to sweeten the past by pretending it had not been bitter.

“I remember how wrongly I spoke to you,—that is all,” she said; and her dialect, the plain sincerity of her north-and-south heart, made no room for self-justification.

“Look here! Lift up your head. I have something to show you!”

She slowly faced him, glowing with beautiful shame, as if the blood in her cheeks were the only language she could safely use.

“Do you know these roses?” he said, drawing out his pocket-book.

Within it were treasured some dead flowers—pressed, faded, yet kept with a devotion that made them live again. They were brittle as old parchment, and had the faint, ghostly scent of something once fresh: a trace that rose when he opened the fold, like memory stirred.

“No!” Margaret replied, with innocent curiosity. “Did I give them to you?”

“No! Vanity; you did not.” A soft humour touched him. “You may have worn sister roses very probably.”

She looked at them, and for a moment did not understand; then her face altered, and a little smile came—small but full, like the first sweetness after a long fast.

“They are from Helstone, are they not? I know the deep indentations round the leaves.” She leaned closer, as if sight and touch might do what words could not—recognize what belonged. “Oh! have you been there? When were you there?”

“I wanted to see the place where Margaret grew to what she is,” he said—his voice low, as if speaking of a sacred kitchen or a guarded seed-bed—“even at the worst time of all, when I had no hope of ever calling her mine. I went there on my return from Havre.”

The name of the place brought with it its own sound-world: the remembered hush of lanes, the talk of birds, the soft rural cadences that seemed, now, like a language endangered by distance and the louder, harsher tongue of mills and markets. Yet here was proof that the old music had not been wholly silenced; he had gone to listen for it, and had carried away these pressed petals as if they were a script.

“You must give them to me,” Margaret said, and reached to take them with gentle violence—like a woman claiming back a family receipt written in a hand she thought lost.

“Very well,” he said, yielding them into her keeping. “Only you must pay me for them!”

She held the roses as if they warmed her. After some time of delicious silence—the kind that tastes sweet on the tongue without being spoken—she whispered, with sudden practical fear slipping in like cold air: “How shall I ever tell Aunt Shaw?”

“Let me speak to her.”

“Oh, no! I owe to her,—but what will she say?”

“I can guess.” He smiled, and the smile had both tenderness and defiance in it. “Her first exclamation will be, ‘That man!’”

“Hush!” said Margaret, half laughing, half frightened at her own laughter, “or I shall try and show you your mother’s indignant tones as she says, ‘That woman!’”
