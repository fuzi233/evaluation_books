In the sole comfortably appointed chamber within the offices of *The Record*, the telephone upon Sir James Molloy's desk emitted a sharp buzzing sound. Sir James made a dismissive gesture with his pen, prompting Mr. Silver, his secretary, to abandon his current task and approach the instrument.

"Who is calling?" he asked.

A muffled exchange followed. "Who?... I can't hear you clearly.... Oh, it's Mr. Bunner, is it?... Yes, but... I know, but he is dreadfully occupied this afternoon. Can't you... Oh, truly? Well, in that event—please hold on for a moment, will you?"

Mr. Silver held the receiver to Sir James's ear. "It is Calvin Bunner, Sigsbee Manderson's right-hand man," he stated succinctly. "He demands to speak to you directly. He claims it is the most grave piece of intelligence. He is calling from the residence down by Bishopsbridge, so clarity is essential."

Sir James regarded the telephone with neither warmth nor curiosity, lifting the receiver. "Well?" he commanded in his resonant tone, and listened.

"Yes," he replied shortly.

Moments later, Mr. Silver, watching him with eager anticipation, witnessed a flash of sheer astonishment and horror cross the journalist's face.

"Good heavens!" Sir James whispered. Clutching the receiver, he slowly rose to his feet, his ear pressed firmly against the mouthpiece. At intervals, he merely repeated, "Yes."

Presently, glancing at the clock as he listened, he leaned forward and spoke rapidly to Mr. Silver over the transmitter. "Go and fetch Figgis and young Williams immediately. Make haste."

Mr. Silver darted from the room.

Sir James was a towering figure, a robust and shrewd Irishman of fifty, characterized by a swarthy complexion and a thick black moustache. A man of tireless business acumen, he was widely recognized in the world he navigated with a half-cynical mastery typical of his race. Yet, he possessed no trace of the charlatan; he revealed no mysteries and feigned no expertise, piercing through pretense in others with instant insight. While his handsome, well-bred, and immaculate attire often bore a slightly sinister edge when anger or intense focus etched lines around his eyes and brow, beneath such restraint lay the most cordial of natures. As managing director of the conglomerate owning both the mighty morning paper, *The Record*, and the indispensable evening sheet, *The Sun* (whose offices lay across the street), he also served as editor-in-chief of *The Record*. Over the years, he had assembled a staff of varied capability throughout the nation. His guiding principle was clear: where favors could not be secured, one must strive for excellence through solid merit; consequently, he utilized both strategies extensively. He commanded a respect from his employees that was rare in a profession notoriously hostile to sentiments of reverence.

"You are certain that is all?" Sir James asked after several minutes of earnest inquiry. "And how long has this been known?... Yes, naturally, the police are aware. But the servants? Surely the news has spread throughout the area by now.... Very well, we shall attempt to verify it.... Listen, Bunner, I am infinitely indebted to you for this information. I owe you a significant favor. You know I keep my word. Come visit me the first day you arrive in town.... Understood? Now I must act upon your news. Goodbye."

Sir James hung up the receiver, snatched a railway timetable from the rack beside him, and consulted it rapidly. With a decisive toss of the document onto the desk, he awaited Mr. Silver's return, accompanied by a stern-faced man wearing spectacles and a youth with an alert gaze.

"I require you to record specific details, Figgis," Sir James instructed, banishing any visible traces of agitation and adopting a rapid, composed demeanor. "Once transcribed, format them immediately for a special edition of *The Sun*."

The hard-featured man nodded, glanced at the clock indicating a few minutes past three, retrieved his notebook, and pulled a chair to the large writing desk.

"Silver," Sir James continued, "instruct Jones to wire our local correspondent with extreme urgency. Command him to cease all other duties and descend to Marlstone immediately. Do not include the reason in the telegram. Absolutely no superfluous words regarding this news must appear until *The Sun* hits the streets with the full report—you all comprehend this. Williams, take a shortcut and inform Mr. Anthony to prepare himself for a two-column lead story that will shake the foundations of the town. Simply tell him he must implement every precaution possible to secure a scoop. Inform him that Figgis will arrive in five minutes with the facts, and advise him to allow Figgis to draft the narrative in his private quarters. On your way back, request that Miss Morgan join me here immediately, and instruct the switchboard operators to attempt connecting me with Mr. Trent. Upon concluding your meeting with Mr. Anthony, return here and await further orders."

The alert-eyed youth vanished with the speed of a ghost.

Turning instantly to Mr. Figgis, whose pencil hovered over his pad, Sir James began. "Sigsbee Manderson has been murdered," he announced quickly and clearly, pacing the floor with his hands clasped behind his back.

Mr. Figgis scrawled a line of shorthand with an emotional detachment akin to someone reporting pleasant weather—the standard posture of his trade.

"He and his wife, along with two secretaries, have resided at a property named White Gables, located in Marlstone near Bishopsbridge, for the past fortnight. He purchased the estate four years prior. Both Mr. and Mrs. Manderson have spent portions of each summer there. Last night, he retired to bed around half-past eleven, as per custom. No one knows when he departed the premises. He was not discovered missing until this morning. Approximately ten o'clock, a gardener found his body. It lay near a shed in the grounds. He had been shot in the head, specifically through the left eye. Death was undoubtedly instantaneous. The body showed no signs of robbery, though markings on the wrists indicated a violent struggle had occurred. Dr. Stock of Marlstone was summoned immediately and will perform the autopsy. Police from Bishopsbridge arrived promptly but remain reticent; however, it is believed they possess absolutely no leads regarding the identity of the killer. That covers the essentials, Figgis. Mr. Anthony awaits you. I must now contact him to finalize arrangements."

Mr. Figgis looked up. "One of the most capable detectives at Scotland Yard has been assigned to the case," he noted. "That is a safe assumption."

"If you wish," Sir James conceded.

"And Mrs. Manderson? Was she present?"

"Yes. How does she fare?"

"She appears utterly devastated by the shock," the reporter suggested, "and refuses to see anyone. Pure human interest angle."

"I would omit that detail, Mr. Figgis," a calm voice interjected.

It belonged to Miss Morgan, a pale yet graceful woman who had appeared silently during the dictation process.

"I have spoken with Mrs. Manderson," she continued, addressing Sir James. "She appears entirely healthy and lucid. Has her husband been murdered? I doubt the shock would render her catatonic. She is far more likely to be doing everything in her power to assist the police."

"A touch of your own unique style, then, Miss Morgan," Sir James remarked, offering a fleeting smile. Her unshakeable efficiency was a proverb within the office.

"Leave it be, Figgis. Get moving!" she commanded gently. Turning to Sir James, she added, "Now, Madam, I presume you understand what is required."

"Our biography of Manderson is remarkably current," Miss Morgan replied, lowering her dark lashes as she assessed the situation. "I reviewed it only a few months ago; it is practically ready for tomorrow's publication. I believe *The Sun* would benefit from utilizing the sketch of his life written approximately two years ago, when he traveled to Berlin to resolve the potash dispute. I recall it was an excellent piece, and they likely cannot expand upon it significantly. Regarding our own paper, we certainly possess a vast array of clippings, though largely trivial material. The sub-editors will receive them upon their arrival. Furthermore, we hold two high-quality portraits that are our exclusive property; the superior one is a drawing created by Mr. Trent when they shared a voyage together. It surpasses any available photograph, although the public often prefers a mediocre image to a fine illustration. I shall dispatch them to you immediately, allowing you to select whichever suits best. From my perspective, *The Record* is well-positioned relative to the unfolding events, save for the fact that you will be unable to deploy a special correspondent in time to contribute to tomorrow's issue."

Sir James sighed profoundly. "What utility do we serve, anyway?" he queried dejectedly of Mr. Silver, who had returned to his desk.

"She memorizes the Bradshaw timetable," Miss Morgan noted with patient amusement, adjusting her cuffs.

"Is there anything else?" she asked as the telephone bell rang sharply.

"Yes, one item," Sir James replied, seizing the receiver. "I need you to commit a significant error sometime, Miss Morgan—an eternal blunder—simply to give us a chance to recover our dignity."

She allowed herself a fraction of a charming smile before departing.
