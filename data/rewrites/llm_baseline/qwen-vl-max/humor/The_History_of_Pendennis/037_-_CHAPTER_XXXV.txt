On the appointed day, our two friends—Pen and Warrington—arrived at Mr. Bungay’s residence in Paternoster Row, not through the bustling front entrance where booksellers’ boys emerged like overburdened ants with sacks full of Bungay’s latest volumes, nor where hopeful authors stood trembling with virgin manuscripts ready to be sold to Sultan Bungay himself. No—this was the private door, reserved for Mrs. Bungay’s grand departures in her chaise, where she’d settle herself on plush cushions and glare defiantly across the street at Mrs. Bacon’s windows, as if to say: “You may have four children, Emma, but I’ve got a carriage.”

And indeed, Emma Bacon had no carriage—yet. But that didn’t stop her from throwing open her drawing-room sash every time Flora Bungay passed by in her chariot, holding up her four little darlings like trophies. “Look at these,” she’d cry silently, “Flora! This is why I can’t afford a coach-and-four!” And thus, armed with maternal envy, Emma shot arrows of pride into the heart of her sister-in-law.

As Pen and Warrington approached Bungay’s door, a carriage and cab rolled up to the Bacon house. Out stepped Old Dr. Slocum, heavy as a stone statue and twice as dignified, his equipage matching his ponderous prose—both resonating with authority in the literary Row. From the cab, two dazzling white waistcoats emerged, shining like new snow under a midday sun.

Warrington chuckled. “Ah, there’s Bacon’s dinner party too. That’s Dr. Slocum—author of *Memoirs of the Poisoners*. Hardly recognisable as our old friend Hoolan in that dashing white waistcoat. Doolan’s one of Bungay’s men—and look, here he comes!”

Indeed, Messrs. Hoolan and Doolan had shared a cab from the Strand, tossing a coin mid-journey to decide who paid the shilling. Mr. Doolan now appeared, arrayed in black, clutching a pair of enormous white gloves that he couldn’t help but admire, like a man admiring his own reflection in a puddle.

Inside Bungay’s house, the passage was lined with porters in evening coats and gentlemen in gloves so large they looked like they’d been made for circus performers. Hats and coats were collected, names bellowed up the stairs. A few guests had already arrived, but only Mrs. Bungay, resplendent in red satin and a turban, represented the fair sex. She curtsied to each guest with practiced grace—but her mind was elsewhere. Her thoughts were fixed on the rival dinner across the way, and whenever a new guest entered, she’d dart back to the window to watch the arrivals at Emma Bacon’s.

The sight of Dr. Slocum’s massive carriage, pulled by gaunt job-horses, crushed her spirit. On her side of the street? Only hack cabs had come. It was a quiet day for splendour.

All the guests were literary men—though unknown to Pen. There was Mr. Bole, the real editor of the magazine (Mr. Wagg being merely its nominal head); Mr. Trotter, once a tragic poet whose suicide attempts were legendary, now reduced to reading manuscripts for Bungay; and Captain Sumph, an ex-beau reader about town, vaguely related to Literature and the Peerage. He claimed to have written a book, been Byron’s pal, and was somehow kin to Lord Sumphington. His entire conversation revolved around Byron: “I remember poor Shelley, at school, being sent up for good for a copy of verses—every line of which I wrote, by Jove!” Or: “I recollect, when I was at Missolonghi with Byron, offering to bet gamba.” Pen noted that Mrs. Bungay listened to him with rapt attention—his tales of aristocracy thrilled her. If he’d arrived in his own carriage, she’d have bought every word he ever wrote.

Mr. Bungay moved among the guests with cheerful formality. “How are you, sir? Fine day, sir. Glad to see you, sir. Flora, my love, let me have the honour of introducing Mr. Warrington to you. Mr. Warrington, Mrs. Bungay; Mr. Pendennis, Mrs. Bungay. Hope you’ve brought good appetites, gentlemen. You, Doolan, I know have—always had a deuce of a twist.”

“Lor, Bungay!” said Mrs. Bungay.

“Faith, a man must be hard to please, Bungay, who can’t eat a good dinner in this house,” Doolan replied, winking and stroking his lean chops with his giant gloves. He made friendly overtures to Mrs. Bungay, which she rebuffed with icy disdain. “She couldn’t abide Doolan,” she confided later. Indeed, all his flattery fell flat.

As they chatted, Mrs. Bungay kept her eye on the street. Then—a magnificent vision: a huge grey cab-horse galloped into view. Behind it, a pair of white reins held by tiny white gloves. A pale face, adorned with a chin-tuft, peered out. An exiguous groom bobbed over the cab’s edge. The sight delighted Mrs. Bungay.

“The Honourable Percy Popjoy’s quite punctual,” she declared, and swept toward the door to greet the nobleman.

“It’s Percy Popjoy,” Pen said, peering out the window. And indeed, it was—the eldest son of Lord Falconet, known to us all—come to dine with the publisher—his publisher of the Row.

“He was my fag at Eton,” Warrington said. “I ought to have given him a thrashing.”

They’d clashed at Oxbridge Union debates, where Pen had clearly won. Now Percy arrived, hat under arm, face round and dimpled, hairless except for a sudden burst of chin-tuft—like nature had tried too hard and given up.

The temporary groom shouted, “The Honourable Percy Popjoy!”—which startled the young nobleman.

“What did the man want to take away my hat for, Bungay?” he asked. “Can’t do without my hat—need it to bow to Mrs. Bungay. How well you look today. Haven’t seen your carriage in the Park—why haven’t you been there? I missed you, truly.”

“I’m afraid you’re a sad quiz,” said Mrs. Bungay.

“Quiz! Never made a joke in my life—hullo! Who’s here? How d’ye do, Pendennis? How d’ye do, Warrington? These are old friends of mine, Mrs. Bungay. Say, how the deuce did you get here?” he asked, turning his lacquered heels toward her. She, suddenly respectful, smiled—now that she knew her husband’s guests were connected to nobility.

“What! Do they know him?” she whispered to Mr. B.

“High fellers, I tell you—the young one’s related to half the peerage,” said the publisher. Both men hurried forward, smiling and bowing, as if greeting royalty.

Then came the great Mr. Wenham and the great Mr. Wagg.

Wenham entered with his usual demure expression, eyes downcast, staring at the tips of his shiny boots—rarely looking up. Wagg, on the other hand, exploded into the room with a grin, his white waistcoat spreading like a fan, his red face glowing with anticipation of jokes and wine. He liked to enter a room with laughter and leave it with a punchline echoing behind him. Nothing could fully dampen his spirits—not even personal tragedies. When he saw a lord, he greeted him with a pun.

Wenham approached Mrs. Bungay with a smug smile, showing off his shoes. Wagg called her charming and marched straight to Percy Popjoy, whom he addressed as “Pop,” immediately launching into a story seasoned with what the French call *gros sel*.

He was delighted to see Pen, shaking hands and slapping him on the back. “How’s Baymouth? How are the Clavering folks? Sir Francis coming to town? Did you visit Lady Rockminster? Fine old lady, Lady Rockminster!”

These remarks weren’t just for Pen—they were for the whole room, proof that Wagg mixed with the elite. Wenham, meanwhile, whispered to Mrs. Bungay, showing her his boots, then smiled faintly. She admired his subtlety.

Pen, who’d read and somewhat admired Miss Bunion’s poetry (especially her description of herself in *Passion-Flower*, where she compared her youth to—

“A violet, shrinking meanly
When blows the March wind keenly;
A timid fawn, on wild-wood lawn,
Where oak-boughs rustle greenly,—”

and her mature beauty as something striking and captivating), was surprised to find her in reality: a large, bony woman in a crumpled satin dress, entering with a step as heavy as a grenadier’s.

Wagg noticed the straw caught in her skirt and reached to pick it up—only for Miss Bunion to beat him to it. She stepped on it, lifted it, and said, “Sorry I’m late, but the omnibus was slow. What a comfort to ride all the way from Brompton for sixpence.”

No one laughed—she spoke so simply. She wasn’t ashamed of her poverty. She wore it like a badge.

“Is that *Passion-Flowers*?” Pen asked Wenham.

“Why, her portrait shows her as a lovely young woman.”

“You know passion-flowers, like all others, will run to seed,” Wenham replied. “Her picture was probably painted years ago.”

“Well, I like her for not being ashamed of her poverty.”

“So do I,” said Wenham, who would have starved before riding an omnibus to dinner. “But I don’t think she needs to flaunt the straw, do you?”

“My dear Miss Bunion, how do you do?” he said. “I was at a great lady’s drawing-room this morning—everyone loved your new volume. Those lines on Lady Fanny Fantail’s christening brought tears to the Duchess’s eyes. I told her I’d meet you today, and she begged me to thank you.”

This tale—told smoothly—eclipsed Wagg’s claims of visiting country seats. Wenham now ruled the room. He continued with anecdotes of aristocrats, trying to draw Percy Popjoy into the conversation.

“I was telling your father this morning…” or “I think you were present when the Duke said…”

But Percy wouldn’t engage. He retreated to the window with Mrs. Bungay, watching the cabs at the Bacon house. At least, if he wouldn’t talk, she could enjoy seeing the Bacons jealous.

Then Saint Paul’s bell tolled—half an hour late. The party was complete, save for two guests who finally arrived: Captain and Mrs. Shandon.

After greetings, Pen and Warrington went to shake hands with Mrs. Shandon, who seemed touched to see them again. Shandon was smartened up—red velvet waistcoat, frill, wife’s best brooch pinned proudly. Mrs. Bungay’s kindness made her nervous—her red satin and bird-of-paradise feather now felt more like a crown than a costume.

“Nice-looking woman,” Percy whispered to Warrington. “Introduce me to Captain Shandon. I’m told he’s brilliant. And dammy, I adore intellect, by Jove I do!”

True—he had little of his own, but he adored others’. “And introduce me to Miss Bunion. She’s rum to look at, certainly, but that don’t matter. Dammy, I consider myself a literary man—I want to know all clever fellows.”

So Percy and Shandon became acquainted.

The dining-room doors opened. The party filed in. Pen found himself beside Miss Bunion and Mr. Wagg—who fled from the seat next to her, leaving Pen no choice.

Miss Bunion didn’t speak much during dinner, but Pen noticed she ate with gusto and accepted every glass of wine offered. Afterward, she confessed: “I took you for one of those Mayfair dandies. You looked like a little undertaker. And since I loathed the odious creature on my other side, I decided to eat and stay silent.”

“And you did both very well,” Pen said, laughing.

“Well, I do. But next time, I’ll talk to you—because you’re neither so solemn, nor so stupid, nor so pert as you look.”

“Ah, Miss Bunion, how I pine for that ‘next time’!” Pen said, gallantly.

But let’s return to the dinner.

It was a feast of “florid Gothic” proportions, Wagg whispered. “Men in creaking shoes and Berlin gloves moved like shadows behind the guests. Doolan yelled, ‘Waither!’—then blushed at his mistake. Mrs. Bungay’s footboy vanished among the crowd.”

“Look at that very bow-windowed man,” Wagg said. “Undertaker from Amen Corner. Attends funerals and dinners. Cold meat and hot, don’t you see? Sham butler. And I observe, my dear Mr. Pendennis, that wherever there’s a sham butler, there’s sham wine. This sherry’s filthy.”

“Glad you like it, Mr. Wagg,” Bungay said. “Glass with you. Got it from Alderman Benning’s store. Paid a good figure for it. Mr. Pendennis, join us?”

“The old rogue—came from the public-house,” Wagg muttered. “It takes two men to carry it off—it’s so strong. I wish I had a bottle of old Steyne’s wine here. Your uncle and I have had many a one.”

“How good this is!” Popjoy exclaimed. “You must have a *cordon bleu* in your kitchen.”

“Oh yes,” Mrs. Bungay said, thinking he meant a jack-chain.

“I mean a French chef,” Popjoy said politely.

“Oh yes, your lordship,” she replied.

“Does your artist say he’s French, Mrs. B.?” Wagg called out.

“Well, I’m sure I don’t know,” she answered.

“Because if he does, he’s quizzin yer!” Wagg cried—nobody got the pun. He was disconcerted.

“The dinner’s from Griggs, in St. Paul’s Churchyard,” he whispered to Pen. “So is Bacon’s. Bungay pays half-a-crown more per head. So does Bacon. They’d poison each other’s ices if they could. As for the made-dishes—they are poison. This—hum—ha—this Brimborion à la Sévigné is delicious, Mrs. B.,” he said, helping himself.

“Well, I’m glad you like it,” she said, blushing, unsure if the dish was actually named that. She suspected Wagg was mocking her. And so she hated him—with feminine fury. She’d have fired him from Bungay’s periodical, but his name was too big.

Warrington sat beside Mrs. Shandon, who wore plain black silk and faded ornaments. Her quiet smile stirred pity in Warrington’s rough heart. No one paid her any mind. She watched her husband, abashed among the company. Wenham and Wagg knew Shandon’s story—he’d worked with Wagg, was infinitely superior in wit and genius, but remained unknown. He drank quietly, taking whatever wine was offered. Bungay had warned the undertaker not to fill his glass too often. A melancholy precaution—made worse by its necessity. Mrs. Shandon glanced anxiously across the table.

Wagg, embarrassed by his failed pun, stuck to Pen for the rest of dinner, talking mostly about their neighbours. “This is one of Bungay’s grand days,” he said. “We’re all Bungavians here. Did you read Popjoy’s novel? It was an old magazine story by poor Buzzard, forgotten until Trotter dug it up. Bob wrote a few chapters, Popjoy allowed his name, maybe contributed a page here and there—and *Desperation, or the Fugitive Duchess* was born. The fun is to quiz Popjoy about it—he doesn’t know a word.”

“Glad you like it,” Popjoy said. “It’s a favourite bit of my own.”

“There’s no such thing in the whole book,” Wagg whispered. “I invented it. Gad! It wouldn’t make a bad plot for a high-church novel.”

Captain Sumph began: “I remember poor Byron, Hobhouse, Trelawney, and myself dining with Cardinal Mezzocaldo in Rome. We had Orvieto—Byron liked it. The Cardinal regretted being single. We went to Civita Vecchia—Byron’s yacht was there—and by Jove, the Cardinal died within three weeks. Byron was sorry—he rather liked him.”

“A devilish interesting story, Sumph,” Wagg said.

“You should publish some of those stories, Captain Sumph,” Shandon said. “Such a volume would make Bungay’s fortune.”

“Why don’t you ask Sumph to publish ’em in your new paper—the what-d’ye-call-’em—hay, Shandon?” Wagg bellowed.

“Why don’t you ask him to publish ’em in your old magazine, the Thingumbob?” Shandon replied.

“Is there going to be a new paper?” Wenham asked—knowing perfectly well, but ashamed.

“Bungay going to bring out a paper?” Popjoy cried, proud of his literary connections. “You must employ me. Mrs. Bungay, use your influence—make him hire me. Prose or verse—what shall it be? Novels, poems, travels, leading articles—begad. Anything! Just pay me, and I’m yours—right now, my dear Mrs. Bungay, begad!”

“It’s to be called the Small Beer Chronicle,” Wagg growled. “And little Popjoy’s to handle the infantine department.”

“It’s to be called the Pall Mall Gazette, sir,” Shandon said. “And we’d be happy to have you.”

“Pall Mall Gazette—why Pall Mall Gazette?” Wagg asked.

“Because the editor was born in Dublin, the sub-editor in Cork, the proprietor lives in Paternoster Row, and the paper’s published in Catherine Street, Strand. Won’t that suffice you, Wagg?”

Shandon was getting angry. “Everything must have a name. My dog Ponto has a name. You’ve got a name—and a name you deserve, more or less. Why grudge ours?”

“By any other name it would smell as sweet,” Wagg said.

“I’ll have ye remember its name’s not what-d’ye-call-’em, Mr. Wagg,” Shandon snapped. “You know its name well enough—and you know mine.”
