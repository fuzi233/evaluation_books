If Harry Warrington possessed a fervor for martial pursuits, the turmoil of Europe provided ample fuel. War stirred across the continent, and the salons of London buzzed with its rhetoric, inflaming his spirit. Yet, despite the defeat of their own gracious Hanoverian prince, the world was intoxicated by the glory of the "Protestant Hero," the King of Prussia. It was fortunate, in my humble opinion, that Harry did not participate in these campaigns. Had he fought at Rossbach on that fateful fifth of November, 1757—the celebrated Gunpowder Fête—or at the subsequent slaughter of Leuthen, my narrative would have been forced to chronicle battles already immortalized by another pen. I prefer not to compete with such a grim chariot of thundering war. Could my jaunty, yellow park-phaeton rival that steed of foam and flame? Could my meek little Pegasus withstand the shock of battle?

Dear reader, with whom I delight in conversing from the wings of history, clad in the attire and dialect of a bygone age, consider this a mercy: Harry Warrington did not follow the King of Prussia. Otherwise, I would have been compelled to describe conflicts destined for the brush of Thomas Carlyle, and I fear the comparison would prove odious.

Instead, Harry led a sulky, useless existence. He haunted military coffee-houses, caring little for literature save the daily newsprint. He held novels in contempt, unable to comprehend why ladies wept over the sentimental ravings of Mr. Richardson. He possessed a hearty laugh, yet it invariably arrived a moment too late, long after the jest had expired. Why should every gentleman possess a literary bent? Are we diminished by friends who never compose a couplet? Ruined, idle, and dependent on his brother for sustenance, Harry would drift off to sleep over any book he touched, leaving his great, strong hands with no purpose. Consider Achilles, exiled to the court of a distant king to escape harm, wasting his youth among women and distaffs. Where was Pyrrhus born? A mettlesome soul like Achilles should not be led by strings; left to dawdle, he risks far greater mischief than mere idleness.

Two elder ladies of the Lambert family, possessing hearts of uncommon tenderness, offered Harry an unfailing pity—a comfort unique to the female sex. They cheered him in grief, consoled him in trouble, soothed his wrath, and doubled his joy in prosperity. When Fortune strikes with slings and arrows, who but the gentle sea-nymphs—those sympathetic women—kiss our writhing feet and moisten our parched lips with tears? They do not abandon us after our overthrow.

Yet, within the Lambert household, sentiments varied. While Theo and her mother overflowed with compassion, Hetty's heart seemed hardened, bordering on savagery toward Harry. She chafed at his lack of glory and his continued dependency. With the world at arms, could he not wield a musket? Harvest was underway, and thousands swung their sickles; could he not cut down a sheaf of glory instead?

"Why, how savage the little thing is with him!" Papa observed after witnessing Hetty's relentless barrage against Harry, who stood as a quivering target in the drawing-room.

"Her conduct is abominable!" cried Mama. "She deserves a whipping and confinement to bed."

Theo interjected gently, suggesting Hetty's anger stemmed from affection. "Perhaps it is because she likes him better than any of us. If I loved someone, I would desire to admire them always, to deem every action right, and to feel my beloved superior to all other gentlemen."

Mrs. Lambert agreed, attributing the family's high standards to their father's excellence. "He has spoiled us. Have you ever seen anyone to compare?"

Theo admitted few could match him in temper or bravery, noting that while others were brave or learned, Mr. George possessed a loftiness of manner and a tender heart hidden beneath his satire. She recalled instances of George treating children to apples or giving coin to beggars, proving his nobility through action. Her discourse drifted into reciting verses from Molière, her voice faltering slightly as she realized her mother's odd gaze followed her every word. Blushing like a rose, she fled the room, leaving her mother to wonder at the fluttering heart and the sudden silence.

Indeed, a pattern emerged: when Harry visited, Hetty descended to greet him; when George arrived, Theo appeared. It was a curious, unspoken arrangement. Theo, with keen observation, noted the distinction between Harry's golden hair and George's dark locks, ensuring she never confused the brothers. Conversely, Hetty remained composed and polite whenever the dark-haired gentleman entered.

One afternoon, Harry sat on a couch, radiating profound despondency. Suddenly, Hetty appeared.

"And pray, sir, what makes your honor look so glum this morning?" she asked.

"Oh, Hetty!" Harry sighed. "I have nothing else to do but look glum. As boys, I begged holidays to swing on gates or make ducks and drakes, yet those idle days were the most melancholy. What fills my time now? Breakfast, walks, dinner, walks, tea, supper—and a pipe of Virginia tobacco."

"I laugh to imagine you at the head of a class, summoned by a master!" Hetty teased.

"I should not be," Harry replied humbly. "George could lead any class, but I am no scholar. Neglecting myself in youth, I failed to cultivate my mind. Perhaps a cane would have helped."

"It might do you good now!" Hetty suggested. "Does Tom's account of his schooling stir any longing in you?"

Tom's tale confirmed Harry's self-loathing. He realized he lacked genius for books and was merely suited to squandering his inheritance, lounging in coffee-houses, racing horses, or galloping hounds. "I am good for nothing," he confessed despairingly.

"What, such a great, brave, strong fellow good for nothing?" Hetty exclaimed indignantly. "I would not admit such a truth to any woman, even if I were twice as useless!"

"What am I to do? I seek leave to join the army, but Madam Esmond ignores me. It is the only path for me. I have exhausted my funds and borrowed heavily from my brother. If my mother would send me, I would leap at the chance."

"A gentleman of spirit requires no woman to buckle his sword or polish his firelock!" Hetty declared, pacing the room with a newfound military bearing. "Recall Sir John Armytage at court. Engaged to a famous beauty, he volunteered immediately when the King asked when he would depart. 'Tomorrow, Your Majesty,' he replied. That is a soldier's answer. Even my father longs to go, leaving us brats behind. Oh, why wasn't I a man? My brothers are bound for the Church, but I could have been a famous little soldier!"

Harry watched her with tender admiration. "I think I would hesitate to see a musket on that little shoulder or a wound on that pretty face, Hetty."

"Wounds! Who fears wounds?" she cried. "If I could carry a musket, I would use it. Men think we are fit only for puddings and samplers. Why wasn't I a man? George read Tasso to us yesterday. Look, here is the book marked where we left off."

She quoted passionately: *"Alas! why did not Heaven these members frail / With lively force and vigour strengthen, so / That I this silken gown..."*

"Silken gown?" Harry queried.

"Well, sir, it is actually calimanco—but the verse continues: *'...this silken gown and slender veil / Might for a breastplate and a helm forgo; / Then should not heat, nor cold, nor rain, nor hail... / Withhold me; but I would, both day and night, / In pitched field or private combat, fight.'"*

"Fight? Yes, that I would!" Hetty insisted. "Why are both my brothers becoming parsons? One child of Papa's ought to be a soldier!"

Harry laughed softly, feeling no inclination to strike such a tender warrior. "Here is a finger nearly as large as your arm," he mused. "How would you stand before a great, strong man? I would like to see a man attempt to injure you. You are a delicate, tender creature. Do you suppose any scoundrel would dare?"

Excited by this protective fantasy, Harry paced the room, chafing at the thought of any French rogue daring to insult Miss Hester Lambert. This silent courage captivated Hetty. In reality, she was no bolder than the fictional Erminia she had been reading about, unaware that Harry knew nothing of her. He was lost in his own troubles, bewildered by the literary giants and enchantresses surrounding them. Nature dictates that great, strong men often attract frail, tender women, yet Harry's potential union with Hetty promised a quiet domesticity rather than heroic conquest.

While Hetty fired her sarcastic arrows, Harry barely felt the sting, too magnanimous to grasp her subtle hints. Was she trying to shame him by offering her own casque and breastplate? The simple-minded youth either melted at the prospect of her danger or dissolved into laughter at the absurdity of her fighting.
