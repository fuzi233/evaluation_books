Mrs. Jennings was a widow with an ample jointure, possessing the financial stability to treat matrimony as a logistical operation rather than a romantic ideal. Having successfully placed her two daughters, she viewed the remaining unmarried population of England as a queue of assets awaiting acquisition. Her strategy was zealous and relentless; she scanned the horizon for potential pairings with the efficiency of a field commander assessing terrain. Possessing a keen radar for emerging attachments, she delighted in inflating the vanity of young ladies by whispering rumors of their irresistible appeal to specific men. This intelligence gathering allowed her to arrive at Barton with absolute certainty: Colonel Brandon was deeply enamored with Marianne Dashwood. The diagnosis was made within hours of their reunion, confirmed by his attentive silence during Marianne's singing sessions, and solidified when he repeated the performance at the Middletons' dinner. It was a perfect transaction: he possessed wealth, she possessed beauty. Mrs. Jennings had championed his marriage since his introduction to her circle by Sir John, driven by a maternal instinct to secure a provider for every available female. The personal dividend was substantial; a united couple provided endless ammunition for her social commentary. At the park, she mocked the colonel's earnestness; at the cottage, she ridiculed Marianne's naivety. While the colonel remained indifferent to her barbs, Marianne found the treatment baffling. Initially confused, she eventually interpreted the mockery as a cruel indictment of the colonel's advanced age and his status as a solitary relic.

Mrs. Dashwood, unable to reconcile the image of a man merely five years younger than herself with the concept of antiquity, defended her mother's judgment. "Colonel Brandon is certainly younger than Mrs. Jennings," she argued, "but he is old enough to be my father. If he possesses the capacity for passion, he must have long outlived it. It is absurd!"

"Infirmity!" Elinor interjected sharply. "Do you classify Colonel Brandon as infirm? I concede his age may appear exaggerated to your youthful perspective, but you cannot claim he lacks the use of his limbs."

"Did you not hear him complain of rheumatism?" Marianne countered. "Is that not the universal symptom of decline?"

"My dearest child," Mrs. Dashwood laughed, "if we proceed on this logic, I shall spend my days fearing my own decay. It seems miraculous that I have survived to the advanced age of forty."

"Mama, you misunderstand," Marianne insisted. "Colonel Brandon is not yet a liability. He may live another twenty years. Yet, thirty-five has no place in matrimony."

"Perhaps," Elinor reasoned calmly, "thirty-five and seventeen should remain incompatible. However, if a woman remains single at twenty-seven, I find no objection to Colonel Brandon's age regarding her."

"A woman of twenty-seven," Marianne paused, then declared, "cannot hope to feel or inspire affection again. Should her circumstances be dire—homeless or impoverished—I might imagine her submitting to the duties of a nurse for the security of a dowry. In such a case, a union based purely on convenience would satisfy the world. But to me, it would be no marriage at all; it would be a commercial exchange where each party seeks profit at the other's expense."

"It is impossible," Elinor replied, "to convince you that a woman of twenty-seven could harbor genuine love for a man of thirty-five, making him a desirable companion. Nevertheless, I must object to your sentencing Colonel Brandon and his future wife to perpetual confinement in a sickroom simply because he complained yesterday—a day notably cold and damp—of a minor rheumatic ache in his shoulder."

"But he spoke of flannel waistcoats!" Marianne retorted. "To me, flannel is synonymous with aches, cramps, rheumatism, and every ailment afflicting the aged and the weak."

"Had he been suffering from a violent fever, you would not despise him so readily. Confess, Marianne: is there not something compelling in the flushed cheek, the hollow eye, and the rapid pulse of a fever?"

Shortly thereafter, as Elinor withdrew, Marianne voiced a deeper concern. "Mother, I harbor an alarm regarding illness that I cannot suppress. I am certain Edward Ferrars is unwell. We have been here nearly two weeks, yet he has not arrived. Only genuine indisposition could explain such an extraordinary delay. What holds him at Norland?"

"Had you anticipated his arrival sooner?" Mrs. Dashwood asked.

"I had no expectation. On the contrary, I recall occasions when he displayed a distinct lack of enthusiasm for my invitations. Does Elinor expect him already?"

"I have never mentioned it to her, but surely she assumes it."

"You are mistaken," Mrs. Dashwood corrected. "Yesterday, when discussing a new grate for the spare bedroom, Elinor noted there was no urgency, implying the room would remain vacant for some time."

"How peculiar! What is the meaning of this? Their conduct toward one another has been inexplicable! How cold, how composed were their final farewells! How languid their conversation on the eve of our separation! In Edward's goodbye, there was no distinction between Elinor and me; it was merely the polite sentiment of an affectionate brother. Twice did I deliberately leave them alone earlier that morning, and each time he followed me out of the room with unnatural haste. And Elinor, departing Norland and Edward, did not weep as I did. Her self-control remains unbroken. When is she dejected? When does she seek solitude or display restlessness?"
