Elinor recoiled, her face pale with horror, and turned instinctively to flee the room. Her hand was already upon the latch when it halted, frozen by his sudden advance. In a voice that brooked no refusal, bordering on command rather than plea, he demanded, "Miss Dashwood, for half an hour—for ten minutes—I beg you to stay."

"No, sir," she replied, her resolve unshaken. "I shall not stay. Your business cannot concern me. The servants, I presume, failed to inform you that Mr. Palmer is not at home."

"Suppose they had told me," he shouted with intense fervor, "that Mr. Palmer and every relative of his were consigned to hell, it would not have driven me from this threshold. My business is solely with you, and only with you."

"With *me*!" she exclaimed, stunned. "Very well, sir—hasten your speech, and pray, try to be less violent."

"Sit down, and I shall be both prompt and composed."

She hesitated, unsure of her next move. The terrifying possibility that Colonel Brandon might arrive and find her trapped in this confrontation crossed her mind. Yet, having pledged to listen, her curiosity and her sense of duty compelled her to remain. Concluding that prudence demanded speed and that her compliance would facilitate it, she moved silently toward the table and seated herself. He took the chair opposite, and for thirty seconds, neither spoke.

"Hurry, sir," Elinor said impatiently. "I have no time to waste."

He sat in a posture of profound contemplation, seemingly deaf to her urgency. Finally, with abruptness, he addressed her: "Your sister... is out of danger. I learned it from a servant. Praise God!—But is it true? Is it actually true?"

Elinor refused to answer. He pressed the question with renewed desperation. "For heaven's sake, tell me—is she safe, or is she not?"

"We hope she is."

He rose and paced the length of the room. "If I had known this truth half an hour ago—" he continued, returning to his seat with a forced liveliness—"but since I am here, what difference does it make? Once, Miss Dashwood, perhaps the last time, let us share a moment of cheerfulness. I am in a remarkably festive mood. Be honest with me: do you consider me a knave or a fool?"

Elinor stared at him with mounting astonishment. She began to fear he must be intoxicated; the strangeness of his visit and his erratic behavior seemed intelligible only through that lens. Seizing upon this impression, she stood abruptly. "Mr. Willoughby, I advise you to return to Combe immediately. I am not at leisure to remain with you. Whatever your business may be, it will be better recalled and explained tomorrow."

"I understand you," he replied, smiling expressively with a voice perfectly calm. "Yes, I am very drunk. A pint of porter with my cold beef at Marlborough was sufficient to unsettle me."

"At Marlborough!" cried Elinor, utterly bewildered.

"Yes. I left London this morning at eight. The only ten minutes I have spent outside my carriage since then secured me a luncheon at Marlborough."

The steadiness of his manner and the clarity in his eyes convinced Elinor that, however foolish his presence here might be, it was not caused by intoxication. After a brief pause, she said, "Mr. Willoughby, you *ought* to feel, and I certainly *do*, that after everything that has transpired, your arrival here in this manner, forcing yourself upon my attention, demands a very specific excuse. What is it you intend to convey?"

"I mean," he said with grave intensity, "to reduce your hatred of me by even a single degree. I intend to offer an explanation, an apology for the past; to open my entire heart to you, and by proving that though I have often been a blockhead, I have never been a rascal, to secure some semblance of forgiveness from your sister."

"Is this the true reason for your coming?"

"Upon my soul, it is," he answered, with a warmth that evoked the former Willoughby, compelling her, despite herself, to believe his sincerity.

"If that is all, you may be satisfied already; for Marianne *has*—she has *long* forgiven you."

"Has she?" he cried, in the same eager tone. "Then she forgave me before she ought to have. But she shall forgive me again, and on more reasonable grounds. *Now* will you listen to me?"

Elor nodded her consent.

"I do not know," he said after a pause filled with her anticipation and his reflection, "how *you* may have interpreted my behavior toward your sister, or what diabolical motives you may have attributed to me. Perhaps you will hardly think better of me—but the trial is worth it, and you shall hear everything. When I first became acquainted with your family, I harbored no other intention than to pass my time pleasantly while detained in Devonshire, more enjoyably than ever before. Your sister's lovely appearance and engaging manners pleased me; indeed, her behavior toward me, almost from the start, was of a nature that makes me marvel, reflecting now on what she was, that my heart remained so insensible. Initially, I must confess, my vanity was merely elevated by it. Indifferent to her happiness, focused solely on my own amusement, yielding to habits I had long indulged, I strove by every means to please her without any design of reciprocating her affection."

At this point, Miss Dashwood turned her gaze upon him with angry contempt and interrupted, "It is hardly worth while, Mr. Willoughby, for you to recount, or for me to endure further. Such a beginning cannot be followed by anything acceptable. Do not pain me by hearing any more on this subject."

"I insist you hear the whole of it," he replied. "My fortune was never substantial, and I had always been extravagant, accustomed to associating with those of higher income. Every year since my majority, or perhaps before, had increased my debts. Though the death of my old cousin, Mrs. Smith, promised freedom, that event being uncertain and potentially distant, it had long been my intention to restore my circumstances by marrying a woman of fortune. To attach myself to your sister, therefore, was unthinkable. With a meanness, selfishness, and cruelty that no indignant or contemptuous look from you, Miss Dashwood, can condemn too harshly, I acted thus, striving to engage her regard without a thought of returning it. However, one thing may be said in my defense: even in that horrid state of selfish vanity, I did not comprehend the extent of the injury I meditated, because I did not *then* know what it was to love. But have I ever known it? Well may it be doubted; for had I truly loved, could I have sacrificed my feelings to vanity, to greed? Or, more importantly, could I have sacrificed hers? Yet, I have done it. To avoid a comparative poverty that her affection and society would have rendered unbearable, I raised myself to affluence only to lose everything that could make it a blessing."

"You did then," Elinor said, slightly softened, "believe yourself attached to her at one time?"

"To resist such attractions, to withstand such tenderness! Is there a man on earth capable of it? Yes, I found myself, gradually, sincerely fond of her; and the happiest hours of my life were those spent with her when I felt my intentions were strictly honorable and my feelings blameless. Even *then*, however, when fully determined to pay my addresses, I allowed myself most improperly to delay, day by day, the moment of declaration, unwilling to commit while my circumstances were so embarrased. I will not reason here—nor will I stop for *you* to expound on the absurdity, and worse-than-absurdity, of scrupling to pledge my faith where my honor was already bound. Events have proven I was a cunning fool, meticulously preparing for an opportunity to render myself contemptible and wretched forever. Eventually, however, my resolution was taken. I had decided that, as soon as I could engage her alone, I would justify the attentions I had invariably shown and openly assure her of an affection I had already displayed with such care. But in the interim—in the few hours before I could speak privately—a circumstance occurred, an unlucky one, to ruin all my resolution and my peace. A discovery was made," he hesitated, looking down. "Mrs. Smith had somehow, perhaps via a distant relation interested in depriving me of her favor, been informed of an affair, a connection—but I need not elaborate further," he added, gazing at her with flushed cheeks and an inquiring eye—"your particular intimacy. You have probably heard the whole story long ago."

"I have," returned Elinor, coloring and hardening her heart anew against any compassion for him. "I have heard it all. How you will explain away any part of your guilt in that dreadful business, I confess, is beyond my comprehension."

"Remember," cried Willoughby, "from whom you received the account. Could it be impartial? I acknowledge that her situation and character ought to have been respected by me.
