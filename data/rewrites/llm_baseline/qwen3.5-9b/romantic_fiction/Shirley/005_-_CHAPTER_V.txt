Moore's spirits remained buoyant as he rose the following morning. Both he and Joe Scott had spent the night in the mill, utilizing sleeping quarters ingeniously fashioned from recesses within the front and rear counting-houses. The master, invariably an early riser, had awakened even earlier than his custom. He roused his man by humming a French melody while attending to his toilette.

"Ye're not custen dahn, then, maister?" cried Joe.

"Not a stiver, *mon garçon*—which means, my lad. Rise, and we shall take a walk through the mill before the hands arrive; I intend to explain my future plans. We shall secure the machinery yet, Joseph. Perhaps you haven't heard of Bruce?"

"And th' arrand (spider)? Yes, but I have. I've read the history of Scotland and know as much about it as you; and I understand you mean to say you will persevere."

"I do."

"Is there mony o' your mak' i' your country?" inquired Joe, as he folded his temporary bed and stored it away.

"In my country! Which is my country?"

"Why, France—isn't it?"

"Not it, indeed! The fact that the French seized Antwerp, where I was born, does not make me a Frenchman."

"Holland, then?"

"I am not a Dutchman. Now you are confusing Antwerp with Amsterdam."

"Flanders?"

"I scorn the insinuation, Joe! I am an *Anversois*. My mother was an Anversoise, though she descended from French lineage, which explains why I speak French."

"But your father was Yorkshire, which makes you partly Yorkshire too; and anyone can see you're akin to us, you're so keen on making brass and getting ahead."

"Joe, you're an impudent dog; but I've always been accustomed to a boorish sort of insolence since my youth. The *classe ouvrière*—that is, the working people in Belgium—behave brutally toward their employers; and by *brutally*, Joe, I mean *brutalement*—which, perhaps, when properly translated, should be *roughly*."

"We always speak our minds in this country; and those young parsons and grand folks from London are shocked at our 'incivility'; and we'd love to give them something to be shocked at, because it's sport to watch them turn the whites of their eyes and spread out their tiny hands, as if they were flayed with bogards, and then hear them say, cutting off their words short, 'Dear! dear! What savages! How very coarse!'"

"You *are* savages, Joe. You don't suppose you're civilized, do you?"

"Middling, middling, maister. I reckon that us manufacturing lads in the north are a deal more intelligent and know a deal more than the farming folk in the south. Trade sharpens our wits; and those who are mechanics like me are forced to think. You know, with looking after machinery and suchlike, I've gotten into the habit that when I see an effect, I look straight for a cause, and I often lay hold of it to purpose; and then I like reading, and I'm curious to know what those who reckon to govern us aim to do for us and with us. And there are many cleverer than me; there are many among those greasy chaps who smell of oil, and among the dyers with blue and black skins, who have a long head and can tell what a fool of a law is as well as you or old Yorke, and a deal better than soft fellows like Christopher Sykes of Whinbury, and those hectoring nothings like yonder Irish Peter, Helstone's curate."

"You think yourself a clever fellow, I know, Scott."

"Ay! I'm fairly so. I can tell cheese from chalk, and I'm very well aware that I've improved such opportunities as I've had, a deal better than some who reckon to be above me; but there are thousands in Yorkshire who are as good as me, and a two or three who are better."

"You're a great man—you're a sublime fellow; but you're a prig, a conceited noodle with it all, Joe! You need not think that because you've picked up a little knowledge of practical mathematics, and because you've found some scrap of the elements of chemistry at the bottom of a dyeing vat, that therefore you're a neglected man of science; and you need not suppose that because the course of trade doesn't always run smooth, and you, and such as you, are sometimes short of work and bread, that therefore your class are martyrs, and that the whole form of government under which you live is wrong. Moreover, you need not for a moment insinuate that virtue has taken refuge in cottages and wholly abandoned slated houses. Let me tell you, I particularly abominate that sort of trash, because I know so well that human nature is human nature everywhere, whether under tile or thatch, and that in every specimen of human nature that breathes, vice and virtue are ever found blended, in smaller or greater proportions, and that the proportion is not determined by station. I have seen villains who were rich, and I have seen villains who were poor, and I have seen villains who were neither rich nor poor, but who had realized Agar's wish, and lived in fair and modest competency. The clock is going to strike six. Away with you, Joe, and ring the mill bell."

It was now the middle of February; by six o'clock, therefore, dawn was just beginning to steal upon the night, penetrating its brown obscurity with a pale ray and lending a demi-translucence to its opaque shadows. Pale enough that ray was on this particular morning: no color tinged the east, no flush warmed it. To see how heavily the day slowly lifted its lid, how wan a glance it flung along the hills, one might have thought the sun's fire had been quenched in last night's floods. The breath of this morning was as chill as its aspect; a raw wind stirred the mass of night-cloud, revealing, as it slowly rose, a colorless, silver-gleaming ring all around the horizon—not blue sky, but a stratum of paler vapor beyond. It had ceased to rain, but the earth was sodden, and the pools and rivulets were full.

The mill-windows were alight, the bell still rang loudly, and now the little children came running in, in too great a hurry, we hope, to feel very much nipped by the inclement air; and indeed, by contrast, perhaps the morning appeared rather favorable to them than otherwise, for they had often come to their work that winter through snow-storms, through heavy rain, through hard frost.

Mr. Moore stood at the entrance to watch them pass. He counted them as they went by. To those who came rather late he said a word of reprimand, which was a little more sharply repeated by Joe Scott when the lingerers reached the work-rooms. Neither master nor overlooker spoke savagely. They were not savage men either of them, though it appeared both were rigid, for they fined a delinquent who came considerably too late. Mr. Moore made him pay his penny down ere he entered, and informed him that the next repetition of the fault would cost him twopence.

Rules, no doubt, are necessary in such cases, and coarse and cruel masters will make coarse and cruel rules, which, at the time we treat of at least, they used sometimes to enforce tyrannically; but though I describe imperfect characters (every character in this book will be found to be more or less imperfect, my pen refusing to draw anything in the model line), I have not undertaken to handle degraded or utterly infamous ones. Child-torturers, slave masters and drivers, I consign to the hands of jailers.

Instead, then, of harrowing up my reader's soul and delighting his organ of wonder with effective descriptions of stripes and scourgings, I am happy to be able to inform him that neither Mr. Moore nor his overlooker ever struck a child in their mill. Joe had, indeed, once very severely flogged a son of his own for telling a lie and persisting in it; but, like his employer, he was too phlegmatic, too calm, as well as too reasonable a man, to make corporal chastisement other than the exception to his treatment of the young.

Mr. Moore haunted his mill, his mill-yard, his dye-house, and his warehouse until the sickly dawn strengthened into day. The sun even rose—at least a white disc, clear, tintless, and almost chill-looking as ice, peeped over the dark crest of a hill, changed the livid edge of the cloud above it to silver, and looked solemnly down the whole length of the den, or narrow dale, to whose strait bounds we are at present limited. It was eight o'clock; the mill lights were all extinguished; the signal was given for breakfast; the children, released for half an hour from toil, betook themselves to the little tin cans which held their coffee, and to the small baskets which contained their allowance of bread. Let us hope they have enough to eat; it would be a pity were it otherwise.
