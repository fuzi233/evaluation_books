The next morning, as the Dashwood sisters stepped into the drawing-room of the park, Mrs. Palmer burst through the opposite door, her face alight with a cheerful energy that seemed entirely restored. She greeted them with an affectionate grasp of each hand, her eyes sparkling with delight at their return.

"How utterly wonderful to see you!" she exclaimed, settling herself snugly between Elinor and Marianne. "I was truly frightened you wouldn't come; the weather has been dreadfully dismal, and I feared you'd stay cooped up. But we must leave tomorrow, alas! The Westons arrive next week, and it was a sudden decision on our part. Mr. Palmer didn't tell me until the carriage was already at the door, and then he asked if I'd accompany him to Barton. He is such a peculiar man! He never reveals anything unless pressed. I am so sorry we cannot remain longer, but I trust we shall meet again in London very soon."

The Dashwoods felt compelled to dampen this hopeful expectation.

"To London?" Mrs. Palmer cried, laughing. "I shall be terribly disappointed if you do not go. I could secure the finest house in the world for you, right next door to ours in Hanover Square. You must come, indeed. I am certain I would be delighted to act as your chaperone whenever Mrs. Dashwood feels unable to venture into public society."

They offered their thanks but firmly resisted her persistent pleas.

"Oh, my dear," Mrs. Palmer called out to her husband, who had just entered the room, "you must assist me in persuading the Miss Dashwoods to spend this winter in London."

Her husband offered no reply; after a polite bow to the ladies, he turned his attention to the gloomy weather.

"Horrible!" he declared. "Such weather renders everything and everyone repulsive. Dullness is generated indoors just as readily as outdoors by the rain; it makes one detest every acquaintance. What on earth does Sir John mean by lacking a billiard room? How few people understand true comfort! Sir John is as obtuse as the weather itself."

Soon, the rest of the party filtered in.

"I fear, Miss Marianne," Sir John remarked, "that you were unable to take your customary walk to Allenham today."

Marianne's expression grew grave, and she remained silent.

"Oh, do not be so coy before us," Mrs. Palmer chided playfully. "We know all about it, I assure you. I admire your taste immensely; I find him extremely handsome. We do not live far from him in the country, you know—perhaps not ten miles."

"Much nearer thirty," her husband corrected dryly.

"Ah, well! There is little difference. I have never visited his estate, though they say it is a charming place."

"As vile a spot as I have ever seen in my life," Mr. Palmer countered sharply.

Marianne remained perfectly still, though her countenance betrayed a keen interest in the exchange.

"Is it truly so ugly?" Mrs. Palmer pressed. "Then it must be some other location that is so delightful, I suppose."

Later, seated in the dining room, Sir John expressed regret that their gathering consisted of only eight people.

"My dear," he said to his wife, "it is most vexing that we are so few. Why did you not invite the Gilberts to dine with us today?"

"Did I not tell you, Sir John, when you first suggested it, that it was impossible? They dined with us yesterday."

"You and I, Sir John," Mrs. Jennings interjected warmly, "should not stand on such formalities."

"Then you would be exceedingly ill-bred," Mr. Palmer retorted.

"My love, you contradict everyone," his wife laughed, her usual merriment undimmed. "Do you realize how rude you are?"

"I did not know I was contradicting anyone by calling your mother ill-bred."

"Ay, you may abuse me as you please," the kindly old lady replied. "You have taken Charlotte off my hands, and you cannot give her back. So, there, I hold the upper hand over you."

Charlotte laughed heartily at the thought that her husband could not escape her, declaring that she cared not how cross he became, for they were bound to live together. It was impossible to find someone more thoroughly good-natured or more determined to find happiness than Mrs. Palmer. Her husband's studied indifference, insolence, and discontent caused her no pain; rather, when he scolded or abused her, she found it highly diverting.

"Mr. Palmer is so droll!" she whispered to Elinor. "He is perpetually in a bad mood."

After observing him closely, Elinor was not inclined to credit him with genuine, unfeigned ill-nature or ill-breeding. His temper might indeed be slightly soured by discovering, as many men do, that through some inexplicable bias toward beauty, he was married to a very foolish woman. Yet, she knew such blunders were too common for any sensible man to be permanently wounded by them. Rather, she believed it was a desire for distinction that fueled his contemptuous treatment of others and his general abuse of everything around him. It was the wish to appear superior to his peers. The motive was common enough to warrant no wonder, but the means, however successful in establishing his superiority in bad manners, were unlikely to attract anyone to him save his wife.

"Oh, my dear Miss Dashwood," Mrs. Palmer said shortly afterward, "I have a favor to ask of you and your sister. Will you come to spend some time at Cleveland this Christmas? Please do—and come while the Westons are with us. You cannot imagine how happy I shall be! It will be absolutely delightful! My love," turning to her husband, "do you not long to have the Miss Dashwoods visit Cleveland?"

"Certainly," he replied with a sneer, "I came to Devonshire with no other purpose."

"There now," she said triumphantly, "you see Mr. Palmer expects you; therefore, you cannot refuse."

Both sisters eagerly and resolutely declined her invitation.

"But indeed you must and shall come. I am sure you will like it above all things. The Westons will be present, and it will be quite charming. You cannot conceive what a lovely place Cleveland is; and we are so lively now, for Mr. Palmer is constantly traveling the country canvassing for the election, and so many people have come to dine with us that I have never seen before—it is simply delightful! But, poor fellow! It is very fatiguing for him, for he is forced to make everyone like him."

Elinor could scarcely maintain her composure as she acknowledged the hardship of such an obligation.

"How charming it will be," Charlotte continued, "when he enters Parliament! Won't it? How I shall laugh! It will be so ridiculous to see all his letters addressed to him with 'M.P.' appended. But do you know, he says he will never frank for me? He declares he won't. Don't you, Mr. Palmer?"

Mr. Palmer ignored her completely.

"He cannot bear writing, you know," she persisted, "he says it is quite shocking."

"No," he replied, "I never said anything so irrational. Do not attribute all your misuse of language to me."

"There now; you see how droll he is. This is always his manner! Sometimes he won't speak to me for half a day, and then he bursts out with something so amusing—about anything in the world."

She surprised Elinor greatly as they returned to the drawing-room by asking if she did not like Mr. Palmer excessively.

"Certainly," Elinor answered. "He seems very agreeable."

"Well, I am so glad you feel that way. I thought you would; he is so pleasant. And Mr. Palmer is excessively pleased with you and your sisters, I can tell you. You cannot imagine how disappointed he will be if you do not come to Cleveland. I cannot fathom why you should object."

Elinor was once again obliged to decline her invitation, changing the subject to halt her entreaties. She hoped that, living in the same county, Mrs. Palmer might provide a more detailed account of Willoughby's general character than could be gleaned from the Middletons' limited acquaintance. She was eager to secure any confirmation of his merits that might alleviate Marianne's fears. She began by inquiring if they saw much of Mr. Willoughby at Cleveland and whether they were intimate with him.

"Oh, yes, I know him extremely well," Mrs. Palmer replied. "Not that I ever spoke to him, indeed; but I have seen him frequently in town. Somehow, I never happened to be staying at Barton while he was at Allenham. Mama saw him here once before, but I was with my uncle at Weymouth. However, I dare say we should have seen a great deal of him in Somersetshire if it had not been unluckily that we never found ourselves in the country together. He is very rarely at Combe, I believe; but even if he were there often, I do not think Mr. Palmer would visit him, for he is in the opposition, you know, and besides, it is so distant. I know why you inquire about him, very well; your sister is to marry him. I am monstrously glad of it, for then I shall have her as a neighbor."

"Upon my word," Elinor replied, "you know much more about the matter than I do, if you have any reason to expect such a match."

"Don't pretend to deny it, because you know it is what everyone is talking about. I assure you I heard of it on my way through town."

"My dear Mrs. Palmer!"

"Upon my honor, I did. I met Colonel Brandon Monday morning in Bond Street, just before we left town, and he told me of it directly."

"You surprise me very much. Colonel Brandon telling you of it! Surely you must be mistaken. To impart such intelligence to a person who could not possibly be interested in it, even if true, is not what I should expect from Colonel Brandon."

"But I do assure you it was so, for all that, and I will explain how it happened. When we met him, he turned back and walked with us. We began discussing my brother and sister, and various other topics. I said to him, 'So, Colonel, I hear a new family has arrived at Barton Cottage, and Mama sends word they are very pretty, and that one of them is to be married to Mr. Willoughby of Combe Magna. Is it true, pray? For of course you must know, as you have been in Devonshire so recently.'"

"And what did the Colonel say?"

"Oh, he did not say much; but he looked as if he knew it to be true. From that moment, I set it down as certain. It will be quite delightful, I declare! When is it to take place?"

"Was Mr. Brandon well, I hope?"

"Oh! Yes, quite well; and so full of your praises he did nothing but say fine things of you."

"I am flattered by his commendation. He seems an excellent man; and I think him uncommonly pleasing."

"So do I. He is such a charming man that it is a pity he should be so grave and so dull. Mama says *he* was in love with your sister too. I assure you it was a great compliment if he was, for he hardly ever falls in love with anyone."

"Is Mr. Willoughby much known in your part of Somersetshire?" Elinor asked.

"Oh! Yes, extremely well; that is, I do not believe many people are acquainted with him, because Combe Magna is so remote; but they all think him extremely agreeable, I assure you. No one is more liked than Mr. Willoughby wherever he goes, so you may tell your sister. She is a monstrous lucky girl to secure him, upon my honor; not that he is much more lucky in securing her, for she is so very handsome and agreeable that nothing can be good enough for her. However, I do not think she is hardly at all handsomer than you, I assure you; for I think you both excessively pretty, and so does Mr. Palmer too, I am sure, though we could not get him to admit it last night."

Mrs. Palmer's information regarding Willoughby was not particularly substantial, yet any testimony in his favor, however slight, was pleasing to her.

"I am so glad we have finally become acquainted," Charlotte continued. "And now I hope we shall always be great friends. You cannot imagine how much I longed to see you! It is so delightful that you live at the cottage! Nothing can compare to it, surely! And I am so glad your sister is going to be well married! I hope you will visit Combe Magna frequently. It is a sweet place, by all accounts."

"Have you been acquainted with Colonel Brandon for long?" Elinor asked.

"Yes, for a great while; ever since my sister married. He was a particular friend of Sir John's. I believe," she added in a low voice, "he would have been very glad to have had me, if he could. Sir John and Lady Middleton wished it very much. But Mama did not think the match good enough for me; otherwise, Sir John would have mentioned it to the Colonel, and we should have been married immediately."

"Did Colonel Brandon know of Sir John's proposal to your mother before it was made? Had he never owned his affection to you?"

"Oh, no; but if Mama had not objected to it, I dare say he would have liked it above all things. He had not seen me then more than twice, for it was before I left school. However, I am much happier as I am. Mr. Palmer is the kind of man I like."
