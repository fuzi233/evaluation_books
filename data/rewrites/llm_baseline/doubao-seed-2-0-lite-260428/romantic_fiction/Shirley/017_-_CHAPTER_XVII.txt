This procession, led by the rector and shepherded by sharp-eyed village women, had not set out spoiling for a fight or hunting down enemies, yet the brass band at its head blared bold, martial tunes. For some of their number, most notably Shirley Keeldar, those rousing notes lit a wild, bright spark under their skin, kindling a longing that had no place in their quiet, unthreatened countryside. Old Helstone chanced to turn, caught the glint in her dark eyes, and laughed outright. Shirley laughed back, unashamed.

“There’s no battle waiting for us today, lass,” he told her, shaking his head. “Our land doesn’t need us to take up arms. No tyrant threatens our liberty. We’re nothing more than a group out for a walk. Rein in that fiery spirit of yours, Captain—there’s no call for it, more’s the pity.”

“Might I suggest you take your own advice, Doctor?” Shirley shot back, grinning, before leaning in to murmur to Caroline at her side, “If reality won’t give me a grand cause, I’ll steal one from imagination, shall I? We’re not soldiers, heaven knows I’ve no taste for bloodshed—if we carry a banner, it’s the banner of the Cross. Let’s say it’s hundreds of years back, and we’re Scots Covenanters trekking up the hills, sneaking past persecuting troopers to hold our prayer meeting. We know the fight might come right after the hymns, but we’d stain the peat bogs with our blood before we’d give up our faith. That music wakes every sleeping part of me, Caroline—makes my heart beat so fast it’s like it learned a whole new rhythm. I almost wish for danger, something worth fighting for: a cause, a home, or… or even a man to defend.”

“Shirley, look!” Caroline cut in, pointing to the distant rise of Stilbro’ Brow. “There’s a little red speck up there. You’ve sharper eyes than me—what is it?”

Shirley squinted, then her brows lifted. “More than one. It’s a whole line of red. Cavalry, six of them, riding hard. Wait—they just swerved right. They spotted our procession, and they’re circling round to avoid us. Off on some ride of theirs, I suppose.”

Mr. Helstone called out that they’d be taking Royd Lane as a shortcut to Nunnely Common, and the whole party filed into the narrow, sun-dappled lane, barely wide enough for two to walk abreast without tumbling straight into the muddy ditches on either side. Halfway down the path, the rector and Mr. Boultby both jolted to attention, the young curates nudged each other snickering, and gentle Mr. Hall turned to the ladies with an amused little smile.

“What on earth is it?” someone asked.

He pointed with his walking stick to the far end of the lane. Another procession, just as long, led by a line of black-coated Dissenting ministers, with their own band tramping behind them, had stepped into the lane at the exact same moment.

“Our doppelgänger?” Shirley laughed. “Looks like we’ve drawn a very unexpected card.”

“Looks like you’re about to get your wish for a battle, at least of the sharp-staring variety,” Caroline whispered back, giggling.

“We won’t give way an inch!” the curates all yelped at once.

“Give way?” Helstone rounded on them, sharp as a tack. “Who said anything about giving way? Boys, hold your tongues and stand straight. The ladies I know will stand firm for the honour of the Established Church. Miss Keeldar, what say you?”

“I say they’ve got abominable manners, cutting us off like this,” Shirley said, her eyes glinting. “And I hate bad manners. Of course they deserve a lesson.”

“A lesson in politeness, not rudeness,” Mr. Hall hurried to interject, ever the peacemaker.

But Helstone was already striding ahead, marching yards in front of his group, before the leader of the opposing party — a large, plump spirit merchant who was the most prominent Nonconformist in the district — called his own procession to a halt. He pulled out a hymn book, boomed out a verse, and his whole crowd started singing the slowest, dreariest canticle ever heard.

Helstone snapped his fingers at the band. The brass blared so loud the leaves on the hedgerows shook, and he called for *Rule Britannia*, waving the children to join in at the top of their lungs. The Dissenters’ hymn was drowned out completely, their voices swallowed up in the roar. Noise-wise, the church party had won handily.

“Now follow me!” Helstone called. “No running, just a steady, firm pace. Stay close, hold onto each other’s skirts if you have to.”

He strode forward with such unshakable purpose, the whole crowd behind him marching cool and solid as a wall, that the Dissenters first gaped, then panicked, then got pushed steadily backwards until they had no choice but to turn tail and scramble out of the far end of the lane. The plump hymn-singing merchant ended up sat right in the ditch, so covered in mud it was said he drank more water that afternoon than he had in the entire prior year. By the time the whole procession had cleared the lane, Shirley and Helstone were shaking hands, grinning like co-conspirators, and even meek Mr. Hall was smiling at the absurdity of it all.

By four o’clock, the whole party was back at the school. Long lines of benches were set out in the freshly mown surrounding fields, stacked with plump, jam-oozing currant buns and great steaming tins of sweet, hot tea. No one was stinting that day: every child got twice as much as they could possibly eat, the extra wrapped up to carry home for siblings or grandparents too sick or too old to make it to the feast. The musicians and singers got their own share of buns and beer, and once the benches were cleared, the kids ran wild playing games across the grass.

A bell called the patrons and ladies back inside the schoolroom. The white walls were draped in evergreens and wild roses, the tables laid out with polished silver teapots and bright painted porcelain, and the room hummed with soft, merry chatter, canaries trilling in their high-hung cages by the windows. Caroline took her place serving at the main front table, her long curls falling loose down her back, her modest muslin dress cool against her skin in the warmth.

Shirley sat just beside her, uncharacteristically quiet, her gaze darting to the door every few seconds. She kept spreading her satin skirt out across the empty spot next to her, tucking her gloves and embroidered handkerchief down on the bench to mark the space. When Caroline asked who she was waiting for, Shirley leaned in, her rosy lips almost brushing Caroline’s ear, her voice soft and honeyed with that private, tender thrill only Caroline had ever heard from her.

“I’m waiting for Mr. Moore. I saw him last night, made him promise to come with his sister Hortense and sit right here with us. He won’t break his word, I know he won’t — but if he’s late, all the seats will be taken before he gets here.”

The words had barely left her mouth when Squire Wynne the magistrate, his wife, and his two children filed into the room in all their finery, and his oafish son Sam plopped himself down right on top of Shirley’s gloves, her handkerchief, and the very spot she’d been saving. Sam had been making no secret of his intentions to court Shirley, and his father had even made a public fool of himself saying it would be such a grand match to combine the Fieldhead and De Walden estates. Shirley’s jaw tightened, and she shot Caroline a look that said she was plotting murder.

When Caroline finally got a chance to glance around the room once she’d finished serving the first round of tea, she spotted Hortense Moore in her familiar yellow bonnet and black silk dress, standing by the wall with quiet old Miss Mann. She half rose to run over and hug her old teacher, when a hand rested light on her shoulder, and a low, familiar voice whispered behind her ear: “Wait till after tea, Lina. I’ll bring her to you.”

Caroline looked up, and her breath caught. Robert Moore was standing right behind her, smiling at her eagerness, looking handsomer than she had ever seen him, his face lit with a warm, soft light she’d never noticed before — so bright it was like lightning had etched his image straight into her memory, sharp and vivid and impossible to look away from for long.

He moved to Shirley’s side, and she snapped at him immediately for being so tardy, cross that Sam Wynne had planted himself in her reserved spot. Moore said nothing, just stood there quiet and patient, until Shirley’s temper melted three minutes later, and she held out her hand to him. He took it, a tiny, amused shake of his head teasing her for her pettishness, his palm pressing gentle and warm against hers.

“There’s no spot left for you here,” Shirley told him, still half grumpy. “You’ll have to go sit over at Mrs. Boultby’s table, all the way across the room.”

Moore didn’t go. He lingered, wandering the room to greet other gentlemen who’d also been left standing without seats, but he kept drifting back to Shirley’s side, murmuring little observations that made her lips twitch despite herself.

Poor Sam Wynne, stuck next to her, soon found he could not be more miserable. Shirley fanned herself wildly, complained the air was stifling, announced she was about to faint from lack of space. When he offered to walk her outside, she snapped that he’d give her her death of cold. He swallowed his last sip of tea, and practically ran for the door.

The stout, wealthy corn factor who stepped forward to take the empty seat next was even less lucky. Shirley made a great sweep with her scarf, knocked her full teacup straight over, spilling tea all over her satin dress and the bench, and raised such a fuss calling for napkins and waiters that the bewildered man backed away, beat a hasty retreat, and did not dare come near the spot again.

Moore wandered back just in time, surveyed the chaos with that quiet half-smile of his, and sat down in the newly empty spot like it had been made exactly for him. Shirley’s scowl faded at once, all her sharp, peevish lines softening, and she turned to grumble that he’d put her to all that trouble, that now she’d have to go apologize to Sam Wynne and the corn factor tomorrow to make up for it. He only laughed low, quiet and calm, the easy equality of his bearing making him look far nobler than any wealthy landowner in the room, no trace of the poor struggling mill master to be seen in his steady, relaxed posture. Their heads bent close, murmuring to each other, and Shirley’s face softened, glowing, as if every word he said was a secret only the two of them got to hear.

Caroline grew faint from the stuffy warm air, and slipped outside soon after, finding Hortense Moore waiting for her. Her old teacher was stiff and formal at first, still cross with Mr. Helstone for separating Caroline from the Moores, but the second she saw how thin and pale Caroline had grown, her face softened, and she kissed her on both cheeks, fretting over her health. Soon after, Hortense left to walk the tired Miss Mann home, and Caroline was left standing alone, as the group of young village girls she’d been talking to were chased off to play games by the over-eager Sam Wynne.

She was just about to slip back into the quiet of the schoolhouse when Shirley spotted her from across the lawn, and came hurrying over to her side, linking their arms together.

“Let’s walk up to the top of the highest field,” Shirley said softly. “I know you hate crowds, Caroline.”

“Oh, but you don’t have to leave all those people fawning over you just to keep me company,” Caroline protested.

Shirley only smiled, and squeezed her arm tighter, the sound of the feast’s music and laughter fading soft behind them as they started up the sunlit path.
