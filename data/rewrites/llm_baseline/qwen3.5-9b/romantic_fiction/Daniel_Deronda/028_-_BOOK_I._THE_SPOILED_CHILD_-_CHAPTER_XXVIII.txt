"It is easier to know man in general than to know a particular man." — La Rochefoucauld.

An hour after Grandcourt departed, the significant news of Gwendolen's engagement rippled through the rectory. Mr. and Mrs. Gascoigne, accompanied by Anna, spent the evening at Offendene.

"My dear, allow me to congratulate you on securing such a profound attachment," the rector remarked warmly. "You appear quite grave, and I find no surprise in that; a lifelong union is a solemn undertaking. Yet, judging by Mr. Grandcourt's recent conduct and words, I believe we may already discern some silver lining emerging from our adversity. It has afforded you a rare opportunity to observe your future husband's delicate generosity."

Mr. Gascoigne subtly alluded to Grandcourt's manner of hinting that he would provide for Mrs. Davilow—a detail Gwendolen had recalled citing to her mother with precise accuracy during their earlier conversations.

"But I have no doubt that Mr. Grandcourt would have behaved with equal magnanimity had you not fled to Germany," Mrs. Gascoigne interjected, feeling compelled to fulfill her social duty on this occasion. "Had you remained engaged to him, as you surely might have been months ago, his conduct would undoubtedly have been impeccable. Now, however, there is no room for caprice; indeed, I trust you harbor no inclinations toward it. A woman owes a great debt of gratitude to a man who persists in offering himself despite obstacles. No doubt you feel this properly?"

"I am not entirely certain that I do, Aunt," Gwendolen replied with a saucy gravity. "I do not know precisely what feelings are deemed 'proper' when one becomes engaged."

The rector patted her shoulder, smiling as if at a piece of innocent mischief, while his wife interpreted his demeanor as a signal that she need not be displeased. As for Anna, she kissed Gwendolen and whispered, "I truly hope you will be happy," before sinking into the background, struggling vainly to hold back tears. In recent days, she had nurtured a small romance regarding Rex—imagining that if he still longed for Gwendolen, her heart might eventually soften under the weight of sorrow until they could marry. That fantasy had transformed into a prayer that she, Anna, might rejoice as a faithful sister, finding purpose only in serving Gwendolen as long as Rex lacked wealth. But now, she sought grace to celebrate something else entirely.

Miss Merry and the four young girls—Alice with her high shoulders, Bertha and Fanny the whisperers, and Isabel the attentive listener—were all present for this family occasion. Everything seemed to pivot appropriately around the honor and glory of Gwendolen, rendering real life as captivating as *Sir Charles Grandison*. The evening unfolded primarily through the rector's decisive remarks, responding to conjectures from the two elder ladies. According to him, the situation was not one where mentioning financial settlements was his duty; everything must, and doubtless would safely, be left to Mr. Grandcourt.

"I should like to know exactly what sort of places Ryelands and Gadsmere are," Mrs. Davilow asked curiously.

"Gadsmere, I believe, is a secondary residence," Mr. Gascoigne noted. "However, Ryelands is known to be one of our finest seats. The park is extensive, the woods of considerable value. The house was constructed by Inigo Jones, and the ceilings are painted in the Italian style. The estate is reportedly worth twelve thousand a year, and there are two livings, including a rectory, in the gift of the Grandcourts. There may be some burdens on the land, still, Mr. Grandcourt was an only child."

"It would be most remarkable," Mrs. Gascoigne exclaimed, "if he were to become Lord Stannery in addition to everything else. Only think: there is the Grandcourt estate, the Mallinger estate, *and* the baronetcy, *and* the peerage"—she counted off the items on her fingers, pausing on the fourth while adding, "though they say there will be no land accompanying the peerage." It seemed a pity there was nothing for the fifth finger.

"The peerage," the rector observed judiciously, "must be regarded as a remote possibility. There are two cousins between the current peer and Mr. Grandcourt. It is certainly a sobering reflection how death and other causes sometimes concentrate inheritances upon a single individual. However, such an excess ought to be deprecated. To be Sir Mallinger Grandcourt Mallinger—with corresponding properties—is a valuable talent sufficient for any man entrusted with it. Let us hope it will be well utilized."

"And what a position for the wife, Gwendolen!" Mrs. Gascoigne declared. "A great responsibility indeed. But you must lose no time in writing to Mrs. Mompert, Henry. It is fortunate you have an engagement to offer as an excuse; otherwise, she might feel deeply offended. She is rather a formidable woman."

*I am rid of that horror,* Gwendolen thought internally, to whom the name of Mompert had become a sort of mystical jargon. She remained remarkably silent throughout the evening, and that night she could scarcely sleep in her small white bed. It was a rarity in her robust youth to be wakeful; perhaps an even greater anomaly for her to be cautious enough to hide her restlessness from her mother. Her state of mind was entirely novel: she, who had always felt secure in herself and capable of managing others, had just taken a decisive step she had previously believed she would never take—or perhaps, was bound not to take. She could not retreat now; she liked much of what lay ahead, and there was nothing left to enjoy if she returned. Yet, her resolution was haunted by the shadow of that prior resolve, which had initially moved her entire being with undoubting certainty.

While lying on her pillow with wide-open eyes, "gazing on darkness which the blind do see," she was appalled by the realization that she was proceeding with what she had once fled from with repugnance. It was new to her that a question of right or wrong in her conduct should stir such terror; she had known no compunction that atoning caresses and gifts could not soothe. But here came a moment when a new consciousness was awakened. She seemed poised to adopt deliberately, as a guiding principle for the rest of her life, what she had rashly proclaimed in her bitterness when her discovery drove her away to Leubronn: that it mattered not what she did; she merely needed to amuse herself as best she could. That lawlessness, that abandonment of all concern for justification, suddenly terrified her. It arrived with the shadowy array of possible calamity trailing behind it—calamity that had ceased to be a mere abstract name for her. All the infiltrated influences of neglected religious teaching, alongside deeper impressions of something awful and inexorable enveloping her, seemed to converge in the vague concept of an avenging power.

The brilliant position she had longed for, the imagined freedom she envisioned creating through marriage, the deliverance from the dull insignificance of her girlhood—all stood immediately before her. Yet, they approached her like tainted food, carrying the flavor of sacrilege, which she must snatch with trembling fear. In the darkness and loneliness of her little bed, her more resistant self could not withstand the first onslaught of dread following her irrevocable decision. That unhappy-faced woman and her children—Grandcourt and his relations with her—repeated themselves in her imagination like the clinging memory of a disgrace, gradually erasing all other thought, leaving only the awareness that she had dragged those scenes into her life. Her prolonged wakefulness seemed a delirium; a faint, faint light penetrated beside the window curtain; the chill intensified. She could bear it no longer and cried, "Mamma!"

"Yes, dear," Mrs. Davilow responded instantly, her voice awake.

"Let me come to you."

Soon she fell asleep on her mother's shoulder, sleeping until late. Dreaming of a brightly lit ballroom, she opened her eyes to find her mother standing by the bedside holding a small packet.

"I am sorry to wake you, darling, but I thought it better to give this to you immediately. The groom has brought *Criterion*; he has come on another horse and says he is to stay here."

Gwendolen sat up in bed and opened the packet. Inside was a delicate enameled casket containing a magnificent diamond ring, along with a letter enclosing a folded piece of colored paper bearing these words:

*"Pray wear this ring when I come at twelve in sign of our betrothal. I enclose a check drawn in the name of Mr. Gascoigne for immediate expenses. Of course Mrs. Davilow will remain at Offendene, at least for some time. I hope, when I come, you will have granted me an early day, when you may begin to command me at a shorter distance.—Yours devotedly, H. M. GRANDCOURT."*

The check amounted to five hundred pounds. Gwendolen turned it toward her mother along with the letter.

"How very kind and delicate!" Mrs. Davilow said with deep emotion. "But I really prefer not to be dependent on a son-in-law. Myself and the girls could manage very well."

"Mamma, if you say that again, I will not marry him," Gwendolen snapped angrily.

"My dear child, I trust you are not marrying solely for my sake," Mrs. Davilow pleaded, trying to deflect the blow.
