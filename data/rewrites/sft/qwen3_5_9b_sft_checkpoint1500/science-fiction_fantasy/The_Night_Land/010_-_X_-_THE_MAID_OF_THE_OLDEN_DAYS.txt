In an instant, the abyss of my despair swelled into a vast, blinding gladness, a hope so potent it seemed I would clasp my beloved in moments. Yet, I knew this was a delusion, a fleeting illusion unlikely to yield swift satisfaction. The truth, sharp and immediate, pierced through: I saw nothing but the silhouette of a colossal pyramid ascending into the velvet void of the night.

I understood instantly that this monolith stood atop a hill in the heart of that desolate country; only elevation could grant it such grandeur amidst the surrounding dark. With a surge of momentum, I sprinted downward, plunging into the land to race toward the Pyramid.

For mere minutes I ran, and then—*crash*. I fell headlong, my spine striking the ground with a violence that felt like a snapped neck. Pain, searing and absolute, seized me, rendering me immobile. I lay there, a broken heap, helpless and whimpering, vulnerable to any predator that might stumble upon me.

Slowly, however, the agony receded. I sat up, clutching my neck, and eventually rose again. This time, I moved with caution, a heavy anxiety weighing down my heart. How could the Pyramid be utterly dark if it were truly the Lesser Refuge? A chilling suspicion bloomed within me: perhaps it was a dwelling of Evil, a trap designed to bewilder my sight with a false pretense. Yet, looking closely at its form against the distant, flickering fires of the land, the reality became plain. It *was* the Lesser Refuge.

At first, seeing the dark shape, I had been devoid of reason, driven only by a frantic, blind urgency born of months of searching. I had intended to summon Naani via the Master-Word, to broadcast my arrival through the brain-elements. But now, prudence reigned. I needed to verify the nature of this darkness before risking a futile call.

So, I descended again, initially with careful steps, but soon fueled by a fierce, renewed eagerness. The dulling effect of my fall had worn off, replaced by a singular focus. I had spent thirteen hours climbing the volcanic plateau, and now, traversing downhill in ten, I had gained significant speed, despite the lingering tremors in my limbs.

After four hours, I emerged onto the great plain of the land. The Refuge itself was hidden behind the curvature of the hill, obscured by the deepening shadows of the night, but I could discern the massive bulk separating me from the far-off glimmers. These lights, now fading, marked the vicinity of the hill where the Pyramid stood.

Another four hours passed. I crossed fields punctuated by glowing fissures—fire-holes—that cast a dim, reddish hue over the landscape, preventing total darkness. As I approached the base of the hill, the distant lights vanished completely, swallowed by the encroaching blackness of the hillside. I realized I was nearing the summit. Another hour remained.

During these final five hours, descending from the volcano, the night had echoed with strange noises: the skittering of runners, a guttural roar akin to a giant, and a horrid, piercing scream that chilled my blood.

I began the ascent. At first, a wild excitement surged through me; I nearly shouted Naani's name into the void, hoping for a reply. But as I climbed, the fervor faded, replaced by a cold, creeping caution. Something gnawed at my spirit, a warning that my conscious mind refused to acknowledge.

Almost at the top, after three grueling hours, the grim silhouette of the Pyramid revealed itself. It rose silently and desolately into the night, stripped of all life. A terrible shaking fear gripped me. My intuition whispered a cruel truth: there was no human soul within that dark bulk. Instead, monstrous, horrid entities awaited inside, forces destined to devour my soul.

Turning back, I descended quietly into the darkness, fleeing the place. Four hours later, I was clear of the hill, yet a profound desolation settled over me. There was no sanctuary in this land. Blindly, I wandered, lost in a haze of uncertainty.

Presently, I reached the shore of the Olden Sea. I had no memory of arriving there, for it seemed leagues away, yet the geography suggested the dried seabed curved around this point, or perhaps multiple ancient seas existed in this Country of Night.

I collapsed, weak and bewildered, my heart feeling dead within my chest. My spirit told me with terrifying certainty that evil resided in the dark Pyramid. The Peoples of the Lesser Refuge had perished, slaughtered by vile creatures and malevolent powers. If this were true, I was far too late to save the Maid. A strange, morbid relief washed over me: I would welcome any evil force that allowed me to fight and die quickly. In this wasteland, life offered no joy.

Sitting there, I weighed the wisdom and folly of my thoughts. I had not been *sure* the dark Pyramid was the Refuge, yet my spirit knew with absolute conviction.

Suddenly, a thought struck me: I must send the Master-Word. Was there any other way to know if Naani lived? Though I held little hope, I remembered faint beats of the Word echoing from the darkness during my journey. If she did not answer, and an Evil Power instead sought to destroy me, at least I would find peace in my death, ending my heartache.

I rose, scanning the black expanse, and sent the Master-Word through the brain-elements. Immediately, I called Naani's name three times, projecting the call into the void.

Lo! Out of the mystery of the night, a low, solemn beat of the Master-Word resonated. Within my brain, a tiny, lonely, faint voice emerged, as if drifting from the edge of the world. It was the voice of Naani and the voice of Mirdath, calling me by my old love-name.

Choking back utter affright mixed with overwhelming joy, I felt my despair vanish. Naani lived. She called to me through the brain-elements. I had not heard the voice of my own for an eternity of grim labor and dread.

Her voice sounded as if coming from a distant corner of the earth. While dazed by the joy of her survival, I sensed the terrible distance between us and the perils she faced. Before I could reach her side to defend her life and secure my own joy, I needed to know what threatened her.

Instantly, my spirit warned me: someone was nearby, concealed in the bushes beside a burning fire-hole. I made no response to the Maid across the dark of the world. Instead, I slipped swiftly into the dense brush near the firehole.

Peering through the foliage, I saw a small figure kneeling and sobbing on the earth beside the embers. It was a slender maiden, her desperation evident in every tear as she wept. And then, my soul *Knew*, in a single, blinding flash. She was unaware of the cry of the spirit that had reached her, thinking it emanated from the desolation of the night—or perhaps from the Mighty Pyramid. Often, she had cried out to me in those lonely months, receiving no answer. Her weakness was so great she lacked the power to project the Word strongly or to clarify her spiritual cries through the vast spaces of the aether.

I drew a sharp breath, steadying my jaw. From the cover of the bush, I spoke natural human words: "MIRDATH."

The Maid ceased her weeping. She looked around with a newfound, terrified hope, her tears shimmering in the firelight. I pushed aside the branches and stepped out, revealing myself in my grey armor. I paused, suspended in a turmoil of emotion. My heart screamed to take her into my arms, to embrace Mirdath after an eternity of loss. Yet, I hesitated. Truly, she was Naani and she was Mirdath, but she was a stranger to my eyes, shaken by woe, grief, and sorrow.

In that same moment, she screamed, recoiling from me, trying weakly to flee into the nearest bushes. She did not recognize the human man standing before her, mistaking him for a monster. Instantly, I spoke the Master-Word aloud, granting her knowledge of peace and aid. I declared my name, stating, "I am That One."

Recognition flooded her face. She cried out in a broken voice, rushing toward me, thrusting her small hands into my grasp. She fell, weeping and shivering, and I struggled to soothe her, holding her hands firmly, careful not to crush them with armored gloves.

She leaned against me, frail and childlike. Soon, her sobbing subsided into ragged breaths. I realized she suffered from starvation; weeks of wandering alone had brought her to the brink. She stood silent, trembling, unable to command her mouth to speak. I opened my left hand, examining the skin within my palm—it was unnaturally thin and wasted. Without delay, I lifted her, setting her gently upon a smooth rock propped against the firehole.

I stripped off my cloak and wrapped it around her. Her clothes were tattered, torn by the brambles, offering scant protection against the biting cold. She shivered violently, racked by both the chill and the crushing weight of weakness, near death by starvation and isolation.
