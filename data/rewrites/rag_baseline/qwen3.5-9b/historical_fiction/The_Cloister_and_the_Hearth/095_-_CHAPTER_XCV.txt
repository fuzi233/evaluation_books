The hermit's gaze darted frantically between the slumbering infant and Margaret, his eyes wide with a shock that bordered on madness, fueled by the sheer impossibility of her return. In his left arm, the child slept peacefully; at his right knee stood Margaret, transformed from the pale, trembling girl he had dominated mere hours prior into an imperious vision of beauty. Her cheeks burned with color, her eyes sparkled with a triumphant light, and her lips parted in a sweetness that radiated a new, unreadable emotion: maternal pride.

He stared, transfixed, his wonder-stricken eyes oscillating endlessly between the sleeping form and the woman holding it.

"Us?" he gasped, the word tearing from his throat. Even as he spoke, his gaze continued to wander, unable to reconcile the reality before him.

Surprised in her own turn, Margaret cried out, her voice echoing the chaotic nature of an age defined by fleeting impressions rather than solid facts. "What!" she exclaimed. "Does not a father recognize his own child? And a man of God, too? Fie, Gerard, to pretend! Nay, thou art too wise, too virtuous not to know—why, I watched thee! Look at you both now! It is thine own flesh and blood held to thy heart."

Clement trembled violently. "What words are these?" he stammered, his voice breaking. "Is this... is this my angel?"

"Whose else? Since he is mine."

Turning his gaze upon the sleeping child, Clement displayed an expression beyond the power of ink to capture, his entire frame shaking as his eyes seemed to drink in the tiny vessel of love. Margaret's eyes followed his trajectory. "He bears no resemblance to me," she declared proudly. "Yet, at intervals, he is thy very image in miniature; observe this golden hair. Thine was precisely this hue at his age; ask thy mother elsewhere. And behold this mole upon his little finger. Now look at thine own; there! 'Twas thy mother who informed me thou wast marked so before him. And oh, Gerard, 'twas this very child who found thee for me! By that small mark upon thy finger, I recognized thee as his father when I watched above thy window and saw thee feeding the birds." Seizing the child's hand, she kissed it eagerly, pressing half of it into her mouth—a gesture born of Heaven-knows-what desperation—"Ah! Bless thee, thou didst find thy poor daddy for her, and now thou hast restored us to friendship after our brief quarrel; the first, the last. Thou wast very cruel to me but now, my poor Gerard, yet I forgive thee; for the sake of loving thy child."

"Ahh! Ah! Ah! Ah! Ah!" sobbed Clement, choking on his grief. Lowered by fasting and unnerved by prolonged solitude, the once-strong man succumbed to hysteria, teetering on the brink of fainting.

Margaret felt alarm rise within her, yet her experience tempered her fear with profound pity. "Nay, do not distress thyself so," she murmured soothingly, placing a gentle hand upon his brow. "Be brave! So, so. Dear heart, thou art not the first man to depart abroad and return enriched by a lovely little self more than he departed. As a man of God, take courage, and declare that He sends thee this to comfort thee for what thou hast lost in me; and that is not so grievous, my lamb; for surely the better part of love shall never cool toward thee, though it may in thine, and ought, being a priest and parson of Gouda."

"I? Priest of Gouda? Never!" Clement whispered in a faint voice. "I am a friar of St. Dominic: yet speak on, sweet music, tell me all that has befallen thee before we are parted again."

Some might have protested the necessity of parting at all, raising the eternal question of separation. Yet women like Margaret do not repeat their mistakes. It is exceedingly difficult to defeat them twice when their hearts are set upon a thing.

She assented, turning her back on the Gouda manse as if it were a place not worth returning to, and began her tale. She dwelt above all on the kindness shown to her by his parents. As she recounted her tribulations, his hand instinctively sought hers; often she felt him wince and tremble with suppressed rage, and often he pressed her hand, sympathizing with her in every vein.

"Oh, piteous tale of a true heart battling alone against such bitter odds," he said.

"It all seems small now that I see thee here again, nursing my boy. We have received a warning, Gerard. True friends like you and me are rare, and it is madness to part ere death divides us."

"And that is true," Clement admitted, caught off guard.

Then she urged him to recount his sufferings for her sake. He begged her to excuse him, and she consented; yet by asking questions, she quietly revoked his request and elicited the truth. Many a sigh she heaved for him, and more than once she hid her face in her hands, terrified by the perils he had faced, though they were now past. To console him for everything he had endured, she knelt, sliding her arms beneath the little boy to lift him gently. "Kiss him softly," she whispered. "Again, again, kiss thy fill if thou canst; he is sound. 'Tis all I can do to comfort thee until thou art out of this foul den and in thy sweet manse yonder."

Clement shook his head.

"Well," she said, "let that pass. Know that I have been sorely affronted for the lack of my lines."

"Who hath dared affront thee?"

"No matter; those who will do it again if thou hast lost them, which the saints forbid."

"I lose them? Nay, there they lie, close to thy hand."

"Where, where, oh, where?"

Clement hung his head. "Look in the Vulgate. Heaven forgive me: I thought thou wert dead, a saint in heaven."

She looked, and upon the blank leaves of the poor soul's Bible, she found her marriage lines.

"Thank God!" she cried. "Thank God! Oh, bless thee, Gerard, bless thee! Why, what is here, Gerard?"

On the other leaves were pinned every scrap of paper she had ever sent him—their two names once written together in jest, the lock of her hair she had given him, half a silver coin she had shared with him, and a single straw she had used to suck her soup the first day he ever saw her.

When Margaret beheld these proofs of love and signs of a gentle heart bereft, even her exultation at recovering her marriage lines was overwhelmed by gushing tenderness. She almost staggered, her hand flying to her bosom, leaning her brow against the stone cell wall and weeping so silently that he did not perceive her tears; indeed, she refused to let him see, feeling that to befriend him now she must remain the stronger, for emotion weakens resolve.

"Gerard," she said, "I know you are wise and good. You must have a reason for what you are doing, however unreasonable it may seem. Let us talk like old friends. Why are you buried alive?"

"Margaret, to escape temptation. My impious ire against those two had its root in the heart; that heart I must now deaden, and, Dei gratia, I shall. Shall I, a servant of Christ and of the Church, court temptation? Shall I pray daily to be led into it, and walk into it with open eyes?"

"That is good sense anyway," Margaret remarked, with a consummate affectation of candor.

"'Tis unanswerable," Clement sighed.

"We shall see. Tell me, have you escaped temptation here? Why I ask is, when I am alone, my thoughts become far more wild and foolish than in company. Nay, speak sooth; come!"

"I must confess I have been worse tempted here with evil imaginations than in the world."

"There now."

"Ay, but so were Anthony and Jerome, Macarius and Hilarion, Benedict, Bernard, and all the saints. 'Twill wear off."

"How do you know?"

"I feel sure it will."

"Guessing against knowledge. Here 'tis men who are sillier than we women. Wise in their own conceits, they refuse to see; their stomachs are too high to be taught by their eyes. A woman, if she went into a hole in a bank to escape temptation and found it there, would simply lift her farthingale and step out upon it, not even knowing how wise she was until she watched a man in like plight."

"Nay, I grant humility and a teachable spirit are the roads to wisdom; but when all is said, here I wrestle but with imagination. At Gouda, she I love as no priest or monk must love any save the angels; she will tempt a weak soul, unwilling, yet not loath to be tempted."

"Ay, that is another matter; I should tempt thee then? To what, i' God's name?"

"Who knows? The flesh is weak."
