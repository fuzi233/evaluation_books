Elinor stepped back, her face pale with shock at the sight of him. Her first instinct was to flee—to turn and leave the room without a word. Her hand had already reached for the door latch when he rushed forward, his voice sharp with urgency rather than pleading.

“Miss Dashwood,” he said, “just half an hour—ten minutes—I beg you, stay.”

“No, sir,” she replied firmly, refusing to be swayed. “I shall not stay. Your business cannot possibly concern me. Perhaps the servants forgot to tell you that Mr. Palmer is not at home.”

“Even if they’d told me,” he burst out, “that Mr. Palmer and every soul in his family were damned to hell, I wouldn’t have turned away. My business is with you—and only you.”

“With *me*?” she echoed, stunned. “Well, sir—be quick—and if you can—less passionate.”

“Sit down,” he insisted, “and I’ll be both.”

She hesitated. The thought of Colonel Brandon arriving unannounced crossed her mind. But she had promised to listen, and now curiosity wove itself with duty. After a brief moment of resolve, she decided silence and speed were best. She walked quietly to the table and sat down. He took the chair opposite, and for half a minute neither spoke.

“Please be quick, sir,” Elinor urged, impatient. “I have no time to spare.”

He sat motionless, deep in thought, as if he hadn’t heard her.

“Your sister,” he said abruptly, “is out of danger. I heard from the servant. God be praised!—But is it true? Is it really true?”

Elinor remained silent.

He pressed on, more urgently: “For heaven’s sake, tell me—is she out of danger or not?”

“We hope so,” she murmured.

He rose and paced the room.

“If I’d known this half an hour ago—but since I’m here,” he said, returning to his seat with forced cheerfulness, “what does it matter? For once, Miss Dashwood—it may well be the last time—let us be cheerful together. I’m in a fine mood for laughter. Tell me honestly,” he added, his cheeks flushing slightly, “do you think me more of a rogue or a fool?”

Elinor stared at him, bewildered. She began to suspect he was drunk—the oddness of his visit, his manner, seemed explicable only by liquor. With that idea, she stood up at once.

“Mr. Willoughby,” she said, “I advise you to return to Combe immediately. I cannot stay any longer. Whatever your purpose, it will be better served tomorrow, when you’ve had time to think.”

“I understand you,” he replied, smiling with quiet composure. “Yes, I am very drunk. A pint of porter with my cold beef at Marlborough was enough to unhinge me.”

“At Marlborough!” she exclaimed, utterly confused.

“Yes,” he said. “I left London this morning at eight o’clock. The only ten minutes I’ve spent outside my chaise since then were for a meal at Marlborough.”

His steady gaze and clear expression convinced her that, whatever folly had brought him to Cleveland, it wasn’t drunkenness. After a pause, she said, “Mr. Willoughby, you ought to know—*I* do—that after everything that has passed, your coming here like this, forcing yourself upon my notice, demands a most serious explanation. What is your purpose?”

“I mean,” he said, with quiet intensity, “if I can, to make you hate me just a little less than you do now. I mean to offer some kind of explanation, some kind of apology—for what I’ve done. To open my heart completely, and show you that though I’ve always been a fool, I’ve never been entirely a villain. And perhaps, through that, to earn something close to forgiveness—from your sister.”

“Is this truly why you’ve come?”

“Upon my soul, it is,” he answered, his tone so sincere it stirred memories of the man she once knew. Despite herself, she found his honesty almost believable.

“If that is all,” she said, “then you may be satisfied already. Marianne *has* forgiven you—she has long forgiven you.”

“Has she?” he asked, his voice filled with sudden fervor. “Then she forgave me too soon. But she shall forgive me again—on better grounds. Now, will you hear me?”

Elinor nodded.

“I don’t know,” he began, after a pause heavy with expectation, “how you’ve interpreted my behavior toward your sister—or what dark motive you’ve imagined me capable of. You may not think more kindly of me after this—but it’s worth trying. You shall hear everything. When I first became acquainted with your family, I had no other intention than to pass my time pleasantly while I stayed in Devonshire—more pleasantly than ever before. Your sister’s beauty, her charm, could not fail to please me. And her kindness toward me, from the very beginning—how strange it seems now, when I reflect on how much she gave, and how little I returned! At first, I confess, it was only vanity that responded. I cared nothing for her happiness, thinking only of my own amusement. I indulged feelings I’d long grown used to indulging, doing everything I could to win her affection—without ever meaning to return it.”

At this, Elinor looked at him with scornful contempt and interrupted:

“It’s hardly worth continuing, Mr. Willoughby. Such a beginning cannot lead to anything worthwhile. Please—don’t make me suffer further by hearing more.”

“I insist,” he said, “you hear the whole truth.” He paused, then continued. “My fortune was never large. I’d always lived beyond my means, surrounded by people wealthier than myself. Every year since I came of age—even before—had added to my debts. Though Mrs. Smith’s death was meant to free me, it was uncertain and possibly years off. So for some time, I’d intended to restore my fortunes through marriage to a woman of means. Therefore, attaching myself to your sister was never a possibility. And yet—with selfishness, cruelty, and meanness beyond reproach—I pursued her affections, never intending to give them back. But one thing must be said: even in that state of selfish vanity, I didn’t know the full harm I was causing—because I didn’t *then* know what it meant to love. But have I ever truly loved? It may well be doubted. For if I had, could I have sacrificed my own feelings to pride and greed? Could I have sacrificed hers? Yet I did. To avoid a poverty that her love would have made bearable, I chose wealth—and lost everything that could have made life meaningful.”

“So,” Elinor said, her voice softening slightly, “you once believed you were attached to her?”

“To resist such beauty, such tenderness? Is there any man alive who could have done it? Yes—I found myself, gradually, sincerely fond of her. The happiest hours of my life were those I spent with her when I believed my intentions were honorable, my feelings pure. Even then, though I’d fully resolved to declare my love, I foolishly delayed—afraid to commit while my circumstances were so tangled. I won’t argue now—nor will I let you dwell on the absurdity of hesitating when honor already bound me. But the result proves I was a cunning fool, carefully preparing a way to make myself despised and miserable forever. Finally, I made up my mind. I intended, as soon as I could speak with her alone, to justify my attentions and openly declare my love. But—just before that chance came—a disaster struck. A terrible discovery was made.”

He hesitated, looking down.

“Mrs. Smith somehow learned—perhaps through a distant relation who wished to remove me from her favor—of a past connection, a relationship—but I needn’t explain further,” he added, glancing at her with flushed cheeks and searching eyes. “You’ve likely heard the whole story already.”

“I have,” Elinor replied, blushing herself, her heart hardening anew against pity. “I’ve heard it all. And how you expect to excuse any part of your guilt in that dreadful affair… I confess, I cannot comprehend.”

“Remember,” he said quickly, “from whom you heard it. Could it have been fair? I admit her situation and character deserved respect. I don’t seek to justify myself—but I can’t let you believe I have no defense. Just because she was wronged doesn’t mean she was blameless. And just because I was reckless doesn’t mean she was perfect. Her passion, her lack of judgment—though I don’t defend myself—her love for me deserved better treatment. I often recall, with deep shame, the tenderness she once inspired in me. I wish—I truly wish—she had never felt it. But I harmed more than her. I harmed someone whose affection for me—may I say it?—was nearly as strong as hers. Whose mind—oh, how infinitely superior!”

“But your indifference toward that poor girl,” Elinor said sharply, “your callous neglect—no weakness of understanding excuses that. You must have known she was reduced to destitution while you enjoyed yourself in Devonshire, chasing new pleasures, always happy, always gay.”

“But I didn’t know it,” he protested warmly. “I didn’t remember failing to give her my address. Common sense should have told her how to find me.”

“And what did Mrs. Smith say?”

“She accused me outright. My confusion was unbearable. Her purity, her strict morals, her ignorance of the world—everything worked against me. I couldn’t deny the truth, and every attempt to soften it failed. She’d already suspected my general immorality and was dissatisfied with the little attention I’d given her during this visit. In short, it ended in a complete rupture. One thing might have saved me—when she offered, in her moral zeal, to forgive the past if I married Eliza. That I could not do—and I was formally cast out of her favor and her home.”

“The night after,” he continued, “the night I was to leave the next day, I spent in agony, deciding my future. The struggle was great—but it ended too soon. My love for Marianne, my certainty of her love for me—it was all outweighed by my fear of poverty, by false notions of wealth that I’d inherited and society had reinforced. I believed I could secure my current wife if I chose to propose. I convinced myself that nothing else remained but prudence. But before I left Devonshire, a painful scene awaited me—I was supposed to dine with you that day. An apology was needed. But whether to write it or deliver it in person was a long debate. To see Marianne would be agonizing—I even doubted I could see her again and keep my resolution. Yet I underestimated my own courage. I went. I saw her. I saw her suffering—and I left her suffering, leaving her hoping never to see me again.”

“Why did you come, Mr. Willoughby?” Elinor asked, her voice full of reproach. “A note would have sufficed. Why was it necessary to appear?”

“It was necessary to my pride,” he said. “I couldn’t bear to leave the country in a way that might make you or others suspect what had happened between Mrs. Smith and me. So I decided to call at the cottage on my way to Honiton. But seeing your sister—seeing her—was truly dreadful. And worse, I found her alone. You were all gone—I don’t know where. I’d left her only the evening before, so certain, so firm in my resolve to do right! A few hours were to bind us forever. I remember how joyful I felt walking from the cottage to Allenham—pleased with myself, delighted with everyone! But in this final meeting, I approached her burdened with guilt that nearly stripped me of the ability to pretend. Her sorrow, her disappointment, her deep regret when I told her I had to leave so suddenly—I shall never forget it—combined with her trust, her faith in me! Oh God—what a heartless rascal I was!”

They sat in silence for a moment. Then Elinor spoke.

“Did you tell her you’d return soon?”

“I don’t know what I said,” he replied, impatient. “Less than I owed her, certainly—and likely more than I could promise. I can’t bear to think of it. Then came your dear mother—her kindness, her confidence—torturing me further. Thank Heaven, it *did* torture me. I was miserable. Miss Dashwood, you cannot imagine how comforting it is to look back on my own misery. I hold such a grudge against myself for the stupid, shameful folly of my heart that all my past suffering now feels like triumph.”

He paused.

“Well, sir,” Elinor said, though she pitied him, growing restless for his departure, “is that all?”

“All?” he said. “No—have you forgotten what happened in town? That infamous letter? Did she show it to you?”

“Yes,” she said. “I read every note.”

“When her first letter reached me—immediately, since I was in London the entire time—my feelings…” He stopped. “In plain words—my feelings were excruciating. Every line, every word—were, in the old metaphor she’d disapprove of, daggers to my heart. To learn Marianne was in town—was a thunderbolt. Thunderbolts and daggers!—What a rebuke she’d give me! Her taste, her opinions—I know them better than my own, and they are dearer.”

Elinor’s heart, which had shifted many times during this strange conversation, softened again. Yet she felt compelled to stop him from slipping into sentimental regrets.

“This is not right, Mr. Willoughby,” she said gently. “You are married. Speak only what you feel is necessary for me to hear.”

“Marianne’s letter,” he said, “assured me she still loved me as deeply as ever—despite weeks apart, still constant, still trusting. That awakened all my remorse. I say awakened, because time and London, business and distraction, had dulled it. I’d begun to grow hardened, convincing myself I was indifferent, pretending she too must have moved on. I dismissed our past attachment as trivial, shrugging it off, silencing every pang with the secret thought: ‘I’ll be glad to hear she’s happily married.’ But her letter showed me the truth. I realized she was infinitely dearer to me than any other woman—and that I was betraying her horribly. But by then, everything was settled with Miss Grey. Retreat was impossible. All I could do was avoid you both. I sent no reply to Marianne, hoping to cut off contact. For a time, I even refused to call in Berkeley Street. But finally, I judged it wiser to appear casually cool. So I waited until you were all safely out of the house, then left my name.”

“You watched us leave?”

“Yes. You’d be surprised how often I watched you, how close I came to meeting you. I entered shops just to avoid seeing your carriage pass. Living in Bond Street, I saw you nearly every day. Only constant vigilance kept us apart. I avoided the Middletons and anyone else who might connect us. But I accidentally met Sir John on his first day in town, the day after I’d called on Mrs. Jennings. He invited me to a party—dancing at his house that evening. Had he not mentioned you and your sister would be there, I wouldn’t have dared go near him. The next morning, another short note arrived—still tender, open, trusting—everything that made my actions abhorrent. I couldn’t answer it. I tried—but couldn’t form a single sentence. But I thought of her—every moment. If you can pity me, Miss Dashwood, pity me then. With my mind and heart full of your sister, I was forced to play the happy lover to another woman! Those weeks were worse than any torment. Eventually, as you know, you were thrust upon me. And what a spectacle I made! What an evening of agony! Marianne—beautiful as an angel—calling me Willoughby with such a tone! Oh God—holding out her hand, asking for an explanation, her eyes pleading so desperately! And Sophia, jealous as the devil, watching from the other side—well, it doesn’t matter now. It’s over. That was the last time I saw her—the last image I carry. She was white as death. Horrible—but today, when I thought of her dying, it was a comfort to know exactly how she appeared to those who saw her last. She was always before me—on my journey, in that same look, that same pallor.”

They sat in silence again, each lost in thought. Then Willoughby broke it.

“Well, let me go. Your sister is certainly better, surely out of danger?”

“We are assured of it.”

“And your mother—so devoted to Marianne?”

“But the letter, Mr. Willoughby—your own letter—have you nothing to say about it?”

“Yes—yes, especially that. Your sister wrote again the next morning—you saw what she said. I was breakfasting at the Ellisons when her letter, among others, was delivered from my lodgings. It caught Sophia’s eye before mine. Its size, the paper, the handwriting—immediately aroused her suspicion. She’d heard rumors of my attachment to a young lady in Devonshire, and the events of the previous evening had revealed who she was. Jealousy flared. With playful pretense—so charming when one loves her—she opened the letter and read it. She was punished for her boldness. She read what made her miserable. I could have borne her misery—but her rage, her malice—It had to be appeased. And so—what do you think of my wife’s style of writing? Delicate? Tender? Truly feminine?”

“Your wife?” Elinor said. “The letter was in your hand.”

“Yes—but I merely copied sentences I was ashamed to sign. The original was hers—her thoughts, her grace. But what could I do? We were engaged, everything arranged, the date nearly set. In truth—honest truth—her money was essential. In my position, anything was permissible to prevent a break. And in the end, what did it matter to Marianne or her friends how I phrased it? The goal was clear. I had to declare myself a scoundrel—whether with dignity or defiance mattered little. ‘I am ruined forever in their eyes,’ I thought. ‘Shut out from their company. They already think me unprincipled; this letter will only make them think me a blackguard.’” He paused, then added, “So I copied her words, and parted with the last traces of Marianne. Her three letters—unluckily, they were all in my pocketbook, or I might have denied them, kept them forever—I had to surrender them. I couldn’t even kiss them. And the lock of hair—always carried with me—now searched for by her with cruel sweetness—dear lock—every memento torn from me.”
