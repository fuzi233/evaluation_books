Beneath the trembling hand that penned these final words, a letter rests, heavy with ink and silence. It bears the seal of Dr. Hesselius, a scholar whose mind has long been the labyrinth through which we navigate the uncanny. In his own treatise, he approached this matter with the cold precision of a surgeon dissecting a corpse, stripping away the flesh of superstition to reveal the bone beneath—a duality of existence where the living and the dead walk together, blurred by shadows that refuse to fade. He called it "arcana," those profound secrets hidden within the fabric of our souls, yet here, stripped of his academic armor, the truth feels less like a discovery and more like a confession.

I hold this manuscript now, a relic from a time when the world felt wider and stranger. My first impulse was to reach out, to resurrect the ghost of the woman who first told him her tale, to question her about the nights when the moon hung low and the air grew thick with something ancient. But fate, cruel and swift, had already cut the thread. She is gone, taken by a fever that burned away the light from her eyes, leaving behind only a silence as vast as the grave. Her voice, once so clear, is lost to the dust of the past.

So, I am left alone with what remains. This narrative, written with a conscience that trembled at every step, stands as the only witness to what happened. It is a story of a girl who did not age, of a love that fed on blood and shadow, and of a friendship that turned into a nightmare. There is no need for me to explain the theories of the doctor who studied it; they would only clutter the horror with jargon. The terror is simple, raw, and terrible enough without the weight of philosophy. Let the story speak for itself, unburdened by the learned musings of men who try to cage the infinite. For some doors, once opened, cannot be closed again, and some stories, once read, change the reader forever.

We dwell within the walls of a *Schloss* in Styria, a fortress that speaks less of magnificence and more of isolation. In this land, a modest fortune stretches far further than it ever would in England; eight or nine hundred pounds annually secure a life of considerable ease. My father, an Englishman of retirement, purchased this feudal seat and its surrounding estate—a bargain, I am told—upon the strength of a pension and ancestral lands. Here, amidst a landscape where costs are vanishingly low, wealth seems almost superfluous to comfort.

The setting is nothing short of gothic solitude. Our castle sits atop a slight eminence, hemmed in by a dense forest. Before us lies a road, ancient and narrow, crossing a drawbridge that has never seen the need to rise in my lifetime. Below, a moat teems with perch, traversed by swans and carpeted with fleets of white water-lilies. Above the water, the *Schloss* unfolds: a façade of many windows, flanked by towers and crowned by a Gothic chapel.

Before the gate, the woods break into an irregular, moonlit clearing. To the right, a steep stone bridge arches over a stream that cuts a dark, serpentine path through the timber. This is a desolation of the highest order. From the hall door, the forest sprawls fifteen miles to the east and twelve to the west. The nearest living village lies seven miles to the north-west; the closest *Schloss* with any historical weight belongs to the late General Spielsdorf, twenty miles to the south-east.

Yet, I must speak of a ruin closer at hand. Only three miles westward, nestled between the directions of Spielsdorf and our own gate, lies a deserted village. Its quaint church stands roofless, the aisles echoing with the dust of moldering tombs belonging to the Karnsteins—a proud family now extinguished, like their equally forsaken château that looms silently above the silent ruins of the town. Legends whisper of the curse that drove them away, stories I save for another time.

For now, consider the inhabitants of our domain. If we exclude servants and dependents who live in the annexed wings, the count is chillingly small. My father, kind but aging, and I, at nineteen, were the sole family. Eight years have passed since I wrote this account. My mother, a Styrian, died when I was an infant, a void filled only by Madame Perrodon, a Swiss governess from Bern whose benevolent presence became the anchor of my childhood. She formed a trinity with my father and myself at dinner. A fourth figure, Mademoiselle De Lafontaine, a "finishing" governess fluent in both German and French, completed the circle. We spoke a chaotic Babel of tongues—broken English, fractured French, stilted German—which amused travelers but bound us tightly. Occasionally, young ladies of my age visited for seasons, returning the courtesy, but ultimately, my life was a tapestry woven from solitude.

Even with the influence of my governesses, I was a girl accustomed to indulgence, the spoiling of a daughter whose only parent doted upon her. Yet, the shadows of my youth hold a specific, terrifying memory.

It occurred when I was barely six. The nursery, a vast chamber beneath a steep oak ceiling, belonged entirely to me. One night, I awoke to find the room empty. The maid was gone; the nurse was absent. Alone, I felt not fear—for I was sheltered from the terrors of ghosts and fairies—but a sting of neglect. Whimpering rose to a roar, ready to tear through the silence, when I caught sight of a face.

She knelt by the foot of my bed, hands hidden beneath the heavy covers. Her features were solemn yet strangely beautiful. I stared, bewildered, and the noise died in my throat. She reached out, stroking my hair, and laid herself down beside me, drawing me close with a smile. Instantly, the cold knot in my stomach unraveled, and sleep claimed me again.

Then, the sensation struck. Two sharp pricks, like hot needles driven deep into my breast, sent a scream tearing from my lips.

The lady recoiled, her eyes wide and fixed, then slid backward until she vanished beneath the bedframe.

For the first time, true terror seized me. I screamed until my voice broke. The rush of footsteps echoed—the nurse, the maid, the housekeeper. They gathered around, soothing me, dismissing it as a trick of the light. But their faces betrayed them. Pale, drawn, their expressions held a frantic, unnatural anxiety. They peered under the bed, behind the curtains, opened cupboards. The housekeeper leaned close to the nurse, her voice a hushed whisper: *"Lay your hand along that hollow in the bed... Someone *did* lie there, so sure as you did not; the place is still warm."*

They examined my chest, searching for wounds, finding none. Yet, they sat vigil through the night, and from that hour, a servant never slept in the nursery until I turned fourteen.

That night shattered my innocence. A physician arrived, a pale, elderly man with a saturnine visage scarred by smallpox and topped with a chestnut wig. For weeks, he administered bitter medicines I detested.

The following morning, daylight offered no solace. I could not endure being alone, not even for a heartbeat. My father climbed the stairs, standing by my bed with a cheerful, forced brightness. He asked the nurse questions, laughed at her answers, patted my shoulder, and kissed me, insisting it was merely a dream, harmless and fleeting.

*I knew*, however, with a clarity that terrified me, that it was *not* a dream. The chill of her touch, the warmth of the mattress, the pain in my chest—it was real. I was awfully, deeply frightened.

A sliver of comfort came when the nursery maid insisted it was she, that I must have been half-asleep, failing to recognize her. The nurse backed her up, but the doubt lingered, a seed of unease. Later, I recalled a venerable old man in a black cassock, his face sweet and gentle, joining us in prayer. He took my hands, guiding me to whisper, *"Lord, hear all good prayers for us, for Jesus' sake."* I repeated those words for years, a mantra taught by my nurse.

I remember the old man's thoughtful expression, the stark contrast of his white hair against the dim, shadow-laden room, the clumsy furniture frozen in a fashion three centuries past. Light filtered weakly through the small lattices, casting long, dancing shapes across the floor. He knelt with the three women, and his voice, earnest and quavering, rang out for what seemed an eternity.

I cannot recall a single moment prior to that evening, nor the days that followed immediately after. Those scenes stand out vividly, isolated like paintings in a phantasmagoria surrounded by absolute darkness. That night marked the beginning of something else, something that would soon turn the quiet solitude of the *Schloss* into a nightmare.

II. A Guest

I must confess to telling you a tale so singular, so steeped in the uncanny, that it demands every ounce of your faith in my honesty to accept it as fact. Yet, it is true—a truth witnessed by my own eyes.

It was a sweet summer evening, the air heavy with the scent of blooming woods, when my father invited me to accompany him on a stroll through the forest vista bordering our estate. As we walked, the path winding beneath the canopy, he spoke of a visitor.

"General Spielsdorf cannot arrive sooner than I had hoped," he murmured, his step slowing as the shadows lengthened. "He was to pay us a visit of several weeks, bringing with him his niece and ward, Mademoiselle Rheinfeldt. I have never seen her, though I have heard whispers of her charm. For weeks, I have dreamed of this new acquaintance, of the happy days she would bring to our quiet life."

His disappointment was palpable, a heavy weight in his tone. "How soon does he come?" I asked, feeling a pang of shared sorrow.

"Not until autumn," he replied, his voice dropping. "Perhaps two months yet. And I am relieved, my dear, that you never laid eyes on Mademoiselle Rheinfeldt."

"And why?" I pressed, confused and suddenly chilled.

"Because the poor young lady is dead," he said simply. "I neglected to mention it, for you were not in the room when the General's letter arrived this evening."

Shock washed over me. In letters written six or seven weeks prior, the General had merely noted that his niece was unwell, suggesting nothing of mortal peril. Now, the reality was absolute.

"Here," he said, handing me a crumpled sheet of paper. "Read it. I fear he is in a state of profound affliction; the words themselves seem to tremble with madness."

We sat upon a rough bench beneath the sprawling boughs of majestic lime trees. The sun was dipping below the horizon, painting the sylvan landscape in hues of melancholy gold and violet. A gentle stream, flowing past our home and beneath the ancient stone bridge, mirrored the fading crimson of the sky. Unfolding the letter, I found it a torrent of emotion—vehement, contradictory, and torn by grief. I read it twice, then aloud to my father, struggling to find a logical thread within the chaos.

*"I have lost my darling daughter, for as such I loved her,"* the scrawled words began. *"During the final days of Bertha's illness, I could not write. Before then, I knew not of her danger. I have lost her, and now learn _all_ too late. She died in the peace of innocence... The fiend who betrayed our infatuated hospitality has done it all. The General cursed his blindness, his conceit, his obstinacy, all "too late." He vowed to dedicate his remaining days to hunting down a monster, a creature that had slipped past his defenses. He begged us to pray for him, promising to reveal the truth in two months' time, if he lived.

Though I had never seen Bertha Rheinfeldt, tears pricked my eyes at the tragedy. The sun had fully surrendered to the dark, leaving us in a soft, clear twilight. We lingered, speculating on the violence and incoherence of the General's words, nearly a mile from the road leading to the front of the castle. By then, the moon hung brilliantly above, casting a silver glow over the world.

As we reached the drawbridge, we encountered Madame Perrodon and Mademoiselle De Lafontaine. They had removed their bonnets to enjoy the exquisite luminescence. Their voices bubbled forth in animated chatter as we joined them. Together, we gazed upon the scene: the glade we had traversed lay before us, the narrow road winding away under lordly trees, vanishing into the thickening forest. To our right, the road crossed the steep, picturesque bridge, near a ruined tower that once guarded the pass. Beyond, an abrupt eminence rose, cloaked in trees and shadowed by grey, ivy-choked rocks.

A thin film of mist drifted over the grasslike plains like smoke, veiling the distances, while the river flashed faintly in the moonlight. It was a scene of profound serenity, enchanting in its vagueness, though the news I carried cast a shadow over its beauty.

My father, who relished the picturesque, and I stood in silence. Behind us, the two governesses discoursed passionately on the scenery and the moon. Madame Perrodon, rotund, middle-aged, and deeply romantic, sighed poetry into the night. Mademoiselle De Lafontaine, claiming descent from a German line and embracing metaphysics, declared that the moon's intense brilliance signaled spiritual activity. She spoke of the moon's manifold effects: acting on dreams, inciting lunacy, influencing the nerves, and exerting marvelous physical forces. She recounted the tale of her cousin, a sailor, who had fallen asleep on deck under such a moon. Upon waking from a nightmare of an old woman clawing his face, his features had remained permanently twisted, never regaining equilibrium.

"The moon, this night," she concluded, pointing toward the castle, "is full of idyllic and magnetic influence. See how all the windows flash and twinkle with that silvery splendor, as if unseen hands have lit the rooms to welcome fairy guests."

There is a certain pleasure in listening to the idle fancies of others when one feels inclined to listen, and I gazed on, amused by the ladies' conversation. Then, after a pause, my father quoted Shakespeare.

"In truth I know not why I am so sad. It wearies me: you say it wearies you; But how I got it—came by it."

Just then, the unwonted clatter of carriage wheels and the rhythmic pounding of many hooves shattered the stillness. Approaching from the high ground overlooking the bridge, an equipage emerged. First, two horsemen crossed the bridge, followed swiftly by a carriage drawn by four horses, with two men riding behind. It appeared to be the vehicle of a person of high rank, and we all watched with growing fascination.

Moments later, the drama unfolded. As the carriage crested the summit of the steep bridge, one of the lead horses took fright. Panic spread instantly to the team, causing them to break into a wild gallop. They dashed between the horsemen, thundering down the road toward us with hurricane-like speed.

The scene was exacerbated by the clear, long-drawn screams of a female voice rising from the carriage window. Driven by curiosity and horror, we advanced. I moved forward in silence, while the others uttered cries of terror.

Our suspense did not endure long. Just short of the castle drawbridge, a magnificent lime tree stood by the roadside, opposite an ancient stone cross. At the sight of the cross, the horses, moving at a terrifying pace, swerved violently. One wheel struck the projecting roots of the tree, sending the carriage crashing onto its side, two wheels suspended in the air. The men scrambled to remove the traces.

A lady, tall and commanding, stepped out, her hands clasped, lifting a handkerchief to her eyes. Through the open door, a young lady was lifted out, appearing lifeless. My father, eager to display his medical knowledge, rushed to the elder lady, offering his aid and the resources of our castle. The lady, declaring herself the mother, did not seem to hear him, her gaze fixed solely on the slender girl being placed against the bank.

I approached cautiously. The young lady appeared stunned, though certainly not dead. My father, having checked her pulse, assured the distraught mother that it was faint and irregular but undeniably present. What harm could it do to tell me what I so ardently desired? Did she lack trust in my honor? Why would she not believe me when I swore, solemnly, that not one syllable would leave my lips?

There was a coldness in her smile, a melancholy persistence in her refusals that chilled me beyond her years. We never quarreled, for she would not quarrel on any point. To press her further felt unfair, crude, yet I could not stop myself. The truth she eventually offered amounted, in my view, to nothing. It boiled down to three vague disclosures: First, her name was Carmilla. Second, her family was ancient and noble. Third, her home lay somewhere to the west. She withheld the name of her clan, their heraldry, the estate, or even the country itself.

Do not imagine I harried her incessantly. I waited for opportunities, insinuating rather than urging. Twice I attacked her directly, but my tactics failed utterly. Yet, her evasions were conducted with such pretty melancholy, with such passionate declarations of affection and trust, promising that I would eventually know all, that I could not bring myself to remain truly offended.

She would wrap her pretty arms around my neck, pulling me close until our cheeks touched, and murmur into my ear, "Dearest, your little heart is wounded; think me not cruel because I obey the irresistible law of my strength and weakness. If your heart bleeds, my wild heart bleeds with yours. In the rapture of my humiliation, I live in your warm life, and you shall die—sweetly die—into mine. After such ravishing words, she would press me closer, her lips glowing softly as she kissed my cheek. Her agitation and language were unintelligible to me. In these mysterious moods, I did not like her. A strange tumult swelled within me, pleasurable yet mingled with a vague, creeping fear and disgust. I had no clear thoughts during these scenes, yet I was conscious of a love blooming into adoration, tangled inextricably with abhorrence. A paradox, yes, but the feeling remained.

Now, writing this more than ten years later with a trembling hand, I recall the ordeal through which I passed unconsciously. Though the main current of my story remains vivid, those specific emotional scenes, where my passions were wildly roused, linger in a haze, dimly remembered. Sometimes, after an hour of apathy, she would take my hand, pressing it with a fond, renewed force. Blushing softly, she would gaze at me with languid, burning eyes, breathing so rapidly that her dress rose and fell in tumultuous waves. It was the ardor of a lover, embarrassing, hateful, yet overpowering. With gloating eyes, she would drag me to her, her hot lips tracing kisses along my cheek, whispering almost in sobs, "You are mine. You *shall* be mine. You and I are one forever." Then she would throw herself back in her chair, covering her face with her small hands, leaving me trembling.

"Are we related?" I would ask, desperate for logic. "What do you mean? You remind me of someone I love, yet I hate it. I don't know you, and I don't know myself when you look like that."

She would sigh at my vehemence, turn away, and drop my hand.

"I think it very sweet, on the contrary," I answered, vexed and uneasy lest the passersby notice.

Instantly interrupted again, "You pierce my ears!" Carmilla cried, almost angrily, plugging her tiny fingers into her ears. "Besides, how can you tell that your religion and mine are the same? Your forms wound me, and I hate funerals. What a fuss! Everyone must die, and all are happier when they do. Come home."

"My father went with the clergyman to the churchyard. I thought you knew she was to be buried today."

"She? I don't trouble my head about peasants. I don't know who she is," Carmilla replied, flashing her fine eyes.

"She is the poor girl who thought she saw a ghost a fortnight ago and has been dying ever since," I explained. "Yesterday she expired."

"Tell me nothing about ghosts. I shall not sleep tonight if you do."

"I hope there is no plague," I continued, noting the swineherd's wife who had died similarly, thinking she was strangled by something unseen. "Papa says such horrors accompany fevers."

"Well, *her* funeral is over, I hope, and *her* hymn sung. Our ears shall not be tortured. It makes me nervous. Sit down here, beside me. Hold my hand. Press it hard—harder."

We moved to another seat. As she sat, her face underwent a change that terrified me. It darkened, becoming horribly livid. Her teeth and hands clenched, her lips compressed, and she stared down at the ground, trembling with a continued shudder like ague. All her energies seemed strained to suppress a fit, breathlessly tugging at her nerves. At length, a low, convulsive cry of suffering broke from her, and gradually the hysteria subsided. "There! That comes of strangling people with hymns!" she gasped at last. "Hold me, hold me still. It is passing away."

Slowly, the terror dissipated. To dispel the somber impression, she became unusually animated and chatty, and so we returned home.

This was the first time I had seen any definable symptom of that delicate health her mother spoke of, or any trace of temper. Both passed like a summer cloud, and never again did I witness a sign of anger from her save once

A Wonderful Likeness

That evening, a grim figure emerged from Gratz, a pale, dark-complexioned man burdened by a horse and cart heavily laden with two massive packing cases, each brimming with canvases. The journey from our small capital spanned ten leagues, and whenever a courier traversed the distance, the inhabitants of our secluded halls would swarm around him, eager to devour the latest tidings. This arrival, however, sent a ripple of unease through our quiet domain rather than joy. The heavy cases were left standing in the hall, and the messenger was ushered away by the servants to consume his supper before returning.

Soon, flanked by assistants and armed with hammers, chisels, and turning screws, the man returned to the hall where we had gathered to watch the unveiling. Carmilla sat listlessly amidst the bustle, her gaze fixed on the floor, while the old portraits—nearly all of them, and mostly inherited from her side of the family through my mother's ancient Hungarian lineage—were carefully brushed free from decades of grime and obscurity.

My father held a ledger, reading off numbers as the restorer dug through the layers of varnish and dust. I confess I knew little of art, but these were undeniably ancient relics, some possessing a peculiar, haunting curiosity. For the most part, I beheld them for the first time; the smoke and time of centuries had nearly erased their features, revealing them only now.

"There remains a portrait I have not yet seen," my father remarked, his voice dropping slightly. "In the upper corner, though faded, I can discern the name 'Marcia Karnstein,' dated 1698. I am curious to see how it has survived."

A memory surfaced instantly. It was a small canvas, roughly eighteen inches tall and nearly square, unframed. In its current state, it was a blot of ink-blackened age, unreadable to the eye.

The artist produced it now, his movements betraying a sudden, intense pride. As he lifted it, the darkness shattered. It was breathtaking, jarring, and undeniably alive. It was the effigy of Carmilla herself.

"Carmilla, dearest!" my father exclaimed, his tone shifting from casual to awestruck. "Here is an absolute miracle. Look at her! She lives, she smiles, she seems ready to speak. Is it not beautiful, my girl?" He turned to me, his eyes wide. "And notice, even the tiny mole upon her throat is preserved."

My father chuckled, a sound that felt too bright for the somber air. "Undoubtedly, it is a wonderful likeness." Yet, as he spoke, his gaze drifted away, and strangely, he appeared less moved by the revelation than I was. To my astonishment, he continued conversing with the picture cleaner—who possessed a genuine intellect regarding his craft—as if discussing mundane matters of restoration, while I became increasingly entranced, staring at the painted girl until the room spun.

"Father," I asked, my voice trembling slightly, "may I hang this picture in my chamber?"

"Certainly, dear," he replied, a smile playing on his lips. "I am delighted you find it so striking."

"I'm sure it must be prettier than I imagined," he added, addressing me as if I were a child.

Young Carmilla made no response to his polite words, nor did she appear to hear them. She leaned back in her chair, her long-lashed eyes fixed upon me with an intensity that bordered on madness, a smile of rapturous ecstasy curving her lips.

"Now," she murmured, her voice thick with emotion, "you can read the name clearly inscribed in the corner."

"It is not Marcia," she corrected softly, tracing the gold leaf with her finger. "It reads Mircalla, Countess Karnstein. And here, beneath the date of 1698, lies a tiny coronet." She looked up, her expression languid yet piercing. "I am descended from the Karnsteins, just as Mother was."

"Oh!" the woman sitting beside her sighed, her voice heavy with a strange, hollow resonance. "Then I am a descendant of a very long line, a very ancient bloodline. Do any Karnsteins still walk the earth?"

"No one bears the name, I believe," I answered, feeling a cold prickle run down my spine. "The family was ruined in some civil wars centuries ago. Only the ruins of the castle remain, mere three miles from here."

"How fascinating!" she breathed, glancing toward the open door where the night air drifted in. Her eyes widened as if captivated by something unseen. "Look! What beautiful moonlight!"

"It resembles the night you first arrived," I whispered, the realization striking me with force.

She sighed, a sound that seemed to vibrate within my chest, and smiled. Rising, she took my arm, drawing me close, and together we stepped out onto the cobblestones. In a heavy, suffocating silence, we walked slowly down toward the drawbridge, where the landscape unfolded like a painting under the silver glow.

"And so," she whispered, her breath warm against my ear, "you were thinking of the night I came?"

"Were you glad I came?" I asked, unable to tear my gaze from her face.

"Delighted, dear Carmilla," she answered, though the word tasted like ash. "And you asked for this picture, so alike to me, to hang in your room?" She paused, sinking her head upon my shoulder, her arm tightening around me with a grip that felt too strong. "How romantic you are, Carmilla. Whenever you tell me your story, it will surely be woven from some great, tragic romance."

She pressed a kiss to my neck, silent and chilling.

"I am certain, Carmilla, that you have loved someone. That there is, right now, an affair of the heart consuming you."

"I have loved no one," she whispered, her voice barely audible, "and never shall... unless it be with you."

God, how beautiful she looked in the moonlight! Yet, there was a terrifying vacancy behind those eyes. With a shyness that felt unnatural, she buried her face in the crook of my neck and hair, emitting tumultuous sobs that sounded like gasps for air. Her hand clutched mine, trembling violently. Her soft cheek burned hot against mine.

"Darling, darling," she murmured, her voice dripping with a sickening sweetness. "I live inside you. And you would die for me, I love you so."

Suddenly, I started back as if struck by lightning.

She was gazing at me now, her eyes empty of all fire, all meaning, drained dry. Her face was colorless, mask-like, and utterly apathetic.

"A chill in the air, isn't it, dear?" she said, her voice drowsy and slurred, as if she were floating underwater. "I almost shiver. Have I been dreaming? Let us go inside. Come, come, hurry inside."

"You look ill, Carmilla. You look faint. You must drink some wine," I said, panic rising in my throat.

"Yes," she agreed, nodding slowly. "I will. I feel better already. I shall be quite well in moments. Yes, please, give me a little wine."

As we approached the door, I hesitated. "Let us linger just a moment longer. Perhaps it is the last time I shall see the moonlight with you."

"How do you feel now, dear Carmilla? Are you truly better?" I asked, my voice shaking.

A dread began to coil in my stomach. Was she suffering from that strange epidemic rumored to sweep through the countryside, leaving victims catatonic or dead?

"If Father thought you were even slightly unwell," I stammered, "without telling us immediately, he would be grief-stricken. We have a highly skilled physician staying with us, the same one who visited Father today."

"I am sure he is excellent," she said, her gaze drifting past me. "I know how kind you all are. But, dear child, I am perfectly well again. Nothing has ever troubled me but a slight weakness. People say I am languid, incapable of effort, able to walk no farther than a toddler. Every now and then, this little strength falters, and I become... as you saw. But after all, I am easily revived. In a moment, I am myself. See how I have recovered."

Indeed, she had. The terror vanished from her countenance, replaced by a vibrant, animated liveliness. We talked endlessly, and the remainder of the evening passed without another recurrence of those terrifying, infatuated looks that had so unsettled me.

Yet, that night, an event occurred which shifted my thoughts entirely and seemed to jar Carmilla's lethargic nature into a flash of startled energy.

When we finally settled into the drawing room, the air thick with the scent of roasted cocoa and bitter chocolate, Carmilla seemed to shed the shadow that had clung to her since our arrival. Though she took no refreshment, sitting apart from the others, her demeanor returned to a fragile normalcy. Soon enough, Madame and Mademoiselle de Lafontaine joined us, and a card game began, a trivial distraction from the heavy air in the house. My father eventually entered, claiming his "dish of tea" as he always did.

Once the cards were stacked away, he sank onto the sofa beside Carmilla, his brow furrowed with a quiet anxiety. He asked her if she had received word from her mother since coming to our estate.

"No," she replied, her voice soft.

He pressed further, asking if she knew where a letter could currently reach her.

"I cannot say," she answered, her gaze drifting vaguely, "but I have been considering leaving you. You have been far too hospitable, far too kind. I have caused you endless trouble, and I feel I should depart tomorrow, taking a carriage to pursue her. I know, somehow, where I will ultimately find her, though I dare not reveal it yet."

"But you must not dream of such a thing!" my father exclaimed, the relief washing over him evident to me. "We cannot afford to lose you. And I refuse to let you leave unless your mother directs it personally. She agreed to stay with us until her own return. I would be content knowing you hear from her, but tonight, the reports of this mysterious plague ravaging our neighborhood have grown terrifying. As your guardian, unaided by your mother's counsel, I feel a terrible weight of responsibility. Yet, I promise to do my utmost. One thing is certain: you must not consider leaving without her explicit permission. We would suffer too greatly to agree to it willingly."

"Thank you, sir, a thousand times for your kindness," she murmured, bowing her head with a bashful smile. "You have all treated me with a gentleness I have rarely known. I have never been so happy in my life as within these walls, under your care, surrounded by your beloved daughter."

With a gallantry typical of his older age, my father kissed her hand, pleased by her effusion.

Later, I walked Carmilla back to her chambers as per our routine, sitting with her while she prepared for sleep.

"Do you think," I ventured, breaking a comfortable silence, "that you will ever trust me completely?"

She turned to look at me, a smile playing on her lips, yet offering no words. She simply gazed at me, silent and enigmatic.

"You offer no answer?" I teased gently. "You cannot respond pleasantly; perhaps I should not have asked."

"You were right to ask," she said softly, her eyes losing their playful glint. "Or to ask anything. You do not know how deeply I hold you in my heart, nor could you imagine why I would withhold any truth. But I am bound by vows, perhaps more solemn than those of any nun, and I dare not speak my story even to you—not yet. The time will come soon when you know every secret. You may think me cruel, selfish... but love is inherently selfish. The more ardent the flame, the sharper its hunger. How jealous I am, you cannot comprehend. You must come with me, loving me, even unto death; or else hate me and follow me anyway, carrying that hatred through the grave and beyond. For in my nature, there exists no word for indifference."

"Now, Carmilla, you are about to speak your wild fancies again," I said hurriedly, feeling a chill run down my spine.

"Not I, silly little fool that I am, prone to whims and strange thoughts," she countered, her tone shifting to something almost childlike. "For your sake, I shall speak like a sage. Have you ever attended a ball?"

"No," I admitted, puzzled by the sudden shift in topic. "What is it like? It sounds enchanting."

"I almost forgot; it was years ago."

I laughed, trying to lighten the mood. "You aren't nearly that old. Your first ball shouldn't be forgotten yet."

"I remember everything about it—with an effort. It appears before me as divers see the world above them, through a medium that is dense, rippling, yet transparent. That night, however, some event marred the vision, causing the colors to fade. I was nearly assassinated in my bed, wounded here," she pressed a slender finger against her breast, "and I have never truly been the same since."

"Were you near death?"

"Yes, terribly. A cruel love, a strange passion that sought to consume me. Love demands sacrifice, and no sacrifice comes without blood."

"Let us try to sleep now," she whispered, her energy seemingly draining. "I feel so exhausted. How can I possibly rise and secure my door just now?"

She lay down, burying her small, delicate hands into her rich, wavy hair beneath her cheek. Her head rested heavily on the pillow, yet her eyes remained open, glittering with an uncanny intensity as they followed my every movement across the floor. A shy, unreadable smile played around her lips.

I bade her goodnight and slipped quietly from the room, leaving behind an uncomfortable, heavy sensation that refused to lift.

Often, I wonder if our beautiful guest ever prays. I have never seen her kneel. In the mornings, she does not descend until well after our family devotions conclude; at night, she never ventures into the hall to join our brief evening prayers. Had it not been for a casual remark during one of our idle conversations revealing that she had indeed been baptized, I would have suspected she was not Christian at all. Religion was a subject she never spoke of. If I understood the world better, this peculiar neglect or aversion would not have surprised me so.

Nervousness is contagious, and those of similar temperament inevitably adopt each other's habits. I, too, became infected by Carmilla's paranoia. I adopted her ritual of locking my bedroom door, succumbing to her whimsical terrors regarding midnight intruders and prowling assassins. I also copied her precaution of conducting a swift, thorough search of my room to ensure no lurking thief or murderer was "ensconced" within the shadows.

Taking these wise measures, I finally retired to my bed and drifted off. A candle burned steadily in my chamber, a relic of a habit so ancient I had long ceased to question it. Thus fortified, I believed I could rest in peace. But dreams slip through stone walls, illuminate dark rooms, or shroud bright ones in gloom. Their figures enter and exit at pleasure, laughing at the efforts of locks and keys.

That night, I dreamed a dream that marked the beginning of a very strange agony.

It was not a nightmare, for I remained acutely conscious of being asleep. Equally, I was aware of my location: in my room, lying in bed, exactly as I physically was. I perceived the furniture and the layout of the chamber precisely as I had seen them moments before, save for a profound darkness that had swallowed the space. At the foot of the bed, something moved, indistinct at first, a blur in the void. Gradually, the shape coalesced into a soot-black beast, resembling a monstrous cat. It stretched fully four or five feet long, spanning the entire length of the hearth-rug as it traversed it. It paced back and forth with a lithe, sinister restlessness, the trapped fury of a creature caged.

I could not cry out, though terror seized me. Its pace quickened, and the room plunged deeper into obscurity until I could discern nothing but two eyes. They were wide, luminous, and terrible. Suddenly, the beast leaped lightly onto the bed. Those two broad eyes approached my face, and in an instant, I felt a searing pain, as if two large needles had darted an inch or two apart, piercing deep into my breast.

I woke with a scream. The room was illuminated by the candle that had burned unceasingly throughout the night. Standing at the foot of the bed, slightly to the right, was a female figure. She wore a dark, loose gown, and her hair hung down, covering her shoulders like a veil. She was as still as a block of stone. There was not the slightest stirring of breath.

As I stared, transfixed, the figure seemed to change position, sliding silently closer to the door. Then, close to the frame, the door opened, and she passed out into the hallway.

Relief washed over me, allowing me to breathe and move again. My immediate thought was that Carmilla had played me a trick, that I had failed to secure my door properly. I rushed to it, finding it locked securely on the inside, just as I usually left it. Yet fear gripped me anew. I was horrified. I threw myself back into the bed, pulling the covers over my head, and lay there, more dead than alive, waiting for dawn.

Descending

Even now, the memory of that night clings to me with a visceral, suffocating dread that refuses to fade. It was not a fleeting nightmare, a transient shadow dismissed by the light of dawn. Instead, it had rooted itself, deepening with every passing hour until it seemed to seep into the walls, staining the woodwork, and haunting the furniture around which the specter had danced.

I found no solace in solitude the following day. To sit alone for a single moment felt impossible, a vacuum where sanity might slip away. I held back from telling Papa, torn between two terrors. On one hand, I feared his laughter, the casual dismissal of my ordeal as a childish jest; on the other, I dreaded his panic. He was already an invalid, his health fragile, and I could not bear the thought of him imagining I had fallen victim to the mysterious plague sweeping our village. Yet, inside, I knew no physical ailment plagued me.

Fortunately, I had companions in Madame Perrodon, kindly and patient, and Mademoiselle Lafontaine, whose spirit was as bright as ever. Though they sensed the tremor beneath my skin, the weight of my secret pressed so heavily that silence stretched until I finally confessed.

Mademoiselle laughed, a sharp, brittle sound, while Madame Perrodon's gaze remained fixed on me, heavy with anxiety.

"By the way," Mademoiselle chirped, "the long lime-tree walk behind Carmilla's window is haunted!"

"Nonsense!" Madame Perrodon snapped, likely sensing the inappropriateness of the topic. "Who tells such stories?"

"Martin swears he climbed the wall twice before sunrise, when the gates were being mended, and twice he saw a woman walking down that path."

"So he sees what he expects to see, so long as there are cows to milk in the river fields," Madame retorted.

"I dare say," Mademoiselle agreed, "yet Martin is easily spooked. I have never known a man so prone to fear."

"You must not speak a word of this to Carmilla," I interjected quickly, my voice tight. "She looks down upon that path from her window, and she is, if anything, a greater coward than I."

Carmilla descended later than usual. As soon as we were together, she leaned close, her face pale.

"I was terrified last night," she whispered. "I am certain I would have seen something monstrous if not for that charm I bought from the poor little hunchback—the one I cursed with such vile names. I dreamed something black coalescing around my bed. I awoke in absolute horror, convinced I saw a dark shape by the chimneypiece. But when I felt under my pillow and grasped the charm, the vision vanished instantly. Only the knowledge that it was there calmed me, preventing some fiend from throttling me as it did those unfortunate souls we heard of."

"Well, listen," I began, recounting my own tale. Her eyes widened with genuine horror as I spoke.

"And did you keep the charm near you?" she asked, her voice trembling.

"No," I admitted, "I dropped it into a porcelain vase in the drawing-room. But I shall carry it tonight, just as you advise."

Time has blurred the edges of that night, yet I recall clearly pinning the trinket to my pillow. Sleep claimed me almost immediately, deep and untroubled. The following night passed in similar tranquility.

Yet, when I awoke, a heavy lethargy settled over me, a melancholy so profound it bordered on luxury.

"Well, I told you so," Carmilla said, a smug glint in her eye. "I slept most beautifully last night. I pinned the charm to the breast of my chemise. Last time, it was too distant. I am convinced it was all imagination, save for the dreams. I used to believe evil spirits crafted nightmares, but our physician insists otherwise. He says a fever or some malady knocks at the brain's door, unable to enter, and thus wanders off, leaving behind that alarm."

"And what, pray, do you think this charm is?" I asked.

"It has been fumigated or steeped in some potent drug; it is an antidote against the malaria," she replied confidently.

"So it works only on the body?"

"Certainly! You do not suppose evil spirits flee from ribbons or the perfumes of a druggist? These plagues wander the air, attacking the nerves first, then infecting the brain. But before they can seize you, the antidote repels them. That is surely what it did for us. It is not magic, merely natural science."

I wished, with a desperate longing, to agree with Carmilla, to let her rationality wash away the chill creeping into my bones. I tried, giving the impression that her words were soothing the storm within me.

For several nights, I slept deeply. Yet, each morning, the same languor returned, a heavy cloak that draped over my limbs. I felt altered, a stranger to myself. A strange, sweet melancholy stole over me, a sentiment I would not have interrupted. Vague thoughts of death began to bloom, and the notion that I was slowly sinking into the earth took a gentle, unwelcome, yet strangely comforting hold. If it were sorrowful, the tone of mind it induced was equally sweet.

My soul acquiesced.

I refused to admit I was ill. I would not confess to Papa, nor summon a doctor.

Carmilla's devotion intensified, her paroxysms of lazy adoration becoming more frequent. She gazed upon me with increasing ardor, feeding on my fading strength and spirits. This observation shocked me, flashing briefly like a glimpse of madness.

Unknowingly, I had entered the final stages of the strangest malady a mortal soul could endure. An unaccountable fascination clung to its early symptoms, reconciling me to the debilitating effects of its progression. This allure grew stronger until, eventually, a sense of the horrific mingled with it, discoloring and perverting the entirety of my existence.

The first change was, paradoxically, agreeable. It marked the turning point toward the descent of Avernus.

Vague, strange sensations visited me in sleep. The prevailing one was a peculiar, pleasant cold thrill, akin to moving against the swift current of a river. Soon, dreams followed—interminable, hazy visions where I could recall neither scenery nor figures, yet which left an awful, exhausting imprint. I felt as though I had traversed a long period of immense mental exertion and peril.

Upon waking, I retained the memory of a place nearly dark, of speaking to invisible beings. Most distinctively, I remembered a clear, female voice, deep and resonant, speaking from afar with a slow, solemn cadence that instilled indescribable fear. Sometimes, I felt a soft hand tracing my cheek and neck. Other times, warm lips brushed me, lingering, growing longer and more loving as they neared my throat, where the caress suddenly fixed itself. My heart hammered, my breath rising and falling in rapid, shallow gasps. A sobbing rose within me, transforming into a crushing strangulation, culminating in a dreadful convulsion that stripped my consciousness away, plunging me into darkness.

Three weeks had passed since this unaccountable state began.

My suffering had etched itself onto my features over the last week. My complexion was ghastly pale, my eyes dilated and shadowed by deep, bruised circles. The languor I had long felt now sat heavy upon my countenance.

Papa asked constantly if I was ill. With an obstinacy that now seems inexplicable, I persisted in assuring him I was perfectly well.

In truth, I was free of pain. I could complain of no bodily derangement. My affliction seemed to reside entirely in the imagination or the nerves. Horrible as my sufferings were, I guarded them with a morbid reserve, keeping them almost wholly to myself.

It could not be the *oupire*, the terrible complaint the peasants spoke of. Those victims seldom survived past three days, for death swiftly ended their misery. I had endured three weeks.

Carmilla, too, complained of dreams and feverish sensations, though hers were far less alarming than mine. Hers were terrifying, yes, but not to the point of immediate collapse. Had I been capable of understanding my condition, I would have cried out for aid, begging for wisdom. Some narcotic influence, unsuspected and insidious, acted upon me, numbing my perceptions.

Now, I must tell you of a dream that led directly to an odd discovery.

One night, instead of the usual indistinct voice, I heard one sweet, tender, and simultaneously terrible. It spoke these words: *"Your mother warns you to beware of the assassin."*

Simultaneously, a light flared unexpectedly. I saw Carmilla standing near the foot of my bed, clad in her white nightdress, bathed from chin to feet in a great, spreading stain of blood.

I woke with a shriek, seized by the singular, frantic conviction that Carmilla was being murdered. I leaped from the bed, my next recollection being that I stood in the lobby, screaming for help.

Madame and Mademoiselle burst from their rooms in alarm. A lamp burned perpetually there, and seeing me, they quickly discerned the cause of my terror.

"I insist," I cried, "that we knock at Carmilla's door!"

Search

The air in the room, though shaken by our frantic entry, remained unnervingly still. As the dust settled over the chaos, we managed to compose ourselves enough to order the guards away. Mademoiselle proposed a chilling hypothesis: perhaps Carmilla, startled by the commotion at her threshold, had leaped from her bed in a primal panic, retreating behind a heavy curtain or into the depths of a wardrobe, hidden from view until the majordomo and his minions departed. This theory compelled us to scour the space anew, calling her name with growing desperation.

Silence answered us. Our confusion deepened into a cold dread. We checked every window; they were barred tight against intrusion. I pleaded with Carmilla, begging her not to torment us further, imploring her to reveal herself if she truly lay concealed. Yet, the room remained empty. I became certain she was not here, nor in the adjacent dressing room, whose door stood firmly locked from within. She could not have slipped through. A terrifying question gnawed at me: had Carmilla discovered one of those legendary secret passages whispered about by the ancient housekeeper, tunnels where tradition held sway but knowledge had long faded? Time, I reasoned, must eventually unravel this knot, yet for now, the shadows seemed to swallow us whole.

Past four o'clock, the darkness pressing down felt suffocating. I resolved to spend these final hours in Madame's chamber, praying that dawn might bring answers.

But daybreak brought no solace. By morning, the entire estate was a hive of agitated frenzy, led by my father. Every corner of the château was ransacked, the grounds combed bare. Not a thread, not a footprint, betrayed the missing lady. The stream was about to be dredged; my father trembled on the brink of madness, haunted by the nightmare of explaining this absence to the grieving mother who awaited them. And I, too, teetered on the edge of despair, though my sorrow twisted into something far sharper than simple loss.

Hours bled away in a feverish loop of alarm. Then, at one o'clock, I burst into Carmilla's room. The scene before me stopped my heart. She stood motionless before her dressing table, bathed in the pale light. I stared, unable to comprehend what I saw. With a delicate, silent gesture, she beckoned me closer. Her face, usually radiant, wore an expression of profound, trembling terror.

I rushed to her, overcome with a surge of joy so intense it nearly blinded me, embracing her repeatedly. I clattered the bell, summoning everyone to witness her return and ease my father's torment.

"Dearest Carmilla!" I cried, my voice breaking. "What horrors have you endured? Where have you been?"

She looked at me, her eyes wide. "Last night," she whispered, "was a night of wonders."

"For heaven's sake, speak!" I begged. "Explain anything you remember."

"It grew late," she said, her voice thin, "past two, when I fell asleep as I always do, with both doors locked—the bedroom and the dressing room. My slumber was unbroken, dreamless, I swear. But when I awoke moments ago, I found myself on the sofa in the dressing room. The door between the chambers stood ajar, and the outer door... it had been forced."

"How?" I gasped. "How could you move without waking? And how could you be carried from your bed without stirring? I am the first to wake at the rustle of a leaf!"

Soon, Madame, Mademoiselle, and a throng of servants flooded the room, bombarding her with questions and relief. Carmilla, however, seemed the least capable of offering an explanation, her countenance a mask of bewildered confusion.

When the servants finally withdrew, and Mademoiselle returned with a vial of valerian, leaving only my father, Madame, and Carmilla alone, my father approached slowly. He took her hand gently, guiding her to the sofa where he sat beside her.

"Calm yourself, my dear," he said softly. "May I offer a guess, and ask a single question?"

"Who holds truer authority?" she replied. "Ask whatever you wish, and I shall tell you all. Though my mind is a fog of bewilderment. I know nothing. Remember, my father, the limits Mother places upon my speech."

"I understand, child. Let us avoid those forbidden topics." He paused, studying her. "The miracle of last night lies in your disappearance from a locked room and bed, appearing elsewhere without a sound. Windows were sealed, doors bolted from within. Here is my theory, followed by a question."

Carmilla leaned heavily on her hand, her brow furrowed. Madame and I held our breath.

"Do you recall," my father asked, "being suspected of somnambulism as a child?"

"Never, not seriously," she murmured.

"But you did wander in your sleep?"

"Yes," she admitted quietly. "My old nurse spoke of it often."

My father nodded, a faint smile touching his lips. "Then listen closely. Last night, you rose in your sleep. You unlocked the door, removing the key rather than dropping it inside, then fastened it from the outside. You took the key with you, wandering into one of the twenty-five rooms scattered across this floor, or perhaps higher or lower. This ancient house is a labyrinth of closets and heavy furniture; a thorough search would take weeks. Do you see?"

"I see the path, but not the destination," she confessed.

"And why," Madame interjected gently, "were you found on the sofa in the dressing room, a place we had already cleared?"

"She followed you there," my father said, his voice warm with reassurance, "still drifting in dreams, until she awoke spontaneously. Imagine her surprise to find herself there, just as surely as you were shocked." He laughed lightly, dismissing the tension. "Let us celebrate that the simplest truth prevails: no drugs, no tampered locks, no burglars, poisons, or witches. There is no threat to your safety, Carmilla."

She looked around, her features regaining their luminous charm. Her beauty seemed heightened by a graceful languor, a quiet exhaustion that made her appear even more ethereal. I noticed my father's gaze linger on her, a subtle comparison forming in his eyes.

"I wish," he sighed, turning to me, "that my poor Laura looked half as well as she does now."

With the mystery solved, the alarms subsided. Carmilla was restored to her circle, her strange journey unexplained but ultimately harmless, leaving the house once again shrouded in its deceptive peace.

The silence of the night was broken not by a scream, but by a cold, calculated order. My father, his face a mask of grim resolve, had ensured that no servant slept within the walls of Carmilla's chamber. Instead, a guard was stationed outside her door, a silent sentinel tasked with intercepting her before she could vanish once more into the dark corridors. That night, the air remained heavy with unspoken dread, yet the hours passed without incident.

By dawn, the atmosphere shifted abruptly. A man, summoned by my father without a whisper reaching me, appeared at the threshold. This was Doctor Spielsberg, a figure I had heard whispered about—a grave scholar with snow-white hair and spectacles that magnified eyes devoid of warmth. Madame, acting as my chaperone, led me to the library, where the doctor waited in the shadows of a window recess.

"I have heard your tale," he intoned, his voice dropping as I spoke. "And as you speak, the gravity within me deepens, bordering on the horrific."

He stood opposite me, leaning heavily against the stone wall, his gaze fixed upon me with an intensity that made the hair on my arms stand erect. There was a chill in his interest, a shadow of terror beneath his professional composure. After a long pause, he requested to see my father.

When the count entered, a faint smile played upon his lips, though it vanished instantly as the doctor beckoned him closer. They retreated into the same secluded corner, engaging in a low, urgent debate that burned with the heat of argument. I and Madame stood at the far end of the vast room, our hearts pounding with a feverish curiosity. The thick walls and the deep alcove of the window acted as a soundproof barrier, muffling their words until they were mere whispers lost to the silence. We could only see the count's pale, agitated features and the doctor's stooped silhouette.

Eventually, the count stepped back into the light, his expression thoughtful and shaken. "Laura, come here," he called softly, dismissing Madame. "Doctor Spielsberg has a request."

I approached, feeling a strange mixture of weakness and alertness. A malady clung to me, not with the fever of sickness, but with a lingering, ghostly presence. The count extended his hand, but his eyes remained locked on the physician.

"You have spoken of a sensation," the doctor began, his tone clinical yet ominous, "like two needles piercing the skin near your neck during your first nightmare. Does that mark remain?"

"No soreness," I replied, trying to keep my voice steady.

"Point to it," he commanded.

I lowered my sleeve, revealing the skin just below the collarbone. "Here," I indicated.

"Now, look closely," the doctor said, his gloom deepening into triumph. "Do not mind your father's instruction to lower it further. We must inspect the symptom."

"My God!" the count gasped, his face draining of color.

"There," the doctor declared, pointing a trembling finger. "A small blue spot, no larger than the tip of your little finger."

"What is it?" I cried, fear seizing my throat.

"It is nothing," the doctor lied smoothly, turning to the count. "Yet, it marks the precise locus where the sensation of strangulation begins. And recall the 'thrill' you described? Like a cold stream running against you?"

"Yes," I whispered, the memory surfacing.

"Aye," the doctor confirmed, glancing at the count. "Then the diagnosis is clear. Madame, you must ensure Miss Laura is never alone. It is indispensable."

"We will obey," Madame agreed, her voice tight with anxiety.

"And you, Laura," the doctor continued, "do not stray from the path. I have another patient, a young lady whose symptoms mirror yours, though milder. She is also a guest. You will meet her later tonight."

With final, stern instructions given to both of us, the count and the doctor departed. Outside, on the grassy platform between the moat and the road, they paced together, their conversation earnest and secret. Soon, the doctor mounted his horse and rode eastward, disappearing into the mist-shrouded forest. Almost simultaneously, a messenger arrived from Dranfield, delivering letters to the count.

Inside, speculation ran wild. Madame feared the doctor suspected a sudden, fatal seizure, urging constant vigilance. To me, however, the strictures seemed excessive, perhaps merely a precautionary measure to keep a young girl from wandering off or eating unripe fruit. But the weight of the doctor's gaze lingered.

An hour later, the count returned, holding a letter. "It is from General Spielsdorf," he announced, his demeanor uncharacteristically somber. "He may arrive today, or tomorrow, or not at all."

He handed me the open letter, his face etched with a turmoil he refused to name. "Laura, lay your hand upon my arm," he pleaded, though his eyes betrayed a desperate need for distance. "Tell me..."

"Do you think I am very ill?" I asked, clutching his sleeve.

"No," he murmured, smoothing my hair with a trembling hand. "If the proper steps are taken, you will be well again in days. But pray, let us not discuss this. The General's arrival would be disastrous if you were... compromised."

"But what is wrong?" I pressed, tears pricking my eyes.

"Nothing you need know yet," he snapped, the irritation breaking through his usual gentleness. "You shall know all in a day or two. For now, rest."

Before I could process his harshness, he returned, announcing his departure for Karnstein. He intended to visit a local priest regarding some pressing business, and he demanded that Madame and I accompany him. "We shall hold a picnic in the ruins," he said, a faint, mocking smile touching his lips. "Miss Carmilla has never seen those grounds."

At noon, the carriage was ready. We crossed the drawbridge and followed the winding road westward, over the steep Gothic arches, toward the desolate village and the crumbling fortress of Karnstein. The landscape was a tapestry of gentle hills and hollows, cloaked in wild, untamed woods, free from the rigid geometry of human cultivation. The road twisted and turned, hugging the sides of broken valleys and rising slopes, leading us deeper into the ancient, decaying heart of the land.

Suddenly, emerging from a bend in the path, we met our old friend, the General. He rode toward us, flanked by a mounted servant, his portmanteaus trailing behind in a hired wagon. As we pulled up, he dismounted, exchanging pleasantries with the count. With a nod, the General accepted the vacant seat in the carriage, sending his horse and servant ahead to the *Schloss*, leaving us three bound for the haunted ruins.

Bereaved

A decade had passed since we last laid eyes on him, yet the interval felt like a lifetime of decay. Time, it seemed, had not merely aged him; it had hollowed him out. Where once cordial serenity had softened his features, now a jagged edge of gloom and gnawing anxiety carved deep lines into his face. His hair, previously a rich brown, had turned a matted, shaggy grey, framing dark blue eyes that no longer held their usual penetrating warmth. Instead, they burned with a sterner, colder light, gleaming with a ferocity that suggested angers far older and deeper than simple sorrow usually breeds.

As soon as our carriage rolled back onto the gravel path, the General launched into his tale. With the blunt, unvarnished directness of a man unaccustomed to delicacy, he spoke of his "bereavement"—the loss of his beloved niece and ward. But as he spoke, his voice curdled into a venomous bitterness, a fury that bordered on madness. He inveighed against "hellish arts," words that tasted foul on his tongue, and cursed the heavens for tolerating such monstrous indulgences in sin and malignity. To him, her death was not a tragedy of nature, but a war crime committed by invisible enemies.

"My father," who was quick to read the air around him, sensed immediately that something profoundly unnatural had transpired. He leaned forward, his voice gentle but probing, asking if it would be too painful for the General to detail the circumstances that warranted such vitriolic speech.

"I would gladly tell you all," the General replied, his jaw tight, "if you possessed the capacity to believe me."

"Why not?" my father pressed gently. "What evidence would stop you?"

"Because!" The General snapped, his composure cracking momentarily. "You believe only in what suits your own narrow prejudices and comforting illusions. I remember when I stood where you stand now, blind to the truth, but I have learned the hard way."

"Test me," my father countered, his expression open and earnest. "I am no dogmatist, sir. And I know you demand proof for every conviction. Therefore, I am predisposed to respect whatever conclusion you have drawn."

"You speak the truth," the General murmured, staring ahead through the misty windows as if seeing ghosts in the passing trees. "I have not been led lightly into believing in the marvellous. What I have witnessed defies all reason, tearing apart the theories I once held sacred. I have been made the dupe of a preternatural conspiracy."

Even as he spoke these words with absolute conviction, I watched my father exchange a fleeting, sharp look with the General—a glance heavy with suspicion, perhaps bordering on doubt regarding the soldier's sanity. Fortunately, the General missed it entirely. He remained lost in a gloomy, curious scrutiny of the glades rushing past, his mind fixed on a destination that chilled the blood.

"We are heading toward the Ruins of Karnstein?" he asked suddenly, his gaze snapping back to reality. "Fortunate coincidence, indeed. I was just about to request permission to inspect those grounds myself. I have a specific purpose there. A ruined chapel, yes? And beneath it, the tombs of that extinct line?"

"There are," my father confirmed, though his tone lacked its usual gaiety. "Highly significant, I trust. Are you considering reclaiming the title and estates?"

The question hung in the air, but the General did not catch the faint, polite smile that might have accompanied it. Nor did he register the customary courtesy required when a friend jokes. Instead, his face grew grim, fierce, and terrifyingly serious, consumed by a rage that stirred within him like a storm.

"Not a single thought of inheritance," he growled, his voice rough with emotion. "I intend to dig up the bones of those fiends. By God's grace, I plan to commit a pious sacrilege here, liberating this earth from monsters and allowing honest men to sleep soundly again without fear of murder in their beds. I have stories to tell you, my dear friend, tales that I would have laughed off as impossible fiction a few short months ago."

My father studied him, and this time his gaze was not one of suspicion, but of acute intelligence and profound alarm.

"The House of Karnstein," my father noted softly, "has been silent for over a century. My late wife was maternally descended from them, but the name, the title, the castle itself—all are dust. The village lies abandoned, untouched by smoke for fifty years. Not a roof remains standing."

"That is precisely why I must go," the General interrupted, his voice dropping to a dangerous whisper. "I have heard much since I last saw you, facts that will stagger your imagination. But first, I must recount the events in their proper order. You saw my ward... my child. No creature ever walked the earth more beautiful, and only three months prior, she was in the full bloom of youth."

"Yes, poor soul," my father whispered, his voice thick with sympathy. "She was lovely, and I was heartbroken to hear of her passing. I know how deeply this wound has cut you."

He reached across, taking the General's hand, and they clasped it in a moment of shared, heavy grief. Tears welled in the old soldier's eyes, unashamed and raw.

"We have been friends for generations," the General choked out, his grip tightening painfully. "And because I am childless, she became a daughter to me. She repaid my care with a love that warmed my lonely home and gave meaning to my days. All of that is extinguished. Perhaps the years remaining to me on this wretched earth are few, but by God's mercy, I swear to accomplish a service to mankind before I breathe my last. I shall bring vengeance down upon the fiends who slaughtered my innocent child in the prime of her beauty and hope!"

"You mentioned earlier," my father interjected, pulling his hand away slowly, "that you wished to relate everything as it unfolded. Pray, proceed. I assure you, it is not idle curiosity that drives me, but a need to understand."

At that precise moment, our carriage jolted as it reached the fork where the Drunstall road branched off from our path toward Karnstein. The wheels clattered over the stones, a rhythmic, mocking beat.

"How far still to the ruins?" the General demanded, his head craning anxiously forward, scanning the darkening treeline.

"A half-league," my father replied, his voice steady despite the chill in the air. "Now, please, let us hear the story you promised."

"The Story"

"With every ounce of strength I possess," the General rasped, his voice cracking under the weight of the memory. After a heavy, agonizing pause to gather his shattered thoughts, he launched into a narrative so strange, so chilling, that it remains etched in my mind forever.

"My dearest child gazed forward with radiant joy toward the visit you so graciously arranged for her to meet your own daughter." Here, he offered me a bow that was simultaneously gallant and deeply mournful. "Meanwhile, however, we received an invitation from my ancient friend, Count Carlsfeld. His estate lies roughly six leagues beyond Karnstein. We were summoned to witness the series of festivities he hosted in honor of his illustrious guest, Grand Duke Charles."

"Yes," my father murmured, "and I recall them as truly splendid."

"Royal, yes. Yet his hospitality transcends mere royalty; it possesses a regality that borders on the supernatural. He holds a lamp akin to Aladdin's. That fateful night—the dawn of my eternal sorrow—was consumed by a magnificent masquerade. The grounds were unveiled to the world, the trees draped in a kaleidoscope of glowing lamps. The fireworks erupted with a brilliance that Paris itself had never witnessed. And the music... oh, the music. Forgive me, for music is my fatal weakness. It was ravishing, soul-stirring. The finest instrumental ensemble the world knows, paired with the most exquisite singers gathered from the great opera houses of Europe. As you wandered through those fantastically illuminated gardens, the moonlight casting a rosy, spectral glow from the château's endless rows of windows, you would suddenly hear these haunting, ethereal voices slipping from the shadows of some grove or rising from the silent waters of the lake. In that moment, standing amidst the light and sound, I felt myself transported back to the romance and poetry of my youth, a time thought dead and buried."

"When the pyrotechnics faded and the ball finally began, we retreated to the noble suites of rooms opened to the dancers. A masked ball is a vision of beauty, yet I had never beheld a spectacle of such dazzling brilliance before."

"It was an assembly of the highest aristocracy. I, myself, was nearly the sole 'nobody' among them."

"My darling child looked absolutely radiant. She wore no mask. Her excitement and sheer delight lent an indescribable charm to her features, always lovely to behold. I noticed a young lady, dressed in magnificent splendor yet concealed behind a mask, who appeared to be studying my ward with extraordinary, piercing interest. I had spotted her earlier in the grand hall and again, just moments prior, walking near us beneath the castle windows, engaged in the exact same scrutiny. She was accompanied by another masked lady, dressed richly and with grave dignity, possessing a stately air that marked her as a person of high rank—a chaperone if ever there was one.

If that young lady had not been hidden behind her veil, I could certainly have resolved the mystery of whether she was truly watching my poor darling. I am now fully convinced that she was.

We found ourselves in one of the salons. My beloved child, having danced tirelessly, sat resting in a chair near the entrance. I stood close by. The two women I had noted approached; the younger one took the seat beside my ward, while her companion stepped up to stand beside me. For a brief moment, the older woman spoke to her charge in a hushed, intimate tone.

Then, availing herself of the freedom granted by her disguise, she turned to me. In the voice of an old confidante, addressing me by name, she opened a conversation that sparked a fierce curiosity within me. She referenced countless scenes where our paths had crossed—at Court, at distinguished estates. She alluded to trivial incidents I had long dismissed as forgotten dust, yet which, at her touch, instantly leapt back to life in my memory.

"I grew increasingly desperate to uncover her identity, moment by moment." She deflected my inquiries with adroitness and pleasantness. The depth of her knowledge regarding the passages of my life seemed almost unaccountable. She appeared to derive a peculiar, cruel pleasure in frustrating my curiosity, watching me flounder in eager perplexity, jumping from one wild guess to another.

Meanwhile, the younger lady, whom her mother occasionally addressed by the singular, odd name of Millarca, engaged my ward with the same ease and grace. She introduced herself by claiming her mother was an old acquaintance of mine. She spoke of the agreeable audacity masks afford, conversing like a friend. She admired her dress and, with insinuating sweetness, expressed admiration for her beauty. She amused her with witty, laughing critiques of the crowds filling the ballroom, mocking my poor child's own fun. She was sharp and vivacious when she chose, and soon they had become fast friends. Eventually, the young stranger lowered her mask, revealing a face of remarkable, arresting beauty. Neither I nor my dear child had ever seen it before. Yet, though entirely new to us, the features possessed such an engaging, magnetic loveliness that resistance was impossible. The attraction was powerful, immediate. My poor girl fell under its spell instantly. I had never witnessed such instant infatuation unless, perhaps, it was the stranger herself, who seemed to have completely lost her heart to her.

While this unfolded, I used the license of the masquerade to interrogate the elder lady.

"You have utterly baffled me," I said, laughing nervously. "Is that not enough? Will you not, now, consent to stand on equal footing and grant me the kindness of removing your mask?"

"Can any request be more unreasonable?" she replied coolly. "To ask a lady to yield an advantage! Besides, how do you know you would recognize me? Years bring about changes."

"As you see," I said, bowing and offering what I hoped was a melancholy little laugh.

"As philosophers tell us," she countered. "And how do you know that seeing my face would aid you?"

"I would wager my life on it," I answered. "It is futile trying to pass yourself off as an old woman; your figure betrays you."

"Nevertheless, years have passed since I last saw you, and far longer since you saw me—that is what weighs on me. Millarca, there, is my daughter. I cannot therefore claim to be young, even in the eyes of those whom time has taught to be indulgent. And I dislike being compared to what you remember me as. You wear no mask to remove. You can offer me nothing in exchange."

"My petition rests upon your pity; remove it."

"And mine rests upon yours; keep it where it is," she replied firmly.

"Well, then, at least you will tell me whether you are French or German; you command both languages with perfect fluency."

"I do not think I shall tell you that, General. You intend a surprise, and you are meditating the particular angle of attack."

"At any rate, you cannot deny this," I pressed, "that being honored by your permission to converse, I ought to know how to address you. Shall I say Madame la Comtesse?"

She laughed, and surely, if I could treat any event in an interview whose circumstances I now believe were prearranged with profound cunning as merely liable to modification by accident, she would have met me with another evasion.

"As to that..." she began, but her words were cut short almost the moment they left her lips. A gentleman entered, dressed in stark black. He looked particularly elegant and distinguished, save for one terrible flaw: his face was the most deadly pale I have ever witnessed, resembling nothing so much as the countenance of a corpse. He wore no costume, only the plain evening attire of a gentleman. Without a smile, yet with a courtly and unusually low bow, he spoke:

"Will Madame la Comtesse permit me to say a few words which may interest her?"

The lady turned swiftly to him, touching her lips in a gesture of silence. Then she whispered to me, "Keep my place for me, General. I shall return when I have spoken a few words."

And with this playful injunction, she walked slightly aside with the gentleman in black. They conferred for several minutes, their tones appearing intensely earnest. Then, they strolled away slowly together into the throng, and I lost them from view for a time.

I spent the interval frantically scouring my brain for a conjecture regarding the identity of the lady who remembered me so kindly. I considered turning around to join the conversation between my pretty ward and the Countess's daughter, hoping to learn her name, title, château, and estates before she returned, thus catching her off guard. Just as I resolved to act, she returned, accompanied by the pale man in black.

"I shall return and inform Madame la Comtesse when her carriage is at the door," the man announced.

He bowed and withdrew, leaving me alone with the terrifying aftermath of his presence.

XII. A Petition

"Then we are to lose Madame la Comtesse," I murmured, bowing low, though the air around me felt suddenly thin, as if the shadows had grown teeth. "I pray it is but for a few hours."

"It may be hours," the woman replied, her voice smooth as polished ice, "or it may stretch into weeks. Fate is a fickle mistress; it was unluckiest timing for him to speak to me thus. Do you truly know me?"

"I assure you, I do not."

"You shall know me," she said, a smile playing on lips that seemed too white, "but not yet. We are older, deeper friends than you suspect. I cannot unveil myself today. In three weeks, I shall pass your magnificent *schloss*, about which I have been making inquiries. Then I will look in upon you for an hour or two, to renew a friendship I cherish with a thousand sweet recollections. But now, a thunderbolt of news has struck me. I must depart immediately, traveling by a winding road nearly a hundred miles away, with all the speed of a ghost. My troubles multiply. Only the strict reserve I practice regarding my name holds back a singular request."

She paused, her eyes locking onto mine with an intensity that made the hair on my arms stand erect. "My poor child has not fully recovered her strength. Her horse stumbled beneath her during a hunt she attended; her nerves are still shattered by the shock. Our physician forbids any exertion. Thus, we arrived here by easy stages, barely six leagues a day. Now, however, I must journey day and night on a mission of life and death—a mission whose gravity I shall explain when we meet again, as I hope we shall, in a few weeks, without need for further concealment."

She continued her petition, yet the tone suggested she was bestowing a favor, not asking for one. Yet, in substance, it was far more audacious. She spoke as if granting me the burden of her daughter, rather than requesting it. To take charge of a stranger, especially one so young, was a heavy yoke. And yet, she had stripped away every argument against it, casting herself entirely upon my chivalry.

As if fate itself had intervened to seal my doom, my own niece stepped forward at that precise moment. Beseeching me in a whisper, she urged me to invite this new friend, Millarca, to stay. She had probed the stranger, and believed, if permitted, her mother would approve.

At any other time, I would have bidden her wait. Prudence demanded silence until their true natures were known. But I had no moment to think. The two women assailed me simultaneously. The Countess's grave authority, coupled with the young lady's breathtaking beauty and the electric fire of high birth, overwhelmed me. Overpowered, I yielded too easily, undertaking the care of the girl whom her mother named Millarca.

The Countess signaled to her daughter, who listened with solemn attention as her mother outlined the sudden summons and the arrangements I had made, claiming I was one of her earliest and most valued friends. I offered the polite speeches the situation required, only to realize, with a chill creeping up my spine, that I stood in a position I deeply disliked.

The gentleman in black returned, conducting the Countess from the room with a ceremony that marked her as a woman of immense, perhaps terrifying, power. Her countenance impressed me with the conviction that she was far more important than her modest title suggested.

Her final charge to me was absolute: "Make no attempt to learn more about me than you might already guess, until my return." She pointed to the distinguished host—the man in black—as someone who understood her reasons. "Here," she added, her voice dropping to a hiss, "neither I nor my daughter could safely remain longer than a day. I removed my mask imprudently an hour ago, and I fear you saw me. If I had discovered you witnessed me, I would have thrown myself on your honor to keep my secret for weeks. As it stands, I am satisfied you saw nothing. But if suspicion now lingers, or returns later, I commit myself, and my daughter, entirely to your honor. You will remind her, from time to time, lest she thoughtlessly discloses it."

She whispered urgently to her daughter, kissed her twice with frantic haste, and departed with the pale gentleman in black, vanishing into the throng.

"In the next room," Millarca said, her voice trembling slightly, "there is a window overlooking the hall door. I wish to see my mother leave, and to wave to her."

We complied, guiding her to the glass. Outside, a handsome, old-fashioned carriage waited, flanked by a retinue of couriers and footmen. There, standing in the mist, was the slim figure of the pale gentleman in black, holding a thick velvet cloak. He draped it over the Countess's shoulders, pulling the hood down over her face. She nodded to him, briefly touching his gloved hand. He bowed repeatedly as the doors closed, and the carriage rolled away.

"She is gone," Millarca sighed, turning back to us.

"She is gone," I echoed to myself, the words tasting like ash. For the first time, in those hurried moments following my consent, the sheer folly of my action crashed down upon me.

"She did not look up," Millarca said, her voice plaintive.

"The Countess likely took off her mask and did not care to show her face," I lied, trying to soothe the unease in my gut. "And she could not have known you were at the window."

Millarca sighed, looking at me with wide, innocent eyes. She was so beautiful that I relented instantly. Any regret I harbored for rejecting her warmth vanished, replaced by a desperate desire to atone for the unspoken rudeness of my reception.

Replacing her mask, Millarca joined my ward in persuading me to return to the grounds, where the concert was to resume. We agreed, walking along the terrace beneath the castle windows.

Millarca grew intimate with us quickly, delighting us with lively tales of the great people we encountered. I found her increasingly captivating. Her gossip, devoid of malice, was refreshingly diverting to one so long isolated from the great world. I imagined the vibrant life she would bring to our lonely evenings.

The ball dragged on until the morning sun breached the horizon. The Grand Duke danced with unwavering loyalty, and so, bound by etiquette, none of us dared retire.

Just as we emerged from a crowded salon, Millarca asked where she had seen me. I thought she stood beside me; she fancied she stood beside me. In truth, we had lost her.

Every effort to locate her proved futile. I feared she had mistaken others for her new friends in the confusion of a brief separation, wandering off into the vast, labyrinthine grounds.

"Now," I realized, the horror dawning with crushing force, "I recognize a new folly: undertaking the charge of a young lady without knowing her name. Fettered by my promises, and ignorant of the reasons for them, I cannot even direct my search by identifying her as the daughter of the Countess who departed mere hours ago."

Morning broke, bringing clear daylight, yet I continued my fruitless search. It was not until nearly two o'clock the following day that any news of my missing charge surfaced.

A servant knocked at my niece's door, reporting that a young lady, appearing in great distress, had begged him to locate General Baron Spielsdorf and his daughter, whom she claimed to have been entrusted to by her mother.

There could be no doubt, despite the slight inaccuracy, that our friend had resurfaced. And so she had. Oh, if only we had let her vanish forever!

She recounted a tale to explain her prolonged absence. Late in the evening, driven to despair by failing to find us, she had sought refuge in the housekeeper's bedroom, falling into a deep sleep. That slumber, however long, had hardly sufficed to restore her strength after the exhaustion of the ball.

That day, Millarca returned with us. I was, for all intents and purposes, too happy to secure such a charming companion for my dear girl, unaware that I had just invited a nightmare into our sanctuary.

"Yet, alas, there proved to be serious drawbacks. First, Millasca grew lethargic, plagued by a lingering weakness from her recent illness. She rarely left her chamber until well past noon. Worse still, though she invariably locked her door from within and kept the key hidden until admitting her maid, it was discovered that she vanished regularly. In the pale, pre-dawn grey, and again at unexplained hours during the day, she slipped away before anyone knew she stirred. Witnesses from the windows of the *Schloss* reported seeing her wandering eastward through the trees, moving with the vacant, trance-like gait of a somnambulist. I concluded she walked in her sleep, yet this offered no solution. How could she exit a securely barred room? How could she leave the estate without unlocking a single door or window?

"My anxieties deepened into a terrifying dread when I observed her physical decline. Her beauty and health faded with a mystery that bordered on the horrific. First, she was haunted by nightmares of appalling vividness. Then, she claimed visions of a specter—a shifting form, sometimes resembling Millasca, sometimes bestial and indistinct—that paced the foot of her bed. Finally, the sensations began. She spoke of a cold, icy stream flowing against her breast, followed by the sharp, needle-pointed agony beneath her throat. Nights later, a convulsive sense of strangulation seized her, leading inevitably to unconsciousness."

I listened, frozen, to the words of the kindly old General. By now, our carriage rolled over the short grass flanking the road, approaching the roofless village that had known no chimney smoke for fifty years. Every description he uttered mirrored my own symptoms with chilling precision. I shuddered as he detailed the habits of our beautiful guest, Carmilla—who, save for the tragedy that befell her, would have been visiting my father's château at this very moment.

Suddenly, the forest parted. We found ourselves beneath the crumbling chimneys and gables of the ruined village, while the towers and battlements of the dismantled castle loomed above us, shadowed by giant trees on a slight eminence.

In a nightmare state, I dismounted from the carriage. In silence, for both of us were burdened by terrible thoughts, we climbed the ascent and entered the vast, echoing chambers, winding staircases, and dark corridors of the castle.

"And this was once the palatial residence of the Karnsteins!" the old General declared, peering from a high window across the sweeping forest. "A wicked line, and here their bloodstained history was written." His voice held a grim weight. "It is a cruelty that, after death, they should continue to plague mankind with their vile lusts. Down there lies their chapel." He pointed to the grey, Gothic ruins partially obscured by foliage. "And I hear the axe of a woodman ringing through the trees surrounding it. Perhaps he holds the knowledge I seek, and can direct me to the grave of Mircalla, Countess of Karnstein. These common folk preserve the legends of great houses long after the titles and wealth have vanished."

"We possess a portrait of Mircalla at home," my father offered politely. "Would you care to view it?"

"Not yet, dear friend," the General replied. "I suspect I have already seen the original. My true reason for hastening to you was to investigate the chapel we approach."

"Why, she has been dead for over a century!" my father gasped, incredulous.

"Not so dead as you imagine, I am told," the General answered softly.

"I confess, Sir General, you leave me utterly baffled," my father murmured, glancing at the old man. For a fleeting second, I thought I saw the suspicion return to his eyes—the same suspicion I had felt before—but beneath his anger and hatred, there was no madness, only a feverish intensity.

"There remains for me," the General said, guiding us beneath the massive arch of the Gothic church—"one final purpose to occupy me in these remaining years on Earth. To exact upon her the vengeance which, thank God, a mortal arm can still inflict."

"What vengeance do you speak of?" my father asked, his voice rising in alarm.

"To decapitate the monster!" the General roared, his face flushing with a fierce, violent red. He stamped heavily, the echo reverberating mournfully through the hollow ruin, and raised a clenched fist, mimicking the grip on an axe handle, shaking it ferociously. "To sever her head!"

"Cut her head off?" my father stammered, bewildered.

"Aye! With a hatchet, with a spade, with any iron that can cleave through her murderous throat. You shall hear," he growled, trembling with rage. Hurrying forward, he gestured to a square block of wood lying on the grass-grown pavement. "That beam will serve as a seat; your daughter is weary. Let her sit, and I shall conclude this dreadful tale in a few sentences."

I gratefully sank onto the rough timber. Meanwhile, the General summoned the woodman, who had been clearing branches leaning against the ancient walls. The sturdy, weather-beaten man stepped forward, axe in hand.

"He knows nothing of these tombs," the General noted, "but there is an older man, a ranger of these woods, currently staying with the priest two miles hence. He can identify every monument of the old Karnstein line. For a small fee, he will guide us there in less than half an hour."

"How long have you served in this forest?" my father asked the old woodman.

"I have been a woodman here," the man replied in his rough dialect, "under the forester all my life. So did my father, and his father before him, for generations I cannot count. I could show you the very cottage in this village where my ancestors dwelt."

"Why was the village abandoned?" the General inquired.

"It was tormented by revenants, sir," the woodman explained. "Several were tracked to their graves, identified by the traditional signs, and put to an end by decapitation, the stake, or fire. Yet, despite these lawful executions, the village remained cursed."

"But surely, with so many vampires destroyed, the evil must have ceased?"

"No," the woodman shook his head. "A Moravian nobleman, passing through, heard of our plight. Being skilled in such matters, he offered to free us. On a night of a bright moon, shortly after sunset, he climbed the chapel towers. From there, he watched the churchyard below. He saw the vampire emerge from his grave, placing the linen wrappings he wore as a corpse beside it, before gliding silently toward the village to torment its people.

"The stranger waited. When the vampire returned, missing his clothes, he screamed furiously at the Moravian, who sat atop the tower. The nobleman beckoned him upward, inviting him to retrieve his garments. Trusting the lure, the vampire began to climb. As soon as he reached the battlements, the Moravian struck him down with a single blow of his sword, splitting his skull in two. The body fell to the churchyard below. Descending the spiral stairs, the stranger followed, severed the head, and delivered both the head and the body to the villagers the next day. They impaled and burned the remains."

"This nobleman possessed authority from the head of the family to remove the tomb of Mircalla, Countess of Karnstein," the General continued, his eyes gleaming. "He did so effectively, and soon the site was forgotten."

"Do you know where it stood?" the General asked eagerly.

The forester smiled, shaking his head. "No living soul can tell you that now. Besides, rumors say her body was removed, though no one is certain."

With that, dropping his axe, the woodman departed, leaving us alone to listen to the rest of the General's extraordinary narrative.

XIV. The Meeting

"My dearest child," the General continued, his voice trembling with a mixture of grief and frustration, "was growing swiftly worse. The physician who had tended her had achieved nothing but delay, for I believed at the time that her ailment was merely physical. He perceived my mounting terror and proposed a second opinion. I summoned a far more distinguished colleague from Graz.

Days passed before his arrival. He was a man of profound piety and unimpeachable learning. After examining my poor ward, the two doctors retreated to my library to deliberate. From the adjacent chamber, where I paced in anxious anticipation, I overheard their voices rising above the bounds of polite discourse, erupting into a heated debate. I knocked and entered, finding the elder doctor from Graz holding firm to his theories, while his rival countered with derision, punctuated by loud, unseemly bursts of laughter. The discord ceased abruptly at my entrance.

"Sir," the younger physician said to the older, "my esteemed colleague suggests you require a conjurer, not a healer."

"Pardon me," the doctor from Graz replied, his brow furrowed with displeasure. "I shall articulate my perspective in due course. I grieve, Monsieur le General, that despite my science, I am powerless to aid her. Yet, before I depart, I must offer you counsel."

He appeared deeply thoughtful, sitting at a desk to compose a document. Disappointed, I bowed and prepared to leave. As I turned, the other doctor gestured toward his companion writing at the table, then shook his head and tapped his temple with a look of weary resignation.

This consultation served to confirm my despair. I stepped outside into the grounds, nearly undone by sorrow. Minutes later, the doctor from Graz overtook me. He apologized for following but confessed he could not leave without further words. He assured me that no natural malady presented such symptoms; death hovered imminently, perhaps within twenty-four hours. If the fatal seizure could be halted immediately, her strength might return, but everything now rested upon the razor's edge between life and oblivion. A single further assault could snuff out the final flicker of vitality.

"And what is the nature of this seizure?" I begged.

"I have detailed everything in this note," he said, pressing a folded paper into my hand. "Read it only in the presence of the nearest clergyman. You would otherwise dismiss it as madness, yet it concerns life and death. If the priest fails, then you may read it alone."

He departed, urging me to invite a scholar uniquely knowledgeable on the subject who would surely interest me once I understood his letter.

The priest was nowhere to be found, so I read the letter in solitude. In calmer times, it would have amused me; it was monstrous enough to warrant confinement. He declared the patient was being visited by a vampire! He identified the punctures near the throat as the work of two long, sharp fangs peculiar to these creatures, and the small livid mark around them as the imprint of the demon's lips. Every symptom described by the sufferer aligned perfectly with historical accounts of vampiric visitations.

Though I remained a skeptic regarding the supernatural, the desperation of losing a beloved soul drove me to act on his instructions. I hid in the darkened dressing room adjoining her chamber, watching from behind a sliver of light as a candle burned low. I lay in wait with my sword drawn, as directed, until shortly past midnight. Then, I saw a large, indistinct black shape crawl silently across the foot of the bed, swelling instantly into a great, throbbing mass against the girl's neck.

For a heartbeat, I stood frozen. Then, springing forward with my blade raised, I struck. The black form recoiled, gliding back toward the foot of the bed, only to stand upright on the floor a yard away. It faced me with a gaze of skulking ferocity and horror, revealing itself as Millarca. Confused, I struck again, but she vanished. My sword shattered against the doorframe, splintering into useless shards.

I cannot recount the terrors of that night. The entire household erupted into alarm. The specter of Millarca fled, but her victim succumbed rapidly, dying before dawn broke.

Later, amidst the somber silence of the cemetery, the old General was visibly shaken. My father wandered off, tracing the inscriptions on crumbling tombstones, eventually drifting into a side chapel. The General leaned against a broken pillar, drying his eyes with heavy sighs. Relief washed over me when I heard the approach of Carmilla and Madame. Their footsteps softened as they drew near.

Alone in the shadows, surrounded by the towering, silent foliage and the moldering dust of ancient graves, a deep, primal horror seized me. My heart sank, fearing that even my friends would soon be lost to this cursed place.

Near the base of a shattered monument, beneath a narrow archway carved with grotesque demons, I saw Carmilla emerge from the gloom. Her beauty was radiant, her smile engaging. Just as I prepared to greet her, a cry tore from the General beside me. He lunged forward, brandishing a woodcutter's axe.

Instantly, Carmilla's countenance transformed. The gentle warmth vanished, replaced by a brutal, animalistic fury. She recoiled, making a crouching step backward. Before I could scream, the General swung the axe with all his might. She dived beneath the blow, unharmed, and clamped her tiny, iron-hard fingers onto his wrist. He struggled frantically, veins bulging, but his hand went limp. The axe clattered to the earth, and she slipped away into the darkness like smoke.

The General staggered back, leaning against the cold stone. His grey hair stood erect, sweat pouring down his face as if he were on the brink of death.

The nightmare lasted but a moment. When I recovered consciousness, Madame stood before me, her voice frantic. "Where is Mademoiselle Carmilla?" she repeated, her eyes wide with panic.

"I... I do not know," I stammered, pointing toward the chapel door where she had vanished. "Only moments ago."

"But I stood right there in the passage!" she cried, rushing to the doors and calling out, "Carmilla! Where are you?" Her calls echoed through the corridors and windows, met only by silence.

"She called herself Carmilla?" the General asked, his voice hollow.

"Yes," I whispered.

"Aye," he murmured, his face pale. "That is Millarca. That is the same soul who was once known as Mircalla, Countess Karnstein. Depart from this accursed ground, my poor child, at once. Drive to the clergyman's house and remain there until we arrive. Flee! You shall never see Carmilla again. She is not here."

Ordeal and Execution

A figure emerged from the shadows of the chapel's threshold, a man so grotesque and strange in appearance that he seemed plucked from a nightmare rather than reality. He towered above the others, yet his chest was unnaturally narrow, his shoulders hunched with a bird-like stoop. Clad in somber black, his skin was the color of old earth, etched with deep, cruel furrows. Upon his head sat an ill-fitting hat adorned with a broad, unnatural leaf. Long, grizzled hair cascaded down his back like tangled roots. A pair of heavy gold spectacles perched on his nose, magnifying eyes that darted restlessly—sometimes fixed upward toward the vaulted ceiling, sometimes bowed low toward the dust. A perpetual, rictus grin stretched across his face, revealing nothing of joy. His long, spindly arms swung with a jerky, shambling rhythm, clad in black gloves far too wide for his gaunt hands, waving in a frantic, abstract dance.

"The very man!" cried the General, stepping forward with a radiant, almost feverish delight. "My dear Baron, how fortuitous! I dared not hope to encounter you so swiftly." He signaled to my father, who had just returned, gesturing for the eccentric visitor—the Baron—to approach. Introductions were exchanged with stiff formality, yet immediately the air grew thick with urgent business. The stranger produced a rolled parchment from his coat and unspooled it across the weathered stone of a nearby tomb. From a leather case, he drew a pencil, tracing invisible geometries onto the paper. As he worked, his gaze flickered constantly between the sketch and specific points within the chapel, suggesting to me that he was drafting a blueprint of the sanctuary itself. Occasionally, he would mutter passages from a grimy, yellow-leaved booklet, reading aloud as if chanting a spell.

Together, the General and the Baron wandered down the side aisle, past the silent rows of tombs, debating fervently. They paused to measure distances by pacing, their steps rhythmic and precise. Finally, they halted before a section of the sidewall, examining it with microscopic scrutiny. With practiced hands, they peeled back clinging ivy and tapped the plaster with wooden sticks, listening intently to the hollow echoes beneath the surface. Scrape after scrape, knock after knock, until at last, hidden behind layers of decay, they revealed a broad marble slab inscribed with relief lettering.

It was the lost monument of Mircalla, Countess Karnstein.

Though the General was not a man known for piety, he stopped abruptly. Raising his hands and eyes to the heavens, he offered a silent prayer of thanks, his face bathed in a sudden, holy light.

"Tomorrow," I heard him declare, his voice trembling with authority, "the Imperial Commissioner shall arrive. The Inquisition will proceed strictly according to the laws of the land."

Turning to the old man with the gold spectacles, the General seized both of his hands in a warm, vigorous grip. "Baron, words fail me. How shall we repay your service? By driving this plague from our soil, ending a scourge that has haunted these people for over a century, you have saved us all. Thank God, the monster is finally found."

My father stepped aside, ushering the strangers away. I watched them retreat, aware that they intended to speak in private. Their glances back at me were quick, furtive, filled with a mixture of triumph and caution.

When my father returned, he embraced me repeatedly, his face wet with tears. Leading me from the gloom of the chapel, he whispered, "Time to return. But first, we must summon the local priest, who resides nearby. We need him to join us at the castle."

Our search for the cleric was swift and successful. Though I was utterly exhausted, a profound sense of relief washed over me. Yet, as we walked home, a cold dread settled in my stomach. There was no word from Carmilla. No explanation was offered regarding the chaotic scene in the ruined chapel. My father had decided, for now, to shroud the truth in silence.

Her sinister absence rendered the memory of the chapel's interior infinitely more terrifying. That night, the arrangements were peculiar and foreboding. Two servants and Madame were to remain awake in my chamber, while the priest and my father kept vigil in the adjacent dressing room. I saw the priest perform obscure, solemn rites that night, rituals whose meaning I could not grasp, nor could I comprehend the necessity of such extreme precautions for my safety.

Only days later did the pieces fall into place. With Carmilla gone, the nightly terrors ceased.

You have likely heard tales of the superstitious dread that grips the lands of Upper and Lower Styria, Moravia, Silesia, Turkey, Poland, and Russia—the terror of the Vampire. If human testimony, gathered with judicial solemnity before countless commissions of learned and upright men, holds any weight, then one cannot doubt the existence of this phenomenon. For my part, having witnessed horrors that defy rational explanation, I accept only the ancient, well-attested beliefs of my homeland.

The following day, the formal proceedings commenced in the Chapel of Karnstein.

The grave of the Countess Mircalla was broken open. Both the General and my father stared in horror and recognition at the visage now exposed to the air. Though one hundred and fifty years had passed since her burial, her features burned with the warmth of life. Her eyes were wide open; no stench of decomposition rose from the coffin. Two medical experts, one official and one representing the prosecutor, confirmed the miraculous facts: a faint but distinct breath, a beating heart, limbs perfectly supple, and flesh that remained elastic. The leaden coffin floated in a pool of blood, submerging her body to a depth of seven inches.

There, laid bare before the world, were all the undeniable signs of vampirism. In accordance with ancient custom, the body was lifted. A sharpened stake was driven violently through her heart. She let out a piercing shriek—a sound of agony indistinguishable from that of a living soul in its final torment. Then, her head was severed from her neck, and a torrent of dark blood gushed forth. Her body and head were cast upon a pyre of wood, consumed by flames, and reduced to ash. These remains were scattered into the river, carried away by the current, and the land has never since suffered the visitation of a vampire.

My father possesses a copy of the Imperial Commission's report, bearing the signatures of every witness, verifying this account. From this official document, I have drawn the narrative of this final, shocking ordeal.

I commit these words to paper with a facade of composure, though the truth is, I am a trembling ruin. It takes nothing short of your earnest, repeated pleas to force me to sit and recount a nightmare that has left my nerves frayed for months and resurrected the unspeakable terrors of my past. Even now, delivered from the immediate threat, those shadows make my days and nights a hollow dread, turning solitude into a place where the walls seem to breathe.

Before I close this account, let me speak briefly of Baron Vordenburg, the keeper of that strange and terrible lore. To our fortune—and perhaps to the Countess Mircalla's undoing—it was his knowledge that revealed the location of her grave. He had retreated to Graz, surviving on a meager stipend, the last dregs of a once-princely fortune in Upper Styria. There, he dedicated himself to the minute, laborious excavation of every authenticating record regarding vampirism. He knew the classics by heart—"Magia Posthuma," "Phlegon de Mirabilibus," "Augustinus de cura pro Mortuis"—and John Christopher Herenberg's treatise, *Philosophicae et Christianae Cogitationes de Vampiris*. He possessed a voluminous digest of judicial cases, from which he had distilled a system of principles governing the creature's condition.

Vordenburg taught me a vital lesson: the deadly pallor often attributed to these revenants is merely theatrical fiction. In the grave, and when they emerge to haunt the living, they wear the mask of healthy vitality. When exposed to the light within their coffins, however, the rot reveals itself, just as it did with the long-dead Countess Karnstein.

They possess a terrifying physiology. They slip out of their tombs and return within set hours, disturbing neither the earth nor the linens, their movement defying natural law. Their existence is amphibious, sustained by a daily, renewed slumber in the dark soil, fed by a horrific thirst for living blood. This hunger grants them vigor in the waking world, fueling a fascination that rivals the intensity of earthly love. For specific victims, they display an inexhaustible patience and cunning, employing stratagems to bypass obstacles, courting their prey with the refinement of an epicure to savor the moment of consumption. They crave a semblance of consent, yearning to drain the life from those they have ensnared. Yet, in ordinary instances, they abandon subtlety, striking with brute violence, strangling, and exhausting their victim in a single, brutal feast.

There appears to be a unique constraint on the vampire, particularly in the case of Mircalla. She seemed bound to a name that, if not her true identity, must anagrammatically reconstruct the letters composing it. *Carmilla*. *Millarca*. These were her guises, shifting forms of the same fatal essence.

After Carmilla's expulsion, the Baron stayed with us for some weeks. My father recounted to him the tale of the Moravian nobleman and the tragedy at the Karnstein churchyard. Curiously, the Baron asked how he had ever pinpointed the exact coordinates of the concealed tomb. His features, usually sharp and severe, puckered into a mysterious, knowing smile. Gazing down at a worn spectacles case, he fumbled with it before looking up again.

"I hold many journals and papers penned by that remarkable man," he began, his voice dropping to a conspiratorial whisper. "The most curious of all treats of the visit you mentioned to Karnstein. Tradition, of course, distorts the truth, coloring it with myth. He might have passed as a Moravian nobleman due to his residence there, yet he was, in truth, a native of Upper Styria. In his earliest youth, he was a passionate, favored lover of the beautiful Mircalla, Countess Karnstein. Her premature death plunged him into an inconsolable grief."

He paused, his eyes distant. "It is the nature of vampires to multiply, but they follow an ascended, ghostly law. Assume a territory free from such pestilence. How does the plague begin? A person, driven to wickedness or despair, puts an end to themselves. Under certain conditions, a suicide becomes a vampire. This specter haunts the sleeping, causing them to die, and almost invariably, those who pass into the grave transform into vampires. This was the fate of Mircalla, haunted by a demon. My ancestor, Vordenburg—whose title I still bear—soon discerned the truth. Through his studies, he uncovered the laws of this wretched existence."

"The suspicion inevitably fell upon the dead Countess, the woman who had been his idol," the Baron continued. "Yet, a profound horror seized him at the thought of her remains being profaned by a posthumous execution. He believed that upon expulsion from the amphibious state of the grave, the vampire would project into a far more horrible existence. Driven by love and a twisted mercy, he devised a stratagem. He staged a journey to retrieve her remains, only to execute a real obliteraton of her monument. When age finally claimed him, looking back across the valleys he had left behind, a fresh horror possessed him. He realized the magnitude of his deception. With trembling hands, he traced the errors of his plan, creating notes that would guide future seekers to the beast's lair. He drafted a confession of his own treachery. Had he lived longer, he might have acted further, but death intervened. And now, the hand of a remote descendant directs this pursuit, too late for many, to the final resting place of the monster."

We spoke for some time afterward. Among the chilling details he shared was this: "One sure sign of the vampire is the power of the hand. The slender fingers of Mircalla closed upon the General's wrist like a vice of cold steel when he raised the hatchet. But the grip is not merely crushing; it leaves a lingering numbness in the seized limb, a paralysis that recovers only slowly, if it ever does."

That spring, my father took me on a tour of Italy. We were gone for more than a year, seeking distance from the trauma. Long before the terror of recent events fully subsided, the image of Carmilla returned to my mind with ambiguous, cruel alternations. Sometimes she appeared as the playful, languid beauty; other times, the writhing fiend I had witnessed in the ruined church. Often, awakening from a reverie, I found myself startled, convinced I had heard the light, impossible step of Carmilla pausing just outside the drawing-room door.
