Noble Henry Foker, whom we had briefly lost track of, had not idled away his time. A man of constancy, naturally, turns his full attention to whatever passion consumes him, and Foker's all-absorbing obsession was love. I earnestly suggest to my youthful readers, particularly those prone to such "amusements," that they pause to calculate the staggering cost of this occupation. Consider the hours sacrificed to it! One loses precious sleep, tossing in bed thinking of the adored object, emerging late for breakfast just as noon advances and the family scatters. Once engaged in the day's necessary labors, one performs them with a listless spirit, all mental faculties diverted elsewhere. The work is slurred over; friends and relatives, one's natural companions, are neglected in favor of a fleeting glance at the beloved, a peek through her window, or a spy on her carriage in the Park. By night, the blandishments of home become tedious; Mother's chatter grows stale, and the meal she prepares with such care remains largely untouched, save for one particular dish that somehow retains its flavor. Life, business, and family ties, once sources of comfort, become intolerable burdens, rendering one uneasy anywhere except in the active pursuit of one's flame.

I believe this is frequently the state of mind among ill-regulated young gentlemen, and surely it was Mr. H. Foker's condition. Having been raised to indulge every inclination, he surrendered himself to this one with his customary, selfish enthusiasm. Nor should the world-beaten be surprised that Mr. Foker, despite offering Arthur Pendennis sound advice on a prior occasion, became passion's slave in return. Who among us hasn't preached the virtues of temperance to friends, only to practice them less consistently ourselves? To be sure, you, Madam, may be a perfect being, devoid of wrong thoughts in your frigid, irreproachable existence; or you, Sir, may be so strong-minded that foolish passions cannot disturb your equanimity in chambers or at the 'Change. Such serenity renders you immune to sympathy, for you require none. We reserve our compassion for the humble and weak, those who stumble and rise again to march with the rest of mortals. What need have angels of a hand to lift them? Your serene virtue casts no shadow of passion, nor stirs by temptation, nor darkens with remorse; thus, companionship becomes unbearable. You are, by virtue of your elevation, necessarily lonely. We cannot converse familiarly with such potentates, so we bid them goodbye. Our path lies with humble folk, not serene highnesses like you. And let us note that there are no perfect characters in this history, save perhaps one little one, who is far from perfect, for she never realizes it, believing with a deplorable misapprehension that she is as great a sinner as any.

This young lady is currently absent from London, and it is certainly not for her that Mr. Henry Foker's mind is currently agitated. Yet, who cares for minor failings? Must we all be angels, male or female, to be worshipped as such? Let us admire the kaleidoscope of human tastes. From the oldest and ugliest to the stupidest and most pompous, from the silliest and most vapid to the greatest criminals, tyrants, and boobies—we need never despair. I have read of a transported pickpocket's passion for a female convict, both aged and repulsive, ignorant and quarrelsome, their love as magnificent as Cleopatra's for Antony or Lancelot's for Guinevere. The flame Count Borulawski, the Polish dwarf, inspired in the bosom of the most beautiful Baroness at Dresden, is common knowledge. The fever that burned in young Cornet Tozer's heart, causing him to flee and wed Mrs. Battersby, who was old enough to be his mother, is chronicled in newspapers. Should we be ashamed or pleased to know our hearts are constructed so that the highest Ajax may find himself prostrate before the pattens of his kitchen-maid? That no poverty, shame, or crime will be endured, hugged with delight, and cherished more closely than virtue itself, by the perverse fidelity and admirable, constant folly of a woman?

Thus, Henry Foker, Esquire, longed for his love and cursed the fate separating them. While Lord Gravesend's family retreated to the country, leaving his proxy with the venerable Lord Bagwig, Harry lingered in London, certainly not to Lady Ann's sorrow, to whom he was affianced and who missed him not at all. Whichever direction Miss Clavering moved, this infatuated young man followed. Aware that his engagement to his cousin was public knowledge, he was forced to conceal his passion, locking it within his breast until it was compressed so tightly it threatened to explode, a stormy secret poised to collapse him.

A grand entertainment had taken place at Gaunt House on a beautiful evening in June. The following day's journals listed nearly two columns of the most closely printed nobility and gentry honored with invitations. Among the guests were Sir Francis and Lady Clavering, and Miss Amory, for whom the indefatigable Major Pendennis had secured an invite, alongside our two young friends, Arthur and Harry. Both youths exerted themselves, dancing extensively with Miss Blanche. As for the worthy Major, he assumed charge of Lady Clavering, ensuring her introduction to that department of the mansion where her ladyship particularly excelled: the refreshment-room. Amidst pictures of Titian and Giorgione, regal portraits of Van Dyck and Reynolds, enormous salvers of gold and silver, pyramids of flowers, and constellations of wax candles—in a manner perfectly regardless of expense—a supper was served all night. Of the countless creams, jellies, salads, peaches, white soups, grapes, pâtés, galantines, cups of tea, champagne, and sundries Lady Clavering consumed, it is not proper for us to speak. How much the Major suffered as he followed the honest woman about, summoning solemn male attendants and lovely servant-maids, administering to Lady Clavering's various needs with admirable patience, no one knows—he never confessed. He never allowed his agony to mar his countenance in the least; yet, with constant kindness, he brought plate after plate to the Begum.

Mr. Wagg attempted to tally every dish Lady Clavering consumed, though his powers of calculation were compromised by his own free consumption of champagne throughout the evening. Consequently, he recommended Mr. Honeyman, Lady Steyne's physician, to monitor the Begum's health closely and inquire of her status the following day.

Sir Francis Clavering made his appearance, skulking about the magnificent rooms for a while. However, the company and splendor surrounding him failed to suit the Baronet's taste. After tossing off a tumbler or two of wine at the buffet, he departed Gaunt House for the neighborhood of Jermyn Street, where his friends Loder, Punter, little Moss Abrams, and Captain Skewball were gathered around a familiar green table. In the rattling of their boxes and the agreeable hum of their conversation, Sir Francis's spirits rose to their accustomed point of feeble hilarity.

Mr. Pynsent, who had previously requested Miss Amory's hand, approached to claim her again. However, having exchanged scowls of recognition with Mr. Arthur Pendennis in the dancing-room, Arthur suddenly rose and claimed Miss Amory as his partner for the present dance. Mr. Pynsent, biting his lips and scowling savagely, withdrew with a profound bow, stating that he relinquished his claim. Some men seem destined to cross one's path in life; Pynsent and Pen held this mutual view, regarding each other accordingly.

"What a confounded conceited provincial fool!" thought the one. "Because he has penned a twopenny novel, his absurd head is turned, and a kicking would take his conceit out of him."

"What an impertinent idiot!" remarked the other to his partner. "His soul resides in Downing Street; his neckcloth is foolscap; his hair is sand; his legs are rulers; his vitals are tape and sealing-wax; he was a prig in his cradle and has never laughed since birth, save three times at the same joke of his chief. I share the same disdain for that man, Miss Amory, as I do for that cold boiled veal." Upon which Blanche, of course, remarked that Mr. Pendennis was wicked, méchant, and perfectly abominable, wondering what he would say when her back was turned.

"Say! Say that you possess the most beautiful figure and the slimmest waist in the world, Blanche—Miss Amory, I mean. I beg your pardon. Another turn; this music would make an alderman dance."

"And have you ceased tumbling when you waltz now?" Blanche asked, archly lifting her gaze to her partner's face.

"One falls and rises again in life, Blanche; you know I used to call you so in old times, and it is the prettiest name in the world. Besides, I have practiced since then."

"With a great number of partners, I'm afraid," Blanche said, feigning a slight sigh and shrugging her shoulders. Indeed, Mr. Pen had practiced considerably in this life and undeniably improved his dancing.
