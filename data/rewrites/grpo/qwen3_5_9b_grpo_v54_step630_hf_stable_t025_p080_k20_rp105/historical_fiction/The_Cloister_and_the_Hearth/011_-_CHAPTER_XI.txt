The hatred burning in the burgomaster's gaze, sealed by the cold bars of his prison, had convinced Gerard that Ghysbrecht was his mortal foe. He moved silently around the tower's corner, expecting not a miracle, but the cruel trap of a villain plotting his doom in a cell from which escape would remain a secret.

As he stepped into the shadows, a soft yet firm hand slipped into his. Margaret stood beside him, sharing the peril.

Before they could speak, the haunted tower loomed ahead, and a sight froze them in place. Halfway up the stone wall, a creature with a head ablaze like a giant glowworm climbed steadily upward. Its body was dark, outlined only by the eerie radiance of its fiery crown, stretching nearly four feet in length. At the tower's base stood a figure in white, indistinguishable from a living woman. Gerard and Margaret trembled with awe.

"The rope!" Gerard gasped. "It is ascending the rope!"

As they watched, the glowing entity vanished into the prison window, its light flooding the cell and turning the glass a deep crimson. The white figure remained motionless below.

Those who survive the initial shock of the supernatural often succumb to a terrifying impulse: a wild urge to rush toward the horror, just as a bird might fly into a snake. The great tragedian Macready once portrayed this perfectly in *Macbeth*, when Banquo's ghost appeared; he threw himself at the shadow with his head averted. That same magnetic pull seized Margaret. She released Gerard's hand, standing bewildered for a heartbeat before letting out a wild cry and darting toward the specter. Gerard, unaware of this natural instinct, believed the devil was dragging her to ruin. He fell to his knees.

"Exorcizo vos! In the name of the Blessed Mary, I exorcise you!" he shrieked, his voice cracking with terror.

To his immense relief, the specter emitted a feeble cry of fear. Discovering that even hell possessed weaknesses was encouraging. Gerard redoubled his prayers, and soon the ghastly shape knelt at Margaret's feet, begging piteously for mercy.

Soon, Kate and Giles arrived at the tower. Their surprise was palpable upon finding a new rope dangling from the prisoner's window to the ground.

"I see how it is," the dwarf declared, accepting facts as they came. "Our Gerard has descended this rope. He is free. I shall ascend and verify."

"No, Giles, no!" the superior intelligence cried, blinded by prejudice. "Do you not see this is glamour? This rope is a line cast by the evil one to lure you to destruction. He knows the weaknesses of every heart; he sees how fond you are of climbing. Where would Gerard procure such a rope? How could he fasten it in the sky? It defies nature. Holy saints protect us tonight, for hell is abroad."

"Stuff!" the dwarf retorted. "The way to hell is down, and this rope leads up. I have never had the luck to climb such a long rope. It may be years before I encounter one so prepared. I would rather be knocked on the head now than wait forever without happiness."

With a cry of delight, he sprang onto the rope, much like a cat leaping for a table laden with fish. Every muscle in his frame ignited with energy. The only concession Kate could win was permission to fasten a lantern around his neck first.

"A light frightens ill spirits," she explained.

Thus, with his massive arms and legs moving with feather-light agility, Giles ascended faster than his brother had descended. The lantern at the nape of his neck transformed him into a living glowworm. His sister watched his progress with trembling anxiety. Suddenly, a female figure burst from the solid masonry, flying at her with supernatural speed.

Kate let out a weak cry, the only sound her terror allowed, as her tongue seemed to stick to her palate. She dropped her crutches and sank to her knees, hiding her face and moaning, "Take my body, but spare my soul!"

Margaret, panting, exclaimed, "Why, it is a woman!"

Kate, quivering, echoed, "Why, it is a woman!"

Margaret turned, startled. "How you scared me!"

Kate, still shaking, replied, "I am scared enough myself. Oh! oh! oh!"

"This is strange!" Margaret stammered. "But the fiery-headed thing? It was with you, and you are harmless! Why are you here at this hour?"

"Nay, why are YOU here?"

"Perhaps we are on the same errand? Ah! You are his good sister, Kate!"

"And you are Margaret Brandt."

"Yes."

"All the better. You love him; you are here. Then Giles was right. He has won his freedom."

Gerard stepped forward to confirm the truth, but their reunion was cut short by a horrible, unearthly noise, sounding like a sepulcher speaking through a ventriloquist's dummy:

With each repetition, the volume rose. They looked up to see the dwarf, his hands full of parchments, his face lit with fiendish joy and flushed with diabolical fire. The lantern at his neck made him appear as an infernal transparency. As he spoke, the awful imp hurled documents at the astonished figures below. Records rained down like wounded wild ducks; some collapsed instantly, others fluttered helplessly, while others spread out and wheeled slowly in airy circles. Hardly had they settled when the sepulchral roar returned—"Parchment!—parchment!"—and another flock of documents pattered and sailed down, whitening the grass. Finally, the fire-headed imp, with his light body and horny hands, slid down the rope like a falling star. Putting business before sentiment, he proposed an immediate settlement for the merchandise he had just delivered.

"Hush!" Gerard commanded. "You speak too loudly. Gather them up and follow us to a safer place."

"Will you come home with me, Gerard?" asked little Kate.

"I have no home."

"You shall not say so. Who is more welcome than you, after this cruel wrong, to your father's house?"

"Father! I have no father," Gerard said sternly. "He who was my father has become my gaoler. I have escaped his hands; I will never come within their reach again."

"An enemy did this, not our father," Kate insisted, recounting what she had overheard Cornelis and Sybrandt say. But the injury was too fresh to be soothed. Gerard displayed a bitterness of indignation he had previously seemed incapable of.

"Cornelis and Sybrandt are two ill curs that have shown me their teeth and their heart for a long while, but they could do no more. My father gave the burgomaster authority, or he would not have dared lay a finger on me, a free burgher of this town. So be it. I was his son; I am his prisoner. He has played his part; I shall play mine. Farewell to the burgh where I was born, lived honestly, and was imprisoned. While there is another town left in creation, I will never trouble you again, Tergou."

"Oh! Gerard! Gerard!"

Margaret whispered, "Do not gainsay him now. Give his anger time to cool!"

Kate turned quickly to her. "Let me look at your face?" The inspection seemed favorable, for she whispered, "It is a comely face, and no mischief-maker's."

"Fear me not," Margaret said in the same gentle tone. "I could not be happy without your love, as well as Gerard's."

"These are comfortable words," Kate sobbed. Then, looking up, she added, "I little thought to like you so well. My heart is willing, but my infirmity will not let me embrace you."

At this hint, Margaret gently wound her arms around Gerard's sister and kissed her lovingly.

"Often he has spoken of you to me, Kate; and often I longed for this."

"You, too, Gerard," Kate said. "Kiss me ere you go; for my heart lies heavy at parting with you this night."

Gerard kissed her, and she proceeded on her crutches toward home. The last thing they heard of her was a little, patient sigh. Then tears welled thick in Margaret's eyes, though Gerard, being a man, noticed neither the sigh nor the tears.

As they turned toward Sevenbergen, the dwarf nudged Gerard with his bundle of parchments and held out a concave claw.

Margaret dissuaded him. "Why take what is not ours?"

"Oh, spoil an enemy how you can."

"But may they not make this a handle for fresh violence?"

"How can they? Think you I shall stay in Tergou after this? The burgomaster robbed me of my liberty; I doubt I should take his life for it, if I could."

"Oh, fie! Gerard."

"What! Is life worth more than liberty? Well, I cannot take his life, so I take the first thing that comes to hand."

He gave Giles a few small coins, with which the urchin was gladdened, and shuffled after his sister. Margaret and Gerard were soon joined by Martin, and they departed for Sevenbergen.
