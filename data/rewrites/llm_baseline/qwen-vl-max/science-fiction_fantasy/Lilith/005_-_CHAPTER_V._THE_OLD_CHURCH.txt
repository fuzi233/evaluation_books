I followed him deeper into the pine forest, where silence clung like mist between the trees. We walked in quiet companionship, wrapped in the hushed reverence of ancient woodlands. The air grew thick with memory, and the shadows stretched long and deep beneath the canopy. As we moved forward, the trees grew larger—older, more solitary, their forms twisted by time, some so gnarled they seemed to wear faces carved by centuries.

Then the forest thinned. A clearing opened ahead, edged with heathland stretching toward the horizon.

“You see that hawthorn?” my guide said at last, his beak pointing sharply.

I looked. “I see an old man,” I replied, “bent and white-headed, standing alone.”

“Look again,” he said. “It is a hawthorn.”

“It’s certainly ancient,” I conceded, “but it doesn’t bloom this time of year.”

“The hawthorn blooms when it chooses,” he answered. “That tree stands in the ruins of the church on your home farm. You were going to give orders to the bailiff about its graveyard, weren’t you—the morning of the thunderstorm?”

“I was,” I admitted. “I told him I wanted the whole place turned into a wild rose garden. No plough ever to come within three yards.”

He paused, as if listening to something beyond sound. Then he whispered: “Listen.”

I strained my ears. Was it the wind? Or was it music—faint, distant, like echoes from a forgotten festival? Or was it only my mind playing tricks?

“They still go there,” the raven murmured.

“Who goes? And where?” I asked.

“Those who once prayed there,” he said. “They return. But not for long, I think.”

“And why do they go now?”

“They need one another to shape their thoughts, to hatch their feelings. So they gather, speak, sing—and then, they say, a great idea rises from their hearts like a ship floating free from the river at high tide.”

“Do they pray too?”

“No. They’ve learned that prayer lives best in silence—within the heart itself. Some people are always praying. But look! Look!” He pointed upward.

A snow-white pigeon soared into the sky, wings beating fast, climbing an invisible spiral into the light. Sunlight shimmered across its feathers like liquid silver.

“I see a pigeon,” I said.

“Of course you do,” the raven replied. “But I see a prayer.” He paused. “I wonder whose heart gave birth to that dove. Someone may have awakened in my cemetery.”

“How can a pigeon be a prayer?” I asked. “I understand it might symbolize one—but a living bird born from a heart?”

“It must puzzle you,” he said. “And rightly so.”

“A prayer is spiritual,” I insisted. “A thought. An idea.”

“True,” he said. “But if you knew other worlds, you’d understand your own better. When a heart is truly alive, it gives birth to living thoughts. There is one heart whose dreams are lives, whose every idea walks and breathes. Some prayers are heavy burdens lifted and dropped again. Others rise in living form—the nearest shape they can take. All things alive began as thoughts. That is why they can serve those who think. When someone says to the Great Thinker, ‘Here is one of your thoughts—I am thinking it now,’ that is prayer—a word from a small heart to the great one.”

He gestured downward this time, to the base of a granite boulder.

I looked—and saw a flower.

I had never seen anything like it. Delicate, tender, full of quiet courage. It reminded me of an anemone, but paler—rose-colored, with a golden center that glowed as if lit from within. Its scent was unlike any I’d known—both new and familiar, like the first breath of dawn on a world both ancient and unborn.

“That is a prayer-flower,” the raven said.

“I’ve never seen such a thing,” I whispered.

“There is no other like it,” he replied. “No two prayer-flowers are alike.”

“How do you know it’s a prayer-flower?”

“By its expression,” he said. “More than that, I cannot tell you. If you recognize it, you recognize it. If not, you don’t.”

“Could you teach me to see one?”

“I could not. Even if I could, what would you gain? You wouldn’t know it through your own eyes or your own soul. Why name a thing when you don’t know it? The universe’s work is to make you so foolish that you finally see yourself as fool—and thus begin to grow wise.”

Still, I knew this flower was different. I felt it in my bones. And in that knowing, I sensed a shadow of the prayer that had shaped it—an echo of a heart speaking softly to the world.

Awe washed over me. Not just at the flower, but at the idea: that somewhere, a heart was listening—not to words, but to beauty, to life, to the silent song of creation.
