The following morning, Shirley found her spirits buoyed by the delightful success of the evening's gathering. Turning to Caroline, she confessed her joy in hosting the circle of gentlemen. "I truly relish entertaining a group of men," she remarked, her eyes sparkling. "There is a peculiar amusement in observing how they derive satisfaction from a meal thoughtfully prepared. For ourselves, these exquisite wines and scientifically composed dishes are merely novelties; they hold no sway over our appetites. Yet, gentlemen seem to retain a childlike simplicity regarding food, and it brings a warm satisfaction to please them—provided, of course, they display that becoming, decent self-restraint characteristic of our worthy rector. I often watch Moore to gauge what delights him, though I suspect he lacks that innocent openness. Have you ever discovered his soft spot, Caroline? You know him far better than I."

"It is certainly not the same as my uncle or Dr. Boultby," Caroline replied, a shy smile playing upon her lips. She always derived a secret pleasure from discussing her cousin's character whenever Miss Keeldar initiated the topic. Left to her own devices, she would have avoided such inquiries, but the irresistible temptation to speak of Robert, whom she thought upon constantly, overpowered her reserve. "Nevertheless," she added, "I confess I remain entirely baffled. I have never been able to study Robert without discovering that he was studying me."

"There it lies!" Shirley exclaimed, her tone sharpening. "You cannot fix your gaze upon him without his eyes flashing back immediately. He never lowers his guard. He refuses to grant you an advantage. Even when he does not look directly at you, his thoughts appear busy among your own, tracing every word and action back to its source, contemplating your motives with casual ease. Oh! I understand that type of character perfectly. It provokes me singularly. How does it affect you?"

This query exemplified one of Shirley's sharp, sudden shifts. Caroline, initially fluttered by such directness, had grown accustomed to deflecting these home-thrusts with the quiet resilience of a little Quakeress.

"Pique you? In what way does it provoke you?" she asked gently.

"Here he comes!" Shirley suddenly cried, breaking off her train of thought. She darted to the window, her face lighting up with anticipation. "Here comes a diversion. I never told you of a superb conquest I have recently achieved—one made at those parties where I can never persuade you to attend—and the thing was accomplished without effort or intention on my part, I swear. Here rings the bell—and, by all that is delicious! there are two of them. Do they never hunt save in pairs? You may choose one, Lina, and you may pick your favorite. I hope I am generous enough. Listen to Tartar!"

The black-muzzled, tawny dog, glimpsed briefly in the chapter introducing his mistress, let out a formidable roar from within the hollow hall. Following the bark was a growl more terrifying than the noise itself, menacing like muttered thunder.

"Listen!" cried Shirley again, laughing. "You would think this the prelude to a bloody assault. They will surely be frightened. They do not know old Tartar as I do. They are unaware that his uproars are all sound and fury, signifying nothing!"

Bustle ensued. "Down, sir, down!" rang out a high-toned, imperious voice, accompanied by the snap of a cane or whip. Immediately followed a yell, a scramble, a run—a veritable tumult.

"Oh, Malone, Malone!"

"Down! Down! Down!" the high voice commanded repeatedly.

"He is really worrying them!" exclaimed Shirley. "They have struck him. A blow is something he is not used to, and he will not tolerate."

Out she ran. A gentleman fled up the oak staircase, seeking refuge in the gallery or upper chambers with frantic haste; another backed rapidly toward the foot of the stairs, wildly waving a knotty stick while reiterating, "Down! down! down!" Meanwhile, the tawny dog bayed, bellowed, and howled, while a group of servants bundled from the kitchen. The dog leaped; the second gentleman turned tail and rushed after his companion. The first had already secured himself in a bedroom, holding the door shut against his fellow—who was no less terrified by terror itself. Yet the fugitive struggled hard; the door was yielding to his strength.

"Gentlemen," Shirley's silvery yet vibrating tones cut through the chaos, "spare my locks, if you please. Calm yourselves! Come down! Look at Tartar; he won't harm a cat."

She strode forward, caressing the beast. Tartar lay crouched at her feet, his fore-paws stretched out, his tail still agitated, his nostrils snorting, his bulldog eyes burning with a dull, smoldering fire. He was an honest, phlegmatic, stubborn canine, loving his mistress and John—the man who fed him—but largely indifferent to the rest of the world. Quiet enough unless struck or threatened with a stick, in which case a demon erupted within him instantly.

"Mr. Malone, how do you do?" Shirley continued, lifting her mirth-lit face to the gallery. "That is not the path to the oak parlour; that leads to Mrs. Pryor's apartments. Please request your friend, Mr. Donne, to vacate. I shall take the greatest pleasure in receiving him in a lower room."

"Ha! Ha!" cried Malone, in hollow laughter, stepping away from the door and leaning over the massive balustrade. "Truly, that animal alarmed Donne. He is somewhat timid," he proceeded, stiffening himself and walking trimly toward the stairhead. "I deemed it prudent to follow, in order to reassure him."

"It appears you did. Very well, come down, if you please.—John" (turning to her manservant), "ascend and liberate Mr. Donne.—Be cautious, Mr. Malone; the stairs are slippery."

Indeed, they were, crafted of polished oak. The caution arrived a fraction too late for Malone. He had already slipped during his stately descent, saved only by a desperate clutch at the banisters, causing the entire structure to groan anew.

Tartar seemed to regard the visitor's dramatic exit as unwarranted *éclat*, and thus he growled once more. Malone, however, was no coward. The dog's spring had surprised him, but he now passed it in suppressed fury rather than fear. If a look could have strangled Tartar, Malone would have breathed no more. Abandoning all pretense of politeness in his sullen rage, he pushed into the parlour before Miss Keeldar. He glanced at Miss Helstone; he could scarcely bring himself to bow. He glared at both ladies. He looked as if, had either been his wife, he would have made a glorious husband at that precise moment—in each hand, he seemed poised to clutch and grip them to death.

Yet, Shirley took pity. She ceased her laughter; Caroline, a true lady, refrained from smiling at anyone under such mortification. Tartar was dismissed; Peter Augustus was soothed—for Shirley possessed looks and tones capable of calming even a bull. He had the sense to realize that, unable to challenge the owner of the dog, he would do better to be civil. And civil he tried to be; his attempts being well received, he grew presently *very* civil, returning to his natural state. He had indeed come with the express purpose of rendering himself charming and fascinating. Rough portents had greeted him upon his first entry into Fieldhead, but those were overcome. Charming and fascinating he resolved to be. Like March, having entered like a lion, he intended to depart like a lamb.

For the sake of air—or perhaps for a convenient exit in case of new emergencies—he seated himself, neither on the sofa where Miss Keeldar offered him enthronement, nor near the fireside to which Caroline gently invited him, but on a chair close to the door. No longer sullen or furious, he grew, after his fashion, constrained and embarrassed. He spoke to the ladies in fits and starts, selecting topics that were intensely commonplace. He sighed deeply and significantly at the close of every sentence; he sighed in every pause; he sighed before opening his mouth. Finally, finding it desirable to add ease to his other charms, he drew forth an ample silk pocket-handkerchief. This was to serve as the graceful toy with which his unoccupied hands would dally. He applied himself to it with considerable energy. Folding the red-and-yellow square cornerwise, whipping it open with a waft, folding it again in narrower compass, he fashioned a handsome band. To what purpose would he apply this ligature? Would he wrap it about his throat or head? Should it serve as a comforter or a turban? Neither. Peter Augustus possessed an inventive, original genius. He was about to demonstrate to the ladies graces of action possessing at least the charm of novelty. Sitting on the chair with his athletic Irish legs crossed, he circled these limbs with the bandana and bound them firmly together. Evidently feeling this device worthy of an encore, he repeated it more than once. The second performance sent Shirley to the window to laugh a silent, irrepressible laugh unseen; it turned Caroline's head aside, allowing her long curls to screen the smile mantling her features.
