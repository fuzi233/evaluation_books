For generations, the Dashwood family had made their home in the rolling green countryside of Sussex, where their sprawling Norland Park estate sat at the heart of thousands of acres of ancient oak woods, wild meadow, and well-tended pasture. Their name was synonymous with quiet, generous respectability across the whole surrounding shire, and neighbors for miles around spoke of the Dashwoods’ hospitality as something to be cherished.

The last lifelong sole owner of Norland had been a quiet bachelor who lived well into his eighties, his only constant companion and housekeeper for most of his long life his unmarried younger sister. When she died ten years before him, the old house felt hollow and empty, so he invited his nephew, Mr. Henry Dashwood — the legal heir to the Norland estate, and the man he had long intended to leave it to — to move his whole family in. Suddenly the quiet old walls echoed with the sound of children’s laughter, the soft clatter of teacups at social hours, and the warm, easy cheer of a full household. The old gentleman’s days grew soft and comfortable, and his affection for every member of his nephew’s family deepened year by year.

Mr. Henry Dashwood had one son from his first marriage, a steady, respectable young man named John, who had already been very well provided for by his birth mother’s large fortune: half of it became his the moment he turned twenty-one, and soon after he added even more to his wealth by marrying a woman of equal fortune. For him, inheriting Norland was no life-changing windfall — but for his three half-sisters, the daughters Henry had with his current beloved wife, it was the whole promise of future security. Their mother had no fortune of her own, and Henry only held a life interest in the remaining half of his first wife’s money, which would pass entirely to John upon his death, leaving him with just seven thousand pounds that he could actually leave to his widow and three girls.

When the old gentleman died and his will was read, it brought nearly as much disappointment as it did relief. He had not been ungrateful enough to leave the estate away from Henry entirely — but he had tied it up so tightly, to pass straight from Henry to John, and then to John’s own four-year-old little son, that Henry had no power at all to carve out any share of the land or its valuable timber to secure a future for his wife and daughters. The whole estate was locked away for that small boy, who had won his great-uncle’s late affection over the occasional summer visits: the tot’s stammered mispronunciations, stubborn determination to get his own way, silly little tricks, and endless noisy high spirits had somehow come to outweigh years of steady, loving care from Henry Dashwood, his wife, and their three daughters. The old man had not meant to be cruel, though — he left each of the three Dashwood girls a single thousand pounds, a small token of his fondness.

Henry Dashwood’s disappointment was sharp at first, but he was a naturally cheerful, sanguine man. He told himself he had decades of life ahead of him, and if he managed the large, productive estate frugally, he could easily set aside a large sum of money over the years to make his wife and daughters perfectly comfortable. But fortune never gave him the chance: he survived his uncle by barely twelve months. When he died, all that was left for his widow and three girls was ten thousand pounds total, counting the small legacies the old gentleman had left them.

The moment his illness turned dangerous, Henry sent for his son John, and with all the weak, urgent strength he had left, he begged him to take care of his stepmother and his three half-sisters, to do everything he could to make their lives easy and happy. John Dashwood was not a cruel man, exactly — just cold-hearted, a little selfish, the sort of man who was well-respected in public because he never failed to fulfill his most basic social duties. At his father’s deathbed, he was truly moved, and he promised to look after the three girls as thoroughly as he could.

As soon as his father was buried, though, John’s narrow-minded, equally selfish wife packed up their little boy, all their servants, and moved into Norland Park without so much as a single note of warning sent ahead to the grieving widow. Legally, the house was theirs the moment Henry drew his last breath, but the indelicacy of the act stung deeply. Mrs. Dashwood, the widow, had a keen, romantic sense of honor, and the unapologetic disregard for her comfort filled her with such disgust that she nearly packed her bags and left Norland forever that very day — only the gentle, steady pleading of her eldest nineteen-year-old daughter convinced her to stay, for the sake of her three girls.

That eldest daughter, Elinor, was the steadying force of the whole household. She had a sharp, clear judgment, and the rare gift of being able to govern her own strong, affectionate feelings, a skill neither her mother nor her middle sister possessed. Time and time again, she would step in to stop her mother’s impulsive, passion-fueled decisions that would have led the whole family into ruin.

Marianne, the middle daughter, was just as clever and warm-hearted as Elinor, but every feeling she held was unmoderated, untamed: her joys were so bright they could make the whole room glow, her sorrows so sharp they could overwhelm her completely. Generous, soft, endlessly romantic, she was everything but prudent — and she and her mother were so alike in their sensibilities that they fed each other’s grief after Henry’s death, deliberately lingering on every sad, precious memory, refusing to accept any small comfort that might soften the sharp edge of their loss. Elinor grieved just as deeply as they did, but she forced herself to push through the pain: she met her rude sister-in-law with polite civility, mediated calmly with her brother, and worked tirelessly to keep the peace for the whole family.

The youngest sister, Margaret, was thirteen, good-humored and sweet-natured, but she had absorbed every bit of Marianne’s romantic whimsy without inheriting half her common sense, lost most days in the soft, vivid daydreams that made up the world inside her head.
