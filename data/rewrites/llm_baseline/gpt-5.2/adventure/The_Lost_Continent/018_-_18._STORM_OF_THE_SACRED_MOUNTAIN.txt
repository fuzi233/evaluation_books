Nais herself sent me away from her side.

The first shouts of the assault came rolling through the house like surf in a shell, the sound thickened by stone corridors and the hollow organs of the cleft. Even in her weakness she turned her head to listen, as if the Mountain’s very breath had changed pitch.

“What is that?” she asked, and the words rasped softly, like a spoon in an empty bowl.

So I told her—shortly, because her eyelids were heavy and her mind only newly thawed—how she had been taken from me into that long, unnatural sleep, and how I had held off all barter and all peace until they gave me back the Queen I desired, safe and living, here upon the Sacred Mountain.

A flush rose in her cheeks, as though a hidden ember had been stirred. “My lord is the properest man in all the world to be King,” she whispered.

“I refused to touch the trade till they had given me the Queen I desired, safe and alive, here upon the Mountain.”

“How we poor women are made the chattels of you men!” There was a faint bite of laughter in it, and then a sigh. “But, for myself, I seem to like the traffic well enough. You should not have let me stand in the way of Atlantis’ good, Deucalion. Still, it is very sweet to know you were weak there for once, and that I was the cause of your weakness. What is that bath over yonder? Ah! I remember; my wits seem none of the clearest just now.”

“You have made the beginning. Your strength will return to you by quick degrees. But it will not bear hurrying. You must have a patience.”

“Your ear, sir, for one moment, and then I will rest in peace. My poor looks, are they all gone? You seem to have no mirror here. I had visions that I should wake up wrinkled and old.”

“You are as you were, dear, that first night I saw you—the most beautiful woman in all the world.”

“I am pleased you like me,” she said, and accepted the cup I held. The broth was plain—roots and a little dried flesh, boiled long until it yielded—yet in that chamber it smelled like a promise. I had salted it carefully, pinching the grains between finger and thumb the way the older priests did, with the reverence of men who know that a body returns by mouth as much as by prayer. She sipped, and the sound of it—small, steady swallows—cut cleanly through the distant clangor outside, a domestic rhythm set against the war-drum of the pass.

“My hair seems to have grown; but it needs combing sadly. I had a fancy, dear, once, that you liked ruddy hair best, and not a plain brown.”

“It is yours I like,” I told her, and she let her eyes close again. Sleep took her quickly, not the heavy, drugged fall of the nine-year enchantment, but a healthy sinking as when bread is left to rise in warmth and darkness. Smiles still played at the corners of her mouth. I drew the coverlet higher and kissed her lightly, taking care that my beard did not brush her cheek.

And then I went out.

That beard had become hateful to me in these last hours—an unkempt banner of the years I had been buried. In a side room I found instruments and shaved down to a bare chin. The scrape of the blade, the rinse of water, the clean sting of skin newly bared—these were small acts, but they restored something of order. A robe, too, I found and took in place of my squalid rags. If a man is in truth a king, he owes such decencies to the dignity of his office, even when the crown is only a word shouted over stones.

But if the sound of the fighting was any guide, mine was a kingdom narrowing by the hour.

Each minute the noise thickened and drew nearer: the clang of iron on stone, the ragged cheer of men forcing themselves to courage, the duller, wetter sounds that follow when bodies meet. It was plain that some of the gates in the cleft—gates men had called impregnable, as if a name were mortar—had yielded under the vehemence of Phorenice’s storm. Nor was it marvel. With all her genius spurred into fury by the blow we had struck in the city, she was no light adversary, and the Sacred Mountain was no longer strong.

Stone defenses, cunningly planned and mightily built, we still possessed. But walls do not fight alone. They need men to line them—abundance of men. In such a storm some desperate fellows spit at death and come to grips; slingers and archers slip a shot through; throwing-fire finds a seam; or some new device, like Phorenice’s fire-tubes, makes one wavering dart in a thousand find a throat. And so, though the general attacking loses his hundreds, the defenders are not without their dead.

The slaughter below had been prodigious.

As fast as the stormers came up, the Priests who held the lowest remaining gate rained down great rocks until the narrow stairway was paved with writhing bodies. But Phorenice stood on a spur of rock beneath, urging charge upon charge. Company after company marched up to hurl themselves hopelessly against the defences. They had no engines to batter the massive gates; their assault was as useless as a child hammering a wall with an orange, and all the while the stones from above mowed them down remorselessly.

It was no part of Phorenice’s policy that any should return. In her lust for the final conquest she meant to pour out troops until the passes were filled with the slain, and then march on to a level fight over the bridge of their bodies. She never counted cost. She set down the object to be gained, and it was her policy that the people of Atlantis existed to gain it for her.

Two gates she had carried in this dreadful fashion, slaughtering the priests behind them—those not already shot down. And then I came down from above to take my share.

There was no trumpet to announce me, no herald to proclaim quality. But the Priests, from sheer custom and need, snatched up my name as a cry. “Deucalion!” they shouted, and some called that with a King to lead there would be no further ground lost.

The name carried. Across the abyss of din I saw its effect below: a company of poor doomed wretches, hurrying up to their death, faltered in their charge. “Deucalion” was bandied from mouth to mouth and handed back down their line like a scrap of old song remembered at the lip. I took grim satisfaction in it; here was proof I had not been wholly forgotten.

Shouts came from behind them to press on, but they did not budge. Presently a glittering officer panted up, striking right and left with his sword to drive them forward. From where I stood on the high rampart above the gate I could see his face clearly, and knew him at once.

“It matters not what they use for their battle-cry,” he shouted. “You have the orders of your divine Empress, and that is enough. You should be proud to die for her wish, you cowards. And if you do not obey, you will die afterwards under the instruments of the tormentors, very painfully. As for Deucalion, he is dead any time these nine years.”

“There it seems you lie, my Lord Tatho,” I called down.

He started and looked up. For a moment the tumult seemed to thin around him, as if even the air wanted to listen.

“So you are there in real truth, then? Well, old comrade, I am sorry. But it is too late to make a composition now. You are on the side of these mangy Priests, and the Empress has made an edict that they are to be rooted out, and I am her most obedient servant.”

“You used to be skilful of fence,” I said. There was little enough to choose between us once. “If it please you to stop this pitiful killing, make yourself the champion of your side, and I will stand for mine, and we will fight out this quarrel in some fair place, and bind our parties to abide by the result.”

“It would be a grand fight between us two, old friend, and it goes hard with me to balk you of it. But I cannot pleasure you. I am general here under Phorenice, and she has given me the strongest orders not to peril myself. And besides, though you are a great man, Deucalion, you are not chief. You are not even one of the Three.”

“I am King.”

Tatho laughed. “Few but yourself would say so, my lord.”

“Few truly,” I answered, “but what there are, they are powerful. I was given the name for the first time yesterday; and as a first blow in the campaign there was some mischief done in the city. I was there myself, and saw how you took it.”

“You were in Atlantis!”

“I went for Nais. She is on the mountain now, and to-morrow will be my Queen. Tatho, as priest to priest, let me solemnly bring to your memory the infinite power you bite against on this Sacred Mountain. Your teaching has warned you of the weapons that are stored in the Ark of the Mysteries. If you persist in this attack, at the best you can merely lose; at the worst you can bring about a wreck over which even the High Gods will shudder as They order it.”

“You cannot scare us back now by words,” he said doggedly. “And as for magic, it will be met by magic. Phorenice has found by her own cleverness as many powers as were ever stored up in the Ark of the Mysteries.”

“Yet she looked on helplessly enough last night, when her royal pyramid was trundled into a rubbish heap. Zaemon had prophesied that this should be so, and for a witness—why I myself stood closer to her than we two stand now, and saw her.”

“I will own you took her by surprise somewhat there. I do not understand these matters myself; I was never more than one of the Seven in the old days; and now, quite rightly, Phorenice keeps the knowledge of her magic to herself. But it seems time is needed when one magic is to be met by another.”

“Well,” I said, “I know little about the business either. I leave these matters now to those who are higher above me in the priesthood. Indeed, having a liking for Nais, it seems I am debarred from ever being given understanding about the highest of the higher Mysteries. So I content myself with being a soldier, and when the appointed day comes, I shall fall and kiss my mother the Earth for the last time. You, so I am told, have ambition for longer life.”

He nodded once, hard. “Phorenice has found the Great Secret, and I am to be the first that will share it with her. We shall be as Gods upon the earth, seeing that Death will be powerless to touch us. And the twin sons she has borne me will be made immortal also.”

“Phorenice is headstrong. No, my lord—there is no need to shake your head and try to deny it. I have had some acquaintance with her. But the order has been made, and her immortality will be snatched from her very rudely. Now mark solemnly my words. I, Deucalion, have been appointed King of Atlantis by the High Council of the Priests who are the mouthpiece of the most High Gods, and if I do not have my reign, then there will be no Atlantis left to carry either King or Empress. You know me, Tatho, for a man that never lies.”

He nodded again.

“Then save yourself before it is too late. You shall have again your vice-royalty in Yucatan.”

His face tightened with a bitterness I had not expected. “But, man, there is no Yucatan. A great horde of little hairy creatures, that were something less than human and something more than beasts, swept down upon our cities and ate them out. Oh, you may sneer if you choose! Others sneered when I came home, till the Empress stopped them. But you know what a train of driver ants is, that you meet with in the forests? You may light fires across their path, and they will march into them in their blind bravery, and put them out with their bodies, and those that are left will march on in an unbroken column, and devour all that stands in their path. I tell you, my lord, those little hairy creatures were like the ants—aye, for numbers, and wooden bravery, as well as for appetite. As a result to-day, there is no Yucatan.”

“Then you shall have Egypt.”

He burst out hotly, loud enough that even the men below flinched at the edge of his voice. “I would not take seven Egypts and ten Yucatans. My lord, you think more poorly of me than is kind, when you ask me to become a traitor. In your place would you throw your Nais away, if the doing it would save you from a danger?”

“That is different.”

“In no degree. You have a kindness for her. I have all that and more for Phorenice, who is, besides, my wife and the mother of my children. If I have qualms—and I freely confess I know you are desperate men up there, and have dreadful powers at your command—my shiverings are for them and not for myself. But I think, my lord, this parley is leading to nothing, and though these common soldiers here will understand little enough of our talk, they may be picking up a word here and there, and I do not wish them to go on to their death (as you will see them do shortly) and carry evil reports about me to whatever Gods they chance to come before.”

He saluted me with his sword and drew back. At once the air filled again with motion and hurt. Missiles began to fly; the doomed wretches who had been halting under the steep rock-walls pressed forward. They had scaling-ladders, yes, but no chance to plant them. They could do nothing but fill the narrow way with their bodies. For that they had been sent, and to that end they humbly died.

Our Priests, stripped and sweating, worked with crow and lever, wrenching from their beds the great rocks that had been made ready. Each time a stone tore free there was a groan like an old tree giving up at last, then the crash and the sudden, high screams as it found flesh. It was a hideous music: rock on rock, bone on stone, men yelling to drown out the knowledge of what they did. There is an ecology of sound on a battlefield—how it crowds the mind, how it makes cruelty easier because thought is smothered in noise. I had known forests where the only great sound was wind and water, and a man could hear his own conscience. Here, in the cleft, nothing could breathe quietly. Even prayer felt battered into fragments before it reached the lips.

And always some arrow or sling-stone, or one thin dart from a fire-tube, would slip through and steal a life from our side. Phorenice could be lavish. For her, loss was a figure on a tally-stick; when one levy died, she could press others into service, for the whole breadth of the country lay under her rule. With us it was different. A man down was an arm irrecoverably missing.

I weighed our chances and saw the truth: if we kept fighting gate by gate, they would storm each one in turn, and by the time the last, uppermost gate was reached there would be no Priest alive to hold it.

So, borrowing from Phorenice’s newer doctrine—that a general should sometimes plot from safety rather than lead every rush—I left those at the gate with rough soldier’s cheer and withdrew up the narrow stair.

This approach to the Sacred Mountain had once been far more perilous when it was only the raw cleft the High Gods made. But men had bridged a chasm here, cut a shelf through solid rock there, built roadway on piers set out from distant crags, until what had been a goat’s terror became a uniform path. As I climbed, it came to me that if we destroyed the way itself, we might gain a breathing space.

It was a good thought, or at least no other offered relief. Yet though I had traversed that stair of a pass thousands of times and could have walked it blind, I had never looked on it with an eye to unmaking it. Even now, in this dreadful stress, I had to pace it three times, measuring by instinct and memory, until I fixed upon the best place for demolition.

Once the point was chosen there was little delay. I sent men to the storehouses among the Priests’ dwellings to fetch rams and crows, acids and hammers—whatever would bite stone and loosen the bones of the road. We put every pair of hands we could spare to the work, no matter their age or feebleness. Had Nais been able to walk so far, I would have pressed even her into it; for in a siege all tenderness is rationed.

Then the Mountain’s own handiwork began to die.

Carved balustrade and wayside statue, lettered wall-stones and foot-worn cobbles—marks of old feet, old processions, old hymns—were broken free and sent roaring into the gulf. The crash rose to join the yells and shrieks below. Gods, it was hateful, smashing down that splendid craft of the men of the past; like tearing the pages from an elder’s book because an enemy might read it and mock. Yet better the stones should fall into ruin than that Phorenice should profane them with her impious sandals.

At first I feared we would have to sacrifice the knot of brave men still holding the attacked gate. War often forces a general to leave some to fight to the finish without hope, and all he can do is commend them to the Gods. But as the path’s destruction neared completion, I saw a way to retrieve them.

We had not been content with breaking arches and throwing down piers. We had got our rams and levers under the living rock itself on which the whole fabric stood; fire stood ready to heat the rams so they would expand with resistless force. When the word was given, the remaining ledge could be torn away beyond repair.

All was prepared thus, and then I ordered them to hold.

A narrow strip of ledge still remained. Over this I crossed. The cut we had made lay immediately below the uppermost gate of all. Beneath it were three more massive gates still unviolated, besides the one being attacked. Their garrisons had already been retired. I passed through them unchallenged, closing each behind me with the sense of shutting doors upon old certainties, until I came to the busy rampart where twelve Priests still alive worked stripped to the waist, heaving down the murderous rocks.

For a while I worked with them, catching an occasional arrow or fire-tube dart on my shield, passing news in quick phrases. The attack grew fiercer by the minute. The enemy had packed the pass nearly full of their dead; our stones now had less distance to fall and so did less execution. The stormers pressed forward with their scaling-ladders more eagerly than ever. It was plain they would soon put the place to the storm.

Even in that short time, sling-stones and darts took three more lives from the twelve at my side.

So I called for one last furious avalanche. While the living crawled free of the new-killed and the next wave of reinforcements scrambled up over bodies, I sent my nine remaining men running up the steep stair, and followed. Gate after gate we passed, shutting them behind and breaking the bars and levers that moved them. Not until we were through the last did the roar below tell us the besiegers had found their prize deserted.

One by one we balanced across the narrow ledge left where the path had been destroyed. A Priest with a wound grew dizzy, lost his balance, and toppled down into the abyss before a hand could steady him. His cry was swallowed by the gulf; it is strange how quickly the Mountain eats sound when it wishes.

When we were all over, we put heat to the rams. They expanded and tore the remaining ledges from their hold. The last connecting link between our handful of the Sacred Clan above and the broad, estranged nation below was cut away. I think a pang went through us all at that sight—not only fear, but grief, as though a familiar dialect had gone suddenly silent in the mouth.

Here, at any rate, was a break in the fighting. There were no further preparations we could make; and high though I knew Phorenice’s genius, I did not see how she could do other than accept the check and retire. I set a guard upon the ramparts of the uppermost gate to watch for movement and sent the others to find the rest their bodies cried for.

For myself I tried first to find Zaemon. Dutifully I went as I ought, for little enough kingly state was observed upon the Sacred Mountain though the title had been given me. But Zaemon could not be come at. He was within the Ark of the Mysteries with another of the Three, and I, being only one of the Seven, had not rank enough to break in upon their workings.

So I turned where my likings would have led me first: back to the house that sheltered Nais.

She woke as I crossed the threshold, as though she had been listening for my foot. Her eyes filled with welcome. I went to her and knelt, laying my face on the pillow beside hers. She spoke in tender fragments, sweet endearments, and little household fancies that made me ache at what I had lost by not knowing her earlier—an infinity of delight, like a lifetime of meals eaten without tasting.

She teased me for my new-cleanness of chin and for the robe I had taken. She professed a pretty awe of my kingship and vowed that had she known of my coming dignities she would never have dared to discover love for me. But of my marriage with Phorenice she spoke with less lightness. She put out her thin white hand and drew my face close to her lips.

“It is weak of me to have a jealousy,” she murmured, “knowing how completely my lord is mine alone; but I cannot help it. You have said you were her husband for awhile. It gives me a pang to think that I shall not be the first to lie in your arms, Deucalion.”

“Then you may gaily throw your pang away,” I whispered back. “I was husband to Phorenice in mere word, for how long I do not precisely know. But in anything beyond, I was never her husband at all. She married me by a form she prescribed herself, ignoring all the old rites and ceremonies; and whether it would hold as legal or not, we need not trouble to inquire. She herself has most nicely and completely annulled that marriage, as I have told you. Tatho is her husband now, and father to her children, and he seems to have a fondness for her which does him credit.”

We said other things, too—those small repetitions lovers hoard like spices, the phrases that mean nothing to any ear but the one that has learned their taste. They are not for setting down here. Weightier subjects press their way into the historian’s index.

For Phorenice, it seemed, had more rage against the Priests’ Clan on the Mountain, and more bright genius to help her to vengeance, than I had credited.

Her troops stormed easily the gates we had left to them and swarmed up until they stood where the pathway was broken down. In the heat of their rush the foremost were thrust over the brink by those pressing behind before the advance could be checked, and they went screaming to a horrid death in the gulf. But no lavish spending of men could take a position like that. Presently all were drawn off save a half-score, left as outpost sentries, who dodged behind angles of rock out of arrow-shot.

It seems, too, that the Empress herself came to reconnoitre the break, using caution and quickness, taking the measure with her own eye rather than trusting another’s report. And with extraordinary nimbleness she must have planned the engine she required and given orders for its making; for even with her vast force and resources, the speed with which it rose was prodigious.

Very little noise betrayed what was afoot. Below on the plain at the foot of the cleft, skilled craftsmen cut the wood and tongued it; metal was forged and fitted; each balk, each crosspiece, each plank was finished to its purpose before it ever began the climb. And when at last the ponderous pieces were dragged up the steep pass through the conquered gates, each came ready at once for its appointed place in the machine that Phorenice meant to set against our broken road.
