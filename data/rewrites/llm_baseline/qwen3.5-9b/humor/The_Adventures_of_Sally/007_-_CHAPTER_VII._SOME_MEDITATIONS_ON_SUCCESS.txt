Actors and actresses seem to share a peculiar childhood trait: just as children crumble instantly under the weight of bad news, they also bounce back with surprising speed when fortune smiles upon them. Sally found it baffling that any human being could generate such widespread euphoria simply by handing her brother Fillmore a check for twenty thousand dollars. If the Second Coming had actually occurred, the troupe of the *Primrose Way* Company couldn't have looked more united or content. The heavy fog of lethargy and discouragement, born from a week of doing absolutely nothing, evaporated off them like a wet coat left in the sun.

Suddenly promoting that lowly creature from the depths—the assistant stage manager—to the dizzying heights of "Proprietor" appealed directly to their theatrical instincts. Most of them had starred in plays where the downtrodden heroine is elevated around eleven o'clock, so this twist felt perfectly dramatic and logically sound. Furthermore, now that Miss Hobson was gone, everyone finally admitted she had been a walking blight on the production. Optimism became the air they breathed; cheerful rumors began to circulate like wildfire. Bowler-hatted Teddy claimed, straight from the lift-boy at his hotel, that the theater ban would be lifted by Tuesday at the latest. Meanwhile, the girl running the cigar stand at the Pontchartrain told the actor playing the butler that Toledo and Cleveland were set to open tomorrow. Everyone agreed the sun was breaking through the clouds and that Fate would soon give up its hopeless crusade against keeping good people down.

Fillmore was restored to his former self. Naturally, everyone has their own unique way of expressing elation. For Fillmore, it meant purchasing a brand-new waistcoat, stocking up on a hundred half-dollar cigars, and becoming obsessively particular about his lunch choices. It might have been an optical illusion, but to Sally, he seemed to have gained at least six pounds in the first twenty-four hours of this new regime. Previously, as a servant managing paper knives and props, he had been almost gaunt. Now, as a manager, he bloomed into soft, billowy curves. When he stood on the sidewalk before the theater, gazing proudly at the new posters bearing the legend *"FILLMORE NICHOLAS PRESENTS,"* the general public literally had to walk around him to avoid collision.

In this atmosphere of bubbling joy, it was difficult to understand why Sally, the fairy godmother responsible for the entire turnaround, wasn't equally ecstatic. It puzzled her deeply. Yet, whatever faint shadow lingered in the back of her mind refused to step forward into the light to be examined.

It wasn't until she was driving in a hired car with Gerald one afternoon on Belle Isle that the truth revealed itself. Since Miss Hobson's departure, Gerald had reached his peak form. Like Fillmore, he was a man who thrived in the sunshine of prosperity; his moody demeanor had vanished, replaced by his old charm. However, as the car glided smoothly through the pleasant woods and fields along the river, Sally felt a distinct jolt. Something didn't fit.

Gerald was cheerful and talkative. At least, he saw no faults in life. He spoke expansively about his grand plans. "If this play gets over—and it will—I'll show 'em!" His jaw was squared, his eyes glowing as they fixed on the inviting horizon. "Just one success—that's all I need—then watch me! I haven't had my chance yet, but..." His voice rolled on confidently, but Sally had stopped listening.

It was the season when the chill of evening rushes quickly to follow the mellow warmth of the afternoon. The sun had dipped behind the trees, and a cold wind was rising from the river. Suddenly, as if triggered by that breeze clearing her mind, she grasped exactly what had been lurking in the recesses of her thoughts. For a fleeting moment, it stood exposed and naked, and the world transformed into a desolate place.

She had finally realized the fundamental divide between the male and female outlooks on life. Success! How men worship it, and how little of themselves they possess to offer for anything else. Ironically, it was the very theme of Gerald's play—which she had single-handedly saved from destruction—that served as the subject matter here. Of all the men she knew, how many viewed existence merely as a race to be won, straining every nerve to cross the finish line regardless of what they sacrificed along the way? Fillmore. Gerald. All of them.

There might be a woman in each of their lives, but she existed only as an afterthought—a hobby for their spare time. To Gerald, she was everything. His success would never be more than a side issue to her; he himself, devoid of any trappings of glory, was sufficient. But she was not sufficient for him.

A spasm of futile jealousy seized her. She shivered violently.

"Cold?" asked Gerald. "I'll tell the driver to turn us back... I don't see any reason why this play shouldn't run a year in New York. Everybody says it's good... if it does get over, they'll all be after me. I..."

Sally stared out into a bleak, gray world. The sky above was a dull lead, and the wind sweeping in from the river carried a dismal, freezing chill.
