I descended into the abyss, slow and cautious, swallowed by a darkness so absolute it pressed upon my soul. No night you have ever known could compare. It was as if I wandered through an endless void, untethered from earth and self. At times, my steps grew aimless, my mind adrift—until a jutting rock struck my shin or a hidden boulder sent me sprawling. Pain, sharp and real, yanked me back to the truth: I still trod solid ground, though it felt like the edge of oblivion.

Downward, ever downward. The slope was my only guide. In such blackness, a mile might take an hour—or two. Frustration gnawed at me, bitter as the cold. Then came an idea: I spun the Diskos, letting its eerie glow spill just far enough to etch the path ahead into memory. A few steps in the dark, then light again—each flicker a small defiance against the crushing night. The Diskos' gleam seemed unnaturally bright, a lone spark in an ocean of shadow.

I rationed its use. Power was not endless, and recklessness could doom me.

Six hours passed before I paused, weary to the bone. I sat upon the slope, fumbling in the dark for my tablets and water. Eating blind, I wrapped myself in my cloak, the Diskos close, and slipped into uneasy sleep—dreaming of Naani, her voice just beyond hearing.

I woke with a start, hand gripping the weapon. Something had stirred me—a presence? Silence answered. Still, unease clung like a second shadow.

The next day, I crawled. Knees and palms raw, I inched forward, testing each inch before trusting it. Then—near disaster. My foot plunged into emptiness. I caught myself at the edge of a pit, heart hammering. Groping along its rim, I circled until safe passage revealed itself.

Desperate, I devised a new method: a stone tied to a strap, cast ahead like a probe. Clumsy, yes—but better than plummeting into some unseen chasm.

Days bled together. The air thickened, heavier in my lungs, no longer the thin, biting cold of the Night Land. It was as if I sank into the bowels of the earth itself.

Then—light.

Faint at first, a flicker in the abyss. My breath caught. Was it real? I staggered upright, squinting. There—again! A distant glow, wavering like a mirage. Hope surged, reckless. I ran—and fell, pain flaring through my knees. Cursing, I forced myself to slow.

Hours later, the light resolved: a towering blue flame, roaring from the earth between jagged monoliths. Gas, surely—natural, yet awe-inspiring. It pulsed like a living thing, now a hundred feet high, now a thousand, casting the gorge in stark relief. Cliffs loomed miles away, their faces etched in fire.

And beneath the flame’s thunder—a sound. Piping, shrill and alien, rising from the depths. Something waited in the dark.

I crouched behind rocks, watching. The bottom of the slope lay before me, but the true descent had only begun.
