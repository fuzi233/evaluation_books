"Return to your science,
Which means, the more perfect the thing,
The more it feels good, and thus the pain."

Deronda stood upon the staircase, his heart heavy with a preoccupation that had nothing to do with the steps beneath his feet. He had just returned from a second, harrowing interview with his mother, a meeting that had shaken the foundations of his own quiet world. Only two hours had passed since he parted from her, yet the Princess Halm-Eberstein had already departed the hotel. Logically, he should have been on his way to Mainz, delivering the letter from Joseph Kalonymos and securing the family chest. Yet, a strange paralysis held him. It was not a lack of reason, but a mixture of mental states that refused to crystallize into action. A weight of retrospective feeling pinned him to the spot, forcing him to relive the scenes of his mother's life with a fresh, piercing acuteness. In his solitude, he wept with a compassion that seemed deeper than a woman's, mourning a life so intimately connected to his own, yet so tragically distant.

He felt the world shifting beneath him, altered by the certainty of new ties that disrupted his balance of hopes and fears. It was as if, under the cover of night, he had mistakenly joined a band of wanderers, only to find at dawn that his true kindred pitched their tents far away. He sensed a quivering kinship with a grandfather whose strong impulses and beloved thoughts might now be stirring within his own soul. Through this passionate meditation, Mordecai and Mirah were ever present, clasping hands with him in a silent, sympathetic understanding.

Such was the fabric of Deronda's nature: quick and responsive, wrapped in a mantle of self-controlled reserve forged by early hardship.

The persistent ringing of a bell, signaling the hour, prompted him to glance at his guidebook and consider the necessary preparations for the next train to Mainz. He thought of London, of the beings there who anchored his life, but other wishes clung to Genoa with a magnetic force. There was a presentiment of finality, of overshadowing sorrow, that urged him to linger. He did not consciously decide to stay because it was Friday or because he wished to attend the synagogue service; rather, he simply ceased moving. Instead of packing or summoning his bill, he sat still, his mind drifting to the synagogue. He imagined the faces there, perhaps unchanged from his grandfather's time, hearing the Spanish-Hebrew liturgy that had endured through wandering generations like a plant with wandering seeds, binding far-off lands to the exile's home. His thoughts also turned to Gwendolen, with an anxious remembrance of what had passed between them. He felt a half-admitted conviction that it would be cruel to leave immediately without making some effort to show his sympathy, despite Grandcourt's likely displeasure.

In this suspended state, he deferred his departure, eating his dinner without tasting its flavor. Rising quickly, he sought the synagogue. Passing the porter, he inquired if Mr. and Mrs. Grandcourt were still in the hotel and requested their apartment number. The porter supplied the number but added that they had gone out boating. This piece of information struck Deronda with unexpected force, dividing his attention between the sparse *talithim* and the keen, dark faces of the worshippers. Their easy familiarity with the solemn prayers, a blend of Hebrew and Italian, made him reflect that his grandfather must have been an exceptional Jew, akin to Mordecai. Were not men of ardent zeal and far-reaching hope always exceptional? They were the visionaries who molded the passive life of the world, preventing it from shrinking into the narrow tenacity of insects unaware of thoughts beyond their antennae.

A mournful impatience mingled with his solicitude for Gwendolen, urging him to hasten from the synagogue. He chose to take his evening walk toward the quay, a favorite haunt that now held special attraction due to the possibility of catching the Grandcourts returning from their boating. Resolving to greet them deliberately, he prepared to ignore any grounds Grandcourt might have for wishing him elsewhere.

The sun had dipped behind a bank of clouds, casting only a faint yellow farewell upon the waves, which were agitated by an active breeze. Deronda sauntered slowly within sight of the strand, observing the groups concentrating their attention on a sailing-boat advancing swiftly toward the land, rowed by two men. Amidst the clamorous talk in various languages, Deronda decided against asking questions. Instead, he pushed his way to the foreground to become an unobstructed witness. Telescopes were raised, and loud declarations flew: the boat carried someone who had drowned. One claimed it was the *milord*; another insisted the prostrate figure was *miladi*. A Frenchman, lacking a glass, suggested the *milord* had taken his wife out to drown her, a notion an English skipper immediately dismissed as nonsense, correcting that the reclining figure was clearly a woman.

For Deronda, terribly excited by fluctuating fears, the strokes of the oars were punctuated by swift visions of possible and impossible events that might have led to this tragic fragment of an issue, with a worse half undisclosed. Was the woman apparently snatched from the waters truly Mrs. Grandcourt?

Soon, however, there was no longer any doubt. The boat was being pulled to land, and Deronda saw Gwendolen half-raising herself on her hands by her own effort, encased in a heavy covering of tarpaulin and pea-jackets. She was pale as one of the sheeted dead, shivering, with wet hair streaming down her face. Her eyes held a wild, amazed consciousness, as if she had awakened in a world where judgment was impending and the beings around her were coming to seize her. The first rower, drenched through, ran ashore; the sailors surrounding the boat hindered Deronda from advancing. He could only watch as Gwendolen cast scared glances, shrinking with terror as she was carefully, tenderly helped out and led away by the strong arms of the rough, bronzed men. Her wet clothes clung to her limbs, adding to the impediment of her weakness.

Suddenly, her wandering eyes fell upon Deronda, standing before her. Immediately, as if she had been expecting him and looking for him, she tried to stretch out her arms, which were held back by her supporters. In a muffled voice, she cried out, "It is come, it is come! He is dead!"

"Hush, hush!" Deronda said, his tone authoritative; "quiet yourself." Turning to the men assisting her, he declared, "I am a connection of this lady's husband. If you will get her on to the *Italia* as quickly as possible, I will undertake everything else."

He remained behind to hear from the remaining boatman that her husband had gone down irrecoverably and that his boat floated empty. They had heard a cry, arrived in time to see the lady jump in after her husband, and had managed to extract her quickly enough to save her from further harm.

After this, Deronda hastened to the hotel to ensure the best medical help was provided. Satisfied on this point, he telegraphed the event to Sir Hugo, begging him to come forthwith, and also to Mr. Gascoigne, whose address at the rectory offered the nearest known way to inform Gwendolen's mother. Certain words Gwendolen had spoken in the past returned to him with the effectiveness of an inspiration: in moments of agitated confession, she had spoken of her mother's presence as a possible help, if she could have had it.
