Messer Naldo returned sooner than anyone anticipated. On the evening of the twenty-eighth of November, merely eleven days after his last visit, he appeared, proving he had not ventured far beyond the mountains. The scene that unfolded that night in the Via de' Bardi offers a key to understanding the impulse that drew Tito's steps toward the hill of San Giorgio.

When Tito first secured this dwelling for Tessa upon his return from Rome, more than a year and a half prior, he convinced himself he acted solely under the gentle compulsion of his own kindness following the unfortunate incident that led the innocent Tessa to believe he was her husband. While it was true that his kindness flowed naturally toward such a trusting, pretty creature—one impossible to ignore without a desire to caress—it was equally true that Tito harbored feelings of benevolence toward her separate from any calculated gain. Though her charms and chatter were delightful in idle moments, he might have preferred freedom from her company; for he was not in love with Tessa. For the first time in his life, he was consumed by a passion for a different woman, one whose mere presence vibrated through his hours, whose sweeping tresses across his cheek held a power no mere petting could match. All his youthful idealism had been awakened by Romola. His spirit was too refined, his intellect too bright, to succumb to the habits of a coarse pleasure-seeker.

Yet, Tito had woven a net around himself and Tessa that he felt powerless to break. In the initial moments after the mock marriage, he had allowed her to remain under an illusion driven by a distinct calculation of his future needs. Since that pivotal moment, however, the web seemed to spin itself autonomously, growing beyond his control. The lines between kindness and self-indulgence blur easily in a soft nature like Tito's. The irritation he felt during Tessa's pursuit on the day of his betrothal, coupled with his sincere intention to reveal the truth when he promised to see her again, served as proof to him that his ultimate decision to leave her believing the lie and provide her a home stemmed purely from overwhelming kindness. In these early days of his devotion to Romola, he desperately needed a self-justifying narrative. He had learned to find comfort in the fact that she was deceived about certain matters. Every intense emotion constructs its own conscience, its own form of piety—much like a son's love for a mother, which can endure even amidst profound moral corruption. Thus, Tito could not yet find peace in committing a secret offense against his wedded love.

Consequently, he exercised extreme caution to preserve the secrecy of this transgression. Monna Lisa, who rarely left her quarters except for specific errands or annual confession, knew nothing of his true identity or whereabouts. She only knew that he paid her generously to ensure her comfort, cared little for the details, and had grown fond of Tessa, finding satisfaction in the duties for which she was compensated. An air of mystery undoubtedly surrounded the arrangement, given that Tessa was a peasant girl (*contadina*) while Messer Naldo was a gentleman. However, to Monna Lisa, he might have been a legitimate husband. Tito had thoroughly terrified Tessa into silence regarding their union, warning her that breaking that silence meant never seeing him again. Furthermore, Monna Lisa's deafness necessitated premeditated speech, saving Tessa from the careless revelations she had accidentally made to Baldassarre. For a long time, Tito's visits were so infrequent that it seemed plausible he undertook journeys between them. These trips were motivated primarily by a desire to ensure Tessa's welfare. Although he always found the visit more pleasant than the anticipation of it—renewing his appreciation for that pretty, ignorant lovingness and trust—he did not yet feel an urgent necessity for them. Nevertheless, he was resolved, if possible, to maintain the simplicity upon which her charm depended. He intended to keep Tessa a genuine *contadina*, refusing to place the humble wildflower among conditions that would strip it of its grace. He would have been horrified to see her dressed in attire befitting a higher station; the sharp wit of her conversation would vanish if her world expanded, if her pleasures lost their childish innocence. The squirrel-like joy of enjoying nuts at leisure defined the standard of luxury he provided. Through this strategy, Tito preserved Tessa's charm from defilement and, coincidentally, spared himself from escalating expenses that were already burdensome to a man whose funds were fully committed to his established lifestyle.

Briefly summarized, this was the trajectory of Tito's relationship with Tessa until very recently. It is true that before Bardo's death, the knowledge that Tessa waited up the hill, offering a chance for an agreeable hour, occasionally induced him to flee the weariness of the old man. When, lacking other engagements, he might otherwise have endured the fatigue patiently alongside Romola, the thought of Tessa provided an escape. However, the moment he truly felt a hunger for Tessa's ignorant lovingness and faith in *him* arrived only quite lately, marked by circumstances as indelible as the onset of a disease that permanently damages sight and hearing. It was the day he first encountered Baldassarre and purchased the armor. That night, returning across the bridge with the coat of mail in his hands, he felt an irresistible dread of immediately confronting Romola. Like Tessa, she knew little of the actual world and trusted him; yet, he possessed an uneasy awareness that behind her frank eyes lay a nature capable of judgment. Any misplaced trust she held did not stem from a brutish inability to perceive, but from a nobility that might serve as an alarming test. He craved a measure of ease, a respite from self-control after the agitation and exertion of the day. He sought a place where he could prepare his mind for tomorrow without concern for his current behavior. Within reach was a sweet, adoring creature whose presence was as safe and unrestrained as that of her own children—someone who would believe any tale and remain utterly unaffected by public opinion. So, that evening, while Romola waited and listened for him, he turned his steps up the hill.

It is therefore no surprise that his path followed the same course eleven days later, on this evening when he had recoiled under Romola's first outburst of scorn. He could not wish Tessa in his wife's place, nor could he refrain from hoping his wife would be thoroughly reconciled to him; for it was Romola, not Tessa, who belonged to the realm where the greater desires of an ambitious, capable man must inevitably reside. Still, he sought refuge from a standard that was disagreeably rigorous, one from which he could not liberate himself merely by dismissing it as folly. Tessa's small soul offered that inviting sanctuary.

It was barely past eight o'clock when he ascended the stone steps to Tessa's door. Usually, she heard his entry and rushed to greet him, but not tonight. Upon opening the door, he discovered the cause. A single dim light burned above the dying embers, illuminating Tessa in a kneeling posture beside the bed where the infant lay. Her head had slumped onto the pillow, and her brown rosary, which usually hung above the pillow over the image of the Madonna and golden palm branches, lay loosely gripped in her right hand. She had drifted into sleep over her beads. Tito moved lightly across the small room and sat close beside her. She had likely sensed the creak of the door as part of her dream, for she had not been watching him two moments prior. She opened her eyes without a start, remaining perfectly still as she gazed at him, as if the awareness of his smiling presence shut out any impulse to disturb that happy passivity. When he placed his hand under her chin and leaned to kiss her, she whispered:

"I dreamed it, and then I said it was a dream—and then I woke, and it was true."

"Little sinner!" Tito replied, pinching her chin. "You haven't finished half your prayers. I'll punish you by refusing to look at your baby; he is ugly."

Tessa disliked those words, despite Tito's smile. A look of pouting distress crossed her face as she bent anxiously over the child.

"Oh, that isn't true! He is prettier than anything. You don't think he's ugly. You'll look at him. He's even prettier than when you saw him before—only he's asleep, so you can't see his eyes or his tongue, and I can't show you his hair—but it grows, doesn't that seem wonderful? Look at him! It's true his face looks very similar when he sleeps; there isn't as much to see as when he's awake. If you kiss him very gently, he won't wake. Do you want to kiss him?"

Satisfied, Tito bestowed a butterfly kiss upon the small bundle, then placed his hand on her shoulder and turned her face toward him. "You prefer looking at the baby to looking at your husband, you false one!"

Still kneeling, she now rested her hands on his knee, gazing up at him like one of Fra Lippo Lippi's round-cheeked adoring angels.

"No," she said, shaking her head. "I love you best always, but I want you to look at the bambino and love him. Before, I only wanted you to love me."

"And did you expect me to return so soon?" Tito asked, inclining toward making her chatter. He still felt the aftershocks of the agitation he had endured, feeling like a man violently jarred; this was the easiest relief from silence and solitude.
