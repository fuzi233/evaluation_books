Tunbridge had begun to taste of itself—warm mineral water and colder talk, the same hands forever shuffling the same greasy cards, the same compliments turned and returned until their edges wore thin. Madame de Bernstein, whose appetite for company was as exacting as her palate, declared Bath would restore her: more tables, more lights, more bustle; and, before that, a round of country visits to remind old acquaintances that she had not forgotten them, only outgrown them.

Harry promised—without the softness that makes a promise a gift—to ride with Lady Maria and the chaplain as far as Castlewood. The road took them again by Oakhurst, by that trim village where hedges clipped the wind into a mild whisper, and where the kitchen-smoke hung low and honest over the chimneys as if the very air were being fed. Lady Maria, perched in her carriage like a judge in ribbons, made keen, pecking remarks about the Oakhurst young ladies—the way they had looked, the way they had lingered, the way their mother had measured him as though he were a side of beef to be bargained for.

Her words pricked him. He said, sharply, that since her ladyship hated and abused those kind folk, he would not inflict them upon her again; and he would not so much as turn his head toward their gate. At the inn a few miles on, he carried his sulk like a cloak. Supper was set—roast fowl gone dry at the joints, a dish of eggs over-browned, bread that smelled faintly of the last loaf’s sourness, and wine warmed by the room rather than the hand. Lady Maria tried smiles, those little sugared pellets of the face meant to sweeten bitterness; Harry’s mouth stayed flat. She tried tears—she could summon them as easily as a cook can summon steam by lifting a lid; he watched them as one watches rain on a window, without reaching to wipe.

Her querulous remarks fetched only a growl. The chaplain sat on, pipe and patience, as if by instruction, and Lady Maria—thwarted of the private moment she wanted—was forced at last to rise, to be bowed out with stubborn politeness, and handed to landlady and maid like a parcel he was glad to see sealed away.

What horse was it, then, that galloped out of the inn-yard ten minutes after she had gone? An hour later, when Mrs. Betty came bustling for the smelling-salts, she found Parson Sampson alone, the room hazed with tobacco, the pipe’s faint crackle the loudest sound. Mr. Warrington? Gone to bed, perhaps; gone to take a walk in the moonlight, perhaps—how was a chaplain to know? Sampson answered with a dull calm that made the maid sniff, as if truth itself had an odour she distrusted.

In the morning, Harry was ready as if he had slept like a stone—only his brow looked like a storm. He took his place beside Lady Maria’s carriage, and rode black-faced, hardly speaking. Lady Maria, in her own mind, turned a fear over and over like a bead between finger and thumb: Great heavens! she must have told him that she stole it.

It had begun, indeed, on that steep hill three miles from Oakhurst on the Westerham road. They had walked to ease the horses, Lady Maria leaning on Harry’s arm as though she had a right to his strength. She was in love with him—warmly, shamelessly, with the zeal of one who has endured long hunger and thinks herself at last seated before a feast. She warbled into his ear vows and protests, lines remembered from the few plays she knew, the scraps stuck in her mind like herbs in a mortar, pounded out for use when she thought them needed. As she grew fonder, he grew colder. Her face, fresh enough, well preserved by prudence and powders, still showed the fine webbing of forty years; and the weight of her arm upon his seemed suddenly intolerable. To think—so his mind ran—that all his life that weight was to be there, that clinging fondness, that claim. He cursed the hot evening, the hot wine, the moonlight walk that had ended in a pledge; he felt fatally bound by his own foolish mouth.

And she, delighted by her own tenderness, praised him until praise became an instrument of torture. “My champion—my Henrico—my preserver!” she sighed, clinging closer. “Thy Molinda faithful ever, ever!” And then, with that eager menace lovers use when they fear losing what they have not earned, she murmured, “Ah, child! have I not thy precious image, thy precious hair, thy precious writing here? Shall it not go with me to the grave? It would, sir—were I to meet with unkindness from my Henrico!”

Harry started—felt his arm jerk as if it would drop hers. That little silken case: Madame Bernstein had given it him; she had sworn she burned what it held—hair and note. And yet Lady Maria had it still, pressed to her heart, warm as bread kept under a cloth. Lady Maria felt, at his start, her first pang of remorse—not at lying, but at being found out in a lie; for discovery is the whip many consciences require. Heaven help us: if some people were put to sackcloth and ashes for fibbing, they would never be out of them.

Castlewood received them with its old dignity: damp stone, tall trees that turned the wind into a low organ-note, and a stable-yard alive with the clop and snort of horses, the familiar music of a great house that feeds and is fed by beasts. My lord was away; the ladies too. The only Esmond at home was Mr. Will, who came back from partridge-shooting as the chaise rolled in. The smell of gunpowder still hung about him, and feathers were caught in the seams of his coat like evidence. He turned very pale when he saw Harry; Harry gave him a scowl that was a recognition and a sentence at once.

At supper they sat with Lady Maria and spoke civilly, because her presence was a napkin laid over the mess of their feelings. The talk was flat: races, bets, the common fodder of such men. Will had been to some? To several. He had done well? Pretty well. Harry’s voice stayed too calm.

“And you have brought back my horse sound?” Harry asked.

“My horse! what horse?” Will said.

“What horse? My horse,” Harry returned, curt as a butcher’s cleaver.

“Protest I don’t understand you,” Will said, and tried to laugh.

“The brown horse for which I played you, and which I won of you the night before you rode away upon it,” Harry said, with a sternness that made the very knives seem sharper. “You remember the horse, Mr. Esmond.”

“Mr. Warrington, I perfectly well remember playing you for a horse, which my servant handed over to you on the day of your departure.”

“The chaplain was present at our play,” Harry said, still mild. “Mr. Sampson, will you be umpire between us?”

“I am bound to decide that Mr. Warrington played for the brown horse,” Sampson answered, with a glance that begged Will to be wise.

“Well, he got the other one,” Will muttered, and showed his teeth in a grin.

“And sold it for thirty shillings,” Harry said—always that careful calm, like a cook’s hand steady over a dangerous flame.

“Thirty shillings? and a devilish good price, too, for the broken-kneed old rip! Ha, ha!”

“Not a word more. ’Tis only a question about a bet, my dear Lady Maria. Shall I serve you some more chicken?” Harry’s courtesy, while she remained, was studied and bright as a polished dish. When she rose, he followed, bowed, and shut the door on her with the most courtly farewell—as if closing a pantry against vermin.

He stood a moment listening to her retreating rustle. Then he bade the servants leave. When they were out, he locked the heavy door and put the key in his pocket.

The click sounded like the snap of a trap.

Will, pale now not from surprise but from fear forced into anger, stared over his punch and swore, asking what the devil Harry meant by that.

“I guess there’s going to be a quarrel,” Harry said, almost gently, “and there is no use in having these fellows look on at rows between their betters.”

“Who is going to quarrel here, I should like to know?” Will said, and his hand went to a knife as if it knew its own business better than he did.

“Mr. Sampson,” Harry said, “you were present when I played Mr. Will fifty guineas against his brown horse?”

“Against his horse!” Will bawled.

“I am not such a fool as you take me for,” Harry answered, and his Virginian blood, long held in, spoke through his civility. “Although I do come from Virginia!” He repeated, steadily, “Mr. Sampson, you were here when I played the Honourable William Esmond, Esquire, fifty guineas against his brown horse?”

“I must own it, sir,” Sampson said, deprecatingly.

“I don’t own no such a thing,” Will said, forcing a laugh that sounded like a cracked bell.

“No, sir: because it costs you no more pains to lie than to cheat,” Harry said, and walked up to him. “Hands off, Mr. Chaplain, and see fair play! Because you are no better than a—ha—”

What he called him was swallowed by action. Will, with the swiftness of one who prefers violence to being named, snatched a wine bottle and flung it full at Harry’s head. Harry bobbed aside; the bottle flew across the room and smashed against the opposite wainscot, cracking the painted face of an Esmond ancestor—port spilling in a dark gush that splattered the chaplain’s white wig and cheeks so that he looked, for a moment, baptized in blood.

“Great heavens, gentlemen, I pray you to be quiet!” cried Sampson, dripping with wine and indignation.

But men in that heat do not remember the Church’s commands. Will seized the large silver-handled knife and lunged. Harry caught his right wrist in his left, just as he had seen the boxers do at Marybone, and drove a quick, hard blow into Will’s nose. The crack of it seemed to change the room’s acoustics: the oak panels threw back the sound, sharp and mean. Will reeled against the wall; the knife fell; Harry kicked it under the table with the neatness of one clearing a kitchen floor of broken crockery.

Will had been to Marybone too—to Hockley-in-the-Hole; he knew the language of fists. Blood ran; his breath rasped; then he dashed forward, head down, as if his skull were a ram and Harry’s belly a door.

Harry had seen that blunt savage charge among the negroes on his mother’s estate—two bodies meeting like cannon-balls. But he had also marked the white man’s trick. He skipped aside, and, as Will rushed past, Harry swung a tremendous blow on the right ear. Will went down, striking his head on the heavy oak table with a thud that made the glasses tremble, and lay senseless on the floor.

“Chaplain, you will bear witness that it has been a fair fight!” Harry said, still quivering but forcing his face into coolness. He drew the key, opened the door. Behind it, in the lobby, three or four servants were gathered, their ears and fear pressed close. The crash and shout and oath had carried through the house like a harsh drumbeat.

They entered and saw two victims bedabbled with red: the chaplain painted in port, and Mr. Will stretched out in his own gore.

“Mr. Sampson will bear witness that I struck fair, and that Mr. Esmond hit the first blow,” Harry said. “Undo his neckcloth, somebody—he may be dead; and get a fleam, Gumbo, and bleed him. Stop! He is coming to himself! Lift him up, you, and tell a maid to wash the floor.”

In a minute Will did begin to return. First his eyes rolled—rather, his eye, one swollen shut by Harry’s earlier blow. Then came a gasp and a moan, then a flood of swearing, fluent as ale from a tapped cask.

“He is getting well,” Harry said, dryly.

“Oh, praise be Mussy!” sighed sentimental Betty, whose heart was soft even when her tongue was sharp.

“Ask him, Gumbo, whether he would like any more?” Harry said, with a stern humour.

“Massa Harry say, wool you like any maw?” Gumbo asked obediently, bending over the prostrate gentleman with that grave politeness that can sting worse than insult.

“No, curse you, you black devil!” Will cried, striking up at Gumbo. Gumbo explained afterward to pitying Betty, “So he nearly cut my tongue in two in my mouf!” “No—that is, yes! You infernal Mohock! Why does not somebody kick him out of the place?”

“Because nobody dares, Mr. Esmond,” Harry answered, arranging his ruffles—his ruffled ruffles—with the air of a man setting his table after a disturbance.

“And nobody won’t neither,” growled the men. They had come to love Harry, who spoke to them like men when he remembered to, whereas Will had no one’s good word.

“We know all’s fair, sir. It ain’t the first time Master William have been served so.”

“And I hope it won’t be the last,” shrilled Betty. “To go for to strike a poor black gentleman so!”

Will, wiping his bleeding face with a napkin, gathered himself and slunk toward the door like a whipped cur. His curses left him in a thick trail, as if his mouth were a chimney belching soot.

“Surely it’s manners to say good night to the company. Good night, Mr. Esmond,” Harry called after him; the joke was not brilliant, but it warmed Harry inwardly like a dram.

“He’s ad his zopper, and he goes to baid!” Betty said in her native dialect, and everyone laughed outright except Will, who disappeared.

Harry’s wit continued next morning. He sent Will a note to ask if he fancied a ride to town or anywheres else; if for London, Harry would frighten the highwaymen on Hounslow Heath and look a very genteel figure at the Chocolate House. Will’s reply, if he sent one, suggested a destination not on Hounslow.

And there came, too, the inevitable domestic intrusion of romance: a simpering maiden at Harry’s door, and Gumbo entering with something white and triangular pinched between his dark fingers. A letter, of course. Harry groaned before he opened it, as one groans when a dish arrives too sweet, too rich, too much of itself.

Molinda greeting her Enrico, and so forth—no sleep, no peace, vows, fears, jealousy dressed as devotion; he must never, never quarrel and be so cruel again. The words ran on, a syrup with no sharpness to cut it, and for Harry, already sickened by his own pledge, the sweetness cloyed.

After the scandal with cousin Will, Harry’s dignity would not permit him to stay longer at Castlewood. He wrote a majestic letter to my lord of the house explaining what had passed; and as he called in Parson Sampson to supervise the document, it no doubt contained none of the odd spellings that straggled through his common correspondence. He told poor Maria that, having blackened the eye and damaged the nose of a son of the family, he could hardly remain under that roof with grace; she was forced to agree that his absence would best become him.

Of course she wept at parting. He would go to London and see younger beauties; he would find none who would love him like his fond Maria. Harry, if he felt anything, did not show it. The moment he crossed Castlewood Bridge his spirits rose as if the air itself had changed flavour. He made his horses whisk at ten miles an hour; he sang to them to go along; he nodded to pretty girls by the roadside; he chucked the landlady under the chin. He was not inconsolable.

Truth was, London called him like music heard far off: the thrum of wheels on stones, the shouting of link-boys, the fiddles and laughter from pleasure gardens, the rattle of dice on baize at clubs—an acoustic feast after Tunbridge’s tame murmur. The petty society of women and cards had become child’s-play now that he had tasted the city.

By the time he reached London again, almost all the four-and-forty pounds he had at Tunbridge had slipped away—like flour through careless fingers. Further supplies were necessary. Regarding these he made himself easy. There were two sums of five thousand pounds, in his own and his brother’s name, of which he was master. He would take up a little money; a run or two of good luck at play could replace it. Meanwhile he must live as became his station, and Madam Esmond must understand that a gentleman of his rank cannot keep fitting company, nor appear as he ought, upon a miserable pittance of two hundred a year.

He lodged at the Bedford Coffee-House as before, but only briefly; he wanted a more fashionable kitchen, as it were, for his life—nearer the Court-end, where reputations were baked and served hot. He fixed on rooms in Bond Street and installed himself and Gumbo; his horses stood at a neighbouring livery-stable, where the constant stamping and whinnying, the ring of iron shoes, made a rough counterpoint to the street’s finer noises.

Tailors, mercers, shoemakers were summoned. Not without a pang—like setting aside a mourning cloth too soon—he laid aside black and appeared in lace, hat and waistcoat. Gumbo, deft at hair as any French valet, dressed Harry’s locks, flung powder into them until they smelled of starch and faint perfume; and soon Harry’s head was as modish as any gentleman’s in the Mall. He drove his phaeton in the Ring, where eyes appraised him as they appraise a new dish—curiosity first, then judgement. Reports of his great wealth had long preceded him; London, which loves nothing so much as an exotic fortune, was eager to taste this fortunate Virginian.

Until he could be balloted for in season, Lord March had written down his name at White’s Chocolate-House as a distinguished gentleman from America. There were as yet but few people of fashion in town; but with a pocket full of money at one-and-twenty, a young man can make himself happy even out of season. Harry was determined to enjoy.

He ordered Mr. Draper to sell five hundred pounds of his stock. What would his poor mother have said, could she have heard the scratch of that pen as the first slice was cut from his patrimony? He dined at taverns where the air was thick with roasted meat and old smoke, where gravy clung to plates and laughter rose in waves; he supped at the club, introduced by Jack Morris with loud eulogies to such gentlemen as were in town. Life and youth and pleasure were before him; the wine was set a-running, and he was greedy to drink. Far away in the west, a pious widow might be at her prayers, shaping his name in a tongue that held the old country’s cadences; behind the trees at Oakhurst, perhaps, a tender little heart beat for him, keeping time with the farm’s honest sounds—milk pails, swishing brooms, the lowing of cows. When the Prodigal Son was away carousing, were not love and forgiveness still watching for him?

Among the unprinted letters of the late Lord Orford there is one the learned editor Mr. Peter Cunningham has omitted, perhaps doubting its truth. I have seen only a copy among the Warrington papers in Madam Esmond’s prim handwriting, noted, “Mr. H. Walpole’s account of my son Henry at London, and of Baroness Tusher,—wrote to General Conway.”

“ARLINGTON STREET, Friday Night.

“I have come away, child, for a day or two from my devotions to our Lady of Strawberry. Have I not been on my knees to her these three weeks, and aren’t the poor old joints full of rheumatism? A fit took me that I would pay London a visit, that I would go to Vauxhall and Ranelagh. Quoi! May I not have my rattle as well as other elderly babies? Suppose, after being so long virtuous, I take a fancy to cakes and ale, shall your reverence say nay to me? George Selwyn and Tony Storer and your humble servant took boat at Westminster t’other night. Was it Tuesday?—no, Tuesday I was with their Graces of Norfolk, who are just from Tunbridge—it was Wednesday. How should I know? Wasn’t I dead drunk with a whole pint of lemonade I took at White’s?

“The Norfolk folk had been entertaining me on Tuesday with the account of a young savage Iroquois, Choctaw, or Virginian, who has lately been making a little noise in our quarter of the globe. He is an offshoot of that disreputable family of Esmond, Castlewood, of whom all the men are gamblers and spendthrifts, and all the women—well, I shan’t say the word, lest Lady Ailesbury should be looking over your shoulder. Both the late lords, my father told me, were in his pay, and the last one, a beau of Queen Anne’s reign, from a viscount advanced to be an earl through the merits and intercession of his notorious old sister Bernstein, late Tusher, née Esmond—a great beauty, too, of her day, a favourite of the old Pretender. She sold his secrets to my papa, who paid her for them; and being nowise particular in her love for the Stuarts, came over to the august Hanoverian house at present reigning over us. ‘Will Horace Walpole’s tongue never stop scandal?’ says your wife over your shoulder. I kiss your ladyship’s hand. I am dumb. The Bernstein is a model of virtue. She had no good reasons for marrying her father’s chaplain. Many of the nobility omit the marriage altogether. She wasn’t ashamed of being Mrs. Tusher, and didn’t take a German Baroncino for a second husband, whom nobody out of Hanover ever saw. The Yarmouth bears no malice. Esther and Vashti are very good friends, and have been cheating each other at Tunbridge at cards all the summer.

“‘And what has all this to do with the Iroquois?’ says your ladyship. The Iroquois has been at Tunbridge, too—not cheating, perhaps, but winning vastly. They say he has bled Lord March of thousands—Lord March, by whom so much blood hath been shed, that he has quarrelled with everybody, fought with everybody, rode over everybody, been fallen in love with by everybody’s wife except Mr. Conway’s, and not excepting her present Majesty, the Countess of England, Scotland, France and Ireland, Queen of Walmoden and Yarmouth, whom Heaven preserve to us.

“You know an offensive little creature, de par le monde, one Jack Morris, who skips in and out of all the houses of London. When we were at Vauxhall, Mr. Jack gave us a nod under the shoulder of a pretty young fellow enough, on whose arm he was leaning, and who appeared hugely delighted with the enchantments of the garden. Lord, how he stared at the fireworks! Gods, how he huzzayed at the singing of a horrible painted wench who shrieked the ears off my head! A twopenny string of glass beads and a strip of tawdry cloth are treasures in Iroquois-land, and our savage valued them accordingly.

“A buzz went about the place that this was the fortunate youth. He won three hundred at White’s last night very genteelly from Rockingham and my precious nephew, and here he was bellowing and huzzaying over the music so as to do you good to hear. I do not love a puppet-show, but I love to treat children to one, Miss Conway! I present your ladyship my compliments, and hope we shall go and see the dolls together.

“When the singing woman came down from her throne, Jack Morris must introduce my Virginian to her. I saw him blush up to the eyes, and make her, upon my word, a very fine bow, such as I had no idea was practised in wigwams. ‘There is a certain jenny squaw about her, and that’s why the savage likes her,’ George said—a joke certainly not as brilliant as a firework. After which it seemed to me that the savage and the savages retired together.

“Having had a great deal too much to eat and drink three hours before, my partners must have chicken and rack-punch at Vauxhall, where George fell asleep straightway, and for my sins I must tell Tony Storer what I knew about this Virginian’s amiable family, especially some of the Bernstein’s antecedents, and the history of another elderly beauty of the family, a certain Lady Maria, who was au mieux with the late Prince of Wales. What did I say? I protest not half of what I knew, and of course not a tenth part of what I was going to tell, for who should start out upon us but my savage, this time quite red in the face; and in his war paint. The wretch had been drinking fire-water in the next box!

“He cocked his hat, clapped his hand to his sword, asked which of the gentleman was it that was maligning his family? so that I was obliged to entreat him not to make such a noise, lest he should wake my friend, Mr. George Selwyn. And I added, ‘I assure you, sir, I had no idea that you were near me, and most sincerely apologise for giving you pain.’”
