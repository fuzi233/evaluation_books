One after another, the rest of the guests followed the Ablewhites until we had all twenty-four assembled—the family included. It was a grand sight when they took their seats around the dinner table, and the Rector of Frizinghall, with his elegant diction, rose to say grace.

There’s no need to burden you with a full list of the attendees. You won’t meet any of them again—unless it’s those two exceptions.

Those two sat on either side of Miss Rachel, who, as the guest of honor, naturally drew everyone’s attention. On this occasion, she was especially captivating—not just for her charm, but because (to my lady’s quiet dismay) she wore her extraordinary birthday gift: the Moonstone. When it had first been given to her, it was bare, unmounted; but Mr. Franklin, ever the ingenious man, had cleverly secured it with silver wire and pinned it to the front of her white dress like a brooch. Naturally, every guest marveled at its size and brilliance. Yet only two among them spoke of it in anything but ordinary admiration—the very two seated beside her.

On her left sat Dr. Candy, our local physician from Frizinghall.

He was a cheerful, affable little man, though prone to overdoing his humor—often launching into conversation with strangers without a moment's hesitation, regardless of context. In society, he frequently made awkward remarks or unintentionally stirred up tension. But in medicine, he was far more cautious—his judgment sharpened by instinct, and often more accurate than doctors who relied on rigid procedure. To Miss Rachel, he addressed the Diamond with his usual playful twist. With mock solemnity, he begged her, “for the sake of science,” to let him take it home and burn it. “We’ll heat it to a certain temperature,” he said, “then expose it to air—and slowly, puff!—we evaporate the diamond, sparing you the worry of guarding such a treasure!” My lady, watching with a troubled look, seemed almost relieved that he wasn’t serious—and even wished she could have convinced Miss Rachel to sacrifice her gift for the sake of science.

On Miss Rachel’s right sat a man of considerable repute—Mr. Murthwaite, the famed Indian traveler who had ventured deep into forbidden territories under disguise, risking his life where no European had dared go before.

He was tall, lean, wiry, and deeply tanned, with a weary expression and eyes that never wavered. Rumor had it he was restless, longing to return to the wilds of the East. Throughout the meal, he spoke scarcely a word and drank not a single glass of wine—until the Moonstone came into view. That alone held his interest. The stone’s legend had reached him during his journeys across perilous Indian lands. After studying it in silence so long that Miss Rachel grew uneasy, he spoke to her calmly: “If you ever travel to India, Miss Verinder, do not take your uncle’s gift with you. A Hindu diamond is sometimes sacred. I know a city, and a temple within it, where, dressed as you are now, your life would be worth less than five minutes.” Miss Rachel, safe in England, found the warning thrilling. The Bouncers were even more excited—dropping their cutlery with a crash and shouting together, “Oh! How fascinating!” My lady shifted uncomfortably in her seat and quickly changed the subject.

As dinner progressed, I began to sense something was wrong. This gathering was not going as smoothly as others had before.

Looking back now, through the lens of what followed, I can’t help but wonder if the cursed diamond had cast a shadow over the entire company. I saw to the wine and, as an old servant with special privileges, moved among the guests, offering unpopular dishes with whispered encouragement: “Try it—trust me, it will agree with you.” Nine times out of ten, they did—out of respect for me, they said—but it made no difference. Silences grew longer and more uncomfortable. When conversation resumed, it often turned awkward or inappropriate. Dr. Candy, for instance, said more ill-timed things than I’d ever seen him utter.

Take one example. Among the guests was Mrs. Threadgall, widow of the late Professor Threadgall. She constantly referred to her husband as if he were still alive, assuming everyone knew he was gone. When someone mentioned human anatomy, she casually remarked it was the Professor’s favorite pastime. Dr. Candy, sitting opposite and unaware of the man’s death, seized the chance to be helpful. “The College of Surgeons has some excellent skeletons lately,” he declared cheerfully. “I’d strongly recommend the Professor pay them a visit when he has time.”

A pin might have dropped in the hush that followed. Everyone sat stunned, respectful of the dead professor’s memory. I stood behind Mrs. Threadgall, handing her a glass of hock. She lowered her head and whispered, “My beloved husband is no more.”

But Dr. Candy, oblivious, continued louder than before: “The Professor may not know that a member’s card allows entry between ten and four, any day but Sunday.”

Mrs. Threadgall buried her face in her napkin and repeated, softer still, “My beloved husband is no more.”

I gave Dr. Candy a sharp wink. Miss Rachel touched his arm. My lady shot him a look of pure exasperation. Useless. He pressed on: “I’d be delighted to send the Professor my card—if you’d kindly tell me his current address.”

“His present address, sir,” Mrs. Threadgall snapped, losing control, “is _the grave_. The Professor died ten years ago!”

“Oh, heavens!” Dr. Candy exclaimed. Only the Bouncers laughed. The room fell into a deeper silence—so profound it felt as if all were already headed toward the same destination.

That was Dr. Candy. The others were nearly as troubling. When they should speak, they stayed silent. When they did speak, they missed the mark. Mr. Godfrey, usually eloquent, kept quiet—either sulking or shy after his earlier mishap in the rose garden. He reserved his words for the woman beside him—a spiritual-minded relative of ours, with striking collarbones and a taste for dry champagne. Standing behind them at the sideboard, I caught fragments of their conversation—deep, earnest talk about religion, love, heaven, and earth. Mr. Godfrey claimed love was religion, and religion was love. Earth, he said, was heaven slightly worn down. Heaven, in turn, was earth restored—renewed and perfect. And in heaven, all women would serve on a great committee that never quarreled, while men served them as angels. Beautiful, yes—but why did he keep it all to himself?

Then there was Mr. Franklin. Surely, he would lift the mood?

Not at all. He was fully recovered and in high spirits—perhaps buoyed by Penelope’s account of Mr. Godfrey’s embarrassment. But his attempts to engage the guests misfired. Nine times out of ten, he chose the wrong topic or addressed the wrong person. His foreign influences—French wit, German philosophy, Italian flair—surfaced in confusing ways.

For instance, he discussed how far a married woman might admire a man who wasn’t her husband—delivering it in fluent, sharp French—to the maiden aunt of the Vicar. Then, switching to German thought, he told the landowner, while the latter praised bull-breeding experience, that such knowledge meant nothing. “To breed bulls properly,” he said, “you must look inward, form the perfect bull in your mind, and create it.” Later, when the county member railed against democracy, saying, “If we lose our ancient safeguards, Mr. Blake, what remains?”—Franklin replied, from the Italian perspective: “Three things: Love, Music, and Salad.”

Such remarks startled the guests. And when his English side surfaced, he lost his polish. Speaking of doctors, he mocked them so bluntly that he provoked Dr. Candy into anger.

It began when Franklin admitted he’d been sleeping poorly. Candy diagnosed nerves and recommended treatment. Franklin scoffed: “Medicine and groping in the dark mean the same thing to me.” Candy retorted: “You’re groping in the dark yourself—only medicine can find sleep for you.” Franklin countered: “I’ve heard of the blind leading the blind—but now I understand it.” Their exchange grew heated, cutting back and forth until both were flushed. Candy, defending his profession, lost control. My lady had to intervene, stopping the argument. That ended the evening’s spirit. Conversation flickered briefly, then died. It felt as if evil—or the diamond—had taken hold. Everyone breathed easier when my mistress rose and signaled the ladies to retire.

I was just arranging the decanters before old Mr. Ablewhite when a sound from the terrace froze me mid-movement. Mr. Franklin and I exchanged glances—it was the Indian drum. As I live by bread, here came the jugglers again, returning with the Moonstone!

They rounded the corner of the terrace, and I hurried out to stop them. But the Bouncers beat me to it—darting out like rockets, eager to see the performance. The other ladies followed. Gentlemen poured out too. Before I could react, the rogues were bowing, and the Bouncers were kissing the little boy.

Mr. Franklin stepped beside Miss Rachel. I positioned myself behind her. If our suspicions were correct, there she stood—unaware—displaying the diamond in her dress to the very men who might kill for it.

I cannot describe their tricks. Between the ruined dinner and the shock of seeing the thieves return just as they did, I lost my composure. All I remember clearly is the sudden appearance of Mr. Murthwaite. He slipped behind the jugglers, speaking sharply to them in their own tongue.

Had he thrust a bayonet at them, they could not have reacted faster. They spun around, eyes wide, and bowed low. After a few words exchanged in the unknown language, Murthwaite withdrew quietly. The chief Indian, acting as interpreter, turned back to the guests. I noticed his coffee-colored face had gone pale. He bowed to my lady and announced the show was over. The Bouncers groaned in disappointment. The chief placed his hand on his chest and repeated, “The juggling is finished.” The boy went round with the hat. The ladies returned to the drawing-room. The gentlemen—except Franklin and Murthwaite—went back to their wine. I and the footman escorted the Indians off the property.

Walking back through the shrubbery, I smelled tobacco and found Franklin and Murthwaite strolling among the trees—the traveler smoking a cheroot. Franklin beckoned me to join them.

“This,” he said, introducing me, “is Gabriel Betteredge, the family’s longtime servant and friend. Tell him what you’ve just told me.”

Murthwaite removed his cheroot and leaned against a tree, weary-eyed.

“Mr. Betteredge,” he began, “those three men are not jugglers. Not in the slightest.”

I was stunned. I asked if he had met them before.

“Never,” he said. “But I know real Indian juggling. What you saw tonight was clumsy imitation. Unless I’m utterly mistaken, they are high-caste Brahmins. I accused them of disguise—and you saw how they reacted, despite their skill at hiding emotion. There’s a mystery here. They’ve sacrificed their caste twice: once by crossing the sea, again by pretending to be entertainers. In their homeland, that’s a grave offense. There must be a powerful reason—something urgent enough to justify reclaiming their status upon return.”

I stood speechless. Murthwaite lit his cheroot again. Franklin, after a brief internal struggle, broke the silence.

“I hesitate to involve you in family matters—things you have no stake in, and which I prefer to keep private. But after what you’ve said, I feel compelled to share something that may help you understand. I speak in confidence—please keep it so.”

And so he recounted everything—the events at Shivering Sand, the theft, the recovery. Even Murthwaite, usually impassive, let his cheroot go out.

“Now,” Franklin said, “what does your experience suggest?”

“My experience,” Murthwaite replied, “says you’ve narrowly escaped death more times than I have. And that’s saying much.”

Franklin looked astonished.

“Really that serious?”

“In my opinion, yes. After hearing you, I can’t doubt the restoration of the Moonstone to the idol’s forehead is the motive behind their caste-breaking. They’ll wait like cats, strike like tigers. I can’t imagine how you’ve survived. You’ve carried the diamond between London and here—twice—and yet you’re still alive. Let’s try to explain it. Was it daylight both times you removed it from the bank?”

“Broad daylight,” Franklin said.

“And crowded streets?”

“Very.”

“You planned to arrive at Lady Verinder’s at a specific time? The road is isolated. Did you keep it?”

“No. I arrived four hours early.”

“I congratulate you. When did you bring it to the local bank?”

“One hour after arriving here—three hours before anyone expected me.”

“Another congratulations. Did you return alone?”

“No. I rode back with cousins and the groom.”

“Third congratulations. If you ever plan to travel beyond civilized lands, Mr. Blake, let me know—I’ll come with you. You’re a lucky man.”

I interjected. This didn’t align with my English sensibilities.

“Do you truly mean,” I asked, “they would kill Mr. Franklin to get the diamond?”

“Do you smoke, Mr. Betteredge?”

“Yes, sir.”

“Do you care about the ashes left in your pipe when you empty it?”

“No, sir.”

“In their country, they care just as little about taking a life. If a thousand lives stood between them and the diamond—and if they believed they could destroy them unseen—they would take them all. Sacrificing caste is serious in India. Sacrificing life? Meaningless.”

I called them murderers. Murthwaite called them remarkable. Franklin, silent, brought us back.

“They’ve seen the Moonstone on Miss Verinder’s dress. What now?”

“What your uncle threatened,” Murthwaite replied. “Colonel Herncastle understood these people. Send the diamond tomorrow—guarded by more than one man—to Amsterdam. Cut it into six smaller stones. Destroy its sacred identity—and end the conspiracy.”

Franklin turned to me.

“There’s no choice. We must speak to Lady Verinder tomorrow.”

“What about tonight?” I asked. “Suppose they return?”

Murthwaite answered before Franklin could.

“They won’t risk coming back tonight. They rarely take the direct path—especially in matters where a single mistake could doom their goal.”

“But suppose they’re bolder than you think?”

“In that case,” Murthwaite said, “loose the dogs. Do you have large dogs?”

“Two. A mastiff and a bloodhound.”

“Perfect. In this emergency, Mr. Betteredge, they have one great advantage—they won’t hesitate over the sanctity of human life.”

The piano strummed from the drawing-room as he said it. He tossed away his cheroot and took Franklin’s arm. I noticed the sky darkening. Murthwaite glanced back at me.

“The Indians will want umbrellas tonight, Mr. Betteredge.”

Easy for him to joke. I was no explorer. I hadn’t lived among thieves and killers in distant lands. I retreated to my room, sat in my chair, drenched in sweat, and wondered what to do next. Instead of spiraling into panic, I lit my pipe and opened *Robinson Crusoe*.

After five minutes, I read this passage—page 161:

“Fear of danger is ten thousand times more terrifying than danger itself when seen with the eyes; and we find the burden of anxiety greater, by much, than the evil we fear.”

I closed the book and stared into the fire.
