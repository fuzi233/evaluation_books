The roar of the crowd and the chaotic symphony of sounds that greeted Tito as he entered the piazza brought to mind Nello's warnings about the *Fierucoloni*. With a strange sense of delight, he thrust himself into the throng, enjoying the jostling elbows and the raucous hoots that filled the void. These noises served to dull the bleak calculations of his future, the terrifying prospect of wandering alone in search of an unknown fortune, and momentarily erased the thought of seeking out Baldassarre.

From the opposite entrances, streams of people surged into the square, carrying aloft paper lanterns on sticks that waved uncertainly in the wind. Above the din of a crude, monotonous chant, a tapestry of noise unfolded: screams, whistles, mocking songs sung in piping boyish voices, the beat of drums, and the tinkling of small bells, all colliding in a confused uproar. Periodically, one of the drifting lights vanished with a crash, struck by a stone hurled in a vague attempt at mischief, followed by shrieks and renewed shouting. Yet, on the fringes of this whirlwind, groups observed the Vigil of the Nativity of the Virgin with greater order than the riotous stone-throwers. Ragged men darted sharp glances around while their tongues rattled merrily, inviting country folk to games on generous terms. Two masqueraders on stilts, having snatched lanterns from the crowd, swung the lights in meteoric arcs as they paraded back and forth. A shrewd merchant conducted a profitable trade at a small covered stall, selling hot *berlingozzi*, a favorite flour-based delicacy. Another man stood atop a barrel, backed against a pillar of the loggia before the Foundling Hospital (*Spedale degli Innocenti*), peddling miracle pills invented by a doctor of Salerno, guaranteed to cure toothaches and prevent drowning. Nearby, a juggler performed tricks on a small platform, while a handful of apprentices, bored by the guerrilla-style stone-throwing, engaged in a concentrated match of their favorite Florentine sport at the narrow entrance of the Via de' Febbrai.

Forced through random gaps in the crowd, Tito found himself beside the trotting procession of barefoot peasant women. Their sun-dried, bronzed faces and strange, fragmented garments—dimmed by generations of dust and fashioned from obsolete materials—made them appear to the city dwellers as a weary ancestry returning from a pilgrimage begun a century prior. At that moment, it was the sturdy, sparingly fed women from the mountains of Pistoia entering with a year's labor in a modest bundle of yarn strapped to their backs. In their hearts lay that meager hope of goodness and that vast, dim fear of harm, both entrusted to the protection of the Blessed Virgin. On this Eve of her Nativity, her miraculous image, painted by angels, would have its veil lifted, allowing her divine potency to flow unobstructed.

Elsewhere, near the boundaries of the square, vendors had stationed themselves safely in their rear. Among them, Tito recognized Bratti, leaning against a pillar with a pursed lip of disdainful silence. He eyed every approacher with a cold look of superiority, clutching tightly to a serge cloth that hid the contents of his basket. Surprised by such unusual behavior for an anxious trader, Tito stepped closer. Two women approached the basket with curious glances, prompting Bratti to pull the covering tighter and look away. Provoked, one woman asked what was inside.

"Before I answer, Monna," Bratti replied, "I must know if you intend to buy. I cannot display such wares in this fair for every fly to land on and pay nothing. My goods are too choice for that. Besides, I have only two left, and I have no mind to soil them. Given the rumors of pestilence circulating among the wise, they might be worth their weight in gold. No, no: *andate con Dio*."

The women exchanged glances. "And what is the price?" asked the second.

"Not within what you are likely to carry, *buona donna*," Bratti said in a tone of compassionate condescension. "I advise you to trust in Messer Domeneddio and the saints; poor people can do no better for themselves."

"Not so poor!" the second woman retorted indignantly, pulling out her money-bag. "Come, now! What do you say to a *grosso*?"

"I say you may get twenty-one *quattrini* for it," Bratti answered coolly, "but not from me, for I lack such small change."

"Then two?" the woman demanded, growing exasperated, while her companion watched with envy. "It won't be more than two, I believe."

After further bidding and mercantile coquetry, Bratti feigned concession. "Since you are determined," he said slowly, lifting the cover, "I would be loath to cause you harm. Maestro Gabbadeo used to say that when a woman sets her mind on a thing and fails to obtain it, she falls into greater danger of the pestilence than before. *Ecco!* I have but two left. Let me tell you, the charm lies on the finger of Maestro Gabbadeo, who has gone to Bologna—he is as wise a doctor as sits at any door."

The treasures were two clumsy iron rings, hammered into the style of ancient Roman rings occasionally unearthed. The rust upon them and the completely hidden nature of their magic were so convincing that the *grossi* were paid without complaint. The first woman, lacking such handsome coins, managed after much feigned reluctance to drive a bargain using some of her yarn, eventually carrying off the remaining ring in triumph. Bratti covered his basket, now filled with miscellaneous items likely acquired under similar circumstances, and moved from his pillar directly toward Tito, who would have preferred to avoid recognition.

"By the head of San Giovanni," Bratti exclaimed, pulling Tito back to the pillar, "what a stroke of luck! For I was speaking of you this morning, Messer Greco. I remarked that he has risen among the gentlemen—and I am glad, for I was at the bottom of his fortune—but I rarely catch him, for he is not to be found lying on the stones anymore. But it is your luck, not mine, Messer Greco, save for a small trifle to compensate me for my trouble in this transaction."

"You speak in riddles, Bratti," Tito said. "Remember, I do not sharpen my wits as you do by haggling over iron rings; you must be plain."

"By the Holy Gospel! It was an easy bargain I gave them. If a Hebrew gains thirty-two percent, I hope a Christian may gain a little more. Had I not borne a conscience, I would have secured twice the money and twice the yarn. But speaking of rings, it is your ring—that very ring on your finger—for which I can find a buyer. Yes, a buyer with a deep purse."

"Really?" Tito asked, examining his ring.

"A Genoese heading straight to Hungary, as I understand. He came and inspected my entire shop searching for old items whose prices I did not know; I wager he thought I had a pumpkin on my shoulders. He had rummaged every shop in Florence. He wore a ring—not exactly like yours, but of the same fashion. As we discussed rings, I mentioned knowing a fine young man, a particular friend of mine, who possessed such a ring. He asked, 'Who is he? Tell him I'll give him his price.' Then I thought to follow you to Nello's tomorrow. In my opinion, Messer Greco, you are not the type to watch the Arno turn to broth and stand idle without dipping a finger in."

Tito had listened intently, though his mind remained busy. Why keep the ring? It had been merely a sentimental whim preventing him from selling it with the other gems. Had he been wiser and sold it, he might have avoided the identification by Fra Luca. True, it had been taken from Baldassarre's finger and placed on his own as his hand grew, yet there was no real virtue in such superstitious scruples regarding inanimate objects. The ring had facilitated his recognition, and Tito increasingly disliked recognition—it was a claim from the past. This foreigner's offer, if genuine, presented an opportunity to dispose of the ring without the hassle of hunting a buyer.

"You speak with your usual wisdom, Bratti," Tito said. "I have no objection to hearing what your Genoese offers. But when and where shall I meet him?"

"Tomorrow, at three hours after sunrise, he will be at my shop. If your wit possesses the sharpness I have always attributed to you, Messer Greco, you will demand a heavy price; for he cares little for money. I believe he buys for someone else, perhaps a great lord."

"It is well," Tito agreed. "I will be at your shop, if nothing hinders."

"And you will undoubtedly deal nobly with me for old acquaintances' sake, Messer Greco, so I shall not linger to fix the small sum you will give me as a token of my service. It seems a thousand years since I left the piazza; a fair is dull, indeed wicked, when one has no more goods to sell."
