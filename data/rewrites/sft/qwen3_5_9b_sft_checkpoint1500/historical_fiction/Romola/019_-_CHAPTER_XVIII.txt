As Tito emerged from the Via de' Bardi, a profound sense of relief washed over him. The immediate danger had passed, leaving behind a quiet, almost suspicious tranquility. His mind, unburdened by the urgent gaze of Romola and her father, drifted back to those harrowing hours of uncertainty. He recalled how, in that fog of fear, he had acted contrary to his better judgment, surrendering his ring. Now, the weight of that deception felt lighter, yet it gnawed at him with a subtle irritation. To admit he had sold the gem simply to avoid trouble would look like sordidness to Romola and others who knew its value. Instead, he would have to fabricate a noble pretext—a grand gesture of generosity—to justify the loss. At this moment, however, Tito harbored a weary disdain for all pretense. Fortune had spared him the consequences of his deceit, and he felt a grudging gratitude toward fate, believing it owed him as much freedom as it had granted. Who truly suffered from his lapse? The repercussions for Baldassarre remained murky and distant. What mattered now was shedding every invisible chain that tethered him to Romola, even if those bonds were merely the remnants of his own guilt. He realized, with a chilling clarity, that his current joy in being untethered was slowly numbing the very sensibilities needed to prevent future entanglements.

Yet, compared to these heavy moral knots, the sale of the ring seemed trivial. Even more minor was the illusion that little Tessa clung to. Would her small, hopeful heart shatter upon learning the truth? He debated whether to seek her out alone and dispel her fantasy immediately, or let time and chance unravel it gently. The image of her—sweet, pouting, innocent, and round—was impossible to ignore. Perhaps he could bring her a gift, a token of affection, to gauge how harsh her stepfather had become since her mother's death. Alternatively, he could reveal the truth now and introduce her to Romola, hoping together they could secure her a brighter destiny. But no, the shadows of the Cerreto episode and his own complicity in letting Tessa depart under false pretenses must remain buried from Romola forever. Once exposed, there would be no undoing the damage. Besides, even a child possessed a sharp tongue; if Tessa discovered her "love" had been a lie, she might turn bitter. Yes, she likely loved him, and perhaps it was wiser to sever that thread entirely. After all, a country girl might dwell on such trifles until a rustic lover won her over, breaking her silence and revealing the truth of her freedom. Thus, *Dunque*, farewell, Tessa! May you fare well. Tito resolved that the scandalous affair of the Cerreto marriage would bear no fruit for him. Resolutions born of self-interest often prove fragile, yet he clung to his promise regarding the fifty-five florins earned from the ring. He had devised a clever plan to account for the missing jewel to Romola, a scheme that would also serve as a shield against the monastic reveries that plagued her mind and distressed him. With this objective in focus, he turned toward the Via Gualfonda to locate Piero di Cosimo, the preeminent master of fantastical mythology whose skills Tito required.

Arriving at the courtyard where Piero resided, Tito noted the heavy iron knocker, now swathed in thick wool and secured with intricate cords. Recalling the painter's notorious habit of stuffing his ears against noise, Tito was unsurprised by this makeshift defense against the clamor of visitors. He approached the door, first tapping modestly with his knuckles, then shaking it with increasing vigor. To no avail. Just as Tito prepared to retreat, blaming himself for squandering time on this errand, a small girl emerged from the shadows carrying a basket of eggs. She ascended the steps, stood on tiptoe, and slid up a small iron plate set into the grooved wood. Peering through the aperture, she called out in a piping voice, "Messer Piero!"

Moments later, the bolts gave way. The door swung open, and Piero appeared, dressed in a red nightcap and a loose brown serge tunic with sleeves rolled to the shoulders. He cast a startled glance at Tito but, offering no greeting, extended his hand to accept the basket from the child. Re-entering the house, he soon returned with the empty basket, asking, "How much to pay?"

"Two grossoni, Messer Piero," the girl replied, "they are all freshly boiled, my mother says."

Piero extracted coins from a leather pouch at his belt, handed them to the maid, and watched her trot away, casting upward glances filled with awe at the wealthy young gentleman.

However, Piero's countenance shifted instantly when he addressed Tito directly. "What business brings you to my door, Messer Greco? I saw you this morning at Nello's. Had you asked me then, I could have told you that I welcome no stranger without prior knowledge of his intent and agreement."

"Pardon, Messer Piero," Tito said, his demeanor unflappable and genial. "I acted without due reflection. My mind was occupied solely by your remarkable talent for devising whimsical conceits, and a sudden urge to commission something similar brought me here."

Piero's nature was so eccentric that such a rebuff was rarely perceived as an insult, yet even if Tito had suspected malice, his instinct for winning favor outweighed any spark of resentment. Piero made a habitual grimace, stretching the corners of his mouth and pressing down his brows to obscure his true feelings beneath a mask of stoicism.

"And pray, what is this necessity?" he asked after a brief pause. Though outwardly indifferent, his heart beat with a latent hunger for the creative challenge implied.

"I require a delicate miniature drawn from poetic fables, one that you surely possess the genius to synthesize. It must be painted on a wooden triptych box—the interior to be plain gilded, the exterior adorned with your design. The subject is a favorite of Florence: the Triumph of Bacchus and Ariadne, though I wish to treat it anew. Ovid provides the inspiration. Young Bacchus, seated in a vessel, his brow bound with grape clusters, a spear entwined with vines in his grasp. Dark ivy must coil around the masts and sails; the oars themselves shall be thyrsi, wreathed in flowers. Leopards and tigers crouch before him, dolphins play beside the ship. Beside him stands Ariadne, fair-haired and crowned with gold, rendered immortal—though absent from Ovid's tale, you will envision it. Above them, the young Loves, masters of your craft, shoot arrows tipped with roses..."

"Sufficient!" Piero interrupted sharply. "I possess Ovid in the vernacular. Seek the passage yourself; I dislike being suffocated by another's thoughts. Enter."

Piero guided Tito into the first chamber. Upon the open hearth rested a basket of eggs, surrounded by shattered shells and a mound of ash, creating a jarring contrast to the opulence nearby. A low bedstead of carved ebony lay draped with a fragment of rich Oriental carpet, resembling a step from a Madonna's throne. Adjacent stood a massive *cassone*, its sides and lid decorated with painted scenes. Beyond, the room was sparsely furnished, dominated by plaster casts, wooden steps, easels, and rough crates, all draped in cobwebs.

The adjoining space was even vaster, yet more densely cluttered. Piero evidently celebrated the Feast, for the double doors beneath the window, which admitted the painter's light, stood agape, revealing a garden—or rather, a tangled thicket. Fig trees and vines sprawled wildly among nettles and hemlocks, while a towering cypress rose from a stifling mass of yellow mulberry leaves. This damp, unruly vegetation seemed to seep into the room itself. In one corner, amidst a chaotic pile of carved marble fragments and rusted armor, tufts of long grass and feathery fennel had taken root. A large stone vase, tilted precariously, appeared to spill forth the ivy streaming around it. Hanging from the walls were sketches in pen and oil depicting fantastical sea monsters, dances of satyrs and maenads, Saint Margaret's deliverance from the dragon, Madonnas bathed in supernatural radiance, botanical studies, and grotesque heads. Irregular shelves held scattered books nestled among drooping ears of corn, bullock's horns, dried honeycombs, stones encrusted with rare lichens, skulls, bones, peacock feathers, and broad bird wings. From the filth of the floor rose life-sized effigies: one clad in the robes of a Vallombrosan monk, bizarrely topped with a helmed visor; another smothered in brocade and skins. Amidst this eclectic still life, speckled and white pigeons strutted or perched, too tame to flee at the intrusion of men. Three plump toads crawled in close friendship near the threshold, while a white rabbit, presumably the model for the creature frightening Cupid in the central canvas depicting Mars and Venus, twitched its nose contentedly atop a box of bran.

"Now, Messer Greco," Piero said, gesturing for Tito to sit on a low stool near the door, while he loomed over him with folded arms, "do not attempt to view everything simultaneously, like some omniscient deity. Tell me precisely the dimensions of this triptych."

Tito indicated the desired measurements, and Piero marked them onto a sheet of paper.
