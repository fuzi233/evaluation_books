For Sally, the weeks following her reunion with Ginger Kemp felt less like reality and more like a golden age that had finally descended upon her tiny kingdom. Peace and prosperity ruled the borders of her domain. Every morning she awoke to find the world not just clean, but meticulously ironed and smoothed, leaving even the most cynical pessimist with nothing to complain about.

True, Gerald was still a thousand miles away, busy in Chicago supervising the opening of *The Primrose Way*. Fillmore had agreed to Ike's suggestion to premiere the play there, calling in a distraught manager to fix up a struggling comedy written by a relative. This meant Gerald would be stuck on the spot for a while, a disappointment since Sally had hoped to see him back in New York sooner. Yet, she refused to let herself be gloomy. Life, in general, was simply too perfect for depression. Fillmore's theater was booming, Ginger was clear of any moral debts, she had secured a lovely apartment, her new hat fit perfectly, and *The Primrose Way* was a smash hit. According to Fillmore, Chicago had forgotten everything except securing tickets for the show; national and local problems had vanished from the citizens' minds, replaced entirely by the singular obsession of how to get a seat. The production, Fillmore claimed, was the most electrifying event to shake Chicago since the Great Fire.

However, the single most satisfying development for Sally was the resolution of Ginger's future. Ginger had joined the Fillmore Nicholas Theatrical Enterprises Ltd.—a title Fillmore would have lengthened further, save for the space constraints on the brass plate—and was now stationed daily in the outer office. His duties primarily involved reading the evening papers. Exactly what role he held remained a mystery even to Ginger himself. At times he felt like the captain of the ship; at others, a glorified office boy, though perhaps not glorified enough. Mostly, his job was to act as a human moat, preventing the mob from rushing into the inner sanctum where Fillmore sat in semi-regal contemplation of grand schemes.

Despite occasional flickers of doubt regarding whether he deserved the fifty dollars paid weekly, Ginger's gratitude toward Sally was absolute. He thanked her profusely whenever they met, and meetings were frequent now, as Ginger busied himself furnishing her new apartment. He spared no effort, claiming the physical labor kept him in top condition.

"And what I mean to say is," Ginger panted, pausing while dragging a massive easy chair to the third spot Sally had chosen in the last ten minutes, "if I didn't sweat a bit and help you out after the way you got me that job..."

"Ginger, desist," Sally commanded gently.

"Yes, but honestly—"

"If you don't stop, I'll make you move that chair into the next room."

"Shall I?" Ginger rubbed his blistered hands and adjusted his grip. "Anything you say."

"Silly! Of course not. The only other rooms are my bedroom, the bathroom, and the kitchen. What on earth would I want a lumbering chair in there? Besides, I believe the first one we picked was the best."

"Then back she goes, eh?"

Sally frowned, deep in thought. Setting up house was proving to be a mental workout.

"No," she decided firmly. "By the window is better." She looked at him with feigned remorse. "I'm giving you a lot of trouble."

"Trouble!" Ginger, accompanied by the chair, staggered across the floor. "From my perspective, it works like this: You got me the job, and..."

"Stop!"

"Right ho... Still, you did, you know."

Sally sank into the armchair and stretched. Watching Ginger toil had given her a vicarious exhaustion. She surveyed the room with pride. It was already cozy; pictures hung, the carpet lay flat, and the furniture stood neat. For the first time in three years of boarding-house drudgery, she felt the restful sensation of being truly at home—a place where she could lock the door and exist in solitude. The apartment was small, yet undeniably a haven. She scanned the walls for flaws... except...

"Hullo!" she exclaimed suddenly. "Where's that photograph of me? I'm sure I put it on the mantelpiece yesterday."

Ginger's exertions had flushed his face a brilliant crimson. He inspected the mantel narrowly.

"No. No photograph here."

"I know there isn't. But it was there yesterday. Or was it? I know I intended to put it there. Perhaps I forgot. It's the most beautiful thing you've ever seen. Not a bit like me, but who cares? Photographers touch them up in the darkroom, you know. I value it because it shows how I *should* look if I could."

"I've never had a beautiful photograph taken of myself," Ginger said solemnly, with gentle regret.

"Cheer up!"

"Oh, I don't mind. I only mentioned..."

"Ginger," Sally interrupted, "please pardon my breaking in on your valuable observations, but this chair is—simply—not right! It belongs where it was originally placed. Could you perform your imitation of a pack-mule one more time? And afterward, I'll make us some tea. If there's any tea—or milk—or cups."

"There are cups, all right. I know, because I smashed two the day before yesterday. Shall I nip round the corner for some milk?"

"Yes, please go. All this hard work has drained me terribly."

At the tea table, Sally grew inquisitive.

"What I can't comprehend about this job of yours, Ginger—which, as you are about to point out, I was noble enough to secure—is the sheer amount of leisure it affords. How are you able to spend your precious time—well, Fillmore's precious time—juggling my furniture every single day?"

"Oh, I can usually slip away."

"But shouldn't you be at your post doing... whatever it is you do? What exactly do you do?"

Ginger stirred his tea thoughtfully, wrestling with the question.

"Well, I sort of mess about, you know. I interview various scoundrels, tell them your brother is out, take their names and addresses, and... oh, all that sort of thing."

"Does Fillmore consult you often?"

"He lets me read some of the plays submitted. Terrible nonsense mostly. Occasionally he sends me to a vaudeville house in the evenings."

"As a treat?"

"To see a special act, naturally. To report on it. In case he wants to use it for his upcoming revue."

"Which revue?"

"Didn't you know he's launching a revue? Oh, rather. A whacking big affair. He's going to cut out the Follies and all that."

"But—my goodness!" Sally gasped, alarmed. It was typical of Fillmore to branch out into expensive ventures when he should be consolidating his modest success. All his life he had dreamed in millions while a prudent man would settle for hundreds. An endless fountain of optimism bubbled within him. "That's rather ambitious," she admitted.

"Yes. Ambitious sort of chap, your brother. Quite the Napoleon."

"I shall have to talk to him," Sally declared. She was annoyed with Fillmore. Everything had been so beautiful, peaceful, and prosperous until he had ruined it. Now she would have to start worrying again.

"Of course," argued Ginger, "there's money in revues. Over in London, fellows make fortunes out of them."

Sally shook her head.

"It won't do," she said. "And I'll tell you another thing that won't do. This armchair. Of course it ought to be by the window. You can see that yourself, can't you?"

"Absolutely!" said Ginger, patiently preparing for action once more.

***

Sally's anxiety regarding her ebullient brother was compounded shortly thereafter by a telegram from Miss Winch in Chicago.

While Sally couldn't claim to fully decode the message, the sinister undertone convinced her to investigate immediately. She tore herself away from the joys of furnishing and headed straight to the headquarters of the Fillmore Nicholas Theatrical Enterprises Ltd.

Upon arrival, she found Ginger absent from his usual post. His place was occupied by a young lad of tender years and pimply complexion, who melted instantly upon hearing Sally's name, casting aside his proud reserve to usher her inside. Sally walked right in to find Fillmore with his feet propped on an untidy desk, studying what appeared to be costume designs.

"Ah, Sally!" he said in a distracted, weary voice that spoke of vast preoccupations. Prosperity was quietly but lethally working on the Hope of the American Theatre. What had once been a smooth fullness around the jawline was now frankly, undisguisedly a double chin. He wore a new waistcoat, currently unbuttoned. "I am rather busy. Always glad to see you, but I am rather busy. I have a hundred things to attend to."

"Well, attend to me. That'll only make a hundred and one. Fill, what's all this I hear about a revue?"

Fillmore looked like a small boy caught in the act of stealing jam—the ultimate expression of a great theatrical manager in disgrace. He had been wondering in his darker moments what Sally would say about the project and had hoped she wouldn't hear of it until preparations were complete enough to render interference impossible. Fond as he was of Sally, he knew her cautious nature might lead to criticism.
