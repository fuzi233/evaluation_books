“If some mortal, born too soon,”—so the old poet had imagined—“were laid away in some great trance… and could then record the words they spoke who kept watch by his bed.” Daniel Deronda, reading the lines again, felt the strange fitness of them to his own hour: the ages had been moving while he lay, not in a trance of sleep, but in a trance of ignorance; and now, at last, there was to be a watcher who would speak.

Sir Hugo had put a letter into his hand—quietly, as if passing a dish across a table whose fragrance must not be remarked on too eagerly.

TO MY SON, DANIEL DERONDA.

“My good friend and yours, Sir Hugo Mallinger, will have told you that I wish to see you. My health is shaken, and I desire there should be no time lost before I deliver to you what I have long withheld. Let nothing hinder you from being at the Albergo dell’ Italia in Genoa by the fourteenth of this month. Wait for me there. I am uncertain when I shall be able to make the journey from Spezia, where I shall be staying. That will depend on several things. Wait for me—the Princess Halm-Eberstein. Bring with you the diamond ring that Sir Hugo gave you. I shall like to see it again.—Your unknown mother,

LEONORA HALM-EBERSTEIN.”

The words were pale as water drawn from a deep well—no warmth, no savor, nothing that clung to the mind the way a remembered broth clings to the nostrils when one has been ill and starving. Yet the very bareness had weight. It left him no clue, and he could not press Sir Hugo for one: the baronet’s reticence had the air of a pledge, a sealed cask he must not broach before the owner came.

And with the discovery that all his lifelong conjectures had been mistaken, he found further guessing checked, as if the tongue, prepared for one familiar taste, were suddenly offered something unknown and was wiser to wait than to pretend a judgment. His imagination, swift as flame over dry twigs, ran along possibilities; but he would not let any one possibility become a favorite. He refused to season his mind with desire or repugnance in advance. Whatever the truth might be, he meant to meet it bravely, cleanly, without the cowardice of wishes.

In that mood he could not tell anyone the true reason for the absence he must announce in certain quarters. Least of all could he tell Mordecai; to say, “I am going to learn the truth about my birth,” would be to strike a match in tinder. Mordecai’s hope would flare into a dangerous brightness. So Deronda spoke of his journey as undertaken at Sir Hugo’s wish, and forced into his tone as much indifference as he could manage; he said he was uncertain how long he should be away, perhaps only a short time.

Mordecai’s eyes, after their first mournful searching, turned away as if to find some remedy in the ordinary.

“I will ask to have the child Jacob to stay with me,” he said, catching at that consolation with the eagerness of a man who clings to sound when sight is troubled.

“I will drive round and ask Mrs. Cohen to let him come,” Mirah answered—quick, affectionate, practical, as if she were already at the door with her bonnet strings in her fingers.

“The grandmother will deny you nothing,” Deronda said.

Then, because the strain between them all was too dear to be left without some play of light upon it, he added with a smile, “I’m glad you were a little wrong as well as I. You thought that old Mrs. Cohen would not bear to see Mirah.”

“I undervalued her heart,” Mordecai said, gently reproving himself. “She is capable of rejoicing that another’s plant blooms though her own be withered.”

“Oh, they are dear good people,” Mirah said; there was a small ripple of merriment in her smile, like a spoon’s faint clink against a cup—tenderness finding its way into sound. “I feel as if we all belonged to each other.”

Deronda, with the mischievousness that comes sometimes from love of one’s own claim, said, “What should you have felt if that Ezra had been your brother?” He was a little provoked that she had taken kindly, so quickly, to people who had caused him so much prospective annoyance on her account.

Mirah looked at him in mild surprise, then answered, “He is not a bad man—I think he would never forsake any one.”

But as she said it, the color rose suddenly and deeply in her cheeks. She glanced timidly at Mordecai, and turned away as if to busy her hands with some small task. The words had betrayed her: her father stood behind them like a shadow at midday. “If he should come and find us!”—that thought sometimes made the street daylight to Mirah as uncertain as a haunted forest where each corner might hide an apparition.

Deronda understood; he felt the blush as one feels a sudden hush in a room when a forbidden name is spoken. And how could he be slow to understand, now, when his own life was suddenly shot through with a like dread of painful conditions? That cold letter, announcing a mother and keeping her away, brought her nearer as a fact and remoter as an object of tenderness. For years he had nursed, without fully owning it even to himself, an image of a mother wronged by circumstance—a being whose life might have been the worse for lacking his care, whose dues of reverence or compassion might have gone unpaid. That image had mingled itself with his observation of women, coloring it as a warm sauce colors a plain dish. But now the handwriting itself, the actual words, had chilled his affections into a guarded neutrality. A veiled figure with enigmatic speech had thrust away the mother he had fashioned in longing.

So that when he set off to Genoa, the interest uppermost in his mind was scarcely his mother—rather Mordecai and Mirah, and all the new current of duty and feeling they had poured into him.

Sir Hugo, when they shook hands, had tried to cover seriousness with a flurry of talk, as if he were rattling the cutlery so no one should hear the heart beat.

“God bless you, Dan!” he said. “Whatever else changes for you, it can’t change my being the oldest friend you have known, and the one who has all along felt the most for you. I couldn’t have loved you better if you’d been my own—only I should have been better pleased with thinking of you always as the future master of the Abbey instead of my fine nephew; and then you would have seen it necessary for you to take a political line. However—things must be as they may.”

Deronda reached the Albergo dell’ Italia in Genoa on the appointed day, but there was no Princess Halm-Eberstein. The first night he lay listening to the hotel’s life—the corridor footsteps, the hollow closing of doors, the distant clatter of plates being carried away late from some Englishman’s supper—and in those sounds, as in a sort of acoustic map, he felt himself alone among strangers, suspended between what he had been and what he might become.

On the second day a letter came. Her arrival might be within a week, or it might be deferred a fortnight and more. Circumstances made it impossible to fix the journey more precisely. She entreated him to wait as patiently as he could.

Indefinite waiting—on a matter that would decide the very flavor of his being—was like being kept hungry with the promise of a meal whose hour kept shifting. He set himself, with that disciplined pride which was part of him, to seek amusement on philosophic grounds, as one might take a measured walk to quiet a fever. His former visit to Genoa had been cursory; now he wandered in the cooler hours, studying the city beyond the prescribed sights—turning into narrow lanes where laundry stirred like pale flags, and then emerging into open spaces where the sea-wind came salted and clean. He went often to the quay; and often he hired a boat so that, from the water, the superb curve of the harbor and the city’s rising terraces might enter him with their grandeur as bread enters a starving man.

Yet every sight, every subject, even the anticipated meeting with his mother, found itself drawn into a central union with Mordecai and Mirah. As his boat pushed and turned within view of the great harbor, he kept remembering another scene that had once stained that splendid quay—multitudinous Spanish Jews, centuries ago, driven destitute from their homes, allowed to land only for a brief rest, overspreading the stone with famine and plague. He saw them in imagination as if the air still held their voices: dying mothers with dying children at their breasts, fathers and sons staring at each other’s haggardness as if they had been thrust out of a hundred Hunger-towers into the pitiless midday. The thought returned in waves, and with it—inevitably—dreamy constructions about his own possible ancestry, weaving themselves into those historic memories which Mirah had already made vivid, and which Mordecai’s influence had rendered dominant.

He tried to seal his mind against those constructions. He had not yet fully admitted to himself that he wished the facts to verify Mordecai’s conviction. Wishing, on the question of parentage, seemed to him a mean thing—an anticipatory disowning. He repeated inwardly that he had no choice; his task was to accept what was. There had been resolved concealment; inference was untrustworthy; even his name might be false.

And yet—if Mordecai were wrong, if Daniel Deronda were bound by ties entirely aloof from the course of life his friend’s hope had marked out—Deronda would not say “I wish,” but he could not prevent himself from feeling where the sacrifice would fall. The thought pressed like a hand on his chest.

Across those importunate musings came another anxiety, one he did not banish but rather dwelt on with a mournfulness that seems, at times, the only atonement possible when one cannot help the suffering of another. He was anxious for Gwendolen.

It was not the exclusive passionate love some men and women are capable of; it was something distinct—neither friendship, nor merely benevolent regard. It was nearer than anything to the half-confessed phrase, “I should have loved her, if—”: the “if” covering both an inward history and an outward law. In Deronda’s case the “if” had reasons of both kinds. He had never been free, in his relations with Gwendolen, from a nervous consciousness that there was something to guard against—not only for her sake, but for his own: some precipitancy, some ruinous inroad of what is momentary upon what he had chosen as permanent, some spoiling of her trust. Now that trust haunted him like a sound. Her words—he must remain near her, must not forsake her—came back with the clearness and insistence of imagined noises in the night, arrows tipped with pity that pierce and will not be shaken out.

How could his feelings toward her ever be exactly like his feelings toward other women, even when there was one by whose side he desired to stand apart from them? Strangely, her figure entered into the pictures of his present and future; strangely, and now sadly, their lots had touched—hers narrowly personal, his possibly charged with far-reaching sensibilities and durable purposes, hardly more present to her than the reasons why men migrate are present to the birds that come as usual for crumbs and find them no more. Not that he imagined himself of supreme importance; but her insistence had laid hold of him. It was as though the sea had once lifted a swimmer and then withdrawn, leaving him with the memory of that support and the fear of the next undertow.

Day after day passed, and Italy itself seemed to carry in its air the knowledge that war had been declared against Austria, and that each day was a hurried step of crowded Time toward the world-changing battle of Sadowa. Genoa’s noons grew hotter; the outer roads converging on the city deepened with white dust. Oleanders in tubs along the wayside gardens looked more and more like fatigued holiday-makers, their flowers drooping as if thirst had made them human. Then evening would change her office—scattering abroad those whom midday had driven under shelter—and the paths would be sown with social sounds: the delicate tinkling of mule-bells, the whirr of thrummed strings, light footsteps and voices with pleasure in their haste. The encircling heights, crowned with forts and skirted with villas and gardens, seemed to wake from their long siesta and gaze down in renewed beauty. Strong color melted into moonlight, and the streets became a new spectacle—shadows lying still on cathedral steps or gliding across the façades of massive palaces. Later, as the moon descended, all sank into deep night and a silence that felt like a closed hand, until only the port lights of the great Lanterna shone in the blackness below, and the stars glimmered in the blackness above.

Deronda watched the revolving of the days as one watches a wonderful clock whose striking is made solemn by antique figures advancing and retreating in procession. His ear, always his most faithful sentinel, kept itself open for another signal—the knock, the message, the change. He began to sicken of occupation; all activity looked remote, as if he were a prisoner awaiting ransom. In letters to Mordecai and Hans he avoided writing about himself; yet he was sinking into that state of mind where every subject turns personal. The few books he had brought grew unreadable: the mind will not take ink when life is about to write its own sentence.

Many nights he watched from his open window, looking out on the double darkness—sea and sky, each faintly pierced. Sometimes he struggled under a heavy skepticism which made his lot, and the importance Mordecai gave it, seem no more lasting than a dream—mere shifts of mass and shadow in a universe indifferent to consciousness. Sometimes there came a reaction of feeling that made even disappointment, even sacrifice, seem like satisfied energy, and spread over his youth—whatever it was to be—the attraction of devoted service. Sometimes a sweet, irresistible hopefulness rose: that the best human possibilities might be granted him, personal love flowing in one current with larger duty. And sometimes, too—as no human creature escapes—there came rebellion against things in general because they were thus and not otherwise: a mood in which Gwendolen and her equivocal fate moved as busy images of what was amiss in the world, alongside the concealments that had been his own hardship, now taking the shape of an afflicting doubt about the mother who had announced herself coldly and still delayed.

At last she was come.

One morning, in his third week of waiting, there was a new kind of knock at the door—different in rhythm, carrying with it, like a footfall in a silent house, the certainty of meaning. A servant entered in Chasseurs livery and delivered, in French, the verbal message that the Princess Halm-Eberstein had arrived; she intended to rest during the day, but would be obliged if Monsieur would dine early, so as to be at liberty at seven, when she would be able to receive him.
