CHAPTER VIII. In which Pen is kept waiting at the Door, while the Reader is informed who little Laura was.

Once upon a time—yes, once upon a time—there lived a young gentleman of Cambridge University named Reverend Francis Bell, M.A., Fellow and Tutor, whose life was so full of learning, virtue, and romantic entanglements that it might have made a novel by itself—if only he’d had the sense to keep a diary instead of letting his heart do all the writing.

This gentleman, Mr. Bell, came down from the hallowed halls of academia to spend the long vacation in a quiet village where Miss Helen Thistlewood resided with her mother—the widow of a gallant lieutenant slain at Copenhagen. As cousin to the lady (and nephew to Mrs. Thistlewood), Francis Bell was naturally invited to lodge under her roof, which was modest but charmingly furnished with books, memories, and the faint scent of lavender.

He taught three or four pupils during the summer months, reading them Latin, Greek, and moral philosophy with such gravity that even the children began to suspect he was secretly preparing for sainthood. But beneath this austere exterior beat a heart once wildly given over to love—a love as pure, as passionate, and as ill-fated as any tale ever spun by a moonstruck poet.

You see, young Francis had fallen headlong into affection at seventeen, when he was still too awkward to walk without tripping over his own feet and too impulsive to consider consequences. It happened in the garden of Mr. Coacher, a parson who had tutored him in his youth. There, amid the peas and the peonies, Francis found himself on his knees before Miss Martha Coacher, helping her gather beans and offering her an eternal vow of devotion.

Miss Martha was not exactly a spring blossom; she was several years older than he, and had been wounded by love more times than one could count. The apothecary had jilted her. The dragoon officer had galloped off into the sunset with no intention of returning. Her heart, like a well-worn book, had been dog-eared by disappointment.

But here was Francis—earnest, gawky, sincere—and though she laughed at his youthful earnestness (“Law, Bell, I’m sure you are too young to think of such things!”), she did not refuse his offer. She simply tucked away the idea in her virgin bosom, like a secret letter meant for future reading.

And then—oh, the drama!—she sent him a lock of her hair, tied up in blue ribbon, as proof of her acceptance. Not that she hadn’t done this before. Three previous lovers had received similar tokens—only to vanish like smoke. But Francis was different. He was faithful. He studied hard. He won prizes. He became a Wrangler. He earned a fellowship. And through it all, he wrote letters to Martha that were tender, respectful, and occasionally so poetic they must have made her weep into her teacup.

But time passed. Francis turned twenty-six. Miss Martha turned thirty-four. The glow of their early romance dimmed. The pea-garden sunshine faded. And poor Francis realized, with horror, that he was engaged to a woman who was coarse, ill-tempered, ill-favoured, and frankly quite dreadful.

Yet he couldn’t break it. Not yet. His word was pledged—thousands of times, in letters that now read like confessions of a man slowly losing his mind. And so, instead of going to Bearleader’s Green (Mr. Coacher’s parish), he took refuge in his aunt’s village, where he met Helen Thistlewood—now grown into a beautiful young woman.

They walked together. They read aloud. She sang for him. He taught her everything. And soon, both knew what the other felt. He had shown her the portrait of his fiancée, blushing as he did so. He had let her read the cruel, demanding letters from the woman he was bound to marry. And still, she loved him.

It was a tender, tragic affair. But duty called. And so, when October arrived, Francis returned to college, leaving behind a broken-hearted girl and a shattered dream.

Back at Cambridge, he wrestled with his conscience. Should he confess? Should he give up his savings and beg for release? He hesitated. Time dragged on. Then came the discovery: Martha had learned of his new attachment.

She threatened him with public shame—“I will carry your letters into every court in the kingdom!”—and then, with theatrical finality, declared she would kill herself if he broke his promise.

So Francis did what any man of honour would do: he married the woman. He became vicar of a small living. He tried to be happy. But he was already old, worn out, and spiritually dead.

Years later, in Coventry Island, far from England, he fathered a daughter—Helen Laura. Her mother died shortly after childbirth, having whispered, “I was old, was I? I was inferior, was I? But I married you, Mr. Bell, and kept you from marrying her.” And then she expired.

Francis remarried—a colonial lady, kinder, gentler—but fate mocked him again. She died in childbirth. And so, alone and broken, he sent his daughter back to England, to be raised by Helen Pendennis and her husband, with a parting prayer that they would care for her.

The child arrived from Bristol, dressed in black, weeping bitterly as her nurse departed. But under Helen’s loving care, she dried her tears. Around her neck hung a locket—containing a lock of hair from the woman who had once loved Francis Bell and lost him forever.

Her name was Helen Laura. But John Pendennis, her adoptive father, insisted she be called Laura—not Helen. He disapproved of her origin, her mother’s past, and her very existence. She was afraid of him until the day he died.

Only then did Helen dare to show her true affection for the girl. And thus, Laura Bell became Mrs. Pendennis’s daughter—though neither her husband nor the Major liked her much. She wasn’t noble. She didn’t come from a great family. She had only two thousand pounds. And worst of all—she reminded them of painful truths better left buried.

Now, at last, let us return to our hero—Arthur Pendennis—who has been standing outside the door, bracing himself for battle.

He had rehearsed the scene in his mind: dramatic entrance, stern face, noble bearing. He would confront the dreaded uncle, stand tall, and declare his love with the dignity of a Pendennis.

With a flourish, he flung open the door and marched in—lance couched, plumes flying, ready for war.

The Major, however, was not impressed. He merely grinned at the boy’s ridiculous pomposity. He had anticipated this moment. He had seen it coming.

Instead of thundering threats, he smiled, held out his hands, and said cheerfully, “Well, Pen, my boy, tell us all about it.”

Helen was delighted. Pen was devastated. His grand entrance had been reduced to absurdity. He blushed. He stammered. “I—I didn’t know you were here,” he mumbled. “Is—is town very full?”

The Major nearly burst out laughing. So did Mrs. Pendennis. And so, feeling helpless, she kissed him.

Pen, touched by her tenderness, almost cried.

“What a couple of fools,” thought the Major. “If I hadn’t come down, she’d have driven over in state to bless the young lady’s family.”

“Come, come,” he said, still grinning, “let’s have as little sentiment as possible. Now, Pen, tell us the whole story.”

Pen regained his composure. “The story is,” he said, “as I’ve written to you before. I’ve met a most beautiful and virtuous lady—of high birth, though in reduced circumstances. I’ve found the woman in whom I know my happiness lies. I cannot think of any other. I am aware of the age difference, and other difficulties—but my affection is so strong I believe we can overcome them. She has consented to marry me. I’ve promised her my heart and my fortune.”

“How much is that?” asked the Major. “Has anyone left you money? I don’t know that you’re worth a shilling.”

“You know what I have is hers,” cried Mrs. Pendennis.

“Good heavens, madam, hold your tongue!” the Major thought—but wisely refrained.

“No doubt,” he said calmly, “you would sacrifice anything for him. But really, Pen, it’s *your* fortune you’re offering. And you’re eighteen. You haven’t consulted your mother, have you?”

“I know my mother will give me anything,” Pen said, looking uneasy.

“Yes, but reason exists,” replied the Major. “When you hand over the house and transfer the bank account to Miss Costigan—don’t you think you should ask your sister-in-law’s opinion? I speak as a man of the world to another. Would it not have been more generous to wait? To ask her leave?”

Pen lowered his head. He began to see that his romantic gesture—so noble in his imagination—was actually a selfish, reckless act.

“I did it in a moment of passion,” he confessed. “I didn’t know what I was doing. But now it’s said. I stand by it. I’ll die rather than take it back. And I don’t want to burden my mother. I’ll work for myself. I’ll go on the stage. She says I’d do well there.”

“But will she take you on those terms?” the Major interjected. “I don’t say Miss Costigan isn’t noble. But don’t you suppose your position—your name, your prospects—makes a difference in how she views your attentions?”

“I’ll die rather than break my word,” Pen repeated, clenching his fists.

“Who asks you to?” said the Major. “No gentleman breaks his word. But you can wait. You owe something to your mother. Something to your family. Something to me, as your father’s representative.”

“Oh, of course,” Pen said, relieved.

“Well, then,” said the Major, “give us another promise. That you won’t run off to Scotland. No clandestine marriages.”

“That would be a falsehood,” said Helen. “Pen never lies to his mother.”

Pen’s eyes filled with shame. Hadn’t he lied to her? All along?

He gave his uncle his hand. “No, sir,” he said, “on my word of honour, I will never marry without my mother’s consent.”

And with a bright look at Helen, he left the room.

“He’s an angel,” exclaimed Mrs. Pendennis.

“He comes of good stock,” said the Major, kissing her glove. “On both sides.”

He was pleased with himself. He had handled it with finesse. And so, after dinner, he continued his gentle diplomacy over wine.

“You must bear in mind,” he said, “that you’re throwing yourself away. Your mother may submit. But she won’t be happy. You’re choosing a woman from a country theatre over one of the finest ladies in England. And I’ve often thought—between ourselves—that my sister might have wanted to marry you to Flora, Laura—what’s-her-name? I always intended to prevent it. The child has only two thousand pounds. My sister struggles to maintain her household. I had higher hopes for you. With your name, your talents, and my connections, I could have placed you in a remarkable position. A general? A diplomat? A statesman? But now—marrying a woman of thirty from a barn? Public service is closed. Society is closed. The bar is your only hope—and even then, you’ll be marrying out of a kitchen. Or you may come live here—down here, mon Dieu!—for the rest of your life. Where your mother will welcome your wife with kindness. Where no one will visit you. Where I, for one, shall be shy of visiting you myself. Because I like to live with gentlemen. And you’ll end up drinking rum-and-water with farmers and drunkards. It’s no affair of mine. I’m not angry. But I pity you. And I pity your mother.”

He paused. “Pass the claret. It’s some I sent to your father. I bought it at Lord Levant’s sale.”

Then, with a smug nod, he added: “Of course, having made your promise, you’ll do what’s right. But promise us this: no secrets. No trips. Keep studying. Write to her—how often?”

Pen blushed. “Why… yes. I’ve written.”

And so the conversation ended—not with triumph, but with a quiet understanding that love, like history, is often written in blood, tears, and regret.
