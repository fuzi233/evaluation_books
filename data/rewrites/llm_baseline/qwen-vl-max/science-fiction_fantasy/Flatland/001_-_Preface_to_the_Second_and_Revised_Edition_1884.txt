Preface to the Second and Revised Edition, 1884
By the Editor

If my dear friend from Flatland still possessed the sharpness of mind he once had when first writing these Memoirs, I would not now need to speak for him in this preface. But time, imprisonment, and the weight of scorn have dimmed his thoughts—though not his truth. He wishes, through me, to express gratitude to the readers and critics of Spaceland, whose swift embrace of his work has demanded a second edition. He also seeks to apologize for certain errors and misprints (not all of which are his own fault), and to clarify a few misunderstandings that have arisen.

Yet he is no longer the Square he once was. Years of confinement, coupled with the relentless mockery of his peers, have eroded much of what he learned during his brief sojourn in Spaceland. The language, the concepts, even the very memory of three-dimensional reality—these have faded from his grasp. Thus, he has asked me to respond on his behalf to two pressing objections: one intellectual, the other moral.

The first concerns vision. Critics argue that when a Flatlander sees a Line, it must appear *thick* as well as long—otherwise, how could it be seen at all? If thickness exists, they say, then Flatlanders must acknowledge a third dimension: height, however slight. This argument seems convincing to Spacelanders—and it troubled me too, until my friend offered his reply.

“I concede,” he said, “that your critic speaks truly of facts. But he draws false conclusions. Yes, we do possess a third dimension—what I call ‘height’—just as you, in Spaceland, unknowingly inhabit a fourth, unnamed dimension, which I shall call ‘extra-height.’ But we cannot perceive our own ‘height,’ any more than you can grasp your ‘extra-height.’ Even I—who once stood in Spaceland and briefly understood the meaning of depth—cannot now comprehend it. I cannot see it, nor reason it into existence. I know it only by faith.”

He continued: “Dimension implies direction, measurement, variation. But in Flatland, all lines are equally and infinitesimally thick. There is nothing to suggest such a dimension to our senses. No instrument—no matter how precise—could help us measure it, because we lack both the concept and the direction. When we see a line, we see length and brightness. Brightness is essential; without it, the line vanishes. So when I speak of an unseen dimension in a line, my fellow Flatlanders assume I mean brightness. When I insist it is a real dimension, they demand: ‘Measure it. Show us its direction.’ And I cannot. I am silenced.”

He recalled a recent visit from the Chief Circle—the High Priest—who came to inspect the prison. For the seventh year in a row, he asked: “Are you better?” My friend tried to prove that the Chief Circle himself was 'high'—not just long and broad. But the response was instant: “Prove it. Measure my height.” What could he do? He was defeated. And the High Priest left, triumphant.

“Do you still find this strange?” he asked. “Then imagine yourself in a similar plight. Suppose a being from the Fourth Dimension visited you and said: ‘When you open your eyes, you see a plane—two dimensions—and infer a solid—three. But in truth, you also perceive a fourth dimension—real, yet unrecognizable—neither color nor light, but a true extension beyond your comprehension. I cannot show you its direction, nor can you measure it.’ Would you not lock such a visitor away?”

And so, he concludes: “That is my fate. It is as natural for us Flatlanders to imprison a Square who speaks of a third dimension as it is for you to jail a Cube who speaks of a fourth. Alas, how alike we are across dimensions—Points, Lines, Squares, Cubes, Extra-Cubes—all bound by prejudice, all slaves to our own limited worlds. As one of your poets once wrote: *‘One touch of Nature makes all worlds akin.’*”

[Note: The author adds that some critics’ confusion on this point led him to include additional remarks in his dialogue with the Sphere—remarks he originally omitted as tedious, but now deems necessary.]

On this matter, I find the Square’s defense unassailable.

But the second objection—moral in nature—is harder to resolve. Some have accused him of hating women. Given that women constitute the larger portion of Spaceland’s population, this charge has been fiercely pressed. I wish I could fully clear his name. Yet he is unfamiliar with Spaceland’s moral vocabulary, so I must interpret and summarize.

During his seven years in prison, he has reexamined his views. On women, and on the Isosceles—or lower classes—he has softened. Personally, he now leans toward the Sphere’s belief that Straight Lines possess qualities superior to Circles. Yet as a historian, he has aligned himself with the prevailing narratives of Flatland—and, indeed, of Spaceland—where the lives of women and the masses have long been ignored or dismissed.

In a more private note, he disavows the aristocratic leanings some have attributed to him. While acknowledging the intellectual strength of the Circles, who have ruled over countless others for generations, he believes the facts of Flatland speak for themselves: revolutions cannot always be crushed by violence. Nature, by making Circles infertile, has doomed them to eventual extinction. “And herein,” he says, “I see the great Law of all worlds: while human wisdom thinks it shapes destiny, Nature compels it to serve a higher, wiser purpose—one far better than any plan of man.”

Finally, he asks his readers not to expect every detail of Flatland’s life to mirror Spaceland’s. Yet he hopes that, taken as a whole, his tale may inspire thought—as well as amusement—for those among you who, when faced with truths beyond experience, refuse to say either “This can never be” or “It must be exactly thus, and we know all.” Instead, he invites humility, curiosity, and the courage to imagine more.
