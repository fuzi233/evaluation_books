It was nearly four in the afternoon when Eli returned to his shop, finding the place empty save for the hum of the street outside. His eldest and youngest sons had ventured forth, leaving the household in a state of suspended animation. Catherine and her frail, crippled daughter, Little Lily, had already departed down the lane, their hearts racing with the hope of spotting Gerard returning from the city. In the quiet sitting-room, only Giles remained, a figure of grotesque disproportion against the backdrop of a room that spoke volumes of Dutch ingenuity.

The people of Holland were renowned for their inventions—printing, oil-painting, banking, and above all, cleanliness. While the English gentry of the era still trod upon floors of stale rushes, a foul receptacle of bones, discarded food, and vermin, the hosier's home in Tergou gleamed with immaculate tile. These glazed surfaces were so pristine one might have eaten directly upon them. A single large window dominated the wall, its central stone cross standing in bold relief, a focal point for the family's daily devotions. The panes were small, diamond-shaped, and sealed with lead, a rustic charm that persisted in the countryside to this day.

The furniture was sparse and rough, save for a towering oak armchair. Its back rose at a right angle, so high that a seated person's head hovered two feet below the top, carved with intricate detail. Beside it stood a copper basin filled with holy water and a small besom for sprinkling, alongside a massive oak table. Perched upon the table's edge was a dwarf named Sampsonet, his teeth gripping the wood, his eyes blazing, his claws raised like those of a predatory bird. Nature, it seemed, had crafted Giles with the same care she bestowed upon the dwarf's head and torso, but had left the rest to chance. The result was a human wedge, an inverted cone where the upper body outweighed the lower by a factor of three. As Horace might have quipped, the amphora began well, yet the wheel turned until the jar spilled.

This peculiar imbalance, however, granted Giles a supernatural agility. His brawny arms, unburdened by heavy legs, allowed him to scale vertical poles with squirrel-like ease or hang from branches by a single hand. He possessed a peculiar obsession with the dinner table, often gripping it with both hands before seizing it with his teeth to swing in a frenzy of delight.

"Stop, Giles!" cried Little Kate, interrupting his acrobatics. She stood frozen, her heart aligned with the furniture rather than the gymnast. "Mother is coming! You are denting the table!"

"Go tell her, little tale-bearer," Giles snarled, retaking his perch. "You are the one who brings mischief."

"Am I?" Kate replied calmly, her tone dry. "That is news to me."

"The biggest troublemaker in Tergou," Giles grumbled, fastening his grip tighter.

"Oh, indeed!" she said drily.

With this unexpected barb launched, and Giles seemingly unscathed, Kate sat down quietly and began to weep. Her mother entered moments later, and Giles dove beneath the table, glaring from the shadows.

"What is the matter now?" the woman asked sharply. Turning her experienced gaze from Kate to the hidden figure, she noted his posture and a look of sheepishness, hinting at a cuffing of ears.

"Nay, mother," the girl said softly. "It was but a foolish word Giles spoke. I did not notice it at another time; I was weary and worried for Gerard."

"Let no one be in care for me," a faint voice called from the doorway.

Gerard stumbled in, pale, dusty, and utterly spent. Amidst cries of delight and rising anxiety, he collapsed into the nearest chair, his strength finally giving way. The long journey from Rotterdam, searching for Margaret, had drained him completely. Yet, the elastic vitality of youth soon surged back, and he became the center of an eager circle.

First, they demanded to hear of the prizes. Gerard recounted how he had been permitted to view the competitors' works in a vast hall before the judges' decision.

"Oh, mother! Oh, Kate!" he exclaimed, his eyes shining. "When I saw the goldsmiths' creations, I felt as though I would fall to the floor. I thought no smith on earth possessed so much gold, silver, and jewels, nor such mastery of design. Truly, all arts are divine."

To please the women, he described the reliquaries, chalices, crosses, and ecclesiastical wonders, followed by goblets, chains, brooches, and clocks, causing their mouths to water.

"But, Kate," he continued, his voice softening, "when I came to the illuminated manuscripts from Ghent and Bruges, my heart sank. My own work seemed dirt by comparison. For the first minute, I nearly wept; but I prayed for a better spirit, and soon I was able to admire them, thanking God for those lovely works and the skilled craftsmen who created them. The colored pages were so beautiful I forgot the black and white entirely."

"Then came the writing prizes," Gerard said, leaning forward. "Whose name do you think was called first?"

"Yours," Kate answered instantly.

The others laughed her to scorn.

"You may well laugh," Gerard said, a flush rising on his cheeks. "Yet, Gerard Eliassoen of Tergou was the name the herald shouted. I stood stunned; they pushed me forward. Everything swam before my eyes. I found myself kneeling on a cushion at the feet of the Duke. He spoke to me, but I was so fluttered I could not answer. Then he reached to his side—not to draw a glaive and sever my dull head—but to give me a gold medal. And here it is."

A yell erupted, followed by a scramble.

"And then he gave me fifteen great bright golden angels," Gerard declared, holding them up. "I had seen one before, but never handled one. Here they are."

"Oh, Gerard! Oh, Gerard!" the family cried out.

"There is one for you, our eldest; one for you, Sybrandt; and one for you, Little Mischief," Gerard distributed, his voice warm. "And two for thee, Little Lily, because God has afflicted thee. One for myself, to buy colors and vellum; and nine for her who nursed us all and risked the two crowns upon poor Gerard's hand."

The gold revealed their true natures. Cornelis and Sybrandt clutched their coins with glares of greed and envy directed at Kate, who held two pieces. Giles seized his medal, rolling it across the floor and gamboling after it. Kate set down her crutches, sat, and extended her small arms to Gerard in a gesture of heavenly love and tenderness. The mother, initially numbed by the shower of gold falling on her apron, suddenly cried out, "Leave kissing him, Kate; he is my son, not yours. Ah, Gerard! My boy! I have not loved you as you deserved."

Gerard threw himself on his knees beside her, and she flung her arms around him, weeping for joy and pride upon his neck.

"Good lad! Good lad!" cried the hosier, moved to emotion. "I must go and tell the neighbors. Lend me the medal, Gerard; I will show it to my good friend Peter Buyskens. He is always regaling me with tales of how his son Jorian won a tin mug for shooting at the butts."

"Ay, do, my man," Gerard encouraged. "Show Peter Buyskens one of the angels. Tell him there are fourteen more where that came from. Mind you bring it back!"

"Wait a moment, father," Gerard said, flushing with the joy he had caused. "There is better news behind."

"Better than this?"

Gerard then recounted his audience with the Countess, and the house rang with renewed joy.

"Now, God bless the good lady, and bless Dame Van Eyck!" the mother exclaimed. "A benefice? Our son! My cares are at an end. Eli, my good friend and master, now we two can die happy whenever our time comes. This dear boy will take our place, and none of these loved ones will lack a home or a friend."

From that hour, Gerard was regarded as the pillar of the family. He was a son apart, elevated above the rest. He was always in the right, and nothing was too good for him. Cornelis and Sybrandt grew increasingly jealous, longing for the day Gerard would depart for his benefice, freeing the family's resources for themselves. With these thoughts, Gerard cooperated, though the wound love had inflicted upon him throbbed with a duller, heavier pain. His success and the adoration of his parents made him view himself with greater height, resenting Margaret's ingratitude and discourtesy with growing spirit.

Yet, she held the power to cool his ardor toward women. Now, for every reason, he wished to be ordained a priest as soon as he could pass the intermediate orders. He knew the Vulgate better than most clergy and studied the rubrics and dogmas of the Church with his monastic friends. When the bishop first visited the area, Gerard applied to be admitted as an "exorcist," the third step in holy orders. The bishop questioned him briefly and ordained him immediately. Gerard knelt, offered a short prayer, and received a small manuscript filled with exorcisms.

"Take this, Gerard," the bishop said solemnly. "Have power to lay hands on the possessed, whether baptized or catechumens!"

He took the book reverently and returned home, invested by the Church with the power to cast out demons.

On his return from the church, he was met by Little Kate on her crutches.

"Oh, Gerard! Who, do you think, has sent to our house seeking you? The burgomaster himself."

"Ghysbrecht Van Swieten! What would he with me?"

"Nay, Gerard, I know not. But he seems urgent to see you. You are to go to his house on the instant."

"Well, he is the burgomaster; I will go. But it does not please me. Kate, I have seen him cast such a look upon me as no friend casts. No matter; such looks forewarn the wise. To be sure, he knows."

"Knows what, Gerard?"

"Nothing."

"Nothing?"

"Kate, I'll go."
