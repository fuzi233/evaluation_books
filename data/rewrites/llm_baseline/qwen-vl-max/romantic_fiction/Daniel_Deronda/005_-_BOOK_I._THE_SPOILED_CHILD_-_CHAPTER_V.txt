CHAPTER V. “Her wit Values itself so highly, that to her All matter else seems weak.” —_Much Ado About Nothing._

Gwendolen’s arrival in the neighborhood had quickly lived up to her uncle’s expectations. From Brackenshaw Castle to the Firs at Wanchester—where Mr. Quallon, the banker, maintained a grand and generous household—she was greeted with open admiration. Even those ladies who didn’t quite warm to her found comfort in having such a striking young woman among their guests. After all, hostesses who entertain often must assemble their parties like ministers forming cabinets: not always by personal affection, but by necessity and balance. And since Gwendolen came with Mrs. Davilow, whose quiet grace made an ideal chaperone, and Mr. Gascoigne, whose charm and reputation were universally welcome, no one hesitated to invite her.

One of her earliest invitations came from Quetcham Hall—a house where she was not entirely beloved, yet still welcomed. The dinner party there served as something of a formal introduction to the local society. With thirty guests carefully chosen across generations, few prominent families could be left out. As Gwendolen moved through the long succession of rooms—each glowing with light and adorned with flowers—her presence stood out. Dressed in white, she seemed to float through the halls, first a slender silhouette, then gradually emerging into full view under the soft illumination. It was a scene she’d never experienced before, and she felt a quiet triumph in it: anyone seeing her for the first time might have assumed she belonged to this world of elegance and ceremony. Her cousin Anna, more accustomed to such settings, looked almost flustered, like a timid creature suddenly placed under bright lights.

“Who is that with Gascoigne?” asked the archdeacon, pausing mid-discussion on military tactics—an unusual interruption from a man of the cloth.

On the other side of the room, his son—already known for his scholarly emendations of Greek texts—muttered, “By George! Who’s that girl with the astonishingly well-set head and jolly figure?”

To those with kinder eyes, however, it was frustrating to watch how Gwendolen overshadowed others. Miss Lawe, daughter of Lady Lawe and generally considered beautiful, suddenly appeared broad and dull beside her. Miss Arrowpoint, also in white, looked instantly like a poorly framed photograph—one where only the skirt seemed worth charging extra for.

Miss Arrowpoint was well-liked for her gentle demeanor and modest way of bearing her wealth, which softened the eccentricities of her mother. Yet now, standing next to Gwendolen, she seemed diminished. There was an awkwardness in the contrast: Gwendolen, though not classically beautiful upon closer inspection, carried herself with a style that commanded attention—and then faded just as quickly once the first impression wore off.

Later, Mrs. Arrowpoint confided to Mrs. Vulcany, “She’s not really so handsome when you look closely. It’s a certain manner she has—striking at first, but afterward… less agreeable.”

Indeed, Gwendolen had unintentionally offended her hostess, though not from malice. Mrs. Arrowpoint, though not vindictive, had sensitivities. She was a woman shaped by several contradictions that the townsfolk liked to link together: once a city heiress from a business empire (wet or dry, they didn’t care), she carried a squat frame, a sharp, parrot-like voice, and a towering hairstyle that seemed designed to defy gravity. These traits made her seem ridiculous to many, and so it was natural, they thought, that she should dabble in literature.

But such assumptions were flawed. Wealthy daughters weren’t always ungraceful; pretty women could have harsh voices; and literary ambition knew no bounds of body type. Still, Gwendolen, who had a keen eye for absurdity in others but was generally kind to those who made life pleasant for her, meant to win Mrs. Arrowpoint over. She believed that showing interest in someone’s passions would earn goodwill.

Yet self-assurance often misjudges others. Like the rich speaking softly to the poor, or the young raising their voices to elders, assuming them deaf or dim-witted, Gwendolen unconsciously assumed that because Mrs. Arrowpoint was comical, she must also lack depth. So she played her part without realizing every nuance was being noted.

“You’re fond of books, music, riding, and archery, I hear,” Mrs. Arrowpoint said, approaching her after dinner for a private moment. “Catherine will be delighted to have such a sympathetic neighbor.”

The words were polite, delivered in a low, melodic tone—but with a sudden twang that gave Gwendolen a fleeting sense of superiority as she replied gracefully, “It’s I who am fortunate. Miss Arrowpoint will teach me what real music is. I shall be entirely a learner. I’ve heard she’s a thorough musician.”

“Catherine has had every advantage,” Mrs. Arrowpoint continued. “We have Herr Klesmer here now—perhaps you know his work. I’ll introduce you. You sing, I believe. Catherine plays three instruments, but doesn’t sing. We hope you’ll let us hear you. I understand you’re accomplished.”

“Oh, no!” Gwendolen laughed. “‘Die Kraft ist schwach, allein die Lust ist gross,’ as Mephistopheles says.”

Ah, you study Goethe,” Mrs. Arrowpoint smiled. “Young ladies are so advanced these days. I suppose you’ve read everything.”

“No, truly,” Gwendolen said, earnestly. “I’d love to know what to read. I’ve been looking through the library at Offendene, but it’s all musty—leaves stick together. I wish I could write books to amuse myself, like you do! How wonderful it must be to create your own stories instead of reading others’—home-made books must be so delightful.”

For a moment, Mrs. Arrowpoint’s gaze sharpened—but then Gwendolen added, “I’d give anything to write a book!”

“And why shouldn’t you?” Mrs. Arrowpoint encouraged. “Just begin as I did. Pen, ink, paper—everyone has them. I’ll send you all my writings.”

“Thank you. I’d love to read them. Knowing authors must give you a special understanding of their work—you’d know which parts were funny and which serious. I often laugh in the wrong place.”

Gwendolen paused, sensing danger, and quickly added, “In Shakespeare, of course—great writers we can never see. But I always want to know more than the books tell.”

“If you’re interested in any of my subjects, I’ll lend you manuscripts,” Mrs. Arrowpoint offered—while Gwendolen felt herself in the position of the young lady who claimed to adore potted sprats.

“These are things I may publish someday,” she went on. “Friends have urged me. One doesn’t like to be stubborn. My Tasso, for example—I could have doubled its size.”

“I adore Tasso,” Gwendolen declared.

“Well, you shall have all my papers. So many have written about him—but they’re all wrong. About his madness, his feelings for Leonora, the real reason he was imprisoned, Leonora’s character—she was cold-hearted, I say. Otherwise, she’d have married him despite her brother. I differ from everyone.”

“How fascinating!” Gwendolen said. “I love differing from everyone. I think it’s stupid to agree. That’s the worst thing about writing opinions—you make people agree with you.”

Mrs. Arrowpoint’s suspicion flickered again. But Gwendolen looked utterly innocent, continuing with a docile air: “I know nothing of Tasso except _Gerusalemme Liberata_, which we memorized at school.”

“Ah, his life is more interesting than his poetry,” Mrs. Arrowpoint said. “I’ve reconstructed the early part as a romance. His father Bernardo—there’s much that must be true.”

“Imagination is often truer than fact,” Gwendolen declared decisively—though she couldn’t have explained the words if asked.

“I’d love to learn about Tasso—and especially his madness. I suppose poets are always a little mad.”

“To be sure—‘the poet’s eye in a fine frenzy rolling.’ Someone said of Marlowe, ‘For that fine madness still he did maintain, Which always should possess the poet’s brain.’”

“But it wasn’t always noticed, was it?” Gwendolen asked innocently. “Some rolled their eyes in private. Mad people are often very clever.”

A shadow crossed Mrs. Arrowpoint’s face—but the men entered, halting any further tension.

“Ah, here comes Herr Klesmer,” she said, rising, and soon introduced him to Gwendolen.

Herr Klesmer was a striking figure—Germanic, Slavic, Semitic all at once—grand features, flowing brown hair, dark eyes behind spectacles. His English was fluent, nearly flawless, but now softened by a faint silliness, perhaps born of desire to please a beauty. Music began soon after.

Miss Arrowpoint and Herr Klesmer performed a four-handed piece on two pianos—long enough to test patience, and convincing enough to show Miss Arrowpoint’s mastery. Gwendolen, though aware of her own limitations, remained undeterred. Everyone wanted to hear her sing—especially Mr. Arrowpoint, a gentleman of impeccable manners and cigars, who led her to the piano with ease.

Herr Klesmer closed the instrument, smiled, and stepped back slightly to observe.

Gwendolen sang without hesitation. Her voice was a strong soprano—some said like Jenny Lind’s—good ear, steady pitch. She sang a Belini aria she knew well. The audience murmured approval. Mr. Arrowpoint called it “charming”—a sentiment echoed around the room.

But Herr Klesmer stood frozen—like a statue wearing spectacles. Not a word.

She was urged to sing again. She hesitated, then approached him, smiling, “It would be cruel to a great musician. You can’t possibly enjoy amateur singing.”

“No, truly,” Herr Klesmer said suddenly, his voice clipped, Germanic, abrupt—unlike before. “That makes nothing. It is always acceptable to see you sing.”

An unexpected assertion of superiority—especially in the wake of Teutonic influence. Gwendolen flushed, but kept her composure.

Miss Arrowpoint, nearby and observant, stepped in kindly. “Imagine what I endure with this professor! He barely tolerates our English music. We suffer his severity—but use it to learn what’s worst about ourselves. It’s comforting to know that. And when everyone else admires, one can bear it.”

“I’d be grateful if he told me the worst,” Gwendolen said, recovering. “I dare say I’ve been ill taught—and have no talent—only a liking for music.”

“That’s true,” Herr Klesmer said quietly. “You haven’t been well taught. But you’re not without gifts. You sing in tune. You have a fair voice. But you produce notes poorly. And the music you sing is beneath you. It’s a form of melody expressing a childish culture—dawdling, cloying, repetitive. It lacks passion, conflict, depth. No cries of deep emotion. No sense of the universal. It shrinks men as they listen. Sing something larger. Then I shall see.”

“Oh, not now—later,” Gwendolen said, heart sinking. For a woman aiming to lead, this first encounter was unsettling.

But Miss Arrowpoint helped, saying, “Yes, later. I always need half an hour to gather courage after criticism from Herr Klesmer. Let’s ask him to play now—he must show us what good music sounds like.”

Herr Klesmer obliged with a fantasia of his own—_Freudvoll, Leidvoll, Gedankenvoll_—a vast exploration of subtle melodies. His fingers danced with imperious magic, sending shivers through keys and hammers, coaxing trembling, lingering tones from the piano. Gwendolen, though wounded, felt the power. Her mortification turned into excitement, lifting her above her own performance. Her eyes brightened, cheeks flushed, tongue ready for mischief.

“I wish you’d sing again, Miss Harleth,” said young Clintock, the archdeacon’s son, who had escorted her to dinner. “That’s the kind of music for me. I can’t follow this high-flown playing. It’s like a jar of leeches—no beginning, no end. I could listen to your singing all day.”

“Yes, let’s have another song,” Mrs. Arrowpoint added politely. “Something lighter.”

“That must be because you’re in a childish state of culture,” Gwendolen said, ignoring her, smiling up at Clintock. “I’ve just learned that. I’ve been taught how bad my taste is—and I’m feeling growing pains. They’re never pleasant.”

Mrs. Arrowpoint didn’t miss the rudeness—but merely said, “Well, we won’t press anything disagreeable.”

As conversation burst forth, she sat back, relieved to be spared further duty.

“I’m glad you like this neighborhood,” Clintock said, pleased to stand near her.

“Exceedingly. There seems to be a little of everything—and not much of anything.”

“That’s rather ambiguous praise.”

“Not with me. I like a little of everything. A little absurdity, for instance, is amusing. I’m thankful for a few odd people—but too many would be a bore.”

Mrs. Arrowpoint, listening, sensed a new edge in Gwendolen’s voice—and renewed doubt about her interest in Tasso’s madness.

“I think there should be more croquet,” Clintock said. “I’m usually away, but if I were here, I’d start a croquet club. You’re one of the archers, I think. But croquet is the game of the future. It needs writing up. One of our best men wrote a poem on it—four cantos—as good as Pope. I want him to publish it. You’ve never read anything better.”

“I’ll study croquet tomorrow. I’ll take it up instead of singing.”

“No, no—don’t give up singing. But do try croquet. I’ll send you Jenning’s poem if you like. I have a manuscript copy.”

“Is he a great friend of yours?”

“Well, rather.”

“Oh—if he’s only *rather*, I think I’ll decline. Or if you send it, will you promise not to quiz me on it? Because it’s not so easy to judge a poem without reading it—like judging a sermon without hearing it.”

Mrs. Arrowpoint thought firmly: “This girl is double-tongued and satirical. I shall be on my guard.”

Still, Gwendolen continued to receive polite attentions at Quetcham—not just because hospitality depends on more than personal preference, but because the brief piano scene had stirred a quiet sympathy in Miss Arrowpoint. Though her mother was otherwise occupied, Miss Arrowpoint managed the social arrangements—and now, gently, began to include Gwendolen in their circle.
