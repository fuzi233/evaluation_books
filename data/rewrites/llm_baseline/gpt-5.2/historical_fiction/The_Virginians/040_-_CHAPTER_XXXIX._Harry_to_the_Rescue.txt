On Saturday morning, the five-and-twentieth of August, 1756, Mr. Harry Esmond Warrington, with the clean, eager hand of youth and the confident flourish of a gentleman who had tasted both hazard and speed, set down his news to Lord March from Tunbridge Wells.

He wrote of wagers won, and of new horses that had “kep a-going at a capital rate,” as if the very muscle of the beasts and the clink of harness were part of his triumph. He had driven them himself—postilion beside to point the way—while his black servant sat within the carriage with Mrs. Betty, steadying her against the jolts. He described Blackheath and the two horsemen who rode up to peer at them, then wheeled off again as though discouraged by the set of their faces. The wheels had never slackened; the air had cut; the road had hummed under the spokes; he was at Bromley within two minutes of his hour, and into Tunbridge by forty-five minutes after eleven, where he “transacted” what must be transacted. The whole note had the briskness of a man who thinks the world a course and himself the whip.

It was by that same hurry of wheels—by that same hard-ridden haste—that Lady Maria Esmond was able, before the day was well in, to pass from a house of restraint into liberty.

For she had sent Mrs. Betty away to London as an emissary, and did not look to see her again so quickly; and she and the chaplain, Mr. Sampson, had sat up late, playing their little game of cards to hold their spirits together. The bailiff’s wife—who knew what it is to put comfort into a poor room—had provided them a small refection: cold meat laid out with careful parsimony, a heel of bread, a bit of cheese that smelt of the dairy and the cellar, and a mug of ale whose yeasty sweetness made a kind of courage. The house was quiet in the way country houses are at midnight, when even the boards seem to listen. Outside, the night had its own music: a distant dog that barked once and was ashamed; the long hush between gusts; the soft brush of leaves. It was a world made of breathing.

Then came a sound that did not belong to that order: the rapid whirling of wheels, a hard, eager thunder on the road, drawing nearer as if the night itself were being driven back. The lady laid her trumps down. Her heart beat with a strange violence, as though it recognized, before her mind dared, what the ears had already translated.

Whirr came the wheels; the carriage stopped at the very door. A parley at the gate—voices low, metal clinking, a latch complaining—then steps. Mrs. Betty appeared, her face radiant with joy though her eyes were full of tears, the way a saucepan will shine even while the contents boil over. And behind her—tall, young, flushed with the ride, smelling of road-dust and horse-sweat and the sharp clean leather of reins—entered Harry.

The chaplain, forgetting his clerical gravity, slapped his cards down with a huzzay. Lady Maria turned as white as linen, rose, tottered a pace, and with a shriek that was half laughter and half a sob, flung herself into her cousin’s arms.

If he kissed her a hundred times, or a thousand, or ran through all Catullus’s reckoning and still found himself in debt, no honest person need cry out. She clung to him as though the world had tried to unhand her and he alone was firm. Her tears came hot and plentiful upon his shoulder. In that homely room, with its poor supper and its stale candle-smell, she looked handsomer than she had looked in any fine drawing-room; for there is a beauty that is not bought with powder and silk, but with relief.

“You are both free to go home,” were Harry’s first words, brisk as a key turning.

“Get my lady’s hat and cardinal, Betty. And, Chaplain, we’ll smoke a pipe together at our lodgings. It will refresh me after my ride.”

The chaplain, whose feelings were a tinder always ready, burst into tears as he seized Harry’s hand and kissed it, praying God to bless his generous young patron. Harry felt, with a pleasant glow, the sweetness of being the cause of joy. It is a good thing, indeed, to turn sorrow into comfort; to see faces brighten as a hearth brightens when the fire has been fed. He walked beside his rescued princess with his hat cocked and his young vanity not a little pleased. In his mind, trumpets and shawms played; the very air seemed to applaud him; and if it was only that he had paid money and driven hard, he felt as though he had slain a dragon.

Yet the night did not end at the gate.

Back at their lodgings, Monsieur Barbeau—ever obsequious—had sent over a cold supper as if to crown the adventure: partridges with their skin browned and aromatic even when cold, the bird’s faint wild sweetness lingering under the salt; and ancient Burgundy, deep and perfumed, that rose from the glass with a rich, cellar-breathed warmth. The crackle of the pipe—tobacco’s dark, earthy smell—filled the room. Their talk ran on; the wine ran on; the hours, like a good sauce, reduced themselves to a potent thickness.

Mr. Sampson continued prodigiously affected. He was one of those men whose whole nature is an instrument—quick to take any tune, grief or gratitude, laughter or hate. His profession had trained him to summon tears as readily as he might summon a text, and, for the moment, he was exactly what he acted: a grateful dependant, melted by kindness. He would weep with the widow and the orphan, and, as soon as the door closed, roar with Jack over a bottle in the tavern opposite. He gave money readily and forgot repayment as easily as he forgot last week’s resolution. This night, his heart—if heart it was—overflowed in adoration of Harry Warrington; and in all London, perhaps, the unlucky Fortunate Youth could not have found a more dangerous companion than a man so fluent in feeling.

With each bumper, the chaplain’s admiration swelled; with each bumper, Harry’s notion of the splendour of his house swelled too, for Burgundy had always made him eloquent on that point.

“The younger branch of our family,” said Harry, with a superb air, “have treated you scurvily; but, by Jove, Sampson my boy, I’ll stand by you!”

Sampson’s eyes shone wet. Harry, warmed by wine and the delight of playing Providence, went further still.

“You said you had a sister at boarding-school. You will want money for her, sir. Here is a little bill which may help to pay her schooling.”

And he pushed a bank-note across the table, as lightly as a man might push across the salt.

Again Sampson was affected almost beyond bearing. He set the note a little away from him, like something too hot to touch.

“Mr. Warrington,” he cried, “I—I don’t deserve your kindness—by George, I don’t!” and he swore it as if an oath could make it true.

“Psha!” said Harry. “I have plenty more of ’em. There was no money in that confounded pocket-book which I lost last week.”

“No, sir. There was no money,” said Sampson, dropping his head.

“Hallo!” Harry’s colour rose. “How do you know, Mr. Chaplain?”

“I know because I am a villain, sir,” Sampson said. “I am not worthy of your kindness. I told you so. I found the book, sir, that night, when you had too much wine at Barbeau’s.”

“And read the letters?” Harry started up, crimson with shame and anger.

“They told me nothing I did not know, sir,” said the chaplain, with that odd mixture of boldness and abasement some men can contrive. “You have had spies about you whom you little suspect—from whom you are much too young and simple to be able to keep your secret.”

Harry’s voice sharpened. “Are those stories about Lady Fanny, and my cousin Will and his doings, true then?”

“Yes, they are true,” sighed Sampson. “The house of Castlewood has not been fortunate, sir, since your honour’s branch—the elder branch—left it.”

“Sir, you don’t dare for to breathe a word against my Lady Maria!” Harry cried.

“Oh, not for worlds!” Sampson answered quickly, with a queer look—half fear, half calculation. “I may think she is too old for your honour, and that ’tis a pity you should not have a wife better suited to your age, though I admit she looks very young for hers, and hath every virtue and accomplishment.”

“She is too old, Sampson, I know she is,” Harry said, with a dignity that tried to master the hurt. “But she has my word, and you see, sir, how fond she is of me. Go bring me the letters, sir, which you found, and let me try and forgive you for having seized upon them.”

“My benefactor, let me try and forgive myself!” cried Sampson; and he went to his chamber, leaving Harry alone with the remnants of supper—the partridge bones, the wine-stains, the tobacco-smoke that hung in the curtains like a confession.

Sampson returned presently, looking very pale.

“What has happened, sir?” Harry asked imperiously.

The chaplain held out the pocket-book.

“With your name in it, sir.”

“My brother’s name in it,” said Harry; “it was George who gave it to me.”

“I kept it in a locked chest, sir, in which I left it this morning before I was taken by those people. Here is the book, sir, but the letters are gone. My trunk and valise have also been tampered with. And I am a miserable, guilty man, unable to make you the restitution which I owe you.”

He clasped his hands, and his whole body took the attitude of tragedy—as if grief were a garment he could put on.

Who had been in the rooms during their absence? The landlady, white with alarm at being suspected, was ready to drop to her knees and swear nobody had entered. Indeed, Harry’s room showed no disorder; and from Sampson’s scanty wardrobe and possessions, nothing seemed missing save those papers—those letters—that the chaplain lamented.

Whose interest was it to seize them?

Not Lady Maria’s. She had been a prisoner all day when the seizure was made. If there was guilt here, it was elsewhere. The sudden taking of the two; Case, the house-steward’s secret journey to London; Case, who knew the shoemaker at whose house Sampson lodged in town, and who knew the hidden arrangements and old sores of the Esmond family—these things might have pointed, in Sampson’s mind, to the Baroness Bernstein. Yet why arrest Lady Maria? Sampson knew nothing of the particular letter Maria had lost; the poor lady had not thought to confide that secret to him.

But Harry—swollen with self-satisfaction, warmed by wagers won, by the feeling of rescue, by the savour of partridge and the velvet punch of ancient Burgundy—was in no humour to sift the matter. He accepted Sampson’s vows of contrition, his promises of future fidelity. He reached out his hand with a grand forgiveness.

When the chaplain swore that henceforth he would be Harry’s truest, humblest friend and follower, ready to die for him at any moment, Harry answered, majestically, “I think, Sampson, you would; I hope you would. My family—the Esmond family—has always been accustomed to have faithful friends round about ’em—and to reward ’em too. The wine’s with you, Chaplain. What toast do you call, sir?”

“I call a blessing on the house of Esmond-Warrington!” cried the chaplain, with real tears.

“We are the elder branch, sir. My grandfather was the Marquis of Esmond,” Harry declared, his noble voice growing slightly thick. “Here’s to you, Chaplain—and I forgive you, sir—and God bless you, sir—and if you had been took for three times as much, I’d have paid it. Why, what’s that I see through the shutters? I am blest if the sun hasn’t risen again! We have no need of candles to go to bed, ha, ha!”

And with that, extending his blessing once more, he went off to sleep.

About noon, Madame de Bernstein sent a servant to say she would be glad if her nephew would come over and drink a dish of chocolate with her. Harry rose, dressed, and walked to his aunt’s lodgings.

She remarked at once, not without pleasure, a change in his toilette: the cut of his coat, the air of his linen, the small new elegancies that London tailors and my Lord March’s purveyors could bestow. She called him “my dearest child,” thanked him for his noble, generous behaviour to dear Maria, and spoke with dramatic emphasis of the shock of that seizure in church—how it had struck her as if a pistol had gone off at the altar.

Then, as if the world always must come back to accounts, she sighed over Wednesday night: she had lost three hundred to Lady Yarmouth, and was quite a sec.

“Why,” said the Baroness, “I had to send Case to London to my agent to get me money to pay—I could not leave Tunbridge in her debt.”

“So Case did go to London?” Harry asked, the words coming out with more meaning than he meant them to carry.

“Of course he did: the Baroness de Bernstein can’t afford to say she is court d’argent. Canst thou lend me some, child?”

“I can give your ladyship twenty-two pounds,” Harry said, blushing. “I have but forty-four left till I get my Virginian remittances. I have bought horses and clothes, and been very extravagant, aunt.”

“And rescued your poor relations in distress, you prodigal good boy. No, child, I do not want thy money. I can give thee some. Here is a note upon my agent for fifty pounds, vaurien! Go and spend it, and be merry! I dare say thy mother will repay me, though she does not love me.”

She looked quite affectionate as she held out her hand; Harry kissed it. Her fingers were neat still, though age lived in the joints.

“Your mother did not love me,” she went on, “but your mother’s father did once. Mind, sir, you always come to me when you have need of me.”

When Beatrix chose to be gracious, no one could exceed her. Yet her grace had a blade in it.

“I can’t help loving you, child,” she continued, “and yet I am so angry with you that I have scarce the patience to speak to you. So you have actually engaged yourself to poor Maria, who is as old as your mother? What will Madam Esmond say? She may live three hundred years, and you will not have wherewithal to support yourselves.”

“I have ten thousand pounds from my father, of my own, now my poor brother is gone,” Harry answered. “That will go some way.”

“Why, the interest will not keep you in card-money.”

“We must give up cards,” said Harry, with the brave simplicity of a man who has never yet been truly hungry.

“It is more than Maria is capable of. She will pawn the coat off your back to play. The rage for it runs in all my brother’s family—in me too, I own it. I warned you. I prayed you not to play with them, and now a lad of twenty to engage himself to a woman of forty-two!—to write letters on his knees and signed with his heart’s blood (which he spells like hartshorn), and say that he will marry no other woman than his adorable cousin, Lady Maria Esmond. Oh! it’s cruel—cruel!”

“Great heavens! madam, who showed you my letter?” Harry cried, the blood mounting again.

“An accident. She fainted when she was taken by those bailiffs. Brett cut her laces for her; and when she was carried off, poor thing, we found a little sachet on the floor, which I opened, not knowing in the least what it contained. And in it was Mr. Harry Warrington’s precious letter. And here, sir, is the case.”

She held out the little locket. Harry’s heart gave a sick pang—less at her having read, than at what might be made of it.

“Great heavens! why didn’t she destroy it?” he thought; and aloud, “I—I will give it back to Maria,” he said, reaching for it.

“My dear, I have burned the foolish letter,” the old lady said quietly.

It was as if a hot cinder had been dropped into the conversation: a sharp shock, then a spreading heat. Harry drew back, struck through with relief and shame in the same instant.

“If you choose to betray me I must take the consequence,” Madame Bernstein went on. “If you choose to write another, I cannot help thee. But, in that case, Harry Esmond, I had rather never see thee again. Will you keep my secret? Will you believe an old woman who loves you and knows the world better than you do? I tell you, if you keep that foolish promise, misery and ruin are surely in store for you. What is a lad like you in the hands of a wily woman of the world, who makes a toy of you? She has entrapped you into a promise, and your old aunt has cut the strings and set you free. Go back again! Betray me if you will, Harry.”

“I am not angry with you, aunt—I wish I were,” Harry said, his voice thick with conflicting feeling. “I—I shall not repeat what you told me.”

“Maria never will, child—mark my words!” the Baroness cried eagerly. “She will never own that she has lost that paper. She will tell you that she has it.”

“But I am sure she—she is very fond of me; you should have seen her last night,” Harry faltered.

“Must I tell more stories against my own flesh and blood?” the Baroness sobbed out. “Child, you do not know her past life!”

“And I must not, and I will not!” Harry cried, starting up. “Written or said—it does not matter which! But my word is given; they may play with such things in England, but we gentlemen of Virginia don’t break ’em. If she holds me to my word, she shall have me. If we are miserable, as I dare say we shall be, I’ll take a firelock, and go join the King of Prussia, or let a ball put an end to me.”

“I—I have no more to say.” The Baroness’s voice grew stiff again, as if she were drawing on armour. “Will you be pleased to ring that bell? I—I wish you a good morning, Mr. Warrington.”

She dropped a stately curtsey, rose with the help of her tortoiseshell stick, and turned toward the door. But at the first step she put her hand to her heart, sank back upon the sofa, and shed—strange sight—the first tears that had fallen for long years from Beatrix Esmond’s eyes.

Harry was moved beyond any courtly speech. He knelt by her, seized her cold hand, and kissed it.

He told her, in his artless way, how keenly he had felt her love, and how with all his heart he returned it.

“Ah, aunt!” he said, bowing his comely head over her hand, “you don’t know what a villain I feel myself. When you told me, just now, how that paper was burned—oh! I was ashamed to think how glad I was.”

Hot drops fell from his eyes onto her hand. She had loved this boy; and in half a century of wit and world, never perhaps had she felt anything so tender and so pure as the pain of seeing him honest.

Her hard heart, long practised in mastery, was wounded into softness. She put both her hands on his shoulders and lightly kissed his forehead.

“You will not tell her what I have done, child?” she whispered.

He declared never—never.

And at her summons, demure Mrs. Brett entered, and found aunt and nephew in that sentimental attitude: the old woman’s tears drying like salt on fine china, the young man’s loyalty set like bread in the oven—warm, swelling, and not yet aware what it would cost to eat.
