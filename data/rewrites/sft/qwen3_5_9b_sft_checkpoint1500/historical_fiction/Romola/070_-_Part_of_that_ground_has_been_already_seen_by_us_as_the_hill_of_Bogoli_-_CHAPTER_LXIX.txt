In those hushed, wintry hours when Romola lay spent from her labors, her mind drifted backward through the river of days, peering into the misty shore of tomorrow. Everything she had witnessed since awakening in the boat struck her with the sudden, clarifying impact of a fresh seal pressed upon dissolving wax. For the first time, she felt untethered, unburdened by motive, sinking into a selfish lament that existence offered her no satisfaction, whispering a desperate wish for death. That cry had broken from her lips as sleep took hold, yet from the moment she woke to the sound of the weeping, reflection held no sway. Unlike her former days in Florence, where she had rationalized her survival as a duty to alleviate sorrow, she now simply *lived*. A fierce, impulsive energy surged within her, driving her to share the burdens of the world, to answer every cry of need, and to perform the labor that screamed for completion, without ever needing to construct an argument for why she must endure.

This transformation felt like a second baptism. In Florence, the simple connections between human beings had become tangled for her by the heavy knots of marriage, statecraft, and religious devotion; when these institutions failed her, the blow had severed her from life and numbed her compassion. Now, however, she declared, "It was merely base in me to crave death. If all else remains uncertain, this suffering that I can ease is absolute truth; if the glory of the cross is a phantom, the sorrow it relieves is the only reality." As long as strength remained in her arm, she vowed to extend it to the fainting; as long as vision stayed in her eyes, she would guide those left in darkness.

Then, the past rose up with a renewed urgency. Her work in this green valley was concluded, and the emotions freed from the immediate circle of neighbors rushed back into the deep, ancient channels of habit and love. This rare silence, born of total separation from her accustomed life, allowed her to judge herself with a severity unknown before. The remorse inherent in a sympathetic soul, acutely aware of the pain of others, stirred within her with growing force. She questioned the justice of her own judgments and actions: she had been rash, arrogant, perpetually unsatisfied because others lacked virtue while she had betrayed the highest principles her soul once recognized. She condemned her flight as a cowardly act of self-preservation, realizing that the grounds Savonarola had used to welcome her back were far truer and deeper than the flimsy excuses for her second departure. How could she claim to feel the needs of strangers while ignoring the desperate plight of those closest to her?

Yet, a counter-current swiftly arose against this torrent of self-reproach. The vivid memories of her life with Tito flooded her mind—the circumstances that rendered their spiritual union impossible, while their outward bond forced upon her a mask of false duties, concealing and sanctifying what her spirit revolted against. These recollections convinced her that flight had indeed been her only recourse. All minds, save those dulled by ignorance, must wrestle with this recurring torment where life's intricate tangles forbid the fulfillment of a sacred vow. For in strictest truth, no relation can truly replace another; the arrival of the new does not erase the breach of the old. Life, imperfect and maimed as it is, casts conscience backward constantly, offering doubting glances until the wounds heal completely.

Romola shrank with terror at the prospect of returning to Tito, yet unease gnawed at her regarding his fate. She feared the moment might soon arrive when he suffered and required her aid. A lingering thread of pain testified to Fra Girolamo's words: she could never truly cease to be a wife. Could anything ever utterly vanish from her heart that had once mingled with the very current of her blood?

Florence, and every shadow and sunbeam of her youth there, returned to her like a ravenous hunger. Her feelings refused to wander aimlessly among possibilities; their vital fiber was nourished only by the memory of familiar streets and faces. The thought that she had irrevocably severed ties with them grew increasingly insistent during these idle, empty hours. What if Fra Girolamo had erred? What if the life of Florence was a tapestry woven from inconsistencies rather than truths? Was she truly some exalted being, destined to shake the dust from her feet and declare, "This world is not fit for me"? If she possessed such loftiness, surely she would not have lost her faith so easily.

The indignant grief she had harbored for her godfather no longer held complete dominion over her spirit; instead, her profound sense of debt to Savonarola regained its ascendancy. Nothing that had occurred, nor anything yet to come, could obliterate the fact that he had kindled a new life within her through a great inspiration. Who, in all her vast experience, could rightfully demand equal gratitude? Yet, the shadow of his errors lingered, raising the chilling question: might they not unleash calamity?

Rest eluded her. She could scarcely discern whether it was the returning vigor of spring, signaled by the budding leaves, that fueled her activity, or an eager, magnetic longing to draw nearer to Florence. Though she did not imagine herself having the courage to step directly into the city, a powerful desire compelled her to remain close enough to discern what transpired there, a purpose that eclipsed all others.

On one crisp March morning, the villagers gathered to witness the departure of the Blessed Lady. Jacopo had procured a sturdy mule for her journey and prepared to accompany her over the mountains. The Padre likewise traveled with her to the nearest town, intending to assist her in finding the safest route to Pistoja. Her hoard of trinkets and coin, untouched in the valley, stood ample to meet her needs.

Had Romola's heart been less tethered to the valley, leaving it would have been a harrowing trial. Walking the village street for the final time, she found her progress impeded by the wailing crowd. People knelt to kiss her hands, clinging to her skirts to press kisses onto the grey folds, pleading, "Ah, why will you leave, when the good season begins and the crops promise plenty? Why must you go?"

"Do not mourn," Romola answered gently. "You are well now, and I shall keep your memory alive. I must go to see if my own kin require me."

"Ah, yes—if they have the pestilence!" someone cried out.

"Look upon us again, Madonna!"

"Yes, yes, we will be faithful to little Benedetto!"

Finally, Romola mounted the mule. Yet, a frantic, vigorous scream from Benedetto, seeing her turn away, served as an excuse for the entire assembly to follow, insisting that the child must ride upon the mule's neck down to the foot of the slope. The parting was inevitable, yet as Romola paused and turned repeatedly before vanishing from sight, she beheld the little flock lingering, desperate to catch the final wave of her hand.
