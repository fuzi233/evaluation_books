When the family gathered for the next meal, there was not a trace of displeasure upon Madame de Bernstein's countenance. Her behavior toward everyone, including Harry, was perfectly kind and cordial. She praised the cook, declared the fricassee excellent, and insisted that no eels in the world could compare to those swimming in the Castlewood moats. She refused to admit the wine was corked or entertain the idea of opening a fresh bottle for a "useless old woman" like herself. When the new vintage was served, she raised a toast to Madam Esmond Warrington of Virginia and expressed hope that Harry had secured his mother's permission to bring an English bride back with him.

Harry did not recall his grandmother; he focused instead on Madame de Bernstein's dear mother. The Baroness delighted the company with numerous anecdotes of her mother—her beauty, her goodness, and her profound happiness with her second husband, despite the fact that the wife was significantly older than Colonel Esmond. "To see them together is simply delightful," she remarked, noting that their attachment was celebrated throughout the countryside. She concluded that talking of disparities in marriage was futile after such an example. While My Lady Castlewood and her two children remained silent, allowing the Baroness to prattle on, Harry was completely enraptured, while Maria appeared surprisingly moved.

Lord Castlewood was puzzled by this sudden transformation in his aunt's demeanor, wondering what scheme had caused such prodigious amiability. Yet, he maintained a mask of perfect satisfaction, showing no sign of solicitude or doubt. The Baroness's good humor seemed to infect the entire household; not a single guest escaped a gracious word from her.

In response to a compliment directed at Mr. Will, the artless youth expressing surprise at his aunt's change of heart, she spoke frankly. "Complimentary, my dear! Of course I am. I wish to make amends for being exceedingly rude to everyone this morning. When I was a child, living here with my parents, I adopted exactly the same behavior. If I had been naughty in the morning, I would try to coax my parents at night. I remember doing this right here, at this very table—oh, hundreds of years ago!—coaxing my father, my mother, and your grandfather, Harry Warrington. There were eels for supper, just as we have tonight, and it was that specific dish of collared eels that brought the memory rushing back. I was just as wayward at seven years old as I am today at seventy. So, I confess my sins and ask forgiveness, like a good girl."

"I absolve your ladyship!" cried the chaplain, who was present.

"But your reverence does not know how cross and ill-tempered I truly was," she continued. "I scolded my sister, Castlewood; I scolded her children; I boxed Harry Warrington's ears—all because he refused to accompany me to Tunbridge Wells."

"But I will go, madam! I will ride with you with all the pleasure in life," promised Mr. Warrington.

"You see, Mr. Chaplain, what good, dutiful children they all are. It was I alone who was cross and peevish. Oh, it was cruel of me to treat them so! Maria, I ask your pardon, my dear."

"Sure, madam, you have done me no wrong," Maria replied humbly.

"Indeed I have, a very great wrong, child!" the Baroness exclaimed. "Because I was weary of myself, I told you that your company would be wearisome to me. You offered to come with me to Tunbridge, and I rudely refused you."

"Nay, ma'am, if you were sick and my presence annoyed you..."

"But it will not annoy me! You were most kind to say you would come. I do, of all things, beg, pray, entreat, and implore—and command—that you will come."

My lord filled his glass and sipped it, looking utterly unconscious of the drama unfolding. This, then, was the meaning of the earlier comedy.

"Anything which can give my aunt pleasure, I am sure, will delight me," Maria said, striving to look as happy as possible.

"You must come and stay with me, my dear, and I promise to be good and good-humoured. My dear lord, you will spare your sister to me?"

"Lady Maria Esmond is quite of age to judge for herself regarding such matters," his lordship replied with a bow. "If any of us can be of use to you, madam, you surely ought to command us."

Which sentence, when interpreted correctly, undoubtedly meant: *Plague take the old woman! She is taking Maria away to separate her from this young Virginian.*

"Oh, Tunbridge will be delightful!" sighed Lady Maria.

"Mr. Sampson will go and see Goody Jones for you," my lord continued.

Harry drew pictures with his finger on the tablecloth. What delights had he not already speculated upon? What walks, rides, interminable conversations, delicious shrubberies, and sweet, secluded summer houses had he not imagined? What poring over music books, moonlit nights, and billing and cooing! Yes, the day was coming. Everyone else was departing—My Lady Castlewood to her friends, Madame Bernstein to the waters—and he was to be left alone with his divine charmer. Alone with her and unutterable rapture! The thought of the pleasure was maddening. These people were leaving, leaving him to enjoy that heaven—to sit at the feet of that angel and kiss the hem of her white robe.

*O Gods!* he thought. *'Twas too great a bliss to be real!*

"I knew it couldn't be," thought poor Harry. "I knew something would happen to take her from me."

"But you will ride with us to Tunbridge, nephew Warrington, and keep us from the highwaymen?" asked Madame de Bernstein.

Harry Warrington hoped the company did not notice how red he had grown. He forced his voice to remain calm and steady. Yes, he would ride with their ladyships, and he was certain they needed fear no danger. Danger! Harry felt he would rather embrace danger than avoid it. He would slay ten thousand highwaymen if they approached his mistress's coach. At the very least, he would ride alongside that coach, catching glimpses of her eyes at the window occasionally. He might not speak to her, but he would be near her. He would press her blessed hand at the inn at night and feel it resting on his as he led her to the carriage in the morning.

They would spend two full days traveling to Tunbridge, perhaps staying there a day or two longer. Was not the poor wretch condemned to die at Newgate thankful for even two or three days of respite? One sees that we have merely indicated, rather than described in detail, the condition of Mr. Harry Warrington, or that utter depth of imbecility into which the poor young man had now fallen. Some boys suffer from the complaint of love favorably and gently. Others, when struck by the fever, become sick unto death; or, recovering, carry the scars of the malady to their graves or to remotest old age. I say, it is unfair to record a young fellow's words when he is raging in such a delirium. Suppose he loves a woman twice his own age; have we not all read of the young gentleman who committed suicide due to his fatal passion for Mademoiselle Ninon de l'Enclos, who turned out to be his grandmother? Suppose you are making an ass of yourself, young Harry Warrington of Virginia! Are there not people in England who heehaw too? Kick and abuse him, you who have never brayed; but bear with him, all honest fellow-cardophagi, long-eared messmates; recognize a brother-donkey!

"You will stay with us for a day or two at the Wells," Madame Bernstein continued. "You will see us settled into our lodgings. Then you can return to Castlewood for the partridge shooting and all the fine pursuits you and my lord are to study together."

Harry bowed in acquiescence. A whole week of heaven! Life was not entirely a blank anymore.

"And since there is sure to be plenty of company at the Wells, I shall be able to present you," the lady added graciously.

"Company! Ah! I shan't need company," sighed Harry.

"I mean that I shall be quite contented in the company of you two ladies," he added eagerly, and no doubt Mr. Will wondered at his cousin's peculiar taste.

Since this was to be the last night of cousin Harry's visit to Castlewood, cousin Will suggested that he, the chaplain, and Warrington meet at Harry's quarters to settle accounts. To this proposal, Harry, being a considerable winner in his recent gambling transactions with the two gentlemen, had no objection.

Accordingly, when the ladies retired for the night and my lord withdrew, as was his custom, to his own apartments, the three gentlemen found themselves assembled in Mr. Harry's small room before the punch-bowl, which was Will's usual midnight companion. However, Will's method of settling accounts involved producing two fresh decks of cards and offering to submit Harry's debt to the process of being doubled or acquitted. The poor chaplain possessed no more ready cash than Lord Castlewood's younger brother. Harry Warrington desired to win the money of neither.
