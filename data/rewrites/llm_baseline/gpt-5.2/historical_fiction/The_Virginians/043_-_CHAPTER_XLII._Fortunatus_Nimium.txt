Harry Warrington held, with that stubborn Virginian conscience of his, to the melancholy promise his cousin had wrung from him; yet no charitable soul could fancy the engagement was sweet to his palate. It sat on him like meat swallowed too hastily—honour, yes, but dry as yesterday’s bread. If ever a man wished to be quit of a bond without seeming to cut it, it was Harry.

And so—whether by design, by hot blood, or by that quick temper which is the same in the field and at the card-table—there came the bout with Will Esmond. A private payment of a private score: fists in a room, no witnesses to go gossiping through drawing-rooms as through poultry-yards. Harry’s thought (if he owned it even to himself) ran in this fashion: a boxing-match must breed a family quarrel; a family quarrel might set Maria against him; he would not retract; Will, stung, might call him out; and in that smoke and confusion the hateful compact might burn away, leaving him free. He laid his little train and touched it off.

The powder flashed—then settled to a mere bruise and a swollen nose. Will’s eye went black for a week; his pride, one suspects, for longer; but he sent no challenge. He was knocked down and got up again—an act of sense many men might practise, could they only settle their accounts in a back room and keep the reckoning off the public ledger. Presently the cousins met in other people’s houses and did not maul each other.

Nor did Maria, as Harry had perhaps hoped, run to her family’s colours. She declared for her cousin. So did my lord Castlewood when the disturbance reached him. By the chaplain’s showing, Will had struck first; and it was neither the first nor the tenth time he had been found quarrelsome in his cups. Mr. Warrington, said my lord, had shown proper spirit in resenting an injury; it was for Will to ask pardon, not Harry. Harry answered he would accept no apology while his horse was not returned and his wager not paid. How the matter of the bet was cooked and served at last, this chronicler cannot discover; only that it ended, like many a disputed dish, in being eaten without further clatter.

My lord had at first been willing enough that his sister—unmarried so long, her life trailing behind her like a gown that has brushed too many thresholds and gathered too many burrs—should accept any bridegroom who offered, and, if a gentleman from Virginia, so much the better: let her be carried off to his wigwam in the forest and done with. In the natural course, Harry would outlive so elderly a bride, and console himself afterward or not, as suited him.

But after my lord’s talk with Aunt Bernstein upon coming to London, his mind altered; he even tried to dissuade Maria, and spoke—miracle of tenderness!—of pity for the young fellow forced into misery by a foolish promise given at one-and-twenty. Misery! Maria could not taste the word. What misery? Pity indeed! She knew what pity meant, and it was not my lord’s flavour of it. Her brother had been with Aunt Bernstein; Aunt Bernstein had offered money to break the match. Maria understood very well what his lordship’s compassion signified; but Mr. Warrington was a man of honour, and she could trust him.

My lord, balked and irritated, went off to White’s, or to whatever kennel of fashionable noise then yelped him welcome. And there, in the roaring tide of London—dice rattling like hail on shutters, voices cracking, chairs scraping, snuff-boxes snapping, the very air thick with oaths and laughter—he brooded with a bitterness that made the room’s din seem almost medicinal. Had his sister guessed too neatly the drift of his conversation with Madame Bernstein? If so, the guess galled him.

“And so,” he thought, “the end of my virtue will be that the Mohock falls into other hands; and there is no use in sparing him. If I don’t have him, somebody else will—that is plain. My brother has had a slice; my dear sister would swallow him whole. And here have I been playing guardian, refusing to play beyond a sixpence, fattening a goose for other people to carve! Remorse? By all the furies, my remorse is for what I have not done and might have done. Why did I spare Lucretia? She hated me ever after, and her husband went where he was destined. Why let this lad off—only that March and the rest, who do not want him, may pluck him? And I have the bad name; I am the wicked lord pointed at; women warn their sons against me. Pardi, I am not a penny worse—only more unlucky than my neighbours; my cursed weakness is my worst enemy!”

Perhaps no man ever spoke such a speech aloud; but historians have long put words into dead mouths, and if Livy may do it for heroes, why may not we, who know Lord Castlewood’s temper, set down the shape of his thoughts? One pack of wolves on the hunt for the lamb, and one hungry old hunter to stand aside without so much as a cutlet? The world is amazed when men, on counting their opportunities, find they took so little.

Madame Bernstein, however, was loth to lay before her Virginian nephew all the sour knowledge she kept of his family. She had even been touched by my lord’s forbearance when Harry first came to Europe, and pleased to have her wishes obeyed. Yet in that later talk—when Castlewood spoke of Maria’s designs—he had thrown off restraint with his customary cynicism, called himself a fool for sparing a lad whom sparing would not save from ruin, and pointed to Harry’s undeniable extravagances: nights at faro and hazard, rides to Newmarket, a troop of spendthrift companions always hungry for another course. Why should he alone keep his hands off?

Madame Bernstein pleaded that Harry was poor. Bah! said my lord; he was heir to a principality that ought to have been Castlewood’s and might have set their ruined house on its legs. Were there not money-lenders enough in town who would advance on post-obits? Castlewood knew those gentry to his cost: he had tasted their usury in his father’s lifetime, and they had gnawed away two-thirds of his wretched income, as rats work through a sack of grain.

He spoke with such desperate candour and ill-humour that Madame Bernstein, alarmed for her favourite, resolved to warn him at the first chance.

That evening she began a billet to Mr. Warrington; but all her life she had been slow with her pen and mistrustful of ink. “I never knew any good come of writing more than bon jour or business,” she would say. “Why write ill, when there are so many who can do it well? and even then it were best left alone.” So she sent one of her men to Harry’s lodgings, bidding him come next day for a dish of tea, when she meant to caution him.

But the next morning she was indisposed and could not receive him when he came; then she kept her chamber a couple of days; then there was some great engagement; then Harry was off on an expedition of his own. In the whirl of London, who sees his neighbour? What brother meets his sister? What schoolfellow finds his old friend? Days slipped by before aunt and nephew had their confidential talk.

When at last they sat together, she began by scolding him mildly on his extravagance and madcap frolics—though in truth she was charmed with him for both, as some people are charmed by a young cook’s bold hand even while pretending to fear the spice. Harry replied that young men would be young men; and that, in dutifully attending on his aunt, he had made the acquaintances with whom he chiefly lived now. Then, after some prelude, she began to warn him of Lord Castlewood.

At this Harry broke into a bitter laugh. The good-natured world, he said, had told him plenty already about his cousin.

“To say of a man of his lordship’s rank—of any gentleman—‘Don’t play with him,’ is more than I like to do,” Madame Bernstein continued; “but—”

“Oh, you may say on, aunt,” Harry said, with something like an oath biting the back of his teeth.

“And have you played with your cousin already?” asked the old monitress, who had lived long enough to know how quickly warning turns into prophecy.

“And lost and won, madam!” Harry answered, with a gallantry that tried to make light of the thing. “It don’t become me to say which. If we have a bout with a neighbour in Virginia—a bottle, a pack of cards, a quarrel—we don’t go home and tell our mothers. I mean no offence, aunt!”

And, blushing, he rose and kissed her. He looked brave and brilliant—rich lace at his wrists, fair face and hair, velvet and gold new on his back. He took leave with that easy kindness which made his generosity seem as natural as breathing. The servants gathered round him, and he gave them his usual sumptuous benefaction, so that even gratitude had a sound in that house: curtseys, murmured blessings, the soft clink of coin—a kinder music than the harsh chorus of the gaming rooms.

Outside, the day was rainy and winter-grey; to save his fine silk stockings from the street’s wet filth, he must needs come in a chair. He called through the curtains, “To White’s!” and away the chairmen carried him—feet splashing, poles creaking, voices calling in the watery air—to the place where he spent far too much of his time.

If his friends could have wished him less constant there, so much may yet be said for him: once engaged in play, he fought his battle like a hero. Good luck did not make him noisy; ill luck did not make him mean. Fortune is proverbially fickle to gamblers; but how many gamblers are fickle to Fortune—run frightened from her advances, desert her, and then swear she was faithless first?

“By George, Mr. Warrington,” cried Mr. Selwyn, waking up into one of his rare fits of enthusiasm, “you deserve to win! You treat your luck as a gentleman should. So long as she stays with you, you behave to her with perfect politeness. Si celeres quatit pennas—you know the rest—no? Well, you are none the worse off. You will call her ladyship’s coach, and make her a bow at the step. Look at Lord Castlewood yonder, passing the box. Did you ever hear a fellow curse and swear so at losing five or six pieces? She must be a jade indeed, if she long gives her favours to such a niggardly canaille as that!”

“We don’t consider our family canaille, sir,” Harry said, stiffening, “and my Lord Castlewood is one of them.”

“I forgot—forgot, and ask your pardon! And I make you my compliment upon my lord, and Mr. Will Esmond, his brother,” Selwyn said, with his dry grin. “The box is with me. Five’s the main! Deuce Ace!—my usual luck. Virtute mea me involvo!” and he sank back as if virtue were a cloak he drew over his face to sleep.

Whether this was the very occasion when Harry threw those fifteen mains spoken of in one of Mr. Walpole’s letters I cannot tell; but certain it is that, upon his first appearance at White’s, Harry had five or six evenings of prodigious good fortune. The five hundred pounds taken from his inheritance multiplied into thousands; the purse seemed bewitched, as if Fortunatus had slipped it into his pocket with a wink.

And Harry—who was not one of those Fortunati that tie up the endless purse, drink small beer, and go to bed with a tallow candle—spent as freely as he breathed. Fine clothes came as regularly as bread from an oven; fine horses, too, sleek as if groomed by gold; grand entertainments where the rooms steamed with candle-wax, roast meats, wine, perfume, and wet wool from cloaks hung to dry. The five thousand pounds never seemed to diminish. Giving was easy when the hand felt always full; and yet it must be said, it was his nature to give, not merely his luck.

In the very height of this vein, what must Mr. Harry do but find out from Lady Maria what her debts were—and pay them off to the last shilling. Her stepmother and half-sister, who did not love her, he loaded with magnificent presents.

“Had you not better get yourself arrested, Will?” my lord said sardonically to his brother. “Although you bit him in that affair of the horse, the Mohock will certainly take you out of pawn.”

Then Mr. William felt a true remorse—though not of the humble kind that sends prodigals to their knees. “Confound it,” he groaned, “to think I have let this fellow slip for such a little matter as forty pound! Why, he was good for a thousand at least.”

As for Maria, that generous creature accepted the good fortune sent her with a grateful heart—and was ready to accept as much more as one pleased. Having paid off her debts to milliners, tradesmen, purveyors, she forthwith proceeded to contract new ones. Mrs. Betty, her maid, went round with news on her tongue like a bellman’s cry: her mistress was about to form a matrimonial alliance with a young gentleman of immense fortune; therefore they might give my lady credit to any amount. The tradesfolk, having heard that tune twice or thrice before, might not believe every note; but their bills were paid, and payment is a persuasive language in any dialect. Even to Mrs. Pincott of Kensington Maria showed no rancour; she ordered fresh supplies with a smile, as if former humiliations were only yesterday’s dish set aside for today’s sweeter course.

So she drove about from mercer to toy-shop, from toy-shop to jeweller, in a coach with her maid and Mr. Warrington inside; and the shopkeepers, hearing the wheels, the horses’ snort and jingle, the coachman’s call, thought her a fortunate woman indeed to have secured the Fortunate Youth—though they might wonder at the young man’s taste in choosing so elderly a beauty.

Mr. Sparks, of Tavistock Street, Covent Garden, took the liberty of waiting upon Mr. Warrington at his lodgings in Bond Street with the pearl necklace and the gold étui bought in Lady Maria’s company the day before, and asked whether he should leave them at his honour’s lodgings or send them to her ladyship with his honour’s compliments. Harry, in that mood when money seems to have the lightness of feathers, added a ring from the jeweller’s stock to the necklace and the étui, bade Sparks send in the bill, and dismissed him with a grandeur that made even Gumbo receive a bow as the tradesman withdrew.

Nor did his bounties end there. Within days the pleased young fellow drove up in his phaeton to Sparks’s shop and chose a couple of trinkets for two young ladies whose parents had been kind to him and for whom he felt a sincere regard.

“Ah,” thought he, as the jewels winked in their trays like bright eyes in candlelight, “how I wish I had my poor George’s wit and genius for poetry! I would send these presents with pretty verses to Hetty and Theo. If goodwill and real regard could make a poet of me, I should have no difficulty finding rhymes.”

And so he called in Parson Sampson, and together they fell to concocting a billet. Outside, London went on clattering—coaches, cries, bells, the ceaseless hard-edged music of the town—while inside the young man’s fortune, like a sauce left too long on the fire, thickened fast and sweet, and threatened, even as it pleased, to change its flavour.
