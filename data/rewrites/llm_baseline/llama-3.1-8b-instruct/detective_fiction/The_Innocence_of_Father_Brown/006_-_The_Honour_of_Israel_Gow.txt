As the stormy twilight deepened, casting a sombre hue over the rolling hills, Father Brown wrapped his grey Scotch plaid tightly around him and emerged from the valley, his eyes fixed upon the enigmatic silhouette of Glengyle Castle. Perched atop a hill, it stood sentinel-like, its steep roofs and spires of weathered slate evoking the mystical steeple-hats of medieval tales. The surrounding pine woods, heavy with the scent of damp earth, seemed to darken the atmosphere, as if shrouding the very essence of the place in mystery. This was a land of proud tradition, where the weight of heredity bore down upon the noble houses of Scotland, and the Calvinist doctrine whispered tales of doom and foreboding.

Father Brown's thoughts were drawn back to his friend Flambeau, the amateur detective, who was currently ensconced within the castle walls, investigating the life and untimely death of the late Earl of Glengyle. This was a family whose history was steeped in valor, madness, and cunning, leaving an indelible mark on the annals of Scottish nobility. Their legacy was forever entwined with the labyrinthine intrigue surrounding Mary Queen of Scots, a testament to the darker side of their storied past.

In the local folklore, a haunting rhyme captured the essence of their reputation:

"As green sap to the simmer trees
Is red gold to the Ogilvies."

For generations, the Glengyle family had been shrouded in scandal, their eccentricities seemingly endless. However, the final representative of this lineage had taken a peculiar step, vanishing without a trace – or so it seemed. His name remained on the church register and in the peerage, but none had laid eyes on him since his disappearance. The only person who claimed to have seen him was Israel Gow, a gaunt, red-haired labourer with a dogged jaw and blank blue eyes. Though considered deaf, his industry in the kitchen and gardens hinted at a deeper purpose, as if he was catering to the needs of a master still present in the castle. Yet, whenever questioned, Gow insisted that his lord was not at home.

The provost and minister, summoned to the castle, discovered Israel Gow's multifaceted roles extended to that of an undertaker, with the unsettling revelation that he had nailed his master in a coffin.
