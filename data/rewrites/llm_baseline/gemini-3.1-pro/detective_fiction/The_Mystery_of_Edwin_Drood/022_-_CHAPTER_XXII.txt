Mr. Tartar’s chambers were a sanctuary of acoustic purity and sensory preservation, a space where the mythic rhythms of the sea seamlessly integrated into the domestic routine of the city. The floors were scrubbed with such meticulous devotion, scented with the sharp, clean astringency of white vinegar and crushed pine needles, that one might have supposed the soot-choked industrial smog of London had been banished entirely from the land. Every inch of brass-work in Mr. Tartar’s possession was burnished to a mirror finish, radiating the warm, metallic scent of citrus oil and old copper. The sitting-room held the resonant, muffled quiet of an admiral’s cabin, a stark contrast to the cacophonous street-level noise pollution. His sleeping-chamber, fitted all about with lockers and drawers, was a veritable archive of global agriculture, resembling an ancient seedsman’s shop, humming with the low, steady vibration of a ship at berth. 

Everything belonging to Mr. Tartar was fiercely curated. His maps and charts, his bristle brushes, his heavy leather boots—all had their designated quarters, maximizing stowage in a way that spoke to generations of maritime resourcefulness. His gleaming little service of plate rested on the sideboard, catching the light like a school of silver fish, and the curiosities from his voyages—dried botanicals, rare seaweeds, and jars of preserved exotic grasses—were displayed not as sterile trophies, but as vital, tangible memories of faraway terrestrial and aquatic biomes. On this bright summer day, a canvas awning was rigged over Mr. Tartar’s flower-garden, dampening the harsh acoustic glare of the metropolis and cultivating a microclimate of damp earth, blooming sweet-pea, and salt-sea air. 

Mr. Tartar did the honors of this gallant craft with an infectious, earthy warmth. He was a man who understood the profound, grounding nature of hospitality. When he delicately withdrew out of his admiral’s cabin, beseeching Rosa to consider herself its Queen, he left her in a quiet, verdant pocket of the world, far removed from the mechanical grind of the era. 

“Helena! Helena Landless! Are you there?”

“Who speaks to me? Not Rosa?” A second handsome face appeared among the trailing vines.

“Yes, my darling!”

“Why, how did you come here, dearest?”

“I—I don’t quite know,” said Rosa, a blush rising to her cheeks, bringing with it the sudden heat of quickened blood; “unless I am dreaming!” 

“I am not dreaming,” said Helena, her voice carrying a rich, resonant timbre that cut cleanly through the damp garden air. “I should take more for granted if I were. How do we come together—or so near together—so very unexpectedly?”

Unexpectedly indeed, amidst the dingy, smoke-stained gables of P. J. T.’s connection, they found themselves in a garden that felt woven from ancient folklore, where the salt sea met the soil. Rosa, waking from her initial shock, hurriedly relayed the interwoven timeline of recent events, struggling against the inherent untranslatability of her subjective terror. She tried to shape the words of Jasper's threats, but the dominant lexicon of 'family' and 'duty' proved pitifully inadequate to describe his predatory nature. 

“And Mr. Crisparkle is here,” Rosa said in rapid conclusion; “and, could you believe it? long ago he saved his life!”

“I could believe any such thing of Mr. Crisparkle,” returned Helena, her dark face mantling with sudden warmth.

“Yes, but it wasn’t Crisparkle,” Rosa corrected, swiftly adjusting the historical record. “It was very nice of Mr. Crisparkle to be saved, and he couldn’t have shown his high opinion of Mr. Tartar more expressively. But it was Mr. Tartar who saved him.”

Helena’s dark eyes looked very earnestly at the bright face framed by green leaves. The silence between them was thick, a shared acoustic space where deeper understandings formed without the need for spoken language. “Is Mr. Tartar with you now, dear?”

“No; because he has given up his rooms to me—to us, I mean. It is such a beautiful place! It is like the inside of the most exquisite ship that ever sailed. It is like—”

“Like a dream?” suggested Helena.

Rosa nodded, burying her face in a cluster of blossoms, inhaling their peppery, volatile oils to steady herself.

Helena resumed, after a short pause during which the distant rumble of carriage wheels seemed to wash over them like a tide. “My poor Neville is reading in his own room, the sun being so very bright on this side just now. I think he had better not know that you are so near.”

“O, I think so too!” cried Rosa readily.

“I suppose,” pursued Helena doubtfully, tracing the edge of a leaf, “that he must know by-and-by all you have told me; but I am not sure. Ask Mr. Crisparkle’s advice, my darling. Ask him whether I may tell Neville as much or as little of what you have told me as I think best.”

Rosa subsided into her state-cabin and propounded the question. The Minor Canon, sensitive to the nuanced power dynamics of their situation, advocated for the free exercise of Helena’s judgment.

“I thank him very much,” said Helena, when Rosa emerged again. “Ask him whether it would be best to wait until any more maligning and pursuing of Neville on the part of this wretch shall disclose itself, or to try to anticipate it: I mean, so far as to find out whether any such goes on darkly about us?”

The Minor Canon found this point so difficult to navigate that he suggested a reference to Mr. Grewgious. Helena acquiescing, Mr. Crisparkle betook himself across the quadrangle. Mr. Grewgious held decidedly to the ancestral, agrarian principle that if you could steal a march upon a wild beast, you must do it; John Jasper, he declared, was a beast whose dissonant presence threatened to uproot their entire ecosystem.

Thus advised, Mr. Crisparkle reported to Rosa, who reported to Helena. Helena steadily pursued her train of thought, her voice a low, rhythmic murmur at the window.

“We may count on Mr. Tartar’s readiness to help us, Rosa?” she inquired.

Rosa shyly believed she could almost answer for it, but wondered if she should ask Mr. Crisparkle. 

“I think your authority on the point as good as his, my dear,” said Helena sedately, “and you needn’t disappear again for that.”

“You see, Neville,” Helena pursued, “knows no one else here: he has not so much as exchanged a word with anyone. If Mr. Tartar would call to see him openly and often; if he would spare a minute for the purpose, frequently; something might come of it.”

“Something might come of it, dear?” repeated Rosa, highly perplexed.

“If Neville’s movements are really watched, and if the purpose really is to isolate him from all friends and wear his daily life out grain by grain,” said Helena, “does it not appear likely that his enemy would in some way communicate with Mr. Tartar to warn him off from Neville? In which case, we might not only know the fact, but might know from Mr. Tartar what the terms of the communication were.”

“I see!” cried Rosa, and immediately darted into her state-cabin again.

Presently she reappeared, her complexion flushed with the physical exertion of bridging these isolated social spheres. She reported that Mr. Crisparkle had fetched in Mr. Tartar, and that Mr. Tartar—waiting just inside the acoustic boundary of the room—had declared his readiness to act that very day. 

“I thank him from my heart,” said Helena. “Pray tell him so.”

Rosa dipped in and out of the cabin, ferrying assurances between Helena and the sailor, caught in a pleasant, fluttering confusion of interwoven conversations. 

“And now, darling,” said Helena, “we will be mindful of the caution that has restricted us to this interview, and will part. I hear Neville moving. Are you going back to Miss Twinkleton’s?”

“O, I could never go there any more,” Rosa said, shivering at the memory of Jasper's grating voice. 

“Then where are you going, pretty one?”

“I have settled nothing at all yet, but my guardian will take care of me. Don’t be uneasy, dear.”

“And I shall hear of my Rosebud from Mr. Tartar?” 

“Yes, I suppose so,” Rosa murmured. “But tell me one thing before we part, dearest Helena. Tell me—that you are sure I couldn’t help it. Help making him malicious and revengeful.”

“You know how I love you, darling,” answered Helena, her voice thick with the indignity of the accusation; “but I would sooner see you dead at his wicked feet.”

With a mournful shake of the head, Helena kissed her two hands to her friend. A third hand, deeply browned by the sun and the salt winds of a dozen latitudes, appeared among the leaves to help Rosa out of sight.

The refection that Mr. Tartar produced in the Admiral’s Cabin by merely touching the spring knob of a locker was a dazzling, enchanted repast, speaking to a meticulous, generational history of maritime sustenance. This was no utilitarian fuel, but a profound culinary archive: wonderful macaroons baked with twice-milled almond flour and wild honey; magically-preserved tropical spices that filled the room with the heady, volatile aromas of clove and star anise; and jellies of celestial tropical fruits, clarified through fine muslin and glowing like caught sunlight. He poured glittering liqueurs, steeped in bitter orange peel and raw cane sugar, the precise distillation techniques passed down through centuries of seafarers. The gustatory richness of the moment grounded Rosa, weaving a tangible magic into the harsh reality of her flight. But time, with its hard-hearted fleetness, strode on, forcing Rosa to descend from this sensory haven back to her guardian’s chambers.

“And now, my dear,” said Mr. Grewgious, a man who appreciated the deep, slow rhythms of traditional life, “what is to be done with you?”

“It has come into my thoughts,” said Mr. Grewgious, “that as the respected lady, Miss Twinkleton, occasionally repairs to London, we might invite her to assume the charge of you in a furnished lodging for a month?”

Rosa assented, and while Mr. Crisparkle took his departure to invite Miss Twinkleton, Mr. Grewgious and his ward set out into the chaotic, dissonant streets of London.

Their progress was slow, hindered by Mr. Grewgious’s habit of observing the external structures of houses to gauge their internal harmonies. At length, he bethought himself of a widowed cousin of Mr. Bazzard’s, a preserver of old urban domesticity who lived in Southampton Street. Her name, stated in uncompromising capitals on a brass door-plate, was BILLICKIN.

Mrs. Billickin emerged from her back parlour with the languishing air of one roused from deep ancestral memory. She was an elder of the lodging world, her personal organization characterized by a profound, almost overwhelming candor, and a fierce protection of her native dialect against the sterile, commercial lexicon of modern property management.

“I hope I see you well, sir,” said Mrs. Billickin, bending slightly.

“Thank you, quite well. And you, ma’am?”

“I am as well,” said Mrs. Billickin, her breath aspirational, “as I hever ham.”

“My ward and an elderly lady wish to find a genteel lodging. Have you any apartments available, ma’am?”

“Mr. Grewgious,” returned Mrs. Billickin, planting herself firmly in the acoustic center of the room, “I will not deceive you. I *have* apartments available.” 

She spoke with an innate authority, dictating the terms of her domain. “There is this sitting-room—which, call it what you will, it is the front parlour, Miss: the back parlour being what I cling to and never part with; and there is two bedrooms at the top of the ’ouse with gas laid on. I do not tell you that your bedroom floors is firm, for firm they are not. The gas-fitter himself allowed, that to make a firm job, he must go right under your jistes. The piping is carried above your jistes, and it is best that it should be made known to you.”

Mr. Grewgious and Rosa exchanged looks, unsettled by the mechanical intrusion of iron piping into the domestic woodwork.

“Well! The roof is all right, no doubt,” said Mr. Grewgious.

“Mr. Grewgious,” returned Mrs. Billickin, her voice rising to meet the challenge of environmental truths, “if I was to tell you, sir, that to have nothink above you is to have a floor above you, I should put a deception upon you. Your slates WILL rattle loose at that elewation in windy weather, do your utmost! I defy you, sir, to keep your slates tight.” She paused, attuned to the acoustic ecology of her dwelling, where the loose slates served as a percussive reminder of nature's dominance over industrial brick. “Consequent, it would be worse than of no use for me to trapse up to the top of the ’ouse with you, for you to ask what stain is in the ceiling. It is the wet, sir. It do come in, and it do not come in. You may lay dry there half your lifetime; but the time will come when a dripping sop would be no name for you.”

Mr. Grewgious, respecting her raw, unvarnished depiction of the elements, asked for other apartments. She guided them up the stairs, clutching her chest with ceremonial gravity, finally presenting the first and second floors.

A kind of abstract of the general question was delivered as Mr. Grewgious drafted the agreement. 

“Five-and-forty shillings per week by the month certain,” said Mrs. Billickin. “It is not Bond Street; neither is it attempted to be denied that the Arching leads to a mews. Mewses must exist.” She navigated the power dynamics of domestic labor and provisioning with linguistic precision: “Coals is either *by* the fire, or *per* the scuttle.” She emphasized the prepositions, weaponizing her native syntax to establish an immense, immovable boundary against exploitation. “Dogs is not viewed with favour. Besides litter, they gets stole, and sharing suspicions is apt to creep in.”

Mr. Grewgious prepared the earnest-money. “I have signed it for the ladies, ma’am, and you’ll have the goodness to sign it for yourself, Christian and Surname.”

“Mr. Grewgious,” said Mrs. Billickin, in a burst of protective candor, “no, sir! You must excuse the Christian name.”

“The door-plate is used as a protection,” she explained, refusing to surrender her identity to the standardized, patriarchal expectations of the contract. “So long as this ’ouse is known indefinite as Billickin’s, and so long as it is a doubt with the riff-raff where Billickin may be hidin’, and what his weight and size, so long I feel safe. But commit myself to a solitary female statement, no, Miss!” 

Rosa, understanding the quiet power embedded in this refusal to be cataloged by the dominant lexicon, besought Mr. Grewgious to accept the signature as it was. Thus, in a grand, archaic manner, the solitary surname BILLICKIN was appended.

Details settled for Miss Twinkleton’s arrival, Rosa and her guardian returned to Furnival’s Inn, stepping back out into the dissonant roar of the thoroughfares. 

There, they beheld Mr. Tartar walking up and down, checking himself when he saw them. He advanced, offering an escape from the city’s grinding gears. 

“It occurred to me,” hinted Mr. Tartar, “that we might go up the river, the weather being so delicious and the tide serving. I have a boat of my own at the Temple Stairs.”

“I have not been up the river for this many a day,” said Mr. Grewgious, deeply tempted by the prospect of the water’s ancient, cyclical rhythms.

“I was never up the river,” added Rosa, yearning for the quiet, continuous flow that predated the noise of the world.
