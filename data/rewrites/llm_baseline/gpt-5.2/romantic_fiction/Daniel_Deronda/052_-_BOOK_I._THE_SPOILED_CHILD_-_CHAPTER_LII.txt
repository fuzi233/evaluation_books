Among Deronda’s letters next morning there lay one from Hans Meyrick—four quarto pages in the small, careful hand that seemed hereditary in that house, as if even their flourishes were a family recipe.

Deronda opened it with the faint foreknowledge of what he should find there: an overflow of fancy, a peppering of jokes, and now and then—like a sharp herb in a rich sauce—some sentence that bit deeper than the writer intended.

Hans began, as usual, by turning public affairs into a private farce, tossing “time will show” in the air as lightly as if it were a catch to be made in play. He wrote of cattle plague and swindling telegrams, of compensation to farmers, and then—by some merry leap—of a murrain threatening to break out in the commercial class, the symptom to be the ready purchase of rejected pictures. He made his own ambition into a jest, imagining his “Berenice series” fetching a great price at the hands of men who deserved punishment for rewarding him by accident.

Then he passed into the subject Deronda could never read without a change in his pulse—Mirah and Mordecai, those lives that had come to hang upon his own conscience with a weight he never flaunted. Hans, in his bright way, called Mordecai “our Hebrew prophet,” and described sitting with him in the hours when Deronda used to be there, making studies of his head, getting credit as a learned young Gentile “who would have been a Jew if he could,” and agreeing with him—half in mockery, half in real admiration—that whatever was best must therefore be Jewish.

Hans’s letter ran on, nimble and various as a street musician’s medley: a picture of Jacob Cohen on Mordecai’s knee, stopping his ears with his palms to hear Hebrew through muffled hands, then being gently restrained; Jacob’s face darting through expressions as if he were trying on animals in a menagerie, owl and peccary most of all. Hans made sport of it, but even through the sport there came a kind of tenderness—an unwilling acknowledgment of the bond between the invalid dreamer and that too-quick child. And there, too, was Hans’s confession, thrown out as if it were no more than a flourish: that Mirah’s presence distracted him into longing, that if she were less of a heaven to look at he would have flung himself at her feet and demanded whether she meant him to live or die; that he lived on “Hope” as if on an estate in reversion, and kept away from the ugly two-faced god “Certainty.”

Deronda read the allegories with a patience sharpened by knowledge of Hans’s nature. The imagery was like a kitchen where everything fragrant was brought in at once—blossom and spice and smoke—so that what should have been nourishing became for the reader a little cloying. Yet, as always, the letter could not keep to jesting. Hans spoke of Mirah as seeming “a little sadder,” as if she were constantly trying to hide it; and he spoke too of watching “the slow death of this brother” whom she worshipped with such loving devoutness that Hans, half in envy, half in compassion, could almost wish himself in that place.

Then came more news: Rex Gascoigne in town with a neat sister; cousinship discovered between that sister and “your Vandyke duchess”; invitations jostling—one to a rectory in August, another to Topping Abbey where Sir Hugo meant Hans to paint his three daughters in a Gainsborough attitude on a bank. Hans turned even Sir Hugo’s kindness into a comic lecture on “the hinder wheel,” and then, with a careless drop of information as if it were nothing, mentioned that the duchess was gone yachting to the Mediterranean—adding a string of nonsense about whether Deronda would land from a yacht and continue a theological discussion with her.

At the close, Hans excused his “brevity,” boasted of the Klesmers’ splendid generosity—Mirah “rich with their oriental gifts”—and ended with his old devotion and his new Hebrew lore, offering to go below the triliteral roots if Mirah commanded, but declaring that while her brother lived she would not listen to a lover.

Deronda laid the letter down. Months earlier, this kind of romancing about Mirah would have fretted him, not least because it carried so little reverence for the strain Mirah lived under, so little fear of hurting what was delicate. But since March, the centre of his own life had shifted. Mirah was no longer so critically placed under the Meyricks’ roof and protection; and his own future, though still without any pictured certainty, had been altered and—only lately—crowned by the revelation of his birth. That knowledge, like a new light admitted into a familiar room, changed the look of every old object: even Hans’s extravagant “Hope” no longer seemed a mischievous insolence, but the bird-like skipping of a mind too restless to sit down with sorrow. Deronda found himself arguing, almost coldly, that Hans could not love with roots deep enough to make disappointment into grief: he was too excitable by novelty, too quick to turn everything—even pain—into imaginative material, to wear his feeling like a costume and drop it when another fancy offered a better colour.

“Already he is beginning to play at love,” Deronda said inwardly. “He is taking it as comedy. He knows there is no chance for him. And he never even considers that his outpourings may jar on me—never opens his eyes to any reason I could have to dislike them. Poor Hans! If we were under fiery hail together, he would howl like a Greek; and if I did not howl too, it would never occur to him that I was as badly off. And yet he is affectionate, tender-hearted in intention. He imagines other people’s feelings—only always in the shape most convenient to his own inclination.”

That touch of caustic thought cooled the slight heat his friend’s expansiveness had raised. The mention of Gwendolen—yachting with her husband—stirred a darker disturbance, the thought of his own strange parting from her returning like a sound heard at night when one would rather not listen. But it was not the duchess, nor Sir Hugo, nor Hans’s allegories, that held him. One sentence had lodged like a thorn.

“I wish I did not imagine that Mirah gets a little sadder, and tries all the while to hide it.”

Deronda could not dismiss that as mere romantic embroidery. Hans’s observation here ran counter to his own wishes; and because it ran counter, he was the more apt to credit it. A cause began to shape itself in his mind, anxiously, as if he were feeling along the edge of a wound.

Had something happened in his absence? Or was it only the slow growth of fear? Was there something in the new life arranged for Mirah—something that, while seeming a refuge, carried its own humiliations? Or—this struck him most sharply—had Mordecai, against his habitual reticence, spoken to her of those cherished hopes in which Deronda himself was made to figure? Had Mirah, with her quick sensitiveness, been hurt by finding that her brother’s will, his tenacity of vision, pressed upon Deronda’s friendship like a claim? Might she have feared that what looked like equal regard was, in truth, pity and self-suppression—Deronda lowering himself out of compassion? For beneath Mirah’s quiet renunciation there lay a hunger—no, a thirst—for the acknowledgment that meets one soul on level ground. And if that tribute of equality was sweet, then its withdrawal—or the suspicion that it had never been sincere—must bring a pain she would hide rather than display.

In that insight into Mirah’s nature Deronda was not mistaken. He understood, by an affinity of feeling, what protest lay latent in her gratitude—gratitude sharpened by the memory of a life in which people had thought any coarse charity enough for her. But he was wrong to imagine that Mordecai had broken his reserve. Mordecai had breathed to no one but Deronda the history of their relation, or his confidence about Deronda’s origin; not only because those subjects were sacred, but because he had discerned Deronda’s shrinking from any handling of his birth, and would not thrust speech where a silence was a protection.

Mirah herself had once let her own tendency betray her.

“Ezra,” she had said one day, with a half-smile at herself, “how is it? I am continually going to speak to Mr. Deronda as if he were a Jew.”

Mordecai smiled quietly. “It is because he treats us as if he were our brother. But he loves not to have the difference of birth dwelt upon.”

“He has never lived with his parents, Mr. Hans says,” Mirah continued—because to her, whose own life had been all torn from the natural shelters, such things were not idle curiosity but the search for some clue to every dear person’s wound or strength.

“Seek not to know such things from Mr. Hans,” Mordecai said gravely, and laid his hand on her curls in the familiar blessing-like gesture. “What Daniel Deronda wishes us to know about himself is for him to tell us.”

Mirah felt rebuked. Yet the rebuke from Mordecai did not humiliate her; it lifted her. It made her proud—proud of his dignity, proud of being near a soul that could command itself so.

And that pride, that reverence, ran deep. One day, calling at the Chelsea house on her way home, she found the little mother alone, and her heart—tired with holding itself in—turned to speech.

“I see no one so great as my brother,” Mirah said. “It is difficult to think that he belongs to the same world as those people I used to live amongst. I told you once they made life seem like a madhouse; but when I am with Ezra, he makes me feel that his life is a great good, though he has suffered so much. Not like me, who wanted to die because I had suffered a little—and only for a little while. His soul is so full, it is impossible for him to wish for death as I did.”

Her voice, always gentle, carried a dove-like melancholy—as if tenderness itself were weighted. She went on, trying to shape what she felt into something that could be shared.

“I get the same sort of feeling from him that I got yesterday, when I was tired, and came home through the park after the sweet rain had fallen and the sunshine lay on the grass and flowers. Everything in the sky and under the sky looked so pure and beautiful that the weariness and trouble and folly seemed only a small part of what is; and I became more patient and hopeful.”

Mrs. Meyrick, who had been stitching, let her work fall. She looked at Mirah more closely. At a little distance Mirah’s attitude—hands and feet crossed, her curls pressed flat as if she had smoothed herself into calm—might have been taken for serenity carved and coloured. But to Mrs. Meyrick’s motherly scrutiny there was in her face a new strain, as if patience and hopefulness now needed to be summoned like music to keep back an oppressive silence.

“Is there any fresh trouble on your mind, my dear?” she asked, with that quick, practical tenderness which does not pretend not to see.

Mirah hesitated. “I am too ready to speak of troubles, I think. It seems unkind to put anything painful into other people’s minds—unless one were sure it would hinder something worse. And perhaps I am too hasty and fearful.”

“Oh, my dear,” Mrs. Meyrick said, leaning forward, “mothers are made to like pain and trouble for the sake of their children. Is it because the singing lessons are so few, and likely to fall off when the season comes to an end? Success can’t come all at once.”

“No,” Mirah said, gently shaking her head. “I have been a little disappointed because so many ladies said they wanted me to give them—or their daughters—lessons, and then I never heard of them again. But perhaps after the holidays I shall teach in some schools. Besides—you know—I am as rich as a princess now. I have not touched the hundred pounds Mrs. Klesmer gave me. And I should never be afraid that Ezra would be in want of anything, because there is Mr. Deronda; and he said, ‘It is the chief honor of my life that your brother will share anything with me.’ Oh, no! Ezra and I can have no fears for each other about such things as food and clothing.”

“But there is some other fear on your mind,” Mrs. Meyrick said, her voice lower, as if making room for what must be spoken. “A fear of something that may disturb your peace. Don’t be forecasting evil unless it is what you can guard against. Anxiety is good for nothing if we can’t turn it into a defense. But there’s no defense against all the things that might be. Have you any more reason for being anxious now than you had a month ago?”

“Yes, I have,” Mirah answered, and the words came as if they had been held down and now rose against her will. “I have kept it from Ezra. I have not dared to tell him. Pray forgive me that I can’t do without telling you. I have more reason for being anxious. It is five days ago now. I am quite sure I saw my father.”

Mrs. Meyrick seemed to draw herself into a smaller space, folding her arms tight across her chest and leaning forward, as if bodily bracing against the impulse to fling hard words—like stones—at that distant figure.

“The year has changed him,” Mirah continued, her voice steady but thin. “He had already been much altered and worn in the time before I left him. You remember I said how he used sometimes to cry. He was always excited one way or the other. I have told Ezra everything that I told you, and he says my father had taken to gambling, which makes people easily distressed, and then again exalted. And now—it was only a moment that I saw him—his face was more haggard, and his clothes were shabby. He was with a much worse-looking man, who carried something, and they were hurrying along after an omnibus.”

“Well, child,” Mrs. Meyrick said, with anxious eagerness held in check by her effort to be calm, “he did not see you, I hope?”
