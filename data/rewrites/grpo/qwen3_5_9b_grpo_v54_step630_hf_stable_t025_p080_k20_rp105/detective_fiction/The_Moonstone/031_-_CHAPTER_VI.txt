The envelope arrived with the stiffness of a formal decree, bearing the seal of Miss Clack's indignation. Inside, she presented her compliments to Mr. Franklin Blake, declaring herself utterly unequal to the task of expanding upon such a terrible event as Lady Verinder's death. Instead, she had appended to her manuscript a collection of "copious Extracts" from various publications, hoping these would sound like a trumpet blast in the ears of her kinsman, guiding him through the darkness of the mystery.

Mr. Blake's reply was brief and cool. He thanked her for the chapter but politely declined the extracts. He made no mention of his personal distaste for such sensationalist literature, choosing instead to state plainly that these additions were unnecessary for the purpose he had set out to achieve. He sought only the truth as he saw it, not the embellishments of others.

Undeterred, Miss Clack acknowledged the return of her materials with a mixture of hurt and resolve. She reminded him gently that she was a Christian and that it was impossible for him to truly offend her. Yet, she persisted in her deep interest in Mr. Blake, pledging to offer her extracts again should illness ever lay him low. For now, however, she asked a crucial question: before she began the final chapters of her narrative, might she be permitted to complete her work by incorporating the light shed by later discoveries regarding the Moonstone?

Mr. Blake found himself disappointed in having to disappoint her again. He reiterated the instructions he had given at the very start of her narrative. He requested that she limit her account strictly to her own individual experiences as recorded in her diary. Any subsequent discoveries were matters for other pens, those of actual witnesses who could speak from the scene, rather than the speculative musings of a correspondent.

Miss Clack wrote again, her tone sharpening with frustration. She was painfully conscious that she ought to feel "put down" in worldly terms, yet she had learned Perseverance in the School of Adversity. Her true object, she insisted, was to know if Mr. Blake prohibited the appearance of their correspondence within her narrative itself. Some explanation of the position in which his interference had placed her seemed due on grounds of common justice. She was anxious that her letters should be produced to speak for themselves, standing as evidence of her dedication.

Mr. Blake agreed to her proposal, but with a clear condition: he considered this intimation of consent as the formal closing of their correspondence. There would be no further letters.

Before the exchange ended, Miss Clack felt it her Christian duty to inform him that his last letter, evidently intended to wound her pride, had failed in its objective. She affectionately requested that he retire to the privacy of his own room and consider whether the training required to elevate a poor, weak woman above the reach of insult was not worthy of greater admiration than he currently felt. On receiving an acknowledgment of this sentiment, she solemnly pledged to return the complete series of her extracts.

To this final letter, no answer was received. Comment was needless.
