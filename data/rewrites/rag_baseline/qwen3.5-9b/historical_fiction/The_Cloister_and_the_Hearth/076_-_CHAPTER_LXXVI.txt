When little Gerard was barely three months old, a messenger arrived, flushed with haste from Tergou, bearing news for Catherine. "Go back now," she commanded him, her voice sharp with resolve, "and tell them I cannot come, and I will not: they have taken Kate." The boy departed, and Catherine resumed her own lamentation. "There, child, I must go; they are all thrown into confusion, at sixes and sevens. This is the third time they have asked, and tomorrow my man will come himself to drag me home by the ear, with a flea in his pocket." She then recounted her trials with infants, instructing Margaret on how to manage every conceivable emergency, and pressed money into her hands. Margaret declined with gratitude, but Catherine insisted, her tone turning to anger. Margaret offered excuses so logically sound that Catherine dismissed them with a calm, icy contempt; to her mind, they lacked the necessary feminine spirit. "Come, out with your heart," she demanded, "as we part today; and perhaps we shall never see one another's face again." "Oh! Mother, do not say that." "Alack, girl, I have seen it so often; it comes into my mind now at every parting. When I was your age, I never harbored such a thought. Nay, we were all destined to live forever then: so, get it out." "Well, then, Mother—I would rather not have told you—but your Cornelis must say to me, 'So you are come to share with us, eh, mistress?' Those were his words. I told him I would be very sorry." "Beshrew his ill tongue! What does it signify? He will never know." "Most likely he would sooner or later. But whether or not, I will take no begrudging bounty from any family; unless I saw my child starving—and Heaven only knows what I might do." "Nay, Mother, give me but thy love—I prize that above silver, and they grudge me not that, by all I can find—for not a single stiver of money will I take from your house." "You are a foolish lass. Why, were it me, I'd take it just to spite him." "No, you would not. You and I are apples from the same tree." Catherine yielded with a gracious air; and when the moment of separation truly arrived, embraces and tears erupted from both sides.

Once she was gone, the child cried bitterly. All attempts to pacify him failing, Margaret suspected a pin and searched between his clothes and his skin, finding a gold angel uncomfortably lodged against his spine. "There, now, Gerard," she said to the babe, "I thought Granny gave in rather suddenly." She took the coin, wrapped it in a scrap of linen, and laid it at the bottom of her box, bidding the infant observe that she could be as resolute as Granny herself. Catherine had informed Eli of Margaret's foolish pride and how she had baffled it. Eli declared Margaret was right and Catherine wrong. Catherine tossed her head in disagreement; Eli pondered silently.

Margaret was not without domestic anxieties. She still had two men to feed and could not work as hard as she had done. She had enough to do to keep the house, the child, and cook for them all. But she had a little money laid by, and she used to tell her child that his father would be home to help them before it was spent. With these bright hopes and that treasury of bliss, her boy, she spent some happy months. Time wore on; and no Gerard came. Stranger still, no news of him reached them. Then her mind grew disquieted, and contrary to her practical nature, she was often lost in sad reverie, sighing in silence. And while her heart was troubled, her money melted away. Thus it was that one day she found the cupboard empty and looked into her dependents' faces; at the sight of them, her bosom swelled with pity. She appealed to the baby whether she could let Grandfather and poor old Martin go hungry, and went to retrieve Catherine's angel. As she unfolded the linen, a tear of gentle mortification fell upon it. She sent Martin out to exchange it. While he was gone, a Frenchman arrived with one of the dealers in illuminated manuscripts, who had previously offered her such a poor price. He told her he was employed by his sovereign to collect masterpieces for her Book of Hours. She showed him the two finest things she possessed; he was charmed by one of them—the flowers, raspberries, and creeping vines shaded by Margaret Van Eyck. He offered her an unheard-of sum. "Nay, flout not my need, good stranger," she replied; "three mouths reside in this house, and none to fill them but me." Curious arithmetic! Left out Number One. "I'd outdo thee not, fair mistress. My princess charged me strictly, 'Seek the best craftsmen'; but I will make no hard bargains; make them content with me, and me with them." The next minute Margaret was on her knees, kissing little Gerard in the cradle and showering four gold pieces upon him again and again, relating the entire occurrence to him in very broken Dutch. "And oh, what a good princess! Wasn't she? We will pray for her, won't we, my lambkin, when we are old enough?"

Martin returned, furious. "They will not change it. I trow they think I stole it." "I am beholden to thee," said Margaret hastily, almost snatching the bundle from Martin, wrapping it up again, and restoring it to its hiding place. Ere these unexpected funds were spent, she returned to her ironing and starching. In the midst of this, Martin sickened and died after an illness of nine days. Nearly all her money went to bury him decently. He was gone, leaving an empty chair by her fireside; for he had preferred the hearth to the sun since the Busy Body had departed. Margaret would not allow anyone to sit in this chair now. Yet whenever she let her eye dwell too long upon it, vacant, it was sure to cost her a tear.

Now there was no one to carry her linen home. To do it herself, she must leave little Gerard in the charge of a neighbor, but she dared not trust such a treasure to mortal hands; besides, she could not bear him out of her sight for hours on end. So she set inquiries on foot for a boy to carry her basket on Saturdays and Mondays. A plump, fresh-colored youth named Luke Peterson, who looked fifteen but was actually eighteen, came forward, blushing and twiddling his cap, to ask if a man would not serve her turn as well as a boy. Before he spoke, she was telling herself, "This boy will just do." But she took the cue and said, "Nay; but a man will perhaps seek more than I can well pay." "Not I," said Luke warmly. "Why, Mistress Margaret, I am your neighbor, and I do very well at coopering. I can carry your basket for you before or after my day's work, and welcome. You have no need to pay me anything. 'Tisn't as if we were strangers, ye know." "Why, Master Luke, I know your face, for that matter; but I cannot call to mind that ever a word passed between us." "Oh yes, you did, Mistress Margaret. What, have you forgotten? One day you were trying to carry your baby and also your pitcher full of water; and I said, 'Give me the baby to carry.' 'Nay,' says you, 'I'll give you the pitcher, and keep the bairn myself;' and I carried the pitcher home, and you took it from me at this door, and you said to me, 'I am muckle obliged to you, young man,' with such a sweet voice—not like the folk in this street speak to a body." "I do mind now, Master Luke; and methinks it was the least I could say." "Well, Mistress Margaret, if you will say as much every time I carry your basket, I care not how often I bear it, nor how far." "Nay, nay," said Margaret, coloring faintly. "I would not put upon good nature. You are young, Master Luke, and kindly. Say I give you your supper on Saturday night, when you bring the linen home, and your dawn-meat on Monday; would that make us anywhere near even?" "As you please; only say not I sought a couple of diets for such a trifle as yon."

With chubby-faced Luke's timely assistance and the health and strength which Heaven granted this poor young woman to balance her many ills, the house ran pretty smoothly for a while. But the heart became more and more troubled by Gerard's long, and now most mysterious, silence. Then that mental torturer, Suspense, began to tear her heavy heart with his hot pincers, until she cried often and vehemently, "Oh, that I could know the worst."

Whilst she was in this state, one day she heard a heavy step mounting the stairs. She started and trembled. "That is no step that I know. Ill tidings?" The door opened, and an unexpected visitor, Eli, came in, looking grave and kind. Margaret eyed him in silence, her agitation increasing. "Girl," he said, "the Skipper is come back."
