I come now to what may well be the most astonishing chapter of our entire journey—a moment so improbable, so full of wonder, that it seems almost beyond belief. Yet it happened, and I record it here as truthfully as I can.

I was walking ahead of Sir Henry and Good, along the banks of the stream that flows from the oasis into the vast, hungry sands of the desert. The sun beat down, the air shimmered, and everything seemed still and quiet—until suddenly I stopped in my tracks, rubbing my eyes as if to clear a dream.

There, not twenty yards before me, stood a hut. It was nestled beneath the shade of a fig tree, facing the water, built with grass and woven branches in the style of the Kafir people, but with a proper door—no mere hole like a bee’s nest. “What in the world,” I muttered to myself, “could a hut be doing here?”

Even as I spoke, the door creaked open—and out limped a man. A white man. Clad in animal skins, with a long, thick black beard, and one leg clearly injured. I blinked hard. I must have been hallucinating. No hunter would ever come this far. And certainly none would settle here. Yet there he stood, real as life.

I stared. So did Good when he caught sight of him. Then Sir Henry and Good came up, breathless from the heat.

“Look at that!” I called. “Is that a white man—or am I losing my mind?”

Sir Henry looked. Good looked. Then, with a cry that rang across the silence, the bearded man hobbled forward, arms raised, and collapsed at Sir Henry’s feet as if struck by a sudden blow.

In an instant, Sir Henry was beside him. “Great Powers!” he cried. “It’s George! My brother George!”

At that moment, another figure emerged from the hut—also dressed in skins, carrying a rifle. He ran toward us, then froze upon seeing me.

“Macumazahn!” he shouted. “Don’t you know me? It’s Jim—the hunter! I lost the note you gave me for the Baas, and we’ve been here nearly two years!”

He dropped to his knees, rolling over in tears of joy. “You careless scoundrel!” I said, laughing. “You should be well sjambocked”—that is, skinned alive.

Meanwhile, George Curtis had regained his senses. He rose slowly, and the two brothers embraced, gripping each other’s hands so tightly they could scarcely speak. Whatever past quarrels they’d had—perhaps over a woman, though I never asked—it all melted away in that moment.

“My dear old fellow,” burst out Sir Henry, “I thought you were dead. I crossed Solomon’s Mountains looking for you. I’d given up hope. And now—here you are, stranded in the desert like an old vulture.”

“I tried to cross those mountains nearly two years ago,” George replied, voice weak from disuse. “But a boulder fell on my leg and crushed it. I couldn’t go forward or back. I stayed here, hoping someone might come.”

Then I stepped forward. “How do you do, Mr. Neville?” I said.

He looked at me, stunned. “Why—aren’t you Hunter Quatermain? And Good too? Hold on a minute—I’m getting dizzy again. This is too strange, too wonderful. I thought you’d long since forgotten me, and that you were settled comfortably in England.”

That evening, by the campfire, George told us his story—every bit as dramatic as our own.

Less than two years before, he had set out from Sitanda’s Kraal, determined to reach Suliman’s Berg. He’d heard from the natives about a descent down the mountains shaped like a ladder—which turned out to be a better route than the one marked in Dom Silvestra’s old map. But Jim had lost the note I’d sent him, so he never knew of our plans.

They endured terrible hardships in the desert, but finally reached the oasis. On the day they arrived, George sat by the stream while Jim gathered honey from a nest of stingless bees atop a bank above him. In doing so, Jim disturbed a massive rock, which tumbled down and crushed George’s right leg.

From that day on, he was crippled. He couldn’t move forward or return. He chose to stay, rather than risk dying in the wasteland. But food was not lacking. They had ammunition, and the oasis drew game at night—antelope, deer, even lions. They hunted, trapped, and used the hides for clothing when their clothes wore out.

“We lived like Robinson Crusoe and Friday,” George said, “hoping someone would come. Only last night, we decided Jim would leave me and try to reach Sitanda’s Kraal for help. He was to go tomorrow—but I didn’t expect to see him again. And now—*you*, of all people—appear, just when I least expected it. It’s the most miraculous thing I’ve ever known.”

Sir Henry then recounted our adventures, telling the tale late into the night.

When I showed George some of the diamonds, he gasped. “Well, at least you’ve got something for your troubles—besides me.”

Sir Henry laughed. “The diamonds belong to Quatermain and Good. That was part of the agreement.”

That made me think. After speaking with Good, I told Sir Henry that both of us wished him to take a third share of the treasure—or, if he refused, that it go to his brother, who had suffered more than anyone else in pursuit of it.

Eventually, he agreed. Though George didn’t learn of this until later.

Here, I believe, I shall end my account. Our journey back across the desert was grueling, especially with George’s weakened leg constantly breaking apart, sending splinters of bone through the flesh. But we managed it, though repeating the details would only echo what we’d already endured.

Six months after returning to Sitanda’s Kraal—where we found our guns and supplies untouched, though the old caretaker was shocked we’d survived—we were once more safe at my home near Durban on the Berea, where I write these words.

And so I bid farewell to all who have followed me through this most extraordinary adventure.

P.S.—Just as I finished writing, a Kafir approached along my orange tree avenue, carrying a letter in a split stick. It was from Sir Henry. Since it speaks for itself, I reproduce it in full:

October 1, 1884
Brayley Hall, Yorkshire

My Dear Quatermain,

I send this a few mails back to tell you we’ve all arrived safely in England. We landed at Southampton and went straight to London. You should have seen Good the very next day—perfectly shaved, wearing a frock coat that fit like a glove, brand-new spectacles, and all the trimmings. I took him for a walk in the park, where I met some acquaintances, and immediately told them about his “beautiful white legs.” He’s furious—especially since some malicious soul printed it in a society paper.

Now, business: Good and I took the diamonds to Streeter’s for valuation, as we agreed. I’m afraid to tell you what they said—they’re worth more than I can bear to think of. They admit it’s largely guesswork, since stones of such quantity have never been offered on the market before. But they say (except for a few of the largest) they’re flawless, equal to the finest Brazilian gems. When I asked if they’d buy them, they said no—too much for their capacity. Instead, they suggest selling them gradually over many years, to avoid flooding the market. Still, they’ve offered £180,000 for just a small portion.

You must come home, Quatermain. We need you to handle things—especially if you insist on giving George that third share, which doesn’t belong to me. As for Good—he’s *no good*. His time is taken up with shaving and other vain pursuits. But he’s still haunted by Foulata. He told me he hasn’t seen a woman since returning—either in beauty or expression.

Come home, my old friend. Buy a house near here. You’ve earned it. There’s a lovely property nearby—perfect for you. Come soon. If you leave on receipt of this, you’ll arrive by Christmas. I’ve booked you a room with me. Good and George will be here, and so—by the way—will your boy Harry. I’ve had him down for a week’s shooting. He’s a cool young man. Shot me in the leg, removed the pellets himself, then remarked how useful it is to have a medical student on a hunting party!

Good-bye, old boy. I can’t say more. But I know you’ll come—not just for the diamonds, but for the sake of friendship.

Your sincere friend,

P.S.—The tusks of the great bull that killed Khiva now hang in the hall, above the buffalo horns you gave me. They look magnificent. And the axe I used to chop off Twala’s head is mounted above my desk. I wish we’d brought the chainmail coats. Don’t lose Foulata’s basket—the one you used to carry the diamonds. H.C.

Today is Tuesday. A steamer sails Friday. I think I must accept Curtis’s invitation. I’ll sail for England—just to see you, Harry, and to oversee the printing of this story. It’s a task I won’t entrust to anyone else.
