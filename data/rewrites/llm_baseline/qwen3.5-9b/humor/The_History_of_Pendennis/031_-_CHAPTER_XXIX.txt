Arthur Pendennis's journey to London marked a decisive turning point. He had to leave behind the quiet woods and rocky shores of the West, the endless chatter of Clavering, and the dull, comfortable existence of Fairoaks. Now, aboard the *Alacrity* coach, he hurtled toward the capital, ready to confront the world and seize his fortune.

As the carriage thundered through the night, escaping the welcoming gates of home, Arthur's mind raced with schemes for the future. He dreamed of prudence, success, and fame. He knew, with growing confidence, that he was superior to many who had previously outrun him. His first failure had stung him deeply, sparking remorse and deep reflection, yet it had not shattered his spirit or his self-esteem. A hundred eager fantasies and restless hopes kept him wide awake.

How much wiser he felt now compared to twelve months prior, when he had traveled this same road between Oxford and Cambridge! His thoughts drifted with tender nostalgia to his mother. He recalled her blessing him as they parted, trusting and loving him despite his past errors. "Blessings on her!" he prayed, gazing upward at the stars. "O Heaven, grant me strength to work, to endure, to remain honest, to resist temptation, and to prove myself worthy of such a devoted soul!" Perhaps she was awake too, offering purer prayers for her beloved boy. Her love was his talisman, the shield against danger.

There was also Laura. He wished he could carry her affection with him, but she had rejected him, judging him unworthy. With shame and regret, he admitted that her nature was far nobler than his own. Yet, strangely, he felt glad to be free. "I am not good enough for such a creature," he confessed to himself. He drew back from her spotless beauty and innocence as one retreats from something terrifying. He felt unfit for such a partner, much like a reformed prodigy who avoids the church he once attended—not out of hatred, but because he feels he has no rightful claim to its purity.

These thoughts occupied him until the crisp dawn of an October morning. When the coach halted at the old breakfast stop at B——, where he had enjoyed dozens of hearty meals during his school and college travels, Arthur woke feeling remarkably refreshed.

As they departed, the sun burst forth brilliantly. The pace quickened, the horn blared, and the milestones flashed by. Pen smoked, joked with the guard and fellow passengers, and mingled with travelers on the familiar road. The scene grew busier and more vibrant with every mile. Finally, the last team of grey horses emerged from H——, and the coach roared into London.

Who among the young hasn't felt that electric thrill entering the vast beast of a city? Hundreds of carriages, packed with thousands of men, surged toward the metropolis. "Here is my place," Arthur thought. "Here begins my battle, where I must fight and conquer, or fall. I have been nothing but a boy and a dawdler thus far. Oh, I long, I long to prove I can be a man." From his perch on the roof, the eager youth gazed down upon the sprawling city with the intense longing a soldier feels on the eve of a campaign.

Along the way, Pen had struck up a conversation with a cheerful passenger in a shabby cloak. This man, a reporter for a London newspaper who had recently covered a major wrestling match in the West, spoke passionately about literature. He seemed to know every leading author of the day intimately, discussing figures like Tom Campbell, Tom Hood, and Sydney Smith as if they were his oldest friends. Passing Brompton, he pointed out Mr. Hurtle, the renowned reviewer, walking with his umbrella. Pen craned his neck to get a good look at the giant. "He's a Boniface man," Pen noted. Mr. Doolan, the reporter, confirmed it with a nod, adding that he knew Hurtle very well. To have seen the great Mr. Hurtle was, Pen believed, a true honor.

Still, Arthur held a naive faith in authors, critics, and editors. Even Wagg, whose books didn't strike him as intellectual masterpieces, he secretly revered as a successful writer. Mentioning that he'd met Wagg in the country, Doolan revealed that the famous novelist earned three hundred pounds per volume. Pen immediately began calculating whether he, too, might earn five thousand a year.

Upon arriving at the Gloster Coffee-house, Arthur's first encounter was with his old friend Harry Foker, who arrived prancing down Arlington Street behind a massive cab-horse. Foker wore white kid gloves and white reins, and nature had already gifted him with a significant tuft on his chin. A tiny cab-boy, a replacement for the retired Stoopid, hung precariously behind the vehicle, knock-kneed and clad in tight leather breeches.

Foker eyed the dusty coach and the sweating horses of the *Alacrity*, recalling his own past journeys. "What, Foker?" cried Pendennis. "Hullo! Pen, my boy!" shouted the other, waving his whip in a gesture of friendship. Arthur was delighted to see his eccentric friend's kind face. Mr. Doolan respected Pen immensely for knowing such a distinguished driver. Pen was thrilled to be free and in London. He invited Doolan to dinner at the Covent Garden Coffee-house, where he was staying, hailed a cab, and sped off in high spirits. He rejoiced to see the bustling waiters and the polite, bowing landlord. He asked for the landlady, missed seeing the old Boots, and wished he could shake hands with everyone. He had a hundred pounds in his pocket. Dressing in his finest attire, he dined modestly in the coffee-room with a single pint of sherry (determined to be frugal) before heading to the adjacent theater.

The lights, music, crowds, and general merriment enchanted Pen, as they often do for young men fresh from college and the countryside. He laughed at the jokes and cheered the songs, delighting some weary regulars in the boxes who had long lost their zest for the evening's entertainment and were happy to witness someone so fresh and amused. After the first act, he strutted through the lobby as if he belonged to the highest circles of fashion. Who among the tired frequenters of London's pavement doesn't recall having similar youthful delusions, wishing to relive them?

Young Foker appeared again, an ardent devotee of pleasure. Walking alongside Grandy Tiptoff of the Household Brigade, Lord Tiptoff's brother, and Lord Colchicum—a venerable peer who had been a man of pleasure since the French Revolution—they spotted Pen. Foker eagerly ushered him into his private box. Seated there was a lady with the longest ringlets and the fairest shoulders: Miss Blenkinsop, the eminent actress of high comedy. Snoozing in a wig in the back row was her father, the veteran Blenkinsop. Described in theatrical prints as "the useful Blenkinsop" and "that old favorite of the public," he played the role of the heavy father both on stage and in private life.

Meanwhile, back at Fairoaks, Mrs. Pendennis was asleep, wondering if her dear Arthur had rested well after his journey. In London, however, the farce was proceeding successfully. Mrs. Leary, dressed in a Hussar jacket and braided pantaloons, captivated the audience with her archness, lovely figure, and delightful ballads.

Being new to the town, Pen wanted to enjoy Mrs. Leary's performance, but the others in the box were too busy chatting. Tiptoff knew the origin of her maillots; Colchicum remembered seeing her debut in '14. Miss Blenkinsop claimed she sang out of tune, causing pain and astonishment to Pen, who thought her an angelic beauty singing like a nightingale. When Hoppus appeared as Sir Harcourt Featherby, the young hero of the play, the gentlemen declared him stale, and Tiptoff suggested throwing Miss Blenkinsop's bouquet to him.

"Not for the world!" cried the actress. "Lord Colchicum gave it to me."

Pen remembered the nobleman's name. Bowing and blushing, he thanked Lord Colchicum for proposing him to the Megatherium Club at the request of his uncle, Major Pendennis.

"What, you're Wigsby's nephew, are you?" asked the peer. "Forgive me, we always call him Wigsby." Pen blushed to hear his venerable uncle referred to so casually. "We balloted you last week, didn't we? Last Wednesday night. Your uncle wasn't there."

Delightful news! Pen expressed profound gratitude and delivered a handsome speech of thanks, which the peer listened to intently through his double opera-glass. Pen was electrified by the prospect of joining this polite club.

"Don't always stare at that box, you naughty creature," scolded Miss Blenkinsop.

"She's a devilish fine woman, that Mirabel," said Tiptoff, "though Mirabel was a damned fool to marry her."

"A stupid old spooney," agreed the peer.

"Mirabel!" exclaimed Pendennis.

"Ha! Ha!" laughed Harry Foker. "We've heard of her before, haven't we, Pen?"
