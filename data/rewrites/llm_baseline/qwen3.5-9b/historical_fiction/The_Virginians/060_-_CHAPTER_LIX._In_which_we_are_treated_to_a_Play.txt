In the grand tapestry of human experience, the novelist finds that the mundane threads of daily occupation weave but a small portion of the narrative. When dealing with the profession of arms—where courage, peril, devotion, and heroic sacrifice naturally abound—the writer may confidently recount the actual affairs of life. Yet, for the merchant selling sugar and spice, the lawyer poring over dusty legal codes, or the tailor measuring breeches while seated cross-legged on a board, the raw material of their trade offers little fertile ground for storytelling. To chronicle the gritty reality of hobnailed shoes and eighteenpence-a-day wages would be tedious indeed. Even in the era we inhabit, poets romanticize shepherds in pink breeches and chintz waistcoats dancing before their flocks, ignoring the truth. Thus, with the notable exception of war, the novelist cannot expect to dwell upon the drudgery of commerce, law, theology, or drapery. Instead, authors must depict men stripped of their professions, revealing their passions, loves, laughter, and hatreds, while relegating their trades to the background, understood but unseen.

Consequently, our gaze wanders only through the theatre-lobbies, coffee-houses, pleasure-gardens, and festive rooms of life. Meanwhile, the serious world toils in its chambers and looms, oblivious to our observations. We watch Corydon cart litter and thresh barley only to pause for a moment of romance with Phillis; we glimpse Ancillula washing the nursery and serving breakfast before she can briefly meet Boopis, the policeman, through the railings. All day, the laborer beats the stale pavement, snatching only fleeting kisses or hurried pies. It is only when characters are disengaged from their labors that we can unite them with a disengaged reader.

Madame de Bernstein and the polished circles of White's and Arthur's had grown increasingly cold toward poor Harry Warrington. The Ring, the Mall, and the gaming tables no longer recognized him. Determined to conquer the world's indifference, the old lady urged her nephew to brace himself. "You pull a long face, Harry," she chided, dismissing his complaints. "Everyone must endure impertinences. If you receive a blow now that you are fallen, bear it with a smile and seek revenge later. *Moi qui vous parle!* Have I not eaten humble pie? Swallow yours, become the Clever Youth, and win back your lost place. Attend every rout, be civil to all women, yet display your spirit. With economy and your brother's generosity, you can maintain a genteel figure. Seek out the merchants in the City; they do not notice the loss of favor at Court. Visit my Lady Yarmouth; she favored you greatly!"

Yet, the woman who once embraced Harry with eager affection now met him with icy disdain. Her smiles vanished, replaced by peevishness and patronizing scorn. Before her guests, she cast gibes that flushed Harry's honest face with humiliation, striking deep wounds in his gentle heart. Her servants ignored him; Lady Yarmouth remained indisposed or occupied, refusing the warm hospitality she once offered. Devastated, Harry poured his sorrows to Mrs. Lambert. Sitting glumly in a window-seat while the ladies bustled about their domestic duties, he confessed his aunt's cruelty.

"I know you are the best people alive," he murmured to them, "the kindest, and I must be the dullest company in the world."

"You are not very lively, Harry," observed Miss Hetty, beginning to distance herself. "Is this the hero I thought you?"

"If he is unhappy, why should he be lively?" Theo asked gently. "He has a good heart, pained by his friends' desertion."

"I would rather show spirit than appear hurt," Hetty declared, clenching her fists. "I would smile even if that horrible old painted woman boxed my ears. She is dreadful, Mamma. You agree, Theo?"

Theo turned red. "I may not like her, but I will not call names before Harry's face."

"Provoking thing! You are always right!" Hetty cried, realizing her rudeness. "Harry, it was wrong of me to remark on your relations."

"It doesn't matter about the others," Harry insisted, rising with a flush of anger. "But it is hard that this one should turn upon me. I grew so fond of her. It drives me mad when those I love reject me."

"Suppose George did so?" Hetty suggested innocently.

"You are very clever, Hetty, but you do not know my brother," Harry snapped, his resolve hardening. "You would not dare suppose my brother George capable of anything unkind!" His cheeks burned crimson.

Hetty turned pale, offering no reply, only dropping a stiff curtsey as he departed. Later, whispering confidences to Theo, she lamented, "Oh! I thought it would be so happy to see him every day. Now that I do, I keep offending him. He isn't clever, Theo, but oh! Isn't he good, kind, and brave? Look how handsome he was when angry!"

Theo sighed. "Silly little thing, you try to make him look handsome."

Indeed, the dynamic among the youths shifted. General Lambert's nickname for his wife, "Bo," served as a rebuke to her sentimental matchmaking instincts. While little Hetty harbored fantasies, Harry had clearly moved on, superseded by his own maturity and lack of fortune.

"Now he is poor, we will show him the door," Mrs. Lambert remarked.

"That is what I always do, isn't it, Molly? Turn my back on friends in distress?" the General asked.

"No, my dear! I am a goose, I admit," she conceded, dabbing her eyes. "Let the poor boy come to us. Ours is almost the only house in this selfish city where he can find solace."

True to his nature, Martin Lambert opened his doors. Whenever Harry needed a meal or an evening's diversion, he found a corner at the Lambert table. George, too, was welcomed, though initially with more reserve. Cold and mistrustful compared to his twin, George nonetheless warmed to the family's inherent goodness. He saw Harry's misery and pitied his brother's depression. In his characteristic sarcasm, George would mock his own misfortune to cheer Harry, joking that Florac should have taken his scalp instead of the Indian's, allowing Harry to remain the favorite of the world and marry Maria.

"I never know if you laugh at me or yourself, George," Harry admitted.

"Precisely my case, Harry," George replied. "But I know there was never a better brother, nor better people than the Lamberts."

"And if I am unhappy, 'tis not your fault," Harry continued. "'Tis fate, 'tis having nothing to do. I must work, but how? Until I find purpose, George, I shall be miserable."

"We shall ask Mother," George said.

Later, Harry expressed alarm at the idea of returning to Virginia. "I love the girls here like sisters... but I don't want to hunt, shoot ducks, plant tobacco, and play whist forever. What else is there to do? For a clever man like you, George, you'd make your way anywhere. But for a poor fellow like me, what chance is there?"

They smoked their pipes in friendly silence, reflecting on their divergent paths. Eventually, the brothers separated somewhat; Harry avoided George's studies, sitting silently while his brother worked. They moved residences, escaping Madame de Bernstein's disapproval. George found joy in Sir Hans Sloane's new reading room in Montagu House, settling in Bloomsbury near Hampstead. The Lamberts, following Lord Wrotham's arrival in Mayfair, also relocated to Soho, remaining close neighbors. Harry frequently sought refuge at the Lambert household, joined occasionally by George, who preferred their company to the gambling circles of his aunt or the noisy coffee-houses. Together, these strangers in London explored the city's honest amusements: Vauxhall, Ranelagh, Marybone Gardens, and the Tower.

Martin Lambert ensured his children enjoyed innocent pleasures, and George, generous and open-handed, treated his friends, especially those who had shown kindness to his brother. Above all, Mr. Warrington possessed a profound passion for the theater. Having rarely enjoyed it in Virginia or Quebec, he found London's two great houses irresistible. Eager to share his delight, he invited his friends, who were equally willing. Should they choose Drury Lane for Garrick and Shakespeare, or Covent Garden for the new sensation?

A brilliant star had risen: Mr. John Home of Scotland. His tragedy was hailed as the most classic and elegant since antiquity. Unlike Shakespeare, whose works contained barbarisms that shocked polite audiences, Home refined grief and passion without descending into buffoonery. Admired by the highest ranks and celebrated by wits across Britain, including the famous Mr. Gray of Cambridge, the play was a triumph. Even in Edinburgh, the Scots shouted from the pit, "Where's Wully Shakspeare noo?"

"I should like to see the man who could beat Willy Shakspeare," the General laughed.

"Mere national prejudice," Mr. Warrington countered.

"Pooh, pooh! You've cried more over Mr. Sam Richardson than ever over Shakespeare, Molly!" the General teased. "Few women truly love Shakespeare; they claim to, but they don't."

"Oh, Papa!" cried three ladies, throwing up their hands.
