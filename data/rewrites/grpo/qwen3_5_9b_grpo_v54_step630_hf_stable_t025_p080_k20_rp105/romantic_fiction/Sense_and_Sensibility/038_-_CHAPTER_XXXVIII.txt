Mrs. Jennings spoke of Edward's conduct with such warmth that it bordered on adoration, yet only Elinor and Marianne possessed the depth of understanding to grasp its true value. They alone knew the narrow margin he had walked to avoid disobedience, and the meager consolation awaiting him beyond the simple knowledge of having done right—a consolation all the more poignant given the total loss of friends and fortune. Elinor took pride in his unshakeable integrity; Marianne, overwhelmed by compassion for his suffering, forgave every fault he had committed. Yet, despite the restoration of their confidence following this public revelation, neither found themselves eager to dwell upon the subject when alone. Elinor avoided it on principle, fearing that Marianne's too-warm, too-positive assurances would only cement in her mind a belief of Edward's continued affection for herself that she desperately wished to dissolve. For Marianne, the courage to discuss the topic soon evaporated; every attempt to converse on it left her more dissatisfied with her own conduct than before, as the inevitable comparison between her actions and Elinor's sharpened her self-reproach.

She felt the full weight of that comparison, but not as her sister had hoped—to spur her to immediate action. Instead, it brought the torturous sting of continual regret, a bitter mourning for the fact that she had never exerted herself sooner. It offered only the agony of penitence without the faintest hope of amendment. Her mind, so thoroughly weakened, still convinced her that present effort was impossible, a realization that served only to dispirit her further.

For a day or two thereafter, no new tidings arrived regarding affairs in Harley Street or Bartlett's Buildings. Though Mrs. Jennings had ample material to spread among her circle without seeking further information, she had resolved from the outset to visit her cousins with comfort and inquiry as soon as possible. Only the hindrance of an unusually high volume of visitors had delayed her departure.

On the third day following their acquisition of these particulars, the weather proved so fine and beautiful that it drew many to Kensington Gardens, despite it being merely the second week of March. Mrs. Jennings and Elinor were among the crowd. Marianne, however, knowing that the Willoughbys had returned to town and harboring a constant dread of encountering them, chose to remain at home rather than risk venturing into such a public gathering.

Shortly after entering the gardens, an intimate acquaintance of Mrs. Jennings joined them. Elinor was relieved that this companion engaged Mrs. Jennings's attention, leaving her free for quiet reflection. She saw nothing of the Willoughbys, nothing of Edward, and for some time, nothing of anyone who might hold interest for her, whether in a serious or lighthearted capacity. Finally, however, she was surprised to be accosted by Miss Steele. Though appearing somewhat shy, Miss Steele expressed great delight at meeting them. Encouraged by Mrs. Jennings's particular kindness, she temporarily left her own party to join theirs. Mrs. Jennings immediately whispered to Elinor, "Get it all out of her, my dear. She will tell you anything if you ask. You see I cannot leave Mrs. Clarke."

Fortunately for both Mrs. Jennings's curiosity and Elinor's, Miss Steele was inclined to speak freely without prompting, for otherwise, little would have been learned.

"I am so glad to meet you," said Miss Steele, taking her arm familiarly—"for I wanted to see you of all things in the world." Lowering her voice, she added, "I suppose Mrs. Jennings has heard all about it. Is she angry?"

"Not at all, I believe, with you."

"That is a good thing. And Lady Middleton—is she angry?"

"I cannot suppose it possible that she should be."

"I am monstrous glad of it. Good gracious! I have had such a time of it! I never saw Lucy in such a rage in my life. She vowed at first she would never trim me a new bonnet or do anything else for me again so long as she lived; but now she is quite come to, and we are as good friends as ever. Look, she made me this bow to my hat and put in the feather last night. There now, you are going to laugh at me too. But why should not I wear pink ribbons? I do not care if it is the Doctor's favorite color. I am sure, for my part, I should never have known he liked it better than any other if he had not happened to say so. My cousins have been plaguing me! I declare sometimes I do not know which way to look before them."

She drifted away onto a subject on which Elinor had nothing to say, and thus soon deemed it expedient to steer the conversation back to its original course.

"Well, but Miss Dashwood," she said triumphantly, "people may say what they choose about Mr. Ferrars declaring he would not have Lucy, for it is no such thing, I can tell you; and it is quite a shame for such ill-natured reports to be spread abroad. Whatever Lucy might think of it herself, you know, it was no business of other people to set it down as certain."

"I never heard anything of the kind hinted at before, I assure you," said Elinor.

"Oh, did not you? But it was said, I know, very well, and by more than one. Miss Godby told Miss Sparks that nobody in their senses could expect Mr. Ferrars to give up a woman like Miss Morton, with thirty thousand pounds to her fortune, for Lucy Steele who had nothing at all; and I had it from Miss Sparks myself. Besides that, my cousin Richard said himself that when it came to the point, he was afraid Mr. Ferrars would be off. When Edward did not come near us for three days, I could not tell what to think myself. I believe in my heart Lucy gave it up for lost; for we left your brother's house on Wednesday and saw nothing of him until Thursday, Friday, and Saturday, not knowing what had become of him. Once Lucy thought to write to him, but then her spirits rose against that idea. However, this morning he came just as we came home from church, and then it all came out. He had been sent for on Wednesday to Harley Street, talked to by his mother and everyone, and declared before them all that he loved nobody but Lucy and that nobody but Lucy would he have. He was so worried by what passed that, as soon as he left his mother's house, he got upon his horse and rode into the country, somewhere or other, staying at an inn all Thursday and Friday on purpose to get the better of it. After thinking it all over and over again, he said it seemed to him that, now he had no fortune and nothing at all, it would be quite unkind to keep her on to the engagement, because it must be for her loss. He had nothing but two thousand pounds and no hope of anything else; if he were to go into orders, as he had some thoughts of doing, he could get nothing but a curacy, and how were they to live upon that? He could not bear to think of her doing no better, so he begged, if she had the least mind for it, to put an end to the matter directly and leave him to shift for himself. I heard him say all this as plain as could possibly be. It was entirely for her sake, and upon her account, that he said a word about being off, and not upon his own. I will take my oath he never dropped a syllable of being tired of her, or of wishing to marry Miss Morton, or anything like it. But, to be sure, Lucy would not give ear to such kind of talking. So she told him directly—with a great deal about sweet and love, you know, and all that—Oh, la! one can't repeat such kind of things, you know—that she had not the least mind in the world to be off. She said she could live with him upon a trifle, and how little so ever he might have, she should be very glad to have it all, you know, or something of the kind. So then he was monstrous happy and talked on some time about what they should do. They agreed he should take orders directly and they must wait to be married till he got a living. Just then I could not hear any more, for my cousin called from below to tell me Mrs. Richardson was come in her coach and would take one of us to Kensington Gardens. So I was forced to go into the room and interrupt them to ask Lucy if she would like to go, but she did not care to leave Edward. So I just ran upstairs and put on a pair of silk stockings and came off with the Richardsons."

"I do not understand what you mean by interrupting them," said Elinor. "You were all in the same room together, were not you?"

"No, indeed, not us. La! Miss Dashwood, do you think people make love when anybody else is by? Oh, for shame! To be sure you must know better than that. (Laughing affectedly.) No, no; they were shut up in the drawing-room together, and all I heard was only by listening at the door."

"How!" cried Elinor. "Have you been repeating to me what you only learnt yourself by listening at the door? I am sorry I did not know it before; for I certainly would not have suffered you to give me particulars of a conversation which you ought not to have known yourself. How could you behave so unfairly by your sister?"

"Oh, la! there is nothing in that. I only stood at the door and heard what I could. And I am sure Lucy would have done just the same by me; for a year or two back, when Martha Sharpe and I had so many secrets together, she never made any bones of hiding in a closet or behind a chimney-board on purpose to hear what we said."

Elinor tried to talk of something else, but Miss Steele could not be kept beyond a couple of minutes from what was uppermost in her mind.

"Edward talks of going to Oxford soon," she said. "But now he is lodging at No.—, Pall Mall. What an ill-natured woman his mother is, an't she? And your brother and sister were not very kind! However, I shan't say anything against them to you; and to be sure they did send us home in their own chariot, which was more than I looked for. And for my part, I was all in a fright for fear your sister should ask us for the huswifes she had given us a day or two before; but, however, nothing was said about them, and I took care to keep mine out of sight. Edward has got some business at Oxford, he says; so he must go there for a time. And after that, as soon as he can light upon a Bishop, he will be ordained. I wonder what curacy he will get! Good gracious! (giggling as she spoke) I'd lay my life I know what my cousins will say when they hear of it. They will tell me I should write to the Doctor to get Edward the curacy of his new living. I know they will; but I am sure I would not do such a thing for all the world. 'La!' I shall say directly, 'I wonder how you could think of such a thing? I write to the Doctor, indeed!'"

"Well," said Elinor, "it is a comfort to be prepared against the worst. You have got your answer ready."

Miss Steele was going to reply on the same subject, but the approach of her own party made another conversation necessary.

"Oh, la! here come the Richardsons. I had a vast deal more to say to you, but I must not stay away from them not any longer. I assure you they are very genteel people. He makes a monstrous deal of money, and they keep their own coach. I have not time to speak to Mrs. Jennings about it myself, but pray tell her I am quite happy to hear she is not in anger against us, and Lady Middleton the same. And if anything should happen to take you and your sister away, and Mrs. Jennings should want company, I am sure we should be very glad to come and stay with her for as long a time as she likes. I suppose Lady Middleton won't ask us any more this bout. Good-by; I am sorry Miss Marianne was not here. Remember me kindly to her. La! if you have not got your spotted muslin on!—I wonder you was not afraid of its being torn."

Such was her parting concern; for after this, she had time only to pay her farewell compliments to Mrs. Jennings before her company was claimed by Mrs. Richardson, leaving Elinor in possession of knowledge that might feed her powers of reflection for some time, though she had learned very little more than what had been already foreseen and foreplanned in her own mind. Edward's marriage with Lucy was as firmly determined upon, and the time of its taking place remained as absolutely uncertain as she had concluded it would be; everything depended, exactly after her expectation, on his obtaining that preferment, of which, at present, there seemed not the smallest chance.

As soon as they returned to the carriage, Mrs. Jennings was eager for information. But as Elinor wished to spread as little intelligence as possible that had in the first place been so unfairly obtained, she confined herself to the brief repetition of such simple particulars as she felt assured Lucy, for the sake of her own consequence, would choose to have known. The continuance of their engagement and the means that could be taken to promote its end constituted her entire communication. This produced from Mrs. Jennings the following natural remark.

"Wait for his having a living!—ay, we all know how that will end:—they will wait a twelvemonth, and finding no good comes of it, will set down upon a curacy of fifty pounds a-year, with the interest of his two thousand pounds and what little matter Mr. Steele and Mr. Pratt can give her. Then they will have a child every year! and Lord help 'em! how poor they will be! I must see what I can give them towards furnishing their house. Two maids and two men, indeed! as I talked of the other day. No, no, they must get a stout girl of all works. Betty's sister would never do for them now."
