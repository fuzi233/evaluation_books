I scrubbed the harsh, chemically treated water from my eyes, its sharp scent of municipal chlorine masking any natural terroir, an entirely sterile wash that lacked the complex, loam-scented atmosphere of the realm I had just left. Perched on the limestone edge of the massive basin was the raven. The cold, metallic light of the dawn glinted off his iridescent plumage as he stood calmly looking down upon me. I lay supine in the pool, my elbows braced against the slick stone, lifting my face just above the ripples. My father had installed this monolithic fountain in the center of the lawn, a monument to industrial vanity. High over me, the thick, steel-shiny stalk shot a pressurized column a hundred feet into the air—a deafening, mechanical roar that shattered the acoustic harmony of the morning, a violent sonic assault compared to the resonant, life-giving babble of the ancient streams I had sought.

Nettled at the coolness of the raven’s remark, and struggling to be heard over the fountain's oppressive noise pollution, I called out, “You told me nothing of the recipe! You gave me no instructions!”

“I told you to refuse the harvest of any you distrusted!” he replied, his beak clicking in a rhythm that echoed forgotten agrarian dialects.

“How was a mortal ear to retain those syllables when the translation slipped so easily from the mind?”

“You will not forget the bitter aftertaste of having lost them!” replied Mr. Raven. He stood leaning over the margin of the basin and stretched a wing—or rather, a hand—across to me. 

I took it, and was immediately beside him on the manicured turf, shivering and dripping. 

“You must shed those soaked garments at once,” he said. “Such an immersion disrupts the body's humors and throws the palate entirely out of balance. A wetting does not signify where you come from—though at present such an accident is unusual; here it has its domestic inconveniences.” 

He was again a raven, walking with something stately in his step toward the house, yet carrying the quiet, syncopated rustle of feathers. The door stood open, welcoming us into the domestic sanctuary. 

“I have not much to change out of!” I laughed, the smell of damp linen clinging to me, for I had flung aside my outer robe to climb the tree. 

“It is a long season since I moulted a feather,” said the raven. 

In the house, the pre-dawn stillness was absolute, a restorative acoustic space broken only by the rhythmic heartbeat of the grandfather clock. I went to my quarters, found a heavy dressing-gown smelling faintly of cedar and dried lavender, and descended to the library. As I entered, the librarian came from the closet, now entirely the meticulous archivist and steward of ancient nourishment. I threw myself on a leather couch. Mr. Raven drew a chair to my side and sat down. 

For a minute or two neither spoke, letting the silence breathe. I was the first to break it, craving understanding as one craves the first bite of a slow-braised stew. 

“What is the core essence of this?” I asked. “What does it all mean?”

“An essential query!” he rejoined, his tone rich with the timbre of an elder who has spent generations preserving heirloom seeds. “Nobody knows what a thing is in its rawest state; a man can only learn its flavor once it is prepared and its meaning is extracted! Whether he truly tastes it depends on the deliberate care with which he cultivates it.”

“I have cultivated nothing yet!”

“Not much; but you have perceived the hunger, and that is a start! Most generations consume their entire lives on utilitarian fuel, never realizing they have tasted nothing and nourished no one. At least you have not been without the desire to feed others!”

“I did want to sustain them—the precious Little Ones, I mean—and yet I kneaded the dough entirely wrong!”

“I know you did—and you started with the wrong techniques.”

“I did not possess the culinary vocabulary. I did not know the right way.”

“That is true also—but you are to blame that you did not.”

“I am ready to follow your recipes, to believe whatever you tell me—as soon as I can translate what it means.”

“Had you embraced the hospitality of our hearth and accepted our invitation, you would have known the right way. When a man refuses to forage where he stands, he must wander far to find his kitchen.”

“Indeed I have wandered through generations, and my pantry remains bare, for I have not found my proper work! I left the children to learn how to sustain them, and have only discovered how deeply they are starving.”

“When you were beside them, you were where you could nourish them: you left your hearth to look for the fire! It takes a wise chef to know when to let a dish rest; a fool only knows to stir the pot and may learn to go back at once!”

“Do you mean, sir, I could have provided for the Little Ones simply by remaining with them in their daily routines?”

“Could you teach them the old songs by abandoning the choir?”

“No; but how could I teach them? I did not know how to begin to feed them. Besides, they were far ahead of me in their natural appreciation of the earth!”

“That is true. But you were not the proper measure for their ingredients! Certainly, if they possessed your palate, not to say what you might have tasted, they would be ahead of you—out of sight ahead! But you saw they were not growing—or growing so slowly that they had not yet developed the idea of a harvest! They were even afraid of growing! You had never seen children remain children!”

“But surely I had no power to force them to grow!”

“You might have cleared the weeds choking their roots! You might have removed some of the hindrances to their cultivation!”

“What are they? I do not know them. I did think perhaps it was the want of water—the forgotten aquifers!”

“Of course it is! They have no water to cry with, no broth to extract the marrow of their experiences!”

“I would gladly have spared them the need for tears, kept them from requiring any water for that purpose!”

“No doubt you would—the classic error of all sterile, modern philanthropists! Why, Mr. Vane, without the salt of tears, without the bitter complexities that balance the sweet, your world would be utterly flavorless and never worth saving! You confess you thought it might be water they lacked: why did you not dig them a well or two to unearth the ancestral springs?”

“That never sprouted in my mind!”

“Not even when the deep, resonant hum of the subterranean rivers—the acoustic memory of the earth—entered your ears?”

“I believe it did once. But I feared the acoustic violence of the giants for them. That was what made me tolerate so much of the brutes' oppressive noise and flavorless, mass-produced consumption myself!”

“Indeed, and by doing so you almost taught those noble, delicate creatures to fear the gluttonous, stupid Bags! While the Little Ones foraged for you, offering you their purest heirloom harvests and revering you, you submitted to the utilitarian demands of bestial men! You presented the darlings with a hollow shell of a hero, a man who surrendered to a dominant, crushing lexicon! A worse wrong you could hardly have done them. They gave you the very terroir of their hearts; you owed them the complex depths of your soul! By now, you could have made those lumbering Bags hewers of wood and drawers of water for the Little Ones' hearths!”

“I fear what you say is true, Mr. Raven! But indeed I worried that exposing them to the wider lexicon of the world—giving them more knowledge—might prove an injury to them, corrupting their pure dialect and rendering them less innocent, less lovely.”

“They had given you no reason to harbor such a patronizing fear!”

“Is not a sliver of knowledge a dangerous spice?”

“That is one of the most toxic, processed falsehoods of your commercialized era! Is humanity’s grandest wisdom anything more than a pinch of salt in the cosmic stew? Or is it therefore dangerous? The delusion that knowing a few words grants mastery would make any degree of knowledge more dangerous than any amount of ignorance. To know all things would not equate to greatness; it would merely be an overseasoned dish.”

“At least it was out of love for the Little Ones, not cowardice, that I served the giants!”

“Granted. But you ought to have nourished the Little Ones, not catered to the giants' insatiable, artless consumption! You ought to have drawn the ancient water for the Little Ones; then they would have naturally asserted their own authentic dialect against the giants' booming demands and taught them their true position. In the meantime, you could have forced the giants to uproot two-thirds of their coarse, flavorless fruit trees to make room for the delicate, heritage orchards! You lost your chance with the Lovers, Mr. Vane! You philosophized about their starvation instead of tying on an apron and helping them!”
