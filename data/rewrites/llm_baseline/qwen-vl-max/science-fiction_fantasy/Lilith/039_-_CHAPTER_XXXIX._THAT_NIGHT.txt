That night was one of unrest and dread, a time when sleep offered no refuge and waking brought no peace. The children, huddled in their beds, spoke afterward of things they could not name—things that slipped through the silence like shadows, that filled the house with unseen movement and unspoken terror. They did not hear a sound, yet they felt it all: a presence, a pressure, a slow unraveling of what was real.

Once, the house trembled as if struck by a wind so silent it made no noise at all—a wind that shook the beams and rattled the shutters without a moan. It passed like a breath held too long, vanishing into the dark. Then, they woke to water rising from beneath the floor, swelling upward until it filled the garret, reaching the very rafters. Yet there was no splash, no ripple—only the cold certainty of immersion. And then, just as suddenly, it receded, leaving them dry and warm, though none could explain how.

Later, the air grew thick with cats—hundreds of them, crawling across walls, slipping down the ceiling, pressing against the bedclothes. Their claws scraped at the fabric of their nightgowns, but never broke skin. In the morning, no mark remained. Only once did sound break the stillness: a distant, mournful howl from the desert—the call of the great-grandmother-cat, ancient and vast, summoning her offspring. At that moment, the cats froze, and all was quiet again.

They slept then, deeply, until dawn crept over the edge of the world.

But I had not slept. I watched with Mara, the veiled woman, and Lilith, the princess, throughout the long night. What I saw, what I felt, cannot be told in full. Some truths are beyond words; some moments belong only to silence.

When Mara left the room with the children, my gaze fell upon the white leopardess—once thought lost, now crouched in the corner, trembling. She seemed afraid not of the darkness, but of what might come out of it. A lamp burned on the mantel, casting shifting shapes across the walls—now sharp, now blurred, now melting into forms that were neither flesh nor spirit. The princess lay motionless on the settle, her body rigid, her face pale as stone. Time stretched thin. Every breath felt like an effort.

When Mara returned, she moved the settle to the center of the room, sat opposite me across the hearth, where a small fire flickered. Between us, the flames danced low, yet they did not warm. Something was coming—not from outside, but from within the house, from within the air, from within the earth itself. The shadows wavered. A silvery creature, like a worm of pure light, crawled from the gloom, crossed the floor without sound, and vanished into the fire.

We waited. The hours passed. Midnight approached. No wind stirred. No crack split the silence. But deep inside, something heaved—like the breath of a sleeping giant, or the pulse of the world’s hidden heart. I felt it in my bones, in my blood, in my soul. A weight pressed down, not of fear, but of judgment. And yet, I was not afraid. For I knew: this was the hour.

At midnight, Mara rose. Slowly, deliberately, she unwound the cloth that covered her face. It fell to the floor, and she stepped over it. She stood before the princess, whose feet pointed toward the fire. Mara went to her head, turned, and stood behind it. Then I saw her face.

It was beautiful—beyond description. White, sorrowful, yet not broken. Tears traced paths down her cheeks, but she wiped them away, and her expression stilled. Her eyes held a depth that could not be pierced. She laid her hand gently on the princess’s forehead, breathed upon her sallow brow.

“Will you turn from the wicked things you have done?” Mara asked, softly.

No answer came.

She repeated the question. Still nothing.

A third time, the same voice, gentle as rain.

Then, the princess opened her mouth—and spoke. Not in sound, but in essence. Words formed not from air, but from the core of being. I heard them, though I could not name them.

“I will not,” she said. “I will be myself, not another.”

“You are not yourself,” Mara replied. “You are a shadow. Will you not return to who you were?”

“I will be what I mean to be.”

“You do evil because your nature is good, yet you twist it.”

“I will do as my Self desires.”

“And your Self is ruled by the Shadow?”

“I will do what I will.”

“You have killed your daughter—Lilith.”

“She is mine.”

“She was never yours.”

“I am not another’s. I am my own.”

“Then your hour has come.”

“I care not. I am what I am. No one can take that from me.”

“You are not free. You are bound by the lie you believe.”

“I defy you! I am free!”

“You are a slave to your own pride.”

The silence deepened. Then, a presence filled the house—not of sound, but of flame. The fire on the hearth glowed still, but the silver worm reappeared, now blazing white-hot, crawling slowly across the floor, up the settle, to the princess’s feet. It did not burn. It crept upward, over her robe, into her chest, disappearing into the folds.

I lunged forward to stop it. Mara blocked me. She parted the edges of the robe—no trace remained. The worm had entered not through flesh, but through the black spot on her chest, piercing bone and marrow, thoughts and intent.

The princess shuddered. Then, slowly, tears began to form on her skin—tiny droplets, like dew, growing into pearls, then streams. I saw her pain, but I could not reach her.

“She sees herself,” Mara whispered. She drew me back three steps.

Suddenly, the princess arched her back, sprang to her feet. Her face twisted in horror. Her hair dripped, then sparked, then poured sweat. Her breath came in gasps, yet no air escaped. She stood rigid, torn apart by inner fire.

“Do not go near her,” Mara said. “She is far away—in the hell of self-knowledge. The fire of the universe burns in her now. She sees the truth: she is both good and evil. She is the fire, and she is burning in it. But she does not know that the Light is in the fire.”

Time lost meaning. Minutes? Years? I could not tell. Finally, she collapsed, face down. Mara knelt beside her. Tears fell from Mara’s eyes onto the woman who had never wept.

“Will you change?” she asked.

“I would have made myself different,” Lilith gasped. “I am glad He made me, not I. He alone is to blame. I would never have made such a thing! He meant me to suffer. Let Him kill me!”

“Unmake yourself,” Mara said.

“I cannot. The tyrant keeps me alive. I curse Him. Kill me.”

“Had He not made you, you could not even hate Him. But He did not make you thus. You made yourself.”

“I will not be remade.”

“Then let Him restore you.”

“I will not be His creation.”

“Are you not willing to undo what you have done?”

Silence.

“Put yourself back on the settle,” Mara said.

“I will not.”

Then, a wind arose—soundless, invisible. Water rose from below, cold but not freezing, rising without wave or sound. It lifted her, bore her gently, and set her down on the settle. Then it sank away.

Her mind warred with itself—accusing, defending, breaking. Her soul was stripped bare under the light of truth. She moaned, sighed, murmured to a phantom self. Her kingdom was shattered. One moment she laughed like a demon, the next she wept like a child.

She began to speak—a tale in a language half-forgotten, shaped like dreams I once knew. It touched on things Adam had read in the broken manuscript, on powers and vices I could not name. Then she stopped.

Again, the horror in her hair—sparks, then flow. I looked to Mara.

“Those are not tears of repentance,” she said. “True sorrow gathers in the eyes. These are bitter—self-loathing. But it is a step. The prodigal forgets himself when he returns.”

She leaned close. “Will you give back what you stole?”

“I took only what I had right to,” Lilith said through clenched teeth. “My power proved my right.”

Mara withdrew.

Then came the worst: a darkness deeper than night, a Nothingness that pressed inward. It touched me—Death Absolute. Not absence, but presence of void. I felt it. I knew it.

With a cry that tore her soul, Lilith threw herself from the settle.

“For pity’s sake!” she shrieked. “Tear my heart out—but let me live!”

And then—calm. A perfect stillness, like a summer night after storm. Suffering had reached its peak, and a hand had emptied the cup.

She rose. Looked around. Stood tall. Conqueror. Victorious.

She raised her arm—then froze.

Her eyes fixed on something unseen.

I looked—and saw: before her stood two reflections—one of her true self, radiant, whole; the other, her present form, broken, corrupted. She trembled. Fell to the floor.

She knew now: what God intended, and what she had become.

For the rest of the night, she lay still.

At dawn, she rose. Turned to Mara.

“You have conquered,” she said. “Let me go into the wilderness and mourn.”

Mara studied her. “Begin,” she said. “Set right what is wrong.”

“I do not know how.”

“Open your hand. Let go what is in it.”

She hesitated. Her hand—more paw than hand—clenched tight.

“I cannot,” she said. “I have no power.”

“Then open it for me.”

She extended it. Mara did not look.

“You must open it yourself,” she said. “You can, if you will.”

“I cannot!”

“You have opened it before. It takes effort. Pain. But you CAN.”

“I will not try what I know impossible.”

“You have been playing the fool all your life.”

Defiance flashed.

“I know what you wanted! You failed. I am still queen of Hell!”

Then came the horror—Life in Death. Alive, yet dead. Existing, yet gone. I felt it—something I could not imagine, something that would kill me if it touched me.

It was in her.

Something departed from me—life, warmth, sense. I felt myself fading. The eternal fire dimmed. I was becoming aware only of what had been.

Yet I turned to Lilith.

She stood rigid. Life had withdrawn. All that remained was the dregs—dead, corrupted, conscious. She was a corpse that could not die. She had killed her own life and lived on.

She knew it.

She saw it.

She was a dead thing, knowing it.

She had tried to unmake herself—and could not.

She was trapped.

In her face, I saw more than misery—I saw the abyss behind it. Her eyes were wide open, staring into the heart of horror. Her hand—still clenched—held nothing, yet everything.

Then, without warning, she walked toward Mara.

Mara rose.

“I yield,” Lilith said. “I cannot hold out. I am defeated. Yet—I cannot open my hand.”

“Have you tried?”

“I am trying now.”

“Then I will take you to my father. You have wronged Him most. Therefore, He can help you best.”

“How?”

“He will forgive you.”

“If He would only let me cease…”

“You shall die—but not as you think. You shall die out of death into life. Now is the Life, which was never against you.”

Like her mother, Mara embraced her. Kissed her forehead.

The fiery-cold agony left her eyes. Tears filled them.

She carried her to her bed, laid her down, closed her eyes with tender hands.

Lilith wept.

Mara opened the door.

Outside, dawn waited—with Spring in her arms.

Gently, they slipped in. A soft wind flowed in their garments, rippling around Lilith, stirring the sea of her eternal life. It touched her, awakened her, called her home.

She breathed. She listened.

In the wind, rain fell—soft, healing rain. It soaked the desert, the sands of her heart. It drank in, and healed.

When Mara returned, Lilith’s tears flowed slower than the rain. Soon, she slept.

And the night passed.
