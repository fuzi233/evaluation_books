Once again, Miss Twinkleton had delivered her farewell address, accompanied by white wine and pound cake, and once again the young ladies had dispersed to their respective homes. Helena Landless had departed the Nunnery to attend to her brother's affairs, leaving pretty Rosa alone behind. Cloisterham burned with a brilliant summer sun; the Cathedral and the ruined monastery appeared as if their massive stone walls were transparent, glowing from within rather than reflecting light from without. Their mellowness seemed to radiate outward toward the golden cornfields and the distant, smoke-hazed roads. The town gardens blushed with ripening fruit.

In days of old, travel-stained pilgrims had ridden in noisy caravans through the city's welcoming tree-shades. Now, however, weary travelers living a gypsy existence between haymaking and harvest—figures seemingly composed entirely of the dust of the earth—lounged on cool doorsteps. They attempted to repair their unfixable shoes or surrendered them to the city kennels as hopeless cases, clutching bundles containing their unused sickles wrapped in straw. At every public pump, bare feet sought relief from the heat, mingled with the bubbling sounds of thirsty "Bedouins" drinking directly from the spouts. Meanwhile, the Cloisterham police watched these intruders with suspicious glances, impatient that they should leave the civic bounds and return to frying themselves on the scorching highways.

On such an afternoon, following the final cathedral service, when the side of the High Street housing the Nunnery lay in grateful shade (save where its quaint garden opened to the west between tree branches), a servant informed Rosa, to her terror, that Mr. Jasper desired to see her. Had he chosen a worse moment to find her vulnerable, he could not have done better. Perhaps he had chosen it deliberately. Helena was gone, Mrs. Tisher was away on leave, and Miss Twinkleton, in her current state of leisure, had contributed herself and a veal pie to a picnic.

"Oh, why, why, why did you say I was at home!" cried Rosa, helpless.

The maid replied that Mr. Jasper had not asked; he claimed to know she was at home and requested she be told he wished to speak with her.

"What shall I do! What shall I do!" Rosa thought, clasping her hands. Driven by desperation, she declared she would meet him in the garden. She shuddered at the prospect of being locked inside the house with him, but noted that many windows commanded the garden view. From outside, she could be seen and heard, able to scream into the free air and flee. It was a wild notion fluttering through her mind. She had not seen him since the fateful night, save when she was questioned before the Mayor, at which time he sat in gloomy vigilance, representing his lost nephew and burning with a desire for revenge.

She hung her garden-hat on her arm and stepped out. The moment she saw him from the porch, leaning against the sundial, the ancient, horrifying sensation of being compelled by him seized her anew. She felt she ought to turn back immediately, yet he drew her feet toward him. Unable to resist, she sat down on the garden bench beside the sundial, her head bowed. She dared not look up at him in abhorrence, though she noticed he wore deep mourning. So did she. It had not been so at first; the loss had long been accepted and mourned as final.

He intended to touch her hand. She sensed the movement and pulled hers back. His gaze remained fixed upon her, though her own eyes saw only the grass beneath them.

"I have been waiting," he began, "for some time to be summoned back to my duty near you."

After forming her lips several times into hesitant shapes and finally settling on silence, she answered: "Duty, sir?"

"The duty of teaching you, serving you as your faithful music master."

"I have ceased that study."

"Not ceased, I think. Discontinued. Your guardian informed me you discontinued it under the shock we have all felt so acutely. When will you resume?"

"Never, sir."

"Never? You could have done no more if you had loved my dear boy."

"I *did* love him!" Rosa cried, flashing with anger.

"Yes; but not quite—not quite in the right way, shall I say? Not in the intended and expected manner. Though my dear boy was, unhappily, too self-conscious and self-satisfied (I draw no parallel between him and you in that regard) to love as he should have, or as anyone in his position would have had to love—"

She sat motionless, shrinking further into herself.

"So, to be told that you discontinued your studies with me was to be politely informed that you abandoned them entirely?" he suggested.

"Yes," Rosa said suddenly, with spirit. "The politeness belonged to my guardian, not to me. I told him I was resolved to stop, and determined to stand by my resolution."

"And you still are?"

"I still am, sir. And I beg not to be questioned any longer about it. At all events, I will not answer anymore; I possess that power."

She was painfully aware of his gaze, filled with a gloating admiration for the flush of anger on her cheeks and the animation it brought. As her spirit rose, it faltered again, and she struggled with feelings of shame, affront, and fear, much as she had that night at the piano.

"I will not question you further since you object so strongly; I will confess—"

"I do not wish to hear you, sir," Rosa cried, rising.

This time, he touched her with his outstretched hand. Shrinking from it, she sank back onto her seat.

"We must sometimes act in opposition to our wishes," he whispered. "You must do so now, or inflict more harm on others than you could ever repair."

"What harm?"

"Precisely. Precisely. You question *me*, you see, and surely that is unfair when you forbid me to question you. Nevertheless, I will answer your question shortly. Dearest Rosa! Charming Rosa!"

She sprang up again. This time, he did not touch her. Yet his face looked so wicked and menacing, standing as he leaned against the sundial setting like a black mark upon the very face of day, that her flight was halted by sheer horror.

"I do not forget how many windows command a view of us," he said, glancing toward them. "I will not touch you again; I will not come closer than I am. Sit down, and there will be no great wonder in your music master leaning idly against a pedestal and speaking with you, recalling everything that has happened and our parts therein. Sit down, my beloved."

She would have fled once more—she was almost gone—but once again his face, darkly threatening consequences if she moved, stopped her. Gazing at him with the expression frozen on her features, she sat down on the bench again.

"Rosa, even when my dear boy was affianced to you, I loved you madly; even when I believed his happiness in having you for his wife was certain, I loved you madly; even when I strove to make him more ardently devoted to you, I loved you madly; even when he gave me the picture of your lovely face, so carelessly traduced by him—which I feigned to hang always in my sight for his sake, but worshipped in torment for years—I loved you madly. In the distasteful work of the day, in the wakeful misery of the night, girded by sordid realities, or wandering through paradises and hells of vision into which I rushed, carrying your image in my arms, I loved you madly."

Nothing could render his words more hideous to her than the contrast between the violence of his look and delivery and the composure of his assumed posture.

"I endured it all in silence. So long as you were his, or so long as I supposed you to be his, I hid my secret loyally. Did I not?"

This lie, so gross, while the mere words spoken were undeniably true, was more than Rosa could bear. Answering with kindling indignation: "You were as false throughout, sir, as you are now. You were false to him, daily and hourly. You know that you made my life unhappy by your pursuit of me. You know that you made me afraid to open his generous eyes, and that you forced me, for his own trusting, good sake, to keep the truth from him—that you were a bad, bad man!"

His preservation of his easy attitude rendered his working features and convulsive hands absolutely diabolical. Returning with a fierce extremity of admiration, he said: "How beautiful you are! You are more beautiful in anger than in repose. I do not ask for your love; give me yourself and your hatred; give me yourself and that pretty rage; give me yourself and that enchanting scorn; it will be enough for me."

Impatient tears rose to the eyes of the trembling little beauty, and her face flushed. But as she rose again to leave him in indignation and seek protection within the house, he stretched out his hand toward the porch as though inviting her to enter.

"I told you, you rare charmer, you sweet witch, that you must stay and hear me, or do more harm than can ever be undone. You asked me what harm. Stay, and I will tell you. Go, and I will do it!"
