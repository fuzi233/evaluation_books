The Hearth

It was a winter that clung to the bones, when little Gerard, barely three months past his birth, brought a messenger scurrying through the snow from Tergou. The boy arrived breathless, bearing news for Catherine.

"Go back," she commanded, her voice sharp with the weary resolve of a woman trapped by circumstance. "Tell them I cannot come, and I will not. They have taken Kate." The messenger vanished, leaving Catherine alone in the quiet of her cell. "There, child," she murmured, turning to her own sorrow, "I must return to them. They are all at sixes and sevens; the house is a storm. This is the third time they ask, and tomorrow my man will drag me home by the ear, flea and all."

She paused, reciting the trials of infancy, instructing Margaret with the gravity of a teacher preparing a student for war. She pressed money into Margaret's hands, a desperate measure. Margaret refused, bowing her head with a polite, if strained, gratitude. Catherine, however, grew angry at the refusal, her contempt cool and unyielding. To her mind, Margaret's reasons, though reasonable, lacked the necessary spirit of feminine fortitude.

"Out with your heart," Catherine demanded, her eyes glistening. "Speak it now, before we part, and perhaps never see one another's face again."

"Oh, Mother!" Margaret cried, her voice breaking. "Say not so."

"Alas, girl, I have seen it often. It comes to me now, at every parting. When I was your age, we believed in eternity, that we should live forever. Now, out with it."

"Well, then, Mother..." Margaret hesitated, then whispered, "I would rather not have told you... your Cornelis said, 'So you are come to share with us, eh, mistress?' Those were his words. I told him I would be sorry."

"Beshrew his ill tongue!" Catherine snapped. "What does it signify? He will never know."

"Most likely he would, sooner or later." Catherine stood firm. "Whether or no, I will take no grudged bounty from any family. Unless I saw my child starving—and Heaven only knows what I might do—nay, Mother, give me but thy love. I prize that above silver, and they grudge me not that, by all I can find. Not a stiver of money will I take from your house."

"You are a foolish lass," Catherine chided gently. "Were it me, I'd take it just to spite him."

"No, you would not. You and I are apples off one tree."

Finally, Catherine yielded with a grace that softened her stern features. When the moment of departure truly arrived, embraces tangled together, and tears flowed freely until both women were undone.

Once Catherine was gone, the child wailed, a sound that tore at the heart. All attempts to pacify him failed until Margaret, suspecting a hidden object, searched beneath his clothes and against his skin. There, buried close to his spine, lay a gold angel, irritating his small backbone.

"There, now, Gerard," she cooed to the weeping babe. "I thought Granny gave in rather suddenly."

She took the coin, wrapping it carefully in a scrap of linen, and placed it at the bottom of her wooden chest. She bid the infant watch closely, teaching him that sometimes, as resolute as his grandmother, one must stand fast.

Later, Catherine recounted Margaret's proud defiance to Eli, marveling at how the girl had baffled her mother-in-law. Eli agreed, stating plainly that Margaret was right and Catherine wrong. Catherine tossed her head in disbelief, while Eli pondered the shift in their fortunes.

Yet, Margaret's peace was fragile. Her burdens doubled: she had two men to feed, yet could not labor as fiercely as before. Keeping the house, caring for the child, and cooking for all left her exhausted. Though she had a little money saved, she lived by a delusion, telling her son that his father would return before the funds ran dry. Clinging to these bright hopes and the living memory of her husband, she spent several happy months.

But time, relentless, wore on. No Gerard returned. Stranger still, no word of him reached them.

Disquiet settled over Margaret, overriding her practical nature. Often lost in sad reverie, she sighed in silence. As her heart troubled, her purse melted away. One day, she found the cupboard empty. Looking into the faces of her dependents, her bosom swelled with pity. Could she let Grandfather Eli and poor old Martin go hungry? She reached for Catherine's angel. As she unfolded the linen, a tear of gentle mortification fell onto the sacred coin. She sent Martin outside to exchange it.

While he was gone, a Frenchman arrived, accompanied by a dealer in illuminated works who had previously offered her a pittance. This man claimed employment from his sovereign to collect masterpieces for a Book of Hours. Margaret showed him her finest treasures. He was charmed, particularly by a panel of flowers, raspberries, and creeping vines that Margaret Van Eyck had shaded with delicate skill. He offered a price unheard of.

"Nay, flout not my need, good stranger," she pleaded. "Three mouths breathe in this house, and none to fill them but me."

Curious arithmetic! Number One was left out.

"I would not outdo thee, fair mistress," the stranger assured her. "My Princess charged me strictly: 'Seek the best craftsmen.' I make no hard bargains; I wish to content both them and myself."

In a heartbeat, Margaret knelt beside the cradle, kissing little Gerard and showering four gold pieces upon him, recounting the miracle in broken Dutch. "And oh, what a good Princess! Wasn't she? We will pray for her, won't we, my lambkin?"

Martin returned, furious. "They will not change it! I trow they think I stole it."

"I am beholden to thee," Margaret hastened, snatching the coin from him. She wrapped it again and hid it, restoring it to its sanctuary.

Before these unexpected funds were spent, she returned to her ironing and starching. Midway through, Martin fell ill. After nine days of suffering, he died.

Nearly all her money vanished in his burial, ensuring a decent send-off. He was gone, leaving an empty chair by her fireside. For as soon as the Busy Body had left, Martin had preferred the warmth of the hearth to the sun. Margaret forbade anyone else to sit there. Yet, whenever her gaze lingered too long on the vacancy, a tear inevitably followed.

Now, there was no one to carry her linens home. Doing it herself meant leaving little Gerard in a neighbor's care, a trust she dared not place in mortal hands, especially not for hours at a stretch. She set inquiries afoot for a boy to transport her baskets on Saturdays and Mondays.

A plump, fresh-complexioned youth named Luke Peterson entered. He looked fifteen, though likely eighteen, blushing as he twiddled his bonnet. "Mistress," he stammered, "would not a man serve your turn as well as a boy?"

Before he finished speaking, Margaret thought, *This boy will just do.* But taking the cue, she replied, "Nay; but a man may seek more than I can well pay."

"Not I," Luke said warmly. "Why, Mistress Margaret, I am your neighbor. I do very well at coopering. I can carry your basket before or after my day's work. Welcome! You need pay me nothing. 'Tis not as if we were strangers, ye know."

"Why, Master Luke," she admitted, "I know your face, but I cannot recall a word passing between us."

"Oh yes, you did, Mistress Margaret!" Luke exclaimed. "Have you forgotten? One day you tried to carry your baby and your pitcher full of water. I said, 'Give me the baby to carry.' 'Nay,' you said, 'I'll give you the pitcher, and keep the bairn myself.' I carried the pitcher home, and you took it from me at this door, saying, 'I am muckle obliged to you, young man,' in such a sweet voice—not like the folk in this street speak to a body."

"I do mind now, Master Luke," she said softly. "And methinks it was the least I could say."

"Well, Mistress Margaret, if you will say as much every time I carry your basket, I care not how often I bear it, nor how far."

"Nay, nay," Margaret blushed faintly. "I would not put upon good nature. You are young, Master Luke, and kindly. Shall I give you your supper on Saturday night, when you bring the linen home, and your dawn-meat on Monday? Would that make us even?"

"As you please," Luke smiled. "Only say not I sought a couple of diets for such a trifle."

With Luke's timely aid and the health Heaven bestowed upon this poor young woman to balance her many ills, the household moved smoothly for a while. Yet, the heart remained troubled by Gerard's long, now most mysterious, silence.

Suspense, that mental torturer, began to tear her heavy heart with hot pincers, driving her to cry often and vehemently, "Oh, that I could know the worst!"
