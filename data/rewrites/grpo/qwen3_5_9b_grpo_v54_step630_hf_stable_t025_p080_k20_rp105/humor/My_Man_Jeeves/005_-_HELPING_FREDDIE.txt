I don't want to bore you with the usual preamble, but I simply must tell you about dear old Freddie Meadowes. I'm not exactly a stylistic genius myself, but I'll hire a proper writer to polish the prose once I've finished the rough draft.

Dear old Freddie has been my pal for years. One morning, I found him slumped in a dark corner of the club, staring blankly at nothing, looking like a wilted flower. Naturally, I was alarmed. Usually, the old rogue is the life and soul of the set.

Jimmy Pinkerton, a playwright of considerable intellect, was with me. Between us, we interrogated the poor pop-eyed chap until he confessed. As suspected, it was a girl. He'd had a row with Angela West, his fiancée, and she'd broken it off. She wouldn't let him near her, ignored his calls, and returned his letters unopened.

I felt for Freddie. I knew the pain. I once loved a girl named Elizabeth Shoolbred who couldn't stand me at any price—a fact that will surely be recorded in my autobiography.

"Change of scene is what you need, old scout," I told him. "Come to Marvis Bay. I've rented a cottage. Jimmy's arriving on the twenty-fourth. We'll be a cozy trio."

"He's right," Jimmy agreed. "I know a man who went abroad after a rejection. Two months later, the girl wired 'Come back, Muriel.' He started writing a reply, forgot her surname, and never answered."

Freddie remained unconsoled, looking as if he'd swallowed his last sixpence, but he promised to come. Marvis Bay, in Dorsetshire, isn't thrilling, but it has its charms. Days are spent bathing; evenings involve strolling the shore with gnats. By nine, you're rubbing ointment on bites and sleeping.

Freddie loved it. Once the moon rose, he became a local celebrity among the insects, who waited specifically to bite him. However, by the end of the first week, I wished Jimmy had arrived sooner. As a companion, Freddie was hardly someone to write home about. When not scowling at the carpet or playing "The Rosary" on the piano with one finger (blowing a fuse around the third bar), he was lost.

One morning, as I returned from bathing, he looked up in a hollow voice. "Reggie, I've seen her."

"Miss West?"

"Yes. At the post office. She cut me!"

He tried to play "The Rosary" again but slipped on the second bar. "You shouldn't have brought me here. I must go."

"Don't talk rot. This is where you come out strong."

"She looked clean through me!"

"Be a sportsman. Have another dash."

"But what am I supposed to thank her for?"

"Save her from drowning," I suggested.

"I can't swim," Freddie admitted.

That was Freddie: a dear chap, but useless in a crisis. I sprinted outside to the sands. The brainwork had to be mine. Freddie was great at polo and could mimic cats fighting, but he lacked enterprise.

While my brain whirred like a dynamo, I spotted a blue dress. It was her. She was sitting on the sand, helping a small, fat child build a castle. An elderly lady nearby called her "aunt," so I deduced the child was her cousin. Freddie would have tried to work up some sentiment about the kid; I couldn't. The child was just a round, bulging lump.

Later, the child began to whimper. The girl took him to a sweet stall, and I walked on.

Fellows will tell you I'm a chump. I admit it. All the Peppers are chumps. But occasionally, I get a hot brain-wave. Walking back, I saw the fat child smacking a jellyfish with a spade. The girl was gone.

"If I kidnap this heavy-weight," I reasoned, "and Freddie appears leading him by the hand, claiming he saved the child's life, her gratitude will force a reconciliation."

I grabbed the kid and fled. Back at the cottage, Freddie didn't effervesce with joy when I dumped the screaming infant in the sitting-room. After I fetched honey to quiet the child, I explained the plan.

"You're not such a fool as you look," Freddie admitted. "This seems pretty good."

He took the child to scour the beach for Angela. I felt happier than ever, leaning on the veranda, when I saw Freddie returning, looking miserable.

"Yes, I found her," he said with a bitter laugh. "But this isn't her cousin. He's just a kid she met. She's furious."

"She gave you the cold shoulder?"

"She told me if I ever met Dr. Cook, it would be the biggest duel on record. I crawled away licking my wounds."

"And now?"

"Now, you're in trouble. Find the parents and return him before the police arrive."

I tramped Marvis Bay like a bloodhound. No one claimed the infant. It wasn't until I asked the sweet-stall man that I learned his name was Medwin and his parents lived at Ocean Rest.

I arrived at Ocean Rest. No one answered. A round, pink face with grey whiskers appeared in an upper window.

"Hi!" it shouted. "Can't you come in? Hello, is that Tootles?"

"My name is not Tootles," I yelled. "I've brought back your son."

"Peep-bo, Tootles! Dadda can see 'oo!" The face vanished.

"Do you live here?" the face asked again.

"I'm staying here."

"Pepper? Any relation to Edward Pepper, the colliery owner?"

"My uncle."

"We were wondering what to do with Tootles. We have the mumps here. My daughter Bootles has them. Tootles must not be exposed. It's most fortunate you found him. You must take him to your house. Your wife will like Tootles."

"I haven't got a wife!" I screamed.

The window slammed shut. A package weighing a ton hit me on the head.

"Did you catch it?" the face asked. "Bailey's Granulated Breakfast Chips. Tootles takes them for breakfast. Get Bailey's."

My spirit broke. I accepted the situation. As we walked away, we met Angela. The sight of her made the child point and say, "Wah!" She smiled, bent down, and said, "Well, baby? So father found you again?"

She turned to me. "Your little son and I made friends on the beach this morning."

This was the limit. Coming off the interview with the lunatic, I was so unnerved that she was halfway down the road before I could deny being the infant's father.

Freddie, upon hearing the news, leaped up, glared at the child, and clutched his head. He didn't speak for a long time, then erupted emotionally.

"Well," he finally said, "say something! Why don't you say something?"

"You don't give me a chance," I soothed.

"What are you going to do?"

"We can't act as nurses to this exhibit."

"I'm going back to London," Freddie declared.

"Freddie! Would you desert a pal?"

"This is your business. You've got to manage it."

"Freddie, you've got to stand by me. Besides, I did it all for your sake."

He looked at me curiously. "Reggie, I'll stand a good deal, but I won't stand for being expected to be grateful."

Looking back, I realize what saved me from Colney Hatch was buying out the local sweet shop. By feeding the kid incessantly, we survived the day. At eight, he fell asleep. Getting him undressed was easy; getting him dressed was a nightmare. We stared at a pile of clothes and smiled wanly.

In the morning, we borrowed a nurse from the next bungalow. She dressed the kid in eight minutes. I showered wealth on her.

"And after all," I said, "there's something cosy about having a child about the house."

Just then, the kid spilled milk on Freddie's trousers. Later, Freddie began talking about King Herod and how much he admired the man's impulsive views on infanticide.

Two days later, Jimmy Pinkerton arrived. Seeing the howling child, he packed his portmanteau.

"For me, the hotel. I can't write dialogue with that going on. Whose work is this?"

I told him about Mr. Medwin and the mumps.

"I might work this up for the stage," Jimmy said. "A farce. Act one: the idiot kidnaps the child. Act two: the adventures. It's big. Child center. Girl L.C.; Freddie up stage. Can Freddie play the piano?"

"He plays 'The Rosary' with one finger."

"No, we'll cut the soft music. Look here." Jimmy squatted in the sand. "This stone is the girl. This seaweed is the child. This nutshell is Freddie. The child says, 'Boofer lady, does i'oo love dadda?' Then, 'Kiss Freddie.' Short, crisp, punchy."

"But the girl cuts Freddie. She wouldn't come near him."

"That's awkward. We'll make it an exterior set. We'll corner her on the beach. Meanwhile, we must get the kid letter-perfect."

We decided not to tell Freddie yet. We focused on Tootles, using sweets as a sub-motive. Jimmy noted, "The chief difficulty is establishing a connection between the line and the sweets. Once he knows those two words result in acid-drops, we have success."

Some days the kid nailed the line; others, he went to pieces. Time flew.

"We must hurry," I said. "His uncle may arrive any day."

"And we haven't an understudy," Jimmy replied. "We must work! That kid is a bad study. I've known deaf-mutes who learned faster."

The kid was a trier, though. Failure didn't discourage him. He kept trying whenever sweets were near.

"We're not ready," Jimmy insisted. "Today he said 'Kick Freddie.' That won't win any girl's heart. Postpone production."

But Fate intervened. The curtain went up the very next afternoon.

Freddie was at the piano. I was leading the kid out to exercise when Angela appeared on her way to the beach. The kid yelled, and she stopped.

"Hello, baby! Good morning. May I come up?"

She didn't wait. She came up the steps and started fussing over the child. Six feet away, Freddie hammered the piano.

I tried to break the scene. "We were just going down to the beach."

"So you're having your piano tuned?" she asked. "My aunt has been trying to find a tuner. Do you mind if I go in and tell him to come on to us?"

"Not yet!" I lied. "He can't bear disturbance. It's the artistic temperament."

"Very well. Ask him to call at Pine Bungalow. West is the name. I'll wait."

She started talking to the kid, searching her pocket. "See what I've brought for you, baby."

She held up a chunk of toffee the size of the Automobile Club.

That finished it. The kid, worked up from rehearsal, got the line right first time.

"Kiss Fweddie!" he shouted.

The front door opened. Freddie came out onto the veranda, as if taking a cue.

He looked at the girl; she looked at him. I looked at the ground; the kid looked at the toffee.

"Kiss Fweddie!" he yelled. "Kiss Fweddie!"

The girl held up the toffee, and the kid extended his hands toward it.

"Kiss Fweddie!" he shrieked.

"What does this mean?" she asked, turning to me.

"You'd better give it to him," I said. "He'll go on till you do."

She gave the toffee, and the kid subsided. Freddie stood gaping, speechless.

"What does it mean?" she asked again. Her face was pink, her eyes sparkling. Did you ever tread on your partner's dress at a dance, see her smile like an angel, and then suddenly feel as if you'd stepped on a rake's teeth? That was how Freddie's Angela looked.

"Well?" she said, her teeth clicking.

I gulped. Then I said it was nothing. Then nothing much. Then, "Oh, well, it was this way." After brief remarks about Jimmy Pinkerton, I told her everything. Freddie stood there, silent.

The girl didn't speak. She just listened. Then she began to laugh. I never heard a girl laugh so much. She leaned against the veranda and shrieked. Freddie, the World's Champion Chump, stood there saying nothing.

I sidled toward the steps. I had said all I had to say; the stage direction "exit" was written in my part. I gave Freddie up in despair. If only he had said a word, it might have been all right. But there he stood, speechless.

Just out of sight, I met Jimmy.

"Hello, Reggie! Where's the kid? We must have a big rehearsal today."

"No good," I said sadly. "It's all over. Freddie has made an ass of himself and killed the show."

"Tell me."

I told him.

"Fluffed in his lines?" Jimmy nodded thoughtfully. "Always the way with amateurs. We must go back. Things look bad, but it may not be too late."

"Great Scot!" I cried. "Look!"

In front of the cottage stood six children, a nurse, and the grocer's man staring. From opposite windows, four hundred heads projected. Down the road galloped five more children, a dog, three men, and a boy about to stare. On our porch, unconscious of the spectators, stood Freddie and Angela, clasped in each other's arms.
