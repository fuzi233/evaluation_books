It took the rooms’ new paper—its small sprigs and pale prettiness, the sort of pattern meant to coax cheerfulness out of plain plaster—to make the house in Milton seem even tolerable. And yet it needed more—more than paper and polish could supply; more than could be bought, or carried in a removal-van, or unpacked from straw.

The thick yellow fogs of November had come on before Mrs. Hale crossed the threshold. The bend of the river that was said to open the valley into something like a view lay utterly blotted out; the world beyond the panes had been rubbed away to a smear of choking white. Even indoors the mist seemed to creep, insinuating itself under door and sash, leaving a faint wet on the knuckles when you touched the latch, and a taste like soot on the tongue.

Margaret and Dixon had worked for two days without pause—lifting, setting, scolding, dusting, and making a kind of order out of other people’s disorderly packing. There were still boxes with their cords uncut, chair-legs wrapped in paper, and a litter of straw that clung in corners as if it had rooted there. But all their strength had gone, not to Margaret’s own room, nor to any showy part of the house, but into one purpose: to make Mrs. Hale’s chamber ready—warm, quiet, and as like Helstone as a smoky northern lodging could be made.

Mrs. Hale came in, stood for a moment as if the very air had struck her back, and then, with a blankness that frightened Margaret more than any tears, said—

“Oh, Margaret! are we to live here?”

Margaret’s heart answered before her lips could. She forced herself to speak evenly, as if this were only a trifling change of weather.

“Oh—the fogs in London are sometimes far worse.”

“But then you knew that London itself, and friends lay behind it. Here—well! we are desolate. Oh Dixon, what a place this is!”

Dixon’s face tightened as if she had been slapped.

“Indeed, ma’am, I’m sure it will be your death before long, and then I know who’ll—stay! Miss Hale, that’s far too heavy for you to lift.”

Margaret had her hands already under the edge of a chest. The wood was damp-cold, the iron nails gritty with dust.

“Not at all, thank you, Dixon,” she answered, with a coldness that was less anger than the sort of self-command that comes of having no time to break down. “The best thing we can do for mamma is to get her room quite ready for her to go to bed, while I go and bring her a cup of coffee.”

Coffee was one of the few immediate comforts she could promise—heat, bitterness, the clean, sharp smell of it rising in a room that otherwise smelt of damp paper, coal smoke, and the sourness of unpacked things. She went to the kitchen and made it herself; she measured the grounds with care—what little there was had to last—poured the water when it had just ceased its furious boiling, and let the dark infusion stand to gather strength. The small domestic ritual steadied her. The kettle sang, then subsided; the spoon tapped the cup; the thick liquid poured with a sound almost cheerful, and the smell—warm and honest—seemed for a moment to stand between her mother and that raw fog outside.

But the whole house was steeped in disheartenment. Mr. Hale, too, moved like a man who had lost his road in a familiar field and could not admit he was afraid.

“Margaret,” he said, going to the window as if the sight of the town might supply him with courage, “I do believe this is an unhealthy place. Only suppose that your mother’s health or yours should suffer. I wish I had gone into some country place in Wales; this is really terrible.”

There was no comfort to be given—not the kind he asked for. They were there. The smoke and fog were not a bad dream, to be shaken off by morning; they were circumstance, and circumstance had shut as thickly round them as the mist that pressed its pallid face to the glass.

Only the day before, when the last bills were gathered, and the little store of ready money told out and found nearly spent, Margaret had seen her father’s dismay—quiet, ashamed, and helpless in its gentleness. The move, the fortnight at Heston, the thousand small necessities that seem to spring up like nettles when you change your home—almost all had gone. There was no turning back, no choosing Wales after all.

That night, when the first exhaustion came and left her awake in its place, Margaret sat in her own room and understood it: Milton was not merely where they lodged; it was the wall against which their hopes must now learn to live.

Her bedroom was in the long narrow projection at the back. The window set sideways in the oblong looked upon another blank projection opposite, not ten feet away—brick, damp, and darkened by fog, looming through the vapour like a barrier built on purpose to forbid the idea of distance. The air in the room was heavy with smoke, though no fire burned there; it had travelled in from elsewhere, clung to the curtains, and settled on her skin as if it meant to stain her thoughts.

Inside, everything was confusion. She had spent the day arranging her mother’s comforts; her own trunk stood half-open, and a box served for a chair. On it lay a direction-card, and at the sight of it her mind sprang backward with the suddenness of pain: it might have been written at Helstone. Helstone—green, beloved, with its soft syllables that belonged to her tongue and memory alike. The name was like the taste of a fruit out of season—sweet, and almost unbearable.

She might have sunk into mere wretchedness; but some part of her, practical and proud, resisted the indulgence of despair. She remembered Edith’s letter—the one she had only half read amid the noise of arrival—and snatched at it as at any hand held out in the dark.

It was full of Corfu: their voyage along the Mediterranean; music and dancing on board; the new life opening as easily as a flower. Edith wrote fluently, with a certain graceful abundance—enough particulars to let Margaret build the scene for herself. A villa high upon rocks; a trellised balcony; white cliffs and deep blue sea; days passed in boating or picnics; everything out-of-doors and pleasure-seeking, as if the very air were a kind of freedom.

Captain Lennox attended drill. Edith, the most musical officer’s wife there, copied the new popular tunes from the latest English music for the bandmaster, as if that were labour. These were their hardships. And then, as if it were the simplest thing, she hoped that if the regiment stayed another year Margaret might come and make a long visit—an invitation tossed like a ribbon across leagues of sea and difference.

She reminded Margaret, affectionately, of a day twelve-month ago in Harley Street: rain all day long; her refusal to wear a new gown to a stupid dinner, lest it be splashed in going to the carriage; and how at that very dinner they had first met Captain Lennox.

Yes. Margaret remembered. She remembered the abundant dinners and smooth arrangements; the stately handsomeness; the noiseless service; the settled assumption that life was meant to be easy if only one kept within the right circle. That memory came back to her now with a sharpness that was almost physical: she could have named the scent of the drawing-room—flowers, wax, and warmed upholstery—and heard the soft rustle of dresses like the murmur of a sheltered sea.

That world rolled on without them. The habitual dinners, the calls, the dancing evenings would be going on as if forever; Aunt Shaw might be gone, Edith transported to a Mediterranean balcony, yet the set would close up like water after a ship passes—no mark left to show where any of them had been. And she—Margaret Hale, who had once moved within it—was surely less missed than any.

Perhaps only Henry Lennox would think of her; and he would strive not to, because of the pain she had caused him. He had often boasted of his power to put disagreeable thoughts away, as one shuts a drawer and forgets what lies in it.

Her mind went a little farther still, into that region where “what might have been” lives like a ghost. If she had cared for him as a lover, if she had accepted him, and then this change in her father’s opinions—and with it the loss of position—had come, she could not doubt how impatiently Mr. Lennox would have received it. The world’s rough wholesale judgment was ready to label her father degraded; and such judgment would have galled a man of Henry’s temper.

It mortified her; and yet she could bear it, because she knew her father’s purity of purpose. That knowledge—so plain, so solemn—was like bread in a time of scarcity: not delicacy, but sustenance. It strengthened her to endure his errors, grave though they seemed to her.

By the time she folded Edith’s letter, she had reached a weary kind of gratitude. They were at the lowest now; it could scarcely be worse. When Edith’s astonishment and Aunt Shaw’s dismay arrived in letters, they would meet them bravely.

She undressed slowly, feeling the strange luxury of leisure after all the day’s hurry, though it was late. She fell asleep hoping for brightness—either in the world or in herself. Had she known how long it would be before any real brightness came, her heart might have failed.

The season was unpropitious to health as well as spirits. The fogs seemed to sit on the lungs. Mrs. Hale caught a severe cold almost at once; her voice grew thin and her breathing troubled, as if the air itself demanded payment. Dixon, too, was evidently not well; but Margaret could not insult her more than by proposing to spare her. Dixon would rather die than be “saved” by Miss Hale.

They could hear of no girl to assist. Such as might have come were at the factories. Those who applied were dismissed with sharp scolding by Dixon for imagining that girls of their kind could ever be trusted in a gentleman’s house. So a charwoman was kept in almost constant employ, coming and going with her buckets and her blunt, patient way of scrubbing away at Milton grime that seemed to breed afresh the moment your back was turned.

Margaret longed to send for Charlotte; but Charlotte was too good a servant for what they could now afford, and the distance was too great. Their new economy had edges that cut.

Meanwhile Mr. Hale found pupils—recommended by Mr. Bell, or by the more immediate influence of Mr. Thornton. Most were boys of an age when in other places they might still be at school; but Milton had its own stern education. Here, it was believed that to make a lad into a good tradesman he must be caught young and acclimated to the mill, the office, the warehouse. If he were sent to even the Scotch universities, he came back unsettled for commerce; how much more if he went to Oxford or Cambridge, where he could not enter till he was eighteen? So manufacturers set their sons to work at fourteen or fifteen, pruning away all off-shoots in the direction of literature in hopes the whole vigour of the plant might be forced into trade.

And yet there were wiser parents—men who knew their own deficiencies, and had sense enough to strive against them; and some, no longer youths but grown men, who had the stern humility to learn late what they should have learnt early.

Mr. Thornton was perhaps the oldest of Mr. Hale’s pupils. He was certainly his favourite.

So much did Mr. Hale quote his opinions, and with such regard, that in time it became a domestic joke to wonder what learning could be accomplished in an hour when so much of it appeared to have been spent in conversation. Margaret even encouraged the lightness with which her father spoke of Mr. Thornton, because she felt that her mother watched this new friendship with a kind of jealousy. In Helstone, where Mr. Hale’s days had been occupied with books and parishioners, Mrs. Hale had seemed to care little whether she saw much of him or not; but now, when he looked eagerly forward to each renewal of intercourse with Mr. Thornton, she was hurt, as if for the first time he preferred another companionship to her own.

Mr. Hale’s praise had the usual effect of praise too often repeated: it made others inclined to rebel against hearing Aristides called the Just. After more than twenty years of quiet life in a country parsonage, there was something dazzling to him in the energy that conquered difficulties, in the power of Milton—its machinery, its men. He yielded to the grandeur of it without caring to inquire much into the details of its exercise.

Margaret went less abroad among machinery and men; saw less of power in its public effect; and, as it happened, she was thrown into contact with those who suffered acutely in all measures meant for masses of people—exceptions trampled in the procession. The question that began to form in her mind, as steady as it was quiet, was whether everything had been done to make the sufferings of those exceptions as small as possible; whether the helpless had been gently lifted out of the conqueror’s road—or ground under his wheels.

It fell to her share, too, to look out for a servant to assist Dixon; Dixon had at first undertaken to find just the right person to do the rough work. But Dixon’s idea of a helpful girl was shaped by Helstone—by tidy elder scholars at the village school who had been proud to come to the parsonage on a busy day, and who treated Mrs. Dixon with the respect—and more than respect, with a kind of frightened homage—that they paid to Mr. and Mrs. Hale.

Dixon was not unconscious of that old reverence; she had enjoyed it. In Milton, where no one knew the past and no one cared for it, she was like a dethroned queen who still expected courtiers to shade their eyes from her splendour. Nothing but her faithful love for Mrs. Hale made her endure the rough independent way in which the Milton girls who came to ask for the place answered her questions. They even questioned her in return, doubtful of the solvency of a family that lived in a house of thirty pounds a year and yet kept two servants, one of them so high and mighty.

Mr. Hale was no longer “the Vicar”; he was a man who spent at a certain rate, and might not always be able to.

Margaret grew weary of the accounts Dixon brought to her mother—endless tales of insolence, of manners that seemed to Dixon almost criminal. Not that Margaret was insensible to it: she, too, was repelled by the rough uncourtesy; she, too, shrank with fastidious pride from hail-fellow familiarity, and resented the open curiosity of people who asked, without disguise, what business her family had in Milton if they were not “in trade.”

But the more she felt an impertinence, the more silent she became about it; and if she took the search upon herself, she could at least spare her mother the recital of every disappointment. So she went up and down, in and out of butchers’ shops and grocers’, asking after a nonpareil of a girl; lowering her expectations each week as she learnt how difficult it was, in a manufacturing town, to tempt any one from the better wages and greater independence of the mill.

It was a trial to go out alone in this busy bustling place. Mrs. Shaw’s notions of propriety, and her own reliance on attendants in London, had made it an established thing that a footman must accompany Edith and Margaret if they went beyond Harley Street or the immediate neighbourhood. Margaret had silently rebelled against those limits at the time; and later, in Helstone, she had doubly enjoyed her freedom, walking as fancy led her—sometimes bounding into a run, sometimes standing still as stone to listen.

There, the soundscape had been her companion: the soft scold of a thrush in the holly; the drip of water in ditches; the hush that is not emptiness but living quiet. Even the church-bell, when it spoke, seemed to be speaking the dialect of home, with a tone that belonged to the place and to her childhood.

Here, in Milton, the streets imposed an even decorous pace, and worse than that—an unceasing clatter and roar that seemed to grind the nerves. The town did not so much speak as shout. Carts rattled over stones; iron rang on iron; voices rose and collided; and far off, through everything, there was that continual undertone—like a giant breathing through a sooty cloth—the mills’ labouring sound. It was as if the air itself had become a machine.

The side of the town on which Crampton lay was especially a thoroughfare for the factory people. In the back streets there were many mills out of which poured streams of men and women two or three times a day. Until Margaret learned their hours of ingress and egress, she constantly fell among them.

They came rushing along, bold-faced, fearless, loud with laughs and jests—often aimed at those who appeared above them. The unrestrained voices, the careless breaking of common street politeness, frightened Margaret at first. The girls, with rough but not unfriendly freedom, commented on her dress; even touched her shawl or gown to discover the exact material, as if cloth were a language they all understood. Once or twice she was asked, directly and without embarrassment, where such an article had been bought.

There was such simple reliance on her womanly sympathy in their love of dress, and on her kindliness, that, once she understood, she answered readily enough, even smiling.

She did not mind meeting any number of girls, boisterous as they were. But she alternately dreaded and fired up against the workmen, who commented not on her dress but on her looks, with the same open fearlessness. She, who had always felt that even a refined remark on her appearance was an impertinence, had now to endure undisguised admiration shouted across a street.

And yet the outspokenness had in it a sort of blunt innocence. They meant no harm to her delicacy; but she was too startled by the disorderly tumult at first to perceive it. Out of fear came indignation. Her cheeks would burn, her dark eyes flash, at some speech that struck her like a thrown stone.

Later, safe at home, she would find herself half amused even while she resented it. Once, after she had passed a group of men, several wishing openly that she were their sweetheart, one lingerer called after her, “Your bonny face, my lass, makes the day look brighter.”

Another day, as she walked with an unconscious smile at some passing thought, a poorly dressed middle-aged workman said to her, “You may well smile, my lass; many a one would smile to have such a bonny face.”

He looked so careworn that Margaret’s immediate impulse was not anger but pity. She gave him an answering smile—glad, in spite of herself, to think that her looks, such as they were, might have called up a pleasant thought in a man whose life seemed all labour and want.

He appeared to understand her glance. From that time a silent recognition was established between them whenever chance threw them across each other’s paths. They never exchanged another word; nothing passed but that first compliment, and yet Margaret found herself thinking of him with more interest than of anyone else in Milton.

Once or twice on Sundays she saw him walking with a girl—evidently his daughter—who looked, if possible, more unhealthy than he was.

One day, early spring, Margaret and her father had walked as far as the fields that lay round the town. The air there, away from the close streets, was thinner and seemed for a moment to wash the soot from the lungs; yet even there the distant sounds of Milton followed like a great pulse—now louder, now fainter, never wholly absent. Margaret gathered such hedge and ditch flowers as she could find—dog-violets, lesser celandines—each little blossom a quiet miracle in that soiled neighbourhood. She gathered them with an unspoken lament for Helstone’s profusion, where spring came like a festival and not like a grudged charity.

Her father left her to go into Milton upon some business; and on the road home she met the man and his daughter. The girl’s pale eyes fixed wistfully on the flowers in Margaret’s hand.

Acting on sudden impulse, Margaret held them out.

“Will you take them?”

The girl’s pale blue eyes lightened as she received the small bunch, as if she had been given something far beyond its worth. Her father spoke for her, his voice roughened by feeling he did not know how to show.

“Thank yo, Miss. Bessy’ll think a deal o’ them flowers; that hoo will; and I shall think a deal o’ yor kindness. Yo’re not of this country, I reckon?”

“No,” said Margaret, and the word came out with a half-sigh. “I come from the South—from Hampshire.” She chose the safer description, fearing to wound him by a name he might not know.

“That’s beyond London, I reckon? And I come fro’ Burnleyways, and forty miles to th’ North. And yet, yo see, North and South has both met and made kind o’ friends in this big smoky place.”

Margaret slackened her pace to walk beside them. The girl’s steps were slow, governed by weakness. Margaret turned to her, and spoke gently; the pity in her tone went straight, like warm broth to an empty stomach, to the father’s heart.

“I’m afraid you are not very strong.”

“No,” said the girl quietly, “nor never will be.”

“Spring is coming,” Margaret said, trying to suggest hope, as she might have offered a sweetmeat to a sick child.

“Spring nor summer will do me good,” the girl answered. It was not dramatic; it was simply said, as if she had long ago tasted that truth and found it bitter but certain.

Margaret looked up at the father, almost expecting him to contradict, or soften it. Instead he added, with a grim straightforwardness—

“I’m afeared hoo speaks the truth. I’m afeared hoo’s too far gone in a waste.”

“I shall have a spring where I am boun’ to,” Bessy said, her voice taking on a strange brightness, “and flowers, and amaranths, and shining robes besides.”

“Poor lass, poor lass!” her father murmured. “I’m none so sure o’ that; but it’s a comfort to thee, poor lass, poor lass. Poor father! it’ll be soon.”

Margaret was shocked—shocked to hear death spoken of so plainly, as if it were a neighbour expected at any hour. Yet she was not repelled. On the contrary, something in the blunt naming of things drew her nearer; it was another language, another dialect of feeling, and she could not yet translate it, but she could not doubt its sincerity.

“Where do you live?” she asked. “I think we must be neighbours; we meet so often on this road.”

“We put up at nine Frances Street—second turn to th’ left at after yo’ve past th’ Goulden Dragon.”

“And your name? I must not forget that.”

“I’m none ashamed o’ my name,” he answered, almost as if she had accused him. “It’s Nicholas Higgins. Hoo’s called Bessy Higgins. Whatten yo’ asking for?”

The last question startled Margaret. In Helstone it would have been understood that such enquiries meant a visit—a gentle, unquestioned sequence: name, place, call. Here, her intention seemed suddenly to wear the shape of intrusion. She felt shy; her face grew hot.

“I thought—I meant to come and see you.”

He looked at her as if measuring her right to do so; and she read, in his eye, a suspicion she had not expected.

“I’m none so fond of having strange folk in my house.”

Then, relenting as he saw the heightened colour on her cheeks, he added, with a grudging kindness that was still kindness—

“Yo’re a foreigner, as one may say, and maybe don’t know many folk here, and yo’ve given my wench here flowers out of yo’r own hand;—yo may come if yo like.”

Margaret was half amused, half nettled. Permission offered as if it were a favour conferred did not soothe her pride. For an instant she doubted whether she would ever cross their threshold.

But at the turn into Frances Street the girl stopped, held her flowers closer as if they were a treasure, and said with a quiet insistence—

“Yo’ll not forget yo’re to come and see us.”

“Aye, aye,” said her father impatiently, as if tenderness must be hurried lest it shame him. “Hoo’ll come. Hoo’s a bit set up now, because hoo thinks I might ha’ spoken more civilly; but hoo’ll think better on it, and come. I can read her proud bonny face like a book. Come along, Bess; there’s the mill bell ringing.”

And indeed the bell was ringing—metallic, urgent, not like the clear church-bell of Helstone marking holy time, but like a command that cut the air and drove bodies forward. Its sound folded itself into the town’s larger roar as naturally as smoke into fog.

Margaret went home, still hearing the bell in her head, wondering at her new acquaintances, and smiling in spite of herself at the man’s insight into what had passed in her mind.

From that day Milton grew brighter to her—not because spring made the streets beautiful (it did not), nor because time had reconciled her to smoke and fog (it had not), but because among the harsh voices and grimy stones she had found something living: a human interest, like a small fire kindled in an unkind climate, by which one might warm one’s hands and, perhaps, one’s heart.
