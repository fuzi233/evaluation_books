Preface

A couple of close pals who got a sneak peek at these scribbles muttered that they weren't exactly trash, and a few distant relatives actually put money down on the promise to buy them once printed. So, feeling a bit like a man standing on a tightrope with his hands full of wet noodles, I've decided I don't have the heart to drag this out any further. If there hadn't been this tiny, faint whisper of public interest, I certainly wouldn't have dared serve up these "idle thoughts" as serious mental nourishment to the English-speaking world. After all, what does a modern reader want? They want a book to make their brains sprout wings, to teach them how to tie a bowline, and to lift their spirits above the clouds. This little volume? It couldn't even convince a cow to stand up straighter, let alone elevate humanity. I'd be lying through my teeth if I recommended it for anything so noble as self-improvement. My only honest suggestion is this: when you're bored stiff after devouring the "best hundred books" and need a palate cleanser, pick this up for twenty minutes. It won't fix your life, but it might just give you a nice, lazy sigh.

Here is what lies within these covers:

The Idle Thoughts of an Idle Fellow.

Let me be candid: I consider myself an absolute authority on the subject of idleness. Back when I was a lad, the tutor who baptized me at the fountain of wisdom for nine guineas a term—no hidden fees—used to boast that he'd never known a boy capable of wasting *so* much time. My poor grandmother, meanwhile, often dropped wise little hints while teaching me the intricacies of the Prayer-book. She’d say it was statistically improbable I'd ever accomplish anything I *shouldn't*, but absolutely certain I'd leave undone almost everything I *should*.

I fear I've partially disproven her second prediction. Heaven forbid! I have managed quite a few things I truly shouldn't have touched, despite my natural lethargy. However, regarding her first prophecy—that I would neglect much that required attention—I stand firmly vindicated. Idling has always been my superpower. I don't claim credit for it; it's a rare gift. There are plenty of loafers and slow coaches, but a true idler is a unicorn. He isn't the sort who slouches about with his hands in his pockets. On the contrary, the hallmark of a genuine idler is that he is perpetually, furiously busy.

One cannot truly savor idleness without a mountain of work piled high beside him. Doing nothing when you have nothing to do is merely a chore, and a tiring one at that. To make idling sweet, it must be stolen, like a kiss from a shy maiden.

Years ago, when I was a sprig, I fell violently ill. I never could quite figure out exactly what the matter was, save for a dreadful cold. Yet, the doctor insisted it was grave business, warning that if it had lasted another week, I wouldn't have survived. It's a curious fact: I've never encountered a physician who didn't arrive just in time to declare that a single day's delay would have sealed my fate. Medicine, it seems, operates on the principle of the melodramatic hero, swooping onto the scene at the absolute final moment. Providence, surely.

Consequently, I was ordered to Buxton for a month, with strict commands to achieve "perfect rest." No work allowed. "Not a muscle move," the doctor intoned.

To me, this sounded like paradise. I imagined a glorious four-week *dolce far niente*, seasoned just enough with sickness to give it a poetic edge. I envisioned rising late, sipping hot chocolate, and devouring breakfast in a dressing gown. I pictured lounging in a hammock, reading tragic romances until they slipped from my listless fingers, while I gazed dreamily at the sky, watching clouds sail like white vessels and listen to the birds sing. If I grew too weak, I'd prop myself at the window, looking frail and fascinating enough to make the local beauties sigh. Twice daily, I'd glide in a Bath chair to the Colonnade to sip the "waters."

Oh, those waters! I knew nothing of their reputation then, charmed by the fashion. But after three or four mornings, Sam Weller's description of them tasting like "warm flat irons" became an understatement. They were hideous and nauseating. Nothing would have cured a sick man faster than knowing he had to gulp them down daily. I drank them neat for six days and nearly perished; eventually, I discovered the remedy: a stiff glass of brandy-and-water immediately afterwards. The eminent doctors later claimed the alcohol neutralized the iron, but I'm glad I stumbled upon the truth.

Yet, drinking the waters was merely the appetizer to the torture of that month—the most miserable of my life. For most of it, I obeyed the doctor, doing nothing but pacing the garden and taking two-hour rides in the chair. This broke the monotony slightly. There is a unique thrill to Bath-chairing, especially for the unaccustomed. A constant sense of impending doom haunts the passenger. One is convinced every minute that disaster is imminent, a feeling spikes whenever a ditch looms ahead. Every passing carriage seems destined to clip you, and every hill ascent triggers a mental calculation of survival odds, assuming, as probability dictates, the driver will lose control.

Eventually, even this failed to spark the soul, and the ennui became suffocating. My mind began to crack under the pressure. Around the twentieth morning, I rose, ate a hearty breakfast, and marched straight to Hayfield, a bustling town nestled in a valley, home to two delightfully pretty women. One crossed the bridge, perhaps smiling at me; the other stood by an open door, bestowing unprofitable kisses on a cherubic infant. Years have passed, and I dare say they've both gained weight and temper. Returning, I spotted an old man breaking stones, and a fierce urge to flex my muscles overwhelmed me. I offered him a drink to swap places. He graciously agreed. Armed with three weeks of pent-up energy, I shattered stones for thirty minutes that he had labored over all day. He didn't seem jealous.

Emboldened, I plunged deeper into dissipation, taking long walks and listening to the band nightly. Yet the days dragged on interminably. I was heartily relieved when the final day arrived, whisking me away from the gout-ridden, consumptive air of Buxton back to London, with its stern industry. As the carriage thundered through Hendon, the lurid glow of the great city warmed my heart. Later, as my cab rattled out of St. Pancras, the familiar roar of the streets swelled up around me, sounding sweeter than any music I'd heard in months.

I certainly did not enjoy that month of enforced idleness. I relish idling when I *ought* to be working; I hate it when it is the *only* option. That is my stubborn nature. The moment I love standing by the fire, tallying debts, is when my desk is buried under a mountain of urgent correspondence. The pleasure of dawdling over dinner is greatest when a grueling evening awaits. And if I must rise early due to pressing matters, it is then, above all times, that I cherish lying an extra half-hour in bed.

Ah, how divine it is to roll over and drift back to sleep—"just for five minutes." Does any human being, aside from the protagonist of a Sunday-school tale, ever wake willingly? Some men find rising at the appointed hour an impossibility. If eight o'clock is the time, they linger till half-past. If circumstances shift and half-past becomes early enough, they wait until nine. They resemble the politician famed for being "punctually half-an-hour late." They devise endless schemes: buying alarm clocks that malfunction to wake the wrong souls, instructing servants to knock, receiving a grunt of "awry," and sinking back into dreams. I knew a fellow who took a cold bath to force himself up, only to crawl back into bed to warm his bones.

I believe I could stay awake indefinitely once I had started, but the initial wrenching of the head from the pillow is insurmountable. After a wasted evening, I resolve, "I'll rise early tomorrow!" Only to find that by morning, enthusiasm has evaporated, and the dread of dressing looms large.

Strange, this bed, this mock-grave where we stretch our weary limbs and sink into silence. "O bed, O bed, delicious bed..." as the poor bard Hood sang, you are a kind old nurse to our fretful hearts. Whether clever, foolish, naughty, or good, you cradle us all in your maternal lap, hushing our cries. The burdened giant, the sick sufferer, the maiden weeping for lost love—we lay our aching heads on your white bosom, and you soothe us to sleep.

But trouble arises when you refuse to comfort us. How agonizingly long the dawn seems when sleep eludes us! Those hideous nights of tossing in fever and pain, lying like living corpses staring into the drifting dark. And worse, those still more terrible vigils sitting by another in agony, where the falling ember of the fire startles us, and the ticking of the clock sounds like a hammer striking out the life we watch fade.

Enough of beds and bedrooms. I've dwelt on them too long, even for an idle fellow. Let us step out and smoke. That wastes time just as effectively and looks far better. Tobacco has been a boon to idlers. Hard to imagine what pre-Sir Walter civil-service clerks did to pass the time without it. I attribute the quarrelsome spirit of medieval youths entirely to the lack of this soothing weed. With no work and no smoking, they fought endlessly. If peace held, they instigated feuds with neighbors, or debated whose sweetheart was fairest, settling disputes with battle-axes and clubs. Taste questions were settled quickly: if a twelfth-century youth stepped outside to woo a girl and broke a rival's head, it proved his girl was beautiful. If the rival returned the favor, it proved hers was not. Their art criticism was brutal.

Nowadays, we light a pipe and let the girls settle their differences themselves.

They handle it splendidly. They are increasingly performing all our labor. They are doctors, barristers, artists, theater managers, swindle promoters, and newspaper editors. I eagerly await the era when men need do nothing but lounge in bed until noon, devour two novels a day, enjoy exclusive five-o'clock teas, and tax our brains only with debates over trouser patterns and the composition of Mr. Jones's coat. A glorious prospect—for idle fellows.

You have certainly been in love! If not, you are merely waiting for the inevitable arrival. Love is indistinguishable from measles; it is a universal rite of passage that every soul must endure. Like the measles, however, it is a singular affliction. One suffers through it once, acquiring a permanent immunity that renders future encounters harmless. A veteran of romance may stroll through shady woods, wander leafy aisles, and linger on mossy benches watching the sunset with absolute equanimity. He views a quiet country house no more menacingly than his private club. He joins family parties on the Rhine without a tremor and ventures near the altar to bid a friend farewell, unbothered by the sanctity of the ceremony. He dances a ravishing waltz with a steady head, rests afterwards in a dark conservatory with nothing worse than a mild chill, and braves moonlit lanes or twilight marshes without fear. He crosses stiles safely, scrambles through tangled hedges unseen, descends slippery paths without a tumble, and gazes into sunny eyes without being blinded. He hears the siren calls of passion yet steers his ship with unwavering discipline. He may clasp white hands in his, yet no electric shock—no *Lulu*-like force—binds him to their dainty pressure.

No, the heart does not succumb to love twice. Cupid fires only one arrow per heart. While life’s companionship—the handmaids of respect, admiration, and affection—remain loyal servants, keeping our doors perpetually ajar, the Great Master, Love himself, makes but one royal progress and then departs. We may like, cherish, and hold dear those who remain, but we never love again. A man's heart is akin to a fireworks display: at a specific moment, it flashes heavenward, blazing briefly to illuminate the entire world below. Then, the curtain falls on our mundane existence, leaving the burnt-out casing drifting back to earth, useless and ignored, slowly turning to ash. Once, breaking free from our mortal chains, we dared, like ancient Prometheus, to climb Olympus and snatch divine fire from Phoebus's chariot. Blessed are those who, hurrying back before the flame extinguishes, can kindle the domestic hearth of affection from its embers.

Yet, after reflection, that warm, glowing ember is far better suited to our chilly, cramped parlor than the blinding, consuming spirit of true Love. Love belongs in the great temples of the universe, where its vestal fire burns amidst the rolling thunder of the spheres. Affection, however, is the practical coal-fire that glows cheerfully when Love's white flame has faded. It is a fire fed daily, piling higher as winter approaches. Here, old men and women sit with thin hands clasped; children nestle close; neighbors find a welcoming corner; and even shaggy dogs and sleek cats toast their noses at the grate.

Let us heap kindness upon this fire. Cast in your pleasant words, the gentle press of the hand, and thoughtful, selfless deeds. Fan it with humor, patience, and forbearance. Let the wind blow and the rain fall; your hearth remains warm and bright, casting sunlight upon the faces gathered around it, regardless of the gloom without.

Now, my dear friends Edwin and Angelina, I fear you harbor unrealistic expectations. You imagine that your youthful hearts contain infinite fuel for this devouring passion throughout your long lives. Ah, young souls! Do not place your trust in that unsteady flicker. It will dwindle as months pass, and the supply cannot be replenished. Soon, you will watch it expire in anger and regret. To each, it will seem that the other has grown cold. Edwin, filled with bitterness, notes that Angelina no longer rushes to the gate with smiles and blushes; when he now catches a cough, she offers only a suggestion of a lozenge, delivered with a tone suggesting she wishes primarily to silence the noise. Poor Angelina, meanwhile, weeps silently because Edwin has abandoned the habit of carrying her handkerchief in his waistcoat pocket.

Both are stunned by the decline in the other, yet neither perceives their own transformation. If they understood themselves, they would not suffer so deeply. They would identify the root cause in the inherent limitations of human nature, join hands in mutual humility, and rebuild their home upon a foundation of earthly endurance. Instead, we are so blind to our faults and so hyper-vigilant regarding others'. Every misfortune is blamed on the partner. Angelina would have loved Edwin eternally if only he hadn't changed; Edwin would have adored her forever if she hadn't altered from her former self.

There comes a cheerless hour when the lamp of love has guttered out, and the fire of affection remains unlit. Then you must grope in the raw, cold dawn of life to strike a spark. May it catch before the day is wasted. Many sit shivering beside dead coals until nightfall.

But why preach? Who, feeling the rush of young love coursing through their veins, believes it will ever flow slow and feeble? To a twenty-year-old, it is impossible to conceive that he will love as wildly at sixty as he does now. He cannot recall a single middle-aged gentleman exhibiting symptoms of frantic attachment, yet he remains convinced his own love will never falter, regardless of everyone else's experience. Alas! By thirty, he joins the ranks of the cynics. It is not his fault. Our passions, both virtuous and vicious, end with our blushes. We do not hate, grieve, rejoice, or despair in our thirties as we did in our teens. Disappointment no longer suggests suicide, and success fails to intoxicate.

As we age, we conduct life in a minor key. There are few majestic movements in the later acts of life's opera. Ambition shrinks its aims; honor becomes reasonable and adapts conveniently to circumstances. And love—it dies. "Irreverence for the dreams of youth" creeps like a killing frost over our hearts. Tender shoots and blooming flowers are nipped and withered, leaving behind only a sapless stump where a vine once yearned to wrap its tendrils around the world.

My fair friends will surely deem this rank heresy. Far from believing men stop loving after boyhood, society insists that one does not earn a woman's serious consideration until gray hairs appear. Young ladies derive their concepts of men from novels penned by their own sex. Compared to the monstrous caricatures of humanity masquerading as heroes in that nightmare literature, Pythagoras's plucked bird and Frankenstein's demon were models of sanity.

In these so-called books, the protagonist, often termed a "Greek god"—by the way, they never specify which one, though he looks suspiciously like hunchbacked Vulcan, two-faced Janus, or drooling Silenus—is invariably a blackguard. Even the sliver of masculinity his classical ancestors possessed, he lacks entirely, being a listless, effeminate noodle past forty. Yet, behold the depth and strength of his emotion for some bread-and-butter schoolgirl! Hide your heads, ye young Roméos and Leanders! This *blasé* old beau loves with a hysterical fervor requiring four adjectives for every noun to describe properly.

It is fortunate, dear ladies, that you study only books. If you read actual mankind, you would learn that a boy's shy stammering tells a truer story than a man's bold eloquence. A boy's love springs from a full heart; a man's is frequently the result of a full stomach. Indeed, a man's sluggish current cannot compare to the rushing fountain that wells up when a boy's heart is struck by the heavenly rod. If you wish to taste love, drink from the pure stream youth pours at your feet. Do not wait until it becomes a muddy river before stooping to catch its waves.

Or perhaps you prefer its bitter flavor? Perhaps the clear, limpid water is insipid to your palate, and the pollution of its lower reaches gives it a relish to your lips? Must we believe those who tell us that a hand fouled by the filth of a shameful life is the only one a young girl desires to be caressed?

This is the doctrine bellowed daily from between yellow covers. Do they ever pause to consider what mischief they wreak, crawling through God's garden and telling childish Evés and silly Adams that sin is sweet and decency is ridiculous and vulgar? How many innocent girls do they not degrade into wicked women? How many weak lads do they not point toward the dirty by-path as the shortcut to a maiden's heart? They do not write of life as it truly is. Speak truth, and righteousness will take care of itself. But their pictures are coarse daubs painted from the sickly fancies of their own diseased imaginations.

We wish to envision women not, as their own sex presents them, as Lorettes luring us to ruin, but as good angels beckoning us upward. They possess greater power for good or ill than they realize. It is precisely at the age when a man's character is forming that he falls in love, and then the lass he loves determines whether he is made or marred. Unconsciously, he molds himself to fit her image, for better or worse. I am sorry to be ungallant enough to admit that I do not believe they always wield this influence for the best. Too often, the female world is confined strictly within the boundaries of the commonplace. Their ideal hero is a prince of littleness, and to become such causes many a powerful mind to be "lost to life and use and name and fame."

I suppose I could take a certain pride in wallowing in misery, finding a twisted sort of satisfaction in being utterly wretched; yet, let us be honest, no sane person actually enjoys a genuine attack of the blues. Still, everyone gets them. What is truly baffling—and perhaps the greatest mystery of human existence—is why anyone gets them. They are entirely unaccountable. You might find yourself drowning in gloom the very day after winning a fortune larger than your imagination could conjure, just as easily as you might succumb to a fit of depression the moment you realize you've forgotten your new silk umbrella on the train. The sensation is akin to suffering a triple assault of toothache, indigestion, and a severe cold simultaneously. Suddenly, you are stupid, restless, and irritable; you treat strangers with rudeness and your friends with unnecessary danger. You become clumsy, maudlin, and perpetually quarrelsome—a total nuisance to yourself and everyone within a fifty-yard radius.

Once seized by this malady, you are paralyzed. You can do nothing, yet you feel compelled to do something. You attempt to sit still, but the urge to move is overwhelming, so you don your hat and stride out for a walk. However, before you even reach the corner of the street, a crushing wave of regret hits you, and you immediately turn back. Perhaps you try to read a book to distract yourself, but the result is disastrous: Shakespeare seems trite and common, Dickens drags like a wet blanket, Thackeray bores you to tears, and Carlyle is far too sentimental. You discard the volume, muttering insults at the authors. Next, you try to shoo the cat out of the room, only to kick the door shut behind her with unnecessary force. You resolve to write a letter, starting with "Dearest Auntie: I find I have five minutes..." but after fifteen agonizing minutes of staring at the blank page, unable to formulate another word, you crumple the paper, fling your wet pen onto the tablecloth, and declare your intention to visit the Thompsons. Yet, as you pull on your gloves, the thought strikes you that the Thompsons are absolute idiots, they never serve supper, and you'll be expected to jump their baby. You curse the lot of them and cancel the trip.

By this stage, you feel completely crushed. You bury your face in your hands, wishing for death and a swift passage to heaven. In your fevered imagination, you visualize your own sickbed, surrounded by weeping friends and relatives. You bless them all, particularly the young and pretty ones, convinced they will cherish your memory when you're gone. You secretly hope they will finally appreciate you, contrasting their presumed future veneration with their current, glaring lack of respect.

These fleeting thoughts might offer a brief respite, but soon they evaporate. The next moment, you realize how foolish you were to imagine anyone would care if you vanished. Who really cares two straws—however many that might be—if you are blown up, hanged, married, or drowned? Nobody appreciates you. You have never received your due deserts. Reviewing your entire life, it becomes painfully obvious that you have been ill-treated from the cradle upward.

About half an hour of dwelling on these grim possibilities inflames you into a state of savage fury against the entire universe, and specifically against yourself, whom anatomy conveniently prevents you from kicking. Finally, bedtime arrives, offering salvation from further rash actions. You sprint upstairs, strip off your clothes—which scatter chaotically across the floor—and blow out the candle. You leap into bed with the frantic energy of someone backing a heavy wager to finish the routine against the clock. For two hours, you toss and tumble, breaking the monotony by occasionally throwing off the covers, crawling out of bed, and dressing again. Eventually, you collapse into an uneasy, fitful slumber plagued by nightmares, waking up late the following morning.

Of course, this is merely the plight of the unmarried. Married men, meanwhile, bully their wives, grumble at the dinner table, and insist the children go to bed. This domestic chaos creates quite a disturbance, which must be a tremendous relief to a man in the blues, since rowing is apparently the only form of amusement he finds entertaining.

The symptoms of this affliction remain remarkably consistent across the board, though the terminology varies wildly. The poet speaks of "a feeling of sadness coming o'er him." 'Arry describes the "heavings of his wayward heart" to Jimee, claiming he has "got the blooming hump." Your sister simply knows she is "out of sorts" and fears disaster, hoping nothing terrible happens. The average young man exclaims, "So awful glad to meet you, old fellow!" solely because he "feels so jolly miserable this evening." As for me, I typically announce, "I have a strange, unsettled feeling tonight," followed by the inevitable, "I think I'll go out."

Incidentally, this condition almost never manifests during daylight hours. It exclusively strikes in the evenings. During the sun-times, when the world bounds forward teeming with vitality, we cannot afford to sigh and sulk. The roaring clamor of the working day drowns out the faint, low-toned *miserere* sung by the elfin sprites whispering in our ears. By day, we may be angry, disappointed, or indignant, but we are never "in the blues," never truly melancholic. If a misfortune strikes at ten in the morning, we swear and knock furniture about; but if the same tragedy occurs at ten at night, we retreat into poetry or sit in the dark, contemplating how hollow this world truly is.

However, as a general rule, actual trouble does not cause our melancholy. Reality is often too stern a thing for such sentimental brooding. We linger to weep over a painted picture, but before the original, we would quickly avert our eyes. There is no pathos in genuine misery; no luxury in true grief. We do not toy with sharp swords nor hug a gnawing fox to our breasts for pleasure. When a man or woman loves to brood over a sorrow, carefully keeping it fresh in their memory, you can be certain it is no longer a pain. Whatever initial suffering they endured, the recollection has transformed into a perverse pleasure. Consider the many elderly ladies who daily gaze at tiny shoes nestled in lavender-scented drawers, weeping for the tiny feet whose toddling marches are done, or the sweet-faced young women who tuck a lock of hair beneath their pillows each night, mourning the boyish head that the salt waves have kissed to death. They will undoubtedly label me a nasty, cynical brute and accuse me of nonsense; yet, I firmly believe that if they asked themselves truthfully whether they find it unpleasant to dwell thus upon their sorrow, they would be compelled to answer "No." To some natures, tears are as sweet as laughter. As the old chronicler Froissart noted regarding the proverbial Englishman, he takes his pleasures sadly, and the Englishwoman takes hers in sadness itself.

I am not sneering. I would not, for a moment, mock anything that helps keep hearts tender in this hard, unfeeling world. Men are cold and commonsense enough as they are; we certainly would not want women to be the same. No, my dears, ladies, please remain always sentimental and soft-hearted. Be the soothing butter to our coarse, dry bread. Moreover, sentiment is to women what fun is to us. They do not care for our humor, and surely it would be unfair to deny them their grief. Who can claim that their mode of enjoyment is less sensible than ours? Why assume that a doubled-up body, a contorted purple face, and a gaping mouth emitting ear-splitting shrieks indicate a higher state of intelligence than a pensive face resting on a little white hand, with gentle, tear-dimmed eyes gazing back through Time's dark avenue at a fading past?

I am genuinely glad when I see Regret walking alongside me as a friend—I am glad because I know the saltness has been washed from the tears, and the sting must have been plucked from the beautiful face of Sorrow before we dared press her pale lips to ours. Time has laid his healing hand upon the wound. Now, when we look back upon the pain we once fainted under, no bitterness or despair rises in our hearts. The burden is no longer heavy; instead, we possess only that same sweet mingling of pleasure and pity that we feel when old, knight-hearted Colonel Newcome answers "*adsum*" to the great roll-call, or when Tom and Maggie Tulliver, clasping hands through the mists that have separated them, descend, locked in each other's arms, beneath the swollen waters of the Floss.

Mentioning poor Tom and Maggie Tulliver inevitably brings to mind a saying of George Eliot concerning this subject of melancholy. Somewhere she writes of the "sadness of a summer's evening." How wonderfully true—an observation as exquisite as anything that flowed from that magnificent pen—who has not felt the sorrowful enchantment of those lingering sunsets? The world belongs to Melancholy then, a thoughtful, deep-eyed maiden who despises the glare of day. It is not until "light thickens and the crow wings to the rocky wood" that she stealthily emerges from her groves. Her palace lies in the twilight lands. It is there she meets us. At her shadowy gate, she takes our hand and walks beside us through her mystic realm. We see no form, but we seem to hear the rustling of her wings.

Even in the toiling, humdrum city

It is a peculiar irony of fate. I sat down today with the grand ambition of composing something truly clever, perhaps a biting satire on modern manners or a profound meditation on the human condition. But for the life of me, my brain offers nothing but the singular, unvarnished topic of being hard up. I suspect it is the physical act of sitting with my hands buried deep in my trouser pockets that has short-circuited my wit. This is a habit I maintain with religious devotion, barring the presence of my sisters, cousins, or aunts. These ladies are relentless; they raise such a ruckus—though I prefer to describe it as an eloquent expostulation—that I am forced to surrender and extract my hands. Their collective refrain is always the same: "It is simply not gentlemanly."

Now, I must admit, I cannot fathom why. I could easily concede that it is undignified to reach into *another* person's pocket (especially if said person catches you), but how, O ye stern arbiters of optics, can placing one's own hands in one's own pockets render a man any less of a gentleman? Perhaps you are correct, though. Upon reflection, I recall hearing elderly gentlemen grumble savagely whenever they engaged in this activity. However, we younger lads, as a rule, find absolute comfort in our pockets. Without them, we are awkward, shifty creatures, resembling a Music Hall Lion Comique stripped of his essential opera hat—a vision impossible to imagine. So, let us plant our hands in our trousers: a few coins jingling in the right, a cluster of keys rattling in the left, and suddenly, we are ready to confront a female post-office clerk with dignity intact.

The difficulty arises, however, when the pockets are empty. It requires a certain mental gymnastics to keep one's hands occupied when there is literally nothing in them to hold. In the golden age of my youth, when my entire fortune would occasionally shrink to "what the townsfolk call a bob," I would recklessly squander a single penny solely for the joy of holding the resulting change—all in copper—to listen to it jingle. You do not feel the pinch of poverty nearly as acutely with eleven pence in your pocket as you do with a solitary shilling. Had I been "La-di-la," that infamous impecunious youth about whom the upper classes wag their fingers, I would have diligently exchanged my penny for two ha'pennies immediately.

I possess an authoritative voice on the subject of financial straits. My resume includes a stint as a provincial actor and a tenure as a "gentleman connected with the press." I have subsisted on fifteen shillings a week. I have survived a week on ten shillings, owing the balance of five. On one particularly grim occasion, I lived for a fortnight on a single great-coat.

Being truly strapped for cash grants one a profound, almost spiritual insight into domestic economy. If you wish to discover the true value of a coin, try living on fifteen shillings a week and calculate exactly how much you can hoard for clothing and recreation. You will learn that waiting for a farthing in change is an investment, that walking an extra mile to save a penny is sound strategy, that a glass of beer is a luxury reserved for rare occasions, and that a stiff collar can endure four days of service before looking askew.

I suggest you attempt this regimen shortly before tying the knot. It serves as excellent preparation for marital life. Similarly, let your eldest son experience this austerity before dispatching him to university. Once he has felt the bite of the budget, he will hardly whine about receiving a hundred pounds a year as pocket money. Indeed, there are souls who stand to gain a world of wisdom from such trials. Consider that delicate flower who cannot tolerate a drop of claret below ninety-four and would rather dine on cat's meat than plain roast mutton. Though, to the eternal credit of humanity, these tragic figures are largely confined to the terrifying, wonderful societies known only to lady novelists. I never hear a man discuss a *menu* card without feeling an overwhelming urge to drag him to a humble East End pub and force a sixpenny dinner down his throat—beefsteak pudding for fourpence, potatoes for a penny, and half a pint of porter for another penny. The lingering memory of that meal, mixed with the intoxicating scents of cheap beer, tobacco, and roast pork, might eventually induce him to lower his nose slightly when presented with food.

Then there is the generous soul, the cadger's paradise, who showers small change upon the world but forgets entirely to pay his bills. Such a man might benefit greatly from a lesson in common sense. "I invariably tip the waiter a shilling," explained a young government clerk with whom I was dining recently in Regent Street. "One cannot give him less, you know." I concurred regarding the absurdity of offering elevenpence and a halfpenny, yet I resolved to lure him one day to a modest eatery near Covent Garden. Here, the waiter, in the spirit of efficiency, dons his shirt-sleeves—which are notably dirty towards the end of the month. I know this fellow intimately. If my friend offers him anything exceeding a penny, the man will demand an immediate handshake as proof of his esteem; I am certain of it.

Much has been said and written humorously about the state of being hard up, yet the reality holds no comedy. It is not funny to bargain over pennies. It is not amusing to be labeled mean and stingy. It is a universally admitted truth among the literati that all great writers possess a profound sense of shyness. I, for one, am no exception, although acquaintances often remark that my reserve is hardly perceptible to the casual observer.

I am relieved by this observation. My former shyness was, however, a formidable obstacle, causing me significant distress and rendering my interactions with others—particularly my female companions—a source of considerable friction. They complained most vehemently. Indeed, the destiny of a shy man is rarely enviable. Men tend to disdain him, women seem to pity him with a mixture of contempt, and he, naturally, despises himself. Time offers no remedy, and habit provides no relief, save perhaps for a singular piece of advice I once stumbled upon in a modest weekly journal within the "Answers to Correspondents" column. It read: "Adopt an easy and pleasing manner, especially toward ladies."

Alas for the ill-fated soul who took this counsel seriously! Imagine the ironic smile with which he likely devoured those words. "Adopt a pleasing manner"? Nay, my dear friend, do not attempt to manufacture a disposition. To force oneself into artificial ease inevitably results in a performance that is either gushingly insincere or offensively familiar. The path of least resistance is to remain one's authentic self; at worst, one will be perceived merely as surly and dull.

Yet, the shy man does wield a peculiar form of vengeance against the society that torments him. He communicates his misery with such intensity that he frightens others nearly as much as they frighten him. He acts as a dampener upon the collective spirit of any gathering; even the most boisterous spirits find themselves rendered depressed and jittery in his presence.

Much of this stems from a fundamental misunderstanding. Society frequently mistakes the shy man's timidity for arrogant aloofness, bowing down in awe of what they perceive as superiority. His awkwardness is interpreted as insolent carelessness. When, seized by terror at the mere sound of a greeting, blood rushes to his brow and his tongue ties itself into knots, he is viewed as a grotesque example of someone overcome by passion.

To be misunderstood is the shy man's eternal lot; whatever impression he intends to convey, the world invariably receives its inverse. If he attempts a jest, it is dismissed as a fabrication, and his honesty is called into question. If he employs sarcasm, it is taken literally, branding him a fool. Should he try to ingratiate himself with a hint of flattery, it is construed as biting satire, ensuring his eternal hatred by the recipient.

While these tribulations provide endless amusement to onlookers and have served as fertile ground for comedy since antiquity, a closer inspection reveals a deeply tragic dimension. A shy man is, by definition, a lonely man severed from the tapestry of human connection. He traverses the world yet remains untethered to it. An invisible, impenetrable wall separates him from his fellow creatures, bruising his efforts to climb over it. He witnesses joyful expressions and melodious voices from the other side, but his hand cannot cross the divide to grasp another. Standing amidst merry crowds, he yearns to join them, to claim kinship, yet they glide past him, oblivious. He reaches out, only to find his prison walls shifting with his steps, hemming him in tighter. Whether in bustling streets, crowded halls, or the clamor of industry, wherever humanity congregates, the shy man stands apart, shunned and solitary like a leper. His soul brims with affection and desire, yet the world remains unaware. The iron mask of shyness is fastened tightly over his features, hiding the man beneath. Words of warmth and greeting rise to his lips only to dissolve into silent, unheard whispers behind steel bars. His heart aches for the weary traveler, but his sympathy remains mute. Indignation at injustice swells in his chest, but lacking a safety valve, it turns inward, poisoning his spirit. Consequently, the deep reservoirs of love, hate, and scorn that curse the sensitive nature of the shy man festering internally transform him into a misanthrope and a cynic.

Indeed, shy men, much like women considered physically unattractive, fare poorly in this world. Enduring life with any degree of comfort requires the skin of a rhinoceros. This thick dermis serves as our necessary moral clothing; without it, we are unfit for polite society. A pale, gasping figure, flushed and trembling with shaking limbs, presents a painful spectacle, and if the ailment proves incurable, it is perhaps best that the afflicted individual departs swiftly.

One solace available to the shy is that their condition is no indicator of intellectual inferiority. Shyness signifies extreme sensibility and bears no relation to self-consciousness or vanity, despite the incessant assertions of the philistine school of philosophy regarding these connections.

Vanity, ironically, is the swiftest antidote. Once a man realizes he is vastly superior to everyone else, bashfulness vanishes. Surveying a room, he perceives every other occupant as a child of intellect compared to himself, feeling no hesitation toward them as he would not toward a flock of magpies.

Vanity constitutes the finest armor a man can don. Its smooth, impervious surface causes the petty thrusts of spite and envy to glance harmlessly away. Without this breastplate, the sword of talent cannot penetrate the fray of life, for it must endure blows as well as deliver them. I refer, of course, not to the vanity displayed by an elevated nose and a falsetto voice—that is merely play-acting, akin to children pretending to be monarchs. Indifferent to praise or censure, he can afford to be truthful. Believing himself far above the trivial distinctions of mortals, he is equally comfortable with a duke or a costermonger. Valuing only his own standards, he is never tempted to offer the miserable pretenses that lesser souls sacrifice hourly to the god of public opinion.

Conversely, the shy man is humble, modest in his judgment, and excessively concerned with the opinions of others. A full stomach is a potent catalyst for poetry, and no sentiment whatsoever can endure upon an empty one. We possess neither the time nor the inclination to indulge in fanciful sorrows until we have dispensed with our real misfortunes. We do not sigh over deceased birds when the bailiff is present, nor do we fret over the temperature of our mistress's smiles when we are unsure of our next shilling.

Those foolish individuals—who, I admit, usually refers to anyone holding an opinion divergent from my own—will assert that mental distress is far more agonizing than physical hunger. We, the sleek and well-fed, struggle to comprehend the agony of hunger. We spoke of it, cheering one another with memories. "Come along," we urged, "lest the duck spoil."

The rooms, bathed in the indifferent morning sunlight, offer no invitation. Even Mrs. Higgins's countenance betrays no delight. Unoccupied furnished apartments viewed in daylight possess a certain lifelessness, a vacuum where warmth should reside. It is a vastly different affair once one has settled in. Then, surrounded by one's own household gods, one sees the photos of all the girls one has loved and lost ranged along the mantelpiece, alongside half a dozen pipes that look suspicious enough to be evidence of a crime. One slipper peeps from beneath the coal box; the other rests regally atop the piano. The familiar pictures mask the dingy walls, while the books, higgledy-piggledy everywhere, serve as the true companions. There are the bits of blue china one's mother cherished, and the screen she crafted in those distant, golden days when her face was young and laughing, and her hair, tumbling in gold-brown curls from beneath a coal-scuttle bonnet, seemed to glow.

Ah, old screen! What a magnificent figure you must have cut in your youth, when the tulips, roses, and lilies—all sprouting from a single stem—glistened in their prime! Countless summers and winters have passed since then, my friend, and you have danced with the flickering firelight until you grew weary and gray. Your vibrant hues are fading, and the jealous moths have nibbled your silken threads. You are withering away like the hands that wove you. Do you ever ponder those hands? You seem so somber and contemplative, I almost believe you do. Come, you, I, and the deep-glowing embers, let us converse. Speak to me in your silent tongue of what you recall of those youthful days, when you lay upon my little mother's lap and her innocent fingers toyed with your rainbow locks. Was there never a lad nearby? Never a youth who would seize one of those tiny hands to smother it with kisses, persistently holding it and thereby disrupting your creation? Was your fragile existence not frequently endangered by this same clumsy, impetuous boy, who would toss you disrespectfully aside so that—he, unsatisfied with a single grasp—might hold both hands and gaze upward into those beloved eyes? I can see him now through the haze of the flickering twilight. An eager, bright-eyed boy, wearing pinching dandy shoes and tight-fitting smalls, a snowy shirt-frill, a stock, and—oh! such curly hair! A wild, light-hearted soul! Could he be the great, grave gentleman upon whose cane I used to ride cross-legged, the care-worn man into whose thoughtful visage I gazed with childish reverence, whom I called "Father"? You say "Yes," old screen; but are you entirely certain? It is a grave accusation you bring. Is it conceivable? Did he have to kneel in those wonderful smalls, pick you up, and rearrange you before receiving forgiveness and having his curly head smoothed by my mother's diminutive hand? Ah! Old screen, and did the lads and lassies make love fifty years ago precisely as they do now? Are men and women so immutable? Did little maidens' hearts beat with the same fervor beneath pearl-embroidered bodices as they do under Mother Hubbard cloaks? Have steel helmets and chimney-pot hats altered nothing of the brains working beneath? Oh, Time! Great Chronos! Is this thy power? Hast thou dried up seas and leveled mountains, leaving the tiny human heart-strings to defy thee? Ah, yes! They were spun by a Mightier than thou, stretching beyond thy narrow vision, for their ends are fastened in eternity. Yea, thou mayst mow down the leaves and blossoms, but the roots of life lie too deep for thy sickle to sever. Thou refashionest Nature's garments, yet thou cannotst alter by a jot the throbbings of her pulse. The world rolls on obedient to thy laws, but the heart of man is not of thy kingdom, for therein "a thousand years are but as yesterday."

I fear I am straying, however, from my "furnished apartments," and I struggle to return. Yet, I have cause for this diversion. It is a relic of old furniture that hath led me astray, and fantasies cling to antique furnishings with the tenacity of moss to ancient stones. One's chairs and tables grow to be almost integral parts of one's existence, appearing as quiet confidants. What strange narratives could the wooden-headed elders relate if they chose to speak! At what unsuspected comedies and tragedies have they not aided! What bitter tears have been wept into that old sofa cushion! What passionate whispers must the settee have overheard!

New furniture holds no charm for me compared with the aged. It is the antiquated items we cherish—the old visages, the old volumes, the old jests. New furniture may construct a palace, but it requires old furniture to forge a home. Not merely old in itself—for lodging-house furniture is generally of that vintage—but it must be old to *us*, steeped in associations and memories. The furniture of furnished apartments, however ancient it may be in reality, remains new to our eyes, and we feel as though we could never truly coexist with it. Moreover, in the case of all fresh acquaintances, whether wooden or human (and there is scant distinction between the two species, occasionally), everything strikes us with its worst aspect. The knobbly woodwork and shiny horsehair upholstery of the easy-chair suggest anything but comfort. The mirror is smoky. The curtains require laundering. The carpet is frayed. The table appears ready to collapse the instant anything is placed upon it. The grate is cheerless, the wallpaper abominable. The ceiling seems to have been drenched with coffee, and the ornaments... well, they surpass even the wallpaper in ugliness.

There must surely exist some clandestine manufactory dedicated to the production of lodging-house ornamentation. Precisely the same artifacts are to be found in every lodging-house throughout the kingdom, never seen elsewhere. There are the two—what are they called?—standing one at either end of the mantelpiece, where they are perpetually unsafe, draped in long triangular strips of glass that clank against one another, inducing nervousness. In the more modest chambers, these artistic endeavors are supplemented by a couple of porcelain pieces that might each represent a cow seated upon its hind legs, a model of the Temple of Diana at Ephesus, a canine, or whatever fanciful notion strikes the maker. Somewhere in the room lies a bilious-looking object, initially mistaken for a lump of dough abandoned by a child, but upon closer inspection resembling an undercooked Cupid. The landlady designates this as a "statue." Then there is a "sampler" stitched by some imbecile relative of the family, depicting the "Huguenots," two or three Scriptural texts, and a heavily framed and glazed certificate attesting that the father has been vaccinated, is an Odd Fellow, or possesses some similar distinction.

One examines these various attractions and then, with a heavy heart, inquires as to the rent.

"That is rather substantial," you remark upon hearing the figure.

"To tell you the truth," answers the landlady with a sudden eruption of candor, "I have always charged" (mentioning a sum considerably exceeding the initial estimate), "and prior to that, I used to collect" (an even higher figure).

Every landlady instills in you a profound sense of shame by informing you, whenever the topic arises, that she formerly received double the amount you are currently paying. They say—and I suspect those who say it are often people who ought to be better ashamed—that the secret of a blissful heart lies not in piety, but in the consciousness of being impeccably attired. I am forced to admit, with a heavy sigh, that these cynics are frequently, if not always, correct. I recall, with the foggy nostalgia of a thousand sunsets, when I was a lad of tender years and needed a boost to my spirits, I would retreat to my wardrobe and don my finest attire. If I had been dismissed by my washerwoman, or if my blank verse poem had been returned for the tenth time with an editor's polite regret regarding a lack of space, or worse yet, if I had been snubbed by the woman I adored—a feeling, by the way, which seems to manifest itself in a bewildering array of unique methods that surely will confuse our great-grandchildren—then I would pull on my best clothes.

And what a transformation! A glossy new hat, a pair of trousers folded precisely down the front (kept safe between the bed and the mattress, never on the floor, I assure you), and suddenly I was someone. Someone important. Someone worthy of other washerwomen, and perhaps, if I was reckless enough to hope, of other girls. In those garments, I felt invincible, capable of conquering maidens who previously held me at arm's length.

Clothing, it turns out, is half the battle of courtship. A young suitor spends hours in agonizing deliberation: light suit with a cane and drab billycock, or black tails with a chimney-pot hat and a new umbrella? It is a perilous choice. Should he opt for the light suit and the stick, the heavens may open, leaving him dripping mud and boots hidden shamefully throughout the evening. Conversely, if he chooses the top hat and umbrella—a combination so essential that walking out without one is akin to sending a baby into traffic without a nurse—I confess a profound hatred for the top hat. Mine has lasted me five years, surviving the fashion cycles like a stubborn old dog. Yet, if the afternoon proves swelteringly hot, the perspiration strips the soap from his mustache and transforms his carefully coiffured forelock into a limp, seaweed-like wisp. The Fates seem determined to conspire against the poorly dressed lover. By the time he reaches the door, properly attired, the girl has likely fled with her cousin.

Consider the romantic gallants of seventy years past! Look at them on those Christmas cards: curly hair, natty hats, well-shaped legs encased in smalls, dainty Hessian boots, ruffling frills, canes, and dangling seals. It is no wonder the maiden in the poke-bonnet and light-blue sash casts down her eyes and melts. Could any man win a heart in baggy trousers and a monkey-jacket?

Indeed, our deportment is a direct servant to our dress. Strip a man of his raiment, and he becomes a skulking ghost, dragging his head low like a man fetching his own supper. Dress him in gorgeous linens, and he struts the streets like a bantam rooster, cane swinging, eyeing the ladies with predatory delight. Clothes alter our very DNA. A man with a plume in his bonnet and a dagger at his hip is naturally fierce and daring; clad in an ulster, he seeks refuge behind a lamppost and calls the police.

Now, I am not blind to the sterling virtues found under broadcloth and tweed. There exists deep affection and honest worth beneath the somber hues of the modern era. However, that ancient spirit of chivalry—the one that rode tilts for love and fought for smiles—requires the clatter of steel and the rustle of plumes to awaken it from its dusty slumber.

The world, I fear, is growing old. It dresses so soberly. We have passed the infancy of humanity, when we ran barefoot in loose robes, and the barbaric boyhood where tattoos adorned our skin. Then came the foppish youth of the world, decked in scarlet doublets and flowing curls, courting and boasting. But those merry, foolish days are gone. Now, we are a grave, middle-aged gentleman in the nineteenth century, shocked to think we ever danced as troubadors. We wrap ourselves in black coats, black hats, and black boots, presenting a facade of such respectability that it is almost comical to imagine us ever wearing anything else. Or so we tell ourselves. There is a prevailing theory that sense and dullness walk hand in hand. Goodness, it seems, requires black clothing. The truly virtuous dress entirely in black, perhaps even black shirts soon. Meanwhile, the wicked wander in light suits, and the utterly abandoned dare to wear a white hat, though they are quickly hushed up by polite society.

Speaking of light suits, have you observed how the populace stares when you first appear in something new? By the third outing, London has forgotten you exist. I do not wear such things myself; I consider them sinful. Yet, I often gaze into the mirror at my two long, cylindrical bags (so rugged around the knees!), my stand-up collar, and my billycock hat, wondering what right I have to make God's world so hideous. Then, wild thoughts stir within me. I do not wish to be good or sensible. I want to don lavender tights, red velvet breeches, a green doublet slashed with gold, a light-blue silk cloak, a black eagle's plume, a sword, a falcon, and a prancing horse. Why must we all resemble ants crawling over a dust-heap? Why not dress a little gayly? If we did, I am certain we would be happier. Let the philosophers dress like old crows; I prefer to be a butterfly.

As for women, they ought to dress prettily. It is their divine duty. They are the flowers of the earth, meant to adorn it. We men may abuse them, but the old world would be dreadfully dull without their dresses and fair faces. How they brighten our dingy bachelor chambers! Their ribbons, laces, gloves, and parasols create a delightful litter, like a wandering rainbow dropped in for a visit.

Nothing beats the sight of little maids emerging in summer in pretty colors. Pink, blue, and white glancing between the trees, dotting the green fields. From three miles away, I can clearly distinguish four white dresses climbing a hill near my window. At first, I mistook them for mile-stones placed for a lark. It is charming to see the darlings from afar, especially if they happen to be one's wife and mother-in-law.

This brings me, in all seriousness, to the issue of women's boots. The women of these islands are cursed with footwear too large for their feet. Bootmakers refuse to keep smaller sizes. I have seen countless women sit atop stile rails, declaring they cannot proceed because their boots hurt, invariably complaining that they are simply too big. This must end. In the name of the husbands and fathers of England, I call upon the bootmakers to reform. Our wives and daughters deserve not to be lamed and tortured with impunity. Why cannot "narrow twos" be stocked? That is the size most women require.

The waistband is another grievance. Dressmakers construct them so loosely that the hooks and eyes snap off with reports like thunder. I cannot comprehend why women endure these wrongs. Surely, they are not disinclined to fuss over dress, for it is the one subject they truly obsess over. Two women conversing by a window are almost certainly discussing alterations. You might hear one say, "So I took in the waistband and let out a seam, and it fits beautifully!" while the other replies, "I shall wear my plum-colored body to the Jones's, with a yellow plastron; and they've got lovely gloves at Puttick's for one and eleven pence."

Once, I drove through Derbyshire with two ladies who enjoyed the scenery immensely until I mentioned Chatsworth. Immediately, they shifted gears. "Why not get a yard of sarsenet?" one asked. "Certainly," replied the other. Within moments, they were ignoring the rolling hills to debate pocket-handkerchiefs and the decadence of cambric frilling. I suspect if two women were stranded on a desert island, they would spend eternity arguing over sea-shells and birds' eggs as trimmings, inventing a new fashion for fig-leaves monthly.

Young men, meanwhile, think deeply about clothes but rarely discuss them. A fop is not a favorite among his own sex; he receives more abuse than necessary. Yet, a little foppery in youth is human. A young cock ought to ruffle his feathers and crow as if the world belongs to him. To see a modest, retiring man is to see a disappointment; rows are half the fun of life, and you cannot have rows with humble, meek individuals who turn away our wrath. Consider Xantippe, tied forever to the calmly irritating Socrates. A married woman deserves a quarrel now and then. Heaven knows their lives are dull; they are excluded from smoking carriages and comic papers. Surely, a sensible man provides a little row for her amusement, proving his love through self-sacrificial folly. After all, great philosophy counts for nothing in marriage.

This, then, is the nature of Memory: she delivers nothing complete. She behaves like a spoiled toddler who has dropped all her toys in a mud puddle. I recall vividly tumbling into a massive dusthole when I was a tot, but I possess absolutely zero data regarding how I ever escaped. If I relied solely on my internal records, I would likely conclude that I am currently trapped inside said hole, still waiting for the shovel.

Consider another episode, occurring several years hence. I was present for what appeared to be a highly dramatic romantic encounter. However, the only distinct fragment remaining in my mind is the precise moment someone burst through the door, delivering the news, "Emily, you're wanted," in a sepulchral tone so ominous it suggested the local constabulary had finally tracked her down. All the tender confessions exchanged between us, every beautiful sentiment spoken, has evaporated into thin air.

If we stand before the ruins of a lifetime, what do we see? Not a majestic cathedral, but a shattered column marking where a grand portal once stood; a broken window-frame hinting at a lady's private chamber; a pile of charred rubble where a roaring fire used to dance; and draped over the whole mess, the comforting green of ivy and the soft patina of time.

Time acts as a benevolent filter, blurring the edges of reality until everything looks lovely again. Past sorrows taste like candy; childhood days, filled with nuts, hoops, and gingerbread, seem like eternal summer festivals. We conveniently edit out the scolding, the toothaches, and the tedious Latin conjugations—especially the Latin verbs, which we pretend never existed. We convince ourselves that we were paragons of joy when we were young and in love, wishing desperately to relive those feelings. We rarely pause to consider the heartbreak, the sleepless vigils, or the parched throat accompanying the crushing realization that a beloved woman could offer only a platonic "friendship"—as if any man actually desired a sister!

Indeed, we gaze backward and see only the sunlight. Shadows are invisible from a distance. The road behind us stretches out beautifully, devoid of the sharp stones that once cut our feet. We dwell exclusively on the roadside roses, ignoring the briars that drew blood, mistaking thorns for gentle vines swaying in the breeze. Thank heavens for this illusion; otherwise, the chain of memory would consist entirely of painful links, and the bitterness of today would not even be worth smiling at tomorrow.

It follows, then, that the brightest aspects of existence are invariably its highest and best. As our brief lives sink back into the ocean of oblivion, the lightest, happiest moments float to the surface, bobbing above the waves long after the angry thoughts and stinging pains have drowned deep below. This "glamour of the past" explains why elderly gentlemen regale young listeners with tales of superior virtue and easier winters. They claim the moon shone every night in their youth and that wrestling mad bulls in blankets was standard curriculum. Naturally, if one waits long enough, the old gentleman will eventually swear that the moon did shine every night, and the bull-wrestling stories will become legend.

History repeats this cycle endlessly. The folks of our grandfathers' youth sang songs complaining about the modern world, just as the youths of today drone similar nonsense for the bafflement of the next generation. "Give me back the good old days!" has been humanity's refrain since Adam turned fifty-one. Literary works from 1835 echo the same desperate longing found in German Minnesingers and Norse sagas centuries prior, all sighing for a return to a golden age. From the earliest prophets to the philosophers of Ancient Greece, the consensus has been that the world has steadily deteriorated since creation. If this narrative holds true, the planet must have been a delightfully pristine paradise when first unveiled to the public, requiring merely a bit of sunshine and a good temper to maintain the illusion.

Still, one cannot deny that the dawn of Creation held a certain sweetness. Before the trampling millions wore down the grass to dust, and before the roar of cities silenced the natural quietude, life must have been noble and solemn. Those free-roaming ancestors walked hand-in-hand with God beneath a vast sky, living in sun-drenched tents amidst lowing herds. They took their simple needs directly from Mother Nature, toiling, talking, and thinking while the great earth rolled silently beneath them, unburdened by trouble or sin.

Those days are gone. Humanity's quiet childhood, spent in forest glades and by murmuring rivers, is a closed book. Now, human life deepens into adulthood amidst turmoil, doubt, and hope. The era of restful peace has ended; we have work to finish and must hasten onward. Though we may not comprehend the grand design, our unconscious hands are weaving the vast fabric for God. Like tiny coral insects laboring deep underwater, we strive for petty personal ends, dreaming not of the magnificent structure rising around us.

Enough with the vain regrets for a past that cannot return. Our focus must lie ahead, not behind. "Forward!" is our motto. We must not sit with folded hands, treating the past as the edifice itself when it is merely the foundation. Wasting heart and soul on "what might have been" blinds us to the "may be" lying right in front of us. Opportunities flutter by like moths while we mourn lost chances, failing to notice the happiness arriving now because we are so preoccupied with the happiness that departed.

Years ago, when I wandered from the fireside into the Land of Fairy Tales, I encountered a valiant knight. He had conquered many dangers, traversed many realms, and was known far and wide for his courage and lack of fear. Perhaps, however, there were those rare evenings when even a brave man felt a tremor of dread, though he would never admit it. One day, as Sir Ghelent trudged wearily along a treacherous road, his heart grew heavy. Monstrous rocks hung precariously overhead, threatening to crush him, while chasm-like voids flanked the path, home to ferocious robbers and dragons dripping with blood. A darkness thicker than night enveloped the trail. Overcome by despair, the knight decided to abandon his journey and seek an easier route for his gentle steed.

But when he hastily turned to look behind, a marvel befell him. Of the entire path he had traveled, not a single footstep remained visible. Instead, at his horse's heels, a mighty abyss yawned open, so profound that no mortal eye could fathom its depths. Realizing retreat was impossible, Sir Ghelent knelt to pray to Saint Cuthbert, spurred his steed, and rode forward bravely and joyfully. And guess what? Nothing harmed him.

There is no turning back on the Road of Life. The fragile bridge of time we tread upon sinks back into eternity with every step. The past is harvested and stored; it no longer belongs to us. Not a single word can be unsaid, not a single step retraced. Thus, it becomes the duty of every true knight to ride forward boldly, rather than idly weeping because the destination is no longer visible.

A new life commences with every passing second. Let us advance joyfully to embrace it. We must move forward, whether we wish to or not, and we shall navigate the path far better with our eyes fixed ahead than with them cast backward.

Recently, a friend visited me and passionately advocated for learning a mystical system that guarantees total retention of every detail. I suspect his eagerness stemmed from my occasional habit of borrowing an umbrella and emerging mid-game of whist with the bewildered remark, "Lor! I've been thinking all along that clubs were trumps." Despite the allure of his proposed method, I politely declined. I harbor no desire to remember everything. Indeed, there are numerous facets of most lives that would benefit greatly from being erased. Consider those moments, many years past, when we failed to act with the utmost honor or upright integrity. That unfortunate deviation from the straight and narrow, and the subsequent discovery of our folly, meanness, and wrong-doing... Ah, well! We paid the price, enduring maddening hours of remorse, the searing agony of shame, and perhaps the scorn of those we cherished. Let us let it go. Oh, Father Time, please use your gentle hands to lift those bitter memories from our overburdened hearts, for griefs arrive constantly with the coming hours, and our strength is fleeting as the day.
