The sun, our Lord, was drawing toward the end of His day, casting long, dying shadows over the smoking peak of the mountain. Black smoke, thick and forbidding, billowed before Him like a funeral pall. Below, Phorenice wrung the water from her soaked garments until her skin was pale and her teeth chattered. "Make haste with those paddles, Deucalion," she urged, her voice trembling slightly. "Take me through the water-gate and restore me to my comforts. That merchant will surely rue the day he saw how his fine silks were ruined, and I, too, mourn the loss. As a woman, I have few replacements for these garments." She glanced sideways at me, tossing back her short, fiery red hair from her eyes. "Tell me, what say you to my wisdom in venturing this far without an escort?"

"The Empress can do no wrong," I recited the old formula, offering a weary smile.

"At least I have proven I can fight," she continued, her demeanor shifting back to steel. "I caught you looking at me with approval once or twice. You were a difficult man to thaw, Deucalion, but you warm perceptibly the closer you draw to me. *La*, sir, if this continues, we shall be a pair of rustic sweethearts before the week is out. I am glad I devised the plan to approach those smelly fishermen."

In truth, she had brought me out in the litter unattended with a singular purpose: to invite a fight and display her martial prowess. Perhaps she also wished to witness firsthand how I conducted myself under pressure. If we were to share a life as husband and wife, it was vital that each knew the exact limits of the other's capabilities. Furthermore, I was a veteran warrior, too enamored with the glory of combat to quarrel deeply with anyone who offered a worthy challenge. Still, I could not shake the comparison that plagued me. Comparing Phorenice to Nais... with a similar opportunity for escape, Nais had spared me the struggle out of a sheer pity for the squalid rebels who refused to acknowledge her as their chieftain. Here, however, this Empress frittered away twenty of her hardest subjects merely to gratify a whim.

Loyal to my vows as a priest and bound by the commands of the High Council on the Sacred Mountain, I forced these rebellious thoughts away. As I rowed across the swaying waves toward the harbor forts, I sent silent prayers to the High Gods for the dexterity of speech, a power Phorenice adored. They, through their love for Atlantis and the suffering people, granted me the eloquence needed. Her eyes glowed with delight as I spoke.

The beach where we had fought was secure from external ships due to a jagged chain of rocks rising from the deep, running from the harbor side to the end of the city wall. The local fishermen knew the narrow passes; they could often slip through to the open sea without touching a stone, or leap out with their light boats if danger threatened. But I possessed neither the knowledge nor the dexterity required. I thought, *Now the High Gods will reveal if They intend for this woman, who has defiled Them, to reign over Atlantis, and if They will allow me to serve as her husband.*

These thoughts echoed in my heart as I waited for an omen. There was no half-measure. A great wave surged in the lagoon behind us—a monster born only of an earthquake—and on its sleek back, we were hurled forward. We cleared the reefs, seaweed licking at our sides, without so much as grazing a single stone of the barrier. Bowing my head as I rowed toward the harbor forts, I saw clearly that the High Gods had not yet visited vengeance for the insults offered by this beautiful, errant woman.

Inside the two forts, sentries beat drums in their usual rotation, paying little heed to the fishing boat clamoring to be lowered. Fortunately, a pair of officers were enjoying the evening air atop a stone turret. Recognizing the urgency in our shouts, they silenced the drums. Torches were lowered to verify our faces, and with a splash, the heavy chain was dropped into the water, granting us passage.

Within the harbor, a galley lay nuzzling the wall. Ropes were let down, and a crew descended to man the oars. Customary gifts of fresh clothing were exchanged for our sodden rags in the cabin, a necessary precaution against the fevers that struck those who carried wet clothes after sunset. Though I knew from experience how to ward off illness with herbs, I observed with grim amusement that the Empress possessed so little of the divine within her that she feared human ailments with genuine terror.

The galley sped across the calm waters, mooring itself to the golden rings of the royal quay. While we waited for litters, I watched a lantern flicker in the boat guarding Phorenice's mammoth. The colossal red beast stood shoulder-deep in the harbor water, its trunk upturned. It was tamed now, the lantern's light rippling softly on the water disturbed by its tremors. Yet, I did not choose to intercede for it. If the mammoth sank into the harbor mud, I could bear the loss with equanimity.

Truthfully, that ride on the beast's back had left me with a profound sense of helplessness, nearly intolerable. Perhaps circumstances had made me unduly self-reliant, but I preferred walking on my own two feet—the way the Gods fashioned us. On my own feet, I could guard my own head and neck, a feat I had accomplished on four continents and countless occasions. But on the back of that detestable creature, I had grown as nervous as a frightened child.

Leisure for such musings was scarce. Phorenice questioned the port-captain regarding the fate of Nais. He informed her that the rebel leader had been imprisoned in a dungeon beneath the royal pyramid, with an officer giving his bond for her safety.

"It is to be hoped he understands his work," the Empress remarked sharply. "That pretty Nais knows the labyrinth better than most. The officer may well be sent to the tormentors for placing her in a cell with a secret outlet. Would you feel pleasure if the girl escaped, Deucalion?"

"Assuredly," I replied, knowing secrecy was futile. "I harbor no enmity against Nais."

"But I do," she said viciously. "And I am still minded to bind your faith to me with the wedding gift you know of."

"The deed shall be done," I said. "First and foremost, the Empress of Atlantis."

"Poof!" she laughed, dismissing my formality. "You are too stiff. You ought to feel honored that I deign to be jealous of your favors. Hand me up, sir, to the litter. And now, sit beside me and keep me warm against the chill air. Guards with torches! Step back from the street walls; the perfume you burn stifles me."

Once more, the royal banqueting hall filled with revelry. I sat beside Phorenice on the raised dais beneath the symbols of the snake and the outstretched hand. What had previously been implied was now openly proclaimed: she acknowledged me as her husband-to-be, and the throng of jeweled courtiers hailed me as superior to themselves. There was method to their silence regarding wine toasts to my health, likely a rumor of my preference for older, simpler habits. Each lord and lady approached in turn, spilling a goblet at my feet. When I invited them to share tidbits from my platter, they ate simply and thankfully, devoid of the extravagant gestures common elsewhere.

The flaring jets of earth-breath illuminated a plainer dress code among this otherwise frivolous crowd. Phorenice leaned over, her ruddy curls brushing my cheek, and chided me playfully for usurping her authority.

"Oh, sir," she pleaded mockingly, "do not make your rule over us too ascetic. No orders were given for this change, yet tonight the air lacks perfume, the food is plain, and I half consider burning the cook. As for the clothes and jewels of these diners—they resemble the garb of the old King's reign before I arrived to teach them taste. It is done by no order of mine, Deucalion. They have swung to this austerity by sheer courtier instinct. Look at the beards! Many lack the exquisite curl they displayed yesterday. By my face, they would shave their chins smooth tomorrow if you continued to set the fashions at this prodigious rate and I did not interfere."

"Why hinder them if they prefer cleanliness?"

"No, sir. There shall be only one clean chin where a beard grows in all Atlantis, and that shall belong to the husband of the Empress. Why, my Deucalion, would you impose sumptuary laws? Would you have these nobles and the common folk alike imitate us in every fold of garment and twist of hair? Come, if we declare our sovereignty rests solely on superior strength and wit, they will hate us for our arrogance. But if we reserve a few personal decorations for ourselves, they become mere objects of admiration and pleasant envy."

"You show me that rulership holds depths unseen by the casual observer."

"And yet they tell me, and prove it, that you have ruled with success."

"I employed the older methods. It takes a Phorenice to invent these finer flights."
