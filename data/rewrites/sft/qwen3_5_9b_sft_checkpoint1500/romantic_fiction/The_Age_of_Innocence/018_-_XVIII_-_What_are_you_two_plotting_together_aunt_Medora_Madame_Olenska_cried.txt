"What are you two conspiring, Aunt Medora?" Madame Olenska cried as she entered the room.

She wore a gown designed for a grand ball, shimmering softly as if woven from candlelight itself. She held her chin high, radiating the confident challenge of a beauty aware of her allure.

"We were merely remarking," Mrs. Manson replied, rising gracefully to point at the flowers, "that these beauties would surely delight you."

Olenska halted, her gaze fixing on the bouquet. Her complexion remained unchanged, yet a flash of white-hot indignation swept across her features. "Who on earth is so foolish as to send me flowers? And why flowers? And why *tonight* of all nights?" Her voice sharpened, a pitch the young man had never heard before. "I am not attending a ball, nor am I a young girl awaiting a suitor. Some people are simply incapable of understanding decorum."

Turning swiftly to the door, she called, "Nastasia!"

The faithful handmaiden appeared instantly. Olenska, speaking Italian with deliberate enunciation so the young man could catch every syllable, commanded, "Here—take these to the dustbin!" Seeing Nastasia hesitate, she corrected herself sharply. "No, no—the flowers are not at fault. Take them to the house three doors down, to Mr. Winsett, the dark gentleman who dined here. His wife is ill; they might find comfort in them. The boy is out? Then you go yourself. Here, wrap your cloak around me; I need you to deliver this immediately. And pray, do not let anyone suspect they came from me!"

She draped her heavy velvet opera cloak over the maid's shoulders, stepped back into the drawing-room, and slammed the door. Her chest rose beneath the lace of her bodice, and for a fleeting second, Archer feared tears. Instead, she erupted into a sharp, brittle laugh. Turning to the Marchioness and then to Archer, she demanded, "And you two—have you finally reconciled?"

"It is for Mr. Archer to decide, darling," the Marchioness purred, "though he certainly waited with admirable patience while you cooed over your hair."

"Yes—I allowed you ample time," Olenska admitted, lifting her hand to touch the intricate coils of her chignon. "But this reminds me: Dr. Carver has departed, and you will be tardy for the Blenkersons. Mr. Archer, will you escort my aunt to the carriage?"

She led the Marchioness into the hallway, watched her struggle into a chaotic pile of overshoes and scarves, and shouted from the threshold, "Remember, the carriage returns for me at ten!" Returning to the parlor, she found Archer by the mantle, studying himself in the glass. It was uncommon in New York society for a lady to treat her parlour-maid with such familiarity or dispatch her on errands clad in her own finery. Yet, Archer felt a thrilling excitement, a sense of living in a world where emotion dictated immediate action.

Olenska did not retreat when he approached from behind. For a heartbeat, their eyes locked in the reflection; then she turned, collapsed onto the corner of the sofa, and sighed, "There is time for a cigarette."

He offered the box and lit a match. As the flame flickered near her face, she looked at him with amused eyes. "What do you think of me in a temper?"

Archer hesitated, then answered with sudden resolve. "It illuminates exactly what your aunt has been saying about you."

"I knew she was talking. Well?"

"She remarked that you are accustomed to splendors and amusements far beyond what we could offer here."

Olenska smiled faintly, watching the smoke curl around her lips. "Medora is incorrigibly romantic. It compensates for so many deficiencies!"

Archer paused, then took his chance. "Does your aunt's romanticism align with reality?"

"Do you mean: does she speak the truth?" She considered this. "In nearly everything she says, there is a grain of truth mixed with falsehood. But why ask? What has she told you?"

He looked away into the fire, then back at her luminous presence. His heart constricted; he sensed this was their final evening by the hearstone, and soon the carriage would whisk her away. "She claims Count Olenski has asked her to persuade you to return to him."

Olenska remained silent, holding her cigarette halfway raised. Her expression had not altered, reminding him of the apparent inability to be surprised that he had noted before.

"You knew?" he burst out.

Silence stretched until the ash from her cigarette fell to the floor. She brushed it away. "She hinted at a letter. Poor darling! Medora's hints..."

"Did your husband request her sudden arrival?"

Olenska seemed to weigh this carefully. "One cannot tell. She mentioned a 'spiritual summons' from Dr. Carver. I fear she intends to marry him... poor Medora, there is always someone she wishes to wed. Perhaps the Cubans grew weary of her? I believe she served as a paid companion. Honestly, I do not know why she came."

"But you believe she possesses a letter from your husband?"

Once more, Olenska brooded in silence before murmuring, "After all, it was to be expected."

Archer rose and leaned against the fireplace. A restless unease seized him; the minutes seemed numbered, and the wheels of the returning carriage might strike at any instant. "You know your aunt believes you will return?"

Olenska lifted her head quickly. A deep blush flooded her face, spreading down her neck and shoulders. She blushed rarely, and painfully, as if burned.

"Cruel things have often been believed of me," she said.

"Oh, Ellen—for heaven's sake, forgive me! I am a fool and a brute!"

She offered a faint smile. "You are terribly nervous; you have your own troubles. I know you feel the Wellands are unreasonable regarding your marriage. Of course, I agree. In Europe, they do not comprehend our protracted American betrothals. I suppose they lack our calm." She emphasized the "we" with a subtle irony.

Archer felt the sting of the irony but dared not pursue it. Perhaps she had deliberately steered clear of her own affairs, and after the pain his previous words had caused, all he could do was follow her lead. Yet the waning hour filled him with desperation; he could not endure the thought of words building a wall between them again.

"Yes," he said abruptly. "I traveled south to propose to May after Easter. There is no reason we cannot marry then."

"And May adores you—and yet you could not convince her? I thought her too intelligent to be enslaved by such absurd superstitions."

"She *is* too intelligent; she is not their slave."

Olenska studied him. "Well, then—I do not understand."

Archer flushed and rushed forward. "We had a frank discussion—almost at the start. She interprets my impatience as a negative sign."

"A bad sign?"

"She believes it means I cannot trust myself to continue loving her. In short, she suspects I wish to marry her immediately to escape someone I—" He faltered, then blurted out, "...love more."

Olenska examined this with curiosity. "But if she believes that—why is she not in a hurry?"

"Because she is unlike that: she is far nobler. She insists on the long engagement to grant me time—"

"To abandon her for another woman?"

"If I desire it."

Olenska leaned toward the fire, gazing into the flames with fixed intensity. Outside, down the quiet street, the trot of horses signaled the approach of the carriage.

"That *is* noble," she whispered, her voice breaking slightly.

"Yes. But it is ridiculous."

"Ridiculous? Because you do not care for anyone else?"

"Because I do not intend to marry anyone else."

"Ah." Another long pause ensued. Finally, she looked up. "This other woman—does she love you?"

"Oh, there is no other woman! I mean, the person May suspected was—never—"

"Then, why, after all, are you in such haste?"

"There is your carriage," Archer said.

She rose half-way, looking around with absent eyes. Her fan and gloves lay on the sofa beside her; she picked them up mechanically.

"Yes; I suppose I must depart."

"You are going to Mrs. Struthers's?"

"Yes." She smiled. "I must go where I am invited, or I shall be utterly lonely. Why not accompany me?"

Archer felt that at any cost he must keep her close, must extract the remainder of her evening. Ignoring her invitation, he continued to lean against the chimney-piece, his eyes fixed on the hand holding the gloves and fan, as if testing whether he possessed the power to make her drop them.

"May guessed the truth," he declared. "There is another woman—but not the one she imagines."

Ellen Olenska made no response and did not move. After a moment, he sat beside her and gently unclasped her hand, causing the gloves and fan to fall onto the sofa between them.

She sprang up, freeing herself from his grasp and moving to the opposite side of the hearth. "Ah, do not make love to me! Too many have attempted it," she frowned.
