Princess Claelia had ordered a full-length portrait of herself, and Gerard had gently suggested she commission his dear friend Pietro Vanucci to paint it.

But she waved the suggestion off at once. “There will be time enough to slight our Gerardo when his own work fails to content me.” Gerard, who knew full well his gifts lay in line and draughtsmanship far more than in rich colour mixing, then pitched her a different concept: he would render her not in oils, but as a Roman marble statue, laying on vellum such careful graduated tones no onlooker could fail to mistake the drawing for polished Carrara stone. She was curious at first, but demurred sharply when the enthusiast explained she would need to sit for him draped in the full tunic, toga, and leather sandals of antiquity.

“Why, I’d as lief sit for you in my smock,” she said, with unvarnished medieval frankness.

“Alas, signorina,” Gerard protested, “you have surely never marked the beauty of that ancient habit: so free, so ample, so simple, yet so noble, and made to suit your highness’s Roman features as no modern gown can. It will show off the shapely arm and hand Heaven gave you, that modern tight sleeves hide away from every eye.”

“So you can flatter just like all the rest, Gerardo? Very well. Give me time to think on it. Come on Saturday, and I will tell you yes or no.”

The week of respite she gained was spent in secret in her private chambers, stitching together the tunic and toga from soft, fine white linen, twisting this way and that before her full-length mirror to judge if the thousand-year-old garb suited her beauty well enough to justify its oddity.

Gerard was hurrying to that Saturday meeting when he stopped dead, rooted to the cobblestones by a bookshop window. His sharp calligrapher’s eye fell on an open copy of Lactantius laid out on the display. “That script is perfectly wrought, at any rate,” he thought. He stared a moment longer, and his blood ran cold. It was not written by hand at all. It was printed.

Gerard groaned out loud. “I am lost. My enemy is at the door. The printing press has come to Rome.”

He stepped into the shop, affecting casual indifference, and asked how long the new printing press had been in the city. The shopkeeper shook his head, laughing. “There is no such thing in Rome itself, messer. That Lactantius? It was printed up on the slopes of the Apennines.”

“What, did the printing press fall there out of the moon?” Gerard asked, bewildered.

“Nay, messer, it rose there from Germany,” the trader chuckled, pointing to the title page. Gerard leaned in eagerly, and read the fine print: *Opera et impensis Sweynheim et Pannartz Alumnorum Joannis Fust. Impressum Subiacis. A.D. 1465.*

“Will you buy it, messer?” the shopkeeper pressed. “See how even and fair the letters are. Few scribes left alive can match that hand, and it costs barely a quarter the price of a hand-copied manuscript.”

“I would take it gladly,” Gerard said sadly, “but my heart will not let me. Know that I am a calligrapher, and these pupils of Fust chase me across the whole world, snatching the bread out of my mouth. Yet I wish no ill to them. Heaven forbid.” He hurried out of the shop, his mind flying straight to Margaret. “Dear Margaret, we must lose no time. We must make our hay while the sun shines. One month more, and an avalanche of printed type will roll down those Apennine slopes to Rome, and lay waste every scribe who earns his living by his pen.” He broke into a near-run for the princess’s palace.

He was ushered into an apartment he had never entered before: small, but luxuriously furnished, with a little fountain splashing soft in its centre, and floors laid with panther skins tanned with their fur still on, so no footfall could make a sound. No door broke the opposite wall, only a heavy, draped tapestry of gold and crimson thread that marked the entrance to the princess’s private boudoir. He was left alone there so long he began to fidget, wondering if the maid had mistakenly shown him to a stranger’s chambers.

His doubts dissolved swiftly. A light step hurried behind the tapestry; it parted in the middle, and there stood a figure the old pagans would have knelt to worship. She was not quite Venus, nor quite Minerva: nobler than the goddess of love, softer and more womanly than Jupiter’s warrior daughter. Toga, tunic, sandals, not a single modern stitch on her, and her beauty, that was timeless, took Gerard’s breath away. He jumped to his feet, every inch the artist glowing with unfeigned delight. “Oh!” he cried, innocent in his rapture, and stared as if spellbound.

That unforced, unflattering admiration was the sweetest compliment Claelia had ever received. A soft rose blush tinted her cheeks, her eyes brightened, and her mouth curved in delicious, unguarded pleasure. When they had stood gazing at one another for several long moments, and she saw Gerard could do nothing but gape and gasp in awe, she spoke. “Well, Gerardo, you see I have turned myself into an antique monster for you.”

“A monster? I doubt old Fra Colonna would fall straight to his knees and adore your highness if he saw you thus attired.”

“Nay, I do not care to be adored by an old man. I would far rather be loved by a young one, of my own choosing.”

Gerard pulled out his pencils, laid a sheet of thick, sturdy paper over his canvas, and set to work. He was so absorbed in catching every perfect line of her drapery and the soft curve of her cheek that he had no mercy on his model, making her stand in one unbroken pose for nearly an hour. At last she spoke, faint and trembling. “Gerardo, I cannot stand thus a moment longer, not even for you.”

“Then sit and rest awhile, signora,” he said, immediately penitent.

“I thank you,” she sighed, sinking into a chair, her face going pale. Gerard was alarmed at his own thoughtlessness, scooped a handful of water from the fountain to dash on her face, but she lifted a white hand to stop him. “Nay, do not fling it at me. Press your wet palm to my brow, prithee.”

Gerard laid his damp hand timidly to her forehead, and she sighed in relief. “That is reviving. Again.” He did as she bade, then she asked him to ring the small hand bell on the table. A maid came, was sent to fetch a large fan from Floretta.

Floretta returned with the fan immediately, and no sooner had she stepped close to the princess than Claelia’s highbred nostrils flared wide, like a thoroughbred horse scenting a strange rival. “Wretch!” she cried, leaping up with a sudden burst of vigour. She seized Floretta’s hair in her left hand, and boxed her ears hard three times. The bare, strong arm left free by her loose toga rose and fell like a piston, heavy slaps resounding one after another on Floretta’s shoulders, until the last blow drove the sobbing, screaming maid stumbling out through the tapestry. Her wails echoed long after she was gone.

“Saints of Heaven!” Gerard cried, bewildered. “What harm has she done?”

“She knows full well. It is not the first time. The nasty toad! I will teach her to come near me stinking of musk-cat perfume.”

“Alas, signora, that seems but a small fault, methinks.”

“A small fault? Nay, it was a foul fault.” Then her whole demeanour shifted, melting into sudden humility and soft sweetness. “Are you wroth with me for beating her, Gerar-do?”

“Signora, it ill becomes me to lecture you. But methinks those whom Heaven appoints to rule others should first learn to rule themselves.”

“That is true, Gerardo. How wise you are, for being so young.” She called her other maid, pressed a small purse of coins into her hand, and bade her give it to Floretta, tell her Gerardo had interceded for her, and so she must be forgiven. Gerard blushed bright at her trust, and offered to resume work on the portrait.

“Not yet,” she said. “Beating that hussy has left me breathless. I will sit awhile, and you shall talk to me. I know you can talk, when it pleases you, as beautifully as you draw.”

“That would be easy enough,” Gerard said, fumbling for words.

“Then do it, Gerardo.”

Gerard was taken aback. “But signora, I know not what to say. This is most sudden.”

“Say your real mind. Say you wish you were anywhere but here.”

“Nay, signora, that would not be true. But I do wish one thing.”

“Ay? And what is that?” she asked, soft as a breeze.

“I wish I could have drawn you as you were beating that poor lass. You were awful, yet lovely. Oh, what a subject for a portrait of a Pythoness!”

“Alas! he thinks of nothing but his art.” She laughed, a little bitter. “And why do you make such a fuss over my beauty, Gerardo? You are far fairer than I am. You are more like Apollo than I to Venus. You have lovely hair, and lovely eyes—yet you do not know what to do with them.”

“Ay, but I do. To draw you, signora.”

“Ah, yes, you see my features clearly with them. But you cannot see what any Roman gallant would have seen in your place, long ago. Yet surely you have marked how very welcome you are to me, Gerardo?”

“I can see your highness is always far kinder to me than I deserve, a poor stranger in your land.”

“No, I am not kind, Gerardo. I have been cold to you, even rude, many times. And you are so simple you never saw the cause. Alas! I feared for my own heart. I feared I would become your slave, I who have always made others my slaves. Ah, Gerardo, I am unhappy. Ever since you came here, I have lived only for your visits. The day you are to come, I am bright and light. All the other days, I am listless, and long for them to end. You are not like the Roman gallants. You make me hate all of them. You are ten times braver to my eye, and wise, and scholarly, and you never flatter, never lie. I scorn any man who lies. Gerar-do, teach me your magic. Teach me to make you as happy at my side as I am at yours.”

Her voice dropped to a trembling whisper, half smothered with passion, and her white hand stole soft down his arm, resting on his wrist as light as a bird that might take flight at a single word. Gerard, who had no vanity, no experience of courtly love, wrapped up wholly in Margaret and his art, had never seen this confession coming, though it had built slowly before his eyes for months. He blushed from his hairline to his collar, and not for a single moment did his admiration for the regal beauty before him push the absent Margaret’s image from his mind. But this was a princess, wooing him with a grace and tenderness he had never dared imagine. How to reject her without wounding her, or worse? He trembled and stammered.

The siren saw his hesitation, and leaned in to soothe him. “Poor Gerardo, fear not. No harm will ever come to you under my protection. Will you not speak to me, Gerar-do mio?”

“Signora,” he muttered, trying to deprecate her. At that moment his eye, fallen in confusion, caught the shapely white arm coiled around his elbow, and he remembered exactly what that same lovely, powerful limb had done to Floretta only minutes before. He shivered.

“Alas!” the princess sighed. “I scare him. Am I so very terrible? Is it my Roman robe? I will take it off, and dress as I did when you first came to me. Do you remember? That was the day you came to write a letter to that barren fool Ercole d’Orsini. I hated him the very second I laid eyes on you, your pretty ways, your wise words. I thought I liked him well enough before that. Do you know how many times you have come to me since then? You do not, I see. You do not love me half as I love you. Eighteen times, Gerardo. And each time you are dearer. The day you do not come, it is night, not day, for Claelia. Speak to me, cruel boy. Have you a princess kneeling at your feet every day?”

“Signora,” Gerard faltered, “what can I say that is not better left unsaid? Oh, evil day that ever I came to this palace.”

“Nay, do not say that. It was the brightest day that ever shone on me, or even on you. I will make you confess that, before long, ungrateful one.”

“Your highness,” Gerard began, in a low, pleading voice.

“Call me Claelia, Gerar-do.”

“Signora, I am too young and too little wise to know how to speak to you without seeming blind, or ungrateful. But this I know: I would be both false and ungrateful, and the worst enemy you ever had, if I took advantage of this passing, mad fancy. Some ill spirit must have been allowed to afflict you with it. It is all unnatural, that a princess adorned with every grace should abase her affections on a poor churl like me.”

The princess drew her hand slowly back from his wrist, lingering for one soft, parting moment against his sleeve. “You fear my kinsmen’s daggers,” she said, half sad, half contemptuous.

“I no more fear their daggers than your maids’ bodkins,” Gerard said haughtily. “But I fear God and the saints, and my own conscience.”

“Truth, Gerardo, the truth. Hypocrisy sits ill on you. Princesses, while they are young, are not scorned for love of God, but for some other woman. Tell me who it is you love. If she is worthy of you, I will forgive you.”

“There is no such woman in Italy, on my soul.”

“Ah, so there is someone somewhere. Where?”

“In Holland, my native land.”

“Marie de Bourgoyne is fair, they say, but she is still but a child.”

“Princess, she I love is no noble. She is as common as I am. She is not so fair as you. But she is fair, and she is linked to my heart for ever by her virtues, by all the dangers and griefs we have borne together, for one another. Forgive me, but I would not wrong my Margaret for all the highest dames in Italy.”

Claelia leaped to her feet, standing over him, far more terrible than she had been when she beat Floretta: then her cheeks had been flushed red with anger, now they were pale as bone, and her eyes blazed with concentrated, noble fury. “You dare say that to my face, unmannered wretch!” she cried. “Was I born to be both insulted and scorned by the likes of you? Beware. We nobles brook no rivals. Choose now: the love of a Cesarini, or her hate. After all I have said and done to you, it must be one or the other, between us, to the death. Choose.”

Gerard looked up at her, towering over him in her flowing Roman toga, and felt like a puny mortal who had affronted an ancient goddess. He sighed deep, and said nothing. Perhaps that soft, patient sigh struck a tender chord in her unruly heart; or perhaps the tide of her fury ebbed as fast as it had risen. She sank languidly back into her seat, and hot tears began to stream fast down her cheeks.

“Alas! alas!” Gerard cried. “Weep not, sweet lady. Your tears accuse me, and I will soon weep for company. My kind patron, be yourself again. You will live to see how much better a friend I was to you than I seemed.”

“I see it now, Gerardo,” the princess said, wiping her eyes. “Friend is the right word. The only word that can ever pass between us. I was mad. Any other man would have taken advantage of my folly. You must teach me to be your friend, and nothing more.”

Gerard hailed that promise with joy, and quoted Cicero to her, telling her how godlike true friendship was, far rarer, truer, and more lasting than any romantic love, and even told her tales of his old friend Denys to prove he knew what real friendship was. She listened with her eyes half lidded, watching his face to read every corner of his character, to find his weak spot. At last she spoke, calm and collected. “Leave me now, Gerardo. Come back to me tomorrow, as you always do. You will find your lesson well bestowed.” She held out her hand to him; he kissed it, and left, pondering every strange turn of the interview, wondering if he had acted wisely.

The next day he was received with marked, cold distance. The princess stood before him as still and rigid as the marble statue he had first proposed to draw, and after a very short sitting, excused herself and dismissed him. Gerard felt the sharp chill, but told himself she was acting sensibly. The day after that, he found her surrounded by a crowd of vain young Roman nobles, all fawning over her and flattering her to the skies. They treated Gerard like a performing dog that could do a trick none of them could, and picked apart his portrait with a perfect mix of ignorance and insolence that made his cheeks burn bright red. Claelia watched his face, half lidded, demure, at every sharp little jab they threw, and the moment they left, she barred all of their names from her palace rolls, never to be received again.
