Life and liberty, when unchallenged, are rarely valued—why? Because they are assumed. But when threatened, their worth becomes clear. So too with sunlight: men do not admire it at midday, when it burns brightest, but in the evening, when it glows like molten topaz beneath the shade of ancient elms. Then, though weaker, its beauty is revealed by contrast with the gathering dusk.

So it was with Gerard and Margaret. Though every rustle of a leaf made them start, they moved through the forest now with hearts full of joy and quiet gratitude, gliding among the trees in peace, the silence deep and profound. Yet in their minds still echoed the barking dogs and the harsh voices that had pursued them so cruelly.

But soon Gerard noticed dark stains on Margaret’s ankles. “Martin! Martin!” he cried, his voice breaking. “Help! She’s been wounded—the crossbow!”

“No, no,” she said gently, smiling to calm him. “I’m not hurt. Not at all.”

“But what is this?” he demanded, trembling. “What in Heaven’s name is it?”

“Scold me not, then,” she whispered, her cheeks flushing. “Did I ever scold you?”

“No, dear Gerard,” he replied softly. “Well, then—Martin said the dogs were following blood. So I thought… if I could just have a little blood on my shoes, they might follow me instead, and leave you free.”

She paused, her voice low and tender. “So I scratched my arm with Martin’s knife—forgive me! Whose else could I take? Yours, Gerard? No. You would never let me. Please forgive me?”

He stared at her, choked with emotion. “Let me see it first,” he said hoarsely.

She held out her arm. He gasped. “There—it’s nothing,” she said, trying to laugh lightly.

“A scratch?” he repeated, his voice thick. “I call it a cut—a deep, terrible wound.”

He shuddered as he looked at the small but bleeding gash. “She might have used her bodkin,” muttered the soldier.

“Milksop!” he grumbled. “One who flinches at a scratch and a few drops of blood.”

“No,” Gerard said fiercely. “I could stand a sea of blood—but not hers. Oh, Margaret—how could you be so cruel?”

She smiled, her eyes shining with love. “Foolish Gerard,” she murmured, “to make such a fuss over nothing.” And she wrapped her arm around his neck, drawing him close. “As if I wouldn’t give every drop of blood in my heart for you—let alone a few from my arm.”

And there, under the weight of their shared danger, she wept upon his shoulder—not from pain, but from pity and devotion. He wept with her.

“And I must leave her,” he sobbed. “We two who love so deeply—one in Holland, one in Italy. Ah me! ah me!”

Margaret wept again, but quietly, silently. Instinct, never wrong, guided her. To speak her thoughts now would only deepen his sorrow. So she stayed silent, her tears falling unseen.

Suddenly they stepped onto a well-worn path. Martin stopped.

“This is the bridle-road I told you of,” he said, hanging his head. “And beyond lies the hostelry.”

Gerard and Margaret exchanged frightened glances.

“Come here, Martin,” Gerard whispered, pulling the soldier aside.

When they were apart, he spoke in broken tones: “Good Martin—watch over her for me. She is my wife. I leave her behind. Here—take this gold. It was meant for my journey. I cannot ask her to take it—she would refuse. But you will, won’t you? Oh, Heaven—this is all I can do? Money? But poverty is a curse. You’ll not let her want, will you? The burgomaster’s silver is enough for me.”

“You’re a good lad, Gerard,” Martin said, his voice rough with feeling. “No want, no harm shall come to her. I care more for her little finger than for all the world. And even if she were nothing to me, I’d protect her for your sake. Go with a brave heart. God go with you.”

He gripped Gerard’s hand tightly, then turned away, unable to look further.

After a pause, Martin stepped forward to return to Margaret—but Gerard stopped him.

“No, good Martin,” he said. “Please stay here, behind the thicket. Turn your face away while I—oh, Martin! Martin!”

This way, Gerard spared himself the agony of being watched as he parted from the woman he loved—and Martin spared himself the sight of their farewell.

He did not see them kneel together beneath the trees, renewing their vows before Heaven, broken only by violence. He did not see them cling to each other like drowning souls, unable to let go, pulling apart and returning, again and again, as if life itself depended on holding fast.

But he heard the sobs—Gerard’s deep, ragged cries, and Margaret’s soft, piteous moans.

Then came a sudden cry, and footsteps pounded across the road.

Martin rose, and there was Gerard running wildly, hands clasped above his head in prayer. Behind him, Margaret stumbled back toward him, arms outstretched, face pale as death, eyes fixed on nothing. He caught her in his arms and whispered comfort into her ear—but she could not hear. Only at the sound of his voice did she moan and clutch him tighter, trembling uncontrollably.

He lifted her onto the mule, wrapped his arm around her, and carried her slowly home. Her body, once strong and supple like a boy’s, now felt limp and powerless. She did not cry. She did not speak.

At the edge of the wood, he helped her down and told her to walk to her father’s house. She obeyed without protest.

Martin returned to Rotterdam. Sevenbergen was too hot for him.

Gerard, torn from the one he loved, moved like a man in a dream. At the little inn, he hired a horse and a guide, and rode swiftly toward the German frontier. But everything was mechanical. His senses dulled. Trees, houses, people passed by as if seen through a veil. His companions spoke twice—but he did not answer.

Only once did he burst out, savagely: “Shall we never be out of this hateful country?”

After many hours, they reached the crest of a steep hill. A small brook ran below.

“Halt!” the guide called, pointing across the valley. “There—Germany.”

“Where?” Gerard asked.

“On the other side of the stream. No need to ride down.”

Gerard dismounted without a word. From his belt, he drew the burgomaster’s purse. As he opened it, the guide muttered, half sulky: “You’ll soon be out of this hateful land. Maybe the one you’re going to likes you no better. But anyway—if it’s a church you’ve robbed, they can’t touch you once you cross that stream.”

Words that, at another time, would have earned a rebuke or a blow—now fell on Gerard like wind through empty air.

He paid the boy in silence and walked down the hill alone.

The brook sparkled, clear and cold, murmuring over smooth pebbles that glittered beneath the water. He sat beside it, staring blankly. Then he drank from it. Then he dipped his hands and feet into the icy flow. It stirred him.

He rose, took a run, and leaped across into Germany.

As soon as his foot touched the new soil, he turned sharply and looked back.

“Farewell, ungrateful land!” he shouted. “If it weren’t for her, I’d leave you forever—my kin, my mother, my childhood friends, my native town. Farewell, fatherland—welcome, wide world! Omne solum fortis patria.”

With those words on his lips, he suddenly sank, weak in limbs and spirit, and sat down on the foreign ground, sobbing bitterly.

After some time, he rose, wiped the tears from his eyes like a man, and stepped forward—without looking back, without hesitation—into the vast unknown.

Love and grief filled his soul so completely that fear had no place. Compared to parting from Margaret, walking alone to Italy in that harsh age seemed scarcely a burden.

All nations meet in a convent. Thanks to the monks and his own hunger for knowledge, Gerard could speak most tongues needed on his journey.

“I shall reach Rome soon,” he said to himself. “The sooner, the better.”

After walking a long league, he came to a crossroads—four paths winding through the countryside, confusing even seasoned travelers. He pulled out a small sun-dial Peter had given him, set it in the autumn light, and followed its shadow with sureness, heading south—like a young swallow flying alone, yet unlike the swallow, without any companion.

And so he went—into the world, into exile, into the future.
