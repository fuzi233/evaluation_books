Word of Rosanna's vanishing had rippled quickly through the ranks of the outdoor staff, prompting immediate inquiries. Their search led swiftly to a scrawny youth known as "Duffy," a frequent gardener who claimed to have glimpsed Rosanna Spearman less than thirty minutes prior. Duffy insisted the girl had not merely walked, but sprinted through the fir plantation, heading directly toward the sea.

"Does this boy know the coast?" Sergeant Cuff asked.

"He is a local native," I replied.

"Duffy!" the Sergeant barked, offering a coin for his cooperation. "Come with me. Betteredge, keep the pony-chaise ready."

In a flash, the Sergeant and the boy vanished toward the Shivering Sands, moving with a speed that left me, a man of seventy, panting in their wake. As the pair departed, a strange, paralyzing restlessness seized me. I wandered aimlessly within the house, performing a dozen futile tasks, unable to focus on anything other than the mounting mystery.

Before long, Duffy returned, breathless, carrying a scrap of paper torn from the Sergeant's pocket-book. It bore a frantic scrawl: *Send me one of Rosanna Spearman's boots, and be quick about it.*

Though I knew a messenger would have delivered the boot faster, I hesitated. A stubborn, protective instinct resurfaced at the eleventh hour; I needed to verify the situation personally before entrusting evidence to the authorities. Clutching the heavy leather shoe, I raced toward the shore, pushing my failing limbs to their limit.

The sky had turned a bruised purple, and rain lashed down in driving sheets, whipped into white foam by the gale. Thunder rolled across the bay, shaking the ground beneath my feet. Near the dunes, I spotted Duffy huddled for shelter, but beyond him lay the terrifying scene: the churning grey sea, the crashing rollers, and a solitary, dark figure standing defiantly on the wet expanse of the beach.

It was Sergeant Cuff.

He raised a hand toward the north, shouting over the roar of the storm, "Stay on that side! Come down here!"

I stumbled forward, gasping for air, my heart hammering a frantic rhythm against my ribs. Words failed me; a hundred questions swirled in my mind, yet none escaped my lips. The Sergeant's expression chilled me. There was a look of sheer horror in his eyes, a mask of dread that froze the blood in my veins.

He snatched the boot from my hand and pressed it firmly into a footprint etched into the sand. That print ran south, leading straight toward the jagged, submerged rocks of the South Spit. The mud was fresh, unblurred by the deluge, and the fit was perfect.

Cuff pointed silently to the evidence, then began to trace the path with his gaze, following the trail down where the sand gave way to stone. The South Spit was already being battered by the rising tide, the water heaving over the hidden treacherous slopes of the Quicksand. With a grim, obsessive patience, the Sergeant tested the boot against the prints, confirming they led unequivocally *to* the rocks, yet offered no sign of any steps leading *away*.

Exhausted, he finally dropped the matter. Turning to me, his eyes locked onto mine, then drifted out to the heaving waters. I saw the terrible conclusion forming in his mind. A cold, numb tremor seized me, crawling from my spine to my toes. I sank to my knees on the hard earth.

"She has returned to her hiding spot," Cuff murmured, almost to himself. "A fatal accident has occurred on those rocks."

Instantly, memories flooded back. Rosanna's altered demeanor, the hollow, deadened tone of her voice when I found her sweeping the corridors mere hours ago. These warnings clashed violently with the Sergeant's grim assessment. I tried to stammer out the truth—that her death was a suicide, a choice she had made willingly—but the words choked in my throat. The paralysis held me fast. I could not feel the biting rain, nor see the encroaching tide. In a trance-like vision, I saw her again as I had on that morning weeks prior, telling me that the Shivering Sand seemed to call to her, wondering if her grave awaited her there. The horror of it struck me with crushing force, echoing through the tragedy of my own daughter, who was just Rosanna's age. A mother's grief, raw and ancient, tore through me.

Gently, the Sergeant lifted me from the ground and turned me away from the gruesome sight.

Relieved, I began to catch my breath, seeing the world as it truly was. From behind the dunes, the male servants and a fisherman named Yolland rushed down, asking if the girl had been found. Cuff briefly explained the evidence of the footprints, confirming a tragic loss. Then, he singled out Yolland.

"In such weather," Cuff asked, gesturing to the churning surf, "could a boat have reached those rocks?"

"No boat ever built," Yolland shouted, pointing to the massive walls of foam smashing against the headlands, "could have gotten through that!"

Cuff studied the fading tracks, now beginning to blur under the relentless rain. "There," he declared, "proves she could not leave by land. And here," he nodded at the fisherman, "proves she could not escape by sea." He paused, considering the facts. "She was seen running here an hour ago. By now, the tide should be high on the southern side of the spit, covering the rocks entirely."

"The tide hasn't risen enough yet to drown a kitten on that southern side," Yolland corrected, glancing at the exposed flats. "An hour ago, the Shivering Sand itself would have been barely covered."

Cuff turned north, toward the dangerous quicksand. "Then the accident must have happened on this side."

My tongue suddenly loosened. "No accident!" I cried, my voice cracking. "She came here weary of life, intending to end it."

The Sergeant recoiled slightly. "How do you know?" he asked sharply. The others crowded around, listening intently. Cuff regained his composure immediately, shielding me from the group's scrutiny. "Let him alone for a moment," he commanded gently, then turned back to Yolland. "Is there any chance of recovering her body when the tide goes out?"

"Not a chance," Yolland replied grimly. "What the Sand takes, it keeps forever."

Stepping closer, the fisherman addressed me directly. "Mr. Betteredge, I must speak plainly. Four yards out, along the base of the Spit, lies a shelf of rock, half-fathom deep beneath the sand. If she had slipped accidentally, she would have fallen onto that footing, perhaps waist-deep, but alive. To disappear, she must have waded out into the Deep beyond, or deliberately jumped. It was no accident, sir. The Deep has taken her, and she sought it herself."

Silence fell over the group. The Sergeant, the servants, and I all stood still, absorbing the weight of the confession. Together, we turned back up the slope.

Waiting for us at the dunes was the under-groom, running from the house with a small note clutched in his hand. His face bore a solemn sorrow. "Penelope sent this, Mr. Betteredge," he said softly. "She found it in Rosanna's room."

It was her final farewell to the kind old man who had striven so faithfully to protect her.

"You have often forgiven me, Mr. Betteredge, in days past. When you next view the Shivering Sand, pray to forgive me once more. I have found my grave where it waited for me. I have lived, and died, sir, grateful for your kindness."

Those few words shattered me. Young men may cry easily when starting their journey, and old men when ending it, but I, a broken soul, could not hold back the tears. They poured out freely.

Sergeant Cuff stepped closer, his expression softening with genuine compassion. "Do not touch me," I whispered, shrinking back in fear. "It is the dread of you that drove her to this."

"You are mistaken, Mr. Betteredge," he answered calmly. "But there will be time to discuss this when we are safely indoors."

Supported by the groom's arm, I followed the others back through the lashing rain, returning to the house to face the storm of emotions and the terrible truths that awaited us within.
