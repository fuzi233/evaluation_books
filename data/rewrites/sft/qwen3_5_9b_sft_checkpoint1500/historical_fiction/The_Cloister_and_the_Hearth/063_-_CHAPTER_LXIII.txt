A shadow, heavy and suffocating, descended upon the mind of Gerard. For years, his love for Margaret had served as his north star, guiding his actions with unwavering purity. Now, that light was extinguished. He was adrift upon a vast, gloomy ocean where hope had perished, leaving only the endless, churning waves of despair.

Yet, his torment was not merely sorrow; it was a searing rage. He felt a profound, maddening exasperation at the self-denial he had endured, at the fortitude and dangers he had risked, and at the virtue he had sacrificed—all for nothing. The thought gnawed at him with relentless cruelty: if he had remained lazy, selfish, and comfortable at home, he might have saved Margaret's life.

These twin poisons, despair and bitter regret, boiled in his young blood, driving him to a fever pitch. He plunged headlong into pleasure, embracing it with a desperate ferocity. In those days, pleasure was indistinguishable from vice. He sought wine, women, gambling, and anything capable of procuring an hour of frantic excitement or a fleeting moment of oblivion. He dove into these vices as a drowning man rushes blindly into the mouths of enemies, hoping to escape the water by falling deeper into the mud.

The substantial sum he had set aside for Margaret now fueled his debauchery. Soon, he was the acknowledged leader of a circle of loose companions, men he had once respected and avoided. His heart, already fragile, began to rot alongside his morals. He grew sullen toward his former landlady, resenting her gentle advice and warnings, until he finally packed his bags and moved to a different quarter of the town, determined to escape her reproaches and the painful memories attached to her household.

Time wore down his resolve. His hand, once steady, began to tremble, rendering the pen useless for writing letters to comfort his conscience. As his patience eroded under the weight of his new habits, he abandoned the art of drawing in ink and took up painting in colors. Yet, even this creative outlet slipped from his grasp whenever his idle associates arrived to drag him back into the fray.

Thus, he sank into foul waters, searching for a shell of oblivion that would never hold. It was not necessary to detail every scene of coarse vice in which this unhappy young man now participated. What mattered was the undeniable truth: Gerard Vanucci had become a rake, a drunkard, and one of the most wicked and reckless youths in Rome.

Those who hide the sins of the fallen, claiming they wish to protect the reputation of the virtuous, ultimately rob despairing sinners of hope. Gerard was not a man who acted by halves; neither was his fall.

News of his degradation spread quickly through the city. His humble female friends whispered of his decline. His old landlady, Teresa, spoke to her husband, Lodovico, praying him to track down the lost youth. "Do you remember his face, Lodovico?" she asked. "Lodovico mio, a man of our trade never forgets a face, especially that of a benefactor. But you know I rarely venture abroad by daylight."

"Teresa," Lodovico replied with a sigh, "how long will this last?"

"Until some cavalier drives his sword through me," Lodovico answered grimly. "They will not allow a poor fellow like me to practice any honest trade."

Gerard, however, was not alone in his ruin. Pietro Vanucci, a man who bore adversity far better than prosperity, had recently been ejected from their shared lodging by the landlady for his late-night revelries. Upon crossing paths with Gerard in the street, Vanucci greeted him warmly, and before long, they were sharing quarters once more.

Vanucci brought with him a young apprentice named Andrea, who ground his pigments and served as both pupil and model. Andrea was a youth of striking beauty, sharp-tongued and quick-witted, possessing a vitality that contrasted sharply with Gerard's decay. Though Gerard had not entirely erased the warmth of his old friendships, he offered only a cold, distant politeness to Vanucci's advances.

Together, they formed a bizarre pair: Vanucci, a libertine, half-misanthrope, and avowed atheist; Gerard, a man whose spirit had been hollowed out by grief. They ran together with little in common, a rare phenomenon in the crowded streets of Rome.

One day, having squandered most of his remaining fortune and further undermined his already frail health, Gerard joined Vanucci in organizing a wild excursion up the Tiber. The plan involved a magnificent galley drawn by twelve buffaloes—a novelty imported from Florence by Lorenzo de' Medici. The libertines gathered with their chosen companions, each required to bring a beautiful lady. The prize for presenting the fairest lady was a crown of laurel and the title of public benefactor.

Gerard was notably absent from the initial gathering. Vanucci, feigning concern, claimed Gerard had forgotten to acquire a *fardingale* (a stiffened skirt worn by Renaissance women), suggesting the young man was still searching for a partner worthy of the company. Finally, Gerard appeared, accompanied by a woman of breathtaking beauty.

She was tall and statuesque, yet possessed a fluid grace like a young panther. Her features were flawless: a perfect oval face, a pale brow, olive cheeks flushed with vibrant life, and eyes framed by long, thick lashes that seemed designed to captivate any observer. Her lips were a saucy red, her teeth the color of polished ivory.

As they approached the boat, the effect was instantaneous. The women, previously confident, were silenced and depressed by this vision. The men erupted into cheers, waving their hats. One enthusiastic fool knelt on the gunwale, declaring her of divine origin, only to be gently rebuked by his companion and the goddess herself, who then took her seat at the prow with regal indifference. Pietro Vanucci sat slumped beside her, staring with his mouth agape like a stunned fish.

"The drover speaks to the beasts," Vanucci announced, "and the ropes tighten."

The buffaloes moved upstream. Vanucci turned to his companions. "What say you of this new beef, ladies?"

"We have never seen cattle so vile!" one lady cried, pointing at the animals' massive horns and foul nostrils. "Are those holes or noses?"

Another interjected, "They are so dull. Last time, I ascended the Tiber twice as fast with just five mules and an ass."

"Nonsense," a gallant retorted. Drawing his sword, he leaped onto the muddy bank, ignoring the drivers' protests, and charged the herd, goading them forward.

The buffaloes snorted and swished their tails, moving with sluggish momentum. The crowd laughed loudly. Suddenly, the leader of the bulls, a patriarch of immense size, swung its head. Its horn pierced the spark's doublet, sending the man flying high into the air. He landed sitting in the yellow water, his glittering sword waving unconsciously, soaking wet.

The ladies shrieked and clutched their hands, except for Gerard's companion. She cursed softly and seized the helm, steering the boat away from the chaos. The soaked cavalier scrambled aboard, gripping the gunwale.

"What is this?" he spluttered, confused and peevish. "I cannot understand it. One moment I am pricking African oxen, and the next..."

Gerard's companion, with dry wit, suggested he ask the Tiber. "Perhaps you should try pricking them again, Signor, until the matter becomes clear to you, bit by bit, as lawyers ascend to heaven and buffaloes cross the Tiber."

A knight nearby declared that she surpassed the sons of Adam in wisdom as much as she exceeded the daughters of Eve in beauty. At these compliments, Pietro Vanucci's behavior shifted dramatically. No longer gaping in shock, he sat coiled like a snake, his mouth hidden, two bright eyes twinkling comically as they fixed upon the lady.

However, the lady's gaze remained calm, sweeping over him without recognition or amusement, treating him as little more than furniture.

Suddenly, the harmony of the party fractured. Mortified by the overwhelming attention paid to Gerard's companion, the other ladies began to whisper—and eventually shout—their criticisms. The beautiful stranger proved poor in feminine tact; instead of retaliating through the men, she listened and openly defended herself against her detractors.

"There is not one of you wearing Nature's colors!" she declared, pointing accusingly. "Behold," she continued, exposing the makeup beneath a rival's face. "This is *cerussa*, this is *stibium*, and this is *purpurissum*. I know these articles well—I use them daily—but never on my face."

Her words caused Vanucci's eyes to sparkle with delight. "Why, your lips are colored, and the veins on your forehead are visible!" another lady shouted. "And look at your coarse black hair, drugged to resemble tow! Your bodies are as false as your hearts. Look at your padded bosoms and your wooden-heeled chopines, used to elevate your stunted limbs and deceive the world. Skinny dwarfs, cushioned and stultified into fat giants!"

"Avaunt, vulgar toad!" the accused retorted. "Tell the men everything, will you? Your cheeks are naturally ruddy, and your hair is but a handful of African wool. But who is more padded? You are shaped like a fire-shovel!"
