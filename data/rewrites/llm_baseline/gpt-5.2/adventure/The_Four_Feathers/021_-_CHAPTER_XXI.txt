Mrs. Adair sat with her needlework in her lap and a restlessness in her mind that no stitch could tame. She had spoken—too much, perhaps—to Durrance; she had laid bare the mistake he had made, and now she waited for the price of it. If he chose to be plain with Ethne, if he admitted he had learned the truth—that Ethne’s tenderness for him was a dutiful counterfeit, and her real love ran, like a hidden spring, toward Harry Feversham—then no care in the telling would keep Ethne’s quick, honest gaze from turning upon Mrs. Adair and demanding, in that clear voice of hers, why.

At first, no storm broke. Day followed day in Devonshire with a surface calm that seemed almost indecent. Ethne and Durrance met, spoke, even laughed, as though the strange landing of Willoughby at the garden and that evening of the Musoline Overture—Ethne’s bow biting and singing on the violin while the house listened—had never happened. Mrs. Adair drew a breath, grateful; then, as fear ebbed, a sharper discomfort took its place. Had her interference done nothing? Was the tragedy to continue by mere politeness?

But Durrance had decided quickly, and with the hard gentleness of a man who has learned to spare others because he cannot spare himself. Ethne had wished him not to know. Somewhere in her distress she had clung to one consoling thought—that she had brought happiness, or something like it, to the friend who sat beside her in darkness and spoke to her with so much steady cheer. Durrance understood that, and for the present he let her keep it. There were things he needed first: to know whether Ethne and Feversham were separated by a misunderstanding that might yet be mended, or by a break no courage could bridge; and—more urgently—to learn what had become of Feversham.

So he feigned ignorance. He even abandoned that habit of attention which had once made Ethne feel as though she were forever under a quiet scrutiny. Now he forced an ease upon himself; he made light of his blindness; he turned his misfortune into a topic for smiling courage, and his voice was so naturally composed that Ethne, listening, believed herself safe.

“You see,” he said one morning as they sat where the terrace looked down upon the creek, “one can get used to blindness and take it as the natural thing. But one does not get used to you, Ethne. Each time one meets you, one discovers something new and fresh to delight one. Besides, there is always the possibility of a cure.”

The reward of that careful gaiety was plain. Ethne’s face softened; she took it as proof that he had laid aside suspicion. She began to lean upon his cheerfulness as against her own misery, as a person in a rough sea may lean upon a plank, not because it saves, but because it keeps the mouth above water.

For her misery had returned in full. If she had once, for a single day, seized again the lightness that was hers before the three white feathers fell upon Ramelton like cold ash, she had now slipped back into those long nights of wakefulness when the head burns and the dawn comes grey and indifferent. Yet the grief had changed. Pride, at least, was no longer crushed; and Durrance’s hint—spoken as though it were a mere possibility—had planted a thin green shoot of hope. Harry might be rescued. Harry might still come back. Because she could not pretend any longer that he was simply a friend who had died. He was alive; and she dreaded what that life must be, and yet thirsted to know it, as one thirsts for water that may be brackish but is water still.

Now and then impatience broke through her careful restraint.

“I suppose that escape is possible from Omdurman,” she said one day, smoothing her voice into indifference.

“Possible? Yes, I think so,” Durrance replied, readily. “Of course it is difficult and would in any case take time. Attempts have been made, for instance, to get Trench out, and others, but the attempts have not succeeded. The difficulty is the go-between.”

Ethne’s eyes turned sharply upon him.

“The go-between?” she asked; then, as understanding leapt into place with a painful swiftness, she added, “I think I begin to understand,” and stopped too abruptly.

“You mean the Arab who can come and go between Omdurman and the Egyptian frontier?” she asked, after a beat.

“Yes. Usually some Dervish pedlar or merchant trading with the tribes of the Soudan. He slips into Wadi Halfa or Assouan or Suakin and undertakes the work. His risk is great; he would have short shrift in Omdurman if his business were detected. So it isn’t surprising he shirks the danger at the last moment. As often as not, too, he is a rogue. You make arrangements with him in Egypt and hand him the money. In six months or a year he comes back alone, with excuses. It was summer, and the season unfavourable for an escape. Or the prisoners were more strictly guarded. Or he himself was suspected. And he needs more money. Perhaps the tale is true, and you give more. Then again he comes back alone.”

Ethne nodded slowly, as if to say: yes—exactly. Something in her mind, which had been a tangle of fear and conjecture, straightened. She had been sure—without understanding how—that Harry Feversham meant to help Colonel Trench; and she could not see how his own capture could serve that aim. Now she could. He would be his own go-between. He would move between captivity and freedom by his own daring, carrying messages, laying plans, bribing where bribery was possible. The thought gave her hope a sudden strength, because Harry’s habits—his cold self-discipline, his precision—would make him plan with care. He would not be content to save another man and leave his own token of shame hanging in her mind. If he could bring Trench out, he would not stay behind.

They sat on the terrace while the sunset reddened the creek like spilled wine. The garden below lay in English quiet—trim grass, moist earth, the faint clean smell of water and late flowers. A thrush called, then stopped, and the pause that followed had the soft authority of peace.

“Life would not be easy, I suppose, in the prison of Omdurman,” Ethne said, again as though she did not care.

“Easy!” Durrance exclaimed. “No. A hovel crowded with Arabs, without light or air, the roof perhaps two feet above your head, locked up from sundown to morning. Very likely men have to stand all night, so closely packed are they. Imagine it even here in England, on an evening like this. Think what it would be on an August night in the Soudan—especially if you had memories of a place like this to make the torture worse.”

Ethne looked over the cool garden as though it reproached her by its mere existence. At this very moment Harry Feversham might be fighting for breath in darkness, throat dry, skin fevered, with visions of Ramelton’s slopes and, in his inner ear, the liquid music of the Lennon River.

“One would pray for death,” Ethne said slowly, “unless—” She had almost said, unless one went there deliberately with a fixed thing to do, unless duty were a rope under the hands; but she cut the sentence.

Durrance finished it for her.

“Unless there was a chance of escape,” he said. “And there is a chance—if Feversham is in Omdurman.”

He feared he had painted too fierce a picture and tried to soften it.

“Of course what I’ve described is hearsay. We don’t know. Prisoners may not have such bad times as we think.”

He let the subject fall, and Ethne did not raise it again. Sometimes she wondered what explanation he had found for her abrupt disappearance from the drawing-room on that night when he had spoken of meeting Harry at Wadi Halfa. But he never mentioned it, and she had sense enough to follow his silence. The change in him—the absence of that cautious attention which had once distressed her—lulled her fear. He had, it seemed, discovered some simple, natural story for himself.

And sometimes she asked herself why he had told her of Wadi Halfa at all, and of Harry’s departure to the south. She made an explanation for it that was strange but comforting: that the news had been meant for her, and for her alone; that Durrance had only been the instrument of a will above him. His real reason she had never stayed to hear.

So through September the pretence was maintained. Each morning, if Durrance was in Devonshire, he crossed the fields to The Pool. Mrs. Adair watched them as they talked and laughed without apparent embarrassment or estrangement, and her anger grew, not the blazing anger of a quarrelsome spirit, but the tight, righteous anger of a woman who feels herself trapped into complicity. The month strained all three. And when Durrance went to London to see his oculist, it was a relief so palpable it almost seemed shameful.

Those visits became more frequent and longer. Even Ethne was grateful. In his absence she could lay down the mask for a few days; she could be tired; she could sit alone and gather strength as one gathers fuel against a hard winter. There were hours when despair came upon her with its blunt question.

Shall I be able to keep up the pretence when we are married—when we are always together?

She pushed the thought away as if it were an open flame too near dry cloth. She dared not look forward; even now, she felt, her strength might fail.

After his third visit to London, Durrance said to her quietly, “Do you remember that I once mentioned a famous oculist at Wiesbaden? It seems advisable that I should go to him.”

“You are recommended to go?” Ethne asked.

“Yes—and to go alone.”

Ethne looked up with a quick, shrewd glance, as though she were weighing not only his words but the spaces between them.

“You think that I should be dull at Wiesbaden,” she said.

“There is no fear of that. I can rout out some relative to go with you.”

“No; it is on my own account,” he answered. “I may have to go into a home. It is better to be quite quiet and to see no one for a time.”

“You are sure?” Ethne’s voice tightened. “It would hurt me if I thought you proposed this because you believed I would be happier at Glenalla.”

“No,” he replied, and in that he spoke truth. Separation was necessary—for both. He suffered, no less than she, under the tyranny of perpetual simulation. Only because he knew how much she clung to the resolve not to spoil two lives because of her did he keep himself from crying out that he knew everything.

“I am returning to London next week,” he added, “and when I come back I shall be able to tell you whether I am to go to Wiesbaden or not.”

He had reason afterward to be glad he had spoken of his plan before any news arrived. When Calder’s telegram came from Wadi Halfa, Ethne could not connect the thought of his leaving with the receipt of news about Feversham.

The telegram came one afternoon. In the evening Durrance carried it across to The Pool and, with a new delicacy born from suffering and long thought, contrived that Ethne should read it alone. He moved away as soon as he gave it to her, turning toward the drawing-room where Mrs. Adair sat with a book. He had even folded the slip so that by the time Ethne unfolded it she was by herself on the terrace, with the open air about her.

There were only four words:

Feversham imprisoned at Omdurman.

The September evening lay hushed upon fields and creek. A light mist rose and crept over the garden bank and across the lawn. Far away, in the fierce country where the Niles join, the prison doors would already be shut for the night.

Ethne remembered Durrance’s description and her imagination enlarged it until it seemed she could smell the sour closeness of breath and sweat, feel heat pressing like a hand upon the face.

“He is to pay for his fault ten times over, then,” she cried, revolting against the disproportion. “And the fault was his father’s—and mine too—more than his own. For neither of us understood.”

She thought of the fourth feather she had given in her bitterness, and shame rose like a physical sickness. She leaned on the stone balustrade and closed her eyes. Was Harry alive now? Would he outlive this night? The coolness of the stone under her palms felt like accusation: smooth, safe, English—while he choked in a den under an African roof.

“Something can now be done,” said Durrance, and she heard his steps before his voice. He had come from the window of the drawing-room, speaking as he approached so as not to startle her.

“He was and is my friend; I cannot leave him there. I shall write to-night to Calder. Money will not be spared. He is my friend, Ethne—you will see. From Suakin or from Assouan something will be done.”

He placed the offered help to the credit of friendship alone, as though Ethne had no deeper concern. It was a mercy, and also an iron cruelty, and she could not bear either for long.

She turned suddenly, almost cutting across his words.

“Major Castleton is dead?” she asked.

“Castleton?” Durrance repeated, startled. “There was a Castleton in Feversham’s regiment. Is that the man?”

“Yes. He is dead?”

“He was killed at Tamai.”

“You are sure—quite sure?”

“He was within the square of the Second Brigade, on the edge of the great gulley, when Osman Digna’s men sprang out of the earth and broke through. I was in that square too. I saw Castleton killed.”

“I am glad,” said Ethne.

She said it plainly, without flourish, as if she had spoken of a gate being shut. The first feather had been brought back by Willoughby; it was possible, she thought, that Trench might bring back the second. Harry had succeeded once under peril; perhaps he might succeed again, though the peril now was greater and the difficulties heavier. Her faith, stubborn and unreasoning as roots in stone, held to that. But she was relieved—coldly relieved—to know the sender of the third feather could never be approached. Moreover she hated him; and that, to her mind, ended the matter.

Durrance stared. He was not of the braggart kind, not the loud talker who loves war as an occasion for big words. There was in him the quiet severity of a man who does his work without noise, and whose compassion is deep because it is disciplined. This hard little barbarism from Ethne he could not understand.

“You disliked Major Castleton so much?” he asked.

“I never knew him.”

“Yet you are glad that he is dead?”

“I am quite glad,” Ethne replied, stubbornly.

It was another slip. And Durrance did not let it pass into nothing. He carried it away and turned it over afterward in his gun-room at Guessens, fitting it into the explanation that had been slowly shaping itself under the sharpened wits which blindness had forced upon him.

Captain Willoughby’s visit and the token he brought had given him a clue. A white feather could mean only one thing—cowardice. Yet Durrance could not remember ever seeing cowardice in Harry Feversham; the charge still startled him into incredulity. But something had happened on the night of the ball at Lennon House, and from that hour Feversham had been an outcast.

Suppose a white feather had been sent to Lennon House and opened in Ethne’s presence—perhaps more than one. Ethne, Mrs. Adair had told him, had come back from her long talk with Willoughby holding that feather as though nothing in the world were so precious. It followed that in one particular the accusation had been answered; the cowardice, in one fashion, atoned. Ethne’s return to youth—her sudden lightness—pointed that way.

But Feversham had not returned. He still moved in by-ways like a man who cannot yet come into the open. Then Willoughby was not the only accuser; there had been others—two others. One of them Durrance had already identified. When he had suggested Omdurman, Ethne had answered at once, “Colonel Trench is in Omdurman,” as though she needed no explanation of Harry’s disappearance from Wadi Halfa into the southern Soudan. It had been deliberate: he had gone to be captured, to be taken to Omdurman. And her quick understanding of the go-between had helped Durrance further. There was an obligation on Feversham to come to Trench’s help. Perhaps he had ventured out into the desert to become his own go-between, to rescue Trench and so bring back the second feather.

And now, to-night, Major Castleton joined himself in Durrance’s mind to Trench and Willoughby. Ethne’s satisfaction at the death of a man she had never met could mean only one thing: there would have been the same obligation upon Feversham towards Castleton if he had lived. It seemed likely a third feather had come—and that Castleton had sent it.

So the story grew clearer, joint by joint, like a weapon assembled in the dark by touch alone. There remained one man who could tell him the truth and had refused—who would no doubt refuse still. Yet that man’s help Durrance meant to enlist. And to enlist it, he must come not begging for explanation, but speaking as though he already knew.

“Yes,” he said aloud to the emptiness of the gun-room, “I think that after my next visit to London I can pay a visit to Lieutenant Sutch.”
