Tito had not spoken idly when he let fall, with that lightness which was always half a screen and half a lure, that he was safe.

For three months—months in which he had watched Florence like a cook watching a pot whose boil cannot be hurried, only managed—he had been laying by his stores. He had salted and sealed his provisions of influence at Rome and at Milan, passing on hints, names, and private currents of intention against the Frate and the popular party, and receiving in return the kind of promises that are never written, only tasted in the air like distant smoke. At the same time, he had seasoned his manner for the Piagnoni, cultivating their good will with a deft hand, showing them, by fine touches and careful omissions, that his political convictions were wholly on their side—yet never troubling them with any show of religious ardour. He knew how a kitchen changes when zeal enters it: some men begin to burn what should be browned, others refuse to touch a knife at all. Tito offered them no fervours to distrust; he gave them plausibility, service, and a civil tongue.

And all the while he had not withdrawn from the Mediceans—had, indeed, made himself more necessary to them, more used, more trusted. The triple game did not tire him. It had become a kind of daily craft: like kneading, which is repetition with small variations, and the hands learn the truth of the dough before the mind names it. Besides, the Mediceans themselves admitted duplicity as a principle—so long as it ran outward. To outwit an opponent was fair stratagem; to outwit a friend seemed to them a baseness they had no temptation towards. They did not measure Tito by any standard that could have warned them. He had none of their hereditary bonds, no old alliances, no ancestral resentments that made their own mutual honour, such as it was, intelligible to them. The very absence that made him convenient as an instrument also made him unsafe as a companion; but convenience has a loud voice in councils where men listen for advantage.

Among the Roman and Milanese friends of the aristocratic party—the Arrabbiati, Savonarola’s bitterest enemies—the habit of underhand correspondence and espionage had become almost a trade. In such a trade, the best service was the deepest hypocrisy, and the heaviest pay went to him who could most perfectly keep a face. To suspect an agent because he played his part well would have been, in their own logic, absurdity.

On the other side, the Piagnoni, with the directness that belongs to energetic conviction, were the readier to credit Tito with sincerity in his adhesion because he did not counterfeit their devotions. His very coolness had the air of honesty, as if he were a man who cared for the public good but not for cant. It is one of the oldest powers in the world: to let others supply, from their own hunger, the meaning of your silence.

Under these conditions the three months had been flattering. The result Tito cared for most was not a triumph for Florence but a position for himself—at Rome or at Milan. He had grown more determined, as days went on, that when the favourable moment came he would quit Florence for one of those great capitals where rewards were richer, life less scrupulously observed, and talent and learning had a wider market, like grain carried downriver to a city that eats magnificently and asks fewer questions of the farmer. At present, Milan weighed heavier than Rome. If, within the year, he could render certain services to Duke Ludovico Sforza, a place at the Milanese court offered itself—glittering, spacious, and, to Tito’s imagination, cleaner than this narrow Florentine street-life where every step struck against someone’s memory.

He had foreseen that the Medicean conspirators might be discovered; what he could not foresee was the particular accident by which their secrecy would be torn. Lamberto dell’Antella arrested with a tell-tale letter on his person and rancour in his heart—there was no calculating for that. In the wide net that followed, Tito could not wholly escape implication. He had never expected to escape it if the discovery grew broad. But his mind ran quickly along the necessary course, choosing the surest path with the fewest thorns.

It is pleasant to keep a whole skin; but skin is sensitive to weather, and even safety can feel like damp. By nightfall—before he went home—he had secured the three things he most cared to carry out of the storm. He was to be freed from all proceedings against him for complicity with the Mediceans. He was to retain his secretaryship another year unless he resigned it sooner. And the price by which he bought these guarantees—the most bitter ingredient in his bargain—was to be kept as a State secret.

Most men would have thought that price heavy. Tito himself would rather not have paid it. But he had long trained himself to swallow what was necessary without showing distaste.

His first step had been to win Francesco Valori. Valori was not only one of the Ten under whom Tito held his secretaryship, but one of the special council appointed to investigate the evidence. He stood at the head of the Piagnoni—capable of fine qualities without any softening of his partisanship, arrogant enough to alienate friends, and capable of bitter personal animosities. One of the bitterest of these, well-known to those who listened for such notes, was directed against Bernardo del Nero.

Tito obtained a brief private interview with him, and before he spoke he exacted a pledge of secrecy. Then, with that ease which made confession look like candour rather than necessity, he avowed his own agency for the Mediceans. He was frank about his motives—frank as a man is frank who knows that the right kind of frankness is only another arrangement of concealment. He declared he had always believed the Medicean efforts futile, and that he sincerely preferred the maintenance of the popular government. He affected to confide, as if it were an intimate secret between allies, his personal dislike of Bernardo del Nero. Having thus set out the board and chosen the dishes, he came to his main offering.

There was, he said, another Medicean plot: one meant to secure Florence for Piero de’ Medici, to be carried into execution in the middle of this very August. If the government would grant him certain conditions, he could, by a journey to Siena and into Romagna—where Piero was again trying to gather forces—obtain documentary evidence to lay before the council. But for this to succeed, his character as a Medicean agent must remain unshaken in Medicean eyes. The fact that he was also a source of information to the authorities must be wrapped in profound secrecy. Still, some odour might escape—like a strong spice that clings to the fingers no matter how often they are rinsed. And before he incurred the consequences of acting against men who trusted him, he must be assured of immunity from prosecution as a Medicean and of retention of office for a year.

To Valori, these propositions did not sound as they sound to any reader who thinks of treachery as a single, clear taste. Valori’s mind was not intensely bent on estimating Tito’s conduct; it was intensely bent on procuring an extreme sentence against the five prisoners already taken. There would be immense efforts to save them. It was desirable—on public grounds, Valori would have said—that evidence should be as strong as possible, to alarm all well-affected men at the dangers of clemency.

Legal proceedings in that time had their own coarse kitchen of truth. Evidence was one of those desirable things that were often got only by foul means. To seize a few men and torture them into confessing everybody’s guilt was a step that passed for justice; and it was not always easy to see the next step, unless some traitor turned up, ready to carry the dish farther into the hall. Lamberto dell’Antella had already been tortured to help his willingness to tell more than he knew. Even so, additional and stronger facts were wanted—especially against Bernardo del Nero, who so far seemed only to have refrained from betraying the plot after failing to discourage it. The welfare of Florence demanded, Valori believed, that Bernardo’s guilt should be put in the strongest light. Perhaps Valori did not fully know how much his zeal was mixed—how hatred, like a pungent herb, can make itself seem like the flavour of righteousness.

He decided that Tito’s proposal should be accepted. He laid it before his colleagues without disclosing Tito’s name, and persuaded them.

Late in the day Tito was admitted to the special council. He produced among them a deep sensation by revealing this further plot to restore Piero—one prepared, he said, for the middle of August itself. Documentary proof would do more than anything to make the right course clear. Tito received a commission to start for Siena by break of day; and, besides that, he carried away from the council-chamber the written guarantee of his immunity and his retention of office.

Among the twenty Florentines who bent their grave eyes on him as he stood there—graceful, composed, speaking of startling things with easy periphrasis, and with that air of unaffected admission of motives short of the highest which is often the most calculated affectation—there were several who, even in their preoccupation, revised their estimate of him. They concluded, without speech, that this ingenious Greek was to be used for public needs rather than private intimacy. Unprincipled men were useful. They enabled those with more scruples to keep their hands tolerably clean in a world where dirty work was always waiting, like a pile of fish that must be gutted if anyone is to eat.

Indeed, unless a man held the Frate’s extravagant belief that purity and loftiness could be pursued on earth as more than words, it was not clear to respectable Florentine brains how life could be conducted in any department without instruments it would not be unbecoming to kick or spit upon at the moment of paying wages. Some of these very men were soon to need unscrupulousness as decided as Tito’s for a memorable transaction; but, as their poet Pulci had said, it is one thing to enjoy the fruits of treachery and another to love the traitor.

Florence had always had a harsh ear. The same society could raise a gibbet for a murderer and a gibbet for a martyr; could hiss a dastardly act and hiss, with equal strength, a generous truth spoken aloud. In that mingled sound—censure and applause thrown like stones in a narrow street—there was not hopeless confusion, but struggling order; yet the sound itself mattered. A city lives inside its noises as much as inside its walls: the clatter of hooves on stone, the rasp of shutters, the cries of sellers, the sudden hush that means danger, the hiss that means judgement. Those who despised Savonarola called his words mere breath; yet breath moves crowds. And when judgement turns to public sound, it becomes a kind of weather no man can entirely escape.

Tito, at least, did not escape it inwardly. He was not unaware that he had sunk a little in the estimation of the men who accepted his services. He had the self-contemplation that accompanies a habit of acting on well-considered reasons, whatever their quality; and if he could have chosen, he would have declined to see himself disapproved by men of the world. He had never meant to be disapproved. He had meant always to conduct himself so ably that, even when he departed from other men’s standards, they should not perceive it. With Romola, that concealment had become impossible; and it was that impossibility which had raised the barrier between them.

He shrank from condemnatory judgements as from a climate that would not suit his lungs. Yet events were not so plastic to cleverness as he wished. Things had inconveniently turned.

In truth he bore no rancour against Messer Bernardo del Nero. He even liked Lorenzo Tornabuoni and Giannozzo Pucci. He had served them ably; had their party won, he would have deserved a high reward. But was he to renounce all agreeable fruits because their party had failed? His promise to furnish additional proof against them would, he believed, make little difference to their fate. He was convinced they would escape extreme consequences. But if he had not offered it, his own fortunes—so carefully built—would have been ruined at the foundation.

And what motive could any man really have, except his own interest?

Florentines whose passions were tied up in their precarious political schemes might have no self-interest separable from family pride and old attachments and hatreds. A simpleton might swallow whole some old philosophy, take the indigestion for a divine afflux or an inward monitor, and call his self-conceit virtue. Fanatics who believed in Scourge and Renovation might see their interest in a palm-branch and a white robe hereafter. But a man of clear intellect—so Tito argued to himself—did not let his course be determined by such questionable fumes.

Had not Pontanus, poet and philosopher of unrivalled Latinity, made a splendid oration at Naples to welcome the French king who had come to dethrone Pontanus’s own royal friend and patron? And yet Pontanus held up his head and prospered. Men did not truly care for these matters, unless their personal spleen was touched. Weakness alone was despised. Power of any sort carried its immunity. A man could scarcely mount high without incurring unpleasant necessities that laid him open to enmity—and perhaps to a little hissing when enmity wanted a pretext.

It was that faint prognostic of hissing—gathered from small indications among the council—that made this day feel like an epoch to Tito and drove him to such inward pleading. Not that he had taken a deeper step in wrong-doing; he could not feel any tie to the Mediceans stronger than the tie to his father. But his conduct to his father had been hidden by successful lying: this act did not admit of total concealment. In its very nature it was a revelation, and revelation brings sound with it—the whisper first, then the hiss.

Well. A little patience, and in another year—perhaps in half a year—he might turn his back on these eager Florentines with their futile quarrels and sinking fortunes. His brilliant success here had carried ugly flaws: he had fallen in love with the wrong woman, and Baldassarre had returned under circumstances no mind could measure. But as Tito galloped with loose rein towards Siena, the night air flowing past him with the smell of dust and sweat and crushed herbs from the roadside, he saw a future in which those mistakes would no longer hunt him. He had money already safe out of Florence. He was eight-and-twenty, in the fresh ripeness of strength; he knew his skill had been tried and found serviceable. Could he not strip himself of the past as of rehearsal clothing, throw the old bundle away, and robe himself at last for the real scene?

It did not enter into his meditations that, on issuing from the council-chamber and descending the stairs, he had brushed against a man whose face he had not paused to recognize in the lamplight.

The man was Ser Ceccone—also willing to serve the State by giving information against unsuccessful employers.
