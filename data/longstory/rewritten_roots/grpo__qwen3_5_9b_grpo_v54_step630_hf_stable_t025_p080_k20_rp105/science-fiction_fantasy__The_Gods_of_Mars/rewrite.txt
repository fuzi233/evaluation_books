Twelve years had elapsed since I interred the remains of my great-uncle, Captain John Carter, within the silent, stone-walled crypt of the Richmond cemetery. It was a strange mausoleum, built according to his peculiar final instructions: an open casket, and a vault door secured by a mechanism accessible only from within.

In those twelve years, I had studied the remarkable manuscript of this enigma—a man who remembered no childhood, could not guess his own age, yet seemed eternally youthful. He was a warrior who had dandled my grandfather's great-grandfather on his knee, a soldier who had spent a decade on Mars, fighting both for and against its green inhabitants, battling the red men, and ultimately winning the hand of Dejah Thoris, Princess of Helium, to become a prince of the House of Tardos Mors.

Now, twelve years later, I found myself lost in futile speculation on a sultry August evening. Had John Carter truly perished on the bluff overlooking the Hudson, or did he still wander the dead sea bottoms of a dying world? Had he returned to Barsoom in time to open the great atmosphere plants and save millions from asphyxiation, only to be hurled back across forty-eight million miles of space? Or had he arrived too late, condemned to a living death on a corpse of a planet?

My thoughts were shattered when old Ben, my body servant, handed me a telegram. With trembling fingers, I tore it open:

The next morning, I boarded the first train for Richmond. Within two hours, I stood before the room where John Carter waited. As I entered, he rose, his face illuminated by that familiar, cordial smile. Time had spared him; he remained the straight, clean-limbed fighter of thirty. His grey eyes were sharp and undimmed, marked only by the deep lines of iron will that had defined him since I first knew him nearly thirty-five years prior.

"Well, nephew," he greeted me, "do you feel as though you are seeing a ghost, or merely suffering the effects of too many of Uncle Ben's juleps?"

"Juleps, I reckon," I replied, feeling a surge of vitality I hadn't expected. "But perhaps it is simply the sight of you again. You have returned to Mars? Tell me—did you find Dejah Thoris? Is she well, waiting for you?"

"Yes, I have been to Barsoom again," he said, his voice heavy with unspoken sorrow. "But it is a tale too long for the brief time I have before I must depart. I have learned the secret, nephew. I can now traverse the trackless void at will, moving between the countless planets as I choose. Yet, my heart remains anchored to Barsoom. While it resides there, in the keeping of my Martian Princess, I doubt I shall ever again leave the dying world that is my life."

He paused, looking at me with profound sadness. "I have come because my affection for you compelled me to see you one last time before you pass forever into that other life I shall never know. Though I have died thrice and shall die again tonight, as you know death, I remain as unable to fathom it as you are."

"Even the wise and mysterious Therns of Barsoom," he continued, "that ancient cult credited with holding the secrets of life and death in their impregnable fastnesses upon the Mountains of Otz, are as ignorant as we. I have proven it, though I nearly lost my life in the process. You shall read all of it in the notes I have compiled during the last three months of my return to Earth."

He gestured to a swelling portfolio lying on the table beside him.

"I know you are interested, and I know the world will be, though they will not believe for many ages, for they cannot comprehend. Earth men have not yet evolved to a point where they can grasp the truths written in those notes. Give them what you wish, what you deem harmless, but do not be aggrieved if they laugh at you."

That night, we walked down to the cemetery. At the threshold of his vault, he turned and pressed my hand.

"Good-bye, nephew," he said softly. "I may never see you again. I doubt I can ever bring myself to leave my wife and boy while they live, for the span of life upon Barsoom often exceeds a thousand years."

He stepped inside. The great door swung slowly shut. The ponderous bolts grated into place, and the lock clicked with a finality that echoed in my soul. I have never seen Captain John Carter, of Virginia, since.

Yet, here is the story of his return to Mars on that other occasion, gleaned from the vast mass of notes he left for me upon the table in his Richmond hotel room. There is much I have omitted, much I have dared not tell, but you will find the account of his second search for Dejah Thoris, Princess of Helium, even more extraordinary than the first manuscript I presented to an unbelieving world. Through those pages, we followed the fighting Virginian once more across the dead sea bottoms beneath the moons of Mars.

The cold wind off the Hudson bit through my coat as I stood on the bluff, the river below a silent, grey ghost of a stream. Ten years had passed since I last felt the pull of Mars, a decade of lonely prayer and waiting. But tonight, the red star hung low and bright, and the ancient, terrible magnetism of the God of War seized me again. It was the same pull that had dragged me from that Arizona cave twenty years prior, the same force that had carried me through the void.

My knees buckled. A wave of nausea washed over me, and I pitched forward onto the hard earth. But the moment my skin touched the soil, the familiar horror returned—the phantom screams of the cave, the feeling of paralysis. My muscles locked, then snapped free with a sharp click, like a wire severing under tension. I stood naked and trembling beside the lifeless husk of the thing that had tried to kill me, my eyes fixed once more on the red eye of Mars.

Then, the void opened.

I was gone.

The cold of space vanished, replaced instantly by the searing heat of a twin sun. I opened my eyes to find myself lying on a carpet of red grass, beneath a canopy of trees so vast and strange they seemed to touch the sky. The air was thin, the gravity light. As I attempted to rise, my Earthly strength betrayed me; the lack of resistance threw me several feet into the air before I crashed softly onto the grass. Laughing at my clumsiness, I adjusted, my sinews learning the rhythm of this new world.

The forest was a marvel of unnatural beauty. Trees of ebony, azure, and scarlet stood like polished pillars, their smooth trunks gleaming under the brazen sun. The birds were silent, winged shapes of impossible geometry. Beyond the treeline, a blue sea shimmered, and a great river flowed majestically toward it. This was not the barren wasteland I expected; this was a cultivated paradise.

Yet, as I stepped from the shadows of the trees toward the riverbank, the romance of the scene evaporated. In the meadow, a dozen grotesque figures moved slowly. They were tall, humanoid in torso and legs, but their arms were short, thick, and sinuous, moving like elephant trunks. Their skin was a ghastly blue, save for a broad band of white encircling a single, dead-white eye. Where a nose should be, there was only a ragged, inflamed hole resembling a fresh bullet wound. Below, the face was blank to the chin; there was no mouth.

They were the Plant Men.

Their heads were crowned with a tangled mass of jet-black hair, thick as angleworms, which writhed and crawled as if alive. From their armpits dangled tiny replicas of themselves, suspended by stems, ranging from unopened buds to miniature adults. These creatures fed by running their taloned hands over the grass, sucking the vegetation through mouths located in the palms of their arms.

As I watched, hidden behind a massive tree, a leader of the herd approached. He let out a strange, purring shriek. Instantly, the thousands of worm-hairs on the heads of the herd stiffened, becoming a thousand sensitive ears. The herd turned as one, leaping away in great, kangaroo-like bounds toward the cliffs.

Instinct took over. I launched myself across the meadow, my Earthly muscles producing prodigious leaps in this low-gravity world, chasing the fleeing monsters. They led me to the base of the cliffs, where the river seemed to rise from the earth itself. There, amidst scattered boulders, I saw the source of the commotion.

A small group of Green Men stood back-to-back, facing the Plant Men. These were the true inhabitants of Mars—tall, green-skinned warriors with massive tusks, laterally placed eyes, and extra arms. Two men and four women, armed with long-swords, they faced an overwhelming tide of blue horrors.

The Plant Men attacked with terrifying efficiency. One leaped high above the Green Men and brought his massive tail down in a sweeping arc, crushing a warrior's skull like an eggshell. Others circled, their screeching mouths and flailing tails creating chaos. Two Green Men fell instantly.

Only one warrior remained, along with two women. As two more Plant Men charged, the lone Green Man swung his sword, cleaving one monster in half. But the other struck down the women with a single blow of its tail.

Seeing his companions fall, the Green Warrior charged the remaining herd, swinging his blade in a frenzy. He hacked a path through the attackers and turned to flee into the forest.

I could not watch his noble stand without intervening. With a surge of bloodlust, I sprang from my hiding place, grabbing a long-sword from the ground. I joined the chase, my heart pounding with the thrill of battle.

The Green Warrior was overtaken near the forest edge, backed against a boulder. The Plant Men hissed around him, their single eyes fixed on their prey. I moved silently, closing the distance before they could react. Four of them fell to my blade before they realized I was there.

For a moment, we stood together, a whirlwind of steel and fury. The Green Warrior fought with a skill I had rarely seen, his blade cutting through flesh, bone, and metal in a figure-eight pattern that kept him safe. We were a perfect team, dispatching the monsters with ruthless efficiency. Green, sticky syrup oozed from their severed arteries, coating us as we fought. At one point, a monster's talons sank into my back, its moist lips sucking at the wound, but the Green Warrior tore the beast away with a single, devastating stroke.

Just as we began to gain the upper hand, the shrill wail echoed again from above. I looked up to see a strange figure standing on a natural balcony of the cliff, shrieking and waving his arms.

Following his gaze, my blood ran cold. Converging from the forest, the river, and the distant plains were hundreds of Plant Men, alongside new, swift-running monsters.

"It will be a great death," I said to my companion.

The Green Warrior smiled, a grim expression of acceptance. "We may at least die fighting and as great warriors should, John Carter."

We had just slain the last of our immediate foes when the realization hit me. The voice calling the horde... the Green Warrior's face transformed.

"There before my astonished eyes I beheld the greatest of the green men of Barsoom," I thought, the name rising in my mind with the force of destiny. "Their shrewdest statesman, their mightiest general, my great and good friend, Tars Tarkas, Jeddak of Thark."

There was no pause for exchange of tales between Tars Tarkas and myself as we stood frozen before the great boulder, encircled by the twisted carcasses of our grotesque attackers. From every direction, the broad valley began to fill with a torrent of terrifying shapes, answering the eerie summons of the strange figure perched high above us.

"Come!" cried Tars Tarkas, his voice tight with urgency. "We must make for the cliffs. There lies our only hope of even temporary escape; there we may find a cave or a narrow ledge where two may defend themselves forever against this motley, unarmed horde."

Together we raced across the scarlet grass, I carefully matching my stride to ensure I did not leave my slower companion behind. We had perhaps three hundred yards to cover between our boulder and the cliffs, a distance that required us to locate a suitable shelter before the terrifying things pursuing us could close in.

They were rapidly overhauling us when Tars Tarkas shouted for me to hasten ahead and discover, if possible, the sanctuary we sought. It was a sound strategy; saving even minutes meant life. Throwing every ounce of my earthly strength into the effort, I cleared the remaining distance in great leaps and bounds, reaching the base of the cliffs in a moment.

The cliffs rose perpendicular directly from the almost level sward of the valley. There was no accumulation of fallen debris forming a rough ascent, as is common with nearly all other cliffs I have seen. The scattered boulders that lay upon or partly buried in the turf were the only indication that any disintegration of the massive, towering pile of rocks had ever taken place.

My first cursory inspection of the face of the cliffs filled my heart with forebodings. Nowhere could I discern, save where the weird herald stood still shrieking his shrill summons, the faintest indication of even a bare foothold upon the lofty escarpment.

To my right, the bottom of the cliff was lost in the dense foliage of the forest, which terminated at its very foot, rearing its gorgeous leaves fully a thousand feet against its stern and forbidding neighbor. To the left, the cliff ran, apparently unbroken, across the head of the broad valley, to be lost in the outlines of what appeared to be a range of mighty mountains that skirted and confined the valley in every direction.

Perhaps a thousand feet from me, the river broke directly from the base of the cliffs, and as there seemed not the remotest chance for escape in that direction, I turned my attention again toward the forest.

The cliffs towered above me a good five thousand feet. The sun was not yet upon them, and they loomed a dull yellow in their own shade. Here and there they were broken with streaks and patches of dusky red, green, and occasional areas of white quartz.

Altogether they were very beautiful, but I fear that I did not regard them with a particularly appreciative eye on this, my first inspection of them. Just then I was absorbed in them only as a medium of escape, and so, as my gaze ran quickly, time and again, over their vast expanse in search of some cranny or crevice, I came suddenly to loathe them as the prisoner must loathe the cruel and impregnable walls of his dungeon.

Tars Tarkas was approaching me rapidly, and still more rapidly came the awful horde at his heels.

It seemed the forest now or nothing, and I was just on the point of motioning Tars Tarkas to follow me in that direction when the sun passed the cliff's zenith. As the bright rays touched the dull surface, it burst out into a million scintillant lights of burnished gold, of flaming red, of soft greens, and gleaming whites—a more gorgeous and inspiring spectacle the human eye has never rested upon.

The face of the entire cliff was, as later inspection conclusively proved, so shot with veins and patches of solid gold as to quite present the appearance of a solid wall of that precious metal, except where it was broken by outcroppings of ruby, emerald, and diamond boulders—a faint and alluring indication of the vast and unguessable riches which lay deeply buried behind the magnificent surface.

But what caught my most interested attention at the moment that the sun's rays set the cliff's face a-shimmer, was the several black spots which now appeared quite plainly in evidence high across the gorgeous wall close to the forest's top, and extending apparently below and behind the branches.

Almost immediately I recognized them for what they were: the dark openings of caves entering the solid walls—possible avenues of escape or temporary shelter, could we but reach them.

There was but a single way, and that led through the mighty, towering trees upon our right. That I could scale them I knew full well, but Tars Tarkas, with his mighty bulk and enormous weight, would find it a task possibly quite beyond his prowess or his skill, for Martians are at best but poor climbers. Upon the entire surface of that ancient planet I never before had seen a hill or mountain that exceeded four thousand feet in height above the dead sea bottoms, and as the ascent was usually gradual, nearly to their summits, they presented but few opportunities for the practice of climbing. Nor would the Martians have embraced even such opportunities as might present themselves, for they could always find a circuitous route about the base of any eminence, and these roads they preferred and followed in preference to the shorter but more arduous ways.

However, there was nothing else to consider than an attempt to scale the trees contiguous to the cliff in an effort to reach the caves above.

The Thark grasped the possibilities and the difficulties of the plan at once, but there was no alternative, and so we set out rapidly for the trees nearest the cliff.

Our relentless pursuers were now close to us, so close that it seemed that it would be an utter impossibility for the Jeddak of Thark to reach the forest in advance of them, nor was there any considerable will in the efforts that Tars Tarkas made, for the green men of Barsoom do not relish flight, nor ever before had I seen one fleeing from death in whatsoever form it might have confronted him. But that Tars Tarkas was the bravest of the brave he had proven thousands of times; yes, tens of thousands in countless mortal combats with men and beasts. And so I knew that there was another reason than fear of death behind his flight, as he knew that a greater power than pride or honor spurred me to escape these fierce destroyers. In my case it was love—love of the divine Dejah Thoris; and the cause of the Thark's great and sudden love of life I could not fathom, for it is oftener that they seek death than life—these strange, cruel, loveless, unhappy people.

At length, however, we reached the shadows of the forest, while right behind us sprang the swiftest of our pursuers—a giant plant man with claws outreaching to fasten his bloodsucking mouths upon us.

He was, I should say, a hundred yards in advance of his closest companion, and so I called to Tars Tarkas to ascend a great tree that brushed the cliff's face while I dispatched the fellow, thus giving the less agile Thark an opportunity to reach the higher branches before the entire horde should be upon us and every vestige of escape cut off.

But I had reckoned without a just appreciation either of the cunning of my immediate antagonist or the swiftness with which his fellows were covering the distance which had separated them from me.

As I raised my long-sword to deal the creature its death thrust, it halted in its charge. As my sword cut harmlessly through the empty air, the great tail of the thing swept with the power of a grizzly's arm across the sward and carried me bodily from my feet to the ground. In an instant the brute was upon me, but ere it could fasten its hideous mouths into my breast and throat I grasped a writhing tentacle in either hand.

The plant man was well muscled, heavy, and powerful, but my earthly sinews and greater agility, in conjunction with the deathly strangle hold I had upon him, would have given me, I think, an eventual victory had we had time to discuss the merits of our relative prowess uninterrupted. But as we strained and struggled about the tree into which Tars Tarkas was clambering with infinite difficulty, I suddenly caught a glimpse over the shoulder of my antagonist of the great swarm of pursuers that now were fairly upon me.

Now, at last, I saw the nature of the other monsters who had come with the plant men in response to the weird calling of the man upon the cliff's face. They were that most dreaded of Martian creatures—the great white apes of Barsoom.

My former experiences upon Mars had familiarized me thoroughly with them and their methods, and I may say that of all the fearsome and terrible, weird and grotesque inhabitants of that strange world, it is the white apes that come nearest to familiarizing me with the sensation of fear.

I think that the cause of this feeling which these apes engender within me is due to their remarkable resemblance in form to our Earth men, which gives them a human appearance that is most uncanny when coupled with their enormous size.

They stand fifteen feet in height and walk erect upon their hind feet. Like the green Martians, they have an intermediary set of arms midway between their upper and lower limbs. Their eyes are very close set, but do not protrude as do those of the green men of Mars; their ears are high set, but more laterally located than are the green men's, while their snouts and teeth are much like those of our African gorilla. Upon their heads grows an enormous shock of bristly hair.

It was into the eyes of such as these and the terrible plant men that I gazed above the shoulder of my foe, and then, in a mighty wave of snarling, snapping, screaming, purring rage, they swept over me—and of all the sounds that assailed my ears as I went down beneath them, to me the most hideous was the horrid purring of the plant men.

Instantly a score of cruel fangs and keen talons were sunk into my flesh; cold, sucking lips fastened themselves upon my arteries. I struggled to free myself, and even though weighed down by these immense bodies, I succeeded in struggling to my feet, where, still grasping my long-sword, and shortening my grip upon it until I could use it as a dagger, I wrought such havoc among them that at one time I stood for an instant free.

What it has taken minutes to write occurred in but a few seconds, but during that time Tars Tarkas had seen my plight and had dropped from the lower branches, which he had reached with such infinite labor, and as I flung the last of my immediate antagonists from me, the great Thark leaped to my side, and again we fought, back to back, as we had done a hundred times before.

Time and again the ferocious apes sprang in to close with us, and time and again we beat them back with our swords. The great tails of the plant men lashed with tremendous power about us as they charged from various directions or sprang with the agility of greyhounds above our heads; but every attack met a gleaming blade in sword hands that had been reputed for twenty years the best that Mars ever had known; for Tars Tarkas and John Carter were names that the fighting men of the world of warriors loved best to speak.

But even the two best swords in a world of fighters can avail not forever against overwhelming numbers of fierce and savage brutes that know not what defeat means until cold steel teaches their hearts no longer to beat, and so, step by step, we were forced back. At length we stood against the giant tree that we had chosen for our ascent, and then, as charge after charge hurled its weight upon us, we gave back again and again, until we had been forced half-way around the huge base of the colossal trunk.

Tars Tarkas was in the lead, and suddenly I heard a little cry of exultation from him.

"Here is shelter for one at least, John Carter," he said, and, glancing down, I saw an opening in the base of the tree about three feet in diameter.

"In with you, Tars Tarkas," I cried, but he would not go; saying that his bulk was too great for the little aperture, while I might slip in easily.

"We shall both die if we remain without, John Carter; here is a slight chance for one of us. Take it and you may live to avenge me; it is useless for me to attempt to worm my way into so small an opening with this horde of demons besetting us on all sides."

"Then we shall die together, Tars Tarkas," I replied, "for I shall not go first. Let me defend the opening while you get in, then my smaller stature will permit me to slip in with you before they can prevent."

We still were fighting furiously as we talked in broken sentences, punctured with vicious cuts and thrusts at our swarming enemy.

At length he yielded, for it seemed the only way in which either of us might be saved from the ever-increasing numbers of our assailants, who were still swarming upon us from all directions across the broad valley.

"It was ever your way, John Carter, to think last of your own life," he said; "but still more your way to command the lives and actions of others, even to the greatest of Jeddaks who rule upon Barsoom."

There was a grim smile upon his cruel, hard face, as he, the greatest Jeddak of them all, turned to obey the dictates of a creature of another world—of a man whose stature was less than half his own.

"If you fail, John Carter," he said, "know that the cruel and heartless Thark, to whom you taught the meaning of friendship, will come out to die beside you."

"As you will, my friend," I replied; "but quickly now, head first, while I cover your retreat."

For moments after that awful laugh had ceased reverberating through the rocky room, Tars Tarkas and I stood in tense and expectant silence. But no further sound broke the stillness, nor within the range of our vision did aught move.

At length Tars Tarkas laughed softly, after the manner of his strange kind when in the presence of the horrible or terrifying. It is not an hysterical laugh, but rather the genuine expression of the pleasure they derive from the things that move Earth men to loathing or to tears.

Often and again have I seen them roll upon the ground in mad fits of uncontrollable mirth when witnessing the death agonies of women and little children beneath the torture of that hellish green Martian fete—the Great Games.

I looked up at the Thark, a smile upon my own lips, for here in truth was greater need for a smiling face than a trembling chin.

"What do you make of it all?" I asked. "Where in the deuce are we?"

He looked at me in surprise.

"Where are we?" he repeated. "Do you tell me, John Carter, that you know not where you be?"

"That I am upon Barsoom is all that I can guess, and but for you and the great white apes I should not even guess that, for the sights I have seen this day are as unlike the things of my beloved Barsoom as I knew it ten long years ago as they are unlike the world of my birth.

"No, Tars Tarkas, I know not where we be."

"Where have you been since you opened the mighty portals of the atmosphere plant years ago, after the keeper had died and the engines stopped and all Barsoom was dying, that had not already died, of asphyxiation? Your body even was never found, though the men of a whole world sought after it for years, though the Jeddak of Helium and his granddaughter, your princess, offered such fabulous rewards that even princes of royal blood joined in the search.

"Thank God," I interrupted him. Many years ago you heard the story of the woman who taught me the thing that green Martians are reared to hate, the woman who taught me to love. You know the cruel tortures and the awful death her love won for her at the hands of the beast, Tal Hajus.

"She, I thought, awaited me by the Lost Sea of Korus.

"You know that it was left for a man from another world, for yourself, John Carter, to teach this cruel Thark what friendship is; and you, I thought, also roamed the care-free Valley Dor.

"Thus were the two I most longed for at the end of the long pilgrimage I must take some day, and so as the time had elapsed which Dejah Thoris had hoped might bring you once more to her side, for she has always tried to believe that you had but temporarily returned to your own planet, I at last gave way to my great yearning and a month since I started upon the journey, the end of which you have this day witnessed. "And that was the River Iss, emptying into the Lost Sea of Korus in the Valley Dor?" I asked.

"This is the valley of love and peace and rest to which every Barsoomian since time immemorial has longed to pilgrimage at the end of a life of hate and strife and bloodshed," he replied. "This, John Carter, is Heaven."

His tone was cold and ironical; its bitterness but reflecting the terrible disappointment he had suffered. Such a fearful disillusionment, such a blasting of life-long hopes and aspirations, such an uprooting of age-old tradition might have excused a vastly greater demonstration on the part of the Thark.

I laid my hand upon his shoulder.

"I am sorry," I said, nor did there seem aught else to say.

"Think, John Carter, of the countless billions of Barsoomians who have taken the voluntary pilgrimage down this cruel river since the beginning of time, only to fall into the ferocious clutches of the terrible creatures that to-day assailed us.

"There is an ancient legend that once a red man returned from the banks of the Lost Sea of Korus, returned from the Valley Dor, back through the mysterious River Iss, and the legend has it that he narrated a fearful blasphemy of horrid brutes that inhabited a valley of wondrous loveliness, brutes that pounced upon each Barsoomian as he terminated his pilgrimage and devoured him upon the banks of the Lost Sea where he had looked to find love and peace and happiness; but the ancients killed the blasphemer, as tradition has ordained that any shall be killed who return from the bosom of the River of Mystery.

"But now we know that it was no blasphemy, that the legend is a true one, and that the man told only of what he saw; but what does it profit us, John Carter, since even should we escape, we also would be treated as blasphemers? We are between the wild thoat of certainty and the mad zitidar of fact—we can escape neither."

"As Earth men say, we are between the devil and the deep sea, Tars Tarkas," I replied, nor could I help but smile at our dilemma.

"There is naught that we can do but take things as they come, and at least have the satisfaction of knowing that whoever slays us eventually will have far greater numbers of their own dead to count than they will get in return. White ape or plant man, green Barsoomian or red man, whosoever it shall be that takes the last toll from us will know that it is costly in lives to wipe out John Carter, Prince of the House of Tardos Mors, and Tars Tarkas, Jeddak of Thark, at the same time."

I could not help but laugh at his grim humour, and he joined in with me in one of those rare laughs of real enjoyment which was one of the attributes of this fierce Tharkian chief which marked him from the others of his kind.

"But about yourself, John Carter," he cried at last. "If you have not been here all these years where indeed have you been, and how is it that I find you here to-day?"

"I have been back to Earth," I replied. "For ten long Earth years I have been praying and hoping for the day that would carry me once more to this grim old planet of yours, for which, with all its cruel and terrible customs, I feel a bond of sympathy and love even greater than for the world that gave me birth.

"For ten years have I been enduring a living death of uncertainty and doubt as to whether Dejah Thoris lived, and now that for the first time in all these years my prayers have been answered and my doubt relieved I find myself, through a cruel whim of fate, hurled into the one tiny spot of all Barsoom from which there is apparently no escape, and if there were, at a price which would put out for ever the last flickering hope which I may cling to of seeing my princess again in this life—and you have seen to-day with what pitiful futility man yearns toward a material hereafter.

"Only a bare half-hour before I saw you battling with the plant men I was standing in the moonlight upon the banks of a broad river that taps the eastern shore of Earth's most blessed land. I have answered you, my friend. Do you believe?"

"I believe," replied Tars Tarkas, "though I cannot understand."

As we talked I had been searching the interior of the chamber with my eyes. It was, perhaps, two hundred feet in length and half as broad, with what appeared to be a doorway in the centre of the wall directly opposite that through which we had entered.

The apartment was hewn from the material of the cliff, showing mostly dull gold in the dim light which a single minute radium illuminator in the centre of the roof diffused throughout its great dimensions. Here and there polished surfaces of ruby, emerald, and diamond patched the golden walls and ceiling. The floor was of another material, very hard, and worn by much use to the smoothness of glass. Aside from the two doors I could discern no sign of other aperture, and as one we knew to be locked against us I approached the other.

As I extended my hand to search for the controlling button, that cruel and mocking laugh rang out once more, so close to me this time that I involuntarily shrank back, tightening my grip upon the hilt of my great sword.

And then from the far corner of the great chamber a hollow voice chanted: "There is no hope, there is no hope; the dead return not, the dead return not; nor is there any resurrection. Hope not, for there is no hope."

Though our eyes instantly turned toward the spot from which the voice seemed to emanate, there was no one in sight, and I must admit that cold shivers played along my spine and the short hairs at the base of my head stiffened and rose up, as do those upon a hound's neck when in the night his eyes see those uncanny things which are hidden from the sight of man.

Quickly I walked toward the mournful voice, but it had ceased ere I reached the further wall, and then from the other end of the chamber came another voice, shrill and piercing:

"Fools! Fools!" it shrieked. "Thinkest thou to defeat the eternal laws of life and death? Wouldst cheat the mysterious Issus, Goddess of Death, of her just dues? Did not her mighty messenger, the ancient Iss, bear you upon her leaden bosom at your own behest to the Valley Dor?

"Thinkest thou, O fools, that Issus wilt give up her own? Thinkest thou to escape from whence in all the countless ages but a single soul has fled?

"Go back the way thou camest, to the merciful maws of the children of the Tree of Life or the gleaming fangs of the great white apes, for there lies speedy surcease from suffering; but insist in your rash purpose to thread the mazes of the Golden Cliffs of the Mountains of Otz, past the ramparts of the impregnable fortresses of the Holy Therns, and upon your way Death in its most frightful form will overtake you—a death so horrible that even the Holy Therns themselves, who conceived both Life and Death, avert their eyes from its fiendishness and close their ears against the hideous shrieks of its victims.

"Go back, O fools, the way thou camest."

And then the awful laugh broke out from another part of the chamber.

"Most uncanny," I remarked, turning to Tars Tarkas.

"What shall we do?" he asked. "We cannot fight empty air; I would almost sooner return and face foes into whose flesh I may feel my blade bite and know that I am selling my carcass dearly before I go down to that eternal oblivion which is evidently the fairest and most desirable eternity that mortal man has the right to hope for."

"If, as you say, we cannot fight empty air, Tars Tarkas," I replied, "neither, on the other hand, can empty air fight us. I, who have faced and conquered in my time thousands of sinewy warriors and tempered blades, shall not be turned back by wind; nor no more shall you, Thark."

"But unseen voices may emanate from unseen and unseeable creatures who wield invisible blades," answered the green warrior.

"Rot, Tars Tarkas," I cried, "those voices come from beings as real as you or as I. In their veins flows lifeblood that may be let as easily as ours, and the fact that they remain invisible to us is the best proof to my mind that they are mortal; nor overly courageous mortals at that. Think you, Tars Tarkas, that John Carter will fly at the first shriek of a cowardly foe who dare not come out into the open and face a good blade?"

I had spoken in a loud voice that there might be no question that our would-be terrorizers should hear me, for I was tiring of this nerve-racking fiasco. It had occurred to me, too, that the whole business was but a plan to frighten us back into the valley of death from which we had escaped, that we might be quickly disposed of by the savage creatures there.

For a long period there was silence, then of a sudden a soft, stealthy sound behind me caused me to turn suddenly to behold a great many-legged banth creeping sinuously upon me.

The banth is a fierce beast of prey that roams the low hills surrounding the dead seas of ancient Mars. Like nearly all Martian animals it is almost hairless, having only a great bristly mane about its thick neck.

Its long, lithe body is supported by ten powerful legs, its enormous jaws are equipped, like those of the calot, or Martian hound, with several rows of long needle-like fangs; its mouth reaches to a point far back of its tiny ears, while its enormous, protruding eyes of green add the last touch of terror to its awful aspect.

As it crept toward me it lashed its powerful tail against its yellow sides, and when it saw that it was discovered it emitted the terrifying roar which often freezes its prey into momentary paralysis in the instant that it makes

The cacophony of violence ripped the veil from my consciousness, dragging me back into the harsh reality of existence. For a fleeting moment, the world dissolved into a blur; I could not orient myself within the chamber nor pinpoint the source of the disturbance. Then, the sounds coalesced: the shuffling of heavy boots, the guttural snarls of primal beasts, the metallic clatter of armor, and the ragged, labored breathing of a man in extremis.

Rising to my feet, I cast a hurried glance around the cell where I had just received such a hostile welcome. Along the opposite wall, prisoners and savage brutes lay bound in chains, their eyes fixed upon me with a mixture of sullen rage, startled curiosity, and a flickering spark of hope. That last emotion burned brightest on the face of the young red Martian woman whose warning cry had spared my life. She was the epitome of her race, a being of god-like stature and radiant, copper-toned skin, entirely unadorned by the trappings of status, marking her clearly as a captive or slave in this grim environment.

Seconds ticked by before the noises from beyond the partition registered fully in my mind, and then the truth struck me with the force of a physical blow: Tars Tarkas was engaged in a desperate, life-or-death struggle against wild beasts or savage men.

"Save thy sword, O Mighty Warrior!" the woman cried out, her voice cutting through the tension. "Thou shalt need it more where it will serve a purpose—do not shatter it against senseless metal that yields to the lightest touch of one who knows its secret."

"Do you know the secret?" I demanded, my heart hammering against my ribs.

"Yes," she replied, her gaze steady. "Release me, and I shall grant you entrance to the other chamber of horrors, if you wish. The keys to my fetters lie upon the first fallen foe. But why would you return to face the fierce banths, or whatever monstrosity they have unleashed within that trap?"

"Because my friend fights there alone," I answered, rushing to the carcass of the dead custodian. I found the oval ring of keys and handed them to her.

She quickly selected the master key, unlocking her waist-bound chains, and hurried toward the secret panel. With a needle-thin key, she pried open an almost invisible aperture in the wall. Instantly, the door swung open, and the floor section beneath my feet rotated, carrying me into the chamber where Tars Tarkas battled.

The great Thark stood backed against a corner, a semi-circle of massive monsters crouching before him, waiting for a fatal opening. Their blood-streaked hides and the deep gashes on the green warrior's own body testified to the ferocity of the assault. He was weak, drained by exertion and blood loss, leaning heavily against the wall for support, yet his indomitable courage held firm. He embodied the ancient proverb of his people: "Leave to a Thark his head and one hand, and he may yet conquer."

As I entered, a grim smile touched his lips, though I could not discern if it signaled relief or amusement at my own battered state. Before I could leap into the fray with my longsword, a gentle hand rested on my shoulder. Turning, I found the young woman had followed me, defenseless and unarmed.

"Wait," she whispered urgently. "Leave them to me."

Pushing past me, she advanced upon the snarling banths. At close range, she spoke a single Martian word in low, peremptory tones. Like lightning, the beasts wheeled upon her. I braced myself to see her torn apart, but instead, the creatures slunk to her feet like puppies awaiting a whip. She spoke again, words too low for me to hear, and then turned, leading the six mighty monsters through the secret panel into the room beyond. One by one, she sent them away, leaving us in wide-eyed amazement before she passed through herself.

For a moment, silence reigned. Then Tars Tarkas spoke.

"I heard the fighting beyond the partition, but I did not fear for you, John Carter, until I heard the report of a revolver shot. I knew no man on all Barsoom could face you with naked steel and survive, but the shot stripped the last vestige of hope from me, for I knew you possessed no firearms. Tell me of it."

I recounted the tale, and together we sought the secret panel at the opposite end of the room—the one that might lead to the outside world. To our disappointment, the lock remained stubbornly sealed. The fact that the prisoners were securely chained suggested an avenue of escape must exist, yet we turned from door to door, baffled by the golden panels that refused to yield.

Just as despair began to settle, one of the panels turned silently toward us. The young woman who had led the banths away stood once more beside us.

"Who are you?" she asked, her eyes searching mine. "And what is your mission, that you dare attempt to escape from the Valley Dor and the death you have chosen?"

"I have chosen no death, maiden," I replied. "I am not of Barsoom, nor have I taken the voluntary pilgrimage upon the River Iss. My friend here is the Jeddak of all the Tharks, and though he has not yet expressed a desire to return to the living world, I am taking him with me from the living lie that hath lured him to this frightful place. I am of another world. I am John Carter, Prince of the House of Tardos Mors, Jeddak of Helium. Perchance some faint rumor of me may have leaked within the confines of your hellish abode."

She smiled, a expression of recognition.

"Yes," she replied. "Naught that passes in the world we have left is unknown here. I have heard of you, many years ago. The therns have oft-times wondered whither you had flown, since you had neither taken the pilgrimage, nor could be found upon the face of Barsoom."

"Tell me," I said, "and who be you, and why a prisoner, yet with power over the ferocious beasts of the place that denotes familiarity and authority far beyond that which might be expected of a captive?"

"Slave I am," she answered, her voice trembling slightly. "For fifteen years a slave in this terrible place, and now that they have tired of me and become fearful of the power which my knowledge of their ways has given me, I am but recently condemned to die."

She shuddered visibly.

"What death?" I asked.

"The Holy Therns eat human flesh," she answered. "But only that which has died beneath the sucking lips of a plant man—flesh from which the defiling blood of life has been drawn. And to this cruel end I have been condemned. It was to be within a few hours, had your advent not caused an interruption of their plans."

"Was it then Holy Therns who felt the weight of John Carter's hand?" I asked.

"Oh, no; those whom you laid low are lesser therns; but of the same cruel and hateful race. The Holy Therns abide upon the outer slopes of these grim hills, facing the broad world from which they harvest their victims and their spoils. Labyrinthine passages connect these caves with the luxurious palaces of the Holy Therns, and through them pass upon their many duties the lesser therns, and hordes of slaves, and prisoners, and fierce beasts; the grim inhabitants of this sunless world.

"There be within this vast network of winding passages and countless chambers men, women, and beasts who, born within its dim and gruesome underworld, have never seen the light of day—nor ever shall. They are kept to do the bidding of the race of therns; to furnish at once their sport and their sustenance.

"Now and again some hapless pilgrim, drifting out upon the silent sea from the cold Iss, escapes the plant men and the great white apes that guard the Temple of Issus and falls into the remorseless clutches of the therns; or, as was my misfortune, is coveted by the Holy Thern who chances to be upon watch in the balcony above the river where it issues from the bowels of the mountains through the cliffs of gold to empty into the Lost Sea of Korus.

"All who reach the Valley Dor are, by custom, the rightful prey of the plant men and the apes, while their arms and ornaments become the portion of the therns; but if one escapes the terrible denizens of the valley for even a few hours the therns may claim such a one as their own. And again the Holy Thern on watch, should he see a victim he covets, often tramples upon the rights of the unreasoning brutes of the valley and takes his prize by foul means if he cannot gain it by fair.

"It is said that occasionally some deluded victim of Barsoomian superstition will so far escape the clutches of the countless enemies that beset his path from the moment that he emerges from the subterranean passage through which the Iss flows for a thousand miles before it enters the Valley Dor as to reach the very walls of the Temple of Issus; but what fate awaits one there not even the Holy Therns may guess, for who has passed within those gilded walls never has returned to unfold the mysteries they have held since the beginning of time.

"The Temple of Issus is to the therns what the Valley Dor is imagined by the peoples of the outer world to be to them; it is the ultimate haven of peace, refuge, and happiness to which they pass after this life and wherein an eternity of eternities is spent amidst the delights of the flesh which appeal most strongly to this race of mental giants and moral pygmies."

"The Temple of Issus is, I take it, a heaven within a heaven," I said. "Let us hope that there it will be meted to the therns as they have meted it here unto others."

"Who knows?" the girl murmured.

"The therns, I judge from what you have said, are no less mortal than we; and yet have I always heard them spoken of with the utmost awe and reverence by the people of Barsoom, as one might speak of the gods themselves."

"The therns are mortal," she replied. "They die from the same causes as you or I might: those who do not live their allotted span of life, one thousand years, when by the authority of custom they may take their way in happiness through the long tunnel that leads to Issus. Those who die before are supposed to spend the balance of their allotted time in the image of a plant man, and it is for this reason that the plant men are held sacred by the therns, since they believe that each of these hideous creatures was formerly a thern.

"And should a plant man die?" I asked.

"Should he die before the expiration of the thousand years from the birth of the thern whose immortality abides within him then the soul passes into a great white ape, but should the ape die short of the exact hour that terminates the thousand years the soul is for ever lost and passes for all eternity into the carcass of the slimy and fearsome silians whose wriggling thousands seethe the silent sea beneath the hurtling moons when the sun has gone and strange shapes walk through the Valley Dor."

"We sent several Holy Therns to the silians to-day," said Tars Tarkas, laughing.

"And so will your death be the more terrible when it comes," said the maiden. "And come it will—you cannot escape."

"One has escaped, centuries ago," I reminded her, "and what has been done may be done again."

"It is useless even to try," she answered hopelessly.

"But try we shall," I cried, "and you shall go with us, if you wish."

"To be put to death by mine own people, and render my memory a disgrace to my family and my nation? A Prince of the House of Tardos Mors should know better than to suggest such a thing."

Tars Tarkas listened in silence, but I could feel his eyes riveted upon me, waiting for my answer as one might listen to the reading of a death sentence. What I advised the girl to do would seal our fate as well, since if I bowed to the inevitable decree of age-old superstition, we must all remain and meet our fate in some horrible form within this awful abode of horror and cruelty.

"We have the right to escape if we can," I answered. "Our own moral senses will not be offended if we succeed, for we know that the fabled life of love and peace in the blessed Valley of Dor is a rank and wicked deception. We know that the valley is not sacred; we know that the Holy Therns are not holy; that they are a race of cruel and heartless mortals, knowing no more of the real life to come than we do.

"Not only is it our right to bend every effort to escape—it is a solemn duty from which we should not shrink even though we know that we should be reviled and tortured by our own peoples when we returned to them. Only thus may we carry the truth to those without, and though the likelihood of our narrative being given credence is, I grant you, remote, so wedded are mortals to their stupid infatuation for impossible superstitions, we should be craven cowards indeed were we to shirk the plain duty which confronts us.

"Again there is a chance that with the weight of the testimony of several of us the truth of our statements may be accepted, and at least a compromise effected which will result in the dispatching of an expedition of investigation to this hideous mockery of heaven."

Both the girl and the green warrior stood silent in thought for some moments. The former it was who eventually broke the silence.

"Never had I considered the matter in that light before," she said. "Indeed would I give my life a thousand times if I could but save a single soul from the awful life that I have led in this cruel place. Yes, you are right, and I will go with you as far as we can go; but I doubt that we ever shall escape."

I turned an inquiring glance toward the Thark.

The darkness of the storeroom had held me for hours, a heavy, suffocating blanket that seemed to stretch endlessly. I awoke not to the gentle light of dawn, but to the jagged, staccato rhythm of gunfire echoing through the subterranean veins of the city. The sound was deafening, a chaotic symphony of violence that shattered the stillness.

Before my mind could fully orient itself to the nightmare unfolding around me, I was already on my feet. Through the haze of confusion, I saw the scene: a dozen lesser therns stood blocking the doorway, their wicked rifles lowered but ready, their faces twisted in a mask of panic and chagrin. Scattered across the floor lay the lifeless forms of my companions, save for Thuvia, Tars Tarkas, and myself, who had found refuge in our slumber.

"Master of the Tenth Cycle!" one of the therns cried out, his voice trembling. "Have mercy! Is Sator Throg to be murdered by his own vassals?"

The men edged backward, desperate to flee the presence of the mighty one they had just failed.

"What means this?" I roared, my voice cutting through the tension like a blade. "What is your mission here?"

"Two fugitives from the outer world are loose within your dominions," the thern stammered, casting a fearful glance toward Tars Tarkas. "We hunted them by the Father of Therns' command. One was white with black hair; the other, a huge green warrior."

Thuvia stepped forward, her voice steady despite the chaos. "Here stands the green warrior. And if you examine the corpse by the door, you will recognize the other. We were tasked with capturing them to secure our liberty for Sator Throg. Instead, your incompetence has resulted in the slaughter of my friends and nearly cost Sator Throg his life."

The therns looked sheepish, their bravado evaporating under the weight of their failure.

"Remove the bodies," I commanded, my voice devoid of emotion. "Throw them to the plant men and return to your quarters."

As the men dragged the corpses away, I noticed a subtle shift in the demeanor of the one carrying Sator Throg's body. As he lifted the dead man, his gaze lingered on the upturned face, then darted furtively toward me. A suspicion, sharp and dangerous, flickered in his eyes—a silent accusation he dared not voice. As he bore the body out, a cunning smile of triumph briefly graced his lips before he vanished into the shadows.

Only Tars Tarkas, Thuvia, and I remained. Our chance for freedom had been snatched away by the fatal marksmanship of our captors.

"The outcome is sealed," Thuvia whispered, her eyes scanning the corridor. "Even if this fool dares not accuse you openly, those above hold the power to demand a closer scrutiny. That, Prince, would be our death sentence."

I shrugged, the weight of despair settling upon my shoulders. My wounds throbbed, a constant reminder of the blood I had lost, and the lack of medicinal aid made every step a trial. I longed for the miraculous salves of the green Martian women, which could have restored me in an hour. Hopelessness, a feeling I had never known, now gripped my heart.

Then, a stray draft caught the long, yellow locks of the Holy Thern, Matai Shang, blowing them across my face. Might they still offer a path? If we acted swiftly, could we escape before the general alarm tore the fortress apart?

"How long before they return?" I asked.

"He will go straight to Matai Shang," Thuvia replied. "As a thorian, he will not wait long for an audience. If the Father of Therns believes his tale, the galleries and courts will be filled with searchers within the hour. We must move now."

"Where is the way out?"

"Up the cliffs, through the gardens, across the temples to the outer court," she said, her voice grim. "But the ramparts are impregnable. Since time immemorial, the therns have added stone to stone, building a fortress where a million warriors stand ready. None can pass undetected."

"Then we face it," I said.

"Night offers a slight advantage," Tars Tarkas suggested. "Fewer guards abroad."

"It is nearly sundown," Thuvia noted. "The sun set two hours ago. Come, let us verify."

She led us through winding passages until we reached an opening overlooking the Valley Dor. Below, the sun dipped beneath the western range of Otz, a massive red orb vanishing into the horizon. On Mars, there is no twilight; the transition from day to night is abrupt, plunging the world from brilliant light into utter darkness as if a single lamp were extinguished.

Beneath us, the Holy Thern stood watch on his balcony, his scarlet robes pulled tight against the coming cold. Below the trees, herds of plant men fed, their talons pruning the leaves, revealing the true nature of their civilization. Further down, a canoe carried a dozen doomed souls from the outer world to the river's edge. As the eyes of the herald on the balcony fell upon them, the shrill, weird wail of the demons echoed through the valley. The plant men poured from the grove, their huge, repulsive shapes covering the victims, their sucking mouths fastening onto the flesh.

"The great white apes will finish the meal," Thuvia observed as the monstrous beasts ran across the valley toward the river. "Darkness falls now."

She led us upward, through the maze of subterranean corridors. Twice, great banths blocked our path, but Thuvia's low command silenced their snarls, causing the beasts to slink away.

"You command these fierce brutes as easily as you breathe," I remarked, smiling at her. "How do you do it?"

"I do not know," she admitted, laughing and shuddering. "Years ago, I angered Sator Throg and was thrown into a pit of banths. Instead of killing me, they fawned at my feet. I know them all by name; they are the scavengers of this underworld."

An idea struck me. "Why not release a number of banths ahead of us to draw attention?"

"It would distract them," she agreed.

She began to sing a low, singsong tune, half-purr, and soon soft, padded footsteps followed us. Great green eyes shone in the shadows, and a sinuous, tawny form crept from a diverging tunnel. One by one, the ferocious creatures answered her call. Soon, we were surrounded by fifty brutes, walking like well-schooled guards, their lathering jowls and hungry eyes fixed on Tars Tarkas and myself. It was a strange procession: naked human feet and padded paws moving in unison, golden walls splashed with jewels, dim radium light, and the massive, maned beasts crowding around us. Leading the way was Thuvia, beautiful and commanding.

We entered a great chamber filled with the grotesque hybrids of the underworld—offspring of prisoners and Martians, deformed, maimed, and mostly sightless. They sprawled on the floor like corpses from Dante's Inferno, a veritable hell of lost souls. The banths sniffed hungrily at the defenseless prey, but we threaded our way through, passing other chambers filled with chained prisoners and beasts.

"Why are there no therns here?" I asked.

"They fear the banths," Thuvia explained. "They seldom traverse the underworld at night. By day, the corridors are alive with guards and slaves, but tonight, it is a hunting ground for the beasts."

We reached the upper galleries safely, emerging at the foot of a steep ascent leading to the inner gardens.

"Above us lies the gateway to the outer ramparts," Thuvia said. "Four miles of danger await us. Guards patrol every inch."

"Why such a massive force for a fortress so hidden by superstition?" I questioned.

"They fear the black pirates of Barsoom," she answered solemnly.

The door swung open, and the scent of growing things filled my nostrils. The banths rushed past us, swarming across the gardens beneath the lurid light of the nearer moon. Suddenly, a cry of alarm erupted from the temple roofs, a warning that raced east and west, sounding like a distant echo.

Tars Tarkas drew his long-sword, and Thuvia shrank back to my side as the fortress prepared for war.

"What is it?" I asked the girl.

She pointed upward.

I looked, and there, hovering above the temple, the court, and the garden, were shadowy shapes darting through the thin air. Almost instantly, flashes of light erupted from these strange objects, followed by the roar of musketry and answering volleys from the ramparts.

"The Black Pirates of Barsoom, O Prince," Thuvia said.

In widening circles, the marauders' aircraft swept lower, descending upon the defenders of the Therns. Volley after volley rained down from the sky, crashing through the rarefied atmosphere toward the fleeting fliers. As the pirates drew nearer to the ground, Thern soldiers poured from the sanctuaries into the open gardens and courts. The sight of them in the open drew a swarm of enemy craft darting toward us from every direction.

The Therns fired through shields attached to their rifles, but the grim, black machines pressed on, steadily closing the distance. Most were small, two- or three-man fighters, though a few larger vessels remained aloft, dropping bombs from their keel batteries onto the temples.

Finally, responding to a command signal, the pirates in our immediate vicinity dashed recklessly to the ground, landing directly amidst the Thern soldiery. Scarcely waiting for their craft to settle, the crews leaped among the defenders with the fury of demons. Such a battle! I had never witnessed its like. I had thought the Green Martians the most ferocious warriors in the universe, yet the awful abandon with which these Black Pirates threw themselves upon their foes transcended everything I had ever seen.

Beneath the brilliant light of Mars' two moons, the scene unfolded in vivid detail: golden-haired, white-skinned Therns battling with desperate courage against their ebony-skinned enemies. Here, a knot of warriors trampled a bed of gorgeous pimalia; there, a curved black sword found the heart of a Thern, leaving the dead foe at the foot of a ruby statue; yonder, a dozen Therns pressed a single pirate back onto a bench of emerald, its surface traced with inlaid diamond designs.

A little to one side stood Thuvia, the Thark, and I. The tide of battle had not yet reached us, but the fighters swung close enough for us to observe them clearly.

The Black Pirates fascinated me. I had heard vague rumors during my former life on Mars, little more than legends, but never had I seen one or spoken with a survivor. Popular belief held that they inhabited the lesser moon, descending upon Barsoom at long intervals to commit horrible atrocities. When they departed, they took firearms, ammunition, and young girls as prisoners. Rumor claimed these captives were sacrificed to a terrible god in an orgy ending with their consumption.

I had an excellent opportunity to examine them as the strife occasionally brought individuals close to where I stood. They were large men, likely over six feet tall, with clear-cut, handsome features. Their eyes were well-set and large, though a slight narrowness lent them a crafty appearance. By the moonlight, I could determine that their irises were of extreme blackness, while the eyeballs themselves were stark white and clear. Physically, their structure seemed identical to the Therns, the Red Men, and my own; only the color of their skin differed materially, appearing as polished ebony. Odd as it may seem for a Southerner to say, this dark hue added to, rather than detracted from, their marvelous beauty.

Yet, if their bodies were divine, their hearts appeared quite the reverse. Never did I witness such a malign lust for blood as these demons of the outer air displayed in their mad battle with the Therns.

All about us in the garden lay their sinister craft, which the Therns, for reasons unaccountable to me at the time, made no effort to destroy. Now and again, a Black warrior would rush from a nearby temple carrying a young woman in his arms. He would leap straight for his flier while comrades nearby rushed to cover his escape. On the Therns' side, warriors hastened to rescue the girl, and in an instant, both parties were swallowed in the vortex of a maelstrom of yelling devils, hacking and hewing at one another like incarnate fiends.

But always, it seemed, the Black Pirates of Barsoom emerged victorious. The girl, brought miraculously unharmed through the conflict, was borne away into the outer darkness upon the deck of a swift flier.

Similar fighting echoed in both directions as far as sound carried. Thuvia told me that these attacks were usually made simultaneously along the entire ribbon-like domain of the Therns, which circles the Valley Dor on the outer slopes of the Mountains of Otz.

As the fighting receded momentarily, Thuvia turned to me.

"Do you understand now, O Prince," she said, "why a million warriors guard the domains of the Holy Therns by day and by night?"

"The scene you witness is but a repetition of what I have seen enacted a score of times during the fifteen years I have been a prisoner here. From time immemorial, the Black Pirates of Barsoom have preyed upon the Holy Therns. Yet they never carry their expeditions to a point where the extermination of the Thern race is threatened. It is as though they utilize the race merely as playthings, satisfying their ferocious lust for fighting, and collecting toll in arms, ammunition, and prisoners."

"Why don't they destroy these fliers?" I asked. "That would soon stop the attacks, or at least make the Blacks less bold. See how perfectly unguarded they leave their craft, as though they were lying safe in their own hangars."

"The Therns do not dare. They tried it once, ages ago, but the next night and for a whole moon thereafter, a thousand great black battleships circled the Mountains of Otz, pouring tons of projectiles upon the temples, gardens, and courts, until every Thern who was not killed was driven for safety into the subterranean galleries. The Therns know that they live only by the sufferance of the Black Men. They were near to extermination that once and will not venture risking it again."

As she ceased speaking, a new element entered the conflict, coming from a source equally unexpected by Thern or pirate. The great Banths we had liberated in the garden had evidently been awed initially by the sound of battle, the yelling of warriors, and the loud reports of rifles and bombs. But now, angered by the continuous noise and excited by the smell of fresh blood, a great form shot from a clump of low shrubbery into the midst of the struggling humanity. A horrid scream of bestial rage broke from the Banth as he felt warm flesh beneath his powerful talons.

As though his cry were a signal, the entire pack hurled themselves among the fighters. Panic reigned instantly. Thern and Black Man turned alike against the common enemy, for the Banths showed no partiality. The awful beasts bore down a hundred men by the sheer weight of their great bodies as they hurled themselves into the thick of the fight. Leaping and clawing, they mowed down the warriors with their powerful paws, turning instantly to rend their victims with frightful fangs.

The scene was fascinating in its terribleness, but suddenly it occurred to me that we were wasting valuable time watching this conflict, which might prove a means of our escape. The Therns were so engaged with their terrible assailants that now, if ever, escape should be comparatively easy. I turned to search for an opening through the contending hordes. If we could reach the ramparts, perhaps the pirates had thinned the guarding forces enough to leave a way open to the world without.

As my eyes wandered over the garden, the sight of hundreds of aircraft lying unguarded around us suggested the simplest avenue to freedom. Why had it not occurred to me before? I was thoroughly familiar with the mechanism of every known make of flier on Barsoom. For nine years, I had sailed and fought with the navy of Helium. I had raced through space on a tiny one-man air scout and commanded the greatest battleship that ever floated in the thin air of dying Mars.

To think is to act. Grasping Thuvia by the arm, I whispered to Tars Tarkas to follow me. Quickly we glided toward a small flier lying furthest from the battling warriors. "Dog!" she hissed, the sound vibrating with venom. "Blasphemer! Do you think Phaidor, daughter of Matai Shang, begs for mercy? She commands! Your petty passion for that vile creature from your other life is nothing to her. Phaidor has glorified you with her love, and you have spurned her. Ten thousand deaths could not atone for this insult. The thing you call Dejah Thoris shall die the most horrible death imaginable. You have signed her warrant.

"And you! You shall become the meanest slave in the service of the goddess you sought to humiliate. Torture and ignominy shall pile upon you until you grovel at my feet begging for death. But in my gracious generosity, I shall grant your prayer. From the high balcony of the Golden Cliffs, I shall watch the great white apes tear you asunder."

She had orchestrated the entire tragedy with terrifying precision. It stunned me to see such divine beauty harbor such fiendish vindictiveness. Yet, I noticed a flaw in her revenge: she had forgotten her surroundings. Pointing to the nearest porthole, I drew her attention to the dark, swirling waters outside.

The reality crashed down upon her instantly. A single glance at the abyss sent the proud goddess crumbling onto a low bench, burying her face in her arms and sobbing like a frightened child rather than an all-powerful deity.

As we sank deeper, the heavy glass of the portholes grew warm from the external heat. We descended far beneath the Martian crust until our descent halted. The propellers churned the water at our stern, driving us forward through a narrow, rock-lined tunnel illuminated by our lights and a powerful searchbeam from the submarine's nose.

Soon, the engines died, and we began our swift ascent. Light flooded in from above until we stopped. Xodar entered the cabin with his men.

"Come," he ordered, and we followed him through the hatchway.

We emerged into a subterranean vault where our submarine floated in a central pool, its black hull visible above the water. Around the pool lay a platform, and beyond, the cave walls rose perpendicularly to arch toward a low ceiling. Our captors led us through dim passageways to a steel cage at the bottom of a shaft stretching endlessly upward.

It was a standard elevator car, operated by massive magnets suspended at the shaft's apex. On Mars, where gravity is weak, the ascent was a sickening rush of speed. As soon as the doors closed, we shot upward, stopping abruptly at the landing above.

Stepping out, we found ourselves in a fairyland of impossible beauty. Words from Earth failed to capture the splendor: scarlet grass, ivory trees blooming with purple fire, walks paved with crushed rubies, emeralds, turquoise, and diamonds. A magnificent temple of burnished gold stood amidst a landscape of colors unknown to human eyes. Even my eyes, hardened by the barbaric splendors of a Martian Jeddak's court, were awestruck.

"The Temple of Issus," Phaidor whispered, her eyes wide with awe.

Xodar watched us with a grim smile, mixing amusement with malicious glee. The gardens teemed with trapped black men and women, attended by red and white females serving their every need. Princesses and goddesses from the outer world had been stolen to serve as slaves for the blacks.

Moving toward the temple, we were halted by armed guards. After Xodar spoke with an officer, we were ushered inside. When they returned, they announced that Issus wished to see the daughter of Matai Shang and the strange creature from another world, a former Prince of Helium.

We traversed endless corridors of unimaginable beauty, passing magnificent apartments and noble halls, until we reached a spacious chamber. An officer signaled, and a richly trapped courtier emerged. We were ordered to crawl on our hands and knees, backs turned to the room we were entering, warned that turning our heads meant instant death.

Never had I been in such a humiliating position. Only my love for Dejah Thoris and the faint hope of seeing her again kept me from rising to face the Goddess of the First Born like a gentleman. After crawling hundreds of feet, we were stopped.

"Let them rise," a thin, wavering voice commanded from behind us. "But do not face Issus."

"The woman pleases me," the voice continued after a pause. "She shall serve me for the allotted time. The man you may return to the Isle of Shador. Let the woman turn and look upon Issus, knowing that those of the lower orders who gaze upon the holy vision of her radiant face survive the blinding glory but a single year."

I watched Phaidor. She paled to a ghastly hue. Slowly, irresistibly drawn, she turned to face the Goddess of Life Eternal. I could not see her face, but I felt the shudder that racked her trembling arm as it brushed against mine.

"It must be dazzling loveliness indeed," I thought, "to move the breast of such radiant beauty."

"Let the woman remain. Remove the man. Go," Issus commanded.

The officer's hand fell on my shoulder. Dropping to my knees, I crawled out of the Presence. It was my first audience with a deity, and I confess I was not greatly impressed, save for the ridiculous figure I cut scrambling on my marrow bones.

Outside, the doors closed. Xodar joined me as we retraced our steps to the gardens.

"You spared my life when you easily might have taken it," he said quietly. "I would aid you if I could. Your fate is inevitable; you may never return to the outer world."

"What will be my fate?" I asked.

"That depends on Issus. If she does not summon you, you may live in mild bondage for years."

"Why would she summon me?"

"She uses the lower orders for amusement. A fighter like you would provide fine sport in the temple's monthly rites, pitted against men or beasts for her edification and to replenish her larder."

"She eats human flesh?" I asked, not in horror, but with the resigned acceptance of one who knew the Holy Therns. They were a people drunk on power, viewing other Martians as beasts of the field. Why should they not eat the flesh of the lower orders they deemed beneath understanding?

"She eats only the flesh of the best-bred Holy Therns and red Barsoomians. The rest goes to our boards. The animals are eaten by the slaves. She also eats other dainties."

I did not grasp the significance of "other dainties" then, thinking the limits of ghoulishness had already been reached. I had much to learn about the depths of cruelty to which omnipotence can drag its possessor.

As we neared the gardens, an officer overtook us. "Issus wishes to see this man again," he announced. "The girl told her he is of wondrous beauty and such prowess that he slew seven of the First Born and bound Dator Xodar with his own bare hands."

Xodar looked uncomfortable, clearly displeased that Issus knew of his defeat. Without a word, he turned, and we followed the officer back to the audience chamber.

The ceremony of entrance repeated itself. Issus bid me rise. For several minutes, silence hung like a tomb as the deity appraised me. Then, the thin, wavering voice broke the stillness, droning the ancient words of doom.

"Let the man turn and look upon Issus, knowing that those of the lower orders who gaze upon the holy vision of her radiant face survive the blinding glory but a single year."

I turned, expecting the revelation of divine glory. Instead, I saw a solid phalanx of armed men blocking my view of a dais supporting a great bench of carved sorapus wood. Squatting upon it was a female black, evidently very old. Her wrinkled skull was bald, save for two yellow fangs; she was entirely toothless. Emaciated limbs attached to a torso that seemed mostly distorted abdomen completed the "holy vision of her radiant beauty."

Surrounding her were female slaves, including Phaidor, white and trembling.

"This is the man who slew seven of the First Born and bound Dator Xodar?" Issus asked.

"Most glorious vision of divine loveliness, it is," the officer replied sycophantically.

"Produce Dator Xodar," she commanded.

Xodar was brought from the adjoining room. Issus glared at him with baleful light in her hideous eyes.

"And such as you are a Dator of the First Born?" she squealed. "For the disgrace you have brought upon the Immortal Race, you shall be degraded to a rank below the lowest. No longer be you a Dator, but forever a slave of slaves, fetching and carrying for the lower orders in the gardens of Issus. Remove his harness. Cowards and slaves wear no trappings."

Xodar stood stiffly erect. Not a muscle twitched, nor a tremor shook his giant frame as a guard roughly stripped his gorgeous trappings away.

"Begone!" screamed the infuriated little old woman. "Begone, but instead of the light of the gardens of Issus, let you serve as a slave of this slave who conquered you in the prison on the Isle of Shador. Take him away out of the sight of my divine eyes."

Slowly, with his head held high, the proud Xodar stalked from the chamber. Issus rose and turned to leave. Turning to me, she said, "You shall be returned to Shador for the present. As I started to follow my guard toward the gardens, the girl ran after me.

"Oh, do not leave me in this terrible place," she begged. "Forgive the things I said to you, my Prince. I did not mean them. Only take me away with you. Let me share your imprisonment on Shador." Her words tumbled out in an almost incoherent volley. "You did not understand the honor I did you. Among the Therns, there is no marriage as among the lower orders. We might have lived together forever in love and happiness. We have both looked upon Issus, and in a year we die. Let us live that year together in whatever measure of joy remains for the doomed."

"If it was difficult for me to understand you, Phaidor," I replied, "can you not understand that it may be equally difficult for you to comprehend the motives and social laws that guide me? I do not wish to hurt you, nor undervalue the honor you have shown, but what you desire may not be possible. While I live, my heart beats for but one woman—the incomparable Dejah Thoris, Princess of Helium. When death overtakes me, my heart shall cease; but what comes after that, I know not. And in that, I am as wise as Matai Shang, Master of Life and Death upon Barsoom; or Issus, Goddess of Life Eternal."

Phaidor stared at me intently. No anger remained in her eyes, only a pathetic expression of hopeless sorrow.

"I do not understand," she said, and turning, she walked slowly toward the door through which Issus had passed. A moment later, she was gone from my sight.

The outer gardens, where the guard now led me, became a theater of barbaric outrage. Xodar stood surrounded by a mob of noble blacks, their faces twisted in revulsion. Men slapped his face with open palms; women spat upon his robes, treating the fallen Dator as refuse.

"Ah!" cried one, his voice dripping with mockery. "So this is the creature who overcame the great Xodar bare-handed. Let us see how it was done."

"Bind Thurid," suggested a beautiful woman, her laughter sharp as broken glass. "Thurid is a noble Dator. Let Thurid show the dog what it means to face a real man."

"Yes, Thurid! Thurid!" a dozen voices roared in unison.

Another pointed. "Here he is now."

I turned to see a massive black figure, weighed down with resplendent ornaments and heavy arms, advancing with a bearing of arrogant nobility.

"What now?" he barked. "What would you of Thurid?"

A dozen voices explained the demand instantly.

Thurid turned toward Xodar, his eyes narrowing into nasty, predatory slits.

"Calot!" he hissed, the venom dripping from his tongue. "Ever did I think you carried the heart of a sorak in your putrid breast. My blood boiled. Minutes had passed since I had witnessed the cowardly treatment meted out to this once-powerful comrade because he had fallen from the favor of Issus. I stood close beside Xodar as Thurid swung his foot for the cowardly kick. The degraded Dator stood erect and motionless as a carved image, prepared to take whatever insults his former comrades offered in manly silence and stoicism.

But as Thurid's foot swung, so did mine. I caught him a painful blow upon the shin bone that saved Xodar from this added ignominy.

For a moment, there was tense silence. Then Thurid, with a roar of rage, sprang for my throat—just as he had upon the deck of the cruiser. The result was identical. I ducked beneath his outstretched arms, and as he lunged past me, I planted a terrific right on the side of his jaw.

The big fellow spun around like a top, his knees gave beneath him, and he crumpled to the ground at my feet.

"You asked me to bind Thurid," I cried. "Behold!"

Take this man to your goddess, as I have said, and tell her, too, that as I have done to Xodar and Thurid, so also can I do to the mightiest of her Dators. There was little disrespect in the tone the man used addressing either Xodar or myself. It was evident he felt less contempt for the former Dator since he had witnessed the ease with which I disposed of the powerful Thurid. His respect for me, however, was greater than it should have been for a slave; during the balance of the return journey, he walked or stood always behind me, a drawn short-sword in his hand.

The return to the Sea of Omean was uneventful. We dropped down the awful shaft in the same car that had brought us to the surface. Then Tars Tarkas had been with me, but now I was utterly alone in so far as friendly companionship was concerned. Even should they by some miracle have escaped and been received and spared by a friendly nation, what hope had I of the succor which I knew they would gladly extend if it lay in their power?

They could not guess my whereabouts or my fate, for none on all Barsoom even dream of such a place as this. Nor would it have advantaged me any had they known the exact location of my prison, for who could hope to penetrate to this buried sea in the face of the mighty navy of the First Born? Well, I would make the best of it. Rising, I swept aside the brooding despair that had been endeavoring to claim me. With the idea of exploring my prison, I started to look around.

Xodar sat, with bowed head, upon a low stone bench near the center of the room in which we were. He had not spoken since Issus had degraded him.

The building was roofless, the walls rising to a height of about thirty feet. Half-way up were a couple of small, heavily barred windows. The prison was divided into several rooms by partitions twenty feet high. There was no one in the room which we occupied, but two doors which led to other rooms were opened. I entered one of these rooms, but found it vacant. Thus I continued through several of the chambers until in the last one I found a young red Martian boy sleeping upon the stone bench which constituted the only furniture of any of the prison cells.

Evidently he was the only other prisoner. As he slept, I leaned over and looked at him. There was something strangely familiar about his face, and yet I could not place him.

His features were very regular and, like the proportions of his graceful limbs and body, beautiful in the extreme. He was very light in color for a red man, but in other respects he seemed a typical specimen of this handsome race.

I did not awaken him, for sleep in prison is such a priceless boon that I have seen men transformed into raging brutes when robbed by one of their fellow-prisoners of a few precious moments of it.

Returning to my own cell, I found Xodar still sitting in the same position in which I had left him.

"Man," I cried, "it will profit you nothing to mope thus. It were no disgrace to be bested by John Carter. You have seen that in the ease with which I accounted for Thurid. You knew it before when on the cruiser's deck you saw me slay three of your comrades."

He looked at me in amazement.

"The curse of Issus will fall upon you," he cried. "In another instant you will be smitten down, writhing to your death in horrible agony."

Dawn broke cold and grey over the prison of Shador, casting long, skeletal shadows across the stone floor where Xodar and I began the arduous work of plotting our escape. With the crude tools at our disposal—a buckled strap from my harness and the razor-sharp edge of the gem stolen from Sator Throg—I compelled Xodar to sketch the southern polar regions. From these rough maps, I calculated the trajectory to Helium and the distance to the opening leading to Omean. Next, he drew the layout of Omean itself, marking the precise location of Shador and the breach in the dome that offered a path to the outside world. I studied these diagrams until they burned themselves into my memory, while Xodar recounted the rhythms of the guards: solitary sentries pacing a slow, ten-minute circuit, leaving five minutes of vulnerability on either side of the prison.

"Valuable knowledge," Xodar observed, "but it means nothing until we are free."

"We shall be free," I assured him, a grin tugging at my lips. "Leave that to me."

"When?" he asked.

"The first night a small craft is moored near the shore," I replied.

"How will you know?" he countered. "The windows are too high."

"Not so," I said, springing to the bars. Through the grating, I saw the truth: several small vessels and two massive battleships lay idling mere yards from the prison walls. "Tonight," I decided aloud.

Before I could speak the word, the cell door burst open. A guard stepped inside, his back to me. Panic flared, but I saw my only chance: a partition wall five feet above my head separated our cell from the next. As the guard demanded, "Where is the white man?" and turned, I scrambled up the iron grating, my metal boot scraping loudly against the stone as I vaulted over. I landed silently in the adjacent cell.

"Where is the white slave?" the guard roared.

"I know not!" Xodar lied smoothly. "He was here when you entered. Go find him."

The guard grumbled, unlocking a door to search the next cell. Once the door clicked shut, I leaped back to my own cell, landing beside a stunned Xodar.

"Do you see now?" I whispered.

"I see how you escape," he admitted, "but I remain trapped by these walls."

Soon, the guard returned, his eyes bulging in shock at my sudden reappearance. "Where have you been?" he screamed.

"In prison," I lied, feigning confusion. "Since yesterday. Your eyesight must be failing."

As we walked through the enchanted gardens toward a towering wall, I found the red youth haunting my thoughts. There was a familiarity in his carriage, his speech, his very soul that suggested I knew him, yet I had never seen him before. We were led not to the temple, but to a great amphitheatre half a mile beyond the garden walls. Inside, crowds of blacks mingled with plant-men and great white apes, the beasts treated like pets, shoved aside or struck with swords if they obstructed the nobility.

"What is this assembly?" I asked. "A sacrifice for the First Born?"

"It is the monthly rite," he explained calmly. "Blacks wash their sins in the blood of outsiders. If the captive dies, it proves disloyalty to Issus. If he lives, he is acquitted. Combat varies: against beasts, against warriors, or in groups."

"And victory brings freedom?"

He laughed bitterly. "Freedom? No. Death is our only freedom. Those who enter the domains of the First Born never leave. Fighters are allowed to fight often; the weak simply die sooner."

"My father must have been a mighty warrior," I pressed, sensing a kinship. "Who was he?"

"My father was—"

"Calots!" a guard barked. "To the slaughter!"

We were hustled down a steep incline into the arena, a vast excavation where the fighting floor lay deep below the surface. Directly opposite my cage sat the throne of Issus, surrounded by jeweled slave maidens and ranks of heavily armed soldiers. The amphitheatre was packed with the First Born, a society where nobles did no work, only fought or feasted, while slaves performed every task.

The ceremony began with the Tribute to Issus. Ten beautiful captives, servants of the goddess for a year, were brought into the arena. A huge black inspected them, selecting one to drag away to the kitchens for the goddess's dinner. The horror of it ignited a primal fury within me.

Suddenly, the gate to my cage flew open. Three monstrous white apes charged into the arena, shrieking with bestial frenzy toward the huddled maidens. The blood-red haze of battle swam before my eyes. The guard before my cage did nothing; the bars were unnecessary when the victims were already condemned.

I struck the guard down with a single blow, snatched his long-sword, and leaped into the sand. The apes were upon the girls. The great apes were already dead before the guards could reach me. The red youth raced past them, his speed and agility mirroring my own earthly prowess, and fell upon the guards from the rear.

For half an hour, hell broke loose within the walled confines. Howling, blood-streaked demons clashed in an inextricable mass. Slowly, I drew the prisoners into a rough circle, protecting the doomed maidens at the center. The guards of Issus were decimated, but messengers ran through the stands, and nobles poured into the arena to annihilate us by force of numbers.

I caught a glimpse of Issus on her throne, her face distorted in a grimace of hate and fear. That look spurred me.

"Fifty of you, drop back!" I ordered. "Protect the maidens until I return."

Turning to the outer line, I cried, "Down with Issus! Follow me to the throne!"

The red youth was the first to join the cry. "To the throne! To the throne!" we shouted as one. We became an irresistible fighting mass, mowing down the nobles who tried to stop us.

"To the seats!" I commanded as we reached the barrier. "Ten of us can take the throne!"

Prisoners vaulted the low wall, swords dripping, descending upon the crowded stands. The amphitheatre filled with the shrieks of the dying and the clash of arms. Side by side, the red youth and I fought our way to the foot of the throne. The remaining guards, reinforced by dignitaries, closed in.

Then, a cry rose from the stands: "Rise slaves!"

It swelled to a mighty volume. Across the entire structure, female slaves fell upon their masters. Daggers, swords, and heavy ornaments flashed in the dim light as the women wreaked their long-pent vengeance. The battle cry echoed, a reminder that the war was not yet over.

Only a single thin rank of men stood between us and Issus. Her face was blue with terror, foam flecking her lips. She seemed paralyzed. The red youth and I were the only ones left standing in the arena. Just as my sword was about to strike, a hand clutched the elbow of my adversary, saving me. The youth finished the attacker instantly.

I was saved only because my sword was wedged in the breastbone of a Dator. As the enemy fell, I looked into the eyes of the one who had saved me: Phaidor, daughter of Matai Shang.

"Fly, my Prince!" she cried, her voice trembling. "All within the arena are dead. Only you and this youth remain. Listen! The women are falling. For each of you, there are ten thousand blacks. Break for the open and the sea of Korus. With your sword arm, you may yet reach the Golden Cliffs and the Holy Therns. Tell your story to Matai Shang. Fly while there is a chance!"

But my mission was not flight, nor could I accept the cruel hospitality of the Therns over the tyranny of the First Born.

"Down with Issus!" I shouted.

Together, the boy and I renewed the fight. Two blacks fell before our swords, and we stood face to face with the goddess. As my sword rose to end her, her paralysis broke. With an ear-piercing shriek, she fled. Directly behind her, a black gulf yawned in the dais flooring. She leaped into the abyss, and I followed close at her heels, the red youth stumbling beside me.

Her scattered guard rallied, a mob of religious fanatics enraged by the affront to their goddess. A blow struck the youth's head; he staggered, ready to fall. I caught him in my left arm, turning to face the infuriated mob just as Issus vanished into the dark depths beneath me.

For a heartbeat, I stood frozen as the horde surged forward, but their initial momentum threw me back. My foot sought purchase on the stone but found only void; I had stumbled into the chasm that had swallowed Issus. Toppling onto the precipice, I lost my balance, clutching the boy tightly against my chest, and plummeted into the abyss.

We struck a polished chute, the aperture above sealing itself with supernatural speed, and we shot downward, unscathed, into a dim chamber deep beneath the arena.

As I rose, the first thing that greeted me was the malevolent visage of Issus, glaring through the heavy iron bars of a grated door.

"Rash mortal!" she shrieked. "You shall pay the terrible price for your blasphemy in this secret cell. Here you shall lie alone in darkness with the rotting corpse of your accomplice beside you, until madness and hunger drive you to feast on the maggots that were once a man."

With that, she vanished, and the faint light in the cell dissolved into absolute, Cimmerian blackness.

"Pleasant old lady," a voice murmured at my side.

"Who speaks?" I asked, my heart hammering.

"'Tis I, your companion," the boy replied. "I have the honor of having fought shoulder to shoulder today with the greatest warrior who ever donned metal upon Barsoom."

"I thank the Gods you live," I said, touching the gash on his head. "I feared that nasty cut was fatal."

"It but stunned me," he replied lightly. "A mere scratch."

"Perhaps it were better if it had been final," I muttered. "We seem trapped in a dire situation, likely to perish of thirst and starvation."

"Where are we?"

"Beneath the arena," I answered. "We fell down the shaft that devoured Issus just as she was about to claim us."

He let out a low chuckle of relief, then reached through the darkness to pull my ear close to his lips.

"Nothing could be better," he whispered. "There are secrets hidden within the secrets of Issus that she herself does not comprehend."

"What do you mean?"

"I labored with the other slaves a year ago remodeling these subterranean galleries," he explained. "At that time, we discovered below these chambers an ancient system of corridors sealed for ages. The overseers explored them, taking several of us along. I know the entire network perfectly."

"There are miles of tunnels honeycombing the ground beneath the gardens and the temple," he continued. "And there is one passage leading down to the lower regions that connect to the water shaft giving access to Omean."

"If we can reach the submarine undetected, we may yet make the sea, dotted with islands where the blacks never venture. There, we might survive for a time, and who knows what fate may aid our escape?"

He spoke in a hushed tone, fearing eavesdroppers even here, so I answered in kind.

"Lead back to Shador, my friend," I whispered. "Xodar, the boy, is there. We planned to escape together; I cannot desert him."

"No," the boy insisted. "One cannot desert a friend. It is better to be recaptured ourselves than to leave him behind."

He began to grope across the floor of the dark chamber, searching for the trapdoor leading to the corridors below. Finally, he signaled me with a low "S-s-t," and I crept toward the sound to find him kneeling at the edge of an opening.

"There is a drop of about ten feet," he whispered. "Hang by your hands; you will land safely on a level floor of soft sand."

Quietly, I lowered myself from the inky cell above into the inky pit below. The darkness was so profound we could not see our hands at arm's length. Never before had I known such a total absence of light as existed in the pits of Issus.

For an instant, I hung suspended in mid-air. There is a peculiar sensation attached to such a feat, difficult to describe. When the feet tread empty air and the distance below is shrouded in shadow, a panic grips the soul at the thought of releasing one's hold and plunging into unknown depths.

Though the boy assured me it was but ten feet to the floor, I felt the same thrill as if hanging over a bottomless pit. Then I released my grip and dropped—four feet onto a soft cushion of sand. The boy followed.

"Raise me to your shoulders," he said, "and I will replace the trap."

Once done, he took my hand, leading me very slowly, pausing frequently to ensure we did not stray into wrong passageways. Soon, we began descending a steep incline.

"It will not be long," he said, "before we see light. At the lower levels, we encounter the same stratum of phosphorescent rock that illuminates Omean."

I shall never forget that journey through the pits of Issus. Devoid of dramatic incidents, it was filled with a strange charm of excitement and adventure, hinging principally on the unguessable antiquity of these long-forgotten corridors. The things hidden in the Stygian darkness could not have been half so wonderful as the pictures my imagination conjured, reviving the ancient peoples of this dying world and setting them once more to their labors, intrigues, mysteries, and cruelties as they made their last stand against the swarming hordes of the dead sea bottoms that had driven them step by step to the world's highest pinnacle, where they now hid behind an impenetrable barrier of superstition.

In addition to the green men, there had been three principal races upon Barsoom: the blacks, the whites, and a race of yellow men. As the planet's waters dried and the seas receded, all other resources dwindled until life became a constant battle for survival. The various races had waged war for ages, and the three higher types had easily bested the green savages of the water places. But now, the receding seas forced them to abandon their fortified cities, adopting a nomadic life that separated them into smaller communities, making them vulnerable to the fierce hordes of green men. The result was a partial amalgamation of the blacks, whites, and yellows, producing the splendid race of red men seen today.

I had always believed all traces of the original races had vanished from Mars, yet within the past four days, I had found both whites and blacks in great multitudes. Could it be possible that in some far-off corner of the planet, a remnant of the ancient yellow race still existed?

My reverie was broken by the boy's low exclamation.

"At last, the lighted way!" he cried. Looking up, I beheld a dim radiance ahead.

As we advanced, the light grew until we emerged into well-lit passageways. Our progress became rapid until we suddenly reached the end of a corridor opening directly onto the ledge surrounding the submarine pool.

The craft lay at her moorings with the hatch uncovered. Raising a finger to his lips and tapping his sword significantly, the youth crept noiselessly toward the vessel. I followed close at his heels.

Silently, we dropped to the deserted deck and crawled on hands and knees toward the hatchway. A stealthy glance below revealed no guards, and with the quickness and soundlessness of cats, we dropped together into the main cabin. Even here, there was no sign of life. Quickly, we covered and secured the hatch.

Then the boy stepped into the pilot house, pressed a button, and the boat sank amid swirling waters toward the bottom of the shaft. Still, there was no scurrying of feet as we had expected. While the boy remained to direct the boat, I slid from cabin to cabin in a futile search for any crew member. The craft was entirely deserted. Such good fortune seemed almost unbelievable.

When I returned to the pilot house to report the discovery, the boy handed me a piece of paper.

"This may explain the absence of the crew," he said.

It was a radio-aerial message addressed to the submarine's commander:

"The slaves have risen. Come with what men you have and those you can gather on the way. Too late to get aid from Omean. They are massacring all within the amphitheatre. Issus is threatened. Haste.

"Zithad is Dator of the guards of Issus," the youth explained. "We gave them a bad scare—one they will not soon forget."

"Let us hope it is but the beginning of the end of Issus," I said.

"Only our first ancestor knows," he replied.

We reached the submarine pool in Omean without incident. Here, we debated the wisdom of sinking the craft before leaving, but finally decided it would add nothing to our chances for escape. There were plenty of blacks on Omean to thwart us if we were apprehended; however, many more coming from the temples and gardens of Issus would not decrease our odds.

Now we faced a quandary regarding how to pass the guards patrolling the island around the pool. Finally, I devised a plan.

"What is the name or title of the officer in charge of these guards?" I asked the boy.

"A fellow named Torith was on duty when we entered this morning," he replied.

"Good. And what is the name of the submarine's commander?"

"Yersted."

I found a dispatch blank in the cabin and wrote the following order:

"Dator Torith: Return these two slaves at once to Shador.

"That will be the simpler way to return," I said, smiling as I handed the forged order to the boy. "Come, we shall see now how well it works."

"But our swords!" he exclaimed. "What shall we say to explain them?"

"Since we cannot explain them, we shall have to leave them behind," I replied.

"Is it not the extreme of rashness to thus put ourselves again, unarmed, in the power of the First Born?"

"It is the only way," I answered. "You may trust me to find a way out of the prison of Shador, and I think, once out, that we shall find no great difficulty in arming ourselves once more in a country which abounds so plentifully in armed men."

"As you say," he replied with a smile and shrug. "I could not follow another leader who inspired greater confidence than you. Come, let us put your ruse to the test."

Boldly, we emerged from the hatchway, leaving our swords behind, and strode to the main exit leading to the sentry's post and the office of the Dator of the guard.

At sight of us, the guards sprang forward in surprise and halted us with leveled rifles. I held out the message to one of them. He took it, saw to whom it was addressed, and turned to hand it to Torith, who was emerging from his office to learn the cause of the commotion.

The black read the order, and for a moment eyed us with evident suspicion.

"Where is Dator Yersted?" he asked, and my heart sank as I cursed myself for a fool in not having sunk the submarine to cover the lie I now had to tell.

"His orders were to return immediately to the temple landing," I replied.

Torith took a half-step toward the entrance to the pool as though to corroborate my story. For that instant, everything hung in the balance; had he done so and found the empty submarine still lying at her wharf, the whole weak fabric of my concoction would have collapsed. But evidently, he decided the message must be genuine, nor indeed was there any good reason to doubt it, since it would scarcely have seemed credible to him that two slaves would voluntarily give themselves into custody in such a manner. It was the very boldness of the plan that rendered it successful.

"Were you connected with the rising of the slaves?" Torith asked. "We have just had meager reports of some such event."

"All were involved," I replied. "But it amounted to little. The guards quickly overcame and killed the majority of us."

He seemed satisfied with this reply. "Take them to Shador," he ordered, turning to a subordinate. We entered a small boat lying beside the island, and in a few minutes, we were disembarking upon Shador.

Here, we were returned to our respective cells; I with Xodar, the boy by himself. Behind locked doors, we were once again prisoners of the First Born.

Xodar listened with a mixture of awe and disbelief as I recounted the chaos within the arena during the rites of Issus. Even though he had already voiced his skepticism regarding the goddess's divinity, the image of a mortal threatening her with a drawn sword and surviving unscathed shattered the last vestiges of his superstition.

"It is the final proof," he declared, his voice steady. "Nothing more is required to obliterate my belief in Issus's divinity. She is merely a wicked old woman, wielding immense power for evil through schemes that have kept her own people and all of Barsoom in religious ignorance for ages."

"She remains all-powerful here, however," I replied. "Therefore, it behooves us to depart at the first moment that appears propitious."

"I hope you find such a moment," Xodar said, laughing dryly. "For in all my life, I have never witnessed one in which a prisoner of the First Born might escape."

"To-night will suffice," I answered.

"It will soon be night," Xodar noted. "How may I assist in this venture?"

"Can you swim?" I asked.

"No slimy silian that haunts the depths of Korus is more at home in water than Xodar," he replied confidently.

"Excellent. The red one, however, likely cannot swim," I observed. "There is scarce enough water in their domains to float the tiniest craft. One of us must support him through the sea to our chosen vessel. I had hoped to make the entire journey submerged, but I fear the red youth could not endure the pressure or the terror of deep water; it has been ages since their forebears saw a lake, river, or sea."

"The red one accompanies us?" Xodar asked.

"Yes."

"It is well. Three swords are better than two, especially when the third is as mighty as this fellow's. I have watched him battle in the arena at the rites of Issus many times. Never, until I saw you fight, had I seen one who seemed unconquerable even in the face of great odds. One might mistake you two for master and pupil, or father and son. Recall his face; there is a marked resemblance between you. It is evident when you fight—the same grim smile, the same maddening contempt for your adversary apparent in every movement and expression."

"Be that as it may, Xodar, he is a great fighter. I believe we shall form a trio difficult to overcome. If my friend Tars Tarkas, Jeddak of Thark, were among us, we could fight our way from one end of Barsoom to the other, even if the entire world were pitted against us."

"They will find out from whence you have come," Xodar said. "That is but one of the superstitions Issus has foisted upon a credulous humanity. She works through the Holy Therns, who are as ignorant of her true nature as the Barsoomians of the outer world. Her decrees are borne to the Therns written in blood upon strange parchment. The poor deluded fools believe they receive revelations of a goddess through supernatural agency, finding these messages upon their guarded altars, inaccessible to any but the initiated. I myself have borne these messages for Issus for many years. There is a long tunnel from the temple of Issus to the principal temple of Matai Shang, dug ages ago by the slaves of the First Born in such utter secrecy that no Thern ever guessed its existence."

"The Therns, for their part, maintain temples dotted about the entire civilized world. Here, priests whom the people never see communicate the doctrine of the Mysterious River Iss, the Valley Dor, and the Lost Sea of Korus, persuading the deluded to take the voluntary pilgrimage that swells the wealth of the Holy Therns and increases their ranks of slaves."

"Thus, the Therns serve as the principal means for collecting the wealth and labor that the First Born wrest from them. Occasionally, the First Born themselves raid the outer world, capturing females from the royal houses of the Red Men and seizing the newest battleships and trained artisans to copy what they cannot create."

"We are a non-productive race, priding ourselves upon our idleness. It is criminal for a First Born to labor or invent; that is the work of the lower orders, who live merely that the First Born may enjoy long lives of luxury. With us, fighting is all that counts. Were it not for that, there would be more of the First Born than all the creatures of Barsoom could support, for insofar as I know, none of us ever dies a natural death. Our females would live forever but for the fact that we tire of them and remove them to make place for others. Issus alone is protected against death. She has lived for countless ages."

"Would not the other Barsoomians live forever but for the doctrine of the voluntary pilgrimage which drags them to the bosom of Iss at or before their thousandth year?" I asked.

"I feel now that there is no doubt they are precisely the same species as the First Born," Xodar replied. "I hope I shall live to fight for them in atonement for the sins I have committed against them through generations of false teaching."

As he ceased speaking, a weird call rang out across the waters of Omean. I had heard it the previous evening as well; it marked the ending of the day, when the men of Omean spread their silks upon the decks of battleships and cruisers to fall into the dreamless sleep of Mars.

Our guard entered to inspect us for the last time before the new day broke. His duty was soon performed, and the heavy door of our prison closed behind him, leaving us alone for the night.

I gave him time to return to his quarters, as Xodar suggested he would, then I sprang to the grated window and surveyed the nearby waters. A quarter of a mile from the island lay a monster battleship, while between her and the shore were numerous smaller cruisers and one-man scouts. Upon the battleship alone was a watch. I could see him plainly in the upper works, and as I watched, he spread his sleeping silks upon the tiny platform where he was stationed. Soon he threw himself at full length upon his couch. The discipline on Omean was indeed lax. But it was not to be wondered at, since no enemy guessed the existence upon Barsoom of such a fleet, or even of the First Born, or the Sea of Omean. Why maintain a watch?

Presently, I dropped to the floor and described the various craft to Xodar.

"There is one there," he said, "my personal property, built to carry five men, that is the swiftest of the swift. If we can board her, we can at least make a memorable run for liberty." He then detailed the equipment of the boat, her engines, and everything that made her the flier she was.

In his explanation, I recognized a trick of gearing that Kantos Kan had taught me when we sailed under false names in the navy of Zodanga beneath Sab Than, the Prince. I knew then that the First Born had stolen it from the ships of Helium, for only they possess such gearing. I also knew that Xodar spoke the truth when he lauded the speed of his little craft; nothing that cleaves the thin air of Mars can approximate the speed of the ships of Helium.

We decided to wait at least an hour until all the stragglers had sought their silks. In the meantime, I was to fetch the red youth to our cell so that we would be ready to make our rash break for freedom together.

I sprang to the top of our partition wall and pulled myself up onto it. There I found a flat surface about a foot in width, along which I walked until I came to the cell where I saw the boy sitting upon his bench. He had been leaning back against the wall, gazing up at the glowing dome above Omean. When he spied me balancing upon the partition wall above him, his eyes opened wide in astonishment. Then a wide grin of appreciative understanding spread across his countenance.

As I stooped to drop to the floor beside him, he motioned me to wait. Coming close below, he whispered, "Catch my hand; I can almost leap to the top of that wall myself. I have tried it many times, and each day I come a little closer. Some day I should have been able to make it."

I lay upon my belly across the wall and reached my hand far down toward him. With a little run from the center of the cell, he sprang up until I grasped his outstretched hand, and thus I pulled him to the wall's top beside me.

"You are the first jumper I ever saw among the red men of Barsoom," I said.

He smiled. "It is not strange. I will tell you why when we have more time."

Together we returned to the cell where Xodar sat, descending to talk with him until the hour had passed.

There we made our plans for the immediate future, binding ourselves by a solemn oath to fight to the death for one another against whatever enemies should confront us. We knew that even if we succeeded in escaping the First Born, we might still have a whole world against us; the power of religious superstition is mighty.

It was agreed that I should navigate the craft once we reached it, and that if we made the outer world in safety, we should attempt to reach Helium without a stop.

"Why Helium?" asked the red youth.

"I am a prince of Helium," I replied.

He gave me a peculiar look but said nothing further on the subject. I wondered at the time what the significance of his expression might be, but in the press of other matters, it soon left my mind, nor did I have occasion to think of it again until later.

"Come," I said at length. "Now is as good a time as any. Let us go."

Another moment found me at the top of the partition wall again with the boy beside me. Unbuckling my harness, I snapped it together with a single long strap which I lowered to the waiting Xodar below. He grasped the end and was soon sitting beside us.

"How simple," he laughed.

"The balance should be even simpler," I replied. Then I raised myself to the top of the outer wall of the prison, just so that I could peer over and locate the passing sentry. For a matter of five minutes I waited, and then he came in sight on his slow, snail-like beat about the structure.

I watched him until he had made the turn at the end of the building, carrying him out of sight of the side of the prison that was to witness our dash for freedom. The moment his form disappeared, I grasped Xodar and drew him to the top of the wall. Placing one end of my harness strap in his hands, I lowered him quickly to the ground below. Then the boy grasped the strap and slid down to Xodar's side.

In accordance with our arrangement, they did not wait for me but walked slowly toward the water, a matter of a hundred yards, directly past the guard-house filled with sleeping soldiers.

They had taken scarce a dozen steps when I too dropped to the ground and followed them leisurely toward the shore. As I passed the guard-house, the thought of all the good blades lying there gave me pause, for if ever men were to have need of swords, it was my companions and I on the perilous trip upon which we were about to embark.

I glanced toward Xodar and the youth and saw that they had slipped over the edge of the dock into the water. In accordance with our plan, they were to remain there, clinging to the metal rings which studded the concrete-like substance of the dock at the water's level, with only their mouths and noses above the surface, until I joined them.

The lure of the swords within the guard-house was strong upon me, and I hesitated a moment, half inclined to risk the attempt to take the few we needed. That he who hesitates is lost proved itself a true aphorism in this instance. Another moment saw me creeping stealthily toward the door of the guard-house.

Gently, I pressed it open a crack, enough to discover a dozen blacks stretched upon their silks in profound slumber. At the far side of the room, a rack held the swords and firearms of the men. Warily, I pushed the door a trifle wider to admit my body. A hinge gave out a resentful groan. One of the men stirred, and my heart stood still. I cursed myself for a fool to have thus jeopardized our chances for escape, but there was nothing for it now but to see the adventure through.

With a spring as swift and as noiseless as a tiger's, I lit beside the guardsman who had moved. My hands hovered about his throat, awaiting the moment that his eyes should open. For what seemed an eternity to my overwrought nerves, I remained poised thus. Then the fellow turned again upon his side and resumed the even respiration of deep slumber.

Carefully, I picked my way between and over the soldiers until I had gained the rack at the far side of the room. Here I turned to survey the sleeping men. All were quiet. Their regular breathing rose and fell in a soothing rhythm that seemed to me the sweetest music I had ever heard.

Gingerly, I drew a long-sword from the rack. The scraping of the scabbard against its holder as I withdrew it sounded like the filing of cast iron with a great rasp, and I looked to see the room immediately filled with alarmed and attacking guardsmen. But none stirred.

The second sword I withdrew noiselessly, but the third clanked in its scabbard with a frightful din. I knew that it must awaken some of the men at least, and was on the point of forestalling their attack by a rapid charge for the doorway, when again, to my intense surprise, not a black moved. Either they were wondrous heavy sleepers, or else the noises I made were really much less than they seemed to me.

I was about to leave the rack when my attention was attracted by the revolvers. I knew that I could not carry more than one away with me, for I was already too heavily laden to move quietly with any degree of safety or speed. As I took one of

"My son!" The words tore from my throat, a raw sound of disbelief that shattered the silence of the cockpit. I rose slowly, turning to face the handsome youth standing before me. As my gaze locked onto his, the pieces of the puzzle fell into place with startling clarity. There was an undeniable echo of my wife's incomparable beauty in his sharp, clear-cut features, yet it was tempered by a rugged, masculine strength that was unmistakably mine. His grey eyes, the very color of my own, held a depth of expression that mirrored my soul.

The boy stood frozen, a mixture of desperate hope and trembling uncertainty etched across his face.

"Tell me of your mother," I commanded, my voice thick with emotion. "Speak to me of the years I have been robbed by a relentless fate of her companionship."

With a cry of pure joy, he launched himself toward me, throwing his arms around my neck. For a fleeting moment, holding my son close, the dam broke; tears welled in my eyes, choking me as if I were some maudlin fool. Yet, I felt no shame in this weakness. A long life has taught me that a man may appear frail when confronted with the tender bonds of family and yet remain an iron giant in the sterner arenas of war and survival.

"Your stature, your manner, the terrible ferocity of your swordsmanship," the boy whispered against my shoulder, "are exactly as my mother has described them a thousand times. Yet, even with such evidence, I could scarce credit the truth of what seemed so improbable, however much I desired it to be so. Do you know what finally convinced me?"

"What, my boy?" I asked, holding him tighter.

"Your first words to me—they were of my mother. None but the man who loved her as she has told me my father did would have thought first of her."

"For long years, my son, I can scarce recall a moment that the radiant vision of your mother's face has not haunted my thoughts. Tell me of her."

"Those who have known her longest say she has not changed, unless it be to grow more beautiful—a miracle, surely. Only when she believes I am not watching does her face grow very sad, oh, so wistful. She thinks ever of you, my father. All of Helium mourns with her and for her. Her grandfather's people love her; they loved you also, and they worship your memory as the savior of Barsoom."

"Each year, on the anniversary of the day you raced across a near-dead world to unlock the secret of that awful portal, a great festival is held in your honor. But there are tears mingled with the thanksgiving—tears of real regret that the author of their happiness is not there to share the joy of living he died to give them. Upon all Barsoom, there is no greater name than John Carter."

"And by what name has your mother called you?" I asked gently.

"The people of Helium asked that I bear your name, but my mother refused. She said you and she had chosen a name for me together, and your wish must be honored above all others. So, the name she calls me is the one you desired, a combination of hers and yours—Carthoris."

Xodar had been at the wheel while we spoke, and now his voice cut through the reunion. "She is dropping badly by the head, John Carter," he reported grimly. "So long as we were rising at a stiff angle, it was not noticeable, but now that I am trying to maintain a horizontal course, it is critical. The wound in her bow has opened one of her forward ray tanks."

It was true. Upon examining the damage, I found it far graver than anticipated. The forced angle required to keep the bow level was severely impeding our speed, and with the repulsive rays leaking from the forward tanks, we had perhaps an hour or more before we would be floating stern-up and helpless.

We had slowed slightly with the dawning of security, but now I seized the helm once more and pulled the noble little engine wide open. Again, we raced north at terrifying velocity. Meanwhile, Carthoris and Xodar worked frantically with tools at the great rent in the bow, engaged in a hopeless endeavor to stem the tide of escaping energy.

It was still dark when we passed the northern boundary of the ice cap and the cloud layer. Below us lay a typical Martian landscape: a rolling ochre sea bottom of long-dead seas, low surrounding hills, and here and there, the grim, silent cities of the dead past. Great piles of mighty architecture stood tenanted only by age-old memories of a once-powerful race and by the great white apes of Barsoom.

Maintaining our vessel in a horizontal position became increasingly difficult. Lower and lower sagged the bow until it became necessary to cut the engine entirely to prevent our flight from terminating in a swift, fatal dive to the ground.

As the sun rose, sweeping away the darkness of night, our craft gave a final, spasmodic plunge. It turned half upon its side, and then, with the deck tilting at a sickening angle, swung in a slow circle, the bow dropping further below the stern each moment.

Clinging to handrails and stanchions, we waited for the inevitable. Finally, seeing the end approaching, we snapped the buckles of our harnesses to the rings at the ship's sides. In another moment, the deck reared at a ninety-degree angle, and we hung in our leather straps, feet dangling a thousand yards above the barren ground.

I swung close to the controlling devices and reached for the lever directing the rays of repulsion. The boat responded to my touch, and very gently, we began to sink toward the earth.

It was fully half an hour before we touched down. Directly north rose a lofty range of hills, toward which we decided to make our way, as they offered greater opportunity for concealment from the pursuers we were confident might stumble in this direction.

An hour later, we found ourselves in the time-rounded gullies of the hills, amid the beautiful flowering plants that abound in the arid wastes of Barsoom. There we discovered numerous huge milk-giving shrubs—that strange plant which serves as both food and drink for the wild hordes of green men. It was indeed a boon, for we were all nearly famished.

Beneath a cluster of these shrubs, which afforded perfect concealment from wandering air scouts, we lay down to sleep—for me, the first time in many hours. This marked the beginning of my fifth day upon Barsoom since I had found myself suddenly translated from my cottage on the Hudson to Dor, the valley beautiful, the valley hideous. In all this time, I had slept but twice, though once the clock had ticked within the storehouse of the Therns.

Mid-afternoon found me awakened by a hand seizing mine and covering it with kisses. With a start, I opened my eyes to look into the beautiful face of Thuvia.

"My Prince! My Prince!" she cried in an ecstasy of happiness. "'Tis you whom I had mourned as dead. My ancestors have been good to me; I have not lived in vain."

The girl's voice roused Xodar and Carthoris. The boy gazed upon the woman in surprise, but she seemed oblivious to anyone other than me. She would have thrown her arms about my neck and smothered me with caresses had I not gently but firmly disengaged myself.

"Come, come, Thuvia," I said soothingly. "You are overwrought by the danger and hardships you have passed through. You forget yourself, as you forget that I am the husband of the Princess of Helium."

"I forget nothing, my Prince," she replied, her eyes shining with a fierce devotion. "You have spoken no word of love to me, nor do I expect that you ever shall; but nothing can prevent me from loving you. I would not take the place of Dejah Thoris. My greatest ambition is to serve you, my Prince, forever as your slave. No greater boon could I ask, no greater honor could I crave, no greater happiness could I hope."

As I have before said, I am no ladies' man, and I must admit I felt more uncomfortable and embarrassed than I had ever done in my life. There could be no fairness for John Carter, for his son, or for the great Thark who had once swept through Zodanga's avenues like a storm of fire and steel.

The vast circular coliseum surrounding us was packed to absolute capacity. Every stratum of society, every age, and both sexes filled the stands. As we entered, the low hum of conversation died instantly. When we halted upon the platform, known as the Throne of Righteousness, a silence as profound as death enveloped the ten thousand spectators.

The judges sat in a great circle along the periphery of the platform. We were assigned seats with our backs to a smaller platform in the exact center, forcing us to face the judges and the roaring crowd. On the smaller platform, each defendant would stand while their case was heard.

Zat Arras himself took his seat in the golden chair of the presiding magistrate. As we settled and our guards retreated to the foot of the stairs, he rose, his voice cutting through the silence.

"John Carter!" he bellowed. "Take your place upon the Pedestal of Truth to be judged impartially according to your acts, and here to learn the reward you have earned!" Turning to scan the audience, he began his indictment.

"Know ye, O judges and people of Helium," he declared, "that John Carter, once Prince of Helium, has returned by his own admission from the Valley Dor and even from the Temple of Issus itself. That, before the eyes of many Heliumites, he has blasphemed against the Sacred Iss, against the Valley Dor, against the Lost Sea of Korus, and against the Holy Therns themselves. He has defiled Issus, Goddess of Death and Life Eternal. Know ye further by the witness of your own eyes that he has indeed returned from these sacred precincts, violating our ancient customs and desecrating the sanctity of our religion."

"He who is once dead may not live again," Zat Arras continued, his voice rising. "He who attempts resurrection must be made dead forever. Judges, your duty is plain. What reward shall be meted to John Carter for these crimes?"

"Death!" shouted a judge.

Then, a man leaped to his feet in the audience, raising his hand high. "Justice! Justice! Justice!" It was Kantos Kan. As all eyes turned to him, he surged past the Zodangan soldiery and vaulted onto the platform.

"What manner of justice is this?" he cried at Zat Arras. "The defendant has not been heard, nor has he had the chance to call witnesses in his defense. In the name of the people of Helium, I demand fair and impartial treatment for the Prince of Helium!"

A great roar erupted from the crowd: "Justice! Justice! Justice!" Zat Arras dared not deny them.

"Speak, then," he snarled, turning to me, "but blaspheme not against the things that are sacred upon Barsoom."

"Men of Helium!" I cried, turning to the spectators and speaking over the heads of my judges. "How can John Carter expect justice from the men of Zodanga? He cannot, nor does he ask it. It is to the men of Helium that I state my case; nor do I appeal for mercy. It is not in my own cause that I speak now—it is in yours. For your wives and daughters, and for those yet unborn. It is to save them from the unthinkably atrocious indignities I have witnessed heaped upon the fair women of Barsoom in the place men call the Temple of Issus. To save them from the sucking embrace of the plant-men, from the fangs of the great white apes of Dor, from the cruel lust of the Holy Therns, from all that the cold, dead Iss carries them from homes of love and life and happiness."

"Sits there no man here who does not know the history of John Carter?" I continued. "How he came among you from another world, rose from a prisoner among the green men, through torture and persecution, to a place high among the highest of Barsoom. "There is no man here, or anywhere upon Barsoom today, who does not owe his life directly to a single act of mine, in which I sacrificed myself and the happiness of my Princess that you might live. And so, men of Helium, I think that I have the right to demand that I be heard, that I be believed, and that you let me serve you and save you from the false hereafter of Dor and Issus as I saved you from the real death that other day."

"It is to you of Helium that I speak now. When I am done, let the men of Zodanga have their will with me. Zat Arras has taken my sword from me, so the men of Zodanga no longer fear me. Will you listen?"

"Speak, John Carter, Prince of Helium!" cried a great noble from the audience, and the multitude echoed his permission until the building rocked with the noise of their demonstration.

Zat Arras knew better than to interfere with such a sentiment, and so for two hours I spoke with the people of Helium.

But when I finished, Zat Arras arose and, turning to the judges, said in a low tone: "My nobles, you have heard John Carter's plea; every opportunity has been given him to prove his innocence if he be not guilty; but instead he has but utilized the time in further blasphemy. What, gentlemen, is your verdict?"

"Death to the blasphemer!" cried one, springing to his feet, and in an instant the entire thirty-one judges were on their feet with upraised swords, a token of their unanimous verdict.

If the people did not hear Zat Arras' charge, they certainly heard the verdict of the tribunal. A sullen murmur rose louder and louder about the packed coliseum. Then Kantos Kan, who had not left the platform since first he had taken his place near me, raised his hand for silence. When he could be heard, he spoke to the people in a cool and level voice.

"You have heard the fate that the men of Zodanga would mete to Helium's noblest hero. It may be the duty of the men of Helium to accept the verdict as final. Let each man act according to his own heart. Here is the answer of Kantos Kan, head of the navy of Helium, to Zat Arras and his judges," and with that he unbuckled his scabbard and threw his sword at my feet.

In an instant, soldiers and citizens, officers and nobles crowded past the soldiers of Zodanga, forcing their way to the Throne of Righteousness. A hundred men surged up on the platform, and a hundred blades rattled and clanked to the floor at my feet. Zat Arras and his officers were furious, but they were helpless. One by one, I raised the swords to my lips and buckled them again upon their owners.

"Come," said Kantos Kan. "We will escort John Carter and his party to his own palace." They formed about us and started toward the stairs leading to the Aisle of Hope.

"Stop!" cried Zat Arras. "Soldiers of Helium, let no prisoner leave the Throne of Righteousness."

The soldiery from Zodanga were the only organized body of Heliumetic troops within the temple, so Zat Arras was confident his orders would be obeyed. But I did not think he looked for the opposition that was raised the moment the soldiers advanced toward the throne. From every quarter of the coliseum, swords flashed and men rushed threateningly upon the Zodangans. Someone raised a cry: "Tardos Mors is dead—a thousand years to John Carter, Jeddak of Helium." As I heard that and saw the ugly attitude of the men of Helium toward the soldiers of Zat Arras, I knew that only a miracle could avert a clash that would end in civil war.

"Hold!" I cried, leaping to the Pedestal of Truth once more. "Let no man move till I am done. A single sword thrust here today may plunge Helium into a bitter and bloody war the results of which none can foresee. It will turn brother against brother and father against son. No man's life is worth that sacrifice. Rather would I submit to the biased judgment of Zat Arras than be the cause of civil strife in Helium."

"Let us each give in a point to the other, and let this entire matter rest until Tardos Mors returns, or Mors Kajak, his son. If neither be back at the end of a year, a second trial may be held—the thing has a precedent." And then turning to Zat Arras, I said in a low voice: "Unless you be a bigger fool than I take you to be, you will grasp the chance I am offering you ere it is too late. Once that multitude of swords below is drawn against your soldiery, no man upon Barsoom—not even Tardos Mors himself—can avert the consequences. What say you? Speak quickly."

The Jed of Zodangan Helium raised his voice to the angry sea beneath us.

"Stay your hands, men of Helium," he shouted, his voice trembling with rage. "The sentence of the court is passed, but the day of retribution has not been set. I, Zat Arras, Jed of Zodanga, appreciating the royal connections of the prisoner and his past services to Helium and Barsoom, grant a respite of one year, or until the return of Mors Kajak, or Tardos Mors to Helium. No one moved. Instead, they stood in tense silence with their eyes fastened upon me, as though waiting for a signal to attack.

"Clear the temple," commanded Zat Arras, in a low tone to one of his officers.

Fearing the result of an attempt to carry out this order by force, I stepped to the edge of the platform and, pointing toward the main entrance, bid them pass out. As one man, they turned at my request and filed, silent and threatening, past the soldiers of Zat Arras, Jed of Zodanga, who stood scowling in impotent rage.

Kantos Kan with the others who had sworn allegiance to me still stood upon the Throne of Righteousness with me.

"Come," said Kantos Kan to me. "We will escort you to your palace, my Prince. Come, Carthoris and Xodar. Come, Tars Tarkas." And with a haughty sneer for Zat Arras upon his handsome lips, he turned and strode to the throne steps and up the Aisle of Hope. We four and the hundred loyal ones followed behind him, nor was a hand raised to stay us, though glowering eyes followed our triumphal march through the temple.

In the avenues, we found a press of people, but they opened a pathway for us, and many were the swords that were flung at my feet as I passed through the city of Helium toward my palace upon the outskirts. Here my old slaves fell upon their knees and kissed my hands as I greeted them. They cared not where I had been. It was enough that I had returned to them.

"Ah, master," cried one, "if our divine Princess were but here this would be a day indeed."

Tears came to my eyes, so that I was forced to turn away that I might hide my emotions. Carthoris wept openly as the slaves pressed about him with expressions of affection and words of sorrow for our common loss. It was now that Tars Tarkas for the first time learned that his daughter, Sola, had accompanied Dejah Thoris upon the last long pilgrimage. I had not had the heart to tell him what Kantos Kan had told me. With the stoicism of the green Martian, he showed no sign of suffering, yet I knew that his grief was as poignant as my own. In marked contrast to his kind, he had in well-developed form the kindlier human characteristics of love, friendship, and charity.

The board, according to red Martian custom, was triangular, for there were three in our family. Behind stood a slave as in the days when his mistress had occupied her place at the board, ready to do her bidding. It was the way upon Barsoom, so I endured the anguish of it, though it wrung my heart to see that silent chair where should have been my laughing and vivacious Princess keeping the great hall ringing with her merry gaiety.

Twice a day for many days this youth came to my cell with food, and ever the same greetings from Zat Arras. For a long time I tried to engage him in conversation upon other matters, but he would not talk, and so, at length, I desisted.

I was beside myself with anxiety for knowledge of the progress of the expedition which was to rescue Dejah Thoris. I felt that Carthoris would not let the matter drop, were he free to act, but in so far as I knew, he also might be a prisoner in Zat Arras' pits.

I determined to make at least one more effort to learn something, and to this end I adopted strategy when next the youth came to my cell. I had noticed that he was a handsome fellow, about the size and age of Carthoris. And I had also noticed that his shabby trappings but illly comported with his dignified and noble bearing.

It was with these observations as a basis that I opened my negotiations with him upon his next subsequent visit.

For an hour, I have heard the roar of conflict echoing through the palace halls. I knew not what they signified, but I clung to the desperate hope that it might be the men of Helium, led by my Prince.

"And tell me, where is Carthoris, our son?"

"He was with me less than an hour ago, Dejah Thoris," I answered gently. "It must have been his men you heard battling within the temple precincts."

"Where is Issus?" I asked suddenly, my voice sharp.

Dejah Thoris shrugged, a gesture of weary resignation.

"She sent me under guard to this chamber just as the fighting began within the temple halls. She claimed she would summon me later. Yet, I have never seen her act with such uncertainty, such palpable terror. Now I understand; she must have learned that John Carter, Prince of Helium, was approaching to demand an accounting for the imprisonment of his Princess."

The cacophony of battle—the clash of steel, the shouting of voices, the thunder of many feet—drummed against the walls from every direction. I knew I was needed there, yet I dared not abandon Dejah Thoris, nor could I bear to drag her into the swirling chaos of war.

Then, a thought struck me: the deep pits from which I had so recently emerged. Why not secrete her there until I could return and fetch her away, forever removing her from this nightmare? I explained my plan.

For a fleeting moment, she clung to me with desperate intensity.

"I cannot bear to be parted from you, even for a heartbeat, John Carter," she whispered, her voice shaking. "I shudder at the thought of being alone again, where that terrible creature might discover me. You do not know her. None can truly imagine her ferocious cruelty unless they have witnessed her daily atrocities for over half a year. It has taken me nearly all this time to comprehend even the horrors I have seen with my own eyes."

"I shall not leave you, then, my Princess," I promised.

She was silent for a moment, then she drew my face to hers and pressed a kiss to my lips.

"Go, John Carter," she commanded softly. "Our son is there, and the soldiers of Helium are fighting for the Princess of Helium. Where they are, you must be. I must not think of myself now, but of them and of my husband's duty. I may not stand in the way of that. Hide me in the pits, and go."

I led her to the door through which I had entered the chamber from below. There, I pressed her form close to mine, feeling the warmth of her life against my own. Then, though it tore my heart asunder and filled me only with the blackest shadows of terrible foreboding, I guided her across the threshold, kissed her one final time, and closed the door upon her.

Without hesitation, I hurried from the chamber toward the greatest tumult. Scarce had I traversed half a dozen chambers before I arrived at the theater of a fierce struggle. The blacks were massed at the entrance to a great chamber, attempting to block the progress of a body of red men toward the inner sacred precincts of the temple.

Coming from within, I found myself behind the blacks. Without waiting to calculate their numbers or the foolhardiness of my venture, I charged swiftly across the chamber and fell upon them from the rear with my keen long-sword.

As I struck the first blow, I cried aloud, "For Helium!" Then I rained cut after cut upon the surprised warriors. The reds outside took heart at the sound of my voice, shouting "John Carter! John Carter!" and redoubling their efforts so effectively that before the blacks could recover from their temporary demoralization, their ranks were broken and the red men burst into the chamber.

The fight within that room, had it been recorded by a competent chronicler, would go down in the annals of Barsoom as a historic memorial to the grim ferocity of her warlike people. Five hundred men fought there that day, the blacks against the reds. No man asked for quarter, nor did any give it. As though by common assent, they fought, as though to determine once and for all their right to live, in accordance with the law of the survival of the fittest.

I think we all knew that upon the outcome of this battle would hinge forever the relative positions of these two races upon Barsoom. It was a battle between the old and the new, yet not once did I question the outcome. With Carthoris at my side, I fought for the red men of Barsoom and for their total emancipation from the throttling bondage of a hideous superstition.

Back and forth across the room we surged, until the floor was ankle-deep in blood, and dead men lay so thickly that half the time we stood upon their bodies as we fought. As we swung toward the great windows overlooking the gardens of Issus, a sight met my gaze that sent a wave of exultation over me.

"Look!" I cried. "Men of the First Born, look!"

For an instant, the fighting ceased. With one accord, every eye turned in the direction I had indicated, and the sight they beheld was one no man of the First Born had ever imagined possible.

Across the gardens, from side to side, stood a wavering line of black warriors. Beyond them, forcing them ever back, was a great horde of green warriors astride their mighty thoats. And as we watched, one, fiercer and more grimly terrible than his fellows, rode forward from the rear. As he came, he shouted some fierce command to his terrible legion.

It was Tars Tarkas, Jeddak of Thark. As he couched his great forty-foot metal-shod lance, we saw his warriors do likewise. Then we interpreted his command. Twenty yards now separated the green men from the black line. Another word from the great Thark, and with a wild, terrifying battle-cry, the green warriors charged. For a moment the black line held, but only for a moment—then the fearsome beasts that bore equally terrible riders passed completely through it.

After them came utan upon utan of red men. The green horde broke to surround the temple. The red men charged for the interior, and then we turned to continue our interrupted battle; but our foes had vanished.

My first thought was of Dejah Thoris. Calling to Carthoris that I had found his mother, I started on a run toward the chamber where I had left her, with my boy close beside me. After us came those of our little force who had survived the bloody conflict.

The moment I entered the room, I saw that someone had been there since I had left. A silk garment lay upon the floor. There were also a dagger and several metal ornaments strewn about as though torn from their wearer in a struggle. But worst of all, the door leading to the pits where I had hidden my Princess was ajar.

With a bound, I was before it. Thrusting it open, I rushed within. Dejah Thoris had vanished. I called her name aloud again and again, but there was no response. I think in that instant I hovered upon the verge of insanity. I do not recall what I said or did, but I know that for an instant I was seized with the rage of a maniac.

"Issus!" I cried. "Issus! Where is Issus? Search the temple for her, but let no man harm her but John Carter. Carthoris, where are the apartments of Issus?"

"This way," cried the boy, and without waiting to know that I had heard him, he dashed off at breakneck speed, further into the bowels of the temple. As fast as he went, however, I was still beside him, urging him on to greater speed.

At last we came to a great carved door, and through this Carthoris dashed, a foot ahead of me. Within, we came upon such a scene as I had witnessed within the temple once before—the throne of Issus, with the reclining slaves, and about it the ranks of soldiery.

We did not even give the men a chance to draw their weapons; so quickly were we upon them. With a single cut, I struck down two in the front rank. And then, by the mere weight and momentum of my body, I rushed completely through the two remaining ranks and sprang upon the dais beside the carved sorapus throne.

The repulsive creature, squatting there in terror, attempted to escape me and leap into a trap behind her. But this time I was not to be outwitted by any such petty subterfuge. Before she had half arisen, I had grasped her by the arm. Then, as I saw the guard starting to make a concerted rush upon me from all sides, I whipped out my dagger and, holding it close to that vile breast, ordered them to halt.

"Back!" I cried to them. "Back! The first black foot that is planted upon this platform sends my dagger into Issus' heart."

For an instant they hesitated. Then an officer ordered them back, while from the outer corridor there swept into the throne room at the heels of my little party of survivors a full thousand red men under Kantos Kan, Hor Vastus, and Xodar.

"Where is Dejah Thoris?" I cried to the thing within my hands.

For a moment, her eyes roved wildly about the scene beneath her. I think it took a moment for the true condition to make any impression upon her—she could not at first realize that the temple had fallen before the assault of men from the outer world. When she did, there must have come, too, a terrible realization of what it meant to her: the loss of power, humiliation, the exposure of the fraud and imposture which she had played upon her own people for so long.

There was just one thing needed to complete the reality of the picture she was seeing, and that was added by the highest noble of her realm—the high priest of her religion, the prime minister of her government.

"Issus, Goddess of Death, and of Life Eternal," he cried, "arise in the might of thy righteous wrath and with one single wave of thy omnipotent hand strike dead thy blasphemers! Let not one escape. Issus, thy people depend upon thee. Daughter of the Lesser Moon, thou only art all-powerful. Thou only canst save thy people. I am done. We await thy will. Strike!"

And then it was that she went mad. A screaming, gibbering maniac writhed in my grasp. It bit and clawed and scratched in impotent fury. And then it laughed a weird and terrible laughter that froze the blood. The slave girls upon the dais shrieked and cowered away. And the thing jumped at them, gnashing its teeth and then spitting upon them from frothing lips. God, but it was a horrid sight.

Finally, I shook the thing, hoping to recall it for a moment to rationality.

"Where is Dejah Thoris?" I cried again.

The awful creature in my grasp mumbled inarticulately for a moment, then a sudden gleam of cunning shot into those hideous, close-set eyes.

"Dejah Thoris? Dejah Thoris?" and then that shrill, unearthly laugh pierced our ears once more.

"Yes, Dejah Thoris—I know. And Thuvia, and Phaidor, daughter of Matai Shang. They each love John Carter. Ha-ah! but it is droll. Together for a year they will meditate within the Temple of the Sun, but ere the year is quite gone there will be no more food for them. Ho-oh! what divine entertainment," and she licked the froth from her cruel lips. "There will be no more food—except each other. Ha-ah! Ha-ah!"

The horror of the suggestion nearly paralyzed me. To this awful fate the creature within my power had condemned my Princess. I trembled in the ferocity of my rage. As a terrier shakes a rat, I shook Issus, Goddess of Life Eternal.

"Countermand your orders!" I cried. "Recall the condemned. Haste, or you die!"

"It is too late. Ha-ah! Ha-ah!" and then she commenced her gibbering and shrieking again.

Almost of its own volition, my dagger flew up above that putrid heart. But something stayed my hand, and I am now glad that it did. It were a terrible thing to have struck down a woman with one's own hand. But a fitter fate occurred to me for this false deity.

"First Born," I cried, turning to those who stood within the chamber, "you have seen to-day the impotency of Issus—the gods are impotent. Issus is no god. She is a cruel and wicked old woman, who has deceived and played upon you for ages. Take her. John Carter, Prince of Helium, would not contaminate his hand with her blood," and with that I pushed the raving beast, whom a short half-hour before a whole world had worshipped as divine, from the platform of her throne into the waiting clutches of her betrayed and vengeful people.
