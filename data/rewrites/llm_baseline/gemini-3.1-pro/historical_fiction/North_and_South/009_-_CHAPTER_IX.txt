The day after her encounter with Higgins and his daughter, the subtle acoustic ecology of the Hales’ little drawing-room shifted. The low, relentless thrum of Milton’s industrial mills—a mechanized drone that seeped through the windowpanes and fractured the domestic tranquility—was suddenly overlaid by the erratic, nervous pacing of Mr. Hale. He moved from one heirloom object to another, his boots tapping an anxious rhythm on the floorboards. Margaret recognized the sonic signature of his hesitation; it was his way of deferring something he wished, yet feared, to say. 

Out it came at last, fracturing the quiet: “My dear! I’ve asked Mr. Thornton to come to tea to-night.”

Mrs. Hale was leaning back in her easy chair, her eyes shut against the harsh Northern light. The chronic pain that had etched itself into her features was exacerbated by the abrasive sonic pollution of the manufacturing town, a far cry from the rustling oaks of the South. At her husband’s words, she roused into sharp querulousness, her aristocratic Southern dialect clashing against the harshness of the local lexicon. 

“Mr. Thornton!—and to-night! What in the world does the man want to come here for? And Dixon is washing my muslins and laces, struggling with the alkali harshness of this water. There is no soft water with these horrid east winds, which I suppose we shall have all the year round in Milton, choking out the very smell of lavender we brought from home.”

“The wind is veering round, my dear,” said Mr. Hale, gazing out at the sulfurous soot that drifted from the east. He had not yet mastered the points of the compass in this new land, arranging them *ad libitum* in a desperate attempt to translate the hostile environment into something familiar.

“Don’t tell me!” said Mrs. Hale, shuddering, wrapping her woven shawl tighter as if to insulate herself from the sensory assault of the North. “But, east or west wind, I suppose this man comes.”

“Oh mamma, that shows you never saw Mr. Thornton,” Margaret said, her voice providing a soothing, rhythmic counter-melody to her mother’s distress. “He looks like a person who would enjoy battling with every adverse thing he could meet with—enemies, winds, or circumstances. The more it rains and blows, the more certain we are to have him. But I will go and help Dixon. I’m getting to be a famous clear-starcher. And he won’t want any amusement beyond talking to Papa. Papa, I am really longing to see the Pythias to your Damon. You know I never saw him but once, and then the untranslatability of our two worlds left us so puzzled as to what to say to each other that we did not get on particularly well.”

“I don’t know that you would ever like him, or think him agreeable, Margaret. He is not a lady’s man.”

Margaret wreathed her throat in a scornful curve, rejecting the dominant lexicon that categorized men into such frivolous boxes. “I don’t particularly admire ladies’ men, papa. But Mr. Thornton comes here as your friend—as one who has appreciated you—”

“The only person in Milton,” sighed Mrs. Hale.

“So we will give him a welcome, and some cocoa-nut cakes,” Margaret decided, her mind immediately turning to the profound, grounding ritual of sustenance. “Dixon will be flattered if we ask her to make some. We still have that fine grated cocoa-nut, rich with its fragrant oils. We shall cream the butter until it is pale and airy, fold in the sweet nut and the frothy egg whites, and bake them until the edges are the colour of roasted hazelnut. And I will undertake to iron your caps, mamma.”

Many a time that morning did Margaret wish Mr. Thornton far enough away. She had planned a day of quiet, ancestral rhythms: translating a good piece of Dante, writing a letter to Edith to preserve their fading shared history, and visiting the Higginses. Instead, she stood in the small, warm kitchen, enveloped in the evocative, gustatory-rich aromas of toasted cocoa-nut and caramelized sugar wafting from the oven. She ironed away, the heavy metal hissing sharply against the damp muslin, releasing the clean, starchy scent of hot cotton. She listened to Dixon’s complaints, absorbing the older woman’s native Helstone dialect, hoping that by bearing the weight of this acoustic output, she might prevent Dixon from carrying the recital of her sorrows to Mrs. Hale.

The low-frequency hum of the distant factories vibrated through the kitchen floor, bringing on one of the bad headaches to which Margaret had lately become liable. She had to constantly remind herself of her father’s regard for Mr. Thornton to subdue her weariness. 

When she finally sat down, the scent of scorched starch clinging to her skirts, she told her mother that she was no longer Peggy the laundry-maid, but Margaret Hale the lady. It was a little joke about the fluidity of titles, but she was vexed when she found her mother taking the semantic shift seriously, a testament to how their cultural displacement was eroding their shared understanding.

“Yes! if any one had told me, when I was Miss Beresford, and one of the belles of the country, that a child of mine would have to stand half a day, in a little poky kitchen, working away like any servant, that we might prepare properly for the reception of a tradesman, and that this tradesman should be the only—”

“Oh, mamma!” said Margaret, lifting herself up, rejecting the linguistic power dynamics embedded in her mother's lament. “Don’t punish me for so careless a speech. I don’t mind ironing, or finding meaning in the domestic work that sustains you and papa. I am myself a born and bred lady through it all, even though it comes to scouring a floor, or washing dishes, or whipping batter. I am tired now, just for a little while; but in half an hour I shall be ready to do the same over again. And as to Mr. Thornton’s being in trade, why he can’t help that now, poor fellow. I don’t suppose his education would fit him for much else.”

Margaret lifted herself slowly up and retreated to her own room, seeking the silent refuge her mind so desperately craved.

In Mr. Thornton’s house, at this very same time, a parallel yet distinct domestic mythos was unfolding. A large-boned lady, long past middle age, sat at work in a grim, handsomely-furnished dining-room. Her features, like her frame, were strong and massive, moving slowly from one decided expression to another. She was Mrs. Thornton, and she sat mending a large, long table-cloth of the finest damask texture. She held it up against the light occasionally, her hands tracing the geography of the fabric, finding the thin places that required her delicate care. This cloth was an ancestral record, a witness to decades of rich, meat-heavy Northern dinners, roasted joints, and dense, steaming puddings that had fueled the generations of Thornton men. She was preserving a culinary artifact. 

There was not a book in the room, save for Matthew Henry’s Bible commentaries, six volumes of which lay on the massive side-board, flanked by a shining silver tea-urn—the grand altar of domestic sustenance—on one side, and a lamp on the other. 

In a remote apartment, an acoustic atrocity was taking place. Someone was practicing a *morceau de salon* upon the piano, playing it with a frantic, unfeeling rapidity. Every third note was either indistinct or wholly missed, the loud chords at the end ringing false. It was the superficial commercialization of art, an auditory clutter devoid of passion or soul, yet utterly satisfactory to the performer, Fanny. 

Mrs. Thornton ignored the cacophony. Her ear was tuned to the deeper rhythms of her domain. She heard a step, decisive and grounded, pass the dining-room door. 

“John! Is that you?” 

Her son opened the door, bringing with him the faint olfactory ghost of cotton dust and the metallic scent of the mill. “What has brought you home so early? I thought you were going to tea with that friend of Mr. Bell’s; that Mr. Hale?”

“So I am, mother. I am come home to dress!”

“Dress! humph! When I was a girl, young men were satisfied with dressing once in a day. Why should you dress to go and take a cup of tea with an old parson?”

“Mr. Hale is a gentleman, and his wife and daughter are ladies.”

The word ‘ladies’ hung in the air, a piece of vocabulary loaded with foreign class distinctions. “Wife and daughter! Do they teach too? What do they do? You have never mentioned them.”

“No! mother, because I have never seen Mrs. Hale; I have only seen Miss Hale for half an hour.”

“Take care you don’t get caught by a penniless girl, John.”

“I am not easily caught, mother, as I think you know. But I must not have Miss Hale spoken of in that way, which, you know, is offensive to me. I never was aware of any young lady trying to catch me yet, nor do I believe that any one has ever given themselves that useless trouble.”

Mrs. Thornton did not choose to yield the point to her son. The defense of her bloodline was a primal, agrarian instinct translated into the industrial age. “Well! I only say, take care. Perhaps our Milton girls have too much spirit and good feeling to go angling after husbands; but this Miss Hale comes out of the aristocratic counties, where, if all tales be true, rich husbands are reckoned prizes.”

Mr. Thornton’s brow contracted. He stepped further into the quiet sanctity of the dining-room. “Mother,” he said, with a short scornful laugh, the untranslatability of his subjective experience bubbling to the surface. “You will make me confess. The only time I saw Miss Hale, she treated me with a haughty civility which had a strong flavour of contempt in it. She held herself aloof from me as if she had been a queen, and I her humble, unwashed vassal. Be easy, mother.”

“No! I am not easy nor content either. What business had she, a renegade clergyman’s daughter, to turn up her nose at you! I would dress for none of them—a saucy set! if I were you.”

As he was leaving the room, the ancient, quiet ritual of his mother's mending returning to the fore, he said: “Mr. Hale is good, and gentle, and learned. He is not saucy. As for Mrs. Hale, I will tell you what she is like to-night, if you care to hear.”

He shut the door, and was gone. 

Mrs. Thornton drove her needle through the damask, pulling the thick thread with a sharp, rhythmic *hiss* that echoed her protective fury. “Despise my son! treat him as her vassal, indeed! Humph! I should like to know where she could find such another! Boy and man, he’s the noblest, stoutest heart I ever knew. I don’t care if I am his mother; I can see what’s what, and not be blind. I know what Fanny is; and I know what John is. Despise him! I hate her!”
