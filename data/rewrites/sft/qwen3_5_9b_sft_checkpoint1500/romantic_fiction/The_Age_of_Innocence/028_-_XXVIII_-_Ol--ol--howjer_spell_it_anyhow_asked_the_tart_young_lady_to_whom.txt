"Ol... ol... how-do-you-spell-it, anyhow?" asked the sharp-tongued young lady behind the counter, her fingers hovering over the keys as Archer slid his wife's telegram across the brass ledge of the Western Union office.

"Olenska—O-len-ska," Archer corrected gently, pulling the paper back to print the foreign syllables clearly above May's hurried scrawl.

"It's a peculiar name for a New York telegraph office, at least in this part of town," a smooth, unexpected voice remarked. Turning, Archer found Lawrence Lefferts standing right beside him, smoothing his immaculate moustache with an air of perfect composure, seemingly oblivious to the message.

"Hullo, Newland. Thought I'd catch you here. I just heard news of old Mrs. Mingott's stroke. Since I was heading that way anyway, I saw you turn down this street and tagged along. I suppose you're coming from there?"

Archer nodded, slipping the telegram beneath the lattice partition.

"Terrible business, isn't it?" Lefferts pressed, feigning casual concern. "Wiring the family, I gather? I hear it's quite serious, assuming you're referring to the Countess Olenska."

A cold stiffness seized Archer's lips. A wild, savage urge surged within him to drive his fist into Lefferts' long, vain, handsome face.

"Why?" Archer demanded, his voice low.

Lefferts, a man known for avoiding deep conversation, raised his eyebrows in an ironical grimace that served as a subtle warning: the watchful gaze of the young lady behind the lattice. To lose one's temper in public was considered the height of bad form. Archer, usually indifferent to such social niceties, knew better than to indulge this impulse now. The mere thought of bandying Ellen Olenska's name with Lefferts, especially at such a fraught moment, was unbearable. With a swift motion, he paid for the telegram, and the two young men stepped out into the bustling street.

Once outside, and having regained his composure, Archer changed the subject entirely. "Mrs. Mingott is doing much better," he lied smoothly. "The doctor sees no cause for alarm at all."

Lefferts, relieved, immediately pivoted to gossip. "Wonderful. By the way, have you heard? There are beastly rumors circulating again about the Beauforts."

By that afternoon, the announcement of the Beaufort bankruptcy dominated every newspaper. It cast a shadow over the news of Mrs. Manson Mingott's stroke, leaving only a handful of those aware of the mysterious link between the two events to suspect that old Catherine's illness was anything other than the natural result of aging.

New York was plunged into gloom by the tale of Beaufort's disgrace. As Mr. Letterblair noted, it was perhaps the worst case in his memory, or that of the distant ancestor who had founded the firm. The bank had continued to accept deposits for a full day after failure became inevitable; since many of its clients belonged to the city's ruling families, Beaufort's duplicity seemed twice as cynical. Had Mrs. Beaufort not adopted the tone that such calamities were merely "the test of friendship," compassion for her might have softened the general outrage against her husband. Instead, her cynicism was viewed as exceeding his, and she offered no excuse of being a "foreigner"—or rather, her detractors found no satisfaction in that defense, knowing she was not. It was a small comfort to those whose securities were safe to remind themselves that Beaufort *was* a foreigner. Yet, after all, if a gentleman from South Carolina could speak so glibly of being "back on his feet again," the argument lost its sting. There was nothing left to do but accept this terrible proof of the indissolubility of marriage. Society would simply have to find a way to function without the Beauforts. And yet, for the unfortunate victims—the poor old Miss Lannings, Medora Manson, and several other misguided ladies of good family who, if only they had listened to Mr. Henry van der Luyden...

"The best thing the Beauforts can do," declared Mrs. Archer, summarizing matters as if diagnosing a patient and prescribing a cure, "is to retire to Regina's little estate in North Carolina. Beaufort has always maintained a racing stable; surely he would make an excellent trotting-horse breeder. I am convinced he possesses all the qualities required of a successful horse dealer." Everyone agreed with her, though no one condescended to inquire what the Beauforts actually intended to do.

The following day, Mrs. Manson Mingott had recovered enough to command silence regarding the Beauforts. She also asked Dr. Bencomb, when he visited, what in heaven's name her family meant by such a fuss over her health.

"If people of my age will eat chicken salad in the evening, what are they to expect?" she queried. Once the doctor had modified her diet, her stroke was conveniently reclassified as a severe case of indigestion. However, despite her authoritative tone, old Catherine did not fully regain her former spirit. The encroaching distance of old age, while failing to diminish her curiosity about her neighbors, had dulled her capacity for compassion regarding their troubles. She seemed to effortlessly dismiss the Beaufort disaster from her mind. For the first time, she became obsessed with her own symptoms, developing a sentimental interest in certain family members to whom she had previously been contemptuously indifferent.

Among these, Mr. Welland stood out. Of her sons-in-law, he was the one she had most consistently ignored. All his wife's attempts to portray him as a man of forceful character and marked intellect (provided he had only "chosen") had been met with a derisive chuckle. But his current status as a chronic invalid made him an object of absorbing fascination. Mrs. Mingott issued an imperial summons for him to come and compare diets as soon as his temperature allowed, for old Catherine now recognized that one could never be too cautious about fevers.

Twenty-four hours after Madame Olenska's telegram announcing her arrival from Washington, the matter of who would meet her at Jersey City became urgent. At the Wellands', where the Newland Archers happened to be lunching, the debate was animated by the material difficulties facing the household, which struggled with the efficiency of a frontier outpost. It was agreed that Mrs. Welland could not possibly go to Jersey City, as she was to accompany her husband to old Catherine's that afternoon, and the brougham could not be spared. If Mr. Welland were "upset" by seeing his mother-in-law for the first time since her attack, he might require immediate transport home. The Welland sons would naturally be "down town," Mr. Lovell Mingott would be returning from a shooting trip with his carriage already engaged, and it would be impossible to ask May, at the close of a winter afternoon, to cross the ferry alone, even in her own vehicle.

Nevertheless, it might seem inhospitable—and contrary to old Catherine's express wishes—if Madame Olenska were allowed to arrive without anyone from the family at the station to greet her. It was just like Ellen, Mrs. Welland's weary voice implied, to place the family in such a predicament. "It's always one thing after another," the poor lady lamented, in one of her rare rebellions against fate. "The only thing that makes me think Mamma must be less well than Dr. Bencomb admits is this morbid desire to have Ellen return immediately, however inconvenient it may be."

These words were thoughtless, as utterances born of impatience often are. Mr. Welland pounced instantly.

"Augusta," he said, turning pale and setting down his fork, "do you have any other reason for believing that Bencomb is less reliable than he was? Have you noticed that he has been less conscientious than usual in attending to my case or your mother's?"

Now it was Mrs. Welland's turn to turn pale as the endless consequences of her blunder unrolled before her. Yet she managed to laugh and take a second helping of scalloped oysters before speaking, struggling back into her armor of cheerful fortitude. "My dear, how could you imagine such a thing? I only meant that, after the stand Mamma took regarding Ellen's duty to return to her husband, it seems strange that she should suddenly wish to see her, when there are half a dozen other grandchildren she could have requested. We must never forget that Mamma, despite her wonderful vitality, is a very old woman."

Mr. Welland's brow remained clouded, and it was evident that his perturbed imagination had latched onto this final remark. "Yes: your mother is a very old woman; and for all we know, Bencomb may not be as successful with elderly patients as he is with others. As you say, my dear, 'it's always one thing after another.' In another ten or fifteen years, I suppose, I shall have the pleasant duty of searching for a new physician. It is always better to make such a change before it becomes absolutely necessary." Having reached this Spartan conclusion, Mr. Welland firmly resumed his fork.

"But all the while," Mrs. Welland began again as she rose from the luncheon table and led the way into the wilderness of purple satin and malachite known as the back drawing-room, "I still cannot see how Ellen is to be brought here tomorrow evening. And I do like to have things settled for at least twenty-four hours in advance."
