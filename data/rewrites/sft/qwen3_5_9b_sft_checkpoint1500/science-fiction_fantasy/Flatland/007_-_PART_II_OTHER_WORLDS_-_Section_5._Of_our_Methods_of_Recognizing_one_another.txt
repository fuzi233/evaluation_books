To you, inhabitants of the realm of Three Dimensions, who bask in both shadow and light, who possess the dual vision of binoculars and the profound wisdom of perspective, and who delight in the kaleidoscope of colors—you, whose eyes grant you the power to gaze upon an angle itself and comprehend the perfect, unbroken curve of a circle—how can I hope to convey the sheer impossibility you face when trying to identify the shapes of others?

Remember what I have written: in our world of Flatland, whether animate or inanimate, every being presents to our gaze merely a straight line. If everything looks like a line, how does anyone distinguish a neighbor from a stranger?

The solution lies in a triad of senses. First stands Hearing, an organ far superior in ours than yours. It allows us to recognize friends by voice alone and, crucially, to differentiate between the lower social strata—the Equilateral, the Square, and the Pentagon. Yet, let us not be deceived by this initial success. As we climb the social ladder, auditory identification becomes a treacherous game. Voices grow indistinguishable, and the aristocracy, bred for elegance rather than scrutiny, lacks the refined ear needed to detect subtle disparities. Furthermore, the vocal apparatus of the lower orders is grotesquely oversized, allowing an Isosceles to mimic the tone of a Polygon, or even a Circle, with frightening ease. Thus, sound fails when stakes are high.

Enter the Second Method: Touch, or "Feeling." This is the primary arbiter of identity for women and the common folk. To introduce oneself to a stranger in Flatland is not to shake hands, but to invite physical inspection. The formal salutation, "Permit me to ask you to feel and be felt by Mr. So-and-so," remains the custom among the traditional gentry in rural districts. In the bustling cities, commerce abbreviates this to "Let me ask you to feel Mr. So-and-so," though the reciprocity is implied. The youth of fashion, driven by a desire for efficiency and a disdain for linguistic purity, have shortened it further to "Mr. Smith, permit me to feel Mr. Jones," treating the verb "feel" as a technical term for mutual tactile verification.

Do not imagine, however, that this process involves the tedious tracing of every side. Through rigorous schooling and lifelong practice, we can instantly discern the class of an individual by sensing a single angle. The blunt vertex of an Acute-Isosceles is as obvious to our fingers as a jagged stone is to a child. For most, feeling one corner suffices to categorize the soul within. But for the Nobility, the task grows exponentially difficult. A ten-sided figure may be mistaken for a twelve-sided one; a twenty-sided polygon may elude distinction from its twenty-four-sided counterpart. Even the most learned scholars of our universities often stumble here, unable to render a prompt verdict on the subtle geometry of the highest orders.

Yet, there is a peril inherent in this intimacy. Consider the Ladies, and the Triangles below them. Their eyes sit far from their apexes, rendering them blind to the dangers surrounding their vertices. Moreover, the lower classes are of a rugged, insensitive nature, lacking the delicacy required to withstand the probing touch of a highly organized Polygon. A mere twitch, a nervous shift, or a violent sneeze from the "Felt" individual can prove fatal to the "Feeler." History records instances where a sudden start by a Triangle severed the leg of a visiting Polygon, ending a potential friendship—and perhaps a career—in bloodshed.

My own lineage bears the scar of this tragedy. My grandfather, a respected Isosceles who eventually earned four votes for promotion to the Equilateral class, wept over a disaster visited upon his great-great-grandfather. That ancestor, a working man with a brain-angle of barely fifty-nine and a half degrees, was seized by rheumatic pain during a moment of feeling. He jerked violently, piercing the leg of a high-ranking gentleman through the diagonal. The consequence was catastrophic: the victim was imprisoned and degraded, and the shock reverberated through the family line. For five generations, our brains regressed, settling at fifty-eight degrees. It took half a millennium of slow accumulation just to reclaim the lost sixty degrees and achieve the freedom of the Regulars. All because a reflex of fear turned a handshake into a homicide.

Now, you may cry out, "How can you know angles if you cannot see them? You see only lines!"

I answer that while we cannot *see* angles, we infer them with mathematical precision. Our sense of touch, honed by necessity, surpasses your unassisted sight. We are aided further by the Laws of Nature: the brain of an Isosceles begins at thirty minutes and ascends by half a degree per generation until it reaches the holy threshold of sixty degrees. Consequently, the state maintains a living alphabet of angles in every schoolroom. These specimens, ranging from the stunted thirty minutes to the robust ten degrees, serve as static models. Most are criminals and vagrants, devoid of civic rights and too dull for war, destined instead to educate the children of the middle class. In the cheaper schools, these specimens are fed and allowed to live for years, though their angles degrade over time. In the more enlightened regions, they are starved and replaced monthly, ensuring a fresh, accurate standard. While this "expensive system" costs more, it saves lives and preserves the integrity of the curriculum, proving that in matters of truth, expense is the truest economy.

Thus, Recognition by Feeling is neither tedious nor hopeless, and it is far more reliable than listening to voices. However, given the lethal risks involved—a risk that no amount of caution can entirely eliminate—many in the Middle and Lower classes, and all of the Polygonal and Circular orders, reject the second method in favor of a third. A safer, albeit more abstract, approach awaits in the next section.
