Again, Miss Twinkleton has delivered her farewell speech—complete with white wine and pound cake—and once more the young ladies have departed for their homes. Helena Landless has left the Nuns’ House to attend to her brother’s affairs, leaving Rosa alone. Cloisterham glows under the summer sun, its cathedral and ruined monastery bathed in such golden light that their stone walls seem translucent. A warm radiance seems to emanate from within them, rather than fall upon them from without. The gardens bloom with fruit heavy on the vines. Once, weary pilgrims rode through the city’s cool shadows; now, dusty travelers—so covered in grime they appear made of earth itself—lounge on shaded doorsteps, attempting to mend their worn shoes or surrendering them to the city kennels as hopeless cases. They search their bundles for replacements, along with unused sickles wrapped in straw. At public pumps, bare feet are cooled, and water gurgles into cupped hands. The Cloisterham constables watch with suspicion, impatient for these intruders to leave the town and return to the scorching roads beyond.

On an afternoon like this, after the final service at the cathedral, the side of the High Street where the Nuns’ House stands lies in pleasant shade—except where the garden opens westward between leafy boughs. A servant informs Rosa, to her horror, that Mr. Jasper wishes to see her. If he had chosen a moment to catch her off guard, he could not have done better. Perhaps he did. Helena is gone, Mrs. Tisher is away on leave, and Miss Twinkleton—now reduced to amateur status—has joined a picnic with a veal pie.

“Oh, why, why, why, did you say I was at home?” Rosa cries, helpless.

The maid replies that Mr. Jasper never asked. He simply said he knew she was there and requested she be told he wished to speak with her.

“What shall I do? What shall I do?” Rosa thinks, clasping her hands. Then, seized by a sudden desperation, she decides: she will go to him in the garden. She shudders at the idea of being trapped indoors with him, but the garden is visible from many windows. There, she can be seen—and heard—and if necessary, scream and flee. It’s a desperate hope, but it’s all she has.

She has not seen him since the night of the tragedy, except during the inquiry before the Mayor, when he sat in grim silence, representing his lost nephew and burning with a desire for vengeance.

She drapes her garden hat over her arm and steps outside.

The moment she sees him leaning against the sun-dial, the old dread returns—the feeling that he controls her, even against her will. She wants to turn back, but something compels her forward. She cannot resist. She sits on the garden seat beside the dial, head bowed. She dares not look up—she feels only the grass beneath her eyes—but she notices he wears deep mourning. So does she. It wasn’t always so, but the missing boy has long been presumed dead and mourned.

He begins to reach toward her hand. She pulls hers back instinctively. His gaze remains fixed on her, though she sees nothing but green.

“I’ve waited,” he says, “to return to my duty near you.”

She tries to form words—her lips move, but no sound comes. Finally, she whispers, “Duty, sir?”

“The duty of teaching you. Serving you as your faithful music-master.”

“I’ve stopped studying.”

“Not stopped,” he insists. “Discontinued. Your guardian told me you ceased due to the shock we’ve all felt. When will you resume?”

“Never, sir.”

“Never? You would not have done more if you truly loved my dear boy.”

“I *did* love him!” Rosa snaps, fire flashing in her voice.

“Yes,” he says softly, “but not quite—not quite in the way intended. Not as he should have loved. Not as any man in his place must have loved.” He pauses. “Though I won’t compare him to you in that respect.”

She remains still, but draws herself tighter inward.

“So you’ve abandoned your studies altogether?” he asks.

“Yes,” she answers sharply. “That was my guardian’s politeness, not mine. I told him I meant to stop, and I mean to keep my word.”

“And you still do?”

“I still do, sir. And I beg you not to ask again. I will not answer. I have that power.”

She senses his gaze lingering on her—admiring the anger, the spark in her eyes. It makes her spirit rise—and then sink again, overwhelmed by shame, fear, and revulsion, just as it did that night at the piano.

“I will not question you further,” he says quietly. “I confess—”

“I do not wish to hear you, sir!” Rosa interrupts, rising.

This time, he touches her—his hand outstretched.

She recoils, stumbling back onto the seat.

“We must sometimes act against our desires,” he murmurs. “You must now—or you’ll do greater harm than you can ever repair.”

“What harm?”

“Soon. Soon. You question me, yet forbid me to question you. But I will answer. Dearest Rosa. Charming Rosa.”

She starts up again.

He does not touch her this time. But his face—dark, menacing—leans against the sun-dial, casting a shadow across the daylight. Her flight freezes in terror.

“I know how many windows overlook us,” he says, glancing toward them. “I won’t touch you again. I won’t come closer. Sit down. It’s no great wonder to see your music-master leaning idly on a pedestal, speaking with you, remembering what has passed. Sit down, my beloved.”

She turns to leave—again—only to stop, paralyzed by the threat in his expression. She looks at him, her face locked in fear, and slowly lowers herself back onto the bench.

“Rosa,” he begins, “even when my dear boy was engaged to you, I loved you madly. Even when I believed his happiness with you was certain, I loved you madly. Even when I worked to deepen his devotion to you, I loved you madly. Even when he gave me that picture of your face—so carelessly mocked by him—I feigned to hang it for his sake, but worshipped it in torment for years. I loved you madly—in the dullness of the day, in the agony of the night, surrounded by sordid truths or wandering through visions of paradise and hell, carrying your image in my arms. I loved you madly.”

If anything could make his words worse, it would be the contrast between the violence of his voice and the calmness of his pose.

“I endured it all in silence. As long as you were his—or so I believed—I kept my secret faithfully. Did I not?”

This lie—so blatant, yet spoken with such truth in tone—shatters her composure.

“You were false then,” she cries, “as you are now. False to him—every hour. You made my life miserable with your pursuit. You made me afraid to open his eyes, and forced me—out of love for him—to hide the truth: that you were a wicked, wicked man!”

His face remains composed, but his features twist, his hands tremble—diabolical in their stillness.

“How beautiful you are,” he says, voice thick with admiration. “More beautiful in anger than in peace. I don’t ask for your love. Give me yourself—and your hatred. Give me yourself—and that rage. That scorn. That enchanting defiance. It will be enough.”

Tears well in her eyes. Her face burns. She rises again, determined to escape.

But he reaches toward the porch, as if inviting her inside.

“I told you,” he says, “you rare charmer, sweet witch—you must stay and listen, or you will bring about harm no one can undo. You asked what harm. Stay, and I’ll tell you. Leave, and I will do it.”

She quails. Though she knows not what he means, she stays. Her breath comes in ragged gasps—threatening to choke her—but she presses a hand to her chest and endures.

“I have confessed,” he continues, “that my love is madness. So mad, that had the bond between me and my dear boy been one thread weaker, I might have torn him from your side—even when you favored him.”

A film crosses her eyes. She sways.

“Even him,” he repeats. “Yes—*even him*. Rosa, you see me. You hear me. Judge for yourself: no other man shall love you and live—if his life is in my hands.”

“What do you mean, sir?”

“I mean to show you how mad my love is. Mr. Crisparkle mentioned during the inquiries that young Landless confessed to being a rival to my lost boy. That is an unforgivable crime in my eyes. The same Mr. Crisparkle knows—by my hand—that I am devoted to finding and destroying the murderer, whoever he may be. I vowed to solve the mystery alone, until I held the clue that would trap him like a net. I have spent six months winding it tighter—slowly, steadily—as I speak now.”

“Your belief,” Rosa counters, “if you think Mr. Landless guilty, is not Mr. Crisparkle’s belief. And he is a good man.”

“My belief is my own,” he replies. “And I hold it sacred. Circumstances may grow so strong—even against an innocent man—that they become deadly. One missing piece found, and guilt is proven, however slight the evidence was before. Young Landless stands in mortal danger either way.”

“If you truly believe,” she pleads, turning pale, “that I favor Mr. Landless—or that he has ever addressed himself to me—you are wrong.”

He waves her aside with a curl of his lip.

“I was going to show you how madly I love you. More madly now than ever—because I am willing to renounce the second purpose that divided my life. From now on, I have only one goal: you. Miss Landless has become your closest friend. You care for her peace of mind?”

“I love her dearly.”

“You care for her name?”

“I have said, sir, I love her dearly.”

“I am unconsciously,” he says, smiling, “offending you by questioning. Let me state facts instead. You do care for her peace. You do care for her honor. Then—remove the shadow of the gallows from her, dear one.”

“You dare propose—”

“Darling, I dare. Stop there. If idolizing you makes me the worst of men, then I am the worst. If it makes me the best, then I am the best. My love for you surpasses all others. My truth to you transcends all others. Give me hope. Give me favor. I swear I am a broken man for your sake.”

She clutches her temples, pushes back her hair, and stares at him with wild horror, trying to grasp the full meaning of what he is saying—though he gives her only fragments.

“Do not calculate now, angel,” he says, “only count the sacrifices I lay at those dear feet—feet I would kiss among the dirtiest ashes, or wear upon my head like a savage. There is my loyalty to my dead boy—tread upon it!” He gestures as if casting something precious aside. “There is my offense against your sanctity—spurn it!” Another gesture. “There are my labors for justice—six months of toil—crush them!” A third motion. “There is my wasted past. My present. My heart’s desolation. My soul’s ruin. My peace. My despair. Stamp them into dust—so long as you take me, even if you hate me with every breath!”

The intensity of his passion overwhelms her. The spell breaks. She lunges toward the porch.

In an instant, he is at her side, whispering in her ear.

“Rosa, I am in control again. I walk calmly beside you to the house. I will wait for a sign—of attention, of hope. I will not strike too soon. Give me a sign you hear me.”

She moves her hand slightly—reluctantly.

“Not a word of this to anyone,” he warns. “Or the blow will fall—certain as night follows day. Another sign.”

She moves her hand again.

“I love you. Love you. Love you! If you cast me off now—but you won’t—you will never be rid of me. No one shall come between us. I would follow you to death.”

As the handmaid appears to open the gate, he removes his hat with quiet dignity—a parting salute. He departs with no more agitation than a statue might show.

Rosa stumbles upstairs. She faints. The maids carry her gently to her room and lay her on the bed.

A thunderstorm is gathering, they say. The heat and suffocating air have overwhelmed the poor dear—it’s no wonder. For all day long, even they have felt their knees trembling.
