To truly grasp the architecture of Mrs. Pryor's despair, one must first understand the absolute fragility of her spirit, a vessel cracked by the weight of expectation and the sharp edge of conscience. While Shirley conversed elsewhere, Caroline returned to the upper chambers to find the Governess submerged in a profound melancholy. She would not admit that Miss Keeldar's haste had wounded her feelings, yet the evidence was undeniable: an invisible blade had galled her soul. To any mind not attuned to her specific frequency, she would have appeared insensible to the quiet, tender attentions Miss Helstone offered, but Caroline knew the truth—that beneath her stoic mask, Mrs. Pryor felt, valued, and was healed by these gestures.

"I am deficient in self-confidence and decision," she confessed finally, her voice trembling with the residue of internal conflict. "I have always been so. Yet I believe Miss Keeldar should have known my character well enough to realize that I possess an even painful solicitude to do right, to act for the best. The unusual nature of the demand on my judgment, compounded by the alarms of the night, paralyzed me. I could not venture to act promptly for another; but I trust no serious harm resulted from my lapse of firmness."

Before she could elaborate further, a gentle knock resonated at the door, followed by the latch lifting.

"Caroline, come here," a low voice commanded.

Miss Helstone stepped out into the corridor. Standing there in the gallery was Shirley, her posture radiating contrition, her expression ashamed, sorrowful as any repentant child returning to a parent's knee.

"How fares Mrs. Pryor?" Shirley asked immediately.

"Rather out of spirits," replied Caroline.

"I have behaved very shamefully, very ungenerously, very ungratefully to her," Shirley declared, her tone thick with regret. "How insolent of me to turn on her thus for what, after all, was no fault—merely an excess of conscientiousness on her part. But I regret my error most sincerely. Tell her so, and ask if she will forgive me."

Caroline dispatched the errand with heartfelt pleasure. Mrs. Pryor rose, moving toward the door with the hesitation of someone who dreads confrontation as a storm dreads the open sky. She said falteringly, "Come in, my dear."

Shirley entered with impetuous energy, throwing her arms around her governess. As she kissed her heartily, she insisted, "You know you *must* forgive me, Mrs. Pryor. I could not function at all if there were a misunderstanding between us."

"I have nothing to forgive," was the reply, soft but resolute. "Let us pass it over now, if you please. The final result of this incident is that it proves more plainly than ever how unequal I am to certain crises."

And that was the painful sentiment that would remain etched on Mrs. Pryor's mind. No effort of Shirley's or Caroline's could efface it. She could forgive her offending pupil, but she could not forgive her own innocent self for failing.

Soon, Miss Keeldar, destined to be in constant request throughout the morning, was summoned downstairs again. The Rector called first. A lively welcome and a livelier reprimand awaited him; he expected both, and being in high spirits, accepted them with equal grace. In the course of his brief visit, he entirely forgot to inquire after his niece; the riot, the rioters, the mill, the magistrates, and the heiress had absorbed all his thoughts, excluding family ties. He alluded to the part he and the Curate had played in the defense of the Hollow.

"The vials of Pharisaical wrath will be emptied on our heads for our share in this business," he proclaimed, defiant. "But I defy every calumniator. I was there only to support the law, to play my part as a man and a Briton; characters I deem quite compatible with those of the Priest and Levite in their highest sense. Your tenant, Moore," he continued, "has won my approbation. A cooler commander I would not wish to see, nor a more determined. Besides, the man has shown sound judgment and good sense—first, in being thoroughly prepared for the event which has taken place; and subsequently, when his well-concerted plans secured him success, in knowing how to use without abusing his victory. Some of the magistrates are now well frightened, and, like all cowards, show a tendency to be cruel. Moore restrains them with admirable prudence. He has hitherto been very unpopular in the neighborhood; but mark my words, the tide of opinion will now take a turn in his favor. People will find out that they have not appreciated him, and will hasten to remedy their error; and he, when he perceives the public disposed to acknowledge his merits, will show a more gracious mien than that with which he has hitherto favored us."

Mr. Helstone was about to add to this speech some half-jesting, half-serious warnings to Miss Keeldar regarding her rumored partiality for her talented tenant, when a ring at the door announced another caller. This interruption checked his raillery. Appearing in the doorway was a white-haired elderly gentleman, with a rather truculent countenance and a disdainful eye—in short, their old acquaintance and the Rector's old enemy, Mr. Yorke. The Priest and Levite seized his hat, offering the briefest of adieux to Miss Keeldar and the sternest of nods to her guest before taking an abrupt leave.

Mr. Yorke was in no mild mood, and in no measured terms did he express his opinion on the transactions of the night. Moore, the magistrates, the soldiers, the mob leaders—all came in for a share of his invectives; but he reserved his strongest epithets—and real, racy Yorkshire Doric adjectives—for the benefit of the fighting parsons, the "sanguinary, demoniac" Rector and Curate. According to him, the cup of ecclesiastical guilt was now full indeed.

"The Church," he barked, "is in a bonny pickle now. It was time it came down when parsons took to swaggering among soldiers, blazing away with bullets and gunpowder, taking the lives of far honester men than themselves."

"What would Moore have done if nobody had helped him?" asked Shirley, her voice cutting through the tension.

"Drunk as he'd brewed, eaten as he'd baked."

"Which means you would have left him by himself to face that mob. Good! He has plenty of courage, but the greatest amount of gallantry that ever garrisoned one human breast could scarce avail against two hundred."

"He had the soldiers, those poor slaves who hire out their own blood and spill other folk's for money."

"You abuse soldiers almost as much as you abuse clergymen. All who wear red coats are national refuse in your eyes, and all who wear black are national swindlers. Mr. Moore, according to you, did wrong to get military aid, and he did still worse to accept of any other aid. Your way of talking amounts to this: he should have abandoned his mill and his life to the rage of a set of misguided madmen, and Mr. Helstone and every other gentleman in the parish should have looked on, and seen the building razed and its owner slaughtered, and never stirred a finger to save either."

"If Moore had behaved to his men from the beginning as a master ought to behave, they never would have entertained their present feelings towards him."

"Easy for you to talk," exclaimed Miss Keeldar, who was beginning to wax warm in her tenant's cause—"you, whose family has lived at Briarmains for six generations, to whose person the people have been accustomed for fifty years, who know all their ways, prejudices, and preferences. Easy, indeed, for *you* to act so as to avoid offending them. But Mr. Moore came a stranger into the district; he came here poor and friendless, with nothing but his own energies to back him, nothing but his honor, his talent, and his industry to make his way. A monstrous crime indeed that, under such circumstances, he could not popularize his naturally grave, quiet manners all at once; could not be jocular, free, and cordial with a strange peasantry, as you are with your fellow-townsmen! An unpardonable transgression that when he introduced improvements he did not go about the business in quite the most politic way, did not graduate his changes as delicately as a rich capitalist might have done! For errors of this sort is he to be the victim of mob outrage? Is he to be denied even the privilege of defending himself? Are those who have the hearts of men in their breasts—and Mr. Helstone, say what you will of him, has such a heart—to be reviled like malefactors because they stand by him, because they venture to espouse the cause of one against two hundred?"

"Come, come now, be cool," said Mr. Yorke, smiling at the earnestness with which Shirley multiplied her rapid questions.
