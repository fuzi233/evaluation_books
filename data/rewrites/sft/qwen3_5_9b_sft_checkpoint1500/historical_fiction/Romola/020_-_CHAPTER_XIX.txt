Messer Bernardo del Nero remained as stern and unyielding as Romola had anticipated regarding the postponement of the marriage until Easter. In this affair, Bardo stood wholly under the sway of his shrewd and practical friend. Yet, even Bernardo, though immune to the personal charm that captivated others, could not entirely ignore the seductive argument of success—a force invariably potent with worldly men. Tito was swiftly ascending the heights of high society, gaining particular favor with the young Cardinal Giovanni de' Medici, who had already suggested including him in his learned entourage for an impending journey to Rome. This bright Greek youth, possessed of a tongue ever ready yet never quarrelsome, became increasingly coveted at the lavish suppers of the Via Larga and the Florentine games where he did not strive to excel, but rather admired the incomparable skill of Piero de' Medici with the utmost grace.

By an unbroken sequence of cause and effect, Tito's reputation as an agreeable companion in "magnificent" circles rendered his learning and talents appear even more radiant. He was genuinely accomplished enough to render any exaggeration of his abilities harmless. To the older Bernardo, old prejudices and attachments began to outweigh the newer, weaker bias against the young Greek stranger, who was perhaps a trifle too supple. To the ancient Florentine, it was impossible to dismiss the weight of standing well with the foremost families of the city. Since Tito was now fully embraced by that circle whose views constituted the unquestioned standard of social value, it seemed irrational to deny that the prospect of such a son-in-law for Bardo and such a husband for Romola offered no further checks to satisfaction.

Undeniably, Tito's arrival marked the dawn of a new life for both father and daughter, surpassing even the initial promise. The blind old scholar, whose proud truthfulness had never allowed him to participate in the commerce of feigned and preposterous admiration—the thread woven through all learned discourse alongside its opposite, vituperation—had gradually fallen into neglect even among his fellow citizens. When mentioned, the usual sentiment was that while his blindness and the loss of his son were pitiable, he was tiresome in contending for the value of his labors; his discontent appeared somewhat inconsistent in a man who had formerly been open to religious indifference and had refused various offers of patronage solely on the slight condition that he accept holy orders, which patrons deemed essential for supporting scholars. But since Tito's advent, the monotonous echo of Bardo's name had ceased. It was understood that the old man had laid aside his plaints, and the fair daughter was no longer to be locked away in dowerless pride, waiting for a *parentado*. The winning manners and growing favor of the handsome Greek, destined to become both son and husband, transformed the new interest into something thoroughly friendly. It became common for visitors to enliven the quiet library. Elderly men, prompted by some indefinable urge to renew former acquaintances when an old friend becomes the subject of fresh talk, arrived in droves. Young men whom Tito had graciously permitted to visit once found it easy to return when they met him on his way to the Via de' Bardi; placing a hand upon his shoulder, they fell easily into conversation with him.

For it was delightful to behold Romola's beauty. She sat with a certain grandeur, spoke with gravity, smiled with modesty, and seemed to cast around her an odor of queenliness, just as the old Firenzuola described the majesty of a great woman. Under this genial breath of admiration and homage, she seemed to unfold like a strong white lily. To her, all else paled before her new, bright life intertwined with Tito's love.

Indeed, Tito had become the instrument strengthening the hope in Bardo's mind that, before his death, he might secure the long-desired safety for his library. He feared it would not be merged into another collection, nor transferred to a body of monks to bear the name of a monastery, but would remain forever the Bardi Library, available for the use of Florentines. While the old habit of trusting in the Medici could not vanish while their influence remained the state's strongest lever, Tito, having won the ear of Cardinal Giovanni de' Medici, might accomplish more than Messer Bernardo toward securing this desired interest. Tito could demonstrate to a learned audience the peculiar value of Bardi's collection. Speaking with sanguinity of such a result, Tito sought to cheer the old man, conscious that Romola repaid these gentle words to her father with a sort of adoration that no direct tribute to herself could have elicited.

This question of the library became the subject of numerous discussions between Bernardo del Nero and Romola after Christmas passed and the marriage prospects drew nearer—but always conducted outside Bardo's hearing. For Bardo clung to a vague, undisturbed belief that his property, aside from the library, was sufficient to meet all demands. He would not, save under a momentary pressure of angry despondency, admit to himself that the will disinheriting Dino left Romola heir to nothing but debts, nor that he required anything from patronage beyond the assurance of a separate locality for his library in exchange for a deed gifting it to the Florentine Republic.

"My opinion is," Bernardo told Romola during a consultation beneath the loggia, "that since you are to marry and Messer Tito will possess a competent income, we should begin to wind up your affairs. We ought to ascertain exactly the sum necessary to save the library from being touched, rather than allowing the debts to accumulate any longer. Your father requires nothing but his shred of mutton and his macaroni daily, and I believe Messer Tito may undertake to supply that for the remaining years of his life; it may serve in lieu of the *morgen-cap*."

"Tito has always known that my life is bound up with my father's," Romola replied. "And he is kinder to my father than I am; he delights in making him happy."

"Ah, he is not made of the same clay as other men, is he?" Bernardo remarked, smiling. "Your father believed he could shut woman's folly out of you by cramming you with Greek and Latin. Yet, you proved as ready to believe in the first pair of bright eyes and the first soft words that reached you as if you knew nothing by heart but Paternosters, like other Christian daughters."

"Now, godfather," Romola said, shaking her head playfully, "as if it were merely bright eyes and soft words that make me love Tito! You know better. You know I love my father and you because you are both good, and I love Tito too because he is so good. I see it, I feel it, in everything he says and does. And if he is handsome, too, why should I not love him the better for that? It seems to me beauty is part of the finished language by which goodness speaks. You know you must have been a very handsome youth yourself, godfather"—she looked up with one of her happy, loving smiles at the stately old man—"you were about as tall as Tito, and you had very fine eyes; only you looked a little sterner and prouder, and..."

"And Romola likes to keep all the pride to herself?" Bernardo teased, not impervious to this charming coaxing. "However, it is well that in one way Tito's demands are more modest than those of any Florentine husband of fitting rank we might have found for you; he wants no dowry."

Thus, matters were settled between Messer Bernardo del Nero, Romola, and Tito. Bardo assented with a wave of the hand when Bernardo informed him that it would be wise now to begin selling property and clearing debts, accustomed as he was to view financial matters as a thick wood that his imagination never penetrated, let alone traverse. Meanwhile, Tito set about winning Messer Bernardo's respect, employing his ready faculties to inquire into Florentine money-matters, the secrets of the *Monti* (public funds), the valuations of real estate, and the profits of banking.

"You will soon forget that Tito is not a Florentine, godfather," Romola observed. "See how he is learning everything about Florence."

"It seems to me he is one of the *demoni*, who belong to no particular country, child," Bernardo replied, smiling. "His mind is a little too nimble to be weighted with all the stuff we men carry about in our hearts."

Romola smiled, too, bathed in happy confidence.
