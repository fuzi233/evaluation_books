To truly grasp the architecture of their impending exile, one must first witness the intoxicating, delirious height of their freedom. Gerard and Margaret descended upon Sevenbergen in the first flush of recovered liberty, their spirits buoyant by the sheer audacity of their escape. Yet, this euphoria was a fragile veneer, quickly cracking under the weight of grim realities. Gerard was a fugitive, a ghost haunting the edges of the law, liable to be recaptured and subjected to the brutal machinery of punishment. Thus, separation became not merely a necessity, but a cruel imperative. Furthermore, a profound loathing for his native soil had taken root in Gerard's soul; he viewed the land itself as an antagonist. Margaret, while wishing him to flee the country entirely, recoiled at the specific destination of Italy, her heart fainting at the thought of him crossing the channel alone. Conversely, Gerard's resolve to abandon Margaret was forged solely in the crucible of his ambition to visit Italy—a pilgrimage where he believed he could secure wealth, renown, and the removal of every insurmountable barrier to their union.

He recounted to her the chilling counsel of the demoiselle Van Eyck, repeating her warnings until the logic was undeniable: the gold pieces bestowed upon him were currency for a journey, not a dowry. The path forward was clearly laid out for his survival and elevation. As a craftsman and artist stifled in this boorish, provincial cage, he knew that Italy alone possessed the eyes to recognize his worth. On this pragmatic foundation, the selfless girl granted her consent, though the agreement was sealed with a torrent of tender tears. Hand in hand, they sat until the clock struck midnight, oscillating between despairing eulogies of their hard fate and painting luminous, hopeful canvases of a future that felt increasingly distant. In the midst of these visions, Margaret's sorrow would flood her eyes, silencing even Gerard's eloquent pleas, leaving them to wonder what sin had summoned such a coalition of enemies to conspire against their love.

Dawn broke with a resignation that neither dared fully articulate; the hour of parting hovered like a suspended sentence, neither spoken nor inevitable. But at approximately three in the afternoon, the silence was shattered by the arrival of Giles. Having circled many miles to evade suspicion, he rode up to the door, his presence a thunderclap that turned two pale faces toward the terrible messenger.

"Brother Gerard!" Giles cried, his voice booming with the authority of a prophet delivering doom. "Kate bids you run for your life. They charge you with theft; you have handed them a handle. Do not attempt explanation. Seek not justice in Tergou; the parchments you seized are but a blind. She has seen your death written in the men's eyes; a price hangs over your head. Fly! For Margaret's sake, and for all who love you, do not loiter another second!"

It was a verdict delivered without trial. Giles, breaking from his rote instructions, added his own counsel: "All the constables swarmed our house after you, and so did Dirk Brower. Kate is wise, Gerard. Listen to her advice and fly!"

"Oh, yes, Gerard!" Margaret screamed, her composure shattering. "Fly on the instant! Ah! Those parchments... my mind misgave me. Why did I let you take them?"

"Margaret, they are but a ruse," Giles insisted. "No matter. That old caitiff shall never see them again. I will not depart until I have hidden his treasure where he can never find it."

After thanking Giles warmly, Gerard bid him farewell, instructing him to return and tell Kate he was already gone—"For I shall be gone ere you reach home," he declared. He then summoned Martin, briefing him on the crisis and begging him to patrol the road toward Tergou. "Ay!" Martin agreed, his demeanor shifting to one of grim readiness. "And if I spot Dirk Brower or any of his men, I will shoot an arrow into the oak-tree in our garden. On that signal, you must bolt into the nearby forest and meet me at the Weird Hunter's Spring. There, I will guide you through the woods."

Armed with this contingency plan, Gerard drew a breath he hadn't known he needed. He returned to Margaret, who stood trembling before the oak tree, watching every branch as if expecting an arrow to strike. Gerard began digging a deep hole, burying the parchments one by one. Most were charters and records of the burgh, but one document caught his eye—a private deed between Floris Brandt, father of Peter, and Ghysbrecht.

"Why, this is as much yours as his," Gerard noted, reaching for it. "I will read this."

"Oh, not now, Gerard! Not now!" Margaret cried, her voice rising in panic. "Every moment you waste fills me with terror. Look—the rain begins to fall in great drops, and the clouds lower!"

Yielding to her urgent reprimand, Gerard shoved the deed into his bosom, covering the others with earth and stamping the soil down firmly. Just as he worked, a flash of lightning illuminated the sky, followed immediately by a roll of distant thunder, and the rain descended in sheets. Margaret and Gerard fled into the house, swiftly followed by Martin.

"The road is clear," Martin reported, "but a heavy storm is brewing."

His prophecy proved accurate. Thunder grew louder, crashing overhead like a collapsing roof; flashes strobed in rapid succession, striking like the lashes of a whip, while rain poured in torrents. Margaret buried her face, refusing to witness the violence of the heavens. Inside, Gerard raised the rough shutter and lit a candle. The lovers conferred in the flickering light, Gerard blessing the storm that granted them a few stolen hours together. The sun set unnoticed, swallowed by the darkness, yet the thunder continued to peal and the lightning to flash relentlessly.

Supper was laid out, yet neither could eat; the knowledge that this might be their final meal together choked them. As the storm momentarily lulled, Peter retired to rest, but Gerard and Margaret could not afford sleep. Martin lingered, carefully fitting a new string to his bow, a task requiring his utmost precision. The lovers murmured their sorrows and their undying love beside him.

Suddenly, Martin raised a hand, signaling for silence. The room fell quiet, but the air remained thick with tension. Then, a footstep cracked faintly upon the autumn leaves scattered across the garden behind the house. To those unburdened by fear, such a sound is trivial; to those hunted, it is a death knell. It was a foot attempting to be noiseless.

Martin nocked an arrow and snuffed out the candle. In that sudden darkness, to their horror, they heard more than one footstep approaching the other door of the cottage—not quite as silent as the first, but moving with deliberate stealth—and then, a dead, suffocating pause. Their blood froze in their veins.

"Oh, Kate, oh, Kate! You said fly on the instant!" Margaret moaned, wringing her hands in anguish, terror, and wild remorse for having delayed Gerard's departure.

"Hush, girl!" Martin whispered, his tone stern and commanding.

A heavy knock fell upon the door, resonating not just against the wood, but directly upon the hearts within.
