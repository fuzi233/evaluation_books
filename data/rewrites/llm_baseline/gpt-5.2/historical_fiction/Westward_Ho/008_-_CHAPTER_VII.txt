It had been too late and too black, the night before, to take the measure of Stow. Now October laid it open—clear as a clean trencher set down at noon—while the house woke into its own familiar din: doors thudding soft on their hinges, the far clink of iron in the yard, pigeons chuckling under the eaves, and the wind making a slow, sea-salted music along the walls.

Sir Richard and Amyas walked the south terrace toward eleven of the forenoon. Amyas had slept deep till luncheon-time—till an hour ago, indeed—his youth taking rest as naturally as a hearth takes flame when kindling is offered. Sir Richard had been out at six, down in the valley, giving his blood its morning heat with a brace of terriers and sundry badgers, and coming home with that keen, clean smell about him which men bring from wet fern and turned earth.

Old Stow House stood—stood then, though in later years it would be transformed and pulled down and sold off like yesterday’s feast gone cold; but that future was not yet, and what mattered now was the old building itself, as it had grown by degrees like an oak: part fortress, part dwelling, a long, rambling creature of stone. On three sides the old walls still rose with their dark mouths for casting fire and stone, relics of days when every supper might be eaten under threat of steel. But the southern court, safe enough now, had been tamed into a flower-garden, terraced and clipped into proud shapes—yews and hollies shorn as neatly as a cook trussing a capon, knots of flowers arranged with a pedant’s pleasure in pattern, the very earth made obedient.

To the east the old grim keep—ruined in the Wars of the Roses—had given place to Tudor richness: broad windows for light, and stonework that seemed less to forbid a stranger than to receive him, if he came in peace. The house, like the age, wore two minds at once: the old warlike caution and the new appetite for breadth and brightness.

Below the terrace the land fell away steep and generous, and the prospect had the full English flavor of it—salt and sweet together. At one turn, over the western walls, the ocean showed blue and broken with white, sails passing like pale moths across a darker cloth. At the next, the valley unrolled in layers: park after park, avenues set with great trees already yellowing, woodland in autumn thick as spice, purple heather on the moors, and, higher still, a black old earthwork furze-grown on its conical peak. Eastward, closing the valley, Kilkhampton church-tower stood up, tall and grave, bearing on its stones the offerings and memorials of Grenvilles piled century upon century, like dishes added to a long family board.

A soft yellow haze lay over everything, as if the day had been basted with light. Far below, red cattle lowed to each other and brushed off flies with their tails in the rivulet. In the horse-park to the right, colts whinnied as they played, and from the Queen’s Park opposite, the stallions answered in deeper voices, fainter with distance. From the woods came the hoarse thunder of a rutting stag, and from farther up the valley a rival’s trumpet of defiance—sound carrying clean in that clear air, the whole place stitched together by its voices: sea-wind, beasts, birds, and the far-off human stir of a great household at work.

And close at hand, upon the terrace before the house, Lady Grenville sat among spaniels and golden-haired children. She was St. Leger of Annery, beautiful as the day was calm, and she looked down on her children, and then up at her husband, and out again across the wide, kind West, until happiness itself seemed almost too full a draught.

Yet above all that ease and brightness Amyas and Sir Richard paced, back and forth, long and earnest, as men walk when they come to the turn of a life and must set a foot down wisely. Their steps had a rhythm of their own upon the stone; and under the words, another sound kept time—the unspoken knowledge that childhood had loosened its hold, and manhood was calling, harsh and high.

“Yes,” said Sir Richard at last, when Amyas had told him plainly—bluntly, as he did most things—the whole tale of Rose Salterne and of his brother. “Yes, sweet lad. Thou hast chosen the better part, thou and thy brother also, and it shall not be taken from you. Only be strong, lad, and trust in God that He will make a man of you.”

“I do trust,” said Amyas; and the words had not the thinness of a lesson learned, but the steadiness of something tasted and found nourishing.

“Thank God,” Sir Richard answered, “that thou hast taken from my heart the one great anxiety for thee, from the day thy good father, who sleeps in peace, committed thee to my hands. For all best things, Amyas, become, when misused, the very worst; and the love of woman—because it is able to lift a man’s soul to the heavens—is also able to drag him down to hell. But thou hast learnt better. Our old German forefathers had the right of it: Sera juvenum Venus; ideoque inexhausta pubertas.”

He went on, with a heat that was not cruelty but hard-won certainty, against the French and Italian fashion—boys forever dangling at women’s sleeves, thinking themselves men only when they could smooth and flatter, toying with maids and wives alike as if hearts were dice to be thrown and pocketed. Vanity bred from it, and expense; envy, heart-burning; hatred and murder often enough. And even when no blood came of it, something worse: a man’s reverence squandered until the wife at last got only the dry scrapings—the caput mortuum—of what should have been kept whole for her alone. Then she became an ornament for his table, a means to beget heirs, and no more.

“May God,” he said, “who has saved me from that death in life, save thee also.”

As he spoke he looked down toward his wife upon the terrace, and she—as if she had heard the very turn of his thought—lifted her face and smiled up. The smile was so quiet and so full that Sir Richard’s sternness broke open, and his countenance shone as if a cloud had parted.

Amyas looked at them both and sighed; and then, as if to keep his own heart from dwelling too long on what he had renounced, he turned short to business.

“And I may go to Ireland to-morrow?”

“You shall sail in the Mary for Milford Haven, with these letters to Winter. If the wind serves, bid the master drop down the river to-night and be off; for we must lose no time.”

“Winter?” said Amyas, the old anger quickening. “He is no friend of mine, since he left Drake and us so cowardly at the Straits of Magellan.”

“Duty must not wait on private quarrels, even just ones. But he will not be thy general. When thou comest to the marshal or the Lord Deputy, give either of them this letter, and they will set thee work—and hard work too, I warrant.”

“I want nothing better.”

“Right, lad. The best reward for having wrought well already is to have more to do. He that has been faithful over a few things must find his account in being made ruler over many. That is the true and heroical rest, the only rest worthy of gentlemen and sons of God. As for those who look—in this world or the next—for idleness, hoping God shall feed them with pleasant things as with a spoon, I count them cowards and base, though they call themselves saints and elect.”

“I wish you could persuade my poor cousin of that.”

“He has yet to learn what losing his life to save it means. Bad men have taught him—and I fear these Anabaptists and Puritans at home teach little else—that the one great business is to save a man’s own soul after he dies, each for himself; and that, not divine self-sacrifice, is the one thing needful.”

“I think men are inclined enough already to be selfish, without being taught that.”

“Right, lad. If I could hang up such a teacher on high as an enemy of mankind and a corrupter of youth, I would do it gladly. Is there not cowardice and self-seeking enough in the hearts of us fallen sons of Adam, that these false prophets, with their baits of heaven and terrors of hell, must make our dirtiest vices into heavenly virtues? Farewell to chivalry and desperate valour; farewell to patriotism and loyalty; farewell to England and the manhood of England—if once it become the fashion for our preachers to bid every man, as the Jesuits do, take care first of what they call the safety of his soul. Then every man will be afraid to die at his post, because he will fear he is not fit to die.”

The words rang on the terrace like a blade struck sharp against stone, and beneath them, as if in answer, came the steady domestic sounds from the house: the clatter of a trencher somewhere within, a child’s laughter cut short by a nurse’s murmur, the faint scold of a peacock. Life went on with its common music while great doctrines were weighed that might send men to their deaths.

“Amyas,” Sir Richard ended, “do thou do thy duty like a man—to thy country, thy queen, and thy God—and count thy life a worthless thing, as did the holy men of old. Do thy work, lad, and leave thy soul to the care of Him who is just and merciful in this, that He rewards every man according to his work. Is there respect of persons with God? Now come in, take the letters, and to horse. And if I hear of thee dead there at Smerwick fort, with all thy wounds in front, I shall weep for thy mother, lad; but I shall have never a sigh for thee.”

They went in. Sir Richard turned again to his writing among books and despatches and warrants; for though he had not yet gathered on himself all the offices that later custom flung upon a great man at once, there were few matters of weight in which his hand was not felt, few great men with whom he did not correspond.

But the day had more to bring.

At the threshold they met the old steward, searching the passages as if the walls themselves might offer him counsel.

“There is a manner of roog, Sir Richard—a masterless man—at the door; a very forward fellow, and must needs speak with you.”

“A masterless man? He had better not speak to me unless he is in love with gaol and gallows.”

“Well, your worship,” the steward persisted, “I expect that is what he does want, for he swears he will not leave the gate till he has seen you.”

“Seen me? Halidame! he shall see me—here and at Launceston too—if he likes. Bring him in.”

“Fegs, Sir Richard, we are half afeard. With your good leave—”

“Hillo, Tony,” cried Amyas, laughing, “who was ever afeard yet with Sir Richard’s good leave?”

“What, has the fellow a tail or horns?”

“Massy no: but I be afeard of treason for your honour; for the fellow is pinked all over in heathen patterns, and as brown as a filbert; and a tall roog, a very strong roog, sir, and a foreigner too, and a mighty staff with him. I expect him to be a manner of Jesuit, or wild Irish, sir; and indeed the grooms have no stomach to handle him, nor the dogs neither; or he had been under the pump before now. They that saw him coming up the hill swear he had fire coming out of his mouth.”

“Fire out of his mouth?” Sir Richard said, dryly. “The men are drunk.”

“Pinked all over? He must be a sailor,” said Amyas; for the sea has its own signatures on a man’s skin. “Let me out and see him; and if he needs putting forth—”

“Why, I dare say he is not so big but he will go into thy pocket. Go, lad, while I finish my writing.”

Amyas went out, and at the back door, leaning on his staff, stood a tall, raw-boned, ragged man. His skin showed through the rents of his clothes, marked with blue and black patterns—strange work, like map-lines on flesh—and his face was baked brown as a nut. The air about him carried the sourness of old tar and long-wet cloth, with that sharp, human smell of a man who has lived hard and washed seldom because water has been for drinking, not for comfort.

“Hillo, lad,” said Amyas. “Before we come to talk, thou wilt please to lay down that Plymouth cloak of thine.” And he nodded at the cudgel, which the West-country seamen commonly called by that name.

“I’ll warrant,” said the steward behind him, “that where he found his cloak he found purse not far off.”

“But not hose or doublet,” Amyas answered, eyeing the man’s rags. “So the magical virtue of thy staff has not helped thee much. Put it down, man, and speak like a Christian, if thou be one.”

“I am a Christian, though I look like a heathen; and no rogue, though a masterless man, alas. I want nothing—deserving nothing—and only ask to speak with Sir Richard, before I go on my way.”

There was something in the man’s voice—stately, yet humbled—as if he had once been proud and had learned what breaks pride without breaking the backbone. It drew Amyas in spite of himself.

“Whither go you? And whence come you?”

“From Padstow Port, sir, to Clovelly town, to see my old mother, if indeed she be yet alive—which God knoweth.”

“Clovelly man!” cried the grooms, as if a kinship had been declared over a shared loaf. “Why didn’t thee say thee was Clovelly man?”

The steward, half softened by county fellowship, asked, “What’s thy mother’s name then?”

“Susan Yeo.”

“What, that lived under the archway?” said a groom.

“Lived?” the man repeated, and the word came out carefully, as if he were tasting it for truth.

“Iss, sure; her died three days since, so we heard, poor soul.”

He stood still. Not a sob, not a sway—only a quietness that was more dreadful than crying, like a candle snuffed without a wisp of smoke. Then he said, very low, in Spanish, as if that tongue had become the place where grief lodged easiest: “That which is, is best.”

“You speak Spanish?” Amyas asked, more and more taken.

“I had need to do so, young sir. I have been five years in the Spanish Main, and only set foot on shore two days ago. And if you will let me have speech of Sir Richard, I will tell him that at which both the ears of him that heareth it shall tingle. And if not, I can but go on to Mr. Cary of Clovelly, if he be yet alive, and there disburden my soul. But I would sooner have spoken with one that is a mariner like to myself.”

“And you shall,” said Amyas. “Steward, we will have this man in; for all his rags, he is a man of wit.”

“I only hope he ben’t one of those Popish murderers,” muttered the steward, keeping distance as if the stranger carried infection.

“Popish, old master? There’s little fear of my being that. Look here!” The man dragged back his rags and showed a ghastly scar encircling his wrist and winding up the forearm—a rope-burn made eternal, a signature burned into him by pain.

“I got that on the rack,” he said quietly, “in the Inquisition at Lima.”

“O Father! Father!” cried the steward, suddenly penitential, “why didn’t you tell us that you were a poor Christian?”

“Because I have had naught but my deserts; and but a taste of them either, as the Lord knoweth who delivered me. I wasn’t going to make myself a beggar and a show on their account.”

“By heaven, you are a brave fellow,” Amyas said; and he felt, with a sailor’s quick sympathy, how the man’s voice sat in his throat like something long held back. “Come along straight to Sir Richard’s room.”

So they went in to the library, where Sir Richard sat among his papers, the room smelling of ink and leather and old vellum—dry scents, but honest, and to a man of action as necessary as salt in meat.

“Hillo, Amyas,” Sir Richard called up, “have you bound the wild man already, and brought him in to swear allegiance?”

Before Amyas could answer, the stranger fixed his eyes upon him.

“Amyas?” he said. “Is that your name, sir?”

“Amyas Leigh is my name, at your service, good fellow.”

“Of Burrough by Bideford?”

“Why then? What do you know of me?”

“Oh sir, sir—young brains and happy ones have short memories; but old and sad brains too long ones often. Do you mind one that was with Mr. Oxenham, sir? A swearing reprobate he was—God forgive him, and hath forgiven him too, for His dear Son’s sake—one, sir, that gave you a horn, a toy with a chart on it?”

“Soul alive!” Amyas cried, and caught his hand. “And are you he? The horn? Why, I have it still, and will keep it to my dying day. But where is Mr. Oxenham?”

“Yes,” said Sir Richard, rising, his face tightening as the name stirred older anxieties. “Where is Mr. Oxenham? You are over-hasty in welcoming your old acquaintance, Amyas, before we have heard whether he can give honest account of himself and of his captain. There is more than one way by which sailors may come home without their captains—as poor Mr. Barker of Bristol found to his cost. God grant there have been no such traitorous dealing here.”

“Sir Richard Grenville,” said the man, and there was iron under his humility, “if I had been a guilty man to my noble captain—as I have been to God—I had not come here this day to you, from whom villainy has never found favour, nor ever will; for I know your conditions well, sir. Trust in the Lord: if you will be pleased to hear me, you shall know mine.”

“Thou art a well-spoken knave,” Sir Richard answered. “We shall see.”

“My dear sir,” Amyas whispered, “I will warrant this man guiltless.”

“I verily believe him to be,” Sir Richard returned; “but this is too serious a matter to be left on guess. If he will be sworn—”

At that the man bowed his head a little. “If it would please Sir Richard, I would rather not be sworn.”

“But it does not please me, rascal! Did I not warn thee, Amyas?”

“Sir,” the man said, and now he stood straight, proud as a mast despite the rags, “God forbid that my word should not be as good as my oath. But it is against my conscience to be sworn.”

“What have we here? Some fantastical Anabaptist, wiser than his teachers.”

“My conscience, sir—”

“The devil take it and thee! I never heard a man yet begin to prate of his conscience but I knew he was about to do something more than ordinarily cruel or false.”

“Sir,” the man replied coolly, “do you sit here to judge me according to law, and yet contrary to the law swear profane oaths, for which a fine is provided?”

Amyas expected thunder. But Sir Richard, with a grim self-command, pulled a shilling from his purse and laid it on the table. The coin rang on the wood like a small bell.

“There—my fine is paid, sirrah, to the poor of Kilkhampton. But hearken all the same. If thou wilt not speak an oath, thou shalt speak on compulsion; for to Launceston gaol thou goest, there to answer for Mr. Oxenham’s death, on suspicion whereof—and of mutiny causing it—I will attach thee and every soul of his crew that comes home. We have lost too many gallant captains of late by treachery of their crews; and he that will not clear himself on oath must be held for guilty, and self-condemned.”

“My good fellow,” Amyas broke in, unable to let it stand, “why, for such fantastical scruples, peril not only your life but your honour, and Mr. Oxenham’s also. For if you be examined by question, you may be forced by torment to say that which is not true.”

“Little fear of that, young sir,” the man answered, and his mouth turned with a hard, knowing humour. “I have had too much of the rack already, and the strappado too, to care much what man can do unto me. I would heartily that I thought it lawful to be sworn; but not so thinking, I can but submit to the cruelty of man—though I did expect more merciful things, as a most miserable and wrecked mariner, at the hands of one who hath himself seen God’s ways in the sea, and His wonders in the great deep. Sir Richard Grenville, if you will hear my story—may God avenge on my head all my sins from my youth up until now, and cut me off from the blood of Christ, and, if it were possible, from the number of His elect—if I tell you one whit more or less than truth. And if not, I commend myself into the hands of God.”

Sir Richard smiled, half in scorn, half in reluctant admiration. “Well, thou art a brave ass, and valiant, though an ass manifest. Dost thou not see, fellow, how thou hast sworn a ten-times bigger oath than ever I should have asked of thee? But this is the way with your Anabaptists, who by hatred of forms and ceremonies show of how much account they think them, and then bind themselves, out of fantastical self-will, with heavier burdens than ever lawful authorities laid on them for the commonweal. But what do they care for the commonweal, so they can save—so they fancy—each man his own dirty soul for himself? However, thou art sworn now with a vengeance. Go on with thy tale: and first, who art thou, and whence?”

“Well, sir,” the man said, unmoved, “my name is Salvation Yeo, born in Clovelly Street, in the year 1526, where my father exercised the mystery of a barber-surgeon, and a preacher of the people since called Anabaptists—for which I return humble thanks to God.”

“Fie!” said Sir Richard. “Thou naughty knave; return thanks that thy father was an ass?”

“Nay,” Yeo replied, “but because he was a barber-surgeon. I myself learnt a touch of that trade, and thereby saved my life, as I will tell presently. And I do think a good mariner ought to have all knowledge of carnal and worldly cunning—even to tailoring and shoemaking—that he may turn his hand to whatsoever may hap.”

“Well spoken, fellow,” Sir Richard allowed. “But let us have thy text without thy comments. Forwards!”

“Well, sir. I was bred to the sea from my youth, and was with Captain Hawkins in his three voyages which he made to Guinea for negro slaves, and thence to the West Indies.”

“Then thrice thou wentest to a bad end,” Sir Richard said sharply, “though Captain Hawkins be my good friend; and the last time to a bad end thou camest.”

“No denying that last, your worship,” Yeo answered. “But as for the former, I doubt—about the unlawfulness, I mean; being the negroes are of the children of Ham, who are cursed and reprobate, as Scripture declares, and their blackness testifies, being Satan’s own livery; among whom therefore there can be none of the elect, wherefore the elect are not required to treat them as brethren.”

Sir Richard’s contempt flared like tinder catching. “What a plague of a pragmatical sea-lawyer have we here? I doubt not, thou hypocrite, that though thou wilt call the negro’s black skin Satan’s livery when it serves thy turn to steal him, thou wilt find out sables to be Heaven’s livery every Sunday and set up a godly howl unless a parson preach in a black gown, Geneva fashion. Out upon thee! Go on with thy tale, lest thou finish thy sermon at Launceston after all.”
