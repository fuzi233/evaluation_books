At dawn the next day we turned our faces again toward Calabash Wood, for the sledge waited there with the dishes, bowls, and baskets we had shaped with such patient, homely pride. I took only Fritz with me. The others I left beside their mother; I meant to look beyond the rocky line that fenced our coast, and in such searching work a smaller tread seemed wiser.

The evergreen oaks received us with their clean, resinous breath and the soft, papery hiss of leaves rubbing one another—an honest, living hush that made our own footfalls sound like intruders. Our sow was there, snout deep among the acorns, crunching with a contented rhythm that seemed to mend yesterday’s alarm. If anything, she regarded us more kindly than usual, as though she had decided we were allies against the savage dogs.

Birdsong stitched the grove together—bright calls, dry clicks, a fluting that traveled from branch to branch like a hand passing bread. The birds scarcely stirred until Fritz, eager for sport and for the pot, fired and brought down a splendid blue jay, then two parakeets: one scarlet as embers, the other green and gold like sun through young leaves. He was ramming home his next charge when a new sound struck both our ears and froze us mid-step.

It came like a drum muffled under earth: dull, repeated thumps swelling into a low roll. In that instant my thoughts leapt, as they often did now, to the possibility of men—strangers, savages, a language we could not answer, a lexicon that might claim our shore by naming it. We moved with the caution of those who have only one home left. Keeping low among creepers and thick grass, we edged toward the noise until the undergrowth thinned into an open glade.

There, upon an old fallen log, stood a bird about the size of a cock. His chestnut coat was mottled with dark brown and grey like bark after rain, and on his shoulders rose curious tufts of velvety black glossed with green. He strutted and wheeled with grave ceremony, ruffling, erecting, showing himself off to a ring of plainer companions gathered about him like an audience around a village storyteller. He was not merely feeding or fleeing; he was performing, and the forest itself seemed to hold its breath to listen.

Then he spread his tail like a fan, stiffened his wings, and began striking them in short rapid beats. Faster—faster—until the air itself trembled. A rumble like very distant thunder rolled out, and for a moment his whirring wings wrapped him as a cloud. Now the mystery was plain: the “drum” was his courtship, amplified when the wing-strokes met the hollowed, decayed log beneath him.

I watched, captivated by this wild pageantry—by the way even a creature’s daily need could become art in a world not yet soured by engines and iron clamor. And then, behind me, a shot cracked the grove open.

The drummer toppled dead from his perch. The ring of birds exploded outward in alarm, vanishing into the leaves as if the forest had exhaled.

My indignation flared, sharp as the gun-smoke. Fritz’s impatience had turned rare comedy into needless tragedy. I blamed him openly; he had fired without leave, interrupting what few men ever see. I was all but certain it was a ruffed grouse, and a magnificent specimen—yet my regret was not only for the meat or the rarity, but for the sound itself, that delicate thunder now silenced forever.

We set the bird upon the donkey, who had waited with saintly stillness, and went on.

The sledge lay safe where we had left it, and the day was still young. I kept my resolve: we would follow the line of cliff and rocky hills that, at varying distance from the shore, bounded all the country we knew. I wanted to find a passage inland, or else learn, once for all, whether we were walled into this strip of coast like bees sealed in a glass hive.

Leaving Calabash Wood behind, we crossed ground lush with manioc, potatoes, and unknown plants with fat leaves and saps that stained the fingers. Clear streamlets ran through it, their music steady and medicinal, a reminder that this island’s quiet was not emptiness but order.

Soon a shrub drew my eye, heavy with small white berries that looked as though they had been dipped in pale wax. When Fritz plucked one, it clung to his fingers, sticky and strange. My heart lifted at once; here was Myrica cerifera. I explained what it promised us: candles—real light after sunset, not merely the brief glow of a fire and then sleep by necessity. His mother, who hated to be driven from her work the moment the sky dimmed, would rejoice.

We did not delay. With the donkey beside us we filled a great canvas bag with the wax berries. I told Fritz the wax would be greener, more brittle than beeswax, yet it would burn fairly and give off a pleasing scent, as though the plant remembered sunlight and could release it again at night.

Not far beyond we met another wonder: a whole colony of birds housed beneath one roof. Their nests—brown birds, the size of yellowhammers—were built in a clustered mass around a solitary tree, and a roof of grass and fibres covered the community as neatly as thatch over a village. Openings dotted the irregular sides, like doors into tiny apartments. Twigs protruded here and there and served as perches and lookouts. The entire establishment, from a distance, resembled a vast bath-sponge alive with motion.

Thousands of little bodies swarmed in and out, and among them flashed many small parrots, bright as fruit. Some seemed to be quarrelling with the rightful owners as impudently as tenants who have learned the landlord’s language better than the landlord.

Fritz, nimble as a cat and always hungry for the next marvel, climbed to inspect the holes, hoping to steal a young bird. He thrust his hand into several empty nests, growing bolder each time—until, reaching deep into one cavity, he yelped and snatched his hand back. A powerful beak had clamped down on his finger like a vice. He shook his hand hard, more from surprise than pain.

Stung in pride, he tried again. This time he seized the offender, dragged it out despite its shrieks, and stuffed it into his pocket, buttoning his coat with an air of triumph. Instantly the air filled with furious wings. Relations of the captive burst from the other entrances, screeching, swooping, pecking at the robber’s shoulders and hair.

His prize was not one of the brown builders—the nests belonged to the sociable grosbeak—but a pretty small green parrot, which delighted him. He was already speaking of taming it and teaching it words, as if language were a net we could throw over any creature. For the moment, however, the parrot remained a prisoner against Fritz’s warm side, beating its heart like a trapped drum.

As we walked on, that bird-city gave us much to ponder: their cheerful labour for the common good, their instinct to build together, to shelter together, to live by shared structure. I spoke of other communities in nature—beavers with their dams, bees and wasps and ants with their cities, even the coral insect that, by perseverance and union, raises whole kingdoms under the sea.

Fritz told me he had often watched ants at work at home; the bustle of their little commonwealth had amused him.

“Have you noticed,” I asked, “the care they take with what people call their eggs—how they carry them into the sun for warmth?”

He corrected me, proud and precise: it was not truly eggs they were carrying, but the chrysalis of the larva, produced from the egg. The common name was wrong.

He was right, and I told him so. Yet I could not help adding that in warmer lands the ant’s industry becomes a force beyond imagining: mounds four or six feet high, with streets and galleries and nurseries so firm that, if abandoned and altered, a man might use one as an oven. The ant had been praised since Solomon as a model of diligence, yet it was not a pleasant creature; it exuded sticky moisture, smelled unpleasant, and devoured whatever food it found. In those foreign countries, great ants were ruinous, hard to check—kept down only by enemies: birds, other insects, and beasts.

I described the tamanoir, the great ant-eater of South America: long as a man is tall, with coarse hair trailing like a heavy plume, head narrow and elongated, no teeth, and a tongue like a great red earthworm. Its claws could tear down the hard walls of an ant-hill; then its sticky tongue would coil among the swarming millions and draw them into its hungry mouth. There was also a smaller ant-eater with finer fur, living in trees. Fritz listened as though I were seasoning a dish: each detail a pinch of spice, making the world sharper on the tongue of the mind.

In time we reached a grove of tall trees with strong broad thick leaves. They bore a round fruit like a fig, full of little seeds and sour to the taste—harsh enough to pucker the mouth. Fritz noticed gum exuding from cracks in the bark, and he fell to scraping it off with the same delight boys have in hoarding cherry-tree gum at home. He returned to me kneading it, displeased that it would not soften properly. He was about to throw it away when it stretched between his fingers and sprang back.

“Father—look! It’s elastic! Could it be india-rubber?”

I took it, tested it myself, and felt my pulse quicken. “If it is,” I said, “we have found a treasure.”

He asked what use it could be besides rubbing out pencil marks, and I explained as well as I could: caoutchouc, a milky resin tapped from certain trees in South America; how the natives made bottles by coating earthen molds again and again, then breaking the mold and smoking the finished shape until it darkened and toughened. Europeans cut such bottles into pieces for drawing. Here, on our island, it meant something more urgent than novelty: it might be shaped into useful forms—above all, boots and shoes to keep our feet dry when the rains came and the ground turned treacherous.

Not long after, the cocoanut wood appeared ahead, and beyond it the bay and that great promontory we had named Cape Disappointment, the limit of our wandering so far. Passing beneath the palms, I noticed a smaller sort among its grand companions. One had been broken by wind, and its pith looked mealy. My mind leapt again: sago-palm.

In that pith crawled fat worms—maggots—feeding, as I suddenly remembered hearing, on the sago itself. In the West Indies they were said to be eaten as a delicacy. Hunger and curiosity make philosophers of castaways; also cooks, if they have any courage. I kindled a fire at once, skewered half a dozen on a little wooden spit, sprinkled them with salt, and set them to roast.

Soon rich fat dripped, hissing as it struck the coals. The smell rose warm and savoury, like browned butter and roasted marrow. All revulsion slipped away before that honest perfume. I spread one—soft as a pat of butter—onto a baked potato and swallowed it. It was good. Fritz, watching me closely, forced his own courage into being and tasted them too; and before we were done our dinner of potatoes had become a feast with an unexpected luxury, as though the island itself had offered us a secret ingredient.

When we set off again we found the direct route choked by a dense thicket, so we turned aside toward the sugar-brake near Cape Disappointment. We could not pass it without cutting a handsome bundle of sugar-canes, their joints cool and smooth under the knife, their sweet scent already promising relief to thirsty mouths. The donkey carried the canes along with the bag of wax berries.

At last we reached Calabash Wood and found the sledge. We unloaded the donkey and set everything onto the runners. The patient beast, relieved of the burden on his back, leaned into the traces and drew the load as though it were his natural occupation.

No further adventure befell us. We reached Falconhurst in the evening, and the welcome there warmed us more than any fire. Our stories were listened to with eager attention, our discoveries handled and admired. The pretty green parrot enchanted the boys above all, fluttering and scolding in Fritz’s hands as if already rehearsing the first syllables of a stolen new life.

Supper awaited us—real supper, not mere sustenance: a table of familiar labor turned into comfort. We ate with thankful hearts, and when we climbed into our tree-castle and drew up the ladder, the night sounds of the island—river, leaves, distant surf—seemed like a great protective breathing around our sleep.

If the island had its own music, the next morning our family had one word. Candle. One after another the boys woke with it on their lips. Through breakfast, candles. After breakfast, still candles, until there was no peace to be had except by yielding.

“So be it,” I said. “Let us become chandlers.”

I spoke with confidence, though privately I doubted. We lacked animal fat, which helps candles burn with steady brightness, and I was uncertain how faithfully my memory could reproduce the necessary steps. But I kept my misgivings to myself. We had learned that discouragement, once spoken, behaves like a weed.

Under my direction we began. We stripped the wax berries from their stems and threw them into a large shallow iron vessel over the fire. Heat loosened their secret. The green, sweet-scented wax melted and rose to the surface of the juice. We skimmed it off again and again into a separate pot and kept it warm.

My wife had prepared wicks. I dipped each wick into the liquid wax and passed it to Fritz, who hung it on a bush to cool. The first coating was thin, but we dipped and cooled and dipped again, patient as bees. Slowly the candles gained body and dignity, until they were sturdy enough to be called real.

When our wax ran out we hung the finished candles in a cool shady place to harden. That night, when the sun fell and the forest darkened into its deeper voice, we lit them.

Falconhurst glowed. Not a harsh glare like a city’s brazen lamps, but a civilized, honeyed light with a faint woodland perfume. We sat up three whole hours after sunset, as if we had recovered a lost portion of our former lives. In the quiet, even our speech sounded different—more deliberate, less pressed by darkness.

My wife praised our success; then, with a practical smile that always meant a new campaign, she said she wished I would show her how to make butter. Day after day, she had watched good cream spoil because we had no use for it. Could I invent a plan?

After a moment I answered that I might help—not by my own invention, but by borrowing from the Hottentots. I called for one of our gourd bottles, hollowed and cleaned, with a small hole at one end. I filled it partway with cream and corked it tight.

“Here, boys,” I said, “you can continue this while I turn carpenter and make a cart to replace our sledge.”

They fixed four posts in the ground, fastened a square of sailcloth between them with cords, making a sort of cradle. In that hammock they laid the gourd and, each taking a side, rolled it steadily back and forth for half an hour. The sailcloth creaked softly; the cream sloshed within like distant surf, and the boys’ laughter rose and fell with the rhythm—an island music made by human hands.

At last I called to them to open the gourd and carry the contents to their mother.

They did, and her eyes shone at the sight: a large lump of fresh butter. She touched it as reverently as if it were gold, and when she tasted it, the expression that crossed her face was not merely pleasure but a kind of homecoming. Butter is not only a food; it is a memory you can spread on bread.

Meanwhile, with Fritz’s help, I finished the cart. It was clumsy, but strong enough, and in time it proved of immense use when we began to gather our harvests.

Then we turned to our fruit trees, which had been planted in a plot ready for transplanting. We arranged walnut, cherry, and chestnut in parallel rows to form a shady avenue from Falconhurst to Family Bridge, laying down a tolerable road between them so that we might reach Tentholm even in foul weather. We planted vines around the arched roots of our great mangrove, and set other trees where they would thrive—some near Falconhurst, some beyond Jackal River to adorn Tentholm.

Yet my thoughts about Tentholm were not chiefly of beauty. I wanted it safe—a refuge in emergency. So we planted a thick prickly hedge that could hinder wild animals and even form an obstacle against men, should they appear. We fortified the bridge as well, and mounted two guns on hillocks—guns hauled from the wreck—so that their angry mouths might bark defiance at any enemy, beast or human.

Six weeks slipped away in hard yet pleasant labour. Sundays arrived like cool water. Each one we kept with gratitude, and on that day we thanked God especially for health and safety.

The work strengthened the boys. I encouraged it, making them practice running, leaping, climbing, swimming. Yet it ruined their clothes. They were patched and mended and still grew untidy, disreputable. Necessity drove me back to the wreck. I resolved to replenish our wardrobe and see how much longer the vessel would hold together.

Three of the boys and I went in the pinnace. The old ship looked much as when we left her, though a few more planks had surrendered to sea and wind.

“Come,” I cried, “not an article of the slightest value must be left on board. Rummage her to the very bottom.”

They took me at my word. Sailors’ chests, bales of cloth and linen, two small guns with ball and shot, tables, benches, window shutters, bolts and locks, barrels of pitch—everything we could pry loose or drag free was piled on deck. We loaded the pinnace and carried it ashore. We returned with our tub-boat in tow and made more trips until nothing remained worth taking.

“One more,” I said to my wife before we started again, “and it will be the end of the brave ship that carried us from Switzerland. I have left two barrels of gunpowder on board and mean to blow her up.”

Before lighting the fuse I found a large copper cauldron, too useful to abandon. I lashed it to two empty casks so that when the ship burst it might float clear. Then we placed the powder, laid the train, and returned to shore.

Supper was set outside the tent at a spot from which we could see the wreck. Darkness came. Then—suddenly—a vivid pillar of fire leapt up from the black water, and a sullen roar boomed across the sea. The sound rolled through the bay, answered by echoes off the rocks, and then the night fell strangely quiet again, as if the island were listening after a shout.

Our good old ship was no more.

We had planned it, and knew it was for the best. Yet when we lay down to sleep, sadness pressed us. It felt as though we had destroyed a friend—one that had carried not only bodies but language, tools, habits, and the very names of things we had once taken for granted.

Morning dispelled much of it. The shore was lined with planks and beams, the sea’s repayment. We found the copper cauldron too, successfully floated by its casks. I hauled it up among the rocks and stored under it the powder casks we had landed the day before.

Gathering these valuables took time, and while we worked my wife brought good news: two ducks and a goose had each reared large families among the reeds by the river. Soon they appeared, waddling past with an air of satisfaction, as if pleased with their own domestic triumph. We greeted them with real joy.

Ernest cheered that we would have duck and green peas one day and might imagine ourselves civilized again. Even that jest carried truth: what we longed for, beneath all our courage, was not luxury but the familiar language of meals—dishes that meant seasons, and seasons that meant home.

The sight of those birds reminded me of our life at Falconhurst. I declared we should visit. Everyone wanted to go. As we approached Falconhurst I noticed several young trees in our avenue bent by wind; it resolved me to cut bamboos next day to support them.

Since only Fritz besides myself had visited Cape Disappointment and the surrounding country, my wife and the younger boys begged to accompany me on the next expedition. I consented.

The following morning we set out with the cart, drawn by the cow and the donkey, laden for several days: tent, provisions, ammunition, and a host of implements and utensils. I meant to gather fruits and whatever produce the trees would yield.

The morning was lovely. We passed through our plantations of potatoes, manioc, and cassavas, and came again to the great nests of the sociable grosbeak. The children were charmed by that busy architecture, and even their delighted cries did not feel like noise; they belonged to the place, like birdcall and water.

We reached the wax shrubs and halted. I wished to gather one or two sacks of berries to renew our candle stock. The berries were quickly plucked, their sticky skins perfuming our hands. I stored them among bushes and marked the spot, to collect them on our return.

“Now for the caoutchouc tree,” I said. “Now for waterproof boots and leggings to keep your feet dry, Ernest.”

We sought it out and soon stood at the trunk. Then, with knives and a careful hand, we began to stab the bark and set vessels beneath to catch the milky sap as it bled slowly into our waiting cups.
