I sat in silence, shame burning through me like a cold fire. He was right—my dealings with the Little Ones had been reckless, thoughtless. I had failed them as a neighbor, and worse, as a protector.

Mr. Raven spoke again, his voice low but cutting: “You wronged them not only by your neglect, but by your cruelty. For those creatures, slavery would have been progress. A few lessons—taught gently, even with a branch from their own trees—could have made them strong, wise, capable. Instead, you left them helpless.”

“I didn’t know they were afraid,” I whispered.

“And does that excuse you?” he asked sharply. “The man who acts based on another’s fear is himself no better than a coward. And now—look what has come of it! By now, they might have stood against the princess, perhaps even the giants—though those were never much to worry about. But you? You let her mock them. And now, because of your connection with her…”

“I hate her!” I burst out.

“Did you tell her so?”

Again, I said nothing.

“You’ve been unfaithful—even to her,” he murmured. Then, suddenly, he leaned forward. “Hush. We were followed from the fountain, I think.”

“No one,” I insisted. “Only a ragged-looking cat—she vanished into the bushes.”

“A Persian,” Mr. Raven corrected. “A magnificent one, though soaked and matted, looking far worse than just disreputable.”

“What do you mean?” I gasped, dread coiling in my chest.

“There was a blue Persian around the house—she fled at the sound of water. Could she have been after the goldfish?”

“We’ll see,” he said. “I know cats. There’s something in this room that will reveal her true nature—if I’m not mistaken.”

He rose, walked to the closet, returned with a book—once torn, now whole. I stared. It was intact, untouched, as if never damaged.

“Where was the other half?” I choked.

“Sticking through into my library,” he replied simply.

I held my tongue. One more question might drown me in a sea without bottom. Time was too short.

“Listen,” he said. “I’m going to read a few lines. There’s someone here who won’t enjoy hearing them.”

He opened the vellum cover. The parchment was ancient, stained with time, one page darkened across two-thirds of its surface. He turned slowly, searching for a passage. At last, he began.

But what follows is not what he read—it is only the echo of it in my mind. The words were in a language I’d never heard, yet I understood them perfectly. I could not write them, nor fully name their meaning—only shape them, dimly, through memory:

*“If I found a man who could believe
In what he saw not, felt not, yet knew—
From him I’d take substance, form, and view,
And clothe myself in truth, made real and near.”*

He turned a leaf.

*“In me was every woman. I held power
Over every living man’s soul—
More than any woman ever dared or wore.
I could do what none could, none could ever know.
All women, I, the woman, surpassed,
Outsoared, outsank, outreigned, in hall or bower.”*

Another pause. Another turn.

*“Though unseen, unheard, untouched by hand,
Though never breath stirred his hair or skin,
I could bind his mind, his spine, his brain,
With bonds that Death could not unbind—
Nor life, however long hope was deferred.”*

Then again:

*“By his side I lay—no body, no form—
I breathed not, saw not, felt not, only thought.
Yet I made him love me, hungering
For something he could not name, nor find.
I sang a song with no sound—into his soul—
Lay heartless against his heart,
Giving nothing, yet receiving all,
As he poured his being into me,
To make me real, to give me flesh.
Thus, first, I stole into his living mind.”*

A strange cry tore through the air—feline, wretched, raw.

I jolted upright, eyes darting about. Nothing.

Mr. Raven turned pages, undisturbed.

*“Sudden I woke—knowing not the horror
That gripped me—not like serpent, but like vapor—
Moist, foul, filling soul and blood and bone.
My being lay still, trapped in sickening doubt.
I knew my past—but not my now.
I knew what I had been, but not what I was.
On my brow, I felt the touch of something gone—
I was a dead despair, alive yet dying—
A life mocking life with mockery and pain.”*

Another cry—uglier, more agonized.

He paused. Then continued:

*“I knew I was a queen. Once, I wore splendour
That shone even in darkness, blind and deep.
On neck and arms, jewels blazed,
And men said my closed eyes shed light
That killed the diamond in its silver cage.”*

The cry came again—this time louder, deeper.

“I thought some vile thing was in the room,” the librarian muttered, glancing around. Then, without hesitation, he turned several pages and read:

*“Hideously wet, my golden hair
Fouled my hands—I’d have given rubies, all newly dug,
To have it cut. No eyes had seen such beauty—
No waist had worn such grace.
For a sip of water from a horn,
I’d have given my sapphires—blue as dawn.
Nay, I’d have traded opals for a peasant’s smock—
Clean, coarse, simple—anything to hide
My rotting shroud. Once, I heard a cock
Crow proudly on a green hillside—
Over my coffin. From afar, a ghostly answer
Came back—mocking, hollow, like a dream.”*

Once more, the beast howled.

“I thought something foul was here,” Mr. Raven said, scanning the room. Then he turned a few more pages and read:

*“I had bathed in milk and honey-dew,
In rain from roses that ne’er touched earth,
Ointed with amber nard.
Never a flaw, never a mark—
Not a hair too many, not a spot,
Not a scar, not a sign of want.
I feared the sun—its bronzing light—
So I sat in shadow, in reflected glow,
Bathing in moonlight, slow and soft,
Tinting my skin to ivory tone.”*

Then, abruptly:

*“But now—darkness everywhere.
No phantom-flash from my eyes.
My fingers sank into pulp,
My body drowned in slime—
A mash of rotten horrors…”*

At that moment—a shriek. A monstrous, clammy fur bristling, tail thick as rope, eyes blazing green like chrysoprase, claws tangled in the carpet—out burst a great white cat, racing toward the fireplace.

Mr. Raven moved faster than thought. He threw the manuscript between her and the hearth.

She froze. Crouched. Eyes locked on the book.

But he kept reading, voice steady, gaze fixed on the pages:

*“Ah, the two worlds—so close, yet so apart!
Had I lived bodiless, untouched by sense,
Kept my heart clean from defilement—
Then I might have escaped the canker,
Scaped life-in-death, scaped sorrow’s endless moan!”*

And then—the howl. A prolonged, guttural scream of agony so terrible we both clapped our hands over our ears.

When silence returned, Mr. Raven walked to the hearth, picked up the book, and stood before the cat. With a pointed finger, he held her gaze.

She did not move.

He took a half-burnt stick from the ashes, drew a symbol on the floor, placed the manuscript back in its case—then looked at her with quiet certainty.

Now we have her, he seemed to say.

Then, stepping over her, he spoke in a voice calm, solemn, ancient:

“Lilith. When you came here, seeking your evil will, you did not know into whose hands you were delivering yourself. Mr. Vane—when God created me—not from nothing, as fools claim, but from His own eternal glory—He gave me an angelic splendour to be my wife. She lies there—her first thought was POWER. She called union with me slavery, refused to bear children for Him who gave her life. She bore one child—and then, puffed with pride, demanded I worship her. When I refused, she spilled her own blood to escape me, fled to the enemy’s ranks, ensnared the great Shadow, made him her slave, and became queen of Hell.

What she is now—she knows best. But I know too. That one child she fears, hates, seeks to destroy—claiming rights over what God sent through her into His new world. She knows no creation—only consumption. Like crystal shaped by force, or worm split into two—she knows nothing of making. The vilest of God’s creatures, she lives on blood, on souls, on life—yet cannot create. She consumes, kills—but cannot destroy what is truly born.”

The cat lay still, eyes blazing beryl-green, fixed on him. His eyes held hers—unblinking, unyielding.

“Then God gave me another wife—not an angel, but a woman. To her, light is as darkness.”

The cat screamed—horrible, tearing—then began to grow. Larger. Bigger. Spots flickered across her fur. Her form twisted. A leopardess roared—shaking the walls.

I leapt up.

Mr. Raven did not flinch.

“It is only jealousy,” he said, calm as stone. “Jealousy self-born, frustrated, fruitless. For I stand here—your master, whom you refused as husband—while my beautiful Eve lives, hopes immortally. Your hated daughter lives too—beyond your sight, one day to become what you call her destruction. Even Lilith shall be saved through childbearing. You exult that my human wife brought us despair, bore me a race of suffering—yet my Eve repented, and now shines brighter than any woman or angel. My world groans, yes—but it is the nursery of our Father’s children. I have repented. I am blessed. But you—Lilith—have not. Yet you must.”

He paused.

“Tell me—do you find the great Shadow beautiful? Do you know how long you yourself will remain beautiful? Answer me—if you can.”

Then I understood.

Mr. Raven was Adam—the old man, the new man. His wife, tending the dead, was Eve—the mother of all, the lady of the New Jerusalem.

The leopardess reared. Her spots danced. Then—she stood revealed. Radiant. Perfect.

“I AM beautiful—and immortal,” she declared. And she looked like a goddess.

“As a bush that burns and is consumed,” Adam answered.

“What is under your right hand?”

Her arm rested across her chest, hand pressed to her side.

A spasm crossed her face—swift, sharp, gone.

“Only a leopard-spot that lingers,” she said. “It will follow the others soon.”

“You are beautiful because God made you. But you are the slave of sin. Take your hand from your side.”

She lowered it. As it fell, she met his gaze—fierce, trembling, refusing surrender.

He looked at the spot.

“It is not on the leopard,” he said. “It is in the woman. And it will not leave until it eats to your heart—and your beauty flows out through the wound.”

She glanced down. Shivered.

“Lilith,” Adam said, his voice softer now, pleading, “hear me. Repent. And He who made you will cleanse you.”

Her hand trembled back to her side. Her face darkened.

She cried—the cry of hope vanishing.

It turned into a howl.

She writhed on the floor—again a spotted leopardess.

“The evil you plan,” Adam said, “you shall never achieve. Good is the universe, not Evil. The battle may rage for ages—but it must end. How will you fare when Time itself dissolves in the dawn of eternity? Repent. I beseech you. Repent, and be once more an angel of God.”

She rose. Stood tall. A woman again.

“I will not repent,” she said. “I will drink the blood of your child.”

I watched her—but when Adam spoke, I turned to him.

He stood towering above her. His face changed. His voice became thunder.

“Down!” he commanded. “Or by the power given me, I will melt your bones.”

She collapsed. Shrunk. Diminished—until she was again a gray cat.

Adam seized her by the scruff, carried her to the closet, and hurled her inside.

He drew a strange sigil on the threshold, shut the door, locked it.

Then he returned—old librarian again—eyes red-rimmed, shoulders slumped, wiping away silent tears.

And I, standing there, knew nothing more. Only that the world had shifted beneath my feet.
