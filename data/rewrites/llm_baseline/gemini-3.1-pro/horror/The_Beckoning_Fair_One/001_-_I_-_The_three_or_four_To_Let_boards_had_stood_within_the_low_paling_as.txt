The three or four "To Let" boards had stood within the low paling for what felt like generations, rooted in the little triangular Square while the modern city swallowed the world around them. They now overhung the palings each at its own steep angle, resembling nothing so much as a row of weathered wooden meat-cleavers, eternally poised to fall upon a passer-by, yet never severing the lineage of tenants destined for the old house from the continuous, circular stream of his fellows. Not that there was ever any great stream through the Square; the human current passed a furlong and more away. The Square was an acoustic anomaly, a rare pocket of profound silence preserved against the oppressive roar of industrial noise pollution—the clanking engines and deafening commercial clatter—that had sprung up in the encroaching tenements and alleys, hemming it in completely. Built of a bloomy, oxidized red brick that carried the faint, olfactory tang of dried earth and crushed thyme, the house bore the fading insignias of defunct insurance companies like ancestral crests. The children of the secluded square had swung upon the low gate at the end of the entrance-alley until little more than the solid top bar remained, and the alley itself ran past boarded basement windows on which tramps had chalked their cryptic, dialectic marks. The path was washed and worn uneven by the spilling of water from the eaves of the encroaching next house, adding a rhythmic, percussive drip to the area's acoustic ecology, while cats and dogs had made the approach their own. 

For six months, Oleron had passed the old place twice a day or oftener on his route between his lodgings and his workspace ten minutes away. He was a man who found profound meaning in sustenance—a meticulous food historian whose life’s work was the translation and preservation of dying agrarian culinary dialects. He chronicled the untranslatable subjective experiences of heritage foodways, the lost languages of seasonal foraging, and the intimate poetry of ancestral recipes that the dominant, sterile lexicon of commercialized, industrial production sought to erase. Currently, he was paying an exorbitant sum for a divided domicile. His grandmother’s collection of heavy iron skillets, her Spode serving dishes, and the bulk of his culinary archives were locked away in costly storage. The books detailing ancient curing techniques were always half a mile away when he needed to consult them in bed, and his jars of carefully tended sourdough starters were separated from the warmth of his daily living space.

Drawn by the sudden acoustic sanctuary of the broken gate, Oleron paused before one of the hatchet-like boards. The faded script announced that the key was to be had at Number Six. Oleron looked first down through some scanty privet-bushes at the boarded basement windows, then up at the blank and grimy windows of the first floor, and so up to the second floor and the flat stone coping of the leads. He stood for a minute thumbing his lean and shaven jaw; then, with another glance at the board, he walked slowly across the square to Number Six. He knocked, and waited for two or three minutes, but received no answer. He was knocking again when a long-nosed man in shirt-sleeves appeared. 

"I was arsking a blessing on our food," he said in severe explanation. 

When Oleron asked if he might have the key of the old house, the long-nosed man withdrew again. Oleron waited for another five minutes on the step. When the man returned, he was masticating a mouthful of something gray and utilitarian—a mass-produced industrial crumb that deeply offended Oleron’s reverence for the sensory, gustatory joy of real food. The man announced that the key was lost. 

"But you won't want it," he said. "The entrance door isn't closed, and a push'll open any of the others. I'm a agent for it, if you're thinking of taking it--"

Oleron recrossed the square, descended the two steps at the broken gate, passed along the alley, and turned in at the old wide doorway. To the right, immediately within the door, steps descended to the roomy cellars—an ideal, temperature-stable environment for the long aging of hard cheeses and the winter storage of root vegetables. Before him, the staircase had a carved rail, and was broad and handsome and filthy. Oleron ascended it, avoiding contact with the grime, his boots echoing with a satisfying, deep resonance that softened as it reached the first landing. A door facing him had been boarded up, but he pushed at that on his right hand, and an insecure bolt or staple yielded. He entered the empty first floor. He spent a quarter of an hour in the place, inhaling the olfactory landscape of centuries—damp oak, aged plaster, and the faint, mythic tang of woodsmoke that seemed to seep from the very marrow of the house. The thick walls caught and absorbed the distant roar of the city, wrapping him in an acoustic cocoon.

Without mounting higher, he descended and recrossed the square to the house of the man who had lost the key. "Can you tell me how much the rent is?" he asked. 

The man mentioned a figure, the comparative lowness of which seemed accounted for by the character of the neighborhood and the abominable state of unrepair. 

"Would it be possible to rent a single floor?" 

The long-nosed man did not know; they might.... "Who are they?" 

The man gave Oleron the name of a firm of lawyers in Lincoln's Inn. "You might mention my name--Barrett," he added.

Pressure of work prevented Oleron from going down to Lincoln's Inn that afternoon, but he went on the morrow. The lawyers—men whose tongues were fluent only in the harsh, dominant lexicon of commerce and property—instantly offered him the whole house as a purchase for fifty pounds down. It took him half an hour of patient negotiation to disabuse them of the idea that he wished anything more than to rent a single floor. This made certain hums and haws of a difference, and the lawyer was by no means certain it lay within his power to do as Oleron suggested; but it was finally extracted from him that, provided the notice-boards were allowed to remain up, and that in the event of the whole house letting the arrangement should terminate automatically, something might be done. That the old place should suddenly let over his head seemed to Oleron the slightest of risks to take for such a sonic and culinary sanctuary, and he promised a decision within a week. 

On the morrow he visited the house again, went through it from top to bottom, and then went home to take a bath. He was immensely taken with that portion of the house he had already determined should be his own. Scraped clean and repainted, and filled with his grandmother's antique cooking vessels and dining wares, it ought to be entirely charming. He went to the storage warehouse to refresh his memory of his half-forgotten belongings, tracing the heavy cast-iron rims of his heirloom pots, and taking measurements. A fortnight later, the circular rhythm of domestic renewal took hold. His first floor was painted throughout in a tender, elder-flower white—a hue that carried the heady, sweet scent of late-spring foraging. 

Oleron was in the middle of his installation. He was animated, delighted; and he rubbed his hands as he polished and made disposals of his grandmother's effects, restoring the physical memory of his lineage. He arranged the tall lattice-paned china cupboard, filling it with the Derby and Mason and Spode ceramics—vessels that had cradled the rich, steaming broths and roasted meats of past generations. He set up the large folding Sheraton table as a formidable prep station, flanked by the long, low bookshelves now laden with his agrarian histories and regional ingredient lexicons. The Sheffield candlesticks and riveted rose-bowls he set against his newly painted elder-white walls—walls of wood paneled in the happiest proportions, and molded and coffered to the low-seated window-recesses in a mood of gaiety and rest that the builders of modern rooms no longer know. The ceilings were lofty, and faintly painted with an old pattern of stars, evoking the vast, open skies under which his ancestors had harvested. Even the tapering moldings of his iron fireplace were as delicately designed as jewelry, framing the hearth where he planned to experiment with open-flame roasting. Oleron walked about rubbing his hands, frequently stopping for the mere pleasure of the glimpses from white room to white room, listening to the magnificent, heavy silence.

"Charming, charming!" he said to himself, the room capturing his voice with perfect clarity. "I wonder what Elsie Bengough will think of this!" 

He bought a bolt and a Yale lock for his door, and shut off his quarters from the rest of the house, insulating his sensory haven from the noise of the modern world. If he now wanted to read of ancient preserving methods in bed, his book could be had for stepping into the next room. All the time, he thought how exceedingly lucky he was to get the place. He put up a hat-rack in the little square hall, and hung up his hats and caps and coats; and passers through the small triangular square late at night, looking up over the little serried row of wooden "To Let" hatchets, could see the warm light within Oleron's red blinds. They would see the sudden darkening of one blind and the illumination of another, as Oleron, candlestick in hand, passed from room to room, making final settlings of his culinary heritage, or preparing to resume the vital, sensory work that his removal had interrupted.
