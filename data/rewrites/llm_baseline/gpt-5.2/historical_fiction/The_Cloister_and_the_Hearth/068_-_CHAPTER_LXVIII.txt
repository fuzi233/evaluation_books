A woman carries her griefs as a man carries his—only she is expected to carry them quietly, like a pot kept from boiling though the fire is fierce beneath. So it had been with Margaret Brandt. Up to that day when she crossed Eli’s threshold in anger, read her sweetheart’s letter, and—against her own intent—found her heart softened into friendship, her troubles had been many; yet she spoke of them as sparingly as a cook salts a broth when there are hungry mouths and little meat. Now the greatest trial drew near, and Gerard was far away.

Eli’s sudden favor might have been a shelter, had she been free to take it. But she was not. In that house were two men whose very looks made the skin behind her ears prickle, as if an unseen fiddle-string had been drawn too tight. She had read malice in their faces and a kind of noiseless understanding passing between them—little signals, swift as a knife’s glint, that told of secrets and of hate. She invented reasons, plausible enough, to stay away. She said she must mind her father; she said she was more at ease at home; she said the walk tired her. She never once named the true reason: dread.

So Catherine came to her instead—Catherine, who had the air of a woman born for nothing but the multiplying and the rearing of the multiplied. There are such, in every town and hamlet: when their own arms at last cease to be burdened with babies, they hover about the younger wives as hens about pullets, clucking, advising, warming, forever making nests where another must lay. Catherine went daily, and wrapped herself in Margaret’s expected hour as if it were her own.

It was in this spirit she begged Eli to let her remain in Rotterdam while he returned to Tergou.

“The poor lass hath not a soul about her,” she said, “that knows anything about anything. What avail a pair o’ soldiers? Why, that sort o’ cattle should be putten out o’ doors the first, at such an a time.”

To Margaret those words were comfort, plain and sustaining as brown bread. Yet comfort did not silence fear. As the time drew nearer she was full of it. She would die. Many died. And Gerard away. There was a loneliness in that thought like a room after a feast, when the platters are scraped clean and the hearth is ashes—only the emptiness was not in her kitchen but in her breast.

Still, life balances itself in odd ways. The very poverty that had bowed her, and her father’s helplessness that had forced her into struggles other girls never knew, now stood her friend. Adversity’s hard hand had long ago driven her from softness. She had learned to be always on her feet, always doing—scouring, kneading, lifting, stirring—because idleness was a luxury she could not afford. Catherine, wise in the rude wisdom of women, kept her so to the last. “Keep thyself moving,” she would say; “keep the blood warm; keep the house sweet.” So Margaret worked, not in a fever, but steadily—airing linen, setting water to heat, wiping the table, laying by clean cloths as carefully as if she were preparing for a guest of honor.

Outside her door the town went on with its own accustomed music: the gulls crying over the river, a boatman’s call, the faint thud of mallets from some distant cooper’s yard, and now and then the blessed hush that follows when wind drops and even Rotterdam seems to hold its breath. Catherine listened as much as she watched; she liked to know the world by its sounds. A harsh shout in the street could tighten a woman’s nerves more than a sour taste on the tongue; a quiet house, with only the purr of a kettle and the soft pad of feet, could make courage return. Catherine would silence unnecessary clatter as if she were shooing flies from milk.

So it came, one fine evening, just at sunset, when the sky had that pale-gold look of skimmed cream touched with saffron, and the air smelled of cooling brick and river-water, that Margaret’s hour took her. The room was kept dim and warm. Clean sheets lay like fresh flour. Water steamed; cloths were warmed; Catherine’s hands moved with the practised certainty of one who has fed and tended more lives than she can count. The noises of the street seemed to recede, and the house’s own small sounds—the breath, the whisper, the low directions—became the whole world.

When it was done, Margaret lay weak as water, but safe. By her side was a little face—creased, astonished, already searching the air with a mouth that knew nothing and yet demanded everything. And over Margaret’s tired spirit opened, sudden and immense, that heaven women enter when they become mothers, where pain is not denied but transfigured into a fierce, tender joy.

Catherine bent over her, wiping her brow as one wipes a board after kneading, tenderly, to keep it clean and cool.

“Why dost weep, sweetheart?” she asked. “All of a sudden?”

Margaret’s tears slid silently; her hands, so used to work, lay spent upon the coverlet.

“He is not here to see it.”

“Ah, well, lass,” said Catherine, forcing brightness into her voice the way a cook will coax warmth from a dying fire, “he will be here ere ’tis weaned. Meantime God hath been as good to thee as to e’er a woman born; and do but bethink thee it might have been a girl; didn’t my very own Kate threaten me with one; and here we have got the bonniest boy in Holland, and a rare heavy one, the saints be praised for’t.”

“Ay, mother,” said Margaret, and the word mother to Catherine came natural as the smell of bread to an oven, “I am but a sorry, ungrateful wretch to weep. If only Gerard were here to see it. ’Tis strange; I bore him well enow to be away from me in my sorrow; but oh, it does seem so hard he should not share my joy. Prithee, prithee, come to me, Gerard! dear, dear Gerard!”

And with that she stretched out her feeble arms, as if she could draw him bodily across miles by the sheer pull of longing.

Catherine busied herself—folding, rinsing, setting things straight—busywork like stirring a pot you cannot bear to watch; but she avoided Margaret’s eyes. For the cry had pierced her. Hearing her own absent child named so passionately, she felt her throat thicken and her sight blur. She would not let her tears fall where the young mother could see; she turned them into motion, into orders, into the small domestic duties that can sometimes hold a heart together.

Then, when she turned again, Margaret was looking at her with a strange stillness—as if the room had gone quiet in a deeper way than before, as if even the cradle of sound had been altered.

“Heard you nought?” Margaret whispered.

“No, my lamb. What?”

“I did cry on Gerard, but now.”

“Ay, ay, sure I heard that.”

“Well—he answered me.”

Catherine gave a sharp little sound, half rebuke, half fear. “Tush, girl: say not that.”

“Mother, as sure as I lie here, with his boy by my side, his voice came back to me—‘Margaret!’ So.” Margaret’s eyes were wide, not wild; rather, filled with a solemn wonder. “Yet methought ’twas not his happy voice. But that might be the distance. All voices go off sad like at a distance.” She paused, and the wonder melted into a soft radiance. “Why art not happy, sweetheart? and I so happy this night? Mother, I seem never to have felt a pain or known a care.”

And her gaze turned, hungry and adoring, to the child’s small face, lingering there as if drinking it in the way one drinks in the smell of broth after long hunger—slowly, gratefully, beyond words.

That very night Gerard flung himself into the Tiber.

And that very hour, when Margaret heard him speak her name, he cried aloud from death’s jaws and despair’s:

“Margaret!”

Account for it those who can. I cannot.
