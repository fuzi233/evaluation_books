"Sleep on, my love, in thy cold bed, / Never to be disquieted! / My last Good Night—thou wilt not wake / Till I thy fate shall overtake." —Dr. King

The silence within the Hale household felt unnatural, a heavy stillness born of recent terror and chaotic noise. Mr. Hale had overseen the preparations for his daughter's return, ensuring every detail was attended to, only to retreat once more to his customary chair, sinking into one of his habitual, somber reveries. Dixon had enlisted Mary Higgins to manage the kitchen; however, her scolding lacked its usual volume, delivered instead in an angry whisper. To speak above one's breath seemed irreverent while a corpse lay within the walls.

Margaret resolved not to burden her father with the final, crowning fright of the ordeal. There was no utility in recounting it; the crisis had concluded favorably. Her sole fear remained that Mr. Lenards might somehow secure funds to pursue Frederick to London, hunting him down in the city. Yet, the odds against such a scheme succeeding were immense. Margaret decided not to torment herself with thoughts of prevention. Frederick would remain vigilant, and within a day or two, he would be safely out of England.

"I suppose we shall hear from Mr. Bell tomorrow," Margaret observed quietly.

"Yes," her father replied, his voice flat. "I suppose so."

"If he is able to travel, he should arrive by this evening."

"If he cannot come, I shall ask Mr. Thornton to accompany me to the funeral. I cannot go alone; I believe I would break down entirely."

"Do not ask Mr. Thornton, Papa. Let me go with you," Margaret pleaded, her tone impulsive.

"You?" Mr. Hale looked surprised. "My dear, women rarely attend funerals."

"No," Margaret corrected gently. "Because they lack the capacity to control themselves. Women of our station do not go because they possess no mastery over their emotions, yet feel too ashamed to display them. Poorer women often attend, unbothered by the appearance of being overcome with grief. But I promise you, Papa, if you permit me, I shall cause no trouble. Do not bring a stranger and exclude me. Please, dear Papa! If Mr. Bell cannot come, I shall go. I will not press my wishes against your will if he does."

Mr. Bell, unfortunately, could not come. He suffered from gout. His letter was profoundly affectionate, expressing deep regret for his absence. He hoped to visit them soon if invited, though his Milton estate demanded his immediate attention; his agent had insisted on his presence. Alternatively, he had avoided Milton as long as possible, finding solace only in the notion that seeing his old friend might allow him to offer comfort.

Margaret struggled immensely to dissuade her father from inviting Mr. Thornton. An indescribable repulsion rose within her at the prospect. The night before the funeral arrived, bearing a formal note from Mrs. Thornton to Miss Hale. Written at her son's request, it stated that their carriage would attend the funeral, provided it did not inconvenience the family.

Margaret tossed the note onto the table. "Oh, let us avoid these formalities," she said sharply. "Let us go alone—you and I, Papa. They do not truly care for us, or he would have offered to drive himself rather than propose sending an empty carriage."

"I thought you were so vehemently opposed to his involvement, Margaret," Mr. Hale remarked, surprised.

"And I am," she admitted. "I do not wish him to come at all; I particularly dislike the idea of asking him. But this... this mockery of mourning surprises me. I did not expect it from him."

Startled, she burst into tears. Having been so subdued in her grief, so considerate of others, and so gentle in all things, her sudden agitation confused her father. He could not comprehend her restless demeanor tonight. As he lavished tender reassurances upon her, her crying intensified. She endured a sleepless night, leaving her ill-prepared for the fresh anxiety brought by a letter from Frederick.

Mr. Lennox was out of town, with his clerk predicting a return by Tuesday at the latest, perhaps Monday. Consequently, Frederick decided to remain in London for a day or two longer. He had considered returning to Milton; the temptation had been strong. However, the image of Mr. Bell residing in his father's house, combined with the alarming incident at the railway station, compelled him to stay put. Margaret could be certain he would take every precaution against Leonards' tracking.

Margaret was grateful to receive this letter while her father was absent in his mother's room. Had he been present, he would have demanded she read it aloud, triggering a state of nervous alarm she would find impossible to soothe. Beyond the disturbing fact of Frederick's detention in London, the letter contained allusions to the near-recognition at Milton and the potential for pursuit, chilling her blood. How would such news affect her father?

Many times, Margaret repented of urging the consultation with Mr. Lennox. At the time, it seemed to entail minimal delay and added little risk of detection. Yet, every subsequent event proved it highly undesirable. She fought hard against this regret, battling the self-reproach for having spoken wisely at the moment, only to prove foolish in hindsight. Her father, however, was too physically and mentally depleted to engage in healthy reflection; he would succumb to morbid regrets over the past.

Summoning all her strength, Margaret supported her father. He seemed oblivious to the expectation of Frederick's letter, consumed by a single thought: that the last tangible sign of his wife's presence was to be removed from his sight and hidden forever. He trembled pitifully as the undertaker arranged the crape drapery around him. Looking wistfully at Margaret, he tottered toward her, murmuring, "Pray for me, Margaret. I have no strength left. I cannot pray. I surrender her because I must. I strive to endure it; indeed, I do. I know it is God's will. But I cannot comprehend why she died. Pray for me, Margaret, that I may find the faith to pray. It is a great trial, my child."

Seated beside him in the carriage, Margaret almost supported him in her arms, reciting noble verses of holy comfort and texts of faithful resignation until her voice never faltered. Paradoxically, she drew strength from the act. Her father's lips moved in rhythm with hers, repeating the familiar texts as her words prompted them. It was terrifying to witness his patient, struggling effort to achieve a resignation he lacked the inner strength to accept fully.

Margaret's fortitude nearly crumbled when Dixon, with a subtle gesture, drew her attention to Nicholas Higgins and his daughter, standing slightly apart but deeply attentive to the ceremony. Nicholas wore his usual fustian coat, save for a strip of black cloth sewn around his hat—a mark of mourning he had never displayed for his daughter Bessy's sake. Yet, Mr. Hale saw nothing. He continued to repeat the funeral service mechanically as the clergyman read it, sighing twice or thrice before concluding. Then, placing his hand on Margaret's arm, he silently begged to be led away, acting as if blind, requiring her as his faithful guide.

Dixon sobbed openly, covering her face with a handkerchief, so absorbed in her own grief that she failed to notice the dispersing crowd until someone spoke directly to her. It was Mr. Thornton. He had stood throughout the entire service, head bowed behind a cluster of mourners, unrecognized by anyone.

"I beg your pardon—but, can you tell me how Mr. Hale is? And Miss Hale, too? I should like to know how they both are."

"Of course, sir. They are much as is to be expected. Master is terribly broken down. Miss Hale bears up better than likely."

Mr. Thornton preferred to hear that she suffered naturally. First, there was enough selfishness in him to derive pleasure from the thought that his profound love might come to comfort and console her—the same passionate joy that stings a mother's heart when a drooping infant nestles close, dependent entirely upon her. This delicious vision of what might have been, which he had indulged in despite her rejections only days prior, was miserably shattered by the memory of the handsome young man with whom she had stood in such familiar confidence near the station.

"Miserably disturbed!" was not strong enough to describe it. He was haunted by the recollection, an agony that made him clench his fists tightly to subdue the pain. So far from home, at such a late hour! It required a monumental moral effort to revive his trust—once so absolute—in Margaret's purity and innocence. The moment the effort ceased, that trust collapsed, dead and powerless. Wild fantasies chased one another through his mind. Here was a fragment of miserable, gnawing confirmation.

"She bore up better than likely" under this grief. She must therefore harbor some hope, so bright that even in her affectionate nature, it could lighten the dark hours of a daughter newly bereft. Yes! He knew how she would love. He had loved her without failing to gain that instinctive understanding of her capabilities. Her soul would walk in glorious sunlight if any man, worthy through his power of love, could win her back. Even in mourning, she would rest with peaceful faith upon his sympathy. *His* sympathy! Whose? That of another man. The realization alone turned Mr. Thornton's pale, grave face doubly wan and stern at Dixon's reply.
