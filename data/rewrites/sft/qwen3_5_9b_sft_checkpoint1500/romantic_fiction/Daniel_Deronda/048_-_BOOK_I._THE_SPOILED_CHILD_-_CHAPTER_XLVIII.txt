'Tis a heavy and poorly rewarded burden to try to arrange all things ahead of time according to the rules of our own security, as Machiavelli hints regarding Cesare Borgia. He, it is said, considered every contingency following his father's death and guarded against every misfortune, save one: it never crossed his mind that when his father died, his own death would swiftly follow.

Grandcourt's significance in this kingdom was of a grandly passive sort, derived solely from inheriting land. Political and social currents brushed against him only through the thread of his income. Even a meticulous chronicler would not need to study Schleswig-Holstein, Bismarck's policies, trade unions, household suffrage, or recent commercial panics. He skimmed the leading newspaper columns on these matters, and his views lacked breadth, for he lumped all Germans, all businessmen, and all voters prone to using the wrong soap under the single label "brutes." Yet, he took no action on these agitated issues beyond peering from beneath his eyelids at anyone who mentioned them, maintaining a silence that shook the convictions of timid thinkers.

Within his own sphere, however, Grandcourt displayed qualities akin to the triumphalist diplomacy of the wildest continental nations.

Not a movement of Gwendolen in relation to Deronda escaped him. He would deny being jealous, for jealousy implies a doubt of one's own power to prevent what one has resolved against. It pained him not that his wife might incline toward another man's society; what he required was that she remain as fully aware as if locked in handcuffs, understanding that her inclination was powerless to contradict his resolve. Though his entry into matrimony might have been filled with vacillating whims, his interpretation of the bond showed none. He had not repented of his marriage; it had injected aim into his life, offering new objects for his will. He had not repented of his choice. His tastes were fastidious, and Gwendolen satisfied them. He would not accept a wife who had not received an elevation of rank from him, nor one who did not command admiration through her bearing and beauty, nor one whose nails were not shaped correctly, nor one whose ear lobes were too large and red. Nor could he tolerate a ninny, unable to make spirited replies. These requirements may not seem excessive to refined contemporaries whose ability to fall in love hangs suspended due to a lack of essential details. Fewer, however, may follow him in his contentment that his wife possessed a temperament inclined to fly out if she dared, and that she had been urged into marrying him by feelings other than passionate attachment. Still, for those who prefer command to love, one wonders why the habit of mind should shift precisely at the point of matrimony.

Grandcourt did not feel he had chosen the wrong wife, and having assumed the role of husband, he refused to be fooled or appear pitiable. This was his state of mind—not jealousy, yet his behavior resembled jealousy as yellow resembles yellow, a hue produced by vastly different causes.

He had arrived in town earlier than usual to consult on legal arrangements for his will, mortgage transfers, and the transaction with his uncle regarding the succession to Diplow, secured by the bait of ready money. Another welcome accompaniment to his presence in town was the presentation of himself alongside the beautiful bride he had chosen despite expectations. True, Grandcourt moved with the sense that he cared not a languid curse for anyone's admiration; yet this state of not caring, just as much as desire, required its object: a world of admiring or envious spectators. For if one delights in staring stonily at smiling persons, those persons must smile, a rudimentary truth often forgotten by those who complain of mankind's general contemptibility, since any other aspect of the race disappoints the voracity of their contempt. In town for the first time with his wife, Grandcourt's abstention from cursing enlarged and diversified through splendid receptions, conspicuous rides, and presentations on all distinguished occasions. He wished her to be sought after; he liked that "fellows" should be eager to talk with her and escort her within his observation. There was even a certain lofty coquetry on her part that he would not have objected to. But what he disliked were her ways in relation to Deronda.

After the musical party at Lady Mallinger's, when Grandcourt observed the dialogue on the settee as keenly as Hans did, it was characteristic of him to name Deronda for an invitation along with the Mallingers, tenaciously avoiding any suggestion that Deronda's presence or absence mattered to him. He made no direct observation to Gwendolen on her behavior that evening, lest the expression of his disgust be too strong to satisfy his pride. A few days later, however, he remarked, without regard for *à propos*, "Nothing makes a woman more of a gawky than looking out after people and showing tempers in public. A woman ought to have good manners. Else it's intolerable to appear with her."

Gwendolen made the expected application and felt alarm at the notion of being a gawky. Like her, she preferred that her distaste include admirers. But the sense of overhanging rebuke intensified the strain of expectation toward any meeting with Deronda. The novelty and excitement of her town life were like the hurry and constant change of foreign travel; whatever inward despondency existed, there was a program to fulfill, yielding gratification to her many-sided self. Yet, as always happens with deep interests, the comparatively rare occasions on which she could exchange words with Deronda had a diffusive effect in her consciousness, magnifying their communication and enlarging the place she imagined it held in his mind. How could Deronda help this? He certainly did not avoid her; rather, he wished to convince her by every delicate indirect means that her confidence in him had not been indiscreet, as it had not lowered his respect. Moreover, he liked being near her—how could it be otherwise? She was something more than a problem: she was a lovely woman, for whose turn of mind and fate he had a care which, however futile, solicited him as a responsibility. Perhaps all the more so, when he dared think of his own future, he saw it lying far away from this splendid, sad-hearted creature, who, because he had once been impelled to arrest her attention momentarily, as he might have seized her arm to warn her from stepping where there was danger, had turned to him with a beseeching, persistent need.

One instance in which Grandcourt stimulated a feeling in Gwendolen that he would have liked to suppress without seeming to care related to Mirah. Gwendolen's inclination lingered over the project of singing lessons as a sort of obedience to Deronda's advice, but day followed day with that want of perceived leisure belonging to lives where no work marks off intervals. The continual liability to Grandcourt's presence and surveillance seemed to flatten every effort to the level of the boredom his manner expressed; his negative mind was as diffusive as fog, clinging to all objects and spoiling all contact.

But one morning, while breakfasting, Gwendolen, in a recurrent fit of determination to exercise the old spirit, said, dallying prettily over her prawns without eating them, "I think of making myself accomplished while we are in town, and having singing lessons."

"Why?" said Grandcourt, languidly.

"Why?" echoed Gwendolen, playing at sauciness, "because I can't eat *pâté de foie gras* to make me sleepy, and I can't smoke, and I can't go to the club to make me like to come away again—I want a variety of *ennui*. What would be the most convenient time, when you are busy with your lawyers and people, for me to have lessons from that little Jewess, whose singing is getting all the rage?"

"Whenever you like," said Grandcourt, pushing away his plate and leaning back in his chair, looking at her with his most lizard-like expression, while he played with the ears of the tiny spaniel on his lap (Gwendolen had taken a dislike to the dogs because they fawned on him).

Then he said, languidly, "I don't see why a lady should sing. Amateurs make fools of themselves. A lady can't risk herself in that way in company. And one doesn't want to hear squalling in private."

"I like frankness: that seems to me a husband's great charm," said Gwendolen, with her little upward movement of her chin, as she turned her eyes away from his, and lifting a prawn before her, looked at the boiled ingenuousness of its eyes as preferable to the lizard's. "But;" she added, having devoured her mortification, "I suppose you don't object to Miss Lapidoth's singing at our party on the fourth? I thought of engaging her. Lady Brackenshaw had her, you know: and the Raymonds, who are very particular about their music. And Mr. Deronda, who is a musician himself and a first-rate judge, says there is no singing in such good taste as hers for a drawing-room. I think his opinion is an authority."

She meant to sling a small stone at her husband in that way.

"It's very indecent of Deronda to go about praising that girl," said Grandcourt in a tone of indifference.

"Indecent!" exclaimed Gwendolen, reddening and looking at him again, overcome by startled wonder, and unable to reflect on the probable falsity of the phrase—"to go about praising."
