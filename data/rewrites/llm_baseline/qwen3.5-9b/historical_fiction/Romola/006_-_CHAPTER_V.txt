The Via de' Bardi, a thoroughfare immortalized in the chronicles of Florence, winds through the Oltrarno district, hugging the southern bank of the Arno. Stretching from the Ponte Vecchio to the Piazza de' Mozzi, where the Ponte alle Grazie rises, the street presents a striking duality. To the right, the row of houses climbs a steep incline known in the fifteenth century as the Hill of Bogoli—a notorious stone quarry that supplied the city's paving stones, often becoming treacherous slurry during rains. To the left, the architecture turns toward the river, displaying a facade of quaint, irregular elegance that seems to soften under the water's loving reflection as the sun dips toward the western horizon. Yet, to the historian's eye, these structures appear as a somewhat modern replacement for the legendary palaces of the Bardi family, razed by popular fury in the mid-fourteenth century.

Those Bardi were a lineage of formidable energy and pride. They were among the first to grasp the sword in Florence's internecine wars, when noble towers darkened the narrow lanes and the old war-god Mars might have smiled at the centuries of hollow worship paid to his rival, the Baptist. But the Bardi spirit was not limited to violence; their hands knew the delicate thrill of handling minted coin as well as gripping steel. Possessing the quintessential Florentine gaze, they understood that power could be seized through intellect and commerce just as effectively as through force. By the mid-1300s, they had ascended from commoners (*popolani*) to feudal Counts of Vernio, a title that bred resentment among their republican peers. Their ambition manifested in vast commercial ventures; they became the Christian era's equivalent of the Rothschilds, financing King Edward III of England and receiving revenues in wool, the crown jewel of Florentine trade. However, their financial hubris led to disaster. An unpaid debt to an "august" debtor triggered a panic among Sicilian creditors, resulting in a catastrophic collapse of their credit across the Mediterranean.

Yet, unlike modern bankrupts hiding in shame, the Bardi stood taller than ever. They became paragons of arrogance, drawing the ire of the armed populace in 1343. Fortified in their street between the two bridges, they held off the people's banners like panthers until an assault from the hill forced their surrender. Twenty-two of their great houses were sacked and burned, and many leading Bardi fled the city. But the family tree was deeply rooted. Though they lost their specific holdings on the riverbank—which by 1492 belonged largely to the Neri faction—the Bardi name persisted, resurfacing repeatedly in Florentine affairs, carrying a legacy of alternating glory and disgrace richer than most extended families could boast.

Among the Neri houses on the hillside stood the residence of a Bardi descendant, inheritor of the very branch that had once been Counts of Vernio. This heir possessed the old family fire and the drive to leave a permanent mark on the swirling earth, yet his passions had evolved. He was no warrior, nor a bold merchant-general. Instead, he was Bardo de' Bardi, a blind, impoverished scholar. His hands, once fit for command, were now cramped from endless hours of manuscript copying. Dressed in threadbare garments, first by choice, then by necessity, he dwelt amidst his books and shattered marble relics, illuminated only by the fading light of his memory. He was the man to whom Nello the barber had recently introduced the young Greek, Tito Melema.

Bardo's home was a somber mass of stone, punctuated by small windows and topped by a roofed terrace typical of the venerable city. Grim doors with scrolled hinges opened onto a vaulted courtyard, dominated by a massive iron lamp hanging from the center. A secondary door led to a stone staircase and the ground-floor rooms, which served as warehouses. Both floors were crammed with treasures destined for distant markets—from the Scheldt to the shores of Africa, the Aegean, or the Black Sea. Maso, the elderly servant, had to climb two flights to reach his master's chamber, entering only moments after Nello's conversation with the Greek.

Maso crossed the antechamber and pushed open the door to the left. He paused, nodding respectfully, before stepping aside. From the far end of a long, cavernous room came a clear, youthful voice: "Ah, you are back, Maso. All is well; we need nothing."

The speaker stood before a carved reading desk, reminiscent of those found in Italian church choirs. The room itself was a sanctuary of silence and order. Shelves lined the walls, meticulously stocked with books and antiquities. Between them stood isolated displays: a beautiful female torso, a headless statue brandishing a bladeless sword, severed infantile limbs inviting a kiss, preserved Roman busts, and vases from Magna Grecia. A central table held antique bronze lamps and dark pottery vessels. The palette of the room was predominantly pale and shadowy; the vellum bindings offered little contrast to the marble, which had grown livid with age. Even the once-splendid rug at the far end had faded to dimness. The dark bronzes waited for sunlight to reveal their verdigris, but the sun had not yet climbed high enough to pierce the narrow windows facing the Via de' Bardi.

The sole splash of vibrant color in the gloom belonged to a tall maiden of seventeen or eighteen. She stood before the reading desk, her hair a rich, reddish gold, rippling like sunset clouds against the twilight sky. Confined by a black fillet above her ears, the hair cascaded forward, framing a square-cut gown of black *rascia* serge. Her hazel eyes were fixed on a large volume resting on the desk. One long, white hand rested on the wood, while the other gently clasped the back of her father's chair.

Her blind father sat with his head lifted slightly, turned toward her as if visually engaging her. His delicate pallor, framed by a black velvet cap atop his drooping white hair, highlighted the uncanny resemblance between his aged features and hers. Both lacked the flush of health, sharing refined brows and noses, balanced by full, firm mouths and powerful chins that spoke of proud tenacity and latent impulsiveness. This trait was evident in the girl's posture: the backward tilt of her head, the grand line of her neck and shoulders. Her face was a puzzle—one that inspired not quite love, but a mixture of admiration and apprehension, dependent entirely on the eyes. Her father's eyes were silent forever; hers were currently tracing the Latin lines of Poliziano's *Miscellanea*, reading aloud at the eighty-first chapter.

"There was a certain nymph of Thebes named Chariclo, especially dear to Pallas..." Romola recited, her voice steady. "...and this nymph was the mother of Teiresias. But once, in the heat of summer, Pallas, accompanied by Chariclo, bathed her disrobed limbs in the Heliconian Hippocrene. It happened that Teiresias, coming as a hunter to quench his thirst, inadvertently beheld Minerva unveiled, and immediately became blind. For the Saturnian laws declare that he who sees the gods against their will shall pay a heavy penalty..."

She paused briefly, her hand slipping from the chair to meet her father's, which he had instinctively raised. She did not look up, continuing despite a subtle shift in her tone, moving into the Greek quotation from Nonnus.

"Stop, Romola," Bardo interrupted softly. "Bring me my own copy of Nonnus. It is superior to any in Poliziano's possession, for I made emendations to it that have not yet been shared. I completed it in 1477, when my sight was already failing."

Romola walked to the far end of the room with a queenly stride, effortless and uncalculated. "Is it in the proper place, Father?" Bardo asked, constantly verifying that reality matched the precise mental map he maintained.

"Yes, Father," she replied. "At the west end, on the third shelf from the bottom, behind the bust of Hadrian, above Apollonius Rhodius and Callimachus, and below Lucan and Silius Italicus."

A keen ear might have caught a hint of weary resignation in her otherwise patient delivery. As she approached, seeing his arms stretch out nervously to seize the volume, her hazel eyes filled with pity. She knelt beside him, placing the book on his lap and looking up as if believing her affection could penetrate the darkness blocking his vision. In that moment, the complex tension of pride and passion in her features transformed into pure, lovable tenderness. The deepest wells of her emotion had not yet surfaced in her expression, finding release only through her eyes.

Unaware of this radiant shift, Bardo appeared flushed and agitated as his fingers traced the spine of the book. "The vellum has yellowed in these thirteen years, Romola."

"Yes, Father," she said gently. "But your letters at the back remain dark and legible—fine Roman script. And the Greek characters," she added, opening the book on his knees, "are more beautiful than in any purchased manuscript."

"Indeed, child," Bardo murmured, running his finger across the page as if trying to distinguish margins by touch alone.
