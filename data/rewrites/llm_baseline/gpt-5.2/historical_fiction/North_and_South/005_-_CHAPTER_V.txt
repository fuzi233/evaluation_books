Margaret could sit for hours while her mother arranged small mercies for the parish poor—each plan spoken in that gentle household voice that meant comfort, and yet struck Margaret like a knife.

Before the first hard frost, they were to be gone from Helstone. She saw it already: old Simon, knotted with rheumatism, blinking at the fire with his book unopened because no one read aloud anymore; the little porringer she had always brought him—broth kept rich with marrow-bone and barley—gone cold in memory. She pictured Mary Domville’s crippled boy dragging himself to the threshold, listening for the familiar step on the path and hearing only the wood’s indifferent hush. They would not understand. They would only know she had vanished, and the village would give her absence some blunt name—pride, caprice, London ways—anything but the truth.

Mrs. Hale spoke on, warming to her own purpose. “Your papa has always spent what he had from the living among the parish. Perhaps I am encroaching upon next dues, but the winter is like to be severe. Our poor people must be helped.”

“Oh, mamma—let us do all we can.” Margaret’s eagerness had no prudence in it; it was the clutch of someone trying to leave gifts behind, as if gifts could stand in for a farewell. “We may not be here long.”

Mrs. Hale’s face altered instantly. “Do you feel ill, my darling? You look pale—tired. It is this soft, damp, unhealthy air.”

“No—no, mamma. It is delicious air.” Margaret tried to steady her voice by fastening it to sensation: the scent of wet earth, crushed evergreen, the faint sharpness of wood-smoke that belonged to cottage hearths and not to London chimneys. “It smells of the freshest, purest fragrance after Harley Street. But I am tired. It must be near bedtime.”

“Not far. Half-past nine. Go to bed at once. Ask Dixon for some gruel. I will come when you are in bed. I am afraid you have taken cold—or the bad air from some stagnant ponds—”

“Oh, mamma,” Margaret smiled faintly and kissed her, “I am quite well. Don’t alarm yourself. I am only tired.”

Upstairs, to soothe that anxious love, she let Dixon bring a basin of gruel—oats simmered till they surrendered into softness, a pinch of salt, a little milk stirred in at the last so it did not scorch. The steam smelled faintly of comfort and nursery hours, and yet it sat in her throat like something she must swallow for someone else’s sake. She lay back, languid, while her mother came in with the last questions and the last kiss; and when she heard, after a little interval, the click of Mrs. Hale’s lock, Margaret sprang from bed as if released.

She threw on her dressing-gown and began to pace; then the creak of a board admonished her. The house had its own acoustics—old wood complaining softly, the roof timbers holding night-sounds the way an empty church holds a whisper. She moved more carefully, and curled herself into the window-seat in the deep recess.

That morning her heart had danced at the bright clear light on the church tower—sun promising a day that would be simple and known. Now, scarcely sixteen hours later, she sat too full for tears, pressed by a cold dull pain that seemed to have squeezed the youth out of her. Mr. Henry Lennox’s visit, his offer—those words that ought to have shaken the whole world—had taken on the unreality of a dream. The hard fact stood like stone: her father had let doubt enter him until he would become a schismatic—an outcast. All the coming changes gathered round that one blighting centre.

She looked out at the church tower’s dark-grey lines, square and upright against the transparent blue depths beyond. Space opened and opened, as if her gaze could travel for ever and find nothing—no sign, no answering face. The silence out there felt less like peace than mockery, as if the cries of earth could rise and rise and be lost in that vastness before they ever reached a throne. Even Helstone’s night was full of small sounds—an owl’s call, the river’s far rush, the faint stir of a branch—but beyond them was an immeasurable quiet that made her feel more alone than any roof of iron could have done.

In that mood her father entered without her hearing him. Moonlight was strong enough to show her crouched in the recess, her hair loosened, her posture strange with misery. He came close and touched her shoulder before she knew he was there.

“Margaret. I heard you were up. I could not help coming in—to ask you to pray with me. To say the Lord’s Prayer. That will do good to both of us.”

He did not argue; he did not explain. He asked only that ancient sequence of words, spoken by countless mouths in countless dialects, held in the memory of the world even when meanings blur and hearts wander. They knelt by the window-seat—he looking up, she bowed down, ashamed. God was there close around them, hearing her father’s whispered voice. Her father might be, in the world’s phrase, a heretic; but she had been, five minutes before, a more utter sceptic in her despair.

She did not speak after. She crept back into bed when he had left her, like a child ashamed of her fault. If the world was full of perplexity, she would trust. She would ask only to see the step needed for the hour.

That night Mr. Lennox haunted her dreams. He climbed a tree of fabulous height to reach a branch where her bonnet hung; he fell; she struggled to save him, and was held back by some invisible force. He lay dead. Then the scene shifted, and she was again in the Harley Street drawing-room, speaking to him as she had done a hundred times, while a cold knowledge persisted behind every word: she had seen him killed.

She woke with a start, unrefreshed, her mouth dry, her body heavy as if she had carried weights in sleep. Reality came back sharper than any dream—not merely sorrow, but discord in sorrow. Where had her father wandered in mind? How far from the faith she had grown up breathing as naturally as air? She longed to ask; she would rather have bitten her tongue than hear the answer.

Downstairs the fine crisp morning put colour into Mrs. Hale. At breakfast she seemed almost happy, talking of village kindness, of who must have what when the cold set in—unheeding her husband’s silence and Margaret’s monosyllables. There was tea poured, bread cut, butter spread; the homely sounds—cup on saucer, knife on plate—went on with their accustomed small music, as if the house meant to drown whatever was coming.

Before the table was cleared, Mr. Hale rose. He leaned one hand on the wood as if it steadied him.

“I shall not be home till evening. I am going to Bracy Common, and will ask Farmer Dobson to give me something for dinner. I shall be back to tea at seven.”

He did not look at either of them. But Margaret understood. By seven, the words must be said to her mother.

Mr. Hale would have delayed, if delay could soften what had to be borne. Margaret could not. She could not carry the weight all day, watching her mother’s cheerful plans bloom and knowing she was about to trample them down. Better do it now, and leave the day—short as winter days were—for comfort.

Yet as she stood by the window, waiting for the servant to leave the room so she might begin, her mother went upstairs to dress for the school. She came down quickly, brisker than usual, bonnet tied, cloak arranged, full of purpose.

“Mother—come round the garden with me this morning. Only one turn,” Margaret said, slipping her arm round her waist, as if she could hold her up through whatever might happen.

They passed out through the open window. The air bit pleasantly; the gravel gave its crisp sound underfoot; a robin scolded from the hedge. Mrs. Hale spoke—some light remark, perhaps—but Margaret did not catch it. Her mind seized on a bee pushing into a deep-belled flower, its body almost swallowed by the purple throat. When the bee came out again, dusted with gold, she would speak. That would be the sign, like a childish charm.

Out it came.

“Mamma! Papa is going to leave Helstone!” Margaret blurted, the words hard as stones. “He’s going to leave the Church, and live in Milton-Northern.”

Mrs. Hale stopped as if struck. “What makes you say so? Who has been telling you such nonsense?”

“Papa himself.” Margaret longed for some gentle wrapping to put round the blow, but there was none; only the blow.

They had reached a garden-bench. Mrs. Hale sank onto it and began to cry—not loud, but with that helpless, wounded sound of someone whose world has been quietly stolen.

“I don’t understand you,” she said. “Either you have made some great mistake—or I don’t quite understand you.”

“No, mother. I have made no mistake.” Margaret forced herself into plainness, because anything softer would seem like deceit. “Papa has written to the bishop, saying he has such doubts that he cannot conscientiously remain a priest of the Church of England, and that he must give up Helstone. He has consulted Mr. Bell—Frederick’s godfather, you know—and it is arranged that we go to live at Milton-Northern.”

Mrs. Hale looked up at Margaret’s face while she spoke, reading truth there against her will. Her own features darkened under the shadow of belief.

“I don’t think it can be true,” she said at last, as if by refusing she could reverse it. “He would surely have told me before it came to this.”

The thought struck Margaret with pain: her mother ought to have been told. However much Mrs. Hale repined, however much she narrowed her world to her own ailments and disappointments, this was her husband’s heart being torn, and he had left her to learn it from their child. Margaret sat down beside her and drew her mother’s head to her breast, bending her cheek against that soft hair, stroking as if touch could translate what words could not.

“Dear, darling mamma. We were so afraid of giving you pain. Papa has felt it so acutely. You know you are not strong. There must have been such suspense.”

“When did he tell you, Margaret?” Her tone had a sharp edge of jealousy, though tears still ran.

“Yesterday—only yesterday.” Margaret answered quickly, and then, to turn her mother outward toward pity instead of inward toward grievance, she added, “Poor papa.”

Mrs. Hale lifted her head. “What does he mean by having doubts? Surely he does not mean he thinks differently—knows better than the Church.”

Margaret could only shake her head. Tears came into her eyes; the question touched her own bare nerve. She had not asked her father what those doubts were; she had not dared. It was like standing on the edge of a deep well and fearing to look down, not because of the darkness but because of what her own voice might echo back.

“Can’t the bishop set him right?” Mrs. Hale asked, half impatiently, as if authority were a poultice.

“I’m afraid not. But I did not ask. I could not bear to hear what he might answer. It is settled, at any rate. He is going to leave Helstone in a fortnight. I am not sure—he may have sent in his deed of resignation.”

“In a fortnight!” Mrs. Hale cried. “I do think this is very strange—not at all right. Very unfeeling.” And, as if tears could wash away the humiliation, she took relief in weeping. “He has doubts and gives up his living and all without consulting me. If he had told me his doubts at first, I could have nipped them in the bud.”

Margaret, who had reproached her father in her heart, could not bear to hear him blamed aloud. She knew his reserve had begun in tenderness—cowardly perhaps, but not unfeeling.

“I almost hoped you might be glad to leave Helstone, mamma,” she said after a pause, groping for any thread that could pull her mother toward acceptance. “You have never been well in this air.”

“You can’t think the smoky air of a manufacturing town—chimneys and dirt like Milton-Northern—would be better than this. This air is pure and sweet, if it is too soft and relaxing.” Her voice gathered speed as pride and fear took the rein. “Fancy living in the middle of factories—and factory people! And, of course, if your father leaves the Church, we shall not be admitted into society anywhere. It will be such a disgrace. Poor dear Sir John! It is well he is not alive to see what your father has come to. Every day after dinner, when I was a girl at Beresford Court, Sir John used to give for the first toast—‘Church and King, and down with the Rump.’”

Margaret was almost glad her mother ran to old phrases, those inherited watchwords that sounded sturdy and certain even when their meanings were more posture than prayer. They were a kind of dialect, a badge—belonging, and also power. The pain was not only what her father believed now; it was what language would be used against him once he stepped outside the approved vocabulary. One word—Dissenter—could do the work of a hundred condemnations.

“You know we have very little society here, mamma. The Gormans—our nearest neighbours, to call society—have been in trade just as much as these Milton people.”

“Yes,” said Mrs. Hale, almost indignant, “but at any rate the Gormans made carriages for half the gentlemen of the county. They were brought into some kind of intercourse with them. But these factory people—who on earth wears cotton that can afford linen?”

“Well, mamma, I give up the cotton-spinners. I am not standing up for them. Only—we shall have little enough to do with them.”

“Why on earth has your father fixed on Milton-Northern?”

“Partly,” Margaret said, and sighed as she spoke, “because it is so very different from Helstone. Partly because Mr. Bell says there is an opening there for a private tutor.”

“Private tutor in Milton! Why can’t he go to Oxford and be a tutor to gentlemen?”

“You forget, mamma. He is leaving the Church because of his opinions. His doubts would do him no good at Oxford.”

Mrs. Hale fell silent, crying quietly as if her tears were a private argument she could not yet put into words. Then, abruptly, she clutched at something she could grasp.

“And the furniture—how in the world are we to manage the removal? I never removed in my life. And only a fortnight to think about it!”

Margaret felt a strange relief. Furniture—tables and beds and linens—were at least things that could be counted, wrapped, lifted, labelled. One could not pack doubt into a chest, but one could pack sheets. She began at once to plan and promise, drawing her mother into arrangements: what could be sold, what must go, what might be stored, what must be kept close because it held the smell of home—her mother’s shawls, the tea-service, the very saucepan whose bottom bore the marks of years of careful simmering. She spoke briskly, and for a while Mrs. Hale listened as if practical decisions were a sedative.

Throughout the day Margaret scarcely left her mother, bending her whole soul toward sympathy as feelings veered—anger, grief, shame, fear. Toward evening, her anxiety shifted to her father: she wanted him to find a gentleness waiting at home, a welcome that did not ask him to defend himself. She spoke of what he must have borne in secret for long; Mrs. Hale answered coldly that he ought to have told her, and would then have had an adviser.

At last Margaret heard his step in the hall. Her heart turned faint. She dared not go to meet him and tell him what she had done, for fear her mother’s jealousy would flare. He lingered, as if awaiting Margaret—or some sign. She dared not stir. Mrs. Hale’s lips twitched; her colour changed; she too heard him.

He opened the door and stood uncertain on the threshold, not sure if he had a right to enter. His face was grey and pale; his eyes had a timid, fearful look—something almost pitiful in a man, that despondent uncertainty, that fatigue of mind and body. It touched Mrs. Hale’s heart at last. She rose, crossed to him, and threw herself against his breast.

“Oh, Richard—Richard—you should have told me sooner!”

Margaret could not bear the intimacy of their grief. She slipped away as soon as she could, fled upstairs, and threw herself upon her bed, pressing her face into the pillows to stifle the sobs that broke through at last after the rigid self-control of the day.

How long she lay there she could not tell. The house had gone quiet, but not truly silent; there were always tiny sounds—boards settling, a far door-latch, the muted tick of a clock. Yet no one came until the housemaid entered to arrange the room. The girl, startled by Margaret’s crying, stole out again on tiptoe and told Dixon that Miss Hale sobbed as if her heart would break, and would make herself ill if she went on.

So Dixon came, candle held a little behind her so the light should not strike harshly on swollen eyes. Margaret started up, trying to gather herself, smoothing her wet hair back with trembling fingers, trying to look as if she had only woken.

“Oh, Dixon—I did not hear you come. Is it very late?” She let her feet touch the floor but did not quite stand, as if the act of standing meant facing everything.

“I can hardly tell what time it is,” Dixon replied, aggrieved as if the world’s clock had offended her. “Since your mamma told me this terrible news, when I dressed her for tea, I’ve lost all count. I’m sure I don’t know what is to become of us all. When Charlotte told me you were sobbing, Miss Hale, I thought—no wonder, poor thing! And master thinking of turning Dissenter at his time of life—when, if it’s not to be said he’s done well in the Church, he’s not done badly after all.” Her tongue ran on in the familiar groove of servant commentary. “I had a cousin turned Methodist preacher after he was fifty, and a tailor all his life; but then he’d never been able to make a pair of trousers to fit, so it was no wonder. But for master! As I said to missus—what would poor Sir John have said? He never liked your marrying Mr. Hale, and if he could have known it would come to this, he’d have sworn worse oaths than ever—if that was possible.”

Dixon had been long used to venting such opinions to Mrs. Hale, who received them or not according to mood. She did not notice Margaret’s sudden stillness—the flashing eye, the nostril dilating with indignation. To hear her father spoken of so to her face was intolerable.

“Dixon,” Margaret said in the low tone she used when strongly moved—quiet, but with a distant threatening sound in it, like storm far off. “Dixon. You forget to whom you are speaking.” She rose fully now, upright and firm, and fixed Dixon with steady, discerning eyes. “I am Mr. Hale’s daughter. Go. You have made a strange mistake—one I am sure your own good feeling will make you sorry for when you think of it.”

Dixon hovered, uncertain whether to resent or to cry. Either would have suited Mrs. Hale; neither suited Margaret. Margaret repeated, evenly, “You may leave me, Dixon. I wish you to go.”

Dixon, who would have flared at anyone less decided, was subdued by Margaret’s haughtiness and control. She muttered to herself that Miss Margaret had a touch of “the old gentleman” about her, as well as poor Master Frederick, and at last ventured in a half-humble, half-injured voice, “Mayn’t I unfasten your gown, miss, and do your hair?”

“No. Not to-night, thank you.”

Margaret gravely lighted her out, bolted the door, and leaned her forehead against the wood for a moment, breathing through the tremor. From that hour Dixon both obeyed and admired her. She would say it was because Margaret was like Master Frederick; the truth was simpler: Dixon liked being ruled by a nature that did not waver.

Margaret soon needed Dixon’s help in action and her silence in words—for a time Dixon thought it proper to show offence by speaking little, so her energy came out in doing rather than talking. A fortnight was a short time for so serious a removal. Dixon would begin, “Anyone but a gentleman—indeed almost any other gentleman—” and then, catching a look at Margaret’s straight, stern brow, cough the rest away and meekly take the horehound drop Margaret offered to stop the “little tickling at my chest, miss.”

But it was true: almost anyone but Mr. Hale would have had enough practical knowledge to see how hard it would be, in so short a time, to fix on a house in Milton-Northern—or anywhere—to receive the furniture that must be carried from Helstone Vicarage.

Mrs. Hale, overpowered by grief and by the necessity of immediate household decisions, became truly ill. Margaret almost felt it as relief when her mother took to her bed and left management to her. Dixon attended as a body-guard to her mistress, emerging only to shake her head and murmur to herself in a way Margaret chose not to hear. For Margaret had one thing straight before her: they must leave Helstone. Mr. Hale’s successor was appointed; there must be no lingering now, for her father’s sake as much as for any other.

Each evening he came home more depressed, worn out by the leave-takings he had resolved to make with every parishioner. Farewell after farewell—each one a small tearing—must have filled his ears all day: the sound of voices that had once answered to him as pastor now answering to him as a man who was going away under a cloud. Margaret could imagine how the same words—“God bless you, sir”—changed their tone when spoken to a clergyman who was no longer a clergyman.

She, inexperienced in matter-of-fact business, did not know to whom to turn for advice. The cook and Charlotte worked willingly at the packing—strong arms, stout hearts, cupboards emptied, linens folded, crockery wrapped in old paper that crackled like dry leaves. Margaret’s sense served her there: she could see what should be kept together, what must be marked “fragile,” what should be left accessible for the last days—kettle, tea-canister, a pan for broth, a jar of jam for her mother’s tray upstairs. Yet beneath every practical decision lay the question that made everything else uncertain.

Where were they to go? In a week they must be gone. Straight to Milton—or somewhere on the way? So many arrangements depended on it that one evening Margaret resolved to ask her father, though he looked as if thought itself hurt him.

“My dear,” he said, passing a hand over his brow, “I have really had too much to think about to settle this. What does your mother say? What does she wish? Poor Maria!”

His sigh was answered by another from the doorway. Dixon had come in for another cup of tea for Mrs. Hale, and catching his last words, protected by his presence from Margaret’s rebuking glance, dared to say, “My poor mistress!”

“You don’t think her worse to-day?” Mr. Hale turned hastily, alarm sharpening him.

“I am sure I can’t say, sir. It’s not for me to judge. The illness seems so much more on the mind than on the body.”

Mr. Hale’s face grew more distressed, as if every word were another weight on him.

“You had better take mamma her tea while it is hot, Dixon,” Margaret said, with quiet authority.

“Oh! I beg your pardon, Miss. My thoughts was otherwise occupied—in thinking of my poor—of Mrs. Hale.”

When Dixon had gone, Margaret turned to her father. “Papa—it is this suspense that is bad for you both. Of course mamma must feel your change of opinions—we can’t help that.” Her voice softened. “But now the course is clear, at least to a certain point. I think I could get mamma to help me plan, if you would tell me what to plan for. She has never expressed any wish; she only thinks of what cannot be helped. Are we to go straight to Milton? Have you taken a house there?”

“No,” he replied. “I suppose we must go into lodgings, and look about for a house.”

“And pack the furniture so it can be left at the railway station until we have found one?”

“I suppose so. Do what you think best. Only remember—we shall have much less money to spend.”

They had never had much superfluity; Margaret knew that. Yet hearing it said so plainly made the future narrow at once, like a road that leaves open country and enters between high walls. She felt a great weight drop onto her shoulders.

Four months ago, the only decisions required of her had been what dress to wear for dinner, or helping Edith with lists—who should take down whom, who must be invited lest they feel slighted, who could be omitted without offence. In that house life had run with the regularity of clockwork; even the annual debate about where to spend summer—Isle of Wight, Scotland, abroad—ended with Margaret safely drifting into whatever harbour her aunt chose.

Now, since the day Mr. Lennox came and startled her into a decision of her own, each day brought another question—momentous, sharp-edged—demanding that she choose, act, speak. And in the midst of packing-paper and tea-trays and gruel for her mother, while the familiar sounds of Helstone still soothed the ear—birds, wind, the church-bell marking hours—another sound waited in her mind: the imagined, unceasing roar of Milton-Northern, where even air and speech would be altered, and where the very words people used might make strangers of them before they had even set down their trunks.
