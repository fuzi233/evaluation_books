Miss Butterworth had been raised in a rigorous academy of etiquette. Her posture was immutable; her movements were swift, firm, and devoid of superfluous motion. Fidgeting was alien to her nature. Yet, following that interview, solitude threatened to unravel her composure. With great effort, she suppressed the outward tremors of anxiety—a habit she had always condemned in the more hysterical women of her acquaintance. She was deeply uneasy, displaying the signs of a sensible woman under stress, until Mr. Gryce returned.

Accosting her with a bright smile, he declared almost cheerfully, "Well, that is settled! All that remains is to wait for the verdict. Madam, do you possess any further insights? If so, I would welcome your counsel."

Her poise instantly reclaimed itself. "Would you?" she echoed, regarding him with a trace of skepticism. "I prefer to verify the validity of my current hypothesis before proposing another. Let us hold a conference; compare notes. For instance, explain why neither of us regards Bartow as the culprit."

"I believed we had exhausted that subject," Gryce replied. "Your doubts stemmed from the young couple departing the premises. My own—well, Madam, I may confess to you that the mute's gestures and demeanor suggest to me he is rehearsing something witnessed, rather than something committed. Thus far, I have found no cause to doubt this impression."

"I shared that observation," she countered. "Even absent it, my suspicions pointed elsewhere. Furthermore, it is more logical for a man to succumb to madness through another's action than through his own."

"Yes, provided he loved the victim."

"And did not Bartow love Mr. Adams?"

"He shows no mourning for him."

"But he has lost control of his faculties."

"Indeed; however, if we use his behavior as a clue, we must accept it wholly. Based on his pantomime, we believe he is replicating acts he observed. So, why does his face flash with infernal joy once the performance concludes? It suggests he revels in the deed, or finds profound satisfaction within it."

"Perhaps it is merely a reflection of what he saw; the murderer may have rejoiced. Yet, no—the youth I saw fleeing this scene of carnage bore no such expression. Quite the opposite. Mr. Gryce, we are navigating treacherous waters. I feel nearly submerged."

"Hold your head high, Madam. Every flood recedes. If you sink beneath the waves, what becomes of me?"

"You are inclined toward humor, Mr. Gryce. A favorable sign. You lose your wit when truly baffled. Somewhere, you must see the light."

"Proceed with our argument. Light often emerges from the most unexpected quarters."

"As you wish. Let us classify the old man's joy as a mystery for later resolution. Have you considered him a potential accomplice?"

"Certainly; yet this theory faces the same flaw that disqualified him as the primary motive. One does not go mad from an anticipated horror; it requires a shock to shatter the mind. He was not expecting his master's death."

"Agreed. That point stands. Bartow was an innocent bystander to this crime, possessing nothing to fear, and thus trustworthy in reproducing the event's exact details."

"Precisely. Continue, Madam. Only profit awaits an argument advanced by Miss Butterworth."

The old detective's tone was grave, his bearing impeccable. Miss Butterworth, perpetually vigilant for sarcasm, bristled slightly, though she offered no visible sign of displeasure.

"Let us recall his specific gestures," she began. "We must remember he spotted the attacker from the study doorway, viewing the assault over his master's shoulder."

"In other words, directly facing him. What was his initial movement?"

"First, he raises his right arm, stretching it backward while leaning forward to grasp an imaginary dagger. What signifies this?"

"I struggle to interpret it. However, I did not witness this. Upon arriving, he was thrusting with his *left* hand across his own body—a vicious lunge delivered with the left. This is significant, Mr. Gryce."

"Yes, particularly since the physicians confirm Mr. Adams was struck down by a left-handed blow."

"You don't say! Do you not perceive the dilemma?"

"The dilemma, Madam?"

"Bartow stood face-to-face with the assailant. In imitation, especially in his delirious state, he would naturally lift the arm opposite to the one he mimics—which, in this scenario, would be the assailant's right. Try, for a moment, to mimic my actions. Watch! I raise this hand, and instinctively—and I noticed the reflex, Sir, as quickly as you did—you raise the one directly opposite. It is like gazing into a mirror. You turn your head right, but your reflection turns left."

Mr. Gryce's laugh rang out involuntarily. Rarely caught off guard, he found this woman possessed a unique fascination over him at times. It amused rather than annoyed him to acknowledge defeat.

"Excellent! You have proven your point satisfactorily. But what conclusion draws from this? That the killer was not left-handed, or that he did not stand where you placed him?"

"Shall we contradict the doctors? They assert the blow was left-handed. Mr. Gryce, I would trade anything for an hour spent in Mr. Adams's study with Bartow free to move about. I suspect we would learn more by observing him briefly than by debating for an hour."

She spoke tentatively, almost timidly. Miss Butterworth sensed the audacity of her proposal, despite her claim of lacking curiosity.

"I aim not to be disagreeable," she smiled.

So far from being disagreeable, Mr. Gryce was taken aback. For once in his life, he acted impulsively. "I believe it can be arranged, Madam—that is, after the funeral. Currently, the house is swarming with officials, and—"

"Naturally, naturally," she acquiesced. "I would never dream of intruding now. Yet, the case captivates me, and my connection to it is so peculiar, that I occasionally forget my station. Do you think"—her voice faltered, betraying a rare loss of nerve—"that I have exposed myself to a summons from the coroner?"

Gryce grew contemplative, studying the lady—or rather, her clasped hands—with an air of pity. Finally, he replied, "The facts of this affair emerge so gradually that I doubt the inquest convenes for several days. Meanwhile, we may locate those two youths ourselves. Should we succeed, the coroner may overlook your role in bringing them to our attention."

There was a sly emphasis on the word *overlook*, coupled with a subtle humor in his gaze that revealed the detective at his sharpest. Miss Butterworth did not resent it; she was too preoccupied with a fresh confession.

"Oh," she said, "if they had been the only souls I encountered. But they were not. Another individual entered the house before I departed, and I may be compelled to speak of him."

"Of him? Truly, Madam, you are a mine of information."

"Yes, Sir," came the meek response—meek, considering the speaker. "I should have mentioned him earlier, but I dislike mixing matters, and this elderly gentleman..."

"Elderly gentleman!"

"Yes, Sir. Very old, very much of a gentleman, seemingly unconnected to the crime save for knowing the deceased."

"Aha, but that is a substantial connection, Ma'am. To discover someone who knew Mr. Adams... truly, Madam, patience has its limits, and I must urge you to speak."

"Oh, I shall speak! The time has arrived. Moreover, I am eager to discuss this new thread; it is fascinating."

"Let us commence, then, with a detailed account of your adventures in this house of death," the detective suggested dryly. "Your full adventures, Madam, omitting nothing."

"I appreciate the sarcasm, but nothing has been omitted except what I am about to reveal. It occurred precisely as I was exiting."

"What exactly?" Gryce asked, feigning reluctance. "Though, for the sake of justice—"

"You are correct. As I prepared to leave, I met an elderly gentleman entering. His hand had just grasped the bell pull. You will concede it was a perplexing moment. His face, well-preserved for his age, wore an air of expectation bordering on gaiety. He glanced at me in astonishment; whatever my usual serenity, my countenance undoubtedly bore the marks of deep emotion. Neither of us spoke initially. Eventually, he politely inquired if he might enter, mentioning an appointment with someone in the study. At this, I stepped briskly aside—I assure you—for this could imply... What did you say? Did I close the door? I certainly did. Was I to invite the entirety of ---- Street into the horrors of this dwelling while a poor old man—no, I didn't exit myself. Why should I? Was I to abandon a man nearing eighty—excuse me, not every octogenarian possesses your vigor—to enter such a scene alone? Besides, I had not warned him of the condition of the sole other living occupant."
