Travel, rough and rugged as it often is, holds a peculiar charm for the English mind. So too do the tales of those who have braved its hardships—whether driven by duty, desire, or mere daring. Even when the traveler bears no sacred vow of love, his journey can stir the imagination. And old friendships, like fossils unearthed from time’s soil, carry their own quiet fascination. Yet, since the heart of this story beats in Holland, it is time to return there—to the ordinary folk, the familiar scenes, and the everyday lives that have shaped human experience across the ages.

Jorian Ketel came to Peter’s house, determined to claim Margaret’s promise. But she lay ill in bed, feverish and weak. Upon hearing his purpose, Peter met him with sharp rebuke, driving him from the threshold. A few onlookers were ready to toss him into the nearest pond; both father and daughter were beloved, and the tale of their sorrow had spread through every corner of Sevenbergen. The town was inflamed with sympathy, a fervor that knows no measure. Jorian left in anger, regretting his kindness. Such remorse is not uncommon—and yet, when sincere, it carries its own weight.

Meanwhile, Dierich Brower, caught at “The Three Kings” questioning a drunken man about Martin Wittenhaagen’s whereabouts, was seized and thrown into a horsepond. He was threatened with worse if he ever returned. The outrage stirred municipal pride: the burgomaster of Sevenbergen sent a formal letter to Tergou, accusing them of overstepping their bounds and demanding that any future grievances be addressed through proper channels.

Ghysbrecht Van Swieten, though outwardly calm, seethed beneath. He replied with polite dignity, claiming he had pursued a Tergovian named Gerard—who had stolen the town records—and now fled abroad, likely carrying them with him. Thus, he turned necessity into virtue. But privately, he was consumed by dread. His spies learned nothing from the family at Tergou—they did not know where Gerard had gone. This uncertainty gnawed at him. He began to fear the boy was not far away, but lurking nearby, waiting to strike. From that moment, Ghysbrecht changed. He grew irritable, restless. His eyes darted nervously, as if expecting an unseen blow. Power had not brought peace—only deeper unease.

At Tergou, the family remained outwardly unchanged, but within, the warmth had faded. Little Kate knew her brothers, Cornelis and Sybrandt, had helped banish Gerard. Though she never spoke of it—fearing to worsen the rift—she shuddered at their sight and blushed at their false sorrow. Catherine, sharp-eyed and silent, noticed. She said nothing, but watched closely, her thoughts deepening into suspicion. The two boys, eager to share in the household’s grief, deceived their father and Giles—but not the women. Their secret satisfaction betrayed them. “That, noting all, seem nought to50 note,” they might say, but the truth lingered unspoken.

Gerard’s absence had left a hollow space at the table. His intelligent presence, once the light of the room, was missed—though none dared name it. The old hosier, wounded in pride by his son’s defiance, carried himself stiffly, refusing even to speak Gerard’s name. Yet beneath his rigid exterior, his heart strained. One thing he could not hide: his worry. “If I only knew where he is, and that he is safe,” he would sigh, “I’d care little else.” And then a long, heavy breath would follow. One cannot help thinking that if Gerard had walked through the door at that moment, there would have been tears, embraces—no reproaches, perhaps not even words.

What surprised the family most was how quickly the story became public. Within days, Gerard’s flight and the lovers’ plight were known throughout the town. Worse still, the people sided with Margaret and Gerard—against the stern parents and especially against the meddling burgomaster, who had interfered in what seemed no one’s business but their own.

“Mother,” Kate said softly, “they say Margaret is very ill—burning with fever. Her father fears for her.”

“Margaret?” Catherine asked, feigning calmness, though her voice trembled. “Which Margaret?”

“Oh, mother! How can you ask? Gerard’s Margaret.”

“Gerard’s Margaret!” Catherine cried, her voice cracking. “How dare you speak such a name here? Never mention that girl again—she has ruined my poor boy, the finest of my children. She is the reason he is not a holy priest among us, but wandering the world, while I sit broken-hearted. Do not cry, child—I speak harshly, but oh, Kate—you cannot know what a mother feels. I bear up before you all, I must swallow my fears. But at night, I dream of him—always in danger. Sometimes torn by wild beasts, sometimes bound by robbers, their knives raised above his pale face—so pale it would move a stone. Oh, when I think of that, while I sit warm and safe, perhaps he lies dead in some distant land—because of her! That name is poison to me. I tremble when I hear it.”

“I’ll never speak of her again, mother,” Kate whispered, her voice thick with pity. “I’ll do nothing to grieve you more.” And she sighed.

Yet the very woman whose name was forbidden was spoken of everywhere—with sympathy, even admiration. All of Sevenbergen pitied Margaret. Young men and maidens glanced toward her window, where the village beauty lay “dying for love”—a phrase that underestimated her strength and devotion. Gerard was not dead. She believed in his constancy. Her father was dear to her, helpless now. Had she not been weakened by illness, she would have stayed by his side, though her heart might have drooped. But the strain of emotion and physical exhaustion had brought on a fierce fever. Only youth and strong constitution saved her.

When the fever broke, she was left in a state of profound weakness—where life felt like a burden. Then, love and kindness become mortal angels, their voices soothing, their touch healing. But Margaret had no one at her side. For many days she lay alone, the hours dragging like leaden waves. Existence seemed meaningless. Gerard was gone. There was no one to live for. Often she lay still, tears silently tracing paths down her cheeks.

One day, waking from a troubled sleep, she found two women in her room. One was a servant; the other, plainly of higher station, wore fur-lined sleeves and a narrow band of silver hair peeking from her coif—a sign of age and wisdom. Both looked gentle and kind.

Margaret tried to rise, but the older woman laid a hand gently on her chest. “Lie still, sweetheart. We come not to disturb you, but to comfort you, God willing. Now, cheer up—tell us, who do you think we are?”

“I know you,” Margaret whispered, though she had never seen them. “You are Demoiselle Van Eyck, and this is Reicht Heynes. Gerard has spoken of you often—of your kindness to him. Madam, he has no friend like you near him now.” At this, she sank back, tears welling anew.

Reicht Heynes began to weep—not from sadness, but from shared feeling. The older woman scolded her gently. “Well, you’re a fine one for a sickroom!” she said, and then set about cheering Margaret with quiet humor and tender care. An old woman who has lived through sorrow knows what to say—and what to avoid. She soothed, she amused, she drew Margaret close until the young girl rested her head on her shoulder, her eyes soft with gratitude.

“Ah,” said the lady, running her fingers through Margaret’s hair, “this is hair. Come, Reicht, look at it!”

Reicht examined it, praised it sincerely. The girl, though weak, could not resist a flicker of pride. “In truth, madam, I used to think it ugly. But he praised it—ever since, I’ve almost grown vain of it. Saints forgive me. You know how foolish those are who love.”

“They are greater fools who don’t,” the old lady replied sharply.

Margaret blinked, struck by the meaning.

This was the first of many visits. Almost daily, either the demoiselle or Reicht came to the bedside. Under their care, Margaret improved swiftly. Reicht credited nourishing dishes made in Peter’s kitchen; Margaret, however, believed it was the kindness, the steady presence, the quiet reassurance that gave her reason to live again.

Martin Wittenhaagen, meanwhile, rode straight to Rotterdam, intent on confronting the source of power. The bull he sought was not beast, but man—Philip the Good, Duke of Burgundy, Earl of Flanders, Lord of Brabant. Arriving in Rotterdam, he learned the court was in Ghent. There he went, seeking an audience, but was turned away by lackeys and pages. Undeterred, he threw himself into the duke’s path during a hunting expedition—and, ignoring courtly etiquette, roared out: “Mercy!”

“Why, where is the peril, man?” the duke laughed, scanning the field. “Grace for an old soldier hunted down by burghers?”

Kings, though varied in nature, share one trait: they delight in affability with the low-born. These men pose no threat, no rivalry. Their blunt honesty amuses, their simplicity refreshes. Philip reined in his horse, granting Martin a rare, personal moment.

Martin reminded him of a battlefield where he had taken an arrow meant for the duke. The memory was vivid. The king smiled. “A cheerful tale indeed,” he said. “I could afford to laugh—since it wasn’t me who got hit.”

Then Martin told of Gerard’s capture in the church, his imprisonment, the escape, and the hunt that followed. Whether Martin told it better than I have, or whether the duke simply loved a good story, the result was clear: the king was spellbound. When courtiers interrupted, he swore like a street vendor and threatened, half-jokingly, to chop off the next head that blocked his tale. When Martin finished, the duke exclaimed:

“St. Luke! What sport goes on in my earldom—ay, in my own woods—and I see nothing? You base fellows have all the luck.”

He was indignant at Fortune’s favoritism.

“This was a man-hunt,” he said. “I’ve never had the chance to attend one.”

“My luck was not great,” Martin replied. “I was on the wrong side of the dogs’ noses.”

“Oh, yes—forgotten that.” The king’s mood softened.

“What would you have?” he asked.

“A free pardon, your highness—for myself and Gerard.”

“For what?”

“For breaking prison.”

“Go to—the bird will fly from the cage. 'Tis instinct. Besides, lock up a young man for loving a young woman? These burgomasters must be devoid of sense. What else?”

“For striking down the burgomaster.”

“Oh, the hunted boar turns to bay. 'Tis his right. I hold less than man who grudges it.”

“And for killing the bloodhounds.”

The duke’s expression darkened.

“'Twas their life or mine,” Martin insisted.

“Ay, but I can’t have my bloodhounds—my beautiful bloodhounds—sacrificed to…”

“No, no—those were not your dogs.”

“Whose, then?”

“The ranger’s.”

“Oh. Well, I’m sorry for him. But as I said, I won't have my old soldiers sacrificed to his hounds. You shall have your pardon.”

“And poor Gerard.”

“And poor Gerard too—for thy sake. And more—tell this burgomaster his ways displease me. This is not the role of a burgomaster, but a king. I’ll have no kings in Holland but one. Bid him be humble—or by St. Jude, I’ll hang him before his own door, as I hanged the burgomaster of… what was it? Some town in Flanders? No—Brabant. Doesn’t matter—I hanged him. I remember that.”

He beckoned his chancellor, a stout man riding like a sack, and ordered him to draw up a free pardon for Martin and Gerard. It was written, signed the next day, and Martin hurried home with it.

Margaret had risen from her bed a few days earlier, sitting pale and thoughtful by the fire when Martin burst in, waving the parchment.

“A free pardon, girl—for Gerard as well as me! Send for him back whenever you wish. Not a burgomaster on earth dares touch him!”

Her face flushed with joy. Her hands trembled as she took the scroll, read it, kissed it again and again, then flung her arms around Martin’s neck and kissed him.

When she calmed, she told him of the friendship offered by Demoiselle Van Eyck.

“And I would fain consult her about this news—but I lack strength to walk so far.”

“What need to walk?” Martin grinned. “There’s my mule.”

“My mule?” she asked, surprised.

The old soldier chuckled. “I’ve grown so used to her, I forget Ghysbrecht owns her. Tomorrow I’ll return her—but tonight, she carries you to Tergou.”

It was nearly dusk. Margaret ventured forth. By seven o’clock, she arrived at Tergou—roses blooming on her cheeks, the pardon clutched in her bosom. She startled and gladdened her new, devoted friend with her unexpected return.
