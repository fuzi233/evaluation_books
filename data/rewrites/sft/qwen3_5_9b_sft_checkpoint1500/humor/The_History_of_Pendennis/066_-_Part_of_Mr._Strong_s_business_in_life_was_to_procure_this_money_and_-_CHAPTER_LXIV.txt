Lady Clavering, having finally escaped the social minefields of London, retreated to a charming villa nestled in the picturesque commons near Tunbridge Wells. In tow came Miss Amory, naturally, and Master Clavering, fresh from his holidays. For Blanche, the return home was a mixed bag: her favorite pastime remained the art of fighting and quarreling with her younger brother, though he, alas, possessed zero interest in domestic squabbles. Instead, he threw himself into cricket, horseback riding, and the company of a dozen young gentlemen aged thirteen who lived at the Begum's estate. These boys were a raucous crew, consuming tarts and champagne with alarming gusto, racing across the lawns, and generally frightening the good-natured Begum until she coughed violently enough to make herself ill. The result was a dining room so unbearable for Blanche that she fled it whenever possible.

Yet, for the fair Miss Amory, "change" itself was a delightful drug. For a fortnight, she would have traded silk dresses for bread-and-cheese, and perhaps even a dungeon for a night. The move to Tunbridge was, therefore, a welcome respite. She wandered the woods sketching trees and cottages, devoured French novels, and drove into town to catch any conjurer, musician, or ball that crossed her path. Her mornings were a battlefield of quarrels with Mamma and Frank, followed by a chaotic stint at the local village school where she alternately cooed over the girls, defied the mistress, and then scolded the students while laughing at the teacher.

She also became a fixture at the little ancient church, a tiny Anglo-Norman jewel adorned with painted windows and gilded scriptures. Eager to prove her piety, Blanche designed a strictly correct high-church altar cover. To the local clergyman, she appeared suddenly transformed into a saint. She charmed him with such artful coaxing that poor Mrs. Smirke, the vicar's wife, descended into madness. Mrs. Smirke, whose husband had recently cut off his collar and grown his hair out to pursue a higher calling, watched Blanche with jealous fury. The vicar, a harmless soul who had gone without dinner on Fridays and read the Roman Hours, was now accused by the dissenters and the orthodox alike of being a dangerous Jesuit plotting with the Pope. Yet, despite the rumors of correspondence with Rome and the display of candlesticks upon his makeshift altar, he meant no earthly harm.

Belinda, the vicar's wife, spent her time pacing the grass near two small graves, wishing desperately for a third spot so she could bury her hopes alongside them. She feared that if Blanche stayed another moment, her husband would offer to that "creature" who had infatuated him in a fortnight. "I shall retreat!" Belinda declared, vowing to enter a convent and leave him to his errors. Her neighbors echoed these fears, whispering that he was bewitched by errors far more odious than any he had previously confessed. Still, the vicar thought nothing of it; he saw only Blanche's piety and grace. His music, however, soon grew stale to him. What began as a fascination turned to doubt, then irritation, when she lost her temper and began tapping the fingers of his schoolchildren. Blanche, it seemed, inspired a cycle of admiration and satiety in many a man; she threw her charms wide, dazzling them with jewels and smiles, only to drop them as quickly as they grew tired of her.

It was a joyous evening for Belinda when Blanche departed. The vicar, blushing and sighing, admitted to her that he had been deceived, fearing that Blanche's gifts were merely "tinsel" and her piety just amusement. "There is none like thee, my Belinda," he murmured, lifting his fine eyes to the ceiling, "precious among women!" And Blanche? From the moment she lost sight of him, she ceased to care for either him or his wife.

However, the dynamic shifted entirely when Arthur Pendennis arrived at Tunbridge Wells to spend a few days with the Begum. At this stage, neither Blanche nor the simple clergyman had reached the point of indifference. Smirke still regarded Blanche as an angelic wonder, sitting in silent, rapt adoration as she played the piano in the summer evenings, forgetting tea or bread-and-butter in her ecstasy. He told Arthur stories of her, describing her as a being of rare talent and a heavenly disposition.

The reunion between Arthur and the curate was warm and cordial. Arthur, who loved anyone who loved his mother, listened with amusement to Smirke's affected airs, his long hair, and his blank stock. He found the vicar's devotion both ridiculous and touching. Blanche, equally glad to see Arthur, welcomed him with the zest of a country lass hearing news from the big city. She laughed for hours after dinner, sang her songs with renewed spirit, and even forgave her mother, kissing her with surprising affection. Before bed, she sighed, "Deja!" with a regretful air, squeezing Arthur's hand fondly before retreating.

Later that night, Pen stood at his window, watching the moonlight bathe the little Saxon church. "She has improved immensely," he mused, lighting a cigar despite the open window. "That stupid young cub of a boy ought not to be allowed to get tipsy, but the old woman is jolly, and Blanche is... well, she's devilishly nice." Just then, a voice drifted from a nearby clematis-covered casement—"Oh, heavenly! The moon! But I must close, *les moeurs* forbid!" It was the voice of the author of 'Mes Larmes.' Pen laughed, exchanging pleasantries before singing "Goodnight to Don Basilio."

The following day, they strolled through the fields, a pair of gay friends reminiscing about Laura and the golden days of their youth. Blanche spoke sentimentally of Laura's voice and wondered if she would join them. Then came the walk to Penshurst, followed by the conversation with the curate that lingered in Arthur's mind.

*"Is she truly all this perfection?"* Arthur asked himself, walking beside the vicar. *"Does she teach the poor? Is she kind? Yes, I saw it."* Later, alone with Blanche, he questioned her about the country life.

"I would never see that odious city again," Blanche declared, her voice full of longing. "My good thoughts grow here in these sweet woods like flowers that refuse to bloom in London. The gardener changes our balconies weekly, but I cannot bear the brazen, smoky face of London anymore."

"Why do you sigh, Blanche?" Arthur pressed gently.

"Never mind why."

"Yes, I do mind why. Tell me everything."

"I wish you hadn't come down," she replied, a second edition of *'Mes Soupirs'* suddenly manifesting. "I don't want you to go away. This house won't be happy without you, and that is precisely why I wish you had never arrived."

*Mes Soupirs* were laid aside, replaced by *'Mes Larmes.'*

What answer does a young woman give to the gaze of a lover? What method dries tears? What transpired between these two battered London rakes, momentarily forgetting their world and imagining themselves as Phillis and Corydon? Ah, what a fine night! How jolly a cigar at midnight! And how beautiful that little church looked under the moon. Perhaps, indeed, when one contemplates country houses and country walks, one begins to understand why so many men remain unmarried.
