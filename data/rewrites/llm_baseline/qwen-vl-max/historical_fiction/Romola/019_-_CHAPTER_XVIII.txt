When Tito left the Via de’ Bardi that afternoon, he felt a deep relief, almost a triumph, at having escaped the danger that had once seemed so imminent. Freed from the shadow of fear, his mind turned back to those anxious hours when he had been haunted by regret—not only for what he had felt, but for what he had done. He now saw clearly that he should never have parted with his ring. To Romola, and others who knew it well, such an act would seem petty, even shameful, unless he could invent some plausible reason—like using the money for a special gift. But Tito was weary of pretense. The lie had passed; fortune had spared him, and he thought it just that she should. Who had truly suffered? Baldassarre’s fate remained uncertain, beyond reckoning. But Tito wanted no lingering chains—no invisible ties that might pinch him later, however faintly, beneath the surface of his life with Romola.

Yet he did not realize that the very joy he now felt—the satisfaction of escape—was dulling the conscience that might have warned him against future entanglements. Still, the sale of the ring was a small matter. Was it not also trivial that little Tessa still clung to a dream that would surely shatter her innocent heart? Should he seek her out alone and gently break the illusion, or let time and chance unravel it?

Dreams were sweet, and fragile. They faded easily with morning light and the bustle of the world. And yet, how could he forget her—small, round-faced, pouting with innocence? She stirred something in him, though he tried to dismiss it. Perhaps he would bring her a gift someday, just to see if her stepfather treated her more harshly now that her mother was gone. Or should he reveal the truth at once, then tell Romola so they could find her a better life?

No. That affair—the _cerretano_, the marriage, his letting her go believing he loved her—must never reach Romola’s ears. And since Tessa could not be awakened from her delusion, there would always be a risk of exposure. Besides, the child might one day feel bitterness when her hopes crumbled. Yes, she must be in love with him, however foolishly. It would be kinder not to see her again.

Still, it was only a silly country girl’s fancy—one that might fade when a sturdy peasant boy courted her properly, and she confessed everything, finally understanding she was free. _Dunque_—farewell, Tessa. Best wishes. Tito resolved that this childish episode would end here. And men often believe their own resolutions will hold firm.

As for the fifty-five florins—his ring’s price—he already knew what to do with part of it. A clever idea had formed: one that would help explain the missing ring to Romola, and perhaps protect her from slipping into the monkish fantasies that repelled him so deeply. With this plan in mind, he walked to the Via Gualfonda, seeking Piero di Cosimo, the painter whose skill in mythological whimsy matched exactly what Tito needed.

The courtyard of Piero’s house was quiet. On the heavy iron knocker hung thick woolen wrappings, tied tightly with cords—a familiar defense, Tito recalled, against the painter’s habit of stuffing his ears to block out noise. He tapped lightly first, then shook the door more insistently. No answer. Just as he turned away, chiding himself for wasting time, a small girl entered, carrying a basket of eggs. She stood on tiptoe, pushed up a small iron plate set into the door, and called through the opening in a thin voice: “Messer Piero!”

In moments, bolts scraped, the door creaked open, and Piero appeared—wearing a red nightcap and a loose brown tunic, sleeves rolled to the shoulder. He glanced sharply at Tito, but without greeting, reached out for the basket. After taking it inside, he returned with the empty container and asked, “How much?”

“Two grossoni, Messer Piero,” the girl replied. “They’re all boiled, my mother says.”

Piero pulled coins from a leather pouch at his belt, and the child skipped away, casting several glances upward at the strange young nobleman.

Piero’s gaze, however, was far less admiring. “What do you want at my door, Messer Greco?” he said. “I saw you this morning at Nello’s. If you’d asked then, I could’ve told you—I don’t receive anyone without knowing their purpose in advance.”

“Pardon me, Messer Piero,” Tito answered, unruffled. “I acted without thinking. All I remembered was your talent for beautiful inventions—so when a sudden desire struck me, I came straight to you.”

Piero made his usual grimace—an exaggerated grin, brows pressed down, eyes narrowed—as if resisting flattery. After a pause, he asked, “And what is it you need?”

In truth, the idea excited him. “I want a delicate miniature design, drawn from the poets’ fables,” Tito began. “You’ll know how to combine them. It must be painted on a wooden triptych—here’s the size.” He held up his hands. “The inside can be plain gold leaf. But the outside—I want a new version of a favorite Florentine theme: Bacchus and Ariadne’s triumph. But not as Ovid tells it. Young Bacchus seated in a ship, his head crowned with grape clusters, holding a spear wrapped in vine leaves. Dark-berried ivy winding around the masts and sails, oars shaped like thyrsi, flowers curling about the stern. Leopards and tigers crouched before him, dolphins leaping around. And beside him—Ariadne, fair-haired, crowned with gold—immortalized. That part isn’t in Ovid, but you’ll imagine it. Above them, young Loves, like the ones you paint so well, shooting arrows tipped with roses—”

“Enough!” Piero interrupted. “I have Ovid in the vulgar tongue. Find the passage. I hate being choked with other men’s thoughts. Come in.”

He led Tito through the first room, where the basket of eggs sat near the hearth, beside a pile of broken shells and ashes. Amidst the mess stood a low ebony bedstead, draped carelessly with a rich Eastern carpet—once perhaps used to cover the steps of a Madonna’s altar—and a carved chest with painted scenes on its sides and lid. Elsewhere, casts, wooden steps, easels, and rough boxes hung with cobwebs filled the space.

The next room was larger, more crowded. A double door beneath the high window opened onto a wild garden—more thicket than plot—where fig trees and vines tangled among nettles and hemlocks, and a tall cypress rose above a choking mass of yellow mulberry leaves. Even indoors, nature crept in: long grass and feathery fennel grew in a corner among marble fragments and rusted armor. A tilted stone vase poured out ivy like water.

On the walls hung sketches—sea monsters, dancing satyrs and maenads, Saint Margaret rising from the dragon’s mouth, Madonnas glowing with divine light, plant studies, grotesque faces. Shelves bore books mixed with dried corn, ox horns, honeycombs, lichen-covered stones, skulls, peacock feathers, and bird wings. From the floor rose mannequins—one dressed as a Vallombrosan monk, topped with a barred helmet; another buried under brocade and furs. Among them, pigeons perched calmly, toads crawled near the threshold, and a white rabbit twitched its nose contentedly atop a box of bran.

“Now, Messer Greco,” Piero said, gesturing to a low stool near the door, “don’t try to take in everything at once, like Messer Domeneddio. Tell me—how large shall this triptych be?”

Tito indicated the dimensions. Piero marked them on paper. “Now, the book,” he said, pulling down a manuscript.

“There’s nothing about Ariadne here,” Tito noted, handing over the passage. “But I want her beside Bacchus—crowned, golden hair.”

“Ha,” Piero muttered, lips pursed. “And you want likenesses, eh?” He looked closely at Tito’s face.

Tito laughed, blushing slightly. “I know you’re good at portraits, Messer Piero—but I couldn’t ask Ariadne to sit. The painting must remain secret.”

“Ah,” Piero said, sharp-eyed. “Then I want her to sit. Giovanni Vespucci wants me to paint Oedipus and Antigone at Colonos, as he explains it. I’m fond of the subject. I want Bardo and his daughter to model for it. You—ask them. Then I’ll put their likeness into Ariadne.”

“Agreed—if I can persuade them. And your price for Bacchus and Ariadne?”

“_Baie_! If you get them to sit, that’s payment enough. I’d rather not take your money. You may pay for the case.”

“And when shall I sit?” Tito asked. “If we have one likeness, we need two.”

“I don’t need yours,” Piero said. “I already have it. Only I made you look frightened. I must remove that fear for Bacchus.”

He laid down the book and searched among paintings propped against the wall. Returning with an oil sketch, he said, “This is as fine a portrait as I’ve ever done.”

He turned it toward Tito. There was Tito—right hand raised, wine cup in hand, posture triumphant—but his face twisted in terror, eyes wide, lips pale, as if seeing a horror beyond words. A chill ran through Tito’s body, as though he were reliving the moment.

“You’re beginning to look like it already,” Piero said, laughing shortly, and moved the picture away. “He sees a ghost—that fine young man. One day I’ll finish it—when I decide what kind of ghost is most terrible: solid, like a risen corpse, or half-transparent, like mist.”

Tito, embarrassed by his sudden sensitivity—so unlike his usual calm—said lightly, “That’s a fitting subject for you, Messer Piero—a revel interrupted by a ghost. You seem to love mixing the terrifying with the joyful. That’s why your shelves are full of skulls while you paint those mischievous Loves stealing Mars’s armor. I begin to think you’re a Cynic philosopher, disguised as a master painter.”

“Not I,” Piero replied. “A philosopher is the last thing I’d wish to be. I find it enough to live, without spinning lies to explain it. Fowls cackle, asses bray, women chatter, philosophers spin false reasons—that’s what the world brings out in them. I am an animal that paints instead. And now—our business is done. Will you keep your side of the bargain about Oedipus and Antigone?”

“I’ll do my best,” Tito said, already moving toward the door. “I’ll let you know at Nello’s. No need to come here again.”

“I understand,” Tito added, smiling, lifting his hand in farewell.
