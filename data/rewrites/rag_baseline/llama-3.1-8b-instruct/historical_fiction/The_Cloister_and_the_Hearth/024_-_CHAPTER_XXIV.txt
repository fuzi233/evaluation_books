As the sun's warmth dissipated, casting a golden glow over the crumbling stone walls that encircled our humble world, I found myself drawn to the rhythmic cadence of the road. The scent of damp earth and wildflowers wafted on the breeze, a poignant reminder of the fragility of life. A cluster of travelers caught my attention, their conversation punctuated by the occasional chuckle and the soft clinking of coins.

Two men stood idly beside a magnificent steed, its coat glistening like polished obsidian in the fading light. Their conversation revolved around a figure sprawled across the ground, a man so intoxicated he seemed to have abandoned all sense of dignity. His vibrant yellow cloak, a beacon of opulence, lay discarded across the horse's loins.

Gerard, a traveler I had yet to meet, inquired about the situation, his curiosity piqued. One of the servants, a gruff individual with a rough demeanor, assured him that their master's condition was nothing to worry about. In fact, the servant claimed that a night spent on the hard ground would be just what the doctor ordered – a cure for the master's disorder. The other servant, a young man named Ulric, nodded in agreement, his eyes gleaming with a mixture of amusement and concern.

As the discussion unfolded, Gerard asked a series of questions, seeking to understand the dynamics at play. His naivety was met with a mixture of laughter and suspicion, as the servants began to question his intentions. Ulric, sensing Gerard's genuine nature, proposed a compromise: in exchange for knowledge, Gerard could offer trinkgeld, a small token of appreciation. Gerard, eager to learn, agreed to the terms, and the two servants presented him with two competing theories to explain the Count's behavior.

The first theory posited that the Count, a seasoned drinker, would soon recover from his intoxication and, upon finding his horse standing idle, would likely unleash a torrent of curses and possibly even violence. The second theory, more fanciful, suggested that a horse, being a temperamental creature, required constant care and attention, whereas a man, having imbibed copious amounts of beer, possessed an inner heat that rendered a cloak unnecessary. Both theories were met with enthusiasm, and the conversation escalated into a heated debate, with the servants growing more agitated by the minute.
