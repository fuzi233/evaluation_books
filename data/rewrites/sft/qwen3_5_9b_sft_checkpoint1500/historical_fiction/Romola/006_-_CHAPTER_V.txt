The Via de' Bardi, a thoroughfare immortalized in the chronicles of Florence, winds through Oltrarno, the district hugging the southern bank of the Arno. It stretches from the Ponte Vecchio to the Piazza de' Mozzi, where the Ponte alle Grazie rises. To the east, the street is bordered by a steep incline known in the fifteenth century as the Hill of Bogoli, a legendary quarry that supplied the city with its cobblestones—a foundation notoriously treacherous when the rain turned the stones to sludge. To the west, the architecture leans toward the river, presenting a jagged, irregular façade that offers the water a fractured mirror image as the sun dips toward the western horizon. Though picturesque, these structures, to the historian's eye, feel like a somewhat jarring replacement for the mighty palaces of the Bardi family, which the mob razed to the ground in a fit of popular fury in the mid-fourteenth century.

Those Bardi were a lineage of fierce vigor, prominent in the early internecine wars that tore Florence apart. When the noble towers darkened the narrow lanes and the gutters ran red with neighbor's blood, the old god Mars might have chuckled at the hypocrisy of those who worshipped Jupiter while slaughtering one another. Yet, the Bardi hands were not content merely to grip the sword-hilt; they possessed a refined taste for minted silver and gold. Their vision was equally acute, perceiving that empire could be built through trade as easily as through steel. By the middle of the fourteenth century, they had ascended from commoners to feudal counts of Vernio, acquiring vast estates and strongholds that rankled the republican sensibilities of their fellow citizens. They stood at the forefront of European commerce, the Christian Rothschilds of their age, furnishing specie for King Edward III's campaigns and receiving revenues "in kind"—chiefly wool, the lifeblood of Florentine galleys. When their great debtor defaulted and Sicilian creditors demanded immediate repayment, the resulting financial collapse sent shockwaves through the Mediterranean. Yet, unlike the humbled bankrupts of later ages, the Bardi did not bow their heads in shame. On the contrary, they doubled their arrogance, becoming among the most despised magnates who, according to Giovanni Villani, provoked the wrath of the armed populace in 1343. Fortified in their street between the two bridges, they held the narrow avenues like panthers cornered, until an assault from the hill forced them to retreat. Twenty-two of their great houses (*palagi e case grandi*) were sacked and burned, and many of their chiefs fled the city. Yet, like a plant with deep roots, the family survived. They re-emerged repeatedly in Florentine affairs, their history a tapestry woven with threads of dizzying altitude and crushing depth, shifting from opulence to destitution and back again, though they never reclaimed their old homes by the river, which by 1492 belonged to the Neri.

Within one of these Neri houses, however, dwelt a descendant of the Bardi, belonging to that same branch which had once been Counts of Vernio. In him, the old family pride and the hunger for preeminence survived, though transformed. This descendant was neither a warrior swift in the street nor a merchant bold in speculation. Nor did he delight in the grand strategy of global finance. Instead, he was a man whose hands were calloused by the endless labor of copying manuscripts, a frugal eater clad in threadbare garments, first by choice and eventually by necessity. He sat amidst his books and shattered fragments of antiquity, illuminated only by the lingering glow of distant, youthful eras. He was a penniless, blind old scholar: Bardo de' Bardi, the man to whom the barber Nello had pledged to introduce the young Greek, Tito Melema.

His residence stood on the hillside side of the street, a somber mass of stone punctuated by small, guarded windows and crowned by a roofed terrace. Heavy, grim doors, fitted with scrolled iron hinges and barred with iron grilles, opened onto a vaulted courtyard, empty save for a massive iron lamp hanging from the center. A smaller door led to a stone staircase and the ground-floor rooms, which served as warehouses. Both floors were crammed with precious stores destined for the markets of the Scheldt, the coasts of Africa, or the islands of the Aegean. Maso, the elderly servant, returned from the market laden with cheap vegetables, making his slow ascent to the second story where his master, Bardo, awaited entry.

Maso crossed the antechamber and pushed open the left-hand door. As he entered, a clear, youthful voice echoed from the far end of a long, spacious chamber lined with shelves holding books and antiquities in meticulous order. "Ah, you are come back, Maso," the voice said warmly. "It is well. We have wanted nothing."

Here and there, on separate pedestals, stood a beautiful feminine torso, a headless statue with an uplifted arm brandishing a sword, rounded infantile limbs severed from their trunks, inviting kisses upon the cold marble, alongside well-preserved Roman busts and vases from Magna Grecia. A large central table held antique bronze lamps and small vessels of dark pottery. The palette of the room was predominantly pale and sombre: the vellum bindings, with their deeply ridged spines, offered little contrast to the marble, which had grown livid from long burial. A once-splendid carpet at the far end had worn thin to a dull gray, and the dark bronzes lacked the sunlight necessary to reveal their verdigris, for the sun had not yet climbed high enough to penetrate the narrow windows overlooking the Via de' Bardi.

The sole splash of vibrant color in the gloom belonged to the hair of a tall maiden, perhaps seventeen or eighteen, standing before a carved *leggio*, or reading-desk, reminiscent of those found in Italian church choirs. Her hair was a reddish-gold, enriched by an unbroken, gentle ripple akin to the waves of sunset clouds on a grand autumn evening. It was confined by a black fillet above her small ears, rippling forward to veil her neck above a square-cut gown of black serge (*rascia*). Her gaze was fixed upon a large volume before her; one long, white hand rested on the desk, while the other clasped the back of her father's chair.

Her blind father sat with his head slightly turned, as if visually tracking her movement. His delicate pallor, framed by a black velvet cap atop his drooping white hair, accentuated the striking resemblance between his aged features and those of the young girl. Both possessed cheeks devoid of the rose, a refinement of brow and nostril, balanced by a full yet firm mouth and a powerful chin. This combination lent them an expression of proud tenacity and latent impetuosity, mirrored in the backward poise of the girl's head and the grand line of her neck and shoulders. It was a countenance that inspired not love, but a mixture of unwilling admiration and dread, a verdict ultimately to be decided by the eyes—which, in the father's case, had long been silent, and in the daughter's, were currently bent on the Latin pages of Politian's *Miscellanea*.

She was reading aloud from the eighty-first chapter: "There was a certain nymph of Thebes named Chariclo, especially dear to Pallas... Teiresias coming as a hunter to quench his thirst at the same fountain, inadvertently beheld Minerva unveiled, and immediately became blind. For it is declared in the Saturnian laws, that he who beholds the gods against their will, shall atone for it by a heavy penalty..."

As she reached the conclusion of the story, her hand slipped from the back of the chair to meet her father's, which he had just lifted. Yet, she did not look up, continuing to recite the Greek quotation from Nonnus, though her voice betrayed a slight alteration, hinting at suppressed emotion. Then, the old man spoke.

"Stay, Romola. Reach me my own copy of Nonnus. It is a more correct copy than any in Poliziano's hands, for I made emendations in it which have not yet been communicated to any man. I finished it in 1477, when my sight was fast failing me."

Romola walked to the far end of the room with a queenly step, the natural consequence of her tall, finely wrought frame, requiring no conscious adjustment of posture.

"Is it in the right place, Romola?" asked Bardo, perpetually seeking assurance that the external reality aligned with the minute details stored in his mind.

"Yes, father," Romola replied, her voice carrying a faint, weary undertone beneath habitual patience. "At the west end of the room, on the third shelf from the bottom, behind the bust of Hadrian, above Apollonius Rhodius and Callimachus, and below Lucan and Silius Italicus."

As she spoke, approaching her father, she saw his arms stretch out with nervous excitement to seize the volume. Her hazel eyes filled with pity. She hastened to lay the book on his lap and knelt beside him, looking up as if convinced that the love shining in her face must somehow pierce the darkness shutting out the rest of the world.
