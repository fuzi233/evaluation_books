Outside the cavern, we paused, feeling somewhat absurd in our situation.

"I am going back," declared Sir Henry.

"Why?" queried Good.

"Because it has occurred to me," Henry replied, "that what we witnessed inside may be my brother."

It was a novel hypothesis, prompting us to re-enter the gloom to test it. Emerging from the brilliant daylight outside, our eyes, weakened by the glare of the snow, struggled initially to adjust to the cave's darkness. However, soon they acclimated to the semi-light, and we moved cautiously toward the motionless figure.

Sir Henry knelt, peering intently into the face of the corpse.

"Thank God," he breathed, exhaling a sigh of profound relief. "It is *not* my brother."

I approached and examined the body myself. It belonged to a tall man of middle age, possessing aquiline features, grizzled hair, and a long black moustache. His skin was a perfect, waxy yellow, stretched taut over his skeletal frame. Most of his clothing had been stripped away, save for the remnants of a woolen pair of hose, leaving him nearly naked. Around his neck, hanging stiffly from his frozen throat, dangled a yellow ivory crucifix.

"Who on earth could this be?" I asked.

"Can't you guess?" Good challenged.

I shook my head.

"Why, the old Dom, José da Silvestra, of course—who else?" Good insisted.

"That is impossible!" I gasped. "He died three hundred years ago."

"And what prevents him from remaining preserved for three thousand years in this atmosphere?" Good countered logically. "If the temperature is sufficiently low, flesh and blood remain fresh indefinitely, much like New Zealand mutton. And heaven knows, it is freezing cold here. The sun never penetrates; no beast enters to devour or destroy. Doubtless his slave, mentioned in his writings, removed his clothes before abandoning him. He could not have buried the master alone. Look!" He stooped, picking up a strangely shaped bone sharpened to a point. "Here is the 'cleft bone' that Silvestra used to draw his maps."

We stared in astonishment, momentarily forgetting our own suffering amidst this extraordinary, almost miraculous sight.

"Aye," agreed Sir Henry, pointing to a small wound on the Dom's left arm. "And this is where he obtained his ink. Did every man ever witness such a phenomenon?"

There was no longer any doubt. Personally, the sight appalled me. There he sat—the dead man whose instructions, penned ten generations prior, had guided us to this precise location. Before me lay the crude pen with which he wrote them, and around his neck hung the crucifix his dying lips had kissed. Gazing upon him, my imagination reconstructed the final moments of the tragedy: the traveler succumbing to cold and starvation, yet desperately striving to transmit the great secret he had uncovered to the world. The terrible isolation of his death was palpable. I even fancied I could trace a resemblance in his sharply marked features to my late friend Silvestre, who had died in my arms twenty years earlier, though that may have been mere fancy. Regardless, there he remained—a somber monument to the fate awaiting those who dare penetrate the unknown. Undoubtedly, he would still sit there, crowned with the dread majesty of death, for centuries to come, startling any future wanderers brave enough to invade his solitude. The experience overwhelmed us, already nearly perished from cold and hunger.

"Let us depart," Sir Henry whispered. "Stay, however; we shall provide him with a companion." Lifting the frozen body of the Hottentot, Ventvögel, he placed it beside that of the old Dom. Stooping next, he snapped the rotting string of the crucifix, for his fingers were too numb to untie it. I believe he retains it still. I took the bone pen, which stands before me as I write; occasionally, I use it to sign my name.

Leaving these two—the proud white man of a bygone era and the poor Hottentot—to keep their eternal vigil among the endless snows, we crept out of the cave into the welcoming sunshine and resumed our journey, wondering how many hours remained before we too might join them.

After walking roughly half a mile, we reached the edge of the plateau. The mountain's peak did not rise from its exact center, despite appearing so from the desert side. Below us, hidden by billowing morning fog, lay the unseen landscape. Gradually, the upper layers of mist parted, revealing at the end of a long snowy slope a patch of green grass, approximately five hundred yards beneath us, through which a stream flowed. Moreover, basking in the bright sun by the stream stood and lay a group of ten to fifteen large antelopes. Their specific species was indistinct at that distance.

The sight ignited an irrational joy within us. If only we could secure them, we possessed ample sustenance. Yet, the challenge remained: how to approach them? The beasts were fully six hundred yards away—a formidable distance, unreliable when our lives depended on the outcome.

We rapidly debated stalking the game but ultimately discarded the plan reluctantly. Firstly, the wind was unfavorable. Secondly, traversing the blinding white background of the snow would surely reveal us, regardless of caution.

"Well, we must attempt a shot from here," said Sir Henry. "Quatermain, which shall it be: the repeating rifles or the expresses?"

Again, a difficult choice presented itself. Our two Winchester repeaters, including the one Umbopa carried alongside Ventvögel's, were sighted up to a thousand yards. Beyond that range, shooting became mere guesswork. Conversely, while the repeaters offered range, the express cartridges, being "expanding," were far more likely to bring down the quarry if they landed. It was a knotty problem, but I resolved to risk it and employ the expresses.

"Each of you take the buck opposite him," I instructed. "Aim precisely at the point of the shoulder, high up. Umbopa, give the command so we may all fire simultaneously."

A tense pause ensued as each of us aimed with our utmost precision, aware that life itself hung in the balance.

"Fire!" Umbopa commanded in Zulu.

At almost the same instant, three rifles roared loudly. Three clouds of smoke lingered briefly before us, followed by a hundred echoing reverberations across the silent snow. As the smoke cleared, a cry of pure joy escaped us: there lay a great buck on its back, kicking furiously in its death throes. We yelled in triumph; we were saved; starvation was averted. Though weak, we rushed down the snowy slope. Within ten minutes of firing, the animal's heart and liver lay before us.

However, a new dilemma immediately arose: we possessed no fuel to cook the meat. We exchanged glances in dismay.

"Men starving should not be fanciful," Good stated pragmatically. "We must eat raw meat."

With no alternative, our gnawing hunger rendered the prospect less repulsive than it might have been. We buried the organs in a patch of snow to chill them, then washed them in the ice-cold stream water before eating them greedily. It sounded horrific, yet honestly, I never tasted anything so delicious as that raw meat. Within a quarter-hour, we were transformed men. Life and vigor returned; our feeble pulses strengthened, and blood coursed robustly through our veins. Mindful of the dangers of overeating on empty stomachs, we stopped while still feeling hunger.

"Thank Heaven!" exclaimed Sir Henry. "That brute has saved our lives. What creature is it, Quatermain?"

I rose to examine the antelope, uncertain of its identity. It was roughly the size of a donkey, sporting large curved horns. I had never encountered such a species before. It was brown with faint red stripes and bore a thick coat. Later, I learned that the natives of this wondrous country called these bucks "_inco_." They are extremely rare, inhabiting only high altitudes where no other game survives. The animal had been struck high in the shoulder, though we could not determine whose bullet felled it. I suspect Good, recalling his marvelous shot at the giraffe, privately claimed credit, and we did not contradict him.

So engrossed in satisfying our hunger had we been that we had neglected to observe our surroundings. Now, having tasked Umbopa with cutting off the choicest portions of meat we could carry, we began to inspect the area. The mist had dissipated completely; it was eight o'clock, and the sun had evaporated the moisture, granting us a panoramic view of the countryside. I cannot adequately describe the glorious vista that unfolded before us, nor do I expect to see anything comparable again.

Behind and towering over us rose Sheba's Snowy Breasts. Below, some five thousand feet beneath our position, stretched leagues of the most enchanting countryside. Dense patches of lofty forests dotted the landscape, while a great river wound its silvery path through it. To the left extended a vast expanse of rich, undulating veld or grassland, where countless herds of game or cattle grazed, indistinguishable at that distance. This expanse appeared ringed by a wall of distant mountains. To the right, the terrain was more mountainous, with solitary hills rising from the level ground, separated by stretches of cultivated land where groups of dome-shaped huts were visible.
