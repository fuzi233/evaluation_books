I settled down against the gnarled, mossy trunk of an old ash as dusk thickened, and one by one, or in soft chattering little groups, the children drifted away from my side to clamber up to their woven treetop nests. They were always heavy-limbed with tiredness at sundown, and bright as sparrow fledglings when they woke at dawn, equally happy to curl into sleep and throw themselves back into play the moment they opened their eyes. I, though my own bones ached from days of travel, lay wide awake: Lona had not come to bid me good night, and every instinct told me she would find me. The very second I had seen her again, I had been struck by how much she resembled Lilith the princess, and I knew at once she was the daughter Adam had told me of—but in Lona, the princess’s blinding, almost searing beauty was softened to something soft and childlike, and deepened with the quiet, warm weight of gentle motherhood. “She is busy tending the child of that fleeing woman I met on the road, no doubt,” I thought to myself, the same woman Lona had told me was not half the mother her baby deserved.

At last she came, sank down beside me, and for a long, soft moment said nothing, only stroked my face and my calloused hands in that wordless, overflowing delight that needs no speech. Then she began to tell me every thing that had befallen the Little Ones from the day I vanished from the orchard valley. The moon crept over the treeline as we talked, sending shivering streaks of silver through the leaves to gild her thoughtful face, where a faint, steady care sat so wrapped up in love it did not weigh her down, but glowed like light through stained glass. It was hard to fathom how such a tender, wise child-woman could be born of a being as fierce and terrible as the princess—though, as it turned out, she had not two parents, but three, and that third was the quiet magic of the forest itself. She pulled at my heart with every word that was most like my own deepest, unspoken hopes, and I loved her as a being who could only ever grow more fully a child, no matter how much wisdom she gained. I knew now I had carried that love with me the day I left her, and the hope of seeing her again had been the small, steady light that got me through every dark, hard hour of my journey. Every word she spoke went straight to my chest, and like a shard of unvarnished truth, left my heart cleaner than it had been a moment before.

She told me that the moment I left the orchard, the giants had stopped writing off their tiny neighbours as silly fairy tales, and their old vague dislike curdled into open, furious hostility. The Little Ones would stand right beside them, laughing, as the giants stamped and roared, tearing at bushes, enraged by what they thought they saw of the small folk’s traces. Before long, their anger turned cruel and practical: they began chopping down the fruit trees the Little Ones lived on, hacking at trunks until apples and pears and figs came crashing down to rot on the ground. That was when Lona, the mother of them all, set about finding a way to stop the destruction. She set the sharpest of the children to listen through the dark hours, and learned the giants were convinced I was hiding somewhere nearby, biding my time to creep up and kill them in their sleep. The only way to turn them from their path of destruction, she decided, was to make them truly believe the Little Ones had abandoned the land entirely.

They would move deep into the forest, far enough away that the giants rarely ventured, but close enough they could sneak back under cover of dark to harvest their ripe fruit. The only problem was that part of the forest had almost no underbrush for them to hide in. But if the birds could make homes there, Lona reasoned, so could they. The Little Ones already loved and understood every form of wild life, could learn every trick the forest’s creatures knew—so why could they not take shelter in the treetops, to escape both the cold and their giant enemies? Why, after nesting in low brush for so long, should they not climb to the lofty boughs, and build nests where there was no hollow to dig in the earth? Every single thing the birds could do, the Little Ones could learn to do, save fly.

She laid the plan before them, and the children cheered at once. They already climbed trees faster than squirrels, and had spent hours watching sparrows and thrushes weave their twig and moss nests. The great forest trees stretched higher toward the sky than any of the orchard trees the giants owned, their branches spreading wide and some even dipping down as if reaching out to invite the children to climb aboard. Half giddy, they chattered that in the very top of the tallest tree, they might even find that fabled bird that laid the tiny baby-eggs, sat on them until they were ripe, then tumbled them down to let the new little ones hatch free. They would build their sleep-houses high above the ground where no giant would ever see them—for none of those dull, heavy oafs ever bothered to crane their thick necks back and look up. The bad giants would be sure they had all fled the country, and the Little Ones could sneak down to gather their fruit when the giants were fast asleep. That was the plan, and the whole band of Lovers threw themselves into it with such joy that within days they were as much at home in the treetops as the birds themselves. Before long, the giants had convinced themselves they had frightened the small folk away entirely, forgot about the trees, and almost stopped believing the Little Ones had ever existed at all.

Lona asked me if I had noticed how many of the children had grown since I left. I admitted I had not, but I could well believe it. She assured me it was true, and said that the discovery that their minds had grown sharper and bolder alongside their bodies had softened the alarm she had first felt at the change. They crept down to the valley in the short twilight hours, when the giants would not step foot outside—for twilight was full darkness to their dull eyes, and they hated the moon so fiercely they would have snuffed her out if they could. But soon the children found that fruit picked after dark went sour before midday the next day, and a new idea sprung up: instead of pretending they had run away, why not make the bad giants themselves leave the land entirely?

They had already explored the full stretch of the forest, introduced themselves to every creature that lived there. They knew how strong, gentle, and wise the elephants were, how fast and steady the horses were, how calm and clever the bears could be. So they set out to befriend every one of them, greeting every beast as Brother or Sister at first, until soon each creature had a soft, special name all its own. It took a little longer to greet the bears that way, and longer still to greet the snakes—but just the other day, Lona had heard one tiny boy laugh and call “Ah, Sister Serpent!” to a snake that had nipped him when he played too roughly. Most of them would not touch caterpillars, only sit and watch them through every stage of their slow transformation, but the second the new butterfly unfurled its wings from its cocoon, all the children swarmed to greet it, calling it Sister Butterfly and congratulating it on its “Repentance”—the word they used for that sacred, beautiful metamorphosis, a second, brighter life.

One moonlit night, when they were on their way to pick fruit, they stumbled on a woman sitting huddled on the ground with a baby in her lap—the very woman I had met fleeing from the city of Bulika. The Little Ones mistook her for a giantess that had stolen one of their shared babies, and swarmed her all at once, beating her with soft tiny fists in a bewildering, overwhelming flurry. She would have run, but a boy threw himself down to wrap his arms around her ankles and hold her fast. When she got her wits back, she recognized the small folk she had come looking for, and held the baby out gently. Lona stepped forward and took the baby, tucking it warm and safe inside her own tunic. They noticed as she gave the baby up, she hugged it and kissed it exactly the way they all hugged and kissed the small ones, and realized she must be a good giantess, like the kind giant that had once been kind to them. So they piled her arms high with fruit, and lavished every sort of childish, silly attention on her. The woman had been lost, too afraid to go back to Bulika, for she knew the princess would find out she was the one who had lamed her favourite leopardess. Delighted by the small folk’s welcome, she decided to stay with them for a while: she would not have to worry about her baby, and she hoped she might find a way back to her husband, a rich man who hoarded gold and gems and was very rarely unkind to her.

What Lona did not know, and I have to piece together from the bits I learned in Bulika, was that the woman knew the old city legend: the princess lived in terror of a child fated to destroy her. No one in the city knew the terrible, dark rituals she used to preserve her unnatural youth and beauty—they only saw her growing more hostile to every new baby born, and assumed she could feel her doom creeping closer, even if no one dared to lift a hand against her. So the woman hatched a scheme of her own: if she could make the Little Ones see themselves as the ones fated to bring the princess down, she could use the small folk’s bravery to storm Bulika. The Bulikan people were cowards who were terrified of all animals, after all, and the Little Ones were bold and strong, with all the forest’s beasts on their side. Once the fighting broke out, she would slip away unseen, creep back to her own house and hide, and wait to see who won. If the children drove the giants out of the forest, she could fan their excitement, and push them to aim higher, at the city itself.

The Little Ones hated fighting, of course. They almost never quarrelled, never hurt any living thing if they could help it, and never wanted to suffer pain themselves. But they were curious, and easily won over to new games. The woman started by setting the smallest children to throw stones at a carved wooden mark, and soon every last child was obsessed with the new game, growing more and more skilled by the day. That skill was exactly what they used to rescue me: when they found me asleep, tied up and guarded by giants, they went back to the trees, held a quick council, then came back the next day with all their elephant and horse friends, overwhelmed the few giants watching over me, and carried me away. After the victory, the small boys were boasting like little jays, the older boys were quiet and proud, and the girls, though their eyes sparkled brighter than usual, were far less talkative than normal. The Bulika woman, no doubt, was more encouraged than ever.

We talked long into the night, mostly about the children’s growth, and what that strange, steady change might mean. I had long known Lona saw truth as clearly as a bird sees the open sky, but now I was astonished at how sharp and practical her wisdom was—though if I had been more of a child myself, I suppose I would not have wondered at all. Long before dawn, I heard a soft scrabbling and fluttering in the boughs above me. I propped myself up on my elbow, and watched dozens of the Little Ones climb down from their nests, vanish into the dark underbrush, until a few minutes later the forest went quiet again.

“What are they doing?” I asked Lona.

“They think the stupid giants are going to come search the woods for us,” she answered. “They’re out gathering stones to meet them. There are not many good throwing stones in this part of the forest, so they have to scatter far and wide to find enough. They will carry them back up to their nests, and hurl them at the giants the second they step within range. They know the giants will not come before morning, but if they do, this will be the start of a war to drive them out. One of us has to go, and the giants are the ones who will leave. We do not mean to kill them, you know—their skulls are so thick I do not think we could hurt one badly even if we tried. And it would hardly matter if one did die, anyway: they are barely alive at all. If a giant dropped dead, his giantess would forget he existed after three days.”

“But the children throw so well it could happen, could it not?” I asked.

“Wait till you see them,” she said, a faint, proud smile tugging at her lips. “I have not even told you the strangest thing that happened two nights ago. We had all come back from gathering fruit, and fallen asleep in our nests, when we were woken by the most horrid, snarling noise of beasts fighting. The moon was bright, and all our trees were suddenly full of wide, staring little eyes, watching two huge leopardesses tearing at each other. One was pure as driven snow, the other was covered in black spots, rolling over and over each other, biting and clawing. By the look of the spotted one’s back, she must have been halfway up a tree when the white leopardess jumped her. When I first saw them they were right under my own tree, and I climbed down to the lowest bough to get a better look. The children thought they were playing at first, cheered for one or the other, because we had never seen such great big cats before. But then their roars grew weaker, and we saw blood pouring down their flanks and throats, and I wished neither would be left alive enough to climb a tree. Before I could stop them, the whole crowd of children scurried down to the ground to pat them and try to comfort them. I climbed down fast to pull them back, but before I could reach them, the white leopardess stopped fighting, and let out such a hideous yell that the children flew back up into the trees like panicking birds. Before I could climb back up to my own nest, the two were at it again. The white one won, chased the spotted leopardess running into the woods, then came and laid down right at the foot of my tree. She got up every few minutes to pace, making sure the spotted one was not lurking nearby, and she stayed there the whole rest of the night. She was gone by dawn.”

“I know both of those leopards,” I told her. “The spotted one is Lilith’s cruel pet, who hates all the children and would kill every last one of them. The white one loves them. She only yelled to scare the children away, to keep them from getting hurt if the spotted one won. No one has any reason to fear her.”

By then the children were tramping back into the clearing, chattering loudly, no longer bothering to stay quiet now that they were openly at war with the giants, their pockets and arms crammed full of smooth, heavy throwing stones. They climbed back up to their nests, grunting a little under the weight of their burdens, and fell fast asleep the second they curled down. Lona climbed up to her own nest in the great ash tree, and I lay back down, sleeping all the easier for knowing the gentle white leopardess was somewhere in the woods, standing watch.

I woke not long after the sun rose, and lay still, thinking. Two hours later, the giants began to trudge into the clearing in straggling groups of three and four, until I counted more than a hundred of them. The children were still asleep, and if I called out I would draw all the giants’ eyes straight to us—so I stayed quiet, planning to keep out of sight as long as they did not find me. But soon one blundered right over my legs, tripped, fell, and stumbled back to his feet. I thought he would wander on, but he started patting at the bushes, searching. I jumped to my feet, and punched him dead in the middle of his huge, soft belly. The roar he let out was deafening, and it was the signal. A storm of stones hailed down from the treetops, not one of them touching me, not one of them missing the giant. He fell flat and lay still. More giants stumbled forward to see what the noise was, and the stone storm spread. Every dull, purblind giant that stepped within range of a garrisoned tree became a target for a dozen converging throws. In minutes, almost every giant was laid flat on the ground, and a jubilant, song-like cheer rose up from the tops of fifty trees.

The great elephants came lumbering over at the children’s call, and the little ones climbed down the trunks like monkeys, three or four to an elephant’s back. The elephants began to walk slowly over the giants lying on the ground, and when the oafs kept roaring and making a nuisance of themselves, the elephants gave them a few sharp, soft blows with their trunks to hush them. The giants lay there silent and motionless until night fell. The next morning, every last one of them was gone. They fled all the way to the far end of the orchard valley, and never dared set foot in the Little Ones’ forest ever again.
