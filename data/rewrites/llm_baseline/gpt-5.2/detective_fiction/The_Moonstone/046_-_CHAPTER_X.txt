The two hours Ezra Jennings had condemned me to wait might have sat lightly enough on another man. On me they worked like a bodily fever. I could not keep still; I could not speak—could not even endure the honest face of Betteredge—until I had wrung from Jennings all he knew, and made his knowledge mine.

So I broke off, without ceremony, the visit I had meant to pay Mrs. Ablewhite. I even shrank from meeting my own companion at the inn.

Back at Frizinghall, I wrote Betteredge a line—cold, brief, and miserably unlike myself—telling him I had been called away for a few hours; that I should return towards three; that he was to order dinner at the usual time, and divert himself as he pleased. He had friends enough in that little town; and he would fill the interval more easily than I could.

Then I escaped again into the country.

The moorland round Frizinghall lay wide and spare under a thin sky, the wind combing the heather with a dry, whispering sound—one of those honest sounds which do not lie to the ear. Every step took me farther from the town’s clatter: no wheels grinding, no iron striking iron, no shop-bell’s bright little scream. Only the hush of turf, the scattered call of birds, and (far away) a dog’s bark softened by distance, as if the land itself were teaching the voice to be gentle. The quiet steadied me; it did not comfort me. My mind went round and round the same circle, like a hand grinding spice on a stone—heat, friction, and no release—until my watch told me, at last, that the time had come.

I returned to Mr. Candy’s house, and found Ezra Jennings ready.

He was waiting in a small, bare room that opened by a glazed door into the surgery. The walls were the colour of old buff leather and the decoration upon them was a horror—bright diagrams, garish with disease, as if suffering had been made into a sort of vulgar shop-sign. A skull sat above a row of dingy medical books where another man might have placed a bust; a big deal table was blotched and splashed with ink; the chairs were the plain wooden kind you find in kitchens where broth is stirred and bread is cut; a worn drugget lay like a tired rag in the middle of the boards. In the wall was a sink, with basin and waste-pipe rudely fitted—an innocent thing, in any other house, for washing hands or rinsing a cup; here, it carried the cold suggestion of blood and instruments. On the shelf, the mahogany case of an instrument lay with the quiet weight of concealed purpose; near it, a huge roll of lint—white, patient, and dreadful—kept its own place.

Outside the window, a few pots of flowers stood in the light; bees moved among them with that soft, industrious hum that belongs to summer and gardens and the ordinary sweetness of life. Birds were singing. And now and then, from some neighbouring house, there came the faint, intermittent jingle of a piano—tuneless, careless, played by fingers that had no notion what sort of room their little noise was trespassing upon. In any other place those sounds would have been friendly. Here they came like intruders, as if the world of dinners and household talk, of teacups and children’s feet, had put its ear to a door behind which nothing spoke but pain.

“I make no apology, Mr. Blake, for receiving you here,” Jennings said. “It is the only room where we can be sure of not being interrupted. My papers are ready. And here are two books which we may need to consult. Bring your chair to the table.”

I sat. He handed me two folio leaves—one sparsely covered; the other thick with writing in red and black ink, packed from top to bottom. In the irritation of my suspense, the second page looked like a wall.

“Have some mercy on me,” I said. “Tell me what I am to expect before I try to read this.”

“Willingly. But first—one or two questions more.”

“Ask what you please.”

He looked at me with that sad sweetness of his—no affectation, no professional hardness—only a kind attention that seemed to carry its own fatigue.

“You have told me that you have never, to your knowledge, tasted opium.”

“To my knowledge,” I repeated, and felt the ominous emphasis of his words.

“You will see presently why I keep that reservation. Last year, at this time, you suffered from nervous irritation. You slept badly at night—except on the birthday night, when you slept soundly. Is that true?”

“Quite true.”

“Can you assign any cause for the nervous suffering and the sleeplessness?”

“I can assign none. Betteredge guessed at one—but—”

“Anything is worth mentioning here. What did Betteredge say?”

“That I had left off smoking.”

“Had you been a habitual smoker?”

“Yes.”

“Did you give it up suddenly?”

“Yes.”

He nodded as if I had supplied a missing link.

“Then Betteredge was right. A man must have no common constitution who can throw off a habit of that kind at once without some temporary disorder of the nerves. Your sleepless nights are accounted for, to my mind.” He paused a moment, and then asked quietly: “Do you remember any dispute with Mr. Candy at the birthday dinner, or after it, about his profession?”

The question stirred a remembrance I had left to sleep. I recalled the substance, though not the foolish detail: my rash talk at table, my obstinate attack on medicine, Mr. Candy’s irritation, Lady Verinder’s interference, and our reconciliation before we parted.

“One thing more,” Jennings said. “It is important. Had you any reason for special anxiety about the Diamond at that time?”

“The strongest reasons. I knew it was the object of a conspiracy. I had been warned to protect Miss Verinder as the possessor of the stone.”

“Was the Diamond spoken of immediately before you went to bed?”

“It was the subject of talk between Lady Verinder and her daughter.”

“In your hearing?”

“Yes.”

Jennings took up his notes and placed them in my hands as if he were putting a verdict into them.

“Mr. Blake, read now by the light of what you have told me, and you will make two discoveries about yourself. First: you entered Miss Verinder’s sitting-room and took the Diamond in a trance produced by opium. Second: the opium was given to you by Mr. Candy—without your knowledge—as a practical refutation of what you had said to him at dinner.”

For a moment the room, with all its ghastly objects and its stolen garden-sounds, swam before me. I sat with the papers as if they had turned to stone.

“Try to forgive poor Mr. Candy,” Jennings said gently. “He has done dreadful mischief—but innocently. But for his illness, he would have returned to Lady Verinder’s the next morning and owned the trick. Miss Verinder would have heard. Miss Verinder would have questioned him. And the truth, which has lain hidden for a year, would have been found in a day.”

Anger came back first—anger, and a kind of sick humiliation.

“Mr. Candy is beyond my resentment,” I said. “But treachery is treachery. I may forgive it—I shall not forget it.”

“Every medical man commits that treachery, as you call it, sometimes in practice,” Jennings answered. “The ignorant distrust of opium is not confined to the poor. Doctors are driven to deceive patients. I do not defend the folly of playing you such a trick under such circumstances. I only ask you to judge motives more mercifully.”

“How was it done?” I asked. “Who gave me laudanum without my knowing it?”

“I cannot tell you. Mr. Candy dropped nothing on that part of the matter during his illness. Perhaps your own memory—”

“No.”

“Then it is useless to pursue it. The laudanum was secretly given in some way. Leave that. There are nearer necessities.” His voice sharpened, not with cruelty, but with purpose. “Read my notes. Familiarise yourself with what happened. I have something bold—startling—to propose, that concerns the future.”

Those words pricked me into attention as a knife pricks a numb limb.

I took the sheets in the order he had placed them. The top one held only scattered fragments—broken utterances, as if a mind in delirium had dropped bits of sentence like crumbs on a floor. I read:

“... Mr. Franklin Blake ... clever and agreeable ... down a peg ... medicine ... confesses ... sleep at night ... tell him ... out of order ... medicine ... groping in the dark mean one and the same thing ... all the company at the dinner-table ... I say ... groping after sleep ... nothing but medicine ... he says ... leading the blind ... know what it means ... witty ... a night’s rest in spite of his teeth ... wants sleep ... Lady Verinder’s medicine chest ... five-and-twenty minims ... without his knowing it ... tomorrow morning ... Well, Mr. Blake ... medicine today ... never ... without it ... out, Mr. Candy ... excellent ... without it ... down on him ... truth ... something besides ... excellent ... dose of laudanum, sir ... bed ... what ... medicine now.”

I handed it back.

“That is what you heard at his bedside?”

“Literally and exactly—except for repetitions,” Jennings said. “He repeated certain words and phrases over and over, according as the idea mattered to him. That helped me to piece the fragments together.” He pointed to the second sheet. “And don’t suppose I pretend I give you Mr. Candy’s connected expressions. I give you the connected thought that lay underneath. Judge for yourself.”

Now I turned to the other page, and understood at once what I was looking at: the black ink held Mr. Candy’s broken phrases; between them, in red ink, Jennings had stitched the sense—like a careful cook taking scraps and bones and, with patience and skill, drawing from them a strong clear broth. It was not magic; it was method. Out of ravelled delirium, he had made a plain narrative that any man could follow.

“Mr. Franklin Blake is clever and agreeable, but he wants taking down a peg when he talks of medicine. He confesses that he has been suffering from want of sleep at night. I tell him that his nerves are out of order, and that he ought to take medicine. He tells me that taking medicine and groping in the dark mean one and the same thing. This before all the company at the dinner-table. I say to him, you are groping after sleep, and nothing but medicine can help you to find it. He says to me, I have heard of the blind leading the blind, and now I know what it means. Witty—but I can give him a night’s rest in spite of his teeth. He really wants sleep; and Lady Verinder’s medicine chest is at my disposal. Give him five-and-twenty minims of laudanum tonight, without his knowing it; and then call tomorrow morning. ‘Well, Mr. Blake, will you try a little medicine today? You will never sleep without it.’—‘There you are out, Mr. Candy: I have had an excellent night’s rest without it.’ Then, come down on him with the truth! ‘You have had something besides an excellent night’s rest; you had a dose of laudanum, sir, before you went to bed. What do you say to the art of medicine, now?’”

The shock of it was not merely the accusation against my own conduct. It was the petty vanity of the motive—the doctor’s desire to triumph at breakfast with a neat little sting of “I told you so”—set against the enormous consequence. The mind sickened at such disproportion.

In spite of myself, the first clear feeling that rose was admiration of Jennings’s ingenuity—the way he had brought order out of chaos. I began to speak of it; he stopped me with a modest movement.

“Do you draw the same conclusion I draw?” he asked. “That you were acting under the influence of laudanum in what you did that night?”

“I am too ignorant,” I said. “I can only accept your opinion, and I believe you are right.”

“Very well. Then comes the great question. You are convinced; I am convinced. How do we carry our conviction into other minds?”

I pointed to the pages between us.

“Useless,” he said, and shook his head. “Useless for three reasons. First: they were taken under circumstances outside common experience—men distrust what lies beyond their own habits. Second: they represent a medical and metaphysical theory—men distrust theory. Third: they are of my making—nothing but my word guarantees they are not fabricated. Remember what I told you on the moor, and ask yourself what my word is worth. No, Mr. Blake. The only value of these notes, to the world, is that they show us how to prove you innocent. We must put our conviction to the test—and you are the man to prove it.”

“How?” I said.

He leaned forward, his eyes intent, his voice low.

“Are you willing to try a bold experiment?”

“I will do anything to clear myself.”

“Will you submit to personal inconvenience for a time?”

“To any.”

“Will you be guided implicitly by my advice? You may be laughed at by fools. You may be opposed by friends whose opinions you respect.”

“Tell me what to do,” I burst out. “Come what may, I’ll do it.”

He answered, very steadily:

“You shall steal the Diamond unconsciously for the second time—in the presence of witnesses whose testimony is beyond dispute.”

I sprang up as if the words had struck me. My throat tightened. I tried to speak and could not; I could only stare at him across the stained deal table, with the skull looking down between us.

“I believe it can be done,” he went on. “And it shall be done—if you will help me. Compose yourself. Sit down and hear me.” He waited until I obeyed him—mechanically, like a man dragged back from a precipice. Then he said: “You have resumed smoking. How long?”

“For nearly a year.”

“More or less than before?”

“More.”

“Will you give it up again? Suddenly—as you gave it up then.”

I began to see the outline of his design—like the first smell of yeast in a kitchen tells you bread is being prepared.

“I will give it up from this moment,” I said.

“If the same consequences follow as followed last June,” he continued—“if you suffer again from sleepless nights—we gain our first step. We bring you back towards your nervous condition of the birthday night. If we can then revive—nearly revive—the domestic circumstances that surrounded you; if we can occupy your mind again with the Diamond, as it occupied you then; we may replace you, as nearly as possible, physically and morally, in the same position in which opium found you last year. In that case, a repetition of the dose may lead, in some degree, to a repetition of the result. That is my proposal, in brief. And now you shall see I have authority for it.”

He opened one of the books beside him, at a place marked by a slip of paper.

“I am not going to weary you with a lecture,” he said. “I only mean to prove I am not asking you to do this on a fancy of my own. Science sanctions what I propose.” He handed me the slip. “Here is the principle, stated by Dr. Carpenter. Read it.”

I read:

“There seems much ground for the belief, that every sensory impression which has once been recognised by the perceptive consciousness, is registered (so to speak) in the brain, and may be reproduced at some subsequent time, although there may be no consciousness of its existence in the mind during the whole intermediate period.”

“Plain?” he asked.

“Perfectly.”

He pushed the open book towards me and pointed to a passage marked in pencil.

“Now read this case. The book is Dr. Elliotson’s Human Physiology. The case rests on Mr. Combe’s authority.”

I read:

“Dr. Abel informed me of an Irish porter to a warehouse, who forgot, when sober, what he had done when drunk; but, being drunk, again recollected the transactions of his former state of intoxication. On one occasion, being drunk, he had lost a parcel of some value, and in his sober moments could give no account of it. Next time he was intoxicated, he recollected that he had left the parcel at a certain house, and there being no address on it, it had remained there safely, and was got on his calling for it.”

“Plain again?” he asked.

“As plain as need be.”

He replaced the slip and closed the book.

“Are you satisfied I have not spoken without authority?” he said. “If not, I can point you to more.”

“I am satisfied,” I answered. “Without another word.”

“Then let us be just on both sides. Something can be said against the experiment as well as for it. If we could exactly reproduce last year’s conditions, the result would certainly be the same. But that is impossible. We can only approximate. If we don’t get you near enough, we fail. If we do get near enough—and I hope we shall—you may so far repeat your proceedings as to satisfy any reasonable person that, morally speaking, you are guiltless of the theft. I have stated the question fairly, I think. If anything is unclear, ask me.”

“There is one point,” I said. “I don’t understand the effect of laudanum. I can’t reconcile it with my walking downstairs, along corridors, opening drawers, returning to my room. Those are active acts. I thought opium stupefied a man, and then sent him to sleep.”

“The common error,” Jennings replied. “At this moment, I am exerting my intelligence—such as it is—in your service, under a dose of laudanum ten times larger than Mr. Candy gave you. But don’t take my word even for that. I have brought independent testimony—testimony that will carry weight with you and with your friends.”

He took up the second book and held it out.

“There are the Confessions of an English Opium-Eater. Take it away and read the passage I have marked. You will find that when De Quincey had what he calls ‘a debauch of opium,’ he went to the Opera to enjoy the music—or wandered the London markets on Saturday night, watching the poor bargain and scheme for their Sunday dinners. So much for a man’s capacity to move and act under opium.”

“I am answered so far,” I said, turning the book over in my hands. “But I am not answered yet as to the effect produced by the opium on myself.”
