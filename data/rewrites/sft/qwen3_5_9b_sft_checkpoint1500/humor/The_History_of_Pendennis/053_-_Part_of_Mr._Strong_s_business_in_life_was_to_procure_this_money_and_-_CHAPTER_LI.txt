Long ago, in the opening pages of this chronicle, we paused to glance at the tiny hamlet of Clavering, nestled beside Pen's ancestral home of Fairoaks. We noted its inhabitants, though we barely spared them a moment for amusement, for the social circle there was, to put it mildly, lacking in sparkle. Yet, amidst this drab backdrop, Mr. Samuel Huxter, the dashing spirit we last encountered at Vauxhall, remained a beacon of liveliness. During his vacations, he transformed the local gatherings into raucous affairs, regaling friends with the sharp wit of Bartholomew's Coffee House and the scandalous gossip of high London society.

Then there was Mr. Hobnell, the young gentleman Pen had once beaten soundly following the Fotheringay squabble. While a schoolboy at Clavering's Grammar School, Hobnell was a favorite at Mrs. Huxter's table, a master of the local apothecary's shop knowing exactly where to find the tamarind pots and how to scent his handkerchiefs with rosewater. It was during those school days that he fell hopelessly in love with Miss Sophy Huxter. Upon his father's passing, he wed her and moved into "The Warren," a few miles from Clavering, a property his ancestors had farmed for generations.

His father, a man of grand visions, demolished the humble farmhouse to erect a blinding white mansion complete with sprawling stables, a drawing-room piano, and a pack of hunting dogs. With these additions, the Hobnells ascended from yeomen to the respectable county gentry. When the old Squire died, his son inherited the throne. Young Sam Huxter, meanwhile, delighted in boasting to his London cronies at Bartholomew's about his brother-in-law's new status, his hounds, his horses, and his hospitality. Every year, whenever Mrs. Hobnell's nursery duties allowed, Hobnell would dash to London, secure a room at the Tavistock Hotel, and indulge in every pleasure the city offered alongside his learned brother. From Ascot and the theaters to Vauxhall and the taverns near Covent Garden, the vivacious squire lived life to the fullest, declaring that when in London, one must "go it a bit." Returning to the West Country, he would gift his wife new bonnets and shawls, ready to trade the elegance of town life for the rustic sports of the next eleven months.

Sam and Hobnell maintained a lively correspondence, exchanging metropolitan news for baskets of game and clouted cream. To Sam, Hobnell was a genius; to Hobnell, Sam was the life and soul of the Warren. When Sam arrived, the house erupted in laughter, fueled by songs, jokes, and sheer fun. He was even credited with saving the eldest child's life by extracting a fishbone from her throat. Truly, he was the delight of the circle.

However, fate, in its mischievous way, arranged for Pen to encounter Mr. Huxter merely three days after their disastrous meeting at Vauxhall. True to his vow, Pen had avoided seeing little Fanny, attempting to drown his thoughts in relentless work. As a critic for the *Pall Mall Gazette*, he savaged poems and romances with savage indifference. After slaying these literary victims, he retired to the lonely Polyanthus Club, where vast silences only deepened his gloom. Theater outings offered no relief; instead, he watched an ignoble farce that filled him with sorrow, his dismal countenance surely dampening the spirits of the actor on stage.

Exhausted from walking twenty miles over Hampstead Common seeking rest, Pen decided to visit the Back Kitchen, his old haunt with Warrington. He wasn't tired, merely in need of distraction. There, Bows presided calmly over the piano, leading a session of comic songs that cracked the room with laughter. To Pen, these melodies sounded strangely alien, yet he found himself fixated solely on Bows. Within what he claimed was an extinct volcano of emotion, a sudden flame ignited—a result of two days' indulgence followed by two days of abstinence. Just as he mused on this internal turmoil, drinking glass after glass, Arthur's gaze landed on Mr. Huxter. Like Pen, Huxter had attended the theater and now sat with two companions. Huxter whispered to his friends, clearly causing Pen discomfort, before working his way around the room until he seated himself opposite Pen, offering a familiar nod and a dirty handshake.

Pen accepted the hand, feeling a pang of regret for his previous savagery. Huxter, however, wore a mask of perfect good humor, utterly unaware that he could ever be disagreeable. To him, the Vauxhall altercation was mere "chaff," a trifling jest easily forgotten.

Seeking a suitable topic, Huxter and his party decided to strike a chord most painful to Pen.
"Jolly night at Vauxhall—wasn't it?" Huxter asked, winking knowingly.
"I'm glad you liked it," Pen groaned inwardly.
"I was dev'lish cut," Huxter continued, feigning concern. "Uncommon. Been dining with some chaps at Greenwich. That was a pretty bit of muslin hanging on your arm—who was she?"

The question broke Pen. "Have I asked you any questions about yourself, Mr. Huxter?" he snapped.
"I didn't mean any offence—beg pardon—hang it, you cut up quite savage!" Huxter exclaimed, astonished.
"Do you remember what took place between us the other night?" Pen demanded, his wrath rising. "You forget? Very probably. You were tipsy, as you observed, and very rude."
"Hang it, sir, I asked your pardon," Huxter looked red.
"You did certainly, and I granted it with all my heart," Pen retorted. "But if you recall, I begged you to omit me from your list of acquaintances forever. And when we meet in public, I implore you not to take the trouble to recognize me. Will you please remember this henceforth? And since the song begins, permit me to leave you to enjoy the music without restraint."

Raising his hat, Pen bowed to the stunned Mr. Huxter and departed the table. As Pen left, his companions, after a brief pause of awe, let out a roar of laughter at Huxter's expense, prompting the room's president to bark, "Silence, gentlemen! Do have silence for the Body Snatcher!"—the popular song that was just starting. Pen prided himself on commanding his temper, though he secretly wished Huxter had been pugnacious; he craved a fight. He went home, unsettled by the day's work, the dinner, the play, the whisky, and the quarrel. Sleep eluded him completely.

Days later, Mr. Sam Huxter penned a scathing letter to his brother-in-law, Mr. Hobnell, detailing Arthur Pendennis's exploits. Sam described Arthur as a confounded insolent rogue, an "abandoned criminal" and a "regular Don Juan." He accused Arthur of turning an innocent girl from the lower ranks into a victim, noting that he had identified her from an Irish gentleman formerly in the army who frequented their club. Huxter vowed to warn her father, concluding his missive with thanks for recent parcels of game and a hint of his eagerness for future favors.

About once a year, a christening occurred at the Warren. This particular ceremony took place the day after Hobnell received the letter. The infant, a darling little girl named Myra Lucretia after her godmothers, became the center of attention. Naturally, Hobnell shared Sam's letter with his wife, and Mrs. Hobnell, in turn, disseminated the horrid contents to her gossip network. By afternoon, the entire village of Clavering knew the story.

Myra, too shocked to speak, remained silent, but Mrs. Pybus was another matter. She discussed the scandal not only with Mrs. Portman but also with the Honorable Mrs. Simcoe, Mrs. Glanders, Madame Fribsby, and indeed the whole of Clavering society. Madame Fribsby, eyeing her portrait of a dragoon and reflecting on her own wounded memories, mused that "men will be men" and that deception was inherent to their nature, quoting lines from *Marmion* regarding where deceitful lovers should rest. Mrs. Pybus, however, lacked words strong enough to describe a villain capable of such baseness. She attributed Arthur's downfall to early indulgence, insolence, extravagance, and "aristocratic airs"—noting specifically that Pen had refused to drink tea with her. Blaming the "corrupt and horrid parties" of modern Babylon, she lamented the ruin of a promising boy.

Mrs. Portman feared that her own fatal partiality had spoiled the child, suggesting that literary success had turned his head and his "horrid passions" caused him to forget the principles instilled by Dr. Portman. Captain Glanders, the "atrocious" Dragoon, whistled and made jocular allusions when informed by Mrs. Glanders, earning a rebuke as a brute and the removal of the girls from the room. Mr. Simcoe remained calm, perhaps even pleased, viewing the incident as confirmation of his low opinion of the "wretched young man."
