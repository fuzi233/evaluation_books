The shadow of want no longer darkened my door. The passing of my young kinsman altered my fortunes irrevocably, placing me next in line to a considerable estate. My uncle and his lady, now advanced in years, were unlikely to produce further heirs. "Mark my words," Sampson muttered darkly, "that woman would slit your throat with a psalm on her lips if it served her." Yet in truth, Lady Warrington bore me no such malice. Grief had chastened her proud spirit. The clergymen who flocked to her mourning chambers preached fiery sermons on divine chastisement. "Your heart clung too fiercely to earthly vanities," they thundered. "You sought a coronet for your son—what is gilt and velvet compared to the crown of righteousness?"

A year after the tragedy, she summoned me to London. I found her swathed in black crepe, her Bible open upon the table. She lectured me at length on my moral failings—my scandalous associations with playhouses and poets, my neglect of sacred duties. When I took my leave, she pressed upon me a bundle of sermons for my wife and a hymnal by Miss Dora, who later married a celebrated preacher. The dowager forgave me, but she could never endure the sight of our living child.

My uncle, however, doted on the boy. He would sit for hours watching him play, bringing toys and sweetmeats, begging to be called "Godpapa." In his eyes, I saw my own grief reflected—the raw wound of a lost child that never truly heals. My wife understood this sorrow before I did; her compassion bridged the rift between us. When we buried our second babe, my uncle sent his carriage to follow the small coffin. Afterwards, he took little Miles for an airing, the child chattering obliviously in his new black clothes. How cruelly his innocent prattle pierced us! Even now, decades later, I catch my wife gazing into the fire, communing with that tiny soul in paradise.

We returned to Bloomsbury, where my growing bear of a son—now too sturdy for leading-strings—was packed off to the Charterhouse under Charley's watchful eye. The boy thrived there, boasting that his own sons would follow him to that venerable institution.

Freed from nursemaid duties, I turned to loftier pursuits. My uncle, ever the obsequious courtier, secured me a post licensing hackney-coaches—a sinecure I filled with dignity until political winds shifted. I took my degree at the Temple, donned the barrister's wig, and even accompanied my benefactor Foker to Paris, where the gallant Florac introduced me to Versailles' glittering circles. Our ladies, meanwhile, amused themselves at Wimbledon with my "Parisian letters," forwarded through the obliging Mr. Hume of the Embassy.

Yet even as I enjoyed this tranquil existence, storm clouds gathered across the Atlantic. When Grenville proposed his stamp duties, I warned my wife how fiercely our colonial kin would resent taxation. But none could foresee the hurricane of rebellion that followed. Was it the paltry tax that sparked the fire? No—it was the smoldering resentment of years, the slights endured, the ancient grudges nursed. Had I remained in Virginia, I might have joined the revolt myself, if only to spite my domineering mother.

Then came Hal's fiery letters from Virginia, brimming with patriotic fervor. My dear brother was ever a weathervane, turning to every persuasive wind. "He rebels against Madam Esmond's yoke," I observed. "Or perhaps some local enchantress guides him," Theo suggested slyly. Hal's missives overflowed with devotion—to his new bride, to me, even as he unknowingly profited from our mother's harsh economy. Only later did he learn how she had beggared me to enrich him, a revelation that sparked furious quarrels.

Among his papers, I found the letter announcing his betrothal—a document my sentimental wife preserved, though it pained her. "To think whom he might have married!" she sighed, thinking of poor rejected Hetty. But Hal's heart had been captured by Fanny Mountain, once a shy girl, now blossomed into beauty. Their courtship unfolded like a comedy—clandestine sighs, intercepted letters, and the inevitable explosion when Madam Esmond discovered them.

"An ungrateful, artful, false little wretch!" she stormed when she found Fanny in tears. "What would Mr. Lintot say to your flirtations?" (The apothecary, it seemed, had been her preferred suitor.) Hal, ever the hothead, swore he'd brain any man who insulted Fanny's honor—including his own mother. The scene escalated until Madam, clutching her tortoiseshell stick like a scepter, lamented her divine punishment: "I favored Jacob over Esau, and now my pride is broken!"

Thus did Harry Warrington, once the pampered younger son, bow to the common lot—learning humility through loss, finding love despite opposition, and stumbling toward his destiny, one imperfect step at a time.
