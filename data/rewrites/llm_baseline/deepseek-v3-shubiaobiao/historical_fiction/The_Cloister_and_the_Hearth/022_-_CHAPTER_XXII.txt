The shot rang out—sudden, startling—and for a moment, even the shooter stood frozen, stunned by his own handiwork. Then, with a shout, he called his comrades to join the chase, already sprinting up the hillside. But before he had gone halfway, a figure rose from the earth like a specter: Martin Wittenhaagen, bow bent, arrow nocked.

Eric Wouverman knew that sight too well. In an instant, he flung himself behind the nearest tree, pressing flat against the bark. Martin’s aim was legend, and the dead hound at their feet was proof enough of it. Cautiously, Wouverman edged his eye around the trunk—only to find the arrow’s glinting point still trained on him. He held his breath.

Minutes crawled by before the others arrived, a clamor of boots and voices swelling behind him. Then, with a laugh sharp as a blade, Martin was gone, the thunder of hooves marking his retreat. The men surged forward, cresting the hill just in time to glimpse their quarry—tiny as insects in the distance—fleeing down a narrow, endless glade: Gerard and Margaret, running as if the devil himself were at their heels, with Martin galloping hard behind them.

The hunters had been outplayed.

But how?

The answer lay in a trick of chance—or perhaps fate’s cruel humor. Just as the bolt flew, the burgomaster’s mule had stumbled, her hoof snared in a rabbit hole. She pitched forward, spilling both riders. Gerard caught Margaret, but momentum bore them down, a tangle of limbs upon the earth. The mule, ever docile, scrambled up at once, trembling but unharmed.

Martin, quick as ever, took stock: one pursuer, no more. A gamble, then. He sent the lovers fleeing on foot while he stood his ground, arrow drawn, buying them time. Now, with the enemy stalled, he gave chase, overtaking Gerard and Margaret in a breathless sprint. The mule became their salvation—riding in turns, sharing the burden of speed, swapping rider for runner until even the wind could not have tracked them.

By the time they slowed, the forest had swallowed them whole. No sign, no sound.

Behind, Dierich’s men could only turn back, empty-handed, to tend their fallen hound and nurse their wounded pride.
