"I have lost a beautiful girl, an excellent social standing, and a handsome income," Mr. Godfrey began, his voice trembling slightly. "Yet I have submitted to this loss without a single struggle. What motive could possibly drive someone to such extraordinary conduct? My dear friend, there is no motive."

"No motive?" I repeated, leaning forward with genuine curiosity.

"Allow me to appeal to your experience regarding children," he continued, gesturing vaguely toward the ceiling. "A child follows a certain path. You observe it with wonder and seek the reason. Yet the little creature is utterly incapable of explaining its own actions. You might as well ask the grass why it grows or the birds why they sing. Well! In this matter, I am precisely like that child—like the grass, like the birds. I do not know why I proposed marriage to Miss Verinder. I do not know why I have shamefully neglected my dear Ladies. I do not know why I have abandoned the Mothers' Small-Clothes. You ask the child, 'Why have you been naughty?' And the little angel puts a finger in its mouth and remains silent. That is my exact situation, Miss Clack! I could not confess this to anyone else. I feel compelled to confide it in *you*!"

I began to regain my composure. Here was a mental puzzle, and I am deeply fascinated by such puzzles—and, it is believed, possessed of some skill in unraveling them.

"Best of friends, exert your intellect and assist me," he urged. "Explain to me: why does a time arrive when these matrimonial proceedings seem like a fever dream? Why does it suddenly dawn on me that my true happiness lies in serving my dear Ladies, in making my modest rounds of useful work, and in speaking my few earnest words when summoned by my Chairman? What need do I have of a position? I already have one! What need do I have of an income? I can afford my bread, my cheese, my cozy lodging, and my two coats per annum. What need do I have of Miss Verinder? She has told me with her own lips—dear lady, this is strictly between us—that she loves another man, and that her sole purpose in marrying me is to try and dislodge that other man from her thoughts. What a dreadful union this is! Oh, dear me, what a terrible union! These are my reflections, Miss Clack, as I travel toward Brighton. I approach Rachel with the feeling of a criminal awaiting sentencing. When I discover she has changed her mind—when I hear her propose to dissolve the engagement—I experience (without a shred of doubt) an overwhelming sense of relief. A month ago, I was pressing her rapturously against my bosom. An hour ago, the joy of knowing I shall never press her again intoxicates me like strong spirits. The whole thing seems impossible; it cannot be true. And yet, here are the facts, as I stated clearly when we first sat down in these very chairs. I have lost a beautiful girl, a fine social position, and a generous income; and I have accepted this fate without resistance. Can *you* explain it, dear friend? It is entirely beyond *me*."

His magnificent head sank onto his chest, and he surrendered to his own mental dilemma in despair.

I was profoundly moved. The case—if I may speak as a spiritual physician—was now perfectly clear to me. It is not an uncommon occurrence in life to witness those of exalted ability occasionally humbled to the level of the most ordinary folk around them. Presumably, within the wise economy of Providence, the objective is to remind greatness that it is mortal, and that the power which grants it can also strip it away. Now, in my mind, it was easy to discern one of these salutary humiliations in the deplorable behavior of dear Mr. Godfrey, which I had witnessed unseen. Equally easy was it to recognize the welcome resurgence of his finer nature in the horror with which he recoiled from the prospect of marrying Rachel, and in the charming eagerness he displayed to return to his Ladies and the Poor.

I presented this perspective to him in a few simple, sisterly words. His joy was beautiful to behold. As I continued, comparing himself to a lost man emerging from darkness into light, his gratitude overflowed. When I promised a warm welcome back to the Mothers' Small-Clothes, the grateful heart of our Christian hero swelled. He pressed my hands alternately to his lips. Overwhelmed by the exquisite triumph of reclaiming him among us, I allowed him to do as he pleased with my hands. I closed my eyes. Feeling my head sink onto his shoulder in an ecstasy of spiritual self-forgetfulness, I nearly swooned in his embrace. However, an interruption from the outside world snapped me back to reality. A horrid rattling of knives and forks echoed outside the door, and the footman entered to set the table for lunch.

Mr. Godfrey sprang up and glanced at the clock on the mantelpiece.

"How time flies with *you*!" he exclaimed. "I shall barely catch the train."

I ventured to ask why he was so eager to return to town. His answer hinted at unresolved family difficulties and impending disagreements.

"I have received word from my father," he said. "Business requires him to leave Frizinghall for London today, and he intends to come here, either tonight or tomorrow. I must inform him of what transpired between Rachel and me. His heart is set on our marriage; I fear there will be significant difficulty in convincing him to accept the dissolution of the engagement. I must prevent him from arriving here until he *is* reconciled. Best and dearest of friends, we shall meet again!"

With those words, he hurried out. In equal haste, I ran upstairs to compose myself in my own room before joining Aunt Ablewhite and Rachel at the luncheon table.

I am fully aware—that I must pause briefly to discuss Mr. Godfrey—that the profane opinions of the world have accused him of having selfish motives for releasing Rachel from her engagement at the first opportunity she afforded. Rumor has also reached my ears that his eagerness to restore his standing in my eyes has been attributed in certain circles to a mercenary desire to make peace (through me) with a venerable committee-woman of the Mothers' Small-Clothes, generously endowed with material blessings and a beloved, intimate friend of my own. I mention these vile slanders solely to declare that they never influenced my judgment. Following my instructions, I have recorded the fluctuations in my opinion of our Christian hero exactly as they appear in my diary. For justice's sake, let me add that once reinstated in my esteem, my gifted friend never lost that place again. I write with tears in my eyes, burning to say more. But no—I am cruelly restricted to my actual experience of persons and things. Less than a month from the time of which I write, events in the money market (which reduced even *my* miserable small income) forced me into foreign exile, leaving me with nothing but a loving memory of Mr. Godfrey, which the slander of the world has attacked and failed to destroy.

Let me wipe my eyes and resume my narrative.

I descended to lunch, naturally anxious to gauge how Rachel was affected by her release from the marriage engagement.

It seemed to me—though I admit I am a poor judge of such matters—that regaining her freedom had caused her to reflect anew on the other man she loved, and that she was furious with herself for being unable to suppress a revulsion of feeling of which she was secretly ashamed. Who was the man? I harbored suspicions—but it was pointless to waste time in idle speculation. Once I had converted her, she would, as a matter of course, reveal no secrets to me. I would learn everything about the man; I would learn everything about the Moonstone. Had I no higher aim in stirring her toward spiritual awareness, the mere motive of relieving her mind of its guilty secrets would have been sufficient encouragement to proceed.

In the afternoon, Aunt Ablewhite took her exercise in an invalid chair, accompanied by Rachel. "I wish I could drag this chair," she broke out recklessly. "I wish I could tire myself until I was ready to collapse."

She remained in the same mood in the evening. I discovered passages in one of my friend's cherished publications—the *Life, Letters, and Labours of Miss Jane Ann Stamper*, forty-fourth edition—that bore with marvelous appropriateness on Rachel's current predicament. Upon suggesting I read them aloud, she went to the piano. Imagine how little she must know of serious people if she supposed my patience could be exhausted in such a manner! I kept Miss Jane Ann Stamper beside me, waiting for events with unshakable trust in the future.

Old Mr. Ablewhite never appeared that night. But I understood the weight his worldly greed placed on his son's marriage to Miss Verinder—and I felt a firm conviction (regardless of whatever Mr. Godfrey might do to prevent it) that we would see him the following day. With his interference, the storm I had anticipated would surely break, and the salutary exhaustion of Rachel's resisting powers would inevitably follow. I am not unaware that Old Mr. Ablewhite enjoys a general reputation (particularly among his inferiors) as a remarkably good-natured man. By my observations, however, he deserves that reputation only as long as he has his own way, and not a moment longer.
