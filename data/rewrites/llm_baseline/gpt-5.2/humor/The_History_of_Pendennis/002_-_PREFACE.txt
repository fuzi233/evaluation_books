If this sort of book—stirred with a busy spoon for two full years and now set down before you like a steaming dish on the table—fails in what clever people call Art (as it regularly does, and must, when the cook keeps leaning out of the kitchen to gossip with the guests), it has at least the plain advantage of being honest fare. A more elaborate banquet might be daintier in ornament, more symmetrical in courses, and yet lose the true savour in the over-handling.

For in this continual passing of plates and words between writer and reader, a man is forced into a kind of domestic frankness. He cannot keep his face powdered and his hands gloved the whole time: the talk grows familiar; the apron gets stained; he blurts his mind as it rises—like a pot that will boil over when it has a mind to. Looking back, he sees many a slip of pen and printer, many a seasoning flung in too hastily, many a word that, once served, he would gladly call back to the kitchen and scrape off. But it is a confidential meal, and like most long meals it must sometimes be dull, must sometimes flag. The perpetual speaker—puffing, perspiring, carving, apologising, insisting you take another slice—must, by sheer length of service, betray his own weaknesses, vanities, and peculiarities, as inevitably as a parish baker betrays, by the end of winter, whether he is generous with crusts or stingy with crumbs.

And as we judge a man, after long frequenting his table and his talk, not by one toast, or one temper, or one day’s cheerful hospitality, but by the steady tenor of his conversation and bearing; so, of a writer who gives himself up to you unreservedly, you will ask—Is he honest? Does he tell the truth in the main? Does he seem driven by a desire to find it and speak it? Or is he a quack, who sells sugared sentiment as if it were nourishing bread, and mouths for effect? Does he seek popularity by claptraps—those theatrical dishes made mostly of noise—or any other arts?

I cannot pretend I have been unlucky. I have found many thousands more readers than I ever expected to have sitting at my board, listening to the clink of my plates and the scratch of my knife. I have no right to say to them, You shall not find fault with my cookery, or yawn between courses, or fall asleep with your napkin over your face. But I ask you to believe that the person serving you, however ill he may truss a sentence or overbrown a paragraph, strives to tell the truth. If there is not that, there is nothing—no broth beneath the garnish, no honest grain beneath the icing.

Perhaps the lovers of “excitement”—those who cannot dine unless the dishes come in with a drum and a trumpet—may care to know that this book began with a very precise plan, which was entirely put aside. Ladies and gentlemen, you were to have been treated (and the writer’s and publisher’s pockets comfortably benefited) by a recital of the most active horrors, served up hot and perpetually. What more “piquant” than a ruffian—seasoned with many admirable virtues, as these people always are in the shops—lodging in St. Giles’s, and visited constantly by a young lady from Belgravia, who would rustle in like a white soufflé among frying onions? What more stirring than the contrast of societies? the mixture of slang and fashionable language, like pepper dashed into custard? the escapes, the battles, the murders—those great joints of melodrama brought in at every remove?

Nay, up to nine o’clock this very morning, my poor friend Colonel Altamont was doomed to execution, and the author only relented when his victim was actually at the window—hanging there, as it were, between the street and the noose, between the public appetite and the private conscience, while the whole scene clattered like a badly-managed kitchen at dinner-hour.

But the “exciting” plan was laid aside (with a very honourable forbearance on the part of the publishers), because, on attempting it, I found I failed from sheer want of experience of my subject. Never having been intimate with any convict in my life—never having sat down to supper with a gaol-bird, nor learned his dialect as one learns the cherished names of herbs from an old countrywoman—those manners being quite unfamiliar to me, the idea of entering into competition with M. Eugène Sue was abandoned. To describe a real rascal, you must make him so horrible that he would be too hideous to show; and unless the painter paints him fairly, I hold he has no right to show him at all. The worst thing is not the ugliness, but the falseness: the counterfeit coin of language that passes too easily, and buys applause it does not deserve.

Even the gentlemen of our age—this being an attempt to describe one of them, no better nor worse than most educated men—even these we cannot show as they are, with the notorious foibles and selfishness of their lives and their education. Since the author of *Tom Jones* was buried, no writer of fiction among us has been permitted to depict to his utmost power a MAN. We must drape him, and give him a certain conventional simper, as if truth itself must be muffled and spoken in drawing-room phrases. Society will not tolerate the Natural in our Art. A cry is raised if you let the thing speak in its own tongue, if you let a passion call itself by the word it uses in the club, the college, the mess-room. The very vocabulary is policed: certain honest names are forbidden, and we are forced to substitute genteel counters, as if one could change the taste of garlic by calling it “a foreign perfume.”

Many ladies have remonstrated and subscribers left me, because, in the course of the story, I described a young man resisting and affected by temptation. My object was to say that he had the passions to feel, and the manliness and generosity to overcome them. But you will not hear—it is best to know it—what moves in the real world; what passes in society; what is said and done where your sons sit and eat and laugh, where their talk makes its own rough music, sometimes pleasant as a country fair, sometimes harsh as a steam-mill grinding all day and night. A little more frankness than is customary has been attempted in this story; with no bad desire on the writer’s part, it is hoped, and with no ill consequence to any reader.

If truth is not always pleasant, at any rate truth is best—from whatever chair: from those whence graver writers or thinkers argue, or from that at which the story-teller sits when the last dish is gone, the room grows quieter, the clatter of the world outside still thumps and rattles, and he concludes his labour, and bids his kind reader farewell.

KENSINGTON, Nov. 26th, 1850.

PENDENNIS
