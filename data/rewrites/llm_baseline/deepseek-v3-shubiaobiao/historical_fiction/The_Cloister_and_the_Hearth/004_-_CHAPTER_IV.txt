The afternoon sun slanted low, casting golden streaks across the tiled floor of the hosier’s sitting room in Tergou. Eli was occupied in the shop, his eldest and youngest sons away on their own errands. Catherine, fretful with maternal worry, had taken her crippled daughter a short way down the road, peering into the distance for any sign of Gerard. Left alone, Giles entertained himself in his usual fashion—a spectacle of grotesque agility that would have unsettled any stranger who chanced upon it.

The Dutch took pride in their innovations—printing, oil painting, banking—but above all, cleanliness. While other nations trod upon floors strewn with rushes and refuse, the folk of Tergou kept their homes scrubbed and gleaming. The sitting room was no exception, its tiles polished to a shine that could rival a looking-glass. The large window, its small lozenge panes bound by strips of lead, cast a lattice of light and shadow upon the floor, the central cross of stonework serving as a quiet reminder of devotion.

The furniture was sturdy and plain, save for the high-backed oak armchair, carved at its summit, looming like a throne. A copper pail of holy water sat near the door, its little broom ready for sprinkling blessings. And then there was the table—long, narrow, and solid—where Giles now clung, not with hands, but with teeth, his muscular arms splayed like a bat’s wings, his body a misshapen wedge of sinew and will.

Nature had been unkind to Giles, granting him a robust torso and a head fit for a hero, then abandoning her work halfway, leaving his limbs stunted and twisted. Yet what he lacked in symmetry, he made up for in sheer, improbable strength. His arms, unburdened by the weight of a full body, could haul him up a pole like a squirrel, or suspend him from a branch by a single grip. Now, in a fit of boyish mischief, he had fastened his teeth upon the table’s edge, dangling with the tenacity of a barnacle.

Little Kate, returning with her mother, stopped short at the sight. Her practical heart ached for the furniture.

“Giles!” she cried. “You’ll dent the wood!”

He dropped to the floor with a thud, scowling. “Run and tell, then, you little tattler. You’re the one for stirring trouble.”

“Am I?” Kate’s voice was cool, but her eyes shimmered. The insult, unexpected from her usually gentle brother, struck deeper than she cared to admit.

Before Giles could retort, the door creaked open, and Gerard staggered in, pale and dust-streaked, his strength spent. A chorus of gasps and cries rose as the family rushed to him, hands fluttering like startled birds. He sank into the nearest chair, breathless, his journey from Rotterdam etched in the weariness of his limbs.

Revived by their concern, he soon rallied, and the tale of his triumph spilled forth.

“The goldsmiths’ work!” he marveled. “Such reliquaries, such chalices—I never dreamed so much skill existed in the world. And the illuminations from Ghent and Bruges! Mine looked like scribbles beside them.” He shook his head, humbled. “But then—then they called my name.”

Kate’s face lit. “Yours?”

The others scoffed, but Gerard nodded. “Aye. I knelt before the Duke, half-dead with fright, and he gave me this.” He held up the gold medal, and the room erupted in delight.

“And these.” He spilled fifteen golden angels onto the table, each coin gleaming like a tiny sun. The family’s hands darted forward, each claiming their share—Cornelis and Sybrandt with greedy fingers, Giles rolling his along the floor like a plaything, Kate pressing hers to her heart with a look of pure adoration.

“And two for you, little one,” Gerard murmured, pressing the coins into her palm. “For your patience in suffering.”

Catherine, overcome, clutched him to her breast. “My son! My pride!”

Eli, gruff but glowing, declared he must tell the neighbors at once. But Gerard held up a hand. “There’s more.”

He spoke of the Countess’s promise—a benefice, a future secure. Catherine wept anew. “Now we can die in peace,” she whispered, “knowing you will care for them all.”

From that moment, Gerard stood apart in the family—not merely loved, but revered. Cornelis and Sybrandt seethed with envy, counting the days until he left for his parish, when they might leech from his prosperity. Yet Gerard himself turned his mind toward the priesthood with new resolve. Love’s wound still ached, but ambition and duty soothed it.

When the bishop next visited, Gerard knelt and received the office of exorcist, the sacred words granting him power over demons. He walked home with solemn pride, the little book of rites in his hands—until Kate met him at the door, her crutches tapping urgently.

“Gerard! The burgomaster sent for you.”

“Ghysbrecht Van Swieten?” Gerard frowned. “What does he want with me?”

Kate shook her head. “He would not say. Only that you must go at once.”

Gerard’s fingers tightened around the exorcism manual. He had seen the look in Ghysbrecht’s eyes before—cold, calculating. No friend wore such an expression.

“Very well,” he said softly. “I’ll go.”
