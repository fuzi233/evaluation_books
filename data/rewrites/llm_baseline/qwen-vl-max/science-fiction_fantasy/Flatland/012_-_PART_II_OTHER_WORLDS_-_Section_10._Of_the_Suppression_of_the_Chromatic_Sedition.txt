For three long years, the demand for the Universal Colour Bill raged across Flatland like a fever in the blood of the nation. The air trembled with unrest, and every corner of the realm seemed poised on the brink of collapse. Anarchy, once a distant whisper, now loomed as a living threat. In the final days of that turbulent era, a vast army of Polygons—nobles who had taken up arms as common soldiers—was crushed beneath the overwhelming might of the Isosceles Triangles. The Squares and Pentagons, ever cautious, stood aside, their neutrality sealing the fate of the defenders. Worse still, even the highest echelons of society were not spared. Among the Circles—the ruling class—many noble households were torn apart by the fury of political passion. Wives, consumed by rage over their husbands’ refusal to support the Bill, wore them down with ceaseless pleas. When those pleas failed, they turned to violence: some slaughtered their children and spouses alike, then ended their own lives in a final, tragic act of defiance. It is said that twenty-three Circles met their end this way during the three-year struggle.

The crisis was absolute. The Priests, guardians of order, faced only two choices: surrender or annihilation. But just when all hope seemed lost, an event of astonishing simplicity and grotesque irony shifted the course of history—an event so vivid, so emotionally charged, that it could have been forged by a master strategist. For such moments, though born of chaos, possess a power far beyond reason: they grip the hearts of the people and reshape the soul of a nation.

It began with a lowly Isosceles, barely four degrees above the threshold of intelligence, who—while looting a merchant’s shop—came into contact with a set of pigments. Whether he painted himself or was painted by others remains uncertain; the tale varies. But what is clear is that he adorned his body with the twelve distinct hues of a Dodecagon—a mark of rank and distinction reserved for the most elite. Disguised, he entered the Market Place and approached a young maiden, daughter of a noble Polygon, whom he had once courted in vain. With lies, deception, and a string of improbable coincidences, he won her hand. Her family, blinded by negligence and misplaced trust, failed to detect the fraud until it was too late. When she discovered the truth, she took her life.

News of her death spread like wildfire. From one state to another, women reacted with horror. The tragedy struck at the heart of their deepest fears: that marriage, honor, identity—all would become meaningless if colour were allowed to flourish. What had once been seen as progress now appeared as a curse. Women, once indifferent or even sympathetic to the cause, now turned against it. Many declared themselves enemies of the Bill outright; the rest needed only a spark to ignite their resistance.

Seizing the moment, the Circles summoned an extraordinary Assembly. They brought not only the usual guard of Convicts but also a large contingent of conservative Women, carefully selected for their influence. The hall was packed—more than a hundred thousand Isosceles filled the space, roaring with fury. At the center stood Pantocyclus, the Chief Circle of the age. He was greeted not with reverence, but with jeers and hoots. Yet he raised his voice, calm and commanding, and declared that the Circles would no longer resist. They would yield—to the will of the majority. The Colour Bill would be accepted.

A roar erupted—then, instantly, transformed into thunderous applause. The mood had changed. Chromatistes, the leader of the rebellion, was called forward to receive the submission of the Hierarchy. Then Pantocyclus delivered a speech that lasted nearly a full day—so eloquent, so calculated, that no summary can capture its full weight. With apparent fairness, he laid out both sides of the debate. He acknowledged the appeal of reform, but slowly, inexorably, he revealed its hidden costs.

He warned of the dangers to Tradesmen, Professionals, and Gentlemen—how colour would blur distinctions, how it would erode social order. He reminded the Isosceles that, despite their flaws, the Circles were willing to accept the Bill *if* it passed. But he made plain that the rest of society—artisans, workers, nobles—were not convinced. Their silence spoke volumes.

Then he turned to the Workmen. “Your future,” he said, “must not be sacrificed.” Many of you are near promotion to Regular Triangles. Others dream of better lives for your children. But if colour becomes universal, all differences vanish. Irregularity will be mistaken for Regularity. Progress will turn to decay. Within generations, the Workman may fall below the Military, even the Convict Class. Power will pass to the many—the Criminals—who already outnumber you. And soon, they will surpass all others combined, if nature’s laws are ignored.

A murmur rose among the Artisans. Some nodded. Chromatistes stepped forward, eager to reply—but guards surrounded him, silencing him before he could speak.

Pantocyclus then turned to the Women. “If this Bill passes,” he proclaimed, “no marriage will be safe. No woman’s virtue secure. Deception will reign in every home. Domestic peace—like our Constitution—will perish.”

“And I say,” he thundered, “Sooner than that—come death!”

At those words, the signal was given.

The Convict guards surged forward, spearing Chromatistes where he stood. The Regular Classes parted ranks, allowing a phalanx of Women—back foremost, unseen and unstoppable—to advance upon the unsuspecting Isosceles. The Artisans followed suit, opening their lines. Meanwhile, bands of Convicts sealed every entrance, forming an unbreakable wall.

The battle was brief. Under the precise command of the Circles, each Woman’s charge was lethal. Many withdrew their weapons unharmed, ready for another strike. But no second blow was needed. The Isosceles, panicked and leaderless, attacked one another in blind terror. Faced with invisible foes and blocked escape routes, they screamed “treachery!”—and in that cry, they doomed themselves.

Within half an hour, not one remained alive. Seventy thousand bodies lay scattered—shattered by the angles of their own kind. The victory of Order was complete.

The Circles wasted no time. The Working Men were spared, but decimated. The Equilateral Militia was mobilized. Every Triangle suspected of irregularity—judged by appearance alone—was executed by Court Martial, without measurement. Homes were searched. Towns, villages, hamlets—every settlement was purged of excess lower classes. For over a year, the balance of society was restored through relentless cleansing.

And thus, the use of colour was abolished. Its possession became forbidden. To utter a word associated with colour—except by Circles or approved scholars—was punishable by law. Only in the highest, most secret halls of our University—places I have never been permitted to enter—is colour still used sparingly, to illustrate the deepest mysteries of mathematics. Even that is whispered rumor.

Elsewhere in Flatland, colour has vanished. The art of making it is known to only one person: the reigning Chief Circle. On his deathbed, he reveals the secret to his successor alone. One factory produces it, hidden from sight. Each year, the workers are destroyed—consumed in fire or poison—and replaced. The knowledge dies with them.

To this day, even the aristocracy trembles at the memory of the Chromatic Sedition. Not because of the loss of colour—but because they remember what happens when the fabric of order frays. And they know: the next time, there may be no hero to save them.
