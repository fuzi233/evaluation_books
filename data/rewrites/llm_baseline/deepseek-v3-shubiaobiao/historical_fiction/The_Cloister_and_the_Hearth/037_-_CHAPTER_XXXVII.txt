The prisoners stood bound in the marketplace, exposed for two hours so that any who recognized them as perpetrators of other crimes might come forward. Yet no one did—unsurprising, for they were creatures of the night, preying on weary travelers, most of whom were strangers passing through.

Just as the guards prepared to lead them away, a piercing scream cut through the crowd. A woman, her eyes wide with horror, pointed at one of the accused before collapsing in a faint. The bystanders surged around her, torn between aiding her and satisfying their curiosity.

“She pointed at *him*,” one insisted.
“No, at *this* one,” argued another.
“Fools,” snapped a third, “it was the hangdog with the hair around his neck!”

The woman revived with a gasp, then flew at the landlord like a fury. “My child!” she shrieked. “Give me back my child!” She clawed at the golden locks hanging around his neck, tearing them free and pressing them to her lips. Then, the truth crashing upon her, she wailed, “Dead! My girl is dead!” and crumpled, sobbing, over the stolen tresses.

The crowd roared like a beast, surging toward the murderer. In those days, every man carried death at his belt, and the prisoner’s life would have ended then and there—had Denys not drawn his sword and planted himself between the mob and their prey.

“Back!” he bellowed. “The first man to touch him dies!” His fellow archers rallied to him, steel flashing, holding the furious townsfolk at bay.

“What madness is this?” Denys shouted. “Would you rob the executioner of his due? Let the law grind him slow!”

The mob growled but yielded to the threat of blades. The officers, sensing the danger, formed a guard and hurried the prisoners back to the gaol, the crowd hurling curses after them.

Denys, satisfied they were secure, strode toward *The White Hart*, expecting to find Gerard. On the way, he passed two girls leaning from an upper window, their cheeks rosy, their laughter easy. He tipped his hat. They smiled. He spoke; they answered. Soon, he invited them to dine with him.

They hesitated.

He shrugged.

They agreed.

And so, in charming company, he forgot Gerard entirely—while Gerard, bewildered and afraid, was hauled toward the gaol.

Only as they neared the prison did Gerard’s wits return. “By whose order am I arrested?” he demanded.

“The vice-baillie’s,” said the constable.

“The vice-baillie? What quarrel has he with a stranger? This charge of sorcery is a lie! I am no magician—only a poor traveler far from home.”

The officer scowled. “Show him the warrant, Jacques.”

Jacques held the writ at arm’s length, while the larger constable gripped Gerard, wary lest he snatch the document. But Gerard had no such thought. He craned his neck and read—then gaped in disbelief.

The vice-baillie who had signed the order was the *same alderman* who had been so friendly earlier.

“There’s some mistake,” Gerard insisted. “Take me to him!”

The officers exchanged glances.

“We’ve no orders for that,” Jacques muttered.

“A silver écu each if you do,” Gerard offered.

A pause. A nod.

“Very well,” said the constable. “But mark—this is a favor.”

They led him to the alderman’s house. Gerard, heart in his throat, stammered, “What offense have I given since I left your presence with Denys and Manon?”

The alderman frowned. “None that I know.”

Gerard pointed to the writ.

“Ah!” The alderman rubbed his temple. “My memory fails me. I signed this at dawn—ah, yes. The charge is sorcery. You stand accused of setting a corpse alight with hellfire.”

Gerard paled. “If that were true, I’d burn for it.”

“Precisely.”

“Then for mercy’s sake, let me speak to the curé!”

The alderman grimaced. “The Church is harsher on sorcery than we are.”

“But I am *innocent*!”

“So you say. Very well—take him to the curé, but guard him well.”

They found the curé in his workshop, mending a wheelbarrow. Gerard spilled his tale, desperate. “They would hang me for using phosphorus—a trick of chemistry, not magic!”

The curé’s eyes gleamed. “Show me.”

Gerard hesitated. “The room must be dark.”

The curé shuttered the windows. “Now.”

“But I need a face—a dead one. That was what made it terrible.”

The curé groped about and produced a wooden saint. “Here.”

Gerard recoiled. “A *saint*? That’s blasphemy!”

“Pah! It’ll wash off.”

Reluctantly, Gerard daubed the eyes and nostrils with phosphorus, then the hair. The saint’s face glowed eerily.

The curé gasped. “By the Virgin! No wonder they feared you.”

He seized Gerard’s arm. “Come.”

They returned to the alderman, where a figure in red hose and a black gown stood whispering—Mangis, the accuser, a self-proclaimed *white* sorcerer.

The curé scoffed. “This charlatan cries ‘magic’ at honest men to hide his own frauds. The phosphorus is a harmless trick, known to scholars.”

Mangis bristled. “I am no fraud! I battle Satan’s minions—unlike *him*!” He jabbed a finger at Gerard.

The curé turned on him. “You babbling fool! Your lies burned innocents at Dol—and when the truth came out, who paid? Not you. Shall I tell the bishop how you wear the Devil’s colors?” He pointed at Mangis’ red and black garb.

Mangis blanched. “I—I withdraw the charge.”

“Then begone.”

With the accuser dismissed, the curé turned to the alderman. “The boy’s no sorcerer. Release him.”

The alderman sighed. “So be it.”

Outside, Gerard clasped the curé’s hand. “You saved me! How can I repay you?”

The curé waved him off. “Keep your coin. But come dine with me tomorrow.”

Grateful, Gerard hurried to the inn—only to find Denys dicing with two brazen girls.

Gerard scowled. “*Forget not Jeanneton!*”

Denys laughed. “What of her?”

“‘*How little women are worth!*’”

The girls sneered. “None slander women but those too ugly or dull to please them.”

Gerard flushed. “Brazen toads! My comrade shames himself with you.”

Denys chuckled. “He scolds like a monk. Pay him no heed.”

Gerard slammed the door, then turned to the landlord for wine. The man served him a short measure in a deceitful bottle.

Gerard glared. “This is no pint.”

The landlord shrugged. “I don’t make the bottles.”

“Then I’ll take it to the vice-baillie.”

The landlord paled. “Wait! A mistake—let me correct it.”

As they haggled, Denys stormed in, roaring.

“Robbed! Those vipers took my purse—fifteen gold crowns!”

Gerard urged him to seek the provost-marshal, but Denys refused.

“The law? Useless! They’re long gone.”

Then, grudgingly, he confessed: he’d offered *marriage* to Manon.

Gerard gaped. “You *what*?”

Denys shrugged. “A ruse. I told her, ‘Mademoiselle, though marriage is not my habit, your virtues compel me. Be my wife.’”

Gerard snorted. “And she replied—?”

“With a slap.”

Denys grinned.

Gerard groaned.
