Once Mrs. Haller had retired to the George Hotel, Mr. Foker and his guest settled into a sumptuous meal within the coffee-room. Mr. Rincer, serving as waiter, presented the first course with a gravity that suggested he was attending to the Lord Lieutenant of the county itself. Mr. Foker devoured the turtle and venison with the same relish he had displayed a year prior when feasting on ginger beer and smuggled salami. Pen was forced to admire his host's discernment, especially when Foker declared the champagne tasted of "condemned gooseberries" and winked knowingly at the port. The latter, Foker insisted, was genuine; he claimed there was no fooling him. Indeed, he knew every attendant by their Christian name and displayed a profound interest in their family histories.

As the London coaches rattled up to the George—a common sight in those early days—Foker threw open the coffee-room window. He greeted the guards and coachmen by name, inquired warmly about their kinfolk, and mimicked the tooting of horns with remarkable vigor and precision. He perfectly captured the scene as Jem the ostler whipped the horses' cloths and the carriages departed gaily.

"A bottle of sherry, a bottle of sham, a bottle of port, and a shass caffy," Foker remarked, surveying the remnants of nuts and fruit. "It ain't so bad, hey, Pen?"

Having consumed these delicacies, Foker announced it was time to "toddle." Pen sprang up, eyes bright and face flushed, and they headed toward the theatre. They paid their admission to a wheezy old lady slumbering behind the money-box.

"This is Mrs. Dropsicum," Foker explained to his companion. "Bingley's mother-in-law, and she's great in *Lady Macbeth*." Foker knew her personally. They secured nearly the entire front row of boxes, a rarity for a venue that, despite Bingley's playbills promising a "universal burst of attraction and galvanic thrills of delight," remained sparsely populated. A score of spectators dotted the pit benches, a few kicked and whistled in the galleries, and a dozen free-admission guests occupied the remaining boxes alongside the young gentlemen.

Lieutenants Rodgers and Podgers, along with young Cornet Tidmus of the Dragoons, held a private box. They conversed with the actors whenever the dialogue paused and applauded them by name with excessive fervor. Bingley, the manager, who played every major role except when stepping aside for visiting London stars, starred as 'The Stranger.' Dressed in tight pantaloons, Hessian boots, a large cloak, a beaver hat adorned with a drooping hearse feather, and a raddled face partially concealed by a great buckled brown wig, he completed the look with stage jewelry. He selected the largest, shinier rings for himself, allowing his little finger to quiver conspicuously from beneath his cloak, displaying a sham diamond ring on the first joint. He delighted in letting the youth of his company handle this prop, often answering their questions about its history with theatrical flair. The stage possessed its own heirloom jewels, much like royalty; this ring had supposedly passed from George Frederick Cooke to Mr. Quin, perhaps purchased for a mere shilling. Bingley believed the world was utterly captivated by its glitter.

Reading from a stage-book that was less bound and more rouged and tawdry than the heroes holding it, Bingley pointed passages with dramatic flourish, wagged his head ominously at the audience, and lifted his eyes to the ceiling, claiming a divine connection between the script and heaven. Anyone familiar with great light comedians like "X," known for wearing impossible chintz dressing-gowns while pretending to be idle noblemen, would recognize the pose.

When 'The Stranger' spotted the young men, he immediately turned his attention to them. Lying on the stage bench, he eyed them solemnly over his gilt volume, calculating the impact of his hand, his ring, and his Hessian boots. Determined to fascinate them, knowing they had paid their money, he also noted the families arriving from the countryside to fill the cane chairs in his boxes.

His servant, Francis, interjected with commentary on his master's gloomy demeanor. "Again reading!" Francis observed. "From morn to night. To him, nature has no beauty, life no charm. Three years pass without seeing him smile. Nothing diverts him. Oh, if he would but attach himself to anything living—even an animal! Man must love something."

[Enter Tobias (Goll) from the hut.]

Tobias cried out, "Oh, how refreshing, after seven long weeks, to feel these warm sunbeams once again! Thanks, bounteous heaven, for the joy I taste!" Pressing his cap between his hands and looking upward, he prayed.

'The Stranger' watched him intently.

Francis addressed the actor: "This old man's share of earthly happiness is small, yet mark how grateful he is for his portion."

Bingley replied, "Because though old, he is but a child in the leading-string of hope." He fixed his gaze steadily on Foker, who continued to suck the top of his stick with utter unconcern.

Francis added, "Hope is the nurse of life."

"And her cradle—is the grave," Bingley finished, delivering the line with the mournful resonance of a bassoon in agony. He locked eyes on Pendennis so intensely that the poor lad felt exposed, believing the entire house was staring at him. He cast his eyes down, only to find Bingley's gaze returning instantly. Throughout the scene, the manager focused entirely on Pen. When 'The Stranger' prepared to perform a good deed, sending Francis away so the servant wouldn't witness the act of benevolence, Bingley carefully marked his page, ensuring he could resume reading off-stage if desired. Yet, everything was performed directly in the face of Pendennis, whom the manager intended to subjugate.

Relieved when the scene concluded, Foker tapped his cane and shouted, "Bravo, Bingley!"

"Give him a hand, Pendennis; you know every chap likes a hand," Foker urged. The good-natured young gentleman, Pen laughing, and the Dragoon officers in the opposite box began clapping with all their might.

The action shifted to a chamber in Wintersen Castle, closing over Tobias's hut. Servants bustled about with chairs and tables. "That's Hicks and Miss Thackthwaite," Foker whispered. "Pretty girl, ain't she, Pendennis? But stop—hurray—bravo! Here comes the Fotheringay."

The pit thrilled, umbrellas thumped, and a volley of applause erupted from the gallery. The Dragoon officers and Foker clapped furiously; the noise was so deafening it seemed the house was packed. Peeking from the side-scene was the red-faced, whiskered Mr. Costigan. Pen's eyes widened, bright as Mrs. Haller entered with a downcast look. Rallying at the roar of applause, she swept the house with a grateful glance, folded her hands across her breast, and sank into a magnificent curtsey. More applause, more umbrellas. Pen, flaming with wine and enthusiasm, clapped harder and sang "bravo" louder than anyone.

Mrs. Haller saw him, as did everyone else. Old Mr. Bows, the diminutive first fiddler of the orchestra (augmented tonight by a detachment of the Dragoons' band, courtesy of Colonel Swallowtail), looked up from his perch beside his crutch and smiled at the lad's fervor.

Those who have only seen Miss Fotheringay in later years, after her marriage and entry into London society, cannot imagine the creature she was when Pen first laid eyes on her. I warn my reader beforehand: the illustrator of this work, capable of drawing an ugly face tolerably well but utterly failing at beauty, cannot convey her true splendor. She was tall, twenty-six years old (though she vowed she was only nineteen), and in the prime of her beauty. Her forehead was vast, crowned with black hair waving naturally over it—a style modern beauties vainly try to replicate with crimping irons—confined in shining, voluminous braids at the back of a neck reminiscent of the Louvre Venus. When she lifted her eyes to gaze upon you, before dropping their purple, deep-fringed lids, they shone with tenderness and mystery unfathomable. Love and Genius seemed to peer out from them, then retreat coyly, as if ashamed to have been caught at the lattice. Who could possess such a commanding brow but a woman of high intellect? She rarely laughed (her teeth were not perfect), but a smile of endless sweetness played around her lips and dimpled her cheeks and chin. Her nose defied description; her ears resembled two little pearl shells, which the finest earrings in the theatre only served to insult.

Dressed in long, flowing black robes that she manipulated with wonderful grace, revealing her sandals only occasionally, she looked ravishing. Pen thought them as enchanting as Cinderella's slippers. Yet, it was her hands and arms that this magnificent being excelled in; somehow, one could never see her without noticing them. Whether folded over her bosom in resignation, dropped in mute agony, raised in superb command, or fluttering in sportive gaiety like snowy doves before the chariot of Venus, they beckoned, repelled, entreated, and embraced her admirers.
