Margaret stepped through the heavy oak doors of St. Laurens just as the sermon began. The great church was far from done, as most grand churches of the era were—no building of such scale rose in a single generation. Its side aisles were fully roofed, but the central nave and chancel gaped open to the sky, their rafters bare above scattered workmen’s piles of hewn stone and lime. The pillars and rounded arches were mostly complete, many freshly whitewashed, but only one single window in the entire sprawling structure held glass; all the rest were jagged, rough-hewn openings cut into the outer stone walls.

On that golden summer afternoon, every one of those half-finished flaws turned to magic. Bright sunlight spilled through the uneven gaps in the walls and rafters, breaking into streaks of dazzling gold that lanced through the cool dim of the side aisles like rivers of molten light, glowing warm against the whitewashed pillars. The whole length of the vast central nave was chequered with shifting patterns of light and shadow, the dark patches soft and cool as well water, the sunlit spots glowing like shards of amber lit from within by some quiet heavenly fire. Overhead, stretching unbroken from west to east, the summer blue of the sky curved like a living vault, so close and bright it felt you could reach up and touch it. The sea of white linen coifs perched on the heads of the packed congregation winked like foam beneath that wide blue sky, and for all the thousands of people crammed shoulder to shoulder in the unroofed nave, not a sound broke the hush but the low, mellow, earnest voice of the preacher, holding every last soul spellbound.

Margaret stood frozen a moment, caught fast by the beauty of the place, the quiet devotion, the thick soft calm that hung over the whole crowd. She slipped behind a thick stone pillar in the north aisle, and though she could barely make out a word the preacher said, a soft, drowsy devotional languor stole over her, smoothing all the sharp, frayed edges of the worries she’d carried with her that day. It felt like warm, scented oil seeping into every line of her tense, overworked heart, and she leaned her weight against the stone, eyes half-lidded, every sensation soft and dreamlike. It felt nothing short of holy to be there.

Presently she spotted a well-dressed woman a little ways across the aisle get up to move, fleeing the direct sunlight that was pouring straight down on her head from the only glazed window in the church. Margaret slipped quick and silent across the gap, and claimed the empty spot before anyone else could take it. Now she stood beside a pillar in the south aisle, less than fifty feet from the pulpit, just a little behind the preacher’s shoulder, where she could catch every single word he spoke.

Her focus drifted for a minute, though, caught by the bobbing shadow of a man’s head and shoulders dancing oddly against the stone at her feet. She bit her lip to keep from giggling, until she glanced over her shoulder through the unglazed window and spotted Jorian Ketel the sexton, digging a new grave in the churchyard just outside, his movements the source of that droll bobbing shadow.

Just then, a single cadence of the preacher’s voice hit her ear so sharp and clear it felt like a physical blow, thrumming right through every bone in her body. She clapped a hand to her bosom, gasping a little at the sudden, shocking thrill, and spun around to look up at the pulpit. The preacher’s back was turned to her, and all she could see was the smooth shaved circle of his tonsure glinting in the sun. She sighed, disappointed—what could a stray friar from no place she knew have a voice that familiar? But the sound of him wrapped around her anyway, rising and falling as he warmed to his subject, waking faint, half-buried echoes of a thousand happy memories she’d thought long buried. She didn’t dare look up again to shatter that soft, melancholy pleasure, her eyes fixed on the floor.

Then, mid-flow of a soaring, eloquent sentence, the preacher stopped dead.

Margaret almost sighed out loud, as if a sweet song had been cut short mid-note. Could the sermon be over already? She glanced around, and no one was moving—instead, dozens of faces were turning sharply in her direction. She spun around, terrified, thinking some terrible thing stood behind her, but there was nothing there at all.

Nearby, other parishioners were staring up at the pulpit, their faces white with shock. Margaret followed their gaze, and her blood turned to ice. The friar stood frozen in the pulpit, his face as pale and still as a corpse just risen from the grave. His eyes, already large beneath his thick brows, were blown wide open to an unnatural size, bulging straight at her, fixed unblinking.

The whole church erupted into chaos. People leapt to their feet all across the nave, craning their necks to stare first at the friar, then at Margaret, then back at the friar again. The rustle of hundreds of turned coifs rose to a loud, rushing roar, women shrieked, men muttered and buzzed in confused agitation. Margaret, every eye locked on her, cringed back behind the nearest pillar, one single scared, fleeting glance darting back up to the pulpit.

That glance was enough. She saw the look on his ashen face, and it made her quiver from head to toe. Her knees gave out, and she sank down onto a heap of wood chips the workmen had left piled by the wall, burying her face in her hands.

The preacher recovered himself a moment later, and the sermon resumed. She could hear the hum of his voice, but not a single word of what he said. Her mind spun too fast to think, whirling around one single, unshakable thought: that look seared onto his face, that wide, wild, disbelieving stare, had not been confusion, or fright.

It was recognition.

The sermon wavered for a few minutes, then built up into a soaring, feverish peak of fiery eloquence that swept over the crowd, leaving most of them chattering excitedly as they dispersed, the strange, frozen pause in the pulpit half-forgotten in the wake of that rousing close. Margaret slipped into the throng and followed them out the doors, but instead of turning for her cottage, she veered off into the churchyard, straight for her father Peter’s grave. Poor as she was, she’d scraped together every spare penny to buy him a proper stone slab and carved headstone. She sank down onto the cold slab, pressed a soft kiss to the weathered stone, then pulled her linen apron up over her head to hide her face, so no passing parishioner would spot her tear-streaked face or her bright red hair.

“Father,” she whispered, quiet enough that only the breeze could hear, “you heard me say many times I was wading out in deep waters, but now I know the bottom of them is deeper than any of us ever guessed. I’ll follow that friar round the whole wide world if I have to, but I will get close enough to see his face. He’s going to tell me why he looked at me like a dead man woken from his grave, when there was no soul standing behind me at all. Oh father, you always believed in me, when others said hard things. Speak one good word for me up there, won’t you? Because I’ve wandered far past my depth now.”

Her father’s tomb sat where it had a clear side view of the church’s main door. There she sat, face covered, hidden in the shadow of the old yew tree that grew at the stone’s foot, lying in wait for the holy preacher.
