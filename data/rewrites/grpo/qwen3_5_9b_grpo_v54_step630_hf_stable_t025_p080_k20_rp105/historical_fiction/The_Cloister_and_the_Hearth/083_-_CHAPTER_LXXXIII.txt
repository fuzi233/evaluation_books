Waiting for a letter that carries weight rarely leaves the mind unchanged. Margaret, having poured her weary energy and trembling faith into her response to her dying father's vision—or perhaps his delusion—found herself suddenly adrift. Once the ink was dry and Luke departed, a sharp pang of guilt struck her conscience regarding the man she had sent away. Catherine arrived shortly after, her words sharp as flint, scolding her for the folly of false hope. The price Margaret paid for her momentary elevation of spirit was a plunge into a despondency far deeper than before.

In this state of hollow grief, she was found by a steadfast friend she had recently made, Joan Ketel. Joan entered the room radiant with a sudden inspiration. "Margaret, I know the remedy for your sorrow," she declared. "There is a hermit in Gouda, a man of wondrous holiness. When the mood takes him, he can foresee the future."

"I have heard of him," Margaret replied, her voice devoid of hope. Yet, with some difficulty, Joan persuaded her to walk as far as Gouda to consult the mystic. They carried a basket of butter and eggs, seeking the hermit's cave.

Their friendship had blossomed only six weeks prior, born of desperation. Jorian Ketel had fallen violently ill with a bowel disease that began with agonizing pain. When the pain subsided, leaving him weak, a terrifying symptom emerged: neither liquid nor solid could remain in his stomach for a single minute. The physician had declared, "He must die if this continues; therefore, boil a chicken with a golden angel in the water and let him drink it!" Alas, the gilded broth shared the fate of the humblest fare. Next, the cure steeped the thumb of St. Sergius in beef broth, with the same disastrous result. Desperate, Joan wept to Margaret to borrow linen for a shroud. "Let me see him," Margaret commanded. She entered, felt his pulse, and gasped, "Ah! They have not touched the root. Open the window! You are stifling him. Change all his linen."

"Alack, woman, what for? Why foul more linen for a dying man?" the medieval wife protested.

"Do as thou art bid," Margaret said dully, retreating from the room.

Joan, compelled by her friend's authority, obeyed. Margaret returned with her apron heavy with a flowering herb. She brewed a decoction and brought it to the bedside. Before offering it to the patient, she took a spoonful herself, smacking her lips with feigned delight. "That is fair," Jorian murmured, making a feeble attempt at humor. "Why, 'tis sweet, and now 'tis bitter." She engaged him in conversation immediately after he drank it. This bitter-sweet mixture stayed with him. Observing its effect, she built upon it like a gambler building a hand: she mixed a trace of schiedam in the third spoonful and a beaten egg yolk in the seventh. With the patience characteristic of her sex, she coaxed his body back from Death's grasp. Finally, Nature, patted gently rather than kicked under the bed, set Jorian Ketel upon his legs again. But the doctress made them both swear a solemn oath of silence. "They would imprison me, taking me away from my child," she had whispered.

The simple herb that saved Jorian was called sweet feverfew. She had gathered it from his own garden, her eagle eye having spotted it growing through the window pane.

Thus, Margaret and Joan arrived at the hermit's cave, placing their offering of butter and eggs upon the small stone platform. Margaret pressed her lips to the aperture designed for such prayers. "Holy hermit," she intoned, "we bring thee the best butter and eggs. And I, a poor deserted girl, a wife yet no wife, and mother of the sweetest babe, come to pray thee tell me whether he lives or dies, whether he remains true to his vows or has faltered."

A faint, rasping voice drifted from the darkness. "Trouble me not with the trifles of earth, but send me a holy friar; I am dying."

"Alas!" cried Margaret. "Is it truly so, poor soul? Then let us enter to aid thee."

"Saints forbid! Thine is a woman's voice. Send me a holy friar."

They turned and retraced their steps exactly as they had come. Joan could not suppress her indignation. "Are women imps of darkness, then, that we must not approach a dying bed?" she asked.

But Margaret was too deeply crushed to offer a reply. Joan offered rough consolation, which was ignored until she spoke of Jorian. "And Jorian will speak out soon; he is just on the boil. He is very grateful to thee, believe it."

"Seeing is believing," Margaret answered, her tone quiet and bitter.

"Not that he does not think you might have saved him with something more extraordinary than yon herb," Joan continued. "'A man of my stature cured with feverfew,' he says. 'Why, if there is a sorry herb,' he adds. 'I was thinking of pulling all mine up,' he said. I told him remedies are no better for being far-fetched; you and the feverfew cured him when the grand medicines failed. So I said, 'You may go down on your four bones to feverfew.' But indeed, he is grateful at bottom; you are all his thought and all his talk. But he sees Gerard's people coming around, good friends, and he said only last night—"

"Well?"

"He made me vow not to tell ye."

"Prithee, tell me."

"Well, he said: 'If I reveal what little I know, it won't bring him back, and it will set them all by the ears. I wish I had more headpiece,' he said; 'I am sore perplexed. But least said is soonest mended.' That is his favorite phrase; he returns to it from a mile away."

Margaret shook her head. "Ay, we are wading in deep waters, my poor babe and me."

It was Saturday night, and there was no sign of Luke.

"Poor Luke!" Margaret murmured. "It was very kind of him to go on such an errand."

"He is one out of a hundred," Catherine replied warmly.

"Mother, do you think he would be kind to little Gerard?"

"I am sure he would. So be kinder to him when he returns! Will you promise?"

"Ay."

Brother Clement, guided by the nuns, navigated a bend in the river with determined strides, reaching a station miles closer to the coast than where Luke lay in wait for Gerard Eliassoen. The following morning, he started early and arrived in Rotterdam by noon. He made straight for the port, eager not to keep Jerome waiting.

He observed several monks of his order on the quay and approached them, but Jerome was not among them. He asked one if Jerome had arrived. "Surely, brother," was the reply.

"Prithee, where is he?"

"Where? Why, there!" the monk pointed, indicating a ship cutting through the waves in full sail. Clement noticed that all the monks were gazing seaward.

"What, gone without me! Oh, Jerome! Jerome!" he cried, his voice filled with anguish. Several friars turned to stare.

"You must be Brother Clement," one said eventually. They kissed him and greeted him with brotherly warmth, handing him a letter Jerome had entrusted to them. It was a hasty scrawl. The writer informed him coldly that a ship was about to sail for England and that he loathed to lose time. He told Clement he might follow if he wished, but it would be far better to stay behind and preach to his own countrymen. "Give the glory to God, brother; you have a wonderful power over Dutch hearts; but you are no match for those haughty islanders: you are too tender."

"Know thou that on the way I met one who asked for thee under the name thou didst bear in the world. Be on thy guard! Let not the world catch thee again by any silken net. Remember, Solitude, Fasting, and Prayer are the sword, spear, and shield of the soul. Farewell."

Clement was deeply shocked and mortified by this contemptuous desertion and the icy tone of the message. He promised the good monks he would sleep at the convent and preach wherever the prior appointed, for Jerome had once raised him to the skies as a preacher. Now, wounded to the quick, he withdrew abruptly, needing solitude. He questioned himself: Was there some incurable fault in him, repulsive to such a true son of Dominic? Or was Jerome himself devoid of that Christian Love which St. Paul had placed above Faith itself? Shipwrecked together, saved on the same fragment of wreckage, his pupil, his penitent, his son in the Church, and now for four hundred miles his fellow-traveler in Christ; yet to be shaken off like dirt at the first opportunity, with harsh and cold disdain. "Why are worldly hearts no colder nor less untrustworthy than this," he muttered. "The only one who ever truly loved me lies in a grave nearby. Fly me, fly to England, man born without a heart; I will go and pray over a grave at Sevenbergen."

Three hours later, he passed Peter's cottage. A troop of noisy children played about the door, and the house had been repaired, a new outhouse added. He turned his head hastily away, unwilling to disturb a picture his memory treasured, and walked toward the churchyard.

He searched among the tombstones for Margaret's. He could not find it. He could not believe they had grudged her a marker, so he searched the churchyard again and again.

"Oh poverty! Stern poverty! Poor soul, thou wert like me; no one was left that loved thee when Gerard was gone."

He entered the church, kissed the steps, and prayed long and earnestly for the soul of her whose resting place he could not locate.

Upon exiting, he saw a very old man looking over the little churchyard gate. He approached and asked if he lived in the place.

"Four score and twelve years, man and boy. And I come here every day of late, holy father, to take a peep. This is where I look to bide ere long."

"My son, can you tell me where Margaret lies?"

"Margaret? There are many Margarets here."

"Margaret Brandt. She was the daughter of a learned physician."

"As if I didn't know that," the old man said pettishly. "But she does not lie here. Bless you, they left this place a long while ago. Gone in a moment, and the house empty. What, is she dead? Margaret a Peter dead? Now only think on't. Like enough; like enough. Great towns disagree terribly with country folk."

"What great towns, my son?"

"Well, 'twas Rotterdam they went to from here, so I heard tell; or was it Amsterdam? Nay, I trow 'twas Rotterdam? And gone there to die!"

Clement sighed.

"'Twas not in her face now that I saw it," the old man continued. "And I can mostly tell. Alack, there was a blooming young flower to be cut off so soon, and all old weed like me left standing still. Well, well, she was a May rose yon; dear heart, what a winsome smile she had, and—"

"God bless thee, my son," Clement said, "farewell!" and he hurried away.

He reached the convent at sunset, watching and praying in the chapel for Jerome and Margaret until long past midnight, until his soul had recovered its cold, distant calm.
