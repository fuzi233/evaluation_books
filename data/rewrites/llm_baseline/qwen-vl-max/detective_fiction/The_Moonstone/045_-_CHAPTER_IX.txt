The doctor’s housemaid stood waiting for me, the street door open in her hand. As I turned, morning light poured into the hall, falling full upon the face of Mr. Candy’s assistant.

It was impossible to deny Betteredge’s judgment: Ezra Jennings, from a popular standpoint, made an unfavorable impression. His gypsy-like complexion, hollow cheeks, gaunt features, dreamy eyes, and wildly colored hair all contributed to a strange, unsettling appearance. The odd contrast between his youthful frame and aged face only deepened the sense of mystery. Yet—despite my instinctive caution—I felt something inexplicable drawing me toward him. While my experience warned me to respond briefly to his question—acknowledging that Mr. Candy had changed—and then leave, my curiosity held me fast. It gave him the chance he’d clearly been watching for: a private word about his employer.

“Are you walking my way, Mr. Jennings?” I asked, noting he held his hat loosely in one hand. “I’m going to visit my aunt, Mrs. Ablewhite.”

He said he had a patient to see and that we were headed in the same direction.

We left the house together. I noticed the pretty servant girl—who had greeted me with bright smiles earlier—received a quiet message from Ezra Jennings about his return time. She did so with pursed lips, avoiding his gaze entirely. Clearly, she didn’t care for him. Outside, Betteredge had confirmed it: Ezra Jennings was disliked everywhere. “What a life,” I thought, descending the steps.

Having already mentioned Mr. Candy’s illness, Ezra now waited for me to bring it up again. His silence spoke plainly: *Your turn.* And I, too, had reasons to speak. I took the lead.

“Judging by how much he’s changed,” I began, “Mr. Candy’s illness must have been worse than I imagined?”

“It was almost a miracle,” Ezra replied, “that he survived.”

“Is his memory no better than today? He tried to say something to me—”

“About something that happened before he fell ill?” he asked, sensing my hesitation.

“Yes.”

“His memory of events prior to his illness is completely shattered,” he said. “It’s almost tragic, poor man, that even fragments remain. He remembers vaguely plans he made, things he meant to do or say—but not what those plans were, or what he needed to say. He’s painfully aware of his failure, and desperate to hide it. If he could have recovered in total amnesia, he might have been happier. Perhaps we all would be,” he added with a quiet, sad smile, “if we could forget everything.”

“There must be some memories,” I said, “that men wouldn’t want to lose entirely?”

“That’s true of most men, Mr. Blake,” he replied. “But not necessarily all. Do you think the memory Mr. Candy tried to recall—just now—was one that matters to you?”

He had struck exactly the point I wanted to explore. My interest in this man had led me to give him space to speak first, to test whether I could trust his discretion. What he’d said so far convinced me—he was a gentleman. There was a quiet dignity about him, a self-possession unforced and unboastful, a mark of true breeding. Whatever his motive in asking that question, I felt justified in answering honestly.

“I believe I have a strong reason,” I said, “to recover what Mr. Candy couldn’t remember. Can you suggest any way I might help restore his memory?”

Ezra looked at me, and suddenly his dreamy eyes lit with interest.

“Mr. Candy’s memory,” he said, “is beyond help. I’ve tried often enough since his recovery. I know what I’m speaking of.”

That disappointed me. I admitted it.

“I confess,” I said, “you gave me hope for something more encouraging.”

He smiled faintly. “It may not be final, Mr. Blake. There might be another way—without needing to ask Mr. Candy himself.”

“Indeed? Is it improper of me to ask how?”

“Not at all. The difficulty is explaining it. May I rely on your patience if I return to Mr. Candy’s illness—and this time, without holding back certain medical details?”

“Please,” I said. “You’ve already drawn me in.”

His expression softened slightly, as if pleased by my eagerness. We had passed the last houses of the town. Ezra paused, plucked a few wildflowers from the roadside hedge, and held them out simply. “How beautiful they are,” he said. “And yet how few people here seem to notice them.”

“You haven’t always lived in England?” I asked.

“No. I was born and raised partly in one of our colonies. My father was English. My mother—” He broke off. “We’re drifting from the subject. My fault. These flowers—they remind me of something. But let’s return to Mr. Candy.”

Connecting his brief words with his melancholy view of life—his belief that forgetting the past might be the key to peace—I sensed two truths in his story: he had suffered deeply; and his blood carried something foreign, something other than pure English.

“You’ve likely heard,” he resumed, “what caused Mr. Candy’s illness? That night of Lady Verinder’s dinner party—it rained heavily. My employer drove home in his gig, soaked through. He found a urgent message from a patient and, without changing, went out immediately. I was called away that night to a case far from Frizinghall. When I returned the next morning, I found his groom waiting anxiously to take me to his room. By then, it was too late—the illness had taken hold.”

“It’s been described only as a fever,” I said.

“I can add nothing to make it more precise,” he replied. “From start to finish, it defied classification. I sent for two respected physicians in town. They agreed it was serious, but disagreed sharply on treatment. They saw the rapid pulse and insisted on lowering remedies—gruel, lemonade, barley water. I argued that while the pulse was fast, it was also dangerously weak—an indication of systemic exhaustion. I believed stimulants were essential. Champagne, brandy, ammonia, quinine. A serious disagreement—between two established doctors and a stranger in their midst. For the first few days, I yielded. The patient worsened. I tried again, citing the pulse’s worsening weakness. They resented my persistence. ‘Either we manage this case,’ they said, ‘or you do. Which is it?’ I asked for five minutes to decide. When the time passed, I answered: ‘You refuse to try stimulants?’ They refused. ‘Then I will.’ ‘Try it, and we withdraw.’ I fetched champagne from the cellar and gave half a tumbler directly to the patient. The two doctors silently picked up their hats and left.”

“You took a terrible risk,” I said. “In my place, I’d have hesitated.”

“In your place,” he replied, “you’d remember that Mr. Candy had saved you when you were destitute. You’d see him fading hour by hour. You’d risk anything to save the one man who had once given you everything. Don’t think I wasn’t aware of the danger. There were moments when I felt the weight of my loneliness, the horror of my responsibility. If I had been happy, if I’d known peace before, I might have broken. But I had no past to comfort me—no calm to contrast with this agony. So I held firm. I took a short rest during the day, when his condition was best. Otherwise, for twenty-four hours straight, I never left his side. At sunset, delirium set in—as it often does. It lasted through the night, then eased briefly in the early hours—two to five—when the body’s strength is lowest. That’s when Death reaps most. That’s when I fought him over the bed. I never wavered. When wine failed, I used brandy. When others lost effect, I doubled the dose. After a long, terrible wait—one I pray I never feel again—there came a day when the pulse slowed, just slightly. Then, more importantly, it steadied. Gained strength. *Then* I knew I’d saved him. And then—I broke down. I laid his wasted hand back on the bed and wept. An emotional release, nothing more. Some men are born with female constitutions, physiology says—and I am one.”

He offered that apology quietly, without pretense. Throughout, his tone revealed a deep discomfort with being seen as remarkable.

“You may wonder why I’ve told you all this,” he continued. “It’s the only way I can properly introduce what comes next. Now you understand my position during the illness, you’ll see why I needed relief. For years, I’ve worked on a book—on the brain and nervous system—meant only for my profession. It may never be finished. It won’t be published. But it has kept me company in lonely hours. It helped pass the anxious time at Mr. Candy’s bedside. I mentioned delirium, yes? And when it occurred?”

“Yes.”

“Well, I’d reached a section in my book dealing with delirium. I won’t bore you with my theory. Just this: I’ve often wondered—during practice—if loss of coherent speech necessarily means loss of coherent thought. Mr. Candy’s illness gave me a chance to test it. I know shorthand. I recorded every word he muttered—exactly as it came. Do you see where I’m going?”

I did. I waited, breathless.

“At intervals,” he went on, “I transcribed those notes into regular writing—leaving gaps between fragmented phrases, even single words. Then I treated it like a child’s puzzle. Chaos at first. But if you find the right pattern, order emerges. I filled each gap with what made sense based on surrounding words—revising until the additions flowed naturally. The result? Not only did I pass anxious hours, but I discovered something that seemed to confirm my theory: while speech was gone, thought—higher thought—remained, more or less connected.”

“One moment,” I interrupted. “Did my name appear in any of his ramblings?”

“You shall hear. Among my written experiments—proofs of my claim—is one where your name appears. For nearly an entire night, Mr. Candy’s mind focused on *something* between himself and you. I have the original fragments on one sheet. On another, I’ve reconstructed the links. Together, they form a coherent statement: first, something done in the past; second, something he intended to do in the future—if illness hadn’t stopped him. Could this be the memory he tried to recall this morning?”

“Without a doubt,” I said. “Let’s go back and see the papers!”

“Impossible, Mr. Blake.”

“Why?”

“Put yourself in my place,” he said. “Would you reveal what a dying patient said unconsciously—without knowing there was a reason to?”

I saw his logic, but pressed on.

“My actions would depend,” I said, “on whether revealing it would harm my friend.”

“I’ve already dealt with that,” he said. “Any part of my notes that might have been private—anything Mr. Candy would have wished hidden—I destroyed. What remains includes only what he could have shared freely, had he remembered. In your case, I suspect he actually *wanted* to tell you something.”

“And yet, you hesitate?”

“And yet, I hesitate. Remember how I obtained this? Harmless as it is, I cannot give it to you unless you first show me why it matters. He was so ill, Mr. Blake. So helpless. Is it too much to ask—just a hint—of your interest in this memory? Or what you think it might be?”

To answer honestly would mean admitting I was suspected of stealing the Diamond. Despite Ezra’s growing appeal, I couldn’t bear to expose myself. I retreated again to the safe phrases I’d prepared.

This time, he listened patiently, even intently, until I finished.

“I’m sorry,” he said, “to raise your hopes only to disappoint. Throughout the illness—not one word about the Diamond escaped him. The matter linked to your name has no connection, I assure you, to the loss or recovery of Miss Verinder’s jewel.”

As he spoke, we reached a fork in the road. One path led to Mrs. Ablewhite’s house; the other, to a distant moorland village. Ezra stopped at the latter.

“My way lies here,” he said. “I’m truly sorry, Mr. Blake, that I can’t assist you.”

His voice was sincere. His soft brown eyes lingered on me with a quiet sorrow. He bowed and walked away.

For a minute, I watched him recede, carrying with him what I now believed was the clue I sought. He turned after a few steps, glanced back. Seeing me still standing, he paused, uncertain. There was no time to think—only to act. I called him back.

Then I realized: there was no turning back. I must tell him the truth.

He returned at once. I stepped forward.

“Mr. Jennings,” I said. “I haven’t been fair. My interest isn’t in recovering the Moonstone. It’s personal. I have only one excuse for not being honest: it’s painful beyond words to speak of my position.”

Ezra’s face showed a flicker of unease—unlike any I’d seen before.

“I have no right,” he said, “and no desire, to intrude into your affairs. Please forgive me for putting you through this painful test.”

“You have every right,” I replied, “to set your own terms. I respect your delicacy. How can I expect your confidence if I withhold mine? You must know why I seek this memory. If I’m wrong, and you still can’t help, I trust your honor to keep my secret—and I believe that trust won’t be misplaced.”

“Stop, Mr. Blake,” he said. “I must speak before you go further.”

I stared. A deep emotion gripped him—shaking him to his core. His skin turned ashen, his eyes flashed with intensity, his voice dropped low, stern, decisive—new and terrifying. The hidden strength within him surged forth, sudden and undeniable.

“Before you trust me,” he said, “you must know how I came to live in Mr. Candy’s house. It won’t take long. I don’t tell my story to anyone. It will die with me. But I ask only to tell you what I told Mr. Candy. If, after hearing it, you still wish to speak as you proposed, I will listen—and serve. Shall we walk on?”

The pain in his face silenced me. I nodded.

After a few hundred yards, he stopped at a gap in the stone wall bordering the moor.

“Would you mind resting, Mr.607
Blake?” he asked. “I’m not what I was. Some things… shake me.”
