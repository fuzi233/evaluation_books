For a fleeting interval, my existence narrowed down to a singular, warm obsession: the company of the Little Ones. Yet, the stasis of this paradise soon curdled within me into a restless agitation. A vague, insistent voice began to hum behind my ribs, suggesting that I was not meant merely to fatten these simple souls. I realized then that I inhabited a marvel—a universe whose mechanics and laws waited to be deciphered. If I wished to reciprocate the children's boundless affection, I could not remain a passive guardian; I needed freedom. Surely, no cosmic principle demanded that the blossoming of such innocence be strangled to preserve its "purity"! In any realm where life and law existed in such profound dissonance, where perfection required the stunting of development, the world itself was flawed.

And yet, the growth of the Little Ones *was* arrested. Some invisible hand clamped around their potential. Lona appeared to be the eldest, perhaps fifteen, yet she had presided over a vast multitude for ages, though most of her subjects remained in the behavioral stage of mere children, regarding her as a mother figure. Were they truly maturing? I suspected not. Time was a concept they barely grasped; age was a mystery. Lona believed she had lived forever. She was a vessel of pure, unadulterated wisdom, yet utterly devoid of empirical knowledge—their Love and their Law, fused into one. What I perceived as her ignorance might well be a blindness born of my own limited perspective. Her sole, driving anxiety was clear: they must not grow, lest they transform into "bad giants." This "good giant" protector, lacking deeper understanding of their nature or history, was paralyzed. Without knowledge, he could offer no aid and thus felt compelled to leave. And leaving them? That was the trap. They were not dependent on me; I was merely their shield against the neighboring horrors. My presence, ironically, drew greater danger from those idiots across the ridge. I longed to teach them mathematics, to capture their dainty, forgotten melodies in written form—but first, I needed to understand the biology of their stunted state.

The verdict was irrevocable: I must resume my travels, hoping to uncover the fate of these bewitched creatures.

Yet, design rarely translates to action without circumstance. To ease my impending departure, I sat among them at work, explaining that I would sooner flee the tyranny of the bad giants than abandon these beloved ones. It was then, with startling unanimity, that they descended upon me. They rushed, crowded, scrambling over limbs and leaping up the ancient tree, tumbling onto my head until I was nearly suffocated under a living avalanche. Three clung to my chest, one perched atop my shoulders gripping my neck, another stood defiantly upright on my scalp, while four or five latched onto my legs, others grappling my torso and arms, and a countless swarm climbed and descended this human pillar. I was immobilized, a statue overwhelmed by magma. Lost in the chaotic joy of the struggle, none of them noticed the approach of their tyrant until he was upon me. With a single shriek—"Take care, good giant!"—they scattered like mice, dropped like hedgehogs, and fled upward like squirrels. In that same breath, the bad giant emerged from the shadows, striking me squarely on the head with a gnarled stick. I crashed to the earth.

Later, the children recounted that they had showered him with "so many bumps of big apples and stones" that he fled home, blundering and terrified.

Consciousness returned to me only under the cover of night. Above lay a scattering of pale stars, waiting for the moon. I felt alone, my skull throbbing, a terrible dryness clawing at my throat. Turning wearily, my ear brushed the soil, and immediately the gush and gurgle of water, the soft cooing of life, filled the void. I groaned, and suddenly I was surrounded by a legion of silent children. Delicate fruits materialized before my lips, arriving in endless waves until the fire in my throat was extinguished.

Then, a sound pierced the air—a chorus of tiny sobs, unfamiliar and heartbreaking.

Struggling to sit, I found a mountain of small bodies piling at my back. With immense strength for their size, they pushed and pulled me to my feet.

"You must go away, good giant," they chanted, voices trembling. "If the bad giants see you injured, they will trample you flat."

"I believe I must," I admitted.

"Go and grow strong, and return!" they urged.

"I will," I promised, sinking back down.

"Indeed, you must depart at once!" whispered Lona, kneeling beside me, having supported me through the ordeal.

One of the older boys leaned close, sharing a secret overheard at the tyrant's door. "He spoke to his wife of finding you idle, conversing with moles and squirrels. When he struck you, they sought to kill him. He calls you a wizard, and says they must strike you down, or peace shall never be restored."

"I shall go at once," I declared, "and return the moment I discover what makes you larger and stronger."

"We do not wish to be bigger!" they cried, their faces grave. "WE WILL NOT grow into bad giants! We are strong enough now! You do not know how mighty we are!"

It was futile to sell them a future they refused to envision. I fell silent, rising and moving slowly up the valley's slope. Instantly, they formed a long, winding procession. Some led, some accompanied me, offering assistance, while the rest trailed behind. As I walked, they fed me constantly.

"You are broken," they observed, watching the blood seep from my wound. "Much red juice has spilled from you. Put some back in."

Upon reaching the valley's edge, the moon was just lifting her silver brow above the horizon.

"She has arrived to tend you and guide your path," Lona explained softly.

Questioning the crowd as we walked, I learned of a great domain ruled by a Giantess. When I inquired if it was a city, they shook their heads. They could not tell me its distance, direction, or the ruler's name. All they knew was that she harbored hatred for the Little Ones and desired their death, though she could never find them. How did they know? Lona's answer was absolute: "She has always known." If she ever appeared, they must hide deeply. When I offered to investigate her motives, asking why she hated them, the children erupted in terror.

"No! No! She will slay you, good giant! She will slay you! She is an awful bad-giant witch!"

Desperate for a destination, I asked where I should go. They pointed past the Baby Forest, toward where the moon ascended, describing a smooth, green country, pleasant to tread upon, devoid of rocks or trees. But how was I to navigate there?

"The Moon will tell you," they said confidently.

Carrying me up the second branch of the riverbed, they halted as the moon reached its zenith.

"We have never ventured so far from our trees," they confessed. "Now, watch closely how you travel, so that you may see the path inside your eyes and find your way back."

"And beware of the Giant-Woman who dwells in the desert," warned one of the elder girls as they turned away. "I trust you have heard of her?"

"No," I replied.

"Then guard yourself. Do not approach her. She is called the Cat-Woman. She is frightfully ugly—and she SCRATCHES."

As the elders ceased speaking, the younger ones burst into a run, returning toward the trees. The remaining group gazed at me with solemn gravity for a heartbeat before drifting away slowly. Finally, Lona paused. Holding up a baby for a kiss, she locked eyes with me, whispering, "The Cat-Woman will not hurt *YOU*," before departing without further word.

I stood for a long time, watching them recede into the moonlight, my heart heavy. Then, turning, I began my solitary journey. Soon, the laughter of the Little Ones overtook me, sounding like innumerable sheepbells rippling through the air, echoing off the surrounding rocks. I turned back, watching again, captivated by their carefree gambols, their sweet souls untouched by worry. But Lona walked apart, carrying her infant.

As I traveled, pondering the nature of my companions, memories surfaced. Once, I had proposed leaving their homeland to join me in seeking a kinder world. Their response had been immediate and absolute: "But that would mean ceasing to be *ourselves*!" So potent was their love for their place that it felt integral to their very essence. Devoid of ambition, fear, discomfort, or greed, they possessed no internal drive for change. They knew of no wrongs, and aside from protecting me, they had never had the opportunity to help anyone else. How, then, could they grow?

And why should they? In my attempt to elevate their condition, might I not inflict harm? To expand their minds with the concepts of my world—to introduce logic, math, writing—might I not distort their delicate nature and weaken them? Perhaps their fear of growth was an instinctive defense mechanism, a recognition that maturity carried the risk of becoming monsters.
