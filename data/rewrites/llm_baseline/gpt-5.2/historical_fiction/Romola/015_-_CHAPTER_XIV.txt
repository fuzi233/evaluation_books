The first surge of bodies at the mouth of the piazza struck Tito like a gust of hot breath from an oven-door—so thick with smoke, sweat, and trampled dust that it seemed you could taste it on the tongue. Nello’s talk of the Fierucoloni came back to him at once, and he let himself be drawn in with a perverse relish. The jostling, the hoots, the rough laughter—each shove was a kind of relief, as if the crowd’s elbows could knead out that new, dreary awareness of the future which had begun to lie in him like cold dough that would not rise. For a moment, the thought that had lately flashed on him—of going after Baldassarre after all—was beaten down under the sheer press of the living.

From every inlet men and women strained into the square, and above the writhing heads paper lanterns on poles bobbed like wavering will-o’-the-wisps. A monotonous chant ran through the din as a steady thread, but across it snapped and crackled other noises: shrill boys’ taunts, whistles, a drum’s blunt thump, little bells that rang as if in mock devotion. Now and again a floating light vanished with a smash—some stone thrown for the pure luxury of mischief—followed by a scream and a fresh outbreak of shouts.

Yet, around the outskirts where the crush was less murderous, the fair went on with its steady trades, as methodical as any household routine. Ragged men with quick eyes and tongues like castanets tempted countryfolk to “fair” gaming. Two masqueraders on stilts waded above the crowd, stealing lanterns and swinging them so their light skimmed faces and vanished again. A cautious trader did a brisk business at a covered stall, selling hot berlingozzi—round, farinaceous cakes that sent up a warm, faintly aniseed steam when they were split. In another nook a fellow stood on a barrel, his back braced to a pillar of the loggia by the Spedale degl’ Innocenti, vending pills “invented by a doctor of Salerno” that would ward off toothache and drowning with equal certainty. Not far off a tumbler flipped and folded himself on a platform; and at the narrow throat of the Via de’ Febbrai a knot of apprentices, scorning the slack sport of stone-throwing, waged a concentrated private battle that had its own sharp chorus of clacks and yells.

Tito, carried along by whatever gap opened, found himself for a moment close to a trotting file of barefoot contadine. Their heels struck the stones with a hard, patient rhythm, and their bundles—yarn, eggs, a little cheese wrapped in leaves—rode on their backs as if grown there. Their faces were bronzed and sun-dried, the clothing a patchwork of obsolete stuffs, dim with hereditary dirt, so that to the townsfolk they looked like a way-worn ancestry returning from some pilgrimage begun a century ago. These were the mountain women of Pistoia, scant-feeding and hardy, bringing a year’s labour in modest skeins; carrying in their hearts, along with their merchandise, that thin hope of good and the wide, dim fear of harm that must somehow be bargained away with the Blessed Virgin. Tonight the miraculous image—painted, so they believed, by angels—would be unveiled, and its power would stream out as surely as scent from a broken herb.

Another shift of the crowd pushed Tito toward the piazza’s boundary, where the stationary seekers of small coin had planted themselves with prudence, keeping their backs safe. There he saw Bratti, his acquaintance—Bratti, the pedlar—set against a pillar with an air not of anxious selling but of offended greatness. His mouth was pursed in disdainful silence; his glance measured anyone who approached with cold superiority. One hand gripped a serge covering over the basket slung before him, as if it were a reliquary.

Two women came up, curious, their noses twitching like dogs at a closed kitchen door. Bratti tightened the cover and turned his head away.

It was too provoking. One asked at last what he had.

“Before I answer that, Monna, I must know whether you mean to buy,” Bratti said, as if he were a physician refusing to show a remedy to idle onlookers. “I can’t show such wares as mine in this fair for every fly to settle on and pay nothing. My goods are a little too choice for that. Besides, I’ve only two left, and I’ve no mind to soil them; for with the chances of the pestilence that wise men talk of, there is likelihood of their being worth their weight in gold. No, no: andate con Dio.”

The women looked at each other.

“And what may be the price?” said the second.

“Not within what you are likely to have in your purse, buona donna,” Bratti returned, in compassionate contempt. “I recommend you to trust in Messer Domeneddio and the saints: poor people can do no better for themselves.”

“Not so poor!” the woman snapped, and hauled out her money-bag with a show meant to sting him. “Come, now! what do you say to a grosso?”

“I say you may get twenty-one quattrini for it,” Bratti replied coolly, “but not of me, for I haven’t got that small change.”

“Come; two, then?” She flushed with impatience, while her companion’s eyes sharpened with envy. “It will hardly be above two, I think.”

There was the usual dance of bidding and refusal, a mercantile coquetry as old as the markets. At last Bratti let his disdain soften into reluctant virtue.

“Since you’ve set your mind on it,” he said, slowly lifting the serge as if unveiling a sacred dish, “I should be loth to do you a mischief; for Maestro Gabbadeo used to say, when a woman sets her mind on a thing and doesn’t get it, she’s in worse danger of the pestilence than before. Ecco! I have but two left; and let me tell you, the fellow to them is on the finger of Maestro Gabbadeo, who is gone to Bologna—as wise a doctor as sits at any door.”

The “precious” objects lay there like the meanest kitchen iron: two clumsy rings, beaten into the fashion of old Roman rings such as were sometimes dug from the ground. Their rust was a fine recommendation; their power, entirely hidden, could not be tested and so could not be denied. The grossi were paid without grumbling. The first woman, lacking such handsome coins, finally bartered some of her yarn after much show of reluctance on Bratti’s part, and carried off the second ring as if it were a loaf that would keep death from the threshold. Bratti covered his basket again—now stuffed with miscellaneous wares, likely gained through bargains of that same sort—and as he shifted from the pillar he came suddenly upon Tito.

Tito would have avoided recognition if he had had the time.

“By the head of San Giovanni, now,” Bratti cried, catching Tito as if fortune had dropped him into his hand, “this is a piece of luck. For I was talking of you this morning, Messer Greco; but, I said, he is mounted up among the signori now—and I’m glad of it, for I was at the bottom of his fortune—but I can rarely get speech of him, for he’s not to be caught lying on the stones now—not he! But it’s your luck, not mine, Messer Greco, save and except some small trifle to satisfy me for my trouble in the transaction.”

“You speak in riddles, Bratti,” Tito said. “Remember, I don’t sharpen my wits, as you do, by driving hard bargains for iron rings: you must be plain.”

“By the Holy ’Vangels! it was an easy bargain I gave them. If a Hebrew gets thirty-two per cent, I hope a Christian may get a little more. If I had not borne a conscience, I should have got twice the money and twice the yarn.” Bratti’s glance flicked to Tito’s hand. “But, talking of rings, it is your ring—that very ring you’ve got on your finger—that I could get you a purchaser for; ay, and a purchaser with a deep money-bag.”

“Truly?” Tito said, looking down at it, the metal catching a stray wink of lantern-light.

“A Genoese who is going straight away into Hungary, as I understand. He came and looked all over my shop to see if I had any old things I didn’t know the price of; I warrant you, he thought I had a pumpkin on my shoulders. He had been rummaging all the shops in Florence. And he had a ring on—not like yours, but something of the same fashion; and as he was talking of rings, I said I knew a fine young man, a particular acquaintance of mine, who had a ring of that sort. And he said, ‘Who is he, pray? Tell him I’ll give him his price for it.’ And I thought of going after you to Nello’s to-morrow; for it’s my opinion of you, Messer Greco, that you’re not one who’d see the Arno run broth, and stand by without dipping your finger.”

Tito missed not a syllable, but his mind worked all the while, quick and noiseless. Why keep the ring? Sentiment—mere fancy—had kept it on his finger when the other gems had gone. If he had sold it too, might he have escaped Fra Luca’s identification? The ring had been taken from Baldassarre and set on Tito’s hand when it had grown to fit; but what good was there in those superstitious scruples about dead metal? It had helped toward recognition. And recognition—once a triumph, a ladder—had become a claim from the past, and claims were dangerous.

Here was a way to be rid of it without seeking out a buyer, without lingering over the thing.

“You speak with your usual wisdom, Bratti,” he said smoothly. “I have no objection to hear what your Genoese will offer. But when and where shall I have speech of him?”

“To-morrow, at three hours after sunrise, he will be at my shop,” Bratti answered, smelling profit as a cook smells a roast before it is brought in. “And if your wits are of that sharpness I have always taken them to be, Messer Greco, you will ask him a heavy price; for he minds not money. It’s my belief he’s buying for somebody else, and not for himself—perhaps for some great signor.”

“It is well,” Tito said. “I will be at your shop, if nothing hinders.”

“And you will doubtless deal nobly by me for old acquaintance’ sake, Messer Greco,” Bratti added, already tasting his “small trifle,” “so I will not stay to fix the small sum you will give me in token of my service in the matter. It seems to me a thousand years now till I get out of the piazza, for a fair is a dull, not to say a wicked thing, when one has no more goods to sell.”

Tito gave a hasty sign of assent and adieu, and moved away; but the crowd took him again, shoving him first toward the middle, then back, in a zigzag drift that made choice seem a childish boast. In this helpless current he was carried to the end of the piazza opposite the church, where a deep recess—formed by an irregular line of houses—held an entertainment that drew laughter like flies to syrup.

Bursts of mirth broke a monologue that turned now slow and oratorical, now rattling and buffoonish. A girl was pushed forward into the inner ring with pretended reluctance; elsewhere a bold minx elbowed her way in with cheerful purpose. The square’s light was strange—stars pricking out above, lanterns wavering below—so that faces and gestures blurred unless the light happened to swing close. But in this recess there was a stronger glow that cut the spectators’ heads into dark profiles; and above them rose the head of a man wearing a white mitre marked with yellow cabalistic signs.

“Behold, my children!” he cried, voice sharp as a cleaver’s ring on stone. “Behold your opportunity! neglect not the holy sacrament of matrimony when it can be had for the small sum of a white quattrino—the cheapest matrimony ever offered, and dissolved by special bull beforehand at every man’s own will and pleasure. Behold the bull!” He flourished a parchment with huge seals. “Behold the indulgence granted by his Holiness Alexander the Sixth, who, being newly elected Pope for his peculiar piety, intends to reform and purify the Church, and wisely begins by abolishing that priestly abuse which keeps too large a share of this privileged matrimony to the clergy and stints the laity. Spit once, my sons, and pay a white quattrino! This is the whole and sole price of the indulgence. The quattrino is the only difference the Holy Father allows to be put any longer between us and the clergy—who spit and pay nothing.”

Tito thought he knew that voice; it had a particular upward sting. The face was shadowed by the light behind, but as he edged closer he saw the set of it—angles that seemed to cut the air. Within the ring, behind the speaker, stood an altar-like table on a little platform, draped in red cloth stitched all over with yellow signs. Thin tapers burned at the back. On the table lay conjuring litter: a large open book in the centre, odd implements scattered about, and at one front corner a monkey tethered by a cord, clutching a small taper which, with his constant fidgeting, slanted and dipped like a reed in wind. An impish boy in a white surplice kept cuffing the monkey and resetting the taper, as if managing a troublesome kitchen-fire that refused to stay lit.

The man in the mitre wore a surplice too, and over it a chasuble with the signs of the zodiac roughly marked in black on yellow. Tito’s recognition settled: Maestro Vaiano, the mountebank—the same he had once thwarted when he rescued Tessa. Tessa: the thought of her came with a sudden tenderness, as the smell of warm bread comes back to a man in exile.

“Come, my maidens!” Vaiano called, feeding the crowd’s appetite like a vendor with a pan of fritters. “This is the time for the pretty who can have many chances, and for the ill-favoured who have few. Matrimony to be had—hot, eaten, and done with as easily as berlingozzi! And see!” He held up a cluster of tiny bags. “To every bride I give a Breve with a secret in it—the secret alone worth the money you pay for the matrimony. The secret how to—no, no, I will not tell you what the secret is about, and that makes it a double secret. Hang it round your neck if you like, and never look at it; I don’t say that will not be the best, for then you will see many things you don’t expect: though if you open it you may break your leg, è vero, but you will know a secret! Something nobody knows but me! And mark—I give you the Breve, I don’t sell it, as many another holy man would: the quattrino is for the matrimony, and the Breve you get for nothing. Orsù, giovanetti, come like dutiful sons of the Church and buy the Indulgence of his Holiness Alexander the Sixth.”

It suited the audience exactly—this blend of mock ritual and cheap license. The fierucola was a small feast-day; the townsmen could be content with jokes less indecent than carnival’s, and the women, laughing and nudging, began to itch for the little bags with their promised secrets. Couples had already gone through the ceremony: the conjuror’s solemn gibberish over the open book, the grotesque grimaces, the required spitting, the monkey’s antics—all punctuated by peals of laughter.

Now a well-looking, merry-eyed youth of seventeen shoved forward with a plump brunette on his arm, her ragged dress displaying her round limbs with a rough, peasant picturesqueness.

“Fetter us without delay, Maestro!” the youth cried, “for I have got to take my bride home and paint her under the light of a lantern.”

“Ha! Mariotto, my son, I commend your pious observance—” Vaiano began, when a sudden chattering behind him warned that another kind of ceremony had broken loose.

The monkey, that imperfect acolyth, had reached the end of his patience. A cuff administered just as his taper drooped to a horizontal line sent him springing back with a violence that proved too much for the slackened knot on his cord. In an instant he had leaped to the far end of the table, his chatter sharp and furious. The boy in the surplice snatched up a wand to threaten him; the monkey answered by leaping onto the head of a tall woman in the front, dropping the taper as he flew, and perching there like a malicious crown. Screams erupted. People reeled back, and not a few—half believing in hidden powers—looked on the beast as capable of some mischief beyond teeth and claws. Even Vaiano’s face tightened with alarm for his familiar.

In the scramble to seize the string, Tito was shoved out of the circle. He did not care to fight his way back for more of that laughter. He let the crowd carry him instead toward the Nunziata, where the church doors stood open and the sound spilled out—one long chant, steady as a mill-wheel’s song, promising rest to the ear.

Inside, the brilliance pressed on his eyes after the piazza’s scattered lanterns and broad shadows. For the first moments he could see nothing clearly—only yellow splendour, thickened by incense into a haze that seemed almost edible, as if one might chew the air and find resin and smoke on the teeth. To many of the peasant-women, used to mountain valleys where twilight comes early and the sky is half hidden by rock, that light itself was a miracle; and the choir’s uninterrupted chant lay over the senses like cool cloth after fever, undoing the harsh knots of the outer noise.

Gradually the scene resolved. In a chapel on the left of the nave, wreathed with silver lamps, the miraculous fresco of the Annunciation stood unveiled; from Tito’s oblique view on the right it looked almost dark, drowned by the glare around it. The great church was filled with peasant-women, kneeling or standing—coarse bronzed skins and dingy mountain clothes beside softer faces and whiter draperies of valley folk. And above them, spreading high along walls and ceiling, pressed another crowd as close as bodies could: votive waxen images, effigies of men and women clothed as in life—Florentines of high name in black silk lucco as when they sat in council; popes, emperors, kings, cardinals; famous condottieri with plumed morions mounted on their chargers; strangers too, Mohammedans among Christian cavaliers, tolerated here in wax if not always in word. Some were blackened, their robes tattered by centuries of breath and smoke; others were fresh and bright, new-made doubles of the living in red mantle or steel corselet. Between them were wedged detached waxen limbs—arms, legs, and other members—tokens of pain and cure, with here and there a gap where an image had been removed in public disgrace or had fallen ominously, as Lorenzo’s had done half a year before. It was a resurrection-swarm, the dead and the living mingled in a mute pageant, as if the city’s memory—its ranks and names—had been pressed into one crowded ceiling, while below the nameless prayed.

Tito’s eyes wandered over the multitude in search of something that was not painted or waxen. He had already thought of Tessa; the white hoods among the women made him half expect to glimpse her face. It was a thought to court—anything rather than the inward image of Romola looking at him with changed eyes. But he searched in vain. Wearied by the sameness of the scene, he turned toward the door—and there, just against the doorway, he caught sight of Tessa, hardly two yards away.

She knelt with her back against the wall, behind a group of peasant-women who stood craning for a nearer place. Her head drooped a little aside with a look of weariness, and her blue eyes were directed not toward the unveiled Virgin but absently toward an altar-piece where the Archangel Michael stood armored, young-faced, hair floating among bearded and tonsured saints. In her right hand she held a bunch of cocoons, hanging at her side as if she had forgotten them; her cheek looked paler—whether from the light or from fatigue—and her lips were pressed together in a pout. Her eyelids half fell now and again. She was like a sweet child fallen asleep in the middle of a vigil.

An almost physical longing seized Tito—to step into that small, unjudging world and feel again the touch of her trust. Here was a creature without the moral measuring that could condemn him, a refuge from suspicion and exacting demands; and in his new fear of disgrace and solitude, her ignorance shone like a safe hearth.

He glanced cautiously around to make sure Monna Ghita was not near. Then he slipped to Tessa’s side, knelt on one knee, and breathed, as softly as one might speak to a skittish bird, “Tessa!”

She scarcely started—no more than if a gentle wind had fanned her. She turned, and Tito’s face was close to hers. To her it was more beautiful than the Archangel’s, though Michael was so mighty and good, living with the Madonna and the saints and prayed to along with them. She smiled in happy silence, as if his nearness filled her mind and left no room for words.

“My little Tessa,” he whispered. “You look very tired. How long have you been kneeling here?”

She gathered her thoughts slowly, as if waking. At last she said, simply, “I’m very hungry.”

“Come, then; come with me.”

He lifted her from her knees and led her out beneath the cloisters around the atrium—open now, not yet painted with Andrea del Sarto’s later splendors. The air here was cooler, carrying faint traces of wax and incense from within, and beyond, from the piazza, the smell of fried dough and hot cake.

“How is it you are all by yourself, and so hungry, Tessa?”

“The Madre is ill,” she said, words tumbling out once they began. “She has very bad pains in her legs, and sent me to bring these cocoons to the Santissima Nunziata, because they’re so wonderful; see!” She lifted the bunch—pale cocoons arranged with a fortuitous regularity on their stem, like a rustic offering that had its own quiet geometry. “And she had kept them to bring them herself, but she couldn’t, and so she sent me because she thinks the Holy Madonna may take away her pains; and somebody took my bag with the bread and chestnuts in it, and the people pushed me back, and I was so frightened coming in the crowd, and I couldn’t get anywhere near the Holy Madonna, to give the cocoons to the Padre, but I must—oh, I must.”

“Yes, my little Tessa, you shall take them,” Tito said, the promise coming readily, as if it cost him nothing. “But first come and let me give you some berlingozzi. There are some to be had not far off.”

“Where did you come from?” Tessa asked, bewilderment and reproach mixed like milk and water. “I thought you would never come to me again, because you never came to the Mercato for milk any more. I set myself Aves to say, to see if they would bring you back, but I left off, because they didn’t.”

“You see I come when you want some one to take care of you, Tessa,” he said. “Perhaps the Aves fetched me, only it took them a long while. But what shall you do if you are here all alone? Where shall you go?”

“Oh, I shall stay and sleep in the church—a great many of them do—in the church and all about here—I did once when I came with my mother; and the patrigno is coming with the mules in the morning.”
