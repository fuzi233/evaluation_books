Denys reached out, catching Gerard by the shoulder to stem his downward plunge. Yet, the rescue was precarious; Denys feared that saving his friend's neck might still result in broken limbs. The only solid anchor beneath Denys was the carcass of the dying bear, upon whom Denys's head and shoulders crashed. With frantic strength, Denys tore himself off the beast. It was a futile gesture. The bear, panting heavily with quivering limbs, was already succumbing to exhaustion, yet a hare was not so innocuous. Soon, the animal breathed her last. The practical Denys immediately propped Gerard upright against the soft, warm flesh of the corpse, fanning him gently until consciousness returned.

Gerard revived gradually, though confused and disoriented by the sensation of the bear surrounding him. Startled, he rolled away, letting out a piercing yell.

"Courage!" cried Denys, pointing to the motionless form. "Le diable est mort."

"Is it truly dead?" Gerard questioned, hiding partially behind a tree. His courage burned with a feverish intensity, and the chill of the mountain air had settled deep within him.

"Behold," Denys replied, pulling the brute's ear playfully. He opened the jaw wide, inserting his head inside to perform various insulting antics. In the middle of this grotesque display, Gerard was violently overcome with nausea.

Denys laughed at his companion's distress.

"What troubles you now?" the soldier asked. "Also, why did you tumble from your perch just as we secured victory?"

"I swooned," Gerard admitted.

"But why?"

Receiving no immediate answer, Denys pressed on. "Green girls faint merely by looking at you, yet they choose their time and place wisely. What woman has ever fainted atop a tree?"

"She sent her filthy blood all over me," Gerard retched, wiping his brow. "I believe the stench must have overwhelmed me! Faugh! I detest blood."

"I suspect it did potent work."

"Look at the mess she has made of me."

"But with her blood, not yours," Denys countered, showing unexpected sympathy. "I pity the enemy who strove to satisfy you."

"You need not boast, Master Denys," Gerard accused, glancing at the soldier. "I saw you beneath the tree, your shirt stained with color."

"Let us distinguish," Denys blushed slightly. "It is permissible to tremble for a friend."

As proof, Gerard threw his arms around Denys's neck in a silent embrace.

"Look here," whined the stout soldier, moved by this sudden outpouring of youth and affection. "Was anything ever so reminiscent of a woman? I love thee, little milksop—go on. Good! Behold him on his knees now. What new caprice is this?"

"Oh, Denys, ought we not to return thanks to Him who has saved both our lives against such fearful odds?" Gerard knelt, praying aloud. Shortly thereafter, he found Denys kneeling quietly beside him, hands crossed over his chest in the fashion of his nation, wearing a face as long as his arm. When they rose, Gerard's countenance was radiant.

"Good Denys," he said, "Heaven will reward thy piety."

"Ah, bah!" the Frenchman scoffed. "I did it merely out of politeness. It was to please thee, little one. *C'est égal*, the prayer was well and orderly, and it edified me to the core while it lasted. A bishop could scarcely have handled the matter better. Now that our vespers are sung and the saints enlisted with us, *marchons*."

Before taking two steps, Denys paused. "By-the-by, the cub!"

"Oh, no, no!" cried Gerard.

"You are right. It is late. We squandered time climbing trees, tumbling off them, swooning, vomiting, and praying. Furthermore, the brute is heavy to carry. And now I recall, we shall likely have Papa hunting for it next; these bears create quite a commotion over an odd cub."

Suddenly, Denys noticed something wrong. "What is this? You are wounded! You are wounded!"

"Not I."

"He is wounded; miserable that I am!"

"Be calm, Denys. I am not touched; I feel no pain anywhere."

"You? You only feel when another is hurt!" Denys cried, thrown into great emotion. Throwing himself to his knees, he examined Gerard's leg with glistening eyes.

"Quick! Quick! Before it stiffens!" he urged, hurrying him forward.

"Who makes the commotion about nothing now?" Gerard inquired composedly.

Denys's reply was indirect. "Be pleased to note," he said, "that I have a bad heart. You possessed the fortitude to save my life, yet I must sneer at you, a novice in war. Was I not a novice once myself? Then you fainted from a wound, and I mistook it for fear, calling you a milksop. Briefly, I possess a bad tongue and a bad heart."

"Denys!"

"Plait-il?"

"You lie."

"You are very kind to say so, little one, and I am eternally obliged to you," mumbled the remorseful Denys.

Before they had traveled many furlongs, the muscles of Gerard's wounded leg contracted and stiffened. Eventually, he could barely plant his toe upon the ground, doing so with excruciating pain. At last, he could endure it no longer.

"Let me lie down and die," he groaned. "This is intolerable."

Denys argued that it was afternoon and the nights were becoming frosty; cold and hunger were terrible companions, especially with a notorious figure already proven dead. Thus, Gerard leaned upon his axe, hobbling on. But soon, he gave in all at once, sinking helpless into the road.

Denys drew him aside into the woods. To his surprise, the soldier handed Gerard his crossbow and bolts, strictly ordering him to remain quiet. If any suspicious individuals discovered him, Gerard was to command them to keep aloof; if they refused, he was to shoot them dead at twenty paces. "Honest men keep the paths, and knaves in the woods are only fools who attempt to parley." With this, Denys snatched up Gerard's axe and set off running—not toward Düsseldorf as Gerard expected, but back along the road they had come.

Gerard lay aching, the journey to Rome seeming impossibly distant despite having covered two hundred miles. Soon, however, his thoughts drifted toward Sevenbergen. How sweet it would be one day to hold Margaret's hand and recount everything he had endured for her! The mere thought of her soothed his pain, and amidst the irritation of his nerves, he lay resigned, offering a faint, sweet smile.

More than two hours later, shouts erupted. Suddenly, an object struck a nearby tree with a sharp thud, vibrating within its branches. Gerard looked up to see an arrow. Starting to his feet, he watched as several missiles rattled through the boughs, and the wood echoed with battle cries. Unable to determine their origin, as the vast forests reverberated sound, it seemed the air itself filled with noise. Presently, there was a lull, followed by the fierce gallop of hooves. Louder shouts and cries arose, mingled with shrieks and groans. Above all, strange and terrible sounds, like fierce claps of thunder, bellowed loudly before dying off in cracking echoes. Red tongues of flame shot intermittently among the trees, and clouds of sulfurous smoke drifted overhead. Then, all was still.

Struck with awe, Gerard cried, "What will become of Denys?" "Oh, why did you leave me? Oh, Denys, my friend! My friend!"

Just before sunset, Denys returned, almost collapsing under a hairy bundle—the bear's skin. Gerard welcomed him with a burst of joy that surprised him.

"I thought never to see you again, dear Denys. Were you in the battle?"

"No. What battle?"

"The bloody fray of men or fiends that raged in the wood a while ago!" Gerard described it vividly.

Denys patted him indulgently on the back. "It is well," he said. "Thou art a good painter; and fever is a great spur to the imagination. Once, I lay in a cart-shed with a cracked skull and saw two hosts maneuver and fight for a good hour on eight feet square, which I described fairly to my comrade, though not so gorgeously as thou, for lack of book learning."

"What, then, you believe me not?" Gerard insisted. "When I tell you the arrows whizzed over my head..."

"May the foul fiends fly away with me if I believe a word of it."

Gerard took his arm and quietly pointed to a tree close by. "Why, it looks like—it is—a broad arrow, as I live!" He approached closely to inspect it.

"It came from the battle. I heard it, and saw it."

"An English arrow."

"How know you that?"
