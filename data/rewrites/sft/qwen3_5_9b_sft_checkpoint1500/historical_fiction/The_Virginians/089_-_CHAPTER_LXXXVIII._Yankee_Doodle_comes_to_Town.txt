In the era of our British dominion, we employed America as a sanctuary for our moral failings. Alongside the convicts and indentured servants we shipped across the Atlantic, we released upon its shores the reckless, the dispossessed, and the younger sons for whom the constraints of England had become unbearable. Much like Captain Cook, who gifted newly discovered islands with samples of European fauna, we sent over our "black sheep" to roam free and multiply their peculiar lineage in the colonies.

I confess, however, a certain guilt in this arrangement regarding the Reverend Hagan, husband of my kinswoman. Since we could find no suitable employment for him in England, we gladly dispatched him to Virginia, granting him a colonial pulpit. He conducted himself as a valiant, honorable gentleman, diligently serving both his flock and his monarch, thereby reflecting credit upon my modest patronage. Madame Theo, when I voiced my scruples, invariably reminded me that my actions, in this instance as in all others, sprang from the highest principles of morality and honor. Yet, if I had truly sought to offer Hagan a living at home, selecting him and his wife to minister to our parish, I am certain I would have done so. Though I never doubted Cousin Maria's sincerity in repenting, I admit to a secret relief when she departed for the wilderness. And I speak this openly, acknowledging both my pride and my error: twice, when I needed her sympathy and friendship most, this virtuous woman stood by me. She endured her own trials with fortitude and soothed the sorrows of others with admirable devotion. Alas, I and some of my circle (save for Theo) looked down upon her. Oh, the shame of such arrogance!

Lady Maria was hardly alone among our family in being exiled to the American hinterlands. The Honourable William Esmond, having exhausted his capacity to borrow, steal, and cheat within the bounds of propriety, was accommodated with a post in New York. His noble brother and royal master expressed a hearty desire to see naught further of him. When the troubles commenced, news of his exploits reached us. Deceit and malice were his vanguard wherever he traveled. Lord Dunmore informed me that Mr. William had publicly declared that our estate of Castlewood existed solely at the pleasure of his brother; that his father, moved by consideration for Madam Esmond, Lord Dunmore's half-sister, had granted her the lands for life; and that William himself was currently negotiating with Lord Castlewood to purchase the reversion of the estate! We possessed the deed of gift in our strongroom at Castlewood, and it was duly registered at Williamsburg, rendering us secure on that count. Nevertheless, intent was everything. Hal and I vowed, upon our next encounter with Mr. William, to extract from him confirmation of this charming fabrication. The impact of these words on Madam Esmond need not be detailed. "What! Is my father, the Marquis of Esmond, a liar, and am I a cheat?" she cried. "Will he seize my son's property upon my death?" She was prepared to write not only to Lord Castlewood in England but to His Majesty at St. James's herself, only prevented by my assurance that Mr. William's duplicity was notorious among all his acquaintances and that we could not reasonably expect consistency from him. Soon thereafter, we heard of him as a leading voice among the Loyalists in New York, holding commissions as Captain and later Major of volunteer corps sending addresses to the well-disposed throughout the colonies, announcing their perfect readiness to lay down their lives for the Mother Country.

It became impossible to remain in a house lacking a whole window. After shutting the shutters of our ill-fated rented mansion at Williamsburg, Madam Esmond left our little capital, and my family returned to Richmond, which had likewise been abandoned by the members of the dissolved Assembly. Captain Hal and his wife returned promptly to their plantation. I, however, somewhat annoyed by the trajectory of events, divided my time between my own household and that of our Governor, who professed an eager desire for my counsel and companionship. Despite profound political divergences, no actual quarrel had yet broken out between us. Even following the dissolution of our House of Assembly—whose members adjourned to a tavern, where, I believe, the concept of a continental congress was first conceived—the gentlemen staunchest in opposition remained friends with His Excellency, sharing his hospitality and joining him in recreational excursions. Once the session concluded, the gentry retired to their respective counties to hold local meetings. With Assemblies in most other provinces abruptly dissolved as well, it was universally agreed that a general congress should convene. Philadelphia, as the continent's largest and most significant city, was chosen as the venue. These celebrated conferences began, serving merely as the angry prelude to war. We still sang "God Save the King"; we still presented humble petitions to the throne. Yet, when I visited my brother Harry at Fanny's Mount one afternoon, he rode out on business that morning, and I, following the same path, was told by a groom that Master Harry had proceeded toward Willis's Ordinary. Thinking no harm, I pursued him. Upon reaching a clearing near Willis's, as I emerged from the wood, I encountered Captain Hal on horseback, surrounded by thirty or forty countrymen armed with every conceivable weapon—pikes, scythes, fowling-pieces, and muskets. The Captain, flanked by two or three capable young men acting as officers, was drilling the men. As I drew nearer, a peculiar expression crossed Hal's face. "Present arms!" he commanded, and the makeshift army endeavored to render the salute as competently as possible. "Captain Cade, this is my brother, Sir George Warrington."

"As a relation of yours, Colonel," the man addressed as Captain replied, "the gentleman is welcome," extending his hand accordingly.

"And—a true friend to Virginia," Hal stammered, his face flushing crimson.

"Yes, please God! Gentlemen," I interjected, whereupon the regiment erupted in a hearty hurrah for the Colonel and his brother. Once the drill concluded, the officers and men alike wished to adjourn to Willis's for refreshments. However, Colonel Hal declined to drink with them that afternoon, and we trotted homeward together.

"So, Hal, the cat is out of the bag!" I remarked.

He fixed me with a stern gaze. "I suspect there are wilder cats within the sack, brother. It must come to this. Say, you must not tell Madam."

"Good heavens!" I exclaimed. "Do you mean that with fellows such as those I saw yonder, you and your friends intend to make fight against the greatest nation and the finest army in the world?"

"I fancy we shall receive an awful whipping," Hal admitted, "and that is the fact. But then, George," he added, flashing his sweet, kind smile, "we are young, and a few whippings may do us good. Won't it do us good, Dolly, you old slut?" He playfully touched the whip of an old mongrel of all trades trotting beside him.

I refrained from urging him again (having done so in vain on numerous previous occasions) regarding our British allegiance, the side which appeared to me the superior one. He was accustomed to deflecting my arguments with the refrain, "All mighty well, brother, you speak as an Englishman, and have cast in your lot with your country, as I have with mine." To this plea, I acknowledge, there is no reply, and all that remains for the disputants is to fight it out when the stronger party proves right. In the wars of the last century, which side held the right? The King or Parliament? The side that prevailed was deemed the right, and on the whole, their victory proved far more humane than the Cavaliers would have achieved had they won. Indeed, suppose we Tories had secured the day in America; how frightful and bloody that triumph would have been! One imagines countless ropes and scaffolds, countless noble heads laid low. A strange sensation, I confess; I stood on the Loyalist side, yet I desired the Whigs to win. On the other hand, my brother Hal, who distinguished himself greatly with his regiment, never uttered a word of disrespect against the enemy he opposed. "The officers of the British army," he would say, "are gentlemen: at least, I have not heard that they have altered significantly since my time. There may be scoundrels and ruffians among the enemy's troops; I dare say we could find similar ones among our own. Our business is to beat His Majesty's forces, not to call them names—any rascal can do that." Consequently, owing to a title bestowed by Mr. Lee upon my brother, which many of his rough horsemen failed to comprehend, Harry was frequently dubbed "Chevalier Baird" within the Continental Army. He was, indeed, a knight without fear and without reproach.

Regarding the argument, "What could such people as those you were drilling accomplish against the British army?" Hal offered a confident response.

"They can beat them," he asserted, "Mr. George, that is precisely what they can do."

"Great heavens!" I cried, "do you mean that with your company of Wolfe's, you would hesitate to attack five hundred such?"
