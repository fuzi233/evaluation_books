"Which when his mother saw, she in her mind
Was troubled sore, he wist well what to ween."

Margaret had scarcely vanished from the room before Mr. Thornton burst in, his face flushed with a heat that seemed to burn through his skin.

"I could not come sooner," he panted, his eyes darting around the dining-room as if searching for a ghost. "The superintendent would have delayed me. Where is she?"

He found his mother quietly rearranging the displaced chairs, her movements brisk and untroubled. She did not answer immediately.

"Gone home," she said, her tone clipped.

"Gone home!" The word hung in the air, sharp with disbelief.

"Yes. She was remarkably composed. I do not believe it was a serious injury; some women simply faint at the slightest shock."

"I am sorry she has gone," Mr. Thornton muttered, pacing the floor with restless energy. "She could not possibly have been fit for the journey."

"She insisted she was," his mother replied. "Mr. Lowe confirmed it. I fetched him myself."

"Thank you, mother." He paused, extending his hand in a gesture of gratitude she missed entirely. "What have you done with the Irishmen?"

"Sent them to the Dragon for a meal, poor souls. Fortunately, I intercepted Father Grady and persuaded him to stay and reason with them before they fled in a panic. How did Miss Hale manage to walk home? I am certain she could not have."

"She took a cab. Everything was handled with proper decorum, even the payment. Let us discuss something less volatile."

"I do not know where I should have been without her intervention."

"Have you become so helpless that you require a girl to defend you?" Mrs. Thornton asked, her voice dripping with scorn.

Mr. Thornton's face flamed red. "Few girls would have taken the blows intended for me—blows delivered with such genuine goodwill."

"A girl in love will endure much," his mother replied, turning away.

"Mother!" He stepped forward, then halted, his chest heaving with a suppressed storm.

His mother was startled by the sheer physical force he exerted to maintain his composure. She could not decipher the turmoil within him. Was it rage? His eyes burned, his frame expanded, his breath came in ragged gasps. It was a chaotic blend of joy, fury, pride, and a panting uncertainty that she could not interpret. Yet, the intensity of it unsettled her, as the presence of raw emotion, whose roots remain hidden, invariably does. She moved to the sideboard, opened a drawer, and retrieved a duster, ostensibly to wipe a spot of eau de Cologne from the sofa arm. In truth, she kept her back turned to her son far longer than necessary. When she finally spoke, her voice sounded strained and artificial.

"You have taken steps regarding the rioters, I presume? Do you anticipate further violence? Where were the police? They are never present when needed!"

"On the contrary," he replied, his voice steadying. "I saw three or four of them struggling near the gates when the barrier fell, fighting with surprising vigor. More arrived just as the yard cleared. I could have detained several if I had been quicker. There will be no difficulty; many witnesses can identify them."

"But will they not return tonight?"

"I intend to arrange a sufficient guard for the premises. I have agreed to meet Captain Hanbury at the station in half an hour."

"You should have tea first."

"Tea! Yes, I suppose I must. It is half-past six, and I may be gone for some time. Do not wait up for me, mother."

"Do you expect me to retire before ensuring your safety?"

"Well, perhaps not." He hesitated. "But if I have time, I shall stop by Crampton after settling affairs with the police and Hamper and Clarkson."

Their eyes locked. They stared at one another intently for a minute. Then she asked, "Why are you going via Crampton?"

"To inquire after Miss Hale."

"I will send. Williams will deliver the water-bed she requested and ask how she fares."

"I must go myself."

"Not merely to ask how Miss Hale is?"

"No, not merely for that. I wish to thank her for standing between me and the mob."

"What compelled you to go down at all? You were throwing your head into the lion's mouth!"

He glanced sharply at her, realizing she knew nothing of the exchange between him and Margaret in the drawing-room. He deflected her gaze with a question of his own.

"Will you be afraid to remain alone until I secure the police? Or should we send Williams now so they arrive before we finish tea? There is no time to lose. I must depart in a quarter of an hour."

Mrs. Thornton left the room. Her servants, accustomed to her sharp, decisive commands, found her instructions confused and uncertain. Mr. Thornton remained in the dining-room, ostensibly planning his visit to the police office, but his mind was entirely occupied with Margaret. Everything beyond the immediate moment seemed dim and vague, overshadowed by the memory of her arms around his neck, the soft clinging that had sent a flush of color racing across his cheeks.

The tea service was nearly silent, broken only by Fanny's incessant narration of her own sensations: her alarm, her relief, her subsequent sickness and trembling limbs.

"There, that is enough," her brother said, rising abruptly. "The reality was sufficient for me." He prepared to leave when his mother placed a hand upon his arm.

"You will return here before you go to the Hales'," she said in a low, anxious whisper.

"I know what I know," Fanny murmured to herself.

"Why? Will it be too late to disturb them?"

"John, come back to me for this one evening. It will be late for Mrs. Hale. But that is not the point. Tomorrow, you will... Come back tonight, John!" She rarely pleaded with her son; her pride usually forbade it. Yet, she had never pleaded in vain.

"I will return straight here after my business. You will surely inquire after them? After her?"

Mrs. Thornton was not a talkative companion to Fanny, nor a good listener while her son was away. But upon his return, her eyes and ears were keen to capture every detail of his precautions against further outrage. He clearly understood his objective. Punishment and suffering were the natural consequences for those who had participated in the riot. All that was required to protect property and assert the proprietor's will was a clean, sharp execution of justice.

"Mother! Do you know what I must say to Miss Hale tomorrow?"

The question caught her off guard during a pause where she had momentarily forgotten Margaret.

She looked up at him.

"Yes! You can hardly avoid it."

"Do otherwise! I do not understand you."

"I mean that, having allowed her feelings to overwhelm her, I consider you bound in honor—"

"Bound in honor," he scoffed. "I am afraid honor has nothing to do with it. 'Her feelings overwhelmed her!' What feelings do you mean?"

"Nay, John, there is no need for anger. Did she not rush down and cling to you to save you from danger?"

"She did!" he admitted. "But, mother," he stopped abruptly, standing directly before her. "I dare not hope. I have never been faint-hearted before; yet I cannot believe such a creature cares for me."

"Don't be foolish, John. Such a creature! Why, she might be a duke's daughter, by your account. And what proof more would you desire of her affection? I can believe she struggled against her aristocratic upbringing, but I like her the better for seeing clearly at last. It is a significant concession on her part," Mrs. Thornton said, smiling slowly while tears gathered in her eyes. "For after tonight, I stand second. It was to have you to myself, all to myself, for a few more hours, that I begged you not to leave until tomorrow."

"Dearest mother!" (Yet love remains selfish, and instantly he reverted to his own hopes and fears, casting a cold shadow over Mrs. Thornton's heart.) "But I know she does not care for me. I shall place myself at her feet—I must. If it were but one chance in a thousand—or a million—I would do it."

"Don't fear!" his mother said, suppressing her own mortification at his lack of attention to her rare maternal outburst, hiding the pang of jealousy that revealed the intensity of her disregarded love. "Don't be afraid," she said coldly. "As far as love extends, she may be worthy of you. It must have taken considerable effort to overcome her pride. Don't be afraid, John," she added, kissing him as she bid him goodnight. She exited the room slowly and majestically. Once inside her own chamber, however, she locked the door and sat down to shed unwonted tears.

* * * * *

Margaret entered the room, where her father and mother still sat in hushed conversation, appearing very pale and wan. She approached them closely before trusting herself to speak.

"Mrs. Thornton will send the water-bed, mamma."

"Dear, how tired you look! Is it very hot, Margaret?"

"Very hot, and the streets are rough from the strike."

Margaret's color returned with vivid brightness, only to fade instantly.

"Here is a message from Bessy Higgins asking you to visit her," Mrs. Hale said. "But I am sure you look too exhausted."

"Yes!" Margaret replied. "I am tired; I cannot go."

She remained silent and trembling while preparing tea. She was relieved to see her father so engrossed with her mother that he failed to notice her appearance. Even after her mother retired, he refused to leave her side, taking upon himself the duty of reading to her until sleep claimed her. Margaret was alone.

"Now I will think of it—now I will remember it all. I could not before—I dared not." She sat motionless in her chair, hands clasped on her knees, lips pressed tight, eyes fixed as if gazing at a vision. She drew a deep breath.

"I, who detest scenes—I, who have despised people for displaying emotion—I went down and threw myself into the fray like a romantic fool! Did I accomplish anything? They would likely have dispersed without me, I dare say." But this was an overreach of rationality; her well-balanced judgment immediately corrected itself. "No, perhaps they would not. I did some good. But what possessed me to defend that man as if he were a helpless child! Ah!" she cried, clutching her hands together. "It is no wonder those people believed I was in love with him, after disgracing myself so. I in love—and with him too!" Her pale cheeks suddenly blazed like fire, and she covered her face with her hands. When she lowered them, her palms were wet with scalding tears.

"Oh, how low I have fallen that they should say such things of me! I could not have been so brave for anyone else, precisely because he was so utterly indifferent to me—if, indeed, I do not positively dislike him. It made me anxious that there should be fair play on both sides; and I could see what fair play was. It was not fair," she said vehemently, "that he should stand there—sheltered, awaiting the soldiers, who might catch those poor maddened creatures as in a trap—without making an effort to bring them to reason. And it was worse than unfair for them to attack him as they threatened. I would do it again, let whoever say what they like of me. If I saved one blow, one cruel, angry act that might otherwise have been committed, I did a woman's work. Let them insult my maiden pride as they will—I walk pure before God!"

She looked up, and a noble peace seemed to descend, calming her face until it was "stiller than chiselled marble."

Dixon entered.

"If you please, Miss Margaret, here is the water-bed from Mrs. Thornton's. It is too late for tonight, I fear, as missus is nearly asleep: but it will serve nicely for tomorrow."

"Very," said Margaret. "You must convey our best thanks."

Dixon left the room for a moment.

"If you please, Miss Margaret, he says he is to ask particularly how you are. I think he must mean missus; but his last words were to ask how Miss Hale was."

"Me!" Margaret exclaimed, drawing herself up. "I am quite well. Tell him I am perfectly well." Yet her complexion was as deathly white as her handkerchief, and her head ached with intense pain.

Mr. Hale now entered. Having left his sleeping wife, he wished, as Margaret observed, to be amused and engaged by whatever she had to tell him. With sweet patience, she endured her pain without complaint, dredging up countless trivial subjects for conversation—all except the riot, which she never once mentioned. The thought of it made her sick.

"Good-night, Margaret. I have every chance of a good night myself, and you are looking very pale from your vigil. I shall call Dixon if your mother needs anything. Go to bed and sleep like a top; for I am sure you need it, poor child!"

"Good-night, papa!"

She let her color fade—the false smile dissolve—the eyes grow dull with heavy pain. She released her strong will from its laborious task. Until morning, she might feel ill and weary.

She lay down and never stirred. To move hand or foot, or even a single finger, would have exceeded the powers of either volition or motion. So tired, so stunned, she thought she never slept at all; her feverish thoughts passed and repassed the boundary between sleep and waking, maintaining their own miserable identity. She could not be alone, prostrate and powerless as she was; a cloud of faces looked up at her, offering no sense of fierce anger or personal danger, but a profound shame that she should be the object of universal regard—a shame so acute that it seemed she would rather burrow into the earth to hide, yet she could not escape the unwinking glare of many eyes.
