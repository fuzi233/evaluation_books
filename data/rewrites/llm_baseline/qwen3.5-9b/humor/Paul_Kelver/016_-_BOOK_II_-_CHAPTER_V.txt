Over breakfast—or rather, supper, as it was late—Dan and I swapped life stories. We found we had more in common than I expected. Dan had left school years before I did and headed to Cambridge. However, fate intervened twice: first, his father died, and second, he picked up a wound in the Indian Mutiny that never healed. These blows forced him to cut his university career short.

"You probably wouldn't guess that this would hurt me," Dan smiled, "but it really did. At Cambridge, I finally realized I was a scholar by nature. Back at school, I hated learning because the system was designed to make it boring. Imagine a tour group rushing through a museum; the goal isn't to appreciate the art, just to check off the boxes saying 'we visited.' College was different. I picked my own topics, studied how I liked, and actually *ate* knowledge instead of being force-fed like a Strasbourg goose."

After returning to London, Dan took a job at a bank run by an old family friend. While it paid a pittance, it allowed him time to study. But the mind-numbing routine terrified him. When his mother died, he threw off the dusty atmosphere of the financial district and joined a struggling theater troupe. He stayed on stage for eighteen months, playing everything from Romeo to Paul Pry, painting sets, and pasting posters. He claimed pasting was the hardest skill he learned; the heavy paste often caused the posters to spring back and stick to the amateur's own forehead.

Disgusted with acting, he tried a circus as "Signor Ricardo, the Daring Bare-Back Rider" and performed in a trapeze act with the "Roscius Brothers." Seeking stability, he worked as a waiter in Ostend, then a footman.

"One never knows," Dan noted. "Maybe I'll end up a society novelist. Knowing the ins and outs of the aristocracy gives me an edge over everyone else."

He hadn't stopped experimenting. He tramped through Ireland with a fiddle, toured Scotland giving lectures on Palestine accompanied by magic lantern slides, worked as a billiard marker, and taught school. For the last three months, he'd been a journalist—a dramatic and musical critic for a Sunday paper. I had often dreamed of holding such a title.

"How did you get it?" I asked.

"It just happened," Dan replied. "One lazy afternoon, wandering down the Strand with nothing but a few shillings, I spotted a newspaper office. Luck was smiling on me. I walked up to the first floor, opened the first door without knocking, and wandered through an empty anteroom into a large, cluttered office. It was getting dark. A very young-looking man was pacing frantically, cursing himself for spilling ink, losing matches, and breaking the bell pull. Thinking he was the office boy, I decided to play a prank and pretend he was the editor. Turns out, he *was* the editor. He was a slim youth with the voice of a schoolboy, likely no older than twenty-six, who owed his job entirely to his aunt, the owner.

"He seemed generally aggrieved. 'People treat this place like a bus stop,' he complained. 'Strangers march right up, demanding articles on safety valves or just wanting to cause trouble. I spend half my day on the stairs.'

"I suggested he ask visitors to leave notes. He liked the idea. 'For the last forty-five minutes,' he groaned, 'I've been trying to finish one column, and I've been interrupted four times.'

"Just then, someone knocked. 'I won't see anyone!' he shouted. 'Send them all away!'

"I blocked the door for an elderly gentleman trying to rush past. I told him the editor wasn't available and handed him a notepad. I spent half an hour in that hallway sending away about a dozen people. None of them seemed important, otherwise, I'd have heard about it later. The last visitor was a tired man smoking a cigarette. When I asked his name, he looked at me strangely and said, 'Idiot!'

"I stood my ground. 'It's awkward,' he grumbled. 'Can't you make an exception for the sub-editor on press night?'

'I can't,' I said firmly.

'Fine,' he conceded. 'Just tell me who's going to the Royalty tonight. It's seven o'clock.'

"A spark ignited in my mind. If the sub-editor didn't know who was attending the premiere, the Dramatic Critic's chair must be empty.

'That's settled,' I lied smoothly. 'I'll be there in time.'

He didn't look happy or unhappy. 'Did you arrange it with the boss?'

'I'm just waiting to confirm with him,' I improvised. 'Got the ticket?'

'Haven't seen it.'

'Need a column?' I offered.

'Only three-quarters,' he muttered, and left.

As soon as he was gone, I grabbed a printer's boy. 'Who's the sub?' I asked, describing a tall man with a black mustache.

'Penton,' the boy said.

'Right, Penton,' I confirmed. 'Walk with me.'

I marched straight into the irritable editor. 'Ticket for the Royalty?' I demanded. 'Penton says you have it.'

'I don't know where it is,' he snarled.

I found it on his desk. 'Who's going?'

'Me,' I declared, and I left.

To this day, they haven't figured out I appointed myself. Penton thinks I'm related to the owner, so everyone respects me. Even Mrs. Wallace, the proprietor, believes Penton discovered me, and she gets along famously with me. The paper isn't thriving, and the pay is low, but it fits my style. Journalism is where I belong."

"You've been a bit of a rolling stone," I observed.

He laughed. "From the stone's perspective, I never saw the benefit of being covered in moss. I'd rather stay a stone—visible, mobile, and aware of my surroundings."

"Now, what are your plans?" he asked. "Your opera, which the gods have whimsically named 'Goggles,' seems destined to run through the winter. Any salary?"

"Thirty shillings a week, plus full pay for matinees," I said.

"That's two pounds," he calculated. "With my three, we could start our own life. I have an original idea. Want to collaborate?"

I enthusiastically agreed.

"There are four lovely rooms in Queen's Square," he continued. "Charming furniture, a grand front parlor, two bedrooms, and a kitchen. The previous tenant was a Polish revolutionary who foolishly returned to Russia three months ago and is now living rent-free. The landlord is an eccentric old engraver named Deleglise. He lives in the rest of the house. He's offering us the rooms for thirty shillings a week—normally worth three or four pounds—on one condition: we must manage the house ourselves. He's peculiar; he hates skirts, especially old ones. He employs only one servant, an ancient Frenchwoman who was his mother's housekeeper. The two of them argue constantly over chores, and absolutely no other females are allowed inside, not even a cleaner.

"On the upside, it's a magnificent Georgian townhouse with Adam-style mantelpieces, a stone staircase, and oak paneling. We'd have the entire second floor: no pianos, no landlady. He's a widower with a daughter around fourteen. What do you say? I'm a decent cook; will you be house-and-parlour-maid?"

I didn't hesitate. A week later, we moved in. For nearly two years, we thrived there. Despite the prevailing notion that men are incapable of domestic life without female guidance, we managed perfectly. Dan hadn't exaggerated. His cooking was sublime—better coffee, omelets, and kidneys than I'd ever tasted. If he stuck to basics, he was flawless. But occasionally, spurred by ambition or inspired by Landlord Deleglise's culinary hobbies, he ventured into experimentation.

Pastry was his undoing. His hand was too heavy, his temperament too serious. Lemon sponge, described in a fat cookbook as an airy white cloud, emerged from his kitchen looking like a miniature earthquake. It was spongy, yes, but required a spoon and fork to tear apart gently. His favorite dish, manna-cake, was so dense and crusty that it reminded me of the food the Children of Israel ate in the desert—an explanation for their constant complaining and longing for Egypt. One slice filled a person to bursting.

At dinner parties, he attempted soufflés (which guests insisted were puddings) and pancakes. Initially, his pancake-tossing was disastrous. I advised using a knife and fork; it was less flashy but guaranteed you wouldn't catch a half-cooked pancake on your head or drop it into the fireplace. Yet, "Thoroughness" was Dan's motto. He believed every particle of coal or stray hair could be meticulously removed by a careful eater.

Dan was an even-tempered man for twenty-three hours a day. The other hour was a mystery. Suddenly, he could be irrational, irritable, and ungrateful. Early on, I tried to advise him—explaining why the custard was lumpy or the mayonnaise smelled of hair oil.
