In the old house on Elm Street, the aroma of freshly baked scones wafted through the air, mingling with the scent of simmering soup on the stovetop. It was a typical Sunday morning, and the atmosphere was filled with the warmth of family and the gentle clinking of dishes being washed in the sink. My father, a stout man with a bushy beard, stood at the kitchen counter, expertly rolling out dough for the day's batch of bread. My mother, with her curly brown hair tied back in a neat bun, sat at the kitchen table, carefully chopping fresh herbs for the soup. They worked together in comfortable silence, their movements a testament to their long-standing partnership.

As I watched them, I couldn't help but notice the way they interacted with each other. My father would occasionally glance over at my mother and say, "Maggie, how's the soup coming along?" or "Maggie, can you pass the salt?" My mother would smile and reply, "It's almost done, dear," or "Here you are." Their conversation was laced with affection and ease, a far cry from the formalities I had grown accustomed to with my aunt.

Speaking of my aunt, Fanny, she was a different story altogether. Her entrance into any room was always preceded by a series of loud knocks on the door, followed by a low, throaty cough. It was a signal that she was about to make an appearance, and it never failed to startle me. She would enter backwards, closing the door behind her with a loud thud, and then turn around with a warm smile on her face. Despite her eccentricities, she was a kind soul, and I loved her dearly.

But my parents didn't seem to appreciate her efforts. In fact, the more sympathetic she became, the more irritated they seemed to grow. One day, my mother came up from the kitchen to find Fanny preparing to enter the drawing room by dropping teaspoons at five-second intervals outside the door. "Fanny, don't be ridiculous," my mother said, her voice tinged with annoyance. Fanny, however, remained calm and composed, replying, "Why shouldn't a pair of turtle doves bill and coo, when their united age is only a little over seventy, the pretty dears?"
