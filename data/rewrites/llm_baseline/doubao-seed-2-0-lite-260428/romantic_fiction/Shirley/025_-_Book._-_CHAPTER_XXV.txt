Not all who wage that divine, desperate battle to hold fast to the life of the one they love come out victorious. Night after night, dark beads of agony may burst on the supplicant’s forehead, their soul crying out to the Unseen in that soundless, burning plea no human ear can catch: Spare my beloved. Heal the life of my life. Rend not from me what long, deep affection has woven into every thread of my being. God of heaven, bend, hear, be clement! And after all that strife, that bitter wrestling in the dark, the sun may rise to find them worsted. That dawn which once greeted them with zephyrs soft as a whisper and skylarks caroling high overhead may carry on its very first breath the thin, weak voice of the one who is slipping away, all colour and heat fled from their lips: “Oh, I have suffered so through the night. This morning I am worse. I tried to rise, and I could not. Strange, heavy dreams have troubled me.” Then the watcher leans over the pillow, sees that new, unrecognizable moulding on the familiar beloved features, knows at once the unendurable moment draws nigh, that God’s will is to break the idol they have worshipped, and bows their head, forcing their soul to submit to a sentence they cannot avert, and can scarce bear to endure.

Happy, thrice happy Mrs. Pryor! She was still bowed in prayer, unaware the full summer sun hung bright above the distant hills, when her child stirred softly and woke in her arms. No piteous, half-unconscious moaning — that sound which drains the strength so thoroughly that even if you have sworn to be firm, a flood of unconquerable tears sweeps the oath clean away — preceded her waking. No long stretch of deaf apathy followed, the kind that tells you the sick one’s mind is already wandering halfway to realms the living cannot reach. The first words Caroline spoke were clear, bright, unclouded by any distance from the world around her. She remembered perfectly every moment of her illness.
“Mamma, I have slept so well. I only dreamed and woke twice.”

Mrs. Pryor started to her feet at once, that the girl might not see the flood of joyful tears that rushed to her eyes at that soft, affectionate word “mamma”, and the blessed, welcome assurance that followed it. For many days after, she dared rejoice only with trembling. That first flicker of Caroline’s revival looked no more steady than the flare of a dying lamp: one moment the flame would leap up bright, and the next sink dim and low in the socket. Exhaustion followed close on the heels of even the smallest excitement. Caroline always made a touching effort to *appear* better, but too often her strength refused to obey her will; too often the gentle attempt to sit up, to smile, to chat a little, ended in her sinking back pale and breathless. Many an hour Mrs. Pryor feared the chords of her daughter’s life would never strengthen again, that only the final snap was being slowly delayed.

All that while, the mother and daughter were almost entirely alone in their little corner of Briarfield. It was the end of August, the weather “fine” in name only — parched, thick with dust, for an arid east wind had blown steadily for a full month, sucking every drop of freshness from the air. The sky was cloudless, but a pale, dull haze hung stationary in the atmosphere, robbing the blue of all its depth, the green grass of all its dewy glow, the daylight of all its warmth. Nearly every family in the parish was away on an excursion: Miss Keeldar and her party were at the seaside, so were Mrs. Yorke and all her household. Mr. Hall and Louis Moore, who had struck up a quiet, spontaneous friendship built on shared views and similar temperaments, were off on a walking tour north to the Lake District. Even Hortense, who had longed to stay home and help Mrs. Pryor nurse Caroline, had been entreated so earnestly by Miss Mann to join her at Wormwood Wells, where the insalubrious east wind had aggravated her old ailments terribly, that she could not refuse — it was never in her nature to turn down a request that appealed to both her kind heart and her quiet pride at being needed. As for Robert Moore, he had travelled on from Birmingham to London, and was still detained there on business.

As long as that east wind, sharp and dry as the breath of Asiatic deserts, parched Caroline’s lips and fed the fever in her veins, her physical recovery could not keep pace with the new calm returning to her mind. But then came the day the wind stopped its sobbing against the rectory’s eastern gable, and the old church’s oriel window. A little cloud, no bigger than a man’s hand, rose over the western horizon. Gusts of soft, damp wind from that same quarter drove it forward, spread it wide over the sky, and rain and tempest swept through for a short, wild while. When it passed, the sun broke out warm and genial: the sky regained its deep, vivid azure, the earth its lush, dewy green, that livid, cholera-like tint was gone from the face of nature. The hills rose sharp and clear all around the horizon, freed from that pale, choking, malaria-like haze.

Caroline’s youth could work its magic now, aided by her mother’s tireless, tender nurture. Both, crowned by God’s blessing, and fanned by the soft, fresh west wind blowing steadily through the always-open chamber lattice, rekindled her long-languishing energies. At last Mrs. Pryor dared to let herself hope: a genuine, solid, unshaken convalescence had truly begun. It was not merely that Caroline’s smile was brighter, or her spirits lighter — a certain dread, indescribable look had left her face and eyes, that look anyone who has sat by the bed of a dangerously ill person recognizes at once. Long before the gaunt, emaciated lines of her cheeks began to fill out, or the lost colour returned to her lips, a subtler change had taken hold: every line of her face grew softer, warmer. No longer was there the awful impression of a marble mask and glassy, unseeing eyes. Mrs. Pryor looked down to see a face pale and wasted, perhaps even more haggard than that frightening stillness had been, but far less terrible: it was a living, sick girl, not a cold white mould or rigid piece of statuary.

Now, too, Caroline no longer pleaded constantly for water. The plaintive cry of “I am so thirsty” fell silent. Sometimes, after she had swallowed a small morsel of food, she would say it had truly revived her. No longer did every kind of food taste equally loathsome; she could be induced, at times, to name something she would fancy. With what trembling pleasure, what anxious, careful hands did her mother prepare that little chosen treat! How closely she watched her while she ate it!
Nourishment brought strength. Soon she could sit up unassisted. Then she began to long to breathe the open air, to revisit her flower beds, to see how the fruit on the old trees had ripened. Her uncle, always generous, had bought a wicker garden-chair made expressly for her use. He carried her down the stairs in his own arms, settled her comfortably in it himself, and William Farren the gardener was there to wheel her round the garden walks, show her all the work he had done among her plants, and take her directions for all the little tweaks she wanted made.

William and Caroline had no end of things to talk over, a dozen shared interests that mattered not a whit to anyone else in the world, but were endlessly fascinating to them both. They shared the same soft spot for every sort of animal, bird, and insect, the same gentle doctrine about kindness to all living creatures, the same knack for noticing the tiniest, most trivial details of natural history. The little burrow of ground bees they had found tunneling in the turf under the old cherry tree was one favourite topic; the hidden nest of hedge-sparrows, and the welfare of their pearly eggs and fuzzy, newly hatched fledglings, was another. If *Chambers's Journal* had existed in those days, it would certainly have been their favourite periodical: Caroline would have subscribed for it, lent every copy faithfully to William, and both of them would have believed every word of its marvellous anecdotes about animal sagacity, and found endless joy in reading them. That digression aside, it explains perfectly why Caroline would let no one but William wheel her chair around the garden, why his quiet, earnest conversation made every air hour in the grounds feel like an adventure.

Mrs. Pryor, walking a little way behind them, wondered gently how her daughter could be so unreservedly at ease with a “man of the people”. She herself could not bring herself to speak to him with anything but stiff, distant formality. It felt to her as if a great, uncrossable gulf lay between their stations in life, that to meet him halfway would be to degrade herself. She asked Caroline softly, “Are you not afraid, my dear, to converse with that person so openly? He may presume upon your kindness, and become tiresomely garrulous.”
“William presume, mamma? You do not know him. He never presumes. He is far too proud and far too sensitive to do such a thing. William has the finest, gentlest feelings of anyone I know.”
Mrs. Pryor smiled sceptically at that naïve claim, that a rough-handed, fustian-clad working man could have “fine feelings”. For his part, Farren only gave Mrs. Pryor a sullen, set brow. He knew when he was being misjudged, and had a stubborn streak that rose up against anyone who refused to give him the respect he was due.

The evenings gave Caroline entirely back to her mother, and Mrs. Pryor loved those hours best of all: alone with her daughter, no other living soul stood between her and the thing she loved most in the world. During the day, she fell back into the stiff, formal demeanour that was lifelong habit for her. Between her and Mr. Helstone, they kept up a relationship of perfect respect, but rigid, unyielding ceremony: any trace of familiarity would have bred contempt in one or both of them at once, but by clinging strictly to civility and maintaining a careful distance, they got along very smoothly. Towards the servants, too, Mrs. Pryor was never unkind, but shy, cool, ungenial. It was diffidence far more than pride that made her seem so haughty, of course, but Fanny and Eliza could not make that distinction, and so she was thoroughly unpopular with them. She noticed the effect she had, and it left her sometimes dissatisfied with herself for faults she could not help, dejected, quiet, and cold.

But that mood melted away the moment Caroline turned to her, and only Caroline. The soft, dependent fondness of her nursling, the natural, easy affection of her own child, wrapped around her like warm sunlight. Her frost fell away, her rigidity unbent, and she grew smiling, soft, pliant. Caroline never made flowery, wordy professions of love — that would have ill suited Mrs. Pryor, who would have read such over-the-top speeches as proof of insincerity — she just hung on her arm with easy dependence, confided in her with fearless, total trust. Those small, quiet acts of affection were enough to fill the mother’s heart to bursting. She loved to hear her daughter say, “Mamma, do this”; “Please, mamma, fetch me that”; “Mamma, read to me”; “Sing a little, mamma”. No one else — not a single living soul — had ever claimed her services so naturally, looked to her for help with such total certainty. Everyone else was always as reserved and stiff with her as she was with them; everyone else noticed her odd little weak points, and let their annoyance show. Caroline was no more sharp to her about those incurable, constitutional flaws now than she had been when she was a tiny three-month-old baby at her breast.

Yet Caroline could find fault, artlessly, about the small, acquired habits that could be changed. On those points, she would gently lecture her mother, and far from being hurt, Mrs. Pryor felt a quiet, glowing pleasure to discover that the girl dared tease her, that she was so completely at home with her.
“Mamma, I am determined you shall not wear that old gown any more. Its fashion is not becoming, it is far too tight in the skirt. You shall put on your black silk every afternoon. You look so nice in that, it suits you perfectly. And you shall have a black satin dress for Sundays — real, proper satin, not cheap satinet or any of the shoddy imitations. And mamma, when you get the new one, mind you wear it.”
“My dear, I had thought that black silk would serve me as my best dress for many years yet, and I had planned to spend that money buying you several new things.”
“Nonsense, mamma. My uncle gives me more than enough cash to get everything I want. You know how generous he is. And I have set my whole heart on seeing you in that black satin. Get it soon, and let it be made by the dressmaker I recommend. Let me choose the pattern. You always try to disguise yourself like a frumpy old grandmother. You would have everyone believe you are old and ugly. Not at all! On the contrary, when you are well dressed and cheerful you are very comely indeed; your smile is so pleasant, your teeth are so white, your hair is still that pretty soft light colour. And you speak like a young lady, with such a clear, fine tone, and you sing better than any young lady I ever heard. Why do you wear such dowdy dresses and bonnets, mamma, that no other lady in the parish would ever be seen in?”
“Does it annoy you, Caroline?”
“Very much. It even vexes me. People say you are miserly, and yet you are not at all, for you give so liberally to the poor and to all the religious societies — though your gifts are conveyed so secretly that very few people know about them except the recipients. But I will be your own lady’s maid myself. When I get a little stronger I will set to work, and you must be good, mamma, and do exactly as I bid you.”
And Caroline, sitting close beside her, rearranged her mother’s crumpled muslin handkerchief, and smoothed back her soft hair.
“My own mamma,” she went on, as if wrapping herself in the sweet thought of their bond, “who belongs to me, and to whom I belong! I am such a rich girl now. I have something I can love fully, and never be afraid of loving. Mamma, who gave you this little brooch? Let me unpin it and look at it.”

Mrs. Pryor, who usually shrank from meddling fingers and anyone getting too close, allowed the familiar touch complacently.
“Did papa give you this, mamma?”
“My sister gave it to me — my only sister, Cary. I do so wish your Aunt Caroline had lived to see her namesake, her niece!”
“Have you nothing of papa’s — no trinket, no little gift of his that you kept?”
“I have one thing.”
“That you prize very much?”
“That I prize more than anything else in the world.”
“Valuable and pretty?”
“Invaluable, and sweeter to me than any gem.”
“Show it me, mamma. Is it here, or back at Fieldhead?”
“It is talking to me now, leaning against me. Its arms are round my neck.”
“Ah, mamma! You mean your teasing daughter, who will never let you alone; who, the moment you step out of your room, runs to find you, who follows you upstairs and down like a little loyal dog.”
“Whose fair features still give me a strange, soft thrill sometimes. I half fear your bright looks yet, child.”
“You do not. You cannot. Mamma, I am so sorry papa was not good. I do wish he had been. Wickedness spoils and poisons every pleasant thing. It kills love. If you and I thought the other was wicked, we could not love each other, could we?”
“And if we could not trust each other, Cary?”
“How miserable we would be! Mother, before I met you, I was afraid that you were not good, that I would not be able to esteem you. That dread dampened every bit of my wish to see you. But now my heart is so light and happy, because I find you perfect — almost perfect; kind, clever, so nice. Your only fault is that you are old-fashioned, and I am going to cure you of that. Mamma, put your sewing down. Read to me. I love your southern accent, it is so pure, so soft. It has none of that rough burr, no nasal twang that almost everyone here in the north has. My uncle and Mr. Hall both say you are a wonderful reader, mamma. Mr. Hall said he never heard any lady read with such perfect propriety of expression and such pure, clear accent.”
“I wish I could return the compliment, Cary; but really, the first time I heard your very good friend Mr. Hall preach, I could not understand a single word of his broad northern tongue.”
“Could you understand me, mamma? Did I sound rough to you?”
“Not at all. I almost wished you had, almost wished you looked a little unpolished. Your father, Caroline, naturally spoke very well, quite differently from your worthy uncle — correctly, gently, smoothly. You inherited that gift from him.”
“Poor papa! When he could be so agreeable, why was he not good?”
“Why he was as he was — and happily, child, you cannot even begin to imagine that sort of nature — I cannot tell. It is a deep mystery. The key to it is in his Maker’s hands. There I leave it.”
“Mamma, you keep stitching, stitching away. Put the sewing down. I am an enemy to it right now. It cumbers your lap, and I want it for my head to rest on; it takes up all your eyes, and I want them looking at your book. Here is your favourite — Cowper.”

These gentle, playful importunities were the greatest pleasure Mrs. Pryor had ever known.
