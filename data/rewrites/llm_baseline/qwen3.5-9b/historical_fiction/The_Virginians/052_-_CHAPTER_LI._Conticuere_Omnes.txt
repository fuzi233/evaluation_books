If you, our gracious reader, will follow us across the way, you will find our young gentlemen at Lord Wrotham's residence—a house lent generously to the General—and a small gathering of the family party we previously encountered at Oakhurst and Tunbridge Wells. James Wolfe has pledged to attend dinner; however, James is currently attending exclusively to Miss Lowther, preferring a fleeting glance from her eyes to the most exquisite dishes prepared by Lord Wrotham's chef or the promised desserts awaiting your arrival. You shall occupy the sixth seat. You may take Mr. Wolfe's place, for you may be certain he will not come. As for me, I shall stand by the sideboard to chronicle the conversation.

First, observe the radiant countenance of the ladies. Recall when Harry Warrington was seized by the bailiffs; I had intended to recount how Mrs. Lambert, upon hearing of her son's plight, rushed to her husband. She implored, insisted, and beseeched that Martin assist them. "Forget his recent rebellion," she urged, "forget his anger regarding the returned gifts—anyone would be angry, especially a spirited lad like Harry! Forget our poverty and the need to save for the boys at college; we must secure his release. Remember how you extracted Charles Watkins from a similar predicament two years ago, and how he repaid every penny? Yes, and you made a whole family happy, blessed be God! Mrs. Watkins still prays for you and blesses you daily, and everything has prospered since. I have no doubt this act will make you a Major-General—there is no earthly doubt," the devoted wife declared.

Martin Lambert, requiring little persuasion to perform a kindness, readily agreed. Having resolved to locate friend James Wolfe and provide bail for Harry, he donned his hat, squeezed Theo's hand—who seemingly divined his purpose—or perhaps the sensible mother had inadvertently revealed it—and kissed little Hetty's flushed cheek before departing the room where the girls and their mother sat. Though he exited, he was immediately followed by the latter.

Once alone with him, the enthusiastic matron could no longer restrain her emotions. She threw her arms around her husband's neck, kissed him a hundred and twenty-five times in succession, invoked God's blessing, and wept profusely upon his shoulder. In this sentimental posture, she was discovered by Old Mrs. Quiggett, the lord's housekeeper, who bustled about the house and presumably stood astounded by the domestic spectacle.

"We have had a disagreement and are reconciling! Do not spread tales, Mrs. Quiggett!" the gentleman declared, strolling away.

"Well, I never!" exclaimed Mrs. Quiggett with a shrill, strident laugh reminiscent of a venerable old cockatoo—a white, hook-nosed, long-lived bird to which Mrs. Quiggett bore a striking resemblance. "Well, I never!" she laughed again, shaking her aged frame until her keys and, one might imagine, her old ribs clattered and jingled.

"Oh, Quiggett!" sobbed Mrs. Lambert, "what a man that is!"

"You've been quarrelling, have you, mum, and now making it up? That is correct."

"Quarrel with him? He never told a greater falsehood. My General is an angel, Quiggett. I wish to worship him. I wish to fall at his boots and kiss them! There has never been a man so good as my General. What have I done to deserve such a husband? How dare I possess such a good man?"

"My dear, I believe there is a match made in heaven between you two," replied the old cockatoo. "And what would you like for supper?"

When Lambert returned very late to the meal, announcing that Harry was free, that his brother had revived, and that he himself had been rescued, you can imagine the commotion among the household! If Mrs. Lambert's General was an angel in the morning, what further acts of humble devotion might she crave in the evening? Little Hetty nestled silently against her father, stealing a sip from his glass. Theo and his mother's faces beamed with happiness, like two bright moons. After supper, the four of them knelt at a specific signal, paying glad homage in joyful reverence, rejoicing with a purity akin to the angels described in scripture for the repentant sinner. Then, a loud knocking echoed at the door. Who could it be? My lord was miles away in the countryside. It was past midnight; they had talked so long and stayed up so late. Mrs. Lambert likely guessed the visitor.

"This is George," announced a young gentleman ushering in another. "We visited Aunt Bernstein. We could not retire to bed without thanking you as well. You dear, dear, good..." No further words were audible. Aunt Lambert embraced Harry; Theo snatched up Hetty, who was pale as death, and hugged her back to life. George Warrington removed his hat, and upon concluding Harry's greeting, approached to kiss Mrs. Lambert's hand; the General brushed his fingers across his eyes. I protest they were all in a state of profound tenderness and happiness. Generous hearts often feel this way when wrong is forgiven, when peace is restored, and when love returns, thought lost forever.

"We came from Aunt Bernstein's; we saw lights here, you see; we could not sleep without saying goodnight to you all," said Harry. "Could we not, George?"

"'Tis certainly a famous nightcap you have brought us, boys," remarked the General. "When are you to dine with us? Tomorrow?" No, they must visit Madame Bernstein's tomorrow.

Tomorrow, then? Yes, they would come the following day—which is precisely the day we are chronicling, and the very dinner at which, in lieu of Lieutenant-Colonel James Wolfe, absent on private business, my gracious reader has just been invited to sit.

But why sit? Not to partake of a mere phantom feast, no, no—but to listen to **MR. GEORGE ESMOND WARRINGTON'S STATEMENT**, which he is undoubtedly about to deliver. Thus, they all sat—not in my lord's grand dining room, but in the cozy study or parlor ahead. The cloth was withdrawn, the General had toasted the King, the servants had departed, and the guests sat in silence (*conticuerunt*). After a moment of hesitation and blushing, Mr. George proceeded:

"I recall, at the General's table, how the little Philadelphia agent, whose wit and shrewdness we noted at home, raised valid objections to the conduct of the campaign, the disastrous outcome proving their justice. 'Of course,' he said, 'your Excellency's troops, once before Fort Duquesne, will never be able to resist such a general, such an army, such artillery as will attack it. But do you calculate, sir, on the difficulty of reaching the place? Your Excellency's march will be through woods almost untrodden, over roads you must create yourselves, and your line will stretch some four miles long. This slender column, forced through the forest, will be subjected to endless attacks in front, rear, and flank by enemies unseen, whose constant practice is the dexterous laying of ambushes.'—'Psha, sir!' retorted the General, 'the savages may frighten your raw American militia' (Thank you, Excellency, for the compliment, Mr. Washington seems to imply, sitting at the table), 'but the Indians will never make an impression on his Majesty's regular troops.'—'I heartily hope not, sir,' sighed Mr. Franklin; and naturally, the gentlemen of the General's family sneered at the Postmaster as a pert civilian offering opinions beyond his comprehension.

"We despised the Indians on our own side, and our commander dismissed them lightly. Our officers disgraced the allied chiefs through outrageous behavior toward their women. Fewer than seven or eight remained with our force. Had we possessed a couple of hundred in our front on that fatal ninth of July, the outcome would have been vastly different. They would have repelled the French Indians, preventing the surprise and panic that ensued. It is now known that the French were prepared to surrender their fort, never dreaming of defense, and that the French Indians themselves protested the audacity of attacking such an overwhelming force.

"I was with our General commanding the main body when the firing commenced before us, and one aide-de-camp after another was dispatched forward. Initially, the enemy's assault was met briskly by our vanguard, and our men cheered with good spirits. But soon our fire slackened, while from behind every tree and bush surrounding us came single shots, felling man after man. We marched in orderly formation: skirmishers ahead, colors and two small guns in the center, and baggage well-guarded in the rear. We moved over ground open and clear for a mile or two, with a thick, tangled covert of brushwood and trees lining both sides. After brief frontal firing, the barrage opened from both sides of the encircling wood against our advancing column. Men dropped rapidly, officers falling in greater numbers than privates. At first, as mentioned, they cheered and returned fire, our guns even opening onto the wood, seemingly silencing the French ambush. Yet the hidden rifle fire resumed. Our men halted, huddled together despite the shouts and orders of the General and officers to advance, firing wildly into the brushwood—naturally achieving nothing. Those in the front ran back to the main body in terror, many wounded. They reported five thousand Frenchmen and a legion of screaming Indian demons ahead, scalping our people as they fell.
