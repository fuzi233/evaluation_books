Briarmains, being closer than the Hollow, was where Mr. Yorke took his young companion after the attack. He had seen him laid in the finest bed of the house, with care as tender as if he were one of his own sons. The sight of blood welling from the wound—inflicted so treacherously—had stirred something deep within him. To see that tall, proud figure fallen across the road, that fine southern face lying low in the dust, that strong youth suddenly pale and lifeless—it was a moment that could not fail to win the heart of any man of feeling, especially one like Mr. Yorke.

There was no one else to help, no other voice to speak gently, no other mind to plan what must be done. It fell entirely upon him. And in that moment, with a wounded stranger wholly dependent on his kindness, Mr. Yorke found himself in a position he deeply relished: power over another’s life. It suited him perfectly.

His wife, too, found satisfaction in the turn of events. Some women might have been overcome by fear at seeing a bleeding man carried into their home at nightfall, laid out in their hall like a fallen warrior. But Mrs. Yorke was not one of them. She was more likely to faint over a maid who refused to leave the garden or over her son’s dream of escaping to Australia. Yet here—a man nearly murdered at her doorstep, half-dead in her best bed—she stood firm, composed, even energized. It gave her a sense of purpose. She wore her cap like a turban, her spirit lifted, her authority confirmed.

Mrs. Yorke was the kind of woman who would torment a servant with petty rules while nursing plague victims like a heroine. She almost came to care for Moore. When she saw him placed under her protection—left helpless in her arms, as vulnerable as a newborn child—something in her hard nature softened. She would have struck any servant who dared offer him water or adjust his pillow. She drove Jessie and Rose from the upper rooms, forbade the maids to step near the sick chamber.

Had the accident occurred at the rectory gates, and old Helstone taken charge of the injured man, neither Yorke nor his wife would have shown a shred of pity. They would have called it justice served upon a tyrant. But because it happened at Briarmains, Moore became, for now, the center of their attention.

It was strange, then, that Louis Moore was allowed to come—allowed to sit beside the bed, lean over the pillow, hold his brother’s hand, press his lips to his pale forehead. Mrs. Yorke endured it, even welcomed it. She let him stay half the day; once, she permitted him to sit through the entire night. At dawn, on a wet November morning, she rose herself, lit the kitchen fire, made breakfast, and served it to the brothers with her own hands. Dressed in a wide flannel wrapper, a shawl, and her nightcap, she sat and watched them eat, as contentedly as a hen watches her chicks feed.

Yet she fired the cook for preparing a bowl of sago-gruel for Mr. Moore without her permission. And the housemaid lost favor when, as Louis left, she brought him his coat fresh from the kitchen, helped him into it, and received a smile, a “Thank you, my girl,” and a shilling. Two ladies came calling, pale and anxious, begging just one glimpse of Moore. Mrs. Yorke turned them away—harshly, with no hesitation.

But when Hortense Moore arrived, things were different. Not as bad as they might have been. In fact, the whole Moore family seemed to fit Mrs. Yorke’s temperament better than any others ever had. She and Hortense shared an endless supply of topics—especially about servants. Their views were alike: suspicious, severe, watchful. Hortense never showed jealousy over her sister-in-law’s care of Robert. She let Mrs. Yorke take charge, while she busied herself inspecting the kitchen, reporting back, and generally proving useful. Together, they agreed on one thing: no visitors were to enter the sickroom. They held Moore captive, shielding him from the world.

Mr. MacTurk, the surgeon assigned to Moore’s case, declared the wound dangerous but not beyond hope. At first, he wanted to bring his own nurse. But neither Mrs. Yorke nor Hortense would allow it. They promised strict obedience. So Moore remained under their care—for a time.

They did their best, but something went wrong. The bandages were disturbed, perhaps tampered with. A great loss of blood followed. MacTurk, summoned urgently, arrived on horseback, breathless and furious. He was one of those surgeons whose temper could turn deadly—sharp even in calm moments, brutal when provoked. Seeing Moore’s condition, he vented his fury in colorful language, none of which need be repeated here. A few choice words landed on Mr. Graves, his stony assistant; another bouquet fell on his son, a miniature version of himself. But the full basket of insults was reserved for women—especially meddling women.

For most of that winter night, MacTurk and his men worked around Moore’s bedside, alone in the room. They wrestled with death, three men on one side of the bed, death on the other. The struggle was fierce, lasting until dawn, when the balance seemed so even that either side might claim victory.

At sunrise, Graves and young MacTurk stayed behind to watch over Moore, while the senior surgeon rode out to find reinforcements. He returned with Mrs. Horsfall—the finest nurse on his staff. To her, he handed Moore with solemn warning: she was responsible for his life. She accepted the duty without protest, and took her seat in the easy chair at the foot of the bed. From that moment, she ruled.

Mrs. Horsfall had one virtue: she obeyed MacTurk’s orders without question. His word was law to her. In every other way, she was formidable. Hortense retreated. Mrs. Yorke withdrew—overwhelmed. These two women, each of some standing in their own right, were reduced to silence before her presence. She moved freely between floors, smoked four times a day, drank gin three times, and claimed the room as her own.

No one dared ask after Moore now. Mrs. Horsfall had him under strict control. She was the one who cared for him—and the general belief was that she did so thoroughly.

MacTurk visited twice daily. Moore’s case, complicated by this new crisis, had become a challenge. He saw him as a broken clockwork mechanism—something that, if repaired, would reflect credit on his skill. Graves and young MacTurk, meanwhile, viewed Moore much as they viewed the body in the dissecting room at Stilbro’ Infirmary.

Robert Moore passed the long, cold days of November in pain, weak, unable to move or speak clearly. His keeper was a giantess, his only company the surgeons. He lay through the shrinking daylight and growing darkness, trapped in silence and stillness.

At first, Moore resisted Mrs. Horsfall. He loathed her size, feared her touch. But she taught him submission quickly. She ignored his height, his strength, and turned him in bed as easily as a mother turns a child. When he behaved well, she called him “my dear” or “honey.” When he resisted, she shook him. If he tried to speak during MacTurk’s visits, she raised a hand and said, “Hush!” like a nurse silencing a disobedient child. She smoked and drank—things Moore disapproved of—but MacTurk dismissed it: “Pooh! They all do. But Horsfall has one merit: drunk or sober, she always obeys me.”

* * * * *

Autumn faded. The fogs and rains lifted from England, carrying their sorrow elsewhere. Winter settled in—clear, still, and icy.

A quiet evening deepened into crystal clarity. The world wore colors of the North Pole: white, violet, pale green. Hills glowed lilac-blue; the sunset bled purple into red; the sky shimmered silver-azure. Stars rose like white crystals, not gold. The landscape was cool, pure, transparent—gray, cerulean, emerald—still and serene.

In a wood stripped bare of green, of russet, of warmth—what is that dark blue shape moving? A schoolboy. A boy from Briarfield grammar school, who has left his friends walking the highroad and wandered off to find a certain tree, its roots crowned with moss—a good place to sit. Why does he linger? The air is cold, the hour late. He sits down. What is he thinking?

Does he feel the beauty of the night? A pearl-white moon smiles through the gray branches. Does he notice?

Impossible to say. He is silent. His face shows nothing. Not yet a mirror of emotion, but a mask. He is fifteen—slender, tall for his age. His expression holds no softness, no deference. His eyes watch, ready to detect any attempt to dominate him. His features suggest alertness, readiness to resist. Wise teachers avoid him. Punishment would break him in vain; flattery would only insult. Best to leave him be. Time and experience will shape him.

Martin Yorke—yes, a young Yorke—does not believe in poetry. Speak of sentiment, and he answers with irony. Yet here he is, wandering alone, waiting patiently as nature unfolds a page of stark, silent poetry before him.

He pulls a book from his satchel—not Latin grammar, but a forbidden volume of fairy tales. There’s still light enough for an hour. Besides, the moon waits for him; her dim beam fills the glade.

He reads. He enters a wild mountain region—barren, colorless, desolate. Bells tinkle on the wind. Through the mist rides a vision: a lady in green robes, astride a snow-white palfrey. He sees her jewels, her dress, her steed. She stops him with a mysterious question. He is spellbound, drawn into fairyland.

Another tale carries him to the sea. Waves crash against dizzy cliffs. Rain falls, wind howls. Rocks stretch far into the sea, black and jagged. Spray flashes and leaps, swirling in foam. A lone wanderer walks the rocks, stepping carefully over seaweed, peering into hollows where the water is deep and emerald-clear—where stranger plants grow, stranger shells cluster—green, purple, pearly, coiled like snakes. Then he hears a cry. He looks up. At the edge of the reef stands a tall, pale figure—like a man, but made of spray—transparent, trembling, terrifying. Others rise from the waves—foam-women, Nereids, white and fleeting.

Hush! Close the book. Hide it. Martin hears footsteps. He listens. No—yes. Again, leaves rustle underfoot. He watches. The trees part, and a woman emerges.

She wears dark silk, her face veiled. Martin has never seen a lady in this wood before—only village girls gathering nuts. Tonight, the sight does not unsettle him. As she draws near, he sees she is not old or plain, but young, even beautiful—though he recognizes her immediately as Caroline Helstone, whom he has often mocked as ugly.

She passes him without speaking. He knew she would. All women are proud monkeys, he thinks—and none prouder than Caroline. But the thought barely forms when she steps back, lifts her veil, and looks at him.

“Are you one of Mr. Yorke’s sons?” she asks softly.

No human proof could ever convince Martin he blushed—yet he did, crimson to the ears.

“I am,” he replies bluntly, daring himself to wonder what comes next.

“You’re Martin, I think?” she says.

Perfect. Simple, artless, slightly hesitant—just right. It matched his nature. It silenced him, like a note of music.

Martin valued his identity. It pleased him that she distinguished him from his brothers. Like his father, he despised ceremony. To be addressed as “Martin”—not “Mr. Martin” or “Master Martin”—was respectful. Worse than formality was careless familiarity. Her slight hesitation, her bashfulness, felt natural.

“I am Martin,” he said.

“Are your father and mother well? And Rose and Jessie?”

“I suppose so.”

“My cousin Hortense is still at Briarmains?”

“Oh yes.”

Martin gives a half-smile, half-groan. The smile is returned. She knows exactly how Hortense is regarded in the Yorke household.

“Does your mother like her?”

“They suit so well about servants they can’t help liking each other.”

“It’s cold tonight.”

“Why are you out so late?”

“I lost my way in this wood.”

Martin lets out a laugh of scorn.

“Lost your way in the mighty forest of Briarmains? You deserve never to find it again.”

“I’ve never been here before. I may be trespassing. You could report me, Martin. Have me fined. It’s your father’s wood.”

“I should think I know that. But since you’re so simple as to lose your way, I’ll guide you out.”

“You don’t need to. I’m on the path now. I’ll be fine. Martin…” (a little quickly), “how is Mr. Moore?”

Martin had heard rumors. He decides to test her.

“Going to die. Nothing can save him. All hope gone!”

She removes her veil. She looks into his eyes.

“To die?”

“To die. All because of the women—my mother and the rest. They messed with his bandages. That finished everything. He’d have recovered if not for them. They should be arrested, jailed, sent to Botany Bay!”

She doesn’t respond. After two minutes, she moves forward—no goodnight, no question. Not amusing. Not what he expected. He hoped for drama, emotion. It wasn’t worth frightening her if she wouldn’t react.

“Miss Helstone!”

She doesn’t hear or turn. He hurries after her.

“Are you uneasy about what I said?”

“You know nothing of death, Martin. You’re too young for me to talk about such things.”

“Did you believe me? It’s all nonsense! Moore eats like three men. They make sago, tapioca—always something good. I’ve seen the saucepans boiling in the kitchen. I think I’ll play the old soldier and live off the fat of the land like him.”

“Martin! Martin!” Her voice trembles. She stops.

“It’s terribly wrong of you. You’ve nearly killed me.”

Again, she stops. Leans against a tree, trembling, pale as death.

Martin stares, fascinated. On one level, it was “nuts” to see—revealing so much. He was beginning to enjoy uncovering secrets. On another, it reminded him of hearing a blackbird mourn after Matthew crushed her nestlings. Not a pleasant memory.

He couldn’t think of anything to say to comfort her. Instead, he smiled. The smile transformed his face.

“Eureka!” he cries. “I’ll fix everything soon. You’re better now, Miss Caroline. Walk on.”

He guides her by a shortcut—without thinking how hard it might be for her to climb walls or cross hedges. He helps her over obstacles, grumbling at her weakness, yet enjoying the chance to be useful.

“Martin, before we part, tell me seriously—on your word of honor—that Mr. Moore is better.”

“How much you care about that Moore!”

“No—but—friends may ask. I want to give an honest answer.”

“You may tell them he’s fine—just idle. He eats mutton chops for dinner, arrowroot for supper. I stole half a basin myself once.”

“And who nurses him? Who tends to him?”

“Nurses him? The great baby! A woman as big as our largest water-butt—rough, hard-faced. I suspect she treats him badly. No one else is allowed near. He’s mostly in the dark. I sometimes listen through the wall. I think I hear her hitting him. You should see her fist. She could hold six of your hands in one palm. Still, despite the food, I wouldn’t trade places with him. Honestly, I think she eats most of what goes up to him. I hope she’s not starving him.”

Silence. Deep thought from Caroline. Watchful curiosity from Martin.

“You never see him, I suppose?”

“I? No. I don’t care to.”

Silence again.

“Didn’t you visit our house with Mrs. Pryor about five weeks ago, asking after him?”

“Yes.”

“I bet you wanted to see upstairs?”

“We did. We begged. But your mother refused.”

“Ay. She refused. I heard it all. She treated you like she treats guests sometimes—rude, harsh.”

“She wasn’t kind. You know, Martin, we’re related. It’s natural we’d care.”

“But here we part. We’re at your gate.”

“So? I’ll walk home with you.”

“They’ll miss you.”

“Let them. I can take care of myself.”

Martin knew he’d earned a lecture and dry bread for tea. But it didn’t matter. The evening had been an adventure. Better than muffins and toast.

He walked home with Caroline. On the way, he promised to see Moore—despite the dragon guarding him—and arranged to meet her the next day in the woods, at a certain tree. The plan led nowhere. But he liked it.

When he reached home, the punishment was delivered: dry bread, a lecture, early bed. He accepted it with grim stoicism.

Before going to his room, he slipped into the dining hall—a cold, quiet, seldom-used room. He stood before the mantelpiece, holding his candle to two portraits: two women’s faces. One, serene, innocent, beautiful. The other, more beautiful, but sorrowful, desperate.

“She looked like that,” he murmured, gazing at the second portrait, “when she wept, turned white, leaned against the tree.”
