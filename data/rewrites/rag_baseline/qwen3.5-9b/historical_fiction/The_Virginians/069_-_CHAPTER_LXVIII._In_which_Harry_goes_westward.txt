The narrative arc of departure is usually reserved for the tragic, yet here, amidst the weight of empire and the fragility of life, it unfolds with a quiet, almost mechanical inevitability. Harry Warrington leaves. The text refuses to dwell on his internal landscape; instead, it maps the geography of absence. Thousands of men stand in the same position, suspended between the solid earth of home and the fluid uncertainty of the Atlantic. Mr. Wolfe carries the burden of a mother's kiss and a sweetheart's embrace, while Admiral Holmes, commanding a flotilla of iron-hulled ships—the Somerset, the Terrible, the Northumberland—must navigate the emotional turbulence of bidding farewell to Mrs. Holmes and her daughters. Even Admiral Saunders, sailing the following day, is not immune to the gravity of separation. The visual of the ship shrinking into the sunset is not a romantic trope but a mathematical reduction of presence until the vessel becomes a speck, then a thought, then a silence.

George accompanies his brother to Portsmouth, witnessing the final ritual: the clash of bells, the roar of cannon, the violent punctuation of departure. Once back in Dean Street, George retreats into the domestic sphere, a realm defined by routine and the absorption of trivialities. He observes the empty room where Harry slept, noting the half-burned papers in the grate—a residue of interrupted labor. This moment of stillness is quickly overwritten by the arrival of supper and the family unit, shifting the focus from the epic scale of naval warfare to the intimate theater of dinner conversation. The news of the fleet's destination circulates effortlessly; the strategic assessment is clear: Wolfe possesses the soul necessary to correct the blunders of past campaigns, despite his frail body.

Time moves forward with a rhythmic indifference. Days dissolve into weeks, marked by the turning of pages and the resumption of work. George, the dramatist, finds solace in the fickleness of the theatrical world. The success of *Carpezan* brings him a steady income from Dodsley and the theatre managers, allowing him to pivot to new compositions with philosophical detachment. He accepts the transience of fame, watching as other geniuses seize the stage. His correspondence reveals a mind sharp enough to dissect the political failures of Lord George Sackville and the arrogant constancy of the Duke, finding a perverse admiration in the losers of history. He critiques the pomposity of figures like Mr. P----, preferring the grounded manner of Barry, and engages in the mundane logistics of costume design for *Pocahontas*, debating whether the titular character should bear tattoos or the refined attire of Hampton Court.

Yet, beneath these observations of professional and political maneuvering lies a current of personal vulnerability. George writes to his brother of Cousin Maria, a figure who haunts the galleries of the playhouse, sparking a chain of protective instincts and suppressed confessions. He burns a letter, then whispers its contents, caught between the desire to protect and the compulsion to reveal. This leads to a reflection on their upbringing in Virginia, contrasting the dullness of their childhood with the moral clarity they retained compared to Harry, who was stripped by highwaymen. George projects a future of quiet domesticity—a library, a gentle companion, a small estate—against the backdrop of Harry's destined military glory. He fears this peace, fearing its elusiveness, yet feels no ambition for the sword of Mars.

Finally, the letter turns to the arrival of Miss Lydia, a young woman of seventeen who commands the room with an ease that belies her age. She treats the older generation as peers, discussing politics and fashion with a confidence that suggests she belongs in Mayfair rather than Monument Yard. George's description of her is a cascade of adoration, a torrent of poetic rapture that he ultimately decides to withhold from the reader, citing respect for privacy and memory. In doing so, he acknowledges the universal nature of youthful infatuation, inviting the reader to recall their own lost springs, while gently dismissing the current generation's need to linger on such follies. The narrative concludes not with a resolution, but with the natural cycle of seasons, leaving the characters adrift in the flow of time, their lives continuing regardless of the distance separating them.
