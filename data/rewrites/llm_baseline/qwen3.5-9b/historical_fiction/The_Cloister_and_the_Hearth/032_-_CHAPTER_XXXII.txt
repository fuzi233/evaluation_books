In the refectory, conversation at the table where Gerard sat drifted toward the sudden demise of the monk who had volunteered to transcribe fresh copies of the monastery's charter and rules. Seizing the opportunity, Gerard timidly proffered his services. A pause ensued, which he misinterpreted as rejection.

"Nay, not for hire, my lords, but for love," Gerard hastened to explain, "and as a humble token of gratitude for the many comfortable nights the brethren of your order have granted to a poor wayfarer."

One monk smiled with approval but gently suggested that the deceased brother was a master of the pen, implying that such delicate work required a peer to continue it. Undeterred, and with a tremor in his hand, Gerard withdrew a vellum deed from his wallet. He had carefully cleaned its reverse side and inscribed it as a specimen.

Upon seeing the document, the monk jumped slightly, then hurried up the hall to the high table. Bending his knee until it merely grazed the fifth and tenth steps, he presented the deed to the Prior, offering enthusiastic commentary. Instantly, a dozen discerning eyes locked onto the page, and a murmur rippled through the assembly. Soon, Gerard watched the Prior point repeatedly, while the monk returned, beaming with pride, bearing a savory *crustade royale*—a game pie spiced and gravy-drenched—for Gerard, alongside a silver grace cup brimming with rich pimentum.

Gerard accepted the cup. Bowing low first to the distant Prior, then to his fellow diners, he drained it and passed it around. Immediately, to his astonishment, the entire table hailed him as one of their own. "Are you not convent-bred? Deny it not!"

He affirmed it, giving thanks to Heaven, for without this revelation, he felt as uncouth and ignorant as his companions, Sybrandt and Cornelis.

"But 'tis passing strange how you could know," one monk remarked.

"You drank with the cup in both hands," two others chimed in simultaneously.

For some time, voices had grown raucous around a table at the far end of the hall, but now erupted into a burst of boisterous laughter so prolonged and loud that the Prior dispatched the Sub-Prior down the aisle to quell the disturbance and assign penance to the offenders. Gerard's face burned with shame; amidst the chaotic revelry, his ear had caught the word "courage" and the distinctive trumpet tones of Denys of Burgundy.

Soon after, Gerard was settled in Feu Werter's cell, illuminated by wax lights, equipped with a tilting writing frame, and supplied with all necessary calligraphic materials. Yet, the task proved too arduous for a single evening. The question arose: how could he ask Denys, the self-proclaimed monk-hater, to remain longer? He posed the query, submitting to whatever decision Denys chose. To his delight, Denys replied graciously, "A day's rest will harm neither of us. Write thy script, and I shall pass the time as best I may."

Gerard's work garnered immense admiration; they concurred that the monastery's records had suffered greatly under the late Brother Werter's stewardship. The Sub-Prior pressed a rix-dollar into Gerard's hand along with several brushes and pigments from the convent's ample stores. Warm-hearted and buoyed by this auspicious sign—that the pen might indeed secure his fortune and help him win back his beloved—Gerard resumed his journey.

"Come, Denys," he said cheerfully, displaying his gifts, "see what the good monks have bestowed upon me. Now, strive to be fairer to them; for, to speak plainly, hearing you call my benefactors 'hypocrites' chilled my friendship for a fleeting moment."

"I recant," Denys declared instantly.

"Thank you! Thank you! Good Denys!" Gerard exclaimed.

"I was a scurrilous vagabond," Denys confessed.

"Nay, nay, say not so!" Gerard protested.

"But we soldiers are rough and hasty. I condemn myself, and I offer my highest esteem to all whom I misunderstood. 'Tis unjust that thousands should be defamed for the hypocrisy of a few."

"Now you reason wisely," Gerard noted. "Have you pondered my words?"

"Nay, it was their own doing," Denys shrugged.

Gerard crowed inwardly, for everyone enjoys being vindicated, and listened intently as Denys recounted the tale of his conversion.

"Well then," Denys began, "at dinner on the first day, a young monk beside me opened his jaws and laughed aloud, most musically. 'Good,' I thought, 'at last I have fallen upon a man and not a shorn ape.' To test him further, I slapped his broad back and delivered my customary salute. 'Heaven forbid!' he cried. I stared. For the dog looked as sorrowful as Solomon; a better mimic you never saw, even at a mystery play. 'I see war is no sharpener of the wits,' he said. 'What are the clergy for but to fight the foul fiend? And what else are monks for? When the fiend is dead, the friars are sped. You may plough up the convents, and we poor monks shall have nothing to do—but turn soldiers, and so bring him to life again.' Then there was a great laugh at my expense. 'Well, you are the monk for me,' I said. 'And you are the crossbowman for me,' he replied. 'And I'll be bound you could tell us tales of the war that would make our hair stand on end.' 'Excusez-moi,' I retorted, 'the barber has put that out of the question,' and then I had the laugh."

"What wretched ribaldry!" Gerard observed pensively.

The candid Denys immediately admitted he had witnessed merrier jests born of less cacophony. "'Twas a great matter to have dispelled hypocrisy," he continued. "'So,' I said, 'I can grant you the chair of the rooster, if that pleases you.' 'That we shall see,' was the cry, and a signal went round."

Then Denys burst forth with glee, recounting how at bedtime he was ushered not to the great dormitory but into a private cell, strictly forbidden to sleep. To aid his vigil, a book of pictures depicting a hundred merry adventures of monks pursuing female laity was lent him. Shortly thereafter, he was led barefoot down to the parlour, where a supper fit for a duke awaited, attended by twelve jovial friars—the roughest, rowdiest boys he had ever encountered in peace or war.

How the stories flowed, how toasts were raised, how jests were exchanged, and how wine cups circled! Some played cards with a gorgeous deck where Saint Theresa and Saint Catherine, adorned in gold, served as the four queens, while black, white, grey, and crutch-bearing friars acted as the four knaves. They staked their very rosaries, swearing like troopers when they lost. About midnight, a sly monk slipped out, only to be cunningly followed by others into the garden, where he was seen thrusting his hand into the ivy and pulling out a rope ladder. With this, he scaled the ten-foot wall; though broad, it yielded quickly, and a russet kirtle appeared from the outside world as fast as he. Thus began a scene of billing and cooing that struck Denys as more feline than ecclesiastical, drawing from him the apt comment of a "mew!"

The monks joined the musical chorus, and the lay visitor shrieked in discomfort, but Abelard merely cried, "What, are ye there, ye jealous miauling knaves? Ye shall caterwaul to some tune tomorrow night. I'll fit every man-jack of ye with a fardingale!"

This brutal threat convinced him to stay another day—at Gerard's request. Gerard groaned internally. Meanwhile, unable to disconcert such a brazen monk, and the damsel beginning to whimper, they danced a caterwauling circle before bestowing a solemn benediction on the two "wall-flowers," then retired to the parlour. There they found a pair lying dead drunk and two others weeping affectionately. They promptly carried off the unconscious ones, dragged the tearful ones kicking and screaming into their respective cells, and left them there in "measureless content."

Gerard was disgusted and voiced his displeasure. Denys chuckled and proceeded to describe the following day's exploits: how he and the young monks had drained the fish ponds, secreting pike, carp, tench, and eels for their own use. Then, in the dead of night, he was taken shoeless by crooked paths into the chapel—a ghostly, dark place—and down steps into a crypt beneath the floor, where suddenly paradise burst upon him.

"'Tis there the holy fathers retire to pray," Gerard interjected.

"Not always," Denys corrected. "Dozens of wax candles were lit, and princely cheer awaited. Fifteen soups *maigre*, with marvelous flavors of venison, grouse, and hare, plus twenty different kinds of fish (for it was Friday), cooked with wondrous art. Each dish was flanked by two buxom lasses, and each lass by two lads in cowls—all except me. To think I had to woo by interpreter! I doubt the intermediary spoke three words for himself and one for me; if he didn't, hang him for a fool
