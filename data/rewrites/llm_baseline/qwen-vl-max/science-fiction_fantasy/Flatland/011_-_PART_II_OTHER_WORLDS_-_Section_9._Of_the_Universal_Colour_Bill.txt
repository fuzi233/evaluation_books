By the time the new era of chromatic revolution dawned, the intellectual foundations of Flatland had begun to crumble. The once-honored art of Sight Recognition—once essential for distinguishing shapes and social ranks—had fallen into disuse. With color now the sole identifier, the need to discern angles by sight vanished, and so too did the discipline it required. Geometry, Statics, Kinetics, and all related sciences were soon dismissed as outdated luxuries, even at our most prestigious University. In elementary schools, the tactile method of Feeling, once taught to every child, was abandoned as obsolete.

The Isosceles class, long oppressed and marginalized, seized this moment. They declared that the old system of using Criminal specimens for educational purposes was no longer necessary—and refused to pay the customary tribute. Freed from this burden, which had once tempered their aggression and controlled their numbers, they grew bolder and more numerous, their confidence swelling with each passing year.

Meanwhile, Soldiers and Artisans began to assert their equality with the highest Polygons. Now equipped with color-based recognition, they could navigate life’s challenges just as well as any noble. Why, then, should they be denied the same rights? They demanded not only the end of aristocratic privileges but the abolition of all state-funded education in Sight Recognition, Mathematics, and Feeling—calling these “monopolizing arts” the tools of an unjust elite.

Their movement gained momentum. If color, a gift of nature itself, had erased the need for hierarchy, why should law uphold it? They insisted that all citizens—regardless of shape or class—be treated as equals under the new order. And when the higher classes hesitated, the revolutionaries escalated their demands: everyone, including Priests and Women, must be painted.

When objections arose—particularly that Priests and Women had no sides—the leaders retorted that nature dictated distinction: every being should have a front half (containing eye and mouth) distinguishable from the back. Thus, they proposed a sweeping reform before a grand assembly of all Flatland states: every Woman would be painted red on her front half and green on her rear. Priests, similarly, would bear red on the semicircle containing their eye and mouth, and green on the opposite side.

This proposal, though seemingly simple, was masterfully designed—not by any Isosceles, but by an Irregular Circle spared from childhood destruction due to misplaced mercy. This figure, possessing both cunning and ambition, saw in color not just a tool of identity, but a weapon of subversion.

For one thing, the plan would win over women across all classes. By assigning them the same colors as Priests, the reform ensured that under certain viewing angles, a Woman might appear indistinguishable from a Priest. The prospect of being mistaken for a revered spiritual leader—of commanding respect, hearing secrets, even issuing orders in a Circle’s name—was irresistible to many women. At home, they would eavesdrop on private councils; in public, they would walk with the dignity of nobility, drawing deference from strangers who mistook them for Circles.

Let me clarify this danger for those who may doubt it. Imagine a woman painted as prescribed: red in front, green behind. Viewed from the side, she appears as a straight line, half red, half green. Now consider a Priest, whose front semicircle is red and back green, divided by diameter AB. When viewed along that line, his outline forms a straight segment—half red, half green—identical in color pattern to the woman’s. Though shorter and tapering more sharply, the visual impression would be nearly the same. Especially given the fading skill of Sight Recognition, and the likelihood that women would learn to mimic the rounded edges of Circles, confusion would be inevitable.

And so, the Color Bill threatened to blur the sacred boundaries between genders and classes. A young Woman might pass as a Priest, gaining influence she had never before possessed. Meanwhile, the Circles—already weakened by the decline of intellectual rigor—would suffer disgrace if the reckless behavior of women were attributed to them. The very foundation of the Constitution would tremble.

Yet even within noble households, women supported the bill. For them, it promised power and prestige.

But the true aim of the bill went deeper. It sought not merely to elevate women, but to dismantle the intellectual superiority of the Circular class. While society decayed, the Circles remained untouched—raised in homes without color, trained in pure Sight Recognition from infancy. Their minds stayed sharp, their judgment clear. They alone preserved the ancient wisdom.

Now, the Irregular Circle schemer sought to destroy that advantage. By forcing Circles to adopt color, he aimed to pollute their purity, corrupt their training, and weaken their intellect. Once stained by chromatic patterns, father and son, mother and child would lose clarity in perception. The infant Circle would struggle to tell parent from parent—a task once sharpening its mind, now clouded by deception. Mothers might feign identity, undermining trust and logic itself.

Thus, step by step, the brilliance of the Priestly Order would fade. And when the mind of the elite dimmed, the way would be open for total revolution. The Aristocracy would fall. The Privileged Classes would vanish. And Flatland, once ruled by reason and form, would descend into chaos—guided not by vision, but by hue.
