Sometimes the future whispers a low, ominous warning of the tragedies it brings, like a distant storm gathering on the horizon. Its tones carry the threat of a blast that will scatter wreckage across the sea; its colors hint at a fog carrying the yellow stain of pestilence, dimming the windows of English homes with the breath of Eastern plagues. Other times, the future strikes without warning, as if a rock has split open to reveal a fresh grave, releasing a sleeping corpse into the world. Before you realize it, you stand face-to-face with a calamity you never anticipated—a new Lazarus.

Caroline Helstone returned from Hollow's Cottage believing herself in good health. Yet, when she woke the next morning, she was overcome by an unfamiliar lethargy. By breakfast, and at every meal throughout the following day, she lost all desire to eat. Food tasted only like ash and sawdust.

"Am I ill?" she asked, staring into the mirror. Her eyes were bright, her pupils wide, her cheeks flushed and fuller than usual. "I look well; why can I not eat?"

A rapid pulse hammered in her temples, and her mind raced with strange, frenetic activity. Her spirits were high, filled with hundreds of brilliant, fragmented thoughts that cast a warm glow over her countenance.

What followed was a hot, parched, restless night. Toward dawn, a terrifying dream seized her like a tiger. When she awoke, she knew with certainty that she was sick.

She could not recall exactly how she contracted the fever—though it was clearly a fever. Perhaps, during her late walk home, a sweet, poisoned breeze, scented with honeydew and miasma, had drifted into her lungs and veins. Finding there a pre-existing fever of mental excitement and the lingering fatigue of long conflict and habitual sadness, that breeze had fanned a mere spark into a roaring fire.

Yet, initially, it seemed a gentle blaze. After two scorching days and anxious nights, the symptoms showed no violence. Neither her uncle, Fanny, the doctor, nor Miss Keeldar, who visited her, harbored any fear. Everyone believed she would recover in a few days.

Those few days passed, and although recovery was expected soon, it did not begin. Mrs. Pryor, who had visited daily, entered her chamber one morning when Caroline had been ill for two weeks. She observed her closely for several minutes, taking her pulse, before quietly leaving to go to Mr. Helstone's study. She remained there, locked away, for nearly half the morning.

When she returned to her sick young friend, she removed her shawl and bonnet. Standing by the bedside, she rocked gently, a motion habitual to her. Finally, she spoke.

"I have sent Fanny to Fieldhead to fetch a few things I shall need during a short stay here. It is my wish to remain with you until you are better. Your uncle kindly permits my attendance. Will it be acceptable to you, Caroline?"

"I am sorry you should take such needless trouble," Caroline replied. "I do not feel very ill, but I cannot refuse. It will be such comfort to know you are in the house, to see you sometimes in the room. But please, do not confine yourself on my account, dear Mrs. Pryor. Fanny nurses me very well."

Bending over the pale sufferer, Mrs. Pryor smoothed the hair beneath her cap and adjusted her pillow. As she worked, Caroline smiled and lifted her face to kiss her.

"Are you free from pain? Are you tolerably at ease?" Mrs. Pryor asked in a low, earnest voice.

"I think I am almost happy," Caroline answered.

"You wish to drink? Your lips are parched."

She offered a glass of cool liquid.

"Have you eaten anything today, Caroline?"

"I cannot eat."

"But soon your appetite will return; it *must* return—that is, I pray God it may."

Laying her back onto the couch, Mrs. Pryor encircled her in her arms. In a movement that seemed barely voluntary, she drew Caroline close to her heart, holding her tightly for an instant.

"I shall hardly wish to get well," Caroline whispered, "so that I may keep you always."

Mrs. Pryor did not smile at this. A tremor swept across her features, which she struggled to suppress for several moments.

"You are more used to Fanny than to me," she remarked eventually. "I suppose my attendance must seem strange, perhaps officious?"

"No; quite natural, and very soothing. You must have been accustomed to waiting on the sick, ma'am. You move so softly, speak so quietly, and touch me so gently."

"I am dexterous in nothing, my dear. You will often find me awkward, but never negligent."

Indeed, she was never negligent. From that hour onward, Fanny and Eliza became mere shadows in the sickroom. Mrs. Pryor claimed the domain entirely; she performed every duty, living in the room day and night. The patient protested faintly at first, but soon ceased altogether. Loneliness and gloom vanished from her bedside, replaced by protection and solace. Caroline and her nurse merged in a wondrous union. Caroline, who usually pined for independence, was now eager to be cherished, while Mrs. Pryor was bent on cherishing her. There was no sign of weariness in the nurse that ever prompted the patient to worry. The task required no great hardship, though a hired hand might have found it difficult.

Despite this devoted care, the sick girl did not improve. She wasted away like a snowdrift melting in spring, fading like a flower in drought. Miss Keeldar, who rarely feared danger or death, had initially entertained no worries. But witnessing her friend's gradual decline during her visits, alarm gripped her heart. She confronted Mr. Helstone with such energy that he was finally forced, albeit reluctantly, to admit his niece was suffering from something more than a simple migraine. When Mrs. Pryor calmly requested a physician, he grumbled that she might summon two if she wished. One arrived, but he was an oracle of doom. He delivered a cryptic prophecy, wrote prescriptions, issued orders with crushing authority, pocketed his fee, and departed. He likely knew he could do no good but disliked admitting it.

Still, no rumor of serious illness spread through the neighborhood. At Hollow's Cottage, it was believed Caroline merely had a severe cold; she had even written a note to Hortense confirming this. Mademoiselle responded with two pots of currant jam, a recipe for a tisane, and a note of advice.

Mrs. Yorke, upon learning a physician had been called, scoffed at the hypochondriac fancies of the rich and idle. She argued that those with nothing but themselves to think about would summon a doctor even if their little finger ached.

Meanwhile, the "rich and idle"—represented by Caroline—were sinking rapidly into a state of prostration. Their swiftly consuming weakness baffled everyone who witnessed it, except for one person. Only that one understood how easily an undermined structure can collapse into sudden ruin.

Sick people often possess fancies incomprehensible to ordinary attendants, and Caroline had one that even her tender nurse could not immediately explain. Once a week, at a specific hour, regardless of whether she felt better or worse, she would insist on being dressed and sitting in her chair near the window. She would remain there until noon passed. Despite her wan appearance and evident exhaustion, she gently resisted all attempts to persuade her to rest until the church clock tolled midday. Once the twelve strokes rang, she became docile and would meekly lie down. Returning to the couch, she usually buried her face deep in the pillow, pulling the covers tight around her as if trying to shut out the world and the sun, both of which she seemed weary of. More than once, as she lay thus, a slight convulsion shook the sickbed, and a faint sob broke the silence. Mrs. Pryor noted these things carefully.

One Tuesday morning, as was her custom, Caroline asked permission to rise. Wrapped in her white dressing gown, she leaned forward in the easy chair, gazing steadily and patiently out the lattice. Mrs. Pryor sat slightly behind her, ostensibly knitting, but in truth, watching her intently. A change crossed Caroline's pale, mournful brow, animating her lethargy; a light flashed into her faded eyes, reviving their luster. She half-risen and looked earnestly outward. Drawing softly near, Mrs. Pryor glanced over her shoulder. From this window, the churchyard and the road beyond were visible. There, riding sharply past, appeared a horseman. The figure was not yet too distant for recognition. Mrs. Pryor had keen sight; she knew Mr. Moore. Just as a rising hill concealed him from view, the clock struck twelve.

"May I lie down again?" Caroline asked.

Her nurse helped her to bed. Having settled her and drawn the curtains, Mrs. Pryor stood listening nearby. The little couch trembled; a suppressed sob stirred the air. A contraction of anguish twisted Mrs. Pryor's features; she wrung her hands, and a half-groan escaped her lips. She recalled that Tuesday was Whinbury Market Day. Mr. Moore always passed the rectory on his way there, just before noon on that day.
