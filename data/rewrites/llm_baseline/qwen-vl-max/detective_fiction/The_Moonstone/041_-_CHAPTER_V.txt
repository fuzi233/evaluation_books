Having learned the name of Mr. Candy’s assistant, Betteredge seemed to consider that we had lingered long enough on a matter of little consequence. He resumed reading Rosanna Spearman’s letter, his brow furrowed in concentration. I sat by the window, silent and still, waiting for him to finish. My mind, which had briefly been stirred by the unsettling presence of Ezra Jennings—whose influence, under such circumstances, was both strange and inexplicable—began to settle back into its familiar currents. The weight of my own predicament returned with full force. Once again, I forced myself to confront the truth: I was caught in a web of mystery, guilt, and suspicion, with no clear path forward.

Yet, even now, I had formed a plan. I would return to London that very day. I would lay the entire case before Mr. Bruff, my solicitor, and seek his counsel. Most importantly, I would find a way—by any means necessary, at whatever cost—to speak directly with Rachel Verinder. That was my purpose, as far as I could shape one amid the chaos of my thoughts.

There was still over an hour before the train departed. And there remained a slim hope—perhaps only a desperate one—that Betteredge might uncover something in the unread portion of Rosanna’s letter that could prove vital before I left the house where the Moonstone had vanished.

The letter concluded with these words:

“You have no need to be angry, Mr. Franklin, even if I did feel some small triumph in knowing that I held your future in my hands. But soon enough, anxieties returned. Sergeant Cuff’s methods were thorough—he would surely search our clothes, our linens. There was no safe place in my room, no corner in the house where I could hide the nightgown without fear of discovery. How to conceal it so that not even he could find it? And how to do it without losing precious time? These were urgent questions.

In the end, I chose a course that may seem absurd to you. I undressed and put the nightgown on. You had worn it—and I allowed myself a brief moment of pleasure in wearing it after you.

Then came news from the servants’ hall: Sergeant Cuff wanted to see the washing-book. I found it and brought it to him in my lady’s sitting-room. We had met before—I knew he would recognize me. And I feared what he might do upon learning I worked in a household where a priceless jewel had disappeared. In that uncertainty, I welcomed the chance to face him openly, to know the worst.

He looked at me as though I were a stranger when I handed him the book. He thanked me politely. Those were signs I read as ominous. I could not know what he might whisper behind my back. I could not know when I might be arrested on suspicion and searched.

That was when you returned from seeing Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite off at the station. I went to your favorite walk in the shrubbery, hoping for one last chance to speak to you—perhaps the last chance I would ever have.

You never came. Worse, Mr. Betteredge and Sergeant Cuff passed by where I was hiding—and the Sergeant saw me.

After that, I had no choice but to return to my duties. As I stepped onto the path, you appeared, returning from the railway. You were heading straight for the shrubbery when you saw me—I am certain you saw me—but you turned away as if I carried a deadly disease and entered the house.

*NOTE BY FRANKLIN BLAKE.—The writer is mistaken, poor creature. I never noticed her. I intended to take a turn in the shrubbery, but at that moment I remembered my aunt might wish to see me. So I changed my mind and went indoors.*

I made my way back inside through the servants’ entrance. The laundry-room was empty. I sat alone, and the thoughts the Shivering Sand had once stirred in me returned. I asked myself: would it be harder to endure your indifference—or to leap into the quicksand and end it all?

It is useless to ask me to explain my own behavior. I try—and I cannot understand it. Why didn’t I stop you when you avoided me so cruelly? Why didn’t I cry out, ‘Mr. Franklin, I have something to tell you—something that concerns you deeply, and you must hear it!’ You were at my mercy—I had power over you. And more than that, I could have helped you. I knew you hadn’t stolen the Diamond for mere pleasure. Penelope had heard Rachel speak of your debts. Mr. Betteredge had mentioned your extravagance. It was plain to me: you took it to sell or pledge it, to raise money.

And yet, I could have told you of a man in London who would give you a large sum without asking awkward questions.

Why didn’t I speak? Why didn’t I speak?

Was it too much to bear—the risk of keeping the nightgown—when other dangers were added? Perhaps for some women. But not for me. In my past life, I had faced greater risks, escaped more complex traps. I had been trained, so to speak, in deception—on a scale that made headlines. Could a simple matter like concealing a nightgown truly crush me? Nonsense.

So why then? What explains this madness?

The truth is plain: behind your back, I loved you with every fiber of my being. Before your face, I was afraid. Afraid of angering you. Afraid of what you might say—if I dared reveal that I knew your secret. I had come close once, in the library. You did not turn away then. But I tried to provoke myself into anger, to gather courage. I failed. All I felt was humiliation. ‘You’re plain. You have a crooked shoulder. You’re just a maid—what right have you to speak to me?’ You never said those words, sir—but you said them all the same.

Can such a state of mind be explained? No. There is nothing left but to confess it and let it be.

I beg your pardon once more for the digression. This will not happen again. I am near the end.

The first person to enter the empty room was Penelope. She had known my secret long ago and had tried, kindly, to bring me to my senses.

‘Ah,’ she said, ‘I know why you’re here, brooding alone. The best thing for you, Rosanna, is for Mr. Franklin to leave this house.’

I had never considered that possibility. I could not speak. I could only look at her.

‘I’ve just left Miss Rachel,’ Penelope continued. ‘She’s unbearable with the police here. She insists on speaking to my lady tonight and leaving tomorrow for her Aunt Ablewhite. If she does, Mr. Franklin will soon find reason to go too.’

I regained my voice. ‘Do you mean he’ll go with her?’

‘Only too willingly, if she’d let him—but she won’t. He’s felt her temper. He’s in her disfavor—after everything he’s done for her! No. If they don’t reconcile by tomorrow, you’ll see Miss Rachel leave one way, and Mr. Franklin another. Where he’ll go, I can’t say. But he’ll never stay here after she’s gone.’

I fought down the despair. Yet, I saw a flicker of hope—if indeed there was a rift between you and Rachel.

‘Do you know what their quarrel is?’ I asked.

‘It’s all on Miss Rachel’s side,’ Penelope replied. ‘For all I know, it’s just her temper. I hate to distress you, Rosanna—but don’t imagine Mr. Franklin will ever quarrel with her. He’s far too fond of her.’

She had barely spoken when Mr. Betteredge summoned us. All indoor servants were to gather in the hall, then enter one by one to be questioned by Sergeant Cuff in his room.

My turn came after the lady’s maid and the upper housemaid. Sergeant Cuff’s questions—though carefully veiled—revealed that those two had discovered something near my door on Tuesday afternoon and again on Thursday night. They had told him enough to suspect part of the truth. He believed I had secretly made a new nightgown—but wrongly assumed the paint-stained one belonged to me.

From his tone, I sensed something else: he suspected me of involvement in the Diamond’s disappearance. But he also hinted—deliberately, I thought—that I was not the chief culprit. He believed I was acting under someone else’s direction. Who that person might be, I could not guess.

But one thing was clear: Sergeant Cuff was far from knowing the whole truth. As long as the nightgown remained hidden, you were safe. Not a moment longer.

I could no longer wear it. At any instant, I might be taken to Frizinghall, charged on suspicion, and searched. While I was still free, I had to decide—immediately—whether to destroy it or hide it somewhere safe, far from the house.

If I had cared less for you, I might have destroyed it. But how could I? It was the only proof I had that I had saved you from exposure. If we ever spoke, and you doubted my motives, how could I convince you unless I showed you the nightgown? Was it wrong to believe you might hesitate to let a girl like me share your secret—your crime—especially when your financial troubles had driven you to it?

Think of your coldness toward me, sir. Is it surprising I couldn’t bring myself to destroy the only claim I had on your trust?

I decided to hide it. The place I chose was the one I knew best—the Shivering Sand.

As soon as questioning ended, I made an excuse and asked to go outside for fresh air. I went straight to Cobb’s Hole, to Mr. Yolland’s cottage. His wife and daughter were my only true friends. I did not trust them with your secret—I have trusted no one. I only needed to write this letter and to find a safe place to remove the nightgown. At the house, I could not do either securely.

Now, I am nearly finished writing this letter, alone in Lucy Yolland’s bedroom. When it is done, I will roll up the nightgown and hide it beneath my cloak. I will find a way to keep it dry and safe among the clutter in Mrs. Yolland’s kitchen. Then I will go to the Shivering Sand—do not fear that my footprints will betray me—and bury the nightgown deep in the sand, where no one can find it without my permission.

And then—what?

Then, Mr. Franklin, I shall have two reasons to make one final attempt to speak to you. If you leave, as Penelope believes you will—and if I have not spoken before—my chance will be gone forever. That is one reason. The other is the comfort that, even if my words make you angry, I will have the nightgown ready to plead my case—a proof no words could match.

If these two things fail to steel my heart against your coldness—against the chill that has frozen me so far—then my efforts will end, and so will my life.

Yes. If I miss this chance—if you remain as cruel as ever, and I feel it again as I have before—goodbye to this world that denied me happiness given freely to others. Goodbye to life, which nothing but kindness from you could ever make bearable again.

Do not blame yourself if it ends this way. But try—do try—to feel sorrow for me, even if it is pity.

I will ensure you learn what I have done for you, even if I am no longer alive to tell you. Will you say something kind of me then—in the gentle way you speak to Miss Rachel? If you do, and if ghosts exist, I believe mine will hear it—and tremble with joy.

It is time to stop. I am crying. How can I reach the hiding-place if tears blind me?

Besides, why dwell on the dark side? Why not believe, while I can, that it will end well? Perhaps I will find you in a better mood tonight. Or, if not, perhaps tomorrow morning will bring success. Fretting won’t improve my appearance—will it? Who knows? Maybe all these pages are for nothing. They will go into the hiding-place with the nightgown—for safety’s sake.

It has been hard work writing this letter. Oh, if only we could understand each other—how I would enjoy tearing it up!

I beg to remain, sir,
Your true lover and humble servant,

Betteredge finished reading in silence. He placed the letter carefully back in its envelope, then sat with his head bowed, staring at the floor.

“Betteredge,” I said softly, “is there anything in the letter that might guide me?”

He raised his eyes slowly, sighing heavily. “No, Mr. Franklin. There is nothing to guide you. If I were you, I’d keep the letter sealed until your troubles are over. It will wound you deeply whenever you read it. Do not read it now.”

I slipped the letter into my pocketbook.

A glance back at chapters sixteen and seventeen of Betteredge’s Narrative reveals why I spared myself this pain at such a moment—when my strength had already been tested beyond measure. Twice, the unhappy woman had tried to speak to me. Twice, I had unknowingly repelled her.

On Friday night, she found me alone at the billiard table. Her manner suggested she was about to confess knowledge of the Diamond’s disappearance. For her sake, I pretended indifference, focusing on the balls instead of her. I sent her away—hurt, broken-hearted.

Then, on Saturday—knowing, from Penelope’s words, that my departure was imminent—she tried again, meeting me in the shrubbery. But I was with Betteredge and Sergeant Cuff. The Sergeant, pursuing his own agenda, appealed to my interest in Rosanna. To protect her, I loudly declared—so she could hear—“I feel no interest whatever in Rosanna Spearman.”

At those words, she turned and fled—cautioned, as I believed, from danger; doomed, as I now know, to tragedy.

From that point, the chain of events led me to the shocking discovery at the quicksand.

The full picture is now complete. I may set aside the tragic tale of Rosanna Spearman—still capable, even years later, of stirring deep sorrow in me—and turn to matters concerning the living, to events that were already shaping my slow journey from darkness into light.
