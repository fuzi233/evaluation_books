Friday morning, Armitage, Rice, and Morgan departed by automobile for Dunwich, reaching the village around one in the afternoon. The daylight was bright, yet a specific, quiet dread hung over the landscape, settling upon the strangely domed hills and the deep, shadowed ravines of the stricken region. Occasionally, atop a mountain peak, a gaunt circle of stones stood silhouetted against the sky. From the atmosphere of hushed terror permeating Osborn's store, they deduced that something terrible had transpired; shortly thereafter, they confirmed the total annihilation of the Elmer Frye household. Throughout the afternoon, the trio circled Dunwich, interrogating locals regarding the events and witnessing with escalating horror the dreary ruins of the Frye property—the lingering residue of tar-like stickiness, the blasphemous tracks in the yard, the injured livestock belonging to Seth Bishop, and the vast swathes of displaced vegetation scattered across the terrain. To Armitage, the trail winding up and down Sentinel Hill possessed a cataclysmic significance; he lingered long before the sinister, altar-like stone upon the summit. Eventually, learning that a contingent of State Police had arrived from Aylesbury earlier that morning in response to initial reports of the Frye tragedy, the visitors resolved to locate the officers and exchange information. However, this objective proved easier to conceive than execute; no trace of the party could be found anywhere. Five officers had arrived in a single vehicle, yet now the car stood empty near the ruins in the Frye yard. The locals, having spoken with the police, initially appeared as bewildered as Armitage and his companions. Then, Old Sam Hutchins recalled something, turning pale as he nudged Fred Farr and pointed toward the dank, deep hollow yawning nearby. "Gawd," he gasped, "I tol'd 'em not ter go daown into the glen, an' I never thought nobody'd dew it with them tracks an' that smell an' the whippoorwills a-screechin' daown thar in the dark o' noonday...." A cold shudder traversed the bodies of natives and visitors alike, and every ear strained in an instinctive, unconscious listening. Armitage, now confronted directly with the horror and its monstrous work, trembled under the weight of the responsibility he felt to bear. Night approached swiftly, bringing with it the mountainous blasphemy lumbering upon its eldritch course. *Negotium perambulans in tenebris...* The old librarian rehearsed the formulas he had committed to memory, clutching the paper containing the alternatives he had not. He verified that his electric flashlight functioned properly. Beside him, Rice extracted a metal sprayer from a valise, the type employed in insect control, while Morgan uncased the large-game rifle, relying upon it despite his colleague's warnings that no material weapon would avail. Armitage, having read the hideous diary, knew precisely what manifestation to anticipate, yet he refrained from offering any hints or clues to the Dunwich populace, hoping the creature might be subdued without revealing the monstrous nature of what had escaped. As shadows gathered, the natives dispersed homeward, eager to seal themselves indoors despite the evident futility of locks and bolts against a force capable of bending trees and crushing dwellings at will. They shook their heads at the visitors' plan to maintain a vigil at the Frye ruins near the glen; departing, they harbored little expectation of ever seeing the watchers again. That night, rumblings vibrated beneath the hills, and the whippoorwills piped with threatening intent. Occasionally, a wind sweeping up from Cold Spring Glen carried a touch of ineffable fetor to the heavy night air—a scent all three watchers recognized from standing above a dying thing that had passed fifteen and a half years as a human being. Yet the anticipated terror did not manifest. Whatever resided in the glen bided its time, and Armitage advised his colleagues that attempting an assault in the darkness would be suicidal. Dawn broke wanly, the night sounds ceasing. It was a gray, bleak day, punctuated by a drizzle of rain, while heavier clouds piled themselves up beyond the hills to the northwest. The men from Arkham remained undecided, seeking shelter beneath one of the few undestroyed Frye outbuildings. They debated the wisdom of waiting versus taking the aggressive approach and descending into the glen to hunt their nameless quarry. The downpour intensified, and distant peals of thunder rolled from far horizons. Sheet lightning shimmered, followed by a forked bolt flashing near at hand, seemingly descending into the accursed glen itself. The sky darkened profoundly, and the watchers prayed the storm would prove brief and sharp, yielding to clear skies. It remained gruesomely dark when, not quite an hour later, a confused babel of voices echoed down the road. Moments later, a frightened group of more than a dozen men came into view, running, shouting, and whimpering hysterically. A figure in the lead began sobbing, and the Arkham men recoiled violently when those words coalesced into a coherent narrative. "Oh, my Gawd, my Gawd!" the voice choked out. "It's a-goin' agin, _an' this time by day_! It's aout--it's aout an' a-movin' this very minute, an' only the Lord knows when it'll be on us all!" The speaker panted into silence, but another seized the thread. "Nigh on a haour ago Zeb Whateley here heerd the 'phone a-ringin', an' it was Mis' Corey, George's wife that lives daown by the junction. She says the hired boy Luther was aout drivin' in the caows from the storm arter the big bolt, when he see all the trees a-bendin' at the maouth o' the glen--opposite side ter this--an' smelt the same awful smell like he smelt when he faound the big tracks las' Monday mornin'. An' she says he says they was a swishin', lappin' saound, more nor what the bendin' trees an' bushes could make, an' all on a suddent the trees along the rud begun ter git pushed one side, an' they was a awful stompin' an' splashin' in the mud. But mind ye, Luther he didn't see nothin' at all, only jest the bendin' trees an' underbrush. "Then fur ahead where Bishop's Brook goes under the rud he heerd a awful creakin' an' strainin' on the bridge, an' says he could tell the saound o' wood a-startin' to crack an' split. An' all the whiles he never see a thing, only them trees an' bushes a-bendin'. An' when the swishin' saound got very fur off--on the rud towards Wizard Whateley's an' Sentinel Hill--Luther he had the guts ter step up whar he'd heerd it fust an' look at the graound. It was all mud an' water, an' the sky was dark, an' the rain was wipin' aout all tracks abaout as fast as could be; but beginnin' at the glen maouth, whar the trees bed moved, they was still some o' them awful prints big as bar'ls like he seen Monday." At this juncture, the first excited speaker interrupted. "But _that_ ain't the trouble naow--that was only the start. Zeb here was callin' folks up an' everybody was a-listenin' in when a call from Seth Bishop's cut in. His haousekeeper Sally was carryin' on fit ter kill--she'd jest seed the trees a-bendin' beside the rud, an' says they was a kind o' mushy saound, like a elephant puffin' an' treadin', a-headin' fer the haouse. Then she up an' spoke suddent of a fearful smell, an' says her boy Cha'ncey was a-screamin' as haow it was jest like what he smelt up to the Whateley rewins Monday mornin'. An' the dogs was all barkin' an' whinin' awful. "An' then she let aout a turrible yell, an' says the shed daown the rud hed jest caved in like the storm hed blowed it over, only the wind wa'n't strong enough to dew that. Everybody was a-listenin', an' ye could hear lots o' folks on the wire a-gaspin'. All to onct Sally she yelled agin, an' says the front yard picket fence bed jest crumpled up, though they wa'n't no sign o' what done it. Then everybody on the line could hear Cha'ncey an' ol' Seth Bishop a-yellin', tew, an' Sally was shriekin' aout that suthin' heavy hed struck the haouse--not lightnin' nor nothin', but suthin' heavy agin' the front, that kep' a-launchin' itself agin an' agin, though ye couldn't see nuthin' aout the front winders. An' then ... an' then...." Lines of fright deepened upon every face; and Armitage, shaken though he was, possessed barely enough composure to prompt the speaker. "An' then ...
