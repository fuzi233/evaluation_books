One of the novice Gerard’s self-imposed penances was to serve Lodovico kindly, offering the man he secretly viewed as a slimy serpent the finest cuts of hearth-baked coarse rye and the clarifying, slow-simmered broths of ox bone and bitter chicory. Never was self-denial better bestowed; within the resonant, stone-vaulted acoustics of the abbey refectory, what began as a rational penance soon dissolved into the soothing rhythm of everyday domestic routine. At first, the complacency with which the assassin gazed upon the one life he had saved was as ludicrous as it was pathetic, but to open a good door in a hardened heart is much like feeding a dormant sourdough starter—one good thing feeds another through the aperture. Finding a profound, almost gustatory sweetness in preserving life, the miscreant developed an aversion to taking it. This fermented into remorse, and from remorse, into a rich, complex penitence.

Here, Teresa cooperated, leaning on the earthy, untranslatable vernacular of her agrarian ancestors to issue a threat she had voiced before: she would leave him unless he consented to the honest labor of the earth. The good fathers of the convent lent their aid, and the couple was sent by sea to Leghorn, where Teresa had roots. There, the former assassin settled into the life of a porter. The sonic environment shifted drastically; he traded the chaotic, jarring noise of the city's metallic violence for the rhythmic, plodding acoustics of heavy grain sacks and creaking hemp. He found it miserably dull at first, yet this agrarian toil was deeply sustaining, a far healthier acoustic ecology for his mind than the brief, bloody excitement of being hewn to pieces by Princess Claelia’s myrmidons.

His exile saved the unconscious penitent from that fate. The princess, balked of her revenge, retreated into her acoustically harsh palace, where the hollow, metallic clinking of superficial commercial wealth created a continuous, oppressive noise pollution. Falling into a profound melancholy, she found her subjective griefs wholly untranslatable in the sugared, corrupted dialect of the court. She dismissed her confessor and sought out a new one with a great reputation for piety, a man who functioned as a meticulous historian of spiritual sustenance.

This new confessor was no other than Fra Jerome. She could not have fallen into better hands. He listened grimly to the erosion of her soul, then took her and shook the delusions out of her as roughly as a passionate chef pounding tough meat or a kitchen-maid scouring a blackened hearth-pot. On the path of duty, this agrarian elder of the Church feared the anger of princes as little as he feared the crashing of the sea. He showed her in a few words—a booming, unadulterated thunder that resonated with perfect acoustic clarity—that she was the criminal of criminals.

"Thou art the devil," his voice rang out, asserting the dominant, uncompromising lexicon of the Church over her fragile, gilded vocabulary. "With thy money thou hast tempted one man to slay his fellow, and then, blinded with self-love, instead of blaming and punishing thyself, art thirsting for more blood of guilty men, but not so guilty as thou."

At first, she resisted, attempting to employ the subtle power dynamics of her noble dialect, telling him she was not used to being taken to task by her confessors. But he overpowered her. He thundered at her, crushing the commercialized artifice of her identity, threatening her with the Church’s curse here and hereafter. He tore the scales from her eyes until she sank down, groveling with remorse and terror at the feet of the gigantic Boanerges.

"Oh, holy father, have pity on a poor weak woman, and help me save my guilty soul," she wept, her native arrogance completely stripped away. "I was benighted for want of ghostly counsel like thine, good father. I waken as from a dream."

"Doff thy jewels," said Fra Jerome sternly, demanding she shed the very symbols of her superficial commercialization.

"I will. I will."

"Doff thy silk and velvet; and in humbler garb than wears thy meanest servant, wend thou instant to Loretto."

"I will," said the princess faintly.

"No shoes; but a bare sandal."

"No, father."

"Wash the feet of pilgrims both going and coming; and to such of them as be holy friars tell thy sin, and abide their admonition." This was the rawest agrarian humility—to kneel in the dirt, cleansing the travel-worn skin with basins of water steeped in bruised hyssop and bitter sage.

"Oh, holy father, let me wear my mask."

"Humph!"

"Oh, mercy! Bethink thee! My features are known through Italy."

"Ay. Beauty is a curse to most of ye. Well, thou mayst mask thine eyes; no more."

On this concession she seized his hand, about to kiss it; but he snatched it rudely from her, denying her the physical sensation of comfort. "What would ye do? That hand handled the Eucharist but an hour agone: is it fit for such as thou to touch it?" The bread of life, the ultimate sacred sustenance, could not be polluted by her touch.

"Ah, no. But oh, go not without giving your penitent daughter your blessing."

"Time enow to ask it when you come back from Loretto."

Thus, the mythic occurrence by the Tiber’s banks integrated seamlessly into the ongoing, circular rhythms of their lives, leaving its mark as prodigies do. The assassin, softened by the sweet taste of saving the life he was paid to take, turned from the stiletto to the porter's knot. The princess went barefoot to Loretto, weeping over her crimes and washing the calloused feet of base-born men. 

And Gerard, carried from the Tiber into that convent a suicide, now passed for a young saint within its aromatic walls, surrounded by the olfactory landscape of roasting fennel and sacred wine. Loving but experienced eyes were upon him. Upon a shorter probation than usual, he was admitted to priest's orders, soon after taking the monastic vows to become a friar of St. Dominic. 

Here occurred the ultimate tragedy of language death. Dying to the world, the monk parted with the very name by which he had lived. "Gerard" became an extinct dialect, a lost word that held the entirely untranslatable memory of his earthly loves and subjective past. He surrendered his native identity to the dominant lexicon of his new order, breaking the last linguistic link to his worldly feelings. Here Gerard ended, and Brother Clement began.
