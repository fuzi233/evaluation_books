Beyond the quiet influence of Fifine Beck’s mother, another force held sway over M. Paul and me—something unseen yet powerful, watching closely from behind a veil of silence: Rome, through its sacred confessional, kept a vigilant eye upon her son. That chamber, where I had once knelt in prayer, now became a silent witness to M. Emanuel’s monthly visits—a place where secrets were confessed, hearts laid bare, and vows tempered by faith.

Why was I so glad to call him friend? Had he not long been one already? Had he not shown time and again his quiet affection, his gentle regard?

Yes—he had. Yet still, I cherished hearing it spoken aloud, earnestly, as if he truly believed me worthy of such trust. I liked the humility in his voice, the tenderness with which he spoke of me, the way he seemed to seek permission before daring to hope. He had called me “sister.” That name felt right. I would accept it gladly, so long as it meant nothing more than a bond of soul and spirit. And since he had sworn celibacy, there seemed no risk that this bond might be tested by marriage.

But that night, as I lay awake, I turned over every word we’d shared. I longed for morning to come, for the bell to ring, for the lesson to begin. Once dressed, I found even prayers and breakfast dragging, each minute stretching into an eternity until finally, at last, the hour arrived when I would see him again.

I wanted to understand our new friendship—not just as words, but as action. How would he behave? Would he treat me like a brother? And could I, in return, meet him as a sister—with courage, with warmth, with openness?

He came.

Yet life is built on irony: expectation never matches reality. All day, he said not a word to me. His lesson was quieter than usual—softer, heavier with thought. He was kind to the pupils, fatherly even, but toward me, cold. As he left the classroom, I waited for a smile, a glance, something—anything. I received only a quick, shy nod.

This distance, I told myself, must be temporary. A mistake. A misunderstanding. Patience. It would pass.

But it did not. Days passed. The gap widened. I swallowed my surprise, my hurt, my longing. I remained silent.

Had I not asked, when he offered fraternity, “Dare I rely on you?” And had he not, knowing himself well, withheld all promise? True, he had urged me to test him—to tease, to try. But how could I? That power belonged to others. Not to me. I lacked both instinct and strength. Left alone, I stayed still. Pushed back, I retreated. Forgotten, I said nothing. No hint escaped my lips, no look crossed my eyes. I began to wonder if I had miscalculated—perhaps somewhere along the way, I had misunderstood.

Then came the evening of our customary lesson. Once a week, he gave me private instruction—reviewing past work, preparing for what lay ahead. These sessions often took place wherever the school allowed—sometimes in the large second division, especially when the day pupils had gone and only a few boarders lingered near the surveillante’s platform.

That night, as the clock struck the familiar hour, I gathered my books, pen, ink, and made my way to the division.

The room was empty, bathed in cool shadow. But beyond the open doors, the courtyard glowed with light and life. The sun, low in the west, cast a golden blush across walls and dresses, fusing colors into one radiant warmth. Girls sat quietly, studying or writing; among them stood M. Emanuel, speaking cheerfully to a teacher. His dark coat, his black hair, caught the crimson glow. When he turned, his face lit up with a smile that answered the sun’s kiss.

I took my seat at a desk.

Nearby, orange trees and blooming plants basked in the fading light. They had soaked up the sun all day and now thirsted for water. M. Emanuel loved gardening. I had always thought tending plants soothed him—gave him peace. Now he moved among them, fetching water from the well with his own hands, watering the orange trees, the geraniums, the vibrant cacti. A cigar rested between his lips—his small luxury, his daily comfort. Blue smoke curled gently around the flowers, mingling with the evening air.

He spoke no more to the students or teachers. Instead, he cooed to a tiny spaniel, soft and silky, who adored him above all others. She trotted beside him, eyes full of devotion, tail wagging like a metronome. Whenever he dropped his hat or handkerchief in play, she crouched beside it, guarding it like a queen’s banner.

There were many plants, and his task took time. The great clock ticked on. Another hour passed. The sunset faded. Day slipped away. My lesson would be short tonight—but perhaps not too short.

Yet still, he lingered. There were roses to care for, favorite blooms to water. Little Sylvie followed him down the paths, barking with joy. I put aside some books—I wouldn’t need them all. I sat. I waited. And slowly, twilight crept in.

Sylvie reappeared, bounding forward, announcing his return. The watering pot was set aside. Relief flooded me. He washed his hands in a stone bowl. There was no time for lessons now—the prayer bell would soon sound. But we would meet. He would speak. I could read his heart, if only he would let me.

After washing, he paused, adjusting his cuffs. He looked up at the young moon, pale against the opal sky, glowing faintly on the windows of Jean Baptiste. Sylvie watched, restless. She whined, jumped—trying to break the stillness.

“Petite exigeante,” he said softly. “You cannot be forgotten, not for a moment.”

He lifted her into his arms, strolled across the courtyard, passing within yards of the window where I sat. He walked slowly, cradling her, whispering tender names. At the front steps, he turned once more. He gazed at the moon, at the cathedral, at the rooftops fading into mist. He breathed in the sweet dusk, admired the garden’s bloom. Then suddenly, he looked around.

A sharp glance cut across the façade of the classrooms, sweeping the row of windows. I think he bowed. If he did, I didn’t have time to respond. In a moment, he was gone. The moonlit threshold stood empty, silent.

I gathered my things, returned unused books to their shelf. The prayer bell rang. I obeyed.

The next day brought no relief. He was occupied with college duties. I taught, endured the hours, watched evening fall. Alone, I prepared for the weight of solitude. Was it better to stay with others, or sit by myself? I chose the latter. No one here could offer comfort. Only under the lid of my desk could I find refuge—nestled between pages, warmed by pencil points, touched by ink.

With heavy heart, I opened the desk. One by one, familiar books were pulled out and returned—useless. They offered no solace.

Then I saw it: a pamphlet in lilac cover. I hadn’t seen it before. I had rearranged my desk earlier that afternoon. This book must have been placed there recently—during dinner.

I opened it.

It was neither story nor poem, essay nor history. It didn’t sing or narrate. It preached. It persuaded.

And though small, it held a spell. I listened willingly. It spoke of Roman Catholicism, of conversion—not through fear, but through comfort. It offered tenderness, indulgence, peace. The Church, it claimed, did not threaten. She guided. She loved. _She_ persecute? Never.

This was not for the hardened, nor for the strong. It was milk for babes—a mother’s voice, soft and sweet. Its aim was not reason, but emotion. It sought to win hearts through love, not logic. Like St. Vincent de Paul gathering orphans, it spoke with gentle grace.

One argument stood out: the Catholic could pray for loved ones in purgatory—freeing them from suffering. The writer said nothing of those who believed in immediate salvation. But I thought of that doctrine—and found it more comforting.

Still, I smiled. The little book amused me. It was sentimental, shallow—yet something about it lifted my spirits. I laughed at the idea of a wolf-cub wrapped in sheep’s wool, bleating like a lamb. It reminded me of Methodist tracts I’d read as a child—just as eager to stir emotion.

The author was no villain. Though his methods revealed cunning, I hesitated to accuse him of deceit. But his reasoning was weak—unsteady, ungrounded.

I smiled again, then laughed—though not with the same ease. Something stirred inside me.

I glanced at the title page: “Père Silas.” On the flyleaf, in clear, familiar handwriting: “From P. C. D. E. to L—y.”

At that, I laughed again—but differently. A sudden clarity burst through the fog. The connection was undeniable: Père Silas and Paul Emanuel. The penitent had confessed everything. Every word of our talk had been revealed. He had spoken of us—of our covenant, of his adopted sister.

How could such a bond be permitted? Fraternal fellowship with a Protestant? I imagined Père Silas disapproving, warning him, commanding restraint—in the name of God, in memory of all M. Emanuel held dear—ordering him to withdraw, to guard his heart.

These thoughts may seem harsh. But compared to the fear of M. Paul changing within himself, they felt almost welcome.

Later, I couldn’t tell how much of this I had guessed, and how much came from elsewhere. But help was not far off.

That evening, no sunset painted the sky. Clouds covered east and west. No blue mist softened the view. A damp fog crept in from the marshes, gray and heavy. Rain had fallen all afternoon, and now fell steadily.

No one would wander in the wet alleys. So I was surprised when Sylvie barked—her joyful bark. She was never so excited unless he was near.

Through the glass door and archway, I saw the long path. Sylvie dashed through the gloom, white as a rose. She ran, whined, leapt—disturbing birds in the bushes. Five minutes passed. Nothing followed. I returned to my books.

Then her bark stopped. I looked up. She stood nearby, tail wagging furiously, watching a spade digging fast in the mud.

There he was—bent over the soil, working hard, rain soaking the shrubs around him. He dug as if earning his bread by sweat alone.

In that sight, I saw unrest. He dug when troubled—when grief or guilt pressed upon him. He worked for hours, head down, lips sealed.

Sylvie tired. Then she bounded about, sniffing everywhere—until she spotted me.

She flew to the window, barking wildly, as if urging me to join her master. She had seen me walk with him before. She likely thought it my duty to go now, however wet.

Her noise grew louder. Finally, M. Paul looked up. He saw her, saw me. He whistled. She barked louder.

Exasperated, he dropped the spade, stepped forward, and pushed the door open.

Sylvie burst in, leaping into my lap, paws at my neck, nose and tongue exploring my face, mouth, eyes. Her tail scattered papers and books across the desk.

M. Emanuel approached, collected the mess, and tucked Sylvie under his coat. She nestled quietly, head peeking out. Tiny, beautiful—her dark eyes, silken ears, innocent face. I thought of Paulina de Bassompierre. Forgive the thought—it came naturally.

He petted her. Such affection was no surprise. She begged for love with her very being.

As he caressed her, his gaze drifted to the papers—then settled on the religious tract.

His lips moved. He hesitated. Did he promise never to speak to me again? If so, he broke it now—because he spoke.

“You haven’t read it yet, I suppose? It doesn’t seem very inviting?”

I said I had.

He waited—hoping, perhaps, for a comment. But I was silent. If any concession was needed, it was not mine to make. I was not the docile pupil of Père Silas. I was not bound by his rules.

His eyes met mine—gentle, concerned, tinged with sorrow. He longed to see emotion in me. I could not give it.

Then I remembered the pens. I took them from my desk and began sharpening them—carelessly. I cut my finger—half on purpose.

I knew this would shift his mood. He disliked seeing me struggle. My knife was dull, my hand clumsy. He always chided me.

“Maladroit!” he exclaimed. “You’ll turn your hands into mincemeat!”

He set Sylvie down, placed her beside his hat, and took the pens from me. With swift precision, he sliced, shaped, pointed each quill—like a machine.

“Did you like the little book?” he asked.

I suppressed a yawn. “Hardly know.”

“Did it move you?”

“I thought it made me sleepy.”

(A pause.)

“Allons donc! You’re taking that tone with me? Bad as I am—and I’ll confess all my faults—God gave you too much sensitivity, too much sympathy not to be deeply moved by such a tender appeal.”

“Indeed,” I replied sharply, “I wasn’t affected at all.”

To prove it, I pulled out a perfectly dry, clean handkerchief—still folded.

He launched into a series of sharp remarks—biting, playful. I listened, delighted. After two days of silence, hearing him speak again, in his old way, was music.

We shared chocolate comfits from a bonbonnière he always kept stocked. He liked to see even small gifts appreciated.

He watched us, then put down his knife. Holding the newly cut pens, he touched my hand.

“Tell me, petite sœur—speak frankly. What have you thought of me these past two days?”

I ignored the question. It filled my eyes with tears. I stroked Sylvie instead.

M. Paul leaned forward. “I called myself your brother,” he said. “But I don’t know what I am—brother? Friend? I can’t say. I know I think of you—I care for you—but I must stop. You are dangerous. My best friends warn me. They whisper caution.”

“You do right to listen. Be cautious.”

“It’s your religion—your strange, self-reliant creed. It seems to clothe you in some invincible armor. You are good—Père Silas says you are good. But your proud, fierce Protestantism—that is the danger. It shows in your eyes, in your gestures. Just now—when you handled that tract—my God! I thought Lucifer smiled.”

“Certainly I don’t respect that tract. So what?”

“Not respect it? But it is pure faith, love, charity! I hoped it would touch you. I placed it there with prayer. I must be a sinner—Heaven won’t hear my most heartfelt pleas. You scorn my offering. Oh, cela me fait mal!”

“Monsieur, I don’t scorn it—as your gift. Sit down. Listen to me. I am not a heathen. Not hard-hearted. Not unchristian. Not dangerous. I would not trouble your faith. You believe in God, Christ, the Bible—and so do I.”

“But do you really believe in the Bible? Do you accept Revelation? What limits are there to your wild, reckless beliefs? Père Silas hinted…”

By persuasion, I drew out his meaning—crafty slanders, Jesuitical lies.

That night, we talked seriously. He pleaded, argued. I could not argue—I lacked the skill. But I could speak in my own way—the way he understood. I defended my faith not with logic, but with truth. I let him see the depth of my belief, the reverence in my heart.

He left unsatisfied, but not angry. He understood now that Protestants were not the irreverent pagans his director had described. He glimpsed how we honored God—not with ritual, but with awe, with quiet devotion.

And in that quiet, I felt something shift—not in him, but in me. The distance had not vanished. But the silence was broken. And that, for now, was enough.
