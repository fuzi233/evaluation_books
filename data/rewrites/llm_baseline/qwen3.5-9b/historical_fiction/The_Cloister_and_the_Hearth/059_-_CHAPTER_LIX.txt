Happy is the man who possesses two anchor cables: Merit, and Women. Oh, that I, like Gerard, might possess a "chain of ladies" to haul myself up by! Then I should become a prose laureate, or a professor of the spasmodic style, or whatever fashionable title suits the moment, in no time at all. Until then, let us sketch the portrait of Fra Colonna.

The true revivals of ancient learning and philosophy were largely the work of two fictional writers—Petrarch and Boccaccio. Their labors were not immediately crowned with great public triumph; rather, they sowed a good seed that never perished. Instead, it quickened in the soil, waiting patiently for the sunshine. From their era onward, Italy was never devoid of a native scholar or two versed in Greek; indeed, any learned Greek who landed there was received with fraternal warmth. Before the fourteenth century closed, we saw the births of Poggio, Valla, and the elder Guarino. Early in the fifteenth century, Florence under Cosimo de' Medici became a nest of Platonists. Headed by Gemistus Pletho, a man born Greek, this group began around 1440 to critique Aristotle. For few minds are broad enough to be fair to great A without becoming unjust to capital B. Theodore Gaza defended that great man with moderation; George of Trebizond attacked him with acerbity, retorting sharply against Plato. Then Cardinal Bessarion, another man born Greek, resisted George and his idol in a tract titled *Adversus calumniatorem Platonis*. Whether wise or not, pugnacity is a form of vitality.

Born without this controversial bile in such a zealous epoch, Francesco Colonna, a young Florentine nobleman, devoted himself entirely to the arts. At twenty, he took the habit of a Dominican friar. His objective was quiet study. He retired from idle company and faction fights—the humming and stinging of the human hive—to seek refuge with Saint Dominic and the Nine Muses. An eager student of languages, pictures, statues, chronology, coins, and monumental inscriptions, he found that these latter objects loosened his faith in popular histories. He traveled many years in the East, returning laden with spoils: masterpieces of manuscripts, and fluent in Greek, Latin, Hebrew, and Syriac. He discovered that his homeland had not stood still. Besides Cosimo, other lettered princes had risen, including Alfonso, King of Naples, and the Este brothers. Above all, his old friend Thomas of Sarzana had been made Pope, lending a mighty impulse to letters. He had accumulated five thousand manuscripts in the Vatican library and commissioned Poggio to translate Diodorus Siculus and Xenophon's *Cyropaedia*, Laurentius Valla to translate Herodotus and Thucydides, Theodore Gaza to tackle Theophrastus, and George of Trebizond to render Eusebius and certain treatises of Plato.

The monk found Plato and Aristotle under a truce, but Poggio and Valla at loggerheads over verbs and nouns, burning with *odium philologicum*. All this was heaven to him, and he settled down in his native land, his life a rosy dream. None are happier than the versatile, provided they do not need to earn their daily bread through it. And Fra Colonna was the very definition of versatility. He knew seven or eight languages and a touch of mathematics; he could write, paint, model, sing, and play the lute. Furthermore, he possessed the rare ability to relish superior excellence in all these branches. For this trait alone, he deserved to be as happy as he was. Gauge the intellects of your acquaintances, and you will find few whose minds are not deaf, blind, or dead to some great art or science—"wisdom at one entrance quite shut out." Those who are both conceited and stupid will parade these holes rather than blush for them.

A zealot in art, the friar was a skeptic in religion. In every age, there are a few men who hold the opinions of another age, past or future. Being a lump of simplicity, his skepticism was as naive as his enthusiasm. He pretended to view the religious ceremonies of his day as mere models, while regarding the worship of gods and departed heroes as mummeries fit only for the populace. Yet, his mind drew an unconscious, droll distinction: whatever Christian ceremony his learning taught him was of purely pagan origin, and thus he respected it out of reverence for antiquity. Had he, with his turn of mind, been a pagan living in that era, he would have scorned it from his philosophical heights.

Fra Colonna was charmed by his new artist, Gerard. Having access to half the palaces in Rome, he sounded Gerard's praises until the painter was soon summoned to resign his current post. "But I am so happy with you, Father," objected Gerard. "Fiddlesticks about being happy with me!" replied Fra Colonna. "You must not be happy; you must be a man of the world. The grand lesson I impress upon the young is to be a man of the world. Now, these Montesini can pay you three times as much as I can, and they shall too—by Jupiter!" And the friar slapped a terrifying price on Gerard's pen. It was acceded to without a murmur. Much higher prices were being commanded for copying than authorship ever achieved for centuries under the printing press. Gerard received three hundred crowns for Aristotle's treatise on Rhetoric. The great are mighty sweet to their pets while the fancy lasts; and in the rage for Greek manuscripts, the handsome writer soon became a favorite, caressed by nobles of both sexes like a lapdog. It would have turned a vain fellow's head, but the canny Dutchman saw the steel hand beneath the velvet glove and did not presume.

Nevertheless, it was a proud day for him when he found himself seated with Fra Colonna at the table of his present employer, Cardinal Bessarion. They were perhaps a mile below the summit of that social table, but never mind—they were there. Gerard had the distinct advantage of seeing roast pheasants served with all their feathers intact, as if they had just flown out of a coppice rather than off the spit, alongside chickens cooked in bottles, tender as peaches. But the grand novelty was the napkins, surpassingly fine and folded into cocked hats, bird's wings, and fans, instead of lying flat. This electrified Gerard, though my readers may have witnessed this dazzling phenomenon without tumbling backward out of their chairs.

After dinner, the tables were split apart and carried away, revealing underneath another table spread with sweetmeats. The signoras and signorinas fell upon them and gorged themselves, while the signors eyed them with reasonable suspicion. "But, dear father," objected Gerard, "I see not the bifurcated daggers with which men say his Excellency arms the left hand of a man." "Nay, 'tis Cardinal Orsini who invented that peevish instrument for his guests to fumble their meat withal. One, being in haste, did skewer his tongue to his palate with it, I hear; *O tempora, O mores!* How would the ancients, reclining godlike at their feasts, have spurned such pedantries?"

As soon as the ladies had disported themselves among the sugar-plums, the tables were suddenly removed, and the guests sat in a row against the wall. Then entered, ducking and scraping, two ecclesiastics with lutes. They knelt at the Cardinal's feet and sang the service of the day before retiring with a deep obeisance. In answer, the Cardinal fingered his skullcap as our late Iron Duke once tipped his hat. The company dispersed, and Gerard had dined with a Cardinal and one who had thrice just missed being Pope.

But greater honor was in store. One day, the Cardinal sent for him. After praising the beauty of his work, he took Gerard in his coach to the Vatican, leading him up a private stair to a luxurious little room with a great oriel window. There were inkstands, sloping frames for writing, and all the instruments of art. The Cardinal whispered to a courtier, and presently the Pope's private secretary appeared with a glorious, grimy old manuscript of Plutarch's *Lives*. Soon, Gerard was seated alone, copying it, awe-struck yet half-delighted at the thought that His Holiness would handle his work and read it.

The papal inkstands were all glorious externally, but within, the ink was vile. Gerard, however, carried ever good, home-made ink in a dirty little inkhorn. Praying on his knees for a firm and skillful hand, he set to work. One side of his room was nearly occupied by a massive curtain divided in the center, but its ample folds overlapped. After a while, Gerard felt drawn to peep through that curtain. He resisted the impulse. It returned. It overpowered him. Leaving Plutarch, he stole across the matted floor, took the folds of the curtain, and gently gathered them up with his fingers. Putting his nose through the chink, he ran it against a cold steel halbert. Two soldiers, armed cap-a-pie, held their glittering weapons crossed in a triangle. Gerard drew swiftly back; but in that instant, he heard the soft murmur of voices and saw a group of persons cringing before some hidden figure.
