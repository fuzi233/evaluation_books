Rough travel holds a peculiar charm for us Englishmen, and so do the chronicles of our journeys—even if the traveler is no pilgrim seeking love. Antique friendships possess at least the fascination of fossils. Yet, since the true heart of this narrative beats in Holland, it is time to return there, to those ordinary people and events that constitute the fabric of life across all ages.

Jorian Ketel arrived at Peter's house to claim Margaret's promise. However, Margaret lay ill in bed. Upon hearing his purpose, Peter affronted him, warning him to leave the premises immediately. Those standing nearby were ready to dunk him in water, for both father and daughter were beloved figures, and the entire saga was the talk of everyone. The citizens of Sevenbergen were in a state of heated, indiscriminate agitation—a symptom of intense popular sympathy. Humiliated, Jorian departed in a fury, regretting his earlier kindness. Such repentance is not uncommon and carries the merit of sincerity.

Meanwhile, Dierich Brower, caught at "The Three Kings" attempting to buy information about Martin Wittenhaagen's location by getting a chatterbox drunk, was seized and thrown into a horse pond. He was threatened with further punishment should he ever appear in the borough again. Furthermore, fueled by municipal jealousy, the burgomaster of Sevenbergen sent a formal letter to the burgomaster of Tergou. The missive reminded the latter that he had overstepped legal boundaries and requested that any future complaints—real or imagined—against Sevenbergen's townsfolk be directed solely to Sevenbergen's authorities.

Ghysbrecht Van Swieten, suppressing his rage at this rebuke, replied with a polite message. He claimed the individual he had pursued to Sevenbergen was a Tergovan named Gerard who had stolen the town records. Since Gerard had fled abroad, likely taking the documents with him, the matter was concluded. Thus, Ghysbrecht made a virtue of necessity. In truth, however, his composure was merely a mask. Baffled by the situation in Sevenbergen, he shifted his focus elsewhere. He dispatched emissaries to the household at Tergou to discover where Gerard had fled. To his infinite surprise, they knew nothing. This uncertainty gnawed at him, fueling a fear that Gerard was merely hiding in the neighborhood, waiting to make a discovery and exact terrible revenge.

From this point forward, Dierich and others noted a marked deterioration in Ghysbrecht's demeanor. He became moody and irritable, haunted by a dread that settled heavily upon him. His eyes darted furtively, as if expecting a blow from an unknown direction. Having made others wretched had failed to bring him happiness, for rarely does inflicting pain on others bring joy. The small family at Tergou, which might otherwise have resolved its differences without exiling *spem gregis* (hope of the flock) to a distant land, retained its outward appearance but lost its inner simplicity. Little Kate knew the extent of Cornelis and Sybrandt's role in banishing Gerard. Though she feared causing further strife and thus never spoke to her mother, there were moments when she shuddered at their mere presence and blushed at their hypocritical expressions of regret.

Catherine, possessing a woman's keen vigilance and subtlety, observed these shifts. She said nothing aloud but pondered quietly, watching for more evidence. The "black sheep" of the family, in their attempts to share the general gloom, managed to deceive their father and Giles temporarily. Yet, the demure satisfaction lurking beneath their actions could not escape the sharp eyes of the women—"Noting all, seeming to note nothing." Consequently, mistrust and suspicion replaced the memory of Gerard's intelligent face, which had once brightened the entire circle, remaining unnoticed until it vanished.

As for the elderly hosier, his pride had been wounded by his son's disobedience. He bore his grief stiffly, striving never to utter Gerard's name. Beneath his stoic exterior, however, Nature tugged insistently at his heartstrings. One anxiety he never feigned to hide: "If I but knew where the boy is, and that his life and health remain safe, my cares would be minimal," he would often say, followed by a deep sigh. One wonders if Gerard had simply walked through the door at that moment; surely, there would have been many tears and embraces, and few, if any, reproaches.

Then came a shock to the old couple: publicity. Within a week of Gerard's departure, his adventures were the subject of every conversation. Worse still, public sympathy rallied fervently behind the lovers and against Gerard's "cruel" parents and the meddling burgomaster who had intruded into a private affair.

"Mother," Kate said softly, "it is known throughout the town that Margaret is suffering from a fever—a burning fever. Her father fears greatly for her."

"Margaret? Which Margaret?" Catherine asked, feigning a treacherous calm.

"Oh, Mother! Who else could I mean? Why, Gerard's Margaret."

"Gerard's Margaret!" Catherine shrieked. "How dare you speak such a word to me? I warn you never to mention that hussy's name in this house, that she has defiled. She is the ruin of my poor boy, the flower of my flock. Because of her, he is not a holy priest among us but roams the world, leaving me a desolate, broken-hearted mother. Do not cry, my child; I act wrongly speaking so harshly. But oh, Kate! You do not understand a mother's heart. I endure before you all; I must swallow my fears. Yet at night, I see him in my dreams, always plagued by some trouble. Sometimes he is torn by wild beasts; other times held captive by robbers, their cruel knives raised to strike his pale face—one thinks it would move a stone. Oh! When I recall that, while I sit here in comfort, perhaps my poor boy lies dead in some savage land because of that girl... her very name is poison to me. I tremble whenever I hear it."

"I shall say nothing and do nothing to grieve you further, Mother," Kate replied tenderly, though she sighed deeply.

She whose name was so fiercely forbidden within these walls was widely discussed and even pitied outside. All of Sevenbergen felt sorry for her. Young men and maidens cast pitying glances as they passed the small window where the village beauty lay "dying for love." In this familiar phrase, they underestimated her spirit and selflessness. Gerard was alive, and she remained too loyal to doubt his constancy. Her father was dear to her and helpless. Had she not been physically weak, her love for Gerard would not have prevented her from fulfilling her duties, though she might have performed them with a drooping head and heavy heart.

However, physical and mental strain had triggered a violent fever. Only her youth and strong constitution saved her. The illness eventually subsided, leaving her in a terrifying state of debility where life felt like a burden. It is during such times that love and friendship become mortal angels, offering comfort in their voices and healing in their hands. But this poor girl had to find a way back to life and vigor on her own. For many days, she lay alone as heavy hours rolled over her like leaden waves. In her weakened state, existence seemed burdensome, and life felt like something already passed. Unable to strive for recovery, she often lay motionless for hours, tears gently welling in her eyes.

One day, awakening from an uneasy sleep, she found two women in her room. One was a servant; the other, distinguished by deep fur lining her collar and sleeves, appeared to be a person of consequence. A narrow band of silvery hair, spared by her hairstyle, indicated she was past the age when sensible women conceal their years. Both women looked kind and friendly. Margaret attempted to rise, but the older lady gently pressed a hand against her.

"Lie still, sweetheart. We come not to disturb you, but to comfort you, God willing. Cheer up a bit, and tell us, who do you think we are?"

"Nay, madam, I know you, though I have never seen you before. You are Dame Van Eyck, and this is Reicht Heynes. Gerard has often spoken of you and your goodness to him. Madam, he has no friend like you near him now," she said, sinking back onto the pillows as tears instantly filled her eyes.

The kindly Reicht Heynes began to weep for companionship, but her mistress chided her gently. "Well, you are a pretty one for a sickroom," she remarked, employing a wealth of innocent artistry to cheer the patient, achieving some modest success. An old woman who has witnessed life's trials is a sovereign blessing beside a sorrowful young woman. Knowing what to say and what to avoid, how to soothe and engage, she soon had Margaret resting her head on her shoulder rather than the pillow, her soft eyes gazing upward with gentle gratitude.

"Ahh! This is hair," the old lady murmured, running her fingers through it. "Come, Reicht, let us admire it!"

Reicht approached, handling it and praising it sincerely. The poor owner, not entirely immune to flattery due to her proximity to death, admitted, "In truth, madam, I used to think it hideous; but he praised it, and ever since, I have been almost vain of it, saints forgive me. You know how foolish those are who love."

"They are greater fools who do not," the old lady replied sharply.
