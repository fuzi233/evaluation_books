We have witnessed Gwendolen Harleth spending her time abroad swept up in a fresh fervor for gambling, deluding herself into the role of an Empress of Luck. From these chaotic experiences, she emerged with a vague conviction that in this bewildered world, the actions of individuals mattered little; the sole purpose seemed to be amusement. Furthermore, we observed how certain figures, shrouded in mystery as "Grapnell & Co.," sought to reign within the kingdom of chance and pursue their own diversions regardless of cost. Their machinations resulted in a painful alteration of her family's financial standing. Consequently, she returned home, carrying against her will a necklace she had pawned—which someone else subsequently redeemed.

As she journeyed back to England, Grandcourt was approaching to locate her. However, he approached in his characteristic fashion: not with the urgency of an express train racing directly from Diplow to Leubronn, where her whereabouts were believed to be, but with such deliberate slowness that he allowed himself to be detained by Russian acquaintances at Baden-Baden. He made various appointments with them, only to break them later when his desire to reach Leubronn became paramount.

Grandcourt's passions were of an intermittent, flickering nature; they rarely burned with intense flame. Yet, vast amounts of life proceed without such strong emotion. Countless cravats are meticulously tied, countless dinners attended, and countless speeches delivered proposing the health of august dignitaries—all devoid of the zest born of deep desire. A man can maintain a formidable appearance in high society, feigning classical erudition, scientific reserve, political conviction, and the full sentimentality of the English gentleman, all at a minimal expenditure of vital energy. Moreover, he can remain obstinate or persistent at a low level, occasionally displaying sudden impulses that possess a deceptive aura of demonic strength simply because they appear inexplicable. Often, their true origin lies merely in a lack of regulated channels for the soul to move; without the good and sufficient ducts of habit, human nature easily turns to mere ooze and mud, yielding nothing but a spurt or a puddle under pressure.

Grandcourt was not entirely displeased by Gwendolen's flight from the splendid opportunity he had presented. The act possessed a certain piquancy for him. He preferred to attribute it to resentment regarding his somewhat casual treatment of Cardell Chase—an oversight that, upon reflection, appeared rather cool. To have brought her so close to a tender admission, only to see her walk headlong away from further opportunities of securing her consent, was precisely the kind of provocation that would rouse a spirited girl. And if she were worthy of his mastery, she ought to possess such spirit. Undoubtedly, she expected him to follow, just as he intended to. Yet, for a full week, he took no steps toward departure and did not even inquire where Miss Harleth had gone.

Mr. Lush felt a triumph mixed with profound distrust. Grandcourt had uttered not a word to him regarding her absence and maintained a neutrality akin to that of an alligator; one could never predict what might churn slowly within his mind. Nevertheless, delaying a decision created space for the dissipation of Grandcourt's energy. The guests at Diplow felt a growing curiosity surpassing that of their host. How could nothing more be heard of Miss Harleth? Could it be credible that she had refused Mr. Grandcourt? Lady Flora Hollis, a lively middle-aged woman rich in curiosity, suddenly felt compelled to embark on a round of calls with Mrs. Torrington, visiting the rectory, Offendene, and Quetcham. Through this tour, she not only doubled her efforts but also gleaned from the Arrowpoints that Miss Harleth had departed for Leubronn to join some old friends, the Baron and Baroness von Langen. The immediate agitation and disappointment of Mrs. Davilow and the Gascoignes had crystallized into a collective wish that Gwendolen's disappearance should not be interpreted as eccentric or requiring secrecy.

Indeed, the rector's mind harbored the possibility that the marriage was merely postponed, for Mrs. Davilow had hesitated to reveal the bitter determination with which Gwendolen had spoken. Despite his practical abilities, some of his experience had hardened into maxims and quotations. As Amaryllis fled, she desired her hiding place be known; and in this age of steam, the proverb that love finds a way "over the mountain and over the wave" requires no hyperbole. Gwendolen, he reasoned, was an Amaryllis of excellent sense but coquettish daring; the question remained whether she had dared too much.

Lady Flora, returning laden with news of Miss Harleth, saw no reason why she should not attempt to electrify Mr. Grandcourt by mentioning it during dinner, subtly hinting that rumors had spread of him being a disappointed suitor. Grandcourt listened with quietude but keen attention. The following day, he instructed Lush to arrange a plausible pretext for dispersing the party at Diplow within another week, declaring his intention to yacht to the Baltic or elsewhere. It was impossible to remain at Diplow as if under house arrest with a group of people he had never desired. Lush required no clearer signal that Grandcourt was heading to Leubronn, though he might approach the destination like a creeping billiard ball, sticking to the path. Mr. Lush intended to render himself indispensable so he might accompany his patron, succeeding in his endeavor. Gwendolen's repulsion for him was a fact that only amused his master, making him all the more willing to keep Lush perpetually at hand.

Thus, it came to pass that Grandcourt arrived at the *Czarina* five days after Gwendolen had left Leubronn, finding there his uncle, Sir Hugo Mallinger, along with his family, including Daniel Deronda. It is not necessarily a pleasure for either the reigning power or the heir presumptive when their separate affairs—a touch of gout in one, a touch of willfulness in the other—conspire to bring them to the same spot. Sir Hugo was a man of easy temper, tolerant of differences and defects. However, a differing viewpoint regarding the settlement of family estates fretted him considerably more than disputes over church discipline or voting rights; faults were less forgivable when committed by someone whose very existence was inconvenient.

In no scenario could Grandcourt have been a nephew to Sir Hugo's liking. Yet, as the presumptive heir to the Mallinger estates, he represented the sign and embodiment of the baronet's chief grievance: the lack of a son to inherit the lands, in no portion of which had Sir Hugo held more than a life-interest. An ill-advised settlement by his father, Sir Francis, had left Diplow, with its modest acreage, under the same restrictive conditions as the ancient and extensive inheritance of the two Toppings. Diplow, where Sir Hugo had hunted and lived through many seasons in his youth, and where his wife and daughters ought to have retired after his death, was bound by these terms. This grievance grew sharper with the passing years. Lady Mallinger, after bearing three daughters in quick succession, had remained childless for eight years, now past forty, without producing another girl. Meanwhile, Sir Hugo, nearly twenty years older, found himself at an age when, despite the fashionable delay of everything from dinners to marriages, a man's hopefulness begins to show signs of weariness unless restored by a second childhood.

Having begun to despair of a son, the confirmation of Grandcourt's interest in the estates made his presence increasingly unwelcome. On the other hand, it prompted Sir Hugo to ensure their relationship remained as friendly as possible. This led him to dwell on a plan that had matured alongside his disappointment: to secure Diplow as a future residence for Lady Mallinger and her daughters, thereby keeping this pleasant fragment of family inheritance for his own potential offspring. His knowledge of his nephew's disposition encouraged the belief that Grandcourt might consent to a transaction granting him a substantial sum of ready money in exchange for his prospective interest in the domain and its associated land. Should an unhoped-for son eventually be born, the money would have been wasted, and Grandcourt would have been compensated for relinquishing interests that proved worthless; yet Sir Hugo dismissed this risk as negligible. Of late, he had managed his fortune wisely through mining operations and lease sales, preparing himself for such an outlay.

Here lay an objective that made him cautious of quarreling with Grandcourt. Years prior, while improving the Abbey and needing Grandcourt's concurrence to fell an obstructive mass of timber on the demesne, Sir Hugo had congratulated himself on discovering no active spite in his nephew's peculiar mind. Nothing had since occurred to deepen their hatred beyond what was compatible with perfect politeness or strict mutual accommodation.

From Grandcourt's perspective, his uncle was a superfluity and a bore; he felt that the list of general grievances would improve once Sir Hugo was expunged. However, informed by Lush—a useful intermediary—of the baronet's inclinations regarding Diplow, Grandcourt was gratified to have the option of accepting money. Even if he deemed it unlikely he would choose it, his sense of power would be flattered by his ability to refuse what Sir Hugo desired. These hinted transactions influenced his motives for requesting a year's tenancy of Diplow, which had annoyed Sir Hugo to grant, especially since the excellent hunting nearby might deter Grandcourt from parting with his chance of future possession. A man possessing two locations, one offering inferior hunting, naturally desires a third where the game is better.
