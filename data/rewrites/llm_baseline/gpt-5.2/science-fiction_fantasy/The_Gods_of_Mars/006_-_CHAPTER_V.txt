I cannot tell how long I lay on the storeroom floor—stone hard, smelling of old grain-dust and dried roots, of oil that had once been rubbed into weapons and never quite faded from the pores of the walls. Sleep came in ragged mouthfuls, like stolen bites snatched in a famine, and with it the dull ache of my wounds. Then sound tore through the darkness: an abrupt, ugly clamor—cries, the sharp metallic bark of rifles, and the long after-ring of the shots chasing themselves down the subterranean corridors until the echoes stacked and multiplied like clattering pans in a cavernous kitchen.

I sprang up as the fusillade ceased.

In the great doorway opposite stood a dozen lesser therns with rifles lowered, their white faces pinched between triumph and alarm, as though the very noise they had made accused them. Around me lay the bodies of our companions—strewn like butchered game after a careless slaughter—still, twisted, already cooling on the bare stone. Only Thuvia of Ptarth and Tars Tarkas had lived through the first raking fire, as I had, because we had slept flat upon the floor where the bullets passed high.

The therns stared at me, and I felt, as plainly as the weight of the diadem upon my brow, that I must become Sator Throg again in an instant or die as myself.

“What means this?” I roared, putting rage into my voice the way a cook throws salt into thin stew to give it body. “Is Sator Throg to be murdered by his own vassals?”

The nearest of them—one with a narrow chin and eyes too quick—half fell to his knees. “Have mercy, O Master of the Tenth Cycle!” he cried, and the others edged backward as though the threshold itself might swallow them and hide them from my wrath.

Thuvia, close at my elbow, breathed, “Ask them their mission here.”

“What do you here?” I demanded. “Speak!”

“Two from the outer world are at large within the dominions of the therns,” the fellow answered. “We sought them at the command of the Father of Therns. One was white with black hair; the other a huge green warrior,” and he let his gaze flick to Tars Tarkas with a suspicion that was almost hunger.

Thuvia stepped forward with the calm of one who has fed lions from her hand. “Here, then, is one of them,” she said, indicating the Thark. “And if you will look upon this dead man by the door perhaps you will recognize the other. It was left for Sator Throg and his poor slaves to accomplish what the guard could not—we have killed one and captured the other; for this had Sator Throg given us our liberty. And now in your stupidity you have come and killed all but myself, and like to have killed the mighty Sator Throg himself.”

The therns shifted, abashed, the way underlings do when they realize they have burned the very meal they were meant to serve.

“Had they not better throw these bodies to the plant men and then return to their quarters, O Mighty One?” Thuvia asked me, as though she were suggesting the clearing of refuse before it soured the air.

“Yes,” I said. “Do as Thuvia bids you.”

They moved among the dead, lifting them with the brisk efficiency of men accustomed to carrying meat from a kill. When one stooped over the body of Sator Throg himself, he hesitated; his fingers tightened, and I saw the tiny change in his face as his eyes measured that bald, shining dome, the set of the mouth, the angle of jaw that did not match the title I wore. He stole a glance at me—sideways, like a thief checking a doorway—then back down at the corpse. He said nothing, but as he rose, a faint curl touched his lips.

Again, as he bore Sator Throg away, he turned his head just enough to take me in from the corner of his eye. The last glimpse I had of his profile as he passed from sight was of a smile—quick, cunning, satisfied, like a man who has found a spice another has forgotten and knows he can sell it dear.

Then they were gone, and the storeroom felt emptier than before. The air held the raw, coppery scent of blood, and beneath it the stale dryness of stored provisions that had never been meant for mouths like ours. Tars Tarkas stood very still, massive as a pillar. Thuvia’s hand trembled once and then steadied at her side.

The therns’ marksmanship had erased our companions’ slim chance of reaching the world above. As soon as the last footsteps died down the corridor, Thuvia urged us onward.

“She noted him,” I saw; she had watched the bearer of Sator Throg as one listens for the first wrong note in a familiar song.

“It bodes no good for us, O Prince,” she said softly. “Even though he dared not accuse you in error, there be those above with power sufficient to demand closer scrutiny. And that would indeed prove fatal.”

I shrugged, though the motion pulled at my wounds. Pain traveled through me with every breath, and weakness from blood loss made the world tilt a fraction, like a tray held by a tired hand. No salves, no green Martian ointments here—none of the resinous, sharp-smelling balms that could knit torn flesh in an hour and leave a man ravenous for real food again. In this place there was only stone, old air, and the faint mineral tang of radium lamps.

Hopelessness came over me—thick as the darkness between Mars’ suns and moons. Yet the long yellow hair of the Holy Thern, blown by a stray draft, brushed my cheek, and with it came the reminder that disguise was still a tool in my hand, like a knife not yet dulled.

“If we act quickly,” I said, “we may still slip out before the alarm is general. What will that fellow do first, Thuvia? How long before they return for us?”

“He will go directly to the Father of Therns—old Matai Shang,” she replied. “He may have to wait for an audience, but since he is high among the lesser therns—as a thorian among them—it will not be long. If Matai Shang believes him, another hour will see the galleries and chambers, the courts and gardens, filled with searchers.”

“Then what we do must be done within an hour. What is the shortest way out of this celestial Hades?”

“Straight to the top of the cliffs, Prince,” she said, “and then through the gardens to the inner courts. From there our way lies within the temples and across them to the outer court. Then the ramparts.” Her voice tightened. “O Prince, it is hopeless. Ten thousand warriors could not hew a way to liberty from out this awful place.

“Since the beginning of time, little by little, stone by stone, have the therns been ever adding to the defences of their stronghold. A continuous line of impregnable fortifications circles the outer slopes of the Mountains of Otz. Within the temples behind the ramparts a million fighting-men are ever ready. The courts and gardens are filled with slaves, women, and children. None could go a stone’s throw without detection.”

“If there is no other way,” I said, “why dwell upon the difficulties? We must face them.”

Tars Tarkas’s deep voice rumbled. “Can we not better make the attempt after dark? There would seem to be no chance by day.”

“There would be a little better chance by night,” Thuvia answered, “but even then the ramparts are well guarded—possibly better than by day. There are fewer abroad in the courts and gardens, though.”

“What is the hour?” I asked.

“It was midnight when you released me from my chains,” she said. “Two hours later we reached the storeroom. There you slept for fourteen hours. It must now be nearly sundown again. Come—we will go to a nearby window in the cliff and make sure.”

She led us through winding corridors where the air changed from dry storage-staleness to the colder, cleaner scent of stone that has never known sunlight. At a sudden turn we came upon an opening that overlooked the Valley Dor.

The sun was setting to our right—a huge red orb sinking behind the western range of Otz. The light struck the eastern banks of Korus and turned them to a painted feast of color: crimson sward like spilled wine, forests glowing as though each leaf had been glazed. Yet the beauty carried the wrong music—too much silence, broken only by distant cries and the faint, sickly chorus of feeding.

A little below us a Holy Thern stood watch upon his balcony. His scarlet robe was drawn tight against the cold that comes like a blade on Mars the moment heat begins to drain away. Here the thin air holds little warmth; it is scorching under the sun and bitter when the sun is gone, with no gentle twilight to soften the change. The light does not linger; it vanishes as if someone snuffs a lamp, and the world is plunged into black.

Beneath the trees we saw herds of plant men feeding—adults standing tall upon toes and mighty tails, talons pruning every reachable leaf and twig with a methodical, rasping efficiency. Then I understood at last why the forest had seemed trimmed and kept, like a park tended by careful hands. It was tended—by mouths and claws.

Our eyes moved to the rolling Iss issuing from the base of the cliffs below. Presently a canoe slid out from the mountain, laden with lost souls. A dozen red Martians—highly civilized, richly accoutered even in ruin—crowded its narrow belly. They stepped onto the sward as though arriving at a festival they did not yet understand.

The herald below saw them as we did. He leaned out over his low rail and gave voice to a shrill, weird wail—a sound that tore at the nerves, a call that did not belong to any wholesome soundscape of wind through reed or water over stone. It was a signal meant to summon predators, and as it rang out it seemed to pollute the valley itself, turning the hush into a listening trap.

For an instant the plant men stood with ears stiff, then they poured from the grove toward the riverbank in great ungainly leaps. The party tried to defend itself—briefly, desperately—steel flashing once, twice, then swallowed beneath a mass of repulsive shapes. Silence followed, but it was not peace; it was only the sudden muffling of voices under bodies. Scores of sucking mouths fastened to flesh, and the sound of it carried faintly even up to us: wet, busy, obscene, like a kitchen where the work is all wrong.

I turned away, bile rising. Thuvia’s face was pale in the dying light.

“Their part is soon over,” she said. “The great white apes get the flesh when the plant men have drained the arteries. Look—they are coming now.”

Across the valley a dozen great white monsters ran toward the riverbank. Then the sun slipped beneath the horizon and darkness—so abrupt it could almost be touched—fell upon us. One moment the valley was a table spread in lurid color; the next it was a black bowl waiting.

Thuvia wasted no time. She led us back into the corridors, toward the passage that winds up through the cliffs to the surface thousands of feet above. The radium bulbs set far apart along the roof gave a cold, dim light—no warm flicker, no comforting crackle, only a thin glow like the aftertaste of metal.

Twice great banths wandered loose through the galleries and blocked our progress, their manes bristling, their breath a rank heat in the cool air. Each time Thuvia spoke a low word of command, and the snarling beasts slunk aside, sullen as dogs scolded away from a larder.

“If you can dissolve all our obstacles as easily as you master these fierce brutes,” I said, forcing a smile, “I can see no difficulties in our way. How do you do it?”

She laughed once—quickly—and then shuddered, as though the sound of her own laughter startled her in that place.

“I do not quite know,” she said. “When first I came here I angered Sator Throg because I repulsed him. He ordered me thrown into one of the great pits in the inner gardens. It was filled with banths. In my own country I had been accustomed to command. Something in my voice—something in my manner—cowed the beasts as they sprang to attack me. Instead of tearing me to pieces, as Sator Throg desired, they fawned at my feet.

“So greatly were Sator Throg and his friends amused that they kept me to train and handle the terrible creatures. I know them all by name. There are many wandering through these lower regions. They are scavengers. Many prisoners die here in their chains. The banths solve the problem of sanitation—in this respect.

“In the gardens and temples above they are kept in pits. The therns fear them. It is because of the banths that they seldom venture below ground except as their duties call them.”

Her words struck a spark in my mind. “Why not take a number of banths and set them loose before us above ground?” I asked. “If they run ahead, they may scatter guards and slaves alike.”

Thuvia’s eyes flashed. She laughed again, this time with something like grim delight. “It would distract attention from us, I am sure.”

As we climbed, she began calling in a low singsong voice—half purr, half chant—like a lullaby sung in a dialect the beasts alone remembered. The sound traveled strangely in those corridors; it did not echo harshly like gunfire, but seemed to settle into the stone and then rise again from somewhere behind, as though the tunnels themselves had learned the melody long ago. It reminded me that languages can survive in hidden places, even when men forget them—yet here the surviving tongue was one of command and hunger.

Soft padded feet sounded close behind us. I turned and saw a pair of green eyes shining from the dark. From a diverging tunnel a tawny shape crept toward us, sinew and stealth. Low growls and snarls answered Thuvia’s call from every side. One by one the banths joined, and to each she spoke a word—name, order, reassurance—until the beasts moved with us like a pack trained to heel.

Soon we were entirely surrounded by some fifty of them. Two walked close on either side of Thuvia like guards escorting a queen. Others brushed against my naked limbs, their fur slick and warm, their breath thick with the sour-sweet stink of meat. Now and then I caught the hungry side-glance they gave Tars Tarkas and me, as though our bodies were walking provisions.

It was a strange procession: the almost noiseless pad of bare feet and paws; the golden walls splashed with precious stones that caught and fractured the radium glow; the dim, clinical light overhead; the low constant accompaniment of growl and breath; the mighty green warrior towering above all; myself crowned with the diadem of a Holy Thern; and leading us, Thuvia—slender, steady, her voice the only thing holding back fifty throats.

Presently we approached a great chamber more brightly lighted. Thuvia halted us. Quietly she stole forward, glanced within, then motioned us after her.

The room was filled with specimens of the underworld’s cruel harvest: a heterogeneous collection of hybrids—the offspring of prisoners from the outer world, red and green and thern mingled. Constant confinement below ground had worked odd changes upon their skins; they resembled corpses more than living beings. Many were deformed, others maimed, and the majority, Thuvia whispered, were sightless. They lay sprawled about the floor, sometimes overlapping, sometimes in heaps, as though life had been poured out and left to congeal.

The banths’ nostrils flared. Their hunger thickened the air.

Picking our way carefully, we threaded a winding path across the chamber. The beasts sniffed at the helpless bodies as though at meat laid out too conveniently. The sight struck me with the grotesque force of old illustrations I had once seen of Dante’s Inferno—yet here there was no artistry to distance the horror, no poetic veil, only the physical fact of suffering reduced to a mute pile. Whatever languages these wretches had once spoken—whatever hearth-songs and market-calls and names for bread and broth—had been swallowed by this underground silence. Their mouths were open sometimes, but no word lived there.

Several times we passed entrances to other chambers similarly peopled, and twice again we were compelled to cross directly through them. In others were chained prisoners and beasts, iron biting into flesh, the faint metallic scent of rust and old sweat rising like a bitter steam.

“Why is it we see no therns?” I asked, my voice low now, as though loud speech might curdle the air.

“They seldom traverse the underworld at night,” Thuvia replied, “for then the great banths prowl the dim corridors seeking their prey. The therns fear the denizens of this cruel world they have fostered beneath their feet. The prisoners even sometimes turn upon them and rend them. The thern can never tell from what dark shadow an assassin may spring upon his back.

“By day it is different. Then the corridors and chambers are filled with guards; slaves from the temples above come by hundreds to the granaries and storerooms. All is life then. You did not see it because I led you not in the beaten tracks, but through roundabout passages seldom used. Yet it is possible we may meet a thern even yet. They do occasionally find it necessary to come here after the sun has set. Because of this I have moved with such caution.”

At last we reached the upper galleries without detection. The air changed—fresher, carrying the faint damp-green promise of growing things. Thuvia halted us at the foot of a short, steep ascent.

“Above us,” she said, “is a doorway which opens on to the inner gardens. I have brought you thus far. From here on for four miles to the outer ramparts our way will be beset by countless dangers. Guards patrol the courts, the temples, the gardens. Every inch of the ramparts themselves is beneath the eye of a sentry.”

I could not reconcile such vigilance with the superstition that cloaked this place. Who would dare come here to attack them? What enemy could pierce such mystery and stone?

I voiced the question as we climbed. Thuvia put her hand to the door.

“They fear the black pirates of Barsoom, O Prince,” she said, “from whom may our first ancestors preserve us.”

She swung the door open.

The smell of growing things greeted my nostrils—soil, leaf, night-blooming vines, the cool mineral sweetness of water. Cold air flowed across my cheek, clean after the underground staleness. Under the lurid light of the nearer moon the gardens lay spread—paths, shadows, temple roofs like pale cliffs.

The great banths sniffed the unfamiliar odors, their bodies tensing as if the new scents were seasoning to their hunger. Then, with a sudden rush, they broke past us and swarmed out across the gardens, low growls rolling ahead of them like distant thunder.

And at once—like a sour note struck in a sacred chant—a great cry arose from the roofs of the temples: alarm, warning, a sound taken up from point to point, running east and west from temple to court to rampart until it dwindled into dim echoes in the distance.

Tars Tarkas’s long-sword leaped from its scabbard with a whisper of steel.

Thuvia shrank, shuddering, to my side.
