The ten days that followed our ordeal in the treasure chamber were marked by a peculiar, almost surreal normality. We returned to our quarters at Loo, physically intact save for the subtle erosion of time and trauma: my stubble emerged from the cave three shades grayer than it entered, and Good carried within him a permanent shadow cast by Foulata's passing. Her death moved him profoundly, yet, viewing the matter through the weary lens of a seasoned traveler, I deemed her removal a mercy. An entanglement between Good and such a creature—a being of stately, unearthly beauty and refined intellect—would have been a disaster. As she herself had poignantly asked, "Can the sun mate with the darkness, or the white with the black?" Their union was an impossibility written into the fabric of existence.

Our quest for the exit concluded with resignation. Exhausted from our forty-eight-hour recovery, we descended into the great pit hoping to locate the aperture through which we had escaped, but the earth had conspired against us. Rain had washed away every trace of our spoor, and the cavern walls, a labyrinth of jagged geology, were riddled with the burrows of ant-eaters and other denizens of the deep. Which tunnel had saved us became a mystery we could never solve. On our final reconnaissance of the stalactite cave, driven by a restless ache, we ventured once more into the Chamber of the Dead. Standing beneath the spear of the White Death, we gazed at the immovable mass of rock that sealed our fate, contemplating the priceless wealth beyond, the crushed remnants of the old hag Gagool, and the fair girl whose tomb served as the portal. We searched frantically for the seam of the sliding door, for the secret mechanism that operated it, but the silence of the stone was absolute. It was a marvel of engineering, possessing a massive, inscrutable simplicity characteristic of the age that forged it, a device unmatched anywhere else on Earth.

In disgust, we abandoned the search. Even if the rock had miraculously shifted before our eyes, revealing the path to the treasure, I doubted we possessed the courage to step over Gagool's mangled remains to claim the diamonds. Yet, the thought of leaving behind the greatest accumulation of wealth in human history brought tears to my eyes. Tens of millions of pounds' worth of jewels lay in three stone coffers, destined to remain cold companions to Foulata's bones until the end of time. Dynamite alone could breach the five feet of solid granite separating us from paradise.

We departed with heavy hearts, only to discover, too late, that our disappointment was unfounded. By a stroke of luck prior to our imprisonment, I had stuffed the pockets of my shooting coat and trousers with gems, alongside Foulata's basket, which held double the volume despite the water bottle occupying space. Though several large stones tumbled out during our perilous descent, an enormous hoard remained. We possessed enough to render us not merely millionaires in the American sense, but owners of the three finest gem sets in Europe. We had not fared so poorly.

Upon our arrival at Loo, we were greeted with cordial warmth by Ignosi, who appeared robust and busy consolidating his power, reorganizing the regiments ravaged by the war with Twala. He listened with rapt attention to our tale, his expression growing somber as we recounted Gagool's gruesome end. Summoning an ancient Induna, a counselor who had lived since the days of Ignosi's great-grandfather, the King questioned the old man about the witch-doctoress. The elder confirmed that Gagaoola had been as wizened, ugly, and wicked in youth as she was in death. With her demise, Ignosi declared, an ancient curse was lifted from the land. He rejoiced that she would not have allowed us to perish in the dark place, nor would she have succeeded in killing him to install Twala, the man her black heart favored.

When I finally addressed the necessity of our departure, asking Ignosi to fulfill his promise of justice and lawful rule, the King's demeanor shifted. Covering his face with his hands, he spoke of a heart torn in twain. "What have I done to you, Incubu, Macumazahn, and Bougwan, that ye should leave me desolate?" he lamented. He offered everything: wives chosen from his maidens, land stretching to the horizon, cattle, game, and the loyalty of his Impis. But we declined, stating simply that we sought our own place.

This rejection struck Ignosi with bitter clarity. "Now do I learn," he flashed, "that ye love the bright stones more than me, your friend." He cursed the white stones and those who sought them, declaring that death awaited anyone who stepped into the Place of Death to find them. Yet, for us three, the path remained open forever. We were dearer to him than any breathing thing.

Infadoos, his uncle, and his Induna would guide us across the mountains via a lesser-known pass, hidden behind the towering shapes of Sheba's Breasts. In a moment of profound sorrow, Ignosi decreed that our names, Incubu, Macumazahn, and Bougwan, should become *hlonipa*—names forbidden to be spoken, reserved for the dead kings, ensuring our memory would be preserved eternally in the land. He urged us to depart before his eyes overflowed with tears, recalling our shared battles: how Macumazahn had been the point of the horn that galled Twala's flank, how Bougwan had stood in the ring of the Greys, and how Incubu had shattered the wild bull's strength.

Covering his face with the corner of his kaross, Ignosi watched us silently as we prepared to leave. The next dawn, under the watchful eyes of a heartbroken Infadoos and a regiment of Buffaloes, we marched out of Loo. The entire town lined the streets, showering us with flowers and blessings for having liberated them from Twala. Amidst this solemn grandeur, a ludicrous incident occurred just before we crossed the town limits. A young girl, carrying lovely lilies, ran to Good, captivated by his solitary whisker and eyeglasses, and requested a boon: to see his beautiful white legs. Excited and obstinate, Good initially refused, deeming it indecent, but under the pressure of Sir Henry's persuasion and the rapturous admiration of the crowd, he reluctantly drew up his trousers to the knee. Thus, in a guise of exposed calves, he walked until we cleared the town, a spectacle that ensured his legs would never again be so greatly admired, unlike his melting teeth or transparent eye, which the Kukuanas had already grown tired of.

As we traveled, Infadoos revealed the strategic advantage of our new route. Rather than traversing the treacherous Solomon's Great Road, we would descend a precipitous chasm separated from the desert by the cliffs of Sheba's Breasts. Legend held that ostrich hunters had recently used this path to reach a vast, fertile oasis, escaping the rigors of the mountain pass. Guided by these hunters, we pressed onward, traveling easily until the fourth night, when we found ourselves atop the crest of the mountains dividing Kukuanaland from the desert. Below us, the sand rolled in billowing waves, and ahead lay the precipice, waiting to drop us two thousand feet onto the plain.
