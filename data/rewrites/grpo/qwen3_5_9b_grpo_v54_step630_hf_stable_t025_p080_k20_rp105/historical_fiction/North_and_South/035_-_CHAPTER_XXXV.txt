"There is no thread so fine,
But it must reach the sun."

Mr. Thornton sat on and on. He sensed that his presence brought a quiet comfort to Mr. Hale, and he was moved by the silent, pleading wish for him to stay a little longer—the soft, persistent "Do not go yet" that his weary friend voiced from time to time. He wondered why Margaret did not return, yet he lingered not with the hope of seeing her. In this hour, and in the company of a man who so acutely felt the emptiness of earthly things, he remained reasonable and self-possessed. He listened with deep interest to everything his father-in-law said.

"How death, and the heavy silence,
And the mind that grows dull."

It was remarkable how Mr. Thornton's presence compelled Mr. Hale to unlock secrets he kept hidden even from Margaret. Perhaps it was because her sympathy would be so keen and vivid that he feared the emotional toll upon himself; perhaps it was because his speculative nature demanded resolution for every doubt, and he knew she would recoil from expressing such uncertainties—or from him, as someone capable of conceiving them. Whatever the reason, he found it easier to unburden his frozen thoughts, fears, and fancies to Mr. Thornton than to anyone else. Mr. Thornton spoke little, yet every sentence deepened Mr. Hale's trust and affection. When Mr. Hale hesitated over a remembered agony, Mr. Thornton's few words completed the thought, revealing how deeply he understood its meaning. When a doubt or fear wandered aimlessly, its eyes blinded by tears, Mr. Thornton did not recoil; instead, having passed through that very stage of thought himself, he guided the speaker toward the precise ray of light that would illuminate the darkness. Though a man of action, busy in the world's great struggles, there was a profound religion binding him to God in his heart, transcending his wilfulness and mistakes, a depth Mr. Hale had never imagined. They never spoke of such matters again, but this single conversation transformed them into peculiar souls for one another, knitting them together in a bond that loose, indiscriminate talk on sacred things could never achieve.

While this unfolded, Margaret lay as still and pale as death on the study floor. She had succumbed to her burden. It had been heavy in weight and long carried; she had been meek and patient until, suddenly, her faith broke, and she groped in vain for help. There was a pitiful contraction of suffering upon her beautiful brow, though no other sign of consciousness remained. Her mouth, which had recently projected sullenly in defiance, was now relaxed and livid.

"As from her lips a gentle spirit moves,
Full of love, whispering to the soul: 'Sigh!'"

The first sign of returning life was a quiver about the lips—a mute, soundless attempt at speech—but her eyes remained closed, and the trembling sank back into stillness. Then, leaning feebly on her arms to steady herself, Margaret gathered her strength and rose. Her comb had fallen from her hair. With an instinctive desire to erase the traces of weakness and restore order, she searched for it, sitting down periodically to regain her strength. Her head drooped forward; her hands lay meekly one upon the other. She tried to recall the force of the temptation that had thrown her into such terror, attempting to remember the details that had caused her dread, but she could not. She grasped only two facts: that Frederick had been in danger of being pursued and detected in London, not merely for manslaughter but as the unpardonable leader of the mutiny, and that she had lied to save him. There was one solace: her lie had bought him time. If the inspector returned tomorrow, after she received the letter she longed for to confirm her brother's safety, she would face her bitter penance—she, the lofty Margaret—acknowledging before a crowded justice hall, if necessary, that she had been "a dog, and done this thing." But if he came before she heard from Frederick; if he returned, as he had half-threatened, within hours! Then she would tell the lie again. Though she did not know how the words would emerge after such a terrible pause for reflection and self-reproach without betraying her falsehood, she knew that repeating it would gain time—time for Frederick.

She was roused by Dixon's entrance; she had just been letting Mr. Thornton out.

He had hardly taken ten steps into the street when a passing omnibus stopped beside him, and a man alighted, approaching him with a respectful nod. It was the police inspector.

Mr. Thornton had secured his first position in the police force and had heard updates on his protégé, but they had rarely met, and initially, Mr. Thornton did not recognize him.

"My name is Watson—George Watson, sir, the one you employed—"

"Ah, yes! I hear you are progressing famously."

"Yes, sir. I owe you thanks. But I approached you regarding a small matter of business. I believe you were the magistrate who took the deposition of the poor man who died in the Infirmary last night."

"Yes," replied Mr. Thornton. "I heard a rambling statement, which the clerk deemed of little use. I'm afraid he was drunk, though there is no doubt he died by violence. One of my mother's servants was engaged to him, I believe, and she is in great distress today. What about him?"

"Why, sir, his death is strangely connected with someone in the house I saw you leaving just now; it was Mr. Hales's, I think."

"Yes!" said Mr. Thornton, turning sharply to look into the inspector's face with sudden interest. "What about it?"

"Well, sir, it appears I have a fairly clear chain of evidence implicating a gentleman who was walking with Miss Hale that night at Outwood station as the man who struck or pushed Leonards off the platform, causing his death. But the young lady denies she was there at the time."

"Miss Hale denies she was there!" repeated Mr. Thornton, his voice altered. "Tell me, what evening was it? What time?"

"About six o'clock, on the evening of Thursday, the twenty-sixth."

They walked on in silence for a minute or two. The inspector spoke first.

"You see, sir, there is likely to be a coroner's inquest. I have a young man who is quite positive—at least, he was at first; since hearing of the young lady's denial, he says he wouldn't like to swear. Still, he is convinced he saw Miss Hale at the station, walking with a gentleman, not five minutes before the time when a porter witnessed a scuffle. He attributed it to some of Leonards' impudence, but it led to the fall that caused his death. Seeing you come out of the very house, sir, I thought I might ask if... well, it's always awkward dealing with cases of disputed identity, and one doesn't like doubting the word of a respectable young woman without strong proof to the contrary."

"And she denied having been at the station that evening!" repeated Mr. Thornton in a low, brooding tone.

"Yes, sir, twice over, as distinctly as could be. I told her I would call again, but seeing you just as I was returning from questioning the young man who claimed it was her, I thought I would seek your advice, both as the magistrate who saw Leonards on his deathbed and as the gentleman who got me my place in the force."

"You were quite right," said Mr. Thornton. "Do not take any steps until you have seen me again."

"The young lady expects me to call, from what I said."

"I only wish to delay you an hour. It is now three. Come to my warehouse at four."

"Very well, sir!"

They parted. Mr. Thornton hurried to his warehouse, sternly ordering his clerks to admit no interruptions, then went to his private room and locked the door. He indulged himself in the torture of reviewing every detail. How could he have lulled himself into the unsuspicious calm in which her tearful image had mirrored itself not two hours prior, until he had weakly pitied her and yearned toward her, forgetting the savage, distrustful jealousy that the sight of her—and that unknown to him—at such an hour, in such a place, had inspired? How could one so pure stoop from her decorous and noble bearing? But was it decorous—was it? He hated himself for the fleeting idea that forced itself upon him, thrilling him with its old potency of attraction toward her image. And then this falsehood—how terrible must be the dread of shame to be revealed! For, after all, the provocation given by a man like Leonards, when excited by drink, might, in all probability, be enough to justify anyone stating the circumstances openly! How creeping and deadly that fear which could bow the truthful Margaret to falsehood! He could almost pity her. What would be the end? She could not have considered all she was entering upon; if there was an inquest and the young man came forward. Suddenly, he started up. There should be no inquest. He would save Margaret. He would take the responsibility of preventing the inquest, the outcome of which, from the uncertain medical testimony he had vaguely heard the night before, could only be doubtful. The doctors had discovered an advanced internal disease, sure to prove fatal; they had stated that death might have been accelerated by the fall or by the subsequent drinking and exposure to cold. If he had known how Margaret would become involved, if he had foreseen that he would stain her whiteness with a falsehood, he could have saved her with a word. The question of inquest or no inquest had hung trembling in the balance only the night before. Miss Hale might love another, be indifferent and contemptuous to him—but he would yet perform faithful acts of service she would never know. He might despise her, but the woman he had once loved should be kept from shame; and shame it would be to pledge herself to a lie in a public court, or to stand and acknowledge her reason for desiring darkness rather than light.

Very gray and stern did Mr. Thornton look as he passed through his wondering clerks. He was away for about half an hour; scarcely less stern did he appear when he returned, though his errand had been successful.

He wrote two lines on a slip of paper, put it in an envelope, and sealed it. Giving it to a clerk, he said:

"I appointed Watson—he who was a packer in the warehouse and went into the police—to call on me at four o'clock. I have just met a gentleman from Liverpool who wishes to see me before he leaves town. Take care to give this note to Watson when he calls."

The note read:

"There will be no inquest. Medical evidence not sufficient to justify it. Take no further steps. I have not seen the coroner; but I will take the responsibility."

"Well," thought Watson, "it relieves me from an awkward job. None of my witnesses seemed certain of anything except the young woman. She was clear and distinct; the porter at the railroad had seen a scuffle! Or when he realized it might bring him in as a witness, perhaps it wasn't a scuffle, only larking, and Leonards might have jumped off himself; he wouldn't stick to anything. And Jennings, the grocer's shopman—well, he wasn't quite so bad, but I doubt if I could get him to an oath after hearing Miss Hale flatly deny it. It would have been a troublesome job with no satisfaction. Now I must go and tell them they won't be needed."

He accordingly presented himself again at Mr. Hale's that evening. Her father and Dixon would fain have persuaded Margaret to go to bed, but neither knew the reason for her continued refusal. Dixon had learned part of the truth—but only part. Margaret would not tell any human being what she had said, nor reveal the fatal termination of Leonards' fall. So Dixon's curiosity, combined with her allegiance, urged Margaret to rest, which her appearance, as she lay on the sofa, showed she desperately required. She spoke only when spoken to; she tried to smile back at her father's anxious looks and tender inquiries, but instead of a smile, her wan lips resolved into a sigh. He was so miserably uneasy that finally, she consented to go to her own room and prepare for bed. She was indeed inclined to give up the idea that the inspector would call again that night, as it was already past nine.

She stood by her father, holding onto the back of his chair.

"You will go to bed soon, papa, won't you? Don't sit up alone!"

What his answer was she did not hear; the words were lost in the far smaller sound that magnified itself to her fears and filled her brain. There was a low ring at the doorbell.

She kissed her father and glided downstairs with a rapidity of motion no one would have thought possible, given what they had seen of her the minute before. She put aside Dixon.

"Don't come; I will open the door. I know it is him—I can—I must manage it all myself."

"As you please, miss!" said Dixon, testily; but a moment later she added, "But you're not fit for it. You are more dead than alive."

"Am I?" said Margaret, turning round, her eyes aglow with strange fire, her cheeks flushed, though her lips remained baked and livid.

She opened the door to the Inspector and preceded him into the study. She placed the candle on the table and snuffed it carefully before turning to face him.

"You are late!" she said. "Well?" She held her breath for the answer.

"I am sorry to have given any unnecessary trouble, ma'am; for, after all, they've given up all thoughts of holding an inquest. I have had other work to do and other people to see, or I should have been here sooner."

"Then it is ended," said Margaret. "There is to be no further enquiry."

"I believe I have Mr. Thornton's note," said the Inspector, fumbling in his pocket-book.

"Mr. Thornton's!" said Margaret.

"Yes! He's a magistrate—ah! Here it is."
