The Palmers departed for Cleveland the following day, leaving the households of Barton Park to their own devices. Yet this solitude proved fleeting. Before Elinor could fully process the peculiarities of Charlotte's unexplained happiness or Sir John's simplistic display of competence—a performance that highlighted the frequent absurdity of marital dynamics—Sir John and Mrs. Jennings mobilized their social machinery to introduce her to fresh acquaintances.

During a morning excursion to Exeter, they encountered two young ladies whom Mrs. Jennings identified as distant relatives. This connection was sufficient for Sir John to issue an immediate invitation to the Park, contingent only upon the conclusion of their current engagements in Exeter. These obligations dissolved instantly before the allure of the invitation. Upon Sir John's return, Lady Middleton was thrown into a state of genuine alarm. She was to host two strangers she had never seen, regarding whose elegance—or even basic gentility—she possessed zero evidence. The assurances of her husband and mother held no weight in her pragmatic assessment. Compounding the insult was the fact that they were her relations; consequently, Mrs. Jennings' attempts at consolation were logically flawed, advising her daughter not to worry about their fashionability, as they were all cousins and destined to endure one another. Despite the impossibility of preventing their arrival, Lady Middleton resigned herself to the situation with the practiced philosophy of a well-bred woman, reserving her only outlet for issuing a gentle reprimand to her husband approximately six times daily.

The sisters arrived with an appearance that was neither ungenteel nor unfashionable. Their attire was sharp, their manners civil, and they displayed an effusive delight in the house and its furnishings. Furthermore, possessing a profound, almost pathological affection for children, they secured Lady Middleton's favorable opinion within the hour. She declared them delightful girls, a sentiment that constituted enthusiastic admiration for her ladyship. Sir John's confidence in his own discernment soared at this animated praise, prompting him to rush to the cottage to inform the Dashwood sisters of the Miss Steeles' arrival and to assure them of their status as the sweetest girls in existence. However, such hyperbolic commendation yielded little intelligence; Elinor knew that the title of "sweetest" was distributed liberally across England among women of varying forms, faces, tempers, and intellects. Sir John insisted the entire family accompany him to the Park to view his guests. A benevolent, albeit socially oblivious, man, he found it painful to keep even a third cousin to himself.

"Do come now," he urged, his voice rising in pitch. "Pray come—you must come—I declare you shall come. You cannot imagine how you will like them. Lucy is monstrously pretty, so good-humored, and agreeable! The children are already clinging to her as if she were an old acquaintance. Both sisters are dying to see you; they heard in Exeter that you are the most beautiful creatures in the world, and I have confirmed this truth to them, adding significantly more. You will be delighted, I am sure. They have filled the entire carriage with toys for the children. How can you be so cross as to refuse? After all, you are my cousins, in a fashion. You are mine, and they are my wife's, so the bloodline connects us."

Despite his fervor, Sir John failed to persuade them immediately. He secured only a promise that they would call at the Park within a day or two, leaving the Dashwoods in stunned silence at their indifference. He then retreated to boast anew of the Miss Steeles' charms to his wife, repeating the cycle of mutual flattery.

When the scheduled visit finally occurred, the inspection revealed little merit in the eldest sister, who was nearly thirty and possessed a face that was plainly devoid of sensibility. In contrast, the younger sister, barely twenty-three, exhibited considerable beauty. Her features were pleasing, accompanied by a sharp, quick eye and a brisk demeanor that, while lacking actual grace, conferred distinct presence. Their manners were notably civil, and Elinor quickly granted them credit for a modicum of sense, observing the constant, judicious effort they expended to please Lady Middleton. With the children, they were in a state of perpetual ecstasy, extolling their beauty, courting their attention, and indulging their whims. Any spare moments were consumed by admiring Lady Middleton's activities or copying patterns from her latest dresses, which had previously captivated them.

For those who court through such foibles, a fond mother—who, in her pursuit of praise for her offspring, becomes the most rapacious of humans—is also the most credulous. Her demands are exorbitant, yet she accepts anything. Thus, the excessive affection and endurance shown by the Miss Steeles toward Lady Middleton's children were received without suspicion. Lady Middleton watched with maternal complacency as her cousins subjected her progeny to impertinent encroachments and mischievous tricks. She observed their sashes loosened, their hair tugged behind their ears, their workbags rifled, and their knives and scissors stolen, interpreting these acts as a reciprocal enjoyment. The only surprise she registered was that Elinor and Marianne sat so composedly by, refusing to participate in the chaos.

"John is in such spirits today!" she exclaimed, watching Sir John snatch Miss Steele's pocket-handkerchief and toss it out the window. "He is full of monkey tricks."

Shortly thereafter, upon the second son violently pinching the same lady's finger, she fondly remarked, "How playful William is!"

"And here is my sweet little Annamaria," she added, tenderly embracing a three-year-old who had remained silent for the previous two minutes. "She is always so gentle and quiet—never was there such a quiet little thing!"

Unfortunately, in bestowing these embraces, a pin in her ladyship's head-dress lightly scratched the child's neck. This minor injury elicited screams of such violence that they could hardly be surpassed by any creature professionally designated as noisy. The mother's consternation was extreme, yet it paled compared to the alarm of the Miss Steeles. Every resource of affection was deployed to assuage the suffering infant. She was seated in her mother's lap, bombarded with kisses, while one Miss Steele knelt to bathe the wound with lavender water and the other stuffed her mouth with sugar plums. Such rewards for tears rendered the child too wise to stop screaming. She continued to shriek and sob lustily, kicking her brothers whenever they attempted to touch her, rendering all united soothing efforts futile until Lady Middleton recalled a similar incident from the previous week where apricot marmalade had successfully treated a bruised temple. The same remedy was eagerly proposed, and a slight cessation of screams upon hearing it gave rise to cautious hope. The child was carried out in her mother's arms to retrieve the medicine. As the two boys elected to follow, despite earnest entreaties to remain, the four young ladies were left in a silence the room had not known for hours.

"Poor little creatures!" Miss Steele murmured as soon as they departed. "It might have been a very sad accident."

"Yet I hardly know how," cried Marianne, "unless it had been under totally different circumstances. But this is the usual method of heightening alarm where there is nothing genuinely alarming in reality."

"What a sweet woman Lady Middleton is!" declared Lucy Steele.

Marianne remained silent; it was impossible for her to utter words she did not feel, regardless of the occasion's triviality. Consequently, the burden of fabricating polite lies fell entirely upon Elinor. When called upon, she spoke of Lady Middleton with greater warmth than she truly felt, though with far less enthusiasm than Miss Lucy.

"And Sir John too," the elder sister cried. "What a charming man he is!"

Once again, Miss Dashwood's commendation, being merely simple and just, lacked éclat. She merely observed that he was perfectly good-humored and friendly.

"And what a charming little family they have! I never saw such fine children in my life.—I declare I quite doat upon them already, and indeed I am always distractedly fond of children."

"I should guess so," Elinor replied with a smile, "based on what I have witnessed this morning."

"I have a notion," said Lucy, "that you consider the little Middletons rather too indulged; perhaps they are the epitome of excess. But it is so natural in Lady Middleton. For my part, I love to see children full of life and spirit; I cannot bear them if they are tame and quiet."

"I confess," Elinor replied, "that while I reside at Barton Park, I never think of tame and quiet children with any abhorrence."

A brief pause followed, broken by Miss Steele, who seemed eager for conversation and spoke rather abruptly. "And how do you like Devonshire, Miss Dashwood? I suppose you were very sorry to leave Sussex."

Caught off guard by the familiarity of the inquiry, Elinor admitted she was.

"Norland is a prodigiously beautiful place, is it not?" Miss Steele added.

"We have heard Sir John admire it excessively," Lucy interjected, seemingly feeling the need to apologize for her sister's boldness.

"I think everyone *must* admire it," Elinor corrected, "whoever has seen the place; though it cannot be supposed that anyone can estimate its beauties as we do."

"And did you have many smart beaux there? I suppose you do not have so many in this part of the world; for my part, I think they are a vast addition always."

"But why should you assume," Lucy said, looking ashamed of her sister's presumption, "that there are not as many genteel young men in Devonshire as in Sussex?"
