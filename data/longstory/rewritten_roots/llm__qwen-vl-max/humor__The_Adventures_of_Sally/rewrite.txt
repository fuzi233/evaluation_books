Sally sat back, surveying the long table like a queen inspecting her realm. The party was officially *not* a disaster—though it had started off looking suspiciously like one. There’d been that awkward moment when everyone froze mid-sip of soup, eyes darting to Fillmore’s waistcoat—white, pristine, and about as subtle as a neon sign saying “I’m rich now, and you’re not.” But now? Now laughter was flowing freely, wine was being passed around with reckless abandon (someone had clearly slipped something into the punch), and even the Marvellous Murphys—those two acrobats who’d arrived yesterday and already tried to balance on the chandelier—were behaving themselves. Mostly.

At Sally’s end of the table, the conversation had naturally drifted to the big topic: her inheritance. Rumor had it she’d inherited a fortune—some said half a million, others whispered a full million—and so naturally, her guests were eager to offer financial advice. After all, what’s better than having your own money? Dictating how someone else spends theirs.

“Let me tell you,” began Augustus Bartlett, the Wall Street clerk whose voice always sounded like he was delivering a stock quote while also trying to sell you a life insurance policy, “if I were you, I’d sink two hundred thousand into some solid bonds—preferably ours—we just issued a new one, very promising, and then play around with the rest. Multiple Steel’s a good bet. Up to a hundred and fifty by Saturday, they say.”

Elsa Doland, seated beside him, blinked slowly. “Buy a theatre, Sally,” she suggested, “and put on real plays. Not those musicals where people sing about their laundry.”

“And lose every bean,” muttered a mild young man across the table, who seemed to have spent his entire life in a state of quiet desperation. “If I had a few hundred thousand,” he continued, “I’d put it all on Benny Whistler for the heavyweight title. Private info: Battling Tuke’s been bought off. He’ll fold in the seventh round.”

“Hold up,” another voice cut in. “Let me tell you what I’d do with four hundred thousand…”

“I’d pay my bill from last week,” Elsa interrupted, smirking. “Due this morning.”

Sally shot up from her seat, eyes wide. “Elsa! Are you really broke?” She rushed down the table, wrapped an arm around her friend’s shoulders, and leaned in. “Because if you are… I can help. I mean, I’ve got—”

Elsa laughed, warm and bright. “You’re such an angel, Sally. No one’s like you. Of course I’m not broke. I just came back from touring, and I’ve saved a small fortune. I only said that to see if you’d fall for it.”

Sally returned to her seat, relieved. The company had split into factions. One side, led by Augustus Bartlett, was firmly in favor of three hundred thousand in Liberty Bonds and the rest in safe real estate. The other, smaller group—led by the mild young man—had already placed Sally’s imaginary fortune on Benny Whistler, betting in small increments so as not to “spoil the market.” They were convinced three-to-one odds were possible. The gloomy man with spectacles had quietly added, “If I’d had twenty-five thousand in 1900, I could’ve started a revolution in Paraguay.” He sighed, lost in thought.

Then Sally spoke.

“I don’t know where you’re getting your numbers,” she said calmly, “but I’ve just twenty-five thousand dollars.”

Silence.

For a moment, the room felt like it had shrunk. Twenty-five thousand? To these folks, who’d been mentally budgeting for yachts and stock markets, it was practically pocket change. It was the kind of sum you set aside for taxi fare when visiting Europe. But then, slowly, minds adjusted. Even twenty-five thousand was something. You could buy a car. Or a dog. Or maybe both.

“If I had twenty-five thousand,” Augustus Bartlett said, regaining composure, “I’d buy Amalgamated.”

“If I had twenty-five thousand…” Elsa began.

“If I’d had twenty-five thousand in nineteen hundred,” the gloomy man mused, “I could’ve started a revolution in Paraguay.”

Sally smiled faintly. “Well, here’s what I’m going to do,” she announced. “I’m going to Europe. France, specifically. I’ve heard wonderful things. As soon as I get my passport. Then I’ll come back and find a nice little business—something cozy, something that lets me invest and live in luxury. Any complaints?”

“Even a couple of thousand on Benny Whistler…” the mild man murmured.

“I don’t want Benny Whistler,” Sally said firmly. “If I want to lose money, I’ll go to Monte Carlo and do it properly.”

“Monte Carlo!” the gloomy man perked up. “I was there in '97. If I’d had fifty more dollars… just fifty… I’d have…”

At the far end of the table, a chair scraped. A cough. Then Mr. Maxwell Faucitt, the boarding-house’s oldest resident and most frequent speaker, rose with the dignified grace of a man who’d once played Hamlet in a traveling troupe.

“Ladies,” he began, bowing deeply, “and… gentlemen.” He paused, letting his gaze sweep over the younger men, who were beginning to look restless. “I feel I cannot allow this occasion to pass without saying a few words.”

No one was surprised. Mr. Faucitt had spoken at every meal, every birthday, every farewell, and once during a thunderstorm. The only time he hadn’t spoken was when the fire alarm went off—because, as he later explained, “It would have been inappropriate.”

The Marvellous Murphys, still adjusting to their new environment, made a move toward the left side of the table, possibly to start a protest rally. But Sally gave them a look—one that said, “Don’t even think about it”—and they backed down. They admired her. Deeply. And not just because she was kind. She was small, trim, with hands and feet like delicate porcelain, a smile that could melt ice, and a dimple that appeared when she laughed—which was often. Her hazel eyes vanished when she smiled, and her brown hair fell in soft waves. And she carried herself with a quiet dignity that made her stand out among Mrs. Meecher’s usual crowd.

Mr. Faucitt continued. “I have been asked—though I know there are others here far more worthy—to propose the health of our charming hostess…”

“Who asked you?” the smaller Murphy blurted.

Mr. Faucitt ignored him. “To propose the health of Miss Nicholas—our dear Sally—and her brother, Fillmore Nicholas.”

Fillmore, sitting at the head of the table, nodded once. A condescending nod. The kind you give when acknowledging the existence of a lower caste. Sally watched, tempted to throw an orange at him. One sat within reach. His shirt-front gleamed like polished ivory. But she stopped herself. Chaos, after all, is never the way to run a party.

She leaned back. The temptation was strong. Fillmore had changed since inheriting money. Once humble, now he wore his wealth like a crown. He’d gone from “Fill” to “Sir Fillmore,” and his evening wear tonight had caused a minor social earthquake. That white waistcoat alone had silenced the room during the first course. People who’d known him as a struggling artist or a man who patched his socks with tape now stared, dumbfounded. He looked like a man who’d just discovered he was no longer poor—and was determined to make everyone else feel it.

Mr. Faucitt resumed. “As an Englishman—I was born under the Union Jack, though I’ve since taken out papers—I must say the two things that have impressed me most about America are its hospitality and its girls. Tonight, we’ve seen both. Miss Nicholas has given us a banquet. A true banquet. With alcohol. I don’t know where it came from, but we’ve had it. Miss Nicholas…”

He paused to puff his cigar. Fillmore checked his watch. Sally leaned forward, rapt. She knew how much this speech meant to him. She didn’t mind the content—she just wanted him to be happy.

“Miss Nicholas,” Mr. Faucitt continued, lowering his voice, “why do I call her Miss Nicholas?”

“Because it’s her name,” said the taller Murphy.

Mr. Faucitt glared. “Yes, sir, it is her name. But she has another name—sweet to those who love her, those who worship her, those who’ve watched her for three years beneath this roof. That name,” he said softly, “is Sally. Our Sally. For three years, she’s flitted through this place like a ray of sunshine. She’s made life brighter, sweeter. And now, with sudden wealth and her twenty-first birthday, she must leave us. But not from our hearts. I believe, wherever she goes, she will keep a corner in her golden heart for us, her Bohemian comrades. Ladies and gentlemen, I give you Miss Sally Nicholas, and her brother, Fillmore.”

Cheers erupted. Fillmore stood, smoothed his waistcoat, and prepared to speak.

Sally held her breath. She’d hoped he’d deliver a grand speech. He’d once been a star debater. She’d insisted he come tonight, knowing Mr. Faucitt would say lovely things—and someone had to respond.

Fillmore cleared his throat.

“I’m sure,” he said, “you don’t want a speech. Very good of you to drink our health. Thank you.”

And sat down.

The effect was immediate.

Most of the guests breathed a collective sigh of relief. They’d braced themselves for a ten-minute monologue. This? This was perfect. Concise. Elegant. Done.

But Mr. Faucitt? Mr. Faucitt looked like he’d just been told the universe was flat. He stared ahead, stunned. Like someone who’d stepped off a stair that wasn’t there.

Sally saw it—and gasped. Without thinking, she dashed around the table, threw her arms around Mr. Faucitt’s neck, and whispered, sobbing, “My brother… he didn’t say everything I hoped he would. I can’t make a speech, but… I love you all. I’ll never forget you…”

And then she kissed him and burst into tears.

“There, there,” Mr. Faucitt murmured, patting her back. The speech had been short. Awkward. But somehow, it worked.

Later, outside the boarding-house, Sally was lecturing Fillmore. He’d slipped away, hat in hand, but she’d caught up with him.

“You are a pig,” she declared, arms crossed. “A complete, utter pig.”

Fillmore whimpered. “I didn’t mean—”

“You didn’t mean anything,” she snapped. “You stood there like a statue and said nothing. Nothing!”

He puffed out his chest. “But I didn’t want to embarrass myself. I’m moving on, Sally. I’m not that person anymore. These people—they’re part of the past. I don’t want to be reminded of it.”

“You don’t want to be reminded of *me*?” she asked sharply.

“No, no,” he said quickly. “Of course I do. But—”

“But you treat them like dirt,” she said. “Like they’re beneath you.”

“They *are* beneath me,” he insisted. “Not in spirit, but in station. I’m going to be a big man. I’ve got a deal—big one. Can’t tell you about it, but it’s huge.”

“A nurse,” Sally said flatly.

Fillmore blinked. “What?”

“You need a nurse,” she repeated. “Someone to hold your hand when you cry because you can’t write a proper speech.”

He opened his mouth. Closed it. “That’s not fair.”

“It’s true,” she said. “You’re brilliant. But you’re also a baby.”

Just then, a young man approached—tall, clean-shaven, sharp-eyed. Gerald Foster. He’d just arrived in town.

“Good evening, Mr. Foster,” Sally said.

“Good evening, Miss Nicholas.”

“You don’t know my brother, do you?”

“I don’t believe I do.”

“He left the underworld before you came to it,” Sally said with a grin. “Once, he ate prunes with the proletariat. Mrs. Meecher thinks of him as a son.”

They shook hands. Fillmore, feeling suddenly small, excused himself and hurried off into the night.

Sally watched him go. Then turned to Gerald.

“Well,” she said, “that’s my brother. Don’t let him fool you. He’s just a big baby in a fancy suit.”

And she smiled.

Sally was sprawled against a golden sand dune, half-asleep and half-awake, watching the morning ballet of Roville-sur-Mer unfold like a poorly choreographed soap opera. At seaside resorts—especially French ones—the beach becomes a full-time job for tourists at dawn. Fathers with facial hair that looked like they’d been grown in protest against modernity dotted the foreground. Women clustered under parasols that might as well have been painted by Matisse. Dogs wandered like lost souls. Kids dug holes with the solemnity of archaeologists, occasionally pausing to whack each other with spades because, clearly, the human race had not yet invented fun.

One poodle—military-grade, stiff-backed, and clearly judging Sally’s life choices—approached her, sniffed her box of sweets, and decided: *This is where I’m staying.* It sat down with the dignity of a general accepting surrender.

Sally’s vacation had been perfect—until it wasn’t. She’d floated through France like a lazy thought, from one sun-drenched town to another, until Roville captured her heart with its glittering hotels, blue sky, and Casino that probably played “La Vie en Rose” on loop. She could’ve stayed forever. But America called—specifically, Gerald, who’d written a letter so rushed it looked like it was composed during a fire drill. “*The Primrose Way* is opening in Detroit,” he’d scribbled. “Come back if you want to see it.” No love notes. No emojis. Just existential dread disguised as theater news.

She was leaving tomorrow. But today? Today was still hers. She basked in the sun, listened to waves whisper secrets she didn’t care to know, and enjoyed the rare luxury of doing absolutely nothing.

Except… the sun was warm. The waves were soothing. And before she knew it, her eyes had closed.

Then—*crash!*—a voice broke the spell.

Not just any voice. English.

In Roville? That was like hearing a jazz band play a dirge. Sally snapped awake. Two men had settled into the sand nearby. One of them—dark, sharp-faced, with a mouth that looked like it had been filed down by a lawyer—was lighting a cigarette with the precision of a man who’d once tried to burn his own hand off and failed.

Sally, ever the amateur psychologist, gave him a quick diagnosis: *Hard. Cold. Probably owns a briefcase with a lock.*

She turned to the second man—and immediately regretted it.

He was staring at her. Not awkwardly. Not politely. Like he’d just discovered the Holy Grail and it was wearing a sundress.

“Rather a dear,” Sally muttered, already feeling slightly embarrassed.

He was red-haired—bright, fiery, like a traffic light gone rogue. Thick-set, freckled, square-chinned, with a tiny cut near his jawline that screamed “I’ve thrown things at walls.”

“Temper,” Sally concluded. “But nice. Probably says ‘bloody hell’ when he stubs his toe.”

She looked away. His gaze was too intense. Like someone trying to read your soul while you’re trying to pretend you don’t have one.

The dark man finally succeeded in lighting his cigarette (after three attempts and one minor wind-related crisis) and resumed their conversation.

“And how is Scrymgeour?” he asked.

“Oh, all right,” said the redhead, distracted. He hadn’t taken his eyes off Sally.

“I was surprised he was here. He said he’d stay in Paris.”

A pause. Sally tossed a nougat to the poodle. It ate it like it was a medal.

Then the redhead leaned forward, voice vibrating with genuine awe.

“I say,” he announced, loud enough for the entire beach to hear, “that’s the prettiest girl I’ve seen in my life!”

Sally blinked. Then smiled.

She wasn’t offended. In fact, she found it kind of refreshing. A guy saying something honest in public? Bold move. The dark man, however, looked like he’d just been told the universe was made of cheese.

“My dear fellow!” he gasped.

“Oh, it’s all right,” said the redhead, unfazed. “She can’t understand. Nobody here speaks English. If I hadn’t remembered a few scraps of French, I’d be eating seaweed by now. That girl,” he continued, reverting to his main obsession, “is an absolute topper! I swear I’ve never seen anyone like her. Look at those hands and feet. You don’t get that outside France. Of course, her mouth is a bit wide,” he added reluctantly.

Sally remained motionless. The dark man, relieved, exhaled slowly. He prided himself on propriety. Being near someone who *didn’t* behave properly felt like being caught in a moral earthquake.

“Still,” he said sternly, “you ought to be careful.”

He glanced at Sally, who was now entertaining a scruffy mongrel and a terrier with a black patch over one eye. Then he returned to Scrymgeour.

“How is Scrymgeour’s dyspepsia?”

The redhead seemed barely interested.

“Do you notice how her hair curls over her ears?” he asked. “Eh? Oh, pretty much the same, I think.”

“What hotel are you staying at?”

“The Normandie.”

Sally froze. *The Normandie.* She was staying there too. She’d seen no sign of this man at the hotel. So he must be new.

“If there’s one thing he’s fussy about…” the dark man began.

“There are at least eleven thousand things he’s fussy about,” the redhead interrupted. “Jumpy old blighter!”

The dark man stood up so fast his straw hat flew off and was promptly stepped on by a child in a yellow swimsuit.

Sally watched, transfixed. The man looked like a melodrama villain preparing to disown his wayward son. The redhead, meanwhile, gazed calmly down the beach at a fat man in an orange swimsuit who had just waded into the sea like a sinking battleship.

“Do you mean to tell me,” the dark man thundered, “after all the effort the family went to, after giving you a sinecure with endless possibilities, you threw it away?”

A dog fight erupted.

It started with the terrier with the black patch. Sally blamed herself. She’d given the poodle three consecutive treats, ignoring the terrier’s growing indignation. Third time was the charm. The terrier lunged.

Dogs from across the beach surged forward. Barking. Snarling. A full-scale canine war. Some dogs fought. Others just barked at the chaos. One Sealyham appeared to be attempting a surgical procedure on a stranger.

Sally was frozen. So were the spectators. No one moved. Until—

The redhead stepped in.

He didn’t look like a hero. He looked like a guy who’d just been fired and was trying to impress a girl. But he had a gift. Dog fights, it turns out, require a specific skill set: calm, authority, and a knack for making dogs stop fighting without getting bitten.

He walked into the middle of the melee like he owned the place. Calm descended. Dogs stopped. One by one, they were separated, calmed, and led away by furious owners. Within minutes, only one small Scotch terrier remained, licking a chewed leg like it was deep in thought.

The redhead turned to Sally.

He braced himself, swallowed hard, and attempted French.

“J'espère,” he said, “que vous n'êtes pas—oh, dammit, what’s the word—J'espère que vous n'êtes pas blessée?”

“Blessée?”

“Yes. Wounded. Hurt. Bitten. Oh, dash it. J'espère…”

“Oh, bitten!” Sally giggled. “No, thanks. I wasn’t bitten. And thank you for saving us all.”

He stared at her, horrified. His face flushed crimson. His jaw dropped.

“Oh, my sainted aunt!” he cried.

Then, as if the world had become unbearable, he spun around and fled—so fast it was practically a sprint.

Sally watched him go. She still wanted to know why Scrymgeour had fired him.

Roville’s bedtime hours varied like a pendulum. The fancy hotels stayed up till dawn, drowning the night in jazz. The Normandie, however, operated on a strict “early to bed, early to rise” policy. Which meant Jules, the night clerk and elevator operator, was technically on duty—but mostly asleep.

Sally arrived just before midnight, fresh from the Casino and a moonlit stroll. The lobby was dim. Jules slumbered in a chair, head tilted, snoring softly. Sally felt guilty. She loved Jules—he worked nonstop, always tired, always polite. She didn’t want to wake him.

Then footsteps echoed outside. The front door opened and slammed shut.

Jules stirred. Sat up. Sally turned—and saw the redhead.

“Oh, good evening,” she said cheerfully.

He froze. His feet tangled. His face turned the color of a ripe tomato.

“Er—good evening,” he mumbled.

“Or *bon soir*, I suppose you’d say,” Sally teased.

He dropped his hat. Tripped over it. Stared at the floor like it had personally insulted him.

Jules, still half-asleep, opened the lift cage with a clang and began pulling the rope. The elevator ascended. He didn’t wake up. His brain ran on autopilot.

They sat side by side. Silence. Then—*snore*.

At the third floor, Sally prodded Jules in the ribs. She’d learned in Roville: actions spoke louder than words. Pointing worked. Prodding worked. French? Not so much.

Jules stopped the lift. Then froze. He stared at the door lock. He couldn’t open it.

“Hitch,” Sally said. “Would you mind asking what’s wrong? I only know ‘oo la la!’”

The redhead took a breath. “Oh, esker… esker vous…”

“Don’t weaken,” Sally urged. “You’ve got him.”

“Esker vous… Pourquoi vous ne… I mean ne vous… quel est le raison…”

Then Jules exploded into rapid-fire French. Words poured out like a waterfall. Neither Sally nor the redhead understood a word.

“Stop him!” Sally ordered.

The redhead looked terrified. “Stop him?”

“Blow a whistle or something!”

From the depths of his memory, a single word surfaced: *Zut!*

He barked it.

Jules stopped. Instantly. Like a machine unplugged.

“Quick!” Sally said. “Now ask him what’s wrong!”

The redhead nodded. “Lentement,” he said. “Parlez lentement. Pas si—uh—dashed vite!”

“Ah-a-ah!” Jules said, catching on. “Lentement. Ah, oui, lentement.”

They talked. Sally didn’t understand. The redhead did.

Minutes later, he turned to her.

“The silly ass,” he said, “has made a bloomer. He was half-asleep when we got in. He shoved us in, slammed the door, and forgot the keys were on the desk.”

“I see,” Sally said. “So we’re locked in?”

“I’m afraid so,” he said. “If only I knew French better. I’d curse him with some vim. I wonder what ‘blighter’ is in French.”

By six o'clock the next evening, Sally had resigned herself to the fact that Ginger Kemp would have to navigate life on his own—without the sage advice she’d been itching to deliver. She hadn’t seen him all day, and in just over an hour, she’d be boarding the 7:15 express from Roville to Paris, then on to Cherbourg and her transatlantic liner bound for New York. Still, a flicker of hope remained. At half past six, after depositing her luggage with a porter who seemed genuinely fond of her (and possibly of her suitcase), she made one last dash to the Casino Municipale.

She couldn’t bear the thought of leaving without giving Ginger a little nudge in the right direction. Like many bright, energetic girls, Sally had a talent for meddling—what her brother Fillmore called “interfering with other people’s lives.” And Ginger? He was clearly someone who needed a push. So when she walked into the casino and spotted a fiery red head glowing above the crowd near a roulette table, her heart leapt.

Roville-sur-Mer had two casinos. The one on the Promenade catered to sunbathers and casual boule players. But the real action—the kind that drew serious gamblers with serious intentions—was at the grand Casino Municipale, tucked near the railway station. And it was clear as soon as Sally entered that Ginger wasn’t just playing; he was *winning*.

He sat like a king atop a mountain of square counters, looking every inch the triumphant tycoon. As Sally watched, a wooden-faced croupier slid another pile across the table with a theatrical rake. “Epatant!” murmured a man beside her, elbowing her ribs in excitement. Sally didn’t speak French, but she got the gist: *Wow*. The whole room seemed to agree. There’s something oddly communal about watching someone else win at roulette—like a shared spiritual high. The croupier twisted his moustache with one hand and spun the wheel with the other. Silence fell.

Sally shifted to a less crowded spot and finally saw Ginger’s face—and burst out laughing.

He looked exactly like a terrier peering down a rat hole. His hair stood on end, his eyes were wide, and his entire posture screamed, “I’m about to become rich!”

Her laugh cut through the hush like a foghorn. In the quiet reverence of a lucky streak, it was akin to shouting during a funeral—or, more accurately, a revival meeting. The crowd turned, horrified. Even the croupier paused mid-spin. Ginger, startled, jumped up—and just then, the ball clattered into a red slot.

The crowd groaned. A collective shudder passed through them like a wave of grief. Poor Ginger. He’d picked the wrong color. And everyone blamed Sally.

Ginger, however, seemed unbothered. He gathered his winnings, stuffed them in his pocket, and pushed through the crowd toward her. By now, she was officially the casino’s least favorite person.

“I say,” he said, pulling her aside with surprising strength, “this is brilliant—running into you like this. I’ve been searching everywhere.”

“You didn’t find me?” she said, amused. “That’s because I’ve been looking for *you*.”

“No really?” He grinned, relieved. He led her to a quiet anteroom outside the gambling hall—a cozy corner where only a uniformed attendant stood guard, looking mildly bored but deeply intrigued by the drama unfolding.

“That was very kind of you,” Ginger said, breathless.

“I wanted to talk before my train left.”

“Your train?” he asked, suddenly alarmed. “What do you mean?”

“The puff-puff,” she explained cheerfully. “Leaving tonight.”

“Leaving?” He looked as if she’d announced the end of the world. “You’re not going away from Roville?”

“I’m afraid so.”

“But why? Where are you going?”

“Back to America. My boat leaves Cherbourg tomorrow.”

“Oh, my aunt!”

“I’m sorry,” she said, touched by his distress. She liked being appreciated. “But…”

“I say…” Ginger went bright red and stared hard at the attendant, who was now humming a love song under his breath. “Look here—will you marry me?”

Sally blinked.

She’d known Ginger was unpredictable. But *this*?

“Marry me?” she echoed.

“You know what I mean.”

“Yes, I suppose I do. You’re referring to matrimony. Yes, I understand.”

“So—how about it?”

She composed herself. Her sense of humor kicked in. She studied him carefully. He wouldn’t meet her gaze, instead fixating on the official, who had now begun swaying gently, lost in romantic fantasy.

“Isn’t this… well, slightly premature?” she asked.

“It has to be,” Ginger said, exasperated. “I thought you were staying weeks.”

“But we’re practically strangers,” she said, patting his hand. The attendant sighed dramatically.

“I see what’s happened,” she continued. “You’ve mistaken me for someone else—someone you actually know. Take a good look at me.”

“If I take a good look at you,” Ginger muttered, “I can’t guarantee the outcome.”

“And this is the man I was going to lecture on ‘Enterprise’.”

“You’re the most incredible girl I’ve ever met,” he declared, still staring at the attendant. “I don’t care how sudden it is. I fell in love the moment I saw you. That’s it.”

“But—”

“Now listen,” he said, shifting gears. “I know I’m not much. But I just won a fortune. We could start fresh. I’ve messed up everything I’ve tried, sure—but there must be something I can do. With you around to encourage me, I’d give it my best shot. I mean—”

“Have you considered I might already be engaged?”

“Oh, golly!” He finally turned to face her. And in that moment, something in his eyes—pure, unguarded sincerity—struck her. It wasn’t a joke. He was serious.

“Well,” she said slowly, “as a matter of fact, I am.”

Ginger bit his lip. Silence.

“Oh, well,” he said finally. “That’s that.”

Sally felt a strange mix of pity and amusement. It was maternal, almost. Like watching a child plead for a toy they can’t possibly afford. She wanted to hug him. She couldn’t, though. Not because he was a grown man, but because he didn’t feel like one.

“You don’t really mean it,” she said gently.

“Don’t I?” he whispered. “Oh, don’t I?”

“You can’t,” she insisted. “Love at first sight doesn’t happen in real life. Love comes when you know someone—when you’ve spent time together—”

She stopped.

Because she realized: *she* hadn’t known Gerald Foster for long either. They’d met, fallen in love, and gotten engaged in less than two weeks. What did she know about him except that she loved him?

Her argument crumbled. “It’s ridiculous,” she said weakly.

Ginger slumped into melancholy. “I knew you wouldn’t care,” he said quietly. “I’m not much of a chap.”

And there it was—the perfect opening.

“That’s exactly what I wanted to talk about,” she said, seizing the moment. “I’ve been trying to find you all day to finish what I started in the elevator last night. Mind if I talk to you like an aunt? Or maybe a sister? Honestly, the best plan would be for you to adopt me as an honorary sister. What do you think?”

Ginger didn’t seem thrilled by the idea.

“Because I *do* care about you,” she added.

He perked up. “That’s very kind.”

“I’m going to offer some wisdom,” she said, leaning forward. “Ginger, why don’t you *brace up*?”

“Brace up?”

“Yes! Stiffen your spine, stick out your chin, square your elbows. Stop flopping around like a wet rag and doing nothing while you wait for ‘the family’ to rescue you. Why do you need jobs handed to you? Why don’t you go out and grab one? Why worry about what they think? Why not make yourself independent? Sure, you lost your money. But so has everyone who’s ever done anything worthwhile. It’s part of the game. You’re not a puppy to be picked up and dropped wherever they please. A job is something you choose and fight for. You’ve got money now—use it to look around. Then *do something*. Try to remember you’re alive. And try to forget the family exists.”

She paused, catching her breath.

Ginger stared. “When you talk fast,” he said slowly, “your nose gets all wiggly. Looks great.”

Sally gasped. “You weren’t listening at all!”

“Oh, yes I was!” he protested. “I heard every word.”

“What did I say?”

“You told me to get a job.”

“Well, yes. Though I phrased it better.”

“Right,” he said, nodding solemnly. “I’ll do it.”

He looked at her with such earnest devotion that she nearly melted.

“I wish you’d let me write to you,” he said. “Letters. I think it would help.”

“You won’t have time.”

“I’ll make time. Do you have an address in America? Somewhere I can send letters?”

She gave him Mrs. Meecher’s boarding house address. He wrote it on his cuff with trembling fingers.

“Fine,” she said. “Do write. I’ll want to know how you’re doing.”

“Oh, my goodness!” she suddenly shrieked. “That clock—it’s wrong!”

“What time does your train leave?”

“Leave? It’s *gone*! Or leaving in two seconds!”

She bolted for the swing door, knocking the uniformed attendant sideways. “Goodbye, Ginger! Write to me! Remember what I said!”

Ginger, surprisingly agile, followed her out. They sprinted across the square. “Stick it!” he yelled encouragingly.

They reached the platform just as the train began to move. Ginger lunged for the nearest door, wrenched it open, scooped Sally into his arms, and hurled her inside.

She landed squarely on the toes of a man in the corner seat. Bouncing off, she rushed to the window.

Ginger ran alongside, keeping pace. “Ginger! My poor porter! Tip him! I forgot!”

“Right ho!”

“And don’t forget what I said!”

“Right ho!”

“Look after yourself—and *Death to the Family!*”

“Right ho!”

The train pulled away. Sally turned back one last time. Ginger had stopped, waving a handkerchief.

Then she turned to apologize to the man whose toes she’d crushed.

“I’m so sorry,” she panted. “I hope I didn’t hurt you.”

She found herself face-to-face with Bruce Carmyle—Ginger’s cousin, the dark, grim man from the beach.

Mr. Carmyle, usually impervious to surprises, felt momentarily stunned. This was the French girl from yesterday—the one who’d caught Lancelot’s eye. Except now she was speaking fluent English. How? And how had she gone from total stranger to sprinting with his cousin down station platforms in less than twenty-four hours?

And why did she call him “Ginger”?

Bruce Carmyle disapproved of familiarity. But since the girl was undeniably attractive—even flushed and panting—his disapproval softened into a polite smile.

“Not at all,” he said, though his big toe throbbed like a drum.

“If you don’t mind,” Sally said, sitting down, “I think I’ll breathe.”

She breathed.

The train rolled on.

“Quite a close call,” Carmyle said amiably.

“Yes. Lucky Mr. Kemp was there. He throws straight, doesn’t he?”

“Tell me,” he said, “how do you know my cousin?”

“We didn’t know each other yesterday,” she said. “We stayed at the same hotel. We got stuck in an elevator for hours. That’s when we really got to know each other.”

A waiter entered, announcing dinner in surprisingly good English.

“Would you like dinner?” Carmyle asked.

“I’m starving,” she replied.

As they walked to the dining car, Sally mentally apologized to herself for judging people by their appearance. This man, despite his stern look, was actually quite pleasant.

But once seated, Carmyle transformed. He became rigid, severe. He frowned at a stain on the tablecloth, then scowled at the menu. Sally, meanwhile, charmed the waiter—who seemed instantly smitten—into treating her like a daughter.

Carmyle, irritated, snapped his order in clipped Anglo-French.

“Boum!” the waiter exclaimed proudly, vanishing.

“Nice old man,” Sally said.

“Infernally familiar,” Carmyle muttered.

Sally decided discussion about waiters was futile. She changed the subject.

“By the way,” she said, “my name is Nicholas. Always good to start with names, isn’t it?”

“Mine is—”

“Oh, I know yours. Ginger told me.”

Carmyle stiffened.

“Indeed?” he said coldly. “So you’ve become quite intimate.”

“Why ‘apparently’?” she countered. “I told you we got intimate. You can’t spend half the night trapped in an elevator with someone and *not* get to know them. I found Mr. Kemp very pleasant.”

“Really?”

“And very interesting.”

Carmyle raised an eyebrow. “Interesting?”

“I did call him interesting.”

She was warming to the battle.

“He told me all about himself.”

“And you found that entertaining?”

“Why not?”

“Well…” A faint, icy smile touched his lips. “My cousin has many qualities—he played football well, and he’s a decent amateur boxer—but I wouldn’t call him entertaining. We find him rather dull.”

“I thought only royalty used ‘we.’”

“I meant myself—and the rest of the family.”

“Ah,” Sally said, voice dropping. “The family.”

She couldn’t stop herself from thinking rude things.

“Mr. Kemp told me about Mr. Scrymgeour,” she said.

Carmyle stared at the loaf of bread.

“Indeed?” he said. “He has a charming lack of discretion.”

The waiter returned with soup. “V’la!” he announced, grinning.

Sally’s expression froze. She’d been snubbed.

“I think Mr. Kemp had bad luck,” she said. “If you don’t mind, I’d prefer not to discuss it.”

“Of course,” Carmyle said, regaining his composure. “He was perfectly justified. Mr. Scrymgeour was beating a dog.”

“I’ve heard the details.”

“Oh? Then you must agree with me?”

“I do not. A man who gives up a good position just because—”

“Oh, well, if that’s your view, I suppose we can’t talk.”

“Quite.”

“Still,” she said, “what do you plan to do about Gin—about Mr. Kemp?”

Carmyle grew colder.

“I’m afraid I cannot discuss—”

“Oh, for goodness’ sake!” Sally snapped. “Try to be human! Don’t keep snubbing people. You remind me of those eighteenth-century portraits—wooden faces, gold frames, fishy eyes—like I’m some sort of regrettable incident.”

“Rosbif,” the waiter said cheerfully, reappearing.

Carmyle attacked his beef with grim determination.

Sally sat in silence, bristling with righteous anger.

“I’m sorry,” Carmyle said gravely. “If my eyes are fishy, I hadn’t realized.”

“I suppose you never had sisters,” she said. “They’d have told you.”

He went silent.

After coffee, she stood up.

“I think I’ll go,” she said. “I don’t want any coffee, and if I stay, I might say something rude. I thought I could help Mr. Kemp. But clearly, I can’t. Goodbye, Mr. Carmyle. Thank you for dinner.”

She walked away, leaving him staring after her—indignant, yet strangely captivated.

And deep inside, something unexpected stirred in Bruce Carmyle’s heart.

A few days after their return from Roville—by separate routes, thank you very much—Bruce Carmyle ran into his cousin Lancelot (known to all as Ginger) in Piccadilly. It was a collision of the sort that happens when one man is trying to avoid another and the other doesn’t know it. Ginger, who had been making a valiant attempt to vanish into the London fog like a shy badger, was interrupted mid-stride by Carmyle, who stopped him dead with the kind of casual authority usually reserved for traffic wardens.

“Just the man I wanted to see,” said Carmyle, flashing a smile so wide it might have been designed to start a revolution in someone’s inner peace.

“Oh, hullo!” replied Ginger, sounding about as enthusiastic as a man who’d just discovered he’d been invited to a funeral dressed in a bathrobe.

“I was thinking of dropping by your club,” added Carmyle, as though he were announcing a picnic in the park.

“Ah,” said Ginger, not believing a word of it. “Well, that’s convenient.”

Carmyle then produced a cigarette case like a magician pulling a rabbit out of a hat—except the rabbit was probably still on fire. Ginger stared at it with the wary eye of a man who’s just been handed a key to a room full of traps.

He felt… off-balance. In all their years of acquaintance, Bruce Carmyle had never been this agreeable. Not even during the Great Christmas Cake Incident of ’18, when Ginger had accidentally dropped a whole layer cake into the family dog’s kennel. That hadn’t earned him a single compliment. This, however, felt suspiciously like a trap disguised as friendship.

“Been back in London long?” asked Carmyle, lighting up with the grace of a man who knows exactly how to ruin a perfectly good moment.

“Day or two,” muttered Ginger.

“I heard—quite by accident—that you’d returned. And that you’re staying at the club.” He paused, then added, “By the way, thanks for introducing me to Miss Nicholas.”

Ginger nearly tripped over his own feet.

“What?!”

“You remember. At Roville Station. You threw her right onto me. We agreed it counted as an introduction. Quite an attractive girl.”

Bruce Carmyle wasn’t entirely sure what he thought of Sally yet. But one thing he knew: she couldn’t disappear from his life without him having a say in the matter. Her sudden departure had left him with that peculiar ache only a rejected admirer can truly understand—a feeling somewhere between mild disappointment and existential dread. She’d won their last encounter hands down, and now he felt the need to prove he wasn’t just a walking cliché with a decent wardrobe.

In short, Bruce Carmyle was piqued. And when Bruce Carmyle is piqued, trouble follows. Like a bad smell in a crowded train.

“She’s quite attractive,” he continued, puffing gently. “We had a lovely chat.”

“I bet you did,” said Ginger, voice thick with jealousy. “By the way—did she give you her address?”

“Why?” asked Ginger, suddenly alert. His expression shifted from suspicion to something closer to possessiveness. It was like watching a cat guard a particularly rare mouse.

“Well,” said Carmyle, fumbling slightly, “I promised to send her some books she was keen to read.”

“Books not published in America?” Ginger asked, narrowing his eyes.

“Exactly.”

“Everything gets published in America,” Ginger scoffed. “It’s practically a law of nature.”

“These particular books don’t,” said Carmyle, growing slightly annoyed. “And if you’re going to be difficult…”

“Fine,” said Ginger, giving in with the grudging resignation of a man surrendering his favorite sandwich. “Send them through me. I’ll mail them.”

“No,” said Carmyle sharply. “I can send my own books. Where does she live?”

Ginger hesitated. The sacred number of the holy street—the very address that had become a kind of spiritual landmark in his emotional journey—was not something he gave away lightly. But Bruce Carmyle was persistent. Like a mosquito with a PhD in annoyance.

So Ginger relented. With a sigh that could have powered a small steam engine, he whispered the number.

Carmyle scribbled it down in a tiny morocco-bound notebook with a gold pencil. He was the kind of man who always had a pencil. The backs of envelopes? Those were for peasants. He didn’t even *own* an envelope.

There was silence. Then Carmyle coughed.

“I saw Uncle Donald this morning.”

The geniality vanished. It didn’t fade—it simply ceased to exist, like a lightbulb unplugged. Carmyle’s tone turned icy, edged with that familiar undertone of familial disapproval.

“Yes?” said Ginger, moodily.

Uncle Donald—the man who’d once tried to teach Ginger how to tie a Windsor knot using only a teaspoon and a stern lecture—wasn’t exactly a beloved figure. He was the managing director of the Family, a role which made him both respected and deeply irritating. Ginger considered him a human blister. A permanent, unhealable blister.

“He wants you to dine with him tonight at Bleke’s.”

Ginger’s spirits sank lower than a submarine in a thunderstorm. Dinner with Uncle Donald? At Bleke’s Coffee House? That relic of Victorian gloom where the only excitement was whether the waiters remembered your name—or if they’d forgotten it five years ago?

It was like being sentenced to a night in purgatory with a menu written in Latin.

“To-night?” Ginger asked, as though the idea had just occurred to him.

“Yes,” said Carmyle. “Seven-thirty sharp.”

“Right,” said Ginger, already imagining himself trapped in a room filled with elderly men discussing the weather like it was a war crime.

“Don’t be a fool,” said Carmyle. “You know you’ve got to go. Uncle Donald’s invitations are not requests. They’re decrees.”

Ginger sighed. “All right. Seven-thirty.”

They parted ways. Carmyle headed east toward the Temple, where he had clients who presumably paid him real money. Ginger trudged westward, directionless, lost in thought.

And yet—oddly enough—they were both thinking of the same person.

Carmyle, briskly navigating the crowds of Piccadilly Circus, was mentally rehearsing how to charm Sally back into his orbit. Ginger, meanwhile, was wandering like a sleepwalker toward Hyde Park Corner, bumping into people like a drunken giraffe.

Since returning to London, Ginger had been in terrible shape. He slept poorly. He snapped at doormen. He once accused a pigeon of stealing his lunch. Hopeless love had struck him hard—and fast. It was the worst kind of illness: the kind that makes you want to scream at the sky but instead just stare at your socks.

His previously calm soul was now a hurricane. Things that used to bore him now enraged him. Like Uncle Donald’s moustache. That ridiculous, waxed thing that looked like it belonged on a walrus. And worse—its use as a soup barrier. A *zareba* for broth! It was absurd.

“By gad!” Ginger muttered aloud, stopping in front of Devonshire House. “If he uses that damned shrubbery as a soup strainer tonight, I’ll shove a fork down his throat!”

Hard thoughts. Very hard thoughts. And they were getting harder.

Rebellion, once lit, spreads faster than a wildfire in a dry forest. Ginger was burning. By the time he reached his club, he was practically a danger to society. Especially to anyone related to Uncle Donald.

Dressing for dinner was a nightmare. His tie refused to cooperate. His shirt collar seemed to mock him. Every button felt like a personal insult.

And then—just as he was about to throw the entire outfit out the window—Fate intervened.

Knock. Knock.

A waiter entered with a telegram.

Ginger picked it up. It had been readdressed from the Hotel Normandie. A wireless message, sent from the White Star liner Olympic. The words were brief, chilling, and utterly unforgettable:

Ginger sat down heavily on the bed. The weight of those words pressed down on him like a ton of bricks wrapped in velvet.

At 7:25 PM, a taxi pulled up outside Bleke’s Coffee House in the Strand. The driver glanced at his passenger—Ginger, looking like a man who had just inherited a curse and was debating whether to wear it proudly.

“Bit of a looker,” the cabby muttered. “Definitely looks like he means business.”

And he did.

Sally had grand plans for her return to New York. She envisioned herself checking into the St. Regis, sipping champagne from crystal flutes while gazing at a view that cost more than most people’s annual salaries, and generally living like a character in a magazine ad for luxury travel. After all, she’d just come back from abroad with enough money to buy a small island (or at least a very nice apartment), so why not start by pretending she was royalty?

But then, as she stood on the dock giving instructions to the taxi driver—“Take me to the St. Regis, please”—something stopped her cold. It wasn’t the wind, nor the smell of saltwater mixed with diesel fumes. It was the sudden realization that this whole idea felt suspiciously *Fillmorian*. Like something out of a melodrama starring a man who thinks he’s too good for the world but actually just hasn’t learned how to use a fork properly.

No, Sally decided. She wouldn’t abandon Mrs. Meecher’s boarding house quite yet. Not until she’d found a proper place to call home. And besides, it would be rude—nay, *morally questionable*—to sever ties with the only place that had ever treated her like family just because she could now afford a bathroom with two sinks.

So off she went, back to the familiar streets, where even the pigeons seemed to recognize her. The morning was perfect: crisp air, golden sunlight, and the kind of blue sky that makes you believe in miracles—or at least in good coffee. Streetcars were full of cheerful commuters, police officers waved like they were hosting a parade, and the street cleaners—dressed in white, looking like they’d wandered out of a silent film—went about their duties with quiet dignity. It was almost as if the city itself knew Sally was back and wanted to throw her a welcome-home party without any awkward speeches.

Then came Mrs. Meecher.

She greeted Sally with warm hugs and even warmer gossip, which is always dangerous when delivered by someone who once played “Florodora” and still believes she’s part of the theater elite. After confirming Sally wanted her old room, Mrs. Meecher dropped the bombshell:

“Gerald Foster left this morning.”

“Where?” Sally asked, already feeling the first twinge of disappointment.

“Detroit. Miss Doland too. They’re trying out his play Monday. Rehearsing since you left.”

Sally blinked. Her heart sank slightly. But then she remembered: it was a beautiful day. The sun was shining. The city was alive. And she hadn’t seen Gerald in weeks—so maybe this was just a delay, not a disaster.

“Oh,” she said, forcing cheerfulness. “Is Elsa in the cast?”

“Sure. And very good, I hear. Mr. Faucitt saw a rehearsal. Said she was fine. He’s not easy to please, you know.”

That name brought a new concern. “How is Mr. Faucitt?”

Mrs. Meecher sighed dramatically. “Poor old gentleman. Headache last night. Up early this morning, didn’t look well. Might be Spanish flu. Lots of people dying, if you believe what you read in the papers.”

Sally gasped. “You don’t think—?”

“Well, he ain’t turned black yet,” Mrs. Meecher admitted, clearly disappointed. “They say they turn black. If you believe what you see in the papers. Of course, that might come later.” She added this with the confidence of someone who has never missed a single episode of *The Daily Drama*.

Still, Sally couldn’t help but worry. Mr. Faucitt had been a fixture in the boarding house—kind, witty, occasionally dramatic—and now he was sick. And worse: Toto was with him.

Toto was not a dog. Toto was a tiny, woolly menace with a voice like a squeaky toy possessed by a demon. He barked at shadows, chewed books, and once tried to eat Mrs. Meecher’s slippers during dinner. To anyone else, having Toto in the room would be torture. To Mrs. Meecher, it was therapeutic.

“I must go up and see him,” Sally declared.

“Sure,” said Mrs. Meecher, proud. “You know his room. You can hear Toto talking to him now. He wants a cracker. Toto likes a cracker after breakfast.”

Sally nodded and climbed the stairs, clutching a cracker like a peace offering. When she entered, Mr. Faucitt’s eyes lit up—not from joy, but from sheer relief that someone had finally arrived who understood the concept of silence.

“Sally!” he whispered, weakly.

“One moment. Here, Toto!” Sally called.

Toto, momentarily distracted by food, leapt off the bed and stared at the cracker with intense suspicion. But Sally was clever. She lured him into the hallway, tossed the cracker down, and slipped inside, shutting the door just as Toto began yapping furiously—probably thanking her or cursing her, it was hard to tell.

Mr. Faucitt took a deep breath. “Sally, you come, as ever, as an angel of mercy. Mrs. Meecher means well—but she insists on bringing that infernal pup into my room. Each yap feels like a hammer hitting tin. You shine like a good deed in a naughty world. When did you get back?”

“I’ve only just arrived in my hired barouche from the pier.”

“And you came straight here? I’m grateful. Truly.”

“Of course I did. Do you suppose I’d say ‘Oh, really? That’s interesting’ and carry on discussing the weather? Well, what do you mean by it? Frightening everyone. Poor old darling, do you feel very bad?”

“One thousand mice are nibbling my spine,” he said. “And I need cooling refreshment constantly. But your presence is a tonic. Tell me—how was foreign travel?”

“Wonderful. I had the time of my life.”

“Did you visit England?”

“Only passing through.”

“How did it look?”

“Moist. Very moist.”

Mr. Faucitt chuckled. “I confess, though I love America, there are times I miss London—the cozy brown mist, the muddy pavements oozing like sweat, the distant glow of Bodega lamps like harbor lights. Not that I favor Bodega over others. Rule’s and Short’s were equally pleasant. You missed something, Sally.”

“I know I did—pneumonia.”

He shook his head. “Prejudiced. You’d have loved it if you’d braved its gloom. Where did you spend your holiday?”

“Part in Paris, part by the sea. It was glorious. I don’t think I’d have come back if I hadn’t had to.”

“You’re back to see us again?”

“And to be at the opening of Mr. Foster’s play. Mrs. Meecher said you went to a rehearsal.”

“I attended a dog-fight,” Mr. Faucitt said sternly. “Which I was told was a rehearsal. There is no rehearsing nowadays.”

“Murdered?” Sally whispered.

“Murdered,” Mr. Faucitt confirmed. “She has the face of an angel and the histrionic ability of Mrs. Meecher’s Friday suet pudding. It wasn’t until the following Friday that Sally could finally make her way to Detroit. She arrived on Saturday morning and, after a brief ride in a cab that felt like it had been driven by someone who’d just lost a bet, checked into the Hotel Statler. After confirming Gerald was indeed staying there (a fact she verified with a phone call to his room), she decided to take charge of her own destiny and ordered breakfast.

She sat at a corner table, staring blankly at the menu like it held the secrets of the universe—or at least the location of decent coffee. Her mood was low. Nursing Mr. Faucitt through what amounted to a mild case of pneumonia had left her emotionally drained and physically resembling a dishrag that had seen better days. The train ride hadn’t helped—she’d spent most of it trying not to fall asleep while simultaneously avoiding eye contact with anyone who looked remotely interested in conversation. But the real reason for her melancholy? Gerald’s voice on the phone.

He’d sounded… flat. Like a balloon that had been stepped on by a very unenthusiastic person. His greeting had been as lively as a damp firecracker. “Oh, hi,” he’d said, without even a hint of surprise. As if her return from Europe—which had taken her three weeks to complete, mind you—was merely an inconvenience rather than a triumph of modern travel and personal resilience. It stung. Not because she needed dramatic welcomes, but because she’d hoped for something… warmer. Something that didn’t sound like he was reading from a script written by a depressed squirrel.

A cup of coffee, however, worked wonders. Men were always like this in the morning, she reminded herself. They emerged from their rooms like zombies who’d forgotten how to smile. But surely, once he’d endured the ritual of cold showers and existential dread, Gerald would be back to normal—energetic, charming, possibly even slightly flirtatious. In the meantime, food was food, and she needed it like a drowning woman needs a life preserver made entirely of pancakes.

She was pouring her second cup when a large, round-faced young man entered the dining room. He’d caught her attention earlier, lurking near the lobby like a confused penguin who’d wandered too far from home. Now, as he scanned the room with the intensity of a man searching for a missing tooth, she realized with a start: it was Fillmore.

Sally blinked. What on earth was *he* doing here? She’d assumed he was permanently ensconced in New York, living off the proceeds of minor stock transactions and the occasional well-placed yawn. But then again, men of affairs—especially those whose affairs seemed to consist mostly of lunches and vague promises—tended to flit about like moths drawn to neon lights. Still, here he was, and she called out, “Fillmore!”

He froze mid-scan, turned slowly, and saw her. His face registered a mix of shock, relief, and something vaguely resembling guilt. Sally thought: *Ah yes. The guilty conscience. I see it now.* He must have been planning to break the news that he’d secretly become engaged to someone named Winch (or possibly Wench, though that seemed less likely) without consulting her first. And now he was trapped between the desire to confess and the fear of being disowned by his sister.

“Why, Sally!” he exclaimed, stepping forward. “What are you doing here? I thought you were still in Europe.”

“I got back a week ago,” she said, “but I’ve been nursing poor old Mr. Faucitt ever since. He’s been ill—poor dear. I came here to see Mr. Foster’s play, *The Primrose Way*, you know. Is it a success?”

“You thought he had all the money in the world.”

“He does. But these guys like to let a pal in on a good thing.”

“Is it a good thing?”

“The play’s fine.”

“That’s what Mr. Faucitt said. But Mabel Hobson…”

Fillmore’s face darkened. “She’s an awful woman, Sally! Can’t act, throws her weight around like a spoiled child. The other day there was a fuss about a paper-knife…”

“A fuss about a paper-knife?”

“One of the props. It got mislaid. I’m certain it wasn’t my fault…”

“How could it have been your fault?” asked Sally, bewildered. Love clearly had no positive effects on Fillmore’s mental faculties.

“Well—er—you know how it is. Angry woman… blames the first person she sees… This paper-knife…”

His voice trailed off into silence, as if he’d suddenly remembered something deeply shameful.

“Mr. Faucitt said Elsa Doland was good.”

“Oh, she’s all right,” said Fillmore dismissively. Then his eyes lit up. “But—the girl you want to watch is Miss Winch. Gladys Winch. She plays the maid. Only in the first act, doesn’t say much—just ‘Did you ring, madam?’ and stuff like that. But it’s the way she says it! Sally, that girl’s a genius! The greatest character actress in a dozen years! You mark my words—in a darned little while, you’ll see her name up on Broadway in electric light. Personality? Ask me! Charm? She wrote the words and music! Looks?…”

“All right! All right! I know all about it, Fill. And will you kindly explain how you dared get engaged without consulting me?”

Fillmore blushed furiously. “Oh, do you know?”

“Yes. Mr. Faucitt told me.”

“Well…”

“Well?”

“Well, I’m only human,” he argued weakly.

“I call that a very handsome admission. You’ve got quite modest, Fill.”

He had certainly changed since their last meeting. It was as if someone had punctured him and let out all the pomposity. If this transformation was due, as Mr. Faucitt had suggested, to the influence of Miss Winch, Sally couldn’t help but approve. Romance, especially when it involved a woman who chewed gum during rehearsals, seemed like a step in the right direction.

“I’ll introduce you sometime,” said Fillmore.

“I want to meet her very much.”

“I’ve got to go now. I’ve got to see Bunbury. Thought he might be in here.”

“Who’s Bunbury?”

“The producer. Probably breakfasting in his room. I’d better go up.”

“You’re busy, aren’t you? Little marvel! It’s lucky they’ve got you to look after them.”

Fillmore retreated, and Sally settled back into her chair, no longer offended by Gerald’s earlier tone. Poor Gerald! No wonder he’d seemed upset.

A few minutes later, he arrived.

“Oh, Jerry darling,” said Sally, “I’m so sorry. I’ve just been hearing about it.”

Gerald sat down. His appearance matched his voice perfectly—a man wrapped in a shroud of nervous dullness. He looked like someone who’d just discovered that the entire world was running on a faulty clock.

“It’s just my luck,” he muttered gloomily. “The kind of thing that couldn’t happen to anyone but me. Damned fools! Where’s the sense in shutting the theatres, even if there is influenza? They let people jam against each other all day in stores. If that doesn’t hurt them, why should going to the theatre?”

“It’s not that,” said Sally. “Poor Mr. Faucitt had it badly. That’s why I couldn’t come earlier.”

Gerald didn’t seem to care. He dug his spoon into his grapefruit like it had personally insulted him.

“We’ve been hanging around here day after day, getting bored to death. The company’s falling apart. They’re sick of rehearsing when nobody knows if we’ll ever open. They were all excited a week ago, and now they’re sagging. It’ll ruin the play, of course. My first chance! Just chucked away.”

Sally listened, feeling increasingly desolate. She tried to be fair—surely he was under immense pressure—but self-pity was one of her least favorite traits in men. And then there was the matter of her own pride. Clearly, her arrival had done nothing to lift his spirits. She couldn’t help remembering what Mr. Faucitt had said about Gerald. He was remarkably self-centered—and now he was proving it.

“That Hobson woman is beginning to make trouble,” Gerald continued, stabbing at his scrambled eggs. “She shouldn’t have gotten the part. Elsa Doland could play it a thousand times better. I wrote Elsa in a few lines the other day, and Hobson went ballistic. You don’t know what a star is until you’ve seen one of these promoted clothes-props from the Follies trying to be one. Took me an hour to talk her down.”

“Why not let her throw up her part?”

“For heaven’s sake, talk sense,” Gerald snapped. “Do you think Cracknell would keep the play going without her? He’d close it in a second. And where would I be then? You don’t realize this is a big chance for me. I’d look like a fool throwing it away.”

“I see,” said Sally, tersely. She’d never felt so wretched in her life. Foreign travel, she decided, was a mistake. It might broaden the mind, but it also left you utterly disconnected from everyone you knew. She realized what she was really resenting: Gerald was trying to have it both ways. A man in crisis could either be noble and stoic, or broken and in need of comfort. Gerald wanted both—he demanded sympathy while simultaneously pushing it away. It was like offering someone a sandwich and then refusing to let them eat it.

“By the way,” said Gerald, “there’s one thing. I have to keep her jollying along, so for goodness’ sake don’t go letting it out that we’re engaged.”

Sally’s chin shot up. That was too much.

“If you find it a handicap being engaged to me…”

“Don’t be silly,” Gerald said, resorting to pathos. “Good God! It’s tough! Here I am, worried to death, and you…”

Before he could finish, Sally’s mood shifted—swiftly, dramatically, like a switch flipped by a particularly vindictive electrician. A simple thought struck her: maybe it wasn’t Gerald who’d changed. Maybe it was her. She’d just come off the train—tired, grubby, hair in disarray. Of course he wouldn’t be thrilled. She felt instantly calmer. If her appearance was the problem, all was well.

She reached across the table and took his hand.

“I’m so sorry,” she said. “I’ve been a brute, but I do sympathize, really.”

“I’ve had an awful time,” mumbled Gerald.

“I know, I know. But you never told me you were glad to see me.”

“Of course I’m glad to see you.”

“Why didn’t you say so, then, you poor fish? And why didn’t you ask me if I enjoyed myself in Europe?”

“Did you enjoy yourself?”

“Yes, except that I missed you so much. There! Now we can consider my lecture on foreign travel finished, and you can go on telling me your troubles.”

Gerald accepted the invitation. He spoke at length, though with little variety. It was clear he believed Providence had invented Spanish influenza specifically to sabotage his career. But now he seemed more open, less aloof. The storm had passed. Sally felt included again.

“Well,” said Gerald, checking his watch, “I suppose I’d better be off.”

“Rehearsal?”

“Yes, confound it. It’s the only way of getting through the day. Are you coming along?”

“I’ll come directly I’ve unpacked and tidied myself up.”

“See you at the theatre, then.”

Sally rose and called for the elevator.

2

The rehearsal had already begun when she arrived. As she entered the dark auditorium, voices echoed with that thin, reedy quality that comes from speaking in empty buildings. She found a seat at the back, and as her eyes adjusted, she spotted Gerald sitting beside a man with a bald head and orange hair—Mr. Bunbury, the producer. Scattered throughout the house were members of the cast not needed in the first act. On stage, Elsa Doland—looking lovely—was acting a scene with a man in a bowler hat.

“Why, what do you mean, father?”

“Tiddly-omty-om,” replied the bowler-hatted man. “Tiddly-omty-om… long speech ending in ‘find me in the library.’ And exit.”

He started to leave.

For the first time, Sally sensed the tension. Mr. Bunbury, clearly a man of temperament, picked up his walking stick and hurled it across the room.

“For God’s sake!”

“What now?” asked the bowler hat, pausing halfway across the stage.

“Speak the lines, Teddy,” Gerald snapped. “Don’t skip them like that.”

“You don’t want me to go over the whole thing?”

“Yes!”

“Not the whole damn thing?”

“This is a rehearsal,” Mr. Bunbury snapped. “If we’re not going to do it properly, what’s the point?”

Teddy, seemingly convinced, delivered the line with injured dignity and shuffled off. The atmosphere was thick with anxiety. The theatre world, Sally realized, was just a giant nursery full of easily frustrated children. The waiting, the uncertainty, the endless rehearsing—it had worn down the company’s nerves. One spark could ignite chaos.

Elsa moved to the door, rang a bell, picked up a magazine, and sat down. Moments later, a young woman entered—Miss Winch.

“Miss Winch!” bellowed Mr. Bunbury.

She stopped, looked out over the footlights—not with pain, but with the amused indulgence of someone attending a party for toddlers. She was square, wholesome, good-natured, with a serious face and a faint, knowing smile. Not conventionally pretty, but Sally understood immediately why Fillmore had fallen for her. Deep down, beneath the surface, there was intelligence.

“Hello?” said Miss Winch, amiably.

Mr. Bunbury was visibly shaken.

“Miss Winch, did I or did I not ask you to refrain from chewing gum during rehearsal?”

“That’s right, so you did,” she admitted cheerfully.

“Then why are you doing it?”

Miss Winch rolled the gum around her mouth thoughtfully. “Bit o’ business.”

“What do you mean?”

“Character stuff,” she explained, drawling pleasantly. “Thought it out myself. Maids chew gum, you know.”

Mr. Bunbury ruffled his orange hair in despair. “Have you ever seen a maid?”

“Yes, sir. And they chew gum.”

“I mean a parlour-maid in a smart house,” he moaned. “Do you imagine she’d be allowed to walk into the drawing-room champing that disgusting, beastly stuff?”

Miss Winch considered. “Maybe you’re right.” Then she brightened. “Listen! Great idea! Mr. Foster can write in a line for Elsa calling me down, another giving me a comeback, another for Elsa saying something else, and then something really funny for me. We can work it into a big comic scene. Five or six minutes, all laughs.”

The producer was speechless.

Just then, a dazzling figure in blue velvet and an impossibly stylish hat burst from the wings.

“Say!”

Miss Mabel Hobson had every natural advantage—except a voice. Her figure was perfect, her face flawless, her hair spun gold. But when she spoke in moments of emotion, it sounded like a peacock having a nervous breakdown.

“Say, listen to me for just one moment!”

Mr. Bunbury regained composure. “Miss Hobson! Please!”

“Yes, that’s all very well…”

“You’re interrupting the rehearsal.”

“You bet your sorrowful existence I’m interrupting it,” she agreed. “And if you want to make a little easy money, go ahead and bet somebody ten seeds that I’ll interrupt it again every time there’s any talk of writing up any darned part in the show except mine. Write up other people’s parts? Not while I have my strength!”

A young man with butter-colored hair—Reggie, presumably—tried to calm her.

“Now, sweetie!”

“Oh, can it, Reggie!” she snapped.

Reggie obediently shut up. He wasn’t a brute; he was just resigned.

“I’m the star,” she declared. “And if you think anybody else’s part’s going to be written up… well, pardon me while I choke with laughter! If so much as a syllable is added to anyone else’s part, I walk straight out on my two feet. You won’t see me go—I’ll be so quick.”

Mr. Bunbury stood up, waving his hands. “For heaven’s sake! Are we rehearsing, or is this a debating society? Miss Hobson, nothing is going to be written into anyone’s part. Are you satisfied?”

“She said…”

“Oh, never mind,” Miss Winch said calmly. “It was just a random thought. Working for the good of the show all the time. That’s me.”

“Now, sweetie!” Reggie pleaded.

Hobson relented. “Oh, well, that’s all right, then. But don’t forget—I know how to look after myself.”

She left, followed by Reggie, swallowed by the wings.

“Shall I say my big speech now?” Miss Winch asked.

“Yes, yes! Get on with it. We’ve wasted half the morning.”

“Did you ring, madam?” she said to Elsa, who had been reading her magazine placidly.

The rehearsal continued, and Sally watched with growing dismay. It was all wrong. Even she, a novice, could see it. Miss Hobson was stunning, yes, but in a role requiring depth and nuance, her beauty only highlighted her inability to act. Sally recalled Mr. Faucitt’s story about the lady who got the bird at Wigan. History, she feared, was repeating itself.

A sudden cry from the front row—Mr. Bunbury was on his feet again.

“Miss Hobson!”

She’d just crossed the stage to the desk, a key prop symbolizing her husband’s obsession with business. He loved his desk more than her, and no wife can stand that.

“Oh, gee!” she cried, abandoning her role. “What’s it this time?”

“I suggested at the last rehearsal—and the one before—that you pick up the paper-knife and toy negligently with it. You did it yesterday. Today, you forgot.”

“My God!” she shrieked. “If this doesn’t beat everything! How can I toy negligently with a paper-knife when there’s no paper-knife for me to toy negligently with?”

“The paper-knife is on the desk.”

“It’s not on the desk.”

“No paper-knife?”

“No paper-knife. And it’s no good picking on me. I’m the star, not the assistant stage manager. If you’re going to pick on anyone, pick on him.”

Mr. Bunbury paused, then roared like a wounded bear.

From the wings, a stout figure shuffled out—script in hand, face pale with terror. It was Fillmore.

3

Alas, poor Fillmore! He stood in the middle of the stage, lightning flashing around his head, and Sally, recovering from her shock, sent a wave of sisterly pity across the theatre. She didn’t often feel sorry for Fillmore. He’d always been a bit lush in prosperity, and his past hardships—coffee and buckwheat cakes, frugal fashion choices—had been healthy lessons. But this? This was tragedy.

Somehow, disaster had struck the Fillmore bankroll, and he was back where he started. His presence here could mean only one thing: he was broke. And yet, he’d still managed to bluff his way through breakfast, pretending he was just “mixed up in the show.” How typical.

She remembered his words: “Cracknell suggested I come down and look at it.” Of course. A lie to save face. But now, standing under the glare of Mr. Bunbury’s wrath, he looked like a man who’d just been handed a bill for the entire city.

She felt for him. Truly.

If actors and actresses are like children—quick to cry over a dropped prop and just as quick to bounce back when the curtain goes up—then Sally had reason to feel both proud and slightly bewildered. She’d done something so simple, so utterly unremarkable: lent her brother Fillmore twenty thousand dollars. And yet, the world had tilted on its axis. The Primrose Way Company, previously slumped in existential despair like a bunch of wet socks left in the rain, now radiated joy like a freshly polished chandelier.

The sudden rise of the assistant stage manager—from lowly paper-knife wrangler to full-fledged show proprietor—struck them all as dramatically satisfying. It was practically Shakespearean, minus the bloodshed. “She’s been through the fire!” said one actress, wiping away a tear. “And now she reigns supreme!” (Though, to be fair, she hadn’t actually *reigned* yet—just bought a new hat.)

Meanwhile, Miss Hobson, that walking cloud of doom, had vanished into the ether, leaving behind only faint traces of vinegar and ill will. Her absence was like removing a sour lemon from a punch bowl. Suddenly, everyone felt… lighter. More buoyant. As if the universe had finally decided it liked them.

Rumors flew faster than a Broadway producer chasing a good deal. Teddy, the bowler-hatted gentleman with a suspiciously accurate ear for gossip, swore the theatre ban would end Tuesday at the latest—“straight from the lift-boy at the hotel.” The cigar-stand girl at the Pontchatrain, a woman whose word carried the weight of divine revelation among theater folk, told the butler that Toledo and Cleveland were opening tomorrow. “No joke,” she insisted. “I saw the sign.”

Hope bloomed like spring flowers after a long winter. The sun, they agreed, was breaking through. Fate, it seemed, had given up trying to keep decent people down. Why bother? They always came back up anyway.

Fillmore, once a man who looked like he’d been sculpted from leftover cardboard and desperation, was now positively *glowing*. His mode of celebrating success? A waistcoat so shiny it could start a fire, a hundred half-dollar cigars (which he promptly smoked while reading the morning paper), and an intense preoccupation with lunch options. “Not today,” he’d say, waving off the menu. “We’re having steak. No, wait—chicken. No, beef. I’ll decide later.” He’d grown at least six pounds in one day—possibly from sheer optimism, possibly because he’d stopped dieting out of shame.

Now, standing on the sidewalk in front of the theatre, gazing at the posters that declared, *FILLMORE NICHOLAS PRESENTS*, he was so large that pedestrians had to form a detour around him. “It’s not me,” he’d insist. “It’s the energy of the moment.” But the truth was, he’d become a man shaped by triumph—a soft, billowy monument to financial salvation.

And yet, amid all this jubilation, Sally couldn’t help feeling… incomplete. Like a perfectly tuned piano with one key missing. She’d made it all happen. She was the fairy godmother, the silent benefactor, the invisible hand that turned disaster into destiny. And still, she wasn’t happy.

It wasn’t until she was driving with Gerald across Belle Isle—one of those rare afternoons where the air smelled like grass and regret—that the answer came crashing in like a dropped cue card.

Gerald, freed from Miss Hobson’s oppressive shadow, was radiant. His usual moodiness had vanished. He laughed easily, spoke with conviction, and even offered her a cigar without hesitation (which she politely declined). He talked about the future—big plans, grand gestures, the inevitable fame that would follow his play’s success.

“If this thing gets over,” he declared, jaw set like a statue of determination, “I’ll show ’em! One success—that’s all I need—and then watch me go!”

Sally listened, but her mind wandered. The sun had slipped behind the trees. A cold wind blew up from the river, carrying with it the scent of damp earth and inevitability.

And then—like a spotlight turning on—it hit her.

Men didn’t love life. They competed in it.

Success wasn’t just a goal for them; it was their religion. Their identity. Their entire reason for breathing. For Gerald, for Fillmore, for every man she knew, life was a race. Win or lose, but *win*. And anything else—love, laughter, quiet evenings—was just scenery along the way.

But she wasn’t scenery. She was *everything*.

To her, Gerald wasn’t a project. He wasn’t a stepping stone to greatness. He was simply *him*. Without the trappings of fame, without the applause, without even a single dollar to his name—he was enough. And that was the crux of it: she was never going to be enough for him.

A wave of jealousy—futile, sharp, and strangely tender—washed over her. She shivered.

“Cold?” asked Gerald, glancing over. “I’ll tell the driver to turn back.”

“No,” she said, staring ahead at the grey sky. “Just… think.”

“I don’t see any reason why this play shouldn’t run a year in New York,” he continued, undeterred. “Everybody says it’s good. If it does get over, they’ll all be after me. I…”

She didn’t hear the rest.

Because the world outside had turned bleak. The sky was leaden. The wind howled like a disappointed critic. And Sally realized, with a quiet sorrow, that no amount of success could ever make her feel like the prize. She was the prize *before* the game began.

And that, perhaps, was the saddest kind of victory of all.

When Sally left Detroit on the following Saturday, accompanied by Fillmore (who was returning to New York for a few days to secure offices and generally make his presence felt along Broadway), her spirits had fully rebounded. She felt guilty for having been so melodramatic, even morbid. Naturally, men wanted to climb the ladder of success. It was their job. She told herself she was deeply invested in Gerald’s triumph, and that the last thing she should complain about was the energy he poured into endeavors that would benefit them both. Her mood had been lifted by the excitement of the past few days.

Detroit—city of warm-hearted audiences—had embraced *The Primrose Way* with open arms. The theatre had opened on Tuesday, just as Teddy had predicted, and a full house, starved of entertainment during its enforced closure, greeted the play with rapturous applause. The critics, who usually disagreed with first-night crowds, this time all sang from the same hymn sheet: Gerald was hailed as the rising playwright, Elsa Doland as the future queen of the stage, and even Fillmore got a mention—though only as “the coming manager,” which, honestly, was better than nothing.

But every joy has its tiny snag. The one line devoted to Gladys Winch read: “Also included: Miss Wunch.”
“Wunch?” Fillmore said, seething. “One of the greatest character actresses on the stage!”
Sally laughed. “You’re being dramatic. It’s just a typo.”
“It’s an insult! A slander!”
But Fillmore’s spirit was too robust to be crushed by such petty indignities. Life was too bright to dwell on one dark spot. Business had been booming all week. Elsa Doland improved with each performance. They received a frantic telegram from Mr. Cracknell, begging to buy the play back—apparently Miss Hobson had softened over time. And best of all, the great Ike Schumann—owner of half the theatres in New York—had seen the show and declared it a winner.

On the train, Fillmore radiated importance. He sat across from Sally, puffing contentedly on a cigar like a man who’d just inherited a kingdom.
“You know what you should do?” Sally said, breaking a long silence.
“What?” Fillmore asked, startled out of a reverie involving fur coats and Fifth Avenue.
“Buy a fur coat. With an astrakhan collar. You’ve earned it.”
“I’m not thinking about a fur coat,” he said loftily.
“Oh, yes you are. I saw it in your eyes.”
“No, I wasn’t. I was thinking about Ike Schumann.”
“You mean *Ike*?”
“I mean *Ike*,” Fillmore snapped. “Everyone calls him Ike. Even *you* call him Ike.”
“He wears a fur coat,” Sally murmured.
Fillmore bristled. “Don’t bring up the fur coat again. And why shouldn’t I have one? I’ve got the money now.”
“Well then,” Sally said, grinning, “when you roll down Fifth Avenue in your car, I’ll point and say, ‘That’s my brother!’”
“‘Your brother?’” Fillmore scoffed. “No way.”
“He is, really.”
“You’re joking. That’s the great Fillmore Nicholas.”
“I know. But he really is my brother. And I was there when he bought that coat.”
“Stop it!”
“And it isn’t just the coat,” Sally continued, undeterred. “It’s what’s underneath. Tucked away inside that mass of fur, hiding behind that dollar cigar, is a man we can all be proud of… a man whose name is spoken in hushed tones…”
Fillmore checked his watch. “I’ve got to go see Ike.”
“We’re in hourly consultation with Ike,” Sally said sweetly.
“He wants to talk about the show. He thinks we should take it to Chicago before New York.”
“Oh no!” Sally gasped.
“Why not?”
She paused. She’d imagined Gerald would have to stay with the company if they opened in Chicago—meaning more separation. But then she realized: Gerald wouldn’t need to stay beyond the first few performances.
“You’re thinking we should establish a reputation in New York first,” she said. “That makes sense. But Chicago could help us too. I’ll think about it.”
He frowned dramatically. “All wrong,” Sally said. “You need to look pensive. Compress your lips. Lay your right forefinger against your temple. Like this.”
“Stop it!”
“Fillmore Nicholas,” she said solemnly, “if you knew how much pain it gives me to tease my only brother, you’d feel terrible. But it’s for your own good. Now go. I know Ike’s waiting with his watch out. ‘You do think he’ll come, Miss Nicholas?’ were his last words to me. And oh, Fill, the yearning in his voice! ‘Of course he will,’ I said. ‘For all his exalted position, my brother is kindness itself.’ ‘If I could only think so!’ he said, gulping. ‘But you know what these managers are—thousand calls, brooding over fur coats, forgetting everything.’ ‘Have no fear, Mr. Schumann,’ I said. ‘Fillmore Nicholas is a man of his word.’”

She would have gone on, but Fillmore had already stalked off, rigid with sisterly indignation. Sally watched him disappear, then picked up a magazine. She’d just tracked a gripping story through a jungle of ads—only to discover it was part one of two—when a voice spoke.

“How do you do, Miss Nicholas?”

Into the seat before her—formerly occupied by the coming manager—slid Bruce Carmyle, of all people. He did so with that air of polite restraint that never left him, as though he were stepping into a drawing room rather than a train compartment.

Sally nearly dropped her magazine. Everyone travels, of course, and finding someone you thought was in Europe in America isn’t exactly shocking—but still, it felt like time had rewound. A chapter reopened. A ghost returned.

“Mr. Carmyle!” she exclaimed.
She hadn’t thought of him since their parting on the Paris express. In fact, she’d barely remembered him at all—until now.
“We always meet on trains, don’t we?” she said, regaining her composure. “I never expected to see you here.”
“I came over,” he said.
She almost said, “Well, I gather that,” but caught herself. She’d been rude to him last time. Very rude. And she always regretted it afterward. So she settled for a flat, “Yes.”
“Yes,” he said, “it’s been many years since I took a real holiday. My doctor said I was run down. Seemed a good opportunity to visit America.”
“Ah,” Sally said, trying to sound interested. “And what do you think of our glorious country?”
Carmyle seemed relieved to shift focus. “I’ve been to Chicago,” he said. “A wonderful city.”
“Oh!”
“I heard you were in Detroit,” he added.
Sally blinked. “You heard? How?”
“I called at your New York address and made inquiries.”
“But how did you know where I lived?”
“My cousin—er—Lancelot told me.”

Sally froze. This was beginning to feel like a detective novel. Not that Bruce Carmyle had followed her across the Atlantic, necessarily—but the idea that he’d taken such interest in her whereabouts was unsettling. It was flattering, sure, but also a little unnerving. She didn’t care about Bruce Carmyle. Yet here he was, tracking her down.

She seized on Ginger as a distraction. “How is Mr. Kemp?”
Carmyle’s face darkened. “We’ve had no news of him.”
“No news? What do you mean?”
“He’s disappeared.”
“Disappeared? When?”
“Shortly after I saw you last.”
“Good heavens!”
“He insulted his uncle,” Carmyle said grimly. “His uncle Donald. Grossly. The one man he should have kept in with.”
“Keeping in with?”
“Yes. His future depended on it.”
“But what did he do?”
“I don’t know the details. My uncle won’t speak of it. He invited Lancelot to dinner to discuss plans—and Lancelot defied him. Rude. Insulting. My uncle refuses to have anything more to do with him. Apparently, he won some money at the tables in Roville. It turned his head completely. My uncle insists he’s mad. I agree. Since that night, nothing has been heard.”

Carmyle fell silent, lost in thought. Before Sally could respond, Fillmore loomed in the aisle, looking suspicious.
“Oh, hullo, Fill,” Sally said. “This is Mr. Carmyle. We met abroad.”
“Mr. Carmyle,” Fillmore said, sizing him up. “Pleased to meet you.”
He liked the man—something about the air of distinction appealed to him.
“Strange meeting again like this,” Fillmore said affably.
The porter hovered nearby, ready to make berths.
“You two should go to the smoking room,” Sally suggested. “I’m going to bed.”
She needed to be alone. To think.

Once in her berth, Sally lay awake, buzzing with strange emotions. Ginger had listened to her! Excellent Ginger! She felt proud. She felt like a creator—she had sparked something, unleashed it. She had transformed a meek, dependent young man into a rebellious firebrand who insulted uncles. It was a feat. Something attempted, something done. By all poetic rules, it deserved a peaceful night.

But the train jolted violently, as if testing new dance steps. Sleep refused to come. And then, in the quiet darkness, the Spectre of Doubt appeared—gaunt, questioning.

Had she done the right thing? Had she meddled too much? Was she a menace to society?

“What about it?” the Spectre whispered.

Daylight brought no answer. Breakfast failed to soothe her. At Grand Central, she stepped off the train in a state of remorseful dread. She declined Mr. Carmyle’s offer to drive her to the boarding-house and walked instead, hoping fresh air would clear her mind.

Now she wondered how she could ever have approved of her own rashness. What demon had possessed her to interfere in another’s life? She had egged Ginger on to ruin himself. She remembered that mad wireless message she’d sent him from the boat—*“Do it! Break free!”*—and blushed hotly.

Miserable Ginger! She pictured him—money gone, wandering London, seeking work, rejected by footmen, sleeping on the Embankment, staring into the Thames, climbing the parapet…

“Ugh!” she muttered.
She’d reached the boarding-house. Mrs. Meecher stood at the door, welcoming her with wide eyes, unaware that Sally had effectively murdered a red-headed young man of amiable manners and excellent behavior—except when influenced by meddling, muddling women.

Mrs. Meecher was chatty. *Variety* had reported the Detroit success. The old alumni were making headlines. Toto wore a blue ribbon in honor of the triumph. There was also, unseen, a chicken dinner inside Toto’s stomach. And was it true Mr. Fillmore had bought the play? “A great man,” Mrs. Meecher declared. “Mr. Faucitt always said so.”

“Oh, how is Mr. Faucitt?” Sally asked, ashamed she’d forgotten him.
“He’s gone,” Mrs. Meecher said, with such relish that Sally went white.
“Gone?”
“To England.”
Sally sighed with relief. “Oh, I thought you meant…”
“Oh no, not that,” Mrs. Meecher said, disappointed. “He’s well. Never seen anyone healthier. Seems this Spanish flu is a tonic. Though,” she added, “he’s had good news. His brother died.”
“What?”
“Not that that was good news, far from it. But the brother left him a fortune. Mr. Faucitt had to catch the Wednesday boat and go straight over. Wind up the estate. Left in a hurry. Sent his love. Said he’d write. Funny him having a brother, wasn’t it? Not that folks don’t have brothers. I’ve got two myself—one in Portland, Oregon, and the other goodness knows where.”

Sally escaped upstairs. For a moment, the news about Mr. Faucitt lifted her spirits. Dear old Mr. Faucitt! His later years would be comfortable. But soon, the melancholy returned. She threw herself on the bed, exhausted. She couldn’t sleep. Remorse kept her awake. And then—creaking boards. Yapping dogs. Someone prowling.

She turned over—and froze.

From under her bed, a shoe peeked out. A grey trouser leg. Then a dusty coat. And finally—a head of hair so red it could only belong to one person.

“Ginger!”

Mr. Lancelot Kemp, on all fours, blinked up at her.

“Oh, hullo!” he said.

Sally didn’t actually believe Ginger was dead—well, not until she saw him standing there in the doorway, hair like a bird’s nest, nose smudged with what looked like soot from a coal scuttle, and eyes wide with the kind of panic usually reserved for people who’ve just been caught sneaking into a house they don’t own.

And then it hit her: *He’s alive!* Not floating face-down in the Thames like some drowned opera singer. Not even close. He was breathing. Standing. And slightly damp, probably from crawling under beds like a fugitive in a bad detective novel.

She let out a shriek that would have made a startled parrot blush. It wasn’t laughter exactly—it was more like a burst of pure, unfiltered joy wrapped in a scream. Ginger blinked. Then blinked again. Then said, “I say, you know—this is rather odd.”

“Odd?” Sally gasped, still giggling like a schoolgirl who’d just discovered chocolate. “You’re under my bed! I thought you were gone forever!”

“I’m not gone,” said Ginger, adjusting his collar as if he’d just been rescued from a war zone. “Just temporarily misplaced. Like a sock in the laundry.”

“Why were you under my bed?”

“Because Mrs. Meecher was after me,” he said, as though this explained everything. “She’s got a dog. A woolly rat. Toto. I hate dogs. Especially ones that think they’re detectives.”

Sally paused. “Wait. You’re hiding from a woman because of her dog?”

“Well, yes. But also because I’m behind on rent. That helps. The dog makes it worse.”

“You’re behind on rent? But you were a millionaire last week!”

“I was,” said Ginger, sighing dramatically. “Then I went back to the casino and lost it all. Then I came to America. Then I tried to be a waiter. Then I broke five plates. Then I got docked. Now I’m basically living off hope and the occasional crust of bread.”

“Ah,” said Sally, nodding wisely. “So you’re poor now.”

“Very poor. And very ashamed. Also covered in dust. And possibly fleas. I don’t know about fleas. But I did sleep on the floor.”

“That explains the smut on your nose,” said Sally. “And the fact that you look like a chimney sweep who lost a bet.”

Ginger touched his nose. “Oh! Is it still there? I thought I’d brushed it off.”

“Nope. Still there. Like a tiny, black beauty spot. Very fashionable, really. Though perhaps not on a man who’s supposed to be dignified.”

“I’ll wash,” said Ginger, already moving toward the sink like a man possessed.

“Good idea. Use my soap. We Americans are generous. Unless you’re allergic to generosity. Then I’ll get you something else.”

“Thanks awfully,” said Ginger, splashing water like a man trying to drown his sorrows—and possibly a few germs.

Once clean and vaguely human again, Sally turned to the real issue: why was he hiding?

“Mrs. Meecher,” he said, looking at her with the solemnity of a man confessing to murder. “She’s relentless. She thinks I owe her twelve dollars. For letting me live here. Which I do. But I can’t pay it. So I hide. Like a criminal. Or a squirrel.”

“Twelve dollars?” Sally said, astonished. “That’s not much! Why not just tell her you’ll pay next week?”

“Because she doesn’t believe me. And she has Toto. Who, I repeat, is a woolly rat with a grudge.”

Sally sighed. “Well, I’ll pay her.”

“No!” Ginger protested, turning red. “I can’t let you do that. It’s... it’s not right. I’m not a charity case. I’m a man with dreams and a future!”

“And yet,” said Sally, “you’re currently employed as a waiter at a place on Second Avenue where you break plates and cry into your soup.”

“I do not cry into my soup!”

“Do too.”

“Do not.”

“Do too.”

“Fine,” said Ginger, throwing up his hands. “But I won’t take your money. I’ll work. I’ll earn it. I’ll become someone important. Maybe even a CEO.”

“Or a right-hand man,” said Sally, suddenly inspired. “My brother Fillmore needs one. He’s a theatrical manager. Busy. Important. Needs someone to carry his hat, answer his calls, and occasionally pretend to understand the plot of a play.”

“Right-hand man?” Ginger repeated, as though the phrase had never existed before. “Is that a job? Do they give you a badge?”

“No badge. But respect. And maybe lunch.”

“I could do that,” said Ginger, eyes lighting up. “I’m good at following orders. Mostly.”

“Perfect. Let’s go see him now. He’s at the Astor. We’ll walk. Then we’ll eat. Then we’ll make him hire you.”

“Are you sure he’ll want me?”

“He’ll want anyone who doesn’t quit mid-sentence or faint at the sight of a chicken.”

They reached the door—just as Toto began barking furiously, chasing imaginary mice across the hallway. Mrs. Meecher appeared like a storm cloud, arms crossed, eyes narrowed.

“Mister Kemp!” she announced. “I’ve been looking for you!”

Sally stepped forward, bright as a summer day. “Oh, Mrs. Meecher! What a surprise! Mr. Kemp is a dear friend of mine. We met in France. We’re going to reminisce about old times—then I’m taking him to see my brother. He’s terribly busy. If we’re late, he’ll never forgive us.”

Mrs. Meecher frowned. “Toto needs a walk.”

“Oh, how sweet!” said Sally. “But we’re in such a hurry. And Mr. Kemp is quite tired. He’s just come from a long journey. And frankly, I don’t think he’s up to walking a dog. Not today.”

She swept Ginger away before Mrs. Meecher could protest further. Outside, Ginger took a deep breath.

“You know,” he said, “you’re amazing. No one else would have saved me from a dog and a landlady in one go.”

“Don’t mention it,” said Sally. “Now, let’s talk about your uncle. Mr. Carmyle told me you insulted him.”

“Not insulted,” said Ginger. “More like… emotionally honest. We had dinner. He wanted me to apologize to Scrymgeour. I refused. Then the soup arrived. Thick mulligatawny. And he started inhaling it through his moustache. Like a walrus. I asked him not to. He didn’t like it. Things escalated. Fish course. Steak. Then I said I was done with the family. And left.”

“Noble,” said Sally, impressed. “You stood up for yourself.”

“I was feeling brave,” said Ginger. “Also, I’d just talked to Bruce. He gets on my nerves. Always talking about ‘family legacy’ and ‘duty.’ I said, ‘No thanks. I’m going to be my own person.’”

“Good for you,” said Sally. “And speaking of Bruce—he said he was sending me books. Did he send them?”

“No,” said Ginger. “But I gave him your address.”

Sally stopped walking. “He never sent me any books.”

“Oh,” said Ginger. “Well, he said he would. I assumed he would.”

Sally stared into the distance. “So he traveled three thousand miles just to send me books?”

“Possibly,” said Ginger. “Or maybe he just wanted to see you again.”

Sally blushed. “That’s absurd.”

“Is it?” said Ginger. “Because I wouldn’t mind seeing you again. In fact, I’d like to see you every day.”

Sally smiled. “Well, now that you’re my right-hand man, you will.”

They hailed a cab. Inside, Ginger leaned back, relaxed. “This feels like the beginning of something big.”

“It is,” said Sally. “The beginning of your career. Your dignity. Your future.”

“And possibly,” said Ginger, “a promotion to full-time assistant with benefits.”

“Eventually,” said Sally. “But first—lunch. And then we’ll introduce you to Fillmore. He’ll love you. Probably.”

“I hope so,” said Ginger. “Otherwise I’ll have to go back to being a waiter. And that’s just depressing.”

“Don’t worry,” said Sally. “You’re not a waiter anymore. You’re a right-hand man. And that’s a lot more dignified. Even if you do wear a tie that’s half a size too small.”

“I’ll fix it,” said Ginger. “With dignity.”

For Sally, the weeks after reuniting with Ginger Kemp felt like a golden age—though not one of those ancient Greek kinds where people lived forever on mountains and ate ambrosia. This was more like a golden age of *furnishing*, which, as any woman knows, is far more exhausting but also infinitely more satisfying.

Peace reigned in her little kingdom. Every frontier—from her apartment’s front door to the back of her mind—was calm. She woke each morning to a world so neatly arranged that even a grumpy philosopher might have been tempted to smile. True, Gerald was still a thousand miles away, supervising the Chicago premiere of *The Primrose Way*—a project that had drawn him into the chaotic theater world like a magnet pulling a paperclip into a junkyard. Fillmore, bless his optimistic heart, had agreed to Ike’s idea of launching it in Chicago first, and now Gerald was stuck there, fixing the mess left by some playwright who clearly hadn’t read the manual on comedy. So much for coming home in a few days. But Sally refused to let that ruin her mood. Life, overall, was too good to waste on minor disappointments.

Life, in fact, was practically perfect. Fillmore was thriving. Ginger was off her conscience (and no longer lurking around like a haunted garden gnome). She’d found an apartment. Her new hat? Flawless. And *The Primrose Way*? It was selling tickets faster than a fire sale at a department store during a blizzard. According to Fillmore, Chicago had forgotten its entire history—its founding, its fire, its famous hot dogs—because all anyone could think about was getting into the theater. The production, he claimed, had stirred Chicago more than the Great Fire itself. Which, given how many buildings burned down then, was saying something.

But the best thing of all? Ginger had a job.

Not just any job. A real, legitimate, paid position at Fillmore Nicholas Theatrical Enterprises Ltd. (Managing Director: Fillmore Nicholas). The brass plate was small, but the title was big. Ginger now occupied the outer office, where his duties mostly involved reading newspapers and preventing mobs from storming the inner sanctum where Fillmore sat like a king on a throne made of paperwork, dreaming up schemes that would bankrupt entire countries.

Ginger wasn’t entirely sure what he actually did. Sometimes he felt like the captain of the ship. Other times, like a glorified errand boy—only less glorified, since he didn’t get tips. His main job was keeping people out, which required both strength and diplomacy. He once tried to explain it to Sally.

“Honestly,” he said, pausing mid-lift while carrying a chair that weighed about as much as a small pony, “if I didn’t sweat a bit helping you after the way you got me this job…”

“Ginger,” Sally interrupted, “desist.”

“But honestly—”

“If you don’t stop, I’ll make you move that chair into the next room.”

“Shall I?” Ginger wiped his brow and gripped the chair again. “Anything you say.”

“Silly! Of course not. The only other rooms are my bedroom, bathroom, and kitchen. What on earth would I want a great lumbering chair in them for? Besides, I think we should’ve gone with the first spot.”

“Back she goes, then?”

Sally frowned. Setting up house was turning out to be harder than she expected. She’d always dreamed of a place where she could lock the door and breathe freely. Now she had it—but something felt… off.

“Hullo!” she said suddenly. “Where’s that photograph of me? I’m sure I put it on the mantelpiece yesterday.”

Ginger, flushed from exertion, looked hard.

“No photograph here.”

“I know there isn’t. But it was there yesterday. Or was it? I meant to. It’s the most beautiful photo ever taken. Not a bit like me—thank goodness—but it looks the way I’d look if I could afford a better face. They touch it up in the darkroom. I value it because it’s aspirational.”

“I’ve never had a beautiful photograph taken,” Ginger said solemnly, eyes downcast.

“Cheer up!”

“Oh, I don’t mind. I just thought—”

“Ginger,” Sally said, cutting in, “pardon my interrupting your melancholy reflections—which I know are deeply meaningful—but this chair is *not* right. It needs to go back where it started. Could you give your imitation of a pack-mule one more time? And then I’ll make tea—if there’s any tea, milk, or cups.”

“There are cups,” Ginger said proudly. “I know, because I smashed two the day before yesterday. I’ll nip round the corner for milk, shall I?”

“Yes, please nip. All this hard work has drained me terribly.”

Over tea, Sally became curious.

“What I can’t understand about your job—your noble job, which I generously secured for you—is the amount of leisure it seems to offer. How do you manage to spend your valuable time—Fillmore’s valuable time—juggling furniture every day?”

“Oh, I usually get off,” Ginger said casually.

“But shouldn’t you be doing… whatever it is you do?”

Ginger stirred his tea thoughtfully.

“Well, I sort of mess about. Interview divers blighters, tell ’em your brother’s out, take their names and addresses… oh, all that sort of thing.”

“Does Fillmore consult you much?”

“He lets me read some of the plays sent in. Awful tosh, mostly. Sometimes he sends me to vaudeville houses.”

“As a treat?”

“To see a special act. Report on it. In case he wants to use it for his revue.”

“Which revue?”

“Didn’t you know he’s putting on a revue? Oh, rather. A whacking big affair. Going to cut out the Follies and all that.”

Sally blinked.

“My goodness. That’s ambitious.”

She was alarmed. Fillmore had a habit of leaping into projects that required millions when he should’ve been counting pennies. He was the kind of man who saw a puddle and imagined a swimming pool. An inexhaustible fount of optimism bubbled inside him, and it often led to trouble.

“It won’t do,” she said firmly. “And I’ll tell you another thing that won’t do. This armchair. It belongs by the window. You can see that, can’t you?”

“Absolutely!” Ginger said, already bracing himself.

Sally’s concern only deepened when she received a telegram from Miss Winch in Chicago:

She didn’t fully understand it, but the tone was ominous. It sounded like a warning from a concerned aunt—or possibly a mad scientist. Either way, she decided to investigate immediately. She abandoned the joys of interior design and marched straight to the headquarters of Fillmore Nicholas Theatrical Enterprises Ltd. (Managing Director: Fillmore Nicholas).

Ginger was absent. In his place stood a pimply youth who thawed instantly upon hearing Sally’s name and ushered her in with a flourish. She walked right in—and found Fillmore with his feet propped on a cluttered desk, studying costume designs like they were sacred scrolls.

“Ah, Sally!” he said, voice weary with the weight of destiny. Prosperity had done its work. His jawline, once sharp and heroic, now bore the faint suggestion of a double chin. He wore a new waistcoat—unbuttoned, naturally. “I am rather busy. Always glad to see you, but I am rather busy. A hundred things to attend to.”

“Well, attend to me. That makes a hundred and one. Fill, what’s this I hear about a revue?”

He loved Sally, but he knew she had a dangerous streak of caution. Women, he believed, were meant to admire, not interfere.

“Oh yes, the revue!” he said, trying to sound casual.

“It’s no good saying ‘Oh yes’! You know perfectly well it’s a crazy idea.”

“Don’t be alarmed. I’m all right.”

Bruce Carmyle. The man seemed to be seeping into her life like a slow-moving oil spill. Everywhere she turned, there he was—uninvited, unasked, and always just slightly creepy. She could do nothing but rage helplessly. The situation was becoming unbearable.

“It’s quite all right,” he assured her. “He’s very rich. Large private means, besides his income. Even if anything goes wrong…”

“The gentleman had a head of red hair that lit up the whole lobby,” Miss Winch explained. “Management had turned off the lights to save money. An Englishman. Nice fellow. Named Kemp.”

“I sent Kemp to Chicago,” Fillmore said, “to check the show. It’s my policy to send a representative when I can’t go myself.”

“Save it for winter evenings,” Miss Winch cut in. “Mr. Kemp may have been there to see the show, but his real mission was to tell me to come back to New York and enter my kingdom. Fillmore wanted me here so I could sit in this office and interview my supporting cast. Me! Can you tie it?”

“Don’t say it! I know it just as well as you do. It’s too sad for words.”

If Ginger Kemp had been asked to list his virtues, he’d probably have stared at the ceiling for a while, then muttered something about “trying hard,” and called it quits. Tact? Delicacy? Sensitivity? Not on his resume. But here’s the kicker: in the days following Sally’s emotional breakdown, he managed to display more restraint than any man with a pulse and a heart should reasonably be expected to. He stayed away. Not because he was noble—far from it—but because he genuinely believed that when someone’s soul is in turmoil, what they need most is solitude. And honestly, who better to understand that than Ginger? When he’d been knocked sideways by life (which happened often), all he wanted was a quiet room, a stiff drink, and a chance to wrestle with his demons alone. So he stayed away. For a few days. It wasn’t easy. In fact, it was downright torturous. But he did it.

By Saturday, however, the silence had become unbearable. He needed action. Something. Anything. A grand gesture. A romantic rescue. A chance to prove he wasn’t just a redheaded bumpkin with a bad haircut and worse timing.

He mulled it over. The more he thought about it, the more brilliant it seemed. Especially since the weather was doing its best impression of a divine intervention. Golden light. Crisp air. Trees glowing like they were dipped in honey. It was America’s Indian Summer, and Ginger, having never experienced such beauty before, was utterly hypnotized. Standing on the roof of Mrs. Meecher’s boarding house—after navigating through a forest of flapping laundry—he watched the sun drench everything in warmth and wonder. This, he decided, was not a day to waste indoors. This was a day for open cars, wind in your hair, and possibly a very dramatic proposal involving a bottle of champagne and a sudden burst into song.

The Maison Meecher was a tall, slightly crooked building tucked into a side street near the bottom of the avenue. From its rooftop, you could see Washington Square, where Italians napped under the shade of trees and kids zipped around on roller skates like tiny, determined ants. To the right, towering above the rooftops, were the smokestacks of a Cunard liner slowly drifting down the river. Four funnels. Ginger didn’t need a telescope to know it was the *Mauritania*—the very same ship that had brought him across the Atlantic. sentimental attachment, naturally. He stood there, transfixed, watching her majestic progress until she vanished behind the buildings. Fifteen minutes later, he found himself standing in the hallway of Sally’s apartment building, staring with utter horror at the back of his cousin, Mr. Bruce Carmyle—dressed in a suit so sharp it could cut glass—chatting amiably with a man in overalls. Ginger’s mood dropped faster than a stone dropped into a well. If seeing a rattlesnake while humming a cheerful tune in the desert were shocking, this was worse. Because Mr. Carmyle wasn’t just *here*—he was *here* talking to the janitor. Which meant only one thing: he was here to see Sally.

And then Ginger remembered the sleek, shiny automobile parked outside. Oh. Oh no.

So much for being the only one with the brilliant idea of taking Sally for a drive on this golden afternoon.

He hadn’t moved when Mr. Carmyle turned around, frowning as if the janitor had insulted his mother. Seeing Ginger didn’t improve his disposition.

“Hullo,” said Mr. Carmyle.

“Hullo,” said Ginger.

Silence. Thick, awkward, and full of unspoken betrayal.

“Have you come to see Miss Nicholas?” asked Mr. Carmyle.

“Yes,” Ginger replied, voice barely above a whisper.

“She isn’t here,” Mr. Carmyle said, almost cheerfully, as if delivering good news. “Not here?”

“No. Apparently… for some inexplicable reason, she’s decided to dash off to England.”

Ginger felt the world tilt. His knees wobbled. He clung to the wall like a man who’d just been told the universe was made of jelly.

“What?” he breathed.

“Gone to England,” repeated Mr. Carmyle, now sounding mildly irritated. “Sailed this morning on the *Mauretania*.”

Ginger staggered backward. The irony was so thick it could be carved. *He* had been standing on the roof, watching the *Mauretania* glide down the river, dreaming of romance and open roads. And now, Sally was on board. Without telling him. Without even saying goodbye.

“Why?” Ginger managed.

“How the hell should I know?” Mr. Carmyle snapped. “All I know is what the janitor told me. She left this morning. No explanation. Just gone.”

Ginger walked out into the sunlight like a man in a trance. Mr. Carmyle was already giving orders to the chauffeur.

“I’ll need the car returned,” he said.

The driver, a man whose face looked like it had been sculpted from granite, opened one eye slowly, spat with precision—like Rockefeller would during a merger—and said, “You’ll have to pay for the day.”

“Of course I will,” Mr. Carmyle growled. “How much?”

Money changed hands. The car rolled away.

Ginger stood there, numb. The sun had lost its glow. The city felt empty. Life had become a series of meaningless gestures. Even the thought that his cousin had wasted money on a car he didn’t use failed to stir him. He wandered aimlessly—through parks, past cafés, down streets that suddenly seemed duller than ever. New York without Sally was like a movie without sound. A city without laughter. A world without color.

Night fell. And with it came a letter.

It was the first thing that had felt remotely human all day. The envelope bore the crest of the *Mauretania*. Ginger snatched it from the rack like a starving man grabbing bread. He carried it upstairs, heart pounding.

Mrs. Meecher’s rooms weren’t exactly five-star. Morris chairs were the height of luxury. But tonight, the surroundings didn’t matter. A letter from Sally could turn a basement into a palace. Ginger approached it with the reverence of a child opening a Christmas present. First, he pulled out the photograph he’d stolen from her apartment—the one of her smiling, carefree, unaware of the chaos she was about to unleash. He studied it for a long time, then propped it against the basin. Then he undid his coat, removed his collar, took off his shoes, lit a pipe, and arranged himself in the Morris chair with his feet on the bed. Comfort achieved. Ritual complete. He lit the pipe again. Then, finally, he opened the envelope.

“Ginger, dear.”

That single line sent a jolt through him. He picked up the photo again, stared at it, and forgot to breathe.

He read on.

“...I'm running away, and I haven't even stopped to say good-bye. I can't help it. I know it's weak and cowardly, but I simply can't help it. I stood it for a day or two, and then I saw that it was no good. (Thank you for leaving me alone. Nobody else would’ve done that. But then, nobody ever has been or ever could be so understanding as you.)”

Ginger looked at the photo again. Then lit his pipe. Again.

“There was too much in New York to remind me. That’s the worst of being happy in a place. When things go wrong, you find there are too many ghosts about. I just couldn’t stand it. I tried, but I couldn’t. I’m going away to get cured—if I can. Mr. Faucitt is in England, and when I went down to Mrs. Meecher for my letters, I found one from him. His brother died, and he inherited a dress-making shop in Regent Street. Laurette et Cie. I suppose he’ll sell it eventually, but right now he’s bewildered. He kept saying how much he wished I was there to help him. And I was tempted. And I ran. Anything to get away from the ghosts and have something to do. I don’t suppose I’ll feel better in England, but at least every street corner won’t have associations.”

Ginger exhaled slowly. He was starting to understand.

“Don’t ever be happy anywhere, Ginger. It’s too big a risk, much too big a risk.”

Then came the part about Gerald. The letter from Elsa. The one from Gerald. The one she burned.

“I sat with it in front of me for an hour, just looking at the envelope. What was the use? I could guess exactly what it said. Reading it would only hurt more. So I burned it. Made a bonfire on the bathroom floor. Smouldered. Flared. Lit another match. Until it was just black ashes and a stain. Just a mess.”

Ginger closed his eyes. He could almost hear the crackle of paper.

She went on about pride. About how her spirit had been shattered. About how she used to think she was so wise, counseling others about love, but now she was nothing but a wreck. How Gerald worshipped success, and Elsa was successful, and that was why he chose her. How she had nothing to offer—just herself, which apparently wasn’t enough.

“And now I’m paying for it!” she wrote. “Oh, Ginger, I’m paying for it!”

She ended with a plea: burn the letter. Pour out the poison. Be her pal. Remember her. Don’t make a hash of it. And if she’d found the photo they’d been searching for, she’d send it. Then he could keep it on his mantelpiece, and whenever he felt like failing, she’d catch his eye and give him a stern look.

“Good-bye, Ginger. I shall have to stop now. The mail is just closing.”

“Always your pal, wherever I am. —Sally.”

Ginger laid the letter down. A sound escaped him—half sigh, half curse. He considered, for a moment, heading to Chicago and breaking Gerald Foster’s neck. But then he realized that wouldn’t fix anything. It might just land him in jail. So instead, he re-lit his pipe and started reading the letter again.

Because sometimes, the only thing you can do when the world collapses is reread the words of the person who broke your heart—and hope, just maybe, that somewhere, somehow, she’s feeling a little better too.

Laurette et Cie, Regent Street, London, W., England
January 21st

Dear Ginger,

I’m feeling better—sort of. It’s been three months since I last wrote, so I imagine you’re thinking, “Well, if she hasn’t gone completely off the deep end by now, she must be doing all right.” And honestly? You’d be right. Not that I’ve magically cured myself or anything dramatic like that. No, I’ve just managed to shove my troubles into the cellar and lock the door. They’re still there, lurking in the dark, but at least they’re not sitting on my lap every time I try to sip tea. Progress, I call it.

Now, about London—I suppose I ought to tell you what I think of it, but honestly, I’ve lived here so long I’ve forgotten how to be a tourist. It feels like I’ve been here since the Romans were still trying to figure out what to do with their bathhouses. The only thing that surprises me anymore is when someone speaks English without an accent.

Speaking of which, Mr. Faucitt hasn’t sold his inheritance yet. He keeps saying he will, though. There’s this rich-looking man with a handlebar moustache and eyes like a hawk who lunches with him weekly. I suspect they’re negotiating the sale of England itself. Poor Mr. Faucitt! He’s desperate to escape to the countryside and raise ducks like a proper country gentleman. London has let him down. It’s not the place it used to be. He’s been away nearly thirty years, and when he came back, he wandered around like a ghost from the past, looking for landmarks that had vanished under new paint jobs.

It seems every thirty years, London goes through a fit of architectural madness. A shop gets painted red instead of blue, and suddenly the old-timers lose their minds. Mr. Faucitt was devastated when he found the Empire had become a theatre (it used to be a music hall). Then he heard the Tivoli had been demolished entirely. But the real blow came when he went to look at the baker’s shop where he once lived in the 1880s—only to discover it was now a dressmaker’s. He stood there, stunned, until a lovely magenta fog rolled in, and he sighed: “Ah, at least *that* hasn’t changed.” Relief, I suppose.

Thank goodness I’m busy at Laurette et Cie. (I used to think “Cie” was the name of the junior partner—like, “Miss Nicholas, meet Mr. Cie, one of your greatest admirers.” I was quite excited.) Turns out it just means “Company,” but still, I have a job. I’m the female version of your role at Fillmore Nicholas Theatrical Enterprises Ltd.—the kind of person who sidles up to customers and says, “Chawming weather, moddom!” even when it’s raining sideways. Then I hand them over to the actual workers. It’s not glamorous, but it pays the bills.

The problem is, Mr. Faucitt is determined to sell. I don’t know if you’re like this, but every Englishman I’ve ever met dreams of owning a house in Loamshire or Hants or Salop. Their life goal is to make enough money to “buy back the old place”—which, naturally, was sold in Act One to cover the heir’s gambling debts. Mr. Faucitt grew up in a tiny village near Cirencester—though it’s actually called Cissister, which I bet you didn’t know. After fifty years of forgetting about it, he’s suddenly obsessed with retiring there, surrounded by pigs, chickens, and melancholy.

He took me to see it the other day. Oh, Ginger, the English countryside! Why anyone would live in a city is beyond me. Grey stone houses, yellow haystacks, muddy lanes that squelch underfoot, fat trees, distant blue hills—the peace! If I ever sell my soul, I’ll demand forty years in a place like this as part of the deal.

You might think I’m too busy to remember you, but just to prove I haven’t forgotten, I read in the paper last week about an “International Match.” At first I thought it was some sort of diplomatic incident, but then it hit me—this was the football game you used to play! So I went to Twickenham to see what it was like. There was a crowd so dense I nearly got flattened. But I bore it—for you.

I learned the English team wore white shirts; the Welsh, red. I asked the man next to me, “Could you kindly inform me which is the English scrum-half?” Just then, the players came near, and suddenly a dozen men in red launched themselves onto a small, bewildered fellow who’d just picked up the ball. Ginger, you are *so* lucky you’re safe behind your little stool at Fillmore’s office. If anyone jumps on you now, you can scream “Cop!” and get help. Do you realize you used to do this? You must have hidden depths I never suspected.

Anyway, while riding on top of a bus down Piccadilly, I saw someone who looked familiar. It was Mr. Carmyle. He didn’t see me—thank heavens. I don’t want to run into anyone who reminds me of New York. Thanks for sending news, but please don’t send any more. It brings back memories I’d rather forget.

Here’s the thing, Ginger: I *need* to write to you—it helps. But please, don’t answer. Can you manage that? I know you’ll understand.

Oh, and congratulations—Fillmore and Gladys Winch are married! From what I’ve seen of her, it’s the best thing that’s ever happened to Brother F. She’s wonderful. I must write to him.

Monk’s Crofton, Much Middleford, Salop, England
April 18th

Dear Ginger,

What’s the use? I’m trying to escape New York, and New York keeps finding me. Last week, I was walking aimlessly down the Strand—and who should appear but Fillmore, arm-in-arm with Mr. Carmyle! I couldn’t escape. Mr. Carmyle saw me. And Fillmore? He’s grown *enormous*. In my worst nightmares, I never imagined him so big. Poor Gladys—when she looks at him, she must feel like a second wife.

Apparently, Fillmore’s still full of schemes. He’s talking about buying English plays. He’s here to stage “The Primrose Way” in London. He’s staying at the Savoy, and they invited me to lunch—like I was a lost lamb being led home. Worst idea ever. Fillmore talked Broadway nonstop. By the time he’d finished his French pastry and was lolling back, breathing heavily, waiting for coffee, he’d made me so homesick I almost burst into tears. I should’ve stayed away.

Of course, the Savoy is just Broadway with a British accent. Americans at every table. Might as well have been the Astor.

If Fate insists on bringing New York to England just to torment me, I guess I have to accept it. So I’ve been drifting. Two days ago, I drifted here. Mr. Carmyle invited Fillmore and me to Monk’s Crofton. I couldn’t say no.

And now I’m writing to you in a vast bedroom with an open fire, armchairs, and every luxury imaginable. Fillmore’s out golfing. He sails for New York on Saturday on the Mauretania.

He told me he’s promoting a lightweight boxing match in Jersey City—guaranteeing huge sums to both fighters. I tried to argue, but he just cited statistics about fortunes made in boxing. Too late now. The fight’s in a week or two. Makes my skin crawl.

But enough of that. Let’s talk about Monk’s Crofton. You probably don’t know it—it’s a recent purchase. Mr. Carmyle bought it from a lord who’d lost everything on the stock market. I hope you haven’t seen it, because I want to describe it.

Ginger, what has England ever done to deserve such places? I thought Mr. Faucitt’s Cissister cottage was beautiful—but this? This is a *Seat*. The kind they wrote about in novels: “The sunset fell upon the walls of G---- Castle…” I could picture Tennyson’s Maud living here.

You drive from the station, go about three miles, turn into a massive iron gate flanked by stone posts with stone beasts on top. Nearby is a tiny lodge with an old man who pops out and touches his hat. You think you’ve arrived—then the car rolls on another fifty miles through beech woods full of rabbits and meadows with deer. Finally, around a bend, there it is: the house.

It’s large, low, square, with a tower and a battlemented upper porch. I imagine in medieval times, they’d stand there dropping molten lead on intruders. Lawns, shrubberies, a lake visible beyond the fields. To the left, a rose garden—soon to bloom with a million roses. Alongside, a high red-brick wall enclosing the kitchen garden.

I explored it this morning. Huge greenhouses, a farm at one end with a stable yard full of puppies—adorable, heart-melting creatures. And a big, sleepy cat who sits in the sun, letting the puppies climb all over her. Everything grows in silence. Thrushes, blackbirds… it’s heavenly.

But there’s a catch. “Where every prospect pleases, and only man is vile.” Or at least, terribly stodgy. Now I understand why you couldn’t stand the Family. Because I’ve met them. All of them. Uncle Donald, Aunt Mabel, Cousin Percival—everyone. Is it a family habit to gather like pigeons, or did I just walk into an accidental reunion?

Dinner the first night—drawing room packed. Not just because Fillmore was there, but because uncles and aunts were everywhere. I felt like a lion in a den of Daniels. I now know exactly what you meant about the Family. They *look* at you. Like you’re guilty of something. Of course, I’m pure as snow, but I can imagine how hard it must’ve been for you—with your permanent guilt complex.

By the way, getting this letter to you is going to be tricky—like smuggling messages in a Civil War play. Letters go on the hall table, and someone takes them to the village by bicycle. But if I do that, some aunt or uncle will see it—and I’ll be socially annihilated. To correspond with a human Jimpson weed like you? Unthinkable.

Last night, someone mentioned your name. Instant roasting party. Uncle Donald leading the charge. I said, weakly, “I met him. He’s part human.” Silence. Then everyone exploded, explaining how wrong I was, how naive I am, how incapable I am of seeing the true infamy of society’s dregs. You’re not fit to speak to me—you’re barely a blot on the human race. I’m telling you this so you won’t think you’re popular with the Family. You’re not.

So I’ll have to sneak this letter out. Maybe I’ll take it to the village myself. But it’s hard—because somehow, I’ve become the center of attention. Every few minutes, an aunt or uncle appears for a “cosy chat.” Sometimes I feel like they’re weighing me in the balance.

Well, let ‘em weigh. Time to dress for dinner. Goodbye.

Yours in the balance,
Sally

P.S.—You were right about Uncle Donald’s moustache. But I don’t agree it’s his misfortune. I think he does it on purpose.

Sally’s mood, as she sat in her New York apartment on the morning of her return, was like that of a swimmer who, after hours of hesitation at the edge of a freezing pool, finally dives in—shivering, yes, but also alive. She was hurting, sure, but it was a good kind of pain: the kind that comes from doing something brave instead of pretending you don’t care. For months she’d been avoiding the fight, dodging decisions like a cowardly boxer. But not anymore. She was in the middle of the ring now, gloves up, ready to take whatever came. And it hurt. She’d expected that. But this wasn’t the dull ache of regret—it was the sharp, electric sting of being *real*. She felt awake. Alive. Defiant.

She’d unpacked, cleaned, and now, there was only one thing left to do: see Ginger. The thought of him had suddenly become urgent. His quiet, dependable presence would be like a life raft in this stormy sea of emotions. She wished she’d sent him a cable—then he could’ve met her at the dock. As it was, the customs sheds had echoed with emptiness, sucking the courage right out of her. She’d stood there, suitcase in hand, feeling like a stray cat lost in a city of giants.

She checked her watch. It was still early. Perfect. She could catch Ginger at the office and drag him out for lunch. She put on her hat and stepped into the street.

The restless spirit of change had not spared Fillmore Nicholas Theatrical Enterprises Ltd. In the few months since Sally had last visited, the outer office had undergone a full-scale identity crisis. Gone was the cheerful, pimpled boy she’d once chatted with; in his place stood a new stripling—also generously dotted with zits, but otherwise completely different. This one wore a permanent scowl and radiated suspicion like a bad Wi-Fi signal. He peered at Sally as if she’d just stolen the blotting paper (which, truthfully, she hadn’t), then demanded, with icy disdain, “State your business.”

“I want Mr. Kemp,” said Sally.

The boy scratched his cheek with a ruler, his face grim as a judge who’d just ruled against a dog named Poochie. No one would’ve guessed that mere seconds before, he’d been trying to balance the ruler on his chin while juggling two paperweights. His dream? Vaudeville. One day, he’d be the star of a show where people paid money to watch him do ridiculous things with office supplies.

“What name?” he asked, cold as a January wind.

“Nicholas,” said Sally. “I’m Mr. Nicholas’ sister.”

On a previous visit, this announcement had caused chaos. Today, however, it worked like a charm. It hit him like a well-placed uppercut. He jumped, dropped the ruler, and stared at her as if she’d just declared herself queen of the universe. By the time he bent down to retrieve it, he’d already recalibrated his entire demeanor. What had started as an opportunity to vent frustration on a random visitor had turned into a golden chance to impress the boss’s sister. He straightened up with a smile so warm it could’ve melted snow in Siberia.

“Will you take a seat, lady?” he asked, even going so far as to dust off a chair with the sleeve of his coat. “Fine morning, isn’t it?”

“Thank you,” said Sally. “Could you tell Mr. Kemp I’m here?”

“Mr. Nicholas is out, miss,” said the boy, with exaggerated sorrow. “He’s back in town, but he’s gone out.”

“I don’t want Mr. Nicholas. I want Mr. Kemp.”

“Mr. Kemp?”

“Yes. Mr. Kemp.”

The boy’s face fell like a deflated balloon. “Don’t know anyone by that name around here,” he said, apologetically.

“But surely…” Sally trailed off. A cold dread crept over her. “How long have you been working here?”

“All day, ma'am,” he replied, sounding like a man who’d just witnessed a tragedy.

“I mean—how long have you been employed here?”

“Just over a month, miss.”

“So… Mr. Kemp hasn’t been here all that time?”

“Name’s new to me, lady. Does he look like anything? You know—what does he look like?”

“He has very red hair.”

“Never seen him,” said the boy flatly.

And that was that. The truth hit Sally like a punch to the gut. She blamed herself. Why had she ever left? Of course Ginger would mess up. Left to his own devices, he always did. And this time, apparently, he’d really gone and done it. Not just failed—he’d made such a hash of things that Fillmore had probably fired him. After all, how many times could you protect someone before they became a liability?

“Where is Mr. Nicholas?” she asked. “You said he was out?”

“Really out, miss,” the boy said, grinning like a man who’d just found a winning lottery ticket. “He went to White Plains in his car half an hour ago.”

“White Plains? Why?”

The boy, now fully engaged in conversation, leaned forward. Usually, he liked solitude—his juggling practice required focus—but Sally had somehow unlocked his inner garrulousness.

“I guess he’s going to check on Bugs Butler,” he said.

“Whose butler?” Sally asked, confused.

The boy smiled—a tolerant, pitying smile. Women, he knew, were often clueless about the real world. It wasn’t their fault. It was biology. Or destiny. Or something.

“Bugs Butler is training in White Plains,” he explained. “He’s a fighter.”

“Oh.” Sally blinked. “Who is Bugs Butler?”

A shadow crossed the boy’s face. This was serious business.

“Ah!” he said, losing all pretense of politeness. “Who has Bugs Butler ever licked? That’s what everyone’s asking. Nobody. Just some nobodies out west—ginks no one’s heard of, except Cyclone Mullins. And even then, it took fifteen rounds to get a decision. The boss wants to give him a shot at the champ, but I told him—K-leg Binns is the real contender. K-leg knocked out Cyclone in five. So if anyone thinks Bugs Butler can go six rounds with Lew Lucas, I’ve got two bucks in my vest pocket that says he won’t.”

Sally began to understand.

“Oh—so Mr. Butler is one of the boxers in the fight my brother is involved in?”

“That’s right. He’s fighting the lightweight champion. Lew Lucas. He’s a bird!”

“Is he?”

“Yes, sir!” the boy said, head tilted like a curious bird. “Lew Lucas is a hot sketch. Used to live on the next street to me. I’ve seen his old mother—close as I am to you. Say, I’ve seen her a hundred times. Is any stiff of a Bugs Butler going to beat a guy like that?”

“It doesn’t seem likely.”

“You spoke it!” the boy snapped, swatting at a fly that had landed on the blotting paper.

They paused. Sally stood up.

“And there’s another thing,” the boy said, reluctant to let the conversation end. “Can Bugs Butler make a hundred and thirty-five ringside without being weak?”

“It sounds hard.”

“They say he’s clever.” The boy laughed bitterly. “Well, what’s that gonna get him? The poor fish can’t punch a hole in a nut-sundae.”

“You don’t seem to like Mr. Butler.”

“Oh, I’ve nothing against him,” he said magnanimously. “I just think he’s no license to mix it with Lew Lucas.”

Sally nodded. Enough boxing talk. More important matters awaited.

“How do I find my brother when I get to White Plains?”

“Oh, anyone’ll show you the way to the camp. If you hurry, you can catch the train now.”

“Thanks.”

“You’re welcome.”

He opened the door for her with the grace of a man who’d forgotten how to be polite. Then, with the air of someone returning to work after a pleasant distraction, he picked up the paperweights and placed the ruler carefully on his chin—ready to begin again.

Fillmore sighed with relief and began to sneak toward the door. The room was massive—half barn, half gymnasium—lined with ropes, punching bags, and other equipment that looked like it had been designed by a mad scientist. In the center, a roped-off ring held a small crowd, mostly plutocrats with fifty cents to spare. In today’s world, boxing training was public entertainment. If you had the cash, you could watch. If you didn’t, you could still come—especially if you were a reporter or artist. Reporters would write glowing accounts of Bugs Butler’s “superb condition” and “lack of apprehension,” while artists would draw him in a state of semi-nudity with feet twice the size of normal.

Fillmore was relieved because Mr. Lester Burrowes—the manager of Bugs Butler—had finally wandered off to deal with some urgent matter. That meant Fillmore could escape to the hotel and eat something. He hadn’t eaten since breakfast, and his stomach was now conducting its own protest march.

So he headed for the door—only to collide with Sally.

“Hallo, Fillmore!”

She spoke softly. But the effect was like a bomb going off in a library. Fillmore leapt three inches off the ground, eyes wide, mouth agape, tongue darting out to moisten dry lips. He stared at her as if she’d just materialized from thin air.

Men in moments of weakness deserve sympathy. Fillmore had every reason to jump. He’d left Sally in England less than a week ago—at Monk’s Crofton. She’d said nothing about leaving. Yet here she was, in White Plains, New York, whispering in his ear like a ghost. No wonder he was stunned. And no wonder, as he regained composure, a chill of dread settled in.

Because Fillmore hadn’t missed the significance of that invitation to Monk’s Crofton. When a man invites a woman and her family to his country home, gathers the whole clan, and treats them like royalty—it’s not just a friendly gesture. It’s a declaration. And Fillmore had deduced, with chilling accuracy, that Bruce Carmyle was in love with Sally. He’d mentally joined their hands and blessed the union. And now—here she was, standing in a boxing gym, clearly having changed her mind.

“Sally!” he croaked. “What... what...?”

“Did I startle you? I’m sorry.”

“What are you doing here? Why aren’t you at Monk’s Crofton?”

Sally glanced at the ring.

“I decided I wanted to come back to America. Circumstances made staying unpleasant.”

“Do you mean to say…”

“Yes. Don’t let’s talk about it.”

“Do you mean,” Fillmore pressed, “that Carmyle proposed and you said no?”

Sally flushed.

“I don’t think it’s nice to discuss that sort of thing, but—yes.”

Fillmore felt the world tilt. Everything had been perfect. The match was ideal. He liked Carmyle—quiet, dignified, wealthy, everything a girl like Sally should want. And now she’d ruined it. With the kind of capriciousness only women possess, she’d thrown away a golden future.

“But why?”

“Oh, Fill!” Sally said, annoyed. “Shouldn’t the reason be obvious?”

“You don’t like him?”

“I don’t know whether I do or not. I certainly don’t like him enough to marry him.”

“He’s a darned good fellow.”

“Is he? You say so. I don’t know.”

His hunger returned with a vengeance.

“Let’s go to the hotel and talk. I’ll get you something to eat.”

“I don’t want anything to eat, thanks.”

“You don’t want anything to eat?” Fillmore said, horrified. “I’m starving.”

“Well, run along then.”

“Yes, but I want to talk…”

But he wasn’t alone in wanting to talk. At that moment, Mr. Lester Burrowes bounded up. He wore a checked tweed suit and a brown bowler hat—defying fashion and logic. He clutched Fillmore’s coat-button.

“Say, Mr. Nicholas, you ain’t going? Bugs is just getting ready to spar!”

He looked at Sally.

“My sister—Mr. Burrowes,” Fillmore said faintly. “Mr. Burrowes is Bugs Butler’s manager.”

“How do you do?” Sally said.

“Pleased to meecher,” Burrowes replied. “Say….”

“I was just going to the hotel,” Fillmore said.

Burrowes grabbed him tighter.

“Mellow,” he muttered. “Mellow, indeed.”

“And after all I’ve done for her,” moaned Mr. Reginald Cracknell, his voice cracking like a wet matchstick, his eyes glistening with the twin forces of heartbreak and excessive consumption of his legendary private stock, “after all I’ve done for her—she *throws me down*.”

Sally didn’t answer. The orchestra at the Flower Garden was playing with such relentless volume that even a shout would have been swallowed whole by the din. Plus, she was currently engaged in a life-or-death struggle to keep her feet from being stepped on by Mr. Cracknell’s erratic dance moves, which seemed to be choreographed by a drunkard who’d never heard of rhythm.

They shuffled past Miss Mabel Hobson, the Flower Garden’s newest “hostess,” who sat at her table like a queen exiled from her throne but still determined to look haughty. She wore old gold and black like a Victorian widow who’d just won the lottery, and as Cracknell passed beneath her gaze, he let out a mournful gulp, as if his soul had just been slapped by a feather duster.

“If I told you,” he whispered into Sally’s ear, “what… wait—was that your ankle? Sorry! I don’t know what I’m doing tonight. If I told you what I spent on that woman—you wouldn’t believe it. “I don’t mean *you’re* like that,” he said, puffing himself up like a wounded peacock. “You’re different. I could tell the moment I saw you. You’ve got a sympathetic nature. Sally knew this. She’d seen him often, but this was the first time Lee Schoenstein, the gentlemanly master of ceremonies, had assigned him to her. “I come here every night and dance past her table, but she won’t look at me. What,” asked Cracknell, tears welling in his pale, watery eyes, “would *you* do about it?”

“I don’t know,” Sally admitted honestly.

“Nor do I,” said Cracknell. “I thought you wouldn’t, because you’re sensible and broad-minded... I mean, nor do I. I’m having one last try tonight, if you can keep a secret. Cracknell kicked her shin with a kind of horrified surprise.

“Yes,” said Sally.

“I thought as much,” said Cracknell, deflating like a punctured balloon.

The orchestra stopped with a thunderous *thump-bang*, leaving Cracknell clapping weakly in the middle of the floor like a lost child. Sally slipped back to her table. Her former partner, after a brief, confused glance around as if he’d misplaced his own dignity, zigzagged off toward his seat. The noise of conversation surged back in waves. The air was thick, hot, and full of voices. Sally pressed her hands over her closed eyes and remembered—again—that she had a headache.

Nearly a month had passed since her return to Mr. Abrahams’ employ. It had been a dull, leaden stretch of days, each one dragging like a bad dream. She felt cut off from the world, as if she’d been unplugged from humanity. Weeks had gone by without seeing a familiar face. None of her old boarding-house friends had crossed her path. Fillmore, no doubt guilt-ridden, had avoided her entirely. Ginger was busy chasing destiny on the south shore of Long Island.

She lowered her hands and opened her eyes. The room was packed, as usual. The Flower Garden was one of many establishments that had risen on the wave of New York’s dancing craze. Unlike its short-lived rivals, it thrived—probably because, as its owner claimed, it was “a nice place run nice.” Its ads boasted: “A supper-club for after-theatre dining and dancing,” “large and spacious, sumptuously appointed,” “one of the town’s wonder-places,” with “incomparable dance-floor, enchanting music, cuisine, and service de luxe.” Clearly, Isadore Abrahams thought highly of his own joint.

Once, Sally had liked it. In the beginning, it had been fun, stimulating, full of life. But now? Now it felt like a trap. She hadn’t had headaches before. And this crushing listlessness—this awful, soul-sucking depression—had turned her nightly work into a chore.

“Miss Nicholas.”

The orchestra had restarted, and Lee Schoenstein was introducing a new partner. Sally stood up automatically.

“This is my first time here,” said the man, as they bumped through the crowd. He was big, clumsy, and clearly not built for ballroom elegance. To Sally, it seemed the entire universe had become large and awkward. “It’s a swell place. I’m from upstate. We got nothing like this where I come from.” He cleared a space using Sally as a human battering ram. Despite her recent ordeal with Cracknell, she found herself wistfully missing that experience. This guy was definitely the worst dancer in America.

“Give me li'l old New York,” he declared, unpatriotically. “It’s good enough for me. I’ve been to some swell shows since I got to town. You seen this year’s *Follies*?”

“No.”

“You go,” he insisted. “You go! Take it from me—it’s a swell show. You seen ‘Myrtle Takes a Turkish Bath’?”

“I don’t go to many theatres.”

“You go! It’s a scream. I’ve been to a show every night since I got here. Every night regular. Swell shows—all of ‘em, except this last one. I cert’nly picked a lemon tonight. I was taking a chance, y’see, because it was an opening. Thought it would be something to say when I got home—‘I was at a New York opening.’ Set me back two-seventy-five, including tax, and I wish I’d gotten it in my kick right now. *The Wild Rose,* they called it,” he sneered, as if exposing a scandalous scam. “*The Wild Rose!* It sure made me wild all right. Two dollars seventy-five tossed away, just like that.”

Something stirred in Sally’s memory. Why did that title sound so familiar? Then it hit her—like a brick. It was Gerald’s new play. For weeks after returning to New York, she’d feared running into him—coming out of her apartment, him out of his. Then she’d read a paragraph in the paper: Gerald was touring with a new play. And “The Wild Rose”… yes, that was it.

“Is that Gerald Foster’s play?” she asked quickly.

“I don’t know who wrote it,” said her partner, “but let me tell you—he’s one lucky guy to get away alive. There’s fellows breaking stones on the Ossining Road who’ve done a lot less to deserve a sentence. Wild Rose! I’ll tell the world it made me go good and wild,” he added, spreading his humor like cheap butter. “Why, before the second act was over, the people were beating it for the exits, and if it hadn’t been for someone shouting ‘Women and children first,’ there’d have been a panic.”

Sally found herself back at her table, not quite sure how she’d gotten there.

“Miss Nicholas.”

She started to rise—and realized it wasn’t Schoenstein’s gold-teeth voice calling her. A man had seated himself beside her, speaking in clipped, precise tones that somehow felt oddly familiar. The fog lifted. Bruce Carmyle.

“I called at your place,” Mr. Carmyle was saying, “and the hall porter told me you were here, so I ventured to follow you. I hope you don’t mind? May I smoke?”

He lit a cigarette with a flourish. His fingers trembled slightly as he raised the match, but he pretended it was just a minor tremor of excitement. Bruce Carmyle believed in the strong man who rose above emotion. He knew this was awkward—but he refused to show it. He glanced sideways at Sally and thought: never had she looked more beautiful. Her cheeks were flushed, her eyes bright. The ghost of Uncle Donald, who had accompanied him on this expedition, faded into insignificance.

There was a pause. Mr. Carmyle puffed vigorously.

There was something calming about his cool, dark, stiff English demeanor. He was unlike Cracknell, unlike the man from upstate, unlike the feverish chaos of her nights. Just looking at him felt like stepping into a quiet room.

“I landed tonight,” said Bruce Carmyle, turning to face her directly.

The orchestra had begun a sultry, creamy melody—Broadway’s latest hit, overplayed to death. But now, somehow, it sounded fresh.

“If you like.”

Bruce Carmyle danced with the precision of a man who believed efficiency was divine. He’d mastered dance early in life, considering it part of a gentleman’s education. Sally, bracing herself for another collision course, found herself swept into the arms of a master. A man who danced better than she did. And suddenly—a miracle—a deep peace settled over her. The tension eased. The music, once stale and cheap, now felt almost sacred. Her entire attitude toward Bruce Carmyle shifted.

She’d never really analyzed her feelings for him. But one thing had been clear since their first meeting: she found him physically repulsive. Handsome, yes—but in a sinister way. She’d always recoiled from him. Now, carried away by the dance, that repulsion vanished. As if a wall had crumbled between them.

She felt his arm tighten. His muscles quivered. She caught sight of his face—dark eyes blazing into hers—and stumbled, feeling oddly helpless. A shock jolted her awake. This dance hadn’t delayed the moment—it had only brought it closer.

In a whisper, carried away by the sudden blare of music, he repeated what he’d said under the trees at Monk’s Crofton, on that spring morning in England. Dizzily, she realized she was resenting the timing—but her mind was numb.

And from somewhere near the roof, a bird sang.

Isadore Abrahams kept his promises. He advertised a flower garden, and he’d tried to deliver. Paper roses hung from the walls. Real tulips bloomed in tubs by every pillar. And cages with birds dangled from the ceiling. One, startled by the sudden hush, began to sing.

Sally had often pitied these birds. She’d begged Abrahams to free them, but he’d ignored her. Yet now, as she sat wrestling with fate, the song didn’t sound like a plea. It sounded cheerful. Soothing. Healing. And suddenly, before her eyes, Monk’s Crofton appeared—cool, green, peaceful under the English sun—like an oasis in a desert.

She became aware that the master of Monk’s Crofton had placed his hand on hers. She looked down and shivered. She’d always disliked Bruce’s hands—strong, bony, covered in black hair. She’d hated the idea of them touching her. But she didn’t pull away. The vision of the garden flickered again… a haven…

He leaned in, whispering. The room was hotter, noisier, fuller than ever. The bird sang again—and now she understood its message: *Take me out of this!* What mattered, really? How, where, or by whom? Only that someone took her out.

Monk’s Crofton was cool and green and peaceful…

“Very well,” said Sally.

Bruce Carmyle, newly engaged, felt… off. Not because of Sally’s acceptance—though he’d hoped for more warmth. He was prepared to wait for that. But he couldn’t ignore the fact that he’d chosen the worst possible moment and place for his grand declaration. Moonlight, solitude, a single candle—those were the proper settings. The Flower Garden, despite its “nice-ness,” felt like a circus.

Music started again—but not the soft, romantic kind. It was a brassy, raucous rendition of a vulgar one-step, loud enough to drown any heartfelt confession. Couples swayed and collided across the floor. Behind him, two waiters paused mid-argument, embroiled in a passionate debate about spaghetti versus ravioli.

Romance was impossible. Bruce dropped into small talk.

“Deuce of a lot of noise,” he muttered.

All his life, he’d recoiled from the “light-life” women of cities. Friends in London had married chorus girls, and Bruce had always viewed them as social pariahs. The passion that had brought him from South Audley Street to win Sally was fading fast.

Sally turned. And in her gaze, there was a honesty so pure it banished his doubts. He hadn’t made a fool of himself. Who could look at Sally and not see perfection?

A warm tide of feeling surged through Bruce Carmyle. Return of the hero.

The blood crept back into Sally’s cheeks like a shy visitor returning from a long trip abroad, and her heart—just moments before galloping around her chest like a startled pony—settled down to its usual business of beating, one beat at a time, as if nothing had happened. Which was odd, because something *had* happened: Gerald Foster had just walked through the door, looking like a man who’d lost both his dignity and his lunch in a single night.

And yet, here she stood, perfectly calm. Not a tremor. Not a sigh. Not even a flicker of the panic she’d imagined would consume her when this moment finally arrived. She’d spent months rehearsing this scene in her mind—Gerald appearing, dramatic music swelling, tears falling, hearts breaking—but now that it was real, it felt… anticlimactic. “Hullo, Sally!” he said, voice thick as molasses and smile wider than a barn door. He leaned against the frame, swaying slightly, shirt-sleeves rolled up, collar gone AWOL, face pale and puffy, smelling faintly of regret and cheap gin. Sally didn’t say a word. She simply looked at him. Coolly. Quietly. As if he were a stray dog with bad breath and questionable intentions. Her nerves, exhausted by the previous night’s drama, had entered what psychologists might call “the Indian summer of over-tiredness”—a state where you’re too drained to feel anything, but also strangely alert, like a robot running on fumes.

“Hullo again,” Gerald repeated, as though repeating himself would make it more convincing.

“What do you want?” Sally asked, flatly.

“Hear your voice,” he slurred. “See door open. Thought I’d come in.”

“What do you want?” she repeated.

His smile cracked. A single tear slipped down his cheek. The alcohol had reached its final stage: maudlin melodrama. “Sally… S-Sally… I’m very miserable.” He struggled with the syllables like they were foreign languages. “I see what a fool I’ve been.”

Sally’s mind flashed back—*this* was exactly how Mr. Reginald Cracknell had behaved when he’d tried to win her over with a combination of weeping and whiskey. It was almost funny. Almost.

“I think you should go to bed, Gerald,” she said, voice steady as a stone.

He blinked. “What’s the use? Can’t sleep. No good. Couldn’t sleep. Sally, you don’t know how worried I am.”

She held up a hand. Not to stop him—though she really wanted to—but to prevent the inevitable apology about how he’d wrecked her life, which she wasn’t remotely interested in hearing. But apparently, that wasn’t what was weighing on his soul.

“I was a fool ever to try writing plays,” he continued, voice cracking like old leather. “Got a winner first time, but can’t repeat. It’s no good. Ought to have stuck to newspaper work. I’m good at that. Shall have to go back to it. Had another frost tonight. No good trying any more. Shall have to go back to the old grind, damn it.”

He sobbed softly, a picture of pathos. “Very miserable,” he murmured.

He took a step forward, wobbled, and retreated to the door like a man clinging to the last raft in a stormy sea.

For a split second, Sally’s composure cracked. Contempt flared—sharp, sudden, and hot. Then it passed. She was back in her armor of indifference.

“Go to bed, Gerald,” she said. “You’ll feel better in the morning.”

Gerald winced. “May not be alive in the morning,” he declared solemnly. “Good mind to end it all. End it all!” He raised a hand dramatically—then dropped it, clutching the door like it was his only friend.

Sally sighed. “Oh, go to bed,” she snapped. “Honestly.”

Her icy detachment began to thaw, replaced by a growing resentment—not just at Gerald for making a spectacle of himself, but at herself for ever thinking this man was anything other than a walking cliché. How had she once allowed him to dominate her thoughts, her dreams, her entire emotional landscape? It humiliated her. And humiliation bred hardness. She felt cold, pitiless, almost amused.

Then came the sound.

A muffled crash.

Not loud. Not terrifying. Just… *wrong*. Like something heavy hitting the floor in a room full of fragile things.

Sally froze. Then, ears pricked like a fox, she moved toward the door.

From across the passage, a perfect fusillade of crashes erupted. One after another, each louder than the last, echoing through the stillness of the night. It sounded like a china shop being dismantled by a disgruntled badger.

And suddenly, the detached Sally vanished. Panic surged—brief, sharp, real.

Gerald had mentioned wanting to end it all earlier. What if he’d decided to act?

She ran across the hall and pounded on the door.

He hesitated. Then shuffled off, muttering something about “not needing to be bossed around,” and disappeared into her room.

She exhaled. Relief washed over her. Now she could clean up the mess—both literal and metaphorical.

She grabbed a broom from the kitchen and set to work. Methodically. Efficiently. In half an hour, the floor was clear, the wreckage stacked neatly against the walls. She opened the window, letting in the crisp dawn air.

Outside, the city stirred. A cold breeze whispered. The sky turned a soft blue. The night was gone.

And suddenly, she felt it—a wave of exhaustion so deep it knocked her off her feet. She stumbled to a chair and fell asleep before her head hit the cushion.

She woke to sunlight streaming through the window and the city waking up around her. Horns honked. Feet clattered. Streetcars clanked. She stretched, groaned, and went to the bathroom. Water on her face. Fresh air. A headache throbbed behind her eyes, but it was fading.

She crossed the hall.

Gerald was sprawled in a chair by the window, legs outstretched, mouth open, snoring like a bear in hibernation. Ugly. Unimpressive. A sight she could barely stomach.

But beneath the disgust, something else stirred—relief. A sense of closure. This chapter was over. Forever. She could look at him now and feel only pity and contempt. The glamour was gone. The illusion shattered.

She shook him gently.

He sat up, blinking wildly. “Oh, my God!” he moaned, pressing his hands to his temples. “I’ve got a headache!”

“You earned it,” she thought—but didn’t say. Instead: “You’d better go wash.”

“Yeah,” he mumbled, staggering toward the bathroom.

She sat in his chair. Everything felt dreamlike. The splashing in the bathroom. The quiet street. The sun. It all seemed unreal.

Then the bell rang.

She opened the door.

Bruce Carmyle stood there, smiling like a man who’d just won a lottery he didn’t know existed. He wore a light motor coat, and his face—usually stern—was softened by effortful cheerfulness.

“Well, here I am!” he said brightly. “Are you ready?”

He’d come to make amends. Overnight, he’d realized his abrupt exit from the Flower Garden had been less than gentlemanly. He’d let his nerves get the better of him. Now, he was determined to be charming. To be *perfect*.

Sally stared blankly. She’d completely forgotten he was coming.

Before either of them could speak, the bathroom door opened.

Gerald stepped out, looking like a ghost who’d been dragged through a mud puddle.

Bruce Carmyle froze.

Gerald froze.

They stared at each other.

Gerald looked like death warmed over—pale, unshaven, eyes red, hair wild. Bruce Carmyle saw not a man, but a symbol. A warning. Proof that Uncle Donald had been right all along.

At his elbow, the phantom of Uncle Donald puffed proudly. “I told you so!”

Bruce Carmyle’s eyes narrowed. A cold fury lit within him. He pointed at Gerald.

“Get out,” he said.

Gerald tried to stand tall. Tried to assert his dignity. But a fresh wave of pain shot through his skull, and he collapsed back into a defensive crouch.

“Get out,” Bruce repeated.

Gerald shuffled toward the door.

Bruce watched him go, hands clenched. For a moment, the man inside him—rarely seen—almost rose to violence. Then dignity whispered: *Don’t be ridiculous.*

Gerald was gone.

Bruce turned to Sally.

“So…” he said.

Sally met his gaze. Steadily. Calmly. As if she were reading a menu.

“This,” Bruce said, voice cold, “is very amusing.”

He waited. She said nothing.

“I might have expected it,” he added, bitter laugh escaping.

Sally forced herself awake. “Would you like me to explain?”

“There can be no explanation,” Bruce said.

“Very well,” she said.

Pause.

“Good-bye,” Bruce said.

“Good-bye,” she replied.

He walked to the door. Stopped. Looked back.

Sally stood by the window, sunlight catching her hair.

For one fleeting second, something in him stirred—something warm, something human. He hesitated.

Then he remembered who he was. Who she was. What she’d become.

The door slammed shut.

Outside, he climbed into his car. Glanced up. She was gone.

Back inside, Sally was on the phone.

“Ginger Kemp?” she said.

“Who?” came the sleepy voice.

“It’s Sally.”

There was a pause. Then, a burst of joyous, riotous energy. “SALLY! YOU’RE ALIVE! I KNEW IT!”

Five minutes later, Ginger was in the bath, singing off-key, fully convinced that life had just taken a turn for the spectacular.

The sun, having done its best to be a decent fellow and stay put for just one more minute, finally gave up the ghost with a sigh that could almost be heard over the rustling of leaves. Darkness crept in like a shy guest who’d been invited but wasn’t quite sure he was wanted—though once inside, it made itself at home with impressive efficiency. To the west, beyond the trees, a lingering blush of orange clung stubbornly to the sky, as if refusing to admit defeat. Above the barn, a new moon hung like a silver spoon someone had forgotten to clean.

Sally stepped out onto the gravel, bowed three times with solemn precision (just in case luck was feeling particularly picky that evening), and took a deep breath. The air smelled of hay, damp earth, and the faint, sweet perfume of something that might have been a gardenia or possibly just nostalgia. Life, she decided, was rather excellent.

The darkness, now fully committed to its role, turned the sky into a velvet curtain studded with quiet stars. The state road—the great highway to Patchogue, Babylon, and other places of questionable importance—vanished from sight, leaving only silence and the occasional flicker of light from distant windows across the meadows. From the kennels came a single, drowsy bark, followed by Toto’s characteristic squeak—a sound somewhere between a challenge and a complaint.

Then, from the direction of the village, came the unmistakable thud-thud-thud of Ginger’s footsteps. He was returning from his errands: provisions, evening papers, and wool for the sweater Sally was knitting (a project she had abandoned after two rows because she couldn’t decide whether to make it blue or “not blue”).

Sally couldn’t see him yet, but she knew exactly when he’d arrive. She felt that familiar warmth spreading through her chest—the kind of cozy contentment that had become her nightly ritual.

“Ginger!” she called. “What ho!”

Toto, ever vigilant, scampered down the drive with another dramatic squeak, only to be met with a cold stare. Ginger, despite being fond of dogs in general, had never developed any particular affection for Toto. He’d protested when Sally, finding Mrs. Meecher distraught over her pet’s sudden lethargy, begged him to take the dog in.

“It’s wonderful what you’ve done for Toto, angel,” Sally said as Ginger approached, dodging Toto’s enthusiastic leaps with the grace of a man avoiding a charging badger.

“He’s a different dog.”

“Bit of luck for him,” Ginger replied. “In all the years I was at Mrs. Meecher’s, I never saw him move faster than a dignified stroll. Now he runs about like a lunatic.”

“The blighter had been overeating since birth,” Ginger declared. “That was all that was wrong with him. A little judicious dieting put him right. We’ll be able to ship him back next week.”

“I shall quite miss him.”

“I nearly missed him this morning—with a shoe,” Ginger added. “He was up on the kitchen table, devouring bacon like it was his life’s mission. I took steps.”

“My cave-man!” Sally murmured. “I always said you had a frightfully brutal streak. Ginger, what an evening!”

“Good Lord!” Ginger exclaimed as they stepped into the kitchen light. “Now what?” He stopped, stared at her, and blinked. “Do you know you’re looking prettier than when I left for the village?”

Sally hugged his arm. “Beloved!” she said. “Did you get the chops?”

Ginger froze. “Oh, my aunt! I clean forgot them!”

“Oh, Ginger, you are an old chump.” She patted his shoulder. “Well, you’ll have to go in for a little judicious dieting, like Toto.”

“I say, I’m most awfully sorry. I got the wool.”

“If you think I’m going to eat wool…”

“Isn’t there anything in the house?”

“Vegetables and fruit.”

“Fine! But, of course, if you want chops…”

“Not at all. I’m spiritual. Besides, people say vegetables are good for blood pressure or something. Of course you forgot the mail, too?”

“Absolutely not! I was on to it like a knife. Two letters from fellows wanting Airedale puppies.”

“No! Ginger, we are getting on!”

“Pretty bloated,” Ginger agreed, puffing out his chest. “Pretty bloated. We’ll be able to get that two-seater if things keep buzzing along like this. There was a letter for you. Here it is.”

“It’s from Fillmore,” Sally said, examining the envelope. “And about time, too. I haven’t had a word from him for months.”

She sat down and opened it. Ginger, climbing onto the table like a man who’d already decided he was going to live here permanently, settled into a comfortable position and unfolded his evening paper. After a quick glance at the sporting page, he lowered it and let his gaze rest on Sally’s bent head, filled with a serene, almost holy satisfaction.

Though married for nearly a year, Ginger still wandered through life like a man who’d stumbled into a dream and hadn’t quite convinced himself it wasn’t a hallucination. He’d seen many things that looked good from afar—women, opportunities, fine hats—but none had survived closer inspection. Except this. Marriage. With Sally. It was the only thing in the world that seemed to come without a catch. No hidden clauses, no unexpected fees, no sudden collapse into absurdity. Just pure, unadulterated bliss.

His eyes softened as he watched her.

Then Sally burst into laughter. “Ginger, look at this!”

He reached down and took the slip of paper she handed him. Printed in bold, cheerful letters, it read:

Ginger frowned. “What is it?”

“It’s Fillmore.”

“How do you mean?”

Sally giggled. “Fillmore and Gladys have started a restaurant in Pittsburgh.”

“A restaurant!” Ginger gasped. Though he knew Fillmore’s managerial career had ended in a spectacular crash—something involving a stock market prediction and a goat—he still held a certain reverence for his brother-in-law. To think that Fillmore Nicholas, the Man of Destiny, should descend to running a *restaurant*—and a *small* one at that—seemed almost indecent.

Sally, however, was thrilled. “It’s the most splendid idea!” she declared. “It really does look as if Fillmore’s going to amount to something at last. They started small—just making pork-pies…”

“Why Popp?” Ginger interrupted, baffled.

“Just a trade name, silly. Gladys is a wonderful cook, and she made the pies while Fillmore toddled around selling them. They did so well they opened a proper restaurant—and it’s a success! Listen to this.”

She giggled again and turned the page. “Where is it? Oh yes! ‘…sound financial footing. In fact, our success has been so instantaneous that I have decided to launch out on a really big scale. It is Big Ideas that lead to Big Business. I am contemplating a vast extension of this venture of ours, and in a very short time I shall organize branches in New York, Chicago, Detroit, and all the big cities, each in charge of a manager and each offering as a special feature, in addition to the usual restaurant cuisine, these Popp’s Outstanding Pork-pies of ours. That done, and having established all these branches as going concerns, I shall sail for England and introduce Popp’s Pork-pies there…’”

“Isn’t he a little wonder!”

“Dashed brainy chap,” Ginger said, nodding slowly. “Always said so.”

“I must say I was rather uneasy when I read that,” Sally admitted. “I’ve seen so many of Fillmore’s Big Ideas. That’s always the way with him. He gets something good, then goes and overdoes it until it bursts. But now that he’s got Gladys to look after him, it’s all right. She’s added a postscript. Just four words, but oh! how comforting to a sister’s heart. ‘Yes, I don’t think!’ is what she says—and I don’t know when I’ve read anything more cheering. Thank heaven, she’s got poor dear Fillmore well in hand.”

“Pork-pies,” Ginger mused, rubbing his stomach. “I wish he’d sent us one of the outstanding little chaps. I could do with it.”

Sally stood up, ruffled his red hair, and smiled. “Poor old Ginger! I knew you’d never be able to stick it. Come on—it’s a lovely night. Let’s walk to the village and revel at the inn. We’re going to be millionaires before we know where we are, so we can afford it.”

They walked off together, hand in hand, leaving Toto to guard the gate with a look of profound disappointment.
