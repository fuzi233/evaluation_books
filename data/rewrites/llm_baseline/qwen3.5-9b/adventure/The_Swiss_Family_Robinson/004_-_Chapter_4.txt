The next morning dawned bright and clear. Before the sun had fully climbed the sky, the entire family was awake. The children, full of energy, leaped around the great tree like playful monkeys.

"What shall we begin to do, Father?" they cried in unison. "What do you want us to do today?"

"Rest, my boys," I replied gently. "Rest."

"Rest?" they repeated, confused. "Why should we rest?"

"'Six days shalt thou labour and do all that thou hast to do, but on the seventh, thou shalt do no manner of work,'" I quoted. "Today is the seventh day. Therefore, let us rest."

"But is it really Sunday?" asked Jack enthusiastically. "How jolly! Oh, I won't do any work; but I'll take a bow and arrow and shoot, and we'll climb about the tree and have fun all day!"

"That is not resting," I corrected him. "That is not the way you are accustomed to spend the Lord's Day."

"No! But then we can't go to church here, and there is nothing else to do," argued Franz.

"We can worship here as well as at home," I assured them.

"But there is no church, no clergyman, and no organ," Franz pointed out.

"The leafy shade of this great tree is far more beautiful than any church," I declared. "Here we will worship our Creator. Come, boys, down with you: let us turn our dining hall into a breakfast room."

One by one, the children slid down the ladder. Turning to my wife, I said, "My dear Elizabeth, this morning we will devote ourselves to the service of the Lord. By means of a parable, I hope to give the children some serious thought. However, without books or the usual Sunday occupations, we cannot keep them quiet the whole day. Therefore, after our service, I shall allow them to pursue any innocent recreation they choose, and in the cool of the evening, we will take a walk."

My wife entirely agreed with my proposal. After breakfast, the family gathered around me, sitting in the pleasant shade on the fresh, soft grass. Following a few hymns and heartfelt prayers to the Almighty Giver of all good, I told the children I would relate a parable rather than preach a sermon.

"Oh, that would be delightful! I like the parables in the Bible better than anything," Franz exclaimed. "When can we hear you read out of the Bible again, Father?"

"Ah, my little boy, your words reproach me," I returned, feeling a pang of guilt. "While I was eagerly striving to procure provisions from the ship to feed our bodies and provide for our comfort, I blush to think that I have neglected the Bread of Life—the Word of God. I shall search for a Bible on my next return to the wreck. Although our own books were nearly all destroyed, I am pretty sure to find one."

At these words, my wife arose and fetched her magic bag. From it, she drew a copy of the Holy Scriptures, which I thankfully received. After reading aloud from its sacred pages, I spoke as follows:

"A Great King, ruling in power and splendor over a vast realm of light and love, possessed within its boundaries a desolate and unfruitful island. He made this spot the object of his special care; lavishing upon it all the varied resources of his might and goodness, it bloomed in beauty and became the happy residence of a band of colonists. Each was charged not only with cultivating the soil but with cherishing in his soul the spirit of love and true allegiance to his Sovereign.

"While this faithful union was maintained, the colony flourished, and the noblest virtues exalted and rendered happy the existence of every member. Yet, inevitably, a discontented and rebellious spirit infected these fortunate subjects. Disobedience and pride brought misery and punishment; the fair prospects of the colony were blighted, their labors unblessed, and total separation from the parent kingdom seemed inevitable.

"Nevertheless, a message of pardon—of free forgiveness—was accorded to these rebels. To all who, humbly accepting it, molded their future lives to the will of the Great King (now revealed in a character even more gracious than before), was held out the promise of removal at last from among the ruins caused by the great rebellion, to the glory and undimmed splendor of the realm of Light and Blessedness."

Having captured the children's interest, I left the allegory behind to press simply and earnestly home the truths I sought to teach. With a short prayer for a blessing on my words, the service concluded.

After a thoughtful pause, we separated, each employing himself as he felt disposed. I took some arrows and endeavored to sharpen them with porcupine quills. Franz begged me to make a little bow and arrow for him, while Fritz asked for advice regarding the tiger-cat skin and the cases he planned to construct from it. Jack assisted with the arrow-making, inserting a sharp spine at one end of each reed and securing it with pack-thread, though he soon wished for glue to ensure it remained firm.

"Oh, Jack! Mamma's soup is as sticky as anything!" cried Franz. "Shall I run and ask for a cake of it?"

"No, no, little goose! Better look for some real glue in the tool-box," I advised.

"There he will find glue, to be sure," I continued, addressing Jack. "And the soup would scarcely have answered your purpose. But, Jack, my boy, I do not like to hear you ridicule your little brother's idea. Some of the most valuable discoveries have resulted from thoughts which originally appeared no wiser than his."

Just as we were directing our sons, we were startled by a shot overhead. Simultaneously, two small birds fell dead at our feet. Looking up, we beheld Ernest among the branches, bending his face joyfully toward us.

"Well hit! Well hit! A good shot, wasn't it?" he cried.

Slipping down the ladder, he picked up the birds and brought them to me. One was a kind of thrush; the other was a small dove called the ortolan, esteemed a great delicacy for its exquisite flavor. Since the figs on which these birds fed were only just beginning to ripen, we reasoned that they would soon flock in numbers to our trees. By waiting until we could procure them in large quantities, we might preserve them for the rainy season, placing them in casks with melted lard or butter poured over them when half-cooked.

By this time, Jack had pointed a good supply of arrows and was industriously practicing archery. I finished the bow and arrows for Franz, expecting to be left in peace; but the youngest son immediately demanded a quiver. I had to invent one as well, completing his equipment. It was easily done by stripping a piece of bark from a small tree, fitting a flat side and a bottom to it, and attaching a string. When attached to his shoulders, the youthful hunter filled it with arrows and went off, looking, as my mother said, like an innocent little Cupid bent on conquest.

Not long after, we were summoned to dinner, and all willingly obeyed the call. During the meal, I engaged the boys by proposing to decide on suitable names for the different spots we had visited on this coast.

"For," I said, "it will become increasingly troublesome to explain what we mean unless we do so. Besides, we shall feel much more at home if we can talk as people do in inhabited countries. Instead of saying, 'the little island at the mouth of our bay where we found the dead shark' or 'that wood where we found coconuts and caught the monkey,' let us begin by naming the bay in which we landed. What shall we call it?"

"Oyster Bay," suggested Fritz.

"No, no—Lobster Bay!" cried Jack, "in memory of the old fellow who took a fancy to my leg!"

"I think," observed my mother, "that, in token of gratitude for our escape, we should call it Safety Bay."

This name met with general approval and was forthwith fixed upon. Other names were quickly chosen: our first place of abode became Tentholm; the islet in the bay, Shark's Island; and the reedy swamp, Flamingo Marsh. It took some time to decide on a name for our leafy castle, but finally, it was entitled Falconhurst. We then rapidly named the remaining points: Prospect Hill, the eminence we first ascended; Cape Disappointment, from whose rocky heights we had strained our eyes in vain search for our ship's company; and Jackal River, for the large stream at our landing place. Thus concluded our geographical nomenclature.

In the afternoon, the boys continued their various employments. Fritz finished his cases, and Jack asked for my assistance in carrying out his plan of making a cuirass for Turk, the dog, out of the porcupine skin. After thoroughly cleansing the inside, we cut and fitted it round the patient dog. Once strings were sewn on and it became tolerably dry, he was armed with this ingenious coat of mail, cutting a most singular figure!

Juno strongly objected to his friendly approaches, darting out of his way as fast as she could. It was clear that, protected by armor that was both defensive and offensive, he would easily put to flight the fiercest animal he might encounter. I determined to make a helmet for Jack out of the remainder of the skin, which he received with infinite delight.
