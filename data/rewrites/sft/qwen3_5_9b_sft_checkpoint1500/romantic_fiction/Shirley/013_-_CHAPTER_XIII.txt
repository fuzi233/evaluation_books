A gentle lethargy sometimes seized upon Shirley, a period of profound stillness wherein she found exquisite contentment. There were moments when the mere presence of the world beneath her and the heavens above sufficed to fill her soul, rendering physical exertion unnecessary to sustain her joy. Following a busy morning, she would often recline motionless upon the grass beneath the shade of a friendly tree, needing no company save that of Caroline, provided she were within earshot. Her only entertainment was the vast blue expanse of the sky, drifting clouds, the hum of bees, and the soft whisper of leaves. In such hours, her books were the dim chronicles of memory or the prophetic pages of anticipation, illuminated by a glorious light in her young eyes, accompanied by a smile that hinted at tales both sweet and hopeful. Fate had been kind to this blissful dreamer, promising continued favor.

Yet, one afternoon, when Caroline approached to rouse her, believing she had lingered too long, she beheld Shirley's cheeks wet as with dew, her fine eyes shining with a humid, brimming moisture.

"Shirley, why do *you* cry?" Caroline asked, unconsciously emphasizing the pronoun.

Miss Keeldar smiled, turning her picturesque head toward the questioner. "Because it pleases me mightily to cry," she replied. "My heart is both sad and glad. But why, you good, patient child—why do you not bear me company? I only weep tears, delightful and soon wiped away; you might weep gall, if you chose."

"Why should I weep gall?"

"Mateless, solitary bird!" was the only answer.

"And are not you too mateless, Shirley?"

"At heart—not."

"Oh! who nestles there, Shirley?"

Shirley laughed gaily at this inquiry and sprang alertly to her feet. "I have dreamed," she said, "a mere daydream—certainly bright, probably baseless!"

By this time, Miss Helstone had freed herself largely from illusions regarding the future, maintaining a sufficiently grave perspective on her destiny and that of others. Nevertheless, old associations retained a powerful hold, drawing her frequently in the evenings to the field-stile and the ancient thorn overlooking the Hollow.

On the night following the incident of the note, she stood at her usual post, watching vainly for her beacon; that evening, no lamp was lit. Waiting until the rising of certain constellations signaled lateness, she turned to return. Passing Fieldhead, the moonlit beauty of the estate arrested her step. Under the clear, full orb, the trees and halls rose peacefully; a pearly pallor gilded the building, while mellow brown gloom bosomed it round, and deep green shadows brooded above its oak-wreathed roof. The broad pavement before it shone pale, gleaming as if some spell had transformed the dark granite into glistering marble. Upon this silvery space slept two sable shadows, sharply defined from two human figures. At first motionless and mute, they presently moved in harmonious step, speaking in a low, harmonious key.

Earnest was the gaze that scrutinized them as they emerged from behind the trunk of the cedar. "Is it Mrs. Pryor and Shirley?" Caroline thought.

Certainly it was Shirley. Who else possessed a shape so lithe, proud, and graceful? Her countenance was careless yet pensive, musing and mirthful, mocking and tender. Unfearing of the dew, she had not covered her head; her curls were free, veiling her neck and caressing her shoulders with tender rings. An ornament of gold gleamed through the half-closed folds of her scarf, and a large bright gem glittered on the white hand that confined it. Yes, that was Shirley.

Her companion, of course, was Mrs. Pryor?

Yes, if Mrs. Pryor owned six feet of stature and had exchanged her decent widow's weeds for a masculine disguise. The figure walking beside Miss Keeldar was a man—a tall, young, stately man. It was her tenant, Robert Moore.

They spoke softly; their words were indistinguishable. To remain a moment to gaze was not to be an eavesdropper; with the moon shining so clearly and their countenances so distinct, who could resist the attraction of such interest? Caroline, it seemed, could not, for she lingered.

Long ago, on summer nights, Moore had been wont to walk with his cousin. Often she had ascended the Hollow with him after sunset to scent the freshness of the earth, where fragrant herbage carpeted a narrow terrace edging a deep ravine. From its rifted gloom came a sound like the spirit of a lonely watercourse, moaning amongst wet stones and under a dark bower of alders.

"But I used to be closer to him," thought Caroline. "He felt no obligation to treat me with homage; I needed only kindness. He used to hold my hand; he does not touch hers. And yet Shirley is not proud where she loves. There is no haughtiness in her aspect now, only a little in her port—that which is natural to her, inseparable from her, whether in her most careless or guarded moments. Robert must think, as I think, that he is looking down upon a fine face; and he must think it with a man's brain, not with mine. She has such generous yet soft fire in her eyes. She smiles—what makes her smile so sweet? I saw that Robert felt its beauty, and he must have felt it with his man's heart, not with my dim woman's perceptions. They look to me like two great happy spirits. Yonder silvered pavement reminds me of that white shore we believe to be beyond the death-flood. They have reached it; they walk there united. And what am I, standing here in shadow, shrinking into concealment, my mind darker than my hiding-place? I am one of this world, no spirit—a poor doomed mortal, who asks, in ignorance and hopelessness, wherefore she was born, to what end she lives; whose mind forever runs on the question, how she shall encounter death, and by whom be sustained."

"This is the worst passage I have come to yet," she murmured. "Still, I was quite prepared for it. I gave Robert up, and gave him up to Shirley, the first day I heard she was come, the first moment I saw her—rich, youthful, and lovely. She has him now. He is her lover. She is his darling. She will be far more his darling yet when they are married. The more Robert knows of Shirley, the more his soul will cleave to her. They will both be happy, and I do not grudge them their bliss; but I groan under my own misery. Some of my suffering is very acute. Truly, I ought not to have been born; they should have smothered me at the first cry."

Here, Shirley stepping aside to gather a dewy flower, she and her companion turned into a path nearer the gate. Some of their conversation became audible. Caroline would not stay to listen. She passed away noiselessly, and the moonlight kissed the wall which her shadow had dimmed. The reader is privileged to remain, and try what he can make of the discourse.

"I cannot conceive why nature did not give you a bulldog's head, for you have all a bulldog's tenacity," said Shirley.

"Not a flattering idea. Am I so ignoble?"

"And something also you have of the same animal's silent ways of going about its work. You give no warning; you come noiselessly behind, seize fast, and hold on."

"This is guess-work. You have witnessed no such feat on my part. In your presence I have been no bulldog."

"Your very silence indicates your race. How little you talk in general, yet how deeply you scheme! You are far-seeing; you are calculating."

"I know the ways of these people. I have gathered information of their intentions. My note last night informed you that Barraclough's trial had ended in his conviction and sentence to transportation. His associates will plot vengeance. I shall lay my plans so as to counteract or at least be prepared for theirs—that is all. Having now given you as clear an explanation as I can, am I to understand that for what I propose doing I have your approbation?"

"I shall stand by you so long as you remain on the defensive. Yes."

"Good! Without any aid—even opposed or disapproved by you—I believe I should have acted precisely as I now intend to act, but in another spirit. I now feel satisfied. On the whole, I relish the position."

"I dare say you do. That is evident. You relish the work which lies before you still better than you would relish the execution of a government order for army-cloth."

"I certainly feel it congenial."

"So would old Helstone. It is true there is a shade of difference in your motives—many shades, perhaps. Shall I speak to Mr. Helstone? I will, if you like."

"Act as you please. Your judgment, Miss Keeldar, will guide you accurately. I could rely on it myself in a more difficult crisis. But I should inform you Mr. Helstone is somewhat prejudiced against me at present."

"I am aware—I have heard all about your differences. Depend upon it, they will melt away. He cannot resist the temptation of an alliance under present circumstances."
