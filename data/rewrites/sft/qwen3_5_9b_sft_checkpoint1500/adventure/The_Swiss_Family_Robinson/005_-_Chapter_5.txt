The following morning, as breakfast preparations were underway, I turned my attention to the exquisite hide of the kangaroo, which I was eager to preserve in its entirety. Once Fritz had ensured everything was ready for our expedition to the wreck, I summoned Ernest and Jack to deliver final instructions. However, the moment breakfast concluded, the boys vanished. My wife, guessing they might have gone to gather the potatoes we needed, suggested I reproach them for departing without permission upon their return. Yet, spotting Turk with them, I reassured myself that no harm would befall them, though leaving my beloved wife alone with the young Franz weighed heavily on me. I comforted her with promises of our swift return, brimming with new treasures from the shipwreck.

Our journey advanced steadily across the bridge at Jackal River, where a sudden commotion broke our silence. To our mutual astonishment, Jack and Ernest emerged from a concealed spot where they had been lying in wait, delighted by the shock of their appearance upon their unsuspecting father and brother. Their mischief clearly convinced them they would accompany us to the wreck. I immediately halted this notion, unable to scold the playful rascals for their thoughtless frolic, especially since I urgently needed to send a message to my wife. I ordered them to hurry home to avoid keeping their mother in suspense, noting that despite their distance, they might still collect some salt. I instructed them to explain that my work on board would occupy a significant amount of time, requiring her patience for at least one night—a message I had hesitated to deliver due to fear of her objection, but which the boys' accidental presence now facilitated.

"Goodbye, boys! Take care!" shouted Fritz as I joined him in the tub-boat, and we pushed off. The current swiftly carried us out of the bay, and soon we were moored securely alongside the shattered hull. Clambering up the broken sides onto what remained of the deck, we immediately laid our plans. My objective was to construct a raft capable of transporting a vast array of heavy and bulky items ashore, far exceeding the capacity of our current boat. Empty water-casks seemed ideal for a foundation; we sealed them tightly, pushed them overboard, and arranged twelve side-by-side in rows of three, binding them firmly with spars. Upon this base, we laid a sturdy floor of planks, protected by a low bulwark. Thus, within moments, we possessed a magnificent raft perfectly suited to our needs.

Returning to land that evening was impossible; exhaustion from our labor and a mere light refreshment from our wallets left us with no energy. Rejoicing that we need not rush home, we prepared an excellent supper from the ship's provisions and rested that night on spring mattresses—an absolute luxury compared to our cramped hammocks.

The following dawn, we eagerly began loading the raft and boat. First, we carried off the entire contents of our own cabins. Moving to the captain's quarters, we stripped away furniture, doors, window frames, and their associated bolts, bars, and locks. Next, we tackled the officers' chests and those of the carpenter and gunsmith, removing their contents in manageable portions as their weight defied our strength. One large chest was filled with an assortment of fancy goods, resembling a jeweler's shop with its glittering display of gold and silver watches, snuff-boxes, buckles, studs, chains, rings, and countless trinkets. Alongside a box of money, these dazzled us momentarily, but more practical to our immediate survival was a case of common knives and forks, which I deemed far superior to the elegant silverware we had previously secured.

To my delight, we discovered numerous young fruit trees, meticulously packed and labeled with names pleasing to European ears: apples, pears, chestnuts, oranges, almonds, peaches, apricots, plums, cherries, and vines. This cargo, originally destined for a distant colony, proved to be a rich and nearly inexhaustible treasure trove for us. Ironmongery, plumber's tools, lead, paint, grindstones, cart wheels, and everything essential for a blacksmith's forge—spades, ploughshares, sacks of maize, peas, oats, and wheat, a hand-mill, and even the numbered parts of a saw-mill waiting assembly—were stowed away. So overwhelmed by the surrounding wealth did we become that we struggled to decide what to transport. Given the imminent threat of a storm that would surely destroy the ship, we knew we could not leave anything behind. Selecting the most vital items, including the grain and fruit trees, we gradually filled our raft.

Fishing lines, reels, cordage, and two harpoons were added, along with a mariner's compass. Recalling our encounter with the shark, Fritz positioned the harpoons with anticipation, amusing himself by picturing himself as a whaler, flourishing his weapon with practiced flair. By early afternoon, both crafts were heavily laden, and we were ready to depart. The voyage began with considerable anxiety regarding the raft in tow, but with calm seas and favorable winds, we spread sail and progressed satisfactorily. Soon, Fritz requested the telescope, having spotted something peculiar floating at a distance. Handing it back, he urged me to examine it; I soon identified it as a turtle asleep on the water, oblivious to our approach. "Steer towards it!" he exclaimed. I complied, allowing him a closer look. Little did I suspect the chaos to follow. With his back turned and the broad sail between us, I missed his actions until a sudden shock jolted me, accompanied by the sensation of line running through a reel. Before I could cry out, a second shock surged through, pulling the boat rapidly through the water.

"Fritz, what are you doing?" I cried. "You are driving us to the bottom!" "I have him! Hurrah! Safe!" he shouted in eager excitement. To my amazement, I realized he had struck the tortoise with a harpoon; a rope was attached, and the creature dragged us away. Lowering the sail and grabbing my hatchet, I rushed forward to sever the line and release the turtle and harpoon. "Wait, Father!" pleaded the boy. "There is no danger yet! I promise to cut the line myself when necessary! Let us catch this turtle!" "My dear boy, the turtle will be a terrible bargain if he overturns all our goods, even if he doesn't drown us!" I warned, granting him a few minutes but insisting he cut the line instantly if peril arose. As the turtle headed for the open sea, struggling against the drag, it eventually turned landward, towing us rapidly toward the shore. The beach there shelved gradually, and at some distance from land, we grounded with a sharp shock, fortunately avoiding a capsize. Evidently exhausted from its role as a steam tug, dragging heavily laden vessels, the turtle sought escape. Leaping into the water, I waded up to it and dispatched it with my axe. Its tenacity of life was remarkable; it continued to struggle until I severed its head from its body.

Not far from Falconhurst, Fritz signaled our approach by firing his gun and shouting in glee. While we secured our boats and unloaded the prize, the entire family appeared in the distance, hastening eagerly toward us. Our new acquisition, alongside the well-loaded boat and raft, sparked lively interest. My wife's greatest relief, however, was seeing us safely returned; our night's absence had distressed her, and she was horrified by tales of our dangerous pursuit of the fugitive turtle.

Anxious to unload goods before nightfall, the boys ran to fetch the sledge. Without an anchor, I improvised by mooring the boats using heavy iron blocks we had brought, requiring our united strength to hoist the massive turtle onto the sledge. Combined with the sapling fruit trees, the load was immense. Making our way home, we chatted merrily about our adventures. The first priority upon arrival was cooking the turtle for supper. To my wife, this seemed a daunting task, but I turned the beast on its back and quickly detached a portion of the breast meat with a hatchet, breaking the lower shell. "Let me first cut away this disgusting green fat," she shuddered, pointing to the layer coating the meat. "Leave the fat, whatever you do!" I insisted. "It is the finest part, the delight of epicures. If there is too much, trim it; it makes excellent lard, and the dogs can dine on the refuse." "And the handsome shell!" cried Fritz. "I'd like to make a water-trough for it near the brook, always full of clear water. How useful!" "Capital idea," I agreed. "We can manage it easily if we find suitable clay." "As for clay," Jack interjected, "I found a grand lump under that root." "Well done, lad! When did you find it?" "This morning," his mother replied. "He came home in such a mess I had to scrape his clothes and wash him thoroughly!" "I suppose I never would have found that clay," Jack admitted, "if I hadn't slipped and fallen into it." "Then you must have searched very diligently," his mother teased. "When you've finished discussing the clay and the turtle-shell," Ernest added, "I'd like to show you some roots I found today.
