The rest of the household filed in behind the Ablewhite family, completing the gathering until we numbered exactly twenty-four souls. It was indeed a magnificent spectacle to behold them all seated around the dinner table, ready for the Rector of Frizinghall to rise and offer grace with his characteristic eloquence.

There is no need to burden you with a roster of the guests; you are unlikely to encounter them again within the scope of my narrative, save for two specific individuals. These two sat flanking Miss Rachel, the undisputed queen of the evening and the focal point of every gaze. Her presence was heightened by the magnificent birthday gift she wore: the Moonstone. Though originally unset, the gem's brilliance was amplified by the ingenious workmanship of Mr. Franklin, who had secured it to her white bodice using delicate silver wire. Its sheer size and radiance captivated everyone, yet only the two guests beside Miss Rachel offered commentary that deviated from mere awe.

On her left sat Mr. Candy, our local physician. He was a charming, sociable man, albeit prone to excessive joking and a tendency to plunge into conversation with strangers without sufficient caution. His habit of inadvertently stirring up disputes earned him a reputation for social clumsiness, though his medical instincts remained sharp and often superior to his colleagues. Regarding the Diamond, Mr. Candy treated the matter as a scientific jest. With grave seriousness, he proposed removing the stone to his laboratory to "evaporate" it via heat and airflow, thereby sparing Miss Rachel the burden of guarding such a valuable treasure. My lady watched with evident distress, wishing the doctor were sincere and that Miss Rachel shared his supposed zeal for science.

On her right sat the renowned traveler, Mr. Murthwaite. A tall, gaunt, wiry figure with a weathered complexion and an intense, unblinking stare, he appeared weary of domestic life and eager to return to the wilds of the East. Throughout the entire dinner, he uttered scarcely more than a handful of words and consumed no alcohol. His sole fixation was the Moonstone, whose legendary status had apparently reached him during his perilous journeys. After gazing at it intently until Miss Rachel grew uneasy, he delivered a chilling warning in his calm, immovable tone: "Miss Verinder, should you ever travel to India, do not take your uncle's gift. Certain Hindu diamonds hold religious significance. I know a city and a temple where, dressed as you are now, your life would not be worth five minutes' purchase." Miss Rachel, secure in England, found this alarming news delightful. The servants erupted in delight, dropping their cutlery to exclaim, "Oh, how fascinating!" My lady, visibly agitated, quickly steered the conversation elsewhere.

As the meal progressed, I sensed a palpable shift in the atmosphere; the festivities were failing to thrive as previous celebrations had. Looking back with the benefit of hindsight, I am convinced the cursed Diamond cast a shadow over the entire assembly. I circulated among the guests, encouraging them to drink, hoping to liven the mood. While they politely credited my efforts to my loyalty, the result was merely silence. Gaps in conversation stretched uncomfortably long. When speech resumed, it was often innocuous yet disastrously ill-timed.

Mr. Candy, for instance, ventured remarks far darker than usual. Consider the incident involving Mrs. Threadgall, the widow of the late Professor Threadgall. This lady, unable to suppress her grief, frequently referred to her husband as if he were still alive, assuming everyone knew he was deceased. When someone casually mentioned human anatomy, she seamlessly introduced her late husband's favorite pastime: studying skeletons. Unbeknownst to her, Mr. Candy, sitting opposite and unaware of the professor's death, eagerly seized the topic.

"They have acquired remarkably fine skeletons recently at the College of Surgeons," Mr. Candy announced loudly and cheerfully. "I highly recommend the Professor visit them whenever he finds an hour free."

Silence fell instantly. Behind Mrs. Threadgall, I poured her a glass of hock. She lowered her head and whispered, "My beloved husband is no more." Ignoring the cue, Mr. Candy continued, louder and more polite. "Perhaps the Professor is unaware that membership cards grant access daily except Sundays, between ten and four." Mrs. Threadgall buried her face in her shawl and repeated, "My beloved husband is no more." I signaled frantically to Mr. Candy, and Miss Rachel touched his arm, but he pressed on. "I would be delighted to send the Professor my card if you could provide his current address."

"His current address, sir, is *the grave*," Mrs. Threadgall snapped, her voice rising in fury. "He has been dead for ten years."

"Oh, good heavens!" cried Mr. Candy. Save for the servants, who laughed hysterically, the room descended into a stunned void, as if the guests themselves were departing toward the grave alongside the professor.

Such was the behavior of Mr. Candy; the others were equally frustrating. Mr. Godfrey, usually eloquent, retreated into silence, perhaps sulking over his earlier humiliation in the rose garden. He reserved his discourse exclusively for a female relative seated nearby—a spirited woman with a penchant for dry champagne. Overheard snippets suggested they were debating profound theological concepts, equating religion with love and heaven with a perfected version of earth populated entirely by charitable women and angelic attendants. Yet, why did Mr. Godfrey hoard such wisdom?

Then there was Mr. Franklin. One might expect him to salvage the evening, but he was in a state of manic energy, bouncing between languages and topics with bewildering results. He discussed marital infidelity with the vicar's maiden aunt in refined French, lectured Lord St. John on the philosophy of bull-breeding in German, and quipped to the county member about democracy in Italian ("We have Love, Music, and Salad"). Finally, switching back to English, he ridiculed the medical profession so sharply that he provoked Mr. Candy into a genuine rage.

The argument escalated rapidly. Mr. Candy claimed Mr. Franklin's nerves were deranged and required immediate treatment. Mr. Franklin retorted that seeking medicine was akin to groping in the dark. They traded barbs until Mr. Candy lost all composure defending his profession. My lady was forced to intervene, ordering a cessation of hostilities. This authoritative command extinguished the remaining spark of liveliness. The Devil—or perhaps the Diamond—had clearly possessed the dinner party. Relief washed over everyone when my mistress finally rose to dismiss the ladies, leaving the gentlemen to finish their wine.

Just as I arranged the decanters before Old Mr. Ablewhite, a startling sound echoed from the terrace: the beat of an Indian drum. Here they were, the jugglers returning with the Moonstone!

Instinctively, I moved to intercept them, but the two Bouncers were faster, darting onto the terrace like rockets. The ladies followed suit, and soon the entire group gathered. Before anyone could react, the performers were executing their salaams, and the Bouncers were affectionately greeting the small boy. Mr. Franklin positioned himself beside Miss Rachel, while I hid behind her. If our fears were correct, she stood there, unwittingly displaying the stolen Diamond to the very men who sought it.

I cannot detail the tricks they performed, nor how they executed them. Amidst the frustration of the failed dinner and the shock of the thieves' timely return, I momentarily lost my wits. The first distinct event I recall was the sudden appearance of Mr. Murthwaite. Moving silently behind the circle of guests, he approached the jugglers and spoke to them in their native tongue.

Had he stabbed them, the reaction would not have been quicker. The Indians froze, then turned with tiger-like speed, bowing and salaaming with exaggerated politeness. After a brief exchange, Mr. Murthwaite withdrew as quietly as he had arrived. The lead performer, acting as interpreter, wheeled around to the guests. His face, previously bronzed, had turned ash-gray. He bowed to my lady and declared the exhibition concluded. The Bouncers groaned in disappointment, blaming Mr. Murthwaite for ruining the show. The lead Indian reiterated that the performance was over. The children collected their hats, the ladies retired to the drawing-room, and the gentlemen (save for Mr. Franklin and Mr. Murthwaite) returned to their drinks. I accompanied the Indians to the gate, ensuring they departed safely.

Retreating through the shrubbery, I detected the scent of tobacco and found Mr. Franklin and Mr. Murthwaite pacing among the trees. The traveler smoked a cheroot. Mr. Franklin gestured for me to join them.

"This," Mr. Franklin introduced, "is Gabriel Betteredge, the old servant and friend of our family." Turning to the traveler, he requested, "Tell him what you have just told me."

Mr. Murthwaite removed his cheroot and leaned against a tree. "Mr. Betteredge," he began, "those three men are no more jugglers than you or I."

Astonishment gripped me. Had he encountered these men before?

"Never," Mr. Murthwaite replied. "Yet I recognize true Indian juggling. What you witnessed was a clumsy imitation. Unless I am profoundly mistaken, these are high-caste Brahmins. To cross the sea and disguise themselves as entertainers constitutes a double violation of their caste laws—a tremendous sacrifice in their homeland. There must be a grave reason driving them to reclaim their status upon returning home."
