Supper-time had come, and Eli’s household gathered round the table, all save Margaret. Catherine, surprised, looked to her husband, expecting him to insist on beginning without her. But Eli shook his head. “We’ll wait a moment,” he said quietly.

“Why, I told her plainly you would not wait for the duke,” Catherine murmured.

“She is no duke,” Eli replied, his voice low but firm. “She is a poor, good girl—my own daughter—who has waited not minutes, but years, for a graceless son of mine. You may set the meat upon the board; when she comes, we shall eat, no longer delayed.”

The scent of roasted meats and simmering stews rose warmly from the dishes, tempting even Eli, who usually held fast to his principles. He relented. “She will come if we begin,” he said. “They always do. Sit down, Mistress Joan—you are no servant here, but a guest.” With that, he motioned the covers aside and raised his knife.

Then, with a sudden crash of sound, the door burst open—not by Margaret, but by a Dominican friar, pale as death, eyes blazing with fury. In one stride he was at the table, towering over Cornelis and Sybrandt, his arms outstretched like vultures poised above prey. His voice thundered through the room, raw and terrible:

“Cursed be the lips that lied and said Margaret was dead! May they rot before the grave, and burn upon white-hot iron in hell! Cursed be the hands that forged those letters—may they be severed by the hangman’s blade and grasp eternal fire! And cursed thrice be the cruel hearts that believed the lie, that tore true love apart forever! May they waste away on earth, joyless, loveless, hopeless—may they crumble to dust before their time, and burn in flames unending!”

He cursed the very food before them, every morsel, every drop of wine, every hair upon their heads. Then, turning from the trembling pair who had nearly vanished beneath the table, he tore a letter from his breast and flung it at Eli’s feet.

“Read this, old man!” he shrieked. “You locked up your son, and now see what monsters you have bred! Let the memory of my wrongs—and hers—haunt you all for ever! I shall meet you again at judgment day. On earth, I will never see you again!”

And with that, he was gone—as swiftly as he had come—leaving behind a silence so deep it seemed the house itself had frozen. The family sat motionless, stiff and white as statues around the steaming, untouched meal.

It was then that Margaret and Jorian entered—just in time to witness the scene: figures petrified in horror, the supper forgotten, the air thick with dread. Margaret’s eyes swept the room, and she gasped.

“Oh, joy!” she breathed. “Everyone is here. No blood has been shed. Oh, you cruel, cruel men! Thank God He did not take your lives!”

At the sight of her, Catherine let out a sobbing cry, then turned her face away. But Eli, having read the false letter and finally grasping the truth, saw Margaret enter—her face pale, her eyes filled with sorrow and relief—and surged to his feet in a storm of rage.

“Stand back!” he roared. “Let me reach those traitors—I’ll hang them myself!”

In an instant, he drew his short sword and lunged toward the two cowering men. “Fly!” cried Margaret. “Fly!”

They scrambled under the table, crawled out the other side, and fled. But before they could escape the house, Eli darted around and blocked their path. Catherine wrung her hands, helpless. Blood was certain to flow—until Margaret and Jorian seized Eli’s arms, holding him fast with all their strength. Only then did the fugitives slip free.

Eli, furious and thwarted, stormed after them into the street, shouting curses into the night. They were a furlong away, running like hunted beasts.

Inside, Eli returned, shattered. He grabbed the wooden board inscribed with the names of the accused, smashed it to pieces, and hurled it into the fireplace. The flames licked greedily at the splinters.

Catherine sat rocking, her apron pulled over her face. Joan rushed to her husband’s side. Margaret knelt beside Catherine, her arms wrapped tightly around her, whispering comfort despite her own breathless exhaustion.

But Catherine could not be soothed. “Oh, my poor children!” she wept. “Miserable mother! It’s a mercy Kate was ill upstairs. Thank God for that!” She broke into fresh sobs. “It would have killed her. He should have stayed in Italy. Why come home to curse his own flesh and blood? To tear us all apart?”

“Hold your tongue, woman!” Eli snapped. “You still stand with the guilty! Your weakness made these rogues what they are. I wanted to correct them young—sharp remedies for sore ills—but you always shielded them, undermined me, weakened my hand. And you, Margaret—leave comforting her. Her pain is nothing compared to yours. Yet she has no justice in her bones, nor ever will. Come to me—now. You are my daughter from this hour.”

It was a command. Margaret kissed Catherine gently, then tottered to Eli’s side. He placed her in a chair beside him, and she laid her head upon his chest, silent—no tears, only a depth of sorrow too great for words.

“Poor lamb,” he whispered.

After a long pause, Eli spoke again, his voice cracked but steady. “Come, good folk,” he said to Jorian and Joan, “we are in trouble, as you see. But that does not mean you must go hungry. For the sake of Our Lady, eat. Do not add to my grief by making me seem a churl. What the devil!” he added with a grim attempt at courage, “the more knaves I rid myself of, the greater my need of honest men and women—those who can clear the dish and bring light to my eyes. Eat, I beg you.”

Catherine, still weeping, nodded weakly. Such simple souls—so noble, so old-fashioned in their hospitality.

Jorian, whose appetite had grown sharp since his illness, was ready to obey. But Joan leaned close and whispered something. Instantly, he stepped back.

“No,” he said firmly. “I will not touch food cursed by Holy Church.”

“Indeed,” Eli murmured, abashed. “My son, raised at my table, has cursed my own victuals. That is strange. Well—what God wills, man must endure.”

The supper was carried out into the yard and cast aside.

Jorian took Joan home. That night, sorrow settled heavily over Eli’s house.

Meanwhile, where was Clement?

He lay full-length upon the cold stone floor of the convent church, his lips pressed against the lowest step of the altar, consumed by terror, guilt, and shame. Through the storm of his penitence, however, flickered a fragile joy—Margaret was alive.

Night fell, and he remained there, weeping and praying. Dawn would have found him still, but then a thought struck him like lightning: in his obsession with his own sins and Margaret’s suffering, he had neglected another duty—one far more urgent. A man was dying, and he had failed to tend him.

He rose, groaning under the weight of his many failings, and set out into the rain. The sky darkened; wolves howled in the distance, closing in. But Clement cared little. He was himself again, or nearly so—his soul reborn in contrition. Danger meant nothing now. Duty called.
