Marianne would have believed herself unpardonable if she had slept at all on that first night after Willoughby’s departure. Rest would have seemed a kind of treachery—as if her body could grow calm while her heart was in uproar. Yet no such composure threatened her. The house at Allenham lay quiet under its own familiar sounds—the settling of old timbers, the faint rasp of wind in hedgerow thorns, the far-off, human-small barking of a dog—and she heard them all too clearly, as one hears in illness the ticking of a clock like an accusation. She did not close her eyes. She wept until the tears themselves felt like the only sustenance she could bear, and when morning came, it brought no relief: only a headache, a voice she could not command, and a refusal—stubborn, almost ceremonial—to take nourishment.

Her mother and sisters suffered with her at every moment. They pressed tea upon her, and toasted bread, and the smallest persuasion—“Only a little, Marianne”—as if warmth and sweetness might coax the world back into order. She turned her face away from the steam of the cup, from the buttery scent that would usually have tempted her, and would not be comforted. It was not that she did not feel their love; it was that her grief held her so entirely that even kindness seemed an intrusion on her devotion to what she had lost.

When breakfast ended, she escaped. She went out alone and wandered through the village of Allenham as if its lanes might still contain the echo of his step. The world went on with its ordinary labours. Doors opened; pails clinked; a cart-wheel complained over stones; somewhere a woman shook out linen, and the dry slap of cloth against cloth sounded like a brisk rebuke to Marianne’s ruin. To her it all felt indecently alive. She walked where she and Willoughby had walked, and each familiar turn of hedge and gate brought back a remembered happiness so sharp it became pain. She cried openly, careless of who might see. She indulged her recollections as if they were a sacred duty, and the morning slipped away in that worship of the past.

The evening did not soften her; it only changed the instruments of her grief. She returned to music with the same fierce appetite with which she had rejected food. She sat at the pianoforte and played every air she had played for Willoughby, every song where their voices had once met and braided together. The keys under her fingers were cool at first, then warmed; her own breath was unsteady. She would sing a line and break; begin again; dissolve again. And between the pieces she sat staring at the sheets of music he had copied out for her—his notes, his handwriting—till her eyes could no longer make out the lines and she had wrung from her heart all the additional sadness it could yield in one sitting. The next day she would return to it, and the next—grief, like a hungry guest, served and served again.

Books became another dish of misery she chose with intention. She would read nothing but what they had read together, as if a page, like a melody, could hold the very shape of a former hour; and each passage, once shared, now felt like a language half-killed by absence—meaning surviving, but the living voice gone. It was not that the words had changed; it was that the translation of the old experience into the present would never be faithful. What she had felt with him beside her could not be rendered into solitude without loss; and she clung to that loss as proof.

Such violence could not be maintained forever. Within a few days her anguish sank into a quieter melancholy, but it did not truly leave her. She still took solitary walks and returned to her silent meditations; and from time to time, as if grief were a spring touched by a careless hand, tears would burst forth as freshly as at first.

No letter came from Willoughby. No messenger arrived; no folded paper with his hand upon it was brought in with the morning’s post. Marianne did not even seem to expect one. That, perhaps, was what troubled the others most. Mrs. Dashwood was astonished; Elinor grew uneasy again, for silence, to her, was not a romance but a fact that demanded explanation.

Mrs. Dashwood, however, could always find explanations when she wanted them—explanations that soothed her own heart, at least. “Remember, Elinor,” she said, “how very often Sir John fetches our letters himself from the post, and carries them to it. We have already agreed that secrecy may be necessary; and we must acknowledge that it could not be maintained if their correspondence were to pass through Sir John’s hands.”

Elinor could not deny the truth of this. Sir John’s friendliness had a loudness to it; it was full of cheerful intrusion, like a hunting-horn sounded too close to one’s ear. If there were a secret, it could not thrive under such noise. Still, Elinor’s mind sought a remedy as carefully as a sensible cook seeks to correct a dish gone wrong—by taste, by method, by certainty, not by wishes.

There was one way so direct, so plain, and to Elinor so preferable, that she could not help proposing it. “Why do you not ask Marianne at once,” she said, “whether she is or she is not engaged to Willoughby? From you—her mother, and so kind, so indulgent a mother—the question could not give offence. It would be the natural result of your affection. She used to be all unreserve, and to you more especially.”

“I would not ask such a question for the world,” replied Mrs. Dashwood. “Supposing it possible that they are not engaged, what distress would not such an enquiry inflict! At any rate it would be most ungenerous. I should never deserve her confidence again, after forcing from her a confession of what is meant at present to be unacknowledged to any one. I know Marianne’s heart: I know that she dearly loves me, and that I shall not be the last to whom the affair is made known, when circumstances make the revealment of it eligible. I would not attempt to force the confidence of any one—of a child much less—because a sense of duty would prevent the denial which her wishes might direct.”

Elinor thought this generosity overstretched. Marianne was young; and what Mrs. Dashwood called delicacy seemed to Elinor like leaving a pot unattended because one was too refined to stir it. She urged the matter a little farther, but in vain. Common sense, common care, common prudence—these were drowned in Mrs. Dashwood’s romantic tenderness.

For several days, Willoughby’s name was scarcely spoken before Marianne by any of her family. Sir John and Mrs. Jennings were not so considerate; their jokes and witticisms—good-humoured in intention, loud in delivery—fell like stones dropped into deep water, making fresh disturbance. Their voices were part of the neighbourhood’s constant soundscape: jovial, bustling, never content with quiet. Marianne’s suffering had no room for such noise, and yet it had to endure it.

One evening, Mrs. Dashwood, taking up a volume of Shakespeare by accident, exclaimed, “We have never finished Hamlet, Marianne; our dear Willoughby went away before we could get through it. We will put it by, that when he comes again… But it may be months, perhaps, before that happens.”

“Months!” cried Marianne, with a shock so immediate it seemed to lift her from her languor. “No—nor many weeks.”

Mrs. Dashwood was sorry for what she had said; but Elinor felt a quiet, sharp pleasure, for Marianne’s reply was so full of confidence, so suggestive of knowledge and expectation, that it promised more certainty than all Mrs. Dashwood’s comforting inventions.

About a week after Willoughby had left the country, Marianne was at last persuaded to join her sisters on their usual walk, instead of fleeing alone. Until then she had avoided companionship with an art that was almost violent. If Elinor and Margaret proposed the downs, Marianne would steal away towards the lanes; if they spoke of the valley, Marianne would climb the hills and vanish as if the landscape itself could hide her. Elinor disapproved this constant seclusion; and at length, by steady exertion, she secured Marianne’s company.

They walked along the road through the valley, and chiefly in silence. Marianne’s mind could not be governed; and Elinor, satisfied with gaining one point, would not press for more. Their footsteps made a soft, regular sound on the road—an honest, human rhythm—while above them the wind moved through late leaves and bare twigs with a thin, restless whisper. Elinor listened to it as she listened to everything now: as a measure of the world’s health. The country, at least, was not yet drowned in any harsh, manufactured roar; its noises were the proper ones—wind, birds, distant labour—sounds that belonged, in their very textures, to living.

Beyond the entrance of the valley the land became less wild, more open, though still rich. A long stretch of the road they had travelled when first coming to Barton lay before them; and reaching a point they had never happened to attain in their walks before, they stopped to look around and examine the prospect, as if by seeing their home at a new angle they might better understand their altered lives.

Among the objects in the scene they soon discovered one that moved: a man on horseback riding towards them. In a few minutes they could distinguish him to be a gentleman. In a moment after that, Marianne, seized by hope so fierce it seemed almost like rapture, cried out, “It is he; it is indeed;—I know it is!” and would have hastened to meet him.

“Indeed, Marianne,” cried Elinor, catching at her with the urgency of both caution and love, “I think you are mistaken. It is not Willoughby. The person is not tall enough for him, and has not his air.”

“He has, he has!” cried Marianne. “I am sure he has. His air, his coat, his horse. I knew how soon he would come.”

She walked eagerly on as she spoke. Elinor, almost certain it was not Willoughby, quickened her pace to keep up with her, determined at least to shield Marianne from any particular attention the village might pay to so public a display.

They were soon within thirty yards of the gentleman. Marianne looked again; and in that second, cruelly clear look, her heart seemed to drop within her. She turned abruptly and began to hurry back.

Both her sisters called to detain her; and then a third voice, almost as well known as Willoughby’s though gentler and less vivid, joined theirs in begging her to stop. Marianne turned with surprise—and saw Edward Ferrars.

He was, at that moment, the only person in the world who could be forgiven for not being Willoughby. He was the only one who could coax even a brief smile from her. She wiped her tears and smiled on him; and in the honest warmth of her affection for her sister, she forgot—briefly—her own disappointment.

Edward dismounted, gave his horse to his servant, and walked back with them toward Barton. He had come purposely to visit them. All three sisters welcomed him with cordiality, and Marianne, in particular, showed more warmth than even Elinor did—so ready was she to be grateful for any kindness not entangled with her misery.

Yet to Marianne, the meeting between Edward and Elinor was only a continuation of that unaccountable coldness she had often observed at Norland in their behaviour toward each other. On Edward’s side especially there was a lack of everything a lover should look and say. He was confused; he seemed scarcely sensible of pleasure in seeing them; he looked neither rapturous nor gay. He said little, and what he did say was forced from him by questions. He distinguished Elinor by no particular mark of affection.

Marianne watched and listened with increasing astonishment. Her mind—always quick to compare, to feel contrasts as if they were moral truths—began almost to dislike Edward. And as every feeling with her found its natural conclusion in Willoughby, her thoughts flew back to him, whose manners, whose animation, whose ready eloquence seemed now a blazing foil to Edward’s reserve.

After a short silence that followed the first enquiries, Marianne asked Edward if he came directly from London.

“No,” he said. He had been in Devonshire a fortnight.

“A fortnight!” Marianne repeated, surprised that he had been so long in the same county with Elinor and had not come sooner.

He looked rather distressed as he added that he had been staying with some friends near Plymouth.

“Have you been lately in Sussex?” Elinor asked.

“I was at Norland about a month ago.”

“And how does dear, dear Norland look?” cried Marianne.

“Dear, dear Norland,” said Elinor, with an effort at steadiness, “probably looks much as it always does at this time of the year. The woods and walks thickly covered with dead leaves.”

“Oh,” cried Marianne, “with what transporting sensation have I formerly seen them fall! How have I delighted, as I walked, to see them driven in showers about me by the wind! What feelings have they—the season, the air altogether—inspired! Now there is no one to regard them. They are seen only as a nuisance, swept hastily off, and driven as much as possible from the sight.”

“It is not every one,” said Elinor, “who has your passion for dead leaves.”

“No; my feelings are not often shared, not often understood. But sometimes they are.” As she spoke, she sank into a reverie, as if listening to some inward music no one else could hear—an unrepeatable dialect of the heart that refused to be translated into ordinary words.

Then, rousing herself, she said, “Now, Edward,” calling his attention to the prospect, “here is Barton valley. Look up to it, and be tranquil if you can. Look at those hills! Did you ever see their equals? To the left is Barton park, amongst those woods and plantations. You may see the end of the house. And there, beneath that farthest hill, which rises with such grandeur, is our cottage.”

“It is a beautiful country,” he replied; “but these bottoms must be dirty in winter.”

“How can you think of dirt, with such objects before you?”

“Because,” he answered, smiling, “among the rest of the objects before me, I see a very dirty lane.”

“How strange!” Marianne said to herself as she walked on, and the strangeness, to her, was not merely in the words but in the whole manner—how different people named the same world, how the same scene could be spoken in such unlike languages, one choosing grandeur and feeling, another choosing inconvenience and mud.

“Have you an agreeable neighbourhood here?” Edward asked. “Are the Middletons pleasant people?”

“No, not all,” Marianne answered at once; “we could not be more unfortunately situated.”

“Marianne!” Elinor cried. “How can you say so? How can you be so unjust? They are a very respectable family, Mr. Ferrars, and towards us have behaved in the friendliest manner. Have you forgot, Marianne, how many pleasant days we have owed to them?”

“No,” said Marianne, in a low voice, “nor how many painful moments.”

Elinor took no notice of that, and, directing her attention to their visitor, endeavoured to support something like conversation by talking of their present residence—its conveniences, its arrangements—drawing from Edward occasional questions and remarks. His coldness and reserve mortified her severely. She was vexed, and half angry; but she resolved to regulate her behaviour by the past rather than the present, and avoided every appearance of resentment. Whatever his manner now, he was still connected to them; and she treated him as she believed he ought to be treated—by duty, by kindness, and by a steadiness of temper that could not, like Marianne’s, be overturned by every gust of feeling.
