When John Jasper regained consciousness, he found himself attended by Mr. and Mrs. Tope—summoned by his visitor to care for him after a sudden fit or fainting spell. The visitor sat rigidly in a chair, hands clasped over his knees, watching Jasper’s slow return to awareness with an expressionless gaze.

“There! You’re come round nicely now, sir,” said Mrs. Tope, dabbing at her eyes. “You were utterly worn out, and no wonder!”

“A man,” declared Mr. Grewgious, speaking as if reciting a lesson from memory, “cannot have his rest disturbed, his mind tormented, and his body exhausted without being thoroughly worn out.”

“I fear I’ve alarmed you,” Jasper murmured weakly as he was helped into his armchair.

“Not at all,” replied Mr. Grewgious. “You are too considerate.”

“Not at all,” he repeated, as though rehearsing the phrase. “You must take some wine, sir,” added Mrs. Tope, bustling about. “And the jelly I had ready for you—though you refused it at noon, even when I warned you what might follow. And you haven’t eaten breakfast, either! There’s a wing of the roast fowl that’s been held back more times than I can count. It’ll be on the table in five minutes—and this good gentleman may stay to see you eat.”

The visitor responded with a sharp, dry snort—one that could mean anything or nothing. Mrs. Tope would have been baffled by it, but her attention was already consumed by the meal.

“You’ll join me?” Jasper asked, as the cloth was laid.

“I couldn’t manage a bite,” said Mr. Grewgious, shaking his head.

Jasper ate and drank with almost desperate urgency. His movements were hurried, his focus detached—he seemed less concerned with taste than with fortifying himself against further collapse. He devoured food and wine not for pleasure, but as armor against despair.

Mr. Grewgious remained motionless, his face blank, his posture stiff—a man whose politeness was so rigid it felt like resistance. As if to say: *I cannot offer a single thought, nor engage in conversation, thank you very much.*

After finishing, Jasper pushed away his plate and glass, then sat silent for a moment, lost in thought.

“Do you know,” he began, “that I find some small comfort in the news you’ve given me?”

*Do you?* Mr. Grewgious returned, plainly implying: *I do not. Not in the least.*

“After the shock of learning what happened to my dear boy—so unexpected, so shattering to every hope I’d built for him—and after time to reflect… yes. I find something to hold onto.”

“I shall be glad to gather your crumbs,” said Mr. Grewgious, dryly.

“Is there not—tell me if I’m mistaken—hope that, finding himself in this new situation, burdened with awkward explanations on all sides, he chose to flee? That he avoided the trouble by disappearing?”

“It’s possible,” said Mr. Grewgious slowly. “It has happened. I’ve read of cases where people, rather than face weeks of scrutiny and idle questions, simply vanished—never to be heard from again.”

“Yes,” said Jasper, warming to the idea. “When I believed my son had kept nothing from me—especially such a matter as this—what light was there in that darkness? When I thought his bride was here, their marriage near, how could I imagine he’d leave suddenly, recklessly, cruelly? But now—now that I know—can’t I see a glimmer? Suppose he left of his own accord. Wouldn’t that make his absence more understandable? Less cruel? The fact that he parted from your ward just before vanishing—that alone is reason enough to go. It doesn’t excuse the pain to me—but it lessens the cruelty to her.”

Mr. Grewgious had no choice but to nod.

“And even for me,” Jasper continued, growing more animated, “he knew you were coming. He knew you carried this truth. If your words have stirred new thoughts in me, then surely he foresaw the same conclusions. If he did—then even the cruelty to me—me, John Jasper, Music Master—fades into insignificance.”

Again, Mr. Grewgious could only assent.

“I’ve had doubts—terrible ones,” Jasper went on. “But your revelation, though it struck me hard, also showed me that my boy had hidden something from me—someone he loved deeply. Yet instead of crushing me, it kindles hope. You don’t deny it. You admit it’s reasonable. I begin to believe it’s possible…” He clasped his hands tightly. “…that he disappeared of his own will—and that he may still be alive.”

At that moment, Mr. Crisparkle entered.

Jasper repeated his words: “I begin to believe it’s possible he left of his own accord—and may yet live.”

Crisparkle took a seat. “Why so?”

Jasper explained his reasoning.

Had the arguments been weaker, Crisparkle might have been more skeptical. But they were compelling, and the Minor Canon had long struggled with guilt over his own silence. He had withheld two critical facts: Neville Landless’s second violent outburst toward Edwin Drood, and the jealousy that had flared within him—jealousy Crisparkle knew was real. He believed Neville innocent, yet feared that revealing these truths might only deepen suspicion. He wrestled with conscience, weighing honesty against harm.

But now, witnessing Jasper’s integrity—his willingness to lay bare even painful truths—Crisparkle resolved to speak.

Addressing Mr. Grewgious, who now stood as a figure of authority due to his revelations, Crisparkle spoke plainly:

“I bear witness to Mr. Jasper’s fairness. I have full confidence that Edwin Drood will one day be cleared of all suspicion. My belief in him was formed despite knowing his temper was fierce—and that it was inflamed by his mistaken love for the same young woman. I confess, I have held back two facts: Neville’s second rage against Edwin, and his jealous passion. But I no longer withhold them. I give them now.”

Jasper’s mood brightened, though briefly. Then came the blow—Crisparkle’s confession. Yet Jasper clung to his hope. “Even so,” he insisted, “I will hold fast to what I’ve gained from Mr. Grewgious. If no trace of my boy is found—if we cannot prove he was murdered—I will never abandon the idea that he fled of his own wild will.”

That night, Mr. Crisparkle walked to Cloisterham Weir—drawn by a troubled mind. He didn’t plan the walk; he didn’t notice the path. Only when he heard the rush of water did he realize where he was.

He stood, listening. A line from scripture surfaced unbidden: *airy tongues that syllable men’s names.* He brushed it aside as if it were physical.

The night was starlit. The Weir was two miles above where the boys had watched the storm. No search had been made here—the tide had flowed downstream, making lower stretches more likely to yield evidence. Yet Crisparkle felt something unusual in the air.

He listened. Nothing. He looked. Nothing. But he decided to return at dawn.

He dreamed of the Weir all night. At sunrise, he returned.

It was a clear, frosty morning. Every detail was visible. He studied the scene for minutes—then his eyes locked on a spot. He turned away, looked at the sky, the earth, then back. The spot caught his gaze again.

It was a tiny gleam—stationary, not moving with the water. He stripped off his coat, plunged into the icy river, and swam to it.

Climbing the timbers, he pulled out a gold watch, caught in the woodwork. Engraved on the back: *E.D.*

He brought it ashore, dove again—searching every crevice, every depth—until cold forced him up. He found only a shirt-pin embedded in mud.

With both items, he returned to Cloisterham. He took Neville Landless with him to the Mayor.

Jasper was summoned. The watch and pin were identified. Neville was detained.

The town erupted. Rumors flew—Neville was vindictive, violent, capable of murder. Before coming to England, he had caused the deaths of “Natives”—nomadic people scattered across continents—whom Cloisterham imagined as always black, always devout, always calling themselves *Me*, and others *Massa* or *Missie*. He had nearly broken Mrs. Crisparkle’s heart. He had threatened Mr. Crisparkle’s life. He had vowed to kill everyone, become the last man.

He had been brought to Cloisterham by a philanthropist who said: *“I owe it to my fellow-creatures that he should be, in the words of Bentham, where he is the cause of the greatest danger to the smallest number.”*

But now, the evidence mounted.

He had threatened Edwin. He had a motive—jealousy, born of his own making. He had armed himself for the night. He had left early, after preparing to depart. Bloodstains were found on his clothes—possibly from injury, possibly from murder. His room revealed destroyed papers, rearranged belongings—all on the afternoon of the disappearance.

The jeweler confirmed: the watch had been wound and set for Edwin Drood at 2:20 PM that day. It had run down before being thrown into the river. The jeweler was certain it had not been rewound.

This suggested the watch was taken shortly after Edwin left Jasper’s house at midnight—with Neville, the last person seen with him—and discarded hours later.

Why discard it?

If Edwin had been murdered and disfigured—so that identification would be impossible—then the murderer would remove the most identifiable items: the watch, the shirt-pin.

And the opportunity? Neville had been seen wandering aimlessly, distraught, throughout the city—perfect cover to dispose of evidence.

The meeting between the two young men? It had been arranged not by Neville, but by Crisparkle. Could Neville have gone reluctantly, in a state of fury?

Each point weakened his defense.

Even the theory that Edwin had fled was undermined. The young lady he’d just left said, with sorrow, that Edwin had planned to wait for Mr. Grewgious’s arrival. And yet—Edwin vanished before Grewgious arrived.

Suspicion grew. Neville was detained. Search parties scoured the land. Jasper worked tirelessly.

But nothing more was found.

No body. No proof of death.

Eventually, without conclusive evidence, Neville was released.

Then came the inevitable: he had to leave.

The town rejected him. Mrs. Crisparkle feared for her son. The Dean intervened.

“Mr. Crisparkle,” he said, “human justice may err, but it must act according to its lights. The days of sanctuary are past. This young man must not take refuge with us.”

“You mean he must leave my house?”

“I claim no authority in your home,” the Dean replied. “I merely speak of the necessity you face—of depriving him of your counsel.”

“It is lamentable,” Crisparkle said.

“Very much so,” the Dean agreed. “And if it is unavoidable—”

“As you find it to be,” the Dean finished.

Crisparkle bowed. “It is hard to prejudge his case, sir, but I am convinced—”

“Just so,” the Dean interrupted smoothly. “We clergy keep our hearts warm and our heads cool. We maintain a judicious middle course.”

“I hope you don’t object,” Crisparkle ventured, “if I publicly declare that he will return whenever new evidence arises?”

“Not at all,” the Dean said. “But I wouldn’t say it emphatically.”

*Emphatically?* he repeated, drawing it out. “I think not. In fact, Mr. Crisparkle, keeping our hearts warm and our heads cool—we need do nothing emphatically.”

So Minor Canon Row saw Neville Landless no more.

Only then did John Jasper quietly return to his place in the choir.

Haggard, eyes bloodshot, his hope extinguished. The sanguine mood was gone. His worst fears had returned.

A few days later, while undressing, he took his diary from his coat pocket. He flipped through the pages, paused—and handed it silently to Mr. Crisparkle.

There, written in bold, firm script, was this entry:

> *My dear boy is murdered. The discovery of the watch and shirt-pin convinces me he was killed that night, and his jewelry removed to prevent identification. All false hopes I built upon his separation from his betrothed are blown away. They perish before this fatal truth. I swear, and record this oath on this page: I will never discuss this mystery with any human being until I hold the clue in my hand. I will never relax in secrecy or in search. I will fix the crime of my dear dead boy’s murder upon the murderer. And I devote myself—to his destruction.*
