The immediate peril of the giant serpent had passed, yet a shadow of dread lingered in my heart. I feared another ambush, unseen lurking within the swamp or emerging from the lands beyond Falconhurst. To ensure our safety, I planned two expeditions: first, a thorough sweep of the marsh and thicket; second, a journey straight to the Gap, the only route through which such a monster could have invaded our domain.

Summoning my sons, I found little enthusiasm for the marsh trek. Jack trembled at the memory of the snake's crushing tail, and Ernest hesitated, but I insisted. We crossed the treacherous bog by laying down planks and wicker hurdles, shifting them ahead as we advanced. Our search yielded only evidence of the beast's presence—crushed reeds and trampled vegetation marking its lair.

Beyond the thicket, the ground grew firm beside a sheer cliff wall. Here, a clear, sparkling brook emerged from a dark opening—a cave of considerable size. Inside, the vaulted ceiling dripped with stalactites forming majestic pillars, while the floor was blanketed in fine, snow-white powder with a smooth, soapy texture.

"Excellent!" I declared. "This is natural fuller's earth, perfect for washing and saving us the labor of boiling soap."

Fritz, eager to explore, slipped through the stream's opening to trace its source. Shouting that the passage widened significantly, he beckoned me to follow. Leaving the younger boys in the outer chamber, I fired a pistol shot; the echoing reverberation confirmed the cavern's immense scale. Lighting my candle, we ventured deeper. The flame burned steady despite the vast darkness surrounding us.

"I believe this is a second cave of salt!" Fritz exclaimed, marveling at how the walls glowed under our light.

"No," I corrected gently. "Salt crystals leave no residue when wet, and this water tastes sweet. I suspect we have found a cavern of rock crystal."

"A treasure!" Fritz cheered.

"If we could utilize it," I noted pragmatically, "it would be invaluable. Otherwise, in our current state, it holds little worth compared to the gold found by old Robinson Crusoe."

Fritz attempted to chip off a specimen but ruined its shape. "Go more carefully," I advised. "You destroyed its natural form, a pyramid with six facets."

After exploring every angle, our light dimmed. Fritz secured a magnificent lump displaying perfect crystals, and we departed, firing a final shot to celebrate the echo.

Outside, we found Jack weeping. Upon seeing us, he rushed into my embrace, terrified we had been crushed by falling rocks. Ernest, meanwhile, was busy weaving a funnel-shaped fish trap. He proudly reported shooting a massive eel, nearly four feet long, which would serve as our supper. I praised his courage, noting the eel's tenacious life force, and taught him the proper method of dispatching such slippery foes.

Returning to the clearing, my wife greeted us with relief, delighted by the supply of fuller's earth and the fresh eel. That evening, we recounted our discoveries, including the crystal grotto.

Preparations for the larger expedition—the search beyond the river to the Gap—began immediately. The entire family agreed to join. We packed the cart with tents, provisions, firearms, tools, and torches, drawing it with our horses, Storm and Grumble. Jack and Franz drove, my wife rode comfortably in the cart, Fritz led the way on horseback, and Ernest and I walked alongside, guarded by our dogs and the tame jackal, Fangs.

As we approached Woodlands, we found numerous tracks of the serpent. At Falconhurst, the livestock welcomed us warmly. Arriving at Woodlands at dusk, we found the farm thriving and free of ape disturbances. The following day, we surveyed the area and gathered cotton for bedding. In the afternoon, accompanied by Fangs and Bruno, Franz took his first solo hunting trip along the lake's edge.

Impatient with the ducks flying too fast, Franz targeted a group of black swans. Suddenly, a deep, booming roar echoed through the forest. Franz mistook it for our young onager, Swift, but I identified it as a bittern, a heron-like bird known for its unique vocalization caused by its specialized windpipe and bill position.

Eager to claim such a rare bird, Franz waited in the reeds. A sudden rustle signaled movement, and he fired instantly.

"I've hit him!" he cried.

"What?" I shouted.

"A wild pig! Bigger than Fritz's!"

I rushed to inspect the creature. Covered in coarse bristles, lacking a tail, and sporting broad, blunt snouts, it resembled a pig but was distinct. Examining its webbed feet and unique dentition, I identified it as a capybara, a water-loving rodent from South America. Overjoyed at discovering a "new creature," Franz proposed skinning it to lighten the load. With Bruno helping carry the carcass, we returned to Woodlands.

There, we discovered Ernest surrounded by dozens of dead rats. He explained that Knips, the parrot, had triggered a nest hidden beneath a clay mound in the rice swamp. When the rats poured out, they swarmed aggressively, prompting Juno the dog to intervene and drive them off. Ernest had managed to collect several skins for their value.

Upon closer inspection of the mound, I realized we had encountered a colony of "beaver rats"—muskrats or musquashes. Their skills in building dams and storing food mirrored those of beavers. I instructed Ernest to harvest their skins quickly for making caps.

Back at the hut, Fritz and Jack returned empty-handed regarding snakes but laden with eggs and heath fowl. Supper featured the capybara, though Franz grimaced at its peculiar flavor due to the musk glands. I explained the nature of musk, detailing its sources—from the musk deer to the civet cat—and described the ingenious Dutch method of harvesting the scent from the trapped animal's pouch.

Later, Jack produced some pale green apples he had found, fearing they were poisonous like the manchineel. After cutting one open to reveal a seed circle rather than a hard stone, Knips eagerly tasted it. Finding it harmless, the boys scrambled to share the fruit, which I suspected was the cinnamon apple of the Antilles.

Exhausted, we retired early. The next morning, we established a temporary camp under a canopy of sailcloth near a former arbor between the sugar-brake and the Gap. Searching the cane fields, we found no sign of the boa. Refreshing ourselves with fresh cane juice, we were startled by barking dogs and rustling foliage. Emerging cautiously, we witnessed a herd of grey, tusked creatures fleeing in single file.

"These are peccaries," I recognized. "Their backs contain a gland that spoils the meat if not removed immediately."

Working swiftly, I and my sons dispatched the wounded ones and extracted the offending glands. While Jack fetched the cart, I cleaned the remaining animals. Ernest reported that the peccary herd had passed near the hut, where he and Juno had secured three additional specimens.

We loaded the cart triumphantly, decorating it with flowers and singing as we transported the bounty to the hut. My wife anxiously awaited our return. After dinner, we began processing the meat: singeing the bristles, salting the hams, and preparing a smokehouse constructed from green wood to cure the bacon.

On the second day, the boys experimented with Otaheitean-style cooking. Fritz stuffed a small peccary with potatoes, onions, herbs, and spices, wrapping it in large leaves before burying it in a heated pit of stones and ash. The result was a spectacular success; the aroma of roasted pork mingled with the exotic spices of the *ravensara* leaves (known locally as "good leaf"), combining notes of nutmeg, clove, and cinnamon. Even my skeptical wife praised the dish.

During the subsequent days spent curing the ham, we explored further, acquiring gigantic bamboo stalks suitable for making casks and pots, using their sharp thorns as nails. However, our visit to Prospect Hill revealed the mischievous apes had caused significant damage again. Disheartened, we abandoned repairs for later and pressed on toward the Gap.

Having cleared a path through the cane thicket, we traversed the open terrain until reaching the extreme limit of our coastal territory. Here, nestled behind a small wood overlooking the mountain gorge and the torrent, we chose our new campsite. After confirming the area was safe from dangerous beasts, we settled in for the night.

The heat was oppressive, forcing us to rest until evening cooled the air. Pitching our tent, we prepared for the ultimate challenge: penetrating the country beyond the defile and crossing the savannah, a journey never before undertaken by us.

At dawn, my brave wife stayed behind with Franz, while the three older boys, the dogs (except Juno), and I marched toward the Gap. Our mission was clear: secure the defense of this critical pass and eliminate the threat of the boa constrictor forever.
