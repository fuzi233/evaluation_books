Ethne stood at the open drawing-room window in Hill Street as though the pane were a listening board set to her cheek. London in June glittered below—green flare of the Park at the street’s end, the pale lacquer of carriage roofs, flower-boxes ruffled like garnishes in the opposite windows. Yet it was not colour she was waiting on. It was sound.

Mrs. Adair sat by the tea-table with the small domestic ceremony arranged to look effortless: cups warmed, spoons aligned, the thin slice of lemon floating like a coin in its saucer. The room held that faint, comforting fragrance of black tea just steeped—tannin and citrus—overlaid by the city’s summer breath coming in through the open sash: dust, horse, crushed leaves, a distant whiff of roasting chestnuts from some street brazier. Both women kept their faces turned, not toward one another, but toward the street’s acoustic weather, as if any particular jingle might be a fate.

At half-past five the sound they wanted rose at last—bells, quick and bright, the clipped trot of a cab-horse. Mrs. Adair’s head lifted as sharply as a kettle when it begins to sing.

“That’s a cab,” she said.

“Yes.” Ethne leaned forward, fingers on the sill. For an instant the jingle swelled, then slid past, fading as the wheels went on without pausing.

“But it’s not stopping here,” Ethne murmured, and she could not keep the small loosening in her breath from showing.

Mrs. Adair’s glance went to the clock and then to Ethne, measuring her. “Colonel Durrance is late.”

Ethne did not answer at once. She listened again, as if the street might correct itself and send back the right sound. Mrs. Adair watched her longer than friendship strictly allowed.

“I wonder why you didn’t go to the station and meet Colonel Durrance?” Mrs. Adair asked at last, slowly, as one turns a knife in a joint to find the seam.

Ethne replied without hesitation, too promptly to be entirely natural. “He might think I came because I looked upon him as helpless. I don’t wish him to think that. He has his servant with him.”

She looked out again. More than once she moved as if speech pressed at her, then swallowed it down like a too-hot mouthful. At last she turned from the window with decision that had the air of being practiced.

“You remember the telegram I showed you?”

“From Lieutenant Calder—saying Colonel Durrance had gone blind?”

“Yes. I want you to promise never to mention it. I don’t want him to know I ever received it.”

Mrs. Adair disliked puzzles as she disliked over-steeped tea—bitter and lingering. She had seen the telegram and had been given only the fact of an engagement, as though it had been long settled, as though nothing in it had been sudden, or merciful, or frightened. She had been permitted her mistaken ease, and now that ease was being barred.

“You will promise?” Ethne insisted.

“Certainly, my dear, if you like,” Mrs. Adair returned, with an ungracious lift of the shoulders. “But there’s a reason, I suppose. I don’t understand why you exact the promise.”

“Two lives must not be spoilt because of me.”

There it was again—that phrase, spoken not as a sentiment but as a rule of the house, like not wasting bread, like skimming fat carefully and saving it for another day. Mrs. Adair, who prided herself on plain speaking, thought she knew what lives Ethne meant. She thought of Ethne’s own youth, her motion, her years ahead; she thought of Durrance’s darkness. She did not think—could not be expected to think—of the third life threading through Ethne’s resolve like an unspoken dialect under a dominant tongue.

“Are you quite sure, Ethne,” Mrs. Adair said, “that the two lives will not be more surely spoilt by this way of yours—the way of marriage? Don’t you think you’ll come to feel Colonel Durrance, in spite of your will, something of a hindrance and a drag? Isn’t it possible he may come to feel that too? I wonder. I very much wonder.”

“No,” Ethne said, decisively. “I shall not feel it, and he must not.”

She let Mrs. Adair keep her misunderstanding. It was easier, perhaps kinder, to allow the older woman her simpler vocabulary for what was happening. Ethne went back to the window and to the street’s music, seeing ahead not pictures but snares: moments when a careless word might betray what she must not betray; pauses in conversation where silence might speak too loudly; the small involuntary turns of the head that disclose hope.

It was just a year since, in this very room, in such sunlight, she had asked Durrance to forget her. The year had been threaded with letters that proved forgetting was not in him. Even the last—three weeks ago from Wadi Halfa—scrawled in a child’s wavering hand because he could no longer place his own words on a line, was proof more certain than all the hopeful pages before it. In that letter he had told her of his blindness and had renounced her; and the renunciation itself was a kind of holding fast.

While she waited now, she understood with a steadiness that made her throat ache that it was she who must do the forgetting, or the imitation of it. If she could not erase, she must at least keep her face from translating it.

No—two lives should not be spoilt because of her.

Durrance by birth had inherited movement: the long roads, the far horizons, the camp-fire’s single red eye in a sea of black silence, sleep under stars with the desert wind cooling the skin, the clean work of government and command. Blindness had taken those from him. Everything that made his days a pride and a pleasure had been struck away. One thing, and only one, remained—herself, and only so long as he could believe she wanted him.

She had set her mind on that belief as one sets a lid tightly on a pot to keep heat from escaping. She must not let it lift.

The cab for which she waited came at last, so quietly into the stream of sound that she missed it. Only when Durrance’s servant rang did the bell at the street-door speak up into the room.

“He has come!” Ethne said, starting as though the servant had touched her shoulder.

She told herself there was no reason to fear. Durrance had never been inquisitive; he had always taken friends as they were offered, without holding them to a bright light. And now—now, surely, it would be easier than ever to hide what must be hidden. Yet despite her reasoning she found herself bracing, not welcoming.

Mrs. Adair slipped from the room, leaving Ethne alone.

Ethne stayed by the window, choosing to meet the change in him without flinching. She expected shock—the obvious helplessness, the groping, the broken confidence. But when Durrance entered, the changes she had rehearsed were notable chiefly by their absence.

His face was worn, and grey had come into his hair, but there was no air of a man stranded. He walked as if the room were mapped in his bones. Memory served him like sight: he moved between chair and table with a soldier’s certainty. Only once or twice did he put out a hand where he expected the back of a chair, touching it with the lightness of habit rather than need.

Ethne drew back into the window recess, suddenly at a loss for ordinary words.

He smiled—at once, without searching—and came straight toward her.

“Ethne,” he said.

“It isn’t true, then,” she cried, startled into the wrong sentence by the ease of his movement. “You have recovered.”

“It is quite true,” he answered, “and I have not recovered. But you moved at the window, and so I knew you were there.”

“How did you know? I made no noise.”

“No. But the window’s open. The noise in the street became suddenly louder. I knew someone in front of it had moved aside. I guessed it was you.”

It was not the greeting of lovers newly pledged, yet it spared them the clumsy pity that neither wanted. They shook hands—no more—and in the simple contact Ethne felt the steadiness of him, the controlled pressure, the old discipline that had not dissolved.

“I will give you some tea,” she said. “Then we can talk.”

“Yes,” Durrance replied gravely. “We must have a talk, mustn’t we?”

But the gravity did not last. He spoke with good humour of his journey back—of stations, porters, the odd indignities of being guided through familiar places. He even laughed at his first days of blindness and the mistakes he had made, as though they were a story told about another man. Ethne found herself breathing more freely. The tea’s fragrance thickened as it stood, rising like reassurance.

Then, as if a current had shifted under the surface, something in his posture brought her apprehension back. He sat a little forward, as a man does when he listens for a thread of sound.

“I wrote to you from Wadi Halfa,” he said. “I don’t know whether you could read the letter.”

“Quite well,” Ethne answered.

“I got a friend of mine to hold the paper and tell me when I was writing on it or merely on the blotting-pad,” he continued, with a short laugh. “Calder—of the Sappers—but you don’t know him.”

He spoke the name quickly, as if it were tossed casually onto the table. Yet Ethne felt it like a dropped knife. Lieutenant Calder. The telegram. Her stomach tightened. In the stillness of Durrance’s face there seemed to be an alertness, a waiting—an ear turned to catch the smallest betrayed knowledge.

Was this a trap? Did he suspect? Had he guessed her letter was pity answering pity? Ethne looked into his face and found no answer there, only that strange concentrated stillness. A year ago she would have known by the flicker of his eyes; now his blindness made him unreadable, as though the familiar language between them had died and she must speak in careful translation.

“I could read the letter without difficulty,” she said gently. “It was the letter you would have written. But I had written to you before, and of course your bad news could make no difference. I take back no word of what I wrote.”

Durrance leaned forward, hands on his knees. Ethne waited, unsure whether she had satisfied him or sharpened him. She felt how different this was now: the blind man did not look at her, but he listened with an accuracy that made looking seem a blunt instrument.

“Yes,” he said at length. “I know you. You would take nothing back. But there is my point of view.”

“Yes?” Ethne replied, striving for unconcern. “Will you tell me it?”

He nodded and began as a man who has arranged his thoughts in order, as one might set ingredients out before beginning a difficult dish: measured, spare, lucid.

“I know what blindness means to all men—a growing, narrowing egotism unless one is perpetually on one’s guard. And will one be perpetually on one’s guard? Blindness means that to all men,” he repeated, as though stating a fact of nature.

“But it must mean more to me, who am deprived of every occupation. If I were a writer, I could still dictate. If I were a business man, I could conduct my business. But I am a soldier—and not a clever soldier. Jealousy, a continual and irritable curiosity—there is no Paul Pry like your blind man—a querulous claim upon your attention—these are my special dangers.”

Ethne gave a soft laugh, as if to push the list away from them both.

“Well, perhaps one may hold them off,” he conceded. “But they are to be considered. I have considered them. I am not speaking to you without thought. I have pondered and puzzled over the whole matter night after night since I got your letter—wondering what I should do. You know how gladly, with what gratitude, I would have answered you, ‘Yes, let the marriage go on,’ if I dared. If I dared!”

He paused, then went on with an odd calm that made each word heavier.

“I think—don’t you?—that a great trouble rather clears one’s wits. I used to lie awake at Cairo and think; and the unimportant trivial considerations gradually dropped away; and a few straight and simple truths stood out rather vividly. One felt that one had to cling to them with all one’s might, because nothing else was left.”

“Yes,” Ethne said in a low voice. “That I do understand.”

She did. She had known that winnowing of thought, the stripping down to essentials until only a few unyielding facts remain—facts you cannot soften with prettier words. For the first time, looking at him—or rather, being near him and hearing him—she understood they had entered the same country of mind, though by different roads.

Two lives shall not be spoilt because of me.

“Well?” she asked.

“Well—here’s one of the straight and simple truths. Marriage between a man crippled like myself, whose life is done, and a woman like you, active and young, whose life is in its flower, would be quite wrong unless each brought to it much more than friendship. It would be quite wrong if it implied a sacrifice for you.”

“It implies no sacrifice,” Ethne answered firmly.

He nodded, and she felt—almost with physical certainty—that he was listening to her tone more than her words, weighing it as one weighs a coin by its ring. His concentration made her suddenly aware that what she had imagined as easier might be harder: blindness had sharpened him. The city’s soundscape, which most people endured as a blur, had become for him a script.

“Then do you bring more than friendship?” he asked abruptly. “You will be very honest, I know. Tell me.”

Ethne’s heart knocked once, hard. She could not evade; she must answer at once, clearly, honestly. Yet honesty itself had more than one dialect, and some truths did not translate without harm.

“There is nothing,” she said, steadying her voice, “nothing in the world which I wish for so earnestly as that you and I should marry.”

It was true as she meant it. She knew nothing of plans being shaped elsewhere, nothing of men in grill-rooms speaking of honour and the desert, nothing of letters pulled from mud and ruin on the Nile bank. So far as she knew—and so far as she had resolved—Harry Feversham had gone entirely out of her life. The wish was honest. But it was not the exact answer to his question, and she could only hope again that her tone would stand in for what she could not say.

Durrance seemed content. He did not press.

“Thank you, Ethne,” he said, and took her hand and shook it. His face smiled, as if he had heard what he needed.

No more questions came. Ethne told herself his suspicion, if it had stirred, was quieted—for today.

Mrs. Adair returned then with the discretion of one who times an entrance as carefully as she times the steeping of tea. She greeted Durrance as though his blindness were an irrelevance, as if she had seen him only last week, and her voice had the practised ease of a hostess keeping the room’s harmony in tune.

“I suppose Ethne has told you of our plan,” she said, taking her tea from Ethne’s hand.

“No, not yet,” Ethne replied.

“What plan?” Durrance asked.

“It is all arranged,” Mrs. Adair said. “You will want to go home to Guessens in Devonshire. I am your neighbour—a couple of fields separate us, that’s all. So Ethne will stay with me during the interval before you are married.”

“That’s very kind of you, Mrs. Adair,” Durrance exclaimed. “Because, of course, there will be an interval.”

“A short one, no doubt,” Mrs. Adair said.

“Well—it’s this way,” Durrance replied. “If there’s a chance that I may recover my sight, it would be better that I should seize it at once. Time means a good deal in these cases.”

“Then there is a chance?” Ethne cried, and could not keep the sudden lift in her voice.

“I am going to see a specialist here tomorrow,” he answered. “And, of course, there’s the oculist at Wiesbaden. But it may not be necessary to go so far. I expect I shall be able to stay at Guessens and come up to London when it is necessary.” He turned slightly toward Mrs. Adair. “Thank you very much. It is a good plan.”

He added more slowly, as though tasting the words for accuracy, “From my point of view there could be no better.”

Later Ethne stood again at the open window and watched—or listened as much as watched—Durrance’s cab take him away with his servant toward St. James’s Street, toward the rooms that had belonged to his old life. The city below had changed only in its rhythm: now the carriages were returning from the Park, wheels and hoofs sewing a steady seam of sound along the road.

Ethne remained where she was, in much the same attitude as before he came, but with a different weight in her thoughts. Her fear had not vanished; it had merely taken a clearer shape.

She believed she had quieted whatever suspicion he carried—today, at least. She did not know what had been said between Durrance and Calder in Cairo. She did not know that Durrance believed there was no cure, that the talk of specialists might be only a pretext, a convenient language for other motives. Yet uneasiness held her as firmly as her vow.

Durrance had grown more acute. Not only had his senses sharpened—as any blind man’s might, tuned to shifts of air and the smallest changes in sound—but trouble and thought had sharpened his mind too, making it more penetrating. He could no longer read faces, yet he seemed to read intention in the tiniest tremor of a voice, in the way street-noise swelled when a body moved aside from an open window.

She felt she was entering upon an encounter of wits, and feared she would be worsted.

“Two lives shall not be spoilt because of me,” she repeated, but now it was less a resolve than a prayer—because one truth, at least, had become plain: Durrance saw ever so much more clearly now that he was blind.
