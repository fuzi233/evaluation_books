On a bright summer afternoon, Caroline sat alone in the garden alcove, her uncle away at Whinbury. The hours stretched long and silent—sunlit, breezeless, cloudless—as if time itself had paused in an endless, shadowless desert. Yet this was no barren waste, but the blooming garden of an English home, and still, to Caroline, it felt as desolate as any wasteland. Her fingers moved steadily over her needlework, stitching a child’s slip for the Jew’s basket, while her mind wandered restlessly, burdened by solitude and quiet grief.

Suddenly, Fanny appeared at the door, scanning the lawn and flowerbeds before calling out, “Miss Caroline!”

A soft voice replied, “Fanny!”—coming from within the alcove. Fanny hurried forward, holding a note, which she handed with careful hands. Caroline took it without question, without looking—letting it fall among the folds of her work.

“Harry Scott brought it,” Fanny said.

The name stirred something deep within her. She lifted her head, eyes alight with sudden life, and turned toward Fanny. “Harry Scott? Who sent him?”

“He came from the Hollow.”

Caroline snatched up the letter, tore open the seal, and read it in seconds. It was a warm message from Hortense, telling her that she had returned from Wormwood Wells, that Robert was away at market, and that she would be delighted to have Caroline join her for tea. With gentle insistence, she added that such a change would do Caroline good—especially now, when she had been deprived of both companionship and guidance since the rift between Robert and Mr. Helstone had severed her bond with her dear friend, Hortense Gérard Moore. A postscript urged her: *Put on your bonnet and come down at once.*

She needed no urging. Joy surged through her like a spring tide. She set aside her sewing, climbed the stairs, tied her straw bonnet over her curls, and wrapped the black silk scarf around her shoulders—its simple elegance framing her pale face and delicate dress. She longed to escape the silence, the sorrow, the weight of days that seemed to press upon her like a dream gone wrong. To walk down the green lane toward the Hollow, breathing in the sweet scent of wildflowers, was already a relief. True, Robert wasn’t there—but merely being near his cottage, stepping into the room where he had sat that morning, felt like a reunion. And in that moment, illusion whispered again, veiled in soft wings: *Robert may return while you’re here. You might see him. You might touch his hand. You might sit beside him, just for a moment.*

“Silence,” she murmured, though her heart beat faster.

From the window, Miss Moore likely saw the flash of white against the greenery and stepped out to greet her. As always, she moved with calm dignity—no haste, no flourish. But she smiled warmly, pleased to see Caroline’s joy, happy to receive her kiss and embrace. She led her inside, half deceived, wholly flattered. Half deceived? Perhaps. Had she truly known what stirred beneath Caroline’s happiness, she might have barred the gate and turned her away. Sisters rarely welcome young women falling in love with their brothers. It seems foolish, romantic, even presumptuous. They don’t love those men themselves—and so they resent others who do. The first reaction is often a mix of irritation and disdain. Only reason, if it prevails, can soften such feelings. But if not, the woman becomes an object of lifelong disfavor.

“You expected me alone,” Miss Moore said, guiding Caroline toward the parlour, “but I wrote this morning. Since dinner, we’ve had company.”

She opened the door to reveal a sea of crimson skirts spilling over an armchair by the fire, crowned by a cap more imposing than any crown. The cap had not arrived under a bonnet—it had been carried in a vast black silk bag, shaped like a balloon, stiffened with whalebone. Its frill stood wide around the wearer’s face, and ribbons—love-ribbon, thick and puffed—danced across her head. Mrs. Yorke wore it proudly. She wore the gown too—fitting her perfectly.

The great lady had come uninvited, to take tea with Miss Moore—an honor almost as rare as if the queen herself had dropped in on a humble household. Mrs. Yorke rarely visited; she scorned social calls, dismissed neighbors as “gossips.” Yet today, she had chosen to grace the cottage with her presence—a mark of favor few could claim.

Caroline approached with some hesitation. She knew little of Mrs. Yorke, and as a parson’s niece, she wondered how she’d be received. The reception was cool. She welcomed the chance to remove her bonnet and hide her discomfort. Then, a small figure in blue and sash leapt from behind the chair, arms flying toward her.

“My mother isn’t kind to you,” the girl declared, throwing her arms around Caroline’s neck and demanding a kiss.

“She doesn’t notice you either,” Caroline said gently, returning the embrace.

“Mother is frozen,” the girl said. “Rose never looks up. If a white angel with a star-crown walked in, mother would nod stiffly, and Rose wouldn’t lift her head. But I’ll be your friend—I’ve always liked you.”

“Jessie, restrain yourself!” Mrs. Yorke commanded.

“But mother, why are you so cold?” Jessie pleaded. “Miss Helstone hasn’t done anything wrong. Why can’t you be kind? You sit stiff, speak dry, look icy. That’s how you treat Miss Shirley Keeldar and every other guest. And Rose—she’s an automaton! A machine made in human shape. Between you two, you’ll drive everyone away. Martin says so.”

“I am an automaton?” Rose said, rising from the floor where she sat reading, a book open on her lap. “Then let me be left alone.” She glanced briefly at Caroline. “Miss Helstone, how do you do?”

Caroline studied her quietly—this young girl absorbed in her book, her lips moving slightly as she read, full of character. She had instinct, tact. She sensed Rose was different—unique. Approaching softly, she knelt beside her and peered over her shoulder. The book was *The Italian* by Mrs. Radcliffe.

They read together in silence. Soon, Rose asked, “Are you ready?” before turning the page.

Caroline nodded.

“Do you like it?” Rose asked later.

“I did when I was younger,” Caroline replied. “It promised such mystery—such a strange tale.”

“And when you read it, you feel far from England—really in Italy, under a southern sky, blue and burning.”

“You sense that, Rose?”

“It makes me want to travel,” Rose said. “When I’m a woman, I’ll go. I can’t stay in Briarfield forever. The world is small compared to creation. I must see beyond our round planet.”

“How much of it?”

“All of it. First this hemisphere, then the other. I mean to make my way. Not live like a toad buried in marble—nor like you, shut in that rectory, dying slowly.”

“Like me? What do you mean?”

“Wouldn’t it be better to wander than to stay locked in that house—like a windowed grave? I never see movement there. No sound. No smoke from the chimneys. What do you do?”

“I sew, read, learn.”

“Are you happy?”

“Wouldn’t I be happier wandering, as you wish?”

“Much happier—even if you did nothing but wander. But I’ll have a purpose. If you just drift like a fairy tale’s enchanted lady, you might still be happier than you are now. In one day’s walking, you’d pass hills, woods, streams—each changing with the sun, the weather, the light. Nothing changes in Briarfield rectory. The ceilings, walls, curtains, carpets—they’re all the same.”

“Is change necessary for happiness?”

“Yes.”

“Is it the same as happiness?”

“I don’t know. But monotony feels like death.”

Jessie interrupted. “Isn’t she mad?”

But Rose continued. “Better to try everything and find it empty than to try nothing and leave life blank. That’s the sin of the man who buried his talent in a napkin—a despicable sluggard!”

Mrs. Yorke spoke. “Solid satisfaction comes only from duty.”

“Right, mother,” Rose said. “If my Master gave me ten talents, my duty is to trade them and make ten more. Not bury them in a drawer. Not hide them in a broken teapot. Not smother them in woolen socks. Not lock them in a linen chest. Least of all”—she rose—“will I hide them in a tureen of cold potatoes, stacked with bread and butter on the larder shelf.”

She paused. “Mother, the Lord who gave us our talents will return one day and demand an account. The teapot, the old sock, the rag, the tureen—they’ll give up their barren coins in many homes. Let your daughters at least put their money to the exchangers, so they can repay Him with interest.”

“Did you bring your sampler?” Mrs. Yorke asked.

“Yes, mother.”

“Sit and mark a line.”

Rose obeyed. After ten minutes, her mother asked, “Do you feel oppressed now?”

“No, mother.”

“Yet you railed against domestic work.”

“I didn’t. I’d be sorry not to learn to sew. You’re right to teach me.”

“Even mending stockings and making sheets?”

“Yes.”

“So why rant about it?”

“Am I to do nothing else? I’ll do that—and more. Now, mother, I’ve spoken. I’m twelve. For four years, I’ll be your industrious apprentice. I won’t speak again on this until I’m sixteen.”

Mrs. Yorke turned to Caroline. “You see my daughters, Miss Helstone. Precociously wise in their own minds. Jessie sings ‘I’d rather this, I prefer that.’ Rose cries, ‘I *will*, and I *will not*!’”

“I give reasons, mother,” Rose said. “And my cry is bold, but only once a year. Each birthday, the spirit moves me to deliver one oracle. I say it and leave it. It’s for you to listen—or not.”

Mrs. Yorke addressed Caroline again. “I advise young ladies to study such children before marriage. Think how you’d handle the careless, the stubborn, the hard-to-train.”

“But with love, it needn’t be so hard,” Caroline said. “Mothers love their children deeply—almost more than themselves.”

“Sentimental talk,” Mrs. Yorke replied. “There’s the rough part of life yet to come.”

“But if I hold a baby—any poor woman’s infant—I feel I love it deeply, even if I’m not its mother. I’d do anything for it if it were mine.”

“You *feel*,” Mrs. Yorke said sharply. “Yes, yes. You’re ruled by emotion. You think yourself sensitive. But do you know you’ve trained your face into a lackadaisical expression—more suited to a novel heroine than a woman who must navigate the real world with common sense?”

“No,” Caroline said. “I don’t.”

“Look in the mirror. Compare your face with a milkmaid’s.”

“My face is pale, but not sentimental. Most milkmaids are less practical than I am. I think more clearly. Where they act weakly from lack of thought, I act wisely.”

“Oh, you’d be guided by impulse,” Mrs. Yorke said. “By feeling.”

“Of course I would. Feelings were given to me for a reason. If my feelings tell me to love someone, I *must* love them. I hope, if I ever have a husband or children, my feelings will compel me to love them. I hope my impulses will lead me to care.”

She said it with quiet strength. She enjoyed daring to speak so plainly before Mrs. Yorke. She didn’t care for sarcasm. She flushed—not with anger, but with passion.

Mrs. Yorke replied coolly, “Don’t waste your drama. That was fine. But it means nothing to two old women—an old wife and an old maid. There should have been a gentleman present. Is Mr. Robert hiding behind the curtains, Miss Moore?”

Hortense, who had been in the kitchen, looked puzzled. “Robert is at Whinbury.”

Mrs. Yorke laughed her short, sharp laugh. “Straightforward Miss Moore. You answer literally. Your mind knows nothing of intrigue. Strange things could happen around you, and you’d never notice. You’re not sharp-witted.”

Hortense stiffened. “I’ve always been noted for wisdom and discernment.”

“You never plotted to win a husband, I’ll wager. You haven’t the experience to spot when others do.”

Caroline felt the barb. She couldn’t defend herself—because to reply would be to admit the truth. Mrs. Yorke watched her—eyes downcast, cheeks burning, body trembling with shame. She relished the moment. She disliked sensitive people, nervous temperaments, delicate faces. Rarely did she encounter all three in one person—and even rarer did she find such a person within her grasp. Today, she was especially irritable—like a fierce cow ready to gore.

She lowered her head and struck again. “Your cousin Hortense is a good sister. But some ladies come here hoping to charm the mistress and take control. You enjoy her company, I suppose?”

“Which cousin?”

“The lady’s, of course.”

“Hortense has always been kind to me.”

“Every sister with a single brother is considered kind by spinster friends.”

“Mrs. Yorke,” Caroline said, lifting her eyes—clear, steady, calm—her blush fading into pale resolve—“may I ask what you mean?”

“To teach you about honesty. To disgust you with deceit and false sentiment.”

“Do I need such a lesson?”

“Most young ladies do. You’re modern—delicate, morbid, fond of solitude. That implies you find little worth in ordinary life. But ordinary people—honest folk—are better than you think. Better than any bookish, romantic girl who never ventures beyond her uncle’s garden wall.”

“Consequently, you know nothing of me. Excuse me—it doesn’t matter. You’ve attacked me without cause. I’ll defend myself without apology. You know nothing of my relationship with my cousins. In a fit of ill-humor, you’ve poisoned them with insinuations more cruel and false than anything you could accuse me of. My pallor, my shyness—none of it concerns you. My love of books, my dislike of gossip—that’s none of your business. Calling me a ‘romancing chit’ is mere guesswork. I’ve never romanced before you or anyone you know. Being the parson’s niece is not a crime, though you may think so. You dislike me. You have no reason. Keep your dislike to yourself. If you ever show it again, I’ll answer even less kindly.”

She stopped. She sat still, pale, composed. Her voice had been clear, calm, unshaken. Yet beneath it, her blood raced like a hidden stream.

Mrs. Yorke was not angry. She turned to Hortense, nodding. “She has spirit, after all. Always speak honestly, as you’ve done.”

“I reject such advice,” Caroline said, her voice steady. “Counsel tainted with insult I refuse. I have the right to speak as I choose. I won’t speak harshly unless provoked.”

“Mother, you’ve found your match,” Jessie said, impressed.

Rose remained silent. Then she said, “No. Miss Helstone isn’t your match. She lets herself be troubled. You’d wear her out in weeks. Shirley Keeldar handles you better. Mother, you’ve never hurt Miss Keeldar’s feelings. She wears armor under her silk dress—you can’t pierce it.”
