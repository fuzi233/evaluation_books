When Arthur Pendennis, dressed in his dressing gown, ascended the stairs to Warrington's chambers the following morning, his customary mission was twofold: to report the outcome of his nocturnal audience with his uncle and to solicit George's counsel. However, upon entering the beloved rooms, he discovered only Mrs. Flanagan, the laundress. George was absent. A carpet bag stood ready, and his destination was his brother's estate in Suffolk. Scattered across the table were packages addressed to the newspaper and review for which he labored, waiting for collection.

"I caught him right at the table when I arrived!" Mrs. Flanagan exclaimed, her voice brimming with concern. "He was deep in his papers, one candle already burnt out. Even though his bed is notoriously uncomfortable, he hadn't touched it all night, sir."

Indeed, having endured the Club until the rowdiness became unbearable, George had returned home. He had spent the entire night wrestling with a piece of work he felt compelled to finish, bending his energies to the task with singular intensity. The labor was done; the night had been consumed; and the tardy dawn of November peered into the room to find the young man slumped over his desk.

In the next day's paper, or perhaps in a quarterly review, many readers would admire the brilliance of his genius, the variety of his illustrations, and the fierce vigor of his satire. Yet, hidden within those words was a different current: a tone more melancholy than usual, a bitterness and impatience sharper than anything he would later display. Only a handful who truly knew his style or his name would detect the shadow haunting this specific period of his life. One wonders, could we fully grasp the man's inner turmoil as easily as the author's thoughts? How vastly more interesting—and far less cheerful—books would be if they revealed the private struggles behind the public persona! Consider the harlequin: beneath the painted mask, the face is often grave, if not sorrowful. Every writer who reads this must recall their own solitary hours, the constant cares that sat beside the desk like silent guardians. Perhaps a fever raged in the next room; perhaps a sick child lay weeping while a wife prayed in terror; perhaps grief weighed him down so heavily that the cruel mist obscured the page, yet the inexorable necessity of the deadline drove the pen forward. Who among us has not known such nights? But to the manly heart, however severe the pang, the wound heals, the fever breaks, and the dawn arrives. One eventually learns to look back on that misery without bitterness.

On the table lay the relics of the struggle: two or three reference books, fragments of torn manuscripts, open drawers, pens, an inkstand, faint lines on blotting paper, and a piece of sealing wax twisted and bitten into shards. Arthur threw himself into George's empty chair, noting things as per his habit, or perhaps against his will. He noticed a gap in the bookcase, nestled beside the old *College Plato* and the volume bearing the Boniface Arms. This was where Helen's Bible usually resided.

"He has taken that with him," Arthur mused. He understood why his friend had departed.

"Dear, dear old George!" Arthur rubbed his hand over his eyes. "Oh, how much wiser, how much better, how much nobler he is than I!" he thought. "Where is such a friend? Where is such a brave heart? Where shall I ever hear such a frank voice, such kind laughter? Where shall I ever see such a true gentleman? No wonder she loved him. God bless him! Compared to him, what am I? What choice did she have but to love him? To the end of our days, we shall remain her brothers, for fate decrees we can be nothing more. We'll be her knights, serving her faithfully. And when we are old, we shall recount how we loved her. Dear, dear old George!"

Descending to his own chambers, Arthur glanced at the letterbox of his outer door, which he had previously missed. Inside was a small note addressed to *A. P., Esq.*, penned in George's distinctive hand. George had slipped it in as he left.

*"Dear Pen,—I shall be halfway home by the time you breakfast, and intend to stay over Christmas, either in Norfolk or elsewhere.*

*I have formed my own opinion regarding the matter we discussed in J——— St. yesterday; and believe my presence is quite superfluous (_de trop_).*

Thus, George was gone, leaving Mrs. Flanagan to rule over his vacant quarters. Naturally, Arthur had to visit his uncle the day after their conversation. Being denied entry, he proceeded to Lady Rockminster's apartments. Upon his arrival, the old lady immediately demanded to see "Bluebeard"—Arthur's nickname—and insisted he join them for dinner.

"Bluebeard is gone," Arthur replied, producing George's scrap of paper and handing it to Laura. She examined it briefly, refused to meet Arthur's gaze, and passed the paper back to him before walking away.

Arthur then launched into an eloquent eulogy of his dear old George for Lady Rockminster, who was astounded by his fervor. She had rarely heard him speak so warmly of anyone and frankly remarked that it was not in his nature to care so deeply for another soul.

Later, strolling through Waterloo Place on his daily walk toward the hotel where Laura resided, Arthur spotted an old schoolfriend emerging from Messrs. Gimcrack's celebrated shop. An obsequious shopman followed, laden with parcels. The gentleman was clad in the deepest mourning; the brougham, driver, and horses matched his somber attire. Grief, cushioned by wealth and comfort, was perfectly embodied in the equipage and its owner.

"What, Foker! Hail, Foker!" cried Arthur. The reader, surely, recognizes Arthur's old schoolmate, the heir to the late lamented John Henry Foker, Esq., master of Logwood and other estates, and principal partner in the great brewery of Foker and Co.—the greater portion of Foker's entire holdings. A small hand, gloved in deepest ebony and framed by a snowy wristband, extended to greet Arthur, clutching a small morocco case undoubtedly filled with treasures purchased moments prior.

Arthur's keen, satirical eyes instantly discerned the errand. He recalled the heir in Horace pouring forth the gathered wine of his father's vats, realizing that human nature remains remarkably consistent whether in Regent Street or the Via Sacra.

"*Le Roi est mort. Vive le Roi!*" Arthur declared.

"Oh!" came the response. "Yes. Thank you—very much obliged. How do you do, Pen? Very busy—good-bye!"

Foker jumped into the black brougham and sat like a little black Care behind the black coachman. He had flushed upon seeing Arthur, displaying signs of guilt and perturbation that Arthur attributed to the novelty of his inheritance, speculating sardonically on the scene.

"Yes: so wags the world," Arthur thought. "The stone closes over Harry the Fourth, and Harry the Fifth reigns in his stead. The old ministers at the brewery kneel before him with their accounts; the draymen, his subjects, throw up their red caps and shout for him. What profound deference and sympathy the bankers and lawyers display! There was too great a stake involved for them to ever love each other cordially. As long as one man excludes another from twenty thousand a year, the younger must forever hunger for the crown, and desire becomes the father of possession. Thank Heaven, there was no such thought of money between me and our dear mother, Laura."

"There never could have been," Laura interjected. "You would have spurned it! Why make yourself more selfish than you are, Pen? Allow your mind to entertain such... such dreadful meanness? You make me blush for you, Arthur; you make me—" Her eyes completed the sentence, and she dabbed them with a handkerchief.

"There are some truths women will never acknowledge," Arthur said gently, "from which your modesty always turns away. I do not claim to have felt the emotion myself, merely that I am glad I lacked the temptation. Is there harm in that confession?"

"We are all taught to pray for deliverance from evil, Arthur," Laura whispered. "I am glad you were spared that great crime; I am only sorry to think you might have been led astray. But you never could; nor do you believe you could. Your acts are generous and kind; you disdain base actions. You took Blanche without money, without a bribe. Yes, thank Heaven, dear brother. You could not have sold yourself away; I knew you couldn't when the day came, and you didn't. Praise be—where praise is due. Why does this horrid skepticism pursue you, my Arthur? Why doubt and sneer at your own heart, and everyone else's? Oh, if you knew the pain you cause me! How I lie awake thinking of those harsh sentences, wishing them unspoken, unthought!"

"Do I cause you many thoughts and many tears, Laura?" Arthur asked.

The fullness of innocent love shone in her reply. A smile of heavenly purity, a glance of unutterable tenderness, sympathy, and pity illuminated her face. Arthur beheld these signs of love and virtue, worshipping them as one watches a child or imagines an angel.
