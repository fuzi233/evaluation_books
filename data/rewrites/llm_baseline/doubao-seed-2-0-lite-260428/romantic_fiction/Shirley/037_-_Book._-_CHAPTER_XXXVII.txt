And so, dear reader, it is time at last to settle our accounts: to lay out the final fates of all the beloved faces we have walked with through rain and riot across the moors of Briarfield, shake hands gently, and part ways, for now, with hearts lightened by happy endings.

Let us turn first to the curates—our much-loved, long-neglected band of clergymen, and call forward first our modest merit. Peter Augustus Malone, of course, answers the summons instantly, for he never could resist the chance of the spotlight. But no, Peter, we must wave you gently off. The public has its quiet fancies, and your unvarnished, rough-hewn truth would send them fleeing into hysterics, crying that you are too real, too unpolished, too unfit for their pretty fictions. The honest, messy urchin of reality always gets the scolds, while the neat, petted fancy gets all the sugar plums. So stand aside, rude, unwashed, boisterous Peter—you shall have your quiet exit from Briarfield, and no one need pry further into the details.

Make way now for Mr. Sweeting. He comes trotting forward with his lady on his arm: the magnificent, warm-hearted Dora Sykes, soon to be Mrs. Sweeting, the most beloved and jovial woman in all of Yorkshire. They married under the brightest of omens: Sweeting had just been gifted a comfortable, well-endowed living, and Mr. Sykes had settled a handsome fortune on his daughter. They lived long, happy, and adored by every parishioner and friend who ever crossed their threshold.

There—that little tale is polished to a warm, glowing sheen, as it deserves to be.

Advance next, Mr. Donne. This gentleman turned out far better than either of us could ever have guessed, reader. He married a quiet, sensible, genteel little wife who softened every sharp edge of his nature, and made him a model domestic man. He threw all his considerable energy into tending the outward beauty of his parish church, parsonage, and village school, polishing every surface as carefully as an upholsterer tends his finest furniture. His one unmatched gift was for begging: he badgered everyone from barefoot cottage children to the Prince Regent himself for funds, and somehow coaxed pounds and guineas even from the famously tight-fisted Queen Charlotte, all to build the neat, pretty parish spaces that served his community for decades. He was odd, yes, but useful, and beloved in his own way.

After Peter Malone vanished suddenly from Briarfield, the new Irish curate who took his place was Mr. Macarthey—an honorable, conscientious man who redeemed his country's reputation after Malone's wild antics. He labored faithfully over the parish schools, day and Sunday, making them thrive like well-watered bay trees. His only small, dear quirks were very clerical ones: an invitation to tea with a Dissenter would fluster him for days, and the sight of a Quaker keeping his hat on in church would throw his whole equilibrium off for hours. Otherwise, he was diligent, kind, and deeply respected by his flock.

I am sure you have all been waiting for the would-be assassin of Robert Moore to be brought to justice, but the facts spared us that long, grim tale of courts and dungeons. The man was never caught—no one even bothered to pursue him, once Robert himself refused to press the matter. He knew full well who had fired that shot: half-mad Michael Hartley, the raving weaver who was a fanatic in both religion and politics. Hartley died of delirium tremens a year after the attack, and the kind-hearted Robert gave his grieving widow a guinea to give him a decent burial. The matter was laid to rest, quietly and mercifully.

The long, harsh winter of the Luddite riots has melted away. Soft spring has given way to the heart of summer: mid-June, 1812. The air glows deep azure and burnished red gold, fit for the fevered, giant adolescence of the 19th century, when Napoleon marched six hundred thousand men across the Russian wastes, and Wellington won battle after battle across Spain, storming Badajos and taking Salamanca. But for the mill towns of Yorkshire and Lancashire, the greatest triumph of all fell on the 18th of June that year: the Orders in Council were repealed, the blockaded ports thrown wide open. The shout of joy from every merchant and mill worker shook the ground for miles around. The ringers at Briarfield church pulled so hard they cracked one of the bells, and it still rings a little dissonant to this day. Warehouses emptied in the blink of an eye, ships were loaded, work was plentiful, wages rose, and the long-awaited good times had arrived at last.

When a whole county rejoices, no cottage stays untouched by the festal feeling. That afternoon, Caroline Helstone dressed in her finest soft muslin, and walked over to Fieldhead to help Shirley pick the flowers and veils and gowns for her upcoming wedding to Louis Moore. Shirley, as usual, was in one of her delightfully contrary moods: she had been putting off the wedding day week after week, teasing and coaxing Louis until his usual calm, quiet nature had roused to gentle determination, and at last fixed a firm date that she could not argue her way out of. Defeated by love, she had surrendered, but refused to lift a finger to help with the wedding preparations, telling every servant who came to her for orders to "go ask Mr. Moore." She was perfectly happy to abdicate all her authority, for as she told Louis a year later, he would never have learned to rule the home they shared if she had not stopped governing it first.

Caroline had returned home to the parsonage by sunset, to water her beloved plants. The last one she tended was a rose bush tucked in a green nook behind the house, and when she was done, she climbed up on an old weathered fragment of carved monk's stone, once the base of a long-gone cross, to look out over the moors. Below her, scattered bonfires lit up the dusk, their soft red flames glowing against the deepening blue sky, and the first silver star of the evening—Venus, the star of love—twinkled high above. Caroline sighed softly, watching it, and a warm hand curled gently around her waist.

She thought it was Mrs. Pryor, her mother. "I'm looking at Venus, mamma," she said softly. "See how bright she is, how her white glow shines next to the red of the bonfires."

The reply was a closer, sweeter caress. Caroline turned, and looked up not at her mother's familiar gentle face, but at Robert Moore's dark, handsome, glowing visage. She dropped her watering pot with a clink, and stepped down from the stone plinth.

"I've been sitting with your mother for an hour," Robert said, smiling. "We had a very long, very satisfactory talk. Where have you been?"

"To Fieldhead," Caroline laughed. "Shirley is as naughty as ever. She won't give a straight answer to any question about the wedding. If you scold her, she gives you a look half wistful, half reckless, that leaves you as flustered as she is. I don't know how Louis puts up with her."

"Don't worry about them," Robert said. "They were made for each other. Louis loves her all the more for her little wild freaks. He's had a stormy courtship, but he's won her at last. Caroline, the bells you hear ringing? They're for the repeal of the Orders in Council."

Caroline's eyes went wide. "You are pleased, then?"

"Last night at this very hour, I was packing my books to sail for Canada," he told her, his voice warm and thick with feeling. "I thought I was ruined, that I would have to leave all this, leave you, forever. But not now. The repeal saved me. I will not go bankrupt. I will not close the mills. I will not leave England. I can pay all my debts, every last bolt of cloth in my warehouses has been ordered already, and new commissions are pouring in. Today I have a firm foundation at last, to build my life on."

Caroline's hands flew to his arm, holding tight. "You are saved? All your troubles are gone?"

"All gone," he said softly. "Now I can take on more workers, pay better wages, plan for a kinder, fairer mill. Now I can build a real home, a home that is truly mine. And now—" he paused, his voice softening even more, "now I can seek a wife."

Caroline did not speak, her eyes bright with unshed happy tears.

"Will you forgive me, Caroline?" he asked, his thumb brushing the back of her hand. "Forgive all the months of pain I made you suffer, all the cruel ways I pushed you away when I was too wrapped up in my ambition to see what was right in front of me? Will you let me spend the rest of my life making it up to you, loving you faithfully, cherishing you as you deserve?"

A soft, gentle pressure of her hand on his was all the answer he needed. "Caroline is yours," she whispered.

Suddenly Caroline's lip trembled, and she nestled close to him before pulling back a little, worried. "Poor mamma," she said. "She has no one but me. I cannot leave her, even for you. I could never break her heart."

Robert smiled, and stroked her hair. "We already thought of that. You will never have to leave her. She is going to come and live with us. She will have her own private set of rooms, her own servant, as she stipulated. She does not want to be a burden, but she never wants to be parted from you, either."

Caroline's face lit up, and she laughed, giddy with joy. She teased him gently, "Wait—she will be your mother-in-law, you know."

"Louis and I are not the kind of men to fear our mothers-in-law, my sweet Cary," he laughed. "We do not make enemies of our own household. I know she will adore me, in her quiet way."

"She already does," Caroline told him, grinning. "She said you are one of the very few gentlemen she has ever met who is both polite and sincere. My uncle, too, has given his consent—on the condition I am well provided for, which of course I will be. Robert—" she paused, looking up at him with bright, earnest eyes. "Now that you have your fortune back, you will use it to do good, won't you?"
