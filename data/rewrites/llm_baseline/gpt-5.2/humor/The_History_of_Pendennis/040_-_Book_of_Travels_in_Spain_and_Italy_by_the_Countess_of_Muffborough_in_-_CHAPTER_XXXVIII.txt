Better people than Morgan the valet—persons with incomes, principles, and a general belief in arithmetic—were not half so accurately instructed as that gentleman regarding the true bulk and behaviour of Lady Clavering’s fortune. In London, before her Ladyship’s trunks had so much as rattled into Grosvenor Place, her money had already arrived and taken lodgings in everybody’s mouth. It was enormous; it was fabulous; it had a taste of spice, a gleam of diamond, and a rustle of bank-notes so broad that the very sound of it could have kept the mice from nibbling the wainscot.

They talked of indigo factories that dyed whole rivers blue; of opium clippers that sailed like ghosts and returned like gold mines; of banks bursting with rupees—rupees enough, it was said, to pave St. James’s Street and still leave change for Kensington. There were diamonds, too, and jewels of native princes; and interest-money—interest upon interest—paid by generations of moustached potentates who had borrowed from Lady Clavering’s father as naturally as other men borrow salt from a neighbour. Her account at her banker’s was “positively known” (as positively unknown things always are), and the sum had so many ciphers in it that the hearer’s mouth took the shape of the same letter, and remained so.

It was further whispered—whispered in clubs, in conservatories, under fans, and (most venomously) in carriage-corners—that an envoy from an Indian prince was in England at that very moment, negotiating with the Begum Clavering for the sale of a celebrated nose-ring diamond, “the light of the Dewan.” The envoy was a Colonel Altamont, the Nawaub of Lucknow’s prime favourite; an extraordinary man, who had embraced Mahometanism, or had not embraced it, or had embraced everything in turn from a creed to a card-table; and had undergone a thousand adventures, all perilous, all picturesque, and all narrated with the same oath in the middle of them.

Under the title of Begum, Lady Clavering’s fame went forward like the smell of a rich curry in a boarding-house staircase—irresistible, penetrating, and productive of immediate intimacy among persons who were strangers five minutes before. And as writers of our Constitution are forever bragging that we admit merit into our aristocracy, so might we boast—if we chose to boast honestly—that our best society admits wealth with a promptness and cordiality which merit might envy. Let a person be rich enough, and the iron gates fly back as if greased with butter. The haughtiest people are not haughty at all when there is money; they will take your hand, take your dinners, take your daughters, and (if you are lucky) allow you to take theirs; and all with a frankness so British that one almost weeps at the simplicity of it.

Now, while the town was seasoning Lady Clavering’s fortune with every spice of exaggeration, the Chevalier Strong was in the midst of actual domestic cookery of another kind—arranging, preparing, garnishing; not sauces, but salons. Having superintended the country mansion, he now gave the full benefit of his taste to the London upholsterers, and watched the town house being dressed as anxiously as if it were a roast he meant to carve himself.

Honest Strong’s soul rejoiced in hangings and cornices. He hung and re-hung pictures with the air of a man basting them. He studied the positions of sofas as a serious cook studies the placing of dishes—so that nothing should arrive cold, nothing be spilt, and every guest be tempted at the right moment. He interviewed wine merchants and purveyors; he smelt corks, he discussed binning, he spoke of claret as if it were a moral quality. At the same time, with that provident appetite which makes a gentleman’s conscience wonderfully elastic, the Baronet’s confidential friend took the opportunity of furnishing his own chambers and stocking his own snug little cellar. His friends complimented him upon the neatness of the former; and the select guests who dropped in to share Strong’s cutlet now found a bottle of excellent claret to accompany it—deep, quiet wine, that seemed to make a soft church-music in the glass when it was poured.

The Chevalier declared himself, in his own phrase, “in clover.” He had a comfortable set of rooms in Shepherd’s Inn—rooms with a certain legal smell about them, ink and dust and old parchment; but made agreeable by the human inventions of fire, food, and a tolerably clean tablecloth. He was waited on by a former Spanish Legionary and comrade, whom he had once left at a breach in a Spanish fort and afterwards found, by one of those miracles which occur daily in London, at a crossing in Tottenham Court Road. Strong elevated him to the rank of body-servant—both to himself and to the chum who at present shared his lodgings.

That chum was no other than the valiant Colonel Altamont, the Nawaub of Lucknow’s favourite, the very man whose name went about London like a dramatic flourish.

No man was less curious—or, at least, more discreet—than Ned Strong. He did not care to inquire into the mysterious connection which, soon after the meeting at Baymouth, was established between Sir Francis Clavering and the envoy of the Nawaub. The Colonel knew something—some secret, some old story, some paper, perhaps—regarding Sir Francis, which put the Baronet in his power. Strong, who knew that his patron’s youth had been irregular, and his career in India not brilliant, supposed Altamont had a hold of the kind which makes a man’s laughter short and his purse long.

Indeed, Strong had long understood Sir Francis Clavering’s character: a man utterly weak in purpose, principle, and intellect; a moral and physical trifler; a poltroon who could be coaxed, bullied, flattered, frightened—anything but led by his own will, which was not a will so much as a loose string.

Clavering and Altamont had had one or two interviews after Baymouth—conversations of which Sir Francis told Strong nothing, though he constantly sent letters to the Colonel by Strong’s hand, making the Chevalier ambassador in all sorts of affairs. On one occasion Altamont was in such an ill humour that he crushed Clavering’s letter in his fist as if it were a stale crust, and broke out with his own particular emphasis.

“A hundred, be hanged. I’ll have no more letters nor no more shilly-shally. Tell Clavering I’ll have a thousand, or by Jove I’ll split, and burst him all to atoms. Let him give me a thousand and I’ll go abroad, and I give you my honour as a gentleman, I’ll not ask him for no more for a year. Give him that message, Strong, my boy; and tell him if the money ain’t here next Friday at twelve o’clock, as sure as my name’s what it is, I’ll have a paragraph in the newspaper on Saturday; and next week I’ll blow up the whole concern.”

Strong carried the message. Its effect on Sir Francis was such that, on the day and hour appointed, the Chevalier appeared once more at Altamont’s hotel at Baymouth with the sum required. Altamont, being “a gentleman,” behaved as such: he paid his bill, spoke of honour, drank something with a snap to it, and the Baymouth paper announced his departure on a foreign tour. Strong saw him embark at Dover.

“It must be forgery at the very least,” Strong thought—“forgery, or some such handsome crime—that has put Clavering into this fellow’s power; and the Colonel has got the bill.”

But before the year was out, the happy country saw the Colonel again. A confounded run on the red had finished him at Baden-Baden, he said: no gentleman could stand against a colour coming up fourteen times. He had been obliged to draw upon Sir Francis for the means of returning home; and Clavering, though pressed for money—election expenses, country establishment, London furnishing—found means to accept the bill, though with a face as sour as unripe gooseberries. In Strong’s hearing, Sir Francis wished, with many curses, that the Colonel could have been locked up in a debtor’s gaol in Germany for life, so he might never be troubled again.

These sums Sir Francis was obliged to raise without his wife’s knowledge; for though perfectly liberal—nay sumptuous—in expenditure, Lady Clavering had inherited a tolerable aptitude for business along with Snell’s fortune. She gave her husband a handsome allowance, as she thought befitted his rank; and now and then she would make him a present or pay a gambling debt, but she always exacted a pretty accurate account of the money. Regarding subsidies to Altamont, Clavering fairly told Strong he could not speak to his wife.

Part of Strong’s business, therefore, was to procure these sums, and others. In Shepherd’s Inn many negotiations took place between gentlemen of the moneyed world and Sir Francis Clavering; and many bank-notes—crisp, scented faintly of iron and ink—and many pieces of stamped paper passed between them. When a man has been in the habit from youth of borrowing tomorrow to feed today, no good fortune can fatten him for long. A little prosperity is like a rich gravy poured upon a leaky pie; it smells comforting, but it runs out, and presently the money-lender comes back with a spoon.

Clavering found it more convenient to see these gentry at Strong’s lodgings than at his own; and such was the Chevalier’s friendship that, although he did not possess a shilling of his own, his name might be seen as drawer of almost all the bills which Sir Francis accepted. Strong drew, got them discounted in the City, parleyed when they became due, gave instalments, got time in exchange for fresh acceptances—always whisking about, as brisk as a waiter keeping a table supplied while the cook is quarrelling with the butcher in the yard.

And yet, like soldiers in a besieged garrison who act plays and dance at balls while the enemy are outside and the gallows inside, there are hundreds of gallant spirits in London who dine tolerably, laugh agreeably, and sleep comfortably, with a bailiff always somewhere near and a rope of debt round the neck. Ned Strong bore these trifling inconveniences easily. We shall meet more of Shepherd’s Inn another day; for the present, Lady Clavering and her friends have been too long left waiting on the door-steps of Grosvenor Place.

They were first ushered into the dining-room—gorgeous, solemn, and fitted up, Lady Clavering said, she couldn’t for goodness gracious tell why, in the middle-aged style “unless,” she added, laughing good-naturedly, “because me and Clavering are middle-aged people.” Here were offered the copious remains of luncheon which Lady Clavering and Blanche had just partaken.

And now the little Sylphide reappeared—no airy creature of dew and moonshine when no eyes were upon her. When company watched her at dinner, she ate about as much as those six grains of rice of Amina in the Arabian Nights; but now, when nobody was near, she was brisk with knife and fork, and fell upon the mutton cutlets with an appetite so candid and substantial that one could have respected it, had it not been masked so prettily a moment afterwards. She ate as if mutton were a secret she meant to keep; and in this piece of hypocrisy it is believed she resembled other young ladies of fashion.

Pen and his uncle declined the refection, but admired the room with fitting compliments, and pronounced it “very chaste”—that being the proper phrase, like a sauce one must pour whether one likes it or no. There were high-backed Dutch chairs of the seventeenth century that looked as if they had been built for men with shoulder-blades like shields; a carved buffet of the sixteenth; a sideboard robbed out of a church in the Low Countries, with a pious air of having known better days; a great brass cathedral lamp over the round oak table; old family portraits from Wardour Street; tapestry from France; bits of armour; double-handed swords and battle-axes made of carton-pierre and bravely pretending to have slain somebody; looking-glasses; statuettes of saints; Dresden china—nothing, in a word, could be chaster.

Behind the dining-room was the library, fitted with busts and books all of a size (as if knowledge came only by the yard), wonderful easy-chairs, and solemn bronzes in the severe classic style—cold metal gentlemen who seemed to disapprove of digestion. Guarded by double doors, it was here that Sir Francis smoked cigars, read Bell’s Life in London, and went to sleep after dinner, when he was not smoking over the billiard-table at his clubs, or punting at the gambling-houses in St. James’s.

But what could equal the chaste splendour of the drawing-rooms? The carpets were so magnificently fluffy that your foot made no more sound on them than your shadow. This silence had the unnatural hush of a room that has paid to be quiet; the air seemed padded. On the white ground bloomed roses and tulips as big as warming-pans. About were high chairs and low chairs, bandy-legged chairs, chairs so attenuated that it was a wonder any but a sylph could sit upon them without snapping an ankle of gilded wood. There were marquetry tables crowded with gimcracks, china ornaments of all ages and countries, bronzes, gilt daggers, Books of Beauty, yataghans, Turkish papooshes, and boxes of Parisian bonbons—sweetness from abroad, wrapped and perfumed as if meant to distract from any honest English apple.

Wherever you sat there were Dresden shepherds and shepherdesses convenient at your elbow; light-blue poodles in porcelain; ducks and cocks and hens, for the poultry-yard of fashion; nymphs by Boucher and shepherdesses by Greuze, all “very chaste indeed.” Muslin curtains and brocade curtains; gilt cages with parroquets and love-birds; two squealing cockatoos, each out-squealing and out-chattering the other, so that even splendour could not buy good manners from a bird. A clock sang tunes on a console-table; another boomed the hours like Great Tom on the mantelpiece. The result was a curious warfare of noises—whistles, squawks, jingles, and clangs—against that muffled carpeted hush; as if the room, afraid of the honest sound of a human step, had hired machinery and exotic throats to make a racket in its place. One couldn’t help thinking how much kinder it would have been to the nerves—and to civilisation—had somebody simply opened a window and let in the plain breathing of the street, or the far-off honest rumble of wheels that had not been trained to whisper.

Still, in a word, there was everything that comfort could desire and the most elegant taste devise. A London drawing-room fitted up without regard to expense is surely among the noblest and most curious sights of the present day. The Romans of the Lower Empire, the dear Marchionesses and Countesses of Louis XV., could scarcely have had a finer taste than our modern folks exhibit; and everybody who saw Lady Clavering’s reception-rooms was forced to confess they were most elegant, and that the prettiest rooms in London—Lady Harley Quin’s, Lady Hanway Wardour’s, or Mrs. Hodge-Podgson’s (wife of the great Railroad Croesus)—were not fitted up with a more consummate “chastity.”

Poor Lady Clavering, meanwhile, knew little regarding these things, and had a sad want of reverence for the splendours around her. “I only know they cost a precious deal of money, Major,” she said, “and I don’t advise you to try one of them gossamer gilt chairs: I came down on one the night we gave our second dinner-party. Why didn’t you come and see us before? We’d have asked you to it.”

“You would have liked to see Mamma break a chair, wouldn’t you, Mr. Pendennis?” dear Blanche said with a sneer. She was angry because Pen was talking and laughing with Mamma; because Mamma made blunders in describing the house; and for a hundred other good reasons which keep a young lady’s temper in perpetual training.

“I should like to have been by to give Lady Clavering my arm if she had need of it,” Pen answered, with a bow and a blush.

“Quel preux Chevalier!” cried the Sylphide, tossing her little head.

“I have a fellow-feeling with those who fall, remember,” Pen said. “I suffered myself very much from doing so once.”

“And you went home to Laura to console you,” said Miss Amory.

Pen winced. He did not like the remembrance of the consolation; nor was he pleased to find that his rebuff was public property—served up, as it were, cold, and handed round with a smile. Having nothing ready in reply, he became immensely interested in the furniture, praised Lady Clavering’s taste with all his might, and took refuge in “chastity” as in a screen.

“No, don’t praise me,” said honest Lady Clavering. “It’s all the upholsterer’s doings and Captain Strong’s—they did it all while we was at the Park—and—and—Lady Rockminster has been here and says the salongs are very well,” she added, with a tone of great deference, as if “salongs” were a dish whose name she had tasted but could not pronounce.

“My cousin Laura has been staying with her,” Pen said.

“It’s not the dowager: it is the Lady Rockminster,” said Lady Clavering, correcting him with awe, as if there were but one Rockminster and she sat upon a throne of calling-cards.

“Indeed!” cried Major Pendennis at that great name. “If you have her ladyship’s approval, Lady Clavering, you cannot be far wrong. No, no—you cannot be far wrong. Lady Rockminster, I should say, Arthur, is the very centre of the circle of fashion and taste.” And the Major’s voice hushed, and he surveyed the apartments respectfully, as if he had been in a church where the deity was Upholstery.

“Yes, Lady Rockminster has took us up,” said Lady Clavering.

“Taken us up, Mamma,” cried Blanche, sharp as a parrot’s beak.

“Well—taken us up, then,” Lady Clavering answered patiently; “it’s very kind of her, and I dare say we shall like it when we git used to it, only at first one don’t fancy being took—well, taken up—at all. She is going to give our balls for us; and wants to invite all our dinners. But I won’t stand that. I will have my old friends; and I won’t let her send all the cards out, and sit mum at the head of my own table. You must come to me—Arthur and Major—come, let me see, on the 14th. It ain’t one of our grand dinners, Blanche,” she said, looking at her daughter; and Blanche bit her lip and frowned, very savagely for a sylphide who had so lately been so brave with the cutlets.

The Major, smiling and bowing, said he would much rather come to a quiet meeting than to a grand dinner. He had had enough of large entertainments, and preferred the simplicity of the home circle—simplicity, delivered in a drawing-room full of squealing cockatoos and singing clocks.

“I always think a dinner’s the best the second day,” said Lady Clavering, attempting to mend her first speech, and only making it more nourishing. “On the 14th we’ll be quite a snug little party.”

At this second blunder, Miss Blanche clasped her hands in despair. “O, mamma, vous etes incorrigible.”

Major Pendennis vowed he liked snug dinners of all things in the world, and inwardly confounded Lady Clavering’s audacity for asking a man of his consequence to dine upon the second day. But being economical, and considering that he could always throw these people over if something better offered, he accepted with the blandest air. As for Pen, he was not yet a diner-out of thirty years’ standing; and the idea of a fine feast in a fine house was still welcome to him, like the smell of roast meat to a young man with an honest appetite.

When they walked away together, the Major asked, “What was that pretty little quarrel which engaged itself between your worship and Miss Amory? I thought you used to be au mieux in that quarter.”

“Used to be,” Pen answered with a dandified air, “is a vague phrase regarding a woman. Was and is are two very different terms, sir, as regards women’s hearts especially.”

“Egad, they change as we do,” cried the elder. “When we took the Cape of Good Hope, I recollect there was a lady who talked poisoning herself for your humble servant; and, begad, in three months she ran away from her husband with somebody else. Don’t get yourself entangled with that Miss Amory. She is forward, affected, and under-bred; and her character is somewhat—never mind what. But don’t think of her; ten thousand pound won’t do for you. What, my good fellow, is ten thousand pound? I would scarcely pay that girl’s milliner’s bill with the interest of the money.”

“You seem to be a connoisseur in millinery, Uncle,” Pen said.

“I was, sir, I was,” replied the senior. “And the old war-horse, you know, never hears the sound of a trumpet, but he begins to—he, he!—you understand.” And he gave a killing and somewhat superannuated leer and bow to a carriage that rolled by and entered the Park. “Lady Catherine Martingale’s carriage,” he said; “monstrous fine girls the daughters; though, gad, I remember their mother a thousand times handsomer.

“No, Arthur, my dear fellow, with your person and expectations, you ought to make a good coup in marriage some day or other; and though I wouldn’t have this repeated at Fairoaks, you rogue—ha! ha!—a reputation for a little wickedness, and for being an homme dangereux, don’t hurt a young fellow with the women. They like it, sir; they hate a milksop—young men must be young men, you know. But for marriage,” continued the veteran moralist, “that is a very different matter. Marry a woman with money. I’ve told you before it is as easy to get a rich wife as a poor one; and a doosed deal more comfortable to sit down to a well-cooked dinner, with your little entrées nicely served, than to have nothing but a damned cold leg of mutton between you and your wife. We shall have a good dinner on the 14th, when we dine with Sir Francis Clavering. Stick to that, my boy, in your relations with the family. Cultivate ’em, but keep ’em for dining. No more of your youthful follies and nonsense about love in a cottage.”

“It must be a cottage with a double coach-house, a cottage of gentility, sir,” said Pen, quoting the hackneyed ballad of the Devil’s Walk.

But his uncle did not know the poem, and went on with his remarks, pleased with the aptness of the pupil. Arthur Pendennis was a clever fellow who took his colour readily from his neighbour, and found the adaptation only too easy. Warrington, the grumbler, growled that Pen was becoming such a puppy there soon would be no bearing him; but the truth is, the young man’s success and dashing manners pleased his elder companion. The Major liked to see Pen gay and spirited, brimful of health and life and hope; as a man long past being amused by clown and harlequin still takes pleasure in watching a child at a pantomime.

Mr. Pen’s former sulkiness disappeared with his better fortune; and he bloomed as the sun began to shine upon him.
