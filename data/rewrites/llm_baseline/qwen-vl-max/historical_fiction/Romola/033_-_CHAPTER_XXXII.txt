The day after the French departure, Florence buzzed with relief and celebration. Romola, like every other citizen, felt the weight of their presence lifted—yet her own joy carried a deeper, more personal edge. There was a quiet hope stirring in her heart, half-felt, half-superstitious: that with the French gone, so too might vanish the dread that had clung to Tito since their arrival. She could not explain why, but she knew the fear had come with them—the image of a prisoner grasping him, as drawn by Piero, haunted her thoughts. And now, with the foreign troops withdrawn, perhaps that shadow would fade too.

Wrapped in her mantle, she stood beneath the loggia at the top of the house, watching for glimpses of the departing army and royal entourage crossing the bridges toward Porta San Piero. When the gates closed behind them, she lingered still, wanting to feel the city’s pulse through the great tolling of the bells. It was dusk when she finally descended into the library, lit her lamp, and resolved to conquer the restlessness that had kept her idle all day. She would work on copying the catalogue—something she had long set aside.

Tito had left early; she expected him later. Before he returned, she planned to move to the saloon, with its dancing nymphs and birds—a place she had retreated to each evening since he’d complained the library was cold and gloomy. But just as she began her task, Tito entered.

Her first thought was how bleak the room must seem to him—dark, vast, lit only by a single oil-lamp at one end, the fire nearly dead. She rushed forward. “Tito, dearest—I didn’t expect you so soon,” she said, nervously lifting her arms to unfasten his becchetto.

“I am not welcome then?” he asked, smiling brightly as he embraced her, holding his head back playfully.

“Tito!” she replied, voice soft with affectionate reproach. He kissed her gently, stroked her hair as he always did, and seemed content to leave his cloak where it was. Romola trembled—not from cold, but from joy. The day’s tensions had sharpened her senses, and now, seeing him return to his old ways, she felt a flicker of hope. *It will come back,* she thought. *The old happiness may yet return.*

But Tito was not truly himself. Beneath the warmth, his heart pounded with anxiety. He had prepared this moment carefully, though it seemed effortless.

“If I’d known you’d come so soon,” Romola said, finally helping him remove his coat, “I’d have made a little feast for this joyful ringing of the bells. I didn’t mean to be here when you arrived.”

“Never mind, sweet,” he said lightly. “Don’t worry about the fire. Come—sit down.”

She took her usual seat on the low stool beside his chair, resting her arm on his knee as she once had on her father’s. She looked up at him, waiting.

He had never noticed the portrait leaning against the wall. She had never mentioned it—though it had grown heavier in her mind with each passing hour.

“I’ve enjoyed the bells tonight,” she began. “They shook me, almost deafened me—I felt like a Bacchante possessed. Are the people really so joyful?”

“Joyful, yes—but in a sour, pious way,” Tito replied, shrugging. “And those who remain in Florence have little reason to celebrate. To me, the true cause for gladness would be getting out of Florence altogether.”

He had struck the note he wanted—without effort, without pretense. His tone was casual, but his eyes held gravity. Romola hesitated. “Why, Tito? Are there fresh troubles?”

“No need for new ones, my Romola. There are already three powerful factions in the city, ready to tear each other apart. If the Frate’s party gains strength enough to silence the others—as seems likely—life will be no more pleasant than a funeral. They’re already planning a Great Council. Soon, the man who sings sacred lauds loudest will be the most eligible for office. And then there’s the crushing burden of the subsidy paid to the French king, and the war to reclaim Pisa. Florence will be drained—its future bleak. For such men as those who find pleasure in lashing themselves in crypts, it may be ideal. But for the rest of us… exile has the better claim.”

He paused. “For my part, I’ve been thinking seriously that we should leave Florence.”

Romola froze. “Tito—how could we leave? Surely you don’t think I could—no, not yet. Not for a long time.”

Her voice trembled. She felt cold, shaken. He must know the reasons she carried in her heart.

“That is all imagination, my love,” he said softly. “Your secluded life has given you false importance to a few things. Remember—I told you before marriage that I wished we were elsewhere. You’ve seen too little of the world to understand what I mean. There’s something in the Florentines that reminds me of their sharp spring winds. I prefer people who take life less fiercely. And it would do you good, my Romola, to see a new world—to bathe in softer waters, to forget.”

He leaned forward, kissed her brow, touched her hair. But she felt nothing—only the chilling distance between them. Her love, once so certain, now felt shattered.

“It is not because I believe Florence is perfect that I wish to stay,” she said quietly. “It is because—because we must fulfill my father’s wish. My godfather is old—seventy-one. We cannot leave it to him.”

“And that is precisely why I wish we were two hundred leagues away,” Tito replied. “Those superstitions cling to your mind like clouds, blinding you. I must protect you—even against yourself. If your eyes, so tender, see falsely, then I must see for you. I must save you from wasting your life on dreams that can never be fulfilled.”

Romola sat silent. She saw where he was leading. He wanted her to accept that the library could be handed over to a monastery, or sold—any means to free them from Florence. And she knew she would never yield. This was not merely duty—it was honor, memory, love.

But the pain of realizing they were divided overwhelmed her. She could not speak. Tito, meanwhile, felt pleased by her silence. Though he feared her passion, he could not imagine how deeply she would feel this betrayal. Confined within his narrow intellect, he believed his arguments were irrefutable. He continued, gentle but firm.

“You know, dearest—your own judgment once showed you—that the idea of isolating books and relics under a single name is not truly beneficial. It’s fragile, easily lost. Look at the Medici collections—what remains? And I find it even wrong to hold such petty claims. Why should Florence be favored over any other city? I understand your feelings for the dead—but wisdom sets limits. Otherwise, lives are wasted in futile devotion—like praising gods who cannot hear. You gave your life to your father while he lived. Why demand more?”

“Because it was a trust,” Romola said, voice low but clear. “He trusted me. He trusted you, Tito. I didn’t expect you to feel as I do—but I did expect you to respect it.”

“Yes, dearest,” he said, “of course I would care if his welfare depended on it. But it doesn’t now. If I believed in purgatory, I’d want masses said. If I believed he suffered now, I’d share your strictness. But philosophy teaches us to break free from these invisible chains—these fantasies we bind ourselves with. Your mind, so quick, can distinguish real good from illusion. Ask yourself—what good do these books do locked in Florence under his name, compared to being shared widely? Is not dispersal a greater service? The rivalry between Italian cities is petty. The fall of Constantinople enriched the whole world.”

Romola listened, heart breaking. His words were smooth, rational—yet they revealed a soul devoid of depth. She felt contempt rising, mingled with grief. This man she had loved, who had once seemed noble, now spoke of faithfulness as a weakness, of love as folly.

“You talk of substantial good,” she said, voice growing stronger. “But what of loyalty? Of love? Of gratitude? What good is it to keep our promises, knowing others depend on our truth? What good is it to honor a just life? Or is it good to harden our hearts against those who trusted us? What kind of man does such a thing—clever, perhaps, but living only with himself?”

Her voice rose, edged with scorn. “I see no good in a world built on such men. I’m not thinking of Italy or the civilized world—I’m thinking of my father. Of my love. Of his claim on us. I would give up anything—leave Florence—anything. But not this. What have I to do with your arguments? It was his longing—and therefore mine.”

Her voice had steadied. She felt she had said all she needed.

Tito saw he could not persuade her. He would have to act. The time for resistance was over. He would make the revelation now—before she could interfere.

“I’m sorry to hear you speak so stubbornly, Romola,” he said calmly. “Because it forces me to cause you pain. But I foresaw your opposition. A decision had to be made quickly. So I acted alone—for your sake.”

She stared at him, breathless.

“I’ve arranged for the transfer of both the books and antiquities. They’ll serve better uses elsewhere. The books have been sold to the Duke of Milan. The marbles, bronzes, and artifacts go to France. Both will be safe under strong powers—unlike Florence, which teeters on ruin.”

Before he finished, Romola rose. She stood over him, hands clenched, eyes blazing. For the first time, fury flashed in her face.

“You have _sold_ them?” she whispered, as if doubting her ears.

“I have,” Tito replied, flinching slightly.

“You are a traitor,” she said, voice grating with disdain.

She turned abruptly. “It may still be stopped. I am going to my godfather.”

In an instant, Tito lunged to the door, locked it, and pulled out the key. The moment demanded control. He wasn’t angry—he simply wanted to contain her.

“Try to calm yourself, Romola,” he said, leaning casually against a pedestal beneath a stern Roman bust. “Understand the facts. Going to Messer Bernardo won’t change anything. He cannot reverse what’s done. Sit down. You wouldn’t wish to expose our private quarrel to a third person.”

He knew he had struck a nerve. But she did not sit.

“Why can’t it be reversed?” she asked, voice steady. “Nothing has moved yet.”

“Because the sale is final. Agreements are signed. The buyers have left Florence. I hold the bonds for the money.”

“If my father had suspected you of treachery,” she said, voice sharp with bitterness, “he would have secured the library beyond your reach. But death came too soon. When his ear was deaf, his hand stiff—you stole from him.”

She paused. Then, with gathering fire: “Have you stolen from someone else—someone alive? Is that why you wear armor?”

The words tore from her like a whip. At first, Tito crumpled under the blow. The shame he had dreaded felt worse than imagined. But then, a reaction surged. He would not be mastered by a woman whose rage seemed like fate itself.

“It’s useless,” he said coolly, “to answer madness. Your obsession with your father has driven you mad. Any reasonable person would see I’ve taken the wisest course. Even Messer Bernardo would agree.”

“He would not!” Romola said. “He lives in hope of fulfilling my father’s wish. We spoke of it yesterday. He will help me. Who are these men you sold to?”

“Men of no consequence. Count di San Severino and Seneschal de Beaucaire are now with the king, heading to Siena.”

“They can be caught,” Romola said, hope rising. “Persuaded to return.”

“No,” Tito said firmly. “They cannot.”

“Why?”

“Because I refuse to let them.”

“But if you were paid—*we* will pay you,” she said, voice pleading. “We’ll repay the money.”

No words could reveal more clearly how far she had fallen from him. Yet she spoke not with anger, but desperation.

“No,” Tito said. “Such thoughts are impossible. You wouldn’t ask your godfather to bury three thousand florins more than he’s already spent. Your pride would not allow it.”

She trembled. Coldness seized her. She sank onto the carved chest beside her.

He continued, voice clear and cold as ice: “Besides, it is not my wish that Messer Bernardo advance any sum—even if the plan were not utterly foolish. And I beg you to consider: what will happen if you oppose me? If you try to disgrace me before him? What purpose will it serve? The event is irreversible. The library is sold. And you are my wife.”

Each word was calculated. He watched her closely.

She turned her eyes from him, staring at the floor. Minutes passed.

When she spoke again, her voice was quiet, flat. “I have one request.”

“Name it—if it does not harm us both.”

“That you give me the portion belonging to my godfather. Let me repay him.”

“I need assurance first—of your intentions toward me.”

“Do you believe in assurances, Tito?”

“From you, I do.”

“I will do you no harm. I will say nothing. I will not pain him—or you. You are right—the event is irrevocable.”

“Then I will do as you ask tomorrow morning.”

“To-night, if possible,” she said. “So we need not speak of it again.”

“It is possible.” He moved toward the lamp. She sat still, eyes distant.

He bent over her, placed a paper in her hand.

“You will receive something in return, you know,” he said gently, already feeling secure. “A token.”

“Yes,” she said, taking it without looking at him. “I understand.”

“And you will forgive me, Romola, when you reflect.”

He touched her brow with his lips. She did not react.

She heard him unlock the door. She heard the court gate open and close.

Then, as if released, she rose. She walked to her father’s chair, where his portrait stood propped. She knelt before it—and broke into sobs.
