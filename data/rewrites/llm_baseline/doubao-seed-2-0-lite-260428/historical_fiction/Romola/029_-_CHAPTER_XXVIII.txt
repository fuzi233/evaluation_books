Four days later, Romola made her way along the Via Gualfonda to the home of the eccentric painter Piero di Cosimo, her steps hurried beneath the thrumming, uneasy hum of Florence’s streets. The thoroughfares were lined on all sides by wide-eyed French soldiers gawking at the city’s monuments, and Florentine citizens staring back at the unwelcome guests, neither side’s gaze softening with goodwill. The self-styled greatest nation on the continent, unused to anything but deference outside its own borders, carried itself with a heavy air of unearned superiority, while the Florentines, who had bent over backwards to play gracious hosts through weeks of festivals, elaborate church mystery plays with floating stage clouds and mechanized angels, and royal banquets where the French king had leered openly at the city’s noblewomen, had curdled into sullen, sharp-edged resentment. Once talks of policy began, it became clear the new “Charlemagne” saw Florence as a conquered prize: he had ridden in with his lance at rest, spoken of leaving a viceroy behind, and openly plotted to restore the exiled Medici line. To Florentine ears, this was absurd logic. The only slight the city had ever offered France was the work of the long-disgraced Piero de’ Medici, whom the people themselves had cast out, and the councils meeting day and night in the Palazzo Vecchio had already made clear they would not submit. Everywhere in the public squares, on the broad flagstones of the streets, ordinary citizens took any small chance to snub the insolent French troops.

For a well-born woman to walk abroad unescorted by a retinue was hardly safe in such an atmosphere, but Romola, shrouded head to foot in a thick black veil and mantle, with her loyal old serving man Maso trundling at her heels, faced no risk of unwanted attention. She was desperate to reach Piero, too: he had long before undertaken to paint a copy of her father’s portrait as Oedipus, the only existing likeness of the old scholar in his years of blindness, and it was still unfinished. Piero was famously erratic in his work: when no one pushed him, he would lay a canvas aside for months, shoving it into a dark corner or a cedar chest until he entirely forgot it existed, and Romola could not bear the thought of the portrait slipping away, the fine lines of her father’s face fading from her memory before she could fix it forever in paint. Wracked by quiet, constant guilt that she had not tended to her father as devotedly as he deserved in his final days, she clung to the portrait as a lifeline, staking her own loyalty of heart on preserving his memory.

As a favoured guest, Romola needed no prior announcement to enter Piero’s studio. She lifted the iron sliding panel in his door and called out in a soft, flutelike tone, the exact same cry the little village girl who sold him eggs had used that day Tito had stood beside her in the doorway. Piero wrenched the door open almost instantly, and scowled when he saw her.
“Ah, Madonna Romola? I thought my eggs had come at last. I’ve been waiting for them.”
“I’ve brought you something far better than hard-boiled eggs, Piero,” Romola laughed, easing her veil back from her face and taking a small wicker basket of cakes and coloured confetti from Maso’s hands. “I know you love these treats best when they come to you with no effort on your part. Do not pretend otherwise.”
“Aye, only when they arrive as easily as daylight,” Piero grumbled, folding his arms and staring down at the sweetmeats as she uncovered them, then lifting his gaze to look at her face and unbound gold hair, his painter’s eye lighting up with quiet admiration as her heavy veil dragged her hood slipping back off her head. “But I know well enough these sweetmeats are a bribe to stop me putting off your portrait another month. Go on through to the back room, then. I’ve worked on the Oedipus since last you saw it, though it’s not done. I never promised you a deadline, you know: the old saying holds, he who swears a vow and breaks it never has a whole soul.”

The door leading out to his wild unkempt garden clicked shut behind them, and Piero turned to his workbench. He was not, however, set up at his easel working on Romola’s portrait. That canvas stood propped against the far wall, and Piero stooped to lift it to carry it to the patch of golden afternoon light by the window. But when he moved it, a far smaller oil sketch that had been hidden entirely behind it came fully into view: a half-finished portrait of Tito, to which Piero had added a critical new detail only a few days prior. Piero, peering at a smudge on the edge of the large canvas he held, did not notice what he had uncovered as he carried the Oedipus to his easel.
Romola gasped, her face flushing bright hot with astonishment. “That is Tito!”

Piero turned around, and gave a silent, sheepish shrug, annoyed at his own careless forgetfulness. Romola stared at the sketch, bewildered and uneasy: Tito’s face was twisted with unmistakable, raw terror, and a ragged gaunt man with a thick rope noose knotted around his neck clung to his arm, as if begging for help or clutching him for purchase.
“What a strange, strange picture,” she said, turning to Piero, her voice tight with alarm. “When did you paint it? What does it mean?”
“Nothing but a silly fancy of mine,” Piero mumbled, yanking his skullcap off and scratching at his head, pulling his usual gruff grimace to hide any hint of unshared thought. “I needed a handsome young man’s face for it, and your husband’s was the finest I could find.” He stepped forward, grabbed the sketch, and held its back to Romola, pretending to brush at a stray spot of paint on its surface like it was a worthless throwaway piece, ready to tuck it out of sight.

But Romola’s mind flew straight to the secret chain armour she had found hidden in their home, and the two details clicked sharp and bright in her head, stoking her unease. “Do not put it away. Let me look again. That man with the rope around his neck – I saw him! I saw you tending to him in the Duomo that very morning. What made you paint him clutching at Tito?”
Piero saw no way out but to tell a half-truth. “It was nothing but an accident. That man was running, tripped on the cathedral steps, and grabbed at your husband to keep from falling. I happened to be there, saw the whole thing, thought the wild ragged wretch made a fine, dramatic subject. It’s worthless, just a daub I threw together for fun.” He moved quickly, hoisting the sketch up onto a high, out-of-reach shelf where she could not tug it back down, and gestured impatiently toward the Oedipus portrait. “Come, look at this one, it’s the work you came for.”

His over-eagerness to hide the sketch only confirmed the exact fear he had tried to avoid stoking: Romola was now certain there was some unpleasant, unflattering secret behind the encounter he had depicted. But her pride, and her deep, delicate loyalty to Tito, would not let her press further – to question more would feel like an admission that she could even entertain the smallest suspicion against her husband.
“He looked so piteous, that prisoner,” she said, as evenly as she could. “Do you know anything more of him?”
“Nothing more. I directed him to the city hospital, that is all. Now see here: the blind face of Oedipus is almost complete. Tell me what you think.”

Romola stepped before the portrait and stood silent for a long while, tears pricking at the corners of her eyes. Piero had captured exactly what she had begged him for: that soft, tilted listening look her father always wore, when he would turn his head at the sound of her footsteps, waiting to hear what she had brought him from the library. “You have done perfectly,” she said at last, turning to Piero with bright, wet eyes. “I am so grateful to you, my good Piero.”
“Ugh, that is the thing I cannot stand about you women,” Piero grumbled, turning away to kick a pile of discarded paint rags out of his path. “You gush feelings all over the place where no one asked for them. Why should you thank me for a painting you are paying me for, especially when I made you wait for months for it? A man does not deserve gratitude for not being a rogue or a fool – he only needs to thank God for not making him either. Women act like the whole world runs on honeyed words.”
“You great crusty thing,” Romola laughed, wiping the tears from her cheeks and plucking a crisp, sweet almond biscuit from the basket to tuck into his mouth. Piero accepted it like a gruff old bear accepting a ripe pear he had been craving for weeks: he loved the treat, but he would never let anyone see him show it.
“It is good, Madonna Antigone,” he mumbled around the mouthful, already reaching into the basket for another. He had eaten nothing but hard-boiled eggs for a full fortnight. Romola stood watching him, her sharp new unease held at bay for a little while by the sight of his unguarded, simple pleasure.
“Goodbye, Piero,” she said at last, setting the empty basket down. “I promise I will not thank you at all if you finish the portrait well and soon. I will just tell you you only did what you owed to your own good name.”
“Good,” Piero said shortly, helping her fold her mantle and veil tight around her shoulders to keep her safe from the street wind. When he shut the door behind her, he sighed in relief. “I am glad she asked no more questions about that sketch. I would hate for her to guess I thought her fine noble husband made the perfect model for a coward. I played it off lightly, she will never think of it again.”

Piero was far too optimistic, as open-hearted men often are when they try to pull off a small lie. The image of that painted sketch burned brighter and brighter in Romola’s mind as she walked home. She could not untangle it: the hidden chain armour, the prisoner who had stumbled into Tito that very same morning, that look of unmistakeable terror on his face Piero had captured. It all lined up too neatly to be a random coincidence.
“It means nothing,” she told herself, forcing herself to steady her steps. “It was just a chance encounter. I should ask Tito about it.” Then her pride, and her stubborn devotion to the ideal of perfect trust, pushed back. No, she decided. I will never question him about anything he did not choose to tell me of his own free will. To do so would be an insult to the loyalty I owe him.
And then her heart whispered the quiet, unvarnished truth: she dared not ask. There was a terrible crack in the trust she had built her whole marriage around. She carried her belief in Tito like someone holding a precious, fragile glass vase they fear is already shattered, too afraid to jostle it even the smallest bit to find out for certain.
