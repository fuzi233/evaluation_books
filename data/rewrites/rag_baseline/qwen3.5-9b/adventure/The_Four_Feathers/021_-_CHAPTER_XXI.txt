To decipher the architecture of Ethne's deception, one must first confront the suffocating weight of the silence that had settled over the house. Mrs. Adair watched the interplay between Ethne and Durrance with a growing, visceral dread, sensing that the delicate fabric of their charade was fraying at the edges. She feared the moment Durrance might strip away the illusion, confessing the terrible clarity he had reached: that Ethne's affection for him was a hollow echo, while her heart burned fiercely for Feversham. If he voiced this truth, even with the utmost care to shield her from the full sting of his discovery, the mask would crack. She braced herself for the collision of gazes—the quiet, piercing stare of Ethne demanding an accounting for the lies they had woven together. Yet, initially, a fragile reprieve held. Their interactions flowed with a deceptive normalcy, as if the day Willoughby arrived at the garden gate and the evening Ethne played the *Musolino* Overture had been erased from history. This temporary stability offered Mrs. Adair a fleeting respite, until the realization dawned that her interference had achieved nothing but delay. Then, anger and disappointment curdled in her stomach, replacing the initial relief.

Durrance, however, had already resolved the internal conflict. Knowing that Ethne desperately wished him ignorant of the truth provided her with a solace in her agony—a belief that she had brought happiness to the one man she truly respected. Durrance, perceptive of this psychological balm, chose not to dismantle it just yet. He needed certainty: was the rift between Ethne and Feversham a mere misunderstanding or an irreversible fracture? Did Harry Feversham survive? Consequently, he adopted a performance of ignorance, abandoning his habitual scrutiny. He feigned contentment, treating his own misfortune lightly, and cultivated an atmosphere where Ethne's company appeared to be his sole compensation.

"You see," he told her, his voice laced with practiced cheer, "one learns to accept blindness as a natural condition. But one does not grow accustomed to you, Ethne. With every encounter, I discover something new, something fresh to delight me. Besides, there remains the slim possibility of a cure."

His strategy bore fruit. Ethne sensed the lifting of his suspicions and countered his relentless optimism with the raw texture of her own sorrow. That sorrow was profound. Though she had briefly reclaimed the lightness of heart she possessed before the three white feathers descended upon Ramelton, she now inhabited the same landscape of grief. Yet, a crucial distinction marked her current state: her pride had been restored, and Durrance's words sparked a faint, dangerous hope that Harry might yet be rescued. Still, the long, sleepless nights returned, accompanied by the dull, burning ache in her head as she awaited the grey dawn. She could no longer convince herself that Harry Feversham was merely a departed friend; he was alive, trapped in unknown straits, and she thirsted for knowledge despite the terror it might bring. Occasionally, her impatience shattered her restraint.

"I suppose escape is possible from Omdurman," she ventured one day, forcing her tone into a mask of indifference.

"Possible? Yes, I believe so," Durrance replied, his cheerfulness unwavering. "Naturally, it is fraught with difficulty and requires time. Attempts have been made to extract Trench and others, but they have failed thus far. The obstacle lies in the intermediary."

Ethne's gaze snapped to him. "The intermediary?" she queried, then paused, pulling herself upright. "I think I begin to grasp it. You mean the Arab who can traverse the space between Omdurman and the Egyptian frontier?"

"Precisely. Typically, it is a Dervish pedlar or merchant trading with the Sudanese tribes, slipping into Wadi Halfa, Assouan, or Suakin to undertake the task. Naturally, the risk is immense. Should his trade be detected in Omdurman, his punishment would be swift and severe. Hence, it is understandable that he shirks the danger at the critical moment. Often, too, he is a rogue. You arrange the details in Egypt, handing over the necessary funds. Six months or a year later, he returns alone, laden with excuses. 'It was summer,' he claims, 'the season unfavorable.' 'The guards were stricter.' 'I myself was suspected.' 'I require more funds.' His story may be true, and you provide more money; he returns, only to vanish again, empty-handed."

Ethne nodded slowly. "Exactly."

In doing so, Durrance had inadvertently illuminated a blind spot in her understanding. She was certain Harry Feversham intended to secure aid for Colonel Trench, but the mechanism of his own capture serving that purpose remained obscure. Now, the pieces clicked into place: he was to be his own intermediary. Her hopes surged, fortified by this revelation. It suggested he had planned with meticulous care. He would be acutely anxious for the second feather to reach her, and if he could successfully extricate Trench from Omdurman, he would surely not remain behind to face the consequences.

A silence stretched between them, broken only by the crimson glow of the sunset reflecting off the creek waters as they sat on the terrace.

"Life would not be easy, I suppose, in the prison of Omdurman," Ethne murmured, again striving for emotional detachment.

"Easy!" Durrance exclaimed, his composure cracking slightly. "No, it would not be easy. A hovel swarming with Arabs, devoid of light or air, with a ceiling perhaps two feet above your head, locking you inside from dusk until dawn. Likely, the prisoners would be forced to stand all night in that fetid den, packed so tightly there is no room to breathe. Imagine it, even here in England, on a night like this! Consider what it would be on an August night in the Sudan! Especially if you carry memories, say, of a place like this, to exacerbate the torment."

Ethne gazed out across the cool garden. In that very instant, Harry Feversham might be gasping for breath in that dark, noxious cell, parched and fevered, with visions of the grassy slopes of Ramelton dancing before his eyes and the liquid melody of the Lennon River echoing in his mind.

"One would pray for death," Ethne said slowly, trailing off. "Unless—" She hesitated, poised to add, "unless one goes there deliberately with a fixed purpose," but she severed the sentence. Durrance completed her thought: "Unless there is a chance of escape," he said. "And there is a chance—if Feversham is in Omdurman."

Fear gripped him; he realized he had revealed too much of the prison's horrors. "Of course," he hastily added, "what I describe is mere hearsay, unverified. We possess no concrete knowledge. Prisoners may endure conditions far less dire than we imagine." With that, he dropped the subject. Nor did Ethne pursue it. Yet, questions lingered in her mind regarding his understanding of her abrupt departure from the drawing-room the night he disclosed his meeting with Feversham. He never addressed it, and she deemed it prudent to mimic his silence. The noticeable shift in his demeanor, the absence of the caution that had previously tormented her, soothed her anxieties. It appeared he had constructed a simple, natural explanation for himself. She also pondered why he had informed her of the meeting in Wadi Halfa and Feversham's subsequent journey south. She found an explanation—a peculiar one, perhaps, yet satisfying. She believed the information was a message delivered solely for her ears and comprehension, conveyed by Durrance as an instrument of a higher will. His true motivations remained unheard.

Throughout September, they maintained the pretense. Every morning, whenever Durrance traveled to Devonshire, he crossed the fields to meet Ethne at The Pool. Mrs. Adair, observing them talking and laughing without a trace of embarrassment or estrangement, grew increasingly furious, finding it harder to maintain her peace and allow the ruse to continue. It was a month of intense strain and tension for all three parties, making the eventual visits of Durrance to his oculist in London a source of profound relief. These trips multiplied in frequency and duration. Even Ethne felt gratitude; she could momentarily discard her mask, allowing herself exhaustion and solitude to gather the strength to resume her high spirits upon his return. There were hours when despair seized her. "Will I be able to sustain this pretense when we are married, when we are constantly together?" she questioned internally. But she pushed the inquiry back, unanswered, daring not to look ahead lest her fortitude fail.

Following the third visit, Durrance addressed her: "Do you recall that I once mentioned a renowned oculist in Wiesbaden? It seems advisable that I consult him."

"You are being recommended to go?"

"Yes, and to go alone."

Ethne looked up, her eyes sharp and quick. "You think I would be dull company in Wiesbaden?" she retorted. "There is no cause for fear. I can summon a relative to accompany me."

"No; it is for my own sake," Durrance answered firmly. "I may need to enter a home. It is preferable to remain entirely quiet and avoid seeing anyone for a time."

"You are certain?" Ethne asked. "It would wound me if I perceived you proposing this plan because you believe I would be happier at Glenalla."
