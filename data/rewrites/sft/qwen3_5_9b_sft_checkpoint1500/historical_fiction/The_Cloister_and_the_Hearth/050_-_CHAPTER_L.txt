Among strangers, Margaret Brandt found a comparative happiness, until a new and unexpected source of contentment arose. A civic dignitary, plagued by an illness and fanciful in equal measure, sought physician after physician in desperation. Having reached the gates of death, he summoned Peter. Finding the patient reduced to nothing by excessive bleeding and purging, Peter flung a battalion of bottles from the window and left them standing wide open. Instead, he beat egg yolks in neat Schiedam wine, administered them in small doses, followed by meat stewed in red water infused with mild febrifugal herbs. Slowly, the patient recovered, moving about looking somewhat like a man encased in a pillowcase. Being a voluble man, the dignitary spread Peter's fame through every street, granting the artist, who had long deserved a reputation in vain, a rapid rise by sheer luck.

Things appeared bright. The old man's pride was cheered, and his purse began to fill. Yet he squandered much of his gain on sovereign herbs and choice drugs, intending to invest them all in medicine. It was Margaret who intercepted a portion, effectively "white-mailing" it from his grasp. However, the victory came too late; the happy excitement proved fatal.

One evening, bidding her good night, his voice sounded strangely inarticulate. By the next morning, he lay speechless, barely sensible. Margaret, who had been her father's attentive pupil for years, immediately recognized the symptoms of a paralytic stroke. Trusting no one, she ran for a doctor. One who had previously been obstructed by Peter came, cheerfully confirming her fears. He advocated for bleeding the patient, a procedure she firmly declined. "He was always against bleeding," she argued, "especially the elderly." Peter survived, but he was never the same man again. His memory became severely impaired, rendering him untrustworthy to prescribe, leaving several patients awaiting his convalescence with uncertainty. Misery stared her in the face. Resolved to seek advice and comfort, she turned to her cousin, William Johnson, from whom she had hitherto kept aloof due to pride and poverty.

She found William and his servant sitting in the same room, neither the better for liquor. Mastering her surprise, she greeted them and announced her intention to discuss a family matter, glancing quietly at the servant to signal the sensitivity of the topic. The woman took the hint, but not as expected.

"Oh, you can speak before me, can she not, my old man?"

At this familiarity, Margaret turned crimson. "I cry you mercy, mistress. I knew not my cousin had fallen into the customs of this town. Well, I must take a fitter opportunity," she said, rising to depart.

"I wot not what ye mean by custom o' the town," the woman replied, bouncing up. "But this I know; 'tis the part of a faithful servant to keep her master from being preyed on by his beggarly kin."

Margaret retorted sharply, "Ye are too modest, mistress. Ye are no servant. Your speech betrays you. 'Tis not till the ape hath mounted the tree that she shows her tail so plain. Nay, there sits the servant; God help him! And while so it is, fear not thou his kin will ever be so poor in spirit as to come where the likes of you can flout their dole." Casting a look of mute reproach at her cousin for remaining passive and silent, she turned and walked out haughtily, refusing to shed a single tear until she reached home.

Now, two men needed lodging and feeding by one pregnant girl, with another mouth destined to enter the world. Yet, though the unborn child was the most helpless of all, it would prove their best friend. Nature was strong in Margaret Brandt—that same instinct which makes beasts, birds, and insects so cunning in providing food and shelter for their progeny. Stimulated by nature, she sat and brooded, thinking intensely on how to forestall destitution. Though she still possessed five gold pieces, she foresaw starvation approaching with inevitable footsteps.

Her sex, when deviating from custom to think with male intensity, reasoned with equal purpose. She rose, ordered Martin to move Peter to another room, tidied her own chamber with meticulous care, polished a glass globe, suspended it from the ceiling, dusted a crocodile figure, and nailed it to the exterior wall. After instructing Martin, she set him to act as a lounging sentinel at the street door, telling the "crocodile-bitten" visitors that a great, aged, and learned alchemist resided within, who, in his moments of recreation, sometimes amused himself by curing mortal diseases.

Patients soon arrived, demanding to see the leech. "That might not be," Margaret declared. "He is deep in his studies, searching for the grand elixir, and not even princes have speech of him. You must tell me your symptoms, and return in two hours." With mysterious powers, when they returned, the drug or draught was invariably ready. For worshipful patients, she would scan their faces, feel their pulses, and listen to their stories, then retreat to Peter's room to consider how her father, whose system she had long observed, would treat the case. She would write an illegible scrawl with cabalistic letters, bring it down reverently, and ask, "Could you read that?" Usually, the answer was, "I am no reader," or, with admiration, "Nay, mistress, nought can I make of it."

"Ay, but I can. 'Tis sovereign. Look on thyself as cured!" If she had the materials, she would sometimes let the patient see her compound the mixture, anxiously consulting a sacred prescription lest science suffer in her hands. Thus, she sent them away relieved of cash but filled with medicine and faith, humbugged to their hearts' content. *Populus vult decipi.* When they departed, she would take down two little boxes Gerard had made her. On one she wrote "To-day," and on the other "To-morrow," placing smaller coins in the former and larger sums in the latter, alongside whatever gold pieces had survived the journey from Sevenbergen and the expenses of housekeeping in a strange land. In this manner, she met current expenses and laid by for the rainy day she saw coming, mixing drugs with simples and vice with virtue. On this last score, her conscience pricked her sore. After each day's comedy, she knelt and prayed God to forgive her "for the sake of her child." But lo and behold, cures were reported to her; thus, her conscience began to harden.

Martin Wittenhaagen had lately become a dead weight on her hands. Like most men who had endured great hardships, he had stiffened suddenly. Though less supple, he was as strong as ever, capable of carrying the doctor herself around Rotterdam at his own pace. He carried her slops instead. In this new business, he displayed the qualities of a soldier: unreasoning obedience, punctuality, accuracy, dispatch, and drunkenness. He fell among "good fellows"; the blackguards plied him with Schiedam, and he babbled and bragged.

Doctor Margaret had risen very high in his estimation. All this brandishing of a crocodile for a standard, ambushing a dotard, disposing of slops, and taking good money in exchange struck him not as science but as strategy. He boasted in his cups and before a mixed company how "me and my General we are a biting of the burghers."

When this revelation had time to leaven the city, his General, Doctor Margaret, received a summons from the constables. They took her, trembling and begging subordinate machines to forgive her before the burgomaster. Beside him stood real physicians, a terrible row, in long robes and square caps, accusing her of practicing unlawfully on the bodies of the Duke's lieges. At first, she was too frightened to speak. A novice like herself, the mere name of "Law" paralyzed her. But questioned closely, though not so harshly as if she had been ugly, she told the truth. She had long been her father's pupil and had merely followed his system, curing many. "And it is not for myself, in very deed, sirs," she pleaded, "but I have two poor helpless honest men at home upon my hands, and how else can I keep them? Ah, good sirs, let a poor girl make her bread honestly; ye hinder them not to make it idly and shamefully. And oh, sirs, ye are husbands, ye are fathers; ye cannot but see I have reason to work and provide as best I may." Ere this woman's appeal had left her lips, she would have given the world to recall it, standing with one hand upon her heart and one before her face, hiding it, but not the tears that trickled beneath. All went to the wrong address. Perhaps a female bailiff might have yielded to such arguments, permitting her to practice medicine and break the law until her child was weaned.

"What have we to do with that," said the burgomaster, "save and except that if thou wilt pledge thyself to break the law no more, I will remit the imprisonment, and exact but the fine?"

On this, Doctor Margaret clasped her hands together and vowed most penitently never, never, never to cure body or beast again. Dismissed with the constables to pay the fine, she turned at the door, curtsied, and thanked the gentlemen for their forbearance.
