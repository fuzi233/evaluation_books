The air in the theater was thick with the scent of rosin, sweat, and the metallic tang of anticipation. It was a small, claustrophobic world where the only law was the rhythm of the curtain rising and falling. Outside, the city of London hummed with a restless, hungry energy, desperate for a hero to fill the vacuum left by failures in France. The Duke of Marlborough had proven himself a shadow of his ancestor; Lord George Sackville's genius had been extinguished at Minden. The nation craved a general who could turn the abstract desire for glory into concrete victory. Into this vacuum stepped James Wolfe, returning from America, a man whose name was already being whispered as destiny.

Wolfe had been promoted to Colonel, a second battalion forged in the fires of Cape Breton. Harry, the Virginian, approached his new commander with a deference that was genuine, stripped of the theatrical posturing common among peers. To Wolfe, Harry was not just a subordinate; he was a mirror reflecting the romantic simplicity that defined the era's best soldiers. Wolfe possessed a transparency that bordered on naivety. He spoke of battles as if recounting personal confessions, his heart laid bare on the table of conversation. While the polished circles of St. James's might have sneered at such candor, within their private orbit, Wolfe was revered. He was a boy grown large, intoxicated by music and poetry, a man who claimed on his final day that writing Gray's Elegy held more value than winning a battle.

This literary inclination drew him inevitably to George. Both men were lovers, both seekers of happiness, bound by a shared hunger for sentiment. Their conversations, lost to history, must have been rich with the texture of their souls. Warrington, observing this dynamic from afar, wrote of Wolfe's "glorious bravery," noting how the General hungered for laurels to offer his mistress. To Warrington, Wolfe was not merely a soldier fulfilling duty; he was a knight charging dragons, a figure of chivalric ardour that contrasted sharply with the stoic, Roman-like constancy of the Virginian.

While Wolfe and Lambert navigated the political currents of London, the quieter drama unfolded in the domestic sphere. Warrington's family remained rooted in the rural quiet of Southampton Row, finding solace in the gardens of Bedford. Yet, the pull of the city was irresistible. Warrington visited Castlewood briefly, stepping into rooms once occupied by Colonel Esmond, but his true allegiance lay elsewhere. He preferred the vibrant chaos of London, specifically the company of a young woman whose presence offered a pleasure the aristocratic circle of Lady Maria could not replicate.

Lady Maria, gracious and enchanted, listened to the tragedies read aloud by George and Parson Sampson. It was the Chaplain's enthusiasm, however, that propelled Warrington's play, *Carpezan*, onto the boards. Though Mr. Johnson had attempted to secure a venue at Drury Lane, the competition with Thomas Holcroft's *Douglas* forced a shift. The play found its home at Covent Garden under the management of Mr. Rich.

The night of the premiere was a convergence of fate and fortune. Warrington hosted an elegant gathering at the Bedford Head, a prelude to the journey to the theatre. As the crowd spilled into the house, the atmosphere crackled with nervous excitement. Theo, Warrington's daughter, sat in the box, her heart fluttering with a mixture of pride and dread. Harry, watching from the shadows, was pale, his complexion draining of color until he matched the chalk-white intensity of the lead actor, Barry.

Barry commanded the stage as Carpezan, delivering lines of terror and pathos that silenced the audience. The role of the King of Bohemia fell to a young Dubliner, Hagan, whose grace and elegance won immediate acclaim. There were moments of friction—the introduction of an elephant in the Janissary march, a whimsical touch that confused the historical gravity—but the core of the tragedy held firm. The execution of Sybilla was the pivot point. With her head severed, the tension broke. Friends in the box exhaled, a collective breath released after holding it for hours. Messengers raced through the coffee-house, announcing the triumph.

The applause was deafening. Barry declared the play worthy of repetition, crediting it to a young gentleman from Virginia. In the box, Theo's anxiety transformed into radiant sparks in her eyes. Harry, usually composed, erupted in cheers, his voice joining the roar of the crowd alongside Wolfe and Lambert. Even Mrs. Lambert, typically reserved, wept openly. Her daughter Hetty mocked the tears, claiming the villains deserved their fate, yet the girl herself was swept up in the delirium, sharing the joy with the children and the servants alike.

The celebration continued long past the closing of the curtains. Suppers were held at the Star and Garter and the Bedford Head, fueled by wine and the intoxicating belief in a masterpiece. Toasts were raised, and Lady Maria, in a moment of inspired gallantry, proposed "The King of Hungary." The actor playing the role, a man of fire and eloquence, responded with a flourish, vowing eternal service to her ladyship, regardless of his mortality. His gestures were excessive, bordering on the ridiculous, fueled by punch and the heat of the moment. Theo and her sisters, inexperienced in such displays, recoiled slightly, but Lady Maria absorbed the energy with practiced ease.

By midnight, the festivities wound down. Coaches waited to carry the ladies home. The men lingered, exhausted but exhilarated. The King of Hungary retired with a pounding headache, and the Poet, Warrington, found himself astonished the following morning by the arrival of half a dozen guests he had barely seen the night before, eager to repeat the experience.

The aftermath settled into a new normal. As Mrs. Lambert prepared to send her daughters home, the household buzzed with the echoes of the play. George remained close to Theo, discussing the nuances of the performance, the lighting, the timing. Mrs. Lambert, wary of the dangers lurking in the greenroom, urged caution. George smiled, revealing a secret talisman in his gaze—a look fixed on Theo's face that suggested she alone could ward off all peril.

"Why should he be afraid?" Theo asked, her innocence untouched by the cynicism of the world.

Her mother kissed her, dismissing the fear. Hetty, however, seized upon the opportunity to mock, suggesting the actress was old enough to be George's mother. The rebuke was sharp, maternal, invoking the fifth commandment and the sanctity of age. George, in a display of sentimental affection, declared his mother's age to be ideal.

Yet beneath the surface of this polite exchange ran a strange current. Passion had a way of twisting logic. Before his marriage, a man might find himself strangely attached to his future mother-in-law. Mrs. Lambert treated Theo with a tenderness that bordered on obsession, dressing her carefully, questioning her constantly about George's appearance, his health, his fatigue. She imagined nursing him with gruel, sending recipes for his recovery. When he was absent, she spoke of him incessantly to her daughter.

Theo, in turn, became a vessel for this attention. Whenever George entered the room, she was subtly displaced, told to attend to him while she retreated. She whispered instructions to servants and children outside the door, creating a network of surveillance around the young couple. When she reappeared, George maintained a respectful distance, except for one fleeting moment when he stooped to retrieve her dropped scissors. In that brief contact, a blush colored her cheeks. Was it shame? Or was it the natural blooming of youth? Mrs. Lambert, blind to the significance of the flush, sat down to her work, her heart full of happiness, unaware that the tragedy had ended, but the farce of courtship had only just begun.
