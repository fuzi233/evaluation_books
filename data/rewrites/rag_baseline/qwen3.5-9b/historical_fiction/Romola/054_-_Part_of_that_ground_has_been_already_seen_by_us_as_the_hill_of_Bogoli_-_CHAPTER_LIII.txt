"I would speak with you," Baldassarre said, his voice cutting through the quiet as Romola regarded him in silent expectation. It was evident he had trailed her, waiting for this exact moment. She was finally poised to uncover the secret that haunted him. "Yes," she replied, offering the same hollow submission one might display under an imposed penance. "But you wish to go where no ears can catch us?" "Where *he* will not intercept us," Baldassarre corrected, turning to glance nervously over his shoulder. "Out—in the open air—far from the streets." "I occasionally visit San Miniato at this hour," Romola stated. "If you prefer, I shall lead the way now; you may follow. The distance ensures solitude." He nodded, and Romola moved out. To lesser minds, venturing to a secluded spot with a man bearing the outward marks of the madness Tito had diagnosed would seem reckless. Yet Romola possessed no such personal timidity; she welcomed the buffer of distance, delaying the inevitable impact of whatever blow awaited her.

The afternoon had waned, the sun dipping low in the western sky, casting long shadows when Romola halted on rough terrain beneath the cypress trunks, scanning for Baldassarre. He was close, yet upon reaching her, he collapsed onto the stony ledge. The robust vigor that had defined him since his initial appearance with the rope in the Duomo was gone. Even the transient tremor of climbing the hill had drained his eyes, rendering them vacant and helpless. "The ascent is steep," Romola observed, her tone soft with compassion as she sat beside him. "And I suspect you have been ravaged by want?" He turned, fixing his gaze upon her in silence, paralyzed by the inability to formulate the words required to express his truth. She remained rigid, terrified that any movement might signal impatience. In this moment, he resembled nothing more than a discarded, common-born elder. Yet Romola was accustomed to proximity with such figures, acutely aware of their burdens. Slowly, his stare coalesced into focus. "Ah!" he burst out with sudden intensity. "You would have been my daughter!"

A crimson flash swept across Romola's face, receding instantly to leave her lips parted in a pale, marble-like horror. The revelation struck her mind with the force of a physical blow. She deduced the facts underlying that singular phrase, and in that first heartbeat, the impulsive belief born of her intimate knowledge of Tito's nature surged unchecked. Her visceral reaction served as fuel for Baldassarre; for the first time, his words found purchase. He seized her arm, his grip tightening with gathering desperation. "You possess noble blood—is it not true? You attend the preacher; you despise baseness—the kind that smiles and triumphs. Do you hate your husband?" "Oh God! Were you truly his father?" Romola whispered, her voice lost in the ghost-images of the past, oblivious to his query. "Or did he lie? Did you claim him when he was small?" "Ah, you believe me! You understand his essence!" Baldassarre cried, exultant, pressing his hand harder against her arm as if tactile contact could anchor his fading sanity. "Will you aid me?" "Yes," Romola answered, misinterpreting his intent. She placed her palm gently over his rough fingers, tears welling as she gazed at him. "Oh, it is pitiful! Speak—were you a great scholar? Did you teach him? What became of it?" She faltered. Tito's accusation of insanity echoed in her mind, and where were the traces of former refinement? Still, she maintained her composure, refusing to withdraw her hand. She sat perfectly still, bracing herself to listen with heightened caution.

"It is gone! All of it!" Baldassarre declared. "They refused to believe me because he lied, branding me mad, and had me dragged to prison. I am old; my mind will not return. The world conspires against me." He paused, his eyes sinking under a wave of despair, before lifting them again with renewed fervor. "But *you* stand with me. He manipulated you into loving him, yet he betrayed you; you hate him. Yes, he manipulated *me*: he was beautiful and gentle, while I was a lonely man. I claimed him when they beat him. He slept in my bosom as an infant; I watched his growth, imparting all my knowledge, intending to pass everything I owned to him. I possessed wealth, books, jewels. He took my jewels and sold them; he abandoned me to slavery. He never sought me out. When I returned, destitute and broken, he denied me. He called me a lunatic." "He told us his father was drowned," Romola murmured faintly. "He must have believed it then. Oh! He could not have been so vile *then*!" A vision rose of Tito in those early days, innocent in her eyes, regarding no evil greater than poison in flowers. The yearning regret of that memory offered a fleeting respite from the horror. With a great sob, tears flooded her eyes. "Ah, you are young, and tears flow easily," Baldassarre remarked, with a hint of impatience. "But tears extinguish the inner fire; only the fire accomplishes anything. Tears will hinder us. Listen." Romola shifted slightly. The specter of his madness flickered through her mind, checking the tide of belief. Was this merely a deranged assassin? Yet her deep conviction in his story outweighed her fear; she avoided risking pain to him by displaying doubt. "Tell me," she asked gently, "how did you lose your memory, your scholarship?" "I fell ill. I cannot measure the duration—it was a void. I recall nothing until I sat in the sun among the stones, surrounded by darkness. Slowly, gradually, I felt a longing for something undefined that never arrived. Upon the ship, amidst the waters, I realized what I craved: the return of the Boy, the restoration of my thoughts. I was locked outside them all. And I remain outside now. I perceive only walls and darkness." Baldassarre drifted into reverie, burying his head in his hands, and Romola's faith in him submerged all prudent hesitation. The pity in her voice resonated like an old wound reopening. Did she not witness daily how her own father mourned Dino and the future he had envisioned?

"It all returned once," Baldassarre continued. "I mastered everything. I saw the world anew, my gems, my books; I believed I held him in thrall. I went to expose him where the lights burned and the trees stood tall. He lied again, calling me mad, and they dragged me to prison... Wickedness is formidable; he wears armor." Fierceness flared within him once more. He spoke with his former intensity, seizing her arm again. "But you will assist me? He has betrayed you too. He keeps another wife, and she bears children. He convinces her he is her husband; she is a foolish, helpless creature. I will reveal her dwelling." The first shock rippling through Romola was pure indignation. The woman's sense of insult surged to the forefront. Baldassarre instinctively sensed her solidarity. "You hate him," he pressed. "Is it not true? No love exists between you. I know women can hate; you possess proud blood. You detest falseness, and you crave revenge." Romola sat paralyzed by the collision of emotions, unaware of the bruising grip on her tender arm. "You shall devise it," Baldassarre whispered urgently. "I have memorized that you are his rightful wife. You are a noble woman. You frequent the preacher of vengeance; you will champion justice. But you must think for me. My mind fails—everything slips away save the fire. The fire is God; it is justice; it cannot die. You believe this, do you not? If they refuse to hang him for stealing from me, you must strip him of his armor. You will make him defenseless, and I shall strike him. I carry a knife, and my arm retains strength." He slid his hand beneath his tunic, tracing the hidden blade absently, needing the sensation to sustain his resolve. To Romola, every passing hour promised increasing difficulty. Her judgment was too sharp, too rapid, to waste breath on futile deprecation toward a man in Baldassarre's state. She chose silence, seeking to buy time for his agitation to subside by pivoting to a question of genuine interest. Her voice trembled slightly. "You say she is foolish and helpless—that other wife—who believes him to be her true husband. Perhaps he is: perhaps he wed her before marrying me." "I cannot say," Baldassarre replied, pausing in his ritual of stroking the knife, his expression bewildered. "My memories end there. I only know her location. You shall see her. I will escort you; but not today," he added hastily, "*he* may be present. Night falls." "It is true," Romola started, suddenly aware that sunset had swallowed the hills in gloom. "But you will come and fetch me—when?" "Tomorrow morning," Baldassarre dreamt, assuming she shared his urgency for vengeance. "Then come to me where you met me today, in the church.
