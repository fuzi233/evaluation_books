Looking across the fire toward my accustomed chair—the beloved partner of my hearth during the latter half of my life—I often find myself asking, for middle-aged gentlemen possess the distinct privilege of recycling their jokes, inquiries, and anecdotes: "Were two young people ever more foolish and imprudent than we were when we wed, in the year of the late King's death?"

My son, who has recently taken prodigious leaps in the heat of fox-hunting, tells me that he surveys the gaps and rivers he once crossed with terrifying ease, now filled with a profound sense of astonishment at his own foolhardiness in making such desperate ventures. Yet, there is no more eager sportsman in the two counties than Miles. He loves his amusement so passionately that he cares for nothing else. He has broken his collarbone and suffered a hundred tumbles (to his mother's eternal terror); but so has his father—perhaps thinking of a copy of verse or his speech at the Quarter Sessions—who was thrown over his old mare's head when she slipped on a stone while they dreamed along a park road at four miles an hour. Miles's reckless sport has been the delight of his life, just as my marriage has been the blessing of mine; and I never think of it but to thank Heaven.

Mind, I do not set up my worship as an example. I certainly do not tell all young folks, "Go and marry upon twopence a year"; otherwise, people would look very black at me at our vestry meetings. But my wife is known to be a desperate matchmaker. When Hodge and Susan appear in my justice room talking allowances, we urge them to spend their half-crown a week at home, add a little contribution of our own, and send for the vicar.

Now, when I pose a question to my dear oracle, I know exactly what the answer will be; hence, no doubt, the reason I so frequently consult her. I have but to wear a particular expression of face, and my Diana instantly takes her reflection from it. Suppose I say, "My dear, don't you think the moon was made of cream cheese tonight?" She will reply, "Well, Papa, it did look very much like cream cheese, indeed—you're nobody like you for droll similes." Or, suppose I say, "My love, Mr. Pitt's speech was very fine, but I don't think he equals what I remember of his father." "Nobody was equal to my Lord Chatham," says my wife. And then one of the girls cries out, "Why, I've often heard our Papa say Lord Chatham was a charlatan!" Upon which Mamma remarks, "How like she is to her Aunt Hetty!"

As for Miles, *Tros Tyriusve* is all one to him. He only reads the sporting announcements in the Norwich paper. So long as there is good scent, he does not care about the state of the country. I believe the rascal has never read my poems, much less my tragedies (for I mentioned *Pocahontas* to him the other day, and the dunce thought she was merely a river in Virginia). With respect to my Latin verses, how can he possibly understand them when I know he cannot construe Corderius? Why, this notebook lies publicly on the little table at my corner of the fireside, and anyone may read it who takes the trouble to lift my spectacles off the cover; yet Miles never has. I insert caricatures of Miles, jokes against him, in the loose pages—but he never knows nor heeds them.

Only once, in place of a neat drawing of mine in Chinese ink representing Miles asleep after dinner—a work my friend Bunbury would not disown—I found a rude picture of myself going over my mare Sultana's head, entitled "The Squire on Horseback, or Fish Out of Water." The fellow roared with laughter, and all the girls tittered when I came upon the page! My wife said she was never in such a fright as when I went to my book. But I can bear a joke against myself, and have heard many, though (strange to say, for one who has lived among some of the chief wits of the age) I never heard a truly good one in my life.

Never mind, Miles, though thou art not a wit, I love thee none the worse (there never was any love lost between two wits in a family). Though thou hast no great beauty, thy mother thinks thee as handsome as Apollo—or his Royal Highness the Prince of Wales, who was born in the very same year as thee. Indeed, she always finds Coates's picture of the Prince remarkably like her eldest boy and keeps the print in her dressing-room to this very day. *[Note, in a female hand: "My son is not a spendthrift, nor a breaker of women's hearts, as some gentlemen are; but that he was exceedingly like H.R.H. when they were both babies is most certain, the Duchess of Anester having herself remarked him in St. James's Park, where Gumbo and my poor Molly used often to take him for an airing. Th. W."]*

In that same year, with what different prospects! My Lord Esmond, Lord Castlewood's son, likewise appeared to adorn the world. At that time, My Lord C. and his humble servant had already come to a coolness, and heaven knows! my honest Miles's godmother brought no gold pap-boats to his christening! Matters have mended since, *laus Deo*—*laus Deo*, indeed! For I suspect neither Miles nor his father would ever have been able to accomplish much for themselves, or by their own wits.

Castlewood House has quite a different face now from that venerable one which it wore in the days of my youth, when it was covered with the wrinkles of time, the scars of old wars, and the cracks and blemishes which years had marked on its hoary features. I love best to remember it in its old shape, as I saw it when young Mr. George Warrington went down at the owner's invitation to be present at his Lordship's marriage with Miss Lydia Van den Bosch—"an American lady of noble family of Holland," as the county paper announced her ladyship to be. Then the towers stood as Warrington's grandfather, the Colonel (whom Madam Esmond would like to call her father, the Marquis), had seen them. The woods, thinned not a little to be sure, still stood; nay, some of the very same rooks may have cawed over them which the Colonel had seen sixty years prior. His picture hung in the hall which might have been his, had he not preferred love and gratitude to wealth and worldly honor.

Mr. George Esmond Warrington—that is, *Ego Met Ipse* who writes this page—as he walked the old place, pacing the long corridors, the smooth dew-spangled terraces, and the cool, darkling avenues, felt for a while as if he were one of Mr. Walpole's cavaliers with ruff, rapier, buff-coat, and gorget. He felt as if an Old Pretender or a Jesuit emissary in disguise might appear from behind any tall tree-trunk around the mansion or any antique carved cupboard within it. I had the strangest, saddest, pleasantest, old-world fancies as I walked the place; I imagined tragedies, intrigues, serenades, escalades, Oliver Cromwell's Roundheads battering the towers, or bluff Hal's Beefeaters pricking over the plain before the castle.

I was then courting a certain young lady (Madam, your ladyship's eyes had no need of spectacles then, and on the brow above them there was never a wrinkle or a silver hair). I remember writing a ream of romantic description, under my Lord Castlewood's franks, to the lady who never tired of reading my letters then. She says I only send her three lines now when I am away in London or elsewhere. "'Tis that I may not fatigue your old eyes, my dear!"

Mr. Warrington thought himself authorized to order a genteel new suit of clothes for my Lord's marriage. With Monsieur Gumbo in attendance, he made his appearance at Castlewood a few days before the ceremony. I may mention that it had become expedient to send my faithful Sady home on board a Virginia ship. A great inflammation attacking the throat and lungs, proving fatal in many cases in that year of Wolfe's expedition, had seized and well-nigh killed my poor lad, for whom his native air was pronounced to be the best cure. We parted with an abundance of tears, and Gumbo shed as many when his master went to Quebec; but he had attractions in this country and none for the military life, so he remained attached to my service.

We found Castlewood House full of friends, relations, and visitors. Lady Fanny was there upon compulsion, a sulky bridesmaid. Some of the virgins of the neighborhood also attended the young Countess. A bishop's widow herself, the Baroness Beatrix, brought a holy brother-in-law of the bench from London to tie the holy knot of matrimony between Eugene, Earl of Castlewood, and Lydia Van den Bosch, spinster. For some time before and after the nuptials, the old house in Hampshire wore an appearance of gaiety to which it had long been unaccustomed. The country families came gladly to pay their compliments to the newly married couple. The lady's wealth was the subject of everybody's talk, and no doubt did not decrease in the telling.
