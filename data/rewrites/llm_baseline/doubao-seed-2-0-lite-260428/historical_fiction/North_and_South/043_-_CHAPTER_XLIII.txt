“The meanest thing to which we bid adieu,
Loses its meanness in the parting hour.”

Mrs. Shaw conceived a vehement dislike of Milton as fierce as her gentle, sheltered nature was capable of feeling. The air hummed unceasingly with the clank of looms, reeked of coal smoke that stained fine lace brown by midday; the poor folk in the streets were grimed with mill dust, the wealthy ladies over-dressed in stiff silk that looked absurdly out of place amid the grime, and not a single man she saw, high or low, wore a coat that hung straight and true across his shoulders. She was certain Margaret would never recover her faded, post-illness strength while trapped in that smoky chaos, and she herself could feel the old nervous, fluttering weakness creeping up on her, the kind that only quiet Harley Street drawing rooms could soothe. Margaret must return to London with her at once, no delays. Worn thin by grief and exhaustion, Margaret at last yielded, promising that as soon as the Wednesday of her father’s funeral was past, she would prepare to leave, leaving Dixon behind to settle all outstanding bills, sell the bulk of the household furniture, and lock up the Hale house for good.

That mournful Wednesday saw Mr. Hale laid to rest far from the sun-dappled Helstone parsonage he had loved, and far from Mrs. Hale’s lonely grave in the southern county where she had died — a loss that gnawed at Margaret most of all, for she blamed the heavy, numb stupor that had swallowed her whole in the first days after his death for robbing her of the chance to arrange to bury him beside his wife. Before that Wednesday even dawned, a letter arrived from Mr. Bell.
> I had meant to return to Milton on Thursday, but unluckily our little band of Plymouth Fellows has been called to one of our rare mandatory duties, and I cannot absent myself from my post. Captain Lennox and Mr. Thornton are staying with me here. The Captain seems a sharp, well-meaning sort, and has volunteered to travel to Milton to help you search for your father’s will — though of course there is no such will, or you would have found it long ago if you followed my directions. He swears he will escort you and his mother-in-law back to town, and given Edith’s delicate condition, I cannot see how you can reasonably expect him to stay away later than Friday. Your Dixon is a reliable woman; she can hold down the house, and keep Mrs. Shaw calm, until I arrive. I will hand all legal matters over to my Milton solicitor if no will turns up, for I doubt that fine, moustachioed Captain is much of a hand at business, splendid as his facial hair may be.
> There will have to be a sale of most of the furniture, so mark out what pieces you wish reserved, or send me a list later. Two last things, and I am done. You know (or if you did not, your poor father did) that all my money and goods will be yours when I die. Not that I mean to die any time soon, mind — I only say this to explain what follows. The Lennoxes seem very fond of you now, but that fondness may wear thin, and it is best to have a formal agreement in place: you will pay them two hundred and fifty pounds a year for your board and lodging, which includes Dixon’s wages too — do not let anyone cajole you into paying more for her service. That way, if the Captain ever decides he wants his house to himself, you will not be cast adrift penniless: you can take your annuity and Dixon, and set up a little home of your own, or come to live with me and keep my house, whichever you prefer.
> I know you must be fuming right now, wondering what right this old man has to meddle in your affairs so cavalierly. But I have every right. I loved your father for thirty-five years; I stood beside him on his wedding day; I closed his eyes in death. I am your godfather, and I know full well I cannot help you much in spiritual matters, for you are far more devout than I will ever be. So I will do what little I can to look after your material comfort. I have not a single living relation on earth; “who is there to mourn for Adam Bell?” The whole of my heart is set on this, and Margaret Hale is not the girl to say me nay. Write me two lines by return post to confirm your answer. No thanks, mind.

Margaret picked up her pen, her hand trembling, and scrawled across the paper: “Margaret Hale is not the girl to say him nay.” She was vexed at repeating his exact phrasing, but she was too weak to frame any other words, so exhausted by the tiny exertion that she had to lie back down on the sofa the second she set down the pen, too tired to think. Her aunt fussed over her, convinced that the moment they escaped Milton’s foul air, Margaret would be her old self again. Margaret would only reply that she had no choice but to stay, for she could not leave her parents behind, and that Milton could be very pretty in summer, before she closed her eyes to end the conversation.

Mrs. Shaw filled the house with small comforts: fires lit in every bedroom at dusk, soft jellies and sweetmeats brought from the finest confectioner in town, thick wool blankets draped over every sofa. Margaret barely noticed any of it, only feeling quiet gratitude for the trouble her aunt went to on her behalf. Restless even in her weakness, she wandered from room to room all day, sorting through her belongings to pick what she would take with her. Dixon followed at her heels, per Mrs. Shaw’s orders, prattling nonstop about trivial things to distract her from her grief. Margaret set aside all her father’s books to send to Mr. Bell, for she knew he would value them both for their own sake and for Mr. Hale’s. Then she wrapped up a small, well-loved volume, scribbled a short note to Mr. Thornton asking him to keep it in memory of her father, and bade Dixon deliver it after she was gone. She wandered on, touching every worn, shabby relic from Helstone with soft, lingering fingers, loath to leave them behind, until by evening she was so tired she slept more deeply than she had in the days since her father’s death.

The next morning at breakfast, Margaret said she wished to go say goodbye to a few friends before they left. Mrs. Shaw objected at first, but finally agreed to accompany her, bringing her own maid along to carry shawls and air cushions. Margaret’s first stop was the narrow, dingy court where Nicholas Higgins lived; she was half relieved when Mrs. Shaw, fussing about lurking fevers and damp washing strung across the walkway, decided to wait in the carriage rather than get out. Margaret stepped inside to find Nicholas out at work, only Mary Boucher and the younger children at home. Mary burst into loud, unreserved sobs the second she realised Margaret was leaving Milton forever, and Margaret could do little but pat her shoulder, stammer vague promises of meeting again one day, and ask her to tell Higgins to come visit her at the house before she left. When she asked for a small memento of Bessy, she picked out the chipped fourpenny mug that had sat by Bessy’s bedside throughout her illness, to Mary’s confusion, who tried to press a finer, more expensive keepsake on her. Margaret would not be moved, and left the girl glowing with the quiet joy of having given her something she truly wanted.

Next they called at the Thorntons’ house. Mrs. Shaw got out of the carriage, huddling deep in her shawl in the newly lit drawing room, and complained that the room was icy cold. Mrs. Thornton entered a few minutes later, her usual sharp expression softened at the sight of Margaret’s pale, tear-swollen face and the tremor in her voice as she tried to speak steadily. Margaret apologized for her rudeness the last time they had met, and said she knew Mrs. Thornton had acted out of good intentions, no matter how much they had misunderstood each other. For once, Mrs. Thornton’s hardened reserve cracked, and she agreed she would believe Margaret’s word that she had never acted improperly, even without an explanation. Mrs. Shaw then gushed that if the Thorntons ever visited London, they would be more than welcome in her Harley Street home, never realizing that the “Mr. Thornton” she referenced as a separate stranger was the tall, dark man who just stepped through the doorway, still wearing his fresh mourning suit fresh from Oxford.

Mr. Thornton froze. He did not sit down, pretending to study papers scattered on the table, so lost in his own tangled thoughts he barely registered that the two guests were preparing to take their leave. He jumped up at the last moment to escort Mrs. Shaw back to the carriage, and for a moment he and Margaret stood alone on the doorstep, both thinking of the day of the riot, of the passionate words she had spoken the next day that had haunted him ever since. His heart swelled with longing, but he steeled himself: he had made his declaration once, and lost his chance. He said goodbye in a steady, unemotional tone, took her outstretched hand for a second as if it was a dry, withered flower, and turned away before she could speak another word. No one in his household saw him for the rest of the day.

Margaret was so utterly exhausted when they returned that her aunt and Dixon considered delaying the journey for a few days, but Margaret twisted in what looked like acute pain when the suggestion was made, begging them to leave as soon as possible: she could not bear to stay in Milton one unnecessary hour, she said, she wanted to forget. Captain Lennox arrived that evening, bringing cheerful, unpressured tales of Edith and the new baby, and his easy, unanxious company did Margaret far more good than all her aunt’s fussy fretting. Soon after, Higgins came by. He greeted her gruffly, saying he had been gutted when he heard Mr. Hale had died, that every mill worker in Milton knew the late parson was the kindest man who had ever set foot in the town. He laughed and said Mr. Thornton had told him Margaret was off to London to be a grand lady, but he had sworn he would walk all the way to the capital next Whitsuntide if he had to, just to say goodbye. Margaret handed him her father’s well-worn Bible, bidding him keep it and read it for Mr. Hale’s sake, then pressed a handful of coins into his hand for the Boucher children. Higgins protested he would not take charity, but Margaret laughed and said none of it was for him, it was all for the little ones, and he had no right to refuse it for their sakes. He bowed his head, muttering a dozen quiet blessings on her, and that was the end of Margaret’s life in Milton.
