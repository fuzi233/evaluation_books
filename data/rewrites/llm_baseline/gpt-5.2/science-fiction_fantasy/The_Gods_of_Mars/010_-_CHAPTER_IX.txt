Her confession—wrenched from her by fear as the vessel sank—struck me with a double edge: pity, and a sharp, private shame. I had given her some careless spark to nurse into flame, and now it burned her. I have never been skilled at the soft arts. Give me a blade, a broken door to hold, a plan to force—those are languages I speak without stumbling. But the language of a young woman’s hope, offered bare-handed in the dark? I found myself clumsy as a man wearing another’s boots.

So I did the only honest thing left, and did it badly.

I loosened her fingers from my neck, and while I still held her hands—warm, trembling, too alive for this steel room and its growing pressure—I told her of Dejah Thoris. I spoke her name as one speaks the name of bread after famine, of water after thirst: not to boast, not to wound, but because it was the one truth in me that would not bend. Of all the women of two worlds I had known, admired, fought beside, or served, she alone had taken my heart entire.

The tale did not soothe Phaidor.

She jerked from me as though my words had stung her skin. In an instant the frightened girl was gone and something fierce stood in her place—beautiful as a drawn sword, and as dangerous.

“Dog,” she breathed, and the word was not merely insult but verdict, flung with the cold precision of a knife into meat. “Dog of a blasphemer! Think you Phaidor, daughter of Matai Shang, supplicates? She commands. What is your puny outer-world passion for the vile creature you chose in your other life?

“I have glorified you with my love, and you have spurned me. Ten thousand deaths—unthinkably atrocious—could not atone for this affront. The thing you call Dejah Thoris shall die the most horrible of them all. You have sealed her warrant.

“And you—you shall be the meanest slave in the service of the goddess you have attempted to humiliate. Tortures and ignominies shall be heaped upon you until you grovel at my feet, begging for the boon of death.

“And then, in my gracious generosity, I shall grant it. From the high balcony of the Golden Cliffs I shall watch the great white apes tear you asunder.”

She spoke it as if reciting a household order—this first, then that, the course of a day’s work laid out from dawn to dusk. Only the work was agony, and the day ended in dismemberment. It was astonishing, how vengeance could be arranged with the neatness of a menu.

But she had forgotten one ingredient: where we were.

I pointed, without malice and without triumph, to the nearest port-hole. The water beyond it was not merely dark; it was alive with a slow, swirling blackness that pressed like a hand against glass. The reminder broke through her rage as suddenly as cold through thin cloth. She looked once—and crumpled, as if the bones in her had been removed. She fell to a low bench, buried her face in her arms, and sobbed—not as a goddess who had proclaimed doom, but as a very unhappy child who has realized that threats cannot stop a flood.

Down we went. Down, down, until the heavy glass warmed under the heat of the water without, and the air seemed thicker in my lungs. Then the descent ceased. The propellers took up their churn at the stern, and we were driven forward at speed through a darkness that the small port-lights could only bruise.

A powerful beam from the submarine’s nose cut the black, and in its cone I saw rock walls close on either side—a narrow, tube-like passage, raw stone smoothed by ancient currents. The sound of our passage became its own strange weather: the muffled throb of engines, the whispering scrape of water, the deep, bone-conducted vibration that made the teeth feel as though they remembered thunder.

Then the propellers stopped. We hung, still, as though the world were holding its breath. And at once we began to rise swiftly toward light.

We surfaced in a small subterranean vault. In the center lay the pool where the submarine floated as we had first seen her, only her black back showing, sleek as an animal’s. Around the pool ran a level platform, and above it the cave walls rose straight for a few feet before arching toward a low roof. The ledge was pierced by a number of entrances into dimly lit passageways, each mouth of tunnel exhaling its own damp, mineral breath.

Xodar entered with his men and gestured curtly. “Come.”

We were taken along one corridor and halted before a steel cage at the bottom of a shaft that rose above us so far the eye could not follow it. The cage was a common Barsoomian elevator car, operated by immense magnets suspended at the top. The thing had no ropes, no visible means of lift—only the unseen grip of controlled force. When the door clanged shut behind us and the current took, my stomach seemed to lag a heartbeat behind my body. In the low gravity of Mars there was little to resist the pull; the car shot upward with a sickening speed.

Almost as soon as we began we were slowing, so rapid had been our ascent, and then we emerged into a small structure that housed the upper terminus.

And stepped out into beauty so sudden it felt like being struck.

I have no words fit for it, and the languages of Earth men offer me none. Yet my senses remember, and that is a kind of vocabulary. The ground beneath my feet was a scarlet sward, soft as new-milled grain between the fingers. Trees rose on ivory stems and bore purple blossoms that seemed to drink light and give it back altered—richer, stranger. Walks wound through the gardens, paved not with humble stone but with crushed rubies, emerald, turquoise, and—yes—diamonds, as though someone had taken a jeweler’s tray and scattered it underfoot as casually as a cook strews salt.

There was a temple of burnished gold, hand-wrought with designs so delicate they seemed less hammered than grown. And the colors—there were rays there my eyes could register yet my mind could not name, hues without Earthly cousins, scintillations as if the air itself had been powdered with unknown gems. Even I, long accustomed to the barbaric splendors of Martian courts, stood amazed.

Phaidor’s eyes widened, her lips parting on a whisper. “The Temple of Issus.”

Xodar watched us with his grim smile, half amusement, half malicious gloating.

The gardens swarmed with brilliantly trapped black men and women—the First Born—moving as if the place belonged to them by the oldest right. Among them glided red and white females, serving, carrying, attending, their slavery made to look like ornamentation. It was as though whole cities and sanctuaries had been plundered not for gold but for living beauty, to be set here like captive birds in gilded cages.

Through this spectacle we were driven toward the temple.

At the main entrance a cordon of armed guards halted us. Xodar spoke to an officer who came forward; together they went within the golden doors and were gone long enough that the waiting grew heavy. When they returned, the officer announced that Issus desired to look upon the daughter of Matai Shang, and upon the strange creature from another world who had once been a Prince of Helium.

We were led through corridors that did not merely gleam but seemed to sing with reflected light—endless halls, apartments fit for kings, floors that muffled the footfall as if the very stones had been taught reverence. Somewhere, too, beneath the splendor, there was a constant sound: the distant hiss of fountains, the soft thunder of water moving through hidden channels. It should have been soothing; instead it felt like an engineered hush, a curated soundscape meant to smother thought. Even here, where beauty was weaponized, silence was part of the rule.

At last we were halted in a spacious chamber at the temple’s center. An officer went to a great door and gave some signal. It opened, and a richly trapped courtier emerged.

Then came the humiliation.

We were directed to get down on our hands and knees with our backs toward the room we were to enter. The doors swung wide. Under penalty of instant death we were warned not to turn our heads, and commanded to back into the Presence of Issus.

There are degradations that sting more than wounds. My hands pressed to the cold floor; my knees scraped; I moved like an animal trained to fear a whip. Only my love for Dejah Thoris—and the stubborn hope that I might yet see her again—kept me from rising, turning, and dying as a man should: facing my foe.

We crawled in that disgusting fashion for what seemed a couple of hundred feet before our escort halted us.

“Let them rise,” said a voice behind us—thin, wavering, yet shaped by long habit into command.

“Rise,” said the escort, “but do not face toward Issus.”

“The woman pleases me,” the voice continued after a pause. “She shall serve me the allotted time. The man you may return to the Isle of Shador, which lies against the northern shore of the Sea of Omean. Let the woman turn and look upon Issus, knowing that those of the lower orders who gaze upon the holy vision of her radiant face survive the blinding glory but a single year.”

From the corner of my eye I watched Phaidor. The blood left her face until she looked carved from pale stone. Slowly—very slowly—she turned, as though drawn by a force that was not physical but absolute. She stood close enough that her bare arm brushed mine, and when her eyes met what she was allowed to see, a shudder ran through her so hard I felt it tremble through that touch.

“It must be dazzling loveliness indeed,” I thought bitterly, “to strike such awe into one as radiant as Phaidor.”

“Let the woman remain,” said the voice. “Remove the man. Go.”

A heavy hand fell upon my shoulder. I dropped again to my hands and knees and crawled out of that room like a beaten cur. It had been my first audience with a deity, and I confess that what impressed me most was the ridiculous figure I cut on my marrow bones.

Once the doors closed behind us, I was bid to rise. Xodar joined me, and together we retraced our steps toward the gardens.

“You spared my life when you easily might have taken it,” he said at last, and his tone held an odd strain, as though pride and gratitude fought for space in his throat. “I would aid you if I might. I can help make your life here more bearable. But your fate is inevitable. You may never hope to return to the outer world.”

“What will be my fate?” I asked.

“That depends upon Issus. So long as she does not send for you and reveal her face to you, you may live for years—in bondage, yes, but in a mild form, as mild as I can arrange.”

“Why should she send for me?”

“The men of the lower orders she often uses for purposes of amusement. Such a fighter as you would render fine sport in the monthly rites of the temple. Men are pitted against men, and against beasts, for the edification of Issus and the replenishment of her larder.”

“She eats human flesh?” I asked.

Not in horror—my recent knowledge of the Holy Therns had cauterized surprise. Fanaticism and power had made these people regard other Barsoomians as we regard cattle. The difference was only in the costumes and the words they used to excuse appetite.

“She eats only the flesh of the best bred of the Holy Therns and the red Barsoomians,” Xodar said. “The flesh of the others goes to our boards. The animals are eaten by the slaves. She also eats other dainties.”

I did not understand, then, the weight he put behind those last words. I believed the abyss already had a bottom.

We had nearly reached the last of the many chambers and corridors that led back to the gardens when an officer overtook us at a brisk pace.

“Issus would look again upon this man,” he said. “The girl has told her he is of wondrous beauty and such prowess that alone he slew seven of the First Born, and with his bare hands took Xodar captive, binding him with his own harness.”

Xodar’s jaw tightened. He did not relish that his defeat had become court gossip in the ears of his goddess.

Without a word he turned. We followed the officer back to the closed doors of the audience chamber.

Again the ceremony. Again the crawling backward. Again the command to rise.

Silence lay over the room like a lid. I felt—rather than heard—the attention of something ancient measuring me, weighing me like meat.

Then the thin wavering voice spoke, droning words that had, for countless ages, sealed the doom of victims:

“Let the man turn and look upon Issus, knowing that those of the lower orders who gaze upon the holy vision of her radiant face survive the blinding glory but a single year.”

I turned, expecting—if not splendor—at least the sort of crafted illusion with which rulers dazzle their subjects.

What I saw was a solid phalanx of armed men between myself and a dais. Upon the dais stood a great bench of carved sorapus wood. And on that bench squatted a female black.

She was very old. Bald, her skull wrinkled like dried fruit left too long in sun. She was toothless save for two yellow fangs that jutted like obscene ornaments. Her eyes burned from the depths of horribly sunken sockets, and on either side of her thin, hawk-like nose the skin folded in deep-cut furrows as if her face had been carved by cruelty rather than time. Her body was as wrinkled as her face, emaciated limbs attached to a torso that seemed mostly distorted abdomen.

This was the “holy vision.” This was Issus, Goddess of Life Eternal.

Around her hovered female slaves. Among them was Phaidor, white and trembling, her beauty suddenly made tragic by the nearness of that ruinous thing upon the throne.

“This is the man who slew seven of the First Born and, bare-handed, bound Dator Xodar with his own harness?” Issus asked.

“Most glorious vision of divine loveliness, it is,” replied the officer at my side, voice thick with practiced lies.

“Produce Dator Xodar,” she commanded.

Xodar was brought from an adjoining room. Issus glared at him, and something like a squeal—thin, vicious—escaped her.

“And such as you are a Dator of the First Born?” she cried. “For the disgrace you have brought upon the Immortal Race you shall be degraded to a rank below the lowest. No longer be you a Dator, but forevermore a slave of slaves—to fetch and carry for the lower orders that serve in the gardens of Issus. Remove his harness. Cowards and slaves wear no trappings.”

Xodar stood rigid, head high. Not a muscle twitched as a guard stripped his gorgeous harness from him with rough hands. If he felt humiliation, he swallowed it whole.

“Begone!” Issus screamed. “Begone! But instead of the light of the gardens of Issus, let you serve as a slave of this slave who conquered you in the prison on the Isle of Shador in the Sea of Omean. Take him away out of the sight of my divine eyes.”

Slowly, proudly, Xodar turned and stalked from the chamber.

Issus rose, turning to leave by another exit, her retinue gathering like shadows behind her. Before she passed through, she looked—if that burning stare could be called looking—toward me.

“You shall be returned to Shador for the present,” she said. “Later Issus will see the manner of your fighting. Go.”

Then she was gone.

Only Phaidor lagged behind, as if the room itself held her by the hem. As I started to follow my guard toward the gardens, she broke from the line of slaves and came running after me.

“Oh, do not leave me in this terrible place,” she begged. Her words spilled out too fast, tumbling over one another like grain poured from a ruptured sack. “Forgive the things I said—I did not mean them. Only take me away with you. Let me share your imprisonment on Shador.

“You did not understand the honor I did you. Among the therns there is no marriage, no giving in marriage, as among the lower orders. We might have lived together forever in love and happiness. We have both looked upon Issus and in a year we die. Let us live that year at least together—in what joy remains for the doomed.”

How does one explain, in the few heartbeats allowed by guards and fear, that the same words can be different food to different mouths? That what she called honor sat in me like ash, not because I despised her, but because the meanings of our lives had been seasoned with entirely different salts?

“If it is difficult for me to understand you, Phaidor,” I said gently, “can you not understand that it may be equally difficult for you to understand the motives, the customs, the social laws that guide me? I do not wish to hurt you, nor to undervalue the honor you believe you have done me. But the thing you desire may not be.

“Regardless of the foolish belief of the peoples of the outer world—or of Holy Thern—or ebon First Born—I am not dead. While I live, my heart beats for but one woman: Dejah Thoris, Princess of Helium. When death overtakes me my heart shall have ceased to beat. What comes after, I know not. In that, I am no wiser than Matai Shang, Master of Life and Death upon Barsoom—or Issus, Goddess of Life Eternal.”

She stared at me a long moment. No anger now—only a hopeless sorrow, as if the very language between us had failed and left her hungry, unable to swallow what I had offered.

“I do not understand,” she said at last.

And then she turned and walked slowly toward the door through which Issus and her retinue had passed, her steps soft on the polished floor, swallowed by the temple’s manufactured hush.

A moment later she was gone from my sight.
