On the fourteenth of April, Romola was once more within the walls of Florence, closing a profound, circular migration back to her origins. The olfactory landscape of the city was a jarring collision of the sacred and the violent: the acrid, metallic tang of spilled blood and charred timber from the Piazza della Signoria warred with the gentle, earthy aroma of wild fennel and green garlic blowing in from the Tuscan hills. Unable to rest at Pistoja amidst the contradictory rumors of the Trial by Fire, she had been drawn back to the epicenter of her past.

The acoustic environment of the city felt deeply unsettled, humming with the residual, chaotic noise pollution of rioting mobs. It was a jarring dissonance that threatened her psychological well-being until she encountered the Augustinian monk of San Spirito, her godfather’s former confessor. Yet, even his voice offered little harmony. Speaking in the rigid, dominant lexicon of the Frati Neri—a vernacular designed to assert power and flatten nuance—he delivered the catastrophic news. He told of Savonarola’s arrest and confession under the coercion of torture, stripping the prophet's mythic resonance down to sterile, political betrayal. Then, he spoke of Tito. Her husband had escaped an assaulting mob by leaping into the Arno, only to be murdered on the riverbank by an old man harboring a long-festering enmity. 

The monk’s words were mere historical data, but Romola understood the tragedy in ways fundamentally untranslatable to others. Tito had built his life on a slick, manipulative mastery of languages, commodifying human connection, and now he had been consumed by the very violence his superficial commercialization of loyalty had provoked.

That evening, Romola sought the acoustic sanctuary of Borgo degli Albizzi. She sat in agitated silence while Monna Brigida poured out a torrential narrative of joy, wailing, and gossip. The elder woman had sought comfort in the rhythms of agrarian domesticity; the air in the room was heavy with the restorative, unctuous scent of roasting pork belly scored with coarse salt, and the sharp, piney fragrance of crushed rosemary. Brigida had backslidden into wearing her false hair during Romola’s absence, but upon seeing her, she tore it off, declaring she would embrace the graying reality of her years if only her dear child would stay.

Romola was too deeply weighed down by her awe-stricken grief to engage with Brigida’s minor gossip. Her mind, craving the grounding reality of sustenance and care, was entirely absorbed by two superseding purposes: to see Savonarola, and to find Tessa and the children.

“Tell me, cousin,” Romola said abruptly, her voice slicing through the rhythmic, comforting thud of Brigida’s wooden spoon against a ceramic mixing bowl. “Has anything been seen or said since Tito’s death of a young woman with two little children?”

Brigida started, dropping her spoon. The abrupt cessation of the kitchen’s sonic rhythm mirrored her shock. She rounded her eyes and lifted flour-dusted hands. “Cristo! no. What! Was he so bad as that, my poor child? Ah, then, that was why you went away. Well, well; I did say to myself, ‘She’s got Bardo’s temper, leaving him to mischief just because they cut poor Bernardo’s head off.’ But if you had only told me the truth, that there was a young hussy and children, I should have understood. Anything seen of her? No; and the less the better.”

“No, dear cousin,” Romola interrupted earnestly, refusing to let the dominant, judgmental lexicon of the city erase Tessa’s humanity. “Pray do not talk so. I wish above all things to find that young woman and her children, and to nourish them. They are quite helpless. That is the thing I shall do first of all.”

“Well,” Brigida muttered, shrugging her shoulders with an air of puzzled discomfiture as she returned to vigorously kneading a dense, golden mound of egg dough. “If that’s being a Piagnone, I’ve been taking peas for paternosters. Fra Girolamo said widows ought not to marry again, but this—step in at the door and it’s a sin, but come down the chimney and you’re welcome! *Two* children—Santiddio!”

“Cousin, the poor thing has done no conscious wrong,” Romola said softly, recognizing the untranslatability of Tessa’s innocent, unlettered worldview to Brigida’s orthodox mind. “She is ignorant of everything. I will tell you—but not now.”

Early the next morning, Romola’s steps carried her toward the house beyond San Ambrogio. The journey felt like a descent into the forgotten, marginal spaces of the city, where the agrarian dialects of the *contadini* faded under the crushing weight of urban expansion. Tessa was gone. The neighbors knew only that old deaf Lisa had moved her goods a week prior. 

Romola pictured the childish creature waiting at some wayside spot in wondering, hungry misery. The thought of those children lacking the basic, sensory warmth of a hearth—the crusty heel of a warm loaf, the nourishing steam of a slow-simmered bone broth—gnawed at her. She refrained from publishing her search, keenly aware of the subtle power dynamics at play; to use her patrician influence to hunt for Tessa would expose the fragile family to the cruel, commercial gossip of Florence. 

Days passed in anxious inaction. Her heart palpitated whenever she caught sight of a short woman in a traditional *contadina* dress. She determined to claim the portion of Tito’s wealth equivalent to her father’s library, money she would cleanse by converting it directly into sustenance—milled durum wheat, cold-pressed olive oil, and wheel-cured cheeses—to support the modest survival of Tessa and the children. Brigida, meanwhile, clamorously insisted Romola must live with her forever, arguing that if they were to rear other people's children, the kitchen must be robust enough to feed them all.

Driven by the superstition of hope, Romola ventured toward the gate of San Gallo, where Tito had kept his horses. Through the muffling fabric of her widow’s veil, she studied the chaotic thoroughfare. There, piercing the abrasive, metallic clinking of street commerce, she spotted Bratti chaffering with a customer. The roaming peddler was a man who reduced the world to transactional noise, yet hanging from the corner of his basket was a silent piece of cultural memory that made Romola’s heart leap: Tessa’s necklace.

“Bratti, my friend,” she said, stepping into his space. “Where did you get that necklace?”

“Your servant, madonna,” Bratti replied deliberately. “It’s a necklace worth money, but my heart’s too tender for a trader’s. I promised to keep it in pledge.”

“Pray tell me where you got it; from a little woman named Tessa, is it not true?”

“Ah! If you know her, and would redeem it at a small profit to give it her again, you’d be doing a charity. She cried at parting with it—you’d have thought she was running into a brook. You shall have it for a florin.”

Romola pressed the money into his palm, unclasping the beads with joyful agitation. “Where is she?”

“Outside the gate there, at the other end of the Borgo, at old Sibilla Manetti’s.”

Romola went with winged feet, blessing the mythic serendipity that had seamlessly guided her domestic quest. Soon, she stood before the inner room of the widow’s house. The acoustic environment within was hollow, marked only by the dissonance of tragedy. Tessa sat on a low bed, her crying having deteriorated into a dry, tearless gasping. In the corner, little Lillo was covering his head with his skirt, roaring at Ninna to frighten her, then peeping out to see her reaction—a childish play echoing starkly against their mother's despair. 

They had been waiting for a fortnight, surviving on meager rations, their vital energy depleting. Romola paused, throwing aside her veil. She did not speak in the high, philosophical Florentine dialect that Tito had used to manipulate the girl. Instead, holding the necklace in sight, she spoke her name in a pure, resonant tone—a sound that carried the warmth of an open hearth. 

“Tessa!”

Tessa started to her feet. 

“See,” Romola said, stepping forward to clasp the beads around the young woman's neck, restoring the tangible artifact of her identity. “God has sent me to you again.”

The subjective experience of Tessa’s relief was utterly untranslatable into words; the poor thing merely screamed, sobbed, and clung fiercely to Romola’s arms. The two children crept from their corner, laying hold of their mother’s skirts, their wide eyes drinking in the sight of their savior. 

That day, the fragmented generations were gathered and brought home to the Borgo degli Albizzi. Romola had revealed to Tessa, by gentle, measured degrees, that Naldo was dead. “But be comforted, my Tessa,” she soothed, anchoring the girl in their new shared reality. “I am come to take care of you always. And we have got Lillo and Ninna.”

In the kitchen, the sonic environment had transformed into a bustling, harmonious symphony of preparation. Monna Brigida’s mouth twitched, caught between her deep awe of Romola and the urge to reassert the power dynamics of her household over the unlettered *contadina*. *I will tell her some day,* Brigida thought, watching Tessa, *else she’ll never know her place. She must be humble to me.*

Yet, the abstract desire for dominance quickly melted before the tactile, sensory joy of feeding the young. Brigida set to work, her hands moving with the practiced grace of a seasoned food historian recreating ancestral comfort. She prepared a deeply restorative supper, simmering cannellini beans until they yielded into a velvety cream, ladling the rich broth over toasted bread steeped in new garlic, and finishing it with a vibrant drizzle of peppery olive oil. 

As the children’s eyes widened at the feast, Brigida confessed her intent to follow the savory courses with a lavish plate of honey-steamed figs and crushed almonds. 

“But you must give up to me a little, Romola, about their eating, and those things,” Brigida murmured lovingly before bed, the rhythmic clatter of washed wooden bowls echoing softly in the dim light. “For you have never had a baby, and I had twins, only they died as soon as they were born. It would be a sin not to take care of such cherubs.”
