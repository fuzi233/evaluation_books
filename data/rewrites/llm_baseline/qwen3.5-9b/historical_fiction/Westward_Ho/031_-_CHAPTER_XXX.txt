"How be these gay Spaniards, Which make so great a boast?" sang the Cornish ballad, "Oh, they shall eat the gray-goose feather, And we shall eat the roast!"

Imagine, for a moment, the spectators who last summer stood with swelling pride upon the noble port of Plymouth. They beheld its vast breakwater spanning the Sound, its arsenals and docks, its twin estuaries teeming with gallant ships. They watched the great screw-driven liners turning within their own lengths by invisible forces, or threading crowded fleets with the effortless grace of a toy skiff. Now, suppose that by some magical reversal, the nineteenth century—with all its magnificent wealth and scientific marvels—vanished entirely. Suppose they found themselves cast back three hundred years to the pleasant summer days of 1588.

Mount Edgecombe would remain, beautiful as ever. Yet, where are the docks? Where is Devonport? No vast dry-dock roofs would rise at the water's edge. Drake's Island would carry but a paltry battery, hastily erected by the man whose name it bore; Mount Wise would be merely a solitary gentleman's house amidst the fields; the citadel would be a pop-gun fortification, capable of being reduced to rubble by a third-class steamer for mere afternoon amusement. And the shipping? The floating castles of the Hamoaze would have dwindled to a handful of crawling lime-hoys. The Catwater, now packed with merchant craft, would instead be congested with the vessels destined to commence the greatest naval battle the world has ever witnessed.

To modern eyes, this squadron would seem pitifully small. Their largest vessel would not match the tonnage of a sixty-gun frigate, carrying less weight of metal than one of our modern gunboats, and unable to deploy that metal beyond a quarter of our current effective range. Would our modern tourists, arriving by rail for a brief excursion to witness the cavalry embark and returning in time for dinner, have looked down upon this modest port and its tiny fleet with a contemptuous sneer? Doubtless, they would have begun some flippant monologue regarding the progress of intellect and the triumphs of science, dismissing their benighted ancestors. Such would be the reaction of the multitude who gaze at human achievement as a raree-show to feed their silly wonder, using and enjoying it without gratitude or comprehension, much like an ox eating clover thrust into its rack without knowing how it grew.

However, if among them were those who actually *achieved* these triumphs—the thinkers and workers who, unlike the idle mob, labor themselves; who know by hard experience the struggles, self-restraints, disappointments, and staggering steps required for discovery—then their expression would not be one of pity, but of filial love. They would perceive in those outwardly paltry armaments the germs of the mightier fleets that now load the Black Sea waves. They would understand that producing such power with the limited materials and knowledge of the age demanded an intellect, energy, and spirit of invention equal to, if not surpassing, our own boasted capabilities today.

Yet, if one were a student of men rather than machines, he would find fewer nobler companies to observe than those gathered on the little terrace bowling-green behind the Pelican Inn on the afternoon of July 19th. Chatting in groups or lounging over the low wall commanding a view of the Sound and the distant shipping, sat nearly every notable man of the Plymouth fleet—the entire posse comitatus of "England's forgotten worthies." The Armada had supposedly been scattered by a storm; Lord Howard had ventured as far as the Spanish coast seeking it, but the wind had shifted south. Fearful that the Dons might slip past him, he had returned to Plymouth, uncertain whether the Armada would arrive at all.

Slip on the drawer's apron for a moment; enter through the rose-adorned door with a tray of long-necked Dutch glasses and a silver tankard of wine, and survey the scene. There sit the gallant captains, waiting for the Spanish Armada like lions in their lair awaiting the passing herd of deer.

Observe the five figures talking earnestly in the center of a ring that longs to overhear yet remains too respectful to approach closely. Those soft, long eyes and pointed chin are unmistakable: they belong to Walter Raleigh. The fair youth in the flame-colored doublet, with his arm around Raleigh's neck, is Lord Sheffield. Opposite them stands, beside Sir Richard Grenville, a man equally stately: Lord Charles Howard of Effingham, Lord High Admiral of England. Next to him is his son-in-law, Sir Robert Southwell, captain of the *Elizabeth Jonas*. But who is that short, sturdy man, plainly dressed, standing with legs slightly apart and hands behind his back, gazing upward with keen gray eyes into the faces of the speakers? His cap rests in his hands, revealing a bullet-shaped head of crisp brown hair, a wrinkled forehead, high cheekbones, a short square face, broad temples, and thick lips firm as granite. He possesses a coarse, plebeian appearance, yet his entire posture radiates boundless determination, self-possession, and energy. When he finally speaks a few blunt words, all eyes turn respectfully toward him—for his name is Francis Drake.

A burly, grizzled elder, clad in greasy, sea-stained garments that contrast oddly with the massive gold chain about his neck, waddles forward as if born and living solely within a perpetual gale. The upper half of his sharp, dogged visage resembles red leather, the lower half badger's fur. As he slaps Drake on the back and shouts in a broad Devon accent, "Be you a coming to drink your wine, Francis Drake, or be you not?—saving your presence, my lord," the Lord High Admiral simply laughs and bids Drake go and drink his wine. For John Hawkins, Admiral of the Port, is the patriarch of Plymouth seamen; if Drake is their hero, Hawkins is their grandfather. He says and does precisely what he pleases in any company, especially given that the prospect of an Armageddon-style battle has shaken his usual crabbed reserve, overflowing him with loquacious good humor—even toward his rival Drake.

They push through the crowd, where many others await conversation. Martin Frobisher and John Davis sit on a bench smoking tobacco from long silver pipes; beside them are Fenton and Withrington, who attempted to follow Drake's global path but failed, though not through their own fault. The man pledging them better luck next time is George Fenner, known to "the Seven Portuguese," Leicester's favorite, and captain of the galleon purchased by Queen Elizabeth. That short, prim man in the enormous yellow ruff, with a sharp chin, minute imperial, and self-satisfied smile, is Richard Hawkins, the "Complete Seaman"—Admiral John's future, infamous, and unfortunate son. Talking with him is his good uncle William, whose monument still stands (or should) in Deptford Church; for Admiral John erected it there just one year later, recording that he was "a worshipper of the true religion, an especial benefactor of poor sailors, a most just arbiter in most difficult causes, and of a singular faith, piety, and prudence." That, and his credible performance at Porto Rico, constitutes all I know of William Hawkins. If you or I, reader, could secure even half as much praise when following in his footsteps, we need not complain.

Present also are John Drake, Sir Francis's brother and ancestor of the current Drake lineage; George, his nephew—a man not overly wise who circumnavigated the globe with Amyas; and Amyas himself, conversing with one who replies in fierce, curt sentences: Captain Barker of Bristol, brother of the ill-fated Andrew Barker who discovered John Oxenham's guns and, due to a mutiny, perished at the hands of the Spaniards in Honduras twelve years prior. Barker now commands the *Victory*, one of the Queen's finest ships, and shares grievances with the Dons just as Amyas does; thus, they growl together in a corner while the rest revel like flies upon a vine.

But who is the aged man seated against the sunny southern wall of the tavern? His long white beard flows nearly to his waist, his hands rest on his knees, and his palsied head moves slowly from side to side, catching fragments of discourse from passing captains. At his knee lies his great-granddaughter, a little maid of six, resting her curly head upon him. Beside him stands his granddaughter, a buxom, black-eyed woman of thirty, serving him half as nurse and half as showman, for he is an object of curiosity to all the captains. She must repeatedly plead, "Bless you, sir, please now, don't give him no liquor, poor old soul, the doctor says." This is old Martin Cockrem, father of the ancient host, aged beyond the span of manhood, who remembers the bells of Plymouth ringing for the coronation of Henry VIII and claims to be the first Englishman, perhaps, to set foot on New World soil. He sits there like an ancient Druid tor of primeval granite amidst the tall wheat and rich clover of a modern farm. Having witnessed the death of old Europe and the birth-pangs of the new, he appears uneasy, peering through the groups with rheumy eyes, listening intently for a familiar voice.
