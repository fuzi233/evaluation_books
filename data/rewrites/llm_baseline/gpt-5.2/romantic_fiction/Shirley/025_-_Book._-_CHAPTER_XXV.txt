Not always does the spirit, in its bold wrestle with what it cannot see, come off conqueror. There are vigils when the body sits like a spent hearth and the soul is the one coal left, burning, burning, till the sweat gathers black upon the brow; when prayer is no spoken thing, but a dumb pressure of the heart against the Unanswering—“Spare her. Heal her. Do not wrench out of me what long use has twined with every fibre of my being. Be gentle.” And after that struggle, the dawn may break as it used to break—softly, with birds calling from the hedges—only to bring, from lips grown strange and colourless, the sentence that makes the watcher’s sinews slacken.

“Oh! I have had a suffering night. This morning I am worse. I have tried to rise. I cannot. Dreams I am unused to have troubled me.”

Then the chair is drawn nearer to the pillow; the face, familiar as one’s own hand, is seen newly fashioned—as if pain had been a sculptor in the dark; and at once the mind knows, without being told, that the intolerable minute is on the road. One bows, because rebellion is useless; one subdues, because anguish must not become frenzy.

But Mrs. Pryor was not brought to that bowing. She was still praying, still kneeling in the very posture of entreaty, unconscious that the August sun had climbed above the hills, when the child in her arms woke softly—woke as one returns from a wholesome sleep, not as one drifts back from some far, dim border.

No piteous moan went before her waking—no sound that eats into the nerve till even a sworn courage dissolves in tears. No hollow pause of stupor followed. Caroline’s first look, though languid, was clear; she remembered.

“Mamma, I have slept so well. I only dreamed and woke twice.”

Mrs. Pryor rose with a start and turned her face away, that Caroline might not see the sudden, treacherous gladness gathering in her eyes at that word “mamma,” and at the calm assurance that came after it. For days—and then for more days—she dared not rejoice except with trembling. The first revivals were like the flutter of a lamp that has sunk low: it would flare for a moment, and then, as quickly, the flame would crouch down again, faint in its socket. Close upon hope came exhaustion. Caroline had always that touching desire to look better than she was, to lift herself as if will could do the work of strength; too often ability refused its service. The brave little attempts to eat, to talk, to seem cheerful, failed. Many an hour passed in which the mother feared the cords of life could never be tightened again, though their snapping might be delayed.

And all this time the neighbourhood seemed deserted, as if the very village had taken holiday from sorrow. It was the close of August; the weather was fine—fine in the way that means pitilessly dry, dust on every leaf, dust on every step, and a breath from the east that was no wholesome wind but a parching exhalation, like something blown out of far deserts. The sky held no cloud; only a pale, fixed haze, as if the air were full of powder, stole all depth from heaven’s blue, all freshness from earth’s green, all glow from day. The ear, too, suffered. There was no kindly, rustling, variable music in that stillness; the world sounded wrong—crisp, strained, over-bright. The gable of the rectory heard, night and day, the same thin sobbing at its eastern edge; the oriel of the church took it up and echoed it. Even the domestic noises—the pail set down, the latch lifted—rang as if the air were hard.

Almost every family in Briarfield was absent on some excursion. Miss Keeldar and her friends were away at the sea; so were Mrs. Yorke’s household. Mr. Hall and Louis Moore—between whom a spontaneous intimacy had sprung up, as if their minds had found each other by scent—were gone “up north,” on a pedestrian jaunt to the Lakes. Hortense, who would willingly have remained and lent her quick, capable hands to the nursing, had been entreated so earnestly by Miss Mann to accompany her once more to Wormwood Wells—hoping to ease sufferings sharpened by the unwholesome weather—that she could not refuse. It was in her nature to yield when goodness was appealed to, and when the confession of dependence stroked, too, her amour propre. Robert had gone from Birmingham to London, and still stayed there.

So long as that east wind—breath like hot, dry iron—continued to parch Caroline’s lips and fever her veins, the body could not keep pace with the mind’s returning composure. But at last a day came when the wind ceased to worry the house. A small cloud—no bigger, at first, than a man’s hand—rose in the west; gusts came after it, driving it on, spreading it wide. Rain fell; there was a short reign of wet and tempest, the sound of it on panes and leaves like a benediction after that cruel dryness. Then, when the storm had spent itself, the sun broke out with a geniality that felt like kindness; heaven recovered its true azure, earth her honest green. The livid cholera-tint vanished from nature’s face; the hills stood up clear around the horizon, absolved from the pale malaria-haze.

And with that west wind—soft as water, fresh as a new-washed linen sheet hung out to dry—something in Caroline yielded and turned. The lattice was kept ever open; it let in not only air, but the right kind of sound: the full leaf-rustle, the deeper murmur of distant trees, the wholesome stir of life moving without strain. Youth could help her now, and so could her mother’s care; both, crowned by God’s blessing, rekindled the energies that had long languished like a fire starved of fuel.

At last Mrs. Pryor saw that it was permitted to hope. A true, material convalescence had begun. It was not only that Caroline’s smile came readier, or her spirits rose for an hour; a certain look had left her face and eye—a look dreadful and indescribable, yet easily remembered by those who have watched beside dangerous sickness. Long before the wasted lines filled out, or the lost colour returned, a more subtle change passed over her: all grew softer, warmer, more human. Instead of what had seemed a marble mask and glassy eye, there lay on the pillow a face still pale, still thin—perhaps more haggard than that awful smoothness—but less terrible, because it was a sick, living girl, not a rigid piece of statuary.

Now, too, she was not for ever pleading to drink. The plaint, “I am so thirsty,” ceased to be her constant cry. Sometimes, when she had swallowed a morsel, she would say it had revived her; and those simple words were like meat and wine to the watcher’s heart. All food was no longer equally odious. She could, at times, be induced to name a preference—faintly, as if the very act of choosing were new.

With what trembling pleasure, with what anxious household skill, did Mrs. Pryor prepare what was selected! The smallest details became sacred rites. A cup was warmed so that no chill should offend a tender mouth; a spoon was dipped and wiped, dipped and wiped again, that nothing metallic should mingle with the taste; broth was skimmed till it shone clear as amber, then salted by grains, not by pinches, until it met the palate without startling it. Toast was dried, not scorched, and the crust rubbed with a touch of fresh butter that melted at once and breathed out its nutty scent. When a little custard could be borne, it was strained till it was silk—no grain, no lump—just eggs and milk coaxed over a gentle heat, lifted at the exact moment before they turned, perfumed with the faintest drop of vanilla or a shaving of lemon-peel. The kitchen itself seemed to listen; the clatter of pans was hushed; even the servants’ steps became cautious, as if sound might bruise.

Mrs. Pryor watched Caroline partake as if each spoonful were a verdict. Nourishment brought strength. Caroline could sit up; and then she began to long—not for excitement, but for air, for her flowers, for the honest sight of what had been growing while she lay so near the edge of not seeing anything again. She asked how the fruit had ripened. She asked if the late roses had held out through the east wind’s tyranny.

Her uncle, always liberal, bought a garden-chair for her express use. He carried her down in his own arms—careful, proud, tender in that rough, decisive way of his—and placed her in it himself. William Farren was there to wheel her along the walks, to show her what he had been doing among her plants, to take her directions for further work.

Caroline and William found abundance to say to each other, though their topics were such as would have seemed nothing to most people. They had, in truth, a dozen interests in common—small, minute, domestic interests—yet full, to them, of life. They watched creatures; they observed habits; they took pains not to trample what was little. They held alike those quiet doctrines about humanity to the lower creation which are not preached loudly, but practised steadily.

Under an old cherry-tree, some ground-bees had burrowed in the turf: their nest, their comings and goings, their patient industry, formed one subject. The haunts of certain hedge-sparrows, the welfare of certain pearly eggs and callow fledglings, formed another. Their world, when they spoke thus, had its own soundscape: the bees’ low, purposeful hum under the cherry shade; the sparrows’ small, anxious chatter in the hedge; the soft grit of the garden-path under the chair’s wheels; the west wind’s continual, living accompaniment. It was a music that soothed, not dazzled—a music no manufactured art could rival, and which no industrial clamour had yet learned to drown in Briarfield.

Had Chambers’s Journal existed then, it would certainly have been Miss Helstone’s and Farren’s chosen periodical. She would have subscribed; he would have borrowed each number in turn; both would have trusted the marvellous anecdotes of animal sagacity and found real savour in them, as people do who still possess the taste for wonder in common things. Let the digression stand; it explains why Caroline would have no other hand than William’s to guide her chair, and why his company, his plain talk, sufficed to make her garden airings not merely endurable but pleasant.

Mrs. Pryor, walking near, wondered—half in perplexity, half in a hidden disapprobation—how her daughter could be at such ease with “a man of the people.” She found it impossible to address him otherwise than stiffly. Between her caste and his she felt a gulf; to step across it, or meet him half-way, seemed to her like self-abasement. One day she asked Caroline, gently, as if giving counsel for safety—

“Are you not afraid, my dear, to converse with that person so unreservedly? He may presume, and become troublesomely garrulous.”

“William presume, mamma? You don’t know him. He never presumes. He is altogether too proud and sensitive to do so. William has very fine feelings.”

Mrs. Pryor smiled—sceptical, almost indulgent—at the naïve idea of that rough-handed, fustian-clad clown having “fine feelings.” But William, on his side, showed her only a sulky brow. He knew when he was misjudged. With those who failed to give him his due, he was apt to become unmanageable; and his silence had a kind of resentful weight.

Evening restored Caroline wholly to her mother, and Mrs. Pryor liked the evening. Then, when daylight softened, when the birds settled and the household sounds sank to a gentler pitch, no human shadow came between her and what she loved. In the day she had still her stiff demeanour and her cold moments, as was her habit. Between her and Mr. Helstone a respectful but rigidly ceremonious intercourse was maintained. Familiarity would have bred contempt at once in one or both; but strict civility and well-kept distance made their relations smooth.

To the servants Mrs. Pryor’s manner was not openly uncourteous, yet shy, freezing, ungenial. Perhaps it was diffidence, not pride, that made her look so haughty; but Fanny and Eliza did not trouble themselves to distinguish. She felt the effect; and it rendered her, at times, dissatisfied with herself for faults she could not help, and, dissatisfied with all else, dejected, chill, taciturn.

This mood changed under Caroline’s influence—and under that influence only. The dependent fondness of her nursling, the natural affection of her child, came over her like warmth over frost. Her rigidity unbent. She smiled; she yielded; she grew pliant. Caroline did not love with loud protestations—that would have ill suited Mrs. Pryor, who would have read wordiness as insincerity. Instead, Caroline hung on her with an easy dependence; she confided in her with fearless reliance. Those quiet claims—those simple, unquestioning trusts—contented the mother’s heart.

She liked to hear, in that voice now daily growing clearer, “Mamma, do this;” “Please, mamma, fetch me that;” “Mamma, read to me;” “Sing a little, mamma.”

No one—no living creature—had ever so claimed her services, so looked for help at her hand. Other people were always, with her, reserved and stiff, as she was reserved and stiff with them; other people betrayed consciousness of her weak points and annoyance at them. Caroline showed no such wounding sagacity now than she had shown when she was a suckling of three months old.

Yet Caroline could find fault. Blind to constitutional defects that were incurable, she was sharp-eyed for acquired habits that might be mended; and on these she would lecture, artlessly, with a young girl’s frank authority. Instead of being hurt, Mrs. Pryor felt a secret pleasure in it: the pleasure of discovering that the child dared to chide her—was so much at home.

“Mamma, I am determined you shall not wear that old gown any more. Its fashion is not becoming; it is too strait in the skirt. You shall put on your black silk every afternoon. In that you look nice; it suits you. And you shall have a black satin dress for Sundays—a real satin, not a satinet or any of the shams. And, mamma, when you get the new one, mind you must wear it.”

“My dear, I thought of the black silk serving me as a best dress for many years yet, and I wished to buy you several things.”

“Nonsense, mamma. My uncle gives me cash to get what I want. You know he is generous enough; and I have set my heart on seeing you in a black satin. Get it soon, and let it be made by a dressmaker of my recommending. Let me choose the pattern. You always want to disguise yourself like a grandmother. You would persuade one that you are old and ugly. Not at all! On the contrary, when well dressed and cheerful you are very comely indeed; your smile is so pleasant, your teeth are so white, your hair is still such a pretty light colour. And then you speak like a young lady, with such a clear, fine tone, and you sing better than any young lady I ever heard. Why do you wear such dresses and bonnets, mamma—such as nobody else ever wears?”

“Does it annoy you, Caroline?”

“Very much; it vexes me even. People say you are miserly; and yet you are not, for you give liberally to the poor and to religious societies—though your gifts are conveyed so secretly and quietly that they are known to few except the receivers. But I will be your lady’s-maid myself. When I get a little stronger I will set to work, and you must be good, mamma, and do as I bid you.”

And, sitting near, she rearranged her mother’s muslin handkerchief, resmoothed her hair with fingers that had lately been so weak they could scarcely lift a spoon. Then she went on, as if the thought of their relationship pleased her palate like sweetness—

“My own mamma—who belongs to me, and to whom I belong! I am a rich girl now. I have something I can love well, and not be afraid of loving. Mamma, who gave you this little brooch? Let me unpin it and look at it.”

Mrs. Pryor, who usually shrank from meddling fingers and too near an approach, let her have this licence with complacency.

“Did papa give you this, mamma?”

“My sister gave it me—my only sister, Cary. Would that your Aunt Caroline had lived to see her niece!”

“Have you nothing of papa’s—no trinket, no gift of his?”

“I have one thing.”

“That you prize?”

“That I prize.”

“Valuable and pretty?”

“Invaluable and sweet to me.”

“Show it, mamma. Is it here or at Fieldhead?”

“It is talking to me now, leaning on me. Its arms are round me.”

“Ah, mamma, you mean your teasing daughter, who will never let you alone; who, when you go into your room, cannot help running to seek for you; who follows you upstairs and down, like a dog.”

“Whose features still give me such a strange thrill sometimes. I half fear your fair looks yet, child.”

“You don’t; you can’t. Mamma, I am sorry papa was not good. I do so wish he had been. Wickedness spoils and poisons all pleasant things. It kills love. If you and I thought each other wicked, we could not love each other, could we?”

“And if we could not trust each other, Cary?”

“How miserable we should be! Mother, before I knew you I had an apprehension that you were not good—that I could not esteem you. That dread damped my wish to see you. And now my heart is elate because I find you perfect—almost; kind, clever, nice. Your sole fault is that you are old-fashioned, and of that I shall cure you. Mamma, put your work down; read to me. I like your southern accent; it is so pure, so soft. It has no rugged burr, no nasal twang, such as almost every one’s voice here in the north has. My uncle and Mr. Hall say that you are a fine reader, mamma. Mr. Hall said he never heard any lady read with such propriety of expression or purity of accent.”

“I wish I could reciprocate the compliment, Cary; but, really, the first time I heard your truly excellent friend read and preach I could not understand his broad northern tongue.”

“Could you understand me, mamma? Did I seem to speak roughly?”

“No. I almost wished you had, as I wished you had looked unpolished. Your father, Caroline, naturally spoke well, quite otherwise than your worthy uncle—correctly, gently, smoothly. You inherit the gift.”

“Poor papa! When he was so agreeable, why was he not good?”

“Why he was as he was—and happily of that you, child, can form no conception—I cannot tell. It is a deep mystery. The key is in the hands of his Maker. There I leave it.”

“Mamma, you will keep stitching, stitching away. Put down the sewing; I am an enemy to it. It cumbers your lap, and I want it for my head; it engages your eyes, and I want them for a book. Here is your favourite—Cowper.”

These importunities were the mother’s pleasure. If she ever delayed compliance, it was only to hear them repeated, and to enjoy the soft, half-playful, half-petulant urgency with which Caroline insisted. And then, when she yielded, Caroline would say, with an arch little air of self-knowledge—

“You will spoil me, mamma. I always thought I should like to be spoiled, and I find it very sweet.”

So did Mrs. Pryor.
