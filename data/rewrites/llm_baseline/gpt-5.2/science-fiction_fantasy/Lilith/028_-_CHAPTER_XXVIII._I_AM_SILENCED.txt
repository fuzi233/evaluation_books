I wiped the water from my lashes, and the first thing the dawn gave back to me was the raven.

He perched on the rim of the great stone basin as if it had been made for his feet alone, his feathers taking the cold morning light the way a well-oiled pan takes fire—steel-dark, glossy, untroubled. He looked down at me with a steadiness that made the fountain’s uproar seem like mere background.

I lay on my back in the water, buoyed as if the basin were a broad bowl and I the unconsidered broth within it. Propping myself on my elbows, I lifted my face to breathe. The air was sharp with wet stone and lawn-grass, with that clean metallic tang fountains always bring—water striking water, water striking carved rock. High above, the thick stalk of the jet rose like a polished column, hard and shining, shouldering its torrent a hundred feet into the sky before it burst into a white blossom of foam. The sound was not a simple roar but a layered life: the continuous rush, the scatter-hiss of spray, the slap of falling sheets, the thin singing of droplets when the wind caught them. It was a whole acoustic garden, and it steadied me even while it drowned thought.

Nettled—more by the raven’s tone than by the cold soaking through me—I said, “You told me nothing!”

“I told you to do nothing any one you distrusted asked you.”

His voice cut cleanly through the fountain’s noise, as if it knew where to place itself in my hearing.

“Tt—how was mortal to remember that?” I snapped, half shivering, half angry, tasting stone-cold water on my tongue.

“You will not forget the consequences of having forgotten it,” he returned.

He leaned over the margin. A hand—his hand now, not a claw—stretched across the basin. I took it, and in the same instant I was no longer in the water at all but standing beside him on the lawn, my clothes clinging and streaming as if the fountain had spat me out.

“You must change your clothes at once!” he said. “A wetting does not signify where you come from—though at present such an accident is unusual; here it has its inconveniences!”

Even as he spoke he was again a raven, walking toward the house with something measured and almost ceremonial in his step. The door stood open.

“I have not much to change!” I laughed, though my teeth clicked with the cold; for I had flung aside my robe to climb the tree, and what I wore was scarcely worth calling a suit.

“It is a long time since I moulted a feather!” said the raven, as if that settled the matter between us.

Inside, the house held the hushed heaviness of early morning—no footsteps, no voices, only the distant, persistent water outside, threading its way through corridors and floors like a reminder you could not shut out. I went to my room, found a dressing-gown, and exchanged dripping for damp. Cloth against skin was suddenly loud to me: the soft rasp of dry fabric, the faint suction where wet had been. I pulled the gown close as one draws a blanket over a child.

Then I descended to the library. As I entered, the librarian came out from the closet—so quietly that I might have thought him a shadow detaching itself from the wall. I threw myself on a couch as if my body had been carrying a sack of stones and had at last found a place to drop it.

Mr. Raven—still raven, and yet not—drew a chair to my side and sat down.

For a minute or two neither of us spoke. The silence was not empty; it was full of the fountain’s far-off insistence, and of the house’s breathing—timbers settling, a faint draft worrying a curtain. I listened as if my mind could eat sound the way the body eats bread, hoping for some nourishment in it.

At last I broke it. “What does it all mean?”

“A good question!” he rejoined. “Nobody knows what anything is; a man can learn only what a thing means! Whether he do, depends on the use he is making of it.”

“I have made no use of anything yet!”

“Not much; but you know the fact, and that is something! Most people take more than a lifetime to learn that they have learned nothing, and done less! At least you have not been without the desire to be of use!”

“I did want to do something for the children—the precious Little Ones, I mean.” The very thought of them brought back their small hands and grave eyes, their careful ways of tending what they had, as if every crumb were a relic.

“I know you did—and started the wrong way!”

“I did not know the right way.”

“That is true also—but you are to blame that you did not.”

“I am ready to believe whatever you tell me—as soon as I understand what it means.”

“Had you accepted our invitation, you would have known the right way. When a man will not act where he is, he must go far to find his work.”

“Indeed I have gone far, and got nowhere, for I have not found my work! I left the children to learn how to serve them, and have only learned the danger they are in.”

“When you were with them, you were where you could help them: you left your work to look for it! It takes a wise man to know when to go away; a fool may learn to go back at once!”

His words landed with a thud, like a loaf dropped too early from a peel—heavy, misshapen, undeniable.

“Do you mean, sir, I could have done something for the Little Ones by staying with them?”

“Could you teach them anything by leaving them?”

“No; but how could I teach them? I did not know how to begin. Besides, they were far ahead of me!”

“That is true. But you were not a rod to measure them with! Certainly, if they knew what you know, not to say what you might have known, they would be ahead of you—out of sight ahead! but you saw they were not growing—or growing so slowly that they had not yet developed the idea of growing! they were even afraid of growing!—You had never seen children remain children!”

The fountain’s steady thunder seemed, for a moment, like a rebuke too: water endlessly rising and falling, refusing stagnation.

“But surely I had no power to make them grow!”

“You might have removed some of the hindrances to their growing!”

“What are they? I do not know them. I did think perhaps it was the want of water!”

“Of course it is! they have none to cry with!”

The sentence struck me with a strange, bitter clarity, as if he had named an ingredient I’d been missing without knowing it—something common and essential, something you cannot substitute with good intentions.

“I would gladly have kept them from requiring any for that purpose!”

“No doubt you would—the aim of all stupid philanthropists! Why, Mr. Vane, but for the weeping in it, your world would never have become worth saving!”

I heard again, as if my ears were opening to a remembered landscape, the faint subterranean murmur I had once noticed near them—a watery sound under earth, like a hidden brook speaking its own language to roots and stones. It was the sort of sound a farmer knows, an elder with soil in his nails: the promise of a spring, the secret pantry beneath the field. And I had let it be only a passing curiosity, a noise among other noises.

“You confess you thought it might be water they wanted: why did not you dig them a well or two?”

“That never entered my mind!”

“Not when the sounds of the waters under the earth entered your ears?”

“I believe it did once. But I was afraid of the giants for them. That was what made me bear so much from the brutes myself!”

“Indeed you almost taught the noble little creatures to be afraid of the stupid Bags!” His voice sharpened on the word, as if the very syllable were coarse in his beak. “While they fed and comforted and worshipped you, all the time you submitted to be the slave of bestial men! You gave the darlings a seeming coward for their hero! A worse wrong you could hardly have done them. They gave you their hearts; you owed them your soul!—You might by this time have made the Bags hewers of wood and drawers of water to the Little Ones!”

Water again—drawers of water. The phrase made a domestic picture despite its sting: the simple, daily labor that keeps a household alive; the carrying, the pouring, the careful sharing. In my world, so much of that had been pushed into pipes and hidden machinery until we forgot the dignity of the sound of a bucket filling, the hush when a thirsty mouth meets a cup. Here, water was not convenience; it was voice. It could be the difference between a people remaining forever child-silent and a people learning to weep—learning, perhaps, to speak.

“I fear what you say is true, Mr. Raven! But indeed I was afraid that more knowledge might prove an injury to them—render them less innocent, less lovely.”

“They had given you no reason to harbour such a fear!”

“Is not a little knowledge a dangerous thing?”

“That is one of the pet falsehoods of your world! Is man’s greatest knowledge more than a little? or is it therefore dangerous? The fancy that knowledge is in itself a great thing, would make any degree of knowledge more dangerous than any amount of ignorance. To know all things would not be greatness.”

“At least it was for love of them, not from cowardice that I served the giants!”

“Granted. But you ought to have served the Little Ones, not the giants! You ought to have given the Little Ones water; then they would soon have taught the giants their true position. In the meantime you could yourself have made the giants cut down two-thirds of their coarse fruit-trees to give room to the little delicate ones! You lost your chance with the Lovers, Mr. Vane! You speculated about them instead of helping them!”

Speculated. The word tasted dusty—like flour left too long in a sack, the life gone out of it. And in the library, with the fountain’s sound still pressing in from the world beyond the walls, I could not deny the charge: I had treated living souls like a subject for talk, as if naming their sweetness were the same as feeding it, as if admiring their table were the same as setting it with what they needed.

I sat there, damp and chastened, listening to water I had not given.
