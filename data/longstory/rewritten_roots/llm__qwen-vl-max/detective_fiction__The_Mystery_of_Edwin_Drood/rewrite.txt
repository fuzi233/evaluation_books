A cathedral tower—ancient, gray, and square—rises starkly against the sky. But how can it be here? This is no English countryside, no quiet village nestled beneath the shadow of a historic church. Yet there it stands, unmistakable in its solidity, looming over a scene that should never have room for such a relic. Between the viewer and the tower, something juts into the air—a spike of rusted iron, sharp and crude, like a forgotten weapon left to corrode. It shouldn’t exist. And yet, it does.

Could it be the Sultan’s decree? A grim stake meant for the impalement of Turkish thieves, one by one? The thought flares with sudden vividness: cymbals crash, a royal procession winds through the streets—ten thousand scimitars flashing in the sun, thirty thousand dancing girls scattering petals. White elephants, draped in dazzling silks, follow behind, attended by endless servants. Still, the cathedral looms in the background, impossible and unyielding. And still, no body writhes upon the spike.

Wait—could it be nothing more than the twisted iron post of an old bedstead, broken and askew? The idea brings a faint, drowsy laugh. Then, shaking violently from head to toe, a man stirs. His scattered thoughts coalesce, piecing together fragments of dream and reality. He rises slowly, bracing himself on his arms, and looks around.

He is in a cramped, filthy room. Through the torn curtain of a window, the pale light of dawn filters into a squalid courtyard. He lies dressed across a large, unclean bed, atop a frame that has collapsed under weight. Beside him, also lying across the bed, are three others: a Chinaman, a Lascar, and a gaunt woman. The two men are unconscious or half-asleep; the woman holds a pipe, blowing gently at its ember, trying to ignite it.

As she blows, her hand trembling, the red spark glows faintly in the dim light—just enough to reveal her face. “Another?” she rasps, her voice thin and rattling. “Have another?”

The man rubs his forehead, disoriented. “You’ve smoked five since midnight,” she continues, complaining. “Poor me, poor me—my head aches so. Those two came in after you. Ah, poor me—the trade’s slack! Few Chinamen at the docks, fewer Lascars, no ships coming in. Here’s another ready for you, dear. You’ll remember—market price’s terrible high now. Over three shillings and sixpence for a thimbleful! And only I—and Jack Chinaman across the court, but he doesn’t mix it right—know the true secret. You’ll pay up, won’t you, dear?”

She speaks while puffing at the pipe, occasionally sucking in its fumes. “O me, O me—my lungs are weak, my nerves are shot. Nearly ready for you, dear. My hand shakes like it might fall off! I saw you coming, and I said to myself, ‘I’ll have one ready. He’ll remember the price and pay.’ O my poor head!”

She explains how she makes her pipes from old ink bottles, fits mouthpieces, measures the mix with a tiny horn spoon. “I was Heaven-hard drunk for sixteen years before I turned to this,” she mutters. “But this don’t hurt me—not really. And it kills hunger better than food.”

She hands him the nearly empty pipe and collapses back, turning onto her face.

He rises, staggers to the hearth, places the pipe down, pulls back the ragged curtain, and stares at his companions with disgust. He notices that the woman, under the influence, bears a strange resemblance to the Chinaman—same cheekbones, same eyes, same pallor. The Chinaman convulses, snarling as if wrestling some unseen demon. The Lascar giggles, drooling. The woman is silent.

“What visions could *she* see?” the man wonders, turning her face toward him. “Dreams of butcher shops, taverns, debts owed? Of more customers, this bedstead upright again, this court swept clean? What could she rise to—even with all this opium—higher than that? Eh?”

He leans close, listening to her murmurs. “Unintelligible.”

Watching the spasms ripple across her face and limbs—like lightning in a storm—something seizes him. He feels their madness creeping into his own mind. He retreats to a worn armchair by the fire, grips its arms tightly, and sits rigid until the sensation passes.

Then, he returns, lunges at the Chinaman, grabs him by the throat, and turns him violently onto the bed. The man struggles, gasps, protests. “What do you say?” The man waits, alert. “Unintelligible.”

Slowly releasing his grip, he listens to the garbled words, frowning. Then he turns to the Lascar and drags him out onto the floor. As the man falls, he jerks awake, glaring, flailing wildly, drawing a phantom knife. But the knife is not in his hand—it’s in the woman’s dress. She too has stirred, restraining him, pleading. They collapse back side by side.

Enough noise and nonsense—no coherent word has emerged. The watcher nods grimly, a dark smile forming. “Unintelligible,” he repeats.

He places silver coins on the table, finds his hat, stumbles down the broken stairs, greets a rat-infested doorkeeper sleeping in a black hutch beneath them, and steps outside.

Later that afternoon, the same massive gray cathedral tower appears again—this time before a weary traveler. Bells toll for vespers. He hurries toward the open doors, drawn by urgency. Inside, the choir rushes to don their stained white robes. He slips into his own, joins the procession, and enters the chancel.

The sacristan locks the iron gates between sanctuary and chancel. All take their places, bow their heads. Then, the chant begins—“WHEN THE WICKED MAN—” echoing through arches and beams, stirring thunder in the vaulted silence.

Anyone who has watched the solemn flight of the rook—those quiet, clerical birds—may have noticed how, as dusk settles, a pair will break from the flock, circle back, and hover apart, as if their separation carries some hidden significance. So it is after service ends in the ancient Cathedral with its square tower. The choir files out, and the dignified figures of the clergy disperse like rooks at twilight. But two men linger behind, walking together through the echoing Close.

The day is fading, and so is the year. The sun hangs low, fiery yet cold, behind the ruins of the old monastery. The Virginia creeper clinging to the Cathedral wall has shed half its crimson leaves onto the stone path. Rain had fallen earlier, and now a chill trembles across the puddles on the cracked flagstones, and through the great elm trees that weep gusts of wind-blown tears. Their leaves lie thick on the ground. Some drift toward the arched doorway of the Cathedral, seeking shelter—but two men step out and sweep them aside with their feet. One locks the door with a heavy key; the other slips away with a large music book under his arm.

“Mr. Jasper was that, Tope?”
“Yes, Mr. Dean.”
“He stayed late.”
“Yes, Mr. Dean. I waited for him, your Reverence. He’s been taken poorly.”

“Say ‘taken,’ Tope—to the Dean,” murmurs the younger man beside them, with quiet correction. It’s not mere grammar—it’s an unspoken rule: no disrespect to the Dean, even in small things.

Mr. Tope, Chief Verger and guide to visiting groups, ignores the hint with dignified silence.

“And when and how was Mr. Jasper taken—for, as Mr. Crisparkle has said, it’s better to say *taken*—taken?” the Dean repeats, testing the word. “When and how was Mr. Jasper *Taken*?”

“Taken, sir,” Tope replies humbly.

“Breathed to that extent,” the Dean concedes, pleased by the subtle tribute—though he means only to correct. “Would be preferable.”

“Mr. Jasper’s breathing was so short when he came in,” Tope continues, careful not to stumble, “that it distressed him mightily to get his notes out. That may have caused a kind of fit later. His memory grew DAZED.”

He says the word with emphasis, eyes fixed on Mr. Crisparkle, as if daring him to improve upon it. “And a dimness, a giddiness crept over him—strange as I’ve ever seen. Though he didn’t seem to mind much himself. But a little time and water brought him out of his DAZE.”

He repeats the word, proud of his choice, as if to say: *I did it well once—I’ll do it again.*

“And Mr. Jasper has gone home quite himself, has he?” the Dean asks.

“Your Reverence, he has. And I’m glad to see his fire lit, for it’s chilly after the rain. The Cathedral felt damp today, both in air and touch, and he was shivering.”

They all turn toward an old stone gatehouse across the Close, where a fire glows behind a latticed window. Ivy and creeper hang like shadows over the building’s front. As the deep cathedral bell tolls the hour, a breeze stirs the vines, like a ripple of sound passing through tombs and towers, broken niches and defaced statues nearby.

“Is Mr. Jasper’s nephew with him?” the Dean asks.

“No, sir,” Tope answers. “But expected. There’s his shadow between the two windows—the one facing this way, the one looking down into the High Street—drawing his curtains now.”

“Well, well,” the Dean says, brightening as if ending a meeting. “I hope Mr. Jasper isn’t too fond of his nephew. Our affections, however noble, should never master us in this fleeting world—we must guide them. I find I’m reminded of my dinner, hearing the bell. Perhaps, Mr. Crisparkle, you’ll stop by to check on Jasper before going home?”

“Certainly, Mr. Dean. And tell him you asked after him?”

“Ay; do so, do so. Certainly. Wished to know how he was. By all means. Wished to know how he was.”

With a cheerful air, the Dean tips his hat just enough to suit a Dean in good spirits, then strides toward the warm dining room of his red-brick house, where Mrs. Dean and Miss Dean await him.

Mr. Crisparkle—Minor Canon, fair and rosy, always eager to dive headfirst into life’s deeper currents—is a man of cheer and charm. Early riser, musician, scholar, kind-hearted, sociable, contented, and boyish in spirit. Recently promoted from coaching pagan roads to this Christian post—thanks to a grateful patron—Crisparkle now walks toward the gatehouse, en route to his early tea.

“Sorry to hear from Tope that you’ve not been well, Jasper.”

“Oh, nothing, nothing!”

“You look a little worn.”

“Do I? I don’t think so. What’s better, I don’t feel so. Tope made too much of it, I suspect. It’s his trade to exaggerate everything about the Cathedral, you know.”

“I may tell the Dean—I call expressly from the Dean—that you’re all right again?”

The reply, with a faint smile: “Certainly. With my respects and thanks to the Dean.”

“I’m glad to hear you expect young Drood.”

“I expect the dear fellow every moment.”

“Ah! He’ll do you more good than a doctor, Jasper.”

“More good than a dozen doctors. For I love him dearly—and I don’t love doctors, or their remedies.”

Mr. Jasper is dark, about twenty-six, with thick, glossy black hair and whiskers. He appears older than he is—a common trait among dark men. His voice is deep and resonant, his face and figure strong, but his manner is somber. His room reflects that mood—mostly in shadow. Even in sunlight, the grand piano in the recess, the folio music books, the bookshelves, and the unfinished portrait above the fireplace remain untouched by light.

The painting shows a blooming schoolgirl—brown hair tied with a blue ribbon, her beauty marked by a childish, almost babyish, expression of saucy discontent, comically aware of itself. (It’s not art—it’s a crude daub—but clearly painted with humor—perhaps even mockery—of the original.)

“We shall miss you tonight at the ‘Alternate Musical Wednesdays,’ Jasper. But no doubt you’re best at home. Good-night. God bless you! ‘Tell me, shep-herds, te-e-ell me; tell me-e-e, have you seen (have you seen, have you seen) my-y-y Flo-o-ora-a pass this way!’”

Melodiously, Mr. Crisparkle sings the line as he withdraws from the doorway and descends the stairs. Sounds of greeting come from below.

Mr. Jasper starts from his chair and pulls a young man into his arms.

“My dear Edwin!”

“My dear Jack! So glad to see you!”

“Get off your greatcoat, bright boy, and sit down here in your own corner. Your feet aren’t wet? Pull your boots off. Do pull your boots off.”

“My dear Jack, I’m as dry as a bone. Don’t moddley-coddley, there’s a good fellow. I like anything better than being moddley-coddleyed.”

Jasper hesitates, caught between affection and restraint. Then he watches as the youth removes his coat, hat, gloves—each motion drawing his gaze. Once, and forever after, there is a look on Jasper’s face whenever the boy is near—a look of intense, watchful, devoted affection. Never divided. Always focused.

“Now I’m right, and now I’ll take my corner, Jack. Any dinner, Jack?”

Jasper opens a door at the far end of the room, revealing a small inner chamber warmly lit. A woman is setting dishes on the table.

“What a jolly old Jack it is!” the boy exclaims, clapping his hands.

“Look here, Jack; tell me—whose birthday is it?”

“Not yours, I know,” Jasper replies, pausing thoughtfully.

“Not mine, you know? No; not mine, _I_ know! Pussy’s!”

Though the boy’s eyes are fixed on Jasper, something in the elder man’s gaze suddenly sweeps over the portrait on the mantel.

“Pussy’s, Jack! We must drink many happy returns to her. Come, uncle; take your dutiful and sharp-set nephew in to dinner.”

As the boy lays a hand on Jasper’s shoulder, Jasper places his on the boy’s, and they march in like comrades—Marseillaise-style—to the meal.

“And, Lord! Here’s Mrs. Tope!” the boy cries. “Lovelier than ever!”

“Never you mind me, Master Edwin,” she retorts. “I can take care of myself.”

“You can’t. You’re much too handsome. Give me a kiss because it’s Pussy’s birthday.”

“I’d Pussy you, young man, if I was Pussy, as you call her,” she blushes, after being kissed. “Your uncle’s too wrapped up in you. He makes such a fuss, I’d say you think you can summon your Pussys by the dozen.”

“You forget, Mrs. Tope,” Jasper interposes, taking his seat with a genial smile, “and so do you, Ned, that Uncle and Nephew are words prohibited here by mutual agreement. For what we are going to receive, His holy name be praised!”

“Done like the Dean! Witness, Edwin Drood! Please to carve, Jack, for I can’t.”

This remark begins the dinner. Little is said during the meal—nothing of consequence. When the cloth is drawn, walnuts and a decanter of rich sherry appear.

“I say! Tell me, Jack,” the boy begins, “do you really feel as if our relationship divides us at all? _I_ don’t.”

“Uncles, as a rule, Ned, are so much older than their nephews,” Jasper replies, “that I have that feeling instinctively.”

“As a rule! Ah, maybe! But what’s six years or so? And some uncles, in big families, are even younger than their nephews. By George, I wish it were the case with us!”

“Why?”

“Because if it were, I’d take the lead with you, Jack, and be as wise as Begone, dull Care! that turned a young man gray, and Begone, dull Care! that turned an old man to clay.—Halloa, Jack! Don’t drink.”

“Why not?”

“On Pussy’s birthday, and no Happy Returns proposed! Pussy, Jack, and many of ’em! Happy returns, I mean.”

Jasper lays a laughing, affectionate hand on the boy’s hand—as if touching both his head and heart—and drinks the toast in silence.

“Hip, hip, hip, and nine times nine, and one to finish with, and all that, understood. Hooray, hooray, hooray!—And now, Jack, let’s talk about Pussy. Two pairs of nut-crackers? Pass me one, and take the other.”

Crack.

“How’s Pussy getting on, Jack?”

“With her music? Fairly.”

“What a dreadfully conscientious fellow you are, Jack! But _I_ know, Lord bless you! Inattentive, isn’t she?”

“She can learn anything, if she will.”

“_If_ she will! Egad, that’s it. But if she won’t?”

Crack!—from Jasper.

“How’s she looking, Jack?”

Jasper’s face, still concentrated, takes in the portrait as he answers: “Very like your sketch indeed.”

“I _am_ a little proud of it,” the boy says, glancing up at the picture with satisfaction, then shutting one eye and viewing it through a bridge of nut-crackers held aloft. “Not badly hit off from memory. But I ought to have caught that expression pretty well. I’ve seen it often enough.”

Crack!—from Edwin. Crack!—from Jasper.

“In point of fact,” the boy resumes, dipping into his walnut fragments with a piqued air, “I see it whenever I go to see Pussy. If I don’t find it on her face, I leave it there.—You know I do, Miss Scornful Pert. Booh!”

He twirls the nut-crackers at the portrait. Crack! crack! crack. Slowly from Jasper. Crack. Sharply from Edwin. Silence.

“Have you lost your tongue, Jack?”

“Have you found yours, Ned?”

“No, but really—after all—isn’t it unsatisfactory to be cut off from choice in such a matter? There, Jack! I tell you! If I could choose, I’d choose Pussy from all the pretty girls in the world.”

“But you haven’t got to choose.”

“That’s what I complain of. My dead father and Pussy’s dead father must needs marry us together by anticipation. Why the—Devil, I was going to say, if it had been respectful to their memory—couldn’t they leave us alone?”

“Tut, tut, dear boy,” Jasper says gently. “Tut, tut? Yes, Jack, it’s all very well for _you_. _You_ can take it easily. _Your_ life isn: laid out like a surveyor’s plan. _You_ have no suspicion you’re forced upon anyone, nor does anyone suspect you’re forced upon them. _You_ can choose. Life, for _you_, is a plum with the natural bloom on; it hasn’t been wiped clean for _you_—”

“Don’t stop, dear fellow. Go on.”

“Can I have hurt your feelings, Jack?”

“How can you have hurt my feelings?”

“Good Heaven, Jack, you look frightfully ill! A strange film has come over your eyes.”

Jasper, forcing a smile, reaches out his hand—as if to calm fear and buy time. After a moment, he whispers: “I’ve been taking opium for a pain—an agony—that sometimes overtakes me. The medicine’s effects steal over me like a blight or cloud, and pass. You see them passing; they’ll be gone soon. Look away from me. They’ll go faster.”

The boy, pale and scared, obeys—fixing his eyes on the ashes in the hearth. Jasper, gripping his chair, stares into the fire. His body remains rigid. Then, with beads of sweat on his brow and a sharp intake of breath, he returns to normal.

Once settled, the nephew tends to him carefully until he fully recovers.

When Jasper is restored, he places a tender hand on the boy’s shoulder and speaks—not with sorrow, but with a trace of irony:

“There’s said to be a hidden skeleton in every house; but you thought there was none in mine, dear Ned.”

“Upon my life, Jack, I did think so. But when I consider—even in Pussy’s house—if she had one—and in mine—if I had one—”

“You were going to say (but I interrupted) how quiet my life is. No whirl, no uproar, no commerce, no risk, no change. Me, devoted to my art—my business my pleasure.”

“I really was going to say something like that, Jack; but you, speaking of yourself, naturally leave out much I would have included. For instance: your standing as Lay Precentor, your fame with the choir, your independence in this old place, your gift for teaching—why, even Pussy, who hates being taught, says there’s never been such a Master! And your connections.”

“Yes; I saw where you were going. I hate it.”

“Hate it, Jack?” (Bewildered.)

“I hate it. The cramped monotony grinds me away grain by grain. How does our service sound to you?”

“Beautiful! Quite celestial!”

“It often sounds devilish to me. I’m weary of it. The echoes of my voice mock me in the arches. No wretched monk who droned here before me was more tired than I am. He carved demons into the stalls. What shall I do? Must I carve them into my heart?”

“I thought you’d found your niche, Jack,” Edwin says, astonished, leaning forward to lay a hand on Jasper’s knee.

“I know you thought so. They all do.”

“Well, I suppose they do,” Edwin muses. “Pussy thinks so.”

“When did she tell you that?”

“The last time I was here. Three months ago.”

“How did she phrase it?”

“Oh, she said she’d become your pupil, and that you were made for your vocation.”

The boy glances at the portrait. Jasper sees it.

“Anyhow, my dear Ned,” Jasper says, shaking his head with grave cheerfulness, “I must subdue myself to my vocation. It’s much the same outwardly. It’s too late to find another now. This is a confidence between us.”

“It shall be sacredly preserved, Jack.”

“I’ve told you because—”

“I feel it, I assure you. Because we’re fast friends, and because you love and trust me, as I love and trust you. Both hands, Jack.”

They stand, staring into each other’s eyes. Jasper holds the boy’s hands and continues:

“You know now, don’t you, that even a poor monotonous chorister—in his niche—may be troubled by ambition, restlessness, dissatisfaction? What shall we call it?”

“Yes, dear Jack.”

“And you will remember?”

“My dear Jack, I only ask—am I likely to forget what you’ve said with such feeling?”

“Take it as a warning, then.”

As Edwin releases his hands and steps back, he pauses—considering the meaning. Then, touched:

“I’m afraid I’m shallow, Jack, and my headpiece isn’t the best. But I needn’t say I’m young. Perhaps I won’t grow worse as I grow older. At least, I hope I have something within me that feels—deeply feels—the disinterestedness of your laying bare your soul, as a warning to me.”

Jasper’s stillness becomes astonishing—his breathing seems to cease.

“I couldn’t fail to notice, Jack, that it cost you effort. You were moved, unlike yourself. Of course I knew you loved me, but I wasn’t ready for you—so deeply—to sacrifice yourself for me.”

Jasper, returning to life without transition, lifts his shoulders, laughs, and waves his arm.

“No; don’t put it away, Jack. Please don’t. I’m serious. I have no doubt that the state of mind you described brings real suffering. But let me reassure you: I don’t think I’m in danger. In less than a year, I’ll carry Pussy off from school as Mrs. Edwin Drood. Then we’ll go engineering in the East. Though we have our tiffs now—because our love-making lacks excitement, since the outcome is fixed—I have no doubt we’ll get on well. In short, Jack, to return to the old song I quoted at dinner—my wife shall dance, and I will sing, so merrily pass the day. There’s no doubt about Pussy’s beauty—and when you’re good besides, Little Miss Impudence,” he adds, addressing the portrait, “I’ll burn your comic likeness and paint your music-master another.”

Jasper, chin in hand, watches every gesture and expression. He remains motionless after the words are spoken, as if captivated by the boy’s spirit. Then, quietly:

“You won’t be warned, then?”

“No, Jack.”

“You can’t be warned, then?”

“No, Jack, not by you. Besides, I don’t truly think myself in danger. And I don’t like you putting yourself in that position.”

“Shall we walk in the churchyard?”

“By all means. But I must slip out for a moment to the Nuns’ House—leave a parcel. Just gloves for Pussy—as many pairs as she is years old today. Rather poetic, Jack?”

Jasper murmurs, still in thought: “‘Nothing half so sweet in life,’ Ned!”

“Here’s the parcel in my greatcoat-pocket. It must be delivered tonight, or the poetry’s gone. It’s against regulations to call at night, but not to leave a packet. I’m ready, Jack!”

Jasper breaks his pose. They go out together.

For reasons that will become clear as this story unfolds, the ancient Cathedral town at the heart of our tale must bear a name other than its own. Let it be known here as Cloisterham—a city steeped in silence and shadow, where time seems to have paused long ago. Once, perhaps, it was known by the Druids under another name; later, by Romans, Saxons, and Normans—each leaving behind their mark, yet none lasting enough to matter much in the dust-laden pages of its history.

Cloisterham is not a place for those who crave the clamor of modern life. It breathes slowly, like an old man in deep repose. Its streets are narrow, almost too quiet to be real—so still that even the summer wind hesitates before stirring the sunblinds of shopfronts. The air carries a faint earthiness, drawn from the Cathedral crypt below, and everywhere you look, there are traces of monastic days: graves half-buried beneath gardens, stone markers worn smooth by centuries. Children grow salad in the soil once trod by abbots and abbesses; they bake dirt-pies with the bones of nuns and friars. And out in the fields, every ploughman pays tribute to forgotten lords—Archbishops, Treasurers, Bishops—grinding their relics into bread, just as the ogre in fairy tales would grind his guest’s bones.

It is a drowsy, dreamlike city, where people believe change has already passed them by. As if the past were all there ever could be. Yet irony lies in that belief: for while Cloisterham clings to memory, it remains strangely unchanged—not because it’s preserved, but because it has ceased to live.

The houses lean close together, some built around fragments of ancient walls or ruined chapels, as though the city itself were made up of half-remembered dreams. Even the pawnbroker offers no pledges—his shop holds only unredeemed relics: tarnished sugar-tongs, dimmed watches sweating in slow decay, and books so grim they seem to whisper secrets better left unspoken.

Life in Cloisterham stirs only in the quiet growth of vegetables—its most vibrant signs of continuity. Even the tiny theatre, drooping and disused, has a garden where the devil, when he vanishes from stage, descends into hell among scarlet beans or oyster shells depending on the season.

At the very center of this stillness stands the Nuns’ House—a brick edifice older than most stories told within it. Its current purpose is far removed from its legendary past, but the name persists. A polished brass plate gleams upon the gate, announcing: “Seminary for Young Ladies. Miss Twinkleton.” The contrast between the weathered building and the shiny sign has led visitors to compare it to a battered gentleman wearing a modern eye-glass over a blind eye—awkward, yet oddly dignified.

Who were the nuns who once lived here? Did they bow their heads low to avoid beams in cramped chambers? Did they sit by the windows, counting beads not for adornment but penance? Were any walled alive in hidden corners, victims of nature’s restless spirit? Such questions may haunt ghosts—but not Miss Twinkleton. They do not appear in her accounts, nor in her recitals. Her poetry teacher never includes such themes—too dark, too impractical.

Yet Miss Twinkleton herself lives in two worlds. By day, she is the stern, proper headmistress, overseeing studies and manners. But each night, after the girls retire, she transforms—curls smoothed, eyes brightened, voice lightened. Then she speaks of Tunbridge Wells (“The Wells,” she calls it), of scandals whispered only in candlelight, and of one Mr. Porters—“Foolish Mr. Porters,” she sighs, as if remembering a lost romance.

And through both phases, constant and dutiful, is Mrs. Tisher—widow, caretaker, keeper of wardrobes, and rumored former hairdresser. She moves with a soft step, a chronic sigh, and a voice barely above a whisper. The servants believe her husband was once a barber—a fact passed down like folklore.

Among the pupils, none is more cherished than Miss Rosa Bud—known affectionately as Rosebud. She is lovely, childlike, whimsical. There is a peculiar tension around her, however, for it is known that she has been promised to a husband by her late guardian’s will. He is to come of age soon—and then she must marry him.

Miss Twinkleton pretends to mourn this fate, shaking her head over Rosebud’s dimpled shoulders. But the girls see through her: “O, what a pretending old thing Miss Twinkleton is, my dear!” they whisper in bedchamber.

And indeed, the house trembles with excitement whenever the fiancé arrives. It is understood—by all—that he has a right to visit. Should Miss Twinkleton object, she’d be instantly arrested and shipped off to Australia.

On the afternoon following the dinner at the gatehouse, the bell rings again. “Mr. Edwin Drood to see Miss Rosa,” announces the parlour-maid.

Miss Twinkleton, grave-faced, turns to Rosebud: “You may go down, my dear.”

Rosebud descends, watched by every eye. In the parlour, Edwin waits—a young man of sharp features and restless energy. The room is modestly furnished, with only two globes hinting at academic rigor. They suggest that even in private, Miss Twinkleton might be summoned to journey across the world in search of knowledge.

A new maid, unused to seeing the future husband, peeks through the door. She slips down the stairs guiltily as Rosebud enters—her face hidden behind a silk apron.

“Oh! It _is_ so ridiculous!” she exclaims, stopping short. “Don’t, Eddy!”

“Don’t what, Rosa?”

“Don’t come any nearer, please. It _is_ so absurd.”

“What is absurd?”

“The whole thing. To be an engaged orphan… to have girls and servants scuttling about like mice in the wainscot… to be called upon!”

She has a thumb tucked into the corner of her mouth as she speaks.

“You give me an affectionate reception, Pussy, I must say.”

“Well, I will in a minute, Eddy, but I can’t just yet. How are you?” (briefly.)

“I am unable to reply that I am much the better for seeing you, Pussy, since I see nothing of you.”

At this, a bright, pouting eye emerges from under the apron—then vanishes again.

“Oh good gracious! You’ve had half your hair cut off!”

“I should have done better to have had my head cut off,” Edwin says, ruffling his hair fiercely, glaring at the mirror, and stamping impatiently.

“Shall I go?”

“No, not yet. The girls would ask why.”

“Once for all, Rosa, will you uncover that ridiculous little head of yours and give me a welcome?”

The apron comes off. “You’re very welcome, Eddy. There! That’s nice. Shake hands. No, I can’t kiss you—I’ve got an acidulated drop in my mouth.”

“Are you glad to see me, Pussy?”

“Oh yes, dreadfully glad. Go and sit down. Miss Twinkleton.”

It is customary for Miss Twinkleton to make appearances every few minutes—either in person or via Mrs. Tisher—to pretend to search for something. This time, she glides in and out, saying: “How do you do, Mr. Drood? Very glad indeed to have the pleasure. Pray excuse me. Tweezers. Thank you!”

“I got the gloves last evening, Eddy, and I like them very much. They are beauties.”

“Well, that’s something,” Edwin replies, half-grumbling. “The smallest encouragement thankfully received. And how did you pass your birthday, Pussy?”

“Delightfully! Everyone gave me a present. We had a feast. And a ball at night.”

“A feast and a ball, eh? These occasions seem to go off tolerably well without me, Pussy.”

“De-lightfully!” Rosa cries, genuinely delighted.

“And what was the feast?”

“Tarts, oranges, jellies, and shrimps.”

“Any partners at the ball?”

“We danced with one another, of course. But some of the girls made game to be their brothers. It _was_ so droll!”

“Did anybody make game to be—”

“To be you? Oh dear yes!” Rosa laughs. “That was the first thing done.”

“I hope she did it pretty well,” Edwin says, doubtful.

“Oh, it was excellent!—I wouldn’t dance with you, you know.”

Edwin doesn’t catch the point. “Why not?”

“Because I was so tired of you.”

But seeing his displeasure, she adds quickly, pleadingly: “Dear Eddy, you were just as tired of me, you know.”

“Did I say so, Rosa?”

“Say so? Do you ever say so? No, you only showed it. O, she did it so well!”

“It strikes me that she must be a devilish impudent girl,” Edwin remarks.

“And so, Pussy, you’ve passed your last birthday in this old house.”

“Yes!” Rosa clasps her hands, looks down, sighs. “Somehow, I feel as if it would miss me when I’m gone so far away, so young.”

“You seem sorry, Rosa.”

“I am sorry for the poor old place.”

“Perhaps we had better stop short, Rosa?”

She looks up—bright-eyed—then shakes her head, sighs, and looks down again.

“That is to say, is it, Pussy, that we are both resigned?”

She nods.

Then, after a pause, she bursts out: “You know we must be married, and married from here, Eddy, or the poor girls will be so dreadfully disappointed!”

For a moment, there is more compassion in Edwin’s face than love—for both her and himself. He checks it and asks: “Shall I take you out for a walk, Rosa dear?”

Rosa hesitates, then brightens. “Oh yes, Eddy! Let us go for a walk! And I tell you what—we’ll pretend. You shall pretend you’re engaged to someone else, and I’ll pretend I’m not engaged to anyone. Then we shan’t quarrel.”

“Do you think that will prevent our falling out, Rosa?”

“I know it will. Hush! Pretend to look out of window—Mrs. Tisher!”

Through luck, Mrs. Tisher appears—rustling like a ghost in silken skirts. “I hope I see Mr. Drood well; though I needn’t ask, if I may judge from his complexion. I trust I disturb no one; but there _was_ a paper-knife—O, thank you, I am sure!”—and vanishes.

“One other thing you must do, Eddy,” says Rosebud. “As soon as we’re in the street, you must put me outside, and keep close to the house—squeeze against it.”

“By all means, Rosa, if you wish. Might I ask why?”

“Oh! Because I don’t want the girls to see you.”

“It’s a fine day; but would you like me to carry an umbrella?”

“Don’t be foolish, sir. You haven’t got polished leather boots on,” she says, pouting, one shoulder raised.

“Perhaps that might escape notice,” Edwin mutters, looking down at his boots with distaste.

“Nothing escapes their notice, sir. And then I know what would happen. Some of them would start reflecting on me—saying (for _they_ are free) that they’ll never engage themselves to lovers without polished leather boots.”

“Hark! Miss Twinkleton. I’ll ask for leave.”

Outside, Miss Twinkleton is heard asking aloud, as if conversing with no one: “Eh? Indeed! Are you quite sure you saw my mother-of-pearl button-holder on the work-table in my room?”

Rosa requests permission to walk—and it is granted.

Soon, the pair leave the Nuns’ House, taking care to hide Edwin’s ill-fitting boots. Let us hope these precautions serve peace in the future household of Mrs. Edwin Drood.

“Which way shall we take, Rosa?”

“To the Lumps-of-Delight shop.”

“To the—?”

“A Turkish sweetmeat, sir. My gracious me, don’t you understand anything? Call yourself an Engineer and not know _that_?”

“How should I know, Rosa?”

“Because I’m very fond of them. But oh! I forgot what we’re to pretend. Never mind—you needn’t know anything about them.”

So he is dragged gloomily to the shop. Rosa buys sweets, offers some to Edwin (who refuses sharply), then eats them with relish—first removing pink gloves like rose petals, occasionally bringing her fingers to her lips to wipe away the Dust of Delight.

“Now, be a good-tempered Eddy, and pretend. And so you are engaged?”

“And so I am engaged.”

“Is she nice?”

“Charming.”

“Tall?”

“Immensely tall!”

“Must be gawky, I should think,” Rosa comments quietly.

“I beg your pardon; not at all,” Edwin replies, defensive. “What is termed a fine woman; a splendid woman.”

“Big nose, no doubt,” she says again.

“Not a little one, certainly,” he answers quickly—Rosa’s nose being small.

“Long pale nose, with a red knob in the middle. I know the sort,” she nods, enjoying her treat.

“You _don’t_ know the sort, Rosa,” he insists, warming. “Because it’s nothing of the kind.”

“Not a pale nose, Eddy?”

“No.”

He refuses to yield.

“A red nose? Oh! I don’t like red noses. However—she can always powder it.”

“She would scorn to powder it,” Edwin says, growing heated.

“She would? What a stupid thing she must be! Is she stupid in everything?”

“No; in nothing.”

After a pause, Rosa says, watching him closely: “And this most sensible of creatures likes the idea of being carried off to Egypt, does she, Eddy?”

“Yes. She takes a sensible interest in triumphs of engineering skill—especially when they change undeveloped countries.”

“Oh!” Rosa shrugs, laughing softly. “Do you object, sir, to her feeling that interest?”

“Object? My dear Eddy! But really—doesn’t she hate boilers and things?”

“I can answer for her not being so idiotic as to hate Boilers,” he returns sharply. “Though I cannot answer for her views about Things—really not understanding what Things are meant.”

“But don’t she hate Arabs, Turks, Fellahs, and people?”

“Certainly not.”

“Then she must hate the Pyramids? Come, Eddy?”

“Why should she be such a little—tall, I mean—goose, as to hate the Pyramids, Rosa?”

“Oh! You should hear Miss Twinkleton,” Rosa says, nodding, “bore about them. Tiresome old burying-grounds! Isises, Ibises, Cheopses, Pharaohses—who cares? And Belzoni, or somebody, dragged out by the legs, half-choked with bats and dust. All the girls say: Serve him right, and hope it hurt him, and wish he had been quite choked.”

They wander side by side through the Cathedral Close, not arm-in-arm now, each stepping slowly, leaving deeper marks in the fallen leaves.

“Well,” Edwin says after a long silence, “according to custom. We can’t get on, Rosa.”

Rosa tosses her head. “I don’t want to get on.”

“A pretty sentiment, considering.”

“Considering what?”

“If I say what, you’ll go wrong again.”

“You’ll go wrong, you mean, Eddy. Don’t be ungenerous.”

“Ungenerous! I like that!”

“Then I _don’t_ like that, and so I tell you plainly,” Rosa pouts.

“Now, Rosa, I put it to you. Who disparaged my profession, my destination—”

“You’re not going to be buried in the Pyramids, I hope?” she interrupts, arching her eyebrows.

“You never said you were. If you are, why haven’t you mentioned it to me? I can’t find out your plans by instinct.”

“Now, Rosa, you know very well what I mean, my dear.”

“Well then, why did you begin with your detestable red-nosed giantesses? And she would, she would, she would, she WOULD powder it!” she bursts out comically.

“Somehow or other, I never can come right in these discussions,” Edwin sighs, resigning himself.

“How is it possible, sir, that you ever can come right when you’re always wrong? And as to Belzoni, I suppose he’s dead—I’m sure I hope he is—and how can his legs or chokes concern you?”

“It is nearly time for your return, Rosa. We have not had a very happy walk, have we?”

“A happy walk? A detestably unhappy walk, sir. If I go upstairs the moment I get in and cry till I can’t take my dancing lesson, you are responsible, mind!”

“Let us be friends, Rosa.”

“Oh!” she cries, shaking her head and breaking into real tears. “I wish we _could_ be friends! It’s because we can’t be friends that we try one another so. I am a young little thing, Eddy, to have an old heartache; but I really, really have, sometimes. Don’t be angry. I know you have one too often. We should both have done better, if What is to be had been left What might have been. I am quite serious now, and not teasing you. Let each of us forbear, this one time, on our own account, and on the other’s!”

Disarmed by this glimpse of depth in the spoilt child—even if momentarily offended by its implication—he watches her sob gently, hands clutching a handkerchief to her eyes. Then, as she calms, even begins to laugh at herself, he leads her to a bench beneath the elm trees.

[Illustration: Under the trees]

“One clear word of understanding, Pussy dear. I am not clever out of my own line—now I come to think of it, I don’t know that I am particularly clever in it—but I want to do right. There is not—there may be—I really don’t see my way to what I want to say, but I must say it before we part—there is not any other young—”

“Oh no, Eddy! It’s generous of you to ask me; but no, no, no!”

They are near the Cathedral windows. Suddenly, the organ swells, the choir sings—sublime, solemn music fills the air.

Edwin remembers last night’s confidence, thinks how unlike this harmony is to the discord between them.

“I fancy I can distinguish Jack’s voice,” he murmurs.

“Take me back at once, please,” Rosebud urges, laying her hand on his wrist. “They will all be coming out directly. Let us get away. Oh, what a resounding chord! But don’t let us stop to listen—let us get away!”

Her urgency fades once they leave the Close. Now they walk arm-in-arm, gravely, along the High Street toward the Nuns’ House.

At the gate, the street empty, Edwin leans down to Rosebud.

She laughs, remonstrates. “Eddy, no! I’m too sticky to be kissed. But give me your hand, and I’ll blow a kiss into that.”

He gives it. She breathes lightly into it, then holds it, looking into it.

“Now say, what do you see?”

“See, Rosa?”

“Why, I thought you Egyptian boys could look into a hand and see all sorts of phantoms. Can’t you see a happy Future?”

For certain, neither sees a happy Present. The gate opens. One goes in. One goes out.

In the quiet town of Cloisterham, where tradition clings like ivy to ancient stone, there stands a man whose very presence seems to defy reason—Mr. Thomas Sapsea, Auctioneer. If one were to take the jackass as a symbol of self-sufficient ignorance and arrogance—a notion perhaps more conventional than just—then Mr. Sapsea is the purest specimen in all of Cloisterham. He dresses at the Dean’s house, has been bowed to by mistake for the Dean himself, and once addressed in the street as “My Lord,” under the impression he was the Bishop come down unexpectedly without his chaplain. The incident still warms him with pride.

He prides himself not only on these misidentifications but also on his voice, his manner, and his peculiar style. In fact, when selling landed property, he has attempted to mimic the tone of a clergyman, slightly intoning as if from a pulpit, believing it lends him an air of ecclesiastical authenticity. When he concludes an auction, he does so with such solemnity that it appears he is bestowing a benediction upon the assembled brokers—an effect that even the real Dean, a modest and decent man, could never achieve.

Mr. Sapsea commands admiration in the town. A majority, even those who doubt his wisdom, agree: he is a credit to Cloisterham. His qualities? Portentousness and dullness, yes—but also a certain grandeur in his speech, a rolling gait, and hands that move with grave deliberation, as though he might at any moment lay them upon a soul to confirm its faith.

Now well past fifty, perhaps closer to sixty, he carries a rounded figure, his waistcoat marked with horizontal creases, and is said to be wealthy. He votes for the respectable candidates at elections and firmly believes that nothing in him has changed since infancy—except, of course, his stature. How could such a man, so confidently absurd, fail to be a credit to society?

His premises stand on High Street, directly opposite the Nuns’ House. The building dates back to the same era, though patched and altered over time as generations grew less fond of darkness and disease. Above the doorway hangs a wooden effigy—half-life-size—of Mr. Sapsea’s father, depicted in curly wig and toga, mid-sale, hammer raised. The craftsmanship, particularly the little finger and the pulpit, has drawn much praise.

Inside, Mr. Sapsea sits in his dim ground-floor sitting room, facing first his paved backyard and then his railed garden. Before the fire, which burns early but warmly on this autumn evening, rests a bottle of port wine. Beside it are his portrait, his eight-day clock, and his weather-glass—each a silent witness to his defiance: he would stand against mankind, his glass against the sky, and his clock against time itself.

On the table beside him lies a writing desk, inkwell, and paper. Glancing at a scrap of manuscript, he reads aloud to himself, then slowly paces the room, thumbs tucked into his waistcoat armholes, repeating it from memory. Only one word escapes his lips: “Ethelinda.”

Three clean wineglasses sit in a tray. As his serving-maid enters, announcing, “Mr. Jasper is come, sir,” Mr. Sapsea waves her on and draws two glasses forward. “Glad to see you, sir,” he says. “I congratulate myself on having the honour of receiving you here for the first time.”

“Your kindness is noted,” replies Mr. Jasper. “The honour is mine.”

“You say so, sir,” Mr. Sapsea responds, puffing up with dignity, “but I assure you, it is a satisfaction to me to receive you in my humble home. And that,” he adds, letting the sentence hang, “is something I would not say to everyone.”

A silence follows, thick with implication: *You are not like others.*

“I have long wished to know you, Mr. Sapsea,” Jasper says.

“And I,” replies Sapsea, “have long known you by reputation—as a man of taste.” He pours wine into both glasses. “Let me offer you a toast,” he says, filling his own. “When the French come over, may we meet them at Dover!”

It was a patriotic toast from his youth, and he remains convinced it applies to every age.

“You cannot be unaware, Mr. Sapsea,” Jasper observes, watching the auctioneer stretch his legs before the fire, “that you know the world.”

“Well, sir,” chuckles Sapsea, “I think I know something of it. Something of it.”

“That reputation has always intrigued me,” Jasper continues. “For Cloisterham is small. Cooped up here myself, I know nothing beyond it. It feels… insignificant.”

“If I have not traveled abroad,” Sapsea begins, then pauses. “You’ll excuse me calling you young man, Mr. Jasper? You are much my junior.”

“By all means.”

“If I have not gone to foreign lands,” he resumes, “foreign lands have come to me—through business. I’ve seen a French clock. Never met the maker, but I instantly declared, ‘Paris!’ I’ve seen Chinese cups—never set foot in Pekin, yet I pointed and said, ‘Pekin, Nankin, Canton.’ The same with Japan, Egypt, bamboo, sandalwood—everywhere, I put my finger on it. Once, I even touched a piece of ice and said, ‘Spear of Esquimaux make, for half a pint of pale sherry!’”

“A remarkable way,” Jasper remarks, “to acquire knowledge of men and things.”

“Indeed,” Sapsea replies, glowing with pride. “One does not boast of being what one is. But show how one came to be so—and one proves it.”

“Most interesting,” Jasper says. “We were to speak of the late Mrs. Sapsea.”

“We were,” Sapsea agrees, refilling both glasses and securing the decanter. “Before I seek your opinion—on this matter”—he lifts the manuscript—“which is but a trifle, yet required some thought, some fever of the brow—I ought perhaps to describe the character of the late Mrs. Sapsea. She died three-quarters of a year ago.”

Jasper, who had been stifling a yawn behind his glass, now lowers it and forces interest onto his face—though a lingering gape and watery eyes betray his effort.

“Six years ago,” Sapsea begins, “when my mind had expanded—though I won’t say to what it now is, for that might seem excessive—I looked about for a wife. For, as Scripture says, it is not good for man to be alone.”

Jasper nods, as if committing the sentiment to memory.

“Miss Brobity, at that time, ran what I will not call the rival establishment to the Nuns’ House, but rather the other parallel institution downtown. It was whispered she favored attending my auctions—especially on half-holidays or vacation days. It was said she admired my style. And indeed, over time, traces of my manner began appearing in her pupils’ dictation exercises. Even a rumor arose—though I disbelieve it—that one ignorant parent objected by name. But could any sane person truly expose himself to scorn?”

Jasper shakes his head. Not likely.

Sapsea, lost in grandiloquence, seems to refill Jasper’s glass—already full—and does refill his own.

“Miss Brobity,” he continues, “was deeply devoted to Mind. She revered it, especially when launched upon vast knowledge of the world. When I proposed, she was so overwhelmed with awe she could only whisper, ‘O Thou!’—meaning me. Her blue eyes fixed on me, her hands clasped, pallor spread across her features. Though encouraged, she said no more. I arranged for her school to close by private contract, and we became one—nearly so, under the circumstances. Yet she never found words worthy of my intellect. To the end—her liver failing—she addressed me in the same unfinished phrase.”

Jasper closes his eyes as Sapsea deepens his voice. Then, abruptly, he opens them and exclaims—almost as if stopping himself from saying “men!”—“Ah!”

“I have been,” Sapsea declares, stretching his legs and sipping his wine, “what you see me now. A solitary mourner. Wasting my evenings in empty conversation. I do not reproach myself—but sometimes I ask: What if she had married a man more equal to her? Had she not had to look up so high, what might the effect have been on her liver?”

Jasper murmurs, “I suppose it was to be.”

“We can only suppose,” Sapsea agrees. “Man proposes, Heaven disposes. Or perhaps I rephrase it thus—same thought, different words.”

Jasper nods.

“And now, Mr. Jasper,” Sapsea says, producing the manuscript again, “Mrs. Sapsea’s monument has had time to settle. Let me hear your opinion—as a man of taste—on the inscription I have composed. Take it in hand. The layout matters as much as the meaning.”

Jasper takes the paper and reads:

Reverential Wife of

Whose Knowledge of the World,
Though somewhat extensive,
Never brought him acquainted with
More capable of LOOKING UP TO HIM.

And ask thyself the Question,

Sapsea rises, turns his back to the fire, and watches Jasper’s face—waiting for judgment.

Just then, the maid returns: “Durdles is come, sir!”

Sapsea promptly fills the third glass and calls, “Show Durdles in.”

“Admirable!” Jasper says, handing back the paper.

“You approve?” Sapsea asks.

“Impossible not to,” Jasper replies. “Striking, characteristic, complete.”

Sapsea inclines his head, accepting the compliment as due.

Enter Durdles—the stonemason, known throughout Cloisterham. He is entirely of stone: his trade, his clothes, his life. He wears coarse flannel, horn buttons, a yellow neckerchief, a russet hat, and boots stained with mortar. He carries his dinner wrapped in cloth and often eats atop tombstones. He is famed for his work—though he rarely works—and infamous for his drinking—though he is seldom drunk. He knows the Cathedral crypt better than any living man, perhaps even better than any dead one.

It is said he first gained access by sleeping off liquor there, using his position as contractor for repairs. Over time, he has uncovered strange relics—bones, coffins, forgotten inscriptions. He speaks of himself in the third person, perhaps because he forgets his own name, or because Cloisterham has named him so.

Once, he told Tope: “Durdles come upon the old chap—by striking right into the coffin with his pick. The old chap gave Durdles a look, eyes open, as if to say, ‘Is your name Durdles? Why, my man, I’ve been waiting for you a devil of a time!’ Then he turned to powder.”

Always armed with a two-foot rule and a mason’s hammer, Durdles taps and probes the Cathedral walls. Whenever he says, “Tope, here’s another old ’un in here!” Tope reports it to the Dean as a discovery.

Now, Durdles steps in, unimpressed. Sapsea hands him the manuscript.

Durdles pulls out his rule and measures the lines calmly, dusting them with stone grit.

“This is for the monument, is it, Mr. Sapsea?”

“The inscription. Yes.”

“It’ll come in to an eighth of an inch,” Durdles says flatly.

“Your servant, Mr. Jasper. Hope I see you well.”

“How are you, Durdles?”

“I’ve got a touch of the Tombatism on me, Mr. Jasper. But that I must expect.”

“You mean rheumatism,” Sapsea snaps, annoyed.

“No,” Durd5les replies. “Tombatism. Different from rheumatism. Mr. Jasper knows what Durdles means. You walk among tombs before dawn, and keep walking all your life—then you’ll understand.”

“It is a bitter cold place,” Jasper agrees, shivering.

“And if it’s cold for you up in the chancel,” Durdles says, “what is it for Durdles down in the crypt—among damp earth and dead breath? That, you can judge.”

“Is this to be started at once?” Durdles asks.

“As soon as possible,” Sapsea says, eager.

“Then let me have the key,” Durdles replies.

“But it’s not going inside the monument!”

“Durdles knows where it’s to be put,” he insists. “No man better. Ask anyone in Cloisterham whether Durdles knows his work.”

Sapsea rises, unlocks an iron safe, and retrieves a large key.

“When Durdles finishes a job,” he explains, “inside or out, he likes to see it all round. To know it’s doing him credit.”

He slips his rule into a side pocket, opens his coat, and reveals a deep breast pocket. He takes the key and places it carefully within.

“Why, Durdles!” Jasper laughs. “You’re undermined with pockets!”

“And I carry weight in them too,” Durdles says, pulling out two more keys. “Feel these. Hand me Mr. Sapsea’s likewise. Surely this is the heaviest?”

“You’ll find them much of a muchness,” Durdles says. “All belong to monuments. All open Durdles’s work. Durdles keeps the keys of his work mostly. Not that they’re used often.”

“By the way,” Jasper says, idly examining the keys, “I’ve meant to ask—some people call you Stony Durdles. Do you know that?”

“Cloisterham knows me as Durdles,” he grunts.

“I know. But the boys—”

“Oh, if you mind those young imps—” Durdles interrupts, rough and abrupt.

“I don’t mind them any more than you do. But the choir debated the other day—whether ‘Stony’ stood for Tony,” he clinks one key against another. (“Take care of the wards, Mr. Jasper.”) “Or Stephen,” he clinks again. (“You can’t make a pitch pipe of ’em, Mr. Jasper.”) “Or whether it comes from your trade. How does it stand?”

Jasper weighs the keys, looks up, and hands them back with a friendly smile.

But Durdles is gruff. His state is hazy, uncertain, proud, and easily offended. He drops his keys back into his pocket, buttons it shut, takes down his dinner bundle, ties the third key into it like a cold iron sausage, and leaves without a word.

Sapsea then suggests a game of backgammon. With his endless talk, and a supper of cold roast beef and salad, the evening passes pleasantly until late. Sapsea’s wisdom, though diffuse, is far from exhausted. But Jasper hints he’ll return for more. Sapsea lets him go—content, for now, that he has delivered a measure of his genius.

John Jasper walked through the Close on his way home, the moon casting long shadows across the ancient stones. He came to a sudden halt when he saw Stony Durdles leaning against the iron railing of the burial ground, dinner bundle tucked under one arm, as if in deep thought or perhaps simply waiting for something. Beside him, a ragged boy—small, dirty, and with half his teeth missing—was throwing stones at Durdles, who seemed utterly indifferent to the assault.

The stones struck sometimes, missed others, but Durdles did not flinch. The boy, however, was animated by each hit. When one connected, he blew a shrill whistle through the gap in his front teeth—a sound like a tiny horn of triumph. When he missed, he yelped, “Mulled agin!” and adjusted his aim with fierce concentration.

“What are you doing to that man?” Jasper demanded, stepping into the moonlight from the shadowed path.

“Making a cock-shy of him,” the boy replied without pause.

“Give me those stones,” Jasper said, reaching out.

“No, you don’t!” the boy snapped, backing away. “I’ll shove ’em down your throat if you try to grab me. I’ll smash your eye if you come near!”

“Baby-Devil that you are,” Jasper said, voice low and warning, “what has he done to you?”

“He won’t go home,” the boy answered.

“And what is that to you?”

“He gives me a ha’penny to pelt him home if I catch him out too late,” the boy explained, then broke into a chant, stumbling and dancing slightly in his worn boots:

“Widdy widdy wen!
I—ket—ches—Im—out—ar—ter—ten,
Widdy widdy wy!
Then—E—don’t—go—then—I—shy—
Widdy Widdy Wake-cock warning!”

He swept his arm wide on the last word and hurled another stone at Durdles. It was clearly a signal—both a threat and a warning.

Jasper beckoned the boy forward with a slow nod, knowing it was useless to argue or force him. He crossed to the railing where Durdles stood, lost in contemplation.

“Do you know this creature?” Jasper asked, searching for the right word.

“Deputy,” Durdles said, nodding.

“Is that his name?”

“Deputy,” Durdles confirmed.

The boy stepped forward. “I’m man-servant up at the Travellers’ Twopenny in Gas Works Garding,” he declared. “All us man-servants at Travellers’ Lodgings are named Deputy. When we’re chock full and the travellers are all in bed, I come out for my health.”

He stepped back into the road and resumed his aim.

“Widdy widdy wen! I—ket—ches—Im—out—ar—ter—”

“Hold your hand!” Jasper shouted. “Don’t throw while I stand so close—or I’ll kill you! Come, Durdles. Let me walk home with you tonight. Shall I carry your bundle?”

“Not on any account,” Durdles replied, adjusting the pack. “I’ve been making my reflections here when you came up, sir—surrounded by my works, like a poplar author.”

He gestured toward the tombstones within the railings, one by one.

“Your own brother-in-law,” he said, pointing to a sarcophagus gleaming white in the moonlight.

“Mrs. Sapsea,” he added, indicating a monument.

“Late Incumbent,” he said, nodding at a broken column.

“Departed Assessed Taxes,” he announced, pointing to a vase and towel atop what looked like a soap cake.

“Former pastrycook and muffin-maker, much respected,” he said, gesturing to another stone.

“All safe and sound here, sir,” Durdles continued, “and all my work. As for the common folk—bundled up in turf and brambles—the less said, the better. A poor lot. Soon forgotten.”

Jasper glanced back. “That creature, Deputy, is still behind us. Is he following?”

Their relationship was unpredictable. When Durdles turned slowly, as if weighed down by drink, Deputy darted sideways into the road, taking a defensive stance.

“You never cried ‘Widdy Warning’ before you started tonight,” Durdles said suddenly, as though recalling an offense.

“Yer lie, I did,” Deputy shot back, using his only form of polite contradiction.

“Own brother, sir,” Durdles mused, turning again, his anger forgotten as quickly as it had come. “Own brother to Peter the Wild Boy! But I gave him an object in life.”

“At which he takes aim?” Jasper suggested.

“That’s it, sir,” Durdles replied, satisfied. “At which he takes aim. I took him in hand and gave him purpose. Before? A destroyer. What did he do? Destruction. What did he earn? Short terms in Cloisterham jail. Not a soul, not a horse, dog, cat, bird, fowl, pig—nothing he wouldn’t stone, just because he lacked direction. I gave him that direction. Now he earns his honest halfpenny—three penn’orth a week.”

“I wonder he has no rivals,” Jasper said.

“He has plenty,” Durdles replied. “But he stones them all away.”

He paused, considering. “Now, I don’t know what this scheme of mine amounts to. I don’t know what to call it exactly. Isn’t it a sort of National Education?”

“I should say not,” Jasper said.

“I should say not,” Durdles agreed. “Then let’s not give it a name.”

They walked on. Deputy stayed behind, moving in a loose formation, stoning every wall, post, pillar—anything inanimate along the deserted route.

“Anything new down in the crypt, Durdles?” Jasper asked.

“Anything old, I think you mean,” Durdles growled. “It ain’t a place for novelty.”

“I meant any discovery of your own.”

“There’s an old one under the seventh pillar on the left as you go down the broken steps of the little underground chapel. I make him out—so far as I’ve made him out—to be one of them old ones with a crook. From the size of the passages, steps, doors—by which they came and went—those crooks must have been quite common among the old ones. Two of them meeting promiscuously would probably have tangled mitres, I’d say.”

Jasper studied his companion—covered head to foot in mortar, lime, and stone dust—as if sensing a strange romanticism in Durdles’s life.

“Yours is a curious existence,” he said.

Durdles grunted. “Yours is another.”

“Well,” Jasper replied, “since my fate is cast in the same old earthy, chilly, unchanging place—yes. But there’s more mystery and interest in your connection with the Cathedral than in mine. In fact, I’m beginning to think I might ask you to take me on as a student, or free apprentice—let me go with you sometimes, see some of these odd corners where you spend your days.”

Durdles replied vaguely, “All right. Everybody knows where to find Durdles when he’s wanted.”

Which, though not strictly true, was close enough—meaning Durdles could always be found somewhere drifting, somewhere idle.

“What I dwell upon most,” Jasper continued, “is how accurately you seem to locate where people are buried.”

“What’s wrong?” Jasper asked, noticing Durdles had stopped and backed slightly. “That bundle’s in your way. Let me hold it.”

Durdles had been looking for a ledge or corner to set it down. Now relieved, he said, “Just give me my hammer from that bundle. I’ll show you.”

The hammer was handed over. *Clink, clink.*

“Now, lookee here,” Durdles said. “You pitch your note, don’t you, Mr. Jasper?”

“Yes.”

“So I sound for mine. I take my hammer and tap.” (He struck the pavement. Deputy, ever alert, darted further into the road, as if anticipating a blow.)

“Tap, tap, tap. Solid! I go on. Still solid! Tap again. Holloa! Hollow! Tap again, persevering. Solid in hollow! Tap, tap, tap—try it better. Solid in hollow; and inside solid, hollow again! There you are! Old’un crumbled away in stone coffin, in vault!”

“Astonishing,” Jasper breathed.

“I’ve even done this,” Durdles said, pulling out his two-foot rule. Deputy edged closer, now suspecting treasure might be unearthed—and that he might profit from it, or see the discoverers hanged by the neck, on his testimony.

“Say that hammer of mine’s a wall—my work,” Durdles began, measuring on the pavement. “Two, four, and two is six. Six feet inside that wall is Mrs. Sapsea.”

“Not really Mrs. Sapsea?”

“Say Mrs. Sapsea. Her wall’s thicker, but say Mrs. Sapsea. Durdles taps that wall—represented by the hammer—and says, after good sounding: ‘Something betwixt us!’ Sure enough—some rubbish left behind by my men!”

Jasper nodded. “Such accuracy—it must be a gift.”

“I wouldn’t have it at a gift,” Durdles snapped. “I worked it out myself. Durdles comes by his knowledge through grubbing deep for it, and having it up by the roots when it doesn’t want to come.”

“Holloa you, Deputy!”

“Widdy!” the boy shrieked, stepping back.

“Catch that ha’penny,” Durdles said, tossing it. “And don’t let me see you again tonight after we reach the Travellers’ Twopenny.”

“Warning!” Deputy called, catching the coin—his mystic word of agreement.

They crossed what once was the monastery’s vineyard, now overgrown and silent, and entered the narrow back lane where stood the Travellers’ Twopenny—a crooked wooden house of two stories, warped like the morals of its guests. Its porch, once latticed, hung loosely; its garden fence was gone, stripped by travelers who couldn’t bear to leave. The windows bore fragments of red curtains, translucent and muddy in the night, lit by dim rushlights.

A paper lantern above the door announced the inn’s purpose. And as they approached, half a dozen other ragged boys—lodgers or followers—appeared from the shadows, drawn by the scent of Deputy like vultures to carrion. They surged into the moonlight and immediately began stoning each other and Deputy.

“Stop, you young brutes!” Jasper shouted. “Let us pass!”

They ignored him, hurling stones as if it were their duty. This, Jasper knew, was a custom lately accepted by English law—Christians stoned on all sides, as if Saint Stephen’s time had returned.

Durdles merely observed, “They haven’t got an object,” and led the way down the lane.

At the corner, Jasper, furious, stopped and looked back. Silence.

Then—*rattle!*—a stone bounced off his hat. A distant yell echoed: “Wake-Cock! Warning!” followed by a crowing sound, as if a demon-bred rooster had risen from hell.

Jasper turned the corner into safety, and Durdles stumbled into his yard, tripping over rubble as if ready to fall face-first into an unfinished tomb.

Jasper returned by another route to his gatehouse. He unlocked the door quietly and found the fire still burning. From a locked press, he took a peculiar pipe—not for tobacco—but filled it carefully with something else. Using a small tool, he adjusted the contents precisely, then climbed a short staircase to two rooms.

One was his bedroom. The other belonged to his nephew.

Light burned in both.

His nephew slept peacefully, undisturbed.

Jasper stood over him, pipe unlit, staring down with intense focus. Minutes passed. Then, moving silently, he crossed to his room, lit the pipe, and surrendered himself to the visions it summoned—spectres born of midnight smoke and hidden sorrow.

The Reverend Septimus Crisparkle—Septimus, because six younger brothers had preceded him, each vanishing at birth like a flickering candle—had just broken the thin morning ice near Cloisterham Weir with his head, an act that invigorated his blood and set his spirits alight. Now, he stood before a mirror, boxing with methodical precision, gloves on, fists flying in practiced rhythm. The glass reflected not just a man, but a vision of disciplined energy: lean, alert, every movement sharp and deliberate. His face, though serious, carried a quiet warmth, and his eyes sparkled with the kind of vigor that came from both physical exertion and moral purpose.

It was still early. Mrs. Crisparkle—his mother, not wife—had only just descended the stairs, waiting for the tea urn to be ready. At that moment, Septimus paused, gently cupped her face between his gloved hands, and kissed her cheek. Then, as if nothing had happened, he resumed his bout, feinting left, jabbing right, moving with the grace of a seasoned boxer.

“I say,” remarked the old lady, watching him with fond amusement, “every morning of your life, you’ll do it, Sept.”

“Do what, Ma dear?”

“Break the pier-glass or burst a blood-vessel.”

“Neither, please God,” he replied, grinning. “Here’s wind, Ma. Look at this!”

In a final flurry of motion, he landed blows and dodged them with equal skill, culminating in a light tap that sent her cap into Chancery—a term used by experts in the noble art to describe a perfectly executed maneuver. With gentle humor, he released her, quickly stowed his gloves, and turned toward the window, pretending to gaze thoughtfully outside, just as a servant entered.

Breakfast preparations followed. Once seated, the two were alone again. It was a scene worth witnessing—if anyone had been there to see it. Mrs. Crisparkle stood, reciting the Lord’s Prayer aloud, while her son, though nearing forty, bowed his head in reverence, just as he had done when he was four. There was something deeply touching about the ritual, especially given the quiet dignity of the room and the serene presence of the woman who had raised him.

She was no ordinary woman. Her figure was trim, her movements graceful, her dress delicate—like a porcelain shepherdess, painted in soft pastels, lavender and cherry ribbons adorning her cap. Her eyes, clear and bright, never needed spectacles. And her voice, when she spoke, carried a quiet strength that softened even the most rigid soul.

“My Sept!” she would often say—those two words forming the core of her thoughts, her affection, her entire world.

They sat together in Minor Canon Corner, a quiet retreat nestled beneath the shadow of the Cathedral. The place was alive with echoes: rooks cawing in the trees, distant footsteps, the tolling bell, the deep hum of the organ. Yet none of these sounds disturbed the peace. Time seemed to slow here, as if the weight of centuries had settled into stillness. Once, men had fought and raged here; serfs had labored and died; monks had prayed and plotted. But now, all that was gone. Only tranquility remained—and with it, a peculiar kind of melancholy beauty, born of stories told and lives lived out.

Red-brick walls, ivy-clad, latticed windows, panelled rooms, oak beams, and stone gardens where fruit still ripened on ancient monkish trees—these were the surroundings of the Crisparkles’ modest home.

“And what does the letter say, Ma dear?” Septimus asked, cutting into his toast with hearty appetite.

Mrs. Crisparkle had just finished reading it and placed it carefully on the tablecloth. She handed it to him.

Now, here was a curious thing: the old lady prided herself on her sharp eyes, which allowed her to read without glasses. Her son, equally proud of her ability, had invented a pretense—that he himself could not read without spectacles. So, he put on a pair so large and thick they nearly obscured half his face, making eating difficult and reading nearly impossible.

He squinted through them. “‘Haven of Philanthropy, Chief Offices, London, Wednesday.’ ‘Dear Madam—’ I write in the—what’s this? What does he write *in*?”

“In the chair,” said Mrs. Crisparkle.

Septimus removed the glasses, peering at her. “Why, what should he write *in*?”

“Bless me, Sept,” she said, “you don’t see the context! Give it back.”

Gladly, he did. “My sight for manuscript is getting worse every day,” he muttered, rubbing his ear.

She took up the letter again. “‘I write from the chair, to which I shall probably be confined for some hours.’”

Septimus glanced at the row of chairs against the wall, looking mildly exasperated. “Ah. That makes sense.”

“‘We have a meeting of our Convened Chief Composite Committee of Central and District Philanthropists at our Head Haven as above,’”—she read with slight emphasis—“‘and it is their unanimous pleasure that I take the chair.’”

Septimus sighed in relief. “O! If he comes to *that*, let him.”

“‘Not to lose a day’s post, I take the opportunity of a long report being read, denouncing a public miscreant—’”

“It is extraordinary,” interrupted Septimus, setting down his knife and fork, “that these philanthropists are always denouncing someone. And even more extraordinary that they’re always so overflowing with miscreants!”

“‘Denouncing a public miscreant—’”—she continued—“‘to get our little affair of business off my mind. I have spoken with my two wards, Neville and Helena Landless, on the subject of their defective education, and they give in to the plan proposed; as I should have taken good care they did, whether they liked it or not.’”

“And another extraordinary thing,” Septimus added, “is how these philanthropists seem to grab people by the scruff of the neck and shove them into paths of peace.” He paused. “I beg your pardon, Ma dear, for interrupting.”

“‘Therefore, dear Madam, you will please prepare your son, the Rev. Mr. Septimus, to expect Neville as an inmate to be read with, on Monday next. On the same day, Helena will accompany him to Cloisterham, to take up her quarters at the Nuns’ House, the establishment recommended by yourself and son jointly. Please likewise to prepare for her reception and tuition there. The terms in both cases are understood to be exactly as stated to me in writing by yourself, when I opened a correspondence with you on this subject, after the honour of being introduced to you at your sister’s house in town here. With compliments to the Rev. Mr. Septimus, I am, Dear Madam, Your affectionate brother (In Philanthropy), LUKE HONEYTHUNDER.’”

Septimus leaned back. “Well, Ma,” he said after a moment, “we must try it. We’ve room for an inmate, I have time, and inclination too. I confess I’m glad it’s not Mr. Honeythunder himself. Though that seems prejudiced—doesn’t it?—for I’ve never seen him. Is he a large man, Ma?”

“I’d call him large,” she replied slowly, “but his voice is much larger.”

“Than himself?”

“Than anybody.”

“Hah!” said Septimus, finishing his breakfast with less enthusiasm than before.

Mrs. Crisparkle’s sister, a woman of equal refinement and charm, was the childless wife of a clergyman in London. It was during one of her annual visits that Mr. Honeythunder, in his public role as Professor of Philanthropy, had met Mrs. Crisparkle at a charitable event involving orphaned children fed plum buns and overindulged in festive cheer. That was the extent of their acquaintance.

“Of course,” said Septimus, after a pause, “the first thing is to make these young people feel at ease. It’s not disinterested—because we can’t be comfortable unless they are. Jasper’s nephew is here now. Youth takes to youth. He’s a lively fellow. Let’s invite him to meet them at dinner. That’s three. Can’t ask him without Jasper. That’s four. Add Miss Twinkleton and the bride-to-be—six. Add us—eight. Would eight at dinner put you out, Ma?”

“Nine would,” she said, visibly nervous.

“I said eight,” he reminded her gently.

“The size of the table and the room, my dear.”

So it was decided. When Septimus called on Miss Twinkleton to arrange Helena’s stay at the Nuns’ House, the invitations were extended and accepted. Miss Twinkleton briefly regretted leaving behind her globes, but soon relented.

Instructions were sent to Mr. Honeythunder regarding the arrival time of the two pupils, ensuring they reached Cloisterham in time for dinner. Soup stock began to simmer in the kitchen.

Back then, there was no railway to Cloisterham. Mr. Sapsea insisted there never would be—and indeed, he declared there never should be. Yet today, express trains race through the town without stopping, scattering dust as proof of its insignificance. A fragment of the main line once threatened to ruin the money market if it failed, and Church and State if it succeeded—but even that had disrupted traffic enough to force travelers onto a back route marked: “Beware of the Dog.”

To this unlikely path, Septimus walked, awaiting the daily omnibus—a short, squat vehicle piled high with luggage, like a tiny elephant burdened with a castle. As it approached, he barely saw it for the massive passenger seated on the box, elbows squared, hands on knees, pressing the driver into a cramped space, glaring about with a stern, angular face.

“Is this Cloisterham?” the man demanded in a thunderous voice.

“It is,” replied the driver, rubbing his back as if sore, then tossing the reins to the ostler.

“And I never was so glad to see it.”

“Tell your master to widen the box-seat,” the passenger snapped. “Your master is morally bound—and ought to be legally required—to ensure the comfort of his fellow-man.”

The driver ran his hands over his spine as if checking for damage. “Have I sat upon you?” the passenger asked.

“You have,” the driver said flatly.

“Take this card, my friend.”

“I think I won’t deprive you of it,” the driver replied, glancing at it coldly. “What’s the good of it to me?”

“Be a Member of that Society,” the man said.

“What shall I get by it?”

“Brotherhood,” he answered fiercely.

“Thankee,” said the driver slowly. “My mother was content with myself, and so am I. I don’t want no brothers.”

“But you must have them,” the passenger replied, stepping down. “Whether you like it or not. I am your brother.”

“I say!” the driver protested, growing agitated. “Not too far! The worm *will*, when—”

But Septimus stepped in. “Joe, Joe, Joe! Don’t forget yourself, Joe, my good fellow!”

Joe touched his hat, subdued. Septimus then addressed the passenger: “Mr. Honeythunder?”

“That is my name, sir.”

“My name is Crisparkle.”

“Reverend Mr. Septimus? Glad to see you, sir. Neville and Helena are inside. Having recently succumbed under the pressure of my public labours, I thought I’d take a breath of fresh air and come down with them, returning tonight. So you’re the Reverend Mr. Septimus, are you?” He studied Septimus with a look of mild disappointment, twisting a double eye-glass by its ribbon as if warming it.

“Hah! I expected to see you older, sir.”

“I hope you will,” Septimus replied, smiling.

“Eh?”

“Only a poor little joke. Not worth repeating.”

“Joke? Ay; I never see a joke,” Mr. Honeythunder retorted, frowning. “A joke is wasted upon me, sir. Where are they? Helena and Neville, come here! Mr. Crisparkle has come to meet you.”

From the omnibus emerged a striking young man and woman—both unusually handsome, lithe, dark-haired, rich in color. The girl bore a gipsy-like quality, wild yet poised. Both carried an air of defiance and mystery, like hunters—or perhaps hunted. Slender, quick-eyed, half-shy, half-challenging, their expressions held a strange pause, as if caught between flight and attack.

Septimus made mental notes: _Unusually attractive. Wild. Alert. Like captives from a tropical land._ He invited Mr. Honeythunder to dinner—though with unease, knowing the effect such a man might have on his mother.

As they walked through the ancient streets, the siblings marveled at the Cathedral and the ruins of the Monastery, their wonder genuine, almost foreign. Mr. Honeythunder, meanwhile, strode down the middle of the road, brushing aside pedestrians, loudly outlining a scheme to arrest every unemployed person in the UK, imprison them, and force them into philanthropy under threat of death.

Mrs. Crisparkle, already overwhelmed, found herself facing a monstrous intrusion. Mr. Honeythunder was not merely loud—he was a blight on civility. A boil on society’s face, he became a raging wen in Minor Canon Corner. His philanthropy was explosive, almost violent. It wasn’t kindness—it was conquest. He demanded obedience, conformity, submission. You abolished war—but only after arresting every military officer who had served honorably. You abolished capital punishment—but only after executing all those who supported it. You sought peace—but only by eliminating anyone who refused to join your cause.

Above all, you could not act alone. You had to register at the Haven of Philanthropy, pay dues, wear the badge, and speak only what the committee dictated. Their resolutions always ended with: “That this assembled Body views with indignant scorn and contempt, not unmixed with utter detestation and loathing abhorrence…”—followed by a list of sins committed by non-members.

Dinner was a disaster. Mr. Honeythunder dominated the table, blocking service, shouting over everyone, treating the guests like a mob. He seized Septimus as a target, accusing him of fraud, hypocrisy, cowardice—though the man had said nothing. The minor canon looked bewildered, then indignant. Mrs. Crisparkle bristled, tears welling. The others froze, speechless.

Yet when it came time for Mr. Honeythunder to leave, the mood shifted. Coffee arrived an hour early. Septimus checked his watch constantly. The young people swore the clock struck three-quarters when it struck one. Miss Twinkleton claimed the walk to the omnibus took twenty-five minutes when it was five. Everyone rushed him into his coat, shoved him into the moonlight as if he were a fugitive being sympathetically escorted. Septimus and Neville practically locked the door behind him, leaving him with half an hour to spare—just in case he caught cold.

“I know very little of that gentleman, sir,” Neville said to the Minor Canon as they turned back from their walk. “You know very little of your guardian?” the Minor Canon repeated.

“Almost nothing!” Neville replied.

“How came he—” the canon began.

“To be my guardian? I’ll tell you, sir. I suppose you know that my sister and I come from Ceylon?”

“Indeed, no.”

“I wonder at that. We lived there with a stepfather after our mother died when we were children. Our life has been wretched. She made him our guardian—and he was a miserly brute who grudged us food, clothes, even warmth. At his death, he passed us on to this man—no better reason than that he was a friend or acquaintance whose name often appeared in the papers, catching the old man’s attention.”

“That was lately, I suppose?”

“Quite lately, sir. This stepfather was cruel as well as harsh. It’s well he died when he did—or I might have killed him.”

Mr. Crisparkle stopped short in the moonlight, staring at his pupil in astonishment.

“I surprise you, sir?” Neville asked, his tone softening into something almost humble.

“You shock me—unspeakably,” the canon answered.

Neville lowered his head as they walked on, then spoke again. “You never saw him beat your sister. I’ve seen mine beaten more than once—and I never forgot it.”

“Nothing,” said Mr. Crisparkle, his voice rising with indignation despite himself, “not even a beloved sister’s tears under such treatment—could justify those words.”

“I’m sorry I used them, especially to you, sir,” Neville said. “I beg your pardon. But let me correct one thing: you mentioned your sister’s tears. My sister would have let him tear her apart before she’d let him think she could shed a single tear.”

Mr. Crisparkle paused, reviewing what he knew of the girl in his mind. He wasn’t surprised by the claim—and didn’t doubt it.

“Perhaps you’ll think it strange,” Neville said hesitantly, “that I should so soon ask you to allow me to confide in you, and to hear a word or two in my defence?”

“Defence?” Mr. Crisparkle echoed.

“You’re not on your defence, Mr. Neville.”

“I think I am, sir. Or at least, I know I would be if you knew me better.”

“Well,” said the canon, “what if I leave you to find it out?”

“Since it is your pleasure, sir,” Neville replied, his tone shifting sharply to sullen disappointment, “since you prefer to stop me now, I must submit.”

There was something in that brief reply that unsettled the conscientious man. It hinted that he might, without meaning to, turn aside a trust that could help shape a troubled young mind—and perhaps even hinder his own ability to guide it.

They were near the lights of the canon’s house now, and he stopped.

“Let us turn back and take another walk,” he said. “You may not have time to finish what you wish to say. You think I mean to stop you—but I don’t. Quite the contrary. I invite your confidence.”

“You’ve invited it, sir,” Neville said, “without knowing it—ever since I arrived. I say ‘ever since,’ as if I’d been here a week. The truth is, my sister and I came here to quarrel with you, insult you, and leave again.”

“Really?” Mr. Crisparkle said, at a loss for words.

“We couldn’t know what you were like beforehand, could we?”

“Clearly not.”

“And since we’d never liked anyone else we’d met, we decided not to like you.”

“Really?” he said again.

“But we do like you, sir. And we see a difference between your home, your welcome, and anything we’ve ever known. That—and being alone with you—and everything around us seeming so quiet after Mr. Honeythunder’s departure—and Cloisterham, so ancient and solemn, bathed in moonlight—these things made me open my heart.”

“I understand, Mr. Neville. And it’s good to listen to such influences.”

“In speaking of my flaws, sir,” Neville continued, “please don’t assume I speak of my sister. She rose above our misery far more than I did—just as that cathedral tower rises above those chimneys.”

Mr. Crisparkle wasn’t entirely convinced of that comparison.

“From my earliest memory,” Neville went on, “I’ve had to suppress a deadly hatred. That made me secretive, resentful. I’ve always been held down by force. In my weakness, I learned to lie, to stoop. I was denied education, freedom, money, decent clothes, the basic joys of childhood, the simple privileges of youth. This has left me lacking in emotions, memories, instincts—I can’t even name what I’m missing. You’ve had these things to work with in others, but I’ve had none.”

“It’s true,” Mr. Crisparkle thought, “but not encouraging.”

“And finally, sir,” Neville added, “I was raised among submissive servants of a lower race. I may have picked up some of their traits. Sometimes… I wonder if there’s a trace of the tiger in their blood in me.”

“Like that remark just now,” Mr. Crisparkle mused.

“In closing, sir—about my sister: we are twins. And though our suffering often broke me, it never broke her. When we ran away—four times in six years, only to be brought back and punished—each escape was planned and led by her. She dressed as a boy, showing courage beyond her years. I remember the first time—we were seven. When I lost the pocketknife she was supposed to use to cut her hair short, she tried desperately to tear it out or bite it off.”

Neville paused. “That’s all I have to say, sir—except that I hope you’ll bear with me.”

“Of that,” Mr. Crisparkle said, “you may be certain. I don’t preach unless I must, and I won’t repay your honesty with a sermon. But I beg you to remember: if I’m to help you, it must be with your help. And you can only give that by seeking strength from Heaven.”

“I will try, sir.”

“And I will try, too. Here’s my hand on it. May God bless our efforts.”

They stood at the door now, and laughter and voices drifted from within.

“We’ll take one more turn,” Mr. Crisparkle said. “I want to ask you something. When you said you’d changed your mind about me, you included your sister?”

“Without question, sir.”

“Excuse me,” he said, “but I don’t believe you’ve spoken to her since I met you. Mr. Honeythunder was eloquent—but perhaps I may say, without ill will, that he took over the conversation. Might you not have spoken for her without proper authority?”

Neville shook his head, a proud smile touching his lips.

“You don’t know yet, sir, what complete understanding exists between my sister and me—even without a word spoken, perhaps not even a glance. She feels exactly as I’ve described, and she knows I’m speaking for both of us tonight.”

Mr. Crisparkle looked at him, skeptical—but the young man’s face held such firm conviction that he turned his gaze to the pavement and fell into thought until they reached the door again.

“I’ll ask for one more turn, sir,” Neville said, a flush rising in his cheeks. “But for Mr. Honeythunder’s—what did you call it?—eloquence?”

“I—yes, I called it eloquence,” Mr. Crisparkle admitted.

“But for that,” Neville continued, “I might not have needed to ask you what I’m about to. This Mr. Edwin Drood—sir, is that his name?”

“Quite correct,” said Mr. Crisparkle. “D-r-double o-d.”

“Did he—did he study under you, sir?”

“Never,” the canon replied. “He visits here to see his relation, Mr. Jasper.”

“Is Miss Bud also related to him, sir?”

Mr. Crisparkle paused, wondering why the question carried such sudden haughtiness. Then he explained aloud what he knew of the betrothal.

“Oh! _That’s_ it, is it?” Neville murmured. “Now I understand his air of ownership!”

It was said quietly, almost to himself—so much so that Mr. Crisparkle instinctively felt it would be rude to acknowledge it, like reading over someone’s shoulder.

A moment later, they entered the house. Mr. Jasper sat at the piano, accompanying Miss Rosebud as she sang. He played without sheet music, and she was careless, often going wrong—so he watched her lips closely, gently guiding the melody with subtle hand movements and soft nods.

Helena stood nearby, her arm around the singer, her eyes fixed more on Jasper than on the song. The moment she saw Neville, an instant recognition passed between them—one that Mr. Crisparkle noticed, or thought he did—a flash of silent understanding.

Neville took his place leaning against the piano, admiring the performance. Mr. Crisparkle sat beside the china shepherdess. Edwin Drood gallantly opened and closed Miss Twinkleton’s fan, while she passively claimed the role of exhibitor, much like Mr. Tope, the Verger, claimed the Cathedral service.

The song was sorrowful—a farewell tune—and the young voice trembled with emotion. As Jasper watched her lips and whispered the notes, her voice faltered. Suddenly, she burst into tears and shrieked, covering her eyes:

“I can’t bear this! I’m frightened! Take me away!”

In one swift motion, Helena caught her and laid her gently on the sofa. Kneeling beside her, one hand over her mouth, the other raised in warning, Helena said to the room:

“It’s nothing. It’s over. Don’t speak to her—not for a minute—and she’ll be fine.”

Jasper’s hands hovered above the keys, still poised as if ready to continue. He remained still, not turning, even as everyone else shifted and reassured each other.

“Pussy’s not used to an audience,” Edwin said. “She got nervous. Besides, Jack, you’re so strict—you make her afraid of you. No wonder.”

“No wonder,” Helena echoed.

“There, Jack,” Edwin said, “you hear? You’d be afraid of him too, wouldn’t you, Miss Landless?”

“Not under any circumstances,” Helena replied firmly.

Jasper lowered his hands, glanced over his shoulder, and thanked Miss Landless for defending his character. Then he began playing again—though silently, without striking the keys—while the little singer was taken to the window for fresh air.

When she returned, Jasper was gone.

“Jack’s gone, Pussy,” Edwin told her. “I’m half afraid he didn’t like being blamed for scaring you.”

She said nothing, but shivered as if chilled.

Miss Twinkleton now declared it late, and Mrs. Crisparkle, noting they were outside the Nuns’ House walls, reminded them of their duty—to form future wives and mothers of England (the last part in a whisper, as if confidential). They must set a better example than rakish habits.

Wrappers were fetched, and the two young men offered to escort the ladies home. Soon, the gate of the Nuns’ House closed behind them.

The boarders had retired. Only Mrs. Tisher remained, watching in solitude for the new pupil. Since her room was next to Rosa’s, little introduction was needed before Rosa was entrusted to her care and left for the night.

“This is a blessed relief, my dear,” Helena said.

“I’ve been dreading all day that I’d be cornered at this hour.”

“There aren’t many of us,” Rosa replied. “And we’re kind girls—at least the others are. I can vouch for them.”

“I can vouch for you,” Helena laughed, studying Rosa’s delicate face with her dark, fiery eyes and gently stroking her small figure. “You’ll be my friend, won’t you?”

“I hope so. But the idea of being a friend to you seems absurd.”

“Why?”

“Oh, I’m so tiny, and you’re so strong, so womanly. You seem to have power enough to crush me. I shrink into nothing beside you.”

“I’m neglected,” Helena said. “Untrained, ignorant, ashamed of what I don’t know.”

“And yet you tell me everything!” Rosa said.

“My pretty one, how can I help it? There’s a fascination in you.”

“Oh? Is there?” Rosa pouted, half-joking, half-serious. “What a pity Master Eddy doesn’t feel it more!”

Of course, their relationship had already been discussed in Minor Canon Corner.

“Why, surely he loves you with all his heart!” Helena exclaimed, her tone fierce with urgency.

“Eh? Oh, well, I suppose he does,” Rosa said, pouting again. “I have no right to say he doesn’t. Perhaps it’s my fault. Perhaps I’m not nice enough to him. I don’t think I am. But it’s so ridiculous!”

“What’s ridiculous?” Helena asked.

“We are,” Rosa said simply. “We’re a ridiculous couple. And we’re always arguing.”

“Why?”

“Because we both know we’re ridiculous, my dear!”

Rosa said it as if it settled everything.

Helena stared at her for a moment, then impulsively reached out both hands.

“You’ll be my friend and help me?”

“Indeed, my dear,” Rosa replied, her voice tender and childlike, “I’ll be the best friend such a little thing can be to such a noble creature as you. And please be a friend to me—I don’t understand myself. I need someone who can.”

Helena kissed her and held her hands tightly.

“Who is Mr. Jasper?” she asked.

Rosa turned her head away. “Eddy’s uncle. My music master.”

“You don’t love him?”

“Ugh!” She covered her face, trembling. “You know he loves you?”

“Oh, don’t, don’t, don’t!” Rosa cried, dropping to her knees and clinging to Helena. “Don’t tell me! He terrifies me. He haunts my thoughts like a ghost. I feel I’m never safe. I feel he can walk through walls when he’s spoken of.”

She actually glanced behind her, as if expecting to see him there.

“Try to tell me more, darling.”

“Yes, I will. Because you’re strong. But hold me—stay with me.”

“My child,” Helena said, “you speak as if he’s threatened you in some terrible way.”

“He’s never spoken to me about it. Never.”

“What has he done?”

“He’s made me his slave with his eyes. He’s forced me to understand him—without a word. He’s made me keep silence—without a threat. When I play, he never looks away from my hands. When I sing, his eyes never leave my lips. When he corrects me, strikes a note, plays a passage—he’s in the sound, whispering that he pursues me as a lover, commanding me to keep his secret. I avoid his eyes—but he forces me to see them anyway. Even when his eyes glaze over, when he seems lost in a terrible dream where he threatens most—he makes me know it, and know he sits beside me, more terrifying than ever.”

“What is this threat, pretty one? What does he threaten?”

“I don’t know. I’ve never dared to wonder.”

“And was this all tonight?”

“This was all—except that tonight, when he watched my lips so closely, I felt terrified, ashamed, deeply hurt. It was as if he kissed me—and I couldn’t bear it. That’s why I screamed.”

“You must never tell anyone. Eddy adores him. But you said tonight you’d never fear him—under any circumstances. That gives me courage to tell only you.”

“Hold me. Stay with me. I’m too frightened to be left alone.”

The lustrous gipsy face drooped over the clinging arms, wild black hair falling like a shield over the fragile form. A spark of fire smoldered in her dark eyes—now softened with compassion and awe.

Let whomsoever it concerned look well to it.

The two young men stood before the courtyard of the Nuns’ House, watching as the women they were escorting entered the gate. The brass doorplate—old, dented, and defiant—seemed to glare at them like a challenge, its cold surface reflecting the moonlight with an almost mocking stillness. They exchanged glances, then turned away together, walking slowly down the quiet street.

“Will you be staying long in Cloisterham, Mr. Drood?” Neville asked.

“No,” Edwin replied carelessly. “I leave for London tomorrow. But I’ll be back from time to time—until next Midsummer. After that, I’m done with this place, and England too. For many years, I expect.”

“You’re going abroad?”

“Going to wake up Egypt a little,” Edwin said, his tone laced with condescension.

“Are you reading?” Neville pressed.

“Reading?” Edwin repeated, a flicker of disdain crossing his face. “No. I’m doing. Working. Engineering. My father left me a share in the firm he founded—part of my inheritance is tied up in it. Until I come of age, I’m under their care. Jack—you met him at dinner—is my guardian and trustee.”

“I heard from Mr. Crisparkle about your other good fortune,” Neville said cautiously.

“What do you mean by ‘other good fortune’?” Edwin snapped.

Neville had approached the question with a watchful step—forward but wary, as if both hunter and hunted. Edwin’s reply was sharp, abrupt, lacking any trace of courtesy.

They stopped. A tense silence passed between them.

“I hope,” Neville said, “that I’ve not offended you by mentioning your betrothal.”

“By George!” Edwin burst out, resuming their walk at a faster pace. “Everyone in this gossiping old town talks about it. I swear someone should open a pub and hang my portrait over the door—‘The Betrothed’s Head.’ Or maybe Pussy’s portrait. One or the other.”

“It wasn’t my place to bring it up,” Neville began, “but Mr. Crisparkle spoke openly about it, and I thought—”

“That’s true,” Edwin cut in. “You’re not accountable for what he said. But you *are* accountable for saying it to me. And you did so, assuming I’d be proud of it.”

There were two invisible forces shaping their words: one born of envy, the other of pride. Neville already admired Little Rosebud and resented how lightly Edwin treated her. Edwin, meanwhile, had begun to feel the weight of Helena Landless—her grace, her spirit—and now found himself irritated by the idea that her brother could so easily dismiss him, push him aside.

But the moment demanded a response.

“I don’t know,” Edwin said, adopting the formal address used by Mr. Crisparkle, “that people who are proudest of something usually talk about it most. Nor that they enjoy others talking about it. I live a busy life, and I speak under correction—by you readers, who, no doubt, know everything.”

By now, both were simmering. Neville wore his anger openly; Edwin masked his with irony, pausing occasionally to admire the moonlit shadows as though genuinely enchanted.

“It doesn’t seem very civil,” Neville said at last, “to mock a stranger who’s just arrived, trying to make up for lost time.”

“But then,” Edwin retorted, “I wasn’t raised among Heathens. My ideas of civility were formed in a world where people mind their own business.”

“Perhaps,” Neville shot back, “the best kind of civility is precisely that. If you’ll set me an example, I’ll follow.”

“And yet,” Edwin replied, stopping abruptly, “you take on far more than your share. In the part of the world I come from, such behavior would get you called to account.”

“By whom?” Neville challenged.

Before Edwin could answer, a hand gripped his shoulder—a sudden, startling touch. Jasper stood between them, having followed them unseen from the shadowed side of the road.

“Ned, Ned, Ned!” he said, his voice soft but firm. “We must have no more of this. I don’t like it. I heard high words. Remember, my dear boy—you’re host tonight. You belong here, in a sense. You represent the place to a guest. Mr. Neville is a stranger. You must honor the obligations of hospitality.”

Then, placing his left hand gently on Neville’s shoulder, he stepped forward, walking between them, hands resting on each of their shoulders like a bridge.

“And you, Mr. Neville,” Jasper continued, “I ask you to govern your temper. Now, what is amiss? But why ask? Let there be nothing amiss. We are all three on good terms, are we not?”

A silent battle of wills passed between the two young men—each waiting for the other to speak first. Finally, Edwin broke in.

“As far as I’m concerned, Jack, there’s no anger.”

“Nor in me,” Neville said, though less freely, less confidently.

“But,” he added, “if Mr. Drood knew what lies behind me—what I’ve endured—far away from here—he might understand why sharp words wound me so deeply.”

Jasper nodded slowly. “Then let us not complicate our understanding. Let us not say anything that sounds like reproach. It wouldn’t be generous. Look—Ned has no anger. And you, Mr. Neville?”

“None at all, Mr. Jasper,” Neville said, though not quite with conviction.

“All right then,” Jasper declared. “My bachelor gatehouse is just a few yards ahead. The heater’s lit, wine and glasses are ready. Not far from Minor Canon Corner. Ned, you’re leaving tomorrow. Let’s carry Mr. Neville in with us for a stirrup-cup.”

“With all my heart, Jack,” Edwin said.

“And with all mine, Mr. Jasper,” Neville replied—though he felt reluctant. He sensed he’d lost control. Edwin’s calm only inflamed him further, making him burn hotter.

Jasper walked ahead, guiding them both, his presence steady and composed. He hummed a tune softly, turning the refrain of a drinking song into a gentle rhythm. Together, they moved toward his rooms.

Inside, the fire crackled. As Jasper lit a lamp, the room brightened—revealing, above the mantelpiece, a portrait. It was not a flattering likeness. Yet it was unmistakable: a painted image of a woman with dark eyes and a serene expression.

Both young men glanced at it—briefly, awkwardly—then looked away. But Jasper noticed.

“You recognize that picture, Mr. Neville?” he asked, dimming the lamp to highlight the painting.

“I do,” Neville said, “but it does the original no justice.”

“Oh, you’re too hard on it,” Jasper said. “It was painted by Ned. He gave it to me as a gift.”

“I’m sorry,” Neville said, genuinely. “If I’d known I was speaking to the artist—”

“A joke, sir,” Edwin interrupted, yawning with exaggerated indifference. “Just a bit of fun with Pussy’s features. One day, if she’s good, I’ll paint her seriously.”

He leaned back in his chair, hands clasped behind his head, radiating a careless confidence that grated on Neville’s nerves.

Jasper watched them closely, then turned to prepare mulled wine at the hearth. The process seemed endless—stirring, adding spices, pouring.

“I suppose,” Edwin said, sensing Neville’s irritation, “if you painted your lady love—”

“I can’t paint,” Neville interrupted sharply.

“That’s unfortunate,” Edwin said. “But if you could, I imagine you’d make her Juno, Minerva, Diana, Venus—all in one. Eh?”

“I have no lady love,” Neville said. “And I can’t say.”

“If I tried,” Edwin said, a spark of youthful arrogance flaring, “to paint Miss Landless—in earnest, mind you—I’d show you what I could do!”

“Only if she agreed to sit,” Neville countered. “And she never will. So I suppose I’ll never see your masterpiece. I’ll have to bear the loss.”

Jasper returned, filled large goblets—one for each man—and handed them over. Then he poured his own.

“Come, Mr. Neville,” he said. “Let’s toast my nephew, Ned. His foot is in the stirrup—metaphorically—so our stirrup-cup is for him. Ned, my dearest fellow, my love!”

He drained nearly half his glass. Neville followed. Edwin murmured, “Thank you both,” and emptied his.

“Look at him,” Jasper said, gesturing proudly. “See how he lounges, so easy, so at ease! The world is open before him. A life of work, excitement, change—domestic peace, love! See how little he cares for it all?”

Edwin’s face flushed with wine. So did Neville’s.

“He hardly seems to notice the golden fruit hanging ripe for him,” Jasper continued, teasing. “And yet—consider the contrast, Mr. Neville. You and I have no such prospects. No stirring work, no excitement, no domestic joy. Just the same dull round, day after day.”

“Upon my soul, Jack,” Edwin said, smugly, “I feel almost ashamed to have it so smooth. But you know what I know—Jack. It’s not as easy as it looks. May it, Pussy?” He snapped his fingers at the portrait. “We’ve got to hit it off yet, haven’t we, Pussy? You know what I mean, Jack.”

His speech slurred slightly. Jasper watched, calm and collected. When Neville spoke, his voice too was thick.

“It might have been better,” he said defiantly, “if Mr. Drood had known hardship.”

“Why?” Edwin asked, turning only his eyes.

“Because it might have made him more aware,” Neville said, “that his good fortune isn’t necessarily earned by his own merit.”

Jasper looked from one to the other, silently encouraging the exchange.

“Have *you* known hardship?” Edwin asked, sitting upright.

“Yes,” Neville said.

“And what have they taught you?”

“I told you earlier,” Neville insisted.

“You didn’t,” Edwin said.

“I did. That you take too much upon yourself.”

“And you added something else?”

“Yes. That in my part of the world, you’d be held accountable.”

“Only there?” Edwin laughed, contemptuous. “A long way off, I believe. Yes—safe distance.”

“Say here, then!” Neville cried, rising. “Say anywhere! Your vanity is unbearable. You talk as if you’re some rare gem, when you’re just a common boaster. You’re a common man, and a common braggart.”

“Pooh, pooh,” Edwin replied, furious but controlled. “How should you know? You may recognize a black common man—or a black common braggart—but you’re no judge of white ones.”

The insult struck like lightning. Neville’s rage exploded. He hurled the dregs of his wine at Edwin—then lifted the goblet to throw it too.

But Jasper caught his arm just in time.

“Ned, my dear fellow!” he shouted. “I beg you—be still!”

Chairs overturned. Glasses clattered. The room erupted.

“Mr. Neville, for shame!” Jasper commanded. “Give me that glass. Open your hand. I *will* have it!”

Neville shrugged him off, trembling with fury. For a moment, he stood frozen—goblet raised. Then, with a roar, he smashed it against the grate. Shards flew like rain.

He stormed out.

Outside, the world spun. Nothing was steady. The night air felt like blood—red, swirling, alive. He stood in the middle of chaos, breath ragged, heart pounding like a hammer.

But nothing came. The moon hung high, cold and indifferent, as if watching a corpse after a fit.

He clutched his head, staggered, then forced himself to move.

Half-conscious, he remembered the bolted door—the sound of it locking behind him. He was shut out, like a beast.

What now?

Thoughts of the river flashed through his mind—wild, desperate plans. But the moonlight on the Cathedral, the graves, the memory of his sister… and the pledge he’d given to the man who had won his trust—these pulled him back.

He turned toward Minor Canon Corner, knocked softly.

Mr. Crisparkle, accustomed to late-night piano practice, opened the door with candle in hand. His cheerful face fell.

“Mr. Neville! In this state! Where have you been?”

“To Mr. Jasper’s. With his nephew.”

“Come in.”

The Minor Canon supported him—scientifically, firmly—leading him into his bookroom, closing the door.

“I’ve begun dreadfully,” Neville said. “I’ve begun badly.”

“Too true,” Crisparkle replied. “You’re not sober.”

“I’m afraid not, sir. Though I’ve had very little to drink. It overwhelmed me suddenly.”

“Mr. Neville,” the canon said gently, “I’ve heard that before.”

“I think—my mind is confused—but I think the same applies to Mr. Jasper’s nephew.”

“Very likely,” Crisparkle said dryly.

“We quarreled, sir. He insulted me terribly. He stirred that tigerish blood I told you about—before even I realized it.”

“Mr. Neville,” Crisparkle said, shaking his head, “please don’t speak with that clenched hand. Unclench it.”

Neville obeyed instantly.

“He goaded me,” he said, “beyond endurance. I don’t know if he meant to at first—but he did. At the end, he certainly did. In short, sir,” he burst out, “in the passion he roused in me, I would have killed him—if I could. I tried to.”

“You’ve clenched it again,” Crisparkle observed quietly.

“I beg your pardon, sir.”

“You know your room. But I’ll accompany you once more. Your arm, please. Softly—the house is asleep.”

With practiced skill, Crisparkle guided him, supporting him like a seasoned expert, to his room.

There, Neville collapsed into a chair, arms thrown across the table, head resting upon them—face pale, full of shame.

Crisparkle hesitated, then turned back. He touched the doorframe gently.

“Good night.”

A sob was the only reply.

Another knock came. Crisparkle opened the door to find Jasper standing there, holding Neville’s hat.

“We’ve had an awful scene,” Jasper whispered.

“Has it been so bad?”

“Murderous.”

“Don’t use such strong words,” Crisparkle said.

“He might have laid my dear boy dead at my feet. It’s not his fault he didn’t. But had I not been swift and strong—through God’s mercy—he would have cut him down on my hearth.”

The words struck deep.

“Ah,” Crisparkle thought, “his own words.”

“Seeing what I’ve seen,” Jasper went on, “and hearing what I’ve heard—I shall never rest easy when those two meet without interference. It was terrible. There’s something of the tiger in his dark blood.”

“Ah,” Crisparkle thought again, “so he said.”

“You, my dear sir,” Jasper continued, taking his hand, “even you, have taken on a dangerous charge.”

“I need have no fear for myself, Jasper,” Crisparkle replied calmly. “I have none.”

“I have none for myself,” Jasper said, emphasizing the pronoun, “because I am not—nor ever will be—the object of his hostility. But you may be. And my dear boy has been.”

“Good night,” Crisparkle said.

He took the hat—now bearing an unspoken right to hang in his hall—and placed it carefully. Then, he went thoughtfully to bed.

Rosa, orphaned and alone in the world, had lived at the Nuns’ House since she was seven, raised by Miss Twinkleton as a mother figure. Her memory of her own mother was faint but vivid—a young woman, no older than Rosa herself seemed to recall, brought home dead in her father’s arms after drowning during a pleasure party. Every detail clung to Rosa’s mind: the summer dress soaked with water, the long hair still tangled with fallen petals, the quiet sorrow of her father’s face as he grieved. He died a year later, broken by grief.

It was in that time of sorrow that Rosa’s betrothal to Edwin Drood began—arranged by Drood’s close friend and college companion, who had also lost his wife early. But fate moved on. The man who had comforted Rosa’s father passed away too, and so the two young people found themselves bound together by promise, not choice.

From the moment Rosa arrived in Cloisterham, she carried with her an aura of pity and tenderness. It never left her, though it changed colors over time—now golden, now soft pink, now clear blue—as she grew from child to young woman. She was treated like a younger sister, then a cherished daughter, even when she was no longer a child. There were quiet rivalries among the girls at the Nuns’ House—who would be her favorite, who would give her gifts, who would write most often, who she would greet with joy upon return. Beneath the surface, such feelings stirred gently, sometimes sharply. And yet, for all its sweetness, there may have been deeper currents beneath the veils and prayers of the Nuns’ House.

So Rosa became what she was: kind, impulsive, wilful, beloved—but never truly unkind. She gave affection freely, like a spring that never ran dry, enriching the lives around her. Yet deep within her, something remained untouched. What might happen when that well was finally stirred? What changes might come to her light heart and carefree mind? Only time would tell.

How the news reached the Nuns’ House before breakfast—that there had been a quarrel between the two young men, involving some kind of attack by Mr. Neville Landless on Edwin Drood—is unknown. Perhaps it came on the wind, or through the birds outside the open windows. Maybe it was kneaded into the bread by the baker, or delivered in the milk. Or perhaps the housemaids, beating their mats against the gateposts, unknowingly picked up rumors left behind by the town itself. Whatever the source, the story spread through every corner of the building before Miss Twinkleton descended.

And it reached her through Mrs. Tisher, while she was still dressing—what she might have called “sacrificing to the Graces,” if she had been inclined toward mythological speech.

Miss Landless’s brother had thrown a bottle at Mr. Edwin Drood.

Then came the next version: he had thrown a knife.

That led to speculation—perhaps a fork?

And soon, the rumor escalated: bottle, knife, fork, and decanter—all hurled at once, without warning or reason.

As Peter Piper was said to have picked a peck of pickled pepper, so too did the people of Cloisterham demand proof of the actual weapon used. But more importantly, they wanted to know *why*.

The truth, as it turned out, was simpler. Miss Landless’s brother had declared his admiration for Miss Bud. Edwin Drood had told him he had no right to feel that way. In response, the young man had “up’d” (as the cook put it) with everything nearby—bottle, knife, fork, and decanter—and flung them at Edwin.

Poor Rosa covered her ears with both hands when the whispers began, retreating to a quiet corner, begging not to hear more. But Helena Landless, seeking permission from Miss Twinkleton to speak with her brother, went directly to Mr. Crisparkle for facts.

When she returned—first speaking privately with Miss Twinkleton to filter any troubling details—she shared only what was necessary with Rosa. She emphasized the insult Edwin had given her brother, focusing on the final act as the worst offense, though she glossed over the fact that the quarrel had begun because Edwin took things far too lightly. Out of kindness, she avoided mentioning that her lover had been careless in his behavior.

She delivered a message from her brother: please forgive him. With solemn sincerity, she ended the matter there.

But Miss Twinkleton had a duty to restore calm. Stepping into the room—what others might call the schoolroom, but which she referred to with dignity as “the apartment allotted to study”—she stood tall and said, “Ladies!” All rose.

Mrs. Tisher positioned herself behind her, like Queen Elizabeth’s loyal companion at Tilbury.

“Rumour,” Miss Twinkleton began, “has been portrayed by the bard of Avon—needless to say, the immortal Shakespeare, known as the Swan of his native river—possibly because of the old belief that swans sing beautifully before death, though we have no ornithological proof—Rumour has been shown as a creature full of tongues.”

She paused, adjusting her voice. “In Cloisterham, Rumour is no different. A minor disagreement between two young men last night—within a hundred miles of these peaceful walls—has been wildly exaggerated. In our concern for a dear young friend, linked to one of the participants, we allowed ourselves to dwell on this unpleasant topic. But inquiries confirm it was nothing more than an ‘airy nothing,’ as the poet once wrote. We will now set it aside and focus on our duties.”

Yet the topic lingered. That very day, Miss Ferdinand secretly wore a paper mustache at dinner and pretended to throw a water bottle at Miss Giggles, who defended herself with a spoon.

Rosa thought about the quarrel often. She felt caught in it—whether as cause or consequence—because of her engagement. Even when near Edwin, she carried unease; apart from him, it grew heavier. Today, she was alone, unable to talk freely with Helena, whose brother had been involved, and who now avoided the subject.

Just then, Mr. Grewgious arrived.

He was a man chosen for his integrity, not charm. Dry as dust, he looked as if he could be ground into snuff. His thin, yellowish hair resembled a worn fur collar—so unlike real hair, it must have been a wig, except no one could imagine choosing such a head. His face had few expressions, carved deeply into hard lines, like stone. Notches in his forehead suggested Nature had begun to shape him, then abandoned him mid-sculpture, saying, “I can’t be bothered finishing this.”

Tall in the neck, wide in the ankles, awkward in movement, and nearsighted—so much so he didn’t seem to notice how much white stocking showed against his black suit—Mr. Grewgious was clumsy, hesitant, and odd. Yet somehow, despite all this, he made a strange impression of decency.

He found Rosa in Miss Twinkleton’s private room, clearly uncomfortable. He appeared discomposed, as if expecting an examination—and fearing failure.

“My dear, how do you do? I’m glad to see you. You look so much better.”

Miss Twinkleton rose from her desk. “May I retire?”

“Not at all, madam. Please stay.”

“I must insist on moving,” she replied, with gentle firmness. “But I’ll remain if you wish. Shall I wheel my desk to the window?”

“Madam! In the way?”

“You are very kind,” she said. “Rosa, you’re free to speak as you please.”

Left alone with Rosa, Mr. Grewgious repeated, “My dear, how do you do?” Then sat down after she did.

“My visits,” he said, “are like those of angels—not that I compare myself to an angel.”

“No, sir,” Rosa replied.

“Not at all,” he agreed. “Only that my visits are rare. The angels, we know, are upstairs.”

Miss Twinkleton glanced over with a stiff stare.

“I mean,” he continued, placing a hand on Rosa’s as if to steady himself, “the other young ladies.”

She resumed writing.

Mr. Grewgious, realizing he hadn’t handled the opening well, smoothed his head as if pressing water from a dive—an unconscious habit. He pulled out a pocketbook and a stub of pencil.

“I’ve made notes,” he said, flipping pages. “‘Well and happy.’ Truly. Are you well and happy, my dear?”

“Yes, indeed, sir.”

“For which,” he said, nodding toward the window, “we owe warm thanks to the kindness and care of the lady here.”

Again, the sentence faltered. Miss Twinkleton chewed her pen, staring upward, waiting for inspiration from heaven.

He smoothed his head again, then consulted his notes, crossing out “well and happy.”

“‘Pounds, shillings, and pence.’ A dry subject, but important. Life is pounds, shillings, and pence. Death is—” He paused. A memory of her parents’ deaths stopped him. “Death is not pounds, shillings, and pence.”

His voice was flat, dry—like his body. Yet through his limited expression, a hint of kindness surfaced. If Nature had finished him, it might have been visible. But with his forehead unformed and his face rigid, what could he do?

“Pounds, shillings, and pence. Is your allowance sufficient?”

Yes, Rosa answered. She needed nothing.

“And you’re not in debt?”

She laughed. Debt seemed absurd to her.

Mr. Grewgious strained his eyes to confirm her meaning. “Ah!” he murmured, glancing at Miss Twinkleton. “I spoke of angels! I did!”

Rosa knew what came next. She blushed, fidgeting with her dress.

“‘Marriage.’ Hem.” He smoothed his face again, leaned forward slightly. “This is why I’ve come. Otherwise, being particularly angular, I wouldn’t intrude. I feel like a bear—with the cramp—in a Cotillon.”

Rosa laughed.

“It strikes you the same,” he said calmly. “Exactly. Now, Mr. Edwin has visited as planned. You’ve mentioned it in your letters. You like him, and he likes you.”

“I like him very much, sir.”

“So I said,” he replied, missing her emphasis. “Good. You correspond.”

“We write to each other,” she said, pouting slightly, recalling their letter-writing differences.

“That’s what I mean,” he said. “Good. Time passes. At Christmas, we must formally inform Miss Twinkleton of your departure. Your relationship with her goes beyond business, but business remains. I am particularly angular. I don’t give things away. If someone competent could stand in for me, I’d be grateful.”

Rosa nodded, eyes down.

“Certainly,” he said. “The dancing master, for instance—he knows how to conduct himself. He’d move properly, satisfying everyone. I—I am particularly angular. I’d only stumble.”

She remained silent. Perhaps her thoughts hadn’t reached the ceremony yet.

“Memorandum: ‘Will.’ Now, my dear,” he said, pulling out a paper. “Though I’ve already told you of your father’s will, I think it wise to leave a certified copy with you. And I’ll also give one to Mr. Jasper—”

“Not to him!” Rosa interrupted. “Can’t it go to Eddy himself?”

“Well, yes, if you prefer. But Mr. Jasper is his trustee.”

“I do prefer it,” she said quickly. “I don’t want Mr. Jasper between us.”

“Natural,” he said. “You want your future husband to be everything. Yes. Though I suppose—I am a particularly unnatural man. I don’t know from personal experience.”

She looked at him in surprise.

“I mean,” he explained, “I was born late. My parents were old. I suspect I was born advanced in life. While others begin as buds, I began as a chip—a very dry one.”

He paused. “Regarding the copy, your wish shall be honored. As for your inheritance: an annuity of two hundred fifty pounds. Savings and other assets total just over seventeen hundred pounds. I can advance funds for your wedding preparations. All is known.”

“May I ask,” Rosa said, holding the document but not opening it, “if I’m right? I understand you better than legal documents. My father and Edwin’s father made a pact—not binding, but heartfelt—so that we might grow into the same kind of friendship they had?”

“Exactly.”

“For our lasting good and happiness?”

“Precisely.”

“To be even closer than they were?”

“Just so.”

“It wasn’t enforced. No penalty if—”

“Don’t distress yourself. If you didn’t marry, no forfeiture. You’d remain my ward until twenty-one. Nothing worse.”

“And Edwin?”

“He’d inherit his father’s share at majority, just as now.”

Rosa bit the edge of the paper, head tilted, staring at the floor, smoothing it with her foot.

“In short,” Mr. Grewgious said, “this betrothal is a wish, a feeling, a plan. It was sincere. But marriage must rest on free will, mutual love, and certainty—however mistaken that certainty may be. Would your fathers, if alive, still approve if circumstances changed? Impossible to expect otherwise.”

He spoke as if reading aloud, emotionless.

“I’ve fulfilled my duty,” he added, blotting out “Will.” “Now, memorandum: ‘Wishes.’ Is there anything I can help you with?”

Rosa shook her head, hesitating.

“Any instructions for your affairs?”

“I’d like to settle them with Eddy first,” she said, twisting the crease in her dress.

“Of course,” he said. “You should be united in purpose. Will he return soon?”

“He left this morning. Back at Christmas.”

“Perfect. You’ll arrange matters then, inform me, and I’ll discharge my responsibilities—merely as a business associate—to the accomplished lady in the corner window. They’ll accrue then.” He blurred the word “Leave” with his pencil.

“Now, I’ll take my leave.”

“Could I,” Rosa asked, rising as he struggled to stand, “ask you to visit me at Christmas, if I have something important to say?”

He nodded, stiffly, and left.

It is often said that women possess an uncanny ability to judge men’s characters—something seemingly born of instinct rather than reason, a kind of inner knowing that defies logic and resists explanation. This intuition speaks with certainty, even when evidence contradicts it. But rarely do people observe how stubborn this judgment can be once formed. Once a woman has made up her mind about a man, especially if it's unkind, she seldom revises it—even in the face of clear proof to the contrary. In fact, the mere possibility of being wrong gives such judgments a fragility akin to bias: they become entangled with personal feeling, making them as unreliable as testimony from someone with a stake in the outcome.

“Now, don’t you think, Ma dear,” said the Minor Canon one afternoon as his mother knitted quietly in his small bookroom, “that you’re being rather hard on Mr. Neville?”

“No, I do _not_, Sept,” replied the old lady firmly.

“Let us discuss it, Ma.”

“I have no objection to discussing it, Sept. I trust, my dear, I am always open to discussion.” Her cap trembled slightly, as though she were silently adding: *And I should like to see the argument that could change my mind.*

“Very good, Ma,” said her gentle son. “There’s nothing like being open to discussion.”

“I hope not, my dear,” returned the old lady, knitting with unwavering focus—clearly closed to any further debate.

“Well, then,” continued Mr. Crisparkle, “Mr. Neville did act poorly on that unfortunate night—but he was provoked.”

“And under mulled wine,” added the old lady sharply.

“I’ll admit the wine. Though I believe both young men were equally prone to excess.”

“I don’t,” she said flatly.

“Why not, Ma?”

“Because I _don’t_,” she insisted. “Still, I’m quite open to discussion.”

“But, Ma,” he pressed gently, “how can we discuss if you shut down like this?”

“Blame Mr. Neville for it, Sept,” she said with dignified severity. “Not me.”

“My dear Ma—why him?”

“Because,” Mrs. Crisparkle declared, retreating to first principles, “he came home drunk, brought great shame upon this house, and showed disrespect to our family.”

“That cannot be denied, Ma. He regrets it deeply—then, and now.”

“But for Mr. Jasper’s thoughtful courtesy in approaching me the next day, after service, still wearing his gown, and asking whether I’d been frightened or disturbed by what happened—I might never have known of that disgraceful incident at all,” she said.

“To be honest, Ma,” Mr. Crisparkle replied, rubbing his ear, “I would have kept it from you if I could. I hadn’t fully decided what to do. I was following Jasper out to talk to him about hushing the whole thing up—both of us, perhaps—when I found him speaking to you. It was too late.”

“Too late indeed, Sept. He was still pale as ashes, shaken by what had occurred in his rooms.”

“If I had hidden it, Ma, it would have been for your peace, for the sake of the young men, and in fulfillment of my duty as I see it.”

At once, the old lady rose and crossed the room to kiss him. “Of course, my dear Sept, I know that.”

“Yet,” Mr. Crisparkle went on, “it became town gossip, and slipped beyond my control.”

“And I said then, Sept,” she replied, “that I thought ill of Mr. Neville. And I say now that I still think ill of him. I hope he may come to good—but I don’t believe he will.” Her cap vibrated again, emphatically.

“I’m sorry to hear you say that, Ma—”

“I’m sorry to say it, my dear,” she interrupted, knitting steadily, “but I can’t help it.”

“—For,” he continued, “it’s undeniable that Mr. Neville is diligent, attentive, improving rapidly, and—well, I hope I may say—it seems he has genuine affection for me.”

“There’s no merit in that last point, my dear,” she snapped. “And if he claims there is, I think worse of him for boasting.”

“But, Ma, he never claimed it.”

“Perhaps not,” she conceded. “Still, I don’t see that it matters much.”

Mr. Crisparkle watched her with calm amusement—not irritation, but quiet humor, as though contemplating a fine piece of porcelain too delicate for serious argument.

“Besides,” she added, “ask yourself: what would he be without his sister? You know how much influence she has over him. You know how capable she is. Whatever he studies with you, he studies with her. Give her her fair share of credit—and how much remains for him?”

At these words, the Minor Canon fell into silence, lost in thought. He recalled the many times he had seen the brother and sister together, deeply engaged in conversation over one of his old college books—on frosty mornings during his brisk walks to Cloisterham Weir, or on dark evenings when he climbed to his favorite overlook, a jagged ruin of monastery stone. Below, the two figures would pass along the riverbank, lit by the flickering reflections of town lights, the landscape growing colder and more desolate. He remembered how, gradually, he’d realized he wasn’t just teaching Neville—he was teaching both of them. His explanations had naturally adapted to two minds: one close at hand, the other reached only through the first. He thought of whispers from the Nuns’ House—how Helena, once so proud and fierce, had bowed to the fairy-bride (as he called her), learning from her what she knew. He thought of their striking bond, so different yet so perfectly matched. And most of all, he wondered—could it truly be that these moments, so recent, had already woven themselves into the fabric of his life?

Whenever the Reverend Septimus drifted into reverie, his mother took it as a sign he “needed support.” Without delay, she hurried to the dining-room closet to retrieve the necessary aid: a glass of Constantia wine and a homemade biscuit. It was a remarkable closet—worthy of Cloisterham and its famed Minor Canon Corner. Above it hung a portrait of Handel in a flowing wig, seeming to smile knowingly at the contents below, as though ready to harmonize every flavor into a single, sweet fugue. Unlike ordinary closets, this one had a lock suspended in midair, where two sliding panels met—one descending, the other rising. Pulling the upper panel down revealed deep shelves lined with jars of pickles, pots of jam, tin canisters, spice boxes, and exotic blue-and-white vessels holding preserved tamarinds and ginger. Each item bore its name proudly on its label. The pickles—dressed in rich brown double-breasted coats and yellow or drab skirts—announced themselves in bold print: Walnut, Gherkin, Onion, Cabbage, Cauliflower, Mixed. The jams, softer in nature and adorned with curlpapers, whispered in delicate script: Raspberry, Gooseberry, Apricot, Plum, Damson, Apple, Peach. When the lower panel rose, oranges appeared beside a grand japanned sugarbox, meant to temper their tartness. Home-made biscuits stood guard before these treasures, accompanied by a generous slice of plum cake and slender ladyfingers meant to be dipped in wine and kissed. At the very bottom, a leaden vault held the sweet wine and cordials—whispers of Seville Orange, Lemon, Almond, and Caraway-seed. The entire closet seemed infused with centuries of cathedral bells and organ music, as though the air itself had been distilled into honey. Anyone who reached deep into its shelves emerged mellow-faced, transformed by sweetness.

The Reverend Septimus submitted to this sacred cupboard as willingly as he did to another, less glorious one—the medicinal herb-closet, also overseen by the china shepherdess. There, he endured potent infusions of gentian, peppermint, gilliflower, sage, parsley, thyme, rue, rosemary, and dandelion. If his mother suspected a toothache, he’d wrap his face in layers of dried leaves, swathed like a contented child. If she detected a pimple, he’d cheerfully apply botanical patches to his cheek or forehead. This herbaceous penitentiary, tucked away on a narrow staircase landing, was a whitewashed cell where dried herbs hung from rusted hooks and sat on shelves beside mysterious bottles. Here, the Reverend Septimus would go, obedient as a lamb led to slaughter—though unlike that lamb, he harmed no one but himself. Even then, if his mother was busy and happy, he’d swallow whatever was given him, wash his hands and face in bowls of dried rose petals and lavender, and leave, confident in the healing powers of Cloisterham Weir and a sound mind—as certain as Lady Macbeth was hopeless of cleansing herself with all the seas.

That evening, the Minor Canon accepted his glass of Constantia with grace, satisfied his mother’s concern, and resumed his duties. As the day progressed in orderly fashion, Vesper Service arrived, followed by twilight. The Cathedral was cold, so he set off for a brisk walk after prayers, ending in a sprint toward his favorite fragment of ruin—an outpost he intended to storm without pause.

He reached it swiftly, breathless but triumphant, and paused to gaze down at the river. At Cloisterham, the river is near enough to the sea to carry in seaweed with each tide. An unusually large amount had washed ashore with the last surge, mingling with churning water, restless gulls flapping overhead, and a stormy light beyond the blackening barges. A tempest loomed.

In his thoughts, he contrasted the wild sea with the quiet sanctuary of Minor Canon Corner—when suddenly Helena and Neville Landless passed beneath him. He’d been thinking of them all day and immediately descended to speak with them. The footing was treacherous in the dim light, but the Minor Canon was a skilled climber and stood beside them before most could have halfway down.

“A wild evening, Miss Landless! Don’t you find your usual walk with your brother too exposed and cold for this time of year? Or at least, when the sun has set and the wind comes in from the sea?”

Helena shook her head. It was their favorite walk. Very private.

“It is very private,” agreed Mr. Crisparkle, seizing the moment. “A place where one can speak without interruption—just as I wish to do. Mr. Neville, I believe you tell your sister everything that passes between us?”

“Everything, sir.”

“Then,” he said, “your sister knows that I’ve repeatedly urged you to make some sort of apology for that unfortunate incident on the night of your arrival.”

As he spoke, he looked to Helena, not Neville—and it was she who answered:

“Yes.”

“I call it unfortunate,” Mr. Crisparkle continued, “because it has certainly bred prejudice against Neville. There’s a belief around town that he’s dangerously passionate, uncontrollable, furious—so much so that people avoid him.”

“I have no doubt they do, poor fellow,” Helena said, casting a look of proud pity at her brother. “I’d believe it just from you saying so—but your words are confirmed by subtle hints and glances I encounter daily.”

“Now,” Mr. Crisparkle said, firm but gentle, “isn’t this regrettable? Shouldn’t it be corrected? These are early days for Neville in Cloisterham, and I have no fear he’ll outlive such a reputation. But wouldn’t it be wiser to act now, rather than wait for uncertain time? And besides—apart from wisdom—it’s right. There’s no question: Neville was wrong.”

“He was provoked,” Helena offered.

“He was the aggressor,” Mr. Crisparkle countered.

They walked in silence until Helena raised her eyes to his face and said, almost reproachfully: “O Mr. Crisparkle, would you really have Neville throw himself at young Drood’s feet—or at Mr. Jasper’s, who slanders him every day? In your heart, you don’t mean it. You couldn’t ask it, even if it were your own case.”

“I’ve told Mr. Crisparkle, Helena,” Neville interjected, looking respectfully toward his tutor, “that if I could do it from the heart, I would. But I can’t. I recoil from pretense. To suggest otherwise would be mockery. My nature must change before I can make such a move—and it hasn’t. I feel an unbearable insult, and the insult is compounded. I am angry. The truth is, I am as angry now remembering that night as I was then.”

“Neville,” the Minor Canon said quietly, “you’ve repeated that gesture with your hands—the one I dislike so much.”

“I’m sorry, sir,” Neville replied, “but it was involuntary. I admitted I’m still angry.”

“And I confess,” said Mr. Crisparkle, “that I hoped for better things.”

“I’m sorry to disappoint you, sir,” Neville said, “but it would be worse to deceive you. I’d lie grossly if I pretended you’d softened me. The time may come when your influence changes me—but it hasn’t yet. Is this true, Helena? Despite my struggles?”

She watched his face closely, then turned to Mr. Crisparkle and said plainly: “It is true.” After a brief pause, she gave the faintest nod in response to his silent question—and he continued:

“I’ve never had the courage to tell you, sir, what I should have said openly when you first spoke to me about this. It’s difficult to say—and I’ve been held back by fear it might seem ridiculous. That fear is strong even now, and might have stopped me from being honest, if not for my sister.—I admire Miss Bud, sir, so deeply that I cannot bear her treated with indifference or arrogance. Even if I didn’t feel personally wronged by young Drood, I’d feel it on her behalf.”

Mr. Crisparkle stared, stunned, turning to Helena for confirmation. Her expression gave full support—and a plea for guidance.

“The young lady you speak of,” he said gravely, “is soon to be married, Mr. Neville. Therefore, if your admiration is of the nature you suggest, it is wildly misplaced. It’s monstrous to take it upon yourself to defend her against her chosen husband. Besides, you’ve only seen her once. She’s become your sister’s friend—and I wonder why your sister hasn’t checked this irrational, culpable fancy.”

“She has tried, sir,” Neville said, “but uselessly. Husband or no husband, that man is incapable of the feeling I have for her. I say he lacks it entirely—as he is unworthy of her. I say she is sacrificed. I say I love her—and despise and hate him!” His voice cracked, his face flushed, his gesture violent. Helena stepped forward, seized his arm, and pleaded: “Neville, Neville!”

Startled, he regained control, covering his face with his hand, ashamed and broken.

Mr. Crisparkle observed him closely, weighing his next words. They walked in silence for a while, then he spoke:

“Mr. Neville, I am deeply saddened to see in you the same sullenness, anger, and wildness as the storm closing in. These traits are too serious to treat your confession lightly. I take it seriously—and I speak accordingly. This feud between you and young Drood must end. I cannot allow it to continue, knowing what you’ve just revealed, and knowing you live under my roof. Whatever false and bitter conclusions your rage may draw about his character, I assure you—he is honest, kind-hearted. I trust him completely. Now, listen carefully. On reflection, and based on your sister’s account, I’m willing to concede that in making peace with young Drood, you have a right to expect him to meet you halfway. I will ensure he does—indeed, I will ask him to make the first move. Once that is done, I require you to pledge me, as a Christian gentleman, that the quarrel is ended forever on your part. What lies in your heart when you shake his hand, only God can know—but if there’s deceit there, it will bring you no good. As for your feelings toward Miss Bud—this infatuation you’ve confessed—do I understand correctly that it is known only to you, your sister, and me?”

Helena whispered: “Only us three.”

“And not to the young lady herself?”

“On my soul, no!”

“I require you, then, Mr. Neville, to give me a solemn and equal pledge—that this shall remain secret, and that you will take no action upon it except to strive—with all your strength—to erase it from your mind. I won’t pretend it will vanish quickly. I won’t call it a passing fancy. I won’t say such emotions rise and fall hourly among the young. I’ll let you believe it’s rare, lasting, hard to conquer. And because of that, the pledge you give me must be complete, unreserved, and absolute.”

The young man tried to speak several times, but failed.

“Let me leave you with your sister,” said Mr. Crisparkle. “It’s time you took her home. I’ll be alone in my room shortly.”

“Please don’t go yet,” Helena begged. “Just one more minute.”

“I wouldn’t have needed even that,” Neville said, pressing his hand to his face, “if you hadn’t been so patient, so understanding, so unpretentiously good, Mr. Crisparkle. Oh, if only I’d known such a guide in childhood!”

“Follow your guide now, Neville,” Helena murmured, “and follow him to Heaven.”

Her tone touched the Minor Canon so deeply that he could not reply. Instead, he placed a finger to his lips and looked toward her brother.

“To say I give both pledges from my deepest heart,” Neville said, trembling, “and to say there is no deceit in it—is to say nothing!” He bowed his head. “I beg your forgiveness for my lapse into passion.”

“Not mine, Neville,” the Minor Canon said softly. “Not mine. You know who holds forgiveness—above all else. Miss Helena, you and your brother are twin souls. You entered the world with the same dispositions, shaped by the same hardships. What you’ve overcome in yourself—can you not help him overcome it? You see the rock in his path. Who but you can keep him clear of it?”

“Who but you, sir?” Helena replied. “What is my influence, or my weak wisdom, compared to yours?”

“You have the wisdom of Love,” Mr. Crisparkle said, “and it was the highest wisdom ever known on earth. As for mine—let’s say little about that common commodity. Good night.”

She took his offered hand and kissed it gratefully, almost reverently.

“Tut!” he whispered. “I am much overpaid.” And he turned away.

Walking back toward the Cathedral Close in the dark, he tried to plan how to fulfill his promise. “I may be asked to marry them,” he mused. “And I’d wish they were married and gone. But this comes first.”

He debated whether to write to young Drood or speak to Jasper. His popularity within the Cathedral inclined him toward the latter—and seeing the gatehouse lighted, he decided: “I’ll strike while the iron is hot. I’ll see him now.”

Behind the oldest part of Holborn, where ancient gabled houses still stand along the street—like silent witnesses to a past long gone—is a quiet corner known as Staple Inn. It’s a place that feels like stepping out of the city’s noise: a sudden hush, as if the world has been muffled by cotton in the ears and velvet on the soles. Here, a few sparrows hop among smoky trees, chirping as though playing at being country birds. A small patch of soil and gravel gives them just enough space to pretend they’re free. And it’s not only a retreat from the bustle—it’s also a legal nook, with a little hall and a lantern atop its roof, serving some purpose unknown to this story.

In the days when Cloisterham feared the railroad as a threat to its peace—the kind of institution that is always both praised and cursed, depending on what’s happening elsewhere—Staple Inn stood unshaded by modern buildings. The sun lit its walls, and the wind blew through freely. But on this December afternoon, around six o’clock, fog had swallowed everything. Candles flickered weakly behind windows, casting blurred light into the chambers of a corner house in the inner quadrangle. Above its door, in black and white letters, was the enigmatic inscription:

Inside, Mr. Grewgious sat writing by his fire, never giving the sign much thought—though sometimes he wondered if it meant “Perhaps John Thomas” or “Perhaps Joe Tyler.”

Who could tell, looking at Mr. Grewgious, whether he’d ever known ambition—or disappointment? He’d trained for the Bar, aiming to become a conveyancer, to draft deeds, to “convey the wise it call,” as Pistol once said. But Conveyancing had never come to him. She was wooed, but never won. They parted ways without ever truly meeting.

Yet fate sent something else his way—an arbitration case, blown toward him by chance. He handled it with such diligence that he earned a reputation for fairness. Soon after, a lucrative receivership landed in his lap, likely carried by a more predictable breeze. So, by accident, he found his place. Now, Receiver and Agent for two wealthy estates, he handed off their legal matters to a firm below and settled into a life of routine under the dry vine and fig tree planted by P. J. T. in 1747.

His room was filled with ledgers, files, and strongboxes—all arranged with military precision. Nothing was out of place. To leave even one figure incomplete would have struck him dead. Duty was his blood. Not the most exciting kind, perhaps, but the most reliable.

There was no luxury here. Only warmth, dryness, and a faded fireside. His private life centered on the hearth, an easy chair, and a round table brought out after hours from a corner where it stood upright like a polished shield. Behind it, a closet held something good to drink. A clerk worked in the outer room; Mr. Grewgious slept across the stairway and kept a cellar beneath it. Three hundred days a year, he walked to Furnival’s Inn for dinner, then returned to enjoy the simplicity until business resumed.

That evening, as Mr. Grewgious wrote, so did his clerk write by his own fire. A pale, puffy-faced man of thirty, with dark hair and eyes that lacked any spark, Bazzard seemed oddly bound to his master. Like a spirit summoned by magic but unable to be dismissed, he clung to his stool, despite clearly being a burden. Gloomy, tangled-haired, and shadowed by suspicion, he was treated by Mr. Grewgious with inexplicable kindness.

“Now, Bazzard,” said Mr. Grewgious, lifting his head from papers, “what’s the news besides fog?”

“Mr. Drood,” replied Bazzard.

“What about him?”

“He’s called.”

“You might’ve shown him in.”

“I am doing it,” said Bazzard.

The visitor entered.

“Dear me!” said Mr. Grewgious, glancing at the candles. “I thought you’d just left your name and gone. How do you do, Mr. Edwin? Dear me—you’re choking!”

“It’s the fog,” said Edwin. “It stings my eyes like Cayenne pepper.”

“Is it really that bad? Undo your coat. Lucky I’ve got a good fire—but Bazzard’s taken care of me.”

“No, I haven’t,” said Bazzard at the door.

“Ah! Then I must have taken care of myself without realizing it,” said Mr. Grewgious. “Please sit in my chair. No—don’t. Coming from such a fog, you should have it.”

Edwin took the chair. The fog he carried in, and the layers he shed, vanished quickly into the flames.

“I look,” said Edwin, smiling, “as if I’m staying.”

“By the way,” said Mr. Grewgious, interrupting, “do stay. The fog may lift soon. We can get dinner from across Holborn. Better to eat here than outside—please stay and dine.”

“You’re very kind,” said Edwin, scanning the room as if imagining a cozy, impromptu gathering.

“Not at all,” said Mr. Grewgious. “You’re the kind one, joining a bachelor in chambers for potluck. And I’ll ask Bazzard—he might not otherwise agree.—Bazzard!”

Bazzard reappeared.

“Dine with us.”

“If I’m ordered, sir, I will,” he replied grimly.

“Save the man!” said Mr. Grewgious. “You’re not ordered—you’re invited.”

“Thank you, sir,” said Bazzard. “Then I don’t mind.”

“That’s settled. And perhaps you could go to the hotel in Furnival’s and ask them to send over supplies? Let’s have the hottest soup possible, the best made dish, a joint—say, a haunch of mutton—and a goose or turkey if available. In short, whatever they have.”

Mr. Grewgious gave these instructions with the calm precision of someone reciting a list. Bazzard withdrew, pulling out the round table before leaving.

“I was hesitant,” said Mr. Grewgious quietly after the clerk left, “about having him handle the food orders. Because he might not like it.”

“He seems to have his own way, sir,” remarked Edwin.

“His own way?” Mr. Grewgious laughed faintly. “No, poor fellow. If he had his own way, he wouldn’t be here.”

Edwin wondered where he *would* be. But he didn’t say it—Mr. Grewgious had already turned to face the fire, leaning against the mantel, ready to talk.

“I take it,” said Mr. Grewgious, “without claiming prophecy, that you came to tell me you’re going down to Cloisterham—where I can assure you, you’re expected—and to offer any message to my ward, perhaps to give me a hint about any proceedings? Eh, Mr. Edwin?”

“I called, sir, before leaving, as a gesture of respect.”

“Respect!” said Mr. Grewgious. “Ah! Not impatience?”

“Impatience, sir?”

Mr. Grewgious had meant to sound playful—but failed utterly. He leaned too close to the fire, as if trying to burn the humor into himself. But seeing Edwin’s calm expression, he flinched and rubbed his arms.

“I’ve recently been down there,” said Mr. Grewgious, straightening his coat. “That’s why I said you’re expected.”

“Indeed, sir. Yes—I knew Rosa was waiting for me.”

“Do you keep a cat there?” asked Mr. Grewgious.

Edwin flushed slightly. “I call Rosa ‘Pussy.’”

“Oh, really,” said Mr. Grewgious, smoothing his hair. “That’s quite charming.”

Edwin glanced at him, unsure if he was offended. But Mr. Grewgious was unreadable—as steady as a clock face.

“A pet name, sir,” Edwin repeated.

“Umps,” said Mr. Grewgious, nodding. But it was neither full agreement nor refusal—just a strange blend of both. Edwin felt unsettled.

“Did Rosa—” he began.

“PRosa?” echoed Mr. Grewgious.

“I was going to say Pussy, but changed my mind—did she mention the Landlesses?”

“No,” said Mr. Grewgious. “What are the Landlesses? An estate? A villa? A farm?”

“A brother and sister. The sister is at the Nuns’ House and has become a close friend of Rosa’s.”

“She’s strikingly handsome, sir,” said Edwin. “I thought she might have been described to you—or introduced.”

“Neither,” said Mr. Grewgious. “But here comes Bazzard.”

Bazzard returned, followed by two waiters—one slow and rigid, the other swift and frantic. Together, they brought in a fresh wave of fog, which roared through the fire.

The quick waiter carried everything on his shoulders and laid the table with astonishing speed. The slow one stood idle and criticized every move. The fast waiter polished glasses while the slow one inspected them. Then he darted across Holborn for soup, returned, fetched the main course, then the joint and poultry—making multiple trips, each time bringing back fog and breathlessness. The slow waiter scolded him constantly. At the end, he gathered the cloth dramatically, glared at the fast waiter, and pushed him out—sending a clear message: “This reward is mine. That servant claims nothing.”

It was like a miniature satire of bureaucracy—a perfect parody of government inefficiency. One might hang it in the National Gallery.

The fog had sparked this feast, and now it served as seasoning. Hearing clerks sneeze and stamp outside was more thrilling than any gourmet spice. Ordering the exhausted waiter to shut the door before opening it added a deeper flavor than any wine. And note: the young man’s leg, touching the door, showed exquisite timing—always ahead of his body, lingering afterward, like Macbeth’s limb reluctant to leave the stage.

Mr. Grewgious descended to the cellar and returned with bottles of ruby, straw-colored, and golden wines—aged in sunlit lands untouched by fog. They bubbled eagerly, pushing at corks as if eager to escape. If P. J. T. drank such wine in 1747, then surely he was Pretty Jolly Too.

Externally, Mr. Grewgious remained unchanged. The wine might as well have been poured over him—he showed no signs of softening. Yet his eyes watched Edwin closely. When dinner ended and Mr. Grewgious motioned Edwin back to the chair, and Edwin sank into it with minimal protest, Mr. Grewgious smoothed his face and looked at him between his fingers.

“Bazzard!” he called suddenly.

“I follow you, sir,” said Bazzard, who had eaten silently.

“I drink to you, Bazzard. Mr. Edwin, success to Mr. Bazzard!”

“Success to Mr. Bazzard!” echoed Edwin, feigning enthusiasm—though inwardly wondering: *Success in what?*

“And May,” continued Mr. Grewgious, “I cannot be specific—May—my words are so limited that I know I’ll fail—May—this should be poetic, but I lack imagination—May—let me say it plainly: May the thorn of anxiety be removed—May it finally come out!”

Mr. Bazzard frowned slightly, ran a hand through his hair, then into his waistcoat, then his pockets—as if searching for the thorn. Edwin watched, half-expecting to see it appear. But it never did. Bazzard simply said: “I follow you, sir. Thank you.”

“I’m going to toast my ward,” whispered Mr. Grewgious, jingling his glass and leaning close to Edwin. “But I put Bazzard first. He mightn’t like it otherwise.”

He winked—though it was more of a slow blink. Edwin blinked back, clueless.

“And now,” said Mr. Grewgious, “a bumper to Miss Rosa. Bazzard, the fair and fascinating Miss Rosa!”

“I follow you, sir,” said Bazzard, “and I pledge you!”

“So do I!” said Edwin.

“Lord bless me,” exclaimed Mr. Grewgious, breaking the silence. “I’m particularly angular, yet I think—though I have no fancy—I could draw a picture of a true lover’s state of mind tonight.”

“Let us hear it, sir,” said Bazzard.

“Mr. Edwin will correct it where wrong,” added Mr. Grewgious, “and add touches from life. I dare say it’s flawed. I was born a chip—no soft feelings, no soft experiences. Still, I believe the true lover’s mind is fully absorbed by the beloved. Her name is sacred—precious, emotional, never spoken lightly. Any special name he uses for her is kept private. It’s a privilege to whisper it alone with her. To use it elsewhere would be cold, careless, almost dishonest.”

He sat bolt upright, hands on knees, speaking as if reciting a catechism. No emotion stirred—except a faint tingling at the tip of his nose.

“My picture continues,” he said, “the true lover is always eager to be near her. He cares little for others. He seeks her presence—constantly. If I said he seeks her like a bird its nest, I’d make a fool of myself—poetry is beyond me. And I know nothing of birds except those of Staple Inn, who build nests in gutters and chimneys, not by nature’s design. So I skip the bird metaphor. But I do believe the lover lives a doubled and halved life—entwined with her, yet split apart. If I fail to express it, it’s because I lack words—or meaning. Which, I believe, isn’t the case.”

Edwin grew red, then pale. He stared at the fire, biting his lip.

“The speculations of an angular man,” continued Mr. Grewgious, “may be wrong on such a round subject. But I imagine—subject to correction—that a true lover knows no coolness, no indifference, no half-heartedness. Pray, am I near the mark?”

He stopped abruptly, as if mid-speech.

“I should say, sir,” stammered Edwin, “since you ask me—”

“Yes,” said Mr. Grewgious, “I ask you, as an authority.”

“I should say,” continued Edwin, embarrassed, “that your picture is generally accurate. But perhaps you’re a bit harsh on the lover.”

“Likely so,” agreed Mr. Grewgious. “I am hard by nature.”

“He may not show all he feels,” said Edwin, “or he may not—”

He paused, struggling to finish. Mr. Grewgious cut in unexpectedly:

“No, to be sure—he *may* not!”

Silence fell. Bazzard had drifted into sleep.

“The responsibility is great,” said Mr. Grewgious eventually, eyes fixed on the fire.

Edwin nodded, eyes on the flames.

“And let him be sure,” said Mr. Grewgious, “he trifles with no one—neither himself nor another.”

Edwin bit his lip again, staring into the coals.

“He must not treat a treasure as a toy. Woe betide him if he does. Let him remember that.”

He spoke in short bursts, like a child reading scripture. Yet there was a dreamy quality—unusual for such a literal man—as he shook his finger at the embers, then fell silent.

But not for long. Suddenly, he rapped his knees like a statue awakening.

“We must finish this bottle, Mr. Edwin. Let me help you. I’ll help Bazzard too—though he’s asleep. He mightn’t like it otherwise.”

He helped them both, drained his glass, and placed it upside down on the table—like a trap for a bluebottle.

“And now,” he said, wiping his mouth, “to business. You received from me a certified copy of Miss Rosa’s father’s will. You knew its contents, but I sent it as a matter of form. I intended to send it to Mr. Jasper, but Rosa preferred it go directly to you. Did you receive it?”

“Quite safely, sir.”

“You should have acknowledged it. Business is business. But you didn’t.”

“I meant to, sir, when I arrived.”

“Well,” said Mr. Grewgious, “we’ll leave it at that.”

As evening settles over Cloisterham, Mr. Thomas Sapsea—now Mayor of the town—often takes a stroll through the Cathedral Close, where he enjoys the illusion of authority. He walks with hands clasped behind his back, puffing out his chest as if ownership of the churchyard were a personal achievement. He particularly likes to pass by the railings where his own inscription stands proudly: “In memory of Mrs. Sapsea, who was publicly awarded a prize for her meritorious conduct.” He imagines strangers reading it and blushing in retreat, as though they’ve been caught in some moral transgression. To him, this is proof that society functions best when led by men like himself—men of vision, influence, and public service.

His new office has elevated his status, but not his wisdom. He believes mayors are essential pillars of civilization, even claiming credit for inventing the metaphor. He dreams of knighthood for delivering speeches—especially those that elevate English grammar into explosive rhetoric. He is certain he could "go up" with an address, and so might be knighted. Sir Thomas Sapsea! The salt of the earth, indeed.

He has grown fond of Mr. Jasper since their first meeting at the gatehouse, where they shared port, conversation, backgammon, beef, salad, and music. Jasper played the piano, singing old patriotic tunes—songs about George the Third and Britain’s divine right to conquer every island and continent under heaven. It was not mere entertainment; it was a declaration of national superiority, delivered with fervor. Mr. Sapsea admired this: youth who respects elders, who sings no foreign nonsense, but only the true spirit of the realm.

Tonight, walking slowly near the churchyard, Sapsea turns a corner and finds the Dean conversing with the Verger and Jasper. He bows deeply, instantly transformed into a man of ecclesiastical gravity—more reverent than any archbishop.

“You’re clearly writing a book about us,” says the Dean, smiling. “We are ancient, and we ought to make a good story. We lack wealth, but perhaps you’ll mention our misfortunes.”

Mr. Tope chuckles at the suggestion.

“I have no such intention,” replies Jasper. “It’s merely a whim. And even that is more due to Mr. Sapsea than to me.”

“How so?” asks the Dean, nodding toward the Mayor.

“I’m not sure,” says Sapsea, looking around as if searching for clues. “To what does Your Reverence refer?”

“Durdles,” murmurs the Dean.

“Ay!” echoes the Dean. “Durdles, Durdles!”

“It’s true,” explains Jasper. “My interest in him began when Mr. Sapsea introduced us. His knack for drawing out oddities, for uncovering hidden depths—well, it made me look at Durdles differently. You wouldn’t be surprised if you’d seen Sapsea handle him in his own parlour.”

“Oh!” says Sapsea, catching the compliment with delight. “Yes, yes. That’s what you mean? I brought them together. I consider Durdles a Character.”

“And one you turn inside out with just a few strokes,” adds Jasper.

“Not quite,” says Sapsea, puffing slightly. “I may have some influence. Some insight. But remember, I’ve seen the world.” He steps behind the Dean to examine his coat buttons closely.

“Well,” says the Dean, glancing about, “I hope you’ll use your understanding of Durdles to advise him not to break our Choir-Master’s neck. We can’t afford it. His voice—and his head—are too valuable.”

Mr. Tope laughs respectfully, then falls into quiet murmurs, implying that any gentleman would welcome such a compliment—even if it came with a broken neck.

“I shall answer for Mr. Jasper’s safety,” declares Sapsea loftily. “I’ll tell Durdles to be careful. He listens to me. How is he in danger now?”

“Only because I’m planning a moonlight walk with him through the tombs, vaults, towers, and ruins,” says Jasper. “You suggested it when you brought us together—remember? You said, as a lover of the picturesque, it might interest me.”

“I remember!” exclaims Sapsea, though he clearly does not.

“Taking your advice,” continues Jasper, “I’ve walked with him during the day. Tonight, we go at night.”

“And here he is,” says the Dean.

Indeed, Durdles appears, slouching forward with a bundle in hand. Seeing the Dean, he removes his hat, then begins to move away. But Sapsea stops him.

“Mind you take care of my friend,” he commands.

“What friend o’ yourn is dead?” asks Durdles. “No orders come in for any friend o’ yourn.”

“My live friend,” says Sapsea.

“Oh, him?” Durdles shrugs. “He can take care of himself, Mister Jarsper.”

“But you must take care of him too,” insists Sapsea.

Durdles glares, then surveys him from head to toe. “With all due respect to the Dean, if you’ll mind your own business, Mr. Sapsea, Durdles will mind his own.”

“You’re out of temper,” says Sapsea, winking at the others as if to say: *See how I handle him.*

“I admit it,” replies Durdles. “I don’t like liberties.”

Sapsea winks again, satisfied. Then he turns and stalks off.

Durdles gives the Dean a good evening, puts his hat back on, and mutters, “You’ll find me at home, Mister Jarsper, as agreed. I’m going home to clean myself.” Then he disappears into the shadows.

This claim—to clean himself—is one of Durdles’ strange rituals. Yet he remains perpetually dusty, his clothes unchanged, his boots caked with grime. No trace of cleanliness ever shows.

The lamplighter arrives, climbing his ladder with quick steps, lighting the lamps along the Close. His little ladder, a fixture under the cathedral’s shadow for generations, stands as a symbol of continuity. The Dean goes to dinner, Mr. Tope to tea, and Jasper to his piano.

There, in dim firelight, he sings choir music softly, beautifully, for hours—until darkness falls and the moon begins to rise.

Then he closes the piano, changes into a pea-jacket, slips a wicker bottle into his pocket, and dons a low-crowned hat. He leaves quietly. Why so silently? There’s no obvious reason. But something within him stirs—something dark and deep.

He makes his way to Durdles’ unfinished house—a hole in the city wall. A light glows inside. He moves cautiously among the gravestones, monuments, and rubble, already touched by the rising moon. Two saws remain stuck in stone blocks, like silent sentinels. In the shadows, two skeleton figures seem ready to carve the next tombstones—perhaps for the next two souls destined to die in Cloisterham. One wonders which of them it might be.

“Ho! Durdles!”

The light shifts. Durdles appears at the door. He seems to have been cleaning himself—with a bottle, jug, and tumbler. No soap or water in sight. Only alcohol.

“Are you ready?”

“I am ready, Mister Jarsper. Let the old ones come out if they dare. My spirit is ready.”

“Animal spirits or ardent?”

“The one’s the t’other,” says Durdles. “I mean both.”

He takes a lantern from a hook, pockets matches, and they leave—dinner-bundle and all.

A strange expedition. Durdles, always prowling among graves like a ghoulish wanderer, has no need of company. But that the Choir-Master should join him—seeking moonlit effects in such company—is puzzling indeed.

“Watch out for that mound by the yard-gate, Mister Jarsper.”

“I see it. What is it?”

“Lime.”

Jasper stops. Durdles lags behind.

“Quick-lime?” asks Jasper.

“Ay,” says Durdles. “Quick enough to eat your boots. With a little stirring, quick enough to eat your bones.”

They continue, passing the red windows of the Travellers’ Twopenny, then stepping into the moonlit Monks’ Vineyard. They reach Minor Canon Corner, half in shadow, half in light.

A house door shuts. Two men emerge: Mr. Crisparkle and Neville.

Jasper freezes. A sudden smile crosses his face. He places a hand on Durdles’ chest, stopping him.

At this end of the corner, the shadow is deep. There’s a low dwarf wall—the last remnant of an old garden—blocking their path. Jasper and Durdles halt behind it.

“They’re just walking,” whispers Jasper. “They’ll come into the moonlight soon. Stay quiet. If they see us, they’ll stop us—or want to join us.”

Durdles nods. He pulls out fragments from his bundle and munches. Jasper folds his arms atop the wall, chin resting on them, watching.

But he does not watch the Minor Canon. He watches Neville—intently, fixedly—as if his gaze were a trigger, ready to fire.

A sense of deadly power radiates from Jasper’s face. Even Durdles pauses mid-chew, staring at him.

Meanwhile, Crisparkle and Neville walk back and forth, talking quietly. Their words are fragmented, but Jasper catches his name several times.

“This is the first day of the week,” Crisparkle says clearly. “And the last day of the week is Christmas Eve.”

“You may be certain of me, sir.”

Echoes carry the words. Then, as they approach, the sound becomes muffled. But the word “confidence” breaks through—shattered, yet recognizable.

Later, a reply: “Not deserved yet, but shall be, sir.”

Then, as they turn away, Jasper hears again: “Remember that I said I answered for you confidently.”

The voices fade. They disappear into the moonlight at the far end.

Only when they’re gone does Jasper move. Then he bursts into laughter—loud, uncontrollable laughter.

Durdles, still chewing, stares in confusion. Jasper buries his face in his arms, laughing wildly. Finally, Durdles swallows the food in his cheek, as if surrendering to indigestion.

After dark, these secluded corners are nearly lifeless. Even during the day, there’s little activity. At night, the silence is profound. The High Street runs parallel, lively and well-lit, while the Cathedral grounds lie hushed—haunted, almost. People avoid them after dusk. Ask a hundred citizens if they believe in ghosts, and ninety-nine will say no. But ask them to choose between the eerie precincts and the busy street at night, and ninety-nine will pick the longer route.

Why? Not superstition alone—though some swear they’ve seen a ghostly woman with a child and a rope dangling from her neck—but something deeper: the instinctive fear of the living encountering the dead. A feeling that if spirits ever appear, this place would be their natural haunt. So people flee.

Now, Jasper and Durdles pause before descending into the crypt through a small side door—Durdles holding the key. The moonlight bathes the scene. No one else is in sight. The tide of life seems held back by the gatehouse. Beyond, the murmur of life continues. But no wave passes the archway—only the red glow of Jasper’s lamp, like a lighthouse warning.

They enter, lock the door, descend the rough steps, and step into the crypt.

No lantern is needed. Moonlight streams through bare windows, casting patterns on the floor. Heavy pillars cast deep shadows, but between them run lanes of silver light.

They walk slowly. Durdles talks of the “old uns” still buried beneath the walls, slapping a stone as if addressing an old family. His usual silence fades under the influence of the wicker bottle, which circulates freely—into Durdles’ system, while Jasper sips only once, then spits it out.

They climb the great tower. On the dark steps, Durdles stops for breath. Jasper sits beside him. The smell of the bottle drifts through the air. Neither can see the other, yet they talk as if facing one another.

“This is good stuff, Mister Jarsper!”

“It is very good stuff. I bought it especially.”

“They don’t show, you see—the old uns don’t.”

“It would be a more confused world if they did.”

“True,” says Durdles. “But do you think there might be ghosts of other things—things not human?”

“What things? Flower beds? Horses?”

“No. Sounds.”

“What sounds?”

“Cries.”

“What cries?”

“Screeches. Now I’ll tell you, Mr. Jarsper. Wait till I fix the bottle.” The cork pops, then clicks back. “There! _Now_ it’s right. This time last year, just a few days later, I was doing what was proper for the season—giving it the welcome it deserved—when the town boys attacked me. I escaped, came here, and fell asleep. What woke me? The ghost of a scream. Then the ghost of a dog’s howl—long, sad, woeful—like when someone dies. That was my last Christmas Eve.”

“What do you mean?” Jasper snaps, suddenly tense.

“I mean I asked everywhere. No one else heard it. So I say they were ghosts. Though why they came to me, I don’t know.”

“I thought you were a different sort of man,” Jasper says coldly.

“So I thought,” replies Durdles calmly. “Yet I was chosen.”

Jasper rises abruptly. “Come. We’ll freeze here. Lead the way.”

Durdles complies, unsteadily. He opens the door at the top with his key, and they emerge onto the Cathedral level—into a passage beside the chancel.

The moonlight is bright again. Colors from the stained glass fall across their faces. Durdles, standing still, holding the door open, looks like a figure risen from the grave—purple hand across his face, yellow splash on his brow. Yet he bears Jasper’s scrutiny without flinching.

Jasper fumbles in his pockets for a key to unlock an iron gate leading to the tower staircase.

“That and the bottle are enough for you,” he says, handing the bottle to Durdles. “Give me your bundle. I’m younger. I can carry it.”

Durdles hesitates—between bundle and bottle—then chooses the bottle. The bundle goes to Jasper.

Up they go, winding through the tower stairs, lowering their heads to avoid the stones above. Durdles lights the lantern, striking a spark from the cold wall. Guided by its flicker, they climb through cobwebs and dust.

They pass through narrow galleries, where Durdles waves the lantern—making the angels on the corbels seem to watch them. Then into steeper staircases, where wind whips through, startling birds whose wings beat against the walls, showering dust upon their heads.

Finally, they reach the top. Leaving the lantern behind, they look down upon Cloisterham—bathed in moonlight. Ruins at the base of the tower, moss-covered roofs beyond, the river winding from mist to sea, already restless with the knowledge of its journey.

Another inexplicable journey. Jasper gazes at the scene—especially at the part shaded by the Cathedral. But he watches Durdles just as closely. And Durdles, at times, feels his eyes.

Only at times. For Durdles is growing drowsy. Like an aeronaut lightening his load to rise, he has drained the bottle on the climb. Sleep ambushes him—snatches of it catch him mid-step. He hallucinates the ground below as level with the tower, and would walk off into the air if not stopped.

When they begin to descend, he drinks more—from the bottle, to weigh himself down, like an aeronaut preparing to land.

They reach the iron gate—locked only after Durdles tumbles twice and cuts his eyebrow once.

Back in the crypt, they aim to exit as they entered. But Durdles is now unsteady—both in step and speech. He collapses against a pillar, muttering for “forty winks of a second each.”

“If you insist,” says Jasper, “I won't leave you. Take your rest. I’ll walk.”

And so he does—while Durdles sleeps, dreaming of ghosts, of screams, of a world where the dead speak in silence.

The Nuns’ House, Miss Twinkleton’s stately yet modest academy for young ladies, was settling into a quiet hush as the Christmas recess approached. What had once been called “the half” — a term even the scholarly Miss Twinkleton herself had used in earlier days — was now more elegantly referred to as “the term,” and that term would end the following day. For several days, discipline had softened, replaced by an atmosphere of festive anticipation. Club suppers had taken place in private bedrooms; a tongue had been carved with scissors and passed around with curling tongs; marmalade had been served on plates made from curlpaper; and cowslip wine had been sipped from the small glass in which little Rickitts, a frail junior, received her daily dose of medicine.

The housemaids had been bribed with ribbons and worn-out shoes to overlook crumbs in beds. The girls wore their lightest, most daring dresses, and Miss Ferdinand had astonished everyone with a spirited solo on the comb-and-curlpaper before being smothered in her own pillow by two long-haired companions. Boxes filled the rooms, though not nearly as much was packed as appeared. Small gifts — cold cream, pomatum, hairpins — were given to the servants under strict promises of secrecy. Whispers circulated about golden youths expected to call “at home” soon after their return. Miss Giggles, lacking sentiment, claimed she’d respond to such attentions by making faces — but she was soundly outvoted.

On the final night before departure, it was a point of honor that no one sleep. Ghosts were to be encouraged at all costs. But tradition always failed: all fell asleep quickly, only to wake early. The last ceremony occurred at noon on the day of departure. In Miss Twinkleton’s apartment — the globes already draped in brown holland — a drawing-room was held. Glasses of white wine and plates of cut pound cake appeared on the table. Miss Twinkleton, supported by Mrs. Tisher, addressed the gathering:

"Ladies, another year has brought us to this festive season, when our natural feelings rise within our—"

She paused, as she did every year, on the verge of saying "bosoms," then substituted, "hearts."

"Hearts — our hearts. Hem! Once again, the year has brought us to a pause in our studies — let us hope greatly advanced studies — and like the mariner in his bark, the warrior in his tent, the captive in his dungeon, and the traveler in his conveyance, we yearn for home."

She quoted Mr. Addison’s tragedy:
“The dawn is overcast, the morning lowers,
And heavily in clouds brings on the day,
The great, th’ important day—?”

But no — for them, the horizon to zenith was *couleur de rose*, glowing with thoughts of family and friends. She wished them prosperity and happiness, and urged them to remember the words of the Spartan general — though she didn’t name the battle or quote the line, leaving it unspoken, too familiar to repeat.

Then the handmaidens, in their best caps, served trays. The girls sipped and crumbled, while coaches began to choke the street. Farewells were brief. Miss Twinkleton kissed each girl on the cheek and slipped into her hand a neatly sealed letter addressed to the next friend at law, bearing the inscription: *"With Miss Twinkleton’s best compliments."* She handed it with an air as if it had nothing to do with the bill — as though it were a surprise, a delicate gift.

Rosa, who had seen so many such departures, felt no urge to leave. She had known no other home, and now, with Helena Landless still beside her, she felt more content than ever. Yet her friendship with Helena carried a quiet emptiness. Helena, having shared in her brother’s revelation about Rosa and entered into a pact of silence with Mr. Crisparkle, avoided any mention of Edwin Drood’s name. Why, Rosa could not say — but she sensed it clearly. If Helena had not been so guarded, Rosa might have confided in her, found relief from her doubts. As it was, she had no one to turn to, only her own heart to ponder.

She knew — thanks to Helena — that Edwin and her brother were meant to reconcile when Edwin returned. So why the silence? Why avoid the name now, when the future seemed clear?

A picture formed in her mind: so many girls kissing her in the cold porch, Rosa peering out, unaware of the sneaky faces carved on spouts and gables watching her, waving farewell to departing coaches — a symbol of rosy youth remaining behind to keep the place warm in its solitude.

“Good-bye, Rosebud darling!” rang out in silvery voices. The effigy of Mr. Sapsea’s father above the opposite doorway seemed to declare: “Gentlemen, observe this charming final remnant left behind — bid them farewell with spirit!”

Then the street grew quiet again. Cloisterham reclaimed itself.

But if Rosa waited in her bower with an uneasy heart, Edwin, too, was unsettled. Though less resolute than Rosa, he carried a conscience — one pricked sharply by Mr. Grewgious. That man’s convictions were immovable. Without the dinner at Staple Inn, without the ring tucked in his coat pocket, Edwin might have drifted into marriage without thought. But that moment of truth had shaken him. He must either give the ring to Rosa — or return it.

Now, walking from the gatehouse to the Nuns’ House, he decided: “I will be guided by what she says, and how we get on. Whatever happens, I will bear his words in mind — and strive to be true to the living and the dead.”

Rosa was dressed for a walk. She expected him. It was a bright, frosty day, and Miss Twinkleton had granted permission for fresh air. They stepped out together before propriety required any formal offering.

“My dear Eddy,” Rosa said, once they turned off the High Street and into the quiet paths near the Cathedral and river, “I want to speak seriously. I’ve thought about this for a very long time.”

“I want to be serious with you too, Rosa dear. I mean to be earnest.”

“Thank you. And you won’t think me unkind for speaking first? You won’t think I’m only thinking of myself? That wouldn’t be generous — and I know you are generous.”

“I hope I’m not ungenerous to you, Rosa.”

He no longer called her “Pussy.” Never again.

“And there’s no fear,” she continued, “of our quarreling, is there? Because, Eddy,” she clasped his arm, “we have so much reason to be kind to each other.”

“We will be, Rosa.”

“That’s a dear good boy! Let us be courageous. From this day forward, let us be brother and sister.”

“Never husband and wife?”

“Never.”

They walked in silence for a while. Then he spoke, with effort: “Of course I know this has been in both our minds. And I’m bound to admit — it doesn’t originate with you.”

“No, nor with you, dear,” she replied, earnestly. “It grew between us. You’re not truly happy in this engagement. Neither am I. Oh, I’m so sorry — so sorry!”

Tears welled up.

“I’m deeply sorry too, Rosa. Deeply sorry for you.”

“And I for you, poor boy! I for you!”

This gentle sorrow, this mutual regard, softened their situation. Their relationship no longer looked selfish or flawed — it became noble, selfless, true.

“If we knew yesterday,” Rosa said, drying her eyes, “and we did know — on many yesterdays — that we were wrong together, what better could we do today than change things? We’ll be sorry — and we are — but how much better to be sorry now than later?”

“When?” he asked.

“When it would be too late. And then we’d be angry, too.”

Another silence.

“You know,” she said innocently, “you couldn’t like me then. But you can always like me now. I won’t be a burden. And I can always like you — and your sister won’t tease you anymore. I often did when I wasn’t your sister — and I beg your pardon.”

“Don’t go there, Rosa,” he said. “Or I’ll need more forgiving than I care to face.”

“No, indeed, Eddy. You’re too hard on yourself. Let’s sit here, brother, on these ruins. Let me tell you how it was.”

“I think I understand,” she said. “I’ve thought about it since you were last here. You liked me, didn’t you? You thought I was a nice little thing?”

“Everybody does, Rosa.”

“Do they?” She frowned, then smiled brightly. “Well, suppose they do. But surely it wasn’t enough just to think of me as others did?”

He could not argue. It wasn’t enough.

“And that’s exactly how it was,” she said. “You liked me well enough. You’d grown used to me. To the idea of us marrying. You accepted it as inevitable, didn’t you? You thought it would happen — and why question it?”

It was strange to hear himself described so clearly. He had always patronized her — assumed her wit was lesser than his. Was that part of the deeper flaw in their bond?

“All this I say of you,” she added, “is true of me too. Unless it were, I wouldn’t have the courage to say it. The difference was — I began to think about it. My life isn’t as busy as yours. I had time. I thought — and I wept — until my guardian came down to prepare for my leaving. I tried to hint I wasn’t settled — but I failed. He didn’t understand. But he’s a good man. He spoke to me kindly, yet firmly, about how seriously we should consider this. And I resolved to speak to you the moment we were alone.”

“And you seemed so calm now,” he said.

“It wasn’t easy, Eddy. It was very hard. And I’m terribly sorry.”

Her tears returned. He put his arm around her waist, and they walked by the river.

“Your guardian has spoken to me too, Rosa,” he said. “I saw him before I left London.”

His hand reached into his breast pocket, seeking the ring — then stopped. *If I’m going to return it, why tell her?*

“And that made you more serious, didn’t it?” she asked.

“Yes. And if I hadn’t spoken, would you have spoken to me? I hope you can tell me so. I don’t like it to be all my doing — though it’s better this way.”

“Yes,” he said. “I would have spoken. I came intending to. But I never could have spoken to you as you have spoken to me.”

“Don’t say it coldly or unkindly, Eddy,” she pleaded.

“I meant it wisely, delicately, affectionately.”

“That’s my dear brother!” She kissed his hand, radiant.

“The girls will be disappointed,” she laughed, dewdrops sparkling in her eyes. “They’ve looked forward to it so.”

“But Jack,” Edwin said, suddenly, “it will be worse for Jack.”

She looked at him — swiftly, intently. A flash of lightning, impossible to recall. Then she dropped her gaze, confused, breathing fast.

“You don’t doubt it’s a blow to Jack, Rosa?”

She answered evasively: “Why should I? I hadn’t thought of him.”

“He’s so wrapped up in me — Mrs. Tope’s phrase, not mine — that he’ll be shattered. It’ll seem sudden to him.”

She nodded, lips parted, but said nothing.

“How shall I tell Jack?” Edwin mused. He didn’t notice her strange emotion.

“I never thought of Jack,” he added. “It must be broken to him before the town-crier knows. I dine with him tomorrow and the day after — Christmas Eve and Christmas Day — but I can’t spoil his feast. He worries about me, fusses over trifles. This news will upset him. How on earth shall I break it?”

“He must be told,” Rosa said.

“My dear Rosa — who should be in our confidence if not Jack?”

“My guardian promised to come if I asked. I’ll write. Would you like me to leave it to him?”

“A brilliant idea!” Edwin exclaimed. “The other trustee. Nothing more natural. He comes, speaks to Jack, explains everything — better than we could. He’s already spoken to both of us. He’ll present it with feeling. That’s it. I’m not a coward, Rosa — but I’m afraid of Jack.”

“No, no!” Rosa cried, turning pale, hands clasped. “You’re not afraid of him!”

“Why, sister Rosa, sister Rosa, what do you see from the turret?” Edwin teased, rallying her.

“My dear girl!”

“You frightened me.”

“Unintentionally — but I’m as sorry as if I meant to. Could you possibly think I’m literally afraid of the dear fellow? What I mean is — he’s prone to fits. I saw him in one once. Such a shock, coming directly from me — the one he adores — might bring it on. That’s why your guardian should tell him. He’s steady, precise. He’ll shape Jack’s thoughts. With me, Jack’s impulsive — almost womanish.”

Rosa seemed reassured. Perhaps from her own view of Jack, she found comfort in Mr. Grewgious standing between them.

Edwin’s hand closed again on the ring — then paused. *I’m certain now I’ll return it. Why tell her?*

That tender nature, capable of such sorrow for lost hopes, and yet so quietly ready to build a new world, would be hurt by the ring — for what purpose? It was a relic of broken joy, a cruel satire on human dreams. Let it rest. He would return it to his guardian, who would return it to the cabinet — where it would gather dust, forgotten, until someone sold it again, repeating its old cycle.

Let it be. Let it remain unspoken.

Whether he fully formed these thoughts or not, he concluded: *Let it be.*

In the vast ironworks of time and fate, a chain was forged in that moment — riveted to the foundations of heaven and earth, invincible in its strength.

They walked on, talking of plans. He would leave England sooner. She would stay — at least as long as Helena remained. The girls’ disappointment would be eased gently. First, Miss Twinkleton would be told — even before Mr. Grewgious arrived. It would be clear to all that they remained the best of friends. Never had their understanding been so serene.

Yet each held a secret: she, the intention to withdraw from her music master through her guardian; he, a vague speculation about whether he might one day know more of Miss Landless.

The sun dipped into the river behind them. The city glowed red ahead. The water moaned, casting seaweed at their feet. Rooks circled overhead, dark splashes in the dimming sky.

“I’ll prepare Jack for my departure soon,” Edwin murmured. “I’ll see your guardian when he arrives — then leave before they speak. Better without me, don’t you think?”

“Yes.”

“We know we’ve done right, Rosa?”

“Yes.”

“We know we’re better this way — even now?”

“And far better by-and-by.”

Still, tenderness lingered. They delayed their parting. At the elm trees by the Cathedral, where they had last sat, they stopped — by instinct. Rosa raised her face to his, higher than ever before.

“God bless you, dear. Good-bye.”

“God bless you, dear. Good-bye.”

They kissed fervently.

“Now, please take me home, Eddy. Let me be alone.”

“Don’t look back, Rosa,” he cautioned, guiding her arm through his.

“Didn’t you see Jack?”

“No. Where?”

“Under the trees. He saw us. Poor fellow — he has no idea we’ve parted. This will be a blow.”

She hurried on, silent, until they passed under the gatehouse into the street. Then she asked: “Has he followed us? You can look without seeming to. Is he behind?”

“No. Yes — he just passed through the gateway. The dear, sympathetic old fellow likes to keep us in sight. I’m afraid he’ll be bitterly disappointed.”

She pulled the bell. The gate opened. Before stepping in, she gave him one last, wide, questioning look — as if pleading: *Don’t you understand?*

And in that look, he vanished from her sight.

Christmas Eve in Cloisterham. The town is alive with quiet festivity, its usual stillness softened by the season’s warmth. A few unfamiliar faces wander through the streets—visitors from beyond the city walls, men and women once children of Cloisterham who return after years away, only to find their hometown diminished, as if time had shrunk it down like a garment left too long in the wash. For them, the chime of the Cathedral clock and the cawing of rooks from the tower echo not just in the air but in memory—the sounds of childhood, now tinged with nostalgia. Some have even dreamed, on their deathbeds far from home, that their floors were scattered with autumn leaves from the elm trees in the Close, proof that early impressions never truly fade.

The season has touched every corner. Red berries glimmer in the lattice windows of Minor Canon Corner; Mr. and Mrs. Tope carefully tuck sprigs of holly into the carved stalls of the Cathedral, as though decorating the very robes of the Dean and Chapter. Shops overflow with holiday goods—currants, raisins, spices, candied peel, and moist sugar piled high. There’s a sense of indulgence in the air: a massive bunch of mistletoe hangs in the greengrocer’s doorway, and at the pastrycook’s, a small Twelfth Cake—so modest it might be mistaken for a Twenty-fourth or Forty-eighth Cake—awaits raffle, priced at one shilling per ticket. Public entertainments are in full swing: a waxwork exhibit, once admired by the Emperor of China, can be seen during Christmas Week alone at the bankrupt livery-stable-keeper’s shop up the lane; and a new comic pantomime is set to debut at the Theatre, advertised by a life-sized portrait of Signor Jacksonini the clown, grinning awkwardly and asking, “How do you do tomorrow?” In short, Cloisterham is stirring—but not all corners share in the cheer. The High School and Miss Twinkleton’s school remain quiet. The scholars have returned home, each secretly smitten with one of Miss Twinkleton’s pupils (though none of the girls suspect it), and only the maids flutter behind the windows of the latter establishment. It’s observed that these young women grow more lively when left to represent their sex alone—more spirited, perhaps, than when sharing the role with others.

Tonight, three people are meant to meet at the gatehouse. How does each pass the day?

Neville Landless, freed from his studies for the holiday by Mr. Crisparkle—whose own nature is no stranger to the joy of a break—spends the morning in his room reading and writing with intense focus until well past noon. Then he begins to tidy: clearing his desk, arranging books, burning stray papers. He makes a thorough sweep, ordering drawers and destroying every scrap except those tied directly to his education. Once done, he turns to his wardrobe, selects a few essentials—including sturdy shoes and socks—and packs them into a new knapsack bought yesterday in the High Street. Alongside it, he places a heavy walking stick, strong in the grip, iron-shod. He tests it, swings it, balances it, then sets it aside beside the pack.

Dressed and ready to leave, he steps out of his room—only to pause and return for the stick. On the staircase, he meets Mr. Crisparkle emerging from his own chamber. Neville retrieves the stick, and Mr. Crisparkle, pausing, takes it from him with a smile.

“How did you choose this?” he asks.

“I don’t know much about sticks,” Neville replies. “I picked it for its weight.”

“Too heavy,” says Mr. Crisparkle. “Much too heavy.”

“To rest upon during a long walk?”

“Rest upon?” Mr. Crisparkle repeats, adopting a walking stance. “You don’t rest on it—you balance with it.”

“I’ll learn with practice,” Neville says. “I’ve never lived in a walking country.”

“True,” Mr. Crisparkle agrees. “But get into shape, and we’ll take a few miles together. I wouldn’t let you go anywhere now. Will you be back before dinner?”

“I don’t think so—we dine early.”

Mr. Crisparkle nods brightly, bids him farewell, and gives off an unmistakable air of confidence.

Neville goes to the Nuns’ House and requests that Miss Landless be told her brother is there by appointment. He waits at the gate, not crossing the threshold—he’s bound by his word not to come near Rosa.

His sister arrives promptly, mindful of their shared pledge. They greet warmly, avoid lingering, and begin walking toward the higher countryside.

“I won’t tread on forbidden ground, Helena,” Neville says, once they’ve walked a way. “But I must speak of—what shall I call it?—my infatuation.”

“Perhaps you’d better not,” she says. “You know I can hear nothing.”

“You can hear what Mr. Crisparkle has heard—and approved.”

“Yes. I can hear that much.”

“Well, then. I’m not only unsettled myself, but I feel I’m unsettling others. How do I know that, without my presence, you—and the rest of that old party, save our guardian—might not be dining happily at Minor Canon Corner tomorrow? Probably you would be. I see clearly enough that I’m not held in high regard by the old lady, and I understand how burdensome I must seem to her orderly household—especially now, when I must be kept apart from one person, avoided near another, and already carry a reputation that precedes me with yet another. I’ve spoken gently to Mr. Crisparkle—knowing his selfless ways—but I’ve said it. What I’ve stressed most is that I’m fighting a battle within myself, and that a change of scene may help me win it. So, since the weather is clear and firm, I’m going on a walking journey, and plan to remove myself from everyone’s path—my own included, I hope—tomorrow morning.”

“When will you return?”

“In two weeks.”

“And alone?”

“I’m better off alone—even if you were the only one who could bear me.”

“Mr. Crisparkle fully agrees?”

“Fully. At first, he thought it might be a brooding scheme, harmful to a mind like mine. But last Monday night, we took a moonlit walk to talk it over. I laid out the truth plainly: I need to conquer myself, and after tonight, it’s better I’m away than here. I’d likely run into people walking together, and that would do no good—nor help me forget. In two weeks, that risk will pass. And if it returns later, I can go again. Besides, I truly believe fresh air and hard exercise will help. You know Mr. Crisparkle values such things highly—both for body and mind—and he wouldn’t uphold one standard for himself and another for me. When convinced I was serious, he agreed. With his full approval, I leave tomorrow morning—early enough to be gone from the streets and out of earshot of the bells when the townspeople go to church.”

Helena considers this and finds it wise. She trusts Mr. Crisparkle’s judgment, but she also sees merit in it herself—a healthy effort, a real attempt at self-improvement. She feels sorry for him, leaving on Christmas Eve alone, but knows encouragement matters more than pity. She gives it freely.

Will he write?

Every other day. He’ll tell her all his adventures.

Will he send clothes ahead?

“No,” he says. “Travel like a pilgrim—with wallet and staff. My wallet—my knapsack—is packed and ready. And here is my staff!”

He hands it to her. She echoes Mr. Crisparkle’s comment: “Too heavy.” She asks what wood it is.

“Ironwood.”

Up to this point, Neville has been cheerful. Perhaps the need to present his plans in the best light has lifted his spirits. Or perhaps, having succeeded, he now feels a drop. As dusk falls and the city lights flicker on, he grows quieter.

“I wish I weren’t going to that dinner, Helena.”

“Dear Neville, does it matter? It will be over soon.”

“Soon!” he repeats, darkly. “Yes. But I don’t like it.”

There might be a moment of awkwardness, she suggests, but only a moment. He’s certain of himself.

“I wish I felt as sure of everything else,” he says.

“How strange you sound! What do you mean?”

“I don’t know, Helena. Only that I don’t like it. There’s a strange dead weight in the air.”

She points to the coppery clouds beyond the river. “The wind is rising.”

He speaks little more until they part at the Nuns’ House gate. She doesn’t enter immediately, watching him walk away. Twice he passes the gatehouse, hesitating. Finally, as the Cathedral clock strikes quarter past one, he turns sharply and hurries inside.

And so _he_ ascends the postern stair.

Edwin Drood spends the day alone. Something deeper than he realized has vanished from his life. Last night, in silence, he wept for it. Though the image of Miss Landless still lingers in his thoughts, it is not the girl he once imagined—she is stronger, wiser, more grounded than he knew. He thinks of her with a quiet regret for his own shortcomings, for not having taken her seriously sooner, for not valuing her more. If he had treated his future differently—not accepted it as a given, but sought to improve it—perhaps they could have been something more. Yet despite this sorrow, youth’s vanity still holds the image of Rosa in his mind.

That look she gave him when they parted—what did it mean? Did she see beneath his thoughts, into their hidden depths? No, he decides—it was not that. It was sharp, curious, almost startled. He cannot interpret it, though it was deeply expressive.

Now he waits only for Mr. Grewgious. Once he sees him, he will leave. Until then, he walks the ancient city and its edges, recalling times when Rosa and he wandered here as children, proud and engaged. Poor children, he thinks, with a touch of sadness.

His watch has stopped. He stops at the jeweller’s to have it wound. The man, sensing an opportunity, brings out a bracelet—perfect for a young bride, especially one petite. Edwin looks at it coldly. The jeweller shifts to rings—gentlemen’s signets, plain and dignified, often chosen at marriage. Some have the wedding date engraved inside. A responsible memento.

Edwin ignores them. “I wear only my watch and chain—my father’s—and my shirt-pin.”

“I knew that,” the jeweller replies. “Mr. Jasper came in the other day for a watch-glass. I showed him these items, saying, ‘If he should wish to give a gift to a gentleman relative—’ But he smiled and said he had an inventory in his head of everything his relative ever wore: just the watch, chain, and pin.”

Still, the jeweller insists, times change. “Twenty minutes past two, Mr. Drood—I’ve set your watch. Don’t let it run down.”

Edwin takes it, fastens it, and leaves, thinking: *Dear old Jack. If I made a crease in my neckcloth, he’d notice.*

He wanders aimlessly, passing the hours. Cloisterham seems different today—reproachful, as if judging him for not living up to it. But it’s not anger—it’s sorrow. His usual carelessness is replaced by a wistful attention to familiar sights. Soon he’ll be gone—maybe forever. Poor youth. Poor youth.

As twilight deepens, he walks Monks’ Vineyard. He’s paced back and forth for half an hour by the Cathedral chimes, and only now, as darkness settles, does he notice a woman crouched near a wicket gate in a corner. The gate opens onto a narrow, seldom-used path. She must have been there all along—yet he’s only just seen her.

He approaches, and by the light of a nearby lamp, sees her—haggard, chin resting on her hands, eyes fixed forward in a glassy stare.

Always kind-hearted, and especially so tonight, he bends down.

“Are you ill?”

“No, deary,” she answers, without looking at him, her gaze unblinking.

“Are you blind?”

“No, deary.”

“Lost? Homeless? Faint? Why stay here in the cold so long?”

Slowly, stiffly, she focuses on him. Then a film crosses her face, and she begins to tremble.

He straightens, steps back, staring in dread. He knows her.

*Good heavens!* he thinks. *Like Jack that night!*

She lifts her eyes to his and whimpers: “My lungs is weakly; my lungs is dreffle bad. Poor me, poor me, my cough is rattling dry!” She coughs, violently.

“Where are you from?”

“Come from London, deary.” (Her cough tearing through her.)

“Where are you going?”

“Back to London, deary. I came here, looking for a needle in a haystack, and I ain’t found it. Look’ee, deary; give me three-and-sixpence, and don’t you be afeard for me. I’ll get back to London then, and trouble no one. I’m in a business.—Ah, me! It’s slack, it’s slack, and times is very bad!—but I can make a shift to live by it.”

“Do you eat opium?”

“Smokes it,” she replies, struggling. “Give me three-and-sixpence, and I’ll lay it out well, and get back. If you don’t give me three-and-sixpence, don’t give me a brass farden. And if you do give me three-and-sixpence, deary, I’ll tell you something.”

He counts the coins and hands them over. She clutches them tightly, rises with a croaking laugh.

“Bless ye! Hark’ee, dear genl’mn. What’s your Chris’en name?”

“Edwin.”

“Edwin, Edwin, Edwin,” she repeats, trailing off drowsily. Then suddenly: “Is the short of that name Eddy?”

“It is sometimes called so,” he replies, color rising in his cheeks.

“Don’t sweethearts call it so?” she asks, pondering.

“How should I know?”

“Haven’t you a sweetheart, upon your soul?”

“None.”

She starts to move away, murmuring, “Bless ye, and thank’ee, deary!” But he stops her.

“You were to tell me something. You may as well.”

“So I was, so I was. Well, then. Whisper. You be thankful that your name ain’t Ned.”

He stares at her. “Why?”

“Because it’s a bad name to have just now.”

“How a bad name?”

“A threatened name. A dangerous name.”

“The proverb says threatened men live long,” he says lightly.

“Then Ned—so threatened is he, wherever he may be while I am a-talking to you, deary—should live to all eternity!” she whispers, leaning close, her finger trembling before his eyes.

Then she pulls back, mutters, “Bless ye, and thank’ee!” and vanishes toward the Travellers’ Lodging House.

It’s not a comforting end to a dull day. Alone, in a secluded place, surrounded by relics of the past, the encounter sends a chill through him. He heads for the brighter streets, deciding not to mention it tonight—but to tell Jack tomorrow, only as a curious coincidence. Of course, just a coincidence.

Yet the words linger. As he walks another mile, the woman’s voice seems to echo in the wind, in the sky, in the river, in the flickering lights. Even the Cathedral chime, striking suddenly as he enters the gatehouse archway, carries a solemn resonance.

And so _he_ climbs the postern stair.

John Jasper enjoys a more pleasant day than either guest. With no music lessons during the holidays, his time is free except for Cathedral duties. Early, he visits shops, ordering treats his nephew enjoys. “My nephew won’t be with me long,” he tells the dealers, “so I must spoil him.”

On his errands, he calls on Mr. Sapsea and mentions that dear Ned and that “inflammable young spark” of Mr. Crisparkle’s are dining at the gatehouse today—to reconcile.

Mr. Sapsea is not fond of the young man. “Un-English,” he declares. And once labeled Un-English, a thing is forever lost.

Jasper regrets hearing this. He knows Mr. Sapsea never speaks without meaning, and that he’s often right. By remarkable chance, Mr. Sapsea is exactly that opinion.

Jasper sings beautifully that day. In the heartfelt plea to have his heart inclined to keep the law, he astonishes his fellows with his voice—melodic, controlled, flawless. He handles difficult music with greater skill than ever. Usually prone to rush, today his timing is perfect.

This ease likely comes from inner peace. His throat is slightly tender—he wears a thick black silk scarf around his neck, both in choir robe and everyday dress. But his composure is evident. Mr. Crisparkle notices it as they leave Vespers.

“I must thank you, Jasper,” he says. “What pleasure I’ve had hearing you today. Beautiful! Delightful! You couldn’t have surpassed yourself unless you were wonderfully well.”

“I _am_ wonderfully well.”

“Nothing uneven,” Mr. Crisparkle observes, gesturing smoothly. “Nothing unsteady, nothing forced, nothing avoided—everything masterfully done, with complete self-control.”

“Thank you. I hope so, if it’s not too much to say.”

“One would think,” Mr. Crisparkle continues, “you’ve tried a new medicine for that occasional ailment of yours.”

“No, really? That’s well observed. Because I have.”

“Then stick to it,” Mr. Crisparkle says, clapping him on the shoulder. “Stick to it.”

“I will.”

“I congratulate you,” he adds, stepping out of the Cathedral. “On all accounts.”

“Thank you again. I’ll walk with you to the Corner if you don’t mind. I have time before my guests arrive—and I want to speak to you. I think you’ll be pleased to hear it.”

“What is it?”

“We spoke the other evening of my black humours.”

Mr. Crisparkle’s face softens with concern.

“I said I’d make you an antidote,” Jasper continues. “You said you hoped I’d burn them.”

“And I still hope so, Jasper.”

“With every reason! I mean to burn this year’s Diary at year’s end.”

“Because you—?” Mr. Crisparkle brightens.

“You anticipate me. Because I’ve been out of sorts—gloomy, bilious, brain-oppressed. You said I was exaggerating. I was.”

Mr. Crisparkle beams.

“I didn’t see it then, because I _was_ out of sorts. But now I’m healthier, and I admit it with real pleasure. I made much of a little—that’s the truth.”

“It does me good,” Mr. Crisparkle exclaims, “to hear you say it!”

“A man in a monotonous life,” Jasper says, “with nerves or stomach out of balance, dwells on an idea until it loses proportion. That was my case. So I’ll burn the record when the book is full—and start anew with a clearer vision.”

“This is better,” Mr. Crisparkle says, stopping at his door to shake hands, “than I could have hoped.”

And so _he_ goes up the postern stair.

Neville Landless had set out early and walked briskly, so that when the church bells of Cloisterham rang for morning service, he was already eight miles distant. By then, his hunger had grown sharp—having started with only a crust of bread—and he stopped at the nearest roadside inn, The Tilted Wagon, to eat.

The place was so seldom visited by travelers—unless they were horses or cattle, for whom troughs and hay were always ready—that it took a long while to rouse the kitchen into producing tea, toast, and bacon. In the meantime, Neville sat in a dusty, sanded parlour, watching the damp fagots in the fireplace struggle to catch flame, wondering how soon someone else might benefit from their weak heat.

The Tilted Wagon, perched high on a hill, lived up to its name in more ways than one. The ground before the door was muddied with hoofprints and trodden straw. Inside, the landlady scolded a baby—one red sock on, the other missing—while the cheese rested in a rusted iron canoe beside a mouldy tablecloth and a green-handled knife. Bread, pale and weeping crumbs, lay in another such vessel. Linen, half-washed and half-dried, trailed across the floor like discarded rags. Everything drank from mugs; everything else seemed to rhyme with “mug.” It was not a place of comfort, nor even decent lodging—but Neville, having no choice, accepted what was offered and resumed his journey after a longer rest than necessary.

A quarter-mile beyond the inn, he paused, uncertain whether to continue along the main road or take a narrow cart track between tall hedges that cut across a breezy heath. The latter route seemed shorter, though rougher. He chose it, walking with effort as the path rose steeply and sank into deep ruts.

Halfway along, he heard footsteps behind him. As they quickened, he stepped aside against the bank to let them pass. But their behavior was odd. Four men passed swiftly. Another four slowed, lingering as if waiting for him to move on. The remaining half-dozen turned abruptly and ran back.

He glanced at the four ahead, then at those behind. All returned his gaze without expression.

He continued forward. The four ahead kept pace, glancing back constantly. The four behind closed in.

When they emerged onto the open slope of the heath, and still maintained this formation—no matter which way he tried to veer—it became clear: he was surrounded.

He stopped. They stopped too.

"Why do you follow me like this?" he demanded. "Are you thieves?"

"Silence," said one voice, low and sharp. "Better be quiet."

"Better be quiet?" Neville repeated, anger rising. "Who spoke?"

No answer.

"It’s good advice, whoever gave it," he snapped. "But I won’t stand here trapped between four men ahead and four behind. I’m going through, and I’ll go through—those four ahead!"

They stood motionless.

"If eight men—or four, or two—attack one," he went on, fists clenched, "the one has no chance but to strike back. And by God, I will—if I’m not left alone!"

He shouldered his heavy stick and charged past the front group.

One man—large, strong, swift—moved sideways, seized him, and brought him down. But not before the stick struck hard.

"Let him go!" the man muttered, voice strained. "Fair play! He’s built like a girl, and he’s got weight on his back. Let him be—I’ll handle him."

They wrestled briefly, rolling in the grass, both faces streaked with blood. Then the man pushed off and rose.

"There. Take him arm-in-arm—any two of you."

It was done immediately.

"As to being thieves, Mr. Landless," the man said, spitting blood and wiping his face, "you know better. We wouldn’t have touched you unless you forced us. We’re taking you to the high road. You’ll find help there if you need it.—Wipe his face, someone. Look at the blood!"

Once cleaned, Neville recognized the speaker: Joe, the driver of the Cloisterhad omnibus, whom he’d seen once before—on the day of his arrival.

"And now," Joe added, "don’t speak. There’s a friend waiting for you at the road—we split up earlier. Say nothing until you reach him. Bring the stick, someone. Let’s go."

Utterly confused, Neville said nothing. Between two men who gripped his arms, he walked forward like a man in a trance—until they reached the high road and found a small gathering.

The men who had turned back were among them. At the center stood Mr. Jasper and Mr. Crisparkle.

His captors led him to the Minor Canon and released him, as a sign of respect.

"What is this?" Neville cried, stunned. "What happened? I feel as if I’ve lost my mind!"

"Where is my nephew?" Jasper asked, voice raw with panic.

"Where is your nephew?" Neville echoed. "Why ask me?"

"Because you were the last person with him," Jasper shot back. "And he’s vanished."

"Vanished?" Neville gasped.

"Wait," said Mr. Crisparkle. "Mr. Landless, you're shaken. Collect yourself. This matters greatly. Listen to me."

"I'll try," Neville whispered. "But I feel mad."

"You left Mr. Jasper last night with Edwin Drood?"

"Yes."

"At what hour?"

"Was it twelve?" Neville asked, hand to his head, turning to Jasper.

"Exactly," Crisparkle confirmed. "That's the time Mr. Jasper told me. You went down to the river together?"

"Undoubtedly. To watch the wind."

"How long did you stay?"

"Ten minutes—no more. Then we walked to your house. He left me at the door."

"Did he say he was returning to the river?"

"No. He said he was going straight home."

The bystanders exchanged glances.

Jasper, who had been watching Neville closely, suddenly leaned forward.

"What are those stains on your clothes?" he asked, voice low and sharp.

All eyes turned to the blood on Neville’s coat.

"And look here!" Jasper said, snatching the stick from the man holding it. "Same marks! I know this stick—it’s yours. You carried it last night. What does this mean?"

"In God’s name, tell us!" Crisparkle urged.

"That man and I," Neville said, pointing to his attacker, "fought over the stick just now. You can see the same marks on him. What was I supposed to think, when eight strangers attacked me? Could I guess the real reason when they gave none?"

They admitted they'd stayed silent—thought it wiser. And yes, the fight had happened.

Yet even those who had witnessed it now stared darkly at the dried smears on the clothes.

"We must return," Crisparkle said. "You’ll come back to clear your name, of course?"

"Of course, sir."

"Mr. Landless will walk beside me," Crisparkle announced, looking around. "Come, Neville."

They began the walk back. The others followed at varying distances—except Jasper, who walked on Neville’s other side and never moved.

He remained silent throughout. Crisparkle repeated his questions. Neville repeated his answers. They offered theories. But Jasper said nothing.

Not because he didn’t hear. But because Crisparkle’s tone invited participation—and Jasper refused to respond. His face remained fixed, unreadable.

As they neared the city, Crisparkle suggested calling on the Mayor immediately.

Jasper nodded stiffly. Still, he said nothing—until they entered Mr. Sapsea’s parlor.

Crisparkle explained their purpose: a voluntary statement under suspicion.

Then Jasper broke silence.

"I place my full trust—humanly speaking—in Mr. Sapsea’s judgment," he said. "There is no conceivable reason why my nephew would flee, unless you suggest one. Then I defer. No rational likelihood exists that he returned to the river and drowned—unless you think otherwise. Then again, I defer. I wash my hands of all dreadful suspicions—unless you prove they are unavoidable, given my last companion before his disappearance (with whom I was not on good terms). Then, once more, I defer. My own mind is shattered—overwhelmed with doubt. But yours, Mr. Sapsea, is steady."

Sapsea nodded slowly. "This case," he declared, "has a dark look. An un-English complexion." He paused, eyes locking with Neville’s.

Then he drifted into rambling nonsense—more absurd than even a mayor might be expected to utter—before emerging with the revelation: "To take a life is to take something that doesn’t belong to you."

He hesitated—whether to issue a warrant for Neville’s arrest. But Crisparkle protested fiercely, vouching for the young man’s presence in his own home, and promising to produce him whenever required.

Sapsea relented.

Instead, he proposed dragging the river, searching its banks, sending notices to surrounding towns and London, posting placards begging Edwin Drood—should he have vanished for any unknown reason—to spare his uncle’s grief and let him know he was alive.

All agreed. Plans were made instantly.

No one knew who was more horrified: Neville Landless or John Jasper.

But Jasper’s position forced action; Neville’s, silence. Yet both were broken.

At dawn, searchers began work on the river—men in barges with poles and nets, others ashore with hatchets, spades, ropes, dogs. Jack-boots sank into mud. Rushes were torn apart. Watermarks stood like ghosts.

At night, lanterns lit the water. Fires burned along creeks. Watchers waited at shingly causeways, listening for the tide’s whisper, hoping for a body.

But no trace of Edwin Drood surfaced.

Day after day, the search continued—on river and shore—yet nothing.

Jasper worked tirelessly, exhausted, muddy, his clothes torn. When night fell, he returned home—drenched in fatigue.

Just as he collapsed into his chair, Mr. Grewgious appeared.

"This is strange news," Grewgious said. "Strange and fearful."

Jasper merely lifted his eyes—then dropped them again, slumping over the armrest.

Grewgious smoothed his brow, staring into the fire.

"How is your ward?" Jasper asked faintly.

"Poor little thing. You can imagine her state."

"Have you seen his sister?" Jasper asked.

"Whose?"

The abruptness, the slow turn of Grewgious’s gaze from the fire to Jasper’s face—might have angered another man. But Jasper, worn out, simply opened his eyes.

"The suspected young man’s."

"Do you suspect him?" Grewgious asked.

"I don’t know what to think. I cannot decide."

"Nor I," Grewgious replied. "But since you called him 'the suspected young man,' I thought you had made up your mind. I’ve just left Miss Landless."

"What is her condition?"

"Defiant of all suspicion. Unshakable faith in her brother."

"Poor thing."

"But that’s not why I came," Grewgious continued. "It’s about my ward. I have news that will surprise you. At least, it surprised me."

Jasper groaned, turning in his chair.

"Shall I wait till tomorrow?" Grewgious asked. "I warn you—it will surprise you."

Now, a flicker of attention sparked in Jasper’s eyes as he saw Grewgious smooth his head again, fix his gaze on the fire—mouth tight, jaw clenched.

"What is it?" Jasper demanded, sitting upright.

"To be sure," Grewgious said, slowly, deliberately, warming his palms at the fire, "I might have known sooner. She gave me the clue. But I’m such an exceedingly angular man, I took everything for granted."

"What is it?" Jasper repeated.

Grewgious, still warming his hands, still staring sideways at him, continued:

"This young couple—the lost youth and Miss Rosa, my ward—though betrothed for years, and nearly married—"

Jasper saw a white face, trembling lips, muddy hands gripping the chair.

"—came gradually to realize," Grewgious went on, "that they would be happier, healthier, as friends—brother and sister—than as husband and wife."

Jasper saw a leaden face, drops of sweat like steel beads.

"They resolved, finally, to end their engagement—openly, gently, with kindness. They met. After honest talk, they agreed to part forever."

Grewgious saw a ghostly figure rise from the chair, mouth agape, hands flung toward his head.

"One of them—your nephew—feared you’d be heartbroken by such a change. So he kept it secret for a few days. He meant for me to reveal it when I came to speak with you—when he would be gone."

Grewgious saw the figure throw back its head, clutch its hair, twist away.

"I speak to you," he said, "and he is gone."

He paused.

"They parted—firmly, yet with tears—on the very evening you last saw them together."

A scream tore from the room.

Grewgious looked down.

No man. No figure.

Only a heap of torn, muddy clothes on the floor.

Still, he did not move. Still, he opened and shut his palms at the fire. Still, he stared.

When John Jasper regained consciousness, he found himself attended by Mr. and Mrs. Tope—summoned by his visitor to care for him after a sudden fit or fainting spell. The visitor sat rigidly in a chair, hands clasped over his knees, watching Jasper’s slow return to awareness with an expressionless gaze.

“There! You’re come round nicely now, sir,” said Mrs. Tope, dabbing at her eyes. “You were utterly worn out, and no wonder!”

“A man,” declared Mr. Grewgious, speaking as if reciting a lesson from memory, “cannot have his rest disturbed, his mind tormented, and his body exhausted without being thoroughly worn out.”

“I fear I’ve alarmed you,” Jasper murmured weakly as he was helped into his armchair.

“Not at all,” replied Mr. Grewgious. “You are too considerate.”

“Not at all,” he repeated, as though rehearsing the phrase. “You must take some wine, sir,” added Mrs. Tope, bustling about. “And the jelly I had ready for you—though you refused it at noon, even when I warned you what might follow. And you haven’t eaten breakfast, either! There’s a wing of the roast fowl that’s been held back more times than I can count. It’ll be on the table in five minutes—and this good gentleman may stay to see you eat.”

The visitor responded with a sharp, dry snort—one that could mean anything or nothing. Mrs. Tope would have been baffled by it, but her attention was already consumed by the meal.

“You’ll join me?” Jasper asked, as the cloth was laid.

“I couldn’t manage a bite,” said Mr. Grewgious, shaking his head.

Jasper ate and drank with almost desperate urgency. His movements were hurried, his focus detached—he seemed less concerned with taste than with fortifying himself against further collapse. He devoured food and wine not for pleasure, but as armor against despair.

Mr. Grewgious remained motionless, his face blank, his posture stiff—a man whose politeness was so rigid it felt like resistance. As if to say: *I cannot offer a single thought, nor engage in conversation, thank you very much.*

After finishing, Jasper pushed away his plate and glass, then sat silent for a moment, lost in thought.

“Do you know,” he began, “that I find some small comfort in the news you’ve given me?”

*Do you?* Mr. Grewgious returned, plainly implying: *I do not. Not in the least.*

“After the shock of learning what happened to my dear boy—so unexpected, so shattering to every hope I’d built for him—and after time to reflect… yes. I find something to hold onto.”

“I shall be glad to gather your crumbs,” said Mr. Grewgious, dryly.

“Is there not—tell me if I’m mistaken—hope that, finding himself in this new situation, burdened with awkward explanations on all sides, he chose to flee? That he avoided the trouble by disappearing?”

“It’s possible,” said Mr. Grewgious slowly. “It has happened. I’ve read of cases where people, rather than face weeks of scrutiny and idle questions, simply vanished—never to be heard from again.”

“Yes,” said Jasper, warming to the idea. “When I believed my son had kept nothing from me—especially such a matter as this—what light was there in that darkness? When I thought his bride was here, their marriage near, how could I imagine he’d leave suddenly, recklessly, cruelly? But now—now that I know—can’t I see a glimmer? Suppose he left of his own accord. Wouldn’t that make his absence more understandable? Less cruel? The fact that he parted from your ward just before vanishing—that alone is reason enough to go. It doesn’t excuse the pain to me—but it lessens the cruelty to her.”

Mr. Grewgious had no choice but to nod.

“And even for me,” Jasper continued, growing more animated, “he knew you were coming. He knew you carried this truth. If your words have stirred new thoughts in me, then surely he foresaw the same conclusions. If he did—then even the cruelty to me—me, John Jasper, Music Master—fades into insignificance.”

Again, Mr. Grewgious could only assent.

“I’ve had doubts—terrible ones,” Jasper went on. “But your revelation, though it struck me hard, also showed me that my boy had hidden something from me—someone he loved deeply. Yet instead of crushing me, it kindles hope. You don’t deny it. You admit it’s reasonable. I begin to believe it’s possible…” He clasped his hands tightly. “…that he disappeared of his own will—and that he may still be alive.”

At that moment, Mr. Crisparkle entered.

Jasper repeated his words: “I begin to believe it’s possible he left of his own accord—and may yet live.”

Crisparkle took a seat. “Why so?”

Jasper explained his reasoning.

Had the arguments been weaker, Crisparkle might have been more skeptical. But they were compelling, and the Minor Canon had long struggled with guilt over his own silence. He had withheld two critical facts: Neville Landless’s second violent outburst toward Edwin Drood, and the jealousy that had flared within him—jealousy Crisparkle knew was real. He believed Neville innocent, yet feared that revealing these truths might only deepen suspicion. He wrestled with conscience, weighing honesty against harm.

But now, witnessing Jasper’s integrity—his willingness to lay bare even painful truths—Crisparkle resolved to speak.

Addressing Mr. Grewgious, who now stood as a figure of authority due to his revelations, Crisparkle spoke plainly:

“I bear witness to Mr. Jasper’s fairness. I have full confidence that Edwin Drood will one day be cleared of all suspicion. My belief in him was formed despite knowing his temper was fierce—and that it was inflamed by his mistaken love for the same young woman. I confess, I have held back two facts: Neville’s second rage against Edwin, and his jealous passion. But I no longer withhold them. I give them now.”

Jasper’s mood brightened, though briefly. Then came the blow—Crisparkle’s confession. Yet Jasper clung to his hope. “Even so,” he insisted, “I will hold fast to what I’ve gained from Mr. Grewgious. If no trace of my boy is found—if we cannot prove he was murdered—I will never abandon the idea that he fled of his own wild will.”

That night, Mr. Crisparkle walked to Cloisterham Weir—drawn by a troubled mind. He didn’t plan the walk; he didn’t notice the path. Only when he heard the rush of water did he realize where he was.

He stood, listening. A line from scripture surfaced unbidden: *airy tongues that syllable men’s names.* He brushed it aside as if it were physical.

The night was starlit. The Weir was two miles above where the boys had watched the storm. No search had been made here—the tide had flowed downstream, making lower stretches more likely to yield evidence. Yet Crisparkle felt something unusual in the air.

He listened. Nothing. He looked. Nothing. But he decided to return at dawn.

He dreamed of the Weir all night. At sunrise, he returned.

It was a clear, frosty morning. Every detail was visible. He studied the scene for minutes—then his eyes locked on a spot. He turned away, looked at the sky, the earth, then back. The spot caught his gaze again.

It was a tiny gleam—stationary, not moving with the water. He stripped off his coat, plunged into the icy river, and swam to it.

Climbing the timbers, he pulled out a gold watch, caught in the woodwork. Engraved on the back: *E.D.*

He brought it ashore, dove again—searching every crevice, every depth—until cold forced him up. He found only a shirt-pin embedded in mud.

With both items, he returned to Cloisterham. He took Neville Landless with him to the Mayor.

Jasper was summoned. The watch and pin were identified. Neville was detained.

The town erupted. Rumors flew—Neville was vindictive, violent, capable of murder. Before coming to England, he had caused the deaths of “Natives”—nomadic people scattered across continents—whom Cloisterham imagined as always black, always devout, always calling themselves *Me*, and others *Massa* or *Missie*. He had nearly broken Mrs. Crisparkle’s heart. He had threatened Mr. Crisparkle’s life. He had vowed to kill everyone, become the last man.

He had been brought to Cloisterham by a philanthropist who said: *“I owe it to my fellow-creatures that he should be, in the words of Bentham, where he is the cause of the greatest danger to the smallest number.”*

But now, the evidence mounted.

He had threatened Edwin. He had a motive—jealousy, born of his own making. He had armed himself for the night. He had left early, after preparing to depart. Bloodstains were found on his clothes—possibly from injury, possibly from murder. His room revealed destroyed papers, rearranged belongings—all on the afternoon of the disappearance.

The jeweler confirmed: the watch had been wound and set for Edwin Drood at 2:20 PM that day. It had run down before being thrown into the river. The jeweler was certain it had not been rewound.

This suggested the watch was taken shortly after Edwin left Jasper’s house at midnight—with Neville, the last person seen with him—and discarded hours later.

Why discard it?

If Edwin had been murdered and disfigured—so that identification would be impossible—then the murderer would remove the most identifiable items: the watch, the shirt-pin.

And the opportunity? Neville had been seen wandering aimlessly, distraught, throughout the city—perfect cover to dispose of evidence.

The meeting between the two young men? It had been arranged not by Neville, but by Crisparkle. Could Neville have gone reluctantly, in a state of fury?

Each point weakened his defense.

Even the theory that Edwin had fled was undermined. The young lady he’d just left said, with sorrow, that Edwin had planned to wait for Mr. Grewgious’s arrival. And yet—Edwin vanished before Grewgious arrived.

Suspicion grew. Neville was detained. Search parties scoured the land. Jasper worked tirelessly.

But nothing more was found.

No body. No proof of death.

Eventually, without conclusive evidence, Neville was released.

Then came the inevitable: he had to leave.

The town rejected him. Mrs. Crisparkle feared for her son. The Dean intervened.

“Mr. Crisparkle,” he said, “human justice may err, but it must act according to its lights. The days of sanctuary are past. This young man must not take refuge with us.”

“You mean he must leave my house?”

“I claim no authority in your home,” the Dean replied. “I merely speak of the necessity you face—of depriving him of your counsel.”

“It is lamentable,” Crisparkle said.

“Very much so,” the Dean agreed. “And if it is unavoidable—”

“As you find it to be,” the Dean finished.

Crisparkle bowed. “It is hard to prejudge his case, sir, but I am convinced—”

“Just so,” the Dean interrupted smoothly. “We clergy keep our hearts warm and our heads cool. We maintain a judicious middle course.”

“I hope you don’t object,” Crisparkle ventured, “if I publicly declare that he will return whenever new evidence arises?”

“Not at all,” the Dean said. “But I wouldn’t say it emphatically.”

*Emphatically?* he repeated, drawing it out. “I think not. In fact, Mr. Crisparkle, keeping our hearts warm and our heads cool—we need do nothing emphatically.”

So Minor Canon Row saw Neville Landless no more.

Only then did John Jasper quietly return to his place in the choir.

Haggard, eyes bloodshot, his hope extinguished. The sanguine mood was gone. His worst fears had returned.

A few days later, while undressing, he took his diary from his coat pocket. He flipped through the pages, paused—and handed it silently to Mr. Crisparkle.

There, written in bold, firm script, was this entry:

> *My dear boy is murdered. The discovery of the watch and shirt-pin convinces me he was killed that night, and his jewelry removed to prevent identification. All false hopes I built upon his separation from his betrothed are blown away. They perish before this fatal truth. I swear, and record this oath on this page: I will never discuss this mystery with any human being until I hold the clue in my hand. I will never relax in secrecy or in search. I will fix the crime of my dear dead boy’s murder upon the murderer. And I devote myself—to his destruction.*

Half a year had passed, and Mr. Crisparkle sat in the waiting room of the London headquarters of the Haven of Philanthropy, awaiting an audience with Mr. Honeythunder.

In his youth, during his college days, Mr. Crisparkle had been acquainted with professors of the Noble Art of boxing—men who trained in the ring and gathered for bouts under gloves and rules. Now, as he observed the men around him in this charitable institution, he found a striking resemblance between these so-called Professors of Philanthropy and those pugilists. The shape of their skulls, particularly at the back, bore a remarkable similarity—the same bulging contours that phrenologists associated with aggression, dominance, and a readiness to “pitch into” others. These philanthropists moved through the room with the same combative air he remembered from the old prizefighting circles: eyes sharp, shoulders squared, as if prepared for confrontation at any moment.

There was talk of organizing a moral campaign somewhere on the rural circuit—what might have been called a “moral mill.” Other members debated which speaker would deliver the most effective oratory, just as sporting publicans once discussed the merits of boxers before a match. One man, notably celebrated for his rhetorical flair on platforms, wore a black suit and struck Mr. Crisparkle as the living image of a long-dead benefactor known in his time as Frosty-faced Fogo—a man who once managed the ropes and stakes of the boxing ring. Only three differences stood out between these two kinds of "professors."

First, the philanthropists were poorly conditioned—overweight, flabby, their faces and figures bloated with what boxing experts call “suet pudding.” Second, they lacked the discipline and temper of true fighters; their language was coarse and bitter. Third—and most damning—their code of conduct was far more brutal than any fighting rulebook. They not only allowed themselves to wear down their opponents but also to attack them when down, strike them anywhere, kick, stomp, gouge, and slander them behind their backs without mercy. In these respects, the real pugilists were far nobler.

Mr. Crisparkle lost himself in thought, comparing the two worlds, while watching the constant flow of people entering and exiting—always seeming to snatch something from someone else, never giving anything in return. He didn’t notice his name being called until it was repeated twice.

When he finally responded, he was led by a shabby, underpaid assistant—so ill-dressed and dispirited that he seemed to serve an enemy of humanity rather than its champion—to Mr. Honeythunder’s office.

“Sir,” said Mr. Honeythunder in a voice like a schoolmaster reprimanding a disobedient pupil, “sit down.”

Mr. Crisparkle obeyed.

While Mr. Honeythunder signed the final few hundred of thousands of circulars demanding that poor families “stump up” and become philanthropists—or face damnation—a second shabby assistant collected the papers and carried them away.

"Now, Mr. Crisparkle," said Honeythunder, turning his chair half toward him, arms folded, brows furrowed, as though he meant to make short work of the matter, “we hold different views, you and I, on the sanctity of human life.”

"Do we?" asked the Minor Canon.

"We do, sir."

"Would you care to explain your view on that subject?"

"That human life is sacred, sir."

"And yours?"

"By George!" replied Honeythunder, leaning forward sharply, “that's best known to yourself.”

"Granted," said Crisparkle calmly. "But you began by claiming we differ. That implies you believe you know my views. So pray—what are they?"

"There is a young man," said Honeythunder, as if the youth made the tragedy worse, “swept off the face of the earth by violence. What do you call that?"

"Murder," said Crisparkle.

"And the one who did it?"

"A murderer."

"I'm glad you admit that much," retorted Honeythunder, stiffening further. "I didn’t expect it." He glared.

"Then perhaps you could clarify what you mean by those unjustifiable terms."

"I don’t sit here," said Honeythunder, raising his voice, "to be browbeaten."

"As the only other person present," replied Crisparkle quietly, "no one knows better than I do. But I interrupt your explanation."

"Murder!" thundered Honeythunder, falling into a kind of theatrical reverie, folding his arms, nodding slowly after each word. "Bloodshed! Abel! Cain! I hold no terms with Cain. I recoil from the red hand when it’s offered me!"

Instead of leaping to his feet and shouting as the Brotherhood would have done, Crisparkle simply uncrossed his legs and said, “Don’t let me interrupt your explanation—when you begin it.”

"The Commandments say: No murder. NO murder, sir!" continued Honeythunder, pausing dramatically as if Crisparkle had dared suggest murder was permissible in moderation.

"And they also say: You shall bear no false witness," observed Crisparkle.

"Enough!" bellowed Honeythunder, with such solemn severity that it would have silenced a crowd. "E—e—nough! My late wards are now of age. I am released from a trust I cannot contemplate without horror. Here are the accounts you’ve agreed to accept on their behalf, and here is the balance statement you’ve undertaken to receive—and should receive without delay. And let me tell you, sir, I wish you were better employed—as a man and a Minor Canon." He nodded once. "Better employed." Another nod. "Bet—ter em—ployed!" A third nod, all added together.

Crisparkle rose, slightly flushed but composed.

"Mr. Honeythunder," he said, picking up the papers, "whether I am better or worse employed is a matter of opinion. You might think me better off enrolling as a member of your Society."

"Ay, indeed, sir!" snapped Honeythunder, shaking his head threateningly. "It would have been better for you if you had done that long ago!"

"I disagree."

"Or," he continued, shaking his head again, "I might think one of your profession better occupied in uncovering guilt than leaving it to laymen."

"My profession," replied Crisparkle, "teaches me that its first duty lies with those in need, those oppressed, desolate, and suffering. But since I’ve concluded it’s not my role to make professions, I’ll say no more. Yet I owe it to Mr. Neville, to his sister—and to myself—to say this: I *know* I understood Mr. Neville’s mind and heart at the time of this event. I see nothing to conceal or excuse in him that requires correction, and I am certain his story is true. Because I believe that, I stand by him. As long as that belief holds, I will stand by him. If any force could shake me, I’d be ashamed of my weakness—so ashamed that no one’s approval, man or woman, could compensate for the loss of my own self-respect."

A good man. A brave man. And modest too. There was no boastfulness in him—only quiet, steadfast loyalty, like a schoolboy keeping wicket in a field. True souls are always thus. Always have been. Always will be. Greatness of spirit has no smallness.

"So then," asked Honeythunder, turning abruptly, "who do you think committed the deed?"

"Heaven forbid," said Crisparkle, "that in seeking to clear one man I should lightly accuse another. I accuse no one."

"Tcha!" Honeythunder spat with contempt. This was not how the Brotherhood operated. "And remember, sir—you are not an impartial witness."

"How am I biased?" Crisparkle asked, genuinely puzzled.

"There was a stipend paid to you for your pupil," said Honeythunder bluntly, "which may have influenced your judgment."

"Perhaps I expect to keep it?" Crisparkle replied, catching on. "Do you mean that too?"

"Well, sir," said Honeythunder, rising and stuffing his hands into his trousers pockets, "I don’t go about measuring people for caps. If they find one that fits, they can wear it. That’s their business, not mine."

Crisparkle looked at him with growing indignation.

"Mr. Honeythunder," he said, "I hoped to avoid commenting on the intrusion of platform tactics into private conversation. But you've given me no choice. Your behavior is detestable."

"They don’t suit you, I suppose," Honeythunder muttered.

"They are," Crisparkle continued, ignoring the interruption, "detestable. They violate both Christian justice and gentlemanly restraint. You assume a great crime was committed by someone I, knowing the circumstances and having strong reasons, firmly believe innocent. Because I disagree with you on this point, what is your response? Instantly turn on me, accusing me of not understanding the crime’s enormity—and of aiding it! Next time, you set up a platform credulity: a motion passed unanimously in favor of some absurd delusion. I refuse to believe it, and you declare I believe nothing—because I won't bow to your false god, I deny the true God! Then you claim war is a calamity and propose abolishing it with a series of empty resolutions. I reject your claim and your remedy, and you accuse me of reveling in battlefields like a fiend! Or you condemn the sober for the drunken, and when I defend the sober, you proclaim I want to turn God’s creatures into swine! In every case, your supporters—your regular Professors—run wild like mad Malays, attributing the worst motives recklessly, quoting statistics skewed as badly as a ledger with only one side. Therefore, Mr. Honeythunder, I consider the platform a bad influence even in public life—but in private, it becomes unbearable."

"These are strong words, sir!" exclaimed Honeythunder.

"I hope so," said Crisparkle. "Good morning."

He left the Haven quickly, but soon settled into his usual brisk stride. A smile touched his lips as he walked, wondering what the china shepherdess would have said if she'd seen him take on Honeythunder. He had just enough harmless vanity to believe he’d struck hard—and to glow with pride that he’d trimmed the Philanthropic Jacket neatly.

He went to Staple Inn—not to P. J. T. or Mr. Grewgious—but climbed many creaking stairs to reach attic rooms in a corner. He turned the latch of the unbolted door and stepped inside, finding Neville Landless at his table.

The rooms had an air of retreat and solitude. Both the space and its occupant were worn. Sloping ceilings, rusty locks, heavy beams—all slowly decaying—gave the place a prison-like feel. Neville had the haggard look of a prisoner. Yet sunlight streamed through the ugly garret window, which jutted out among the tiles. On the cracked, smoke-blackened ledge outside, sparrows hopped rheumatically, like tiny crippled birds who’d left their crutches behind. Nearby, leaves rustled gently, creating a faint music that would have been melody in the country.

The rooms were sparsely furnished but filled with books. Everything spoke of a poor student. Crisparkle’s presence among them—whether as chooser, lender, or donor—was evident in the warm glance he cast upon them as he entered.

“How goes it, Neville?”

“I’m in good heart, Mr. Crisparkle, working steadily.”

“I wish your eyes weren’t so large and so bright,” said Crisparkle, releasing the hand he’d taken.

“They brighten at the sight of you,” Neville replied. “If you were to leave me, they’d soon grow dull.”

“Rally, rally!” urged Crisparkle, encouragingly. “Fight for it, Neville!”

“If I were dying,” said Neville, “a word from you would revive me. If my pulse stopped, your touch would make it beat again. But I’ve rallied, and I’m doing well.”

Crisparkle turned Neville’s face toward the light.

“I want to see a ruddier flush here,” he said, pointing to his own healthy cheek. “More sun must shine on you.”

Neville drooped slightly.

“I’m not strong enough for that yet,” he whispered. “I may become so, but I can’t bear it now. If you’d walked those Cloisterham streets as I did—if you’d seen the averted eyes, the silent distance people gave me so I wouldn’t touch them—you wouldn’t think it unreasonable that I still avoid daylight.”

“My poor fellow,” said Crisparkle, his voice full of sympathy. “I never thought it unreasonable. But I’d like you to try.”

“And that would give me reason to,” Neville said. “But I can’t yet. I can’t convince myself that strangers’ eyes don’t suspect me. I feel marked—even at night. But darkness hides me. It gives me courage.”

Crisparkle laid a hand on his shoulder, looking down.

“If I could change my name,” said Neville, “I would. But as you wisely told me, I can’t—it would seem like guilt. If I could flee to another land, I might find peace. But that’s impossible—same reason. Hiding would be seen as guilt. It feels harsh to be tied to a stake, innocent though I am. But I don’t complain.”

“And you mustn’t expect a miracle, Neville,” said Crisparkle, kindly.

“No, sir. I know that. Only time and circumstance can help me.”

“It will right you, Neville.”

“I believe it. I hope I live to see it.”

Seeing that his mood was slipping and sensing the hand on his shoulder tremble slightly, Neville lifted his spirits.

“Excellent conditions for study, anyway!” he said. “And you know how much I need it. Not to mention your advice to study law—especially law. I follow your guidance, of course. Such a good friend and helper!”

He took Crisparkle’s hand from his shoulder and kissed it. Crisparkle smiled at the books—though not as brightly as when he’d entered.

“I gather from your silence that my late guardian opposes you, Mr. Crisparkle?”

“The man is utterly unreasonable,” replied Crisparkle. “Whether he’s adverse, perverse, or reversed matters not to anyone sensible.”

“Well for me,” sighed Neville, “that I can live on economy while I learn and wait to be cleared. Else I might prove the proverb: while the grass grows, the steed starves!”

He opened his books and soon immersed himself in annotated pages, while Crisparkle sat beside him, guiding, correcting, advising. His Cathedral duties made these visits rare—coming only every few weeks—but they were invaluable to Neville.

After their studies ended, they leaned on the windowsill, looking down at the garden patch.

“Next week,” said Crisparkle, “you’ll cease to be alone. You’ll have a devoted companion.”

“And yet,” replied Neville, “this seems an unsuitable place for my sister.”

“I don’t think so,” said Crisparkle. “There’s duty here. And a woman’s courage and sense are needed.”

“I meant,” explained Neville, “the surroundings are dull, unwomanly. Helena has no proper society here.”

“You only need to remember,” said Crisparkle, “that you’re here—and she has to draw you into the sunlight.”

They fell silent. Then Crisparkle began again.

“When we first spoke, Neville, you told me your sister had risen above your past, as the tower of Cloisterham Cathedral towers over the chimneys of Minor Canon Corner. Do you remember?”

“Perfectly.”

“I thought it enthusiastic then. I don’t care what I think now. What matters is this: under the head of Pride, your sister is a powerful example.”

“Under all heads that form a noble character, she is.”

“Say so. But take this one. She has learned to govern her pride. Even when wounded by sympathy for you, she masters it. She has suffered deeply in the same streets you did. Her life is darkened by the same shadow. Yet she bends her pride into quiet dignity—not haughtiness, but steady confidence in you and in truth. She walks those streets now with honor, respected by all. Every day since Edwin Drood vanished, she has faced malice and folly—for you—only a brave soul can endure. She will continue so. A weaker pride might break—but hers never will. It knows no fear, and no power over her.”

The pale cheek beside him flushed with emotion.

“I’ll do all I can to imitate her,” said Neville.

“Do so,” replied Crisparkle firmly. “Be a truly brave man, as she is a truly brave woman. It’s growing dark. Will you walk with me when it’s fully dark? Mind—I’m not the one waiting for darkness.”

Neville agreed. But Crisparkle said he had a brief courtesy call to make on Mr. Grewgious, and would cross to his chambers and return to Neville’s door.

Mr. Grewgious sat upright as ever, drinking wine at his open window. His glass and decanter rested on a round table beside him; his legs stretched out on the windowsill. He had only one hinge in his body—like a bootjack.

“How do you do, reverend sir?” said Grewgious, offering hospitality which Crisparkle declined with equal warmth. “And how is your charge getting on in the rooms I recommended to you?”

Crisparkle replied appropriately.

“I’m glad you approve,” said Grewgious. “Because I have a fancy for keeping an eye on him.”

As Grewgious had to tilt his head upward to see the chamber, the phrase was clearly metaphorical.

“And how did you leave Mr. Jasper, reverend sir?”

“Pretty well.”

“And where?”

“At Cloisterham.”

“And when?”

“This morning.”

“Umps!” said Grewgious. “He didn’t say he was coming?”

“Coming where?”

“Anywhere, for instance?”

“No.”

“Because here he is,” said Grewgious, who had been scanning the street outside. “And he doesn’t look agreeable, does he?”

Crisparkle leaned toward the window just as Grewgious added:

“Look.”

Around this time, a stranger arrived in Cloisterham—a man with a head of thick, white hair and dark, striking eyebrows. Dressed in a tight blue surtout, a buff waistcoat, and gray trousers, he carried himself with an air that suggested military discipline, though he declared at the Crozier Hotel (the town’s most respectable inn) that he was nothing more than a retired gentleman living off his savings. He announced further that he intended to rent lodgings in the old city for a few months, perhaps longer—possibly settling there permanently.

He made these remarks in the hotel’s coffee room, standing before the empty fireplace, waiting for his fried sole, veal cutlet, and pint of sherry. The waiter—whose duties were sparse enough to allow him full attention—was the only audience.

The newcomer had an unusually large head and a shock of white hair so abundant it seemed to defy gravity. As he shook it like a Newfoundland dog preparing for dinner, he turned to the waiter.

“I suppose,” he said, “a decent lodging for a single man might be found hereabouts?”

The waiter assured him it could.

“Something old,” the man added. “Take my hat down from that peg, will you? No, don’t bring it—just look inside.”

The waiter read the name stitched on the lining: *Datchery*.

“Now you know my name,” said the man. “Dick Datchery. Hang it up again.”

He continued: “I’d prefer something aged, odd, out of the way—something venerable, architecturally interesting, and inconvenient.”

“We’ve a fair number of inconvenient lodgings,” replied the waiter, confident in the town’s offerings. “But architectural… well, that’s harder to find.”

“Anything Cathedral-like?” Mr. Datchery suggested.

The waiter brightened. “Mr. Tope,” he said, rubbing his chin, “would be the best person to ask about such things.”

“Who is Mr. Tope?” asked Datchery.

The waiter explained that he was the Verger—the keeper of the Cathedral’s gatehouse—and that Mrs. Tope had once offered rooms for rent. But no one ever took them, and her sign, long a landmark in Cloisterham, had vanished—likely fallen and never replaced.

“I’ll call on Mrs. Tope after dinner,” said Datchery.

And so, after finishing his meal, he set off with directions from the waiter. But the Crozier was a quiet, unassuming place, and the instructions were too precise to be helpful. Soon, Datchery found himself wandering near the Cathedral Tower, circling it aimlessly, convinced Mrs. Tope’s house was nearby—warm when he saw the tower, cold when he didn’t.

Eventually, he stumbled upon a patch of burial ground where a sheep grazed unhappily. A small boy, armed with stones, was pelting it through the railings. The animal had already been injured in one leg, and the boy was thrilled by the idea of crippling the rest.

“’It ’im agin!” he shouted as the sheep jumped. “Made a dint in his wool!”

“Leave it alone,” said Datchery. “Don’t you see you’ve hurt it?”

“Yer lie,” the boy retorted. “’E went and lamed isself. I seen ’im do it. Gave ’im a shy as a warning not to bruise the master’s mutton no more.”

“Come here,” Datchery ordered.

“I won’t. I’ll come when yer can ketch me.”

“Stay where you are,” said Datchery, “and point out Mr. Tope’s house.”

“How can I stay and show you where Topeseses is, when Topeseses is t’other side the Kinfreederal, over the crossings, and round ever so many comers? Stoo-pid! Ya-a-ah!”

“Show me where it is,” said Datchery, “and I’ll give you something.”

“Come on, then.”

With that, the boy led him. After some walking, they stopped near an arched passage. The boy pointed.

“Lookie yonder. That winder and door?”

“That’s Tope’s?”

“Yer lie—it ain’t. That’s Jarsper’s.”

“Indeed?” Datchery said, intrigued.

“Yes, and I ain’t a-goin’ no nearer ’IM. Not if I knows it. Wait till I fling a jolly good flint at the back o’ ’is jolly old ’ed!”

He gestured to the other side of the arch. “There’s a low door, down two steps. That’s Topeseses—with ’is name on a hoval plate.”

“Good,” said Datchery, pulling out a shilling. “You owe me half of this.”

“Yer lie! I don’t owe yer nothing. Never seen yer.”

“I tell you you do,” said Datchery. “Because I have no sixpence. So next time we meet, you’ll do something else for me—to settle the debt.”

“All right, give us ’old.”

“What’s your name?”

“Deputy. Travellers’ Twopenny, ’cross the green.”

The boy darted off with the coin, just in case Datchery changed his mind. He paused at a safe distance, dancing wildly to mock the idea of returning it.

Datchery removed his hat, shook his white hair once more, and walked on—calm, resigned, purposeful.

Mr. Tope’s dwelling, connected to Mr. Jasper’s gatehouse by an upper stair, was modest and dim—more dungeon than home. Thick ancient walls, groined ceilings, narrow windows set deep into stone. Two chambers opened into each other, both dark and cramped. These were the rooms Mrs. Tope had offered for years—unwanted, until now.

Datchery, however, liked them. With the main door open, he could watch passersby. If Mr. and Mrs. Tope used their private side stair leading directly into the Precincts, he would have privacy. The rent was reasonable. Everything was quaintly inconvenient—exactly what he wanted.

He agreed to take the lodgings immediately, paying in advance. Possession would begin the following evening, on condition he could reference Mr. Jasper, who lived across the gateway.

Mrs. Tope mentioned that Mr. Jasper was very solitary, very sad—but she believed he would vouch for her.

“Have you heard anything about what happened here last winter?” she asked.

Datchery admitted his memory was fuzzy—so much death and disappearance in the world that a man like himself, merely passing through life on his own means, could scarcely keep track.

“I beg your pardon,” he said, “if I get the details wrong.”

“It’s all right,” Mrs. Tope said kindly. “Just a little confused.”

When Mr. Jasper confirmed he would speak for her, Datchery sent up his card and was invited upstairs.

The Mayor was present, Mrs. Tope noted—but not as company. He and Mr. Jasper were close friends.

“I beg your pardon,” said Datchery, bowing with his hat under his arm, “but as a man trying to live quietly on his savings, I must ask: Are the Tope family reputable?”

Mr. Jasper answered without hesitation.

“That is sufficient,” said Datchery.

“The Worshipful the Mayor,” added Jasper, gesturing toward Sapsea, “will also confirm their character.”

“The Worshipful the Mayor,” said Datchery, bowing deeply, “places me under great obligation.”

“Very good people,” said Mr. Sapsea condescendingly. “Well-behaved. Respectful. Approved by the Dean and Chapter.”

“The Worshipful the Mayor gives them a character,” said Datchery, “they may well be proud of.”

He then asked whether the city held many points of interest.

“We are an ancient, ecclesiastical city,” said Sapsea. “Constitutional, as befits such a place. We uphold our privileges.”

“This,” said Datchery, bowing again, “inspires me to learn more—and confirms my wish to end my days here.”

“Retired from the Army?” Sapsea suggested.

“His Honour does me too much credit,” Datchery replied.

“Navy?”

“Again, His Honour overestimates.”

“Diplomacy is a noble profession,” Sapsea remarked.

“There,” said Datchery, smiling, “His Honour has me beaten. Even a diplomatic bird must fall to such a gun.”

Sapsea was pleased. Here was a man who spoke with grace and deference—yet without fawning. The third-person style, the formal address—it flattered the Mayor’s sense of dignity.

“But I must beg pardon,” said Datchery. “I’ve taken up His Honour’s time. My hotel, the Crozier, claims my attention.”

“Oh, not at all,” said Sapsea. “I’m going home. If you’d like to see the Cathedral, I’ll show it to you.”

“His Honour is more than kind,” said Datchery.

As they descended, Datchery followed with his hat under his arm, his white hair fluttering in the evening breeze.

“Might I ask,” he said, “whether the gentleman we’ve just left is the one rumored to be grieving deeply over the loss of a nephew—and seeking vengeance?”

“That is he,” said Sapsea. “John Jasper.”

“And are there strong suspicions?”

“More than suspicions,” replied the Mayor. “All but certainties.”

“Only think!” said Datchery.

“But proof,” said Sapsea, “must be built stone by stone. Justice must be not only morally certain—but legally so.”

“His Honour reminds me,” said Datchery, “of the nature of law. Immoral. How true!”

“The arm of the law,” continued Sapsea, “is strong and long. That’s how I put it.”

“How forcible—and yet, how true!” murmured Datchery.

“Without betraying the secrets of the prison-house,” said Sapsea, “I predict—knowing the iron will of that man—that the long arm will reach, and the strong arm will strike.”

They reached the Cathedral. Sapsea pointed out every feature, as if he had designed it himself. A few flaws he dismissed lightly—workmen’s mistakes, made in his absence.

Then they walked through the churchyard. Sapsea paused to admire the evening light—near Mrs. Sapsea’s epitaph.

“And by the by,” he said, as if remembering suddenly, “that is one of our small lions. Our people favor it. Strangers sometimes copy it. It’s a little work of mine. Troublesome to turn, sir—I may say, difficult to turn with elegance.”

Datchery praised it so warmly that he nearly copied it on the spot—though he resisted, knowing he might return later.

At that moment, Durdles, the local stonemason, approached. Sapsea hailed him proudly.

“Ah, Durdles! This is the mason, sir—one of our worthies. Mr. Datchery, this is Durdles, a gentleman who’s going to settle here.”

“I wouldn’t do it if I was him,” growled Durdles. “We’re a heavy lot.”

“You surely don’t speak for yourself, Mr. Durdles,” said Datchery, “any more than for His Honour.”

“Who’s His Honour?” demanded Durdles.

“His Honour the Mayor.”

“I never stood before him,” said Durdles, looking less than loyal. “And it’ll be time enough to honour him when I am. Until then—‘Mister Sapsea is his name, England is his nation, Cloisterham’s his dwelling-place, Aukshneer’s his occupation.’”

Then Deputy appeared—preceded by a flying oyster shell—and demanded three pence “chucked” to him from Durdles as overdue wages.

While Durdles slowly counted out the coins, Sapsea told Datchery about the mason’s habits, reputation, and home.

“Suppose a curious visitor wished to see your works?” asked Datchery.

“Any gentleman welcome any evening,” said Durdles, a penny between his teeth, “if he brings liquor for two. Or twice two—he’ll be doubly welcome.”

“I shall come,” said Datchery. “Master Deputy, what do you owe me?”

“A job.”

“Mind you pay me honestly—with a job showing me Mr. Durdles’s house when I want to go there.”

Deputy whistled sharply through his gap-toothed mouth—receipt complete—and vanished.

The Mayor and Datchery parted with formal courtesies at the Mayor’s door. Even then, Datchery kept his hat under his arm, letting his white hair stream in the wind.

That night, at the Crozier, Datchery stood before the gas-lit mirror above the coffee-room mantel, shaking his hair.

“For a single buffer,” he said to himself, “living idly on his means, I’ve had a rather busy afternoon.”

Cloisterham glows under the summer sun, its cathedral and ruined monastery bathed in such golden light that their stone walls seem translucent. A warm radiance seems to emanate from within them, rather than fall upon them from without. The gardens bloom with fruit heavy on the vines. Once, weary pilgrims rode through the city’s cool shadows; now, dusty travelers—so covered in grime they appear made of earth itself—lounge on shaded doorsteps, attempting to mend their worn shoes or surrendering them to the city kennels as hopeless cases. On an afternoon like this, after the final service at the cathedral, the side of the High Street where the Nuns’ House stands lies in pleasant shade—except where the garden opens westward between leafy boughs. A servant informs Rosa, to her horror, that Mr. Jasper wishes to see her. If he had chosen a moment to catch her off guard, he could not have done better. Perhaps he did. Helena is gone, Mrs. Tisher is away on leave, and Miss Twinkleton—now reduced to amateur status—has joined a picnic with a veal pie.

“Oh, why, why, why, did you say I was at home?” Rosa cries, helpless.

The maid replies that Mr. Jasper never asked. He simply said he knew she was there and requested she be told he wished to speak with her.

“What shall I do? What shall I do?” Rosa thinks, clasping her hands. Then, seized by a sudden desperation, she decides: she will go to him in the garden. She shudders at the idea of being trapped indoors with him, but the garden is visible from many windows. There, she can be seen—and heard—and if necessary, scream and flee. It’s a desperate hope, but it’s all she has.

She has not seen him since the night of the tragedy, except during the inquiry before the Mayor, when he sat in grim silence, representing his lost nephew and burning with a desire for vengeance.

She drapes her garden hat over her arm and steps outside.

The moment she sees him leaning against the sun-dial, the old dread returns—the feeling that he controls her, even against her will. She wants to turn back, but something compels her forward. She cannot resist. She sits on the garden seat beside the dial, head bowed. She dares not look up—she feels only the grass beneath her eyes—but she notices he wears deep mourning. So does she. It wasn’t always so, but the missing boy has long been presumed dead and mourned.

He begins to reach toward her hand. She pulls hers back instinctively. His gaze remains fixed on her, though she sees nothing but green.

“I’ve waited,” he says, “to return to my duty near you.”

She tries to form words—her lips move, but no sound comes. Finally, she whispers, “Duty, sir?”

“The duty of teaching you. Serving you as your faithful music-master.”

“I’ve stopped studying.”

“Not stopped,” he insists. “Discontinued. Your guardian told me you ceased due to the shock we’ve all felt. When will you resume?”

“Never, sir.”

“Never? You would not have done more if you truly loved my dear boy.”

“I *did* love him!” Rosa snaps, fire flashing in her voice.

“Yes,” he says softly, “but not quite—not quite in the way intended. Not as he should have loved. Not as any man in his place must have loved.” He pauses. “Though I won’t compare him to you in that respect.”

She remains still, but draws herself tighter inward.

“So you’ve abandoned your studies altogether?” he asks.

“Yes,” she answers sharply. “That was my guardian’s politeness, not mine. I told him I meant to stop, and I mean to keep my word.”

“And you still do?”

“I still do, sir. And I beg you not to ask again. I will not answer. I have that power.”

She senses his gaze lingering on her—admiring the anger, the spark in her eyes. It makes her spirit rise—and then sink again, overwhelmed by shame, fear, and revulsion, just as it did that night at the piano.

“I will not question you further,” he says quietly. “I confess—”

“I do not wish to hear you, sir!” Rosa interrupts, rising.

This time, he touches her—his hand outstretched.

She recoils, stumbling back onto the seat.

“We must sometimes act against our desires,” he murmurs. “You must now—or you’ll do greater harm than you can ever repair.”

“What harm?”

“Soon. Soon. You question me, yet forbid me to question you. But I will answer. Dearest Rosa. Charming Rosa.”

She starts up again.

He does not touch her this time. But his face—dark, menacing—leans against the sun-dial, casting a shadow across the daylight. Her flight freezes in terror.

“I know how many windows overlook us,” he says, glancing toward them. “I won’t touch you again. I won’t come closer. Sit down. It’s no great wonder to see your music-master leaning idly on a pedestal, speaking with you, remembering what has passed. Sit down, my beloved.”

She turns to leave—again—only to stop, paralyzed by the threat in his expression. She looks at him, her face locked in fear, and slowly lowers herself back onto the bench.

“Rosa,” he begins, “even when my dear boy was engaged to you, I loved you madly. Even when I believed his happiness with you was certain, I loved you madly. Even when I worked to deepen his devotion to you, I loved you madly. Even when he gave me that picture of your face—so carelessly mocked by him—I feigned to hang it for his sake, but worshipped it in torment for years. I loved you madly—in the dullness of the day, in the agony of the night, surrounded by sordid truths or wandering through visions of paradise and hell, carrying your image in my arms. I loved you madly.”

If anything could make his words worse, it would be the contrast between the violence of his voice and the calmness of his pose.

“I endured it all in silence. As long as you were his—or so I believed—I kept my secret faithfully. Did I not?”

This lie—so blatant, yet spoken with such truth in tone—shatters her composure.

“You were false then,” she cries, “as you are now. False to him—every hour. You made my life miserable with your pursuit. You made me afraid to open his eyes, and forced me—out of love for him—to hide the truth: that you were a wicked, wicked man!”

His face remains composed, but his features twist, his hands tremble—diabolical in their stillness.

“How beautiful you are,” he says, voice thick with admiration. “More beautiful in anger than in peace. I don’t ask for your love. Give me yourself—and your hatred. Give me yourself—and that rage. That scorn. That enchanting defiance. It will be enough.”

Tears well in her eyes. Her face burns. She rises again, determined to escape.

But he reaches toward the porch, as if inviting her inside.

“I told you,” he says, “you rare charmer, sweet witch—you must stay and listen, or you will bring about harm no one can undo. You asked what harm. Stay, and I’ll tell you. Leave, and I will do it.”

She quails. Though she knows not what he means, she stays. Her breath comes in ragged gasps—threatening to choke her—but she presses a hand to her chest and endures.

“I have confessed,” he continues, “that my love is madness. So mad, that had the bond between me and my dear boy been one thread weaker, I might have torn him from your side—even when you favored him.”

A film crosses her eyes. She sways.

“Even him,” he repeats. “Yes—*even him*. Rosa, you see me. You hear me. Judge for yourself: no other man shall love you and live—if his life is in my hands.”

“What do you mean, sir?”

“I mean to show you how mad my love is. Mr. Crisparkle mentioned during the inquiries that young Landless confessed to being a rival to my lost boy. That is an unforgivable crime in my eyes. The same Mr. Crisparkle knows—by my hand—that I am devoted to finding and destroying the murderer, whoever he may be. I vowed to solve the mystery alone, until I held the clue that would trap him like a net. I have spent six months winding it tighter—slowly, steadily—as I speak now.”

“Your belief,” Rosa counters, “if you think Mr. Landless guilty, is not Mr. Crisparkle’s belief. And he is a good man.”

“My belief is my own,” he replies. “And I hold it sacred. Circumstances may grow so strong—even against an innocent man—that they become deadly. One missing piece found, and guilt is proven, however slight the evidence was before. Young Landless stands in mortal danger either way.”

“If you truly believe,” she pleads, turning pale, “that I favor Mr. Landless—or that he has ever addressed himself to me—you are wrong.”

He waves her aside with a curl of his lip.

“I was going to show you how madly I love you. More madly now than ever—because I am willing to renounce the second purpose that divided my life. From now on, I have only one goal: you. Miss Landless has become your closest friend. You care for her peace of mind?”

“I love her dearly.”

“You care for her name?”

“I have said, sir, I love her dearly.”

“I am unconsciously,” he says, smiling, “offending you by questioning. Let me state facts instead. You do care for her peace. You do care for her honor. Then—remove the shadow of the gallows from her, dear one.”

“You dare propose—”

“Darling, I dare. Stop there. If idolizing you makes me the worst of men, then I am the worst. If it makes me the best, then I am the best. My love for you surpasses all others. My truth to you transcends all others. Give me hope. Give me favor. I swear I am a broken man for your sake.”

She clutches her temples, pushes back her hair, and stares at him with wild horror, trying to grasp the full meaning of what he is saying—though he gives her only fragments.

“Do not calculate now, angel,” he says, “only count the sacrifices I lay at those dear feet—feet I would kiss among the dirtiest ashes, or wear upon my head like a savage. There is my loyalty to my dead boy—tread upon it!” He gestures as if casting something precious aside. “There is my offense against your sanctity—spurn it!” Another gesture. “There are my labors for justice—six months of toil—crush them!” A third motion. “There is my wasted past. My present. My heart’s desolation. My soul’s ruin. My peace. My despair. Stamp them into dust—so long as you take me, even if you hate me with every breath!”

The intensity of his passion overwhelms her. The spell breaks. She lunges toward the porch.

In an instant, he is at her side, whispering in her ear.

“Rosa, I am in control again. I walk calmly beside you to the house. I will wait for a sign—of attention, of hope. I will not strike too soon. Give me a sign you hear me.”

She moves her hand slightly—reluctantly.

She moves her hand again.

Rosa came to herself with the entire weight of her recent encounter vividly before her. It felt as though the moment had followed her into unconsciousness, leaving no gap in her memory. Her mind reeled—what could she do? One thought alone was clear: she must escape this man, this monstrous figure who had just declared his love with such terrifying intensity. But where could she go? And how? She had confided her fears only once—only to Helena—and now, if she told her what had happened, she might unleash the very disaster he threatened. He had made it plain he had both the power and the will to destroy; she knew it. The more she recalled his presence, the deeper her dread grew, especially when she considered that a single misstep on her part—either in action or hesitation—could doom Helena’s brother.

For months, Rosa had wrestled with a half-formed suspicion, one she had never dared voice. Jasper’s obsession with his nephew while he lived, and his relentless pursuit of answers after his disappearance, had become common talk. Yet no one suspected foul play—least of all her. She had asked herself: *Could I be so wicked to imagine evil where others see none?* Then she questioned whether her own earlier aversion to him had planted the seed of doubt. If so, did that prove the suspicion baseless? And then came the harder question: *What motive could he possibly have?* She hesitated to answer even in silence: *The desire to possess me.* At the mere thought, she covered her face, as if the idea of murder born from such vanity were nearly as terrible as the act itself.

She replayed every word he’d spoken by the sun-dial. He insisted the boy’s disappearance was murder—consistent with his public behavior since the discovery of the watch and shirt-pin. But if he feared being caught, wouldn’t he prefer the idea of a voluntary vanishing? Instead, he had said that had their bond been weaker, he might have removed “even him” from her side. Was that the language of guilt—or of rage? He spoke of laying six months of effort at her feet, not as a pretense, but as an offering of justice, intertwined with his broken heart, wasted life, and despair. His first sacrifice, he claimed, was his loyalty to his dead nephew. Such depth of feeling seemed impossible to fake. Yet—how could she ignore the terror he inspired?

She knew nothing of criminal minds—their logic, their darkness. Most people failed to understand them because they tried to fit them into ordinary human terms. But Rosa saw only one truth: he was dangerous. And she must flee.

She had been Helena’s pillar throughout this ordeal, always affirming her belief in Neville’s innocence and sharing her grief. “It’s not large,” he said frankly, “though perfectly suited for a day’s provisions for a canary. Did you bring a canary?”

She smiled and shook her head.

“If you had,” he said, “he’d be welcome. I’d hang him outside to spar with our Staple sparrows—whose execution, alas, falls short of their intention. Like so many of us!”

She still hadn’t answered which meal she wanted.

“Have a jumble of all,” he suggested.

She declined, saying she could manage only tea.

He darted in and out several times, shouting orders—marmalade, eggs, watercress, salted fish, frizzled ham—then raced off without his hat to deliver them. Soon, the table was set.

“Lord bless my soul!” he exclaimed, placing the lamp upon it. “What a new sensation—for a poor old bachelor!”

Her eyebrows lifted in inquiry.

“The sensation of having a sweet young presence here—one that whitewashes the walls, paints the floor, papers the ceiling, and gilds the whole place with glory!”

He sighed. “Ah me! Ah me!”

There was sorrow in it. She touched his hand with hers, then her teacup.

“Thank you, my dear,” he said. “Ahem. Let’s talk.”

“Do you always live here, sir?”

“Yes, my dear.”

“Always alone?”

“Always—except for Bazzard, my clerk. He doesn’t live here. He goes home after hours. In fact, he’s not working here now. A firm downstairs lends me a substitute. But replacing Bazzard would be difficult.”

“He must be very fond of you,” she said.

“He bears up with commendable fortitude—if he is,” he replied, thoughtful. “But I doubt it. He’s discontented.”

“Why?”

“Misplaced,” he said, with great gravity.

Her brows rose again.

“So misplaced,” he went on, “that I feel constantly apologetic. And he feels—I don’t know if he’d admit it—that I have reason to be.”

Now he was so mysterious, she didn’t know how to respond.

Then, abruptly, he burst out: “Let’s talk. We were speaking of Bazzard. It’s a secret—his secret—but your presence makes me unusually open. What do you think Bazzard has done?”

“Oh dear!” she said, leaning forward. “Nothing dreadful, I hope?”

“He’s written a play,” he whispered. “A tragedy.”

She relaxed.

“And nobody,” he continued, “will hear of bringing it out—on any account.”

She nodded slowly. “Such things exist. And why do they?”

“Well,” he said, “I couldn’t write a play.”

“Not a bad one, sir?” she asked, eyes twinkling.

“No,” he said. “If I were sentenced to decapitation and an express arrived with a pardon—provided I wrote a play—I’d return to the block and ask the executioner to proceed. Meaning,” he added, passing a hand under his chin, “the singular number, and this extremity.”

She pondered.

“Thus,” he continued, “Bazzard feels superior to me. Especially when I’m his master. The case is greatly aggravated.”

He shook his head solemnly.

“How did you become his master?”

“A natural question,” he said. “Let’s talk. Bazzard’s father was a Norfolk farmer. He’d have beaten his son senseless with flail and pitchfork at the hint of a play. So the son, bringing his rent to me, revealed his secret. He meant to pursue his genius, but feared starvation. He wasn’t formed for it.”

“For pursuing genius?”

“No. For starvation. It was undeniable—he wasn’t built to starve. So he asked me to stand between him and such a fate. That’s how he became my clerk. And he feels it deeply.”

“I’m glad he’s grateful,” she said.

“I didn’t mean that,” he corrected. “He feels the degradation. There are other geniuses he knows—also playwrights, also unproduced—who dedicate their works to one another in glowing terms. Bazzard was the subject of one such dedication. And I—never had a play dedicated to me!”

She looked at him as if she wished he had a thousand.

“Which,” he added, “rubs against Bazzard’s grain. He’s short with me sometimes. And I know he thinks, ‘This blockhead is my master—a man who couldn’t write a tragedy even under threat of death, and who’ll never have one dedicated to him!’ Very trying. But I consider his feelings when giving directions. ‘Perhaps he won’t like this.’ So we get along.”

“Is the tragedy named, sir?”

“Strictly between us,” he said, “it has a fitting title: *The Thorn of Anxiety*. But Bazzard hopes—and I hope—it will be performed someday.”

Clearly, he had shared this story not just to amuse her, but to distract her from the horror that had driven her to London.

“Now, my dear,” he said, “if you’re not too tired, tell me what happened today—but only if you feel able. I may digest it better if I sleep on it.”

She composed herself and gave a full account. He smoothed his head often, asking her to repeat parts involving Helena and Neville. When she finished, he sat in silence, deep in thought.

“Clearly narrated,” he said at last. “And, I hope, clearly put away here.” He patted his head.

Then he took her to the window.
“There,” he said, “they live. The dark windows across the way.”

“May I go to Helena tomorrow?” she asked.

“I should like to sleep on that,” he replied, uncertain. “But let me take you to your room. You must be exhausted.”

He helped her with her hat, hung her tiny bag on his arm, and led her across Holborn, walking with a stately awkwardness, as though performing a minuet. At the hotel, he handed her over to the Unlimited Head Chambermaid.

“I’ll wait below,” he said, “in case you want a different room or anything else.”

Her room was airy, clean, cheerful—almost joyful. The Unlimited had supplied everything missing from her bag. She descended the stairs to thank him.

“Not at all,” he said, delighted. “It’s I who thank you—for your confidence, your company. Your breakfast will be served in a neat, compact, graceful sitting-room—fit for your figure. I’ll come at ten. Do you feel strange here?”

“Oh no,” she said. “I feel safe.”

“You may be sure the stairs are fire-proof,” he said. “Any outbreak would be noticed and suppressed.”

“I didn’t mean that,” she replied. “I mean—I feel safe from *him*.”

“There’s a stout iron gate to keep him out,” he said, smiling. “Furnival’s is fire-proof, specially watched, and *I* live right across the street!”

With knightly pride, he believed that last point alone was sufficient protection. He told the porter: “If anyone in the hotel needs to send a message across the road at night, a crown will be ready.”

Then he paced outside the iron gate for nearly an hour, watching, checking between the bars—like a man guarding a dove in a cage of lions, fearing she might fall.

The night passed quietly, undisturbed by any alarm or unease—like a bird resting in peace, the weary soul awoke refreshed. At ten o’clock sharp, Mr. Grewgious arrived, accompanied by Mr. Crisparkle, who had made the journey from Cloisterham without delay, arriving straight from the river’s edge.

“Miss Twinkleton was so anxious,” Mr. Crisparkle explained to Rosa, “that she came rushing over to my mother and me with your note, all agitated and bewildered. To calm her, I offered to come at once—the very first train of the morning. I confess I wished you had turned to me first, but now I see that your choice was wise: coming to your guardian was the right course.”

“I did consider you,” Rosa replied, “but Minor Canon Corner was so close to him—”

“I understand perfectly,” said Mr. Crisparkle. “It was entirely natural.”

“I’ve already told Mr. Crisparkle everything you shared with me last night,” Mr. Grewgious added gently. “Of course, I would have written to him immediately—but his arrival could not have been more timely. And it was most thoughtful of him to come, especially since he’d only just left.”

Rosa turned to both men, her voice filled with concern. “Have you decided what should be done for Helena and her brother?”

“Honestly,” Mr. Crisparkle admitted, “I’m at a loss. If even Mr. Grewgious, whose mind is far more seasoned than mine, cannot settle on a plan after a full night of thought, then what hope do I have?”

Just then, the Unlimited appeared at the door—having knocked and received permission to enter—announcing a gentleman who wished to speak briefly with someone named Crisparkle, if such a person were present. Should no such man be there, he apologized for the mistake.

“There is such a gentleman,” Mr. Crisparkle said, “but he’s occupied just now.”

“Is it a dark gentleman?” Rosa asked, stepping back toward her guardian.

“No, miss,” the servant replied. “More of a brown gentleman.”

“You’re certain he doesn’t have black hair?” Rosa pressed, gaining courage.

“Quite sure, miss. Brown hair and blue eyes.”

Mr. Grewgious paused, his usual caution surfacing. “Perhaps, reverend sir, it might be prudent to meet him—if you don’t object. When one is uncertain, one never knows where help might appear. It’s a principle I follow: keep every avenue open. I could tell a story to illustrate, but that would be premature.”

“If Miss Rosa will allow it,” Mr. Crisparkle said, “let the gentleman come in.”

The visitor entered, politely apologizing for interrupting. With a modest yet sincere grace, he turned to Mr. Crisparkle and smiled. Then, with an unexpected twist, he asked: “Who am I?”

“You are the gentleman I saw smoking beneath the trees in Staple Inn just moments ago,” Mr. Crisparkle replied.

“True,” the man acknowledged. “And there, I saw you. But who else am I?”

Mr. Crisparkle studied the handsome, sun-bronzed face before him. Slowly, a memory stirred—a ghost of a younger boy rising faintly in the room. The man noticed the flicker of recognition and smiled again.

“What shall we have for breakfast this morning?” he asked lightly. “You’re out of jam.”

“Wait!” Mr. Crisparkle exclaimed, raising his hand. “One moment! Tartar!”

They embraced warmly, their hands clasping tightly, then each placing a hand on the other’s shoulder, gazing into one another’s eyes with unmistakable joy.

“My old fag!” Mr. Crisparkle declared.
“My old master!” the other replied.
“You saved me from drowning!”
“And afterward, you took to swimming, didn’t you?”
“God bless my soul!”
“Amen!”

They shook hands again, heartily, as if sealing a bond long overdue.

“Imagine,” Mr. Crisparkle said, eyes shining, “Miss Rosa Bud and Mr. Grewgious—imagine Mr. Tartar, when he was barely a junior, diving into the water to save me, a heavy senior, grabbing me by the hair, and swimming like a water-giant toward shore!”

“And imagine me refusing to let him sink,” Mr. Tartar added, “even though I was his fag. But the truth is—he was my greatest protector, my truest friend. He did me more good than all the masters combined. So when I saw him struggling, an instinct took hold: either I save him—or go down with him.”

“Hem!” Mr. Grewgious interjected, stepping forward with extended hand. “Permit me the honor—for it is truly an honor. I am proud to make your acquaintance. I hope you didn’t catch cold. I hope the water didn’t trouble you. How have you been since?”

His words seemed unformed, as if spoken without full awareness, though clearly meant to convey warmth and respect. Rosa watched, thinking: *If Heaven had sent such courage and strength to my poor mother!* And to think—this man had once been so slight, so young.

“I don’t wish to be praised,” Mr. Tartar said humbly, “but I believe I have an idea.”

He began pacing across the room in a sudden, almost erratic manner—so unusual that everyone stared, wondering if he were choking or seized by cramp.

“I _think_,” he announced, “I believe I’ve had the pleasure of seeing Mr. Tartar’s name listed as tenant of the top set in the house next to the corner one?”

“Yes, sir,” Mr. Tartar replied. “You’re correct so far.”

“Tick that off,” Mr. Grewgious said, tapping his thumb against his palm. “Now, might you know the name of your neighbor in the top set on the other side of the party wall?” He leaned close, his short sight demanding proximity.

“Landless.”

“Tick that off,” Mr. Grewgious said, taking another quick step, then returning. “No personal knowledge, I suppose?”

“Only slight—but some.”

“Tick that off,” he repeated, pacing again. “Nature of your knowledge, Mr. Tartar?”

“I thought he looked like a young man in difficult circumstances. Only recently—within a day or two—I asked his permission to extend my flower garden to his windows—that is, to share my blooms with him.”

“Would you kindly take seats?” Mr. Grewgious requested. “I _have_ an idea.”

They sat. Mr. Tartar complied readily, despite seeming slightly disoriented. Mr. Grewgious, seated in the center, hands resting on his knees, spoke with his customary precision—though it was clear he’d rehearsed the words.

“I cannot yet decide whether it’s safe to maintain contact, under current conditions, between the fair member of this company and Mr. Neville or Miss Helena. I know a local associate of ours—on whom I beg to bestow a passing, but heartfelt, curse, with the kind indulgence of my reverend friend—moves stealthily, skulking about. He may have an informant: a watchman, porter, or some other lowly figure attached to Staple. If not himself, he might have planted someone to monitor Mr. Neville’s chambers. That person would report to our friend, who would then deduce identities from prior knowledge. But no one can watch every chamber, nor track every visitor—unless, perhaps, mine.”

“I begin to grasp your meaning,” Mr. Crisparkle said, nodding. “And I fully approve of your caution.”

“I know nothing yet of the reason or motive,” Mr. Tartar said, “but I understand your intent. Let me say plainly: my rooms are yours to use.”

“There!” Mr. Grewgious declared, smoothing his head with triumph. “Now we all have the idea. Do you understand, my dear?”

“I think so,” Rosa replied, blushing slightly as Mr. Tartar glanced toward her.

“You’ll go to Staple with Mr. Crisparkle and Mr. Tartar,” Mr. Grewgious explained. “I’ll move in and out alone, as usual. You’ll go up to Mr. Tartar’s rooms, inspect his flower garden, wait for Miss Helena’s appearance, or signal her that you’re nearby—and communicate freely. No spy will suspect a thing.”

“I’m afraid I’ll be—” Rosa began.

“Be what, my dear?” Mr. Grewgious asked gently.

“Not frightened,” she answered shyly. “But… in Mr. Tartar’s way. We seem to be borrowing his home so casually.”

“I assure you,” Mr. Tartar said warmly, “if your voice echoes in those rooms—even once—I shall always count it a blessing.”

Rosa, unsure how to respond, lowered her eyes and turned to Mr. Grewgious. “Shall I put on my hat?”

“Nothing better you could do,” he replied. She withdrew to prepare.

While she was gone, Mr. Crisparkle gave Mr. Tartar a brief account of Neville’s troubles and Helena’s plight—the time sufficient, as the hat required careful adjustment.

When they returned, Mr. Tartar offered Rosa his arm. Mr. Crisparkle walked ahead, separate, leading the way.

“Poor, poor Eddy,” Rosa thought as they moved along.

Mr. Tartar waved his right hand, leaning down to speak animatedly. Rosa glanced at it—once so small, so pale, yet now strong and weathered. *It must have been steady and fearless even then,* she mused.

He told her he had been a sailor, roaming the seas for years.

“When are you going back?” she asked.

“Never,” he said.

Rosa wondered what the girls would think if they could see her crossing the wide street on the arm of a sailor. She imagined passers-by seeing her—small, delicate—beside this powerful figure, capable of lifting her from danger, carrying her miles without rest. His blue eyes, she noted, seemed used to watching threats approach from afar, meeting them without flinching.

Then, lifting her own gaze, she realized he was looking at her—thinking something about *her*.

A flush rose in her cheeks. Perhaps it explains why, later, she couldn’t quite remember how she climbed (with his help) to his rooftop garden, or how it felt like stepping into a magical land—suddenly blooming, like the country atop the enchanted beanstalk.

May it flourish forever.

Mr. Tartar’s chambers were the most immaculate, orderly, and perfectly arranged rooms ever seen under the sun, moon, or stars. The floors gleamed so brightly they seemed scrubbed clean of all earthly stains—so polished that one might have thought the London blacking trade had been abolished for good. Every piece of brass in his possession shone like a mirror, reflecting light with such precision it could almost be used to signal ships at sea. Not a speck, spot, or smear marred any surface; even his household trinkets—large, small, or medium—were kept in flawless condition. His sitting room resembled an admiral’s cabin, his bathroom a dairy, and his bedroom, lined with lockers and drawers, looked like a well-stocked seedsman’s shop. In the center stood his cot, balanced just so, as if it breathed softly with every shift of air.

Everything in Mr. Tartar’s world had its place: maps and charts in their own section, books neatly aligned, brushes hung precisely, boots stored in their designated nook, clothes folded with military care, medicine bottles tucked away, telescopes and instruments each in their assigned compartment. Nothing was out of reach. Shelves, brackets, hooks, and drawers were designed not only for convenience but also for maximizing space—every inch cleverly utilized, every object placed where it fit best. His silverware sat on the sideboard in perfect order, so exact that a misplaced salt spoon would have stood out like a crime. His toiletries on the dressing table were arranged with such precision that a toothpick left askew would have drawn immediate notice. Even his collection of curiosities—birds, fish, reptiles, arms, garments, shells, seaweed, coral fragments—was preserved and displayed according to type and origin, each in its ideal position. Paint and varnish were kept hidden, always ready to erase any accidental smudge. No ship ever sailed more cleanly than these chambers were kept.

On this bright summer day, a neat awning stretched over Mr. Tartar’s flower garden, rigged with nautical precision. The whole scene carried a distinct sea-going atmosphere—so complete that the garden might have belonged to the stern windows of a vessel at sea, and the entire residence might have set sail at any moment if Mr. Tartar had simply picked up the speaking trumpet slung in the corner and bellowed orders to “heave the anchor up” and “get all sail upon her!”

And Mr. Tartar himself, hosting this maritime retreat, matched the setting perfectly. When a man is deeply devoted to a hobby that never disappoints him, there's something charming about seeing him enjoy it with humor and pride. If he’s naturally warm-hearted, genuine, and full of life, then few things are more pleasing than watching him revel in his passion. Rosa, though she hadn’t yet been formally introduced to the role of First Lady of the Admiralty—or perhaps First Fairy of the Sea—would have found it delightful to see Mr. Tartar half-laughing at his inventions, half-delighted by them. She would have thought it especially striking when, after showing her around, he stepped back from his admiral’s cabin, politely inviting her to consider herself its queen, and waved her free into the garden with the same hand that once held Mr. Crisparkle’s life in balance.

“Helena! Helena Landless! Are you there?”

“Who speaks? Not Rosa?” Then another face appeared—handsome, calm, and familiar.

“Yes, my darling!”

“Why, how did you come here, dearest?”

“I—I don’t quite know,” Rosa said, blushing. “Unless I’m dreaming.”

Why blush? Because the two of them were alone among the flowers. Were blushes among the harvest of the magic beanstalk?

“I am not dreaming,” Helena replied, smiling. “I’d take more for granted if I were. How did we end up together—so near, so suddenly?”

Indeed, unexpectedly, amidst the dull gables and chimneys of P. J. T.’s connection, and the blooms born from saltwater soil. But Rosa, coming to, quickly explained how they came to meet, and all the circumstances behind it.

“And Mr. Crisparkle is here,” Rosa added, finishing swiftly. “And do you believe it? He was saved long ago!”

“I could believe anything of Mr. Crisparkle,” Helena answered, her cheeks warming.

(More blushes bloomed in the land of the beanstalk.)

“But it wasn’t Crisparkle,” Rosa corrected quickly.

“I don’t understand, love.”

“It was very kind of Mr. Crisparkle to be saved,” Rosa said, “and he couldn’t have shown more respect for Mr. Tartar. But it was Mr. Tartar who saved him.”

Helena’s dark eyes studied Rosa’s bright face through the leaves. “Is Mr. Tartar with you now, dear?”

“No,” Rosa replied. “He’s given up his rooms to me—to us, I mean. It’s such a beautiful place!”

“Is it?”

“It’s like the inside of the most exquisite ship ever built. Like—like—”

“Like a dream?” Helena suggested.

Rosa nodded slightly and inhaled the scent of the flowers.

After a brief silence, during which Rosa fancied Helena seemed to feel pity for someone unseen, Helena spoke again: “My poor Neville is reading in his room—the sun is too bright on this side right now. I think he should not know you’re so close.”

“Oh, I agree!” Rosa exclaimed at once.

“I suppose,” Helena continued, hesitating, “he’ll have to learn everything you’ve told me eventually—but I’m not certain. Ask Mr. Crisparkle what he thinks. Ask him whether I may tell Neville as much or as little as I choose.”

Rosa retreated into her private chamber and relayed the question. The Minor Canon advised full trust in Helena’s judgment.

“I thank him very much,” Helena said when Rosa returned. “Now ask him whether it would be better to wait until further signs of harm toward Neville appear—such as threats or harassment from this man—or to try to uncover any secret schemes already underway?”

The Minor Canon struggled to offer a confident opinion. After several failed attempts, he suggested consulting Mr. Grewgious. Helena agreed, and he made his way across the quadrangle to P. J. T.’s house—pretending, rather poorly, to be casually strolling. He delivered the request. Mr. Grewgious responded firmly: if you can catch a brigand or wild beast off guard, you should. And he declared without hesitation that John Jasper was both.

With this guidance, Mr. Crisparkle returned to Rosa, who passed it on to Helena. She sat quietly at her window, deep in thought.

“We can count on Mr. Tartar’s help, Rosa?” she asked.

Oh yes, Rosa believed so. Oh yes, she felt sure she could almost guarantee it. But should she ask Mr. Crisparkle? “Your word on the matter is as good as his, my dear,” Helena said calmly. “You needn’t go again.”

Odd, that Helena should say so.

“You see, Neville,” Helena continued after a pause, “knows no one else here. He hasn’t spoken to anyone. If Mr. Tartar would visit him openly, often—even daily—something might come of it.”

“Something might come of it?” Rosa repeated, looking at her friend with puzzled wonder. “Something might?”

“If Neville is truly being watched—and if the goal is to isolate him, to wear down his spirit grain by grain—as you suggested—is it not likely,” Helena asked, “that his enemy would warn Mr. Tartar to stay away? In that case, we wouldn’t just learn of the threat—we might learn exactly what was said.”

“I see!” Rosa exclaimed, and darted back into her chamber.

Soon her face reappeared, flushed and eager. She reported that she had told Mr. Crisparkle, who had brought Mr. Tartar, and that Mr. Tartar—“who is waiting now, in case you want him,” she added, glancing back between the cabin and the garden—had pledged his full cooperation and intended to begin immediately.

“I thank him from my heart,” Helena said. “Please tell him so.”

Again, Rosa hesitated, torn between the garden and the cabin, delivering her message with a flutter of nerves. She returned with renewed assurances from Mr. Tartar, standing uncertainly between Helena and him—a confusion not awkward, but oddly graceful.

“And now, darling,” Helena said, “we must remember our caution. We’ll part for now. I hear Neville moving. Are you going back?”

“To Miss Twinkleton’s?” Rosa asked.

“Oh, I could never return there,” Rosa said, shaking her head. “Not after that terrible meeting!”

“Then where will you go, sweet one?”

“Now that I think of it,” Rosa admitted, “I don’t know. I haven’t decided anything. But my guardian will take care of me. Don’t worry, dear. I’ll be somewhere.”

(It did seem likely.)

“And I’ll hear from my Rosebud through Mr. Tartar?” Helena asked.

“Yes, I suppose so—through—” Rosa glanced back, flustered, unable to finish. “But before we part, tell me one thing, dearest Helena. Tell me—you’re sure, absolutely sure, I couldn’t help it.”

“Help it, love?”

“Help making him malicious and vengeful. I couldn’t talk to him, could I?”

“You know how much I love you,” Helena replied, voice rising with emotion. “But I’d rather see you dead at his wicked feet than betray your soul.”

“That means a great deal to me,” Rosa said. “And you’ll tell your brother so? You’ll give him my thoughts and sympathy? And ask him not to hate me?”

With a slow, sorrowful shake of her head—almost as if the plea were unnecessary—Helena kissed her hands tenderly, and Rosa kissed hers in return. Then, a third hand—a brown one—appeared among the foliage and gently guided her friend from sight.

Later, in the Admiral’s Cabin, Mr. Tartar opened a locker and pulled out a drawer with a single touch. Instantly, a dazzling feast appeared: shimmering macaroons, sparkling liqueurs, exotic spices preserved like jewels, and jellies made from fruits of distant lands. It was a magical repast, conjured from nowhere. But time refused to stand still. With relentless speed, it dragged Rosa back to earth—from the enchanted garden to her guardian’s chambers.

“And now, my dear,” Mr. Grewgious said, “what shall we do next? Or, to put it another way—what shall we do with you?”

Rosa could only look apologetically, aware she was in everyone’s way. A fleeting idea crossed her mind—living fireproof, high above the city in Furnival’s Inn for the rest of her days. That was all she had.

“It has occurred to me,” Mr. Grewgious said, “that Miss Twinkleton occasionally visits London during term breaks, seeking connections and meetings with parents in town. Might we invite her to stay with you for a month—until we can plan better?”

“Stay where, sir?”

“Could we take a furnished lodging in town for a month, and invite Miss Twinkleton to care for you during that time?”

“And afterward?” Rosa asked.

“And afterward,” Mr. Grewgious replied, “we’d be no worse off than we are now.”

“I suppose that could smooth things,” Rosa agreed.

“Then let us go find one,” Mr. Grewgious said, rising. “Nothing would please me more than to have your sweet presence every evening for the rest of my life—but this isn’t suitable for a young lady. Let’s seek adventure and find a lodging. Meanwhile, Mr. Crisparkle, returning home, will kindly speak to Miss Twinkleton and invite her to join us.”

Mr. Crisparkle accepted and left. Mr. Grewgious and Rosa began their search.

His method of inspecting lodgings involved standing across the street from a house with a sign, staring at it; then circling to the back and staring again; then refusing to enter. They moved slowly. Eventually, he remembered a distant cousin of Mr. Bazzard’s—once a lodger in need of influence—who lived in Southampton Street, Bloomsbury Square. Her name, emblazoned in bold letters on a brass plate, was BILLICKIN.

Mrs. Billickin was faint, frail, and astonishingly honest. She emerged from her private back parlour as if summoned from a trance, her breath shallow, her movements languid.

“I hope I see you well, sir,” she said, bowing slightly.

“Quite well, thank you. And you, ma'am?”

“I am as well,” she replied, nearly gasping, “as I hever ham.”

“My ward and an elderly lady wish to rent a modest lodging for a month or so. Do you have anything available?”

“Mr. Grewgious,” she said solemnly, “I will not deceive you. I _have_ apartments.”

She added silently: *Burn me at the stake, but I will not lie.*

“And what are they, ma'am?” he asked, trying to ease the tension.

“There is this sitting-room—which, call it what you will, is the front parlour, Miss,” she said, drawing Rosa into the conversation. “The back parlour is mine, and I never part with it. And there are two bedrooms at the top of the house, with gas laid on. I won’t pretend your floorboards are firm—they aren’t. The gasfitter himself said to make them solid, he’d have to go under your joists, and that would cost too much for a yearly tenant. So the pipes run above the joists. Best you know that.”

Mr. Grewgious and Rosa exchanged glances—worried, though they didn’t grasp the danger.

“Well,” he said, regaining composure, “the roof is sound, no doubt?”

“Mr. Grewgious,” she replied, “if I told you that having nothing above you means having a floor above you, I’d be deceiving you—and I won’t. No, sir. Your slates WILL rattle loose in wind, no matter what you do! I defy you, sir, to keep them tight. Try as you may, they’ll move.”

She paused, cooling slightly, as if fearing she’d overpowered him.

“Consequently,” she continued, softer but still firm, “it would be worse than useless for me to escort you upstairs, only for you to point at the ceiling and say, ‘Mrs. Billickin, what stain do I see?’ and for me to reply, ‘I don’t understand.’ No, sir. I understand before you speak. It’s the wet. It comes in. It doesn’t. You may live dry for half your life—but the time will come. And it’s best you know it now. A dripping sop would be no name for it.”

Mr. Grewgious looked embarrassed, caught in a future disaster.

“Any other rooms, ma'am?”

“Mr. Grewgious,” she said gravely, “I have. You ask, and I answer: I have. The first and second floors are vacant and sweet.”

“Ah,” he said, reassured, “then there’s nothing wrong with those?”

“Mr. Grewgious,” she replied, turning to him with formality, “there is the stairs. Unless your mind is prepared for them, disappointment is inevitable. You cannot, Miss,” she addressed Rosa reproachfully, “place a first floor—or a second—on the same level as a parlour. No, you cannot. It’s beyond your power. Why try?”

She phrased it as if Rosa had stubbornly insisted on the impossible.

“May we see them, ma'am?”

“Mr. Grewgious,” she said, “you may. I will not hide it from you. You may.”

She sent for her shawl—according to ancient custom, she could never leave her home without being wrapped—and led them up. She paused frequently on the stairs, clutching her chest as if her heart might escape.

“And the second floor?” Mr. Grewgious asked, satisfied with the first.

“Mr. Grewgious,” she replied, turning sharply, “the second floor is over this.”

“Can we see it too?”

“Yes, sir,” she said. “It is open as the day.”

It proved satisfactory. Mr. Grewgious stepped aside with Rosa to discuss terms, then requested pen and ink and drafted a short agreement. Mrs. Billickin took a seat and delivered a detailed summary:

“Five-and-forty shillings per week, payable monthly, is fair for both parties. It’s not Bond Street nor St. James’s Palace—but it’s not claimed to be. Nor is it denied—that the Arching leads to a mews. Mews must exist. Attendance: two servants, paid well. There have been disputes with tradesmen, but dirty shoes on fresh hearth-stoning were blamed—not commissions. Coals are either _by_ the fire or _per_ the scuttle.” She emphasized the prepositions, as if they meant worlds.

“Dogs are not welcome. Besides litter, they get stolen, and suspicion spreads. Unpleasantness follows.”

By now, Mr. Grewgious had signed the contract and handed over earnest money.

“I’ve signed for the ladies, ma'am,” he said. “Kindly sign for yourself—Christian name and surname—here.”

“No, sir,” Mrs. Billickin said, bursting into candor again. “You must excuse the Christian name.”

Mr. Grewgious stared.

“The door-plate is a protection,” she explained. “It works, and I won’t remove it.”

He turned to Rosa.

“No, Mr. Grewgious,” she said, “I must refuse. As long as this house remains unknown—Billickin’s, indistinct—no one knows where I hide. Near the door or down the alley, what size or weight I am. I feel safe. But if I commit myself to a single female signature—no, Miss! Nor would you, for a moment, wish to exploit your sex in such a way, if you weren’t forced by thoughtless example.”

Rosa flushed, as if she’d attempted some grave betrayal. She urged Mr. Grewgious to accept any signature. So, in grand baronial fashion, the sign-manual **BILLICKIN** was appended.

Details were settled for taking possession the day after next, when Miss Twinkleton might arrive. Rosa returned to Furnival’s Inn on her guardian’s arm.

And there—walking up and down the inn, checking himself as he saw them approach—was Mr. Tartar.

“It occurred to me,” he said, stepping forward, “that we might go up the river. The weather is lovely, and the tide favors us. I have a boat at the Temple Stairs.”

“I haven’t been up the river in many years,” Mr. Grewgious said, tempted.

“I’ve never been,” Rosa added.

And so, beneath the summer sky, the trio prepared to set forth—not on a ship, but on a quiet river journey—where secrets might float up like bubbles from the deep.

Mr. Crisparkle and John Jasper crossed paths daily beneath the shadow of the Cathedral, yet not a word passed between them about Edwin Drood—since that distant moment, more than half a year prior, when Jasper had silently shown the MinorCanon the final entry in his diary. Though they met often, their conversations never touched upon the vanished young man. Still, it is unlikely that either ever met without his thoughts drifting to the other’s hidden intentions. Their positions were too sharply opposed: Jasper, relentless in his pursuit of Neville Landless; Crisparkle, steadfast in his defense. Each must have wondered what the other was plotting, how far he would go, and whether the next move would be a step toward resolution—or ruin. Yet neither spoke.

Crisparkle, honest by nature, could not feign indifference. He made no secret of his readiness to discuss the matter, even to reopen it. But Jasper remained sealed shut—a man of fixed purpose, immovable in silence. Stoic, solitary, consumed by a single obsession, he lived apart from the world. Though his art required harmony with others, his spirit was utterly isolated. He had once confided this truth to his lost nephew—before the darkness took hold.

He knew, surely, of Rosa’s sudden departure—and guessed its cause. Did he believe he had frightened her into silence? Or did he suspect she had told someone—perhaps even Crisparkle—the full horror of their last meeting? Crisparkle could not decide. But as a just man, he could not condemn Jasper for loving Rosa, any more than he could accuse him of sin for choosing love over vengeance.

The dreadful suspicion that had shaken Rosa—Jasper’s possible guilt—never stirred in Crisparkle’s mind. Nor did Helena or Neville voice it aloud. Mr. Grewgious, though openly hostile to Jasper, never hinted at such a motive. Yet he was secretive, and never mentioned the night he warmed his hands at the gatehouse fire, gazing down at a heap of torn, muddy clothes on the floor.

Cloisterham, drowsy and slow to remember, occasionally stirred at the old tale—dismissed long ago by the magistrates. The town split evenly: some believed Edwin had been murdered by his jealous rival; others thought he’d fled willingly. Then, it would notice that Jasper still hunted, still raged, still sought revenge—and then fall back into sleep. That was the state of things when our story now reaches this point.

The Cathedral doors closed for the night. The Choir-master, granted a brief leave for a few services, turned his face toward London. He traveled the same route Rosa had taken, arriving on a hot, dusty evening.

His baggage was light enough to carry himself. He walked to a small, hybrid lodging near Aldersgate Street, behind the General Post Office—a place that called itself hotel, boarding-house, or lodging-house, depending on the guest’s whim. It advertised itself in railway pamphlets as a new kind of enterprise, timidly emerging. With polite reserve, it suggested travelers might prefer to have their boots shined rather than their stomachs filled. For a fixed price, one could have bed, breakfast, service, and a porter all night. From such signs, many melancholy Britons concluded that the times were leveling—except, perhaps, for roads, which would soon vanish entirely from England.

He ate without appetite and left shortly after. Eastward, through the stale city streets, he walked until he reached his destination: a grim court, worse than most.

He climbed a broken staircase, opened a door, peered into a dark, stifling room, and said: “Are you alone here?”

“Alone, deary; worse luck for me, better for you,” came a croaking reply. “Come in, come in—though I can’t see you till I light a match. But I know your voice. We’ve met before, haven’t we?”

“Light your match and see.”

“So I will, deary. But my hand trembles—can’t find the match right away. And I cough so hard, my matches jump like live things when I do. Are you back from sea?”

“No.”

“Not a sailor?”

“No.”

“Well, there’s land customers and water customers. I’m mother to both. Unlike Jack Chinaman across the way—he’s father to neither. No true secret of mixing, though he charges as much as I do, and more if he can get it. Here’s a match—now where’s the candle? If my cough takes hold, I’ll burn twenty before I light one.”

She found the candle and lit it just before the fit struck. She sat rocking, gasping: “O, my lungs are awful bad! Worn down to cabbage-nets!” Until the spell passed. During it, she saw nothing. When it ended, she strained her eyes and suddenly cried out: “Why—it’s you!”

“Surprised to see me?”

“I thought I’d never see you again. I thought you were dead, gone to Heaven.”

“Why?”

“I didn’t think you could stay away so long—alive—from the poor soul who knows the real recipe. And you’re in mourning too! Why didn’t you come for a pipe or two of comfort? Did they leave you money?”

“No.”

“Who died, deary?”

“A relative.”

“Of what?”

“Probably—Death.”

“We’re short tonight!” she laughed weakly. “Short and snappish. But we’re out of sorts without a smoke. Got the all-overs, haven’t we, deary? This is the place to cure them—in here, where the all-overs are smoked off.”

“You may make ready,” he replied, “whenever you like.”

He removed his shoes, loosened his cravat, and lay across the foot of the squalid bed, head propped on his left hand.

“There,” she said, “you look like yourself again. Now I recognize my old customer! Been trying to mix it yourself all this time, poppet?”

“I’ve taken it now and then—my own way.”

“Never take it your own way. Bad for trade. Bad for you. Where’s my ink-bottle? My thimble? My little spoon?” She began her work, blowing gently on the faint spark in her cupped hands. “He’s going to take it in an artful form now, my dear.”

She spoke in snuffles, never stopping. He spoke without looking at her, as if already lost in thought.

“I’ve given you plenty of smokes, first and last, haven’t I, chuckey?”

“A good many.”

“When you first came, you were new to it—weren’t you?”

“Yes. Easily disposed of.”

“But you grew in the world. Soon took your pipe with the best of them?”

“Ah—and the worst.”

“It’s ready for you. What a sweet singer you were when you first came! Used to drop your head, sing yourself off like a bird! Ready now, deary.”

He took the pipe carefully, placed the mouthpiece to his lips. She sat beside him, ready to refill it.

After a few silent drags, he asked doubtfully: “Is it as potent as it used to be?”

“What do you mean, deary?”

“What should I mean but what’s in my mouth?”

“It’s just the same. Always the identical same.”

“It doesn’t taste the same. Slower.”

“You’ve grown used to it.”

“That may be. Look here.” He paused, dreamy, as if forgetting he’d spoken. She leaned close, whispered in his ear.

“I’m listening. You said ‘Look here.’ I said, ‘I’m listening.’ We were talking about your being used to it.”

“I know. I was only thinking. Suppose you had something in your mind—something you meant to do?”

“Yes, deary—something you meant to do?”

“But hadn’t decided yet?”

“Yes.”

“Might or might not do. Understand?”

“Yes.” She stirred the bowl with a needle.

“Would you do it in your imagination, lying here?”

She nodded. “Over and over again.”

“Just like me. I did it hundreds of thousands of times in this room.”

“It’s to be hoped it was pleasant, deary.”

“It _was_ pleasant!”

He said it fiercely, jerking forward. She calmly refilled the pipe, undisturbed. Seeing her focus, he settled back.

“It was a journey—a dangerous one. Over abysses. A slip would mean death. Look down—look down! See what lies below?”

He lunged forward, pointing as if at a chasm. She looked at his face, not his finger. She seemed to know the effect of her calmness. And she was right—he recoiled.

“I told you—I did it millions of times. So often, through such vast time, that when it actually happened, it seemed almost unreal—so fast.”

“That’s the journey you’ve been on,” she murmured.

He glared, then, eyes clouding, answered: “That’s the journey.”

Silence. His eyes flickered open and shut. She stayed beside him, tending the pipe.

“I’ll warrant,” she said after a while, “you made the journey in many ways, since you did it so often?”

“No. Always in one way.”

“Always the same?”

“Ay.”

“In the way it was done at last?”

“Ay.”

“And always took the same pleasure in it?”

“Ay.”

For a time, he could manage no more than monosyllables. Perhaps to test if he was truly conscious, she reversed her question.

“Did you never grow tired, deary? Try to imagine something else?”

He sat up abruptly. “What do you mean? What did I want? What did I come for?”

She laid him back gently, revived the fire in the pipe with her breath, and said softly: “Sure, sure, sure! Yes, yes, yes! Now I understand. You came on purpose. I should have known—by the way it stood beside you.”

He laughed—then clenched his teeth: “Yes. I came on purpose. When I couldn’t bear life, I came for relief. And I got it. It WAS one! It WAS one!” He repeated it, savage, like a wolf’s snarl.

She watched him closely, feeling her way. Then: “There was a fellow-traveller, deary.”

“Ha, ha, ha!” He laughed—more a yell.

“To think,” he cried, “how often fellow-traveller—and never know it! How many times he went the journey—and never saw the road!”

She knelt by the bed, arms crossed, chin resting on them. Watched him. The pipe slipped. She returned it, placed her hand on his chest, moved him slightly. He spoke, as if she had spoken.

“Yes. I always made the journey first—before the colors changed, the landscapes rose, the glittering processions began. They couldn’t start till it was off my mind. No room for anything else.”

Again, silence. Again, she touched him. Again, he spoke.

“What? I told you so. When it finally comes to pass, it’s so short it seems unreal. Hark!”

“Yes, deary. I’m listening.”

“Time and place are both at hand.”

He stood, whispering, as if in darkness.

“Time, place, and fellow-traveller,” she echoed, holding his arm softly.

“How could time be at hand unless the fellow-traveller was? Hush! The journey’s made. It’s over.”

“So soon?”

“That’s what I said. So soon. Wait. This is a vision. I shall sleep it off. Too short, too easy. I need a better vision—this is the poorest. No struggle, no peril, no entreaty—and yet I never saw _that_ before.” He started.

“Saw what, deary?”

“Look at it! Look what a poor, mean, miserable thing it is! _That_ must be real. It’s over.”

He gestured wildly, then sank into stupor. Lied like a log.

But she was not done. She stirred him again—cat-like—listened. Stirred. Listened. Whispered. Found him unresponsive. Slowly rose, disappointed, flicked his face with the back of her hand as she turned.

Yet she did not leave. She sat in the chair by the hearth, elbow on arm, chin in hand, watching him. “I heard you say once,” she croaked, “when I was lying where you lie now, and you were speculating on me—‘Unintelligible!’ I heard you say it of two others. But don’t be too sure, beauty. Don’t be too sure.”

Unblinking, cat-like, she added: “Not so potent as it once was? Ah. Perhaps not at first. You may be right. Practice makes perfect. I may have learned how to make you talk, deary.”

He talked no more. Twitched grotesquely, face and limbs. Silent. The candle burned low. She took the dying end, lit another, jammed the guttering stub deep into the holder, rammed the new one in—like loading a foul weapon. The second candle burned down. Still, he slept.

At last, the last flame died. Daylight crept in.

It had not lasted long when he sat up, chilled, trembling, slowly regained awareness. Prepared to leave. She received his payment with a grateful, “Bless ye, bless ye, deary!” and seemed to begin preparing for sleep.

But seeming lied. The moment his footsteps faded, she followed, muttering: “I’ll not miss ye twice!”

No exit from the court except the entrance. She peered from the doorway, waited. He did not look back. She followed, watched him waver, then held him in sight.

He went to the back of Aldersgate Street. A door opened instantly to his knock. She crouched in another doorway, watching. Understood he was staying temporarily. Her patience held.

For food, she bought bread within a hundred yards, milk as it passed.

He emerged at noon—changed clothes, empty-handed. Not returning to the country yet. She followed a short way, hesitated, then turned confidently and entered the house he had left.

“Is the gentleman from Cloisterham indoors?”

“Just gone out.”

“Unlucky. When does he return?”

“At six this evening.”

“Bless ye and thank ye. May the Lord prosper a business where a poor soul’s civil question is answered so civilly!”

“I’ll not miss ye twice!” she muttered in the street, less kindly. “I lost ye last—when that omnibus carried you near your journey’s end, plied between station and destination. I wasn’t certain you even arrived. Now I know ye did. My gentleman from Cloisterham—I’ll be there before ye. I swear I’ll not miss ye twice!”

That evening, she stood in Cloisterham High Street, staring at the Nuns’ House, passing the time until nine o’clock—when she expected the omnibus passengers to arrive.

The dusk made it easy to watch. And she saw him among them.

“Now let me see what becomes of you. Go on!”

A whisper to the air—but he obeyed, walking along until he reached an arched gateway, where he vanished unexpectedly.

She quickened pace, nearly caught him entering. Saw only a postern staircase on one side, and on the other—an ancient vaulted room. A large-headed, gray-haired man sat writing, open to the thoroughfare, eyeing all who passed as if collecting toll—though the way was free.

“Halloa!” he called quietly, seeing her pause. “Who are you looking for?”

“A gentleman passed in here just now, sir.”

“Of course. What do you want with him?”

“Where does he live, deary?”

“Live? Up that staircase.”

“Bless ye! Whisper. What’s his name, deary?”

“Surname Jasper. Christian name John. Mr. John Jasper.”

“Has he a calling, good gentleman?”

“Calling? Yes. Sings in the choir.”

“In the spire?”

“Choir.”

“What’s that?”

Mr. Datchery rose, stepped to the doorway. “Do you know what a cathedral is?” he asked, jocosely.

She nodded.

“What is it?”

She looked puzzled, then pointed—massive against the dark sky and stars.

“That’s the answer. Go in there at seven tomorrow morning, and you may see Mr. John Jasper—and hear him too.”

“Thank ye! Thank ye!”

Her triumphant cry did not escape the idle buffer nearby. He glanced at her, clasped his hands behind him, and strolled beside her.

“Or,” he suggested, nodding back, “you can go up now to Mr. Jasper’s rooms.”

She smiled cunningly, shook her head.

“Oh? You don’t want to speak to him?”

She repeated her silent “no.”

“You can admire him at a distance three times a day. Long way to come for that, though.”

She looked up quickly. If Mr. Datchery thought to draw her out, he misjudged her. But she saw no trick in his easy manner—just a man idling, gray hair blowing, hands rattling loose coins.

The sound drew her ears. “Wouldn’t you help me pay for my lodging, dear gentleman? I’m a poor soul, indeed—troubled with a grievous cough.”

“You know the travellers’ lodging, I see. Making straight for it,” he remarked, still rattling. “Been here often, my good woman?”

“Once in all my life.”

“Ay, ay?”

They reached the Monks’ Vineyard. The sight stirred a memory. She stopped at the gate.

“By this token, though you mayn’t believe it—” she declared firmly—“a young gentleman gave me three-and-sixpence as I coughed on this very grass. I asked for it, and he gave it.”

“Wasn’t it a bit bold to name the sum?” Mr. Datchery hinted, still jingling.

“I wanted medicine—good medicine I deal in. Told him so. He gave it. I spent every brass farden honestly. I want to spend the same now. If you give it, I’ll spend it just as honest—upon my soul!”

“What’s the medicine?”

“I’ll be honest—before and after. It’s opium.”

Mr. Datchery’s face changed. He stared at her sharply.
