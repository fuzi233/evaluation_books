Marianne had slept more, in those few hours, than Elinor had dared to hope; but the gift of sleep was like a lid laid briefly over a pot already boiling. She woke into the very same misery with which she had lain down—no new shape to it, no softening, only the raw certainty that pain could keep its place through darkness and dawn as faithfully as any household duty.

Elinor, who had watched the morning light steal between the curtains with the same steady patience she gave to everything that must be endured, drew her sister gently, again and again, to speech. Before the breakfast-tray had even been carried up, they had gone over the same ground repeatedly: Elinor’s quiet conviction, her affectionate counsel, her careful questions; Marianne’s impetuous turns, now generous to Willoughby, now unable to breathe except in the certainty of his guilt. One moment she could picture him unfortunate, ensnared, as wounded as herself; the next she could find no refuge from the plain impossibility of excusing him. She swore herself indifferent to the world’s notice, then shuddered at the thought of ever being seen again, then roused herself to defy it—all within the space of a few sentences, as if her feelings, like a restless fire, changed colour with every gust of memory.

In one particular, however, she was steady: she avoided Mrs. Jennings whenever she could, and when avoidance failed, she shut herself up in a silence so determined it looked like a kind of pride.

“No, no, no—it cannot be,” she cried, as if her own words might build a wall. “She cannot feel. Her kindness is not sympathy; her good-nature is not tenderness. All she wants is gossip; she only likes me now because I supply it.”

Elinor, who had long known how readily Marianne’s fine sensibility sharpened into injustice, could only grieve at this harshness without being surprised by it. Marianne demanded in other people the very mould of her own feelings; she weighed motives by how an action struck herself; and thus even a well-meaning impulse could become, by the angle of her pain, a new offence.

So it was that, after breakfast, while they were together in their room, a small incident sank Mrs. Jennings even lower in Marianne’s estimation—because it struck Marianne at precisely the point where hope, absurd as it might have seemed to anyone watching from without, still lay tender.

Mrs. Jennings came in without ceremony, holding a letter out as if it were a warm roll fresh from the oven and meant to comfort a hungry soul. Her face wore that gaily smiling confidence of one who believes she brings relief.

“Now, my dear,” she said, “I bring you something that I am sure will do you good.”

Marianne heard only that—something—good—and her imagination, quicker than reason, leapt to the one thing in the world it craved. For one piercing instant she had him again: a letter from Willoughby, full of tenderness and contrition; a clearing of every dark corner; explanations made sweet by the very hand that wrote them; and then—because Marianne’s mind never stopped at the first pleasure—Willoughby himself, breathless, rushing in to seal every written assurance with the eloquence of his eyes at her feet.

The next instant destroyed the first. The handwriting—so familiar, so beloved, so little suspected of ever bringing pain—was her mother’s.

If the first flash had been too bright, the drop into disappointment was like tasting honey and then biting into bitterness so sharp it smarted through the palate and up behind the eyes. She felt, with an astonishment that was itself a torment, as if she had never truly suffered until that second. The tears that burst from her were violent enough to be a reproach; but it was a reproach wholly wasted on Mrs. Jennings, who, having no imagination to guess what Marianne had expected, understood only that her poor young friend was unhappy and ought to be consoled.

After many exclamations of pity, she withdrew, still referring Marianne to “the letter of comfort” as if comfort lay folded there as neatly as the page.

But when Marianne could read at last, there was little comfort to be found. Willoughby filled every line without meaning to; her mother’s whole tenderness ran towards him as well as Marianne. Mrs. Dashwood, still confident of an engagement, still relying warmly on his constancy, had only been roused by Elinor’s previous letter to entreat greater openness from Marianne—greater openness to them both. She wrote with that affectionate blindness which makes love generous and makes error feel like faith. Her conviction of Marianne’s future happiness with Willoughby was so entire, so lovingly assumed, that Marianne’s heart seemed to split beneath it. She wept over every expression; for the more her mother trusted, the more cruel the truth felt which Marianne herself could not yet name.

All her impatience to be at home returned with a force almost wild. Barton, with its familiar rooms, its honest sounds—the rustle of trees, the known cadence of voices—rose before her like a country air after a long choking day in town. Her mother was dearer than ever; dearer, too, through the very excess of her mistaken confidence. Marianne was urgent—almost frantic—to be gone.

Elinor, who could not decide whether London’s constant motion was worse for Marianne than the quiet of home, offered no counsel beyond patience. They must wait, she insisted, until their mother’s wishes were known. At length Marianne consented, not because she believed it wise, but because Elinor’s steadiness gave her something to lean upon.

Mrs. Jennings left them earlier than usual. She could not rest, it seemed, until she had carried her grief into other drawing-rooms and made the Middletons and Palmers “grieve as much as herself.” She refused Elinor’s offered attendance and went out alone, her steps brisk with that kind of benevolence which must be active even when it is clumsy.

Elinor, her heart heavy with what she had to do, sat down to write to her mother—an account of what had passed, and a request for directions. Marianne came into the drawing-room as Mrs. Jennings went away and remained fixed at the table near Elinor, following the progress of the pen as if each stroke were a sound she could not bear to hear and yet could not endure to miss. She grieved for Elinor in having to write such a letter; she grieved still more, with a tenderness almost childlike, over the pain it must give their mother.

They had been thus perhaps a quarter of an hour when a sudden rap at the door made Marianne start as if the noise struck her nerves like a sharp utensil against porcelain.

“Who can this be?” cried Elinor. “So early too! I thought we had been safe.”

Marianne moved quickly to the window. The street below had its usual London chorus—wheels, footsteps, distant calls—a ceaseless murmur that never allowed a thought to settle in quiet.

“It is Colonel Brandon!” she said, and there was vexation in her tone, as if his very presence were an additional sound to her already over-full world. “We are never safe from him.”

“He will not come in,” Elinor answered, “as Mrs. Jennings is from home.”

“I will not trust to that,” Marianne said, retreating to her own room with the stiffness of injured feeling. “A man who has nothing to do with his own time has no conscience in his intrusion on that of others.”

The event proved her conjecture right, though it sprang from injustice. Colonel Brandon did come in. Elinor rose to meet him; and at once she read solicitude in his disturbed and melancholy look, in his anxious, brief inquiry after Marianne. Whatever Marianne could say of him, his concern was not a thing to be mistaken.

“I met Mrs. Jennings in Bond Street,” he said, when the first salutations were over. “She encouraged me to come on; and I was the more easily encouraged because I thought it probable I might find you alone, which I was very desirous of doing.”

He paused, as if the words were difficult to set in their right order.

“My object—my wish—my sole wish in desiring it—I hope, I believe it is—to be a means of giving comfort; no, I must not say comfort—not present comfort—but conviction—lasting conviction to your sister’s mind. My regard for her, for yourself, for your mother—will you allow me to prove it by relating some circumstances which nothing but a very sincere regard—nothing but an earnest desire of being useful—would lead me to speak of? I think I am justified—though where so many hours have been spent in convincing myself that I am right, is there not some reason to fear I may be wrong?”

He stopped, his chest rising with a controlled breath, as if even speaking strained something tender within him.

Elinor’s face steadied.

“I understand you. You have something to tell me of Mr. Willoughby that will open his character farther. Your telling it will be the greatest act of friendship that can be shown Marianne. My gratitude will be insured immediately by any information tending to that end, and hers must be gained by it in time. Pray—pray let me hear it.”

“You shall,” he said; “and to be brief—when I quitted Barton last October—but that will give you no idea—I must go farther back.” He gave a short, rueful motion of the head. “You will find me a very awkward narrator, Miss Dashwood. I hardly know where to begin. A short account of myself, I believe, will be necessary; and it shall be a short one. On such a subject,”—he sighed heavily—“I can have little temptation to be diffuse.”

He stood a moment, collecting himself, and then began again, the words coming as if pulled out with effort, each one weighted with years.

“You have probably entirely forgotten a conversation between us one evening at Barton Park—it was the evening of a dance—in which I alluded to a lady I had once known, as resembling, in some measure, your sister Marianne.”

“Indeed,” Elinor answered, “I have not forgotten it.”

He looked pleased—genuinely, quietly pleased—by her remembrance.

“If I am not deceived by the uncertainty—the partiality—of tender recollection, there is a very strong resemblance between them, as well in mind as person. The same warmth of heart, the same eagerness of fancy and spirits. This lady was one of my nearest relations; an orphan from her infancy, and under the guardianship of my father. Our ages were nearly the same; and from our earliest years we were playfellows and friends. I cannot remember the time when I did not love Eliza; and my affection for her, as we grew up, was such as perhaps—judging from my present forlorn and cheerless gravity—you might think me incapable of having ever felt.”

His voice faltered into a softer register on her name, as if the syllables carried an old music no longer sung in ordinary life.

“Hers for me was, I believe, fervent—as fervent as the attachment of your sister to Mr. Willoughby—and it was, though from a different cause, no less unfortunate. At seventeen she was lost to me for ever. She was married—married against her inclination—to my brother. Her fortune was large, and our family estate much encumbered. And this, I fear, is all that can be said for the conduct of one who was at once her uncle and guardian.”

Elinor sat still, her hands folded, her whole attention fixed, as if the room had narrowed to his voice alone. Outside, London’s noise ran on; within, his recollection created its own solemn quiet.

“My brother did not deserve her,” he went on. “He did not even love her. I had hoped her regard for me would support her under any difficulty, and for some time it did; but at last the misery of her situation—for she experienced great unkindness—overcame all her resolution, and though she had promised me that nothing—” He stopped abruptly, shaken. “But how blindly I relate! I have never told you how this was brought on. We were within a few hours of eloping together for Scotland. The treachery—or the folly—of my cousin’s maid betrayed us. I was banished to the house of a relation far distant, and she was allowed no liberty, no society, no amusement, till my father’s point was gained.”

His words grew tighter, as if spoken through a restrained bitterness.

“I had depended on her fortitude too far, and the blow was a severe one—but had her marriage been happy, so young as I then was, a few months must have reconciled me to it, or at least I should not now have to lament it. This, however, was not the case. My brother had no regard for her; his pleasures were not what they ought to have been; and from the first he treated her unkindly. The consequence of this upon a mind so young, so lively, so inexperienced as Mrs. Brandon’s, was but too natural.”

He did not soften the fact by any indulgent phrasing. Elinor felt the delicacy of it, the shame wrapped about it like a veil, and yet she heard in his tone not condemnation, but grief that had been living a long time.

“She resigned herself at first to all the misery of her situation; and happy had it been if she had not lived to overcome those regrets which the remembrance of me occasioned. But can we wonder that, with such a husband to provoke inconstancy, and without a friend to advise or restrain her—for my father lived only a few months after their marriage, and I was with my regiment in the East Indies—she should fall? Had I remained in England, perhaps—but I meant to promote the happiness of both by removing from her for years, and for that purpose had procured my exchange.”

His agitation rose, audible now, as if the very air in the room had become more difficult to draw in.

“The shock which her marriage had given me,” he continued, “was of trifling weight—was nothing—to what I felt when I heard, about two years afterwards, of her divorce. It was that which threw this gloom,—even now the recollection of what I suffered—”

He could say no more. He rose hastily and walked a few minutes about the room, as if movement could carry him past the worst of the memory.

Elinor, affected by the story and still more by the pain of telling it, could not speak. When he returned, he saw her concern; and, with an earnestness that seemed less like gallantry than like gratitude for being listened to, he took her hand, pressed it, and kissed it with respectful warmth. Elinor coloured, but did not withdraw it; there was too much sincerity in his manner to make the action anything but touching.

A few minutes of silent struggle brought him again to composure.

“It was nearly three years after this unhappy period before I returned to England. My first care, when I did arrive, was of course to seek for her; but the search was as fruitless as it was melancholy. I could not trace her beyond her first seducer, and there was every reason to fear she had removed from him only to sink deeper in a life of sin. Her legal allowance was not adequate to her fortune, nor sufficient for her comfortable maintenance; and I learnt from my brother that the power of receiving it had been made over some months before to another person. He imagined—calmly could he imagine it—that her extravagance, and consequent distress, had obliged her to dispose of it for some immediate relief.”

His disdain for his brother’s calmness sharpened his pronunciation, then faded into weariness.

“At last, however, and after I had been six months in England, I did find her. Regard for a former servant of my own, who had since fallen into misfortune, carried me to visit him in a spunging-house, where he was confined for debt; and there, in the same house, under a similar confinement, was my unfortunate sister. So altered—so faded—worn down by acute suffering of every kind! hardly could I believe the melancholy and sickly figure before me to be the remains of the lovely, blooming, healthful girl on whom I had once doted.”

He drew in a breath, as if the memory had a physical taste—bitter and metallic—on his tongue.

“What I endured in so beholding her—but I have no right to wound your feelings by attempting to describe it. I have pained you too much already. That she was, to all appearance, in the last stage of a consumption, was—yes, in such a situation it was my greatest comfort. Life could do nothing for her beyond giving time for a better preparation for death; and that was given. I saw her placed in comfortable lodgings, and under proper attendants; I visited her every day during the rest of her short life: I was with her in her last moments.”

Again he stopped. Elinor, her heart full, could only exclaim, in a low voice, her tender concern for the fate of his unhappy friend.

“Your sister, I hope, cannot be offended,” he said, “by the resemblance I have fancied between her and my poor disgraced relation. Their fates—their fortunes—cannot be the same; and had the natural sweet disposition of the one been guarded by a firmer mind, or a happier marriage, she might have been all that you will live to see the other be.”

He checked himself, as if fearing he had wandered too far.

“But to what does all this lead? I seem to have been distressing you for nothing. Ah, Miss Dashwood—a subject such as this—untouched for fourteen years—it is dangerous to handle it at all! I will be more collected—more concise.”

He fixed his eyes upon a point beyond Elinor, as if reading the rest of his story from a page he could not alter.

“She left to my care her only child, a little girl—the offspring of her first guilty connection—who was then about three years old. She loved the child, and had always kept it with her. It was a valued—a precious—trust to me; and gladly would I have discharged it in the strictest sense, by watching over her education myself, had the nature of our situations allowed it; but I had no family, no home; and my little Eliza was therefore placed at school. I saw her there whenever I could; and after the death of my brother—which happened about five years ago, and which left to me the possession of the family property—she visited me at Delaford. I called her a distant relation; but I am well aware that I have in general been suspected of a much nearer connection with her.”

Elinor listened, understanding now the quiet reserve about his domestic life that had always wrapped him like a cloak.

“It is now three years ago—she had just reached her fourteenth year—that I removed her from school, to place her under the care of a very respectable woman in Dorsetshire, who had the charge of four or five other girls of about the same time of life; and for two years I had every reason to be pleased with her situation. But last February—almost a twelvemonth back—she suddenly disappeared.”

Elinor’s breath caught.

“I had allowed her—imprudently, as it has since turned out—at her earnest desire, to go to Bath with one of her young friends, who was attending her father there for his health. I knew him to be a very good sort of man, and I thought well of his daughter—better than she deserved; for with a most obstinate and ill-judged secrecy, she would tell nothing, would give no clue, though she certainly knew all. He—her father—a well-meaning, but not a quick-sighted man, could really, I believe, give no information; for he had been generally confined to the house while the girls were ranging over the town and making what acquaintance they chose; and he tried to convince me, as thoroughly as he was convinced himself, of his daughter’s being entirely unconcerned in the business.”

His voice hardened on the word business, as if the blandness of it insulted the ruin it named.

“In short, I could learn nothing but that she was gone. All the rest, for eight long months, was left to conjecture. What I thought—what I feared—may be imagined; and what I suffered too.”

“Good heavens!” Elinor cried, unable now to keep her composure smooth. “Could it be—could Willoughby—”

“The first news that reached me of her,” he continued, “came in a letter from herself last October. It was forwarded to me from Delaford, and I received it on the very morning of our intended party to Whitwell; and this was the reason of my leaving Barton so suddenly, which I am sure must at the time have appeared strange to everybody, and which I believe gave offence to some.”

Elinor remembered it perfectly: his abrupt departure, his visible disturbance, the way Willoughby’s looks had judged him.

“Little did Mr. Willoughby imagine,” Colonel Brandon went on, “when his looks censured me for incivility in breaking up the party, that I was called away to the relief of one whom he had made poor and miserable; but had he known it, what would it have availed? Would he have been less gay or less happy in the smiles of your sister? No. He had already done that which no man who can feel for another would do.”

His controlled tone shook, not into loudness, but into a deeper severity.

“He had left the girl whose youth and innocence he had seduced in a situation of the utmost distress, with no creditable home, no help, no friends, ignorant of his address. He had left her, promising to return; he neither returned, nor wrote, nor relieved her.”

“This is beyond everything!” Elinor exclaimed, and the room seemed suddenly closer, the air heavier, as if this knowledge itself had changed its quality.

“His character is now before you,” said Colonel Brandon. “Expensive, dissipated, and worse than both. Knowing all this, as I have now known it many weeks, guess what I must have felt on seeing your sister as fond of him as ever, and on being assured that she was to marry him: guess what I must have felt for all your sakes. When I came to you last week and found you alone, I came determined to know the truth; though irresolute what to do when it was known. My behaviour must have seemed strange to you then; but now you will comprehend it.”

Elinor did comprehend it; and with comprehension came a new weight. She thought of Marianne upstairs—of her proud silences, her quick tears, her insistence on Willoughby’s possible innocence—and felt the cruel necessity of breaking those last defences.

“To suffer you all to be so deceived,” he continued, “to see your sister—but what could I do? I had no hope of interfering with success; and sometimes I thought your sister’s influence might yet reclaim him. But now, after such dishonourable usage, who can tell what were his designs on her? Whatever they may have been, however, she may now, and hereafter doubtless will, turn with gratitude towards her own condition, when she compares it with that of my poor Eliza—when she considers the wretched and hopeless situation of this poor girl, and pictures her to herself, with an affection for him so strong, still as strong as her own, and with a mind tormented by self-reproach which must attend her through life.”

His voice softened, not to excuse, but to urge mercy and perspective.

“Surely this comparison must have its use with her. She will feel her own sufferings to be nothing. They proceed from no misconduct, and can bring no disgrace. On the contrary, every friend must be made still more her friend by them. Concern for her unhappiness, and respect for her fortitude under it, must strengthen every attachment.”

He looked at Elinor with a humility that seemed almost strange in a man so upright.

“Use your own discretion, however, in communicating to her what I have told you. You must know best what will be its effect; but had I not seriously, and from my heart believed it might be of service—might lessen her regrets—I would not have suffered myself to trouble you with this account of my family afflictions, with a recital which may seem to have been intended to raise myself at the expense of others.”

Elinor’s thanks came with grateful earnestness; and with them, the assurance that she did expect material good to Marianne from this dreadful enlightenment.

“I have been more pained,” she said, “by her endeavours to acquit him than by all the rest; for it irritates her mind more than the most perfect conviction of his unworthiness can do. Now, though at first she will suffer much, I am sure she will soon become easier.”

After a short silence, she asked, carefully, “Have you ever seen Mr. Willoughby since you left him at Barton?”

“Yes,” he replied gravely. “Once I have. One meeting was unavoidable.”

Elinor, startled by his manner, looked at him anxiously. “What? have you met him to—”

“I could meet him no other way. Eliza had confessed to me, though most reluctantly, the name of her lover; and when he returned to town—which was within a fortnight after myself—we met by appointment, he to defend, I to punish his conduct. We returned unwounded, and the meeting, therefore, never got abroad.”

Elinor sighed over what she imagined the necessity of such a scene must have been; but she presumed not to censure it.

“Such,” Colonel Brandon added, after a pause, “has been the unhappy resemblance between the fate of mother and daughter; and so imperfectly have I discharged my trust!”

“Is she still in town?” Elinor asked.

“No. As soon as she recovered from her lying-in—for I found her near her delivery—I removed her and her child into the country, and there she remains.”

The words lying-in, child, country fell into Elinor’s mind with a strange heaviness, like items in a ledger of sorrow: the cost of one man’s pleasure tallied in another woman’s lifelong burden.

Soon after, recollecting that he was probably dividing Elinor from her sister at a time when every moment might be required upstairs, Colonel Brandon rose to go. He ended his visit with the same quiet consideration with which he had begun it, receiving again Elinor’s grateful acknowledgements, and leaving her full of compassion and esteem—her thoughts already turning, with reluctant courage, to the cruel task before her: to carry this knowledge, like a bitter draught, to Marianne, in the hope that bitterness might at last purge away illusion.
