The night at Gaunt House had left Major Pendennis utterly spent—so much so that the very thought of moving felt like a military campaign. He groaned his way to Buxton, where he sought solace in the mineral waters, as if hoping to soak away not just his fatigue but also the memory of his own existence. Parliament dissolved. Sir Francis Clavering and family vanished into the countryside, leaving behind only whispers of unfinished affairs. The town emptied out like a drained bathtub.

The season was over. Pen’s lawyer neighbours had taken off for circuit, their briefcases clutched like sacred relics. Fashionable folk had fled to the Continent or the Scottish moors—some for health, some for adventure, most just to escape the dullness of London. The Clubs were ghost towns; Pall Mall pavement was as lonely as a monk’s cell. Red jackets disappeared from Palace gates. St. James’s tradesmen were abroad, enjoying themselves—tailors with new mustaches sailing up the Rhine, bootmakers blushing at Ems or Baden, either meeting old customers or losing money at roulette tables. Clergymen preached to half-empty pews, where no notable sinner dared show his face. The band in Kensington Gardens had packed up its brass instruments, leaving only two or three ancient carriages crawling along the Serpentine. Clarence Bulbul, still chained to his Treasury clerk duties, rode through Rotten Row and declared it resembled the Arabian desert—himself a lone Bedouin wandering across the sands of ennui.

Warrington stuffed his carpetbag with Cavendish tobacco and headed for Norfolk, as he always did during the holidays. Pen remained behind, trapped by the demands of *The Pall Mall Gazette*, which he edited with the grace of a man who’d rather be elsewhere. Captain Shandon, the paper’s true owner, was currently recovering at Boulogne-sur-Mer, where he was said to be drinking water instead of brandy—and possibly regretting his life choices.

Now, despite Pen’s repeated claims that he was utterly jaded, bored beyond belief, and emotionally retired from all passion, the truth was that he was a remarkably healthy young man. He ate with gusto at least once a day, enjoyed company like a starving dog enjoys a bone, and could find joy in anything: a tavern parlor, a steamboat ride to Greenwich, a jaunt to Hampstead with Mr. Finucane, or a visit to Vauxhall Gardens, where he knew the great Simpson personally and once shook hands with the comic singer of the equestrian act.

He watched performances with a satirical eye, yet never without sympathy. He observed the revelers—the roistering youth, the delighted parents, the children clapping, the poor souls laughing too loudly, dancing till dawn, trying to forget their troubles with bread and cheap gin. He liked this—this human tapestry. He called it anthropology, his favorite pastime. Whether it was an old dowager flirting at a ball, a young beauty blushing, a guardsman flirting with a servant girl in the park, or little Tommy feeding ducks while his nurse looked on—Pen found charm in it all. And because his heart was clean, he could enjoy it deeply, secretly, even sadly—because, though surrounded by people, he often felt strangely alone.

“Look at me,” he once told Warrington, “I was in love so fiercely I think I burned out my flame. If I ever marry, it’ll be a marriage of reason—a sensible woman, well-bred, good-tempered, with a little money. Romance? That’s done. I’m old before my time—I’m proud of it.”

“Rubbish,” Warrington growled. “You claimed you were going bald last week, and bragged about it like it was a medal. Then you started using bear’s grease and now smell like a barber shop.”

“You’re Diogenes,” Pen replied, blushing slightly. “You want everyone to live in a tub like yourself. Violets smell better than stale tobacco, you grizzly cynic.”

But Pen, though pretending to care nothing for appearances, took great pains to look presentable. He wore tight boots and polished shoes like a man preparing for a royal audience—though he was merely a weary pilgrim of fashion.

It was on such a Friday evening in autumn, clear and crisp, that Pen, having just finished a brilliant leading article for the *Gazette* (one that would have made Captain Shandon proud—if he weren’t too busy drowning his sorrows in sea air), decided to cross the Thames and enjoy the fireworks at Vauxhall. He tucked his editor’s pass into his pocket—“Editor of *Pall Mall Gazette* and friend”—and paid for the bridge toll with a coin that might have been genuine.

The walk to the Gardens was lovely. Stars glittered above, watching the fireworks not yet launched. Before entering the enchanted realm of twenty thousand lamps, one passed through a dim, narrow passage guarded by wickets. On the walls were two illuminated holes where attendants collected money or admission orders.

Pen approached the second hole, where a gentleman and two ladies were already arguing.

The man, hat tilted at a rakish angle, wearing a shabby cloak with the flair of a dandy, was shouting.

“Bedad, sir, if ye doubt me honour, will ye obleege me by stipping out of that box, and——”

“Lor, Capting!” cried the elder lady.

“Don’t bother me,” snapped the man in the booth.

“And ask Mr. Hodgen himself, who’s in the gyardens, to let these leedies pass. Don’t be froightened, me dear madam, I’m not going to quarl with this gintleman, at anyreet before leedies. Will ye go, sir, and desoire Mr. Hodgen (whose orther I keem in with, and he’s me most intemate friend, and I know he’s goan to sing the ‘Body Snatcher’ here to-noight), with Captain Costigan’s compliments, to stip out and let in the leedies—for meself, sir, I’ve seen Vauxhall, and I scawrun any interfayrance on moi account: but for these leedies, one of them has never been there, and of should think ye’d harly take advantage of me misfartune in losing the ticket, to deproive her of her pleasure.”

“It ain’t no use, Captain. I can’t go about your business,” the check-taker said.

Captain Costigan swore a blue oath. The elder lady gasped: “Lor, ow provokin!”

The younger one looked up at him, said, “Never mind, Captain Costigan, I’m sure I don’t want to go at all. Come away, mamma.” But then, overwhelmed, she burst into tears.

“Me poor child!” the Captain cried. “Can ye see that, sir, and will ye not let this innocent creature in?”

“It ain’t my business,” the doorkeeper snapped.

Just then, Arthur Pendennis arrived, recognizing the Captain instantly.

“Don’t you know me, Captain? Pendennis!” he said, removing his hat and bowing.

“Me dear boy! Me dear friend!” Captain Costigan boomed, extending a hand. “A most unluckee conthratong! I had an order for two from Mr. Hodgen—he’s inside, singing the ‘Body Snatcher’ tonight—and I thought it admitted three. But I lost the ticket on the way. Now the leedies must go back—my daughter’s heart is broken!”

Arthur, ever generous, couldn’t refuse. He saw the girl’s tear-streaked face, her quivering lip, her quiet dignity beneath the grief. Even if it cost him his last shilling, he’d get her in.

“Is there any way I can help?” he asked kindly. “I have a ticket myself—it admits two. May I use it?”

The Prince of Fairoaks nearly offered to pay for the whole party. But instinct told him: don’t play the grand seigneur. So he handed his purse to Costigan, pulled out his ticket, and offered his arm to the elder lady—though “ladies” was stretching it. They wore bonnets, shawls, ribbons, and the younger one had a dainty foot peeking from under a modest grey dress. Still, Pen treated her with the same gallantry he’d show a queen.

“Fanny, take the gentleman’s arm,” the elder said. “Since you’re so kind—I’ve seen you come in at our gate, sir, and go to Captain Strong’s at No. 3.”

Fanny curtseyed and placed her small, shabby-gloved hand on Arthur’s arm. She wasn’t a child, nor quite a woman. Her tears had dried, her cheeks flushed, her eyes sparkled with gratitude.

Arthur gently placed his other hand over hers. “Fanny’s a pretty name,” he said. “And you know me?”

“We keep the lodge at Shepherd’s Inn, sir,” she said shyly. “I’ve never been to Vauxhall. Papa didn’t like me to go—and—and—O—O—law, how beautiful!”

She stepped back, dazzled by the sudden blaze of light—ten thousand lamps, more than any fairy tale could dream of. Pen smiled, touched by her wonder, and drew her closer.

“What would I not give for a little of this pleasure?” he mused aloud.

“Your purse, Pendennis, me dear boy,” Captain Costigan called from behind. “Will ye count it? It’s all roight—no—ye thrust in old Jack Costigan (he thrusts me, ye see, madam). Ye’ve been me preserver, Pen (I’ve known um since choildhood, Mrs. Bolton; he’s the proproietor of Fairoaks Castle, and many’s the cooper of clart I’ve dthrunk there with the first nobilitee of his neetive countee)—Mr. Pendennis, ye’ve been me preserver, and of thank ye; me daughtther will thank ye—Mr. Simpson, your humble servant, sir!”

If Pen was courteous, Captain Costigan was theatrical. He bowed to every singer, shouted “Bravo!” at the top of his lungs, and seemed to believe he was attending a coronation.

Meanwhile, Captain Costigan, noble descendant of Hibernian kings and chieftains, walked beside the ladies with dignified superiority—after all, they were merely housekeepers, not peers. But Pen had no such qualms. Miss Fanny Bolton didn’t make his bed or cook his meals. She was simply a charming girl, and he wouldn’t let go of her.

Fanny, meanwhile, glowed. She looked around at other couples, and thought: *My gentleman is handsomer than any of them.* There were rogues, clerks, dandies, and even Lord Colchicum, escorting Mademoiselle Caracoline, who spoke French with alarming force and leaned heavily on his lordship’s arm.

Colchicum was attending Mademoiselle Caracoline. Tom Tufthunt was attending Colchicum. When Don Juan climbs the wall, Leporello is never far behind.

When Pen and Fanny passed the Viscount’s party, Colchicum gave Arthur a dismissive glance, but Tufthunt waved cheerfully. “How are you, old boy?” he said, knowing full well he was being used as a glorified valet.

“That’s the rider from Astley’s,” Fanny whispered, pointing. “And who’s that old man?”

“That’s Lord Viscount Colchicum,” Pen said, protectively. He meant no harm. He just liked being protective.

Fanny was lovely—dark eyes, pearl-white teeth, lips redder than Madame Caracoline’s. Her laugh was pure, unlike the woman’s loud, vulgar cackle. Only recently had Fanny realized she was beautiful. A year ago, she was a clumsy, awkward girl, sneered at by her father and ignored at school. Now, blooming like a flower in a forgotten corner, she was radiant.

So they walked together through the gardens. Though London was empty, two million people still lingered—and among them, Henry Foker, Esq., pale, silent, hands in pockets, nodding ruefully at Pen.

Henry was trying to distract himself. But Blanche haunted him. He consulted fortune-tellers, who told him he loved a dark-haired woman who would make him happy—only to disappoint him when she wasn’t Blanche. At the concert, Mr. Momus sang his loudest songs, asked his silliest riddles—but no smile touched Foker’s lips. He didn’t hear a word.

Pen and Fanny stood nearby, listening.

“Why does that odd little man look so miserable?” Fanny asked.

“He’s crossed in love,” Pen said. “Isn’t that enough to ruin anyone?”

He looked down at her, noble and protective—like Egmont with Clara, Leicester with Amy.

“Crossed in love, is he?” Fanny sighed. “Poor gentleman.” She turned her eyes toward him with kindness—but Harry didn’t notice.

“How dy do, Mr. Pendennis!” a voice called.

It was Samuel Huxter, Clavering, in a white coat, red neckcloth, dingy shirt collar, glass buttons, trousers screaming “Look at me—I’m cheap!” He carried a cane and a pink-satin-clad lady on his arm.

“Pretty bobbish, thanky,” he said, wagging his head. “I say, Pendennis, you’ve been coming it strong since the row at Wapshot’s. Great author, hay? Saw your name in the *Morning Post*. Too much of a swell to come sup with an old friend? Charterhouse Lane tomorrow—some devilish good fellows from Bartholomew’s, stunning gin-punch. Here’s my card.”

He pulled it out with his teeth and handed it over.

“You’re exceedingly kind,” Pen said, taking it with a lofty bow. “But I have an engagement.”

Huxter shrugged. “Never mind, old fellow. If you’re passing, drop by. I’m mostly home Saturdays. Cheese cupboard’s open. Ta, ta.”

As he ran off toward the fireworks, Pen glanced at Fanny and smiled.

Then, hand in hand, they raced toward the pyrotechnics, Mrs. Bolton following, Captain Costigan lagging behind—too majestic to run. The fireworks exploded overhead, painting the sky in gold and crimson, while the city below slept, unaware of the tiny drama unfolding beneath the stars.
