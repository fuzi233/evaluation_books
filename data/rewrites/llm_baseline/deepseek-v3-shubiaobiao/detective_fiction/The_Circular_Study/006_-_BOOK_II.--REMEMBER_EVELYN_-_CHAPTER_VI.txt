Miss Butterworth’s usual composure was absent when Mr. Gryce met her gaze. There was an uncharacteristic unease in her expression—an admission of vulnerability that even a stranger might have found striking.

"You’ve caught me," she said briskly, her tone edged with sharpness. "That gray parasol you so kindly returned to my residence is not mine. But since you’ve deduced my presence in Mr. Adams’s study, I see no point in evasion. I was there. And now, here I am to confess it."

Mr. Gryce’s interest sharpened. He drew a chair closer and sat. "You were there," he repeated. "At what time? And—though I hesitate to pry—for what purpose?"

She lifted her chin. "I must explain, if only to mitigate my own disadvantage. I have no connection to Mr. Adams, nor any excuse for inserting myself into this affair, as I did in past matters. This time, I sought it out—a lapse in judgment I now regret. Had I gone to Jericho instead, it might have spared me this humiliation."

She paused, then continued with uncharacteristic hesitation. "Mr. Gryce, I never intended to involve myself in another murder. My past successes in deduction may have earned your approval, but they did not tempt me to repeat the experience. Yet here I am, entangled once more—not by your designs, but by circumstance."

Mr. Gryce waited, patient.

She exhaled. "I was passing the house—an old, notable residence I’ve often observed with curiosity. As I glanced up, the vestibule door burst open. A young man rushed out, carrying a woman in his arms. She seemed unconscious; he was in a state of violent agitation. Before the crowd could take notice, he set her on her feet and hurried her away. Then—I heard a cry from inside. A cry of terror."

Her voice lowered. "I acted on impulse. I entered, closing the door behind me. The house was silent, but that cry drew me forward. I found myself in the circular study—and there he lay. Mr. Adams, dying, the dagger in his heart, the cross on his breast. His last breath passed as I knelt beside him."

Mr. Gryce leaned in. "The young man you saw—describe him."

"Tall, well-dressed, in a checked suit. But it was his expression that struck me—a turmoil of passion, barely restrained. The woman was pale, almost lifeless in his grip."

"And the butler? Did you encounter him?"

"No. He was not in the study when I arrived. Yet I heard his cries from within—or perhaps it was the bird, shrieking in its cage. It called a name—'Eva. Lovely Eva.'"

Mr. Gryce’s eyes narrowed. "You examined the room?"

"I did. I moved the inkstand—carefully—searching for any scrap of writing. The woman had said, 'You’ve left that line of writing behind.' I thought it might hold the key."

"And did you find it?"

"No. But I noticed something else—the look on Mr. Adams’s face as he died. Not just pain, but resolve. A final, desperate decision."

Mr. Gryce studied her. "You have a theory."

She met his gaze. "I do. And I believe you’ll find it worth considering."

With that, she leaned forward and whispered in his ear.

His eyebrows rose. Then, slowly, he smiled.

"Madam," he said, standing, "your insight does you credit. We will act on it at once."

And with a bow, he left the room.
