The night turned bitter, a freezing void that encased me in ice. The distant, guttural cries of wolves grew louder, their padded footsteps a soft, relentless drumming on the rock beneath me. Their breath, hot and quick, fogged the air. Through the impenetrable dark, I saw a circle of glowing eyes contracting, tightening around me. My hour had arrived. I sprang up—only to realize, with a jolt of horror, that I possessed not a single stick.

Then, the rush. Their eyes blazed with a greedy, furious light, black throats gaping wide to swallow me alive. I stood paralyzed, waiting for the end. Just as they lunged from their formation, a sudden, silent cloud of emerald eyes descended upon their flank. Heads erupted from the mist, flying forward with shrieks that were somehow weaker yet far more fierce than the wolves' own snarls. By the sound, I knew them: cats, led by a massive grey sentinel whose form I could barely discern save for those terrible, knowing eyes. Yet I knew him—their leader—and through his gaze, I felt the sheer scale of the beast.

A terrific battle ensued, its details lost to me save for the chaotic noise that filled the night. I wanted to flee, certain this skirmish was destined for me—but what use was there in running? My first step would send me tumbling, and my foes, both wolf and cat, could see and smell me in the gloom. Suddenly, the howling ceased, replaced by a wild, caterwauling cacophony. The soft padding returned, signaling flight. The cats had won.

In an instant, the sharpest teeth sank into my legs. A heartbeat later, the cats were a living cataract over me, biting at every exposed inch, scratching furiously across my skin. A multitude clung to my body; I could not escape. Desperately, I dove into the hateful swarm, every finger driven by instinct to destroy. I tore at them, choked them in vain. When I tried to fling them off, they adhered to my hands like stubborn limpets. I trampled them underfoot, gouged their eyes, bit with jaws stronger than theirs, yet I could not dislodge a single one. Relentlessly, they discovered new spaces for their mouths, hauling at my skin with vast, curved claws that hissed and spat in my face. Never touching my skin until, in utter despair, I collapsed to the ground. Instantly, they abandoned my body to dart at my face. I rose, and they immediately deserted it to return to my legs.

In agony, I broke free and ran, caring not for direction, cleaving through the solid darkness. They pursued me in a surrounding torrent, rubbing and leaping against me, tormenting me with every step. When I stumbled, they allowed me time to rise; when fear slowed my pace, they surged anew. That miserable night, they forced me to run—a comparatively smooth path, for I avoided the gullies, passing the Evil Wood unseen and leaving it behind in the shadows.

At last, dawn broke. I stood beyond the channels, on the verge of the orchard valley. Overwhelmed by joy, I sought to make peace with my persecutors, but not a cat was to be seen. I threw myself onto the moss and fell fast asleep.

A kick roused me. Bound hand and foot, I was once more the thrall of the giants. "What fitter?" I whispered to myself, "to whom else should I belong?" Laughter bubbled up within me, born of a triumphant self-disgust. A second kick silenced my false merriment. Thus aided, I struggled to my feet.

Six giants surrounded me. They cut the rope binding my legs, lashed a new line to each ankle, and dragged me away. I trudged as best I could, though frequent double-pulls sent me crashing down, requiring them to kick me upright each time. They marched me straight to my former labor site, tied my legs to a tree, released my arms, and pressed the hateful flint into my left hand. Then they lay down and pelted me with fallen fruit and stones, rarely striking home. If I could have freed my legs and seized the stick I'd spotted nearby, I might have taken down all six! "But the Little Ones will come at night!" I murmured, finding comfort in the promise.

The day passed in grueling toil. As darkness fell, they bound my hands and left me suspended against the tree. I slept fitfully, waking often from dreams of lying amidst heaps of children. With morning, my enemies returned, bringing their kicks and their brutish company.

Around noon, exhaustion and hunger nearly claimed me, when a sudden commotion erupted in the brushwood, followed by a burst of bell-like laughter so cherished by my heart. A cry of delight and welcome tore from my throat. Immediately, a trumpet call like baby elephants, a neighing like foals, and a bellowing like calves filled the air. Through the bushes poured a crowd of Little Ones, riding diminutive horses, small elephants, and little bears—the noises emanating from the riders, not the beasts. Among the mounted children walked the older boys and girls, including a woman cradling a baby that crowed softly. The giants sprang to their lumbering feet but were instantly assailed by a storm of sharp stones. Horses charged their legs; bears rose to hug them at the waist; elephants coiled their trunks around their necks, pulling them down for a trampling unlike any they had ever received. In moments, my ropes were undone, and I was engulfed in the seemingly endless arms of the Little Ones. For a time, I saw no more of the giants.

They seated me, and my Lona approached. Without a word, she began feeding me the loveliest red and yellow fruits. I ate, the entire colony standing guard until I finished. Then, two of their largest elephants were brought forth. Placed side by side, their trunks were hooked together and their tails bound. Though docile enough to undo the knots with a single shake or forget the hooks entirely, they held firm, maintaining perfect parallelism as their little masters demanded. I rose and lay in the hollow between their backs. As the wise animals leaned against one another to counterbalance my weight, they created a most comfortable litter. My feet protruded beyond their tails, but my head rested pillowed upon an ear of each. Smaller children mounted as a bodyguard, lining the back of each bearer. The assembly formed a train, and the procession moved.

Where they carried me, I did not guess; I yielded to their pleasure, almost as happy as they. At first, chattering, laughing, and performing endless joyful tricks, the moment they sensed I was drifting off, they fell as still as judges.

I awoke to a sudden, musical uproar. We traveled through the forest where they had found the babies, a woodland stretching from the valley to the hot stream. A tiny girl sat with her feet near my face, gazing down at me coaxingly before speaking, causing the others to hush.

"We make a petisson to king," she declared.

"What is it, my darling?" I asked.

"Shut eyes one minute," she answered.

"Certainly! Here goes!" I replied, closing them tight.

"No, no! Not before I tell you!" she cried.

I opened them, and we talked and laughed for another hour.

"Close eyes!" she commanded suddenly.

I obeyed, keeping them shut. The elephants halted. I heard a soft scurry, a slight rustle, then a profound silence—for in that world, *some* silences are heard.

"Open eyes!" twenty voices shouted from a distance. But when I complied, no creature was visible save the elephants bearing me. I knew the children were swift at vanishing, a lesson the giants had taught them, yet as I raised myself and scanned the open, shrub-less forest, I saw neither hand nor heel. Staring in blank astonishment, I waited.

Sunset approached, and darkness gathered quickly. Yet soon, a multitude of birds began to sing. I lay listening, convinced the children must be behind it, wondering how they could command the birds. Soon, however, looking up into the tree beneath my elephants, I spied a faint motion among the leaves. Peering keenly, I saw white spots emerge in the dark foliage. The music faltered, a gale of childish laughter rippled through the air, and white spots poured out in every direction. The trees were full of children! In wild merriment, they began to descend, some dropping from bough to bough so rapidly I scarcely believed gravity held them. I left my litter and was instantly surrounded—a target for all their jubilant artillery. With stately composure, the elephants walked away to bed.

"But," I said, as their uproarious gladness played out, "how is it that I never heard you sing like birds before? Even when I thought it must be you, I could hardly believe it!"

"Ahhh," said one of the wildest, "but we were not birds then! We were run-creatures, not fly-creatures! We had our hide-places in the bushes then; but when we came to no-bushes, only trees, we had to build nests! When we built nests, we grew birds, and when we were birds, we had to do birds!
