Since the first gods walked the earth and shaped man from clay, there have always been stretches of land too cruel, too hungry, for human life to take root. But none are half so brutal as that wild western edge of Atlantis, facing the unbroken savagery of ancient Europe and Africa.

Common folk shun it entirely. Their old superstition swears the spirits of the most wicked dead roam there in broad daylight, tearing apart anyone the land’s natural horrors spare. Even murderers and traitors, running from the capital’s justice, will turn tail of their own free will and drag themselves back to face execution rather than spend a single night lost in that wilderness’s unnameable terrors. The educated laugh many of those old myths off… but no one steps over the boundary cairns without a cold prickle down their spine. The mountains rumble nonstop, earth tremors shake the ground under your boots every single day, invisible clouds of bitter poison gas slip through the trees so fast you can taste the sulphur before you have time to run. Rivers run crystal clear but brim with deadly neurotoxin, great cracks split the earth open to the molten fires below, and when cold rain pours down into them the whole land detonates in scalding steam and mud. Boiling mud geysers and flows of glowing molten rock can swallow a camp before you hear the warning rumble.

But for all that, it holds vast, shadowed forests of black ironwood, trees so old no living man remembers a time they weren’t standing. Under the canopy, every inch of ground sits submerged in warm, steaming water, the air thick with perpetual damp that saps the strength right out of your bones. And it is the undisputed domain of the lizards: scaled beasts of every size, most of them large enough to make a man think twice, some so colossal no human blade or spear could leave a mark. I saw one once, heaving itself out of a deep lake after it had finished feeding for the day, and the whole marsh thudded and pulsed under its tons of weight. That beast could have swallowed a fully grown war mammoth whole, and even a mammoth’s full charge would not have scratched the thick, horny plates that cased its skull and spine. Great spines ran along its back, tail, and limbs, and it sheared through 10-foot-wide tree trunks like they were dry twigs as it trundled off to its wallow. Millennia later, a paleontologist digging in Wyoming would unearth its kind’s skeleton: 130 feet long, 50 tons, 8 feet of solid thigh bone, enough empty space in its ribcage to seat 40 grown men.

These colossi rarely get caught in the land’s fissures. They move faster than you would ever believe, and they are far cleverer than most men give them credit for: they can feel the mountains rumbling hours before an eruption, and they will retreat to deep, cool lakes or thick mud wallows to wait out the danger. Their hides can stand heat no human could survive. I watched one lie completely peaceful in a pool where steam bursts shot boiling water 20 feet into the air, and another sprint straight across a slow flow of molten rock without so much as a singe on its scales.

That was the world I was dumped into, exiled by Empress Phorenice to die a slow, horrible death. For the first months I came closer to knowing raw, unthinking fear than I ever had in my life, and only my unshakable faith that the High Gods still had a purpose for me kept me from breaking entirely. My clothes rotted off my back until I was as naked as a wandering European savage. Ague wracked my limbs day and night, and I could find no healing herbs to ease the fever. For weeks on end I survived only on fat tree grubs and bitter leaves, and more than once I ate unfamiliar jungle game that left me vomiting and writhing in pain for hours.

But a man adapts, or he dies. As the months dragged on, I learned the land’s tricks. I found a massive hollow ironwood, cut handholds up its inner trunk to a perch 10 man-heights off the ground, and built a rough floor of branches for a shelter. Later I carved arrow slits into the wood for light and air, and dragged bucket after bucket of sand up to make a hearth, so I could cook my food safely above the ground where no wandering lizard could smell the smoke. I learned to trap the giant scaled fish in the rivers and snare the smaller freshwater turtles, and soon my ribs stopped sticking through my skin. The bitter, tar-tasting wild greenery I never could force myself to eat, but I no longer starved.

Years slipped by. The brute, survival part of me was content enough, but the ache for Nais never faded. I carved her face over and over out of a hippo’s ivory tusk, my fingers clumsy, my art terrible, but even the lumpy, crude little figurine felt like a piece of her when I traced the curve of her jaw. It did not sate my longing, though. It only made me hungrier for news of her, for a single glimpse of the tomb where I had sealed her beneath the granite throne of the Sacred Mountain to keep her safe from Phorenice’s wrath.

Every night, from my arrow slit, I watched the distant snow-capped peak far to the east burn blood red as the sun set behind it. I remembered, back when I was a new initiate to the priesthood, that the sun rose every morning right over that very summit. And I knew: if I climbed to that peak’s crest, I could see all the way across the steaming, swampy lowlands to the Sacred Mountain, to the city where my love lay.

I set out at dawn the next day. The thick, clinging mists of the lowlands swallowed my path, I wandered blind for weeks, covering three times the direct distance. A hundred times I rounded a tree and came face to face with a 30-foot lizard, and only the grace of the Gods made the beast turn away without seeing me. Twice I had to hew whole rafts out of driftwood to cross rivers of scalding, boiling water that blocked my path. I did not care. I had waited years for this, and every step carried me closer.

As I climbed higher, the trees thinned out and the air turned cold. I wove myself buskins and a thick coat out of tough alpine grass to fend off the chill. Higher still, the permanent snow started, then the glacial ice, its mazes of crevasses trying to swallow me with every step. A giant man-eating vulture followed me up the slopes for days, diving at my head until I lunged at it with my spear and it veered off, waiting for me to collapse. I pushed on, sick with altitude, gasping for thin air, until finally I hauled myself over the summit.

Far, far below, glinting through the clear, biting wind, was the Sacred Mountain. Its eternal ring of temple fires burned faint and pale in the midday sun. I could not see the city at its foot, but I knew exactly where it lay, and my whole heart ached for Nais. I was so lost in the memory of her face that I did not hear my name being called until the third time.

I spun, spear in hand. An old man was tramping toward me over the snow: white hair, white beard, bent and frail, but moving faster than any healthy young soldier could ever run, the glowing sun symbol of our order burning bright in his fist. It was Zaemon. He traced the sacred sign of the Seven, and I returned it with all the ritual reverence due him. He was the head of the High Council of Priests, the highest authority left on Atlantis.

“Your summons comes from the full Council,” he said, his voice sharp over the mountain wind. “Phorenice’s madness has no bounds. She has laid siege to the Sacred Mountain, swears every last soul that will not bow to her will dies. The Council has no general left alive who can lead our defenses. You will return at once. Atlantis will fall if you do not.”

My grip on the spear tightened. “I obey. But first: what of Nais?”

Zaemon’s face did not soften. “Phorenice has stolen most of our ancient magic. She knows you still live. She has left Nais unharmed in that tomb, waiting to use her as a weapon against you. Do not be soft, brother. Even I, her own father, would sacrifice her in a heartbeat if it saved Atlantis.”

I stepped forward, my voice cold. “I will lead your armies. I will fight every soldier Phorenice throws at us. I will die in the last ditch if I have to. But you swear to me, right now, that you will get Nais out of that tomb unharmed, and give her to me. If you do not, I will not lift a finger to save your sacred city. I will burn every last stone of Atlantis to the ground to make Phorenice’s funeral pyre.”

Zaemon snarled, his old face twisting with rage. He called me faithless, called me traitor to my oaths, said the Council never thought they would have to bargain with one of their own. But there was no one else. He had no choice. At last he spat out the oath, swearing by the sun itself that Nais would be mine, unharmed, the second we retook the Sacred Mountain.

He turned without another word, and set off down the slope. He moved faster and faster, his footprints in the deep snow growing shallower and shallower, until they vanished entirely. The whole flank of the mountain lay before me, trackless, untouched by any other human foot. He was already hundreds of miles away, back at the Sacred Mountain, by the time I slung my spear over my shoulder and started down the slope after him.

I had taken oaths to Atlantis once. I had put the good of the nation before all else. But those oaths were dead now. Nothing mattered. Not the temples, not the council, not the whole fate of the lost continent. I would win this war, and I would walk away with Nais. Everything else could burn.
