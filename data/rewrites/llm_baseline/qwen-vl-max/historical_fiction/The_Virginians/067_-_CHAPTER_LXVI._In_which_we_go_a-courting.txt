Some of my dear readers may be familiar with the famous garden in Regent’s Park, where birds, beasts, and fish alike are housed and displayed for our delight and edification. There, amid the quiet bustle of nature and human curiosity, I found myself one day—still musing on the fortunes of our Virginia friends—gazing into the glassy depths of the aquarium. A creature glided past, swift and elegant, its form cutting through the water in graceful arcs: now showing its dark, shimmering back, now turning to reveal a pale, silken breast. It was, as it turned out, none other than the humble flounder—the very same fish we’ve all devoured at Greenwich, unaware that beneath its plain exterior lay such beauty and agility.

So it is with men, and so with flounders. When placed in their proper element, both thrive—strong, noble, and full of life. Remove them from it, and they falter, their grace lost, their purpose gone. The flounder, cast upon dry land, flops helplessly, gasping its last breaths. Yet lift him gently, return him to the Thames, and he will swim once more, free and proud. But let us not dwell too long on this analogy—for surely the proverb “a fish out of water” has been told before by wiser naturalists than I.

Harry Warrington had long been out of his element. For years he had drifted, uncertain and unmoored. But when he stepped into the army, something within him stirred. Like the flounder returned to the river, he revived. Health, strength, spirit—all rushed back into him. With a single tap of an epaulet upon his shoulder, he became a new man. He embraced his calling with eagerness, mastering every detail of military life with astonishing speed. Had I the gift of my friend Lorrequer, I would follow him into camp—through marches, messes, parades, and the camaraderie of the field. I would share his laughter over wine, sleep under canvas beside him, and learn the art of war from the ground up. But since I cannot do so, I must leave the reader to imagine the scene: Harry Warrington in his bright red coat and golden trimmings, proud to wear the King’s colours, eager to serve, and content at last to find his place in the world.

George, too, rejoiced in his brother’s success. He wrote to their mother in Virginia, pouring out his admiration for Harry’s progress and his own joy at seeing him flourish. He spoke of how Harry had earned his first rank through merit alone, and how he, George, hoped to ask their mother’s permission to help fund a higher commission for him. It gave him great pleasure, he confessed, to assist his brother—especially since Harry’s sudden return to fortune had deprived him of an inheritance he had thought rightfully his. In his innocence, Harry had lived beyond his means, believing himself heir to greater wealth. Now, George felt it only fair—and almost like a thank-offering for his own rescue—that he should contribute generously to his brother’s advancement.

And then, having spoken of Harry, George turned to matters closer to his own heart. He addressed his beloved mother on a subject that weighed heavily upon him. She knew well the kindness of Mr. and Mrs. Lambert, the family who had become their closest friends in England. While their own kin had often proved distant or indifferent, the Lamberts had offered warmth, generosity, and unwavering support. General Lambert was respected throughout the army; his wife, a former schoolfellow of Madam Esmond, had nurtured both her children with deep affection. And now, George confessed, he had come to love the elder Miss Lambert—Theo—with a passion that, he believed, would determine the course of his entire life.

He had not yet made a formal proposal, nor spoken plainly to her parents—but he had not hidden his feelings. Indeed, they seemed to be met with gentle encouragement. Theo, he said, was everything a woman should be—modest, kind, intelligent, and devoted to duty. Her devotion to her mother had been admirable, and he trusted she would carry the same care into her marriage. He described her as a model of perfection, and declared that his future happiness depended entirely on whether he could win her hand.

Why not publish this letter? some sentimental soul might ask. Why withhold the tender words of a young man’s heart?

Because love is sacred. To expose the private confessions of youth—to read aloud the trembling vows, the whispered hopes, the secret sighs—is to trespass on a holy ground. Shall we pry behind closed doors, count the glances exchanged at twilight, record the tremor in a voice, or trace the pulse beneath a maiden’s breast? No. Let us respect silence where tenderness dwells. Love is not meant for public display. It belongs to two souls, and to no third party. As the ancients warned: *Qui Deae sacrum vulgarit arcanae*—he who reveals the sacred secrets of the goddess, desecrates them. Beware the man who dines with you today, for tomorrow he may print your most intimate talk. Sail with him, and he may cast you overboard.

While Harry sought glory in battle—charging into smoke and fire, carrying wounded comrades from the fray—George pursued a quieter, gentler war. His battlefield was the moonlit walk beneath Theo’s window, his weapons verses penned in secret, flowers plucked from Covent Garden, and notes passed between hands that trembled with longing. He walked to St. James’s Church, singing hymns without hearing the sermon, his mind fixed on her. He charmed her younger siblings, bantered with her sister Hetty, and won the affections of her mother, whom Martin Lambert claimed was already half in love with him.

Gumbo and Sady were ever busy shuttling between Southampton Row and Dean Street. In summer, outings were planned—Ranelagh, Hampstead, Vauxhall, Marylebone Gardens—each invitation a chance to be near her. George asked Theo to copy out a famous tragedy for the stage, knowing full well her handwriting was among the most beautiful in London. As the pages passed between them, so did little notes—questions, thanks, compliments—each one a spark in the growing flame.

Here lies a packet marked in George’s neat script: “T’s letters, 1758–9.” Should we open it? Should we expose those virgin lines, written only for one ear? Years later, perhaps, the husband read them again—his heart stirred by memory, his eyes moist with tenderness. But to show them now would be sacrilege. Let us trust that the letters were pure, modest, and full of reverence. That is enough.

In truth, we have said little—but in these last months, George Warrington had come to know certainty. He had found the woman of his dreams. Not necessarily the most beautiful—there were others, Flora, Coelia, Ardelia—yet her face held a sweetness that no other could match. She might not be the cleverest, but her voice was music to his ears. And in her presence, he felt himself rise—witty, eloquent, generous, noble. He was better when she was near. And she, no doubt, saw him as the hero of her dreams—endowed with every virtue, every charm, every quality she could desire.

When George’s letter reached Virginia, it stirred excitement around Madam Esmond’s hearth. So he was in love—and wished to marry! A natural thing, and one that would keep him safe from reckless pursuits. If he chose a well-bred Christian girl, Madam saw no harm.

“Of course they’ll set their caps at him,” Mountain remarked. “They think his wealth matches his estate. He doesn’t say if she has money. I fear otherwise.”

“And people here would try to catch Mr. Esmond Warrington for their daughters,” Madam replied grimly, eyeing her servant. “Who are no richer than Miss Lambert.”

“I suppose you mean me?” Mountain asked. “My Fanny is poor, as you say. Kind of you to remind me.”

“I said people would set their caps at him. If the cap fits, *tant pis!* as my father used to say.”

“You think I’m scheming to keep George for my daughter? I thank you, sir! A good opinion you have of us after all these years!”

“My dear Mountain,” Madam said with dignity, “I know you better than to believe you’d ever imagine your daughter a fit match for a gentleman of Mr. Esmond’s rank.”

Mrs. Mountain cried out. “Then you *did* think of marrying her to my son! I shall write to Mr. Esmond Warrington and tell him how sorry I am you’re disappointed!”

And so, we may suppose, Mrs. Mountain was indeed disappointed—perhaps harboring ambitions for her daughter. Else why such anger at the idea of George’s marriage?

Madam Esmond replied warmly. She praised George’s fraternal care for Harry, and agreed that it was right to help him recover what he had lost. As for funding Harry’s promotion, she would gladly bear half the cost, grateful that his courage had earned him his first step. This act of generosity, she added, was far more pleasing than certain other extravagances—like the ransom paid to the French captain’s family—a sum she had refused to cover. She had a firm will, like her son. What she called his pride, he called persistence. Whether she herself was proud, only time could judge. But people then, as now, liked to have their way.

George, meanwhile, had spent much of his patrimony—on his own ransom, his brother’s debts, and half the price of Harry’s advancement. Yet he did so willingly. His mother always reminded him of his grandfather’s name, urging him to uphold the dignity of the Esmonds. She sent him commissions—goods to buy, clothes to order, horses to purchase—even though her remittances were small. Still, she spoke constantly of their exalted lineage, of the need to move in the highest society, to frequent court, to live with distinction. George believed, without question, that her funds would arrive when needed. And so he lived accordingly.

The records of the time show bills for fine dinners, tailor-made court suits, liveries for servants and chairmen, receipts from horse dealers—evidence that George, too, was living with considerable expense. But unlike his brother, he was not reckless. No scandals of gambling or racehorses stained his name. His table was generous, his carriages elegant, his purse never empty. His inheritance was vast, and known to all. These facts, I mention, because they likely shaped both George’s conduct and the views of those around him.

The young heir of Virginia was abroad, travelling for pleasure and education. His mother, the queen, wrote to him daily, urging him to live with the grandeur befitting his station. She ordered him to make purchases—twelve new sets of wagon harness, an organ capable of playing fourteen specific psalm tunes. George obeyed dutifully. Though payment had not yet arrived, he assumed it would eventually. He never discussed these matters with his friends, nor questioned his mother’s affairs. They, in turn, took it for granted that he was secure—and while not mercenary, the Lamberts were pleased to see their daughter attached to a man of such character, talent, and prospects.

Mr. Esmond Warrington was honest in word and deed. He carried himself with a quiet dignity, neither haughty nor supercilious. Toward strangers, especially the great, he was reserved. But toward women, he was courteous; toward friends, warm, kind, and lively. It was no wonder, then, that one young woman came to believe him the finest man in the world—even above her father.

A true love makes a man better, in the eyes of the beloved, than all others. George himself marveled at how witty, wise, and eloquent he became when speaking to Theo. Yet we must not linger on such whispers. Those soft words were never meant for the page. If you are inclined to sentiment, put down the book and remember—remember the feeling that once clung to your arm, the tears that fell upon your heart. Perhaps it is gone—buried, forgotten. But in a moment, it rises again, smiling, whispering, alive. And yet—so far away. The hearth is cold, the ship has sailed. The roses are gone.

But enough of sentiment. As for George and Theo—no one doubted the outcome. The Lamberts were fully satisfied. Though no formal proposal had been made, everyone knew what the answer would be. Only Mama might grow impatient.

“Psha! my dear,” the General said, “there’s time enough. Theo is barely seventeen; George is under forty. Besides, he must write to Virginia and wait for mamma’s reply.”

“But suppose she refuses?”

“That would be a sad day for us all,” the General replied. “Better to say—suppose she consents? I can’t imagine anyone refusing Theo anything she sets her heart on. And I’m sure her heart is set on this match.”

So they waited—eagerly, anxiously—until the letter from Madam Esmond arrived. And they trembled, fearing the packet-ship might fall prey to French privateers before it reached English shores.
