A short time after Colonel Altamont’s miraculous windfall at Epsom—which, let it be noted, was won not by skill but by sheer, dumb luck—the gallant gentleman embarked on his grand Continental tour. The society pages, ever eager to document the movements of the *ton*, reported his departure with typical understatement:

*"Among the distinguished company aboard the Soho to Antwerp last Saturday were Sir Robert, Lady, and the Misses Hodge; Mr. Serjeant Kewsy (who, we are assured, is every bit as dull as his title suggests); Mrs. and Miss Kewsy (who, alas, are no livelier); and Colonel Altamont, Major Coddy, & Co. (who, we suspect, drank the ship dry)."*

The Colonel traveled in the style befitting a gentleman—or at least, a gentleman who had recently come into money. His wardrobe was resplendent, his stomach impervious to the sea’s treachery (unlike poor Serjeant Kewsy, who reportedly turned "a shade of green not found in nature"), and his faithful Irish valet trotted behind him, looking as though he still couldn’t believe his master had two pennies to rub together.

Before setting sail, Altamont treated his old chum, Captain Strong, to a farewell feast at Blackwall—a feast paid for, naturally, by Altamont himself, now that fortune had smiled upon him.

"Strong, my boy," the Colonel bellowed between gulps of brandy, "if you’re strapped for cash, now’s your moment! I’m flush, I tell you—flush as a tick on a fat dog! Name your sum!"

Strong, with the dignity of a man who has borrowed too often to borrow again, declined. "No, no, my dear fellow—I’m perfectly solvent. Well, not *solvent* enough to repay you just yet, but solvent enough to scrape by without further humiliations."

They parted as friends—or as near to friendship as two men of their respective characters could manage. Later, Strong mused to himself:

*"Here’s a man who’s been a convict, a scoundrel, and a general liability—yet he’s ten times the gentleman Clavering is. Clavering wouldn’t know honesty if it bit him on the ankle. And what’s Altamont’s great crime? Signing his father-in-law’s name? Pah! Half the House of Lords has done worse before breakfast!"*

Still, Strong refused to dip into Altamont’s newfound generosity again. Some lines, once crossed, cannot be uncrossed—especially when one’s pride is the only thing left intact.

---

Meanwhile, back in Shepherd’s Inn, Strong found that life without his disreputable companion was… *quieter*. Too quiet. Gone were the days of shared brandy, shared schemes, and shared disgust at Clavering’s latest folly. Now, the Chevalier was left to his own devices—which, tragically, included *painting his own boots* and *broiling his own mutton chops*. A grim fate indeed for a man of his refined tastes.

He briefly considered reuniting with his estranged wife, the fiery Spanish Juanita (who had once brained him with a dish in a fit of conjugal enthusiasm), but thought better of it. Some wounds, even those of the heart, are best left un-reopened.

---

As for Sir Francis Clavering, his fortunes remained as dismal as ever. The "pony" he’d extracted from Altamont had vanished faster than a politician’s principles—lost on a dubious boxing match in Essex, where his chosen pugilist had the poor taste to *kill* his opponent in the thirteenth round.

"Damme, Strong!" Clavering wailed. "Three-to-one odds, and I was *certain* of thirty pounds! Now Lightfoot’s dunning me for fourteen, and my lady won’t part with a farthing. Can’t you find *somebody* to discount a bill for me?"

Strong, with the stern resolve of a man who has finally hit rock bottom (and brought a shovel), refused. "Not a penny, Sir Francis. I’ve sworn off your schemes—and if you try to swindle Lady Clavering again, I’ll be the first to warn her."

This noble declaration was met with a torrent of curses, a vow of eternal vengeance, and a slammed door. (Clavering, it must be said, had a flair for the dramatic when thwarted.)

---

Elsewhere in Shepherd’s Inn, young Sam Huxter—erstwhile walnut thief of Clavering, now aspiring medical man—was making a name for himself as Sir Francis’s latest admirer. He gushed about the Baronet’s "old English gentility," much to the amusement of Fanny Bolton, who knew better.

"*Borrowed* half a sovereign off you, did he?" Fanny laughed. "Mar’s been owed seven shillings since Whitsuntide, and he looks the other way when he sees her!"

Sam, ever the stickler for grammar, corrected her speech while Fanny corrected his judgment—an equitable arrangement, if ever there was one.

---

Reader, you may wonder: *Why all this philosophizing in a tale of rogues and rascals?*

But consider—when a man inherits wealth, does he not also inherit the sycophants? The tenants who once doffed caps to his father now doff them to him. The wine in his cellar improves with age, and so, presumably, does his self-importance.

And yet, let us not judge young Pendennis too harshly for enjoying his good fortune. Grief fades, hats turn from black to white, and life, inexorable life, marches on.

(Though if *you* ever find yourself broiling mutton chops in a garret, take heart—even the great Napoleon had his off days.)

---

Pen and Warrington, through thick and thin, remained inseparable—proof that true friendship, like good wine (or Strong’s brandy), only improves with time.
