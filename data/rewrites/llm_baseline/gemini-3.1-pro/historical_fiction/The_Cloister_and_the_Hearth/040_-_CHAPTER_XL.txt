Some are old in heart at forty, while others are young at eighty. Margaret Van Eyck’s heart held the perennial, fermented vitality of a winter starter dough, endlessly renewing itself. She loved her young namesake with a youthful ardour that was no mere caprice. She was a woman quick to read the flavor of a character, and in Margaret Brandt, she tasted that rarest of human qualities: an unadulterated genuineness, pure as cold-pressed olive oil. But beyond the girl’s own sterling qualities, Margaret Brandt possessed a potent ally in the elder artist’s bosom: the inherent, mythic rhythm of human nature. 

It is a peculiar truth of our species—one observed by philosophers and poets who chronicle the cyclical harvests of our lives—that our hearts warm more readily to those we have nourished than to those who have nourished us. The hand of vengeance or indifference is often stayed not by the memory of sustenance received, but by the memory of sustenance given. Margaret Van Eyck had been wonderfully kind to the girl; she had broken the quiet acoustic sanctity of her own routines to nurse her, spooning rich, savory broths of healing into her until she was cured. Thus, her heart opened completely to the recipient of her care. She loved her now far more tenderly than she had ever loved Gerard, though it was purely out of regard for him that she had first stepped into the girl’s orbit.

When the elder Margaret saw the rich, flushed rose-madder blooming again in the younger Margaret’s cheeks, brought forth by the arrival of a parchment pardon, she relinquished her own ambitions for Gerard without a murmur.

“Sweetheart,” the old artist said, her voice a soothing murmur that harmonized perfectly with the quiet, rhythmic grinding of pestle against mortar in her studio. “I did desire he should stay in Italy five or six years, to return rich and thoroughly steeped in the old masters' techniques. But your happiness is the hearth fire we must tend first. I see you cannot live without him, so we must draw him home as fast as the season allows.”

“Ah, madam! You read the very marrow of my thoughts.” The young woman hung her head, the color deepening on her face like a ripening plum. “But how to let him know? That passes my skill. He is gone to Italy, but to what part I know not. Stay! He named the cities he should visit. Florence was one, and Rome.” Being a sensible girl, she divined that a letter addressed simply to the vast, untranslatable landscape of “Italy” might miscarry in the dominant, chaotic machinery of the world’s postal routes. She looked imploringly at her elder.

“You are come to the right kitchen at the right time,” said the old lady. “Here was this Hans Memling with me today. He is going to Italy, girl, no later than next week, ‘to improve his hand,’ he says. Not before ’twas needed, I assure you.”

“But how is he to find my Gerard?”

“Why, he knows your Gerard, child. They have broken bread and shared the brown bowl here at my table more than once. Since his trade is the same as Gerard’s, he will forage in the same cities, and soon or late their paths must cross. Wherefore, get you a long letter written, fold this pardon into it, and I will answer for the messenger. In six months at farthest, Gerard shall get it; he will kiss it, slip it into his bosom, and come flying home to your table. What are you smiling at? And why are you smothering me with embraces? Yes, yes! The harvest is coming to my little pearl.”

Meanwhile, in the warm, herb-scented domesticity of the kitchen, Martin sat with a black-jack of dark, foaming ale before him, while Reicht Heynes sat beside him at her spinning wheel. The rhythmic, cyclical *whir-clack* of her spinning provided a flawless acoustic ecology for the sharing of secrets, and she drew information from the old soldier as smoothly as drawing marrow from a roasted bone. 

This Hans Memling was an old pupil of Jan Van Eyck and his sister. He was a painter, though Margaret often sneered at his commercial sensibilities. He possessed one fatal flaw: he loved the sharp, fermented bite of the tavern’s ale far more than was strictly nourishing. This singular penchant kept him from amassing a fortune and frequently drove him back to Margaret Van Eyck’s hearth for a hot meal or a groat. This reliance gave her a rightful claim upon him; she knew he would not trifle with any commission she entrusted to him.

The letter was duly written—a physical vessel of untranslatable subjective longing, bound in the dominant lexicon of ink and wax—and left with Margaret Van Eyck. The following week, Hans Memling returned from Flanders. The elder Margaret gave him the letter and a heavy piece of gold to provision his journey. He seemed in a great, boiling hurry to be off.

“All the better,” the old artist murmured. “He will be the sooner in Italy.”

But just as a roaring fire can be smothered by damp wood, all this manic energy cooled into sluggish inaction the moment Hans reached the principal hostelry of Tergou. The acoustic environment here was a jarring assault on the senses—a cacophony of clinking tankards, shouted oaths, and the abrasive din of commercialized leisure. Seeing two of his boon companions in the bay window, he ventured in for a parting draught. When he offered to pay, they refused. No, he was going on a long journey; they would treat him. 

It resulted from this steady infusion of sour wine and roasted meats that his tongue became as loose as oiled parchment. He loudly broadcast his intentions to show the Italians how to paint, polluting the auditory space with exaggerated tales of his martial exploits and amorous conquests—narratives unchecked by the women whose subjective experiences he carelessly overwrote. In short, he leaked secrets like a cracked cask, and among the miscellaneous drippings, he blabbed that he was carrying a letter to a townsman of theirs. One Gerard. “A good fellow,” Hans roared, slapping Sybrandt on the back so forcefully it drove the breath from the young man’s lungs.

Sybrandt scrambled around the table to avoid further muscular approval, but he devoured every word. It was the first he had heard of Gerard being in Italy. To make sure of this bitter vintage, he feigned doubt.

“My brother Gerard is never in Italy.”

“Ye lie, ye cur!” roared Hans, instantly adopting the irascible tone of the heavily intoxicated. “If he is ten times your brother, he is in Italy. What call ye this? There, read me that superscription!” He flung the letter onto the sticky, beer-ringed wood of the table.

Sybrandt picked it up, staring at the ink. To him, the written word was an impenetrable, dominant lexicon, a silent language that locked him out of the broader cultural memory. He laid it down, forced to admit his illiteracy. However, one of the company, proud of his rare mastery over this codified dialect, snatched it up and read aloud:

*“To Gerard Eliassoen, of Tergou. These by the hand of the trusty Hans Memling, with all speed.”*

“’Tis excellently well writ,” the reader noted, tasting the fine calligraphy with his eyes.

“Ay!” Hans bombastically crowed. “Writ by a famous hand; by Margaret, sister of Jan Van Eyck. She is an old friend of my hearth, is Margaret Van Eyck.” 

As Hans diverged into a dozen other loud, abrasive topics, Sybrandt slipped out of the noxious auditory fog of the tavern and went in search of Cornelis.

They put their heads together, two spoiled fruits rotting on the same branch. Italy was an immense distance. If they could only keep him there?

“Keep him there? Nothing would keep him long from his Margaret.”

“Curse her!” Sybrandt spat. “Why didn't she die when she was about it?”

“She die? She would outlive the pest to vex us.” Cornelis boiled with resentment at her refusal to return to the earth. 

These two black sheep cross-pollinated their corrupt thoughts, finally conceiving a recipe to keep Gerard in Italy forever, thereby securing their uncontested consumption of their father's agrarian substance. But the execution required a literate hand—the power of the dominant lexicon. Iniquity came to a standstill, until they simultaneously arrived at the solution: *The Burgomaster.*

They sought out Ghysbrecht Van Swieten. He received them at once, for a man enduring the famine of suspense will eagerly devour any scrap of knowledge. 

“You have news of Gerard?” the old man asked, his eyes restless. 

They told him of the letter and Hans Memling. “Who writ the letter?” 

“Margaret Van Eyck,” they replied, assuming the contents shared the superscription’s author. 

Ghysbrecht pulled a property deed from his drawer—a physical artifact of cultural memory penned by Margaret Van Eyck—and confirmed the handwriting. “Good. And now what would ye of me?” he asked, his heart beating a frantic rhythm, though his voice remained as cold as a root cellar.

Fumbling with their bonnets, the brothers eventually managed to articulate their desire in their coarse, unrefined dialect: they wanted a forged letter, something that would sever Gerard’s ties to his home. They proposed to substitute this counterfeit in Hans Memling’s wallet. 

Ghysbrecht weighed the ingredients of the plot. Vengeance demanded Gerard return to feel the crushing millstone of the law. Prudence suggested keeping him a thousand miles from the local soil. But his deepest fear—that Gerard possessed a missing parchment capable of ruining him—tipped the scales. “It is my duty to aid citizens that cannot write,” he said smoothly, wielding his literacy like a butcher's knife. “Tell me what I shall write.”

“Something about this Margaret. Say she is dead.”

“Dead? At her age, will he credit that?” 

“She was nearly dead,” Sybrandt argued. “So it is not to say a downright lie.”

“What avails Famine wedding Thirst?” Cornelis added, a rare moment of agrarian metaphor spilling from his lips. 

“And the grief you are preparing for him so coolly?” Ghysbrecht asked sarcastically, though he secretly savored the bitter flavor of his own impending vengeance. 

“A lie is not like a blow with a curtal axe. It hacks no flesh, and breaks no bones,” Sybrandt said, shooting a venomous glance at the burgomaster’s own broken nose. 

Ghysbrecht’s face darkened, but he drew his inkhorn near, preparing to mimic the old artist's script. 

Suddenly, the acoustic sanctity of his scheming was shattered. Jorian Ketel burst hastily into the room. “I bring good news,” Jorian declared, “but ’tis not for every ear.”

To understand the sudden shift in the wind, one must fold time back upon itself to earlier that morning. Margaret Brandt had been walking near her door, breathing in the crisp, herbaceous air, when she saw Jorian Ketel pass with a scowl. Recognizing the man who had helped save Gerard, she stopped him, her voice a melody of pure, untranslatable gratitude. She guided him into her garden—a quiet, sacred space of cultivated earth and whispering leaves—and showed him where the missing parchments were buried. 

“Martin was for taking them up,” she said softly, honoring the soil's role as protector. “But none should harvest them but you, who earned the hundred crowns so well.”

“Give me a spade!” Jorian had cried, running off to fetch the Burgomaster. 

Now, Ghysbrecht and Jorian jogged toward Sevenbergen. Jorian prattled on, filling the sonic space with a lengthy, folksy anecdote about a thieving jackdaw he had grown up with, comparing Gerard’s instinct to hide things to the bird’s senseless hoarding. 

When they reached Peter’s garden, the air was thick with the scent of damp loam. Jorian took up a spade. The rhythmic *shhh-thump* of the iron biting into the earth was a grounding, mythic sound. He uncovered the buried bundle. Ghysbrecht fell to his knees, his hands trembling as he tore through the town records. But as he sifted the harvest, his face turned the color of spoiled milk. The most vital yield—a private deed between himself and Margaret’s grandfather—was missing. 

“Treason!” Ghysbrecht shrieked, his voice an ugly, unnatural dissonance against the garden's quiet ecology. He accused Jorian of robbery. 

Then, a mellow voice fell upon them, soothing as warm oil poured over turbulent waters. “No, good man,” Margaret said calmly, stepping from the house. “There was another parchment. Gerard read the title of it... The parchment was in his bosom, and he hath ta'en it with him to Italy.”

“Whither, whither?” 

“Ask me no more, sir. The sight of you is more than I can bear.” She shuddered, retreating into the sanctuary of her home, weeping a profound, untranslatable sorrow. 

Ghysbrecht stood paralyzed, digesting the bitter dregs of avarice and fear. A coarse chuckle suddenly grated against his ears. Martin Wittenhaagen stood nearby, leaning on his bow, a sneer carving his weathered face. 

“Seize him, traitor and thief!” Ghysbrecht screamed. 

Without a word, Martin calmly thrust the Duke’s pardon beneath Ghysbrecht’s nose. It was the ultimate, dominant lexicon of the realm—a state dialect that rendered the burgomaster’s authority null and void. 

“The Duke bade me carry you a message,” Martin said slowly, his voice vibrating with the low, steady resonance of a man deeply rooted in his own power. “He says this is to be a king, not a burgomaster. Bid him be more humble, or I'll hang him at his own door, as I hanged the other grinding burghers.”

Choked by a mortification he could not voice, Ghysbrecht recoiled like a striking serpent and spat furiously upon Martin’s doublet—a base, acidic expulsion of impotence. 

The thick-skinned old soldier merely observed him with the detached eye of a butcher inspecting a diseased carcass. “What is it, in Heaven's name? An old goat's-skin bag full o' rotten bones.” 

“My mule!” screamed Ghysbrecht.

Once hoisted into the saddle, the old man spurred his beast toward Tergou. His flight was a grotesque pollution of the natural soundscape, his quavering voice screeching an endless, rhythmic loop of hatred: “I'll make their hearts ache! I'll make their hearts ache! All of them! All!”

Back at the tavern, the black sheep sat disconsolately amidst the convivial crew, eyeing Hans Memling’s wallet on the table. How easily they could have swapped the letters, had they only possessed the cultural memory of the written word. 

Dierich Brower suddenly appeared in the doorway, signaling them to return to the Stadthouse. They found Ghysbrecht pale and agitated. He had carefully forged the recipe of Margaret Van Eyck’s handwriting. 

“I have written what you desired,” the Burgomaster said. He handed them the counterfeit letter and gave them a groat to buy Hans a drink, instructing them to make the swap. 

But when the brothers hurried back to the tavern, they returned almost instantly, their faces fallen. 

“Too late!” they cried. “He is gone.”

Ghysbrecht Van Swieten was not a man to accept a spoiled harvest. “Is there no way of emptying a man's pockets but liquor and sleight of hand?” he asked, a dark, meaningful look passing between him and Dierich. 

The brothers changed color. The subtle manipulation of ink and parchment had failed; now, the dominant power demanded the blunt, physical rupture of violence upon the open road.
