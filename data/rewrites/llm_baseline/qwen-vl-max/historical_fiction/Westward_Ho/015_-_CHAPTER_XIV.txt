“Ignorance and evil, even in full flight, deal terrible backhanded strokes at their pursuers.”—HELPS.

It was no small peril, in those turbulent times, to journey from Plymouth toward northern Devon. Unless one chose a long detour, the route passed through the domain of a foreign and hostile chieftain—a man who had repeatedly raided English lands and bested royal forces under Queen Elizabeth’s rule. He was known, though never aloud, as the King of the Gubbings. “So now I dare call them,” wrote Fuller, “secured by distance, which one of more valor durst not do to their face, for fear their fury fall upon him. Yet hitherto have I met with none who could render a reason of their name. We call the shavings of fish (which are little worth) gubbings; and sure it is that they are sensible that the word importeth shame and disgrace.”

As for the suggestion of my learned friend Mr. Joseph Maynard—that those dwelling on the gibberous hills were called Gubbings—such a notion would amuse only those who reject truth for wit. I have read of an England beyond Wales, but the land of the Gubbings is a Scythia within England, and its people pure heathens. It lies near Brent. Legend says that two pregnant women fled there two centuries past, seeking hiding. To them came rough men, and thus began their tribe. They are a self-made people, free from bishop, archdeacon, or any lawful authority—civil or ecclesiastical. They live in hovels, scarcely houses, like swine, sharing all things, breeding without marriage into hundreds. Their speech is the dregs of Devonian dialect; the more learned a man, the less he can understand them. During the civil wars, no soldier was quartered among them, for fear of being lost in their midst. Their wealth is stolen goods—sheep taken from the moors. No search of their homes is ever made; such work is beneath a sheriff, beyond a constable. Swift as wind, they outrun horses; hardy as stone, they outlive most men; living in ignorance of luxury, which extinguishes life. They stand together like bees—offend one, and all will avenge him.

But now I am told they begin to change: they baptize their children, return to human ways, and become Christians again. I hope no civilized folk among us shall turn barbarians, while these barbarians grow civilized.

* * *

With this jest against the Anabaptists of his age, Fuller ends his tale. But I leave him to recount how Amyas, fearing these very Scythians and heathens, rode out of Plymouth on a fine horse, clad in full armor, lance and sword at hand, and two great horse-pistols strapped across his saddle. Behind him rode Salvation Yeo and five or six men from north Devon—those who had served with him in Ireland and now returned on furlough. All wore headpieces and quilted jerkins, each armed with pike and sword, Yeo with arquebus and match. Two sumpter ponies carried their baggage.

They pressed forward as fast as possible, through Tavistock, aiming to reach Lydford before nightfall. But delays in buying horses and other matters held them back until noon. By the time they reached the borderlands of the enemy’s territory, darkness had fallen. A bleak place it was, lit only by the dying glow of sunset. A high plateau of heath, bounded on the right by the jagged crags of Dartmooor, sloping southward and westward toward the base of Brent-Tor, a towering cone resembling an extinct volcano (as some say it truly is), crowned with a tiny church—a votive offering from a Plymouth merchant who once vowed to build a chapel to the Blessed Virgin upon the first stretch of English soil he saw after a desperate voyage.

Far down those barren slopes, thin blue wisps of smoke rose from the huts of the Gubbings. More than once the party halted, scanning distant furze-bushes and wandering ponies, wondering if they might be patrols of a hidden army. In our own century, we may laugh at such fears—but then, they were no joke. They soon found that out.

On the open downs stood a roadside inn: a grim, lichen-streaked lump of granite, windows patched with paper, thatch sagging and weighted down with stones and straw. Behind it sprawled a ramshackle courtyard of barns and walls, where pigs and barefoot children wallowed in shared filth. At the door stood the innkeeper—a burly, sullen man, eyes bleary, face swollen with drink, six feet of brutishness. One hand held up his hose, lacking points; the other scratched his matted hair, flecked with feathers. These feathers marked him: either he’d just risen from bed after a night of sheep-stealing, or he’d embraced the philosophy of Fridericus Dedekind and his disciple Dekker, who wrote: “Consider that as cobweb lawn, woven in May mornings, adorns mountain crests and valley bosoms; or as snowy fleece, stolen by briars from innocent sheep, becomes winter livery—so too, feathers scattered through your hair enhance you, set off your crown nobly. None shall accuse you of lying on straw or musty flock, for those feathers will rise to defend you, proving your bed was of softest down.” Thus did the feathers of Rogues’ Harbor Inn proclaim its master well-supplied with geese.

Suddenly he spied Amyas and his party cresting the hill. His ears pricked up. He counted them, saw Amyas’s armor, shook his head, grunted—and then, in his few words, bellowed:

“Mirooi!--Fushing pooale!”

A sturdy girl—clad only in a green bodice and red petticoat, neither wide enough nor long enough to hide her—brought forth his fishing rod and basket. The man tied his hose with bits of string, examined the footlink.

“Don vlies' gone!”

“May be,” said Mary. “Shouldn’t hay’ left mun out to coort. May be old hen’s ate mun off. I see her chocking about a while agone.”

The host cursed, struck her sharply on the head. She dodged, and returned the blow with good effect. Then, accustomed to such exchanges, he shuffled off, howling:

“Tell Patrico!”

Mary darted inside, combed her hair, slipped on stockings and her best gown, and waited for guests. The travelers frowned at the “mucksy sort of a place,” yet preferred it to camping near the enemy.

The old hen who had swallowed the dun fly was killed, plucked, and roasted. Black Dartmoor mutton went onto the gridiron—only to reveal itself, under fiery torment, as venison. Meanwhile, Amyas secured his horse and ponies in a shed with no lock or key. Grumbling, he returned, uneasy for his steed’s safety. Baggage piled in a corner; Amyas stretched his legs before a turf fire. Yeo, suspicious of the place, posted himself at the door. The men, drawn by the cooking, likely because Mary was the cook, gathered round.

Then Yeo returned.

“There’s a gentleman coming up, sir, all alone.”

“Ask him to join us,” said Amyas. “With my compliments.”

Yeo went out, returned in five minutes.

“He’s gone in by the back court, sir.”

“Well, he has odd taste, if he makes himself at home here.”

Yeo left again, returning after another five minutes—excited.

“Come out, sir! For goodness’ sake come out. I’ve got him. Safe as a rat in a trap!”

“Who?”

“A Jesuit, sir.”

“Nonsense, man!”

“I tell you truth, sir. I went round the house—he didn’t look right. I knew him the moment he stepped up. The way he turned his toes inward, placed his feet so still and careful, as if afraid of offending God at every step. So I peered between the wall and gate—saw him knock at the back door, call ‘Mary!’ quiet as a priest. The girl flies out, ready to eat him alive. ‘Go away,’ I heard her say, ‘there’s a dear man.’ Then something about a ‘queer cuffin’—a justice in thieves’ talk. Then he pulled out a thing—I swear it was a Popish Agnus. Gave it her. She kissed it, crossed herself, asked if she did it right. Put it in her bosom. He said, ‘Bless you, my daughter.’ That sealed it. Then he slipped into the stable, peered in. Seeing no one, he went in. I followed, shut the door, blocked it with a cart, sent a man to watch. The girl’s crying like mad.”

“What a fool’s trick, man! How do you know he’s not some honest gentleman?”

“Fool or not, sir—honest gentlemen don’t give maidens Agnuses. I’ve got him. If you want him freed, you’ll have to do it yourself. My conscience won’t let me release the Lord’s enemies. It’s written: ‘If any let one of them go, his life shall be for the life of him.’”

Amyas hurried out with him. He pulled back the cart, opened the door, and began apologizing—to his cousin Eustace.

There he stood, face half foolish, half venomous, like Reynard when the last spadeful of earth falls back and he’s caught sitting disconsolate on his tail, mere yards from the terriers’ snouts.

Neither spoke for a moment. Then Amyas:

“Well, cousin hide-and-seek, how long have you added horse-stealing to your other trades?”

“My dear Amyas,” said Eustace, meekly, “I may surely enter an inn stable without meaning to steal what’s inside.”

“Of course, old fellow,” said Amyas, mollified. “I was jesting. But what brings you here? Not prudence, certainly.”

“I am bound to know no prudence save for the Lord’s work.”

“That’s giving Agnus Dei and deceiving poor heathen girls, I suppose,” said Yeo.

Eustace replied sharply:

“Heathens? Yes, truly. You Protestants leave these wretches heathens, then persecute those who risk their lives to convert them. Mr. Amyas Leigh, you may hang me at Exeter if you wish to disgrace your family. But from this spot, neither you nor all your queen’s soldiers shall drive me while a soul remains unsaved.”

“Come out of the stable, at least,” said Amyas. “You don’t want to make the horses Papists too, do you? Come out, man, and go to the devil your own way. I shan’t inform on you. And Yeo will keep silent if I tell him.”

“It goes sorely against my conscience, sir—but since he’s your cousin…”

“Of course. Now come in and eat. Supper’s ready. Let bygones be bygones.”

How much forgiveness Eustace felt, I cannot say. But he knew he ought to forgive. Eating with Amyas was an act of grace, and a means to further the Church’s cause. Motives and actions correct—what more was needed? So he entered. Yet he never forgot the scar on his cheek. And whenever Amyas looked at him, Eustace feared his gaze lingered on the mark, peering from under his lashes to see if triumph gleamed in those honest eyes.

They talked over the venison, cautiously at first. But Amyas’s warmth thawed Eustace’s frozen heart. Soon they recalled old haunts, boyhood memories—uncles, aunts, cousins. Without malice, Eustace asked why Amyas was going to Bideford, while Frank and his mother were in London.

“To tell you the truth,” said Amyas, “I cannot rest until I hear the whole story about poor Rose Salterne.”

“What about her?” cried Eustace.

“Do you not know?”

“How should I know? For heaven’s sake, what has happened?”

Amyas told him, surprised by his eagerness—for he’d never suspected Eustace’s love.

Eustace shrieked:

“Fool, fool that I have been! Caught in my own trap! Villain, villain that he is! After all he promised me at Lundy!”

He rose, stamped, gnashed his teeth, tossed his head, clawed at empty air—the gesture of despair that seeks blindly for what is lost forever.

Amyas sat stunned. His first impulse was to ask: “Lundy? What did you know of him? What business had you there?” But pity overcame curiosity.

“Oh, Eustace! And you loved her too?”

“Don’t speak to me! Loved her? Yes, sir. I had as good a right to love her as any of your precious Brotherhood of the Rose. Don’t speak to me, or I’ll do you harm!”

So Eustace knew of the brotherhood too! Amyas longed to ask how—but what use? If he knew, he knew. So he said:

“My good cousin, why be angry with me? If you truly love her, now is the time to counsel with me how best we may—”

Eustace cut him short. Conscious of betrayal, he stopped mid-stride, collected himself, and eyed Amyas from beneath his brows with cold disdain.

“How best we shall do what, my valiant cousin?” he asked, voice half-scornful. “What does your noble Brotherhood of the Rose propose in such a case?”

Amyas, slightly nettled, stood firm.

“What the Brotherhood intends, I cannot yet say. What it ought to do, I have a fair guess.”

“So have I. To hunt her down like an outlaw, because she dared love a Catholic. To murder her lover in her arms, drag her home stained with his blood, force her to renounce the Church into whose maternal arms she has doubtless found peace and holiness!”

“If she has found holiness, it matters little to me where she found it, Master Eustace. But that is precisely what I wish to know.”

“And you will go discover it yourself?”

“Have you no desire to discover it too?”

“And if I had, what would that matter to you?”

“Only,” said Amyas, striving to control his temper, “that if we share the same purpose, we might sail in the same ship.”

“You intend to sail, then?”

“I mean simply that we might work together.”

“Our paths lie on different roads, sir!”

“I am afraid you never spoke truer. In the meantime, before we part, please tell me—what did you mean by saying you met this Spaniard at Lundy?”

“I shall refuse to answer.”

“You will remember, Eustace, that however close friends we’ve been this half-hour, you are in my power. I have a right to know the truth—and by heaven, I will know it.”

“In your power? See that you are not in mine! Remember, sir, you are within miles of those who obey me, their Catholic benefactor, but owe no allegiance to Protestant authorities who have left them to the beasts which perish.”

Amyas grew furious. He almost seized Eustace, shook him, handed him over to Yeo. But he knew it would bring death and dishonor. Remembering Frank’s conduct at Clovelly, he held back.

“Take me,” said Eustace, “if you will. You, who complain we Catholics keep no faith with heretics, will recall that you invited me in as your guest—and in your good faith, I trusted you.”

A weak argument in law—Eustace had been a prisoner before a guest, and Amyas guilty of misprision of treason. Still, all he did was walk to the door, open it, bow, and bid his cousin depart, since he seemed resolved to end his days in hell.

And Eustace vanished.

“Pooh!” muttered Amyas. “I can learn enough, and too much, without help from such crooked vermin. I must see Cary. I must see Salterne. And if I’m ready to do my duty, I shall learn what it is. Now to sleep. Tomorrow, up and away—wherever God sends.”

“Come in here, men!” he shouted down the hall. “Sleep here. Haven’t you had enough of this sour cider?”

The men yawned, settled on the floor.

“Where’s Yeo?”

No one knew. He’d gone out to pray and hadn’t returned.

“Never mind,” said Amyas, suspecting a plot. “He’ll take care of himself.”

“No fear of that, sir,” the men replied. Soon they snored in unison around the fire, while Amyas lay on the settle, saddle for a pillow.

* * * * *

At midnight, Amyas leapt—no, fell—backwards, overturning saddle, settle, table, convinced ten thousand dragons burst through the window, howling fiercely. But the dragons were merely terrified geese, flapping and screaming around the house. The noise that startled them did not cease. Another minute proved a sharp fight raged in the courtyard—and Yeo was shouting for help.

The men rushed out, swords drawn, burst the back door, stumbled over buckets, into the yard. There, back against the stable door, Yeo fought bravely with sword and buckler against a dozen men.

Screams echoed—geese, chickens, pigs, donkeys, Mary from an upper window. Even plovers, drawn by the din, wheeled overhead, adding their cries to the chaos.

Yet the fight ended. As Amyas charged into the yard, the ruffians fled over a low hedge.

“Are you hurt, Yeo?”

“Not a scratch, thank Heaven! But I’ve got two of them—the leaders. One’s against the wall. Your horse finished the other.”

The wounded man was lifted. A huge brute, nearly Amyas’s size. Yeo’s sword pierced his body. He groaned, choked.

“Carry him in. Where’s the other?”

“Dead as a herring, in the straw. Have care, men—horses are near mad!”

After a while, the body was brought out. No pulse, no breath.

“Carry him in too. Poor wretch. Now, Yeo—what happened?”

Yeo’s tale was quick. He couldn’t shake the idea—though unfounded—that Eustace meant to steal the horses. He’d seen the innkeeper slip away. Expecting attack, he’d slept in the stable.

As expected, men tried to enter. The door opened—how, he couldn’t guess, for he’d locked it. Two men came in, began untying the horses. Yeo seized the big one, who drew a knife. He broke free. Horses panicked, kicked. One man fell, the other ran, calling for help—Yeo pursued.

“Seeing a dozen more with clubs and bows,” said Yeo, “I thought best to shorten their number. I ran the rascal through and stood on guard. Just in time, too—two arrows in the wall, three in my buckler. I grabbed it as I left—it was hanging by the door, ready.”

Hardly had they entered the back-kitchen when fresh shouts arose. Amyas rushed forward, banging his head on the doorway. Through the flashes in his eyes, he saw an old acquaintance—held by two sailors.

One arm in doublet sleeve, the other in a dirty shirt; holding up his hose with one hand, candle in the other—lighting his own confusion. Foaming with rage stood Mr. Evan Morgans, alias Father Parsons, looking, as Yeo put it, “the very moral of a half-plucked turkey-cock.” Behind him stood Eustace Leigh.

“We found the maid letting these two out by the front door,” said one captor.

“Well, Mr. Parsons,” said Amyas, “and what are you doing here? A fine nest of thieves and Jesuits we’ve uncovered tonight.”

“About my calling, sir,” said Parsons firmly. “By your leave, I shall prepare this wounded lamb for the account to which your man’s cruelty has untimely sent him.”

The wounded man, lying on the floor, heard Parsons’ voice, moaned for the “Patrico.”

“You see, sir,” said Parsons, pompously, “the sheep know their shepherd’s voice.”
