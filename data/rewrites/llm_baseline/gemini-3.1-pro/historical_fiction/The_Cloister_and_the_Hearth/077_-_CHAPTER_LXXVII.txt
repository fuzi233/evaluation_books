The rhythmic wooden clatter of spoons against earthenware bowls—the ancestral heartbeat of the little family’s dinner at Tergou—was abruptly shattered. A jarring, breathless dissonance tore through the domestic sanctuary as Luke Peterson burst through the door, his boots scattering the fine layer of hearth-ash and flour that coated the floorboards. 

“Good people, Mistress Catherine is wanted instantly at Rotterdam,” he gasped, the sharp, panicked cadence of his voice completely upending the quiet acoustic ecology of the room, where just moments before, the only sounds had been the comforting simmer of a rich, root-vegetable pottage and the low hum of familial contentment.

“My name is Catherine, young man,” the matriarch said, her hands still dusted with the rye flour of the afternoon’s baking. “Kate, it will be Margaret.”

“Ay, dame. She said to me, ‘Good Luke, hie thee to Tergou, and ask for Eli the hosier, and pray his wife Catherine to come to me, for God His love.’ I didn't wait for daylight.”

Catherine sprang up, the deep agrarian instincts of a woman who understood the sacred alchemy of nourishment instantly activated. “What is his trouble?”

“Nay, I know not. But it has been peaking and pining worse and worse this while.” 

A furtive rustle of clothing and a low, muted exhalation of breath passed between Cornelis and Sybrandt—a dissonance Catherine mercifully did not hear. Her focus was on her husband. “Now, Eli, if you say a word against it, you and I shall quarrel.”

“Who gainsays thee, foolish woman? Quarrel with your own shadow, while I go borrow Peter's mule for ye,” Eli rumbled, his voice a steady, grounding bass note beneath her frantic tenor. 

She packed her bundles with meticulous care—dried fennel, wrapped crocks of clarified butter, and the deeply ingrained, unwritten medicinal knowledge of generations. The cart ride to Rotterdam was a harsh transition. As they approached the port, the restorative, wind-swept quiet of Tergou was swallowed by the chaotic, metallic screech of urban industry: the clinking of foreign coins, the grinding of commercial mills, and the abrasive shouts of merchants. It was a barren sonic landscape, completely devoid of the natural rhythms of life.

Margaret met her at the door. The young mother was a hollow-eyed ghost, her physical form withered, her voice a reedy, despairing whisper that lacked the resonance of health. Catherine took one look at the failing infant in Margaret’s arms, then at the mother. The diagnosis required no written parchment; it was inscribed in the pallor of the skin and the sour, metallic scent that hung in the room.

“Lucky you sent for me,” Catherine said, her voice dropping into the firm, commanding dialect of the old world. “The child is poisoned.”

“Poisoned! By whom?” 

“By you. You have been fretting.”

“Nay, indeed, mother. How can I help fretting?”

Catherine moved to the hearth, immediately analyzing the cold, neglected cooking pots. “A nursing mother has no business to fret. She must turn her mind away from her grief to the comfort that lies in her lap. Know you not that the child pines if the mother vexes herself? Your milk is steeped in the bitter tang of your sorrow. This comes of your reading and writing, Margaret. Those idle, sterile crafts befit a man; they belong to the dominant lexicon of parchment and politics, but they keep all useful, embodied knowledge out of a woman. They drown out the old language of sustenance. The child must be weaned.”

“Oh, you cruel woman!” Margaret cried, her voice a sharp, jagged frequency that caused the infant to wince. “Would you rob me of the only bit of comfort I have in the world? A-nursing my Gerard, I forget I am the most unhappy creature beneath the sun.”

“That you do not,” Catherine retorted, beginning to build a fire, restoring the crackling, vital sound of the hearth. “Or he would not be the way he is. Your grief is an untranslatable dialect to this boy, and you are feeding it to him.”

Margaret wept, but she yielded to the unyielding truth of Catherine's agrarian wisdom. To Catherine's eyes, the survival of the bloodline required immediate, sensory intervention. On her way into the city, Catherine’s sharp ears had picked up the low, soothing, rhythmic lullaby of a healthy young woman sitting outside a nearby door. Catherine went to her, finding a woman whose very presence radiated the rich, olfactory warmth of sun-baked hay and sweet cream. 

Catherine explained the case in the fluid, unpretentious cadence of their shared provincial tongue. The young woman smiled, laid her own plump child in a wooden cradle, and followed Catherine. 

When the wet nurse, Joan, took the failing boy into her arms, she purred over him—a deep, resonant vibration in her chest that instantly calmed the infant's frantic, reedy cries. She offered her breast, and the boy, starving for untainted nourishment, latched on. Margaret, entirely paralyzed by the sight of another woman providing the fundamental sustenance she could not, burst into a discordant wail.

“What is to do?” Joan asked, looking up. “Wife, ye told me not the mother was unwilling.”

“She is not; she is only a fool,” Catherine said harshly, prioritizing the physical reality of the child’s survival over the mother’s emotional collapse. “You are robbing her own to save yours, Margaret, and you can think of nothing better than bursting out a-blubbering in the woman's face. Out fie, for shame!”

“Nay, wife,” Joan said, her voice dropping into the familiar, rolling vowels of Tergou. “Thank Heaven, I have enough rich milk for my own and for hers to boot. Maybe the troubles o' life ha' soured her own.”

“And her heart into the bargain,” Catherine added.

Margaret turned her face to the wall, unable to bear the erosion of her motherly identity, watching her son thrive on a new maternal dialect. Yet, as the wet nurse spoke, a bridge of cultural memory was revealed. Joan was a townswoman of Tergou, married to humble folk, carrying within her the same ancestral lexicon of domestic survival as Catherine. The bond of their shared native dialect created an acoustic haven of safety for the child, shutting out the oppressive industrial noise of Rotterdam.

Over the next weeks, under the nourishment of Joan's sweet milk and Catherine's restorative broths of simmered marrow and crushed barley, the boy recovered his vigor. Then came the next mythic threshold of his life: teething. To Catherine and Margaret, the emergence of the boy’s tiny teeth was a monumental shift in his culinary evolution. 

“Teeth, mother?” Margaret breathed, her voice finally softening into a harmonious register as she watched the boy gnaw on a wooden spoon. “I call them not teeth, but pearls of pearls.”

Each white ridge that pierced the red gums was celebrated with a shriek of triumph, a joyous echo that filled the house. These pearls meant the boy would soon partake in the ancient, agrarian traditions of solid sustenance—the crushed spelt, the roasted apples, the thick, life-giving porridges of their ancestors. 

During this time, Catherine gossiped with Joan and uncovered a vital thread in their interwoven tapestry. Joan was the wife of Jorian Ketel of Tergou, formerly a servant to Ghysbrecht Van Swieten. Jorian had fallen out of favor and returned to his native Rotterdam to work as a sexton and carpenter. Joan hinted, her voice hushed beneath the rhythmic thud of her rocking chair, that her husband knew the truth behind Gerard’s mysterious disappearance, but that he was tightly closed off, retreating into silence.

Catherine, ever the matriarch seeking to restore her family’s broken circle, visited Jorian. She found him surrounded by the harsh, grating sounds of his carpentry—saws biting into wood, a violent, industrial noise that masked his internal bitterness. Catherine attempted to probe him, questioning if the burgomaster held the secrets to her lost son. Jorian merely snorted, his answers short, sullen, and impenetrable. He weaponized his silence, a stark power dynamic leveraging his withheld knowledge against the women's desperate inquiries. 

“Think you the burgomaster takes such as me into his secrets?” he grumbled, his voice a defensive rasp. When Catherine pressed him, he shut down entirely, becoming as prickly and guarded as a hedgehog.

Driven by the agony of not knowing, Margaret took her son in her arms and went to Jorian's door just before sunset, stepping into his wood-shaving-scented workshop. But at the sight of her, Jorian rose, his face hardening. Without a word, he burst out of the house, letting the heavy door slam shut—a violent acoustic rejection.

Joan, watching from the hearth, spoke over the crackling fire. “You must excuse him. He lays it to your door that he is a poor man instead of a rich one. It is something about a piece of parchment. One was missing, and he got nought from the burgomaster all along of that.”

Margaret’s heart sank. Once again, the written word—that cold, dominant lexicon of power and commerce—had poisoned the organic bonds between people. Jorian’s lost fortune, tied to Gerard's desperate actions, stood as an untranslatable barrier of resentment between them.

Recognizing the limits of her influence, Catherine returned to Tergou after six weeks, leaving Margaret to navigate the circular rhythms of her new life. 

Soon after came the child's birthday. Margaret set aside all labor. She baked a small, dense loaf of ceremonial bread, rich with honey and seeds, filling the air with the deep, grounding aroma of toasted grain. Carrying her thriving boy, she walked to the church. Stepping out of the abrasive, commercialized din of Rotterdam's streets, she entered the vast, echoing stone sanctuary. The acoustic shift was profound—a sudden, sweeping silence that invited the divine. She knelt and prayed long and fervently for Gerard's safe return, her spoken vernacular rising into the vaulted ceiling.

And in that precise, mythic moment, transcending the vast geographical expanse, Father Clement stood before the altar in the minster church at Basle. He lifted the communion bread—the ultimate symbol of spiritual sustenance—and chanted a mass in the sonorous, ancient Latin tongue. He prayed for the departed soul of his Margaret. 

Their voices, bound by blood, bread, and memory, vibrated simultaneously across the interwoven timelines of their shattered world, two halves of a profound, untranslatable love echoing blindly through the ether.
