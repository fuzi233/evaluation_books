Within four-and-twenty hours of that little garden business, Madame made me smile again—nay, laugh, and afterwards taste a bitterness as sharp as vinegar on the tongue.

Villette can turn in a night, as a kitchen turns when a draught catches the flame. After a mild sunset came a night of loud wind; the next day kept the same temper—dry storm, no rain, a sky like lead, and the streets blurred with sand and powdered grit whirled off the boulevards. Even had the weather been soft as warmed milk, it would not have tempted me back to my usual evening nook amongst the shrubs. My alley, and all the walks, had taken on a new interest—not pleasant, but watchful. That casement which had rained billets had vulgarised the once dear quiet it overlooked; the flowers seemed to have learned eyes; the knots in the tree-boles had grown ears.

Some plants Dr. John had trodden down in his hurried search. I could not bear to leave them so. At dawn, before common eyes were abroad, I propped a bruised stalk, settled loosened earth with my fingers, and gave water as carefully as if I had been basting a delicate joint—drop by drop, not to drown, only to revive. I smoothed away his footmarks too; the strong wind had begun to worry the borders, yet there was a moment’s leisure to efface what I wished forgotten.

So, with a pensive content, I sat at my desk and my German while the pupils composed themselves to the evening lessons, and the other teachers drew out their needlework. Our “étude du soir” was always held in the refectory—a smaller room than the classes; only boarders entered there, and they were about a score. Two lamps hung low over the tables. At dusk Rosine kindled them, and that lighting was like the striking of a bell in a convent kitchen: books shut, chairs drew in, a grave air put on, and silence laid like a lid.

Then began “la lecture pieuse.”

I had soon discovered its aim: not to feed the mind, but to fast it; to take the reason by the nape and press it down; to set before common sense a mess she must swallow and digest as she could. The book—never changed, only recommenced when finished—was a venerable old volume, grey as the Hôtel de Ville, old as the hills. I would have paid two francs merely to hold it, to turn the yellow, thick leaves, to read for myself what my ears were forced to drink. For drink it one did—like a hot draught poured down when you are not thirsty.

It was legends of saints. God forgive me, I say it reverently, but what legends! If those saints first boasted the feats, they must have been gasconading; if they did not, then monkish fingers invented them. Some extravagances one could laugh at inwardly; but with them were priestly matters—priestcraft—worse than the monkery: tales of moral martyrdom inflicted by Rome; confessor’s boasts, wickedly abusing an office to trample high-born women into the dust, turning countess and princess into tormented slaves. Again and again came stories like Conrad and Elizabeth of Hungary, with their black impiety, their tyranny, their nightmare privations. My temples would burn as if a tight band were drawn round them; my heart beat against my ribs; my wrist throbbed. More than once I broke the points of my scissors by driving them, without knowing, into the worm-eaten board.

For some nights I bore it as quietly as I could. At last I found that if I stayed, my sleep afterwards was broken with feverish excitement, like a room where a machine has been left running and you cannot get the humming out of your head. Prudence counselled flight. No Mause Headrigg ever felt a stronger call to testify against Sergeant Bothwell than I to speak my mind against this popish “lecture pieuse.” Yet I curbed myself. When Rosine came to light the lamps, in that little stir before the enforced hush, I rose and vanished—quickly, but with no noise—while the boarders put away their books.

When I vanished, it was into darkness. Candles were not carried about. The teacher who left the refectory had only the unlit hall, the empty schoolrooms, or the dormitory for refuge. In winter I paced the long classes to keep warm, grateful if the moon gave a little silver; and if there were only starlight, I learned to see by it; and if there was nothing, I endured the blank as best I could. In summer it was never wholly dark. Then I would climb to my quarter of the long dormitory—lit by five tall casements like great doors—open my own, and lean out upon the city beyond the garden. From the park or palace-square band-music would drift, thin and sweet, then smothered as a gust pushed it away—music always fighting the town’s harsher sounds: wheels over stone, distant voices, the thin whip-crack of modern hurry. Those coarse noises, multiplying year by year as if the streets bred them, did something to one’s spirit; they scraped at thought the way grit scrapes a pan if you scour it too hard. Yet between blasts I could catch a clarinet phrase or a horn-call, and it was as if some older, gentler Villette tried to speak through the new clangour. I would listen and think my own thoughts, living my own life in that still, shadow-world where words—whether French or English—never quite named what the heart knew.

This evening, fugitive as usual before the Pope and his works, I mounted the stairs, reached the dormitory, and opened the door, which, like every door in that house, revolved noiselessly on well-oiled hinges.

Before I saw, I felt life in the great room usually void—not by any stir or breath, but by the absence of true solitude. All the white beds—the “lits d’ange,” poetically called—lay in a row and were empty; no sleeper was there. Yet a sound touched the ear: a drawer, slid out with caution.

I stepped aside. The curtains did not hinder my sight now. I commanded my own bed and my own toilet: the work-box on the bed, the drawers beneath—locked. Or so they ought to be.

A dumpy, motherly little figure stood at my table, in a decent shawl and the cleanest nightcap imaginable, labouring at my “meuble” as if doing me a kindness. The lid of my work-box stood open. The top drawer gaped. Each drawer in turn was opened; each paper glanced over; each small box unlidded; each folded thing lifted, unfolded, refolded. Beautiful was the adroitness; exemplary the care. Madame wrought like a star—unhasting, yet unresting.

I watched her with secret glee. Had I been a gentleman, I think I should have liked Madame the better for the neat, compact way she did everything. Some people vex you with loose awkwardness; she satisfied you by trim thoroughness. I stood fascinated—yet knew I must break the spell and retreat. If she turned and caught me, there would be a scene; conventionalities would go down at one blow, disguises swept away, and we should look into each other’s eyes and know we could not work together more. What was the use of tempting catastrophe?

I was not angry. I had no wish to leave her. Another employer might lay on a heavier yoke; hers, for all its craft, sat light. Besides, her system did me no harm. Loverless, and expecting no love, I was safe from spies in my heart-poverty as a beggar is safe from thieves in his empty purse.

So I turned and fled—down the stairs swiftly and soundlessly, like a spider running the bannister.

When I reached the schoolroom, I laughed—laughed outright. Now I understood: she had seen Dr. John in the garden; she had fed her suspicions and set them to work in my drawers. The spectacle of a suspicious nature misled by its own inventions tickled me.

But when the laughter died, wrath smote; then came bitterness, as if one had struck a rock and drawn out water—Meribah’s waters, not sweet. For an hour my heart was shared between soreness and mirth, fire and grief. I wept hot tears—not because Madame mistrusted me (I cared not twopence for her mistrust), but for other reasons, tangled and disquieting. Thoughts broke up my inward repose, as wind breaks the smooth skin of broth in a pot, showing the turmoil beneath.

By next day the tempest had sunk: I was Lucy Snowe again.

Revisiting my drawers, I found them all locked. The closest examination could not discover a change. My few dresses were folded as I had left them. Between the folds of my best dress lay a little bunch of white violets—once silently presented by a stranger (a stranger to me, for we had never exchanged words), dried and kept for its faint, persistent perfume. They lay unstirred. My black silk scarf, my lace chemisette and collars, were unrumpled. Had she creased one solitary article I should have found forgiveness harder; but finding all straight and orderly, I said, Let bygones be bygones. I am unharmed: why bear malice?

Yet a riddle troubled me as obstinately as hunger troubles the body. How had Dr. John—if he were not accessory to the dropping of that casket—known it was dropped, and appeared so promptly to seek it? The wish to clear this point grew daring. I began to think: Why may I not, if I ever have the opportunity, ask Dr. John himself?

So long as Dr. John stayed away, I believed I had courage.

Little Georgette was convalescent. Her physician’s visits grew rare; he would have ceased them altogether, had not Madame insisted on an occasional call till the child was quite well. One evening Madame entered the nursery just after I had listened to Georgette’s lisped prayer and put her to bed. Taking the little hand, she said, “Cette enfant a toujours un peu de fièvre.” Then, with a quicker glance than usual, “Le Docteur John l’a-t-il vue dernièrement? Non, n’est-ce pas?”

Of course she knew better than any one.

“Well,” she pursued, “I am going out, pour faire quelques courses en fiacre. I shall call on Dr. John, and send him to the child. I will that he sees her this evening; her cheeks are flushed, her pulse is quick; you will receive him—for my part, I shall be from home.”

The child was well enough; only warmed with July. It was scarcely more needful to send for a doctor than for a priest with extreme unction. Besides, Madame rarely made “courses” in the evening; and this was the first time she chose to be absent on the occasion of Dr. John’s visit. The whole arrangement smelled of plan—like a dish too artfully seasoned, where you detect the cook’s intent. I saw it plainly, and felt not the least anxiety.

“Ha! ha! Madame,” laughed Light-heart the Beggar within me, “your crafty wits are on the wrong tack.”

She departed, dressed smartly, in a costly shawl and a chapeau vert tendre—hazardous tint for any complexion less fresh than her own, but to her not unbecoming. I wondered what she intended: whether she truly would send Dr. John, whether he would come, whether he might be engaged.

Madame had charged me not to let Georgette sleep till he came. So I occupied the time with nursery tales, and with that soft palaver of baby-language which makes words into food a child can swallow. I was fond of Georgette. To hold her warm little body on my lap, to carry her in my arms, was a treat—like a draught of clean water where the house offered little feeling. Tonight she begged me to lay my head on the pillow of her crib; she even put her arms round my neck. The clasp, the nestling press of her cheek to mine, made me almost cry with a tender pain. It was too sweet, that pure little drop from a pure source; it sank deep, subdued the heart, and sent a gush to the eyes.

Half an hour passed, perhaps an hour. Georgette murmured she was growing sleepy.

“And you shall sleep,” I thought, “malgré maman and médecin, if they are not here in ten minutes.”

Hark—there was the bell; and then a tread on the stairs, quick as if the steps were startled by the speed. Rosine introduced Dr. John; and with a freedom not peculiar to her alone, but common amongst Villette domestics, she stayed to hear what he had to say. Madame’s presence would have awed her away to vestibule and cabinet; mine, or any teacher’s, she cared not a jot for.

Smart, trim, pert, she stood with a hand in each pocket of her gay grisette apron, eyeing Dr. John as if he were a picture and she had paid to look at it.

“Le marmot n’a rien n’est ce pas?” she said, jerking her chin towards Georgette.

“Pas beaucoup,” he answered, scribbling a harmless prescription with his pencil.

“Eh bien!” Rosine pursued, stepping quite near while he put up the pencil. “And the box—did you get it? Monsieur went off like a coup-de-vent the other night; I had not time to ask him.”

“I found it: yes.”

“And who threw it, then?” continued Rosine—speaking with all the ease the very question I had longed to ask and had not the address to bring out. How short some people make a road that for others is a mountain.

“That may be my secret,” said Dr. John briefly, with no hauteur. He seemed to understand Rosine’s kind.

“Mais enfin,” she persisted, unabashed. “Monsieur knew it was thrown, since he came to seek it—how did he know?”

“I was attending a little patient in the college near,” he replied, “and saw it dropped out of his chamber window, and so came to pick it up.”

So simple. The note had spoken of a physician examining “Gustave.” My riddle unlocked itself with a click, and all the fine suspicions fell apart like pastry cut too thin.

“Ah ça!” Rosine went on; “il n’y a donc rien là-dessous: pas de mystère, pas d’amourette, par exemple?”

“Pas plus que sur ma main,” said he, showing his palm.

“Quel dommage!” she returned. “Et moi—à qui tout cela commençait à donner des idées.”

“Vraiment! vous en êtes pour vos frais,” was his cool rejoinder.

She pouted. He laughed at her “moue,” and when he laughed there was something so genial and good-natured in his look that one heard it, almost, like a kinder note in a harsh soundscape. I saw his hand incline to his pocket.

“How many times have you opened the door for me within this last month?” he asked.

“Monsieur ought to have kept count of that,” said Rosine readily.

“As if I had not something better to do!” he retorted; but he gave her a piece of gold. She took it without scruple, and danced off to answer the bell again, which rang every five minutes as servants came for the half-boarders.

Do not think too hardly of Rosine. On the whole she was not bad; she simply had no notion there could be disgrace in grasping what she could get, or effrontery in chattering like a pie to the best gentleman in Christendom.

From that scene I learned something besides the explanation of the ivory box: not on the robe de jaconas—pink or grey—nor on the frilled, pocketed apron, lay the blame of breaking Dr. John’s heart. Those items of array were as guiltless as Georgette’s little blue tunic. So much the better. But who, then, was the culprit? What was the origin, the perfect explanation of the whole business? Some points cleared, but how many remained obscure as night.

“However,” I told myself, “it is no affair of yours.” And turning from Dr. John’s face—on which I had been unconsciously dwelling with a questioning gaze—I looked out of the window upon the garden.

He stood by the bedside, slowly drawing on his gloves, watching his little patient as her eyes closed and her rosy lips parted in sleep. I waited for him to go, as usual, with a quick bow and scarce-articulate “good-night.”

Just as he took his hat, my eyes, fixed on the tall houses bounding the garden, saw the one lattice, already commemorated, cautiously open. A hand projected—white—and a white handkerchief. Both waved. I know not whether some viewless quarter of our own dwelling answered the signal; but immediately after, from that lattice fluttered a falling object, white and light: billet the second.

“There!” I ejaculated involuntarily.

“Where?” Dr. John demanded, with sudden energy, making straight for the window. “What is it?”

“They have gone and done it again,” I replied. “A handkerchief waved and something fell.” I pointed to the lattice; it was now closed, blank, hypocritical.

“Go at once; pick it up and bring it here,” he said promptly; adding, “Nobody will take notice of you: I should be seen.”

I went. After a little search, I found the folded paper lodged on a low shrub-branch, caught there like a scrap of white cloth on a thorn. I seized it and brought it directly to Dr. John. This time, I think not even Rosine saw me.

He tore the billet into small pieces without reading.

“It is not in the least her fault, you must remember,” he said, looking at me.

“Whose fault?” I asked. “Who is it?”

“You don’t yet know, then?”

“Not in the least.”

“Have you no guess?”

“None.”

“If I knew you better,” he continued, “I might be tempted to risk some confidence, and thus secure you as guardian over a most innocent and excellent, but somewhat inexperienced being.”

“As a duenna?” I said.

“Yes,” he replied absently. “What snares are round her!” he added, musing.

Then, for the first time, he examined my face, as if to see whether any kindly expression there might warrant him in placing his “ethereal creature” under my care—some angel, it seemed, for whom powers of darkness plotted.

I felt no vocation to watch ethereal creatures. Yet the memory of Madame at my drawers rose before me, and with it a sense that I owed him a good turn. If I could help, I would; it was not mine to decide how.

So I intimated, with as little reluctance as might be, that I was willing to do what I could towards taking care of any person in whom he might be interested.

“I am no farther interested than as a spectator,” he said, with a modesty admirable to witness. “I happen to be acquainted with the rather worthless character of the person who, from the house opposite, has now twice invaded the sanctity of this place. I have also met in society the object at whom these vulgar attempts are aimed. Her exquisite superiority and innate refinement ought, one would think, to scare impertinence from her very idea. It is not so, however; and innocent, unsuspicious as she is, I would guard her from evil if I could. In person, however, I can do nothing; I cannot come near her—” He paused.

“Well, I am willing to help you,” I said; “only tell me how.”

And in my mind I ran over our inmates, hunting this paragon, this pearl of great price. It must be Madame, I concluded. She alone amongst us had the art even to seem superior; but as to being unsuspicious and inexperienced, Dr. John need not vex himself. However, it was his whim; he should be humoured. His angel should be an angel.

“Just notify the quarter to which my care is to be directed,” I continued gravely, chuckling inwardly at the thought of being set to chaperon Madame Beck or one of her pupils.

Dr. John had fine nerves. He felt, by instinct, what a coarser mind would not have detected: that I was a little amused at him. Colour rose to his cheek; with half a smile he turned and took his hat—he was going.

My heart smote me. I sprang to mend what I had hurt.

“I will—I will help you,” I said eagerly. “I will do what you wish. I will watch over your angel; I will take care of her, only tell me who she is.”

“But you must know,” he urged, earnest now, speaking very low. “So spotless, so good, so unspeakably beautiful! impossible that one house should contain two like her. I allude, of course—”

At that instant the latch of Madame Beck’s chamber-door—which opened into the nursery—gave a sudden click, as if the hand holding it had been slightly convulsed. There followed the suppressed explosion of an irrepressible sneeze.

These little accidents will happen to the best of us.

Madame—excellent woman!—was on duty. She had come home quietly, stolen upstairs on tiptoe, and stood in her chamber. Had she not sneezed, she would have heard all, and so should I. But that unlucky sternutation routed Dr. John. He stood aghast.

Madame came forward alert, composed, in the best and yet most tranquil spirits. No novice to her habits would have believed that her ear had been glued to the keyhole for ten minutes. She affected to sneeze again, declared she was “enrhumée,” and proceeded volubly to recount her “courses en fiacre,” every detail laid out as neatly as a well-ordered supper service.

The prayer-bell rang, and I left her with the doctor.
