A fortnight of severe toil had passed since they bade farewell to the green ocean of the eastern plains. They had crossed the Cordillera, casting a longing glance at Santa Fe, the wealthy city nestled in gardens upon its lofty plateau, only to realize it was far too vast for their ambitions. Yet, their time was not wasted. Their Indian lad had discovered a gold-train descending from Santa Fe toward the Magdalena, and they waited for it beside a miserable rut that served as a road. Encamped in a forest of oaks that might have reminded them of Europe were it not for the tree-ferns forming the undergrowth and the deep gorges opening at their feet, they found themselves in a strange limbo. Above them blew the cool breezes of a temperate zone, while far below, dim through an everlasting vapor-bath of rank hot steam, lay the mighty forms and gorgeous colors of the tropic forest.

They had pitched their camp among the tree-ferns, overlooking a steep hillside where a road once existed, perhaps when Cundinamarca was a civilized kingdom. Now, Spanish misrule had reduced it to a few steps slipping from their places at the bottom of a narrow mud ditch. Like the aqueducts, bridges, and post-houses of that strange empire, all traces of civilization had decayed in the mad search for gold, save architecture alone, which survived only in the splendid cathedrals rising upon the ruins of the temples of the Sun. Rapid tropic vegetation had reclaimed its old domains, leaving Amyas and his crew utterly alone, within miles of an important Spanish settlement, as isolated as they would be in the solitudes of the Orinoco or the Amazon.

Attempts to find sulphur and nitre had failed, forcing them to rely on swords and arrows—a method that disgusted Yeo, though Amyas reminded him that Drake had taken Nombre de Dios with no better weapons. Blocking the road above by felling a large tree across it, the men sat among the flowers chewing coca, meditating on a mysterious roar that had followed them nightly since leaving the banks of the Meta. It was neither the snap of a branch nor the call of a bird, yet they dismissed it, for the gold train was far more important than any noise.

Suddenly, a sharp crack and a loud cry echoed from beneath. "That was a whip's crack," said Yeo, "and a woman's wail. They are close here, lads!" "A woman's?" Amyas asked. "Do they drive women in their gangs?" "Why not, the brutes?" Yeo replied, pointing out the glittering basnets of the approaching column.

Amyas ordered silence, instructing them to wait for his signal before shooting, then to use arrows first, followed by swords. Up came the troop, slowly, and hearts beat loud at their coming. First, about twenty soldiers, half on foot and half borne in chairs on the backs of single Indians, while the marchers had consigned their heavy armor and arquebuses to attendant slaves who were pricked on at will by the pikes of the soldiers behind them. "The men are mad to let their ordnance out of their hands," observed someone. "An Indian will pray to an arquebus not to shoot him; he sure their artillery is safe enough," said Yeo.

Following the soldiers came a procession that made them forget all else: a sad and hideous sight, yet one too common in those remoter districts where humane edicts were disregarded. A line of Indians, Negroes, and Zambos, naked, emaciated, scarred with whips and fetters, and chained together by their left wrists, toiled upwards, panting under the burden of baskets held by straps across their foreheads. Among them were old men, youths, women, and slender young girls with children at their knees. A low murmur of indignation rose from the ambushed Englishmen, worthy of the free and righteous hearts of those days, when Raleigh could appeal to man and God on the ground of common humanity, ere the seventeenth century had brutalized them with the fatal gift of negro-slaves.

The first forty bore a burden that made all but Amyas and Yeo forget the wretches carrying it. Each basket contained a square package of carefully corded hide. "What's in them, captain?" "Gold!" And at that magic word, all eyes strained greedily forward. Amyas had to whisper, "Be men, be men, or you will spoil all yet!" The last twenty bore larger baskets, seemingly with food, followed by twenty more soldiers and the officer in charge, who smiled away in his chair, twirling his huge mustachios, unaware of the English arrows itching to be through his ribs.

Amyas felt a shrinking from drawing his bow in cool blood on men so utterly unsuspicious and defenseless, even though they were committing devilish cruelty. Drivers armed with whips lingered up and down the slowly staggering file, avenging every lagging or stumble with a blow of the cruel manati-hide that cracked like a pistol-shot against the naked limbs of the silent victims.

Suddenly, the *casus belli* arose of its own accord. The last but one of the chained line was an old gray-headed man, followed by a slender, graceful girl of about eighteen. Amyas's heart yearned over them as they came up. Just as they passed, the foremost of the file rounded the corner above; there was a bustle, and a voice shouted, "Halt, senors! there is a tree across the path!"

"A tree across the path?" bellowed the officer, with a variety of passionate addresses to the Mother of Heaven and various saints, while the trembling Indians surged up and down upon the ruinous steps until the poor old man fell groveling on his face. The officer leaped down and hurried upward. Finding the old man, he pricked the prostrate wretch with the point of his sword. "Sin peccado concebida! Grandfather of Beelzebub, is this a place to lie worshipping your fiends?" The old man tried to rise, but the weight on his head was too much; he fell again, motionless. The driver applied the manati-hide across his loins with fearful force, declaring him "used up."

"What does the intendant mean by sending me out with worn-out cattle like these? Forward there!" shouted the officer. "Clear away the tree, senors, and I'll soon clear the chain. Hold it up, Pedrillo!" The driver held up the chain fastened to the old man's wrist. The officer stepped back and flourished a Toledo blade, whose beauty made Amyas break the Tenth Commandment on the spot. The man was tall, handsome, and broad-shouldered. Amyas thought he was going to display the strength of his arm in severing the chain at one stroke.

Even he was not prepared for the recondite fancies of a Spanish adventurer. The blade gleamed in the air, once, twice, and fell—not on the chain, but on the wrist which it fettered. There was a shriek, a crimson flash, and the chain and its prisoner were parted indeed. One moment more, and Amyas's arrow would have been through the throat of the murderer, who paused, regarding his workmanship with a satisfied smile; but vengeance was not to come from him.

Quick and fierce as a tiger-cat, the girl sprang on the ruffian, clasped him in her arms with the intense strength of passion, and leaped with him from the narrow ledge into the abyss below. There was a rush, a shout; all faces were bent over the precipice. The girl hung by her chained wrist; the officer was gone. There was a moment's awful silence; and then Amyas heard his body crashing through the tree-tops far below.

"Haul her up! Hew her in pieces! Burn the witch!" shouted the driver, pulling at the chain while all sprang from their chairs to stoop over the brink. Now was the time for Amyas! Heaven had delivered them into his hands. Swift and sure, at ten yards off, his arrow rushed through the body of the driver, and then, with a roar as of the leaping lion, he sprang like an avenging angel into the midst of the astonished ruffians.

His first thought was for the girl. In a moment, by sheer strength, he had jerked her safely up into the road, while the Spaniards recoiled right and left, fancying him for the moment some mountain giant or supernatural foe. His hurrah undeceived them in an instant, and a cry of "English! Lutheran dogs!" arose, but too late. The men of Devon had followed their captain's lead: a storm of arrows left five Spaniards dead and a dozen more wounded, and down leapt Salvation Yeo, his white hair streaming behind him, with twenty good swords more, and the work of death began.

The Spaniards fought like lions, but they had no time to fix their arquebuses on the crutches, nor room in that narrow path to use their pikes. The English had the wall of them, and to have the wall there was to have the foe's life at their mercy. Five desperate minutes, and not a living Spaniard stood upon those steps; certainly no living one lay in the green abyss below. Two only, who were behind the rest and in full armor, escaped without mortal wound and fled down the hill again.

"After them! Michael Evans and Simon Heard; and catch them, if they run a league." The two long and lean Clovelly men, active as deer from forest training, ran two feet for the Spaniard's one; and in ten minutes returned, having done their work. Amyas and his men hurried past the Indians to help Cary and the party forward, where shouts and musket shots announced a sharp affray. Their arrival settled the matter. All the Spaniards fell but three or four, who scrambled down the crannies of the cliff.

"Let not one of them escape! Slay them as Israel slew Amalek!" cried Yeo, and ere the wretches could reach a place of shelter, an arrow was quivering in each body as it rolled lifeless down the rocks. "Now then! Loose the Indians!" They found armorer's tools on one of the dead bodies, and it was done.

"We are your friends," said Amyas. "All we ask is, that you shall help us to carry this gold down to the Magdalena, and then you are free." Some few of the younger groveled at his knees, kissing his feet and hailing him as the child of the Sun; but the most part kept a stolid indifference, sitting quietly where they stood, staring into vacancy. The iron had entered too deeply into their souls. They seemed past hope, enjoyment, even understanding.

But the young girl, who was last of all in the line, as soon as she was loosed, sprang to her father's body. Speaking no word, she lifted it in her thin arms, laid it across her knees, kissed the fallen lips, and stroked the furrowed cheeks, murmuring inarticulate sounds like the cooing of a woodland dove, of which none knew the meaning but she. Suddenly the truth flashed on her; silent as ever, she drew one long heaving breath, rose erect with the body in her arms, and leaped into the abyss.

They watched her dark and slender limbs, twined closely round the old man's corpse, turn over and over until a crash among the leaves and a scream among the birds told that she had reached the trees; and the green roof hid her from their view. "Brave lass!" shouted a sailor. "The Lord forgive her!" said Yeo. "But, your worship, we must have these rascals' ordnance." "And their clothes too, Yeo, if we wish to get down the Magdalena unchallenged."

Amyas reminded them to remember Mr. Oxenham and his crew, warning them not to make God's gift their ruin by faithlessness, greediness, or mutinous haste. "You shall find none in us!" cried several men. "We know your worship. We can trust our general." "Thank God!" said Amyas. "Now then, it will be no shame or sin to make the Indians carry it, saving the women, whom God forbid we should burden. But we must pass through the very heart of the Spanish settlements, and by the town of Saint Martha itself. So the clothes and weapons of these Spaniards we must have, let it cost us what labor it may."

"How many lie in the road?" "Thirteen here, and about ten up above," said Cary. "Then there are near twenty missing. Who will volunteer to go down over cliff, and bring up the spoil of them?" "I, and I, and I;" and a dozen stepped out, for they knew that Amyas meant to help at the doing of it himself.

"Very well, then, follow me. Sir John, take the Indian lad for your interpreter, and try and comfort the souls of these poor heathens. Tell them that they shall all be free." "Why, who is that comes up the road?" All eyes turned in the direction he spoke. And, wonder of wonders! up came none other than Ayacanora herself, blow-gun in hand, bow on back, and bedecked in all her feather garments, which were rather the worse for a fortnight's woodland travel.

All stood mute with astonishment as, seeing Amyas, she uttered a cry of joy, quickened her pace into a run, and at last fell panting and exhausted at his feet. "I have found you!" she said; "you ran away from me, but you could not escape me!" And she fawned round Amyas, like a dog who has found his master, and then sat down on the bank, bursting into wild sobs.
