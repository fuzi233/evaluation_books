That evening, when Mr. Sillerton Jackson had at last gathered up his comments as neatly as his gloves and taken himself away, and the ladies had withdrawn behind the chintz curtains of the bedroom—where a maid’s low voice, the click of a hairbrush, and the soft fall of silk upon silk made a little domestic music—Newland Archer went upstairs to his study.

Someone vigilant had kept the place as carefully tended as a kitchen range banked for morning: the coals nestled under their white ash, the lamp-wick trimmed to a steady, honey-coloured flame. The room received him with its familiar smells—leather bindings warmed by the fire, a faint peppery dust from old paper, the clean tang of oiled bronze on the mantel where “The Fencers” held their eternal pose. It was, in its way, as reassuring as the well-run back of a house: everything in readiness, everything in its place.

He sank into his chair by the hearth, and his eyes went, as they always went now, to the large photograph of May Welland. In the first days of his courtship she had given it to him with that straightforward air she had of offering what she had, without flourish and without reserve; and it had displaced every other portrait on the table, as a single, dominant flavour overwhelms the lesser notes of a dish.

Tonight he looked at it with a new, startled attention, as though he were tasting something he had believed he knew and suddenly found altered by an ingredient he had not noticed. The frank forehead, the serious eyes, the gay innocent mouth—features as familiar as the rooms of his mother’s house—seemed to look back at him from behind a veil. There she was, and yet there was also, superimposed upon her, the young creature his world had manufactured with such patient, ancestral skill: a girl who knew nothing and expected everything, not from vanity but from training; a soul kept, like a rare preserve, sealed from air and bruising touch.

And it came over him, with a chill like stepping out of a warm dining-room into the raw street, that marriage was not the snug anchorage he had always heard it called, but a voyage with reefs unmarked on any chart. The Countess Olenska—Ellen, with her foreign ways and her inconvenient candour—had stirred settled convictions from their resting place, as a spoon stirs sediment from the bottom of a cup, clouding the whole draught.

His own words came back to him, spoken in heat and with that easy confidence men had when they argued about women: Women should be free—as free as we are. It struck now like a knife pushed too far into a joint, cutting to the root of what everyone in his circle agreed not to name. “Nice” women, however wronged, would never claim such freedom; and men—generous-minded men like himself—were therefore lavish, in debate, in conceding it. Yet he saw, with the unpleasant clarity of a food historian recognizing adulteration in a familiar staple, how such verbal generosity masked the hard, binding conventions underneath. One spoke of liberty while tightening the knot.

Here was the bitter absurdity: he was pledged to defend, for May’s cousin, a line of conduct which, if it were his own wife’s, would justify him—by all the thunders of Church and State—in crushing her beneath condemnation. Of course, the predicament was “hypothetical.” He wasn’t a blackguard Polish nobleman; it was ridiculous to speculate on what his wife’s rights would be if he were. Yet he was too imaginative not to feel how the tie might gall for subtler reasons than brutality and betrayal. What could he and May truly know of each other, when it was his duty, as a decent fellow, to conceal his past, and hers, as a marriageable girl, to have no past to conceal?

He saw, as clearly as if he had been seated at the head of a long table tasting one marriage after another, how most unions around him were composed: the same dishes served in different houses, the same sauces disguising the same boiled facts. Suppose, without any gross scandal, they tired of one another; suppose they misunderstood, irritated, bruised. He reviewed the marriages of his friends—the “happy” ones—and found none that resembled the ardent, tender comradeship he had always pictured as his own fate with May. That picture, he began to understand, presupposed in her the very things she had been denied: experience, versatility, freedom of judgment. She had been trained, like a delicate fruit, to ripen without sun and wind; and with a foreboding shiver he saw his marriage becoming what so many others were: a dull association of material and social interests, held together by ignorance on one side and hypocrisy on the other.

Lawrence Lefferts floated up in his mind as the husband who had most completely achieved the social ideal. As high-priest of form, Larry had shaped his wife to his convenience as surely as a cook shapes a paste—pressing, smoothing, sealing out any air that might make it rise unexpectedly. In the most conspicuous moments of his frequent affairs with other men’s wives, Mrs. Lefferts moved about in smiling unconsciousness, saying that Lawrence was “so frightfully strict”; and she had been known to blush and turn away, as if offended by a coarse smell, when someone alluded to Julius Beaufort’s “other establishment”—a vulgarity that, in New York, was always attributed to foreigners as taints are attributed to imported meats.

Archer tried to console himself: he was not such an ass as Larry Lefferts, nor May such a simpleton as poor Gertrude. But the difference, he admitted, was one of intelligence, not of standards. In truth they all lived in a hieroglyphic world, where the real thing was never said or done or even thought, but only represented by agreed-upon signs—as when Mrs. Welland, who knew perfectly why Archer had pressed to have the engagement announced at the Beaufort ball (and had expected him to do no less), still felt obliged to simulate reluctance and the air of having her hand forced, like those “savage” brides described in the new books on Primitive Man—dragged shrieking from their parents’ tent to prove the value of the bargain.

The result was that the very girl at the centre of this elaborate system of mystification remained inscrutable, not in spite of her frankness but because of it. May was frank, poor darling, because she had nothing to conceal; assured because she knew of nothing to be on guard against. And with no better preparation than this, she was to be plunged overnight into what people, with the prudence of those who name nothing sharply, called “the facts of life”—as if they were a coarse cut of meat to be covered with sauce and served without mention of its origin.

He was sincerely, placidly in love. He delighted in her radiant good looks, her health, her horsemanship—the clean, outdoor scent that seemed to cling to her after a ride—her grace and quickness at games, and the shy interest in books and ideas she was beginning to develop under his guidance. She had advanced far enough to join him in ridiculing the Idyls of the King, but not to feel the strange beauty of Ulysses and the Lotus Eaters. She was straightforward, loyal, brave; and her sense of humour—chiefly proved by laughing at his jokes—made him feel, with an indulgent tenderness, that there were depths of feeling in her that it would be a joy to waken.

Yet each time he made this little inventory he came back discouraged, as if he had praised the sweetness of a fruit only to remember it had been ripened in a hothouse. This frankness and innocence were an artificial product. Untrained human nature was not frank and innocent; it was full of twists, defences, instinctive guile. He felt oppressed by this factitious purity, so cunningly manufactured by a conspiracy of mothers and aunts and grandmothers and long-dead ancestresses—manufactured because it was supposed to be what he wanted, what he had a right to, so that he might exercise his lordly pleasure in smashing it like an image made of snow.

There was, he knew, a certain triteness in such reflections; they were those habitual to young men nearing their wedding day. But they were usually seasoned with compunction and self-abasement, and of that Archer felt no trace. He could not deplore—like Thackeray’s heroes, who always exasperated him—that he had not a blank page to offer his bride in exchange for her unblemished one. He could not escape the knowledge that if he had been brought up as she had, they would both have been as helpless as babes in the wood; nor could he, for all his anxious turning-over, find any honest reason—any reason not tied to his own momentary pleasure and the male vanity of possession—why his bride should not have been allowed the same freedom of experience as himself.

Such thoughts, at such an hour, were bound to drift through a man’s mind; but Archer was aware that their uncomfortable persistence and their sharpness of outline came from the inopportune arrival of the Countess Olenska. Here he was, at the very moment of betrothal—a moment meant for pure thoughts and cloudless hopes—pitchforked into a coil of scandal that brought up all the special problems he would rather have let lie, like bones and gristle that spoil an appetite.

“Hang Ellen Olenska!” he muttered, as he covered the fire and began to undress.

He could not honestly see why her fate should have the least bearing on his; and yet, somewhere in him, he felt that he had only begun to measure the risks of the championship his engagement had forced upon him.

A few days later the bolt fell.

The Lovell Mingotts sent out cards for what was known as a formal dinner—three extra footmen, two dishes for each course, and a Roman punch in the middle, that frosty, citrus-laced interlude that arrived like a bell struck between movements—and they headed the invitations with the words: To meet the Countess Olenska. It was the hospitable American fashion, treating strangers as if they were royalties, or at least their ambassadors; as though to offer a new arrival one’s most elaborate table were the same as offering her a treaty.

The guest list had been chosen with a boldness and discrimination in which the initiated recognised the firm hand of old Catherine—Catherine the Great, as people called her behind fans and in carriage corners. Alongside immemorial standbys like the Selfridge Merrys, who were asked everywhere because they always had been, were the Beauforts, for whom there was a claim of relationship, and Mr. Sillerton Jackson and his sister Sophy, who went wherever her brother told her to go. There were also some of the most fashionable and yet most irreproachable of the “young married” set: the Lawrence Leffertses; Mrs. Lefferts Rushworth, the lovely widow; the Harry Thorleys; the Reggie Chiverses; young Morris Dagonet and his wife, who was a van der Luyden. It was, in short, a perfectly assorted company—people who, through the long season, moved together daily and nightly with undiminished zest, like a well-trained chorus that never missed an entrance.

And then, forty-eight hours later, the unbelievable happened.

Everyone refused the Mingotts’ invitation except the Beauforts and old Mr. Jackson and his sister.

The intended slight was sharpened by the fact that even the Reggie Chiverses—of the Mingott clan—were among those inflicting it; and by the uniform wording of the notes: every one “regretted that they were unable to accept,” without even the decent varnish of a “previous engagement.” In a society so small, so tightly laced together, and so scantly supplied with evenings and entertainments, everyone—including livery-stable keepers, butlers, and cooks—knew exactly when people were free. The refusals were not merely refusals; they were a proclamation, sounded without mercy through every pantry and kitchen in town, that the writers were determined not to meet the Countess Olenska.

New York’s snubs travelled not only on paper but on sound. The whisper at the fishmonger’s, the suppressed laugh in a servant’s hall, the rattle of a delivery cart pausing just long enough for the man on the box to trade news with a footman at an area door—these were the city’s true messengers. And for all its elegance, the place had an undertone, a constant clink and murmur, in which reputations were carried like scents on air.

The blow was unexpected; but the Mingotts, as their way was, met it gallantly. Mrs. Lovell Mingott confided the case to Mrs. Welland; Mrs. Welland confided it to Newland Archer; and Archer, aflame at the outrage, appealed passionately and authoritatively to his mother.

Mrs. Archer resisted inwardly, temporised outwardly, and then—because she always succumbed to his instances—gave way at last. Once she had yielded, she embraced his cause with an energy redoubled by her earlier hesitation. She put on her grey velvet bonnet, as solemnly as one might tie on an apron before undertaking a task that must be done properly, and said: “I’ll go and see Louisa van der Luyden.”

The New York of Archer’s day was a small, slippery pyramid, still uncracked, in which footholds were hard won and quickly lost. At its base stood the firm foundation of what Mrs. Archer called “plain people”—an honourable but obscure majority of respectable families who had been raised a little above their level by marriage into a ruling clan. People, Mrs. Archer always said, were not as particular as they used to be; and with old Catherine Spicer ruling one end of Fifth Avenue and Julius Beaufort the other, one couldn’t expect the old traditions to last much longer.

Narrowing upward from this wealthy but inconspicuous substratum was the compact, dominant group the Mingotts, Newlands, Chiverses, and Mansons so actively represented. Most people imagined them to be the apex; but those of Mrs. Archer’s generation knew that, in the eyes of professional genealogy, only a still smaller number could claim that height.

“Don’t tell me,” Mrs. Archer would say, “all this modern newspaper rubbish about a New York aristocracy. If there is one, neither the Mingotts nor the Mansons belong to it; no, nor the Newlands or the Chiverses either. Our grandfathers and great-grandfathers were respectable English or Dutch merchants who came to the colonies to make their fortune and stayed because they did so well. One of your great-grandfathers signed the Declaration, another was a general on Washington’s staff and received General Burgoyne’s sword after Saratoga. These are things to be proud of; but they have nothing to do with rank or class. New York has always been a commercial community, and there are not more than three families in it who can claim aristocratic origin in the real sense of the word.”

Mrs. Archer and her children knew, as everyone knew, who those privileged beings were: the Dagonets of Washington Square, descended from an old English county family allied with the Pitts and Foxes; the Lannings, who had intermarried with descendants of Count de Grasse; and the van der Luydens, direct descendants of the first Dutch governor of Manhattan and connected by pre-Revolutionary marriages with French and British aristocracy.

The Lannings survived only in the persons of two very old but lively Miss Lannings, who lived cheerfully among family portraits and Chippendale, feeding on memory as on a well-stocked pantry. The Dagonets were a considerable clan, allied to the best names in Baltimore and Philadelphia. But the van der Luydens, who stood above all of them, had faded into a kind of super-terrestrial twilight, from which only two figures emerged with impressive distinctness: Mr. and Mrs. Henry van der Luyden.

Mrs. Henry van der Luyden had been Louisa Dagonet. Her mother had been the granddaughter of Colonel du Lac—of an old Channel Island family—who had fought under Cornwallis and, after the war, settled in Maryland with his bride, Lady Angelica Trevenna, fifth daughter of the Earl of St. Austrey. The ties between the Dagonets, the du Lacs, and their Cornish kinsfolk the Trevennas had remained close and cordial, like an old recipe handed down in a household where the original tongue was still spoken over the kneading-board.

Mr. and Mrs. van der Luyden had more than once paid long visits to the present head of the house, the Duke of St. Austrey, at his seat in Cornwall and at St. Austrey in Gloucestershire; and his Grace had often announced his intention of returning their visit—without the Duchess, who feared the Atlantic.

They divided their time between Trevenna, their place in Maryland, and Skuytercliff, the great estate on the Hudson granted in colonial days to the famous first Governor, and of which Mr. van der Luyden was still “Patroon.” Their large, solemn house on Madison Avenue was seldom opened; and when they came to town they received there only their most intimate friends. Their silence had the authority of a held note; when they chose to speak, the whole chorus adjusted pitch.

“I wish you would go with me, Newland,” his mother said, pausing at the door of the brown coupé. In the street below, wheels hissed over winter grit, a hawker called, a far-off hammer rang—modern noises always pushing in at the edges of the old quiet. “Louisa is fond of you; and of course it’s on account of dear May that I’m taking this step—and also because, if we don’t all stand together, there’ll be no such thing as Society left.”
