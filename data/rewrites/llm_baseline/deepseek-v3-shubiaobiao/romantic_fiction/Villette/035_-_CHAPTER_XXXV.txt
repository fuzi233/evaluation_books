Madame Beck’s words lingered in the air, a command I should have obeyed—had it been possible. But wisdom often miscalculates the heart. That night, she should have left me untouched, indifferent, my solitude unbroken. Instead, she stoked an ember into flame.

*Forget him?* A futile decree. They wished me to forget, and so they painted him in strokes of gold—my sharp, fiery little man recast as a saint, a martyr of love. Père Silas and Madame Beck (for I knew they conspired) laid bare the sanctum of his heart: a devotion so fierce it defied death itself, a fidelity that had knelt at a tomb for twenty years. This was no hollow sentiment—he had proven it in sacrifice, in the quiet surrender of vengeance for something far heavier: a cross borne willingly.

And Justine Marie? I knew her as though she stood before me—pale, placid, one of those bloodless creatures who drift through life leaving no mark. If she wore a halo, it was his love that gilded her. If she seemed an angel, it was his poetry that gave her wings.

Did they think such revelations would deter me? That the ghost of a nun could frighten me away? Or that his vows of charity, his sworn chastity, would make me retreat? No. They had handed me a riddle, an obsession. For nights, I turned it over—until the answer became clear. The only solution lay in the man himself, in the sharp angles of his face beneath that scholar’s cap, in the ink-stained sleeves of his shabby coat.

I *needed* to see him again.

Opportunity came swiftly. The next afternoon, Madame Beck’s orderly lesson was shattered by the whirlwind arrival of that very paletôt. One moment, I sat absorbed in copying an engraving (a painstaking art I foolishly prized); the next, my work was crushed in his grasp, my chair uprooted, and I deposited unceremoniously in the grand salle—a room reserved for dancing, now a courtroom.

Two men stood before me—Boissec and Rochemorte, professors of the college, dandies with waxed moustaches and sneers tucked behind their finery. M. Paul, dark with fury, gestured to me like an advocate to a witness.

A trial, then. They accused him of forging my work, of claiming my essays as his students’ triumphs. I was to answer their questions, to write under their scrutiny.

They began with classics—blank silence. French history—a shrug. Science, philosophy—*“Je n’en sais rien.”*

M. Paul’s face darkened further. He had expected better.

Then, general knowledge—subjects I *did* know. Ideas surged, but words failed. My tongue locked; nerves or stubbornness held me mute.

*“Is she an idiot?”* Boissec murmured.

*Yes,* I thought bitterly. *An idiot for you.*

But M. Paul’s look—wounded, impatient—pierced deeper. To spare us all, I choked out: *“Let me go. You’ll get nothing from me.”*

Then, humiliation: tears, hot and furious. Not grief—rage. Had I been stronger, I would have struck them.

They demanded I write. *“Human Justice,”* Rochemorte declared.

A cold abstraction—until memory struck. *These* were the men who had hunted me through Villette my first night, who had laughed as I fled.

Inspiration flared. My pen flew. *Human Justice* became a slovenly matron, deaf to pleas, generous only to the strong, doling out sugar-plums or blows as whim dictated.

M. Paul read over my shoulder. I curtsied and left.

We met again that evening in the garden, the air thick with unspoken words.

*“You think me heartless,”* he said at last. *“A man without ties, without affection.”*

I countered, sharper than intended: *“You live in a hole, black your own boots, dine in solitude. You are a philosopher—cynical, independent.”*

His laugh was short. *“And you are too perceptive.”*

Then, unexpectedly, he softened. *“What if I told you I have a home? A house in the Basse-Ville, with old trees and a quiet study?”*

I knew it. I *described* it—the steps, the grey flags, the boudoir-turned-oratory.

He stared. *“How?”*

But I told him of my detour to the Rue des Mages, of Père Silas’s story—the Priest’s Pupil, ungrateful, cold, yet bound by duty.

His jaw tightened. *“You know too much.”*

Then, abruptly, he asked: *“Can you and I be friends? True friends—closer than blood?”*

I could not speak.

He took my hand. *“I need a sister’s affection. Will you give it?”*

No lover’s pledge could have moved me more. If this was real—if he meant it—then I wanted nothing else in this world.

*“Try me,”* I said. *“See if I prove reliable.”*

His grip tightened—an anchor in the storm. For now, that was enough.
