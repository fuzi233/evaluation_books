The cold wind off the hills bit at the exposed earth, carrying with it a silence that felt less like absence and more like a held breath. In the township of Dunwich, far from the clustered warmth of the village and isolated by four miles of jagged terrain and a lonely stretch of road from any neighbor, stood a farmhouse that clung to the hillside like a leech. It was here, at the break of dawn on the second of February, 1913, that Wilbur Whateley entered the world. The date marked Candlemas, a festival observed in Dunwich with a superstitious reverence that bordered on the occult, but the true omens lay in the unnatural stillness broken by the persistent, frantic barking of every dog within hearing distance. Throughout the preceding night, the hills themselves had groaned with a subterranean hum that vibrated in the teeth of the locals.

Born to Lavinia Whateley, a woman whose pale, albinism-stricken skin seemed to absorb the scant light rather than reflect it, the child was a study in grotesque contrasts. Lavinia, thirty-five and deformed, lived with her aged father, Old Whateley, a man whose reputation for black magic had turned the local folklore into a tapestry of terrors. While Lavinia possessed no legal husband, she wore the child's existence with a strange, feverish pride, refusing to disown him despite the scandal. To her, the dark, goatish infant was not a shame but a prophecy made flesh, a vessel for powers that would reshape the future. Her mind, uneducated by schools but filled with the disjointed, rotting scraps of ancient lore passed down through generations, wandered constantly between the reality of her filth-ridden home and grandiose, terrifying visions.

The night of Wilbur's birth was marked by a scream that tore through the mountain air, louder and more terrible than the howling of the wind or the chorus of baying hounds. Yet, no human hand—no doctor, no midwife—attended the delivery. For weeks, the world outside knew nothing of the boy. It was only a week later, when Old Whateley dragged his sleigh through the deep snow into Dunwich Village, that the secret spilled out. The old man, usually a figure of quiet, if eccentric, dread, appeared changed. A new layer of furtiveness had settled over his clouded eyes, transforming him from an object of superstition into a subject of genuine, primal fear. Standing before the loungers at Osborn's General Store, he spoke of the child with a mixture of shame and defiance. "I dun't keer what folks think," he slurred, his voice raspy. "Ef Lavinny's boy looked like his pa, he wouldn't look like nothin' ye expeck... I calc'late her man is as good a husban' as ye kin find this side of Aylesbury." He paused, staring into the middle distance, his voice dropping to a whisper that carried across the frozen square. "_Some day yew folks'll hear a child o' Lavinny's a-callin' its father's name on the top o' Sentinel Hill!_"

For the first month, Wilbur's only companions were his grandfather and Mamie Bishop, the common-law wife of Earl Sawyer. Mamie visited with frank curiosity, her stories later painting a picture of a creature that defied description, but it was Zechariah Whateley, representing the untainted line of the family, who brought the Alderney cows to the farm. This purchase signaled the start of a bizarre era for the Whateleys, a cycle of cattle buying that would drag on until the final horror of 1928. Yet, the barn remained unnervingly sparse. Those brave enough to sneak onto the steep hillside above the farmhouse to count the grazing herd could never find more than a dozen animals. They were thin, bloodless things, afflicted by mysterious sores and incision-like wounds that hinted at a blight rising from the tainted soil and the decaying timber of the shed. Even the old man and his daughter bore traces of these maladies, faint scars around their necks suggesting they, too, were victims of some unseen contagion.

As spring approached, Lavinia resumed her solitary wanderings into the storm-laden hills, carrying the swarthy child in her misshapen arms. The villagers, having seen the infant once, lost their interest, failing to register the terrifying speed at which Wilbur grew. Within three months, he possessed the musculature and stature of a toddler nearing two, moving with a deliberate, predatory restraint. By seven months, he walked unaided, his steps faltering briefly before becoming steady.

Then came Halloween, and the fire. At midnight, atop Sentinel Hill where the ancient stone table stood surrounded by a mound of bleached bones, a great blaze erupted. Silas Bishop, a man of the undecayed lineage, claimed to have seen the boy running up the hill ahead of his mother an hour prior. In the dim glow of his lantern, the figures had moved with a silent, fluid grace, seemingly naked save for a fringed belt and dark trousers—a stark, shocking contrast to the modesty expected of children. Later, it became clear that Wilbur could never be seen alive without complete, tightly buttoned clothing; any threat to his attire ignited a surge of pure, animalistic rage within him.

By January, the town gossiped of "Lavinny's black brat" speaking. At eleven months, the boy articulated words with a fluency that belied his infancy, devoid of the usual lisps. But it was not just what he said; it was *how* he said it. An elusive, alien quality resonated in his intonation, a sound that seemed to vibrate from the very core of his being, unmoored from the dialect of Dunwich. His face, though sharing the jawlessness of his kin, featured a firm, prematurely adult nose and large, dark eyes that burned with a preternatural intelligence. Yet, beneath this semblance of brilliance lay a profound ugliness. His lips were thick and goatish, his skin yellow and pocked, his hair coarse and crinkled, and his ears strangely elongated. He was hated with a visceral intensity, his presence causing dogs to cower and flee. And whispers circulated, spiced with madness, about the boy echoing the dreadful name _Yog-Sothoth_ in circles of stone, shaking the foundations of the hills before he had ever drawn breath.
