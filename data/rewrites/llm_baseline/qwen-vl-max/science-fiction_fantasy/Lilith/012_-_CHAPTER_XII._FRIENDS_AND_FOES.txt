As I moved along one of the narrow channels, something small and green caught my eye—what looked like a shrub, perhaps the outpost of a hidden forest. I knelt to examine it more closely. It bore tiny fruit, unfamiliar to me, and though hunger gnawed at my belly, I hesitated. I didn’t know what it was, and caution whispered that ignorance could be deadly. But unseen by me, from behind jagged rocks and crevices, hundreds of eyes watched—wide, bright, and full of curiosity.

I pressed on, passing another plant, then another, each larger than the last, until I found myself surrounded by clusters of them. Only then did I realize they were not shrubs at all, but stunted trees—dwarfed yet proud in their quiet growth. The channel narrowed, choked with these strange plants, and I had to leap or squeeze through where I could. In one stretch, a sudden rustling erupted—like wings beating against ivy-covered stone—but when I turned, nothing stirred.

Beyond lay a grove of larger trees, heavy with coarse-looking fruit, standing on the edge of a shallow depression once filled with water. A forest flowed down from the highlands into this hollow, its upper slopes rich with varied timber, while below, almost every tree bore edible fruit. I descended a grassy slope dotted with moss, weary from the journey, and stretched out to rest.

Nearby stood a slender tree, no taller than my hand, laden with tiny apples the color of dawn—rosy, perfect, no bigger than cherries. My fingers reached out instinctively. I plucked one and bit into it. The flavor burst across my tongue—sweet, crisp, alive. I raised my hand for another, when a chorus of voices broke the silence.

“*He likes our apples!*” they cried, laughter ringing like wind chimes over a stream. “*He likes our apples! He’s a good giant! He’s a good giant!*”

I sat up, startled. Around me, above and below, children swarmed—dozens of them, some barely able to walk, others nearly as tall as I was. A few looked older, standing apart, calm amid the chaos. They chattered in clusters, debating, teasing, laughing, their voices sharp and bright, full of intelligence and joy. I understood most of what they said—not because language came easily, but because their hearts spoke plainly.

They had seen me take the first apple. From that single act, they judged me kind. One child, smaller than the rest, stepped forward with a solemn face. “He IS rather big,” he admitted, “but littleness isn’t everything! It won’t keep you from growing big and stupid unless you take care!”

I smiled, though I said nothing. I feared my voice might startle them. Better to listen, to learn.

Then, from a nearby thicket, a little boy emerged—innocent-faced, mischievous-eyed—offering me a massive green apple. The crowd fell silent. All eyes turned to me.

“Eat, good giant,” he said softly.

I sat up, accepted the fruit, and smiled my thanks. I took a bite—then froze. The taste was bitter, sour, almost poisonous. Without thinking, I hurled the apple far away.

A roar of delight exploded from the children. They rushed toward me, tumbling over themselves in joy. They climbed onto my arms, kissed my hands, clung to my legs, wrapped their arms around my neck. I collapsed under their weight, overwhelmed by their warmth and exuberance.

“*Good, good giant!*” they sang. “*We knew you would come! Oh, you dear, strong, kind giant!*”

Their voices rose and fell like waves—joyful, endless. Then, abruptly, silence.

The children drew back. Those atop me scrambled down, helping me rise. A young girl pointed upward, her face grave.

“Get up, good giant!” she urged. “Hurry! He saw you throw your apple away!”

Before she finished, I stood. On the hilltop stood a figure—a man, or something like one, slightly taller than I, with a brutish face and a sneer twisted across his lips. He wore no weapon, yet his presence radiated threat. As he began descending, I started up the slope, seeking better ground.

He growled, low and guttural, and turned toward me. When we met, he held out his hand. I reached to shake it, but he snatched it back, threatened a blow, then offered it again. Now I understood—he wanted the apple I’d thrown away.

I shook my head, made a grimace of disgust, gestured firmly: *No.*

His rage erupted in a howl—*“You dare say my apple was unfit?”*

“I have no quarrel with your tree,” I replied. “But one bad apple may grow on the best.”

Whether he grasped the meaning, I do not know. But he lunged forward, and before I could react, another giant—similar in build and cruelty—appeared from behind. Together, they attacked.

I struck one hard in the face, but the other slammed me from behind. Between them, I was overpowered. They dragged me up the slope into the woods, to their settlement—huts built of fallen branches and rough stones, crude and dark. Into one they thrust me, threw me to the floor, and kicked me.

A woman stood nearby, watching without expression. During my captivity, I learned little about gender among them—they seemed so alike in form and manner that I often wondered if they were not some strange, fungal race—sentient only enough to move, to hate, to hoard.

Their food—roots, bulbs, and bitter fruits—was revolting to me. But to them, refusal was an offense. I was beaten by both men and women for refusing to eat. That night, I lay half-dead, unable to move, but sleep came, and with it, a fragile strength.

At dawn, they dragged me back to the valley. They bound my feet to a tree with a long rope and placed a flat, serrated stone in my left hand. I shifted it to my right. They kicked me, forced it back into my left. Gestures told me: *Scrape the bark off every branch without fruit.* I obeyed, hoping that submission might earn me freedom—or at least time to plan escape.

And fortune smiled. Close beside me grew one of the dwarf-trees, bearing the same sweet, tiny apples I had tasted before. Every few minutes, I stole a fruit. Each one restored my strength, sharpened my mind. Though I labored under their cruel rule, I lived—because of the kindness of children, and the secret gift of a single tree.
