The Palmers were gone back to Cleveland the very next day, their carriage-wheels diminishing into the damp Devon lanes until even the cawing rooks seemed to close over the vacancy they left behind. Barton, relieved of visitors and yet somehow lonelier for the relief, sank again into its accustomed interchange between Park and cottage: the same walks, the same calls, the same tea-things set out and cleared away with practised hands. Elinor, who had not yet finished marvelling—at Charlotte’s airy contentment without any visible reason; at Mr. Palmer’s odd mixture of sense and indolent plainness; at the perpetual mystery of husbands and wives so ill matched and yet so irrevocably tied—found herself, before she could settle into yesterday, pressed onward into tomorrow by Sir John and Mrs. Jennings, those indefatigable gardeners of society, forever planting new acquaintances in the beds of their neighbours’ lives.

They had been to Exeter that morning—an “excursion,” as Sir John called it, though it was as much a hunt as a drive—and there Mrs. Jennings, with the triumph of a woman whose net has at last tightened, had discovered two young ladies to be relations. That word, in her mouth, had the force of law. Sir John, to whom kinship was only a finer excuse for hospitality, immediately invited them to the Park as soon as their Exeter engagements could possibly allow.

Their engagements, whatever they had been, melted at once before so hearty an invitation. Lady Middleton, on hearing the news, was thrown into a small, well-bred panic. Two girls she had never seen; two girls of whose “elegance”—whose tolerable gentility even—she could have no proof; for her mother’s assurances were too sanguine, and her husband’s confidence too loud, to carry weight with a woman who measured worth by quiet polish and the right sort of restraint. That they were relations made it worse. Relations could not be dismissed after one visit, or mistaken for strangers and forgot.

Mrs. Jennings attempted comfort in the only manner she understood: by laughing at the anxiety and advising her daughter not to care whether they were fashionable, because cousins must put up with one another. It was consolation that stung. Since it was now impossible to prevent their coming, Lady Middleton resigned herself with the philosophy of a woman trained to smile over irritations; and she contented herself, instead, with giving Sir John a gentle reprimand on the subject five or six times every day—always in the same tone, always at the same safe distance from real feeling, as if repetition might boil down impropriety into acceptability.

When the young ladies arrived, their appearance did not justify alarm. They were smartly dressed, with that air of having taken pains and being conscious of it; their manners were civil; they were delighted with the house and in raptures with the furniture; and, above all, they professed themselves so passionately—so extravagantly—fond of children, that Lady Middleton’s opinion, which could be won by no argument so quickly as by admiration of her offspring, was engaged in their favour within the hour. She pronounced them “very agreeable girls indeed,” which, coming from her, was enthusiasm. Sir John’s faith in his own judgment rose like yeast with that faint praise. He set off directly to the cottage to proclaim the Miss Steeles.

He burst in with the same hearty bustle with which he rode to cover in the hunting season, eager to share his find as though kindness were a dish that spoiled if not served at once.

“Do come now—pray come—you must come—I declare you shall come. You can’t think how you will like them. Lucy is monstrous pretty, and so good-humoured and agreeable! The children are all hanging about her already, as if she was an old acquaintance. And they both long to see you of all things, for they have heard at Exeter that you are the most beautiful creatures in the world; and I have told them it is all very true, and a great deal more. You will be delighted with them, I am sure. They have brought the whole coach full of playthings for the children. How can you be so cross as not to come? Why, they are your cousins, you know, after a fashion. You are my cousins, and they are my wife’s, so you must be related.”

There was something almost touching in his inability to keep pleasure to himself; even a third cousin must be shared, as if affection were a pot forever on the hob, meant to be ladled out while it steamed. Yet neither Elinor nor Marianne could be prevailed on to go instantly. Sir John obtained only a promise that they would call at the Park within a day or two, and left them in genuine astonishment at their indifference—only to walk home and boast anew of the Dashwoods’ charms to the Miss Steeles, as he had already been boasting of the Miss Steeles to them.

When the promised visit took place and the introductions were made, Elinor’s first impression settled quickly. The elder Miss Steele, nearly thirty, was very plain, and her face had that want—not merely of beauty, but of sensibility—which no civility can conceal. The younger, two or three and twenty, had considerable prettiness: features that were light and pleasing, a sharp quick eye, a smartness of air that, though it could not be mistaken for true elegance or grace, gave her a certain distinctness, like a brisk spice that is noticed even when it is not delicate.

Both were particularly civil, and Elinor, watching rather than speaking, soon allowed them credit for some kind of sense—at least the sense of self-interest—when she observed with what constant, judicious attention they made themselves agreeable to Lady Middleton. Their praise fell exactly where her vanity was hungriest. They were in continual raptures with the children—extolling their beauty, courting their notice, humouring their whims with a perseverance that might have passed for devotion, had it not been so obviously strategic. When not required to applaud an infant’s shout or fetch back a toy flung across the room, they admired whatever her ladyship happened to be doing—if she happened to be doing anything—or they begged patterns of some new dress, and talked as if yesterday’s ribbon were an invention.

Lady Middleton, like many fond mothers, was insatiable in her thirst for admiration and boundless in her credulity. Her demands were enormous; but she would swallow any offering, however coarsely prepared, so long as it was sweetened with praise. She watched with complacency the encroachments and tricks to which her cousins submitted. She saw their sashes untied, their hair pulled about their ears, their work-bags searched, their knives and scissors stolen; and she never suspected that endurance could be anything but enjoyment.

“John is in such spirits to-day!” she said, when her eldest boy seized Miss Steele’s pocket-handkerchief and threw it out of the window. “He is full of monkey tricks.”

Soon after, when the second boy pinched one of the same lady’s fingers with all the eager strength of a child discovering power, Lady Middleton observed tenderly, “How playful William is!”

“And here is my sweet little Annamaria,” she added, gathering her three-year-old daughter to her with complacent affection. “And she is always so gentle and quiet—never was there such a quiet little thing!”

At that very moment, a pin in Lady Middleton’s head-dress, shifting as she bent, scratched the child’s neck. The pattern of gentleness answered with screams so violent and piercing that the room seemed suddenly all noise: a shrill, raw note cutting through the softer domestic sounds—the crackle of the fire, the rustle of muslin, the distant clink of tea-things—until everything else was drowned.

The mother’s consternation was extreme; and it could not exceed the alarm of the Miss Steeles, who flung themselves into sympathy as if sympathy were a duty to be performed with dispatch. All three did everything affection could suggest. The child was seated on her mother’s lap and covered with kisses; her tiny wound was bathed with lavender-water by one Miss Steele, kneeling as if before an altar; her mouth was stuffed with sugar-plums by the other, who searched quickly for the brightest, the sweetest, the most bribing. Rewarded so richly for her tears, Annamaria was too wise to stop. She screamed and sobbed with renewed lustiness, kicked her brothers when they offered to touch her, and rejected, with noisy contempt, all attempts at soothing.

At last Lady Middleton remembered that in a similar distress the week before, some apricot marmalade had been applied with great success to a bruised temple. The same remedy was proposed now with eager faith; and the child’s brief pause, upon hearing the word, gave them hope. She was carried from the room in her mother’s arms in search of this “medicine.” The boys chose to follow, though their mother earnestly begged them to stay behind; and in their wake the house seemed, for a moment, to exhale. The room, which had not known quiet for hours, fell into a sudden hush so deep that Elinor could hear the soft settling of fabric, the faint ticking of a clock, and, beyond the windows, the mild rural chorus: a wind brushing bare branches, a distant farm-dog answering another, and the steady, uncommercial music of life unforced.

“Poor little creatures!” said the elder Miss Steele, as soon as they were gone. “It might have been a very sad accident.”

“Yet I hardly know how,” cried Marianne, her eyes bright with impatience, “unless it had been under totally different circumstances. But this is the usual way of heightening alarm, where there is nothing to be alarmed at in reality.”

“What a sweet woman Lady Middleton is!” said Lucy Steele.

Marianne was silent. She could not say what she did not feel, even when the occasion was trivial. The entire burden of civility—of those small untruths which oil the wheels of society—fell, as it always did, to Elinor. She did what she could, praising Lady Middleton with more warmth than she truly felt, though with far less than Lucy’s extravagant zeal.

“And Sir John too,” cried Miss Steele, the elder, “what a charming man he is!”

Here Elinor’s commendation, being simple and just, came without any sparkle. She merely observed that he was perfectly good-humoured and friendly.

“And what a charming little family they have! I never saw such fine children in my life.—I declare I quite doat upon them already, and indeed I am always distractedly fond of children.”

“I should guess so,” said Elinor, with a smile, “from what I have witnessed this morning.”

“I have a notion,” said Lucy, “you think the little Middletons rather too much indulged; perhaps they may be the outside of enough; but it is so natural in Lady Middleton; and for my part, I love to see children full of life and spirits; I cannot bear them if they are tame and quiet.”

“I confess,” replied Elinor, “that while I am at Barton Park, I never think of tame and quiet children with any abhorrence.”

A short pause followed. The air seemed to settle again into domestic stillness, like flour after it has been shaken. Miss Steele, who appeared eager for talk, broke it rather abruptly.

“And how do you like Devonshire, Miss Dashwood? I suppose you were very sorry to leave Sussex.”

Elinor, surprised by the familiarity of the question—or at least by the manner of it—answered that she was.

“Norland is a prodigious beautiful place, is not it?” added Miss Steele.

“We have heard Sir John admire it excessively,” said Lucy, as if she felt an apology was needed for her sister’s freedom.

“I think every one must admire it,” replied Elinor, “who ever saw the place; though it is not to be supposed that any one can estimate its beauties as we do.”

“And had you a great many smart beaux there? I suppose you have not so many in this part of the world; for my part, I think they are a vast addition always.”

“But why should you think,” said Lucy, colouring with mortification, “that there are not as many genteel young men in Devonshire as Sussex?”

“Nay, my dear, I’m sure I don’t pretend to say that there an’t. I’m sure there’s a vast many smart beaux in Exeter; but, you know, how could I tell what smart beaux there might be about Norland? And I was only afraid the Miss Dashwoods might find it dull at Barton if they had not so many as they used to have. But perhaps you young ladies may not care about the beaux, and had as lief be without them as with them. For my part, I think they are vastly agreeable, provided they dress smart and behave civil. But I can’t bear to see them dirty and nasty. Now there’s Mr. Rose at Exeter, a prodigious smart young man, quite a beau, clerk to Mr. Simpson, you know, and yet if you do but meet him of a morning, he is not fit to be seen. I suppose your brother was quite a beau, Miss Dashwood, before he married, as he was so rich?”

“Upon my word,” replied Elinor, “I cannot tell you, for I do not perfectly comprehend the meaning of the word. But this I can say, that if he ever was a beau before he married, he is one still, for there is not the smallest alteration in him.”

“Oh! dear! one never thinks of married men’s being beaux—they have something else to do.”

“Lord! Anne,” cried Lucy, “you can talk of nothing but beaux—you will make Miss Dashwood believe you think of nothing else.” And, eager to turn the discourse, she began admiring the house and furniture again, as if objects at least could be praised without offence.

This first specimen of the Miss Steeles was enough for Elinor. The vulgar freedom and folly of the elder offered no recommendation; and Elinor, not blinded by the younger’s prettiness or her sharp look into mistaking smartness for refinement, saw too plainly the want of real elegance and artlessness beneath her civil exterior. She left Barton Park without any desire to know them better.

The Miss Steeles, however, had come from Exeter well provisioned with admiration, as if it were packed in their trunks for convenient use. Sir John, his family, all his relations—every person and thing connected with Barton Park—was praised with unmeasured readiness. To his fair cousins they dealt out no niggardly portion. Elinor and Marianne were declared the most beautiful, elegant, accomplished, agreeable girls they had ever beheld; and they professed themselves particularly anxious to be better acquainted.

And better acquainted, Elinor soon found, was to be their unavoidable fate. Sir John was entirely on the side of the Miss Steeles; his party was too strong for opposition. That kind of intimacy must be submitted to which consists of sitting an hour or two together in the same room almost every day—an intimacy measured not by sympathy or trust, but by proximity and repetition. Sir John could do no more; and he did not suspect that any more might be required. To be together, in his opinion, was to be intimate; and while his schemes for their meeting succeeded, he never doubted the friendship was established.

To do him justice, he did everything in his power to promote unreserve—by making the Miss Steeles acquainted with whatever he knew, or imagined, of his cousins’ situations in the most delicate particulars. Elinor had not seen them more than twice before the elder Miss Steele wished her joy on her sister’s having been so lucky as to make a conquest of a very smart beau since coming to Barton.

“’Twill be a fine thing to have her married so young, to be sure,” said she; “and I hear he is quite a beau, and prodigious handsome. And I hope you may have as good luck yourself soon—but perhaps you may have a friend in the corner already.”

Elinor could not doubt the source. Sir John was no more cautious in proclaiming his suspicions of her regard for Edward than he had been about Marianne. Indeed it was his favourite joke of the two, being newer, more conjectural, and therefore more amusing to him. Since Edward’s visit they had scarcely dined together without Sir John drinking to her “best affections” with such significant nods and winks as to draw every eye. The letter F— had been invariably produced and paraded, wrung for its merriment until its character as the wittiest letter in the alphabet was, for Sir John, firmly established.

The Miss Steeles now shared all the benefit of these jokes; and in the elder they raised a curiosity to know the name of the gentleman hinted at—a curiosity expressed so impertinently, so persistently, that it only matched her general habit of prying into other people’s affairs as if their private histories were recipes she meant to copy.

But Sir John did not long sport with the curiosity he delighted to raise; he had almost as much pleasure in telling the name as Miss Steele had in hearing it.

“His name is Ferrars,” said he, in a very audible whisper; “but pray do not tell it, for it’s a great secret.”

“Ferrars!” repeated Miss Steele. “Mr. Ferrars is the happy man, is he? What! your sister-in-law’s brother, Miss Dashwood? A very agreeable young man, to be sure; I know him very well.”

“How can you say so, Anne?” cried Lucy, quick as ever to correct, to soften, to amend. “Though we have seen him once or twice at my uncle’s, it is rather too much to pretend to know him very well.”

Elinor heard this with a start of attention that she took pains not to show. An uncle? Where did he live? How came they acquainted? The careless way in which Miss Steele had spoken of Edward struck her as something more than mere folly; it sounded edged, almost ill-natured—like a word pronounced in a dialect meant to dominate a conversation, to imply familiarity and power.

She wished the subject continued; yet she would not join in it herself. But nothing more was said. Mrs. Jennings, for the first time in Elinor’s experience, seemed deficient either in curiosity for petty information or in a desire to communicate it. The moment passed; the room returned to common talk; and though Sir John continued to bandy the name of Ferrars openly, the elder Miss Steele made no further remark, and Lucy let the matter drop with a composure that only sharpened Elinor’s uneasiness.
