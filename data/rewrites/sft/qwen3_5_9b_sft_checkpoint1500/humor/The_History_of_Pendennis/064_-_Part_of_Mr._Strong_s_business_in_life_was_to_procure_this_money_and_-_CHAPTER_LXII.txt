Not long after Colonel Altamont's stroke of luck at Epsom, he set sail for Europe aboard the *Soho*, bound for Antwerp. The gossip columns of London Bridge duly noted his departure alongside Sir Robert and Lady Hodge, the Kewsyes, Major Coddy, and others of the fashionable fraternity. Colonel Altamont traveled in high style, clad in a sumptuous traveling suit, immune to seasickness thanks to his steady consumption of brandy-and-water, and accompanied by his devoted Irish body-servant, a legionary who had served him faithfully since the days of their shared lodging at Shepherd's Inn.

Before boarding, the Chevalier treated his departing friend to a lavish dinner at Blackwall. Amidst the clinking glasses and toasts to Altamont's health, the Chevalier's companion leaned in with a conspiratorial grin. "Strong, old boy," he urged, "if you're in need of a trifle, now is your hour. I'm your man. You've always been a true friend, and a twenty-pound note wouldn't hurt me a whit."

Strong, however, waved the offer away with a nonchalant shrug. "No, I'm flush," he declared, puffing his chest out. "Quite flush—though not quite flush enough to repay your last loan, Altamont, but sufficient to keep me moving for a while." With a greeting that was cordial enough but lacked deep warmth, they parted ways. One might wonder if money truly had softened Altamont's edges, making him more honest and amiable, or if it simply made him appear so in Strong's eyes. Perhaps it was the sheer radiance of wealth that blinded Strong to the Chevalier's flaws. Inside himself, Strong muttered a defense of his friend: *"This poor devil, this unlucky outcast returned from the penal colonies, is ten times the gentleman that Sir Francis Clavering ever was. He possesses pluck and a strange sort of honesty. He sticks to friends and faces enemies. The other fellow lacks the courage to do either."*

Strong harbored a lingering guilt about the lie he'd told Altamont—that he wasn't desperate for cash. It was a "truthful lie," after all; Strong could manage, albeit precariously. Besides, Clavering had promised funds, and the Chevalier, being of an optimistic disposition, pinned his hopes on intercepting stray payments meant for his patron, where Mr. Strong's business skills would surely shine.

Life at Shepherd's Inn grew gloomy without Altamont. The solitude grated on Strong's social nature, and he found himself indulging in extravagant habits, employing a servant to polish his boots and prepare his meals. It became a somewhat tragicomic sight to watch the portly gentleman meticulously painting his own leather and broiling his own chops. The Chevalier, who had recently returned from Spain after a brief and turbulent union with a lady from Vittoria—who had famously ended their engagement by breaking his skull with a dishware item—began to ponder returning to Juanita. His spirits sank, a condition the Chevalier termed being "down on his luck." He reflected on how every hero, from Marius to Napoleon, faced periods of ill fortune.

Meanwhile, Sir Francis Clavering remained in dire straits. The twenty-five pounds ("the pony") he had extracted from Altamont had vanished as quickly as his previous loans. Downriver, he had joined a party of sportsmen who evaded the police to land in Essex, where they staged a boxing match between Billy Bluck and a cabman. The betting favored the cabman three to one, yet Clavering, confident in his backing, lost miserably. Worse still, his debt to a man named Lightfoot mounted to fourteen pounds, a sum compounded by Lightfoot's relentless demands. Clavering, cursing the situation, begged Strong for help, promising half of whatever he could squeeze from his wife, or anyone else.

"Ned," Strong responded sternly, "I gave my word of honor as a gentleman never to participate in any further bill transactions involving your husband. My wife and I have given ours, too." The Chevalier, true to his word, vowed he would polish his own boots for the rest of his days rather than break his promise. He further declared his intention to warn Lady Clavering immediately if Clavering showed any sign of betraying his marital vows.

True to form, Sir Francis erupted in a torrent of curses and tears, swearing that death was his only escape. As he descended the stairs of Shepherd's Inn, he transformed from a sobbing beggar into a furious invective, calling Strong the "infernal villain," "traitor," and "blackguard" under the sun, wishing him buried in hell, yet paradoxically wishing him alive to endure Clavering's future vengeance.

Inside Strong's chambers, however, the atmosphere shifted to something lighter. Enter Mr. Samuel Huxter, a young gentleman of Clavering's acquaintance. Huxter, who had once poached walnuts in Clavering Park and witnessed the Baronet's ostentatious display of four-horse carriages, approached Strong with immense respect and trepidation. He introduced himself as a "Clavering man," detailing his father's service to Sir Francis's keeper, Coxwood, whose gun accident had cost him three fingers. Sir Francis received this tribute with affability, and Huxter delighted in recounting tales of the Baronet's magnificence to the lads at Bartholomew's.

At the lodge, a young maid named Fanny listened with wide-eyed skepticism. "Sir Francis is an ojus creature," she declared, shaking her head. "He's wicked, low, and mean!" When Sam insisted that the Baronet was affable and had kindly borrowed half a sovereign from her, Fanny burst into laughter. She tugged at Sam's long, unwashed hair, patted his chin, and labeled him a "stoopid, old foolish stoopid," noting that the Baronet had been turned away twice by her mistress, Mar, only to return three months later for seven shillings.

"Don't say 'er' but 'her'," Sam corrected, pointing out grammatical errors.
"Well then, 'her,' and 'borrow,' and 'haftually'..." Fanny retorted, and the grammar lesson concluded with Sam's face twisted in a pretty grimace, leaving the grammar master appeased and eager for more tuition.

Mrs. Bolton, ever present, observed the scene with a mixture of amusement and resignation. Time, she felt, had brought Fanny certain consolations, softening the bitter regrets of her youth, reducing them to the level of a toothache—a temporary pang, but ultimately transient.

Strong sat reflecting on the ironies of life. Surrounded by respect and affection in his old age, he mused on the inevitable transfer of glory to the successor. The servants bowed to the son as they bowed to the father; the tenants shouted for the heir. Prosperity and flattery, like sunlight, followed the estate. A great man must keep his heir close, a living *memento mori*. If life held too much sway, the presence of the heir became a constant sting and reminder of mortality. "Make ready to go," the successor seemed to whisper, "I am waiting. And I could hold it as well as you."

Yet, amidst these reflections, Strong turned to the thoughts of Pen and Warrington. They often spoke of Pen's mother, a saint canonized in their memories. Lucky was the man who knew such a woman, a providential gift of innocence, love, and beauty. But just as they drifted into sentimentality, the public intruded, stepping in to halt their confidential chat and remind them of the gritty realities of the world they inhabited.

Arthur Pendennis, newly arrived with his inheritance, initially behaved with modesty and equanimity, earning Warrington's praise. However, his Uncle Major Pendennis disapproved of his nephew's lack of pomp. The Major wanted Arthur installed in handsome quarters, riding showy hacks in cabriolets. "I am too absent-minded," Arthur laughed, "to drive a cab in London; the omnibus would cut me in two. And you wouldn't have me driven about by a servant like an apothecary, uncle?"

"No, never!" the Major protested indignantly. "The representative of the House of Pendennis must not demean himself. You're like the fellow in Sterne—the Marquis demanding his sword back. Your father got it restored for you. You are a man of landed estate, by Gad, sir, and a gentleman—never forget you are a gentleman."

Arthur playfully turned the tables, asking who cared about his "paltry means" in the society he frequented. "It would be absurd to compete with the great folks; all they require is a decent address and good manners."

"But I should belong to a better club," the uncle argued, "give occasional dinners, and escape that horrid garret in the Temple." Compromised, Arthur moved to the second floor of Lamb Court, remaining close to Warrington, who still occupied his old quarters. The two friends resolved never to separate, sharing chambers and a servant, a bond forged in the generosity of youth.

"Young men laugh and talk freely before them. The girl sees people she has no right to know." He accused them of being contaminated by bad company.
