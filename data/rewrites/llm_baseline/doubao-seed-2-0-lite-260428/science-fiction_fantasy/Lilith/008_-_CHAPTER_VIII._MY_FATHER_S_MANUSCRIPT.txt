Awe clings to every word I am about to set down, heavy as sea fog. The sun hangs gilded high above the rooftops, the sea stretches unbroken and sapphire under its gaze, the same old world pushes grass up through the soil and sends larks darting through the air I have breathed every day since I was a babe. But I know now all that outspread splendour is no more than a painted drop-scene on a stage, and at any moment it could be lifted away to reveal something far more strange, far more beautiful, beneath.

Not long after my father was laid to rest, I sat in the library one drowsy morning. I had been staring listlessly up at the portrait hung between the oak bookshelves, a man whose face I had always known only as that of some distant forgotten ancestor, and idly wishing I could learn even a sliver of the life he had lived. I pulled a random volume from the shelf and began to read, but when I glanced up a minute later, he was there: a thin, pale man in threadbare rusty black, stepping not from the direction of the door, but from the gap between me and that very portrait.

He was sharp-eyed, eager, with a long, prominent nose that made me think at once of the chipped earthen jug my sisters used to keep in the scullery, the one they’d nicknamed Mr. Crow.
“Finding myself in your vicinity, Mr. Vane, I thought I’d take the liberty of calling,” he said, his voice odd but soft, not unpleasant at all. “Your honoured grandfather treated me as a friend, you know—no presumption to say so, I should think, seeing I was his father’s librarian, raised alongside him from childhood.”
It didn’t even strike me then how impossibly old that would make him. “Forgive me, but where do you make your home now, Mr. Crow?” I said.

He smiled, amused. “You almost hit my name right on the head—shows the old Vane family insight. You have seen me before, once, when you were far too small to remember what you’d heard that day.”
“Where was that?”
“Right here in this very room. You could not have been more than four years old.”
I groped for the memory, half-sure I caught a flicker of it at the edge of my mind, and begged him to tell me his true name.
“There is such a thing as holding a memory without recognising it for what it is,” he remarked. “As for my name—you are close enough already—once it was Raven.”
I had heard that name, of course. Wild, half-forgotten tales of a strange, shadowy librarian had drifted down through the family for generations.

“It is so kind of you to call,” I said. “Won’t you sit?”
He sank into the nearest chair at once. “You knew my father then, I presume?”
“I knew him,” he said, a little odd, dry smile tugging at his mouth. “But he had no taste for my acquaintance, and we never once spoke. That man up there, though—” He nodded at the portrait on the wall. “Old Sir Up’ard, that’s what his villagers called him—he was a far closer friend to me than even your grandfather ever became.”
That was when I first began to think the interview was far stranger than any ordinary visitor call. But after all, it was no more odd for this man to remember Sir Upward, a man who had lived three generations back, than it was to claim he had been my great-grandfather’s personal librarian.

“I owe him a great deal,” he went on. “For all I’d read ten times more books than he ever had, his peculiar, focused line of study showed me a relationship between modes of being I never would have found on my own, or learned from anyone else alive back then.
“Would you tell me all of that story?” I asked.
“By all means—at least, as much as I am able. There is no such thing as a secret kept for no reason,” he said, and began to recount the tale.
“Sir Upward kept his personal library in that old closet over there, a hundred handwritten manuscripts or so, for printed books were a new, rare thing back then. One morning I sat in there cataloguing them, when he poked his head around the door and said ‘Come.’ I set down my pen and followed him: across the great hall, down a steep, rough stone stair, and along an underground passage to a small tower he’d just finished building. It was nothing but a winding stone stair and a single room at the very top. The door to that room had a massive, ancient lock, which he undid with the tiniest key I had ever seen in my life.
“I had barely crossed the threshold after him, when my eyes played a trick: he began to dwindle, shrinking smaller and smaller, until suddenly my vision snapped back to clarity, and I saw he was not shrinking at all, but moving away from me at impossible speed. In a minute he was no bigger than a speck on the horizon, with the blue peaks of mountains rising behind him against a sky of paler, softer blue. I knew that country well—for I had travelled back and forth to it dozens of times before, though I’d never found this particular shortcut to it.
“Many years later, long after that tower had crumbled to dust, I taught one of his descendants the same secret Sir Upward taught me. To this day, I still use your house when I want to take the shortest way home. Without your leave, I apologise, I must have earned a right of way through it by now—not from front door to back door, you understand, but from the bottom of it to the top.”

“So you are telling me, Mr. Raven,” I said, “that you walk straight through my house into another world, ignoring all the space that should separate the two?”
“That I walk through it is proof space exists, not that I defy it,” the old librarian replied.
“Please don’t quibble, Mr. Raven,” I said. “You know exactly what I mean.”
“There is a door hidden in your house, one single step through which carries me into a world that is entirely other than this one.”
“Is it a better world?”
“Not all of it. It is so different, in fact, that most of its physical laws, and many of its mental ones, do not work the way they do here. Moral laws, though—those are fundamentally the same, everywhere.”
“You stretch my belief almost to breaking,” I said.
“You think I’m a madman, then?”
“You do not look like one.”
“A liar, then?”
“You have given me no reason to think that.”
“You just don’t believe me?”
“I will step through that door with you right now, if you like. I trust you enough to risk the journey.”
“The blunder all my children make,” he murmured, half to himself. “The only door out is the door in.”

I began to wonder if he was the mad one. He sat silent for a minute, elbow propped on the table, head resting on his hand, his eyes fixed on the spines of the books across the table.
“A book,” he said, louder now, “is a door in—and therefore a door out. I see old Sir Up’ard before my eyes now,” he went on, closing them, his tone softening. “My heart swells with love for him. What world is he in, then?”
“The world of your heart,” I answered. “The idea of him lives there, that’s all.”
“Then there is at least one world your front hall door does not open into, is there not?”
“I will grant you that much. But the things in that world are not real things, you cannot hold them in your hands.”
“Think a little further,” he said. “Did anything ever truly become yours, that you did not first bring into that inner world of thought and feeling? That line of thinking is too far beyond you for now, though. Let me tell you—there are more worlds, and more doors to them, than you could imagine in a hundred years of thinking.”

He stood then, left the library, crossed the hall, and walked straight for the garret stair, moving with the ease of a man who knew every inch of the house better than I did. I followed him, staring at his back: his long dark hair hung straight and glossy down past his shoulders, his wide black coat pooled almost at his heels, his shoes looked far too big for his feet.
Light seeped in around the edges of the great slate roofing slabs up in the garret, showing gaps where no flooring had ever been laid, so we had to step carefully from one exposed joist to the next. Right in the middle of one of those open gaps stood a rough wooden partition, with a plain door set into it. I followed Mr. Raven through it into a small, dim chamber, its walls slanting upwards to meet at the sloped roof above.
“That is the door I told you of,” he said, pointing to a full-length oblong mirror propped against the far wall, standing right on the bare floor.
I stepped in front of it, and saw our two figures dimly reflected in its dusty, tarnished glass. Something about it made my skin prickle, uneasy. It looked old, forgotten, but for all its ordinary appearance, the carved eagle perched on its top edge, wings spread wide, looked somehow menacing.
“As a mirror,” the librarian said, “it has grown dull with age. That does not matter. Its clearness does not depend on its glass—it depends on the light.”
“Light?” I said. “There is hardly any light in here at all!”
He did not answer. Instead he reached up and pulled a small brass chain hanging on the opposite wall. A slow, heavy creak echoed through the room: the entire sloped top of the chamber was turning, revolving slowly. He paused, checked his pocket watch, then pulled the chain again.
“We are perfectly on time,” he said. “It is almost exactly noon.”
The roof creaked and turned for another minute, then he pulled two more chains, one after the other, before returning to the first. A moment later the chamber flooded with light: a perfect patch of noonday sun fell on to a small polished mirror set in the opposite wall, and I could see its reflected ray cutting across the dust motes straight to the big floor mirror. But no reflection came back. The light did not bounce off the glass— it passed right through it, and there was no second patch of sunlight anywhere on the floor of the chamber.
“Where did the sun rays go?” I cried.
“I cannot say,” Mr. Raven answered. “Perhaps they went back to wherever they first came from. I think, now, they belong to a sense we have not even developed yet.”
He started to talk then, about the strange links between mind and matter, between our senses and the hidden qualities of the world, in terms I could barely follow, then moved on to stranger things still I could not understand at all. He spoke of dimensions far more than the three we know, some of them tied to powers that lie dormant inside every human being, that we have not even guessed exist yet. But his words landed on me no better than that sunlight landed on the mirror— I thought, foolishly, that he could not possibly know what he was talking about.

Then, all at once, I realised our reflections were gone from the big mirror. The glass was filled not with our faces, but thick white mist. As I stared, the mist began to clear, and I saw the peaks of a high mountain range slowly rising into view, growing sharper and sharper. The mist vanished entirely a moment later, leaving a wide, windswept heath stretched out before me. Far off on its surface, the figure of a man was moving swiftly away.
I turned to speak to my companion. He was not there beside me.
I looked back at the mirror, and I saw that wide black coat flapping in the wind that blew on that far heath, his dark hair lifting in a gale that did not touch me at all where I stood in the garret.
Terror seized me, and I turned and ran from that dark room as fast as my legs could carry me.
