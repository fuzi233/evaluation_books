No sooner had my mistress left us than I had a moment to turn my thoughts back to Sergeant Cuff. I found him tucked into a snug corner of the hall, poring over his memorandum book, the corners of his mouth twisted up in that sour, vicious little curl of his I knew so well.

“Making notes on the case?” I asked.

“No,” said the Sergeant. “Checking what my next professional engagement happens to be.”

“Oh,” I said. “So you think it’s all wrapped up here, then?”

“I think,” answered Sergeant Cuff, “that Lady Verinder is one of the cleverest women in all England. I also think a rose is far better worth looking at than a diamond. Where is the gardener, Mr. Betteredge?”

Not another word could I drag out of him about the Moonstone. He’d lost every scrap of interest in his own inquiry, and was hell-bent only on hunting down our gardener. An hour later, I heard the two of them shouting at each other in the conservatory, with that accursed dog-rose right at the root of the whole quarrel.

Meantime, I had business of my own to see to: confirming whether Mr. Franklin still planned to leave on the afternoon train. Once he’d heard the full account of the closed-door talk with Lady Verinder and how it had ended, he immediately changed his mind, deciding to wait for news to come back from Frizinghall. This perfectly natural shift in plans — which for any ordinary person would have amounted to nothing at all — had one thoroughly annoying outcome for Mr. Franklin. It left him adrift, with nothing but empty idle time on his hands, and that was all it took for every last one of his mixed, foreign sides of character to come spilling out, one right after another, like rats bolting out of a sack.

One minute he was acting like an Italian-Englishman, the next a German-Englishman, the next a French-Englishman, drifting in and out of every sitting room in the house, with nothing to ramble on about but the way Miss Rachel had snubbed him, and nobody to vent to but me. I found him, for example, in the library, sat right under the map of Modern Italy, with no clue how to cope with his heartache other than talk it to death. “I’ve got all kinds of worthy aspirations, Betteredge — but what am I supposed to do with them now? I’m full of dormant good qualities, if only Rachel would have helped me bring them out!”

He was so eloquent painting the picture of his own neglected merits, and so pathetic when he finished lamenting over it, that I was at my wits’ end trying to think how to console him — till it occurred to me there was no better cure for this kind of mood than a wholesome dose of *Robinson Crusoe*. I hobbled off to my room, hobbled back with that immortal book under my arm, and found the library empty. The map of Modern Italy stared back at me; I stared right back at it.

I tried the drawing room. His handkerchief was on the floor, proof he’d drifted in — and the empty room was proof he’d drifted straight back out again. I tried the dining room, and found Samuel stood there with a biscuit and glass of sherry, staring blankly at thin air. A minute before, Mr. Franklin had rung furiously for a small light refreshment. Samuel had dashed to bring it to him, and Mr. Franklin had vanished before the bell downstairs had even finished ringing from the hard yank he’d given it.

I found him at last in the morning room, stood at the window drawing messy hieroglyphics with his finger in the condensation on the glass. “Your sherry’s waiting for you, sir,” I said. I might as well have talked to one of the four walls; he was sunk so deep in his own tangled thoughts there was no pulling him back to the surface. “How do *you* explain Rachel’s conduct, Betteredge?” was the only answer I got.

Not having that answer ready, I pulled out *Robinson Crusoe*, which I remain fully convinced holds the solution to every mystery if you just search long enough for it. Mr. Franklin shut the book on the spot, and immediately launched into that muddled German-English gibberish of his. “Why not look into it?” he said, like I’d personally objected to the idea. “Why the devil lose your patience, Betteredge, when patience is all you need to get at the truth? Don’t interrupt me. Rachel’s conduct is perfectly intelligible, if you just do her the common decency to take the Objective view first, the Subjective view next, and the Objective-Subjective view to wrap things up. What do we know? We know the loss of the Moonstone last Thursday morning threw her into a state of nervous excitement she hasn’t recovered from yet. Do you mean to deny the Objective view, so far? Very well, then — don’t interrupt me. Now, when a person’s in that state of frayed nerves, how can you expect her to behave the way she normally would to anyone around her? Arguing that way, from the inside out, what do we land at? The Subjective view. I defy you to contradict it. Very well then — what follows? Good Heavens, the Objective-Subjective explanation follows, of course! Rachel, properly speaking, isn’t *Rachel* right now — she’s Somebody Else. Do I mind being treated cruelly by Somebody Else? You’re unreasonable enough, Betteredge, but you can hardly accuse me of that. So where does that leave us? It leaves me perfectly happy and comfortable, in spite of your confounded narrow English prejudice. Where’s the sherry?”

By that point my head was so muddled I wasn’t entirely sure if it was my own head or Mr. Franklin’s. In that terrible state, I somehow managed to pull off three very Objective acts: I fetched Mr. Franklin his sherry, I retreated to my own quarters, and I soothed myself with the most calming pipe of tobacco I have ever smoked in my life.

Don’t imagine I got rid of Mr. Franklin that easily, though. Adrift again, he wandered out of the morning room and into the hall, then found his way to the servants’ wing, smelled my pipe smoke, and suddenly remembered he’d been fool enough to give up smoking entirely for Miss Rachel’s sake. In the blink of an eye he burst in on me, cigar case in hand, and went off on that same everlasting subject, this time in his neat, witty, cynical French way. “Give me a light, Betteredge. Is it conceivable a man can smoke as long as I have and never work out that there’s a full system for handling women hidden right in his cigar case? Follow me carefully, I’ll prove it in two words. You pick a cigar, try it, and it disappoints you. What do you do? Throw it away and try another. Now see the parallel! You pick a woman, try her, and she breaks your heart. Fool! Take a lesson from your cigar case. Throw her away and try another!”

I shook my head at that. Clever as it no doubt was, my own experience ran dead against it. “Back when the late Mrs. Betteredge was alive,” I said, “I felt many a time tempted to try your philosophy, Mr. Franklin. But the law says you have to smoke that cigar once you’ve chosen it, sir.” I winked to drive the point home. Mr. Franklin burst out laughing, and we were as merry as crickets till the next weird facet of his character turned up in due course. That’s how things went between the two of us — while the Sergeant and our gardener were still at each other’s throats over roses — passing the time before news came back from Frizinghall.

The pony chaise returned a full half hour earlier than I’d dared to expect it. My lady had decided to stay on, for the time being, at her sister’s house. The groom brought back two letters from his mistress: one addressed to Mr. Franklin, the other to me. I sent Franklin’s letter on to him in the library, where his wanderings had led him for the second time. My own letter I read in my room. A cheque fell out the second I opened it, and before I’d even digested the written words, I knew Sergeant Cuff was well and truly dismissed from the Moonstone inquiry.

I sent word to the conservatory asking the Sergeant to come speak to me right away. He appeared, his mind still full of the gardener and the dog-rose, swearing no man alive or dead had ever been as stubborn as Mr. Begbie. I asked him to set that trivial nonsense aside and pay full attention to a serious matter. That was enough to pull him out of his daze, and he noticed the letter in my hand.

“Ah,” he said, all worn out. “You’ve heard from her ladyship. Have I anything to do with that, Mr. Betteredge?”

“You can judge for yourself, Sergeant.” I proceeded to read him the letter, with all the emphasis and discretion it deserved:
> I ask you to inform Sergeant Cuff that I kept the promise I made to him. This is the result, so far as Rosanna Spearman is concerned. Miss Verinder solemnly declares she has never spoken a private word to Rosanna since that unhappy woman first entered our house. They never met, not even by accident, on the night the Diamond was lost, and no communication of any kind passed between them from the Thursday morning the alarm was first raised in the house, to this present Saturday afternoon, when Miss Verinder left us. After I told my daughter suddenly, and in so many plain words, of Rosanna Spearman’s suicide — this is what came of it.

I looked up when I reached that point, and asked Sergeant Cuff what he made of the letter so far. “I’d only offend you if I spoke my mind,” he answered. “Go on, Mr. Betteredge. Go on.”

Remembering this man had the audacity to complain about our gardener’s stubbornness, my tongue itched to rewrite his words for him. But my good old Christian resolve held firm. I went on reading steadily:
> Having appealed to Miss Verinder in the way the officer thought best, I next spoke to her in the way I thought most likely to impress her. Twice, before my daughter left this house, I warned her privately that she was exposing herself to suspicion of the most unbearable, degrading kind. I have now told her, in the plainest terms, that those fears have come true.
> Her answer, on her own solemn oath, is as clear as words can make it. First, she owes no private money to any living person. Second, the Diamond is not now, and never has been, in her possession, after she put it in her cabinet on Wednesday night.
> The confidence my daughter has placed in me goes no further than this. She stays stubbornly silent when I ask her to explain the Diamond’s disappearance. She refuses, through tears, when I beg her to speak out for my sake. “The day will come when you will know why I am careless of being suspected, and why I am silent even to you. I have done much to make my mother pity me — nothing to make my mother blush for me.” Those are my daughter’s own words.
> After all that has passed between the officer and me, I think — stranger as he is — he should be made acquainted with what Miss Verinder has said, just as you are. Read him my letter, then place the enclosed cheque in his hands. In ending all further claim on his services, I have only to say I am convinced of his honesty and his intelligence — but I am more certain than ever that the circumstances of this case have fatally misled him.

That was the end of the letter. Before I handed over the cheque, I asked Sergeant Cuff if he had any comment to make. “It’s no part of my duty, Mr. Betteredge,” he answered, “to pass remarks on a case I’ve finished with.” I tossed the cheque across the table to him. “Do you believe that last part of her ladyship’s letter?” I said, indignantly.

The Sergeant looked at the cheque, lifted his dreary eyebrows in acknowledgement of her ladyship’s generosity. “This is such a generous estimate of the value of my time,” he said, “that I feel bound to give something back for it. I’ll bear the amount of this cheque in mind, Mr. Betteredge, when the right occasion comes round.”

“What do you mean?” I asked.

“Her ladyship has smoothed things over very cleverly for the present,” said the Sergeant. “But this particular family scandal is the kind that bursts right up again when you least expect it. We’ll have more detective work on our hands, sir, before the Moonstone is many months older.”

If those words meant anything, and the way he spoke them meant anything, it all boiled down to this: my mistress’s letter had convinced him that Miss Rachel was hardened enough to resist every appeal, and that she’d lied to her own mother — good God, under what terrible circumstances! I don’t know how anyone else in my shoes would have replied to him. I said plain and simple: “Sergeant Cuff, I consider that last observation an insult to both my lady and her daughter!”

“Mr. Betteredge, if you take it as a warning to yourself, you’ll be far closer to the mark.”

Hot and angry as I was, that calm, unshakable confidence of his shut me right up. I walked to the window to calm myself down. The rain had stopped — and who should I spot in the courtyard but Mr. Begbie, the gardener, waiting outside to pick right back up with Sergeant Cuff on their dog-rose argument. “My compliments to the Sairgent,” said Mr. Begbie the second he laid eyes on me. “If he’s minded to walk to the station, I’m agreeable to go with him.”

“What!” cried the Sergeant, right behind me. “You’re not convinced yet?”

“The de’il a bit am I convinced!” answered Mr. Begbie.

“Then I’ll walk to the station!” says the Sergeant.

“Then I’ll meet you at the gate!” says Mr. Begbie.

I was still furious, as you know — but no man’s temper could hold out against a disruption like that. Sergeant Cuff noticed my mood shift, and leaned in to prod it along. “Come, come! Why not treat my view of the case the same way her ladyship does? Why not say the circumstances have fatally misled me?”

To take things the way my lady took them was a privilege worth enjoying — even if it was Sergeant Cuff offering it to me. I cooled slowly down to my usual level. I regarded any opinion of Miss Rachel that didn’t match my lady’s or my own with lofty contempt. The only thing I couldn’t manage was to drop the subject of the Moonstone! I know my own common sense should have told me to let the matter rest, but there — the virtues the current generation brags about hadn’t been invented back in my day. Sergeant Cuff had hit me right on a raw spot, and even though I looked down on him with contempt, that tender spot still tingled. Before I knew it, I was deliberately dragging him back to the subject of her ladyship’s letter.

“I’m perfectly satisfied myself,” I said. “But never mind that. Go on, act as if I’m still open to being convinced. You think Miss Rachel can’t be trusted to keep her word, and you say we’ll hear more about the Moonstone soon. Back your opinion, Sergeant,” I finished, all casual and airy.

Instead of taking offence, Sergeant Cuff seized my hand and shook it till my fingers ached. “I swear to heaven,” this strange officer said solemnly, “I’d take a job as a domestic servant tomorrow, Mr. Betteredge, if I had a chance to work side by side with you. To say you’re as transparent as a child is paying children a compliment nine out of ten of them don’t even deserve. There, there, we won’t start arguing again. I’ll tell you what I think on easier terms than that. I won’t say a word more about her ladyship, or about Miss Verinder — I’ll just play prophet, for once, and for your sake. I warned you already you haven’t seen the last of the Moonstone. Very well. Now I’ll tell you, as a parting gift, of three things that are going to happen in the future, that will force themselves on your attention whether you like it or not.”

“Go on!” I said, completely unabashed, just as casual as ever.

“First,” said the Sergeant, “you’ll hear something from the Yollands — when the postman delivers Rosanna’s letter at Cobb’s Hole, next Monday.” If he’d thrown a bucket of cold water over my head, I doubt I’d have felt it half as unpleasantly as those words. Miss Rachel’s claim of innocence had left every part of Rosanna’s strange behavior — the new nightgown she’d sewn, the stained nightgown she’d hidden, all the rest of it — completely unexplained. It had never once occurred to me till Sergeant Cuff forced it right to the front of my mind.

“Second,” the Sergeant went on, “you’ll hear of the three Indians again. You’ll hear of them hanging around the neighborhood if Miss Rachel stays here, or in London if she moves to London.” Now that I’d written those three jugglers off and fully convinced myself of my young lady’s innocence, I took this second prophecy easily enough. “That’s two of the three things you say are coming,” I said. “Now the third!”

“Third and last,” said Sergeant Cuff, “sooner or later, you’ll hear something of that London moneylender I’ve mentioned to you twice before. Give me your pocket-book, and I’ll write down his name and address, so there’s no mistake if it really comes to pass.” He scribbled it on a blank leaf: *Mr. Septimus Luker, Middlesex-place, Lambeth, London.*

“There,” he said, pointing to the address. “Those are the last words on the Moonstone I’ll trouble you with for now. Time will show if I’m right or wrong. Meantime, sir, I carry away a real personal liking for you, that I think does both of us credit. If we don’t meet again before I retire from the force, I hope you’ll come visit me at the little house near London I’ve had my eye on. There will be grass walks in my garden, Mr. Betteredge, I promise you. And as for the white moss rose —”
