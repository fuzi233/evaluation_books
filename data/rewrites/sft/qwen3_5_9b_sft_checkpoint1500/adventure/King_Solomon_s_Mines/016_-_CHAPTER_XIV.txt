Within moments, the regiments tasked with the flanking maneuvers vanished into the silence, slipping carefully behind the ridges to mask their approach from Twala's vigilant scouts. An interval of half an hour or more passed before the "wings" of the army stirred, allowing the "Greys"—the vanguard—and their supporting "Buffaloes," which served as the army's chest, to prepare. Both units were nearly pristine in condition and at full strength; the Greys had been held in reserve during the morning's skirmish, suffering minimal losses, while the Buffaloes, positioned as a third defensive line on the left, had barely engaged.

Infadoos, a cautious and seasoned general, utilized this pause to bolster the morale of his men. Speaking in poetic terms, he extolled the honor bestowed upon the Greys by placing them at the forefront of battle, noting the privilege of fighting alongside the Great White Warrior from the Stars. He promised substantial rewards of cattle and promotions to survivors if Ignosi's arms proved victorious.

I gazed down the long lines of waving black plumes and stoic faces, a heavy sigh escaping me. Within an hour, most—if not all—of these magnificent veterans, none older than forty, would lie dead or dying in the dust. It was inevitable, a consequence of the great general's wise recklessness regarding human life, a trait that often saved his forces and achieved his ends. They were condemned to slaughter to secure victory for their cause and the remainder of the army. Foredoomed to death, they knew the truth, yet they never hesitated, nor did a single warrior betray a sign of fear. There they stood, prepared to abandon the light of day forever, contemplating their doom without a tremor. Their absolute devotion to duty and indifference to its bitter fruits struck me as awe-inspiring, a stark contrast to my own uneasy state.

"Behold your king!" Infadoos concluded, pointing to Ignosi. "Fight and fall for him, as is the duty of brave men. Cursed and shameful forever shall be the name of anyone who shrinks from death for his king or turns his back on the foe. Behold your king, chiefs, captains, and soldiers! Pay homage to the Sacred Snake, and then follow on, that Incubu and I may show you the path to the heart of Twala's host."

A moment of silence ensued, broken suddenly by a low murmur rising from the serried phalanxes—a sound akin to the distant whisper of the sea, generated by the gentle tapping of six thousand spear handles against their shields. Slowly, the noise swelled, deepening and widening into a thunderous roar that echoed against the mountains, filling the air with heavy, rolling waves of sound. Then, gradually, it subsided, fading into nothing until the royal salute crashed out.

Ignosi, I realized, must be a proud man that day; no Roman emperor had ever received such a salutation from gladiators "about to die." Acknowledging the homage, he lifted his battle-axe. The Greys then filed off in a triple-line formation, each line comprising approximately one thousand fighting men. Upon advancing roughly five hundred yards, Ignosi took command of the Buffaloes, drawn up in a similar formation, and gave the word to march. Off we went, I praying with all my heart to emerge from this ordeal with my skin intact. Many strange positions I have occupied, but never one so unpleasant, nor one where my chances of survival were so slim.

By the time we reached the edge of the plateau, the Greys were halfway down the slope leading to the tongue of grassland jutting into the mountain's bend. Excitement in Twala's camp below was palpable; regiment after regiment started forward at a swinging trot to reach the root of the tongue before the attackers emerged onto the plains of Loo. This narrow corridor, some four hundred yards deep at its widest point, spanned merely sixty-five paces across at its throat and narrowed to ninety at its tip. As the Greys descended and spread out, they resumed their triple-line formation and halted dead.

Behind the last line of the Greys, about one hundred yards back on slightly higher ground, the Buffaloes took their stand in reserve. From this vantage, we observed Twala's entire force, clearly reinforced since the morning attack and numbering no fewer than forty thousand despite their losses. As they approached the gorge's entrance, they hesitated, realizing only one regiment could advance at a time. There, some seventy yards from the mouth, stood the famous Grey regiment—the pride and glory of the Kukuana army—ready to hold the pass against overwhelming odds, much like the three Romans holding the bridge against thousands.

Their eagerness to cross faded into hesitation. These three grim ranks stood firm and silent. Soon, however, a tall general adorned with nodding ostrich plumes appeared, attended by chiefs and orderlies—I presumed it was Twala himself. He gave an order, and the first regiment, raising a shout, charged forward. The Greys remained perfectly still and silent until the attackers closed to within forty yards, at which point a volley of *tollas* (throwing knives) rattled through their lines.

Suddenly, with a bound and a roar, the Greys sprang forward with uplifted spears, meeting the enemy in deadly strife. The clash of shields rolled like thunder, and the plain seemed alive with flashes of light reflecting from shimmering spears. A surging mass of struggling, stabbing humanity swayed back and forth, but not for long. The attacking lines began to thin, and slowly, inevitably, the Greys passed over them like a great wave heaving over a submerged ridge. That regiment was completely destroyed, though the Greys retained two lines; a third of their number lay dead.

Closing shoulder to shoulder, they halted in silence once more, awaiting the next assault. I was relieved to catch sight of Sir Henry's yellow beard as he moved about, arranging the ranks. He was still alive!

Meanwhile, we moved closer to the scene, which was littered with four thousand prostrate bodies, dead, dying, and wounded, staining the earth red. Ignosi issued an order that no enemy wounded should be killed, a command scrupulously followed. It would have been a horrifying sight if we had time to reflect on it.

Soon, a second regiment, distinguished by white plumes, kilts, and shields, advanced against the two thousand remaining Greys. They waited in ominous silence until the foe was within forty yards, then threw themselves upon them with irresistible force. Again came the terrible roll of meeting shields. The tragedy repeated itself, though this time the issue hung in the balance longer. The attacking regiment, composed of young men, fought with utmost fury, seemingly driven back by sheer weight at first. The slaughter was awful, hundreds falling every minute, punctuated by the hissing undertone of "*S'gee, s'gee*"—the triumphant cry of each victor piercing the body of his fallen foe.

Yet, perfect discipline and unchanging valor wrought miracles. One veteran soldier is worth two youths, as soon became apparent. Just as we thought the Greys were lost, Sir Henry's deep voice rang out through the din, and I glimpsed his circling battle-axe waving high above his plumes. The tide turned instantly. The Greys ceased yielding; they stood like a rock against which the furious waves of spearmen broke repeatedly, only to recoil. Then, they moved forward again. With no firearms to obscure the view, we saw everything clearly. Within a minute, the onslaught grew fainter.

"Ah, these are *men*, indeed; they will conquer again," called out Ignosi, grinding his teeth with excitement at my side. "See, it is done!"

Like puffs of smoke from a cannon's mouth, the attacking regiment broke away in flying groups, their white head-dresses streaming in the wind. They left their opponents victorious, but alas, no longer a regiment. Of the gallant triple line, thirty minutes prior three thousand strong, only six hundred blood-spattered men remained; the rest lay underfoot. Yet, they cheered and waved their spears in triumph. Instead of falling back, they ran forward a hundred yards, seized a rising knoll, and formed a threefold ring around its base. Standing atop the mound for a moment, I saw Sir Henry, apparently unharmed, alongside our old friend Infadoos. Then Twala's regiments rolled down upon the doomed band, and the battle closed in anew.

As readers of this history may gather, I am, to be honest, a bit of a coward, never inclined to fight, though often forced into unpleasant positions and obliged to spill blood. I have always hated it, striving to keep my own blood undiminished, sometimes by a judicious use of my heels. But at this moment, for the first time in my life, my bosom burned with martial ardour. Fragments of warlike legends from "Ingoldsby Legends" and sanguinary verses from the Old Testament sprang to mind like mushrooms in the dark. My blood, hitherto half-frozen with horror, beat fiercely through my veins, igniting a savage desire to kill and spare not. Glancing at the serried ranks of warriors behind us, I wondered if my face now mirrored theirs. There they stood, hands twitching, lips apart, features instinct with the hungry lust of battle, their eyes gleaming like a bloodhound sighting its quarry.

Only Ignosi's heart, judging by his comparative self-possession, seemed to beat as calmly as ever beneath his leopard-skin cloak, though even he ground his teeth. I could bear it no longer
