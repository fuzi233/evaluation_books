In an instant, my soul’s crushing despair was transformed into a surge of overwhelming joy and hope. It felt as though I would soon be reunited with Naani—my beloved, my heart’s true light. But this hope was too bright, too sudden; it could not be fulfilled swiftly. For all I knew, the only certainty was the silhouette of a colossal pyramid rising into the endless night sky.

I recognized at once that the structure stood upon a high hill in the center of this desolate land. Only from such elevation could it appear so immense against the darkness. Without hesitation, I began to run down into the lower reaches of the world, driven by urgency to reach the Pyramid.

I ran for only a few minutes before I stumbled—and fell violently, headfirst. The impact was brutal, and for a moment, I thought my neck had snapped. I lay there helpless, moaning faintly, utterly vulnerable. Any creature that passed could have ended me then.

But slowly, I regained strength. I sat up, cradling my neck, and after a time, the pain faded. I rose again—but now I moved with caution, my heart heavy with unease. Why was the Pyramid completely dark? If it truly was the Lesser Refuge, why did it show no sign of life? A cold dread crept into my mind: perhaps it was not refuge at all, but a fortress of evil—a trap, a deception woven by some malevolent force. Yet, when I looked toward the distant fires flickering across the land, the Pyramid stood clearly defined against the glow. I could not deny the truth: it *was* the Lesser Refuge.

At first, when I saw it, I had been consumed by blind instinct—run!—after months of searching. Then, I had considered calling to Naani through my brain-elements, sending the Master-Word, my voice echoing across the void to tell her I was near. But now, reason returned. I must proceed with care. What if the darkness meant something terrible?

So I descended again, cautiously at first, then with growing urgency, the fire of hope rekindled in my chest after the shock of my fall.

I had climbed to the summit of the great volcano in roughly thirteen hours. Now, descending, I reached the plain in ten—faster, yes, but shaken, unsteady, still weakened by the fall.

By the tenth hour, I stood once more on the vast plain beneath the stars. The Pyramid was no longer visible—it was hidden behind the hill’s shadow. But I could see the massive bulk of the hill itself, silhouetted between me and the distant fires.

I walked four hours across the land, passing through scattered fire-holes that cast dim red glows into the night. Though the darkness was deep, it was never total—the fires kept the shadows from being absolute.

After four hours, the distant lights vanished entirely, blocked by the hill’s base. That was when I realized I was close. But even then, it took another hour to reach its foot. During those five hours since leaving the volcano, I had heard strange sounds—creatures running through the dark, the thunderous roar of a giant, and once, a shriek so twisted and unnatural it sent chills through my bones.

Now I began the climb. At first, my heart pounded with excitement. I almost shouted Naani’s name into the night, longing for her voice to answer, even if only in spirit. But as I ascended, the thrill faded. Caution returned. A chill settled over me—not just fear, but a deeper, quieter horror. My spirit sensed something beyond sight, something ancient and hostile.

Near the top, I finally saw the Pyramid in full—its jagged form piercing the black sky, silent and abandoned. And then, the truth struck me like lightning: *No one lived there.* My spirit knew it with absolute certainty. No human breath stirred within those walls. Instead, monstrous things dwelled there—beasts of nightmare, waiting to devour any who approached.

I turned away. I walked back down the hill, quietly, quickly, until I was far from that cursed place.

It took four long hours to escape the hill’s shadow. When I finally stood clear, I felt no safety anywhere. The entire world seemed poisoned. I wandered aimlessly at first, lost in grief and exhaustion.

Then, without warning, I found myself on the shore of an ancient sea—dry, cracked, and dead. How I had gotten there, I did not know. Perhaps the seabed curved around this region, or maybe there were multiple ancient seas buried beneath the night.

I collapsed onto the ground, weak and broken. My heart felt dead. I knew now that the Pyramid held evil—perhaps the entire Lesser Refuge had fallen. The people were gone. The Maid was likely dead. And if that was true, then I had come too late.

Yet, in that moment of utter desolation, a thought came: *If she is gone, let something terrible find me. Let me die swiftly. There is nothing left to live for.*

And so, I sat in silence, numb with sorrow.

Then, suddenly, I remembered—there was still one thing I could do. I could send the Master-Word. Not knowing if she lived, not expecting anything, but needing to know.

I rose. I looked out into the endless dark. And with every ounce of will, I projected the Master-Word through the aether. Then, three times, I called Naani—using my brain-elements, my voice, my very soul.

And then—*it came*.

From the depths of the night, low and solemn, the Master-Word echoed back. A single, distant voice—tiny, trembling, yet unmistakable—spoke within my mind. It was Naani. It was Mirdath. She called me by my old love-name.

I nearly choked. Joy flooded my body, fierce and terrifying. My despair vanished as if it had never existed. She was alive. She was calling me. After all this time—after the loneliness, the suffering, the endless journey—I had found her.

But the voice sounded so far away. As if she were at the edge of the world. And in that same moment, my spirit told me: *She is not here. She is somewhere else. And danger may be near.*

Then, instantly, I sensed something else—someone nearby. Hidden in the bushes beside a fire-hole. My spirit knew it before my eyes did.

I said nothing to her. I stepped silently into the thicket, moving fast, and crouched low.

Through the branches, I saw her. A small figure kneeling by the fire, sobbing into the dirt. A slender girl, thin and pale, wrapped in tattered clothes. She was listening—desperately listening—to something only she could hear. To a cry from the spirit-world, one she believed came from the Mighty Pyramid.

And in that instant, I *knew*.

She was Naani. Mirdath. My own.

She didn’t recognize me yet. She trembled, weeping, huddled in the cold. I drew in a breath, clenched my teeth, and spoke aloud—plain human words:

She froze. Looked around wildly. Fear flashed in her eyes, then a fragile hope.

I pushed through the bush and stepped into the open. I stood before her in my grey armor, tall and strange in the firelight.

For a heartbeat, she screamed. She tried to flee, stumbling backward into the undergrowth. But then she saw—human, not monster. And I said the Master-Word aloud, so she would know peace.

“I am That One,” I said. “I am the One you called.”

Her eyes widened. Recognition flooded her face. She gasped, then threw herself forward, grabbing my hands with both of hers. She broke into fresh sobs, collapsing against me, shaking uncontrollably.

I held her gently, not daring to touch her too much—my gloves were off, and her skin was cold. I couldn’t speak. I simply held her, letting her cry.

She leaned against me, frail as a child. Her breath came in ragged bursts. I noticed her hands—so thin, so wasted. I lifted her carefully and placed her on a smooth rock, propping her back with a stone. Then I removed my cloak and wrapped it around her. Her clothes were torn, barely covering her. She shivered—not just from cold, but from weakness, from hunger, from grief.

I opened my pack and pulled out a tablet. I broke it into my cup, mixed it with water, and heated it on a hot rock near the fire. Then I fed her the broth—her hands shook so badly she would have spilled it all.

She drank. Slowly, her breathing steadied. The tears slowed. I held her hands in mine, and gradually, the trembling ceased. The broth helped.

After a while, she stirred. Her fingers curled slightly in my grip. I loosened my hold. She grasped mine—weakly, gently—then lowered her gaze. She was gathering strength.

I was afraid. Afraid of what might come. Now that she was safe, I could not bear the thought of losing her. I listened constantly, scanning the dark, alert for movement.

Then, suddenly, she tried to rise. I stepped back to help. She grabbed my hand, sank to her knees, and kissed it. Then she wept again.

I was stunned. I pulled away. This could not be. So I knelt before her, took her hands, and kissed them—once, humbly. I wanted her to know everything: my love, my sorrow, my devotion.

She sobbed harder. Not because of shame, but because I had come. Because I had crossed the night of the world for her.

I released her hands, but she left them in mine. She bowed her head, still weeping, yet somehow, in that gesture, she gave me her whole self—her spirit, her trust.

Then I pulled her into my arms. Gently. Without passion. Just holding her. I stroked her hair, whispered her names—Naani, Mirdath—said things I don’t remember now. But she understood.

She was quiet in my arms. Content. Still crying, but softly now. She had endured so much—loneliness, terror, grief, starvation.

Eventually, she grew still. I tried to move her to rest, but she clung to me, nestling close, her warmth seeping into my heart. She murmured words—soft, broken, half-formed.

I made more broth. She drank it, using her own hands. I ate tablets and drank water—my own hunger had long been forgotten.

As the warmth returned to her, she began to speak. Her voice was weak, but steady. She told me of the disaster at the Lesser Redoubt.

An Evil Force had attacked. Some of the people, weakened by failing Earth-Currents, had opened the Great Door and fled. Then, monsters surged in—giant, brutish creatures—slaughtering many. Some escaped into the night.

Among them was Naani. Her father—the Master Monstruwacan—had been killed by a shaggy, monstrous man. Three girls had fled with her. But two were taken by creatures in the night. The third ran off. Naani ran alone.

She had been lost ever since. All this time—while I journeyed across the wasteland—she had wandered, starving, hunted.

She had called to me—often. But her spirit was too weak. The Word did not travel far. And her calls, though desperate, had drawn predators. Beasts had followed her scent. She learned to stay silent, to hide.

Her clothes were torn away by rocks and brambles. She wore little. She ate moss, bitter berries, drank sulfurous spring water—sometimes sickening, sometimes saving her.

She had seen horrors: the death of a young girl nearby, the pounding feet of giants, the cries of fleeing humans who ignored her pleas. She had once found survivors hiding—but they ran from her, terrified.

The cold drove her to seek fire-holes. But the fires attracted the beasts. So she stayed far away—shivering in the dark.

And once, twice, thrice—she had nearly died in her sleep, hunted by creatures that crept close while she rested.

Now, as she lay by the fire, exhausted, dying, she had seen them—spider-crabs, squatting all around her, waiting. She feared to sleep. She believed death was near.

Then—out of the void—the Master-Word came. Strong. Thunderous. But she thought it came from the Mighty Pyramid. So it brought no hope—only deeper despair.

Then—her name. Spoken aloud. A different name than the one in the Word.

And I appeared.

She recoiled—thinking a monster had found her. Then she saw me. Grey armor. Familiar face. The One from her dreams. The One who had spoken to her across the world.

And I had come.

She was safe.

But shattered.

And as she spoke of the spider-crabs, I looked around.

They were still there.

A ring of silent, watchful horrors, surrounding us.

Anger flared in me. Disgust. I rose, stepped to the edge of the firelight, and kicked at them—one after another. Dozens. They scuttled away, but did not fight. They were cowardly. Foolish. Yet their presence was obscene.

And that was how I knew: the world was not just empty. It was *alive*—with nightmares.

But tonight, I had found her.

And that was enough.
