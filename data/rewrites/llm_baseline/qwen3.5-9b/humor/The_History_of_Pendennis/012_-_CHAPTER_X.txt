Major Pendennis strolled leisurely back toward the George Inn, the weight of the evening's events settling comfortably upon his shoulders. Just as he approached the entrance, his faithful valet, Mr. Morgan, emerged from the shadows of the doorway. With that particular blend of deference and omniscience characteristic of long-serving servants, Morgan intercepted his master just as he reached for his bedroom candle.

"I think, sir, if you'd grace the coffee-room with your presence, there's a young gentleman inside you'd be keen to meet," Morgan announced.

"Is Mr. Arthur here?" the Major demanded, his voice rising an octave in genuine irritation.

"No, sir. But his bosom buddy, Mr. Foker, is. It's Lady Agnes Foker's son, sir. He's been comatose in the coffee-room since lunchtime and just rang for his coffee. I reckon, p'raps, you'd enjoy a chat with him," the valet suggested, swinging open the door.

The Major stepped inside. Indeed, there sat Mr. Foker, the sole inhabitant of the establishment. He was vigorously rubbing the sleep from his eyes, seated before a table cluttered with empty decanters and the skeletal remains of a heavy dessert. He had intended to attend the theater, but a copious meal had summoned a sudden, irresistible desire for slumber; consequently, he had thrown his legs onto the bench and surrendered to a nap rather than the drama.

The Major paused, mentally rehearsing an approach, but Foker anticipated the awkwardness.

"Care for the evening paper, sir?" Foker asked, his tone instantly warm and communicative. He picked up *The Globe* from the table and held it out.

"Much obliged," the Major replied, accepting the paper with a gracious bow. "If I'm reading the family tree correctly, I'm addressing Mr. Henry Foker, son of Lady Agnes. I've the distinct honor of knowing her ladyship, and you certainly carry the Rosherville countenance."

"Hullo! Pardon me," Foker interrupted, correcting himself mid-sentence. "I nearly called you a 'commercial gent,' but I caught myself in time. To whom have I the privilege of speaking?"

"To a relative of a friend and schoolmate of mine—Arthur Pendennis, my nephew, who speaks of you in the highest terms. I am Major Pendennis, of whom you may have heard. May I join you at your table? I've sat at your grandfather's before."

"Sir, you flatter me!" Foker exclaimed, displaying considerable courtesy. "So, you're Arthur's uncle?"

"And guardian," the Major added proudly.

"He's a swell fellow, sir," Foker observed. "Glad to hear you say so."

"And sharp, too. Though I confess, I'm a bit of a dullard myself. But I know a clever chap when I see one, and I like 'em."

"You display both taste and humility," the Major noted approvingly. "Arthur speaks highly of your talents."

"Not so much with the books," Foker admitted, shaking his head. "Never could crack that code. Pendennis could—he used to pen half the fellows' verses. Yet—" Here the young man broke into a candid confession. "Since you're his guardian, I'll risk it: I think he's what we call 'flat.'"

The Major immediately found himself ensconced in a fascinating, confidential dialogue. "In what way is Arthur 'flat'?" he asked, smiling.

Foker leaned in, winking conspiratorially. He possessed a rare quality of boldness born of intoxication and confidence; he would have winked at the Duke of Wellington with equal ease. "Flat, sir? About women, I mean."

"He's not unique in that department, my dear Harry," the Major replied smoothly. "Tell me more."

"Well, sir, it's partly my fault. He went to the play one night—you see, I'm currently down here reading for my small role during the Long Vacation, though I pop over from Baymouth frequently in my carriage. Anyway, we hit the theater, and Pen was absolutely smitten with Miss Fotheringay. Her real name is Costigan; she's a stunning creature. Next morning, I introduced him to the General, whom we call her father—a proper old rogue and a devotee of whisky-and-water. Naturally, he fell for her hook, line, and sinker. In fact, he's already proposed!" Foker slammed his hand on the table, causing the remaining dessert dishes to rattle ominously.

"What! You knew this?" the Major gasped.

"Knew it? Don't I? And plenty more besides. We were discussing it at mess yesterday, roasting Derby Oaks until he was frothing at the mouth. Do you know Sir Derby Oaks? We dined together recently. Remember standing at the door smoking when you slipped past for dinner?"

"I recall Sir Thomas Oaks, his father, before he became a Baronet. He lived in Cavendish Square and served as physician to Queen Charlotte."

"The young rake is spinning money fast, I assure you," Foker declared.

"So, Sir Derby Oaks is another suitor?" the Major asked, delighted and anxious simultaneously.

"Another what?" Foker queried innocently.

"Another admirer of Miss Fotheringay?"

"Lord bless you! We call him Mondays, Wednesdays, and Fridays, and Pen holds Tuesdays, Thursdays, and Saturdays. Mind you, no foul play involved! Miss Fotheringay is far too alert for that. She plays them off against each other; she's got two strings to her bow."

"I find you remarkably awake yourself, Mr. Foker," the Major laughed.

"Pretty well, thanks, sir. How are you?" Foker replied, unflappable. "I may not be brilliant, perhaps, but I'm 'downy.' Friendly folks say I keep fairly good track of the hour. Can I tell you the time?"

"On my word," the Major beamed, "you could be of immense service. You're a man of the world, someone one enjoys dealing with. Now, as such, I needn't hint that our family is less than thrilled by this absurd entanglement Arthur has gotten into."

"I shouldn't doubt it," Foker agreed. "Connection ineligible. Excessive beer consumption on the premises. No Irish need apply. That seems to be your drift."

"That is precisely it," the Major confirmed, though he wasn't entirely sure what Foker meant by the specific phrasing. He pressed on, interrogating his new acquaintance about the respectable Costigan household. To his surprise, Foker spoke highly of Mr. and Miss Costigan's moral fiber.

"You see," Foker explained, "I believe the General loves a hearty tankard. If I wanted to bet big, I wouldn't put my money in his pocket—but he keeps a hawk-like eye on his daughter. Neither he nor she tolerate anything but propriety. Pen's attentions are the talk of the entire company. I hear details from a young lady who used to be close to her; we occasionally share tea with her family. Miss Rouncy tells me Sir Derby Oaks has been hovering around Miss Fotheringay ever since his regiment arrived, but Pen has recently swooped in and ousted him. This has driven the Baronet nearly mad; he's almost ready to propose himself. Wish he would! Then you'd see which of the two Miss Fotheringay would leap at."

"I suspected as much," the Major said, relieved. "You bring me great joy, Mr. Foker. I wish I'd met you sooner."

"Didn't want to stick my oar in," Foker shrugged. "I wait to be asked, and if there are no objections, I speak freely. Heard your man was sniffing around my servant. Didn't realize what was brewing until Miss Fotheringay and Miss Rouncy started screaming about ostrich feathers, when Miss R. spilled the beans."

"Miss Rouncy, I gather, was the confidante?"

"Confidante? Yes. She's twice as smart as Fotheringay, literary and all that, while Miss Foth can barely read."

"She can write," the Major interjected, recalling the contents of Pen's breast pocket.

Foker erupted into a sardonic cackle. "He, he! Rouncy writes her letters. Every single one. Since they quarreled, she doesn't know how to function. Miss Rouncy has a lovely hand, whereas the old girl makes a terrible mess of spelling and grammar unless Bows is nearby. Rouncy has been setting her copies lately; she writes beautifully."

"I assume you know this well," the Major teased archly. On this, Foker winked again.

"I'd pay dearly for a sample of her handwriting," the Major continued. "Surely you could provide one?"

"No, no, that would be wicked," Foker protested. "Perhaps I shouldn't have said so much. Miss F.'s writing isn't so bad, I suppose; she just had Miss R. draft the first letter and has stuck with it. But mark my words: until they reconcile, the letters stop."

"I hope they never do," the Major stated sincerely. "I cannot express my delight at meeting you. As a worldly man, you must appreciate how fatal this step is for my nephew's future, and how eager we are to liberate him from this ridiculous engagement."
