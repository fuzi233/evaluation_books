As the sun dipped below the horizon, casting long shadows across the clearing, the campfire crackled with the promise of a hearty meal. The stewing bear paws, simmering in the pot, released a savory aroma that drifted through the evening air. We sat gathered around the flames, our conversation punctuated by anxious glances toward the treeline, waiting for the return of our young explorers.

Finally, the rhythmic tramp of hooves and the sharp beat of hooves against the earth broke the silence. A little troop of riders appeared, galloping across the open ground at a sharp trot. A shrill, ringing cheer erupted from our throats as we rose to meet them.

The boys sprang from their saddles, releasing the animals to graze and refresh themselves. They eagerly approached us, beaming with pride to display their acquisitions and recount their adventures. What funny figures they made! Franz and Jack each had a young kid slung across their backs, the four legs tied together and sticking out beneath their chins like strange appendages. Fritz's game-bag looked peculiarly lumpy, filled with round shapes and sharp points, occasionally shifting as if containing living creatures within.

"Hurrah for the chase, Father!" cried Jack, his face alight with excitement. "Nothing compares to real hunting after all. Just to see how Storm and Grumble handle a grassy plain! It is perfectly splendid! We soon tired out the little antelopes and caught them."

"Yes, Father," added Franz, "and Fritz has two Angora rabbits in his bag. We also wanted to bring you some honey. Only think! Such a clever bird—a cuckoo—showed us exactly where it was hidden!"

"My brothers forget the chief thing," said Fritz, correcting them gently. "We have driven a little herd of antelopes right through the Gap into our territory. There they are, all ready for us to hunt whenever we like—or to catch and tame!"

"Well done!" I exclaimed. "Here is indeed a list of achievements. But to your mother and me, the most important thing of all is God's goodness in bringing you safe back to us. Now, let us hear the whole story so we may have a definite idea of your performances."

"We had a splendid ride," Fritz began, his voice animated. "Down Glen Verdant and through the defile of our Rocky Barrier, the morning was so cool and fresh that our steeds galloped nearly the whole way at full speed. Once through the Gap, we slowed to a trot, keeping our eyes open for game. We spotted two herds grazing by the stream below. We were about to charge down for a shot, but I realized it would be wiser to drive the entire herd through the Gap into our own domain. Effectively, we would be enclosing them in a park, free yet within reach."

"Down the hill we rode as hard as we could, forming a semicircle behind the larger herd of magnificent antelopes. Aided by the dogs, with shouts and cries, we drove them along the stream toward the Gap. As we neared the opening, they hesitated, turning like sheep about to be driven to the butcher's yard. It was all we could do to prevent them from bolting past us, but eventually, one made a rush at the opening, and the rest followed. Soon, they were all on the other side of the frontier, inhabitants of New Switzerland."

"Capital, my boy!" I said. "But I don't see what will keep them from returning through the Gap whenever they feel inclined."

"Stop, Father," he replied. "You interrupted me too soon. We thought of that possibility and provided against it. We stretched a long line right across the defile, stringing on it feathers and rags of all sorts. They danced and fluttered in the wind, looking so strange that I am certain the herd will never attempt to pass it. In fact, Levaillant mentions in his *Voyage au Cap de Bonne Esperance* that the Hottentots use this exact method to pen in antelopes they have caught."

"Well done," I said, pleased to see him recall his reading. "The antelopes are welcome to New Switzerland. But, my boy, I cannot say the same for the rabbits you have there. They increase so rapidly that if you establish a colony, your next difficulty will be to get rid of them."

"True," he admitted. "But my idea was to place them on Whale Island, where they would find abundant food and trouble us in no way. May I not establish a warren there? It would be so useful. Do you know my eagle caught these pretty little fellows for me? I saw them running about, so I unhooded him, and in a few minutes he brought me three—one dead, with whose body I rewarded him, and these two here, unhurt."

"Now, Father," interrupted Jack, "do listen to me and hear my story, or else Fritz will begin upon his adventures and tire you out with his rigmarole descriptions."

"Certainly, Jack," I said, ready to listen. "First and foremost, how did you bring down those beautiful little animals?"

"Oh, we galloped them down," Jack explained enthusiastically. "The dogs sniffed about in the grass while Fritz was away after the rabbits. Suddenly, these little fawns popped out and bounded away at thirty miles an hour, with Storm, Grumble, and the dogs at their heels. In about a quarter of an hour, we left the dogs behind and were close upon our prey. Down went the little creatures into the grass, overcome with terror and fatigue, at our mercy. So we shouted to Fritz, and—"

"My dear boy," I interjected, "according to your statement, Fritz must have been seven and a half miles off."

"Oh, well, Father, perhaps we didn't ride for quite a quarter of an hour, and I can't say exactly how fast we were going. Besides, the fawns didn't run in a straight line. At any rate, Fritz heard us, and Fritz, Franz, and I leashed the legs of the pretty creatures. Then we mounted again and saw a wretch of a cuckoo who led us ever so far out of our course by cuckooing and making faces at us before hopping away. Franz declared it must be an enchanted princess, so I thought I would rid it of its spell. But Fritz stopped me from shooting it, saying it was a 'Honey Indicator' leading us to a bees' byke. So we spared its life, and sure enough, it stopped close by a bees' nest in a hollow tree."

"This was capital," Jack continued. "We were in a great hurry to taste the honey, so I threw in a lot of lighted lucifer matches. Somehow, it didn't kill the bees at all, but only made them awfully angry. They flew out in a body and stung me all over. I rushed to Storm and sprang on his back, but though I galloped away for bare life, it was an age before I got rid of the little wretches. Now my face is in a perfect fever. I think I will get Mother to bathe it for me," and off rushed the noisy boy, leaving Fritz and me to attend to the fawns and examine the rabbits.

With the latter, I determined to follow Fritz's proposal and colonize Whale Island. I was all the more willing because I had been considering establishing a fortress on that island to which we might retreat in extreme danger, and where we would be thankful to possess a constant supply of animal food.

Having attended to the antelopes, I tried to interest the boys in my discovery of the block of talc, but just then their mother summoned us to dinner.

The principal dish consisted of the bear paws—savory-smelling delicacies so tempting that their close resemblance to human hands, and even Jack's roguish "Fee-fo-fum," did not prevent a single member of the family from enjoying them heartily.

After supper, we lit our watch-fire, retired to our tent, and slept soundly.

We had been working diligently; the bear meat was smoked, the fat melted down and stored, and a large supply of bamboos collected. Yet I wished to make another excursion. At early dawn, I aroused the boys. Fritz mounted the mule, I rode Lightfoot, and Jack and Franz took their usual steeds. With the two dogs, we galloped off—first to visit the euphorbia to collect the gum, and then to discover whether the ostrich had deserted her eggs in the sand.

Ernest watched us depart without a sigh of regret, returning to the tent to assist his mother and study his books.

Our steeds carried us down the Green Valley at a rapid rate, following the direction of our former expedition. We soon reached Turtle Marsh, filled our water-flasks, and arrived at the rising ground where Fritz had previously discovered the mounted Arabs.

As Jack and Franz wanted a gallop, I allowed them to press forward while Fritz and I visited the euphorbia trees. A quantity of red gum had exuded from the incisions I had made and coagulated in the sun. I rolled it into little balls and stored it in a bamboo jar I had brought for the purpose.

As we rode after the boys, who were some way ahead, Fritz remarked, "Did you not tell me that the juice of that tree was poisonous, Father? Why have you collected such a quantity?"

"I did indeed say so," I replied. "It is a most deadly poison. The inhabitants of the Cape of Good Hope use it to poison springs where wild animals assemble to quench their thirst, thus slaughtering immense numbers for their hides. I intend, however, to use it to destroy the apes should they again commit depredations, and also in preparing animal skins to protect them from insect attacks."

The two boys were still at some distance when suddenly four magnificent ostriches rose from the sand where they had been sitting. Jack and Franz perceived them and, with a great shout, drove them toward us. In front ran a splendid male bird, his feathers of shining black and his great tail plume waving behind. Three females of an ashen grey color followed him. They approached us with incredible swiftness, within gun-shot before they perceived us. Fritz had had the forethought to bind up the beak of his eagle so that, should he bring down an ostrich, it might be unable to injure him.

He now threw up the falcon, which towered upwards and swooped down upon the head of the foremost bird. Confused and alarmed, the ostrich could not defend himself or continue his flight. His speed was so greatly checked that Jack overtook him, hurling his lasso to enfold his wings and legs in deadly coils and bring him to the ground. The other ostriches were almost out of sight, so leaving them to their own devices, we leaped from our steeds and attempted to approach the captured bird. He struggled fearfully, kicking with such violence right and left that I almost despaired of getting him home alive.

However, it occurred to me that if we could cover his eyes, his fury might be subdued. I instantly acted upon this idea, flinging over his head my coat and hunting bag, which effectually shut out the light. No sooner had I done this than his struggles ceased, and we were able to approach. We first secured round his body a broad strip of sealskin, on each side of which I fastened a stout piece of cord to lead him easily. Then, fastening another cord in a loop round his legs to prevent him from breaking into a gallop, we released him from the coils of the lasso.

"Do you know," I said to the boys, "how the natives of India secure a newly captured elephant?"

"Oh, yes!" said Fritz. "They fasten him between two tame elephants. We'll do that to this fine fellow and tame him double quick."

"The only difficulty will be," remarked Jack, "that we have no tame ostriches. However, I daresay Storm and Grumble will have no objection to perform their part, and it will puzzle even this great monster to run away with them."

So we at once began operations. Storm and Grumble were led up on either side of the recumbent ostrich, and the cords secured to their girths. Jack and Franz, each armed with a stout whip, mounted their respective steeds. The wrappers were removed from the bird's eyes, and we stood by to watch what would next occur.

For some moments after the return of his sight, he lay perfectly still. Then he arose with a bound and, not aware of the cords which hampered him, attempted to dash forward. The thongs were stout, and he was brought to his knees. A fruitless struggle ensued, and then, seeming to accommodate himself to circumstances, he set off at a sharp trot, his guards making the air re-echo with their merry shouts. These cries stimulated the ostrich to yet further exertions, but he was at length brought to a stand by the determined refusal of his four-footed companions to continue such a race across loose sand.

The boys having enjoyed the long run, I told them to walk with the prisoner slowly home, while Fritz and I returned to examine the ostrich's nest. The eggs were quite warm, and I was certain the mother had quite recently left. Leaving about half, I packed the rest in a large bag I had brought for the purpose and slung it carefully on the saddle before me.

We soon caught up our advance guard and, without other notable incident, reached our tent. Astonishment and dismay were depicted on the face of my wife as we approached.

"My dear husband," she exclaimed, "do you think our provisions so abundant that you must scour the deserts to find some great beast to assist us to devour them? You must discover an iron mine next, for iron is what ostriches chiefly live on, is it not?"
