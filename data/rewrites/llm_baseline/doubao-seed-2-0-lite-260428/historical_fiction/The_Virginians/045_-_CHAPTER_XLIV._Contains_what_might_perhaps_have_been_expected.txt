When his carefully chosen peace offerings were turned away, our hot-headed young Virginian chief flew into a towering rage, not only with Colonel Lambert, but with every last member of that gentleman’s household. “He has humiliated me before the girls!” he fumed to himself. “He and that paragon Wolfe, forever lecturing me on morality and lording over me with their airs of superiority and protection—they’ve turned the whole family against me, painted me as nothing but a reckless scapegrace and ungrateful prodigal. So virtuous they won’t deign to shake my hand, and when I try to show them a little common gratitude for their kindness, they fling my gifts right back in my face!”
“Lord, sir, those trinkets you brought them must be worth a small fortune!” Parson Sampson murmured, his eyes glinting covetously as he leaned over the pair of morocco leather cases, where Mr. Sparks’ fine golden gewgaws rested plump on white satin cushions.
“They cost a pretty penny, Sampson,” Harry said. “Not that I’d grudge ten times the sum for folks who’d shown me half the kindness those Lamberts have.”
“Never a doubt of it, your honour!” Sampson cut in, ever ready to heap praise on his patron. “Not if I know your generous heart!”
“That repeater, they told me back in Paris, was a bargain at a hundred pounds. I remember little Miss Hetty chattering once that she’d give anything to own a repeating watch.”
“What a perfect treasure!” the chaplain cried. “A little circle of pearls set round the back, a tiny diamond knob for the stem! It would win any woman’s heart on the spot, sir!”
“There’s an apple-woman passing by in the street with her basket,” Harry snarled, half yanking the watch out of its case. “I’ve a mind to fling the whole thing down to her right now.”
When Harry set out an hour later on business that took him to the City and the Temple, his parasite didn’t dare follow him far past the Strand, turning off with a muttered excuse that he had a mortal terror of Chancery Lane and all its sharp-tongued lawyers and grim, narrow precincts. Harry met his broker, and the pair walked together to the Bank, where after a few short formalities and a quick signature or two, Harry left with a thick wad of crisp new banknotes tucked deep in his coat pocket. The broker treated him to dinner at one of the great, famous City dining houses, then walked him through the throng on the Exchange, where Harry hung his head in shame. What would his mother back at Castlewood on the Potomac say, he wondered, if she knew he was emptying most of his father’s patrimony straight into a gambler’s pocket? Every merchant on the floor, he was sure, was whispering about him: *That’s young Esmond, Madam Esmond’s son. He’s losing every last penny at the cards, selling off his estate bit by bit.*
His spirits only lifted once he’d passed under the grinning carved traitor heads above Temple Bar, and escaped the City’s noxious, prying streets. He wandered home through the Strand, glancing in at St James’s Street on his way, but the Chocolate Houses were still nearly empty, most of their patrons lingering over late dinners.
Back in his lodgings, Harry pulled out his bundle of banknotes, slipped three of them into a sheet of paper, and sealed it carefully, scrawling inside: *Much good may they do you. H. E. W.* He addressed the packet to the Reverend Mr. Sampson, left it propped on the chimney glass over the mantel, and told his servants to hand it to the parson the moment he returned.
His phaeton was drawn up at the door by then, and he climbed in, fully intending to drive round the park—until a sharp east wind bit at his cheeks, and he turned his horses’ heads straight down St James’s Street, pulling up at White’s a little after three o’clock. Hardly anyone else had arrived yet, the usual hour for dinner still dragging on. The only other soul in the coffee room was his cousin Lord Castlewood, lolling over a copy of the *Public Advertiser* fresh from his duties at St James’s Palace nearby.
Castlewood yawned and flipped a page idly. What to do with an hour to kill? A quick game of piquet? Harry had no objections. “Just an hour,” Castlewood said. “I dine over in Arlington Street at four.” “Just an hour,” Harry agreed, and the cards were called for.
“Shall we play upstairs, in the little back tea room?” Castlewood suggested. “Away from all this noise down here.”
“Certainly,” Harry said. “Out of the noise.”
By five o’clock, half a dozen gentlemen had drifted in after their dinners, loitering over coffee, cards, and gossip. Two of them leaned against the window frame, toothpicks stuck in the corners of their mouths, staring out at the street.
“Whose phaeton is that parked out front?” the first man asked.
“The Fortunate Youth’s,” his companion replied.
“Not so fortunate these last three nights, then. Luck’s turned dead against him. Lost thirteen hundred pounds to the table last night. John, has Mr. Warrington been in today?”
“Mr. Warrington is in the house right now, sir,” the footman answered. “He’s in the little back tea room with Lord Castlewood, been there since three, playing piquet.”
“Fine sport for Castlewood, that,” the first man sniffed, shrugging.
The second swore under his breath. “Curse the man. He never ought to be allowed in this club at all. He won’t pay a debt if he loses. No gentleman ought to play with him. Sir Miles Warrington told me at Court the other day Castlewood has owed him money on a bet these three years running.”
“Castlewood never loses when he plays one on one,” the first man grinned, showing his polished, clean teeth. “A big table flusters him, that’s why he never joins the big games down here.”
“Let’s go up and stop them,” the second man growled.
“Why bother?” the other said, nodding out the window. “Far better to watch the lamplighter climbing his ladder. Look at that old fool in the sedan chair down there—did you ever see such a droll old quiz?”
“Wait, who’s that just rushing out the door? It’s Fortunatus himself! Looks like he forgot his phaeton’s been waiting out here for hours. Two to one he’s lost every penny he had to Castlewood.”
“Jack, do you take me for a fool?” the first man scoffed. “Look at that fine pair of greys he’s got. My, how he’s flogging them!” They watched Harry gallop up St James’s Street, sedan chair men and coachmen scrambling out of his way, before Lord Castlewood slipped out a few minutes later, climbed into his own waiting chair, and vanished down the street.
Harry drove straight back to his lodgings, the horses’ hooves clattering on the wet pavement all the way. His servant Gumbo was lingering on the steps chatting to a country-looking maidservant, who dropped a quick curtsy and scurried off when she saw Harry. Gumbo was forever flirting with some pretty maid or other, wherever they went.
“Gumbo—did Mr. Sampson come by while I was out?” Harry called down from his driving seat.
“No, sar. Mr. Sampson ain’t been here!” Gumbo answered. Harry told him to run upstairs and fetch the sealed letter he’d left for the parson on the mantel.
“Letter for Mr. Sampson? Oh yes, sir,” Gumbo said, though he could not read a word of writing.
“The sealed one, you fool, in the glass above the chimney!” Harry snapped. Gumbo trundled slowly upstairs to fetch it. The moment Harry had the letter in his hand, he turned his horses’ heads back towards St James’s Street, and the two gossiping gentlemen still leaning out White’s window stared in surprise as the Fortunate Youth came galloping back past them.
When he and Castlewood had left the tea room, Harry had noticed a whole crowd of new patrons had gathered in the main gaming room, a new bank already set up. Several men had already donned their special gaming jackets, the heavy coats they kept stashed at the club for all-night sessions at the tables. Harry walked straight to the clerk’s desk, paid off his debt from the night before, and sat down at the table, calling for a stack of fresh counters. It had been a brutal unlucky week for the so-called Fortunate Youth, and this night was no different. He called for more counters, then more, his face pale and quiet, though he remained perfectly polite and affable to anyone who spoke to him. But he could not win, no matter how many hands he drew.
At last he stood up to leave. “Hang on, stay and turn your luck!” Lord March called, sitting beside him with a great heap of green and white counters stacked in front of him. “Take a hundred of mine, go on playing.”
“I’ve had enough for tonight, my lord,” Harry said. He trundled down to the coffee room, ate a broiled chop, and walked home to his lodgings a little after midnight.
A man often sleeps sound enough after a great, shaming catastrophe. It is the waking in the dark hours before dawn that is the cruel part. Last night you proposed to Miss Brown, you quarrelled and pulled Captain Jones’s nose over the wine, you sat down to cards with Colonel Robinson and left him a pile of IOU’s you can never hope to pay. All those memories come crowding in on you at dawn, throbbing behind your eyes along with your headache. What a vast, dreary gulf there stretches between today and yesterday! You feel like you have aged ten years in a single night. You yank your nightcap over your eyes, pull the blankets up over your nose, trying to sleep the whole disaster away. It was only a dream, you tell yourself. But sleep will not come. The watchman’s cry echoes up the street. Harry remembers the repeating watch he bought for Hetty, tucked under his pillow. Ting, ting, ting! It chimes six times in the darkness, then a little extra trill to mark the half hour. Poor little Hetty, bright and sweet and innocent, he thinks. He would have loved to see her face light up when he gave her that watch. What will Maria say? (Boring old Maria, he thinks suddenly, she has become such a drag.) What will his mother back at Virginia say when she learns he has lost every last penny of his ready money, five thousand pounds on top of all his former winnings, in just three nights? He tells himself Castlewood could not have cheated him—no, his cousin simply plays piquet far better than he does. Harry is even glad his kinsman, who so badly needed the money, walked away with it. And he had not played a penny more than he could afford to lose, at the very least. But the curse of his bad company, his foolish extravagance, the shame and remorse still burns hot in his chest. “Will my mother ever forgive me?” the young prodigal whispers to himself. “Oh, that I were home again, that I had never left the banks of the Potomac.”
At last the grey London dawn peeps through the shutters. The housemaid comes in to light the fire and let the thin pale morning light spill into the room, followed soon by Gumbo, who warms Harry’s dressing gown and sets out his shaving plate and clean linen. Then the hairdresser arrives to curl and powder his hair, while Harry sips his tea and reads his morning letters. By breakfast time, Parson Sampson shows up, eager-faced and smiling, his usual servile self. He explains he never made it to the lodgings the night before, some jolly fellows dragged him off to dinner at the St. Alban’s Tavern and they made a full night of drinking of it.
“Oh, Parson!” Harry groans. “That was the worst night you ever spent in your whole life! Look here, sir.”
“This torn open envelope with the words ‘Much good may it do you’ written inside?” Sampson says, glancing at the scrap of paper.
“Look at the address on the outside!” Harry cries. “That envelope was meant for you.” The parson’s face goes white with alarm. “Did someone break it open, sir?”
“Someone did, yes—me,” Harry says. “I broke it open myself, Sampson. If you had turned up yesterday afternoon as you said you would, you would have found that envelope stuffed full of banknotes. As it is, every last penny of it went last night, straight to the infernal macao table.”
“All of it?” Sampson gasps.
“All of it,” Harry says. “Along with all the money I drew from the City, every last penny of ready cash I had left in the world. Yesterday afternoon I played piquet with my cousin—with a gentleman at White’s—and he won every penny I had on me. When I remembered there was that money left here, unless you had fetched it, I drove back, grabbed it, and took it straight back to the tables, lost it along with every other shilling I owned. Great heavens, Sampson—what is the matter with you, man?”
“It’s my luck!” the poor chaplain wails, bursting into full, unashamed tears. “It’s always my cursed luck!”
“What, you are snivelling like a baby over the loss of a couple hundred pounds?” Harry snaps, angry and flustered. “Gumbo, get out of here! Confound you, why do you always poke your woolly head in at the door when you’re not wanted!”
“Some gentleman downstairs want to see master, with a little bill,” Gumbo mumbles.
“Tell him to go to Jericho!” Harry roars. “I’m not at home, sir, not to anyone at this hour of the morning!”
There’s a scuffle and a few muffled shouts out on the landing, then silence. Harry’s mood is no lighter after that altercation, and he rounds savagely on Sampson, who sits hunched over, his face buried in his hands.
“Why don’t you knock back a bumper of brandy to cheer yourself up, Mr. Sampson?” he sneers. “Hang it, man, don’t blubber like a silly woman!”
“It’s not for myself I’m crying!” Sampson snaps, lifting his head, wiping the tears off his face. “I’m used to bad luck, sir.”
“Not for you, then?” Harry blinks. “Are you weeping because someone else is hurt, pray?”
“Yes, sir,” the parson says, his voice cracking. “Because an honest family is going to be ruined, and it is all my fault. I’ve lodged for years above a bootmaker’s shop, a good hardworking man. A few days back, certain I could count on a friend to lend me the money I needed, I borrowed sixty pounds from him that he was supposed to pay his own landlord. Now I can’t get the money anywhere. The bailiff will be here tomorrow to seize all his goods, and his wife and his dear little children will be thrown out onto the street. All through my fault. But as you say, Mr. Warrington, I have no right to snivel like a woman. I thank you for all your past kindness, sir, and I will bid you good day.”
He grabs his broad-brimmed hat and walks straight out of the room.
Harry bursts out into a savage, bitter laugh, half mad with rage at himself, at his bad luck, at the whole world. Sampson hears the laugh from the stairs behind him, then Harry shouting after him: “Sampson! Hang you, come back! It was all a mistake, I beg your pardon!” But the parson is cut to the quick, and he keeps walking. Harry hears the street door slam behind him, and the sound thuds like a blow right against his own chest. He stumbles back into his room, sinks heavy into his plush armchair. He is the prodigal, wallowing knee-deep in the filth of his own bad choices: gambling, waste, drunken nights, dissolute companions, foolish choices that have trampled him flat.
But he is not quite beaten yet. He shoves all those ugly, gnawing remorses away from him, grabs the full glass of brandy he had offered Sampson a few minutes earlier, rips off his fine damask dressing gown, and rings for the trembling Gumbo. “Not that one!” he roars, when Gumbo hands him a bright green coat with plated buttons and a gold cord trim. “Give me a plain suit—the plainer the better. The old black mourning coat.” Gumbo brings out the heavy plain black suit Harry has not touched for months.
Harry lays all his valuables out on the table: first his new fine gold watch, then the pearl-backed repeater he had meant for Hetty, then the pearl necklace he had bought for Theo, his half dozen fancy rings (all save his grandfather’s old carved seal ring, which he kisses tenderly and tucks safely on his pincushion), his three gold snuff boxes, and last of all his old knit purse his mother had stitched back in Virginia, which holds only three shillings and sixpence and a worn old silver pocket piece he had carried with him across the ocean. He tucks all the trinkets under his arm, claps his hat on his head, and walks out the door.
On the landing he meets his landlord, Mr. Ruff, who bows and cringes, and holds out a bill a yard long. “I would be much obliged if Mr. Warrington could settle this today. My wife has a large account to pay off.” Ruff was one of the head waiters at White’s, close to the club’s owner Mr. Mackreth, and his demand only makes Harry’s temper worse.
“Of course I will settle the bill,” Harry says, glowering down at the man from the top of the stairs.
“Perhaps Mr. Warrington could settle it now?”
“No, sir, I will not settle it now!” Harry barks, bullying forward.
“I am very, very much in need of the money, sir,” Ruff pleads. “My wife—”
“Hang you, get out of my way!” Harry roars, shoving the man back hard against the wall, nearly tipping him head over heels down the landing. He tramps down the stairs and out into Bond Street.
