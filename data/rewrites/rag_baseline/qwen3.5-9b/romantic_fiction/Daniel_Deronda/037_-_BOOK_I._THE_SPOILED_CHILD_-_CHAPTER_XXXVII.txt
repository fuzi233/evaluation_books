The rain lashed against the leaded glass of the library windows, a rhythmic drumming that felt, to Daniel Deronda, like the frantic, indifferent heartbeat of London itself. He sat at his mahogany desk, the heavy scent of beeswax and damp wool filling the room, his gaze fixed upon the thick sheaf of letters that had arrived by the morning post. They were from Sir Hugo Fronsberg, his sprawling, confident script detailing a pardon held safely in bank for Sigismund Aspern, a man Deronda suspected was plotting some mad project with broken wings.

But Deronda pushed the letter aside. His mind was elsewhere, caught in the trap of a peculiar test of mettle. In spite of his anxiety for Mirah and his curiosity regarding the enigmatic Mordecai, he had failed to reach the city before Sir Hugo, who had preceded his family to prepare for the opening of Parliament. Instead, Deronda found himself quartered in Park Lane, unaware that his chambers were already tenanted by Hans Meyrick. He had expected this; he had not expected the transformation awaiting him inside.

Most of us remember Retzsch's drawing of destiny: Mephistopheles playing chess with man for his soul, making feints of unintended moves to beguile the mortal into defending against the wrong point of attack. The fiend mocks preparation, persuading us against taking out waterproofs when the sky is clearing, foreseeing that our imbecile caution will become a prejudice against the very signs we ignored. It is a cruel irony when, after painfully adjusting oneself to what seems a wise provision, one finds all mental precaution beside the mark, excellent intentions reduced to miscalculated dovetails cut from a wrong starting-point. Deronda's magnanimity had prepared itself to meet misbehavior, only to find a far stranger call upon it.

His first impression was one of pure pleasure and amusement at finding his sitting-room transformed into an *atelier*, strewn with miscellaneous drawings and the contents of two chests from Rome. The lower half of the windows were darkened with baize, and the blonde Hans presided over the littered place as its weird genius. Hans's hair was longer than of old, his face more whimsically creased, his high voice rising further under the excitement of rapid talk. The friendship between them, kept warm since Cambridge through correspondence and episodes of companionship abroad, had evolved into a practiced exchange of spiritual borrowing and indulgence.

"I knew you would like to see my casts and antiquities," Hans said, after the first hearty greetings, "so I didn't scruple to unlade my chests here. But I've found two rooms at Chelsea not many hundred yards from my mother and sisters, and I shall soon be ready to hang out there—when they've scraped the walls and put in some new lights. That's all I'm waiting for. But you see I don't wait to begin work: you can't conceive what a great fellow I'm going to be. The seed of immortality has sprouted within me."

"Only a fungoid growth, I dare say—a growing disease in the lungs," Deronda replied, treating Hans in brotherly fashion despite the absurdity. He walked toward some drawings propped on the ledge of his bookcases: five rapidly sketched heads, different aspects of the same face. He stood at a convenient distance, making no remark. Hans, too, fell silent for a minute, took up his palette, and began touching the picture on his easel.

"What do you think of them?" he asked finally.

"The full face looks too massive; otherwise the likenesses are good," Deronda said, more coldly than was usual with him.

"No, it is not too massive," Hans decided. "There is always a little surprise when one passes from the profile to the full face. But I shall enlarge her scale for Berenice. I am making a Berenice series—look at the sketches along there—and now I think of it, you are just the model I want for the Agrippa." Hans moved to Deronda's side, pencil and palette in hand, adding hastily, "No, no, I forgot; you don't like sitting for your portrait, confound you! However, I've picked up a capital Titus. There are to be five in the series. The first is Berenice clasping the knees of Gessius Florus... Then, this, where she stands on the Xystus with Agrippa... The third is Berenice exulting in the prospects of being Empress..."

"You must put a scroll in her mouth, else people will not understand that. You can't tell that in a picture."

"It will make them feel their ignorance then—an excellent aesthetic effect. The fourth is Titus sending Berenice away... *invitus invitam*... I've found a model for the Roman brute."

"Shall you make Berenice look fifty? She must have been that."

"No, no; a few mature touches to show the lapse of time. Dark-eyed beauty wears well, hers particularly. But now, here is the fifth: Berenice seated lonely on the ruins of Jerusalem. That is pure imagination. That is what ought to have been—perhaps was. Now, see how I tell a pathetic negative. Nobody knows what became of her—that is finely indicated by the series coming to a close. There is no sixth picture." Hans pretended to speak with a gasping sense of sublimity, drawing back his head with a frown as if searching for a like impression on Deronda. "I break off in the Homeric style. The story is chipped off, so to speak, and passes with a ragged edge into nothing—*le néant*; can anything be more sublime, especially in French? The vulgar would desire to see her corpse and burial... But now come and look at this on the easel. I have made some way there."

"That beseeching attitude is really good," Deronda said after a moment's contemplation. "You have been very industrious in the Christmas holidays; for I suppose you have taken up the subject since you came to London." Neither of them had yet mentioned Mirah.

"No," Hans said, putting touches to his picture, "I made up my mind to the subject before. I take that lucky chance for an augury that I am going to burst on the world as a great painter. I saw a splendid woman in the Trastevere—the grandest women there are half Jewesses—and she set me hunting for a fine situation of a Jewess at Rome. Like other men of vast learning, I ended by taking what lay on the surface. I'll show you a sketch of the Trasteverina's head when I can lay my hands on it."

"I should think she would be a more suitable model for Berenice," Deronda said, struggling to express his discontent.

"Not a bit of it. The model ought to be the most beautiful Jewess in the world, and I have found her."

"Have you made yourself sure that she would like to figure in that character? I should think no woman would be more abhorrent to her. Does she quite know what you are doing?"

"Certainly. I got her to throw herself precisely into this attitude. Little mother sat for Gessius Florus, and Mirah clasped her knees." Hans stepped back to inspect the effect of his touches.

"I dare say she knows nothing about Berenice's history," Deronda said, feeling more indignation than he could justify.

"Oh, yes, she does—ladies' edition. Berenice was a fervid patriot, but was beguiled by love and ambition into attaching herself to the arch-enemy of her people. Whence the Nemesis. Mirah takes it as a tragic parable, and cries to think what the penitent Berenice suffered as she wandered back to Jerusalem and sat desolate amidst desolation. That was her own phrase. I couldn't find it in my heart to tell her I invented that part of the story."

"Show me your Trasteverina," Deronda said, chiefly to hinder himself from saying something else.

"Shall you mind turning over that folio?" Hans asked. "My studies of heads are all there. But they are in confusion. You will perhaps find her next to a crop-eared undergraduate."

After Deronda had been turning over the drawings a minute or two, he said, "These seem to be all Cambridge heads and bits of country. Perhaps I had better begin at the other end."

"No; you'll find her about the middle. I emptied one folio into another."

"Is this one of your undergraduates?" Deronda asked, holding up a drawing. "It's an unusually agreeable face."

"That! Oh, that's a man named Gascoigne—Rex Gascoigne. An uncommonly good fellow; his upper lip, too, is good. I coached him before he got his scholarship. He ought to have taken honors last Easter. But he was ill, and has had to stay up another year. I must look him up. I want to know how he's going on."

"Here she is, I suppose," Deronda said, holding up the sketch of the Trasteverina.

"Ah," Hans said, looking at it rather contemptuously, "too coarse. I was unregenerate then."

Deronda was silent while he closed the folio, leaving the Trasteverina outside. Then, clasping his coat-collar and turning toward Hans, he said, "I dare say my scruples are excessive, Meyrick, but I must ask you to oblige me by giving up this notion."
