The sun-drenched days stretched on, offering neither the answer Romola craved nor the catastrophe she feared. There remained no word from Baldassarre, and despite the government's heightened vigilance, no evidence surfaced regarding the suspected conspiracy. Yet, fate delivered other trials that pressed close upon her spirit, bridging the chasm of anxious speculation with the tangible urgency of present suffering. These were the Plague and the Excommunication of Savonarola. Both events served to halt her drifting away from the Friar, anchoring her once more to the man who had unveiled a life of duty for her, and who appeared now to be losing a fierce battle between principle and corruption.

Romola found it impossible to navigate the streets of pestilence and woe carrying within her the exhilarating glow of a joy born solely from her own existence, without acknowledging that she owed this elevated moral life entirely to Fra Girolamo. Furthermore, she could not bear to witness the silencing and banishment of a figure distinguished not by heresy or superstition, but by the sheer energy with which he strove to make Christianity a living reality, without feeling pulled irresistibly toward his cause.

Deep into the scorching heat of June, the Excommunication decree, having traveled from Rome over several weeks, was solemnly proclaimed within the Duomo. Romola attended, hoping that the fervent resistance it sparked would fortify her sympathy for Savonarola, a wellspring of her own strength. The scene stood in stark, memorable contrast to the gatherings she had known before. Gone were the upturned faces of citizens filling the vast nave under the morning light—the young forming an amphitheater toward the walls, weaving a garland of hope around the elders' memories. Gone was the mighty voice that thrilled every heart with visions of grand struggles, both visible and unseen.

In their place stood the bare, somber walls illuminated only by the flicker of taper-light; a black-and-grey congregation of monks and secular priests with bowed, expectant faces; the occasional, faint chiming of small bells interrupting a monotone recitation of sentences long posted in church doors. Finally, the lights extinguished, leaving behind the slow, shuffling retreat of monkish feet into a dim silence.

Romola's devotion to the Frate was doubled by the gleeful triumph etched onto hardened, coarse faces, and by the terrified confusion evident in the expressions and speech of many of his staunchest allies. The boundary between the duty of obedience and the right of resistance was rarely clear, yet here it became overwhelmingly difficult due to the prevailing belief that the Church was not merely a political compromise, but a living organism infused with divine power to bless and curse. For most devout Florentines, this faith was not a chosen opinion but an inalienable certainty, akin to the curve of the sky. Consequently, Savonarola's bold arguments—that the Excommunication was unjust and therefore void—only caused them greater trembling, viewing his defiance as a challenge thrown at a mystical image against whose immense power no defense existed.

But Romola, whose mind had not been nourished by the traditional currents of the Christian community where her father lived apart, viewed her relationship with the Church solely through Savonarola. His moral force was the only authority she had ever bowed to. Thus, his excommunication appeared to her not as a tragedy, but as a threat from hostile vice. On one side stood a man dedicated to public virtue and spiritual purity; on the other, the assault of alarmed selfishness led by a lustful, greedy, lying, and murderous old man—once Rodrigo Borgia, now elevated to infamy as Pope Alexander VI.

Nuanced facts often soften such sharp contrasts, visible only to neutral observers unburdened by distress at seeing folly in martyrs or wisdom in their executioners. Yet Romola needed a strength neutrality could not provide. This Excommunication, simplifying and nobling Savonarola's stance by highlighting its broader implications, felt to her like a rescue from the isolating pressure of criticism and doubt. The Friar was no longer trapped in petty conflicts with Florentine foes arising from his pulpit's unchecked fervor; instead, he stood simply as a standard-bearer leaping into the breach, appealing to the entire Christian world against a vicious abuse of ecclesiastical power. Life seemed clearest and easiest when the heart raced at witnessing such generous self-sacrifice. In those moments, the soul's highest prize becomes undeniable, and one almost believes in the power to achieve it.

A fresh current of enthusiasm carried Romola through these arduous summer days. She had said nothing to Tito concerning her recent meeting with Baldassarre or the revelations he shared. What good could such agitated words accomplish? Only a cold sarcasm regarding her sympathy for his alleged assassin. Baldassarre was clearly helpless; the true danger was not that he would harm Tito, but that Tito, tracking him down, might devise a new scheme to eliminate the man who haunted his conscience.

Romola felt she could take no decisive action until she saw Baldassarre again and learned the full truth about the "other wife"—whether she was indeed the woman to whom Tito was originally bound. Possibilities surrounding this mystery, involving the deepest wound to her family's pride, mingled with her memories of an illusory love. These suspicions ate away at the tender remnants of her past marriage, while her absolute trust in Baldassarre's account filled her with a horror that drove her to avoid Tito. Had he been present, this fear might have compelled a passionate confrontation, but he was unusually absent from home.

Like many wealthy citizens during the plague, Tito spent his leisure hours in the countryside. The pleasant villa of Melema welcomed guests, and since Romola had refused to flee the city, he had no need to maintain a separate rural residence. However, by late July, as the public turmoil began to subside, Romola was left to face the quieter, heavier weight of her personal destiny. The Plague had nearly vanished, and Savonarola's position looked brighter thanks to a favorable magistrates who wrote urgent appeals to Rome requesting the revocation of the Excommunication.

Though her robust body succumbed to the natural lethargy following weeks of intense exertion, her restless mind refused idleness, demanding purpose save during the stifling midday heat. In the cool mornings and evenings, she walked constantly, varying her route with a vague hope: if Baldassarre still lived, she might meet him. Perhaps some illness had paralyzed his memory, causing him to forget her address—or even her existence. This was her most optimistic explanation for his silence. The probability she leaned toward, however, was far sadder: that he had died of the Plague.
