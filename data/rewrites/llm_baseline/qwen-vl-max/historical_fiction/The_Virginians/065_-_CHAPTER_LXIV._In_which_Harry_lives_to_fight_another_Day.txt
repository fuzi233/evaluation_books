Gumbo could not bear the thought of his master’s departure. When he heard that Mr. Harry had enlisted, the boy fell into such a frenzy of sorrow—howling, weeping, and wringing his hands—that one might have sworn his heart would break. No wonder he sought solace among the maids at Mr. Lambert’s lodgings. Wherever Gumbo went, he gravitated toward women, as if their gentle hearts were the only refuge for his own troubled soul. They, in turn, showed him kindness, not because of his station or skin, but because they saw in him something tender and true beneath the dark exterior. Indeed, history has shown that many Africans—like the famed Chevalier de St. Georges—have found favor with the fair sex, and Gumbo, though humble, was no exception. The servants wept with him; the maids treated him not just as Harry’s man, but as a brother.

Hetty, however, could not help laughing when she saw Gumbo sobbing over the fact that Master Harry had gone off as a volunteer—and without taking him along. “He’d die for Master Harry,” Gumbo declared, “and be cut into twenty thousand pieces before he’d let harm come to him!” Yet even in grief, he managed to eat—plenty of cold meat and beer in the kitchen, where nature demanded her due.

Greedy, idle, and prone to exaggeration, yes—he was all those things. But Hetty still gave him half a crown, and treated him with unusual tenderness. Her usual sharp tongue, once so quick with jests, now lay quiet. She moved through the house like a shadow—meek, subdued, almost reverent. She spoke softly to her mother, thanked John and Betty for serving dinner, endured Polly’s tugs on her hair during combing, and bore Charley’s clumsy footfalls without complaint. In her father’s presence, she was silent—so silent it seemed as if she had been transformed into a different girl altogether.

If Papa had asked her to roast the mutton or walk to church arm-in-arm with Gumbo, she would have curtsied low and said, “Yes, if you please, dear papa.”

But what did it matter? What kind of meal were poor volunteers eating, with cannonballs flying overhead? And church—how could she pray when the very words “Thou shalt do no murder” echoed in her ears, making her knees tremble as she knelt? She lowered her head whenever the parson read from the communion rail, afraid he might be looking directly at her. She thought constantly of travelers—by land or sea—and sickened each time she ran to fetch the newspaper, hoping for news of the expedition. She watched her father closely when he returned from the Ordnance Office, searching his face for signs of good or ill fortune. Was he well? Had he become a general? Was he wounded—or worse? Perhaps both legs had been taken by a single shot, like that old pensioner seen in Chelsea Garden the other day?

She would gladly wear wooden legs all her life—if only his could bring him home safe. At least, she vowed, she would never rise from her knees until he did.

“Have you heard of people whose hair turned grey in a single night, Theo?” she asked one day. “I wouldn’t be surprised if mine did—wouldn’t be surprised at all.” And she stared into the mirror, half-expecting to see silver strands already creeping through the brown.

“Theo,” her elder sister replied gently, “you weren’t this nervous when Papa was away in Minorca.”

“Oh, Theo,” Hetty sighed, “one can easily see George is not with the army—he’s safe at home.” At this, Theo blushed and looked down, her thoughts drifting elsewhere.

Indeed, if Mr. George had been in the army, matters would have been quite different.

But enough of such sorrow. We shall not prolong the anguish. Let us say plainly: Harry Warrington, for the moment, was as safe as any officer stationed in Regent’s Park Barracks.

His first campaign was neither glorious nor decisive—but it was real. The British Lion, after all, does not always meet a worthy foe. Sometimes, he sets out to battle a tiger and finds only a goose. He may roar and paw, but the goose is quickly dispatched. Lions must eat, just as men must live. But suppose, instead of one tiger, six emerge from the forest? Then the lion turns tail and retreats—wise to do so. To charge six tigers would make him no hero, but a fool.

Such was the case with Harry Warrington.

On June 1st, he sailed with about thirteen thousand others from the Isle of Wight. By dawn on the 5th, the fleet stood in the Bay of Cancale, Brittany. From their ships, the volunteers enjoyed a view of the French coast while the Commander-in-Chief and Commodore reconnoitered in a cutter. Cattle grazed nearby; dragoons trotted off into the distance. A small fort with two guns dared fire upon the officers in the boat. By two o’clock, the entire British fleet was anchored, and signals were sent for grenadiers from eleven regiments to board flat-bottomed boats and gather around the Essex, the Commodore’s ship.

Meanwhile, Admiral Howe hoisted his pennant aboard the frigate Success and sailed close to shore, followed by other frigates, to protect the landing. Under Lord George Sackville and General Dury, the volunteers, grenadiers, and three battalions of guards rowed ashore.

But no heroic deeds were possible. The French, who should have fought, fled. The frigates silenced the fort’s guns, and the army stormed it, capturing the entire garrison—just one old gentleman, who fired both cannons gallantly before surrendering. “If every Frenchman had acted like me,” he told his captors, “you would not have landed at Cancale at all.”

The advance force occupied the village of Cancale and rested under arms all night. There, Harry was teased by comrades for his eagerness to go on the war-path and bring back scalps. But no scalps came his way. The only death of the day was a French nobleman riding with his servant, whom Lord Downe surprised marching ahead with Kingsley’s company. My Lord offered quarter, which the man refused. Both he, his servant, and their horses were shot.

The next day, the full force landed and marched to St. Malo. Villages emptied before them; roads were narrow, forcing single-file march. Hedges rose high—had there been enemies, they could have been ambushed. At dusk, the army reached St. Malo, greeted by artillery fire from the town—though darkness limited damage. Under cover of the shots, the British set fire to ships, warehouses, pitch and tar stores, creating a blaze that burned through the night.

No resistance came. Though it was believed a strong French force remained in the town, they chose not to emerge. So the Duke of Marlborough and Lord Sackville decided not to attack. Instead, they withdrew to Cancale—and then boarded their ships again.

Had this been fiction, one might have sent Harry off on grander adventures. But history is not so obliging.

Exactly four weeks after leaving England, Harry arrived back at Portsmouth. He wrote to his brother George, who rushed to Dean Street with the letter in hand.

“Glorious news, ladies!” George announced, finding the Lamberts at breakfast. “Our champion has returned! He faced dangers—dragons, even! He says so himself!”

“Dragons?” Mrs. Lambert asked, startled.

“Not killed any,” George added, laughing. “But he saw them.”

“What does he mean?” Hetty cried.

“Immense creatures with long tails and steel scales,” Papa explained, “who breathe fire and devour virgins. Haven’t you read *The Seven Champions*?”

“St. George’s flag scared them off,” George said.

“I’ve read of them,” little Charles from Chartreux chimed in solemnly. “They eat women. One tried to eat Andromeda. Jason killed another guarding the golden apples.”

Harry’s letter continued:

> “Dearest Brother—
> I am glad to tell you I am returned—without commission, without wounds, without glory—but alive and hearty. Our ship was as crowded as poor Mr. Holwell’s Black Hole at Calicut. We had rough seas, and some of the gentlemen volunteers grumbled. Their stomachs are dainty. After Braund’s cookery and White’s kick-shaws, plain rum and biscuit didn’t sit well. But I, having sailed before, took my rations and flip with contentment. I meant to show our English macaronis that a Virginia gentleman is as good as the best.
>
> I wish I had more to brag about. But all I can say is—we went to France and came back. Why, I don’t think even your tragic pen could make anything of such a campaign. We landed on the 6th at Cancale Bay. We saw a few dragons on a hill—who rode away without fighting. We slept under canvas. We marched to St. Malo and burned many privateers. Then we returned to ship—never crossed swords with an enemy, except a few poor devils plundered by the troops. Better luck next time! This wasn’t glorious—but I liked it. I smelled powder, rosin, and pitch. I saw the enemy. Slept under canvas. Was dreadfully crowded and sick at sea. I liked it.
>
> Best compliments to Aunt Lambert. Tell Miss Hetty I wasn’t very frightened seeing the French horse.
>
> Your most affectionate brother,

Hetty’s fears were not eased by this news. But soon after, Mr. Lambert visited the troops at Portsmouth, and George accompanied him. They found Harry greatly changed—spirited, healthy, eager to learn military drills. He was no longer the listless youth who lingered in coffeehouses and drawing rooms. Now he was alert, cheerful, and full of energy—a new man entirely.

George walked arm-in-arm with him through the streets of Portsmouth, recalling his own brief military service two years prior. He marveled at the size of the army compared to the disastrous one he had once joined. Though the campaign was unglorious, morale was high. The nation celebrated—claiming victory over the proud Gaul, even if no great battle had been fought. What valour, after all, could compare to British valour?

And then came the next expedition—one that promised more glory.

This time, a distinguished volunteer led the charge: His Royal Highness Prince Edward. He boarded the Essex in a twelve-oared barge, the English standard flying proudly. The Admiral followed, then the captains in order of rank. The fleet sailed forth, Harry waving his hat from the deck as friends cheered from the shore.

He would surely earn his commission soon. George believed it was only a matter of time. Money was available to buy it—but better yet, if Harry could win it through courage and conduct. The regiment’s colonel praised him highly; officers and men alike liked him. He was clearly a young man of promise.

Ten days later, news arrived that thrilled the nation.

On August 7th and 8th, His Majesty’s troops had landed in the Bay des Marais, west of Cherbourg, facing a large enemy force. But the French fled. Cherbourg surrendered. The English flag flew over three outer forts. Twenty-seven ships were burned, dozens of brass cannons captured. Iron guns were destroyed, the harbor basin demolished, and both piers at the entrance shattered.

London erupted in celebration. Captured colors from Louisbourg were brought to St. Paul’s, escorted by life-guards and horse-grenadiers. At the West Gate, the Dean and Chapter received them—then the Tower and Park guns thundered in salute.

Next day, the Cherbourg cannons rolled through the streets—adorned with lilies, crowns, and mottoes. People shouted and cheered. The Teneraire, the Malfaisant, the Vainqueur—what a triumph!

And Hetty? She believed Harry had dragged every cannon from the batteries himself, vanquishing the gunners single-handed. His name soared. Madame de Bernstein inquired after him. Lady Maria begged George to visit and give her news.

George, once the center of attention, felt suddenly overshadowed. Wit, learning, tragedy—what were these compared to victories and brass cannons?

Still, hope burned bright. Fifteen thousand Britons—why not march on Paris itself? Some ladies believed it possible.

They embraced, congratulated one another, and dreamed of triumphs.

But then—clouds gathered.

The commanders, emboldened by success, returned to St. Malo, determined to destroy it this time. They landed in the Bay of St. Lunar, marched toward the town, and intended to finish what they had begun.

But the harbor proved unsafe. The fleet moved to St. Cas, maintaining supply lines.

Then came word: St. Malo was now defended by a full French army sent by the Governor of Brittany. The surprise attack was impossible.

So the British retreated to their ships, waiting in the Bay of St. Cas.

As they marched down to embark, they discovered a French army advancing from Brest.

Two-thirds of the troops and all the artillery were already aboard when the enemy descended.

Four companies of Guards and the grenadier units formed up on the beach, holding the rear. The rest were carried off in boats.

But as the French advanced, these defenders left their strong position—a raised dyke along the shore—and marched forward to attack, outnumbered ten to one.

After a brief struggle, they broke and fled to the boats—“Sauve qui peut!”

Seven hundred of the eleven hundred were killed, drowned, or captured. The General fell. And—where was Harry?

A man of peace myself, I confess I care less for the battlefield than for those left behind.

Jack the Guardsman and La Tulipe of the Royal Bretagne—facing each other, striking for blood. It is their nature. Like lions and bears, they must fight. But what of the girl at Tower Hill who clung to Jack before he left? Or the lass in Quimper who gave her lover tobacco and a brule-gueule before he stepped onto the noir trajet?

What have you done, poor hearts, that you must grieve so?

My concern is not with armies, but with the people they leave behind.

And so—what state must Miss Hetty Lambert be in, hearing of the disaster? What agony grips George Warrington? What sorrow fills Martin Lambert’s eyes as he sees his daughter’s face, pale and trembling?

Howe, the brave Commodore, rowed under fire, rescuing scores in his boats. Many drowned. Hundreds were prisoners or slain on the beach.

And among them—where was our Virginian?
