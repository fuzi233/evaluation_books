Two nights following this event, Pietro Vanucci and Andrea sat in the dim chamber, waiting for Gerard's supper. The air grew heavy with anticipation, and Pietro's patience frayed into peevishness. The clock had struck nine, yet Gerard remained absent. In a final, desperate gamble, Pietro dispatched Andrea to the master's room, hoping perhaps the scholar had slipped in unnoticed. Andrea shrugged, accepting the errand, and departed.

He returned empty-handed regarding Gerard, but clutching a folded scrap of paper. Though Andrea lacked the scholarly training to decipher the script—a skill reserved for the learned of his age—he possessed a sharp eye and had long memorized the distinctive hand of Pietro Vanucci.

"That is for you, I trow," Andrea declared, swelling with a pride in his own deduction.

Pietro snatched the note, his eyes darting over the lines while he read aloud to Andrea, his tone laced with bitter satire. "'Dear Pietro, dear Andrea, life is too great a burden.' So it is, my lad, yet that offers no excuse for wandering abroad when supper is served. Food is no burden."

"'Wear my habits!' Said the poplar to the juniper bush."

"'And thou, Andrea, mine amethyst ring; and me in both your hearts a month or two.' Why, Andrea?"

"'For my body, ere this ye read, it will lie in the Tiber. Trouble not to look for it. 'Tis not worth the pains. Oh unhappy day that it was born! Oh happy night that rids me of it! Adieu! Adieu! The broken-hearted Gerard.'"

"Here is a sorry jest of a peevish rogue," Pietro muttered, though his pallid cheeks and trembling teeth betrayed the terror gripping his heart.

Andrea, however, could not contain his grief. He filled the house with wailing cries. "O, miserable day! O, calamity of calamities! Gerard, my friend, my sweet patron! Help! Help! He is killing himself! Oh, good people, help me save him!" His screams tore through the household, and he burst into the street, bareheaded, imploring every Christian soul to aid him in rescuing his friend.

Soon, a crowd gathered, drawn by the clamor. Yet, poor Andrea found their sluggishness immovable. "Go down to the river?" they asked, shaking their heads. "No. It is not our business." "What part of the river?" "A wild goose chase." It was ill fortune to venture near the water after sunset; too many ghosts haunted those banks throughout the night.

However, a lackey who had stood idly opposite the house stepped forward, offering to accompany Andrea. This small gesture rallied three or four of the younger men, and the little band set off toward the Tiber.

As they approached the riverbank, the lackey questioned Andrea. Sobbing, the boy recounted the letter and Gerard's increasingly moody demeanor. Unbeknownst to them, this lackey was a spy of Princess Claelia. Their Italian tongues flew rapidly until they neared the water's edge.

But the moment the cool, damp air of the river struck them, mingling with the scent of the stream on that calm spring night, silence fell upon the group like a shroud. The moon shone clear and unclouded above, casting a stark light on the scene. Their footsteps echoed loudly and ominously against the stillness; their tongues were hushed.

Hurrying around a corner, they encountered a man. He halted, irresolute, at the sight of their approaching forms. He was bareheaded, his dripping hair glistening under the moonlight, and with the next step, they saw his garments were soaked through with water.

"Here he is!" cried one of the youths, who did not recognize Gerard's face or figure.

The stranger turned instantly and fled. They pursued him with all their might, Andrea leading the charge, the princess's lackey close behind. Andrea gained on him briefly, but the fugitive twisted sharply into a narrow alley. Andrea shot past, unable to check his momentum, and the pursuers soon found themselves lost in a labyrinthine maze. It was futile to chase a quick-footed fugitive who knew every inch of the streets; they could follow him only by the sound of his retreating footsteps.

Returning to their companions, they found them standing frozen at the spot where the man had paused, utterly confounded. For Pietro had insisted the fugitive bore neither the features nor the stature of Gerard.

"Are ye verily sure?" the others demanded. "He had been in the river. Why, in the saints' names, did he flee at our approach?"

Then Vanucci spoke, "Friends, methinks this has nought to do with him we seek. What shall we do, Andrea?"

Here the lackey interjected. "Let us track him to the water's side to make sure. See, he hath come dripping all the way."

This counsel was approved, and with little difficulty, they retraced the man's path. Yet, soon they encountered a new enigma.

Scarcely fifty yards had they gone when the trail of water turned away from the river, leading instead to the gate of a large, gloomy building—a monastery. They stood irresolute before the dark pile, gazing at its imposing walls, sensing that it hid some horrible mystery.

Suddenly, Andrea let out a shout. "Here be the drops again!" he cried. "And this road leadeth to the river."

They resumed the chase. Soon it became clear that the moisture was guiding them home. The track grew wetter and wetter, leading them directly to the Tiber's edge. There, on the bank, a bucketful of water appeared to have been discharged from the stream.

At first, they shouted in triumph, believing they had made a discovery. But reflection quickly sobered them. It amounted to nothing. Certainly, a man had been in the water and emerged safely, but that man was not Gerard. One suggested they know a fisherman nearby who possessed nets and drags. They located the fisherman and paid him liberally to sink nets in the river below the spot and drag them above and below, promising gold if he found the body.

Then they ran vainly up and down the river, which flowed so calm and voiceless, holding a thousand strange secrets. Suddenly, Andrea, with a cry of hope, ran back toward the house.

He returned in less than half an hour.

"No," he groaned, wringing his hands in despair.

"What is the hour?" asked the lackey.

"Four hours past midnight."

"My pretty lad," the lackey said solemnly, "say a mass for thy friend's soul: for he is not among living men."

Morning broke, gray and cold. Worn out by fatigue and heart-sick with uncertainty, Andrea and Pietro returned home. The days rolled on, mute as the Tiber itself, offering no answer to Gerard's fate.
