## Wherein Paul flees his own miserable solitude, stumbles on the oddest collection of housemates imaginable, and gets tangled up in the orbit of a very haughty former star of the provincial stage.

Even the self-inflicted melodramatic suffering of shy, over-sensitive 20-something homebodies eventually runs its course, thank goodness. Winter rolled around, and I threw myself headfirst into work—there’s no better drowning pool for all your dumb, self-pitying woe than a good solid work habit. My tiny garret “apartment” got cozy fast: a handful of glowing coals in the grate, two guttering tallow candles on the rickety table, a stack of cheap paper, and a new bottle of ink. What else does a would-be writer need, anyway? I was fully convinced I was about to be the great medium for all the spooky, brilliant story voices banging around in the ether—though in hindsight, the ether clearly left its front door locked the whole time.

London, with its ten million weirdos, romances, tragedies, and silly little jokes, was right there at my fingertips, just waiting for me to look at it and write it down honestly. But did it ever occur to me I could write stories about the actual people I saw walking down the street every day? Of course not. All my terrible tales starred cottage maidens, gruff yeoman farmers, and windmill backdrops, or remote moated granges, or craggy mountains I’d never once seen in my life. I’m fairly sure every single 19-year-old wannabe fiction writer goes through this specific phase of brain rot.

All of my stories dripped with soft, mournful melancholy. Back then, sentiment was all the rage, and like every newbie creator ever, I copied the trend so hard you could see the smudge marks. The worst part? Being a heroine in my story was basically a death sentence. No matter how bouncy her curly hair was (which I was sure was the official marker of top-tier vitality), no matter if she had “dancing eyes” or “ringing laughter” or the grace of a baby deer in the first chapter, the poor lass was guaranteed to drop dead of consumption before page 15, buried under a blanket of snowdrops and early primroses (I’d memorized a little illustrated pamphlet called *Our Garden Favourites* for that very specific detail) that grew in her tiny churchyard like they’d been force-fed fertilizer. If the graveyard was near the coast, the local sea breezes would make a beeline straight for it to gallivant around disrespectfully, no other graveyard allowed. Then 50 years later, a white-haired sad guy would show up with a handful of small kids, drag them to her grave, and tell them the whole miserable story—what a terrible Saturday outing that must have been for the toddlers.

Every once in a while, to mix things up a bit, I’d kill off the leading man instead. He’d always give a grand final speech on his deathbed going, “Eh, it’s fine, this is for the best.” No snowdrops or primroses for him, though, sorry: he’d be planted under a rude stick cross in the most deserted patch of African veldt imaginable, or halfway across the Sahara. I’d pop into the British Museum reading room for 20 minutes to fact-check what those places looked like, scribble the descriptions down, and call it a day.

Those little fledgling stories were so scrawny and sad. I’d send them flying out of my garret nest full of hope, and every single one of them would come fluttering back to me, crumpled, stained, crumpled, and clearly beat up by the post office. The only person who ever read them and cried over them was me.

I tried every trick in the book to get them accepted. Sometimes I’d tuck them into an envelope with a curt, tough-guy business note like “Take it or leave it, chump.” Other times I’d scrawl a pathetic, sobbing appeal begging the editor to give my poor little baby a chance. Sometimes I’d name a price of five guineas, demand a crossed cheque, and act like I was a big shot professional. Other times I’d try to trick them by offering my work for free, like it was a free sample of dodgy pickles from a travelling salesman, how could they turn down a free sample? Sometimes I’d get snarky, slip in a pre-stamped return envelope and write a note daring the editor to reject me. Other times I’d lay my whole heart out, admit I was a total beginner, and beg them to tell me exactly what my faults were—if they even had any, obviously.

Not a single one found a home. They’d be gone for a month, or a week, or two whole days, and then they’d boomerang right back. I never lost a single manuscript, which at a certain point got so monotonous I found myself actively wishing the post office would lose one just to shake things up.

I grew to hate the poor little slavey who’d burst into my room, grinning like she’d caught me doing something embarrassing, and hold the rejected manuscript out between her apron-covered thumb and finger. Her constant little sniff sounded to me like unmasked contempt. If I flew down the stairs the second I heard the postman’s knock and grabbed the envelope straight from his hands, I swore he was snickering at me behind his mustache. I’d tear the envelope open, yell at the stupid story, fling it straight into the fire—then dive to fish it out before it was more than scorched, smooth the crumpled pages out, and reread the whole thing to make sure it was still brilliant. There was no way the editor had actually seen it, obviously. Some lazy, jealous underling had tossed it straight into the rejection pile out of spite. I’d sent it to the wrong magazine! It arrived on the exact day the editor’s dog died! Any day now, their real triumph would come. I’d rewrite the first and last pages, stuff it in a fresh envelope, and send it out again, brimming with brand new stupid hope.

While all this was going on, I’d decided that if I was going to be a successful famous genius, I couldn’t just hide in my garret writing sad stories. I had to learn how to be cool in social settings too. The two big sins I had the most trouble forcing myself to enjoy? Smoking and drinking. This isn’t me bragging about being virtuous, by the way. This is me admitting my body is a total coward. My brain was fully game to be a cool bohemian artist, but my flesh was weak as wet toast. Back in school, I’d tried smoking a cigarette once or twice just to impress the other boys, and every single time I’d ended up hunched over the school toilet heaving my guts up. I quit trying for a while, and told everyone holier-than-thou that smoking was bad for growing boys, even though I knew perfectly well I was just a big baby making excuses. Same with drinking: I’d snuck sips of our family’s decanted claret now and then, and fully agreed with St. Paul that a little wine is for your stomach’s sake—but only as medicine, never for fun. I noticed even full grown men made horrible faces when they drank my mom’s home brewed wine, so I told myself that liking strong drink was a skill, not a natural talent, and I could put it off a little longer.

But now I was a grown man living on my own, my education in being a bohemian could not be delayed any further. A quarter of a century ago, if you claimed to be a genius and didn’t smoke and drink way more than was healthy, everyone would write you off as a total fraud no questions asked. I was pretty sure I was a genius, though not 100% certain—but at the very least, I could nail the smoking and drinking part. I set to it systematically. Smoking, I learned, was best practiced on Saturday nights, so I had all of Sunday to go for long walks and shake the dizziness out of my system. After enough patience and willpower, I pulled it off: I could smoke a whole cigarette and look like I was actually enjoying it, no heaving required. Most lads would have stopped there, but I’m nothing if not an overachiever. Cigarettes conquered, I moved straight onto cigars. I still remember my first one—most people do. It was at a smoking concert in the Islington Drill Hall, and my friend Minikin had dragged me along. I couldn’t tell if the swimming in my head was from the cigar or the overcrowded, overheated room full of smoke, so I made my excuses and snuck out to the little courtyard. There it was 100% the stupid cigar making me want to die. My first instinct was to yank it out of my mouth and fling it as far across the yard as I could. But my stupid stubborn conscience was yelling at me to persevere. Then I got a brilliant dumb idea: I’d climb the low wall separating the courtyard from the neighbor’s garden, and walk the whole length of it. If I could do it, I’d earned the right to be a cool artist. If I couldn’t? I was allowed to toss that evil thing in the gutter and stumble home to bed. I hauled myself up onto the wall, and made it all of two yards before I ended up draped over it like a wet towel, legs dangling one side, head hanging over the other. Perfect, I thought, this counts as the wall being too hard. I very nearly swore off smoking forever right there—but I’m proud to say I fought that temptation off, whispering to myself over and over: “Never give up. What other men can do, I can do! All victory is won through suffering!”

Liking alcohol somehow was even harder. I almost gave up on spirits entirely once, when I mixed up the bottles and drank a huge swig of hair oil by mistake—and it was less disgusting than the whiskey. Still, twice a week I’d force myself to stand there over a glass of beer, chug the whole horrible thing down to the last drop, then reward myself with a whole box of chocolates to wash the taste out. I kept telling myself it was good for me, that one day I’d love beer, and I’d be so grateful I made myself tough it out.

Other, more sensible improvements were going pretty well too. My crippling shyness was fading, slow as molasses. The trick I figured out was to stop fighting it, just accept it as part of my weird personality, and laugh at it along with everyone else. The indifferent, cold world has a way of toughening up your too-sensitive skin pretty fast. All the little scrapes of daily life were sanding off my sharp, awkward edges. I started being able to talk to people without stammering, and I even started liking hanging around with them.

Fate did me a huge favor by dumping me in that shabby shared house in Nelson Square. That leaky-roofed joint was one of the funniest, most chaotic menageries I’ve ever lived in. Our landlady, Mrs. Peedles, lived down in the basement with the little slavey, and she’d once been a minor actress in Charles Keane’s company back at the old Princess’s Theatre. She’d only ever played tiny walk-on parts, but she’d explain to anyone who’d listen that even back then, London audiences were terrible judges of real art, and the actor-manager was dragged down by his useless wife, obviously. Back in the provinces, though? She’d been glorious. Juliet, Amy Robsart, Mrs. Haller in *The Stranger*—she’d played every single great “legitimate” role in the canon, no exceptions. If we so much as squinted like we didn’t believe her, she’d dig out foot-long old playbills plastered with exclamation marks: *“On Tuesday Evening! By Special Desire!!! Blessington's Theatre! In the Meadow, adjoining the Falcon Arms!”*, *“On Saturday! Under the Patronage of Col. Sir William and the Officers of the 74th!!!! In the Corn Exchange!”* And if that still wasn’t enough, she’d launch mid-conversation into Lady Macbeth’s sleepwalking scene, or Ophelia’s mad entrance from Hamlet Act 4, just for fun. We’d all rush to assure her we were 100% her biggest fans immediately.

Listening to her gush about the old days, you could almost picture her as the “arch Miss Lucretia Barry” everyone had loved. Looking at her, though—small, faded, watery-eyed—you’d have to pull out her tattered old scrapbook of underlined newspaper clippings just to prove she wasn’t making it all up. Fate had draped a whole heavy blanket of depression over her since then. The slightest little thing would make her plop down on the nearest chair and immediately launch into a story about all her misfortunes. She had no idea she did it, though. She was fully convinced she was the cheeriest person alive.

“Ah well, fretting’s no use! We’ve got to grin and bear it, right?” she’d say, wiping her eyes on her apron. “It’s better to laugh than cry, that’s what I always say!” And then she’d cackle so hard she’d wheeze, to prove it.

Years of running up and down the stairs had left her permanently short of breath, no amount of huffing and puffing could fix it.

“You don’t know how to breathe!” our second-floor front tenant tried to explain to her once, very kindly. “You don’t swallow the air, you just gargle with it! Take a big draught, shut your mouth, don’t be scared of it, and don’t let it out till it’s done some work! That’s what air’s for!” He even stood over her holding a handkerchief over her mouth to help, but it was no use. “There don’t seem to be any space left inside me for it,” she panted.

Bells were her whole life. She spent every second of the day listening for a doorbell to ring. Conversations were just something to do to kill time while waiting for the next interruption.

One Christmas, a commercial traveller friend gave me a bottle of whiskey as a gift. I hated whiskey, so it was no hardship at all to keep it by my door for Mrs. Peedles, for the days she’d stumble up the stairs, breathless, hands clutching her side, and sink onto the chair just outside my room. Her washed-out little face would light right up when I offered her a sip. “Oh, I don’t mind if I do!” she’d say. “It’s a poor heart that never rejoices!” Then she’d sit there, the booze loosening her tongue, and tell me endless stories about all the previous young lodgers who’d lived in my room, and all the terrible things that had happened to them. Turns out, I’d accidentally rented the most cursed house in all of London. The guy who lived in my exact garret before me looked just like me, she said, and wrote poetry on that same rickety table I was using. Now he was in Portland Prison doing five years for forgery. Mrs. Peedles acted like writing poetry and forging cheques were basically just two sides of the same artistic coin. Another former lodger had been a super hardworking, ambitious student. He could have been anything, according to her, but right now he was a very unwell inmate of the Hanwell Lunatic Asylum. “I’ve always noticed it!” she’d say, shaking her head. “It’s always the nicest, hardest-working lads that get all the trouble. I don’t know why the world’s like that.” I was so relieved when that bottle of whiskey ran out. If we’d had a second one, I might have talked myself into suicide out of morbid fear of the curse.

There was no Mr. Peedles around the house, at least not for Mrs. Peedles—though he was still alive somewhere, last we heard. He’d been general utility man back at the Princess’s, a strapping handsome fellow in his day, the kind who’d get led astray by any pretty minx that batted her eyes at him. Mrs. Peedles spoke very bitterly about general utility men, saying they were useless at their jobs. On weekdays, she wore the same plain black dress and plain black cap, no frills at all. But on Sundays and holidays? She transformed completely. She’d kept almost all of her old stage wardrobe, right down to the paste-studded shoes and the tinsel necklaces. Suddenly you’d see her padding around the landing looking like a very lumpy Hermia in classic flowing Greek robes, or a very bulgy Lady Teazle in heavy brocade, receiving her fellow washed-up former actor friends on Sunday evenings, reminiscing about the old days before their careers broke. Or she’d drape an old mackintosh over all the finery to hide it from the rude street strangers, and go visit her few old friends. Her cheeks would flush pink, her eyebrows would darken and arch again, all the old liveliness coming back. The only clues that it was still the same woman were her thin grey hair and her work-worn, rough hands. The change wasn’t just surface level, either. The drab, tired Mrs. Peedles hung up on a hook behind the kitchen door, like a discarded rag. Out of the old wardrobe, along with the silks and satins, came out Miss Lucretia Barry, a little aged, a little faded, but still perfectly “arch.”

Next door to my room lived an old law-writer and his wife. They were ancient, and miserably poor through no fault of their own. All those schoolbook maxims about hard work paying off? The world is full of that boring, stubborn bad luck that wears you down until you stop fighting. Their story was so simple and sad it was almost boring. He’d been a schoolmaster, she’d been a pupil teacher, they married young and everything went great for a few years. Then they both came down with typhoid from bad drains. No moral, no big dramatic lesson to be learned, just bad drains. They clawed their way back out of debt, just to lose it all again when they helped a poor friend who’d once helped them. You couldn’t blame them for that, right? They couldn’t have just left him to rot. And that’s how it went, over and over, one small disaster after another. Now they were just tired, patient, working as hard as they could, so used to bad luck they didn’t even care anymore, just happy if they got enough bread and tea to get by. Their only luxury was an occasional sip of beer or spirits. Half the time he couldn’t get any work, and then they’d go hungry. Law work always got handed out late at night, to be finished and dropped off at the office first
