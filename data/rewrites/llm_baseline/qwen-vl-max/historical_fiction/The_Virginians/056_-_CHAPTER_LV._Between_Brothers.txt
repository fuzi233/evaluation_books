Of course, the two young men had spoken at length of home—of the people they knew, the happenings in their absence, and all that had shaped their lives since last they met. How were Harry’s dogs? Was little Dempster still as lively as ever? What of old Nathan, who had served their family for generations? And was Mountain well? Had Fanny grown into a fine young woman? George listened with amusement as Harry recounted how Parson Broadbent’s daughter had become engaged to Tom Barker of Savannah, and how they were planning to settle in Georgia.

Harry confessed he had once been deeply smitten by the parson’s daughter—so much so that he’d spent more than his share of pocket money at cards and consumed gallons of strong spirits just to keep near her. But, as he ruefully admitted, Madam Esmond would never have allowed such a match. “She’d have said I was throwing myself away on Polly Broadbent,” he laughed. “And she was right—though I did love her enough to make fools of myself.”

George then shared news of Colonel Washington’s wife—a pleasant, attractive woman of good fortune whom he had brought to Richmond. He described, with wry humor, the formalities of the visit between his mother and the new Mrs. Washington, and how his mother had greeted her with an almost comical politeness. “Never mind, George,” Mrs. Mountain had said, chuckling. “The Colonel has taken another wife, but I’m certain two young gentlemen I know nearly ended up with a six-foot-two stepfather!”

Indeed, Mountain was always imagining matches where none existed. Two people could hardly sit down to tea or play a hand of cards without her declaring it was clearly a sign of destiny. It was she—the busybody—who had first whispered about the Indian woman, Biche. When Parson Stack mentioned it to Madam Esmond, she dismissed the idea with scorn: “I’d as soon believe Mr. Esmond stole the silver spoons or married a kitchen maid.” Yet though she refused to believe the accusation, her coldness toward Biche was unmistakable. The poor woman, hurt and humiliated, withdrew into the servants’ quarters, where she drowned her sorrow in drink.

George painted a bleak picture of his time at home. “The birthright is mine, Harry,” he said, “but you are the favorite—and God help me, I think my mother resents that. Why must I be the elder? If you had come first, you’d have had the best cellar, the finest horse, the most respect in the county. Instead, I am the one no one trusts, the one who frightens them with my silence. I should have been second son—gone to law, or come to England for my degrees, become a parson, and said grace at your table. But fate has placed me here, and now I’m cursed with being the one who must fix what’s broken.”

“Why, Georgy,” Harry replied, grinning, “you’re quoting poetry now! You sound like some scholar from a book.”

“I suppose someone spoke those lines before me,” George said, smiling faintly. “It’s from one of your books. You read every word ever written, don’t you?”

“That’s true,” Harry admitted, voice thick with emotion. “And I remember how you used to talk about them—how much they meant to you. That’s why I liked seeing the authors at Tunbridge. I took off my hat to them—not because I cared for their writing, but because I remembered how much they mattered to you.”

He paused, eyes glistening. “To see you again—it feels like a dream. Do you remember that bloody Indian with his knife at your throat? I’d give anything to repay Monsieur de Florac for saving you—but I’ve nothing left but my gold knee-buckles. They’re worth less than two guineas.”

“You have half of what I possess,” George said gently. “We’ll divide it when I’ve paid the Frenchman.”

At this, Harry erupted not only in praise but in fervent oaths—outbursts born of deep affection and relief. He swore there had never been a better brother than George. For days after his arrival, Harry followed him with his eyes. He’d drop his knife and fork mid-meal, or lay aside his newspaper, just to watch George and smile to himself. Walking through Hyde Park or along the Mall, he’d turn to the crowd as if to say, “Look—this is my brother! This is the man who was dead and now lives again! Is there any other brother in Christendom like him?”

He insisted George fulfill his promise to pay Museau’s heirs the twelve thousand livres he’d pledged for his ransom. But this had caused great distress at home. With Museau dead, Madam Esmond argued passionately—nearly angrily—that the bargain was void. The man had been a rogue, she declared. She refused to reward sin. Mr. Esmond had his own inheritance; let him spend it if he wished. But she would not consent to giving money to peasants in Normandy—enemies of England—who would likely send every penny to priests and the pope. It was extravagance, wickedness. If George wanted to squander his father’s fortune (and she noted he hadn’t shown such eagerness before, especially when Harry’s inheritance was at stake), why not give it to his own blood—to poor Harry, suddenly stripped of his future? Why send it across the sea?

This argument had raged between mother and son throughout George’s final days in Virginia. It remained unresolved. On the morning of his departure, Madam Esmond came to his bedside after a sleepless night and asked whether he still intended to throw away his father’s property. He answered, heavy with grief and duty, that his word was given—he must honor it. She replied that she would continue praying Heaven might soften his proud heart and help her bear her burdens. The last image George carried of her was of her standing beside his bed—pale, silent, eyes dry—before slowly turning and leaving.

“Where did you learn to win everyone over, Harry?” George asked later. “How is it that you and everyone else begin as friends? Teach me some of your secrets—though I may not want to learn them. I find it oddly pleasing when people hate me.”

At first, in Richmond, he had been admired. Mr. Esmond Warrington, the sole survivor from Braddock’s defeat, had been welcomed with kindness—by the town, by the clergy, even by Madam Esmond herself. The parson preached a sermon praising his escape; neighbors visited him; the family coach was sent out. There were whispers of admiration—perhaps even flirtation. But Mother swiftly quashed any warmth. Her haughtiness frightened people away. His popularity faded before the final blow: the appearance of Biche.

He wasn’t jolly enough for the officers, who preferred drinking, dice, and coarse talk. Too sharp-tongued for the ladies, whose endless gossip bored him as much as the men’s boasting. “I can’t tell you, Harry,” he said, “how lonely I felt among all that noise and scandal. I missed my prison—even Duquesne—where I had peace and freedom of thought.”

He was shy, he admitted. He could speak freely to few. Most of the time, he sat in silence. At home, it had been Harry who carried the conversation at meals, occasionally earning a smile from their mother. When George and she were together, they had little in common—no subject to discuss until they clashed over theology or law.

So the gentlemen deemed him aloof and dull. The ladies found him cold, sarcastic—never sure if he meant what he said. Before long, they decided he was unpleasant. Then came Biche.

“Oh, what a character they made of me!” George cried, furious. “What a life they gave me after Museau’s messenger arrived! Boys hooted at her if she walked the street. Ladies offered half-curtseys and crossed to the other side. That hypocritical clergyman went from tea party to tea party preaching about seduction and the sins of popish lands. And when Biche returned home weeks later—well, they declared it was a plot between us. Some said she waited on the other side of the river until I signaled her to cross. Officers mocked me at the coffeehouse, cracking jokes about the woman I’d chosen.”

“The world,” he spat, “is a cruel place.”

He considered fleeing to Castlewood—his mother found the estate dreary, and her only comfort was Mr. Stack’s sermons—when news reached him of Harry’s troubles. “That gave me a reason to leave,” he said. “And I think we were all glad to have an excuse.”

“I’d like to see any of those scoundrels who spoke against you,” Harry roared, pacing the room. “I’d break their bones! I did something similar for Bob Clubber.”

“For that sneaking, backbiting, toad-eating wretch who follows my lord around Greenway Court?” George asked. “If you whipped him, I hope you gave him a proper beating!”

“We were bound over to keep the peace,” George replied. “I offered to go to Maryland and settle things there—but of course, the good folk said, ‘Well, he’s already broken the seventh commandment—why not the sixth?’ So, with that and that—I left home, Harry, with a reputation worse than any young man ever earned—though I’m as innocent of the crime as you are.”

Ah, what a chance this offers for moralizing! If only the reader and I could gather—could write down, could publish with illustrations—all the lies told about each of us since we became men—what a harrowing, thrilling tale it would make! Not only do people know everything about us, but far more. Recently, the postman delivered a paper containing a critique that stated: “This author claims he was born in such-and-such a year. It is a lie. He was born in another.” The critic knew better—of course he did. Another, from the land that gave birth to Mulligan, warned a friend: “Don’t mention New South Wales to him. He has a brother there, and the family never speaks his name.”

But such subjects are too vast for a single paragraph. One day, I shall prepare a memoir—or better yet, let a society of literary men compile a series of biographies—lives of gentlemen, as told by friends they never knew.

Having laid bare his trials, George now turned to hear Harry’s story. Harry spoke frankly of his feelings toward the family of Castlewood—not for losing at cards, for he was honest in games, but for refusing aid to the chaplain in need and dismissing him with false excuses. As for Mr. Will, Harry had settled matters after the horse-dealing affair, marking his disapproval plainly upon the man’s face.

Regarding the Countess and Lady Fanny, Harry spoke cautiously but not kindly. He had heard stories—about the Countess as a card-playing old woman, Lady Fanny as a reckless flirt. Who told him? A person who knew them well—Maria herself, during those days of open trust mentioned in the previous volume. Maria had confided many tales about her stepmother and half-sister, none of which reflected well on either.

But when it came to Lady Maria, Harry was firm. “It may be imprudent,” he said. “I won’t deny it. I may be a fool—I think I am. I know there will be a terrible fight at home. Madam and she will clash. But we can live apart. Our estate is large enough. I can go elsewhere—anywhere but Richmond or Castlewood. When you inherit, you’ll give me a portion—at worst, Madam will rent it cheaply. Or I’ll become a farmer or factor. I cannot, I will not, leave Maria. She has done so much for me—she brought every jewel she owned, dear brave soul, and threw them into my lap with her last guinea. God bless her!”

Here he wiped his eyes with his sleeve, stamped his foot, and declared, “No, brother—I won’t part from her. Not even if I were made Governor of Virginia tomorrow. And you—my dearest old George—would never advise me to do so.”

“I am here to advise you,” George said quietly. “I am here to try to end this engagement—if I can. I cannot imagine a more unfortunate match. But I cannot counsel you to break your word, my boy.”

“I knew you couldn’t,” Harry replied, darkly. “What’s said is said, George. I’ve made my bed. Now I must lie in it.”

Such was their understanding when they first discussed Harry’s love affair. But after George’s conversation with his aunt, and the deeper insights gained from Mr. Warrington—whose skeptical nature led him to question everything—doubt began to creep in. Was Maria truly in love with Harry, or was it his wealth? His looks? Were the stories Aunt Bernstein had told true? Certainly, he could not urge Harry to break his promise. But perhaps—just perhaps—he could devise a way to test her sincerity. And it was this thought, this quiet calculation, that shaped the actions he would take next—actions that seemed unkind, but were born of concern.
