Gerard and Pietro worked side by side, their usual camaraderie replaced by a strained atmosphere. Gerard attacked his latest canvas with fervor, producing remarkable work in mere minutes, only to abandon it moments later, frustrated by his own lack of focus. Meanwhile, the Dutchman, unfazed by the commotion, continued to work steadily, his calm demeanor a stark contrast to Gerard's turmoil.

Gerard's landlady, Fiammina, had unwittingly played matchmaker, pairing Gerard with her friend Teresa, a Roman matron whose husband boasted connections with influential figures in the city. Gerard's chance encounter with Teresa had left a lasting impression on her, and she now sought him out, eager to discuss the challenges faced by artists in Rome.

As Gerard descended to the common room, he spotted Teresa, her eyes shining with warmth. "Ah, madama," he said, "you're here. I didn't expect to see you today." He inquired about her young son, who had recently returned from a harrowing journey by sea. "He's well," Teresa replied, her voice trembling slightly.

Fiammina, observing the exchange, raised an eyebrow. "What are you two discussing?" she asked, her tone laced with curiosity. Gerard hesitated, unsure how much to reveal, but Teresa intervened, her words tumbling forth in a heartfelt tale of Gerard's heroism during her son's ordeal. Gerard's face flushed as he listened, his actions reduced to a blur as Teresa recounted his bravery.

Fiammina's expression softened, and she apologized for her earlier skepticism. "I'm grateful for your service, Gerard," she said, her voice sincere. "In helping you, I've helped myself." The two women delved into a lively discussion about the obstacles facing artists in Rome, the labyrinthine system of commissions, fees, and patronage that seemed insurmountable.

Teresa's words struck a chord within Gerard. "You're right," he said, his voice tinged with disappointment. "I shouldn't have expected anything different." She counseled him to seek patronage elsewhere, in cities less consumed by greed and more open to artistic innovation. "La corte Romana non vuol' pecora senza lana," she quoted, her words echoing through the room.
