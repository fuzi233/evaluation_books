Loyal Britons may well shudder at the audacity of Mr. George Warrington’s refusal—his graceless dismissal of a princely offer, extended by none other than His Royal Highness, the Captain-General of the British Army and beloved son of the Crown. To spurn such favor! To cast aside the golden ladder of advancement! Even Will Esmond, never one to spare an oath, cursed his cousin’s folly with relish.

"A savage may scalp himself if he pleases," remarked Cousin Castlewood dryly. "Or perhaps young George has heard one war-whoop too many since Braddock’s folly and lost his taste for battle."

Will sneered. "The elder brother races to ruin, and the younger follows. The next time I see George, I’ll give him a piece of my mind."

"And if you do," snapped Lady Maria, "pray ensure Harry is not within earshot."

At this, Will’s curses multiplied.

My lord, ever the peacemaker—or at least the amused spectator—waved a languid hand. "Must we all be warriors? Will is champion enough for this house. As for the Warrington boys, let George be the scholar and Harry the fighter. A pity, Will, that when you sought a quarrel, you found Harry’s fist instead of George’s bookish chin."

"Your own hand excels at cards, my lord," retorted Will’s mother.

"Indeed," simpered my lord, admiring his fingers. "A delicate instrument, unfit for war but most adept at piquet. Yours, my lady, is no less skillful—though I bless the day it was bestowed upon my late father."

Thus the family sparred, my lord always the victor in their barbed exchanges.

Madame de Bernstein, upon hearing of George’s blunder, raged like a tempest—though age had softened her once-ferocious spirit, and she dared not storm at George himself. When he visited her, she greeted him with a groan.

"You have murdered your prospects at court, nephew!"

"I never sought them, madam," George replied stiffly.

"You might have aided Harry’s rise had you accepted the Duke’s favor. Princes do not forgive slights."

"General Lambert said as much," George admitted, flushing. "I see now I was wrong. But I am no soldier, and Harry would have served better."

"Ah, so you offered the Prince a better mount?" The Baroness arched a brow.

"*Expende Hannibalem*," George muttered. "Our Hannibal is no lightweight."

She blinked. "Your Latin is wasted on me, sir."

"I made the same defense to Wolfe and Lambert. They granted my excuse, if not my wisdom. Who knows? Perhaps they’ll plead my case with His Highness. Battles weary me—I prefer Homer’s wars to real ones."

"Then why play the soldier at all?" she demanded.

"My mother loved Harry best," George said darkly. "And when the enemy came, duty called. Had I been born Scots, I might have—"

"Silence!" she cried—though her face betrayed amusement.

Yet where Madame de Bernstein chuckled, Sir Miles and Lady Warrington recoiled in pious horror.

"What upbringing," lamented her ladyship, "could produce such ingratitude?"

Sir Miles, ever dutiful, attended the Prince’s next levee to proclaim his own unwavering loyalty. The Prince, with icy grace, remarked, "No one ever doubted Sir Miles would refuse a royal offer." A compliment her ladyship trumpeted far and wide.

Thus when George next faced them, their looks were funeral. Ah, that familial pity—the mournful gaze reserved for those who have blundered beyond redemption! Yet Sir Miles’s anger thawed as the Prince’s star waned. Defeat at Hastenbeck and the capitulation that followed saw His Highness stripped of command, retreating into private life—a fallen idol.

One day in St. James’s Park, George and Harry, walking with Lambert, encountered the Prince—no star upon his coat, no retinue at his heels. He paused, spoke briefly, and to George said:

"You did well, sir, not to follow me in the spring."

"Then I was sorry, sir," George replied with a deep bow. "Now I am sorrier still."

The Prince touched his hat and walked on.

Lambert, watching, laughed. "Harry, your brother is like Charing Cross’s lion—turning his back on the rising sun to salute the setting one."

"Because I honor fallen greatness?" George asked.

"Because you are a fool," Lambert chuckled. "Your uncle will rage anew—and so would I, if I weren’t so amused."

And with that, the three strode on, the lion at their backs gazing steadfastly westward.
