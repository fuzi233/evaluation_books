As the Compagnacci set out on their grim errand, another man moved through the chill, grey twilight on the opposite bank of the Arno. His purpose bore no connection to theirs; yet fate had drawn him to this desolate stretch of riverbank, where the city’s walls enclosed a forgotten corner. No houses overlooked this place—only warehouses and granaries stood in the distance, their backs turned as if ashamed of the solitude they guarded. The ground sloped gently toward the water, thick with long grass and rushes, dampened by open gutters that emptied into the river. It was here, amid the silence and the sluggish flow, that he came each dawn, drawn not by comfort but by memory.

Once, this very stream had brought him bread—once, a raw carrot, an apple peel—gifts from unseen hands. Such small things mattered when hunger gnawed and shame clung like frost. He would wait here, unseen, hoping for one more chance, one more scrap, anything that might spare him the silent begging he loathed. For Baldassarre hated begging—not merely the act, but the surrender it implied. He would have chosen death over it, had not one thought kept him alive: a hope so fragile it was no longer hope at all, but a persistent shadow clinging to his mind like breath in a dream.

After leaving the convent hospital of San Miniato, where monks had tended him during his helplessness, he had waited for her—the Wife. But she never came. The pestilence had swept through Florence, and he could only imagine her lost among the dead. With her gone, vengeance had seemed impossible. His knife was gone, his strength broken, his will scattered. Yet he lived. Not because he wished to, but because something within him refused to let go—something dim and shapeless, like a distant sound heard just before waking. He waited for it, though he did not know what it was.

Meanwhile, the Compagnacci advanced. Divided into two forces, they moved swiftly—one toward Ponte Rubaconte, the other toward Ponte Vecchio—both converging on the Via de’ Bardi. A single word passed between them, a signal whispered through the ranks: *Sack the house.* They knew their target, though few understood why. The leaders alone carried the full weight of the command: they were to meet at a certain dwelling near the eastern end of the street, where the master—Tito—was expected to be asleep.

But Tito was not sleeping. The events of the previous day had sharpened his desire to flee Florence. Investigations would follow, questions asked, delays imposed. And delay meant danger. Though he no longer wore armor or feared Baldassarre, a deeper fear had taken root—fear not of violence, but of exposure, of being caught in the web of lies he had spun. He had cast off his old life, severed ties, and now prepared to vanish. Maso would hand over the house; horses and mules awaited in San Gallo; Tessa and the children were already lodged beyond the gate, ready to join him.

He descended the stone steps into the courtyard, stepped through the great doorway—no longer the same man, yet still bearing the brilliance of his former self. He turned toward the Piazza dei Mozzi, intending to cross the Ponte Rubaconte. But as he walked, sounds reached his ears—muffled shouts, trampling feet. Was the mob coming into Oltrarno? It was a nuisance. He preferred the quieter route. Now he must take the Ponte Vecchio.

He pulled his mantle close, quickening his pace. No one saw him in the fading light. But before he reached the end of the Via de’ Bardi, the noise returned—louder, closer. The mob was crossing the bridge. Panic seized him. He turned back instinctively, then stopped. There was no time to retreat. He pressed forward, toward the bridge, the roar growing louder behind him.

Then he saw them—the crowd pouring from the Ponte Vecchio, filling the street. He was trapped. Before he could react, he was surrounded—not by common rabble, but by armed men. His cap flew off. He was shoved violently forward, thrust into the narrow passage of the bridge.

“Piagnone! Medicean! Piagnone!” the voices roared. “Throw him over!”

His mantle was torn away, his scarsella snatched at. But it slipped from their grasp. Still, he was dragged, pushed, half-drowned in noise. He felt himself nearing the central arches—his last hope: to be thrown over before they killed him.

And then—he acted.

With a desperate lunge, he broke the clasp of his belt and flung both belt and scarsella toward the parapet, shouting, “There are diamonds! There is gold!”

The crowd hesitated. A rush followed. In that instant, he leapt onto the parapet and plunged into the dark river below.

It was his only chance—and it worked.

He rose to the surface, gasping, swimming with all the strength left in him. The current carried him downstream. If he could reach beyond Ponte alla Carrara, he might escape. The mob above would think him drowned. They did. Peering over the edge, they saw nothing but the black water. Only later, from the opposite bank, would someone glimpse a pale face, half-submerged, eyes wide, nostrils flaring, veins taut on the temples.

Onward he floated—past Santa Trinita, past the last bridge. Exhaustion threatened to claim him. He was not fresh from sleep. But terror drove him onward. He heard cries—perhaps real, perhaps imagined—chasing him down the river.

And then—eyes.

Old eyes, sharp despite age, fixed on the dark stream. Baldassarre sat among the rushes, staring blankly at the runlet that brought him nothing. Then he saw it—a white shape drifting downstream. Could it be fortune? He watched. It grew clearer. A man. A body.

He leaned forward, heart pounding. The body crashed onto the grass beside him. He sprang up like a beast, clutching the velvet tunic, falling onto the form, staring into the face.

Dead?

The eyes were still. But not quite. Baldassarre knelt, fingers gripping the neck of the tunic, knees pressing against the chest. He waited. Fiercely. Trembling. Justice had brought him here. This was the moment.

The eyes flickered. Opened. Wide.

“Ah, yes! You see me—you know me!”

Tito recognized him. But did he recognize the moment? Was this life—or death? Was this redemption—or damnation?

Baldassarre cared not. He pressed his knuckles hard against the throat, kneeling upon the chest, holding fast. Let death come. Let it come now. He would not release the grip until justice was witnessed.

He watched the face. Waited. When the eyes closed again, he dared not trust them. He would not let go. Not until someone found them. Then he would speak—declare that he had slain the traitor who once called him father. They might believe him now. And then he would die content, knowing justice had been done—even if it was only in this world.

So he knelt. So he held. Until the light grew strong and his body could no longer bear the strain. Then he sat, still gripping the tunic, still watching.

Hours passed. No one came. No one saw. Florence was elsewhere—caught in its own storm.

Not long after, Savonarola was being tortured, screaming, “I will confess!”

And still, no one came.

It was not until the sun began to sink that a wagon, drawn by a gentle grey ox, approached the riverbank. The driver bent to gather stones, and saw something strange in the grass. An old man, fallen forward, his hands locked around the garment of a younger man.

They could not separate them. So they loaded both into the wagon and drove toward the Piazza, where the Eight would be told.

As the wagon entered the streets, a crowd gathered. No one knew the bodies at first. The old man’s face was hidden, half-buried in the younger’s shoulder.

But soon, recognition came.

“I know that old man,” said Piero di Cosimo. “I painted his likeness once. He is the prisoner who clutched Melema on the steps of the Duomo.”

“And I,” said Bernardo Rucellai, one of the Eight, “remember him from my gardens. I thought he had died in prison. But now… we cannot know the truth.”

Who can point to justice and say, “This is it”? Justice is not a deed, nor a law, nor a verdict. It is a longing—deep, quiet, enduring. Like the Kingdom of God, it is not outside us, but within. And sometimes, in the silence of a riverbank, it comes—slow, inevitable, and final.
