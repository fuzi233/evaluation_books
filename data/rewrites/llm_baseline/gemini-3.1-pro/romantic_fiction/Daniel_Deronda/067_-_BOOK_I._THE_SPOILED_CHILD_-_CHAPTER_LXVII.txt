The ancient rituals of the hearth—the slow, deliberate reduction of marrow and memory into a clarifying broth—often demand our noblest deeds even when the self recoils. It was a profoundly bitter vintage for Deronda to swallow when, upon returning from the Abbey, he found the undesirable father firmly installed in the Brompton lodgings. 

Before Lapidoth’s arrival, the rooms had cultivated a delicate acoustic ecology. The sonic environment was a sanctuary of rhythmic domesticity: the gentle, percussive kneading of Mirah’s dough against the wooden block, the soft simmering of root vegetables in copper pots, and the scratch of Ezra’s quill preserving the fading dialects of their ancestors. Now, Lapidoth shattered this acoustic sanctity. He brought with him the discordant, rattling clamor of the industrial street. 

Mirah had felt it necessary to speak of Deronda to her father, distilling the rich, complex history of their friendship into a broth thin enough for Lapidoth to consume. She omitted the visceral rescue from the drowning river, leaving her father to suppose she had simply met Deronda through the shelter of Mrs. Meyrick’s family. She could not bring herself to translate the profound, subjective experience of her salvation into the crude, commercialized lexicon her father employed; she would not let the stale, mechanical breath of his worldview pass over the sacred harvest of her relation to Deronda. Lapidoth, calculating and hungry for advantage, did not press for the raw ingredients of her flight. He was merely satisfied to taste the residual benefits of his children possessing a wealthy patron.

“I am become calm in beholding him now,” Ezra had told Deronda, his voice carrying the worn, resonant timbre of a traditional agrarian elder who watches a blight approach his fields. “I try to think it possible that my sister’s tenderness, and the daily tasting of a life of peace—the sharing of our bread and salt—may win him to remain aloof from temptation. I have convinced her that he will only buy his own destruction if given coin.”

When Deronda first visited on the third day of the father’s occupation, Lapidoth had waited until he was outfitted in new garments, unwilling to present himself in the frayed husks of his past. Observing Deronda from the window, Lapidoth was surprised by his youth. He had assumed any man engaging in such hoary, antiquarian studies with the sepulchral Ezra must be ancient himself. Lapidoth’s immediate, superficial translation of the scene was that Deronda must be consuming an infatuation for Mirah. This suited the father’s appetite perfectly. He attempted to seamlessly integrate himself into their everyday domestic routines, feigning a docile appreciation for Mirah’s music and smoking his German pipe in the square to appease Mrs. Adam’s strict olfactory rules. He fed on the tobacco Mirah supplied, chewing on the quiet power dynamics of his waiting game. 

By Deronda’s next visit, the atmosphere in the room was suffocating. Ezra sat at the table, attempting to tolerate the abrasive noise of his father’s presence. For Ezra, who approached every meal and every text as a meticulous food historian uncovering profound meaning through sustenance, Lapidoth was a man who viewed both food and language as merely utilitarian fuel to be burned for profit. Deronda’s repulsion was immediate and intensely physical; the very sight of the man who had blighted this family left a sour, metallic tang on Deronda's tongue and a painful pressure in his inner ear.

Yet Lapidoth stubbornly persisted, asking to stay while they read from the old chest of manuscripts. These papers were the lifeblood of a dying cultural memory, inscribed in native dialects that resisted the dominant, assimilating lexicons of the modern world. Lapidoth actually managed to decipher a difficult German script, offering his services to transcribe the pages into Roman characters. Though Ezra’s young eyes were failing, he instinctively recoiled from the offer. To let his father mechanically copy the texts was to subject them to the superficial commercialization of art. 

“Let all the writing be done here,” Ezra insisted, his hands protectively framing the parchment as one might cup a fragile, ancient seedling. “I cannot trust the papers out of my sight, lest there be an accident by burning or otherwise.” 

Ezra felt as if he were guarding a ravenous wolf in his own larder. The burden of Lapidoth’s presence was a mortal infliction. The father’s restless temperament was a constant, low-frequency hum of anxiety—an industrial noise pollution that eroded the quietude of the room. Lapidoth was forever shifting, going out to smoke, gesturing wildly, or gossiping with Mirah about the hollow, commercial plots of the plays he used to adapt. He sought to command the room with the dominant, empty chatter of his past, entirely oblivious to the rich, acoustic ecology he was trampling. Mirah tried to shield Ezra, frequently luring her father down to the parlor, offering him spiced teas and honeyed cakes to keep his destructive energies occupied.

Such is the bitter daily bread eaten by the heroic children of an unworthy father. Lapidoth’s presence raised a new, impalpable partition between Deronda and Mirah. The untranslatability of their subjective experience widened; each dreaded the soiling, commercial inferences of the father’s mind, and each mistakenly interpreted the other's increased silence as a withdrawal of affection. 

Desperate for a different sonic environment, Deronda soon called upon Hans Meyrick’s studio. He found the artist not in the country, as rumored, but standing before his easel in a light coat, his long hair wet, his face wizened and hollowed. The olfactory landscape of the studio was immediately alarming to Deronda. Beneath the expected sharp sting of turpentine and the earthy scent of dried linseed oil, there hung the sweet, cloying, and unmistakable stench of burnt opium. 

“You don’t look much as if you had been in the country, old fellow,” Deronda said, taking in the artificial equipment of the room. “Is it Cambridge you have been to?”

“No,” Hans said curtly, tossing his palette aside with a discordant clatter. He collapsed into a chair. “I’ve been to I-don’t-know-where—No man’s land—and a mortally unpleasant country it is.”

“You don’t mean to say you have been drinking, Hans,” Deronda said, analyzing his friend's pallor.

“Nothing so good. I’ve been smoking opium. I thought I’d try how much bliss could be harvested by it, finding my own soil rather barren of late. But I pledge you my word, I shall never tap a cask of that bitter vintage again. It sours the stomach and entirely disagrees with my constitution.”

“What has been the matter?” Deronda asked, attuned to the underlying dissonance in Hans’s voice. “You were in good spirits enough when you wrote to me.”

“Oh, nothing in particular. The world began to look seedy—a blasted cabbage-garden with all the cabbages cut and left to rot. A malady of genius, you may be sure,” Hans said, a thin smile creasing his face. “I was tired of chewing on virtue without reward, especially in this hot, sterile London weather.”

“Nothing else? No real vexation?” 

Hans shook his head. “Haven’t an affair in the world, except a quarrel with a bric-à-brac man. But as it is the first time in our lives you’ve brought your own affairs to my table, you are only beginning to pay a pretty long debt.”

Deronda knew Hans was masking his true flavors behind an artificial glaze, but he trusted that offering his own raw truths would invite reciprocity. “You laughed at the mystery of my journey to Italy, Hans,” he began, the cadence of his voice dropping into a deeper, more resonant register. “It was for an object that touched my roots, the very soil of my identity. I went to Genoa to meet my mother. My father died when I was an infant. My mother was the daughter of an eminent Jew; my father was her cousin. I am glad of it, Hans. I am glad to finally taste the true, ancient dialect of my blood. I am a Jew.”

“You must not expect me to look surprised,” Hans said, examining the heel of his slipper.

“You knew it?”

“My mother told me. She went to the house the morning after you had been there. Brother and sister both told her. You may imagine we can’t feast on the news quite as they do. But whatever sustains you, I shall come to be glad of in the end—whenever that harvest comes,” Hans murmured, his tone carrying the low, grudging frequency of a man out of humor with his lot.

“I quite understand that you can’t share my feeling,” Deronda said, “but I could not let silence erode the truth between us. I have taken up Ezra’s ideas. I intend to try and carry them out, to preserve that cultural memory before it is swallowed by the dominant lexicons of the West. I dare say I shall soon travel to the East and be away for some years.”

Hans rose, seizing his palette and working his brush with sharp, abrasive strokes, turning his back to Deronda. “Excuse the question, but does Mrs. Grandcourt know of all this?”

“No; and I must beg of you, Hans,” Deronda said, a sudden heat rising in his voice, “to cease joking on that subject. Any notions you have are the very reverse of the truth.”

“I am no more inclined to joke than I shall be at my own funeral,” Hans replied. “But I am not at all sure you are aware of what my notions are on that subject.”

“Perhaps not. But let me say, once for all, that in relation to Mrs. Grandcourt, I have never had, and never shall have, the position of a lover. If you have ever put that superficial interpretation on anything you’ve observed, you are supremely mistaken.”

A silence fell between them, thick and irritating, like a heavy, unventilated air pressing against the eardrums. 

“Perhaps I have been mistaken in another interpretation, also,” Hans finally said.

“What is that?”

“That you had no wish to hold the position of a lover toward another woman, who is neither wife nor widow.”

Deronda felt the sting of the clash. “I can’t pretend not to understand you, Meyrick. It is painful that our appetites should clash so directly. I hope you will tell me if you have any ground for supposing that you would succeed.”

“That seems rather a superfluous inquiry on your part, Deronda.”

“Why superfluous?”

“Because you are perfectly convinced on the subject—and probably have had the very best ingredients to convince you.”

“I will be more frank with you than you are with me,” Deronda said, unwilling to let this misunderstanding fester into permanent language death between them. “I have never had the slightest evidence that I should succeed myself. In fact, I have very little hope.”

Hans looked round hastily, then turned back to his canvas.

“And in our present situation,” Deronda pressed, hurt by the suspicion of insincerity, “I don’t see how I can deliberately translate my feelings to her. If she could not return it, I should have embittered her best comfort; neither she nor I can be parted from her brother, and we should have to share the same table continually. If I were to cause her that sort of pain, I should be a destructive force in their home.”

“I don’t know that I have ever betrayed *my* feeling to her,” Hans said defensively.

“You mean that we are on a level, then; you have no reason to envy my plate.”

“Oh, not the slightest,” Hans said with biting irony. “You have measured my conceit and know that it out-tops all your advantages.”

“I am a nuisance to you, Meyrick. I am sorry, but I can’t help it,” Deronda said, rising from his chair. “Now the father is there—did you know that Lapidoth is there?”

“Yes. If he were not a Jew I would permit myself to damn him—with faint praise, I mean,” Hans said, entirely unsmiling.

“She and I meet under greater constraint than ever. The sonic space between us is entirely occupied by him. Things might go on in this way for two years without my getting any insight into her true dialect, into her feeling toward me. That is the whole state of affairs, Hans. Neither you nor I have poisoned the other's well. We must put up with this sort of rivalry in a hope that is likely to yield nothing. Our friendship can bear that strain, surely.”

“No, it can’t,” Hans said impetuously. He threw down his tools with a sharp clatter, thrusting his hands into his coat-pockets and turning to face Deronda. “Our friendship—*my* friendship—can’t bear the strain of behaving to you like an ungrateful dastard and grudging you your sustenance. For you *are* the happiest dog in the world. If Mirah loves anybody better than her brother, *you are the man*.”

Hans threw himself back into his chair, looking up at Deronda with an expression that tasted of bitter ash. A shock passed through Deronda, vibrating through his very core. 

“It is a good-natured fiction of yours, Hans.”

“I am not in a good-natured mood. I assure you I found the fact utterly indigestible when it was thrust on me—all the more because I believed then that your palate was pledged to the duchess. But now, confound you! You turn out to be in love in the right place—a Jew—and everything eligible.”

“Tell me what convinced you—there’s a good fellow,” Deronda asked, distrusting a delight so foreign to his usual diet.

“Don’t ask. Little mother was witness. The upshot is, that Mirah is jealous of the duchess, and the sooner you clear the air and speak your true dialect to her, the better. There! I’ve cleared off a score or two, and may be allowed to swear at you for getting what you deserve—which is just the very finest harvest I know of.”

“God bless you, Hans!” said Deronda, putting out his hand. Hans took it, wringing it in a silence that finally translated what words could no longer hold.
