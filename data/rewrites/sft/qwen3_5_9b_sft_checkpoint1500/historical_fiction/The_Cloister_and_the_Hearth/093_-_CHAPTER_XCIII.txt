For days, Clement lay in his cavern of despair, a prayerless, tuneless, hopeless wreck, until a singular thought pierced the fog of his misery. *What I have given freely to the poor and the weary traveler,* he reasoned, *why should I not give it to myself?* He resolved to fill his den of ruin with the Name of God, with the sacred words of Scripture, and with the comforting piety of the Church.

Like the pilgrim Christian standing before the gates of Hell, he thrust out his hand—not for a sword, which he lacked—but for the humble tool of peaceful labor, his chisel. With a vigor born of necessity, he worked as if the salvation of his soul hung by a thread from his arm.

History tells of Michelangelo, who, unlike timid sculptors who model in clay and delegate the carving to mechanics, would seize the block himself. Conceiving the image instantly, he would strike with hammer and steel, sending marble chips flying wildly, causing the rough stone to sprout into form. Thus did Clement proceed. He drew no guiding lines; instead, he mined his memory for gracious words and dashed them into the soft stone with frantic energy, chipping away at his work between the labors of living and the battles of faith.

He begged visitors for candle stubs and rancid oil. "Anything will serve," he declared, "so long as it burns." Consequently, at night, his cave glowed like a blacksmith's forge, visible through the window and the cracks of his crude stone door. The local villagers, beholding the eerie light, crossed themselves, suspecting devilries. Yet, within, the holy talismans—verses and prayers—appeared one by one upon the walls, while sparks and stone chips flew ceaselessly, day and night. This was the soldier of Solitude and the Church, wielding his bloodless weapon, sighing and groaning, as he carved between the grind of labor and the fight for grace.

Beneath the great crucifix fixed to the wall, he inscribed a passage from Augustine: *"O Christian soul, behold the wounds of the suffering One, the blood of the dying One, the price of our redemption! Consider how immense these things are, and weigh them in the balance of your mind, so that He who was nailed entirely to the Cross for you may become entirely nailed to your heart. For if you recall the Passion of Christ, there is nothing on earth too hard to bear with composure."*

Soothed, albeit slightly, by these sweet and pious words surrounding him, and crushed by the endless watch and toil, Clement sank prostrate one morning, overcome by profound fatigue. A deep sleep seized him for many hours. When he awoke quietly, a tiny cheeping sound caught his ear. Opening his eyes, he saw a robin redbreast perched upon his breviary, resting on a low stool near his feet. The bird ruffled its feathers with a single pull, smoothed them instantly, and cocked its bill with a display of cunning that was purely imaginary.

Clement held his breath. He half-closed his eyes, terrified lest he frighten this airy guest. Down the bird hopped onto the floor. There, it enacted a pantomime of astuteness; then, with three stiff little hops, like a ball of worsted on vertical wires, it landed squarely on the hermit's bare foot. Swelling and contracting with the ebb and flow of its plumage, the robin remained there. Clement closed his eyes completely, scarcely drawing breath, fearing to lose his visitor. Content merely to feel the minute claw upon his foot—he could barely feel it, relying on knowledge rather than sensation—the game continued.

Soon, the fluttering wings signaled the bird's return to the breviary. Determined not to frighten this fidget away, Clement attempted to feed it. He held a piece of bread within reach, but moving his hand proved impossible. After five minutes of creeping motionless toward the loaf, he managed to slip a crumb between his finger and thumb. Like children shooting marbles, he flicked the crumb forward, keeping his hand utterly still.

"Cock-robin" observed the falling morsel, displayed sagacity, and did not move. When another crumb followed, then another, the bird finally popped down, snatched one, and fled to a higher eminence—the hermit's knee—for greater security. The game persisted until a unusually large fragment rolled along. Cock-robin pounced, lifted the prize aloft, and fled swiftly into the world, the cave resonating with the buffeting air.

"Now, bless thee, sweet bird," sighed the solitary sufferer. "Thy wings are music, and thou, a feathered ray, hast come to lighten my darkened soul."

From that moment, his devotions gained a spark of courage, and this became his daily work:

*"Come, Creator Spirit, visit the minds of men, fill with heavenly grace those hearts Thou hast created. Kindle the light in our senses, visit the minds of Thy people, strengthen the weakness of our bodies with perpetual virtue."*

Thus, the days rolled on. The weather grew colder, yet Clement's heart grew warmer, and despondency, having arrived like a cloud, departed like one. Soon, everything reversed. His cell seemed bathed in joy. His work brought pleasure; his prayers flowed with unction; his psalms became hymns of praise. Flocks of small birds followed their crimson leader, fleeing the snow, making friendship with the "parish full of Cains"—transforming enemies into Abels.

On one keen, frosty night, as he sang praises to God upon his tuneful psaltery, causing his hollow cave to ring with holy psalmody—as if the cave itself were Tubal's surrounding shell or David's harp—he heard a clear, melodic whine. It grew louder, though slightly untuned. Peering through the cracks of his rude door, he saw a great red wolf moaning melodiously, its nose held high in the air.

Clement was overjoyed. "My sins depart!" he cried. "The creatures of God acknowledge me, one after another!" In a burst of enthusiasm, he struck up the Laud: "Praise Him, all ye creatures of His! Let everything that hath breath praise the Lord!" All the while, the wolf bayed at intervals.

Above all, he felt drawn nearer to that celestial intercourse, the sign and bliss of the true hermit. His dreams of saints and angels became so vivid they resembled actual visions. He saw bright figures clad in woven snow, bending upon him eyes lovelier than those of the antelope he had seen in Rome, fanning him with broad wings hued like rainbows, their gentle voices bidding him speed on his course.

However, not long after enjoying this felicity, his dreams took on a strange and ominous complexion. He wandered with Fra Colonna across the relics of ancient nations. The friar was lame, leaning on a staff which he waved over mighty ruins. Wherever the staff pointed—whether Egyptian, Greek, or Roman—the temples and palaces, previously mere wrecks, rose again like an exhalation, thronged with the famous dead. Songsters capable of eclipsing Apollo poured forth their lays; women, godlike in form and draped like Minerva, danced voluptuously yet gracefully around marble courts. Sculptors carved amidst admiring pupils, and forms of supernatural beauty emerged from Parian marble in moments; grave philosophers discoursed on high and subtle matters while youths listened reverently. It was a realm of eternity, long past.

Yet, beneath this wonderful panorama, a lurking suspicion gnawed at the dreamer. "This is a prologue, a flourish," he thought. "There is something behind; something that means me no good, something mysterious, awful."

One night, when the wizard Colonna had transcended himself, he pointed with his staff. Many great ancient cities were swallowed up, and the pair stood on a vast sandy plain under a huge crimson sun sinking to rest. Great palm trees and bulrush hives, scarce a man's height, dotted the horizon.

"These are the anchorites of the Theban desert," Colonna said calmly. "Followers not of Christ and His apostles, nor of the great Fathers, but of the Greek pupils of the Egyptian pupils of the Brachmans and Gymnosophists."

Clement felt a burning desire to embrace these holy men, to tell them his troubles, and seek their counsel. But he was tied by invisible fetters, unable to move. The crimson sun sank, dusk fell, and the hives became scarce visible. Colonna's figure grew shadowy and shapeless, yet his eyes glowed ten times brighter.
