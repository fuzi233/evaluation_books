It was the first time Baldassarre had set foot again in the Piazza del Duomo since the day of his escape—since the day he had stood there half naked, his hair a matted offense, a rope biting his throat like a leash. The very stones remembered. The air itself seemed to retain the crowd’s old hiss and ripple—those quick little laughs, the sharp intake when someone points, the cruel, light music of strangers enjoying a spectacle that cost them nothing.

He had wanted to hear the monk preach again. He had hungered for that voice as a man hungers for bread when his mouth is dry and empty—hungered for the cadence that gathered up a city’s scattered minds and made them tremble together. Yet he had shrunk from returning, because the words that had struck him there were not only a wound but a verdict.

“Some madman, surely.”

Tito’s voice—polished, easy, the kind of voice that could glide over any truth and make it seem an ornament—had dropped those words as if they were nothing. But they had clung like burrs. Not because they were base—though they were; not because they were cruel—though they were; but because in the instant they were spoken Baldassarre had felt a colder thing than anger: the fear that he might not be able to prove them false.

He was not mad. He carried within him the piteous stamp of sanity: the clear knowledge that parts of his mind were broken, that the old chambers of memory had collapsed and left him wandering among rubble. He could measure his own feebleness. He could feel the gaps, like missing teeth that the tongue cannot help but seek.

And with the first hot surge of vengeance there had wakened, too, a wary caution—the caution of a wild creature that knows it is fierce but not whole; the caution of an insect whose foothold has crumbled, leaving it clinging and still. That distrust—of himself, of his tongue, of the treacherous slipping away of thought—had made him turn from Piero di Cosimo’s friendly hand. It had made him cautious in the hospital, answering the brethren in phrases that could not betray him: he had been made prisoner by the French on his way from Genoa. Nothing more.

Yet even there, among the smells of boiled herbs, stale straw, and the sour breath of many bodies lying too close, the monks had looked at him and seen what he could not entirely hide—age, yes, and misery, but also the remnant of another station. They gave him more than the ordinary alms: a coarse woollen tunic that scratched his skin like nettles but kept the damp from his bones; peasant shoes that creaked with fresh stitching; and a few danari, the smallest Florentine coins, light as contempt in the palm and yet heavy with possibility.

He had set out on the road to Arezzo at dawn. The countryside was washed in a thin November light; vines stood stripped and black against pale sky, and the wind moved through bare branches with a dry, whispering hush. But after the first little town he stopped, and with two danari bought the one thing he needed more than food: he bought a face he might dare to show.

The barber’s shop smelled of lather and iron—warm water, stale pomade, the metallic tang of scissors and razors wiped on cloth. A pot of soap simmered near the brazier, sending up steam that carried a fatty sweetness. The barber had a little hand-mirror of bright steel. Baldassarre had not looked at himself in years, not truly. Now, when his eyes caught that small shining circle, a thought shot through him so sharply it made his breath catch.

Was he so changed that Tito had not known him?

The thought stopped his inner currents as a stone stops water. He trembled, pushing away the barber’s arm with a hand that shook like a leaf.

“Give me the mirror,” he said. He wanted to see himself before the razor scraped away what time had grown.

The barber, noticing his tremulousness, held it up. In the dull steel, Baldassarre saw his own face as if it were an image dredged from silt.

No. He was not so changed as that. The wrinkles were the same ones he had known three years ago—only deeper now, ploughed and hardened. The skin on his brow rose in little superficial bosses like cipher-marks—meaningless signs of long use. It was yellower, more like rind than flesh. The shaggy white beard—no disguise to eyes that had looked closely at him for sixteen years; no disguise to eyes that, if they had searched for him at all, should have expected him to be altered, as men search for the beloved among drowned bodies cast up by the sea.

Something in his glance was different, yes; but it was a difference that should have made recognition more—not less—terrible, as a familiar voice heard in a cry.

The doubt was folly. He had felt it in the Piazza: Tito had known.

He pushed the mirror away. The momentary arrest broke. Hatred rushed on again like a river released.

He turned back toward Florence, but he would not enter until dusk. He left the highroad and sat by a little pool that lay like a dark coin sunk in earth, shadowed on one side by alder-bushes still freckled with yellow leaves. The air was calm; the pool’s surface held the sky without a tremor. And because it was still, it tempted him.

A mirror again.

He bent over it and stared, slow now, without the barber’s eyes watching. Was there something wandering in his face—something imbecile, like what he felt in his mind?

Not in this moment. Now his look was eager, intent, full of purpose. But at other times—yes. In the long hours when he felt the vague ache of an unremembered past—when he seemed to sit in a dark loneliness, visited by whispers that died the instant he strained his ear, and by forms that approached and floated away as he thrust out his hand—then, doubtless, there was continual frustration in his gaze. And worse still, when the thick cloud parted for an instant and hope sprang up—only for the cloud to roll together again and leave him helpless—then there must be a blank confusion on his face, like sudden blindness.

Could he prove anything? Could he even begin to allege anything and trust that the links of thought would not snap and fall? Would any man believe that he had once had a mind full of rare knowledge, busy with close thoughts, ready with various speech? It had slipped away from him, that store he had gathered laboriously year by year, like grain laid up against famine—spilled and scattered as if some careless hand had overturned the bin.

Was it gone forever, like water poured from an urn into the wide ocean? Or was it still within him, dammed up by some obstruction that might break?

He clung to that hope, because without it the future narrowed to a single coarse action. And his mind, since he had first risen from his straw and walked feebly into sunlight only to discover a new darkness inside himself, had indeed changed in uneven ways—partly gradual, partly sudden. His body had strengthened; his will had hardened; the memories most entwined with feeling had returned—his tenderness, his joy, his pain—and alongside them had grown the steady, burning sense of lost knowledge.

Once or twice, when excitement had seized him, there had come a flash—an instant when he seemed wholly himself again, as old men doze and wake for a heartbeat into the youth they thought gone: he had seemed to see Greek pages and understand them, to feel his mind move unbenumbed among familiar ideas. The darkness had returned at once, more horrible for the light that had mocked him.

But what if it came again—and stayed long enough?

Long enough to achieve revenge. Not the blunt, brief revenge of a quick death. Something slower, under the same sky that had watched him forsaken; on the same earth where he had fainted in despair. Something that would make Tito’s fair, smiling hardness cry out and moan, not once, but again and again.

He straightened from the pool, folded his arms, and tried to force his mind into planning. Knowledge and opportunity: he must wait for both. And while he waited he must live without begging. For what he dreaded most now—more than hunger, more than cold—was to have any man think him a foolish, helpless old creature. No one must suspect that half his memory was gone. The lost strength might return; and if it returned only for a little while, that might be enough.

He knew how to begin.

Bratti Ferravecchi.

He had repeated the words so often since he first heard them that they were like a refrain beating in his head, hard to dislodge. In Genoa, a man with Tito’s ring on his finger had told him the ring was bought in Florence, from a young Greek—well dressed, handsome dark face—in the shop of a rigattiere named Bratti Ferravecchi, in the street also called Ferravecchi.

That information had shaken him with a violence almost like joy. Until then, his fervent nature had clung to faith in Tito; he had not believed himself wilfully forsaken. At first he had said, My parchment has never reached him. That is why I still toil at Antioch. But he is searching. He knows where I was lost. He will trace me out and find me.

Then, when he was taken to Corinth, he had persuaded his owners—by the assurance that he would be sought out and ransomed—to make sure inquiries at Antioch would not fail. And in Corinth he had thought, Here at last he must find me. Here he cannot miss me, whichever way he comes.

But before another year had passed, illness had come—the fever that left him shattered in body and mind, worse than worthless to his owners except for a ransom that did not arrive. He remembered himself then, sitting helpless in morning light, head between his hands, eyes blank, lips moving uncertainly. He had looked so like a hopeless imbecile that his owners were content to rid themselves of him. A Genoese merchant, compassionate to him as an Italian, took him aboard his galley.

In long months among islands and along the seaboard, he had regained strength of limb, but on landing at Genoa he had felt so weary and desolate that he had almost wished the illness at Corinth had killed him. Only one possibility held him back from that wish: perhaps Tito was not dead, but imprisoned or destitute; perhaps Tito still lived, and if he lived there was still hope—faint, long deferred—that Baldassarre might find his child again, clasp hands, meet eyes that remembered him as he had been before his mind broke.

Then he had chanced upon the stranger with Tito’s onyx ring. Though Baldassarre could never have described the ring beforehand, the sight of it stirred dormant fibres; he knew it. The fact that Tito, nearly a year after his father’s loss, had been living in prosperity in Florence and had sold a gem he ought not to have sold but in extremity—Baldassarre shrank from accounting for it. Better to be stunned than to think. He tried to feel only joy: I shall see him again. Perhaps he thought I was dead. The mystery will be explained. At least I shall meet eyes that remember me. I am not alone.

And now he told himself again: I am not alone in the world; I shall never be alone, for my revenge is with me.

It was as the instrument of that revenge—as something external, subservient—that he bent down once more to examine himself with hard curiosity. He told himself he did not care for this withered old man, unloved, forsaken—his soul like a deserted home where the ashes were cold and the walls bare except for marks of what had been. Yet passion, even when it is low, has a point where it ceases to be properly egoistic; it becomes like a fire kindled inside us, and everything else—body, memory, pride—is mere fuel.

He stared at the pale, black-browed image in the water until it seemed a separate creature listening to his thought.

“I was a loving fool,” his mind said to it. “I worshipped a woman once and believed she could care for me. Then I took a helpless child and fostered him. I watched him as he grew to see if he would care for me—care for me over and above the good I gave. I would have torn open my breast to warm him with my life-blood if I could only have seen him care a little for the pain of my wound. I laboured, I strained to crush out of this hard life one drop of unselfish love.

“Fool! Men love their own delights; there is no delight to be had in me. And yet I watched until I believed I saw what I watched for. When he was a child he lifted soft eyes to me and held my hand willingly. I thought, This boy will surely love me a little. Because I give my life to him and strive that he shall know no sorrow, he will care a little when I am thirsty—the drop he lays on my parched lips will be a joy to him.

“Curses on him! I wish I may see him lie with those red lips white and dry as ashes; and when he looks for pity, I wish he may see my face rejoicing in his pain. It is all a lie—this world is a lie—there is no goodness but in hate. Not one drop of love came with all my striving. Life has not given me one drop. But there are deep draughts in this world for hatred and revenge. I have memory left for that. There is strength in my arm—there is strength in my will—and if I can do nothing but kill him—”

But his mind recoiled from the thought of that swift punishment. His whole soul had been thrilled into a fierce belief in an eternity of vengeance: he, an undying hate, clutching forever an undying traitor, hearing that fair, smiling hardness cry out through endless anguish. Yet he wanted, first, a slow revenge here, under this mortal sky.

And as soon as he tried again to fix on means, the sense of his weakness pressed on him like frost in the joints. This despised body—the instrument of a sublime vengeance—must be nourished and clothed. If he must wait, he must work. But what work? He had no craft, no skill. He wondered whether the sight of written characters might kindle his lost power, whether he might dare to seek work as a copyist, win credence for his past scholarship. But he dared trust neither hand nor brain.

He must take the labor of a beast of burden. Florence was mercantile; it needed porters. He could carry weights. Thanks to the strange justice that struggled in this confused world on behalf of vengeance, his limbs had recovered something of their old sturdiness. He had nothing else men would pay for.

Then an urgency in his thought brought a suggestion, like the smell of food suddenly remembered when one is starving.

Something hung by a cord around his bare neck: something so paltry that the piety of Turks and Frenchmen had spared it—a tiny parchment bag blackened with age. It had hung there when he was a boy, a charm his mother had put upon him. He had kept it not because he believed in its magic, but because it was a relic of her love. Such brevi were not meant to be opened; piety demanded they remain sealed.

At any earlier moment of his life he would have said no hunger could tempt him to tear it open for the chance of an amulet worth coin. But now a thirst had come like the thirst that makes men open their own veins. The possibility flashed—what if, inside, there was not parchment but a stone?

His fingers snatched at it. He felt in an instant the long years he had worn it, and the far-off sunny balcony at Naples where he had leaned against his mother’s knee and looked toward blue water. For a heartbeat the past was so near that he could almost hear it—the low sea-sound, the soft Neapolitan lilt that had once named him. And then that sound was swallowed by the harsher Florentine air, by the city’s dominant tongue—sharp-edged, public, careless with private lives. The old words were unreachable, untranslatable now, even to himself; his own history had become a dialect he could no longer speak.

It brought no hesitation. All piety had been melted and poured into one shape: revenge.

He bit and tore at the doubles of parchment until they split. And then—the sight made him pant—there was an amulet.

Very small. But as blue as those remembered waters: an engraved sapphire. Worth gold ducats.

The moment he saw the ducats in his mind, he saw some of them turned into a poniard. Not to use at once—no, not yet—but to possess. To feel a handle in his fist, to try an edge with his thumb—so that when memory failed and thought broke, he would not feel so helpless. Steel did not ask for talents; steel did not slip away like recollection. It could be the unfailing friend of feeble justice.

A hard, sparkling triumph lit under his black eyebrows as he wrapped the little sapphire again in parchment and wound the string tight.

Dusk was falling. He rose and walked toward Florence. With his danari he bought bread—coarse, dark, smelling of sour grain and the heat of the oven that had baked it. He ate slowly, feeling the crust abrade his gums, feeling the simple act of chewing anchor him in his body. All around, the world had its evening noises: distant carts, a dog’s bark answered by another, the soft clop of hooves on stone—sounds that settled on the mind like weather. He listened as if listening could keep him whole.

He could sleep in the open air if he must; plenty did, tucked into corners where the city’s stone held a little warmth. In the next few days he sold the sapphire, bought more clothing, bought a bright dagger, and still had two gold florins left. He hoarded them like seeds in winter. His lodging was an outhouse with a heap of straw in it, in a thinly inhabited part of Oltrarno. The straw smelled of old harvest—dry, dusty sweetness mixed with the musk of damp—and it pricked through his tunic when he lay down. He meant to look for work as a porter.

He had bought his dagger at Bratti’s.

When he paid his meditated visit one evening at dusk, he found the singular rag-merchant just returned from his rounds, emptying his basketful of broken glass and old iron among a handsome display of second-hand goods—smart pieces of apparel, musical instruments, weapons—lit broad in the window as if poverty itself could be arranged to look inviting.

As Baldassarre entered and looked toward the window, his eye fastened at once on a dagger hanging high against a red scarf. The scarf made a field of color behind the steel, like meat behind a blade. By buying the dagger he could satisfy his longing and open his errand indirectly, without naming the onyx ring too soon.

He bargained with a caution that masked urgency. The shop smelled of old leather, dust, tarnish, and the faint sweetness of cloth that has been folded too long. Bratti’s voice was nasal and lively; his words came in flourishes, as if he were selling not objects but the story of himself.

In the course of haggling, Baldassarre let fall, with careful carelessness, that he came from Genoa and had been directed to Bratti’s by an acquaintance who had bought a very valuable ring there. Had the respectable trader any more such rings? Was that sort of treasure often within reach?

Bratti launched at once into a boastful stream: rings like that were not for common hands. But Bratti Ferravecchi had rare connections—owing to his known wisdom and honesty. Yes, yes, it might be true that he was a pedlar; he chose to be a pedlar. Rich enough, he was, to kick his heels in his shop all day if he pleased. But those who thought they had said all there was to say about Bratti when they called him a pedlar were further from truth than the other side of Pisa.

How had such a ring come his way? Because he—Bratti—had particular knowledge of a handsome young signor who had not looked quite so fine a feathered bird when Bratti first laid eyes on him as he did now. That young man had been found sleeping under the Loggia de’ Cerchi, and see what had come of it! Bratti’s discerning eye, Bratti’s connections, Bratti’s—Bratti’s everything.

By a question or two, repeated as if from deafness—for Baldassarre seemed rather deaf, asking Bratti to say many things twice—he drew out, without trouble, as rough and rambling an account of Tito’s life as the pedlar could supply: the early sale of jewels; the immediate, quiet settlement in Florence; the marriage; the great prosperity.

Bratti never suspected the decent customer cared about Tito. The curiosity, of course, must be about Bratti—about the remarkable rigattiere who stood at the center of so many fortunate turns.

Baldassarre left the shop with the dagger at his side and a general knowledge of Tito’s conduct and position. But the question that burned most—What story had he told about his previous life, about his father?—that would be harder to answer.

Meanwhile, Baldassarre wanted to learn all he could about Florence. Yet to his acute distress, the new details he gathered would not stay. They slid away as if his mind were a sieve. He could retain only a few, and only by constant repetition. He began to fear listening to new discourse, lest it wash away what he had already forced himself to remember. The city was full of talk—markets bursting with cries, church steps buzzing with gossip, workshops clanging and singing with labor—but all that sound could become torment: too much noise was not music, only erosion, wearing down what little structure remained inside him.

On the day Tito had seen him in the Piazza del Duomo, this anguish was fresh; and Tito’s ready speech had fallen on him like the mockery of a glib, defying demon.

That evening, as he went back to his straw and passed the booksellers’ shops in the Via del Garbo, he paused. Volumes lay open, their pages spread like cut loaves showing crumb—inviting, promising nourishment. Could he, by long gazing, seize again the slippery threads of memory? Could he plant his fingers somewhere firm and pull himself up from these waters that flowed over him?

Temptation won. He bought the cheapest Greek book he saw.

He carried it home and sat on his heap of straw, holding the book where the light from the small window fell. The paper had its own smell—dry, slightly sour, like old linen; the ink lay black and matte, strokes that had once been living speech caught and pinned. He stared at the characters, waiting for the inward light—the familiar quickening, the sense of meaning rising like yeast.

Nothing rose.

Soon evening thickened. The window’s light faded, the lines blurred, and still he stared. Darkness made little difference. His strained eyes seemed still to see the white pages with unintelligible black marks upon them, as if the very language he had lived in had died inside him, leaving only shapes—silent, dominant, refusing him entrance.
