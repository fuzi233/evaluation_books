I knelt beside a strange little shrub, its leaves trembling as if alive. A single fruit clung to its branches—small, unfamiliar, and tempting. Hunger gnawed at me, but caution held my hand. I did not yet know that unseen eyes watched from the rocks, waiting to see if I would dare to taste it.

Moving deeper into the channel, the shrubs grew taller, thickening into dwarf trees. Their gnarled roots twisted across my path, forcing me to leap and scramble. A sudden rustle—like the beating of countless wings—made me freeze, but nothing emerged. Ahead, the land dipped into a hollow, the ghost of an ancient lakebed. Trees crowded its basin, heavy with coarse, unappetizing fruit.

Exhausted, I collapsed onto a patch of soft moss. Nearby, a tiny tree bore rosy apples no larger than cherries. I plucked one and bit into it—sweetness burst across my tongue. I reached for another.

Then came the laughter.

High, clear voices rang out like chimes. *"He likes our apples! He likes our apples!"* A chorus of giggles followed. *"He's a good giant!"*

I turned my head slowly. Dozens of children surrounded me—some barely able to toddle, others on the cusp of adolescence. Their eyes sparkled with mischief and delight. A few lingered apart, quieter, watchful. The rest chattered like birds, their words tumbling over one another in joyous chaos.

One small boy, his face alight with mischief, darted forward and pressed a massive green apple into my hand. Silence fell.

*"Eat, good giant,"* he urged.

I took a bite—and recoiled. The taste was foul, rotten. I flung it away.

The children erupted into cheers. They swarmed me, clambering onto my shoulders, hugging my arms, their tiny hands patting my face. *"We knew you were good!"* they cried. *"We knew you’d come!"*

Then, without warning, their laughter died. The crowd stilled. Small hands tugged at me urgently.

*"Get up! Hurry!"* a girl whispered, her eyes wide with fear.

I staggered to my feet just as a shadow fell across the slope. A hulking figure loomed there—broad, brutish, his face twisted in a snarl. The children vanished like mist.

The brute lumbered down toward me. I braced myself, but before I could react, a second attacker struck from behind. Blows rained down. I fought, but they overpowered me, dragging me into the woods where their tribe dwelled in squalor—huts of broken branches, the air thick with the stench of spoiled fruit.

That night, I lay bruised and aching. The next morning, they bound my feet and forced a crude stone tool into my hand. *Scrape the bark,* their kicks commanded. *Work.*

But as I toiled, I stole glances at the dwarf-tree nearby, its branches heavy with those small, red fruits. One by one, I plucked them and ate—each bite a spark of strength, each moment a step closer to escape.
