"Don't let the nerves get the better of you," advised O'Kelly, adjusting my collar with the air of a seasoned surgeon operating on a patient. "And stop trying to do too much. Your voice is decent, lad, but it lacks the power of a steam engine. Just keep your chin up, open that mouth like a trap, and breathe."

It was eleven o'clock. We stood before the grimy entrance of the narrow alleyway that served as the theater's throat. For two weeks, O'Kelly had been drilling me, a process that had strained both our tempaments, though O'Kelly's patience seemed particularly frayed. His wife, a thin, vinegar-scented lady named Mrs. O'Kelly, whom I had glimpsed occasionally pacing Belsize Square while waiting for a cue, was a woman of serious propensities and a staunch objection to anything musical that wasn't strictly sacred. Eager to convert one of her husband's sinners, the piano had been banished from the drawing-room, replaced by an American organ of such lugubrious, mournful tones that it sounded like a funeral march played by a depressed accordion player. With this instrument, O'Kelly—a genuine genius despite the limitations—had managed to weave an accompaniment to "Sally in Our Alley" that was less confusing than usual, though hardly cheerful. Consequently, my rendition of the ballad had acquired a depth of sorrow that the composer surely hadn't intended. As I sang, the song transformed, using a definition that has since become cliché in art circles, into a "problem ballad." One couldn't help wondering if the marriage depicted would prove as satisfactory as the young man seemed to expect. After all, the hero was plagued by a melancholic, pessimistic streak that clashed horribly with the frivolous, shallow instincts of the charming Sally.

"Make it lighter!" O'Kelly would cry, as the solemn, jerky notes spilled from the groaning beast beneath his fingers. "Less soulful! More jiggy!"

We nearly got caught once, when Mrs. O'Kelly returned unexpectedly from a district visitors' committee meeting. I was hastily shoved into a small conservatory adjoining the first-floor landing, where I crouched behind flower pots, listening in terror to O'Kelly's interrogation.

"William, do not fib! It wasn't a hymn!"

"My dear, timing is everything. Let me illustrate what I mean."

"William, pray, do not toy with sacred melodies in my presence. If you lack reverence for religion, remember I have it. Besides, why play hymns at ten in the morning? It isn't like you, William. I don't buy your explanation. And you were singing. I clearly heard the word 'Sally' when I opened the door."

"Salvation, my dear," O'Kelly corrected smoothly.

"Your enunciation, William, is rarely so slipshod."

"A touch of hoarseness, my dear," O'Kelly explained, feigning illness.

"You didn't sound hoarse. Perhaps we'd best drop the subject."

O'Kelly nodded in agreement.

"She's a bit hard to handle when you're feeling sprightly," O'Kelly would muse later. "But if you're sick, she's the kindest, most devoted nurse imaginable. Back in 1892, when I had typhoid, she tended me with a tenderness no man could match. I'll never forget it. And tomorrow, if anything serious were wrong with me, she'd be there again."

I murmured the famous quote.

"Mrs. O'Kelly to a T," agreed O'Kelly. "I often wonder if Lady Scott might have been cut from the same cloth."

"The trouble is," O'Kelly continued, striking a somber chord, "that I'm such a robust beggar; it doesn't give her a chance. If I were a chronic invalid, now, there's nothing she wouldn't do to make me smile. As it stands—" He hit a chord. We resumed practice.

Back at the stage door, O'Kelly shook my hand vigorously. "Meet me at the Cheshire Cheese at one. If we don't gel here, we'll try elsewhere. I've spoken to Hodgson, and I think you've got a shot. Good luck, lad!"

Off he went, and I followed. Behind the door, inside a glass box, sat a curved-nosed, round-eyed little man who looked like an irate bird trapped in a cage. He demanded my business. I flashed my appointment letter.

"Up the passage, across the stage, down the corridor, first floor, second door on the right," he barked, snapping the window shut before I could blink.

I ascended the passage. To my surprise, I wasn't nearly as jittery about facing "behind the scenes" as I had anticipated. I recalled my father asking a young soldier returning from the Crimea about his initial shock at charging.

"Well," the lad had replied, "I was worried all the time about forgetting to turn off the beer tap in the canteen."

Getting onto the stage itself, I realized my impressions weren't so much disillusionment as the realization of my darkest fears. In a single heartbeat, all the glamour surrounding the theater evaporated. I saw no magic, only the tawdry decorations of the auditorium and the childish make-believe scattered across the stage. The Theater was a dollhouse built of shreds and patches—the grown child's playground. Yes, drama elevates, teaches, and interests us. Long may it flourish! But so does the dressing and undressing of dolls, the opening of the house, and the tender placement of them to bed, training us to the duties and joys of motherhood. Toys! What wise child despises them? Is Art, fiction, and music not merely preparing us for the eventual day when we grow up?

Lost in a labyrinth of backstage corridors, I finally found a large room furnished sparsely with worn settees and chairs. Inside sat perhaps thirty men and women, all united by an indefinable, unmistakable aura of theatricality. The men mostly lacked facial hair but sported plenty atop their heads; the ladies, uniformly, possessed remarkably pink and white complexions and eyes that sparkled with artificial brightness. Their hushed, penetrating conversation revolved almost exclusively around "him" and "her." Everyone seemed to be on affectionate terms with everyone else, addressing one another as "My dear," "My love," "Old girl," "Old chap," or simply by first names. I hesitated with the door in hand, fearing I had stumbled into a family reunion. However, seeing no sign of alarm, I ventured to sit next to an extremely small, boyish-looking gentleman and asked if this was where applicants for the chorus of the upcoming comic opera waited.

He stared me up and down with large, fishy eyes. So long did he remain silent that a massive gentleman nearby, who had overheard, felt compelled to interject.

"Yes, this is it," the giant declared. "From what I know of Butterworth, we'll all be waiting here a damn sight longer than any gentleman should keep other folks waiting for no reason."

"I think that's exceedingly bad form," observed the fishy-eyed gentleman in a deep contralto, "for anyone to answer a remark addressed to someone else."

"I beg your pardon," retorted the massive man. "I thought you were asleep."

"I find it very ill-mannered," the small gentleman countered slowly, "for a gentleman to tell another gentleman, who is plainly awake, that he thinks he's asleep."

"Sir," the massive man replied, adopting a Johnsonian posture with the aid of a large umbrella, "I refuse to adjust my manners to suit your tastes."

"If you're content with them," the small man rejoined, "I can't help it. But I believe you're making a mistake."

"Do you know what the opera is about?" asked a bright little woman at the far end.

"Do you ever know what a comic opera is about?" asked another, a lady whose appearance suggested extensive experience.

"I once asked the author," a weary-looking gentleman noted from the corner. "'Well, if you'd asked me at the start of rehearsals, I might have known. But damned if I can tell you now!'"

"It wouldn't surprise me," a good-looking gentleman in a velvet coat chimed in, "if there's a drinking chorus for male voices somewhere."

"Possibly, if we're good," added a thin lady with golden hair, "the heroine will confide her love troubles, which will certainly interest and excite us."

Just then, the door at the far end swung open, and a name was called. An elderly lady rose and departed.

"Poor old Gertie!" sympathized the thin golden-haired lady. "I'm told she actually had a voice once."

"When poor young Bond first arrived in London," the massive gentleman on my left recounted, "he told me he'd applied to Lord Barrymore's 'tiger,' Alexander Lee—who ran the Strand Theatre back then—for a chorus spot. Lee heard two lines, then leaped up. 'Thanks, that'll do; good morning,' says Lee. Bond knew he had a fine voice, so he asked Lee what was wrong. 'What's wrong?' shouts Lee. 'Do you think I hire a chorus to show up my principals?'"

"Considering the company present," the fish-eyed gentleman commented, "I consider that anecdote distinctly lacking in tact."

The mood of the room seemed to align with the fish-eyed youth.
