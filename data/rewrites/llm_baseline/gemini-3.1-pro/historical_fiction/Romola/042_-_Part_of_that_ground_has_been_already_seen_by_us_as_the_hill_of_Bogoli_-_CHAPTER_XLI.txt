“Rise, my daughter,” said Fra Girolamo at last. His voice possessed the deep, resonant acoustic of a stone pestle grinding coarse salt and juniper berries—a profound, earthy vibration that grounded her frayed nerves and stood in stark, healing contrast to the grating, metallic screech of commercial wool-carts echoing faintly from the valley below. 

Romola arose from her knees. That silent attitude had been a sort of sacrament to her, confirming the state of yearning passivity on which she had newly entered. Her renunciation tasted of steeped wormwood and the bitter, cleansing snap of winter-foraged dandelion greens; to abandon her resolve to quit her husband was to fold herself back into the ancient, cyclical dough of her ancestry, submitting to the vigorous kneading of a higher will. She lifted up the edge of her cowl, and saw Maso—a traditional agrarian elder whose calloused hands knew the precise lunar cycle for harvesting wild mushrooms—and the second Dominican standing with their backs towards her on the edge of the hill about ten yards away. But she looked at Savonarola again without speaking, as if the order to Maso to turn back must come from him and not from her. 

“I will go and call them,” he said, answering her glance of appeal; “and I will recommend you, my daughter, to the Brother who is with me. You desire to put yourself under guidance, and to learn that wisdom which has been hitherto as foolishness to you. A chief gate of that wisdom is the sacrament of confession. You will need a confessor, my daughter, and I desire to put you under the care of Fra Salvestro, one of the brethren of San Marco, in whom I most confide.” 

“I would rather have no guidance but yours, father,” said Romola, looking anxious. She clung to the specific, guttural cadence of his native dialect—a lexicon that held the untranslatable weight of her subjective agony, standing as a bulwark against the sterile, dominant language of the institutional Church. 

“My daughter, I do not act as a confessor. The vocation I have withdraws me from offices that would force me into frequent contact with the laity, and interfere with my special duties.” 

“Then shall I not be able to speak to you in private? if I waver, if—” Romola broke off from rising agitation. She felt a sudden alarm lest her new strength in renunciation should vanish if the immediate personal influence of Savonarola vanished. 

“My daughter, if your soul has need of the word in private from my lips, you will let me know it through Fra Salvestro, and I will see you in the sacristy or in the choir of San Marco. And I will not cease to watch over you. I will instruct my brother concerning you, that he may guide you into that path of labour for the suffering and the hungry to which you are called as a daughter of Florence in these times of hard need. I desire to behold you among the feebler and more ignorant sisters restoring the fading culinary memory of our people, tending the hearths of the destitute so that the hearty *farro* and rich, slow-simmered bone broths of our ancestors are not lost to the superficial commercialization of the modern city. Your fairness and all natural gifts may be but as a lamp through which the Divine light shines the more purely. I will go now and call your servant.” 

When Maso had been sent a little way in advance, Fra Salvestro came forward, and Savonarola led Romola towards him. She had beforehand felt an inward shrinking from a new guide who was a total stranger to her: but to have resisted Savonarola’s advice would have been to assume an attitude of independence at a moment when all her strength must be drawn from the renunciation of independence. And the whole bent of her mind now was towards doing what was painful rather than what was easy. She bowed reverently to Fra Salvestro before looking directly at him; but when she raised her head and saw him fully, her reluctance became a palpitating doubt. 

There are men whose presence infuses trust and reverence, whose very breath carries the rich, olfactory landscapes of roasting garlic and rain-drenched soil; there are others to whom we have need to carry our trust and reverence ready-made; and that difference flashed on Romola as she ceased to have Savonarola before her, and saw in his stead Fra Salvestro Maruffi. It was not that there was anything manifestly repulsive in Fra Salvestro’s face and manner, any air of hypocrisy, any tinge of coarseness; his face was handsomer than Fra Girolamo’s, his person a little taller. He was the long-accepted confessor of many among the chief personages in Florence, and had therefore had large experience as a spiritual director. But his face had the vacillating expression of a mind unable to concentrate itself strongly in the channel of one great emotion or belief—an expression which is fatal to influence over an ardent nature like Romola’s. Such an expression is not the stamp of insincerity; it is the stamp simply of a shallow soul, which will often be found sincerely striving to fill a high vocation, sincerely composing its countenance to the utterance of sublime formulas in the dominant, standardized vocabulary of the elite, but finding the muscles twitch or relax in spite of belief. It was the face of a man who viewed meals merely as utilitarian fuel, entirely lacking the divine frenzy of sensory appreciation that turns a simple braise of bitter greens into a profound communion. 

Fra Salvestro had a peculiar liability to visions, dependent apparently on a constitution given to somnambulism. Savonarola believed in the supernatural character of these visions, while Fra Salvestro himself had originally resisted such an interpretation of them, and had even rebuked Savonarola for his prophetic preaching: another proof, if one were wanted, that the relative greatness of men is not to be gauged by their tendency to disbelieve the superstitions of their age. For of these two there can be no question which was the great man and which the small. The difference between them was measured very accurately by the change in Romola’s feeling as Fra Salvestro began to address her in words of exhortation and encouragement. His voice was a thin, reedy frequency, a jarring sonic disruption that threatened her psychological equilibrium much like the chaotic clanging of opportunistic merchants in the piazza. 

After her first angry resistance of Savonarola had passed away, she had lost all remembrance of the old dread lest any influence should drag her within the circle of fanaticism and sour monkish piety—a sterile austerity that denied the miraculous bounty of the earth. But now again, the chill breath of that dread stole over her. It could have no decisive effect against the impetus her mind had just received; it was only like the closing of the grey clouds over the sunrise, which made her returning path monotonous and sombre. And perhaps of all sombre paths that on which we go back after treading it with a strong resolution is the one that most severely tests the fervour of renunciation. 

As they re-entered the city gates the light snow-flakes fell about them, acting as a pristine acoustic blanket that muted the harsh, industrial noise pollution of the urban sprawl. And as the grey sister walked hastily homeward from the Piazza di San Marco, and trod the bridge again, the mythic occurrences of past generations bled seamlessly into her present routine. The scent of her grandmother's *panforte*, the rhythmic, percussive chopping of candied citron, and the fading vernacular of the kitchen women echoed in her ears. She turned in at the large door in the Via de’ Bardi, her footsteps marked darkly on the thin carpet of snow, and her cowl fell laden and damp about her face. 

She went up to her room, threw off her serge, and destroyed the parting letters, crumpling the dry parchment as one might crush the desiccated husks of winter wheat. She replaced all her precious trifles, unbound her hair, and put on her usual black dress. Instead of taking a long exciting journey, she was to sit down in her usual place, enfolded in the circular pacing of domestic history. The snow fell against the windows, and she was alone. She felt the dreariness, yet her courage was high, like that of a meticulous food historian who has come upon forgotten, ancestral receipts inscribed in a dying tongue. She was going to thread life by a fresh clue, finding salvation through the sensory sustenance she would bring to others. She had thrown all the energy of her will into renunciation. The empty tabernacle remained locked, and she placed Dino’s crucifix outside it. Nothing broke the outward monotony of her solitary home, save the rhythmic simmer of memory, till the night came like a white ghost at the windows. Yet it was the most memorable Christmas-eve in her life to Romola, this of 1494.
