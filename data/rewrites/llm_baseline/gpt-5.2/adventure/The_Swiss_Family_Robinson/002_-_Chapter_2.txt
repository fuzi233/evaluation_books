Night came down on us like a lid set carefully over a pot: all the day’s glare and salt had cooked us to weariness, and without the shelter of our tent we should have shivered miserably. As it was, the canvas held what little warmth our bodies could spare. The sand cooled, the sea quieted into a long, breathing hush, and we slept—each of us drained to the marrow by the labor of getting ashore and safe.

The last sound I remember before sleep took me was our vigilant cock, full of self-importance, saluting the rising moon as if he were still in a proper farmyard and not on the edge of a nameless coast. That crow, sharp and clean as a struck bell, woke me at daybreak too. In the pale interval when the world is mostly listening—when the surf speaks softly and even birds hold their tongues—I roused my wife. We stole out while the boys still slept, and took counsel in whispers, our voices small against the wide acoustics of water and cliff.

It was plain to us both what must be done first: learn, if we could, the fate of our late companions; and then, as quickly as prudence allowed, examine the nature and resources of the country that had received us.

So we resolved that after breakfast Fritz and I would set out on an expedition for those two purposes, while my wife remained near our landing-place with the three younger boys.

When the sun had just begun to warm the sand again, I clapped my hands softly and called, “Rouse up, rouse up, my boys. Come and help your mother to get breakfast ready.”

“As to that,” said my wife, smiling with that brave thriftiness which is its own kind of feast, “we can but set on the pot and boil some more soup.”

“Why! You forget Jack’s fine lobster,” I answered. “What has become of it, Jack?”

Jack’s eyes shone with triumph. “It has been safe in this hole in the rock all night, father. You see, I thought as the dogs seem to like good things, they might take a fancy to that as well as to the agouti.”

“A very sensible precaution,” said I. “I believe even my heedless Jack will learn wisdom in time. And it is well the lobster is so large, for we shall want to take part of it with us on our excursion to-day.”

At the word excursion the four of them burst into bright commotion—hands clapping, feet stamping in the sand, a little storm of boyish noise. The sound was pleasant, like the bustle of a kitchen when the fire is up and everyone has a task, yet I checked them with a lifted hand.

“Steady there. You cannot expect all to go. Such an expedition as this would be too dangerous and fatiguing for you younger ones, and this place seems perfectly safe. Fritz and I will go alone this time, with one of the dogs, leaving the other to defend you. Fritz, prepare the guns, and tie up Flora so that she will not follow us.”

At the word guns, Fritz colored to the roots of his hair. The shame of yesterday’s rashness rose in him like a bitter taste. He struggled to straighten his weapon, his hands careful now where once they had been reckless. I let him wrestle with that lesson a moment longer, then allowed him, seeing contrition had done its work, to take another gun.

When he went to lay hold of Flora to tie her, she showed teeth and a low growl rolled in her throat—a sound that is not loud but has weight, like a warning drum. Turk, too, held back, stiff and offended. They remembered the blows. I had to call them with my own voice before they would come close enough to be touched.

Fritz’s eyes filled. He turned to his mother. “May I have some biscuit?” he begged. “I would rather go without the rest of my breakfast to make my peace with the dogs.”

My wife gave it, and Fritz carried the biscuit as if it were an offering. He stroked and spoke to them, every movement a humble apology. Flora, whose heart was quick to soften, relented at once and licked the hand that fed her. Turk, fiercer and more independent, kept his distance, his distrust like a taut rope between them.

“Give him a claw of my lobster,” cried Jack, “for I meant to give it to you anyway, for your journey.”

That rich morsel—salt-sweet, with the sea still living in it—did what biscuit could not. Turk’s nostrils flared; his pride yielded to appetite; he accepted Fritz again with a grudging wag and a slow step forward. It was a small domestic reconciliation, but on a stranded shore such things are as important as any map.

We armed ourselves: each of us a gun and a game-bag; Fritz tucked pistols into his belt with the seriousness of a young soldier trying on a man’s duties; I fastened a small hatchet at mine. Breakfast was simple but sustaining: the familiar thin soup warmed in the pot, then the lobster shared out—firm flesh that demanded teeth and attention, the kind of food that makes you feel your own jaw and therefore your own life. We stowed the remainder of it, along with biscuits and a flask of water, for the road.

“Stop!” I cried, just as we were about to set off.

“Surely not,” said Fritz, startled.

“Yes,” I answered. “We have still left something very important undone. We have not yet joined in morning prayer. We are only too ready, amid the cares and pleasures of this life, to forget the God to whom we owe all things.”

So we commended ourselves to His protecting care, and then I took leave of my wife and children. I charged them not to wander far from the boat and tent. We parted with smiles put on like brave clothing, but underneath was that quiet anxiety one hears in the voice of the wind before a change.

We soon found a small advantage in the harsh shape of the land. The banks of the stream near our camp were rocky on both sides, and we could only reach the water by one narrow passage; there was no corresponding path on the opposite side. This pleased me, for it meant my wife and boys were in a comparatively inaccessible spot, guarded on the other side of the tent by steep, precipitous cliffs.

Fritz and I followed the stream upward until we reached a fall where the water spilled down from a considerable height into a white, cold chatter. Several large rocks lay half covered by the current; stepping from stone to stone, feeling for purchase with our boots, we crossed safely. Now the sea lay on our left, and to the right a long line of rocky heights stretched inland, here and there softened by clumps of trees.

We had pushed barely fifty yards into long rank grass—partly withered by the sun, snarled and tangled like poorly combed hair—when we heard a rustling behind us. The grass waved as if something heavy threaded through it. Fritz turned instantly, lifted his gun, and waited, not firing, not wasting a motion. I felt a surge of pride at his coolness; it told me I could rely on him when true danger came.

But no savage beast burst out. It was our faithful Turk—forgotten in the flurry of departure—come after us, no doubt sent by my wife’s thoughtful care. I praised Fritz for not yielding to alarm and for waiting until he was sure before resolving to shoot.

Still, the incident was a warning written in sound: on such ground, with grass tall enough to hide a body, danger could arrive silently until it was already too close. We hastened toward the open seashore where, at least, the ear has room to work.

The scene that opened there was indeed delightful: hills behind us, the grass moving in broad green swells, trees grouped pleasantly, some stretching almost to the water’s edge. The surf drew its long breath on the smooth sand—steady, cleansing, the sort of sound that steadies the mind.

We searched the shore with care for any trace of our companions. Nothing—not a footprint, not the drag of a heel, not even a broken twig to suggest a passing hand.

“Shall I fire a shot or two?” asked Fritz. “That would bring our companions, if they are within hearing.”

“It would indeed,” I said, “or any savages that may be here. No, no; let us search diligently, but as quietly as possible.”

“But why, father, should we trouble ourselves about them at all?” Fritz burst out. “They left us to shift for ourselves, and I for one don’t care to set eyes on them again.”

“You are wrong, my boy,” I replied. “In the first place, we should not return evil for evil. Then again, they might be of great assistance to us in building a house of some sort. And lastly, you must remember they took nothing with them from the vessel, and may be perishing of hunger.”

“But father,” he insisted, “while we are wandering here and losing our time almost without a hope of benefit to them, why should we not instead return to the vessel and save the animals on board?”

“When a variety of duties present themselves,” I answered, “we should always give the preference to that which can confer the most solid advantage. The saving of the life of a man is a more exalted action than contributing to the comfort of a few quadrupeds, whom we have already supplied with food for a few days. Also, the sea is so calm at present that we need not fear the ship will sink or break up entirely before we can return.”

Thus talking, we went on until we reached a pleasant grove that stretched to the water’s edge. There we rested under a large tree beside a rivulet that murmured and splashed along its pebbly bed into the ocean—fresh water making its bright, laughing argument against the salt’s endless hush.

Above us birds—gaily plumaged—flitted and twittered, a light net of sound. Fritz tilted his head, listening as much as looking, and suddenly started up.

“A monkey!” he cried. “I am nearly sure I saw a monkey.”

He sprang around the tree and, in his haste, stumbled over a small round object. He picked it up and handed it to me, saying it was a round bird’s nest of which he had often heard.

“You may have done so,” I said, laughing, “but you need not conclude that every round hairy thing is a bird’s nest. This, for instance, is not one, but a cocoanut.”

I turned it in my hand and showed him the fibrous covering like coarse rope. “Do you remember reading that a cocoanut is enclosed within a round, fibrous covering over a hard shell, which again is surrounded by a bulky green hull? Here the outer hull has been worn away by time; that is why the twisted fibres show so plainly. Let us break it and you will see the nut within.”

We split it open with effort—stone and knife and stubborn hands—but, to our disgust, the kernel was dry and uneatable, like old bread forgotten at the back of a cupboard.

“Hullo,” said Fritz, “I always thought a cocoanut was full of delicious sweet liquid, like almond milk.”

“So it is when young and fresh,” I told him, “but as it ripens the milk congeals, and in course of time solidifies into a kernel. This kernel then dries as you see. But when the nut falls on favourable soil, the germ within swells until it bursts the shell, takes root, and springs up a new tree.”

“I do not understand,” said Fritz, frowning at the thickness of the shell, “how the little germ gets through such a great thick thing.”

“Nature provides for all things,” I answered, fitting the broken halves together. “Look here—these three round holes near the stalk. It is through them that the germ obtains egress. Now let us find a good nut if we can.”

Because cocoanuts must be over-ripe before they fall naturally, it was not easy to find one still fit to eat. At last we did. The flesh was a little oily, even faintly rancid, but we were in no mood for fastidiousness. It refreshed us enough that we could postpone what we called our dinner until later, sparing our provisions.

We continued through a thicket so densely overgrown with lianas we had to hew our way—my hatchet biting and ringing, vines snapping with wet, green pops. We emerged again onto the shore beyond and found an open view. The forest swept inland; on the space before us stood single trees at intervals, their trunks oddly bumped and studded.

Fritz pointed. “Oh, what absurd-looking trees, father! See what strange bumps there are on the trunks.”

We approached, and I recognized calabash trees—gourds growing right from the stems, as if the tree itself had begun to hang its kitchenware where any passing hand might reach.

“The fruit of these,” I told him, “is a species of gourd. From the hard rind bowls, spoons, and bottles can be made. The savages are said to form these things most ingeniously, and they use them to contain liquids: indeed, they actually cook food in them.”

“Oh, that is impossible,” said Fritz. “I am quite sure this rind would burn through directly if it was set on the fire.”

“I did not say it was set on the fire,” I replied. “When the gourd has been divided and emptied, it is filled with water, and fish or whatever is to be cooked is put in. Red-hot stones are dropped in until the water boils; the food is cooked, and the gourd remains uninjured.”

“That is a clever plan—simple too. I daresay I should have hit on it, if I had tried,” said Fritz, half proud already of an invention not yet his.

“The friends of Columbus thought it very easy to make an egg stand upon its end,” I said, “when he had shown them how. But now, suppose we prepare some of these calabashes, that they may be ready for use when we take them home.”

Fritz seized a gourd at once and tried to split it neatly with his knife. The blade slipped; the cut went jagged.

“What a nuisance!” he cried, and flung it down. “The thing is spoiled; and yet it seemed so simple.”

“Stay,” I said. “You are too impatient. Those pieces are not useless. Try to fashion a spoon or two from them while I provide a dish.”

From my pocket I drew a piece of string. I tied it tightly around a gourd near one end. Then I tapped the string with the back of my knife, working it in as if it were a saw. When it had bitten into the rind, I tightened the string again and again. Then, drawing the ends with all my might, I split the gourd cleanly in two.

“That is clever!” cried Fritz. “What in the world put that plan into your head?”

“It is a plan the savages adopt, as I have learned from reading books of travel,” I answered.

He held the halves up. “It makes a capital soup-tureen, and a soup-plate too. But supposing you had wanted to make a bottle—how would you have set to work?”

“Easier, if possible,” I said. “Cut a round hole at one end, scoop out the interior, then drop in shot or stones. Shake it; the remaining pulp detaches; the gourd is cleaned, and the bottle completed.”

“That would not make a very convenient bottle,” he objected. “It would be more like a barrel.”

“True. If you want a more shapely vessel, you must begin when it is younger. To give it a neck, tie a bandage round the young gourd while it is still on the tree; all will swell but that part which you have checked.”

As I spoke, I filled the gourds with sand and left them to dry, marking the place in my mind so we might find them again on our way back.

“Are the bottle-shaped gourds I have seen in Europe trained similarly?” Fritz asked.

“No,” I said. “Those are another species; what you have seen is their natural shape.”

For three hours or more we pushed on, alert for any trace of men—listening for any sound not made by wind, water, or bird. At length we reached a bold promontory thrusting into the sea. From its rocky summit I knew we could survey the surrounding country. We climbed without much difficulty, but from the top, for all our careful searching, we saw no sign of human beings—no smoke, no movement, no mark of habitation.

Before us lay a wide bay fringed with yellow sand. The arms of it stretched away and dwindled into shadowy promontories. The enclosed water rippled and flashed, holding the sun like molten metal. Inland was no less fair—yet the beauty had a loneliness that crept into our chests. The world seemed to be holding its breath, and that great silence made even our own speech sound strange, as if we were borrowing a language not meant for this place.

“Cheer up, Fritz,” I said at last. “Remember we chose a settler’s life long ago, before we left our dear country. We did not expect to be so entirely alone—but what matters a few people, more or less? With God’s help, let us live here contentedly, thankful we were not cast upon some bare and inhospitable island. But come—the heat is getting unbearable. Let us find shade before we are completely broiled away.”

We descended and made for a clump of palms in the distance. To reach it we had to pass through a dense thicket of reeds. The air there was close and full of dry whispering; each step made a rasp as stalks slid against one another. It was no pleasant task, and I feared at every footfall that we might tread on some venomous snake hidden beneath.

I sent Turk ahead and cut myself a stout reed, thinking it might serve better against a reptile than a gun that requires aim and distance. After a few steps the cut end began to weep a thick juice. I tasted it, and to my delight it was sweet and clean on the tongue.

Sugar-cane.

Wanting Fritz to discover it for himself, I told him to cut a cane for his defence. He did, beating the ground before him. The reed split, juice smeared his hand. He touched it cautiously with the tip of his tongue, then again, then sprang back to me with a shout that rang through the cane-brake like a boy in a fair.

“Oh, father—sugar-cane, sugar-cane! Taste it. Oh, how delicious! Do let us take a lot home to mother,” he cried, sucking eagerly at the cane as he spoke.

“Gently there,” I said. “Take breath. Moderation in all things, remember. Cut some to take home if you like; only do not take more than you can conveniently carry.”

In spite of my warning he cut a dozen of the largest, stripped off their leaves, and hugged them under his arm like treasure.

We forced our way through and reached the palms. At our entrance, a troop of monkeys that had been disporting themselves on the ground sprang up, chattering and grimacing, and before we could clearly distinguish them they were already at the very top of the trees, a storm of small bodies and sharp voices among the fronds.

Fritz, provoked by their impudent faces, lifted his gun.

“Stay,” I cried. “Never take the life of any animal needlessly. A live monkey up in that tree is of more use to us than a dozen dead ones at our feet, as I will show you.”

I gathered a handful of stones and threw them upward. The stones fell far short, but the monkeys, seized by their mania for imitation, immediately began snatching cocoanuts within reach and sending them down in a lively hail. The nuts thudded into sand, rattled against roots, and rolled at our feet.

Fritz laughed in delight and ran forward to gather the best. I pierced holes in the shells, and we drank the milk. It was not pleasant in flavour—thin and strange, neither quite sweet nor quite fresh—but it cooled our throats wonderfully. What pleased us most was the thick, pale cream clinging to the inside of the shell, which we scraped off with our makeshift spoons—rich and satisfying, like a reward for ingenuity.

After this meal we thoroughly despised the lobster we had been carrying and threw it to Turk, who devoured it gratefully. Still hungry, he gnawed the ends of the sugar-canes and begged for cocoanut with his eyes fixed on our hands.

I slung a couple of nuts over my shoulder, fastening them together by their stalks. Fritz resumed his burden of canes. Then we began our march home.

Before long I saw Fritz begin to feel the weight he had boasted so lightly. He shifted the canes from shoulder to shoulder, carried them under his arm, then stopped with a sigh.

“I had no idea a few reeds would be so heavy,” he admitted. “How sincerely I pity the poor negroes who are made to carry heavy loads of them. Yet how glad I shall be when my mother and brothers are tasting them.”

“Never mind, my boy,” I said. “Patience and courage. Do you not remember Aesop’s story of the breadbasket—how heavy it seemed at first, and how light at the end because it had been eaten away? Let us each take a fresh staff, and fasten the bundle crosswise with your gun.”

We did so and went on. Now and then I sucked at the end of my cane as we walked, letting the sweetness temper the heat and the dust.

Fritz noticed. “Oh, come—that’s a capital plan of yours, father. I’ll do that too.”

He set to work with great vigour, but could draw not a drop. He frowned, tried again, and finally asked in a vexed tone, “How is this? How do you get the juice out?”

“Think a little,” I said. “You are quite as capable as I am of finding the way, even if you do not know the real reason of your failure.”

“Oh, of course,” he cried, and his face cleared. “It is like trying to suck marrow from a bone without making a hole at the other end.”

“Quite right,” I said. “You form a vacuum and expect the air to force down the liquid from the other end, which it cannot possibly enter.”

He laughed and corrected his mistake, and soon was as expert as any schoolboy who has turned a lesson into a game, discovering also by practice that he must make a fresh cut at each joint. He chattered on about teaching his brothers, and how Ernest would enjoy the cocoanut milk with which he had filled his flask.

“My dear boy,” I said, “you need not have added that to your load; the chances are it will be vinegar by the time we get home. In this heat it will ferment soon after being drawn.”

“Vinegar!” Fritz cried, horrified. “That would be a horrid bore! I must look directly and see how it is getting on.”

He swung the flask down and tugged out the cork. With a loud pop the contents burst forth, foaming like champagne.

“There now!” I laughed as he tasted it. “You will have to exercise moderation again, friend Fritz. I daresay it is delicious, but it will go to your head if you venture too deep into your flask.”

“My dear father, you cannot think how good it is,” he said, eyes bright. “Do take some. Vinegar indeed! This is like excellent wine.”

The unexpected draught warmed us and made our steps lighter. We went on so merrily that the distance to the place where we had left our gourd dishes seemed shorter than we expected. We found them quite dry, and so light that carrying them was like carrying empty bowls to a table.

Just as we passed through the grove where we had eaten our first cocoanut, Turk suddenly darted away and sprang furiously among a troop of monkeys that were gambolling on the turf. The whole scene erupted in a confusion of squeals and thumps. Taken by surprise, the dog—now truly ravenous—seized one and tore it before we could reach him.

The victim was a mother with a tiny one clinging to her back. That helpless burden had hindered her flight. When she fell, the little creature tumbled off and tried to hide in the grass, trembling, watching with round eyes as Turk worried the body.

Fritz, horrified, rushed forward to rescue them, flinging down everything he carried and losing his hat in his haste. He was too late for the mother. And then the moment turned, as moments will, from tragedy to something absurd: the baby monkey, seeing Fritz, sprang in one bound to his shoulders and, clutching his thick curly hair, planted itself there as if it had chosen its new refuge. Fritz screamed, plunged about, tried to shake it off, tried to pry its fingers loose, but the little creature only clung tighter, making ridiculous grimaces as if it mocked the whole human struggle.

I laughed so heartily at the sight that for a moment I could scarcely help him. Between my mirth and his distress, the grove rang with strange noises—Fritz’s cries, the monkey’s chattering, Turk’s snarling subsiding into satisfied grunts, and above all the steady, patient speech of the sea.

At last, still laughing, I told Fritz the creature, having lost its mother, seemed determined to adopt him. “Perhaps he has discovered in you something of the air of a father of a family.”

“Or rather,” Fritz panted, “the little rogue has found out he has to do with a chicken-heart who shrinks from the idea of ill-treating an animal that has thrown itself on his protection. But father, he is yanking my hair terribly, and I shall be obliged to you to try once more to get him off.”
