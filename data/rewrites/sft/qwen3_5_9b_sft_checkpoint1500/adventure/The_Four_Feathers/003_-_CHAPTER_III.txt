Alone, Jock Durrance walked the length of the street toward his lodging, his mind drifting back nearly two years. It was a memory sparked by a peculiar whim of Old Dermod Eustace, the man who had once dragged the reluctant youth to a cottage by the Lennon River in Donegal. There, amidst the quiet of the countryside, Durrance had met Dermod's daughter, Ethne. To everyone else, she was merely a girl; to Durrance, she was a revelation. He had spent a night in her father's house, and throughout the evening, she had played her violin. Seated with her back to the room, she turned away from any audience, refusing to let a glance or a gesture break the spell of her concentration.

Those melodies still echoed in his mind. The girl possessed a rare gift; her bow seemed to speak directly to the strings, drawing forth sounds that transcended ordinary emotion. He remembered an overture, *Melusine*, that carried the very sob of the waves. That night, sitting in silence while Ethne's face remained turned away, Durrance had felt the music tell stories of journeys too perilous for mortal tongues, of silver paths across moonlit seas, and of voices calling from the desert's edge. It had spoken of unattainable joys and griefs so profound they became noble in their scale, of longings that defied words—all without a single note of complaint.

And now, as he walked alone, knowing that Ethne's back would forever remain turned to him, that same truth resonated. *True music does not complain.* It simply exists, pure and unburdened.

The following morning, the sun broke over London with the same warmth as ever. Durrance mounted his horse near the cluster of lilacs and laburnums at the end of the Row, expecting his friend. It was the hour and place where Harry Feversham and he had met every chance they had since leaving Oxford. But Harry did not appear. Nor did he show up for the next three weeks. While rumors swirled about the coming war, growing steadily from gossip into grim certainty, Durrance felt a hollow confusion. It seemed, to him, as if he had lost his friend entirely.

Then, finally, Harry kept the appointment, but the mood between them had shifted irrevocably.

"I told you luck might look my way," Harry said, his voice brimming with an exhilaration that cut sharply through the air. "Well, it has. I'm going to Egypt on General Graham's staff. There's talk we'll follow him down the Red Sea to Suakin after."

A spark of unmistakable envy flared in Harry's eyes, startling even Durrance. It was strange, and yet strangely pleasant, to see such a glint in his friend's gaze, especially given how little Harry wanted. But Durrance read the expression through the lens of his own ambition.

"It's rough on you," Durrance said gently, trying to soften the blow. "That your regiment has to stay behind."

Harry rode beside him in silence. As they reached the shaded benches beneath the trees, he finally spoke.

"That was expected. The day you dined with me, I sent in my papers."

"That night?" Durrance asked, turning in his saddle. "After we'd gone?"

"Yes," Harry admitted, accepting the correction. A shadow crossed his face, and for a moment, Durrance sensed a reproach in his friend's silence—but then he realized he was misreading it. Suddenly, Harry laughed, a hearty sound that broke the tension.

"I remember," Harry said. "You gave us your reasons that night. But for the life of me, I can't help wishing we were going out together. When do you leave for Ireland?"

"To-night."

"So soon?"

They turned their horses and rode westward down the tree-lined alley. The morning air was still crisp; the lime and chestnut trees held onto their early greens, and the late May blossoms hung like delicate snow, glowing red against the dark rhododendrons below. The park shimmered in a haze of sunlight, and the distant rumble of the city streets sounded like the tumbling of river water.

"It's been a long time since we bathed in Sandford Lasher," Durrance remarked.

"And frozen in those Easter vacations in the big snow-gully on Great End," Harry replied.

Both men felt a heavy sense of finality settle over them. This morning marked the closing of a volume in their shared book of life. It had been a pleasant chapter, full of laughter and easy company, but they couldn't say whether the pages that followed would hold the same promise. They looked backward through the leaves of memory before putting the book away for the last time.

"You must stay with us, Jack, when you come back," Harry urged.

Durrance schooled his features carefully, masking any sting at the word "us." If his left hand tightened slightly on the reins, it was invisible to his friend.

"If I come back," Durrance said, his voice steady. "You know my creed. I could never pity a man who dies on active service. I'd very much like to come by that end myself."

It was a simple creed, born of the simplicity of the man who spoke it. To die decently was worth far more than a lifetime of living. He said it without melancholy, without a tremor of fear. Yet, a part of him worried that Harry might interpret these words differently. He glanced quickly at his friend, searching for understanding, but found only that same puzzling look of envy in Harry's eyes.

"You see, there are worse things that can happen," Harry continued, shaking off the thought. "Disablement, for instance. Some clever men might manage to live with it, but what in the world should I do if I had to sit in a chair all my days? Just thinking of it makes me shiver." He shook his broad shoulders as if to dislodge the chill. "Well, this is the last ride. Let us gallop."

He spurred his horse forward. Harry followed suit, and side by side they raced down the sandy path. At the bottom of the Row, they stopped, shook hands, and exchanged the briefest, kindest nod before parting ways. Harry rode out of the park, while Durrance turned back, leading his horse up toward the benches beneath the trees.

Long ago, as a boy in Southpool, Devonshire, sitting on a wooded creek by the Salcombe estuary, Durrance had felt a restless pull. He dreamed of sailing down past the familiar woods into the wide, unknown seas, seeking queer, outlandish countries and people. That restlessness had grown with him, coloring his thoughts about "Guessens," the family estate he had inherited. It remained in his mind not as a home to occupy, but as a destination to return to—a place of departure rather than arrival.

Now, riding alone, he deliberately amplified that restlessness, setting it against the words Harry had spoken moments before. *Ethne Eustace would hardly be happy outside her county of Donegal,* he reasoned. Therefore, even if fate had worked differently, there might have been a clash. Perhaps it was best that Harry Feversham was marrying Ethne—and no other than Feversham.

With this argument spinning in his mind, the riders ahead of him faded from sight. Beneath the cool shade of the trees, ladies in colorful dresses stood waiting. The grand trees dwindled in his vision to ragged mimosa bushes; the brown sand at his feet spread outward in a widening circle, taking on the golden hue of honey. Black stones began to pile up shapelessly like coal, flashing in the sun like scattered mirrors. Deep in his anticipations of the Sudan campaign, a soft voice called his name.

Looking up, he found himself close to the rails. "How do you do, Mrs. Adair?" he said, bringing his horse to a halt.

She gave him her hand across the fence. She was his neighbor at Southpool, a few years older than him, a tall woman known for the myriad shades of her thick brown hair and the peculiar pallor of her face. But today, her face was brighter, a flush of color warming her cheeks.

"I have news for you," Durrance said. "Two special items. First, Harry Feversham is to be married."

"To whom?" she asked, eager.

"You should know," he replied. "It was in your house in Hill Street that Harry first met her, and I introduced them. He has been cultivating the acquaintance in Dublin."

Mrs. Adair understood immediately, and the news was clearly welcome.

"Ethne Eustace!" she cried. "Will they be married soon?"

"There is nothing to prevent it."

"I am glad," she sighed, the relief evident in her voice. "What is your second item?"

"As good as the first," Durrance said. "I go out on General Graham's staff."

Silence fell between them. A look of sharp anxiety entered Mrs. Adair's eyes, and the color drained from her face.

"You are very glad, I suppose," she said slowly.

Her tone left no doubt as to her feelings, but Durrance's own voice betrayed none. "I should think I was. I go soon, too, and the sooner the better. I will come and dine some night, if I may, before I depart."

"My husband will be pleased to see you," Mrs. Adair said, her voice somewhat cold.
