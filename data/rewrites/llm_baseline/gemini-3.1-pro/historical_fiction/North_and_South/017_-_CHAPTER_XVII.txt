Margaret went out heavily, her steps dragging against the cobbled pavement, but the sheer bracing current of the Milton air—sharp with the metallic bite of coal-smoke and the faint, sour yeast of unwashed bodies—quickened her blood before she reached the first turning. The profound acoustic disruption of the town seized her attention. The relentless, rhythmic thud of the looms, a mechanical heartbeat that usually vibrated through the marrow of the streets and dictated the psychological tempo of its inhabitants, had ceased. In this unnatural, bruised quiet, a dissonant human clamor had risen to take its place. 

She saw unusual loiterers in the streets. Men stood with hands plunged deep into their pockets, their voices grating in a coarse, clipped Northern dialect that felt like gravel against her Southern ears. Clusters of girls laughed with a boisterous, almost feral independence, their loud voices fracturing the fragile silence. The more discreditable minority hovered around the steps of the beer-houses, smoking cheap, dark-roasted tobacco and exhaling the acrid tang of juniper and raw gin, loudly passing judgment on every passer-by. The air tasted of idleness and ferment. Margaret shrank from the prospect of a long walk through this chaotic sonic landscape to reach the quiet fields she had initially sought. Instead, she turned toward the cramped, damp-smelling alleyway of Bessy Higgins’s home, seeking a different sort of quietude.

Nicholas Higgins was sitting by the hearth when she entered, drawing thick, harsh clouds from his pipe, the biting scent of the burning leaf hanging heavy in the unventilated room. Opposite him, Bessy rocked herself, the rhythmic, wooden creak of her chair serving as a meager, repetitive metronome against the oppressive ambient noise of the alley. Nicholas pulled the pipe from his mouth, its wet stem clicking against his teeth, and pushed his chair toward Margaret, leaning back against the chimney-piece as she asked after Bessy’s health.

“Hoo’s rather down i’ th’ mouth in regard to spirits, but hoo’s better in health,” Nicholas said. The dialect was ancient and heavy, carrying the weight of generational endurance. “Hoo doesn’t like this strike. Hoo’s a deal too much set on peace and quietness at any price.”

“This is th’ third strike I’ve seen,” Bessy sighed, the expulsion of breath a weary surrender to a cycle she could not control. 

“Well, third time pays for all,” Nicholas countered, his voice resonant with defiance. “See if we don’t dang th’ masters this time. See if they don’t come and beg us to come back at our own price. We’ve missed it afore time, I grant yo’; but this time we’n laid our plans desperate deep.”

“Why do you strike?” asked Margaret. She struggled to translate their industrial lexicon into her own agrarian understanding. “Striking is leaving off work till you get your own rate of wages, is it not? Where I come from, I never heard of a strike.”

“I wish I were there,” Bessy murmured, closing her eyes. “But it’s not for me to get sick and tired o’ strikes. This is the last I’ll see. Before it’s ended I shall be in the Great City—the Holy Jerusalem.”

“Hoo’s so full of th’ life to come, hoo cannot think of th’ present,” Nicholas said, gesturing with his pipe. “Now I, yo see, am bound to do the best I can here. I think a bird i’ th’ hand is worth two i’ th’ bush.”

“But,” Margaret pressed, grounding her thoughts in the seasonal cycles of sustenance she revered, “if the people struck, as you call it, where I come from... the seed would not be sown. The earth would not be turned, the hay would rot, the golden wheat would never be reaped to mill into flour.” To Margaret, the cultivation of food was a sacred, mythic inheritance; to sever that cycle seemed an unthinkable violation of the earth’s rhythm.

“Well?” he asked, puffing away, turning the word into a blunt interrogation. 

“Why, what would become of the farmers?” 

He continued to smoke, the red cherry of the tobacco glowing like a tiny forge. “I reckon, they’d have either to give up their farms, or to give fair rate of wage.”

“But they would have no hay, nor corn to sell that year,” she argued, her mind tracing the meticulous path from the sun-warmed field to the hearth-baked crust of a communal loaf. “Where would the money come from to pay the labourers’ wages the next?”

Nicholas released a long plume of smoke. “I know nought of your ways down South. I have heerd they’re a pack of spiritless, down-trodden men; welly clemmed to death; too dazed wi’ clemming to know when they’re put upon.” 

*Clemmed*. The Northern word for starvation struck Margaret’s ear with a visceral, hollow ring, entirely untranslatable to the polite, distant Southern notion of hunger. It tasted of ash and empty iron pots; it was the absolute hollowing of the body. 

“Now, it’s not so here,” Nicholas continued, his voice swelling. “We known when we’re put upon; and we’en too much blood in us to stand it. We just take our hands fro’ our looms, and say, ‘Yo’ may clem us, but yo’ll not put upon us, my masters!’ And be danged to ’em, they shan’t this time!”

“I wished I lived down South,” Bessy repeated, longing for a pastoral myth.

“There’s a deal to bear there,” Margaret cautioned. “There is very hard bodily labour to be gone through, with very little food to give strength.”

“But it’s out of doors,” Bessy pleaded. “And away from the endless, endless noise, and sickening heat.” The relentless mechanical thrum of the factory had been an acoustic assault that had eroded her very cells, stripping away the natural silence required for her lungs to breathe and her soul to rest. 

“It’s sometimes in heavy rain, and bitter cold,” Margaret replied softly, picturing the damp earth and the old agrarian elders bent beneath gray skies. “An old man gets racked with rheumatism, withered before his time; yet he must just work on, or go to the workhouse.”

“And yo say they never strike down there?” Nicholas asked abruptly.

“No, I think they have too much sense.”

“An’ I think,” he replied, dashing the ashes out of his pipe with such fierce vehemence that the clay bowl snapped against the hearth, “it’s not that they’ve too much sense, but that they’ve too little spirit.”

“Oh, father!” Bessy cried out. “What have ye gained by striking? Think of that first strike when mother died—how we all had to clem—you the worst of all. And yet many went in every week at the same wage, till all were gone in that there was work for; and some went beggars all their lives at after.”

“Ay,” he muttered, looking at the broken clay. “That there strike was badly managed. Folk got into th’ management of it, as were either fools or not true men. Yo’ll see, it’ll be different this time.”

“But what are you striking for?” Margaret asked, trying to pierce through the dialect to the core of the conflict.

“Why, yo see, there’s five or six masters who have set themselves again paying the wages they’ve been paying these two years past, flourishing upon, and getting richer upon. And now they come to us, and say we’re to take less. And we won’t. We’ll just clem them to death first; and see who’ll work for ’em then. They’ll have killed the goose that laid ’em the golden eggs.”

“And so you plan dying, to be revenged upon them!”

“No, I dunnot. I just look forward to the chance of dying at my post sooner than yield. That’s what folk call fine and honourable in a soldier, and why not in a poor weaver-chap? Dun yo think it’s for mysel’ I’m striking? I take up John Boucher’s cause, as lives next door but one, wi’ a sickly wife, and eight childer... I take up th’ cause o’ justice. Why are we to have less wage now than two year ago?”

“Ask some of your masters,” Margaret suggested, her belief in an inherent, paternal logic blinding her to the industrial divide. “Surely they will give you a reason for it.”

“Yo’re just a foreigner, and nothing more,” he said contemptuously, recognizing the untranslatability of their subjective experiences. “Much yo know about it. Ask th’ masters! They’d tell us to mind our own business, and they’d mind theirs. Our business being to take the bated wage, and be thankful; and their business to bate us down to clemming point, to swell their profits.”

“But the state of trade may be such as not to enable them to give you the same remuneration,” Margaret argued, using the very vocabulary the masters possessed.

Nicholas sneered. “State o’ trade! That’s just a piece o’ masters’ humbug. It’s rate o’ wages I was talking of. Th’ masters keep th’ state o’ trade in their own hands; and just walk it forward like a black bug-a-boo, to frighten naughty children. We help to make their profits, and we ought to help spend ’em. We’re resolved to stand and fall together; not a man on us will go in for less wage than th’ Union says is our due. So I say, ‘hooray for the strike,’ and let Thornton, and Slickson, and Hamper, and their set look to it!”

“Mr. Thornton of Marlborough Street?” Margaret asked. “What sort of a master is he?”

“Did yo’ ever see a bulldog? Set a bulldog on hind legs, and dress him up in coat and breeches, and yo’n just getten John Thornton.”

“Nay,” Margaret laughed. “Mr. Thornton is plain enough, but he’s not like a bulldog, with its short broad nose, and snarling upper lip.”

“Not in look, I grant yo. But let John Thornton get hold on a notion, and he’ll stick to it like a bulldog; yo might pull him away wi’ a pitchfork ere he’d leave go. He’s worth fighting wi’. As for Slickson, he’ll wheedle his men back wi’ fair promises... He’s as slippery as an eel. He’s like a cat,—as sleek, and cunning, and fierce. It’ll never be an honest up and down fight wi’ him. But Thornton’s as dour as a door-nail; an obstinate chap, every inch on him,—th’ oud bulldog!”

Bessy let out a long, ragged sigh, the sound catching in her chest. 

“Poor Bessy!” Margaret turned to her. “You don’t like struggling and fighting as your father does, do you?”

“No! I’m sick on it. I could have wished to have had other talk about me in my latter days, than just the clashing and clanging and clattering that has ever wearied a’ my life long, about work and wages, and masters, and hands, and knobsticks.”

“Tobacco-smoke chokes me!” Bessy added querulously, her sensory threshold shattered.

“Then I’ll never smoke no more i’ the house!” Nicholas replied tenderly. “But why didst thou not tell me afore, thou foolish wench?” 

He rose and went out of doors, evidently to finish his pipe in the soot-laden street. 

Bessy spoke passionately the moment the door clicked shut. “Now am not I a fool? I knew I ought for to keep father at home, away fro’ the folk that are always ready to tempt a man to go drink. My tongue must needs quarrel with his pipe—and he’ll go off, to where the air smells of cheap gin, and nobody knows where it’ll end. I wish I’d letten myself be choked first.”

“But does your father drink?”

“Not to say drink,” she replied, her voice wild and erratic. “But what win ye have? There are days wi’ you as wi’ other folk, when yo’ get up and go through th’ hours, just longing for a bit of a change—a bit of a fillip. I know I ha’ gone and bought a four-pounder out o’ another baker’s shop... just because I sickened at the thought of going on for ever wi’ the same sight, the same sound in my ears, and the same taste i’ my mouth.” 

Margaret understood this intimately; the sheer human desperation for a different crumb structure, a variance in the yeast, a crust baked upon a different hearth. Food was not merely utilitarian fuel; it was a psychological anchor. To be deprived of sensory variance was its own slow starvation.

“Father—all men—have it stronger in ’em,” Bessy pleaded. “It’s little blame to them if they go into th’ gin-shop for to make their blood flow quicker... and see pictures, and looking-glass. At times o’ strike there’s much to knock a man down... Bless yo’r sweet pitiful face! but yo’ dunnot know what a strike is yet.”

“Come, Bessy,” Margaret said gently, “perhaps you’re only looking on one side.”

“It’s all well enough for yo’ to say so, who have lived in pleasant green places all your life long, and never known want or care.”

“Take care,” Margaret said, her cheek flushing with sudden heat. The untranslatability of her own profound grief flared within her. “I shall go home to my mother, who is so ill that there’s no outlet but death for her out of the prison of her suffering. Her palate has failed; there is no broth, no slow-cured meat, no sustenance on this earth that can coax the warmth back into her blood. And I must speak cheerfully to my father, who does not know. The only person who could comfort her—my brother—is falsely accused and would run the risk of death if he came. Have I not care? Do I not know anxiety, though I go about well-dressed, and have food enough? God is just, and our lots are well portioned out by Him, although none but He knows the bitterness of our souls.”

“I ask your pardon,” Bessy replied, humbled by the raw, unspoken depth of Margaret’s domestic sorrow. “Sometimes, when I’ve thought o’ my life... I’ve believed that, maybe, I was one of those doomed to die by the falling of a star from heaven; ‘And the name of the star is called Wormwood; and the third part of the waters became wormwood; and men died of the waters, because they were made bitter.’ One can bear pain better if one thinks it has been prophesied.”

“God does not willingly inflict,” Margaret urged, disturbed by how Bessy wove mythic, apocalyptic bitterness into the everyday ritual of drinking from a tin cup. “Don’t dwell so much on the prophecies, but read the clearer parts of the Bible.”

“I dare say it would be wiser; but where would I hear such grand words of promise? Many’s the time I’ve repeated the verses in the seventh chapter to myself, just for the sound. It’s as good as an organ.” For Bessy, the acoustic majesty of the ancient lexicon provided an auditory sanctuary, a physical vibration of comfort that the barren, metallic town denied her. “No, I cannot give up Revelations.”

“Let me come and read you some of my favourite chapters,” Margaret offered.

“Ay,” Bessy said greedily. “Come. Father will maybe hear yo’. He’s deaved wi’ my talking; he says it’s all nought to do with the things o’ to-day, and that’s his business.”

Margaret stood, smoothing her skirts. “I must go. You have done me good, Bessy. I came here very sad, apt to think my own cause for grief was the only one in the world. Hearing what you have borne makes me stronger.”

“I done you good? I thought a’ the good-doing was on the side of gentlefolk. You’re not like no one I ever seed. I dunno what to make of yo’.”

“Nor I of myself. Good-bye!”

Bessy stilled the rhythmic creaking of her rocking chair to gaze after her. Margaret left behind a faint, lingering olfactory memory—a scent of crushed lavender and sun-warmed earth that starkly defied the room's dampness. 

“I wonder if there are many folk like her down South,” Bessy whispered to the empty hearth. “She’s like a breath of country air. Who’d a thought that face could have known the sorrow she speaks on? I wonder how she’ll sin. All on us must sin. I think a deal on her, for sure. But father does the like, I see. And Mary even. It’s not often hoo’s stirred up to notice much.”
