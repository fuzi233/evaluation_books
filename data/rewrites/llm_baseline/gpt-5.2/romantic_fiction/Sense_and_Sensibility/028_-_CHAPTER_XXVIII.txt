For three or four days after Elinor had, with a reluctant candour, laid her fears before her mother, nothing occurred to make her repent it; for Willoughby neither came nor wrote. The hours went by with a sameness almost cruel in its quiet—breakfast things cleared away, the dull thud of carriage-wheels below in Berkeley Street, the chink of the tea-spoon against china at evening—each domestic sound marking time, each one asking, in its small unanswerable way, why a voice so lately expected at the door had fallen out of the household’s daily music.

At the end of that interval they were engaged to attend Lady Middleton to a party. Mrs. Jennings was detained at home by the indisposition of her youngest daughter; and Marianne, so far from being roused by the thought of company, prepared with a listlessness that pained Elinor more than any tears could have done. She was not, as she once had been, alive to the smallest distinctions of dress, the soft persuasion of colour against her cheek, the agreeable oppression of a well-warmed room after a winter walk. She seemed careless whether she went or stayed; and after tea she sat by the drawing-room fire without once shifting her posture, as if the crackle of coals were the only language she could bear, and even that she scarcely heard.

When Lady Middleton’s carriage was announced, Marianne started—startled not like one who has been waiting, but like one who has forgotten that the world still expects anything of her. They went down, took their places, and were carried through the thickened streets where every sound was multiplied—hoofs striking stone, harness-jingle, a distant coachman’s call—till they reached their destination. There was the usual ceremony of arrival: the slow patience in the line of carriages, the press of bodies at the foot of the stairs, names called out from landing to landing in voices sharpened by repetition, and then the opening into a room splendidly lit, crowded, and insufferably hot—heat that had the stale, sweet breath of too many perfumes and too many candles, as if the air itself had been over-seasoned and left too long to stand.

They paid their tribute of politeness to the lady of the house and were then permitted, like every other guest, to take their share of inconvenience. After an interval of saying little and doing less, Lady Middleton sat down to Cassino; and as Marianne was not in spirits to move about, the sisters were fortunate enough to secure two chairs not far from the card-table. Elinor, who could not help listening to the room as one listens to a kitchen where something has gone wrong—straining for the clatter that tells of a dropped dish, the hiss that warns of milk boiling over—was surveying faces, tones, movements, all those minute signals by which society advertises its secrets, when she perceived Willoughby.

He stood within a few yards of them, in earnest conversation with a very fashionable-looking young woman. The sight came upon Elinor with that odd, immediate shock of recognition that is almost bodily: as if the room’s murmur changed key. She soon caught his eye. He bowed at once—but he did not attempt to speak to her, nor to approach Marianne, though he could not but see her. He continued his discourse with the same lady, as if nothing in his world had altered by the presence of those two sisters.

Elinor turned involuntarily to Marianne. At the moment Marianne first perceived him; and her whole countenance, so lately fixed and dim, kindled with a sudden delight so vivid that it seemed to put colour back into the air about her. She would have moved towards him instantly had not Elinor caught hold of her hand.

“Good heavens!” Marianne cried. “He is there—he is there—Oh! why does he not look at me? why cannot I speak to him?”

“Pray—pray be composed,” Elinor whispered, bending towards her with urgent tenderness. “Do not betray what you feel to everybody present. Perhaps he has not observed you yet.”

Yet Elinor could not persuade herself of it. He had observed; he had chosen. And to be composed was not only beyond Marianne’s reach—it was beyond her wish. She sat in an agony of impatience that altered every feature, as if the pain had its own expression and insisted on being seen. At last Willoughby turned again and regarded them both. Marianne started up, and pronouncing his name with the warmth of old certainty, held out her hand.

He approached; but he addressed himself rather to Elinor than to Marianne, as if he wished to avoid Marianne’s eye and was determined not to notice the attitude of trust in which she stood. In a hurried manner he inquired after Mrs. Dashwood, and asked how long they had been in town.

Elinor, struck into helplessness by such an address, could not at first command a word. Marianne’s feelings, however, found instant utterance. Her face crimsoned; her voice, when she spoke, was all emotion.

“Good God! Willoughby, what is the meaning of this? Have you not received my letters? Will you not shake hands with me?”

He could not then avoid it; but her touch seemed painful to him, and he held her hand only for a moment. During all this time he was evidently struggling for composure, as a man does who has been forced to swallow something bitter and must still converse as if the taste were not in his mouth. Elinor watched his countenance and saw its expression becoming more tranquil—as if, having resolved on cruelty, he found steadiness in the resolution.

After a moment’s pause he spoke with calmness. “I did myself the honour of calling in Berkeley Street last Tuesday, and very much regretted that I was not fortunate enough to find yourselves and Mrs. Jennings at home. My card was not lost, I hope.”

“But have you not received my notes?” Marianne cried, in the wildest anxiety. “Here is some mistake, I am sure—some dreadful mistake. What can be the meaning of it? Tell me, Willoughby—for heaven’s sake tell me—what is the matter?”

He made no reply. His complexion changed; all his embarrassment returned. But, catching the eye of the young lady with whom he had been previously talking, he seemed to feel the necessity of instant exertion; he recovered himself again, and after saying—forced and distinct—“Yes, I had the pleasure of receiving the information of your arrival in town, which you were so good as to send me,” he turned hastily away with a slight bow and joined his friend.

The words fell like cold drops down Marianne’s spine. She looked dreadfully white, and, unable to stand, sank into her chair. Elinor, expecting every moment to see her faint, tried to screen her from observation—bending closer, shifting her own position—while she revived her with lavender water. The sharp, clean scent rose between them like a frail barrier; it cut through the room’s stale sweetness for an instant, a small domestic remedy doing what it could against a public wound.

“Go to him, Elinor,” Marianne cried as soon as she could speak. “Force him to come to me. Tell him I must see him again—must speak to him instantly. I cannot rest—I shall not have a moment’s peace till this is explained—some dreadful misapprehension or other. Oh, go to him this moment.”

“How can that be done?” Elinor answered, keeping her voice low with the desperation of prudence. “No, my dearest Marianne—you must wait. This is not the place for explanations. Wait only till tomorrow.”

With difficulty could she prevent Marianne from following him herself. To persuade her to check her agitation, to wait—at least with the appearance of composure—till she might speak to him with more privacy and more effect, was impossible; for Marianne continued, in a low voice, to give way to the misery of her feelings by exclamations of wretchedness that were the more dreadful for being half-stifled, like sobs smothered in a pillow.

In a short time Elinor saw Willoughby quit the room by the door towards the staircase. She told Marianne that he was gone and urged the impossibility of speaking to him again that evening as a fresh argument for her to be calm. Marianne, as if the last thread of endurance had snapped, begged Elinor to entreat Lady Middleton to take them home; she was too miserable, she said, to stay a minute longer.

Lady Middleton, though in the middle of a rubber, on being informed that Marianne was unwell, was too polite to object. She made over her cards to a friend; and they departed as soon as the carriage could be found.

Scarcely a word was spoken during their return to Berkeley Street. The city’s night-noises came in at the windows—wheels over ruts, a shout carried and lost, the restless metallic ring of London—yet to Elinor it seemed as if the world were loudly alive while her sister was sinking into a silence too heavy for tears. Marianne sat in a mute agony, oppressed beyond weeping.

Mrs. Jennings was luckily not come home; they could go directly to their own room. There, away from the contaminated gaiety of candles and cards, hartshorn restored Marianne a little to herself. She was soon undressed and in bed; and as she seemed desirous of being alone, Elinor left her.

Waiting for Mrs. Jennings’s return, Elinor had leisure enough for thinking over the past. That some kind of engagement had subsisted between Willoughby and Marianne she could not doubt; and that Willoughby was weary of it seemed equally clear. Marianne might still feed her own wishes—might still insist on mistake, on misapprehension—but Elinor could not. Nothing but a thorough change of sentiment could account for such behaviour.

Her indignation would have been stronger than it was had she not witnessed his embarrassment—that struggle which seemed to confess a consciousness of misconduct, and prevented her from believing him so wholly unprincipled as to have been sporting with Marianne’s affections from the first without any design that could bear investigation. Absence might have weakened his regard; convenience might have determined him to overcome it. But that such a regard had once existed, she could not bring herself to doubt.

As for Marianne—on the pangs which so unhappy a meeting must already have given her, and on those still more severe which might await her in its probable consequence—Elinor could not reflect without the deepest concern. Her own situation gained in the comparison; for while she could esteem Edward as much as ever, however they might be divided in future, her mind might still be supported. But every circumstance that could embitter evil seemed uniting to heighten Marianne’s misery: a final separation from Willoughby—an immediate and irreconcilable rupture with him.
