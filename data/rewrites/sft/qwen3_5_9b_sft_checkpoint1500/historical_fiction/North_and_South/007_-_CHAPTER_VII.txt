"Mist clings to the sunlight,
Smoked-out cottages
Encircle me on every side."

The following afternoon, approximately twenty miles north of Milton, the carriage turned onto a minor railway line leading toward Heston. Heston itself presented as a single, elongating strip of buildings stretching parallel to the coast, possessing a distinct character unlike the languid resorts of southern England or the polished promenades of the Continent. To use a Scottish idiom, the town possessed a "purposeful" quality. The country carts were heavier, burdened with iron rather than wood and leather; the people in the streets, though ostensibly on a pleasure trip, carried a distinctly industrious mindset. The colors of the surroundings appeared grayer, more durable, lacking the frivolous gaiety found elsewhere. Notably, there were no smock-frocks among the rural folk; the garments impeded movement and snagged easily on machinery, leading to their abandonment. In southern villages, shopmen often lounged at their doors when not occupied, soaking in the fresh air and surveying the street. In Heston, however, if a shopkeeper had a moment's leisure from a customer, they immediately resumed business within the shop—often, as Margaret suspected unnecessarily, by unrolling and re-rolling ribbons. These distinctions struck her sharply as she and her mother ventured forth the next morning in search of lodging.

Their two nights spent in hotels had exceeded Mr. Hale's financial projections, and they were relieved to secure the first clean, cheerful rooms available. For the first time in many days, Margaret felt a profound sense of rest. This repose carried a dreamlike quality, rendering it all the more perfect and luxurious. From the windows, the distant sea lapped against the sandy shore with a measured rhythm, while nearby, the cries of donkey boys drifted through the air. Unusual scenes unfolded before her like paintings, which she, in her lethargic contentment, felt no urge to scrutinize thoroughly before they faded from view. A stroll down to the beach allowed her to inhale the sea air, soft and warm despite the late November chill; the vast, misty sea-line touched the tender hues of the sky, and the white sail of a distant vessel turned silver in a pale sunbeam. It seemed as though she could lose herself in such a luxury of pensiveness, making the present absolute, refusing to dwell on the past or speculate on the future.

Yet, the future demanded confrontation, however stern or iron-clad its nature. An arrangement was made for the following evening for Margaret and her father to travel to Milton-Northern to locate a dwelling. Mr. Hale had received correspondence from both Mr. Bell and Mr. Thornton and was eager to ascertain critical details regarding his position and prospects there, matters he could only resolve through an interview with the latter gentleman. Margaret understood the necessity of moving, yet she harbored a visceral aversion to a manufacturing town and believed her mother was benefiting from the Heston air. Consequently, she would have willingly postponed the journey.

For several miles prior to reaching Milton, they observed a deep lead-colored cloud hovering over the horizon. It appeared darker due to the stark contrast with the pale gray-blue of the wintry sky; frost had already signaled its arrival in Heston. Closer to the town, the air held a faint, acrid tang of smoke—a loss of natural fragrance rather than a potent odor. They were whisked rapidly along long, straight, seemingly endless streets of uniformly built brick houses. Here and there, a massive, oblong factory with countless windows rose like a hen amidst her chicks, belching thick, "unparliamentary" smoke and serving as the source of the ominous cloud Margaret had mistaken for a sign of rain. As they traversed the broader avenues from the station to the hotel, the carriage halted frequently, blocked by heavily laden waggons. While Margaret had occasionally accompanied her aunt into cities, those heavy, lumbering vehicles there served varied purposes. Here, every wagon and truck transported cotton, whether in raw burlap sacks or woven calico bales. Pedestrians crowded the sidewalks, mostly well-dressed in terms of material, yet exhibiting a slovenly looseness that struck Margaret as distinct from the threadbare, anxious smartness of a similar class in London.

"This is New Street," Mr. Hale remarked. "I believe it is the principal artery of Milton. Mr. Bell often speaks of how the opening of this street from a lane into a major thoroughfare thirty years ago caused his property values to soar. Mr. Thornton's mill must be nearby, as he rents from Mr. Bell, but I suspect he operates from his warehouse."

"Our hotel, Father?" Margaret asked.

"I believe it is near the end of this street. Shall we dine before or after viewing the houses listed in the *Milton Times*?"

"Oh, let us complete our business first."

"As you wish. First, I shall check if there is any note or letter from Mr. Thornton, who promised to inform me of anything concerning these houses, and then we shall depart. We will retain the cab; it is safer than becoming lost and missing the afternoon train."

No letters awaited him. They proceeded to hunt for a residence. Thirty pounds per annum was the limit they could afford; in Hampshire, they might have secured a spacious home with a pleasant garden for this sum. Here, even basic accommodations consisting of two sitting-rooms and four bedrooms seemed elusive. They viewed numerous properties, rejecting each as they inspected them, until they exchanged glances of dismay.

"We must revert to the second option, I suppose. That one in Crampton, isn't it? It had three sitting-rooms. Do you recall how we laughed at the disparity between the three sitting-rooms and the three bedrooms? Yet, I have devised a plan. The ground-floor front room will serve as your study and our dining room—"though poor Papa!"—since you know Mother deserves the cheeriest sitting-room possible. The front room upstairs, despite its atrocious blue-and-pink wallpaper and heavy cornice, offers a genuinely lovely view over the plain, with a great bend of a river or canal below. You may occupy the small bedroom behind it, in the projection above the kitchen, and you and Mother can share the room behind the drawing room, while that closet in the roof will make a splendid dressing area for you."

"But what of Dixon, and the maid we intend to hire?"

"Oh, wait. I am overwhelmed by the brilliance of my managerial genius. Dixon shall have the rear sitting room. I am sure she will appreciate it; she grumbled so about the stairs at Heston. The maid will take the sloping attic above your and Mother's chambers. Will not that suffice?"

"It may do. However, the papers. What terrible taste! And the excessive coloring and heavy cornices!"

"Do not worry, Father! Surely you can persuade the landlord to re-paper one or two rooms—the drawing room and yours, since Mother will frequent them most—and your bookshelves will conceal much of that garish pattern in the dining room."

"You truly believe it is the best choice? If so, I shall proceed immediately to visit this Mr. Donkin, mentioned in the advertisement. I will return you to the hotel where you may order lunch and rest; by the time the meal is ready, I shall be back. I trust I shall succeed in obtaining new wallpapers."

Margaret shared his hope, though she remained silent. She had never encountered a taste that favored ornament, however flawed, to the extent that it surpassed the plainness and simplicity which constitute the true framework of elegance.

Her father escorted her through the hotel entrance, leaving her at the foot of the staircase before departing to the address of the landlord. As Margaret placed her hand upon the door of their prospective sitting-room, she was overtaken by a brisk-paced waiter.

"I beg your pardon, madam. The gentleman departed so swiftly that I lacked time to notify him. Mr. Thornton arrived almost immediately after you left. Understanding, from what the gentleman said, that you would return within an hour, I informed him of this. He returned about five minutes ago and requested to await Mr. Hale. He is currently in your room, madam."

"Thank you. My father returns shortly, and then you may convey the message."

Margaret entered the room, projecting a straight, fearless, and dignified demeanor habitual to her. She felt no unease; she possessed too many social habits for such discomfort. This was a visitor engaged in business with her father, who had previously demonstrated obliging qualities, and thus she intended to accord him a full measure of civility. Mr. Thornton, conversely, was considerably more surprised and discomposed. Anticipating a quiet, middle-aged clergyman, a young lady emerged with frank dignity—a woman of a strikingly different type from those he usually encountered. Her attire was austere: a close straw bonnet of superior material and shape, trimmed with white ribbon; a large Indian shawl draped about her in long, heavy folds, worn with the gravity of an empress's drapery. He failed to recognize her identity, caught by her simple, direct, unashamed gaze, which indicated that his presence was of no consequence to her beautiful countenance and elicited no flush of surprise from her pale, ivory skin. Though he had heard rumors of Mr. Hale's daughter, he had presumed her to be a child.

"Mr. Thornton, I presume!" Margaret said after a half-second's pause, during which his tongue seemed unable to formulate words. "Will you sit down? My father brought me to the threshold not a minute ago, but unfortunately, he was unaware of your presence and has departed on urgent business.
