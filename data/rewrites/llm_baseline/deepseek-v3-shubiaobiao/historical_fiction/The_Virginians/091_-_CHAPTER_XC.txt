What use was a colonel without a regiment? The Governor and Council, who had so grandly bestowed my commission, had since fled to their ships, engaging in occasional raids along the coast. Lord Dunmore’s alliance with armed freedmen had terrified more than it emboldened; worse still, rumors swirled that he had invited Southern tribes to descend upon us with tomahawk and fire. "And these are our allies?" I said to my mother, exchanging grim glances. The memory of that savage on Braddock’s Field—his knife at my throat—flashed before me.

We fortified Castlewood as best we could, though our true fear lay not in rebel forces but in the specter of frontier violence. My mother, ever steadfast, faltered only once—when shadows moved beyond our gates at night. Convinced they were Indians, she sank to her knees, whispering, "Lord have mercy! The savages—the savages!"

Dunmore’s black recruits scattered to plunder or the sea, but the promised tribal warriors never came. Yet the dread lingered. I watched my mother’s silver hair, my wife’s dark curls, our child’s golden locks, and shuddered at what war might bring. And it was we who had called such horrors upon ourselves! Providence, though inscrutable, seemed to mete justice: the mercenaries we hired faltered in battle, emboldening the rebels, while the murder of a single woman by drunken Indians turned more hearts against the Crown than any lost engagement.

Once the panic faded, my mother’s resolve returned—and with it, insistence that I leave. "What harm can come to an old woman?" she argued. "If rebels come, I’ll hide behind Fanny’s skirts. But you—you must take your family north, to safety." Her words were part sense, part love; she would not abandon Virginia, the land of her father’s grave, though she had already bidden farewell to the new generation.

Before we could depart for Boston, news arrived: the British had quit the city entirely. For months, Washington’s forces lay vulnerable at Cambridge—yet Howe hesitated until Dorchester Heights loomed over the harbor, forcing a humiliating retreat. Victory at Bunker’s Hill was undeniable, yet timidity plagued our commanders—Long Island, Valley Forge—each a missed chance to crush a faltering foe.

Loyalists now treaded carefully. Hagan, our steadfast chaplain, found himself barred from his own pulpit by rebel sentinels. Dragged before Patrick Henry, he sputtered defiance—only to be dismissed with a jest about Shakespearean kings. The new regime flattered Virginians with high offices, peeling away moderates who might have stood firm. Yet in secret, loyal hearts still whispered, passing messages through servants, biding time.

When word came of a southern expedition, I saw my chance. Under guise of visits, my wife, child, and I slipped southward, joining ex-Governor Martin’s forces in Wilmington—only to meet Admiral Parker’s fleet retreating from Charleston. Clinton, grim-faced at my "regiment" of women and infants, relented when Captain Horner of the *Sphinx* recognized my wife as Lambert’s daughter. So we became refugees, like Dunmore, folding our colors quietly.

The assault on Sullivan’s Fort was a disaster. Clinton led bravely, waist-deep in futile charges, while Lee’s defenders rained fire. A well-aimed shot shattered a nearby boat, and I—foolishly—cried, "Bravo!" Clinton’s glare was icy. "Pleased, are you?" "Pleased that my countrymen fight well," I retorted. We retreated, battered.

Yet in defeat, Clinton’s courage shone. Whether at Breed’s Hill or here, he led from the front. As we limped north, news came: the Declaration had been signed. Virginia proclaimed all men free—Gumbo included—and denounced hereditary rule. I laughed bitterly. How would Madam Esmond receive such heresy?

By August, we faced Washington at New York. The battle was inevitable—and cruel, for Harry’s regiment stood among the rebels. The Continentals, trapped after their rout, should have been destroyed. Yet Howe let them slip away in darkness, unopposed. A German picket ignored warnings; by dawn, the enemy had vanished.

My outrage was no secret. "Amateur!" Howe scoffed when confronted. Yet many murmured as I did. His amiability spared him blame, but history would not be so kind. The empire slipped through his fingers—while men like Washington, enduring hunger and treachery, forged a nation from the wreck.
