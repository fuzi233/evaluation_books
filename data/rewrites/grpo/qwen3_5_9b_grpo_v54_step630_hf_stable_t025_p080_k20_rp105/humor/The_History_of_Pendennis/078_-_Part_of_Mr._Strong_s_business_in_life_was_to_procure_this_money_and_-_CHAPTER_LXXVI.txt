The calendar had turned a month, and the stage of our little country town had been thoroughly scrubbed and repainted. Frederic Lightfoot, formerly a humble *maitre d'hôtel* to the illustrious Sir Francis Clavering, had boldly declared his independence. He now stood as the proprietor of the Clavering Arms, a venture marketed with the grandeur of a royal proclamation. His manifesto promised "ancient comfort" refurbished with modern luxuries: a billiard room for the discerning, cellars stocked with wines chosen "without regard to expense" by a man named C.L., and stabling fit for the Dumplingbeare hounds. The scale of charges, he noted with a wink to the times, had been "regulated for all" to suit the "economical spirit of the present era."

Indeed, the inn buzzed with a lively, almost feverish energy. The gateway gleamed with fresh paint, the coffee-room windows sparkled with Christmas holly, and the magistrates had convened their petty sessions in the very card-room of the old Assembly. The farmers' ordinary was packed, the patrons delighted by Mrs. Lightfoot's exotic Indian curries and Mulligatawny soup, a culinary coup endorsed by Major Stokes, Captain Glanders, and the local gentry. Even the incorporation of the Clavering Institute's reading-room featured a dinner where the hostess's cheer was celebrated by Sir Francis Clavering himself, seated beside the esteemed Rector, Dr. Portman.

Amidst this festive chaos, the local press—the *Chatteris Champion, Clavering Agriculturist, and Baymouth Fisherman*—printed a rumor that smelled faintly of scandal and romance. They whispered that Arthur Pendennis had abandoned his candidacy for the borough, allegedly because Sir Francis Clavering's failing health necessitated a vacancy. The paper speculated that a "young gentleman of colossal fortune," related to the highest aristocracy and soon to wed a lovely lady connected to Clavering Park, would fill the seat. The paper hinted that Lady Clavering and Miss Amory had arrived for the holidays, promising festivities of a "peculiarly interesting nature" at the Park's commencement of the new year.

The reader, armed with this gossip, can piece together the missing links. Lady Rockminster, though initially grumbling at Laura's preference for Pendennis over the mysterious Bluebeard, ultimately approved. Knowing Bluebeard's secret, she realized Laura had no other choice. The kind old lady, who had effectively adopted Miss Bell, was delighted that her ward was fulfilling the ultimate destiny of young ladies: marriage. That very night, she informed her maid, Mrs. Beck, who, being kept in the loop by Martha of Fairoaks, was equally thrilled. Mrs. Beck calculated the finances—Pendennis's income plus the railroad dividends versus Miss Bell's dowry—and decided to intervene. She planned to speak to her nephew, George Pynsent, and ensure Pendennis received a promotion, all while arranging a visit to Major Pendennis to smooth the path.

At one o'clock, the Dowager Lady Rockminster arrived at the Major's residence. The Major, expecting news of a broken engagement between Pen and Miss Amory, was instead greeted by a noblewoman. Pen, having just met his new servant, Mr. Frosch, had hurried to the hotel coffee-room to write a note to his guardian. *"Dear uncle,"* he had penned, *"if there has been any question between us, it is over. I found Miss A. has bestowed herself upon Harry Foker, with his fifteen thousand a year. I came upon their loves and left him in possession. Also, Tatham writes he sold three of my fields to the Railroad at a great figure. Yours affectionately, A. P."*

The Major, receiving the news via Lady Rockminster, smiled with courtly grace. He admitted he didn't much regret the affair's conclusion, despite his initial wish for it. Lady Rockminster, playing her part as ambassadress, gently steered the conversation toward consolation. *"We must get him a wife,"* she urged. The Major's mind cleared; he saw the purpose of her visit. The negotiation was surprisingly easy. There was no reason Pen shouldn't marry according to his mother's wishes. Lady Rockminster bolstered the Major's resolve with intimations of weighty consequence—though we shall not detail them, lest we offend the living Countess, who remains a formidable force. Overwhelmed by her determined graciousness and her evident fondness for Laura, the Major capitulated.

When the Major finally met Laura and Pen, shaking hands with "unaffected emotion," Laura blushed with happiness, her eyes beaming with the light of love. The storyteller pauses here to hope that one day, the eyes of his young audience may shine so brightly too.

Meanwhile, the subplot of Harry Foker and Blanche Amory reached its climax. Blanche, having pressed her affections upon the blushing bridegroom with fifteen thousand a year, found herself in a whirlwind of joy. Lady Clavering, buoyed by the news, descended from Tunbridge, and the family erupted in happiness unseen for years. Milliners and jewellers were hired, and Sir Francis, benefiting from his wife's good humor, reconciled with her, returning to London to preside over his own table and gamble with a flush of money.

One evening, dining in Grosvenor Place, Major Pendennis and Arthur encountered Mr. James Morgan, now established as the major-domo. The Chevalier Strong was also present, in high spirits, recounting his amusements abroad. Under his breath, Strong confided to Arthur that Morgan looked "black as thunder" and was "about no good." Strong planned to slip away to wait for them at Hyde Park Gate. As the Major departed, Morgan muttered about accepting a temporary engagement with the Clavering family. Arthur, overhearing a look of hatred, feigned a forgotten handkerchief to retreat to the drawing-room.

There, Blanche was still lingering near her "siren." Pen exchanged a meaningful look with her, and when Blanche emerged to the door, she whispered urgently, *"Oh, Arthur—Mr. Pendennis—I want you to tell dear Laura something!"*

*"What is it?"* she asked, shutting the door.

*"I know it,"* she replied.

*"Morgan will,"* Pen warned.

*"No, he won't,"* Blanche insisted. *"I have promised him—n'importe. Wait until after our marriage."* She suddenly collapsed into despair. *"Oh, how wretched I am,"* she cried, her previous gaiety evaporating.

Arthur implored her to tell Harry immediately. *"Tell him to-night. It is no fault of yours. He will pardon you anything."* Blanche, however, handed him a note from Strong and fled inside, singing and kissing her hand as Morgan ascended the carpeted stairs.

Pen joined his uncle, recounting the drama. The Major, with a shudder, advised them to leave it alone. *"Begad, let us be thankful that we are out of the business,"* he said. *"Miss Amory is a dev'lish wide-awake girl, sir, and must play her own cards."* Strong, attempting to interject, was firmly shut down by the Major, who clung to his nephew's arm, steering clear of the subject entirely.

Outside, Strong laughed as he met them. *"I'm an old soldier, too,"* he said. *"I congratulate you on your marriage and your escape. That rascal Morgan means mischief. He has been lurking about, found out Amory's secret, and bribed the inn porter. He's got into Clavering's service on the strength of his information. He will get very good pay for it."*

Strong explained that Amory was currently in Boulogne, having lost his temper and been thrashed by Strong at Baden for his insolence. *"He broke the bank several nights,"* Strong recounted, *"and was the fable of the place. He lied himself with a fellow named Bloundell, gathering a society of sharpers. I had to thrash him one day within an inch of his life. He begged my pardon afterward, but I couldn't live with him. I pawned everything to pay him what I owed, then went to roulette with my last ten florins. If I had lost, I should have let him shoot me. By Jove, sir, isn't it a shame that a man like me... shouldn't be able to turn his hand to anything?"*

Strong produced a handsome card for *Strong and Company*, sole agents of the celebrated Diamond Manzanilla and Toboso hams. *"Come and taste 'em,"* he urged. Pen laughed, noting he wouldn't be allowed to bachelors' parties soon.

The narrative then shifted to the Clavering Arms, where Harry Foker was in a state of ecstatic intoxication. He treated everyone to drink, dancing around Madame Fribsby, who sat pensively in the bar. Young Lightfoot, now in command of the cellar, was a tippling mess from morning till night, a piteous sight for his fond wife. Morgan, the new butler, watched Lightfoot's vagaries with savage sneers, making Mrs. Lightfoot doubly uncomfortable.

Foker, however, felt invincible. Yet, a shadow fell over him. A letter bearing the Boulogne postmark arrived for Mrs. Lightfoot. When her drunken husband seized it, screaming *"Who's J. A.?",* Foker burst in, shouting, *"Hands off, man alive!"* He snatched the letter, realizing it was for Miss Amory.

*"He's comin'—dammy, who's a-comin'?"* Lightfoot shrieked.

Mrs. Lightfoot, terrified, grabbed her bonnet and ran to the lodge-gate. Foker, reeling, saw a running figure vanish. He stopped, asking if it was Mrs. Bonner, his vision swimming.

Inside the Park, Lady Clavering was in the breakfast room with her son, Frank. *"What's the matter, my dear? What makes you so pale, Harry?"* she asked.

*"Where is Blanche!"* Harry cried in a sickening voice.

Blanche descended, looking pale but eager. Foker handed her the letter. *"I don't know—I should like to know,"* he stammered. *"Has Mrs. Bonner not told you?"*

Lady Clavering took the letter, her limbs shaking. As she read the superscription, she dropped it, clutching her son and bursting into hysterics. *"It's impossible!"* she screamed.

Blanche, with a ghastly smile, claimed the letter was from a poor pensioner. But Foker demanded to see it. *"Read it—and look at her!"* Blanche cried, pointing to her mother.

Foker read the letter aloud:

*"I have not wrote, my darling Betsy, this three weeks; but this is to give her a father's blessing... I don't know whether I quite approve of your throwing over Mr. P. for Mr. F., and don't think Foker's such a pretty name... But he has got the rowdy, which is the thing. So no more, my dear little Betsy, till we meet, from your affectionate father, J. Amory Altamont."*

The revelation shattered the room. Lady Clavering screamed that they had made an outcast of her son. *"They've dishonoured your old mother,"* she sobbed. *"I'm innocent, Frank; before God, I'm innocent."*

Foker, generous as ever, kissed her hand. *"Generous, generous Harry!"* Blanche cried. But Harry withdrew, his lip quivering. *"That's different,"* he said. *"It was for her sake—for her sake, Harry."*

Harry exploded. *"Everything's talked about in London. I knew that your father had come to grief. You don't think it was—it was for your connexion I married you? D—— it all! I've loved you with all my heart and soul for two years, and you've been playing with me, and cheating me!"* He covered his face and sobbed.

Lady Clavering, distraught, offered her fortune and disinherited Frank to save Blanche. *"Take her, take everything I have,"* she begged. *"This boy's disinherited."*

Frank, terrified, cried out. *"You ain't serious, Jemima? You don't mean that?"*

Clavering, the magistrate, roared, *"I'll commit him!"* He revealed the truth: Amory was a "d——d escaped convict scoundrel" who had forged his father-in-law's name, killed a man in Botany Bay, and robbed him years ago. *"And Pendennis knows it, and Strong knows it, and that d——d Morgan knows it, and she knows it, ever so long; and I never would tell it, never: and I kept it from my wife."*

Blanche, horrified, tried to approach her mother, but Lady Clavering shrank away. *"Don't touch me,"* she said. *"You've no heart; you never had. I see why that coward was going to give up his place in Parliament to Arthur... and why you threatened that you would make me give you half Frank's fortune."* She accused Blanche of leaving Arthur for Harry. *"Have nothing to do with her, Harry. You're good, you are. Don't marry that—that convict's daughter."*

Blanche, suddenly feeling exultant, declared she had no heart for her mother's shame. *"I appeal to my country's laws—yes, my country's laws! The persecuted one returns this day. I desire to go to my father."*

As a chaise approached, the women shrieked, expecting Amory. Instead, the door opened to reveal Mr. Morgan announcing Major Pendennis and Arthur Pendennis.
