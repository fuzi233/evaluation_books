Costigan left Pen sleeping soundly, undisturbed by any hostile missives from Mr. Huxter. When Pen finally stirred, it was not with the weary grogginess typical of a Londoner dragged back from dreams by care and consols. Instead, he awoke with a brisk, electric vitality, the kind that usually belongs to a man just beginning a fresh adventure. For a City man, the moment sleep departs is instantly seized by thoughts of 'Change and ledgers; for a lawyer, by looming cases and unkept promises. But Pen, the literary hack, found his mind drifting elsewhere. His labor, unlike the tireless grind of commerce or law, lacked a permanent grip on his soul. The thrill of creation faded quickly, leaving behind the prosaic drudgery of hitting deadlines for the *Pall Mall Gazette*. He was a Pegasus forced into harness, running a stage daily, content enough with a whip or a feed of corn, but rarely finding true joy in the plodding rhythm of the newspaper page.

Society, too, offered him little sustenance. He was, in his own estimation, a man out of step. Too young to command the respect of the older, established gentlemen whose company he admired but from which he was excluded, and too old for the frivolous lads of his own generation who spent their days chasing pleasures. He was destined to walk a solitary path, a fate that many men of taste embrace, though Pen, faithful to his nature, would undoubtedly complain about it later. Yet, for now, he carried his solitude with a surprising equanimity.

"What a lovely, artless little creature!" Pen murmured to himself as the morning light filtered through the window, recalling the Vauxhall affair. "How refreshing her natural manners are compared to the calculated coquetry of the ballroom belles! Who would imagine such a beautiful rose growing in the grim shadow of a porter's lodge, blooming in that dreary pot at Shepherd's Inn? Does she learn to sing from old Bows? If her voice is as sweet as her speech, it must be divine. I adore those low, veiled tones. 'What would you like me to call you?' she asked—poor little Fanny! It tugged at my heartstrings to treat her with the grand air, to bid her call me 'Sir.' But surely, no Faust and Margaret drama for me. That old Bows! Is he teaching her to sing? He's a dear old soul, dressed in those antique garb, a philosopher with a kind heart. He showed me kindness during the Fotheringay business; he, too, knows sorrow. I must cultivate Old Bows. One sees people of all stripes. Besides, the polite world is becoming tiresome, and there's hardly anyone left in town anyway. Yes, I shall visit Bows and Costigan. Costigan is a treasure trove of character! Begad, I'll study him and put him into a book." Thus, our young anthropologist chattered to himself, deciding to take advantage of Saturday's lull in the press. With the *Gazette* waiting for contributions and his brain free from immediate demands, he resolved to pay a call at Shepherd's Inn, primarily to see Old Bows.

In truth, if Arthur Pendennis had ever been a scheming rogue like Lovelace, he could not have devised a more effective strategy for captivating poor Fanny Bolton than the gentle charm he displayed the previous night. His dandified demeanor, his feigned generosity, his good humor, and even the honest hesitation he showed when checking her trembling advances—all worked in concert to win her affection. Her faults and virtues were indistinguishable to him, and that ambiguity only deepened her admiration. If one could peer into Fanny's bed—a cramped space shared with two little sisters where Mr. Costigan administered gingerbread and apples—one would find her tossing restlessly, disturbed by her companions' movements, yet lost in a reverie of that eventful night. Every word, glance, and gesture of Arthur played in her mind like a scene from a novel. She had devoured countless tales of romance and seduction, though the market for cheap, sensational fiction was not yet fully developed. Still, the circulating library kept by Miss Minifer, alongside her school and millinery shop, had fed her imagination. To Fanny, Arthur was the living embodiment of all those heroes in greasy, three-volume books.

Arthur, known for his obsession with fine shirts and haberdashery, had certainly made an impression. Fanny gazed with wonder at the crisp whiteness of his linen, the sparkle of his studs, the elegance of his pocket-handkerchief, and the gleaming polish of his boots. The Prince had arrived, subduing the humble handmaid. His image haunted her restless dreams: the blue light of his eyes, the generous, pity-laden smile, the manly, protective grin, the frank, winning laugh. She still felt his arm around her, saw him filling that delicious glass of champagne. Then, her thoughts drifted to the girls who used to sneer at her—Emma Baker, proud of her engagement to a cheesemonger near Clare Market, and Betsy Rodgers, fussing over her young man, an attorney's clerk who carried a bag.

By two o'clock in the afternoon, the Bolton family had concluded their meal. Mr. B., the porter, was away in the countryside attending to the Countess of Estrich's hearse for Messrs. Tressler, the eminent undertakers of the Strand. Just then, a gentleman in a white hat and white trousers appeared beneath the inn archway, stopping at the porter's wicket. Fanny was not merely surprised; she was delighted, blushing furiously. She knew it was Him. There stood His Royal Highness, beaming upon her from the gate. She called to her mother, who was busy above stairs, "Mamma, mamma!" and rushed to open the wicket, shoving past her siblings. How she blushed as she handed him her hand! How affably he removed his white hat, allowing the children to stare up at him in awe. He asked Mrs. Bolton if she had slept well after last night's fatigue and expressed hope that she suffered no headache. Then, turning to the lodger, he remarked that since he was passing that way, he simply could not leave without asking news of his little partner.

Mrs. Bolton, initially shy and suspicious of these advances, was melted by Pen's inexhaustible good humor. He seemed entirely welcome. Looking around for a seat, finding only dishes covering tables and workboxes cluttering surfaces, he perched himself on one of the children's chairs. An uncomfortable eminence, yes, but it launched a fit of laughter among the children, especially Fanny, who was more amused than terrified. She marveled at His Royal Highness's condescension; actually sitting down in a child's chair! Many times afterward, she would regard that piece of furniture, and perhaps others in her room, filling them with the sweet, smiling faces of those who might never look upon her again.

Pen sat down and talked with great volubility to Mrs. Bolton. He inquired about the undertaking business, how many mourners had descended with Lady Estrich, and the state of the Inn. He expressed keen interest in Mr. Campion's cab and horse, noting he had met the gentleman in society. He speculated on shares in the Polwheedle and Tredyddlum mines and asked if there were chambers to let, remarking that it was preferable to the Temple. He wanted to move into Shepherd's Inn. Naturally, he turned his attention to Captain Strong and Colonel Altamont. The Captain, he noted, was an old friend from home, with whom he had dined in those chambers before the Colonel arrived. Describing the Colonel, Pen became immensely waggish, eliciting hysterical giggles from the ladies. "Wasn't he a stout man?" Pen asked, describing a large quantity of jewellery, a wig, and whiskers so black they were almost "blue black"—a rich greenish-purple hue. "I've met him at Sir F—— in society."

"Oh, we know," the ladies replied. "Sir F—— is Sir Francis Clavering; he's here often, two or three times a week with the Captain. My little boy has gone out fetching bill-stamps for him." Mrs. Bolton, talking herself into good nature, suddenly blurted, "O Lor! I beg pardon, I shouldn't have mentioned no secrets." But she assured Pen that they knew he was a gentleman, for he could behave like one. "Hasn't Mr. Pendennis, Fanny?"

Fanny, loving her mother for this defense, cast up her dark eyes to the low ceiling and confirmed, "Oh, that he has, I'm sure, Ma," her voice thick with emotion.

Pen, curious about the bill-stamps and the transactions in Strong's chambers, asked if Altamont was also out for stamps, who he was, and what circles he frequented. These questions, posed with considerable adroitness by Pen—who was personally invested in Sir Francis Clavering's affairs due to private motives—were answered artlessly by Mrs. Bolton, within the limits of her knowledge, which, to be fair, were not extensive.

Once these inquiries were satisfied, and Pen finding himself at a loss for further topics, he remembered his privileged status as a journalist. He asked the ladies if they would like any theatre orders. The play was their delight, as it is the universal delight of theatrical folk.
