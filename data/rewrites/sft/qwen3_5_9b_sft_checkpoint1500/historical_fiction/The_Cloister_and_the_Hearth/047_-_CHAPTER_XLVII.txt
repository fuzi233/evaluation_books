The following morning, Denys seized an opportunity to reveal the remainder of his tale to Mother and Daughter, carefully shielding Cornelis and Sybrandt from the knowledge. "I cannot stain their names," he explained, his characteristic candor tempered by military discretion. "They are of honorable blood. Yet Gerard views them as enemies in this affair. Since I am Gerard's comrade, and since it is a law among soldiers to deny truth to foes, I am bound to withhold the facts—but I shall speak the lies necessary to preserve honor."

Catherine sighed, offering no rebuttal. The adventures Denys recounted stirred within them a tempest of agitation and sorrow. They wept bitterly at the thought of the friends' separation, a scene Denys could not describe without stumbling. Yet, eventually, their grief coalesced into a radiant hope: the anticipation of Gerard's swift return. Denys shared this confidence, yet gently cautioned them against neglecting his friend's dying wishes. "If Gerard returns next week and finds no Margaret," he mused, "what manner of man shall I prove?"

For the first time, Catherine felt a profound tenderness toward the absent Margaret. Her anxiety mirrored Denys's, yet she refused to agree with his rash plan to scour the entirety of Holland. "She must have confided in someone," she reasoned. "These are not fugitives fleeing debt; they owe naught in Sevenbergen. Dearest Denys, you cannot hunt the breadth of the Netherlands for a single girl."

"Can I not?" Denys asked grimly, his jaw set. "We shall see." After a moment's reflection, he proposed a division of labor: Catherine remained behind, her senses sharpened to catch any whisper, while he would ransack every town if necessity demanded. "She will not stray many leagues," he declared firmly. "Three do not flee as swiftly as one."

"That is sound counsel," Catherine admitted. However, she insisted he visit the Demoiselle Van Eyck first. "She and Margaret were bosom companions. If anyone knows the girl's whereabouts, it is she." Denys, eager to depart instantly, allowed Catherine to guide him. With a dexterous turn of her hand, she smoothed her gown, making herself appear a shade more composed, and escorted him forth.

They were ushered into a sumptuously furnished chamber where the elderly lady sat. Upon hearing their errand, the tapestry slipped from Margaret Van Eyck's hands. "Gone? Departed Sevenbergen without telling me? The ungrateful child!"

This reaction stunned the visitors. "You know nothing? When was she last seen?"

"Perhaps ten days ago," the old lady murmured. "I had taken out my brushes after so many years to paint her portrait. Yet I did not proceed, for reasons."

Catherine noted the strangeness of such a departure—"bag and baggage"—without permission or explanation. "Was ever anything so untoward? Just as our hearts grew warm toward her, here comes Gerard's companion from afar with news of his love, yet she remains missing, and Gerard awaits. What am I to do? Surely she has not fled without cause. Pray, give us the clue, my good demoiselle."

"I possess no clue to offer," the elder lady replied, her tone tinged with petulance.

"Then I shall provide one," interjected Reicht Heynes, stepping into the doorway, her cheeks flushed with sudden animation.

"So you have been listening, have you?"

"What are my ears for, Mistress?" Reicht countered sharply.

"Precisely. Then illuminate this darkness with your wisdom."

"There is no darkness," Reicht declared. "And if the clue is a two-stranded cord, with both ends lying right here in this room, you will not be far astray. Oh, Mistress, I marvel at you pretending ignorance!"

"Marry, draw near," the hostess said, her own complexion flushing nearly as crimson as the maid's. "So it was I who drove the foolish girl away."

"You played your part, Mistress. How did you greet her on her final visit? Did she fail to notice the coldness, especially when she stood friendless? You said, 'I have changed my mind regarding your portrait,' lifting your nose high."

"I did not lift my nose. It is not shaped like yours for gazing heavenward."

"Oh, all noses follow the heart's inclination, regardless of shape. Poor soul! She came to my kitchen. 'I am not to be painted now,' she whispered, tears streaming down. She said no more, yet I understood perfectly. I had seen you."

"Well," admitted Margaret Van Eyck, "I confess as much, and I submit judgment to you, Madam. These young girls can accomplish nothing of their own volition; they merely mimic their sweethearts. Your Gerard is remarkably adept at many trades, including the illuminator's craft. Margaret is his pupil, a patient one. Is there not a natural affinity between a woman's eye for color and a lover to emulate? 'Tis a trick I despise in my heart. By it, the grand art of color—which should be regal, aspiring, and free—degenerates into a servile slave to petty crafts, fettered and imprisoned to match the triviality of books, destined to adorn a rich fool's pocket. Nevertheless, affection governs us all. When the poor wench presented me with thorn-leaves, lilies, ivy, and dewberries, along with butterflies and beetle larvae—all nature's refuse stuck fast in gold leaf like wasps trapped in a honey-pot—and her daily book revealing she had labored hundreds of hours over each minute detail, I was rightfully wrathful. An immortal soul, countless hours of toil, and exquisite manual skill cast away upon nature's trash? Yet, possessing compassion, I restrained myself, damming up my better feelings, and feigned kindness, offering suggestions to improve the work. I told her, 'Seeing as Heaven has doomed us to squander time, soul, and color upon great letters and tiny beetles, omitting saints and heroes, why not present the scum naturally?' I reminded her that grapes hang in the air, not stuck to walls, and that insects do not live miserably wedged in metal prisons but roam freely, infesting the air."

"'Ah, my dear friend,' she replied, 'I see now whither you drive; but this ground is gold; we may not shade it.' 'Who said so?' I asked. 'All teachers of this craft,' she answered—and perhaps to conclude me immediately—'Gerard himself!' 'That for Gerard himself,' I retorted, 'and the entire gang; give me a brush!'"

Consequently, I chose to shade her fruit and reptiles with a hue false to nature but true relative to that monstrous background of glaring gold. Within five minutes, a cluster of raspberries, stalk and all, appeared, almost ready to fly into one's mouth; likewise, a butterfly larva rendered so realistically it might turn the stoutest stomach. My lady threw her arms around my neck, exclaiming, 'Oh!'"

"Did she?"

"The little love!" observed Denys, finally managing to insert a word.

Margaret Van Eyck stared at him, then smiled. She continued, recounting how she had been led step by step to promise resuming the art she had abandoned with a sigh upon her brothers' death—to paint the Madonna anew, using Margaret as a model. Incidentally, she revealed the mechanics of turning girls into saints. "'Thy hair is adorable,'" I told her. "'Why, 'tis red,'" she replied. "'Ay,'" I responded, "but what a red! How brown! How glossy! Most hair is not worth a straw to us painters; thine is the artist's very hue. But thy violet eyes, which currently reflect the earth through languor for lack of Gerard, soon shall blaze with hope; these I shall lift to heaven in fixed, holy meditation. Thy nose, which already aspires upward (though not so piously as Reicht's), I shall modestly lower, and somewhat soften thy chin.'"

"Softening the chin?" Reicht exclaimed. "Alack! What does that signify? You surpass me, Mistress."

"'Tis a resolute chin. Not a jot too firm for this wicked world; but when dealing with a Madonna? No, thank you."

"Well, I never. A resolute chin."

"Denys," Catherine interjected softly. "The darling!"

"And now comes the rub," Margaret Van Eyck continued. "When you informed me of her condition—the way she stands—it gave me a shock; I dropped my brushes. Am I to transform a girl, unable to keep her lover at bay, into the Virgin Mary at my age? I love the poor ninny still. But I adore our Blessed Lady. Say, 'must not a painter be peevish in such matters'? Well, most painters are men, and men are fine fellows. They can do anything. Their saints and virgins are no more nor less than their mistresses, save for the sanctification. Yet, for this very reason, half their craft escapes me; I discover beneath their angels' white wings the very trollops I have seen flaunting themselves on the streets, bedizened like pagan idols, worn like queens in a deck of cards. And I am not a fine fellow, but merely a woman. My painting is but half craft; the other half is devotion. Thus, you may read me. 'Twas foolish, perhaps, but I could not help it; yet I am sorrowful."
