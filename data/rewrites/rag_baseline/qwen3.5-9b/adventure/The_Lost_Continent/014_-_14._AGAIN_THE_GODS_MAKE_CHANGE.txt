The battle was not a victory of skill, but of arithmetic. With a handful of disciplined men against a horde fifteen times our number, we did not win by force; we won by exhaustion. The rebels, lacking training, leadership, and coherent purpose, were merely a collection of individuals whose shared delusion had momentarily unified them. When the mine failed and no reinforcements emerged from the breach, the cohesion shattered. Panic, a contagious disease, swept through their ranks faster than any blade. Without commanders to rally the fleeing or orders to hold the line, the mob dissolved into a chaotic stream running toward the forests and the scorched fields.

We did not fight defensively. We hunted. Turning one flank, charging the center, harrying the rear, we kept them in perpetual motion, denying them the stability needed to organize resistance. By the time the outer lines scrambled for cover and the inner packs followed suit, the rebellion was already dead. These were not cowards; they were Atlanteans, hardened by oppression and fueled by desperation. In an open field, their sheer ferocity would have crushed any conventional army. But scattered, they were harmless. The siege lifted. The city held, provided we suppressed the remaining malcontents with measured severity, avoiding the cruelty that often breeds further despair.

Chasing the fugitives had taken us miles from the walls, deep into the heat of the day. My men were spent. We halted among half-ruined houses, using the debris for makeshift shelter. A drove of frightened livestock, panicked by the rebels' flight, stumbled into our perimeter. Five fell to our blades, providing a soldier's supper. Fires crackled; wounds were dressed; sentries paced the perimeter, calling out to one another in low, rhythmic tones. Officers discussed the mechanics of the charges, reliving the violence with a detached enthusiasm that laymen could never understand. Sleep eventually claimed them, save for me, who feared the silence of my own thoughts. I pressed the conversation until the others, weary of my company, surrendered to unconsciousness.

Then, a sentry approached. "My lord," he reported, voice tight. "A woman from the city. Caught attempting entry."

"Her name?"

"She refuses to speak."

"Does she have business?"

"No answer. She demands only to see you."

"Bring her to the fire." I paused, reconsidering. If she sought me, it was either for news or a threat. Better to intercept privately. "Wait."

I rose and followed the sentry to the outer line. "Show me."

Two guards held her. She was wrapped in heavy cloth, a hood obscuring her face. Yet, to anyone who knew Nais, the silhouette beneath the layers was unmistakable. It was Ylga.

"Release her," I ordered. "Take her away from the camp."

Once beyond the reach of the sentries' ears, I waited. "What brings you here, Ylga?"

"You recognize me, then? Even beneath these rags, Deucalion, you know the shape of my soul." Her tone was bitter. "And yet, your concern is solely for the message, not the messenger."

"I am married to Phorenice no longer."

"And true enough. She has divorced you. Spurned you. After the initial fury, the penalties will follow."

"The Empress does no wrong."

"Oh, drop the fable. It disgusts me. She loves you, and now she hates you. She knows you gave Nais the priest's magic—that sleeping draught—and waits for you to wake her, regardless of the cost. To explain her madness: there is another shaft, adjacent to yours. It leads to a cell occupied by another prisoner. He overheard everything. Desperate for freedom, he sold his secrets. His information was stale; by the time his keeper realized the error, Phorenice had publicly wedded another, and Nais had consumed her poison. The fool paid with his head. Naturally, Phorenice reacted accordingly."

Ylga stepped closer, her voice dropping. "Do not return to Atlantis, Deucalion."

"But I intend to."

"If she cuts off your head, let the High Gods decide."

"You are more resigned to death than I anticipated. But Phorenice's judgment is accurate. I heard her rage. 'Shall I sever his head?' she asked. 'No. Let the torturers have their sport; he would not flinch. I require a punishment that endures. I will remove his hands and feet. He shall be a cripple, cast into the wilds to learn fear. Hunger, foul air, the hunt—he shall know it all, while I keep Nais in her stone coffin, playing with her as I please.' Do you wish to meet that fate?"

"No," I replied. "Mutilation is not part of my plan."

"Good. As a woman of sense, I judge correctly. Furthermore, I possess a kindness left. Do the names of Tob recall anything?"

"Captain of Tatho's navy?"

"That one. Gruff, smelling of tar, yet strangely honest. He sails tonight for unknown shores, presumably to found a kingdom. Lately, he finds little profit in Atlantis, and his family suffers. At the harbor, when I placed my neck under the executioner's blade to secure passage for you, he spoke freely. He said you were the recruit he desired, and that whether you joined him or not, he would serve you in the underworld."

"He taught me to fight the great sea beasts."

"Do it again. It is your only chance. I have negotiated with Tob to slip past the harbor chains before they rise. He awaits you at a designated point on the shore. Come, Deucalion. I will guide you."

"My girl," I said, "I owe you debts I cannot repay."

"Keep your thanks," she dismissed. "I did not walk into danger for gratitude, though I knew little else awaited." She tugged my sleeve. "Walk with me. The camp will demand answers soon, and we need no scrutiny."

We moved away from the bivouac lights, shadows merging with the night. She led; I carried my weapons, prepared for beast or man. Words were scarce, limited to warnings of natural hazards. Once, crossing a rivulet fed by a boiling spring, I hesitated. The water was scalding.

"Sure footing?" I asked.

"No," she admitted, reaching out to grasp my hand. I guided her across, then released her. She sighed, withdrawing her hand slowly. Silence resumed, heavy and enduring, as we walked hour after hour.

Finally, we crested a ridge. Below, through the canopy, the great estuary glimmered—the domain of Atlantis. The terrain was treacherous, soaked and spine-filled. Ylga's breathing grew labored. When I offered my arm, she recoiled sharply. "I am no weakling," she snapped. "If that is your sole motive, withdraw."

Emerging from the trees, the journey's hardest section ended. A mile away, anchored in the shallow surf, lay Tob's ship—a strange silhouette against the stars. Tob watched intently. Challenged at range, he halted us, but his demeanor softened upon recognizing us. He signaled the crew, slackened the anchor rope, and grounded the vessel against the shingle. Oars extended to assist our climb aboard.

Turning to Ylga, I offered thanks. "Never forget this night. Should the High Gods grant me a return to power, you shall feel my gratitude."

"I seek no return. This life is sickness."

"You have your palace, your wealth, your position. Phorenice will not trouble you."

"As for that, I could resume my role as a fan-girl tomorrow. But I do not wish to return."

"It is not the time for a gentle lady to venture forward. I have served as viceroy of Yucatan; I know the cost of colonization. Hardships, privations, suffering—you cannot imagine it. Few survive the beginning, and those who do bear fresh scars daily."

"I do not care. I can share the burden. I can cook, shoot, sew garments from beast skins and sinews. You despise fine clothing, and because you see me only as a spectator, you deem me useless. Bah, Deucalion! You know less of a woman than a schoolboy."

"I have learned all I need of one woman. Due to the memory of her, I could not presume to ask her sister to accompany me."

"Aye," she said, bitterness coloring her voice. "Kick my pride. I knew I was second choice to Nais. Yet no boor would remind me of it. Gods! Half the men of Atlantis courted me, and now I arrive at this!"

"I must go alone. It would have brought me happiness to carry your esteem. Instead, I suppose I shall carry only your hate."

"That is the humiliation," she confessed. "I cannot hate you. I ought to, after your scorn. But I do not. Somehow, I grow fonder. If I thought I could keep you alive and whole in Atlantis, I would not mention Tob's impatience. He will leave without you." Suddenly, she threw her arms around my neck, kissing me fiercely. "That is goodbye, dear. See? I am reckless. I care not what I do, knowing you cannot despise me more than you have for my boldness."

She retreated into the tree line.

"This is folly," I muttered. "I must escort you safely to the city gates before returning to the ship."
