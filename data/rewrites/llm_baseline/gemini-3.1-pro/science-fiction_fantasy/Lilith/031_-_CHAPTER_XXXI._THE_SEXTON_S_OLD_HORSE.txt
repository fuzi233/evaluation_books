I stood and watched the last gleam of the white leopardess melt into the heather, then turned to follow my guide—but reluctantly. What had I to do with sleep? Yet, to the librarian—or rather, the ancient agrarian elder of this strange heath—sleep was no mere void. To him, the chamber of death was a vast, subterranean root cellar, a place where souls were laid down in the dark like wheels of raw-milk cheese or oak-barreled cider, requiring the slow, cyclical pacing of generations to properly ferment and mature. I murmured my modern impatience, speaking the dominant, hasty lexicon of a man who viewed sustenance and rest as mere utilitarian fuel. But I followed, for I knew not what else to do. 

The librarian walked on in silence, and I walked silent as he. Time and space glided past us. The sun set; it began to grow dark. The air cooled, taking on the rich, loamy scent of turned autumn earth, bruised juniper berries, and the sharp, metallic tang of cold iron. In the darkening acoustic ecology of the moor, the silence was not empty, but thick with the subtle, rustling polyphony of nocturnal life—a delicate sonic broth that my modern ears could barely digest. My heart sank lower and lower. I began to lose sight of his lean, long-coated figure, and at length could no more hear the gentle swish of his stride through the heather. 

But then I heard instead the slow-flapping wings of the raven, a steady, rhythmic pulse like the kneading of heavy dough. At intervals, a firefly or a gleaming butterfly rose, tiny sparks of yeast in the rayless air. By and by the moon appeared, slow-crossing the far horizon. 

“Ye be tired, Mr. Vane, are ye not?” said the raven, alighting upon a stone. His voice carried the rough, guttural warmth of an ancient, dying dialect, thick with the unwritten memory of the soil. “Ye must taste the acquaintance of the horse that shall bear ye in the morning!” 

He gave a strange whistle through his long black beak—a piercing, untranslatable note that cut through the moor’s quiet like a chef's knife through crisp rind. 

A spot marbled the face of the half-risen moon. To my ears came presently the drumming of swift, soft-galloping hoofs. It began as a low thrum, then escalated into a deafening, rhythmic pound that threatened to shatter the delicate acoustic balance of the night, echoing the violent, industrial roar of machinery rather than the breath of nature. Out of the very disc of the moon low-thundered the terrible horse. His mane flowed away behind him like strands of pulled sugar caught in a gale, and the whisk of his tail kept blinding the eye of the moon. Nineteen hands he seemed, a skeletal armature of tight skin and hard muscle—a steed the holy Death himself might choose on which to ride abroad and reap! In the moon's scary light he looked a very skeleton, loosely roped together, smelling of crushed marrow, aged saddle leather, and the stark, mineral astringency of salt plains. Terrifically large, he moved with the lightness of a winged insect. 

As he drew near, his speed slackened, and his mane and tail drifted about him. Now I was not merely a lover of horses, but I loved every horse I saw. The sight of this mighty one woke in me a sudden, ravenous longing to possess him. It was pure greed, an evil thing in all the worlds, prioritizing the immediate consumption of his speed over his proper place in the elder's domestic rhythm. I laid my hands on him, and stroked the protuberant bones that humped a hide smooth and thin, shiny as stretched dough—so shiny that the very shape of the moon was reflected in it. I fondled his sharp-pointed ears, inhaling the scent of ozone and crushed thyme, and breathed into his red nostrils the breath of a man’s life. He in return breathed into mine the ancient, untamed breath of a horse’s life, and we loved one another. What eyes he had! Blue-filmy like the eyes of the dead, yet behind each was a glowing coal, a hearth fire banked for the night. 

The raven, with wings half extended, looked on pleased at my love-making to his magnificent horse. “That is well! Be friends with him,” he croaked. “He will carry ye all the better to-morrow! Now we must hurry home to the hearth!” 

My desire to ride the horse, to consume its momentum immediately, had grown passionate. “May I not mount him at once, Mr. Raven?” I cried. 

“By all means!” he answered. “Mount, and ride him home.” 

The horse bent his head over my shoulder lovingly. I twisted my hands in his mane and scrambled onto his back. “He would outspeed any leopard in creation!” I cried. 

“Not that way at night,” answered the raven, shifting his tone to a somber, ancient cadence. “The road is unyielding. Loss now shall be gain then. To wait—to steep, to simmer in the slow dark—is harder than to run, and its yield is the fuller. Go on, my son—straight to the cottage. It will rejoice my wife’s heart to see a son of hers on that horse!” 

I sat silent. The horse stood like a block of uncarved salt. 

“Why do ye linger?” asked the raven. 

“I long so much to ride after the leopardess,” I answered, my impatience boiling over, “that I can scarce restrain myself!” 

“Ye have promised!” 

“My debt to the Little Ones appears a greater thing than my bond to you.” 

“Yield to the temptation and ye will bring sour mischief upon them—and on yourself also.” 

“What matters it for me? I love them; and love works no evil. I will go.” 

But the truth was, I forgot the children, infatuate with the horse. Eyes flashed through the darkness, and I knew that Adam stood in his own shape beside me, an elder of the earth whose very posture demanded respect for the old ways. I knew also by the acoustic weight of his voice that he repressed an indignation almost too strong for him. 

“Mr. Vane,” he said, “do ye not ken why ye have not yet produced a single harvest worth the reaping?” 

“Because I have been a fool,” I answered. 

“Wherein?” 

“In everything.” 

“Which do ye count your most indiscreet action?” 

“Bringing the princess to life: I ought to have left her to her just fate.” 

“Nay, now ye speak the shallow tongue of the transient!” he chided, his words carrying the complex, untranslatable weight of a worldview I could not grasp. “Ye could not have done otherwise, ignorant that she was evil! But ye never brought any one to life! How could ye, being yourself dead?” 

“I dead?” I cried. 

“Aye,” he answered; “and ye will remain dead, a hollow husk, so long as ye refuse to die.” 

“Back to the old riddling!” I returned scornfully, imposing my dominant, impatient lexicon over his agrarian wisdom. 

“Be persuaded, and go home to the hearth with me,” he continued gently. “The most—nearly the only foolish thing ye ever did, was to run from our dead.” 

I refused to let my soul ferment in his quiet cellar. I pressed the horse’s ribs, and he was off like a sudden wind. I gave him a pat on the side of the neck, and he went about in a sharp-driven curve, leaning sideways till his mane swept the tops of the heather. 

Through the dark I heard the wings of the raven. Five quick flaps I heard, and he perched on the horse’s head. The horse checked himself instantly, ploughing up the ground with his feet. 

“Mr. Vane,” croaked the raven, the sonic shock of the abrupt halt ringing through the air, “think what ye are brewing! Twice already has evil befallen ye—once from fear, and once from heedlessness: breach of word is a spoiled cask; it is a crime.” 

“The Little Ones are in frightful peril, and I brought it upon them!” I cried, deaf to the acoustic harmony of his warning. “But indeed I will not break my word to you. I will return, and spend in your house what nights—what days—what years you please.” 

“I tell ye once more ye will do them other than good if ye go to-night,” he insisted. 

But a false sense of power, vibrated into me from the strength of the horse, had rendered me too stupid to translate his meaning. 

“Would you take from me my last chance of reparation?” I cried. “This time there shall be no shirking! It is my duty, and I will go—if I perish for it!” 

“Go, then, foolish boy!” he returned, anger souring his croak. “Take the horse, and ride to failure! May it be to humility!” He spread his wings and flew. 

Again I pressed the lean ribs under me. “After the spotted leopardess!” I whispered in his ear. 

He turned his head this way and that, snuffing the air, tasting the terroir of the night; then started, quickened his walk, broke into a trot, and in a few moments his speed was tremendous. He seemed to see in the dark. I sat as on the ridge of a wave, feeling under me the play of each individual muscle—a perfectly articulated recipe of motion, his joints so elastic that not once did he jar me. The wind met and passed us like a tornado. Across the evil hollow we sped, an industrial thundering that obliterated the quiet songs of the moor. No monster lifted its neck; all knew the hoofs that deafened the earth over their heads! We rushed up the hills, we shot down their farther slopes; from the rocky chasms of the river-bed he did not swerve. 

The moon, half-way up the heaven, gazed with a solemn trouble in her pale countenance. Rejoicing in the power of my steed and in the pride of my life, I sat like a king and rode. 

We were near the middle of the many channels, my horse gathering himself for a great bounding leap, when the moon reached the key-stone of her arch. Then came a wonder and a terror: she began to descend rolling like a massive wheel of pale cheese bowled down a cellar ramp by the gods, and went faster and faster. Like our own moon, this one had a human face, and now the broad forehead, now the chin was uppermost as she jolted. I gazed aghast. Across the ravines came the howling of wolves, a mournful chorus mourning the disruption of the night. An ugly fear curdled in my gut; my confidence was on the wane! 

The horse maintained his headlong swiftness, with ears pricked forward, and thirsty nostrils exulting in the wind his career created. But there was the moon jolting like an old chariot-wheel down the hill of heaven, with awful boding! She rolled at last over the horizon-edge and disappeared, carrying all her light with her, plunging the world into absolute, blinding darkness. 

The mighty steed was in the act of clearing a wide shallow channel when we were caught in the net of the blackness. His head dropped. His momentum carried his helpless bulk across, but he fell in a heap on the margin, and where he fell he lay. The deafening roar of his hooves was instantly replaced by a crushing, ringing silence. 

I got up, kneeled beside him, and felt him all over. Not a bone could I find broken, but he was a horse no more. The magic had evaporated like a failed emulsion; he had returned to his base elements. I sat down on the mound of lifeless bone and skin, buried my face in my hands, and surrendered to the unforgiving silence.
