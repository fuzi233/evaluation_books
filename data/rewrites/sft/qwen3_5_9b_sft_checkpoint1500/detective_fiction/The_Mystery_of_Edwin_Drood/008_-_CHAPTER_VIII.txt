The two youths, having watched the girls—their charges—depart into the courtyard of the Nuns' House, found themselves subjected to a stare from the brass doorplate. It seemed to mock them, an old, battered visage with a missing eye, glaring insolently back at them. Discomfited by this silent judgment, the pair exchanged glances, surveyed the moonlit perspective of the street, and slowly walked away together.

"Do you intend to remain here long, Mr. Drood?" asked Neville.

"Not this time," came Edwin's careless reply. "I depart for London tomorrow. Yet I shall linger here, intermittently, until next Midsummer; thereafter, I shall bid farewell to Cloisterham—and to England itself—for many, many days."

"Will you travel abroad?"

"I shall go to rouse Egypt from its slumber," Edwin replied with a condescension that chilled the air.

"And are you studying?"

"Studying?" Edwin repeated, his tone tinged with disdain. "No. I am acting, working, engineering. My modest inheritance from my father—a former partner of the Firm—left me as a stake within the enterprise itself. Until I reach majority, I am a liability to the Firm, supported by it. In the meantime, Jack, whom you met at dinner, serves as my guardian and trustee."

"I heard from Mr. Crisparkle regarding your other good fortune."

"What do you imply by 'other good fortune,' Mr. Landless?"

Neville had advanced with a mixture of caution and shyness, embodying that unique, dual nature of being simultaneously hunter and hunted. Edwin's retort, however, was abrupt and impolite. They halted, locking eyes with a heat that suggested a storm brewing beneath the surface.

"I trust," Neville ventured, "there is no offense intended, Mr. Drood, in my innocently referencing your betrothal?"

"By George!" cried Edwin, stepping forward with renewed speed. "Everyone in this gossipy old town discusses it. I wonder no tavern hasn't erected a sign bearing my likeness, labeled *The Betrothed's Head*. Or perhaps *Pussy's* portrait. Either would suffice."

"I am not responsible for Mr. Crisparkle's openness on the matter," Neville began.

"No, I grant you that," Edwin conceded. "But I am accountable for bringing it up to you. And I did so under the assumption that you could not help but be profoundly proud of it."

Here, the secret springs of their conversation turned. Neville Landless, already captivated by Little Rosebud, felt a sting of indignation that Edwin Drood—who stood far beneath her in station—held his treasure so lightly. Conversely, Edwin, smitten by Helena, resented that Helena's brother, whom he deemed inferior, could treat him with such casual dismissal, effectively putting him aside.

Addressing the latter point, Edwin said:

"I do not know, Mr. Neville—that is, adopting Mr. Crisparkle's form of address—"know that what people cherish most, they discuss least; nor do I know that what they value above all, they desire others to chatter about. I lead a bustling existence, and I speak under the correction of you readers, who presumably know everything and likely do. However, I dare say I do."

At this juncture, both men became savage. Neville stood exposed in the open, while Edwin hid beneath the translucent cover of a popular ballad, pausing occasionally to feign admiration of the moonlit scenery.

"It does not strike me as particularly civil," remarked Neville at length, "for you to reflect upon a stranger who visits without your advantages, striving to catch up on lost time. Granted, I was not raised in a 'busy life,' and my notions of courtesy were forged among Heathens."

"Perhaps," retorted Edwin, "the highest civility, regardless of upbringing, is to mind one's own business. If you will set me that example, I vow to follow suit."

"Do you realize you assume far too much authority?" Neville shot back angrily. "And in the region whence I hail, you would be summoned to account for such presumption."

"Accounted to whom, pray?" asked Edwin, halting to survey the other with a look of supreme disdain.

Suddenly, a heavy hand landed on Edwin's shoulder, and Jasper stepped between them. It appeared Jasper had circled the Nuns' House and emerged from the shadows of the road.

"Ned, Ned, Ned!" Jasper exclaimed. "Let us cease this immediately. I dislike this. I have overheard sharp exchanges between you. Recall, my dear boy, you are nearly host tonight. You belong to this place, representing it to a visitor. Mr. Neville is a guest, and hospitality demands respect. And, Mr. Neville," placing his left hand on the younger man's shoulder, walking squarely between them, palm to palm, "I apologize, but I urge you to moderate your temper. Pray, what is wrong? Why ask! If nothing is amiss, the question is needless. Are we not all three on perfect terms?"

Following a silent struggle to determine who would speak first, Edwin broke the silence: "As far as I am concerned, Jack, there is no anger in me."

"Nor in me," Neville replied, though less freely, perhaps less casually. "Yet, if Mr. Drood knew the burdens lying behind me, distant from here, he might understand why sharp words cut so deeply."

"Perhaps," Jasper soothed, "we should avoid qualifying our harmony. We should refrain from statements resembling reproach or conditions; it would not seem generous. Frankly and freely, you see no anger in Ned. Frankly and freely, there is no anger in you, Mr. Neville?"

"None at all, Mr. Jasper." Still, the words lacked full frankness, or perhaps, as noted again, utter carelessness.

"All resolved! Now, my bachelor gatehouse lies but yards hence. The fire burns, wine and glasses await on the table, and it is no stone's throw from Minor Canon Corner. Ned, you depart tomorrow. Let us escort Mr. Neville in for a stirrup-cup."

"With all my heart, Jack."

"And with all mine, Mr. Jasper." Neville felt compelled to agree, though his heart urged otherwise. He sensed he had lost command of his temper; Edwin's coolness, rather than catching, burned him to a fever pitch.

Jasper, strolling centrally, hand-on-shoulder on either flank, beautifully refrained the chorus of a drinking song, guiding them upstairs. Upon entering Jasper's chambers, the first sight revealed, once the lamp joined the fire's glow, was the portrait hanging above the mantel. It was hardly an image conducive to reconciliation, awkwardly resurrecting the source of their discord. Both glanced at it consciously, saying nothing. Yet Jasper, seemingly possessing only a fragmentary clue to their strife, immediately drew attention to it.

"You recognize that picture, Mr. Neville?" Jasper shaded the lamp to illuminate it.

"I recognize it, but it fails to flatter the original."

"Oh, you are harsh!" Jasper protested. "It was painted by Ned, who gave it to me as a gift."

"I regret that, Mr. Drood," Neville apologized sincerely. "If I had known I was addressing the artist..."

"Oh, a jest, sir, a mere jest," Edwin interjected with a provoking yawn. "A little humor to please Pussy's whims! I shall paint her seriously, someday, if she proves good."

The air of leisurely patronage and indifference with which this was delivered—as Edwin leaned back, clasping his hands behind his head for support—exasperated the excitable Neville. Jasper observed them alternately, smiled faintly, and turned away to mix a jug of mulled wine at the fire. The process required considerable agitation and compounding.

"I presume, Mr. Neville," Edwin said, quick to resent the implicit protest against himself facing young Landless, "that if you were to paint the likeness of your lady love—"

"I cannot paint," Neville interrupted hastily.

"That is your misfortune, not your fault. You would, if you could. But if you could, I suppose you would render her (regardless of her reality) as Juno, Minerva, Diana, and Venus combined. Eh?"

"I possess no lady love, and I cannot say."

"If I attempted my hand," Edwin declared, a boyish boastfulness rising within him, "upon a portrait of Miss Landless—in earnest, mind you; truly earnest—you would see what I could achieve!"

"My sister's consent to sit, I suppose? As that will never be obtained, I fear I shall never witness your skill. I must bear the loss."

Jasper turned from the fire, filling a large goblet for Neville, then one for Edwin, handing each his share; then he filled his own.

"Drink, Mr. Neville. We toast my nephew, Ned. Since his foot is metaphorically in the stirrup, our stirrup-cup belongs to him. Ned, my dearest fellow, my love!"

Jasper drained his glass nearly empty, and Neville followed suit. Edwin murmured, "Thank you both very much," and mimicked their double example.

"Observe him," cried Jasper, extending his hand with admiration and tenderness, though with a playful edge. "See how he lounges so effortlessly, Mr. Neville! The world stretches before him, limitless choice. A life of stirring work and intrigue, of change and excitement, of domestic ease and love!
