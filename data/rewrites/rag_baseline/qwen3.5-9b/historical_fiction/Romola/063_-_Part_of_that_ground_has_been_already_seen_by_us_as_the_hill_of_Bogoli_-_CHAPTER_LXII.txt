"Ten o'clock. The tide turns toward San Marco."

The morning of the twenty-seventh of February moved with the mechanical inevitability of a clockwork mechanism, driven by the collective desperation of a populace seeking salvation. It was the final day of the Carnival, yet the festive masks had been discarded for the grim reality of the season ahead. Everyone knew the preparations for the second Bonfire of Vanities were underway before the Old Palace, but the gravitational pull of the city's attention had shifted decisively to the Piazza.

The square was a cauldron of humanity. Newcomers surged from every alleyway, a chaotic river pressing against the banks of the established crowd. The front ranks, tightly packed and immovable, formed a living wall around the semicircular barrier guarding the church entrance. Within this enclosure, the Dominican Brethren of San Marco assembled with military precision. Yet, the wooden pulpit erected above the doorway remained vacant—a hollow throne awaiting the occupant who held the city in a state of suspended animation.

This man was the Pope's pariah, the ecclesiastical outcast forbidden to consort with the clergy of Florence, and the heretic whose mere presence warranted excommunication for anyone daring to listen. He had declared the Pontiff a usurper, a man who bought the papal chair with bribes, wielding a hilt without a blade. He had proclaimed that the Pope's curses were broken swords and that obedience to such a ruler was not merely unlawful—it was a crime against the Christian life. Despite the Vatican's terrifying threats of interdict and the promise of sending him to Rome to be "converted," the masses continued to flock to San Marco. They accepted communion from his excommunicated hands, driven by a desperate calculus: *If this Frate truly commanded divine lightning, why should we fear?*

For the common citizen, the theological debate was abstract. But the stakes had become brutally concrete. The belief in the Prophet now threatened their commerce, destabilized their position among the Italian states, and risked plunging the entire city into spiritual and economic ruin. The question had metastasized in the public consciousness: *"What miracle showest thou?"*

Slowly, then with accelerating intensity, this fatal demand had grown in Savonarola's ear. He had responded outwardly by promising the miracle would arrive at the fitting time, and inwardly by clinging to a faith that was far from unwavering. What faith remains solid when the weight of a potential interdict presses down? His conviction wavered, yet his rhetoric hardened. It is the burden of the demagogue to speak in the currency of yesterday's faith, banking on its resurrection tomorrow.

The wooden pulpit had been constructed specifically to stage the spectacle the impatient crowd demanded—a supernatural guarantee of his mission. As the ordinary friars in black mantles filed in, chanting their psalms, the multitude's gaze flickered between the monks and the empty platform. Interest lingered on the faces of the brethren; several belonged to high Florentine families, others to the artisan class that comprised the bulk of the crowd. Only when the roster of monks was complete, and the chanting reached a rhythmic crescendo, did a murmur ripple through the square: *"Fra Girolamo must be coming now."*

It was this anticipation, sharper than the familiar drone of psalmody, that cast a pallid, solemn light over the sea of upturned faces. All eyes locked onto the void where the preacher should stand.

In an instant, the emptiness vanished. A figure draped entirely in black cowl and mantle stepped into the pulpit, kneeling with head bowed, face obscured. To the eager throng, the silence stretched agonizingly long. The black silhouette knelt while the monks chanted, creating a tension thick enough to cut. The stillness was absolute, charged with an electric awe that paralyzed even those ready to stone him; their arms felt unnerved, their aggression dissolving into reverence.

Then, a vibration passed through the crowd, a wave of shared recognition moving from neighbor to neighbor like a silent handshake. The Frate had risen. He turned toward the people and partially pushed back his hood. The monotonous wail of the choir ceased abruptly. To those nearest the platform, the fading sounds of the chant seemed to merge instantly into the force of Savonarola's flashing glance as he surveyed the silence.

He stretched out his hands. In their exquisite delicacy, they appeared transformed—from crude instruments of grasping into vessels of hypersensitive perception, capable of receiving truth without gross contact. These hands spoke a language of pure appeal from the soul masked by his fierce, passionate visage, etched now with the deep lines of forty-four years of struggle.

At the extension of his arms, a reaction rippled through the front ranks. Some collapsed to their knees, and scattered devout disciples further back followed suit. However, the majority remained standing. Some resisted the impulse to kneel before an excommunicated man, fearing that such an act of blessing might invite immediate divine judgment. Others scoffed, viewing the scene as a new comedy staged by an ambitious deceiver, though they admitted a strange impotence in opposing the rising tide of fashion.

Then came the voice. Clear, low, and cutting through the air.

More fell to their knees. As the intonation rose, becoming clearer and more resonant, the number of upright heads dwindled rapidly. By the time he uttered *"Benedicat vos omnipotens Deus,"* the phrase erupted into a masculine cry, a roar of protest against the demonish forces attempting to stifle the blessing. It rang like a trumpet across the extremities of the Piazza. Underneath the sound, every single head bowed.

Following the utterance of the benediction, Savonarola himself sank to his knees, hiding his face in temporary exhaustion. These eruptions of emotion were the fuel of his existence; he had told the people long ago, *"Without preaching I cannot live."* But it was a life that consumed him, shattering him piece by piece.

Minutes later, as some began to rise, a larger number remained prostrate, all faces fixed intently on him. He held a crystal vessel containing the consecrated Host, preparing to address the assembly.

"You remember, my children," he began, his voice carrying clearly in the vast stillness, "three days ago I besought you. When I held this Sacrament in my hand before you all, I asked you to pray fervently to the Most High: if this work of mine does not come from Him, let Him send a fire to consume me, that I may vanish into eternal darkness, away from His light which I have hidden with my falsity."

He paused, the breathless moment hanging heavy in the air. Perhaps no one truly prayed, save for those acting in devout obedience. Instead, every consciousness was seized by the overwhelming sensation that Savonarola himself was praying. His voice was not loud, yet it pierced the wide silence.

*"Lord, if I have not wrought in sincerity of soul, if my word cometh not from Thee, strike me in this moment with Thy thunder, and let the fires of Thy wrath enclose me."*

He ceased speaking. He stood motionless, holding the sacred mystery in his hand, eyes uplifted, his entire aspect trembling with excitation. The rest of the square mirrored his stillness. Sunlight, previously struggling through the grey atmosphere, began to pierce the gloom, casting fitful streaks across the convent wall. Some awe-stricken spectators started timidly.

Soon, however, the sky parted. With a gentle quickness, like a sudden smile, a stream of brightness poured itself onto the crystal vase. The light spread over Savonarola's face, bathing him in mild glorification.

An instantaneous shout tore through the Piazza. *"Behold the answer!"*

The warm radiance thrilled through Savonarola's frame, syncing perfectly with the roar of the crowd. It was his last moment of untroubled triumph. In his rapturous confidence, he felt himself transported to a grander scene—an audience representing all Christendom, where he would be sealed once more as the messenger of supreme righteousness, charged with divine strength.

But the vision lasted only a heartbeat. Before the shout faded completely, he turned away within the church, unable to bear the strain any longer.

When the Frate disappeared and the sunlight returned to its impartial, mundane illumination, washing both clean and unclean alike, the silence shattered. Alongside the general movement of the crowd, a cacophony of voices erupted. Strong discords and varying scales of laughter revealed that the previous universal kneeling had been a submission born of compulsion, not conviction. Hostility and scorn had merely retreated behind a temporary spell.

"It seems to me the plaudits are giving way to criticism," observed Tito, watching the scene from an upper loggia of a house opposite the church.

"Nevertheless, it was a striking moment, eh, Messer Pietro?" Tito continued. "Fra Girolamo is a man to make one understand that there was a time when the monk's frock was a symbol of power over men's minds rather than over the keys of women's cupboards."

"Assuredly," replied Pietro Cennini. "And until I have seen proof that Fra Girolamo possesses less faith in God's judgments than the common run of men—and certainly more—I shall not believe he would brave Heaven in this manner if his soul were laden with a conscious lie."
