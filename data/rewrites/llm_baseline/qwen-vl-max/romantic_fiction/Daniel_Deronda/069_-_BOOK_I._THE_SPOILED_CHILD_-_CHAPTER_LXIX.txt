“The human nature unto which I felt
That I belonged, and reverenced with love,
Was not a punctual presence, but a spirit
Diffused through time and space, with aid derived
Of evidence from monuments, erect,
Prostrate, or leaning toward their common rest
In earth, the widely scattered wreck sublime
Of vanished nations.” —WORDSWORTH: _The Prelude_.

Sir Hugo carried out his plan to spend part of the autumn at Diplow, and by early October, his presence had brought a gentle warmth to the surrounding countryside—from the grand estate of Brackenshaw and Quetcham down to the modest parlors of Wanchester’s shopkeepers. Sir Hugo was a man who enjoyed being seen, liked to be kind, and believed in reform without fearing it would unsettle the soul of English life—especially its deep-rooted admiration for tradition and rank. Thus, he made Diplow a welcoming home, inviting both seasoned solicitors and young curates alike, yet always with care. He knew how to mix guests so that no one felt overlooked or humbled. For instance, while Lord Brackenshaw might happily meet Robinson the attorney, Robinson himself would take offense at being placed among those he considered his equals. Sir Hugo understood these nuances well, and in doing so won affection and respect—two things that suited his generous nature perfectly.

Now, the rector of Pennicote found himself welcomed at Diplow in a way he’d never known under Grandcourt’s rule. It wasn’t because Sir Hugo admired Mr. Gascoigne, but because he wished to maintain a warm, visible friendship with him—for Mrs. Grandcourt. His chivalry toward her had grown steadily, though he could not fully explain why—even to Lady Mallinger. He refused to speak of what he believed was another woman’s secret, even if that woman was his own wife. This silence revealed a rare depth of honor in his devotion.

Deronda, after becoming engaged to Mirah, decided it was right to tell Sir Hugo everything—his future plans, his new purpose in life. He chose to write a letter. He sensed that his dear friend might be hurt or disappointed by this turn of fate, and he feared a face-to-face conversation might spark an impulsive reaction he would regret. In writing, he could spare them both the pain of immediate emotion.

Sir Hugo, though not entirely surprised, was deeply troubled. His first instinct was to share the letter with Lady Mallinger, knowing she would react with shock—giving him the chance to argue against her alarm, thus softening his own distress. And indeed, when she expressed dismay that Daniel’s brilliance and companionship should end in such an “absurd” attachment to the Jews, Sir Hugo could reply with calm assurance:

“Oh, nonsense, my dear! Dan will not make a fool of himself. He has strong ideas about Judaism—political ones you can’t grasp. But don’t worry—he’ll keep his head.”

Yet when it came to the marriage itself, Lady Mallinger offered no comfort. Gently, without bitterness, she admitted she’d never imagined such a thing when Mirah sang at her party or taught Amabel. After a pause, she confessed it had once crossed her mind—after Daniel’s sudden appearance in Genoa—that he might marry Gwendolen. It seemed too strange a coincidence. Though she didn’t like widows, she thought such a match would have been better than Daniel drifting into Jewish life altogether.

But Sir Hugo agreed with her so completely that he couldn’t dismiss her thoughts as mere feminine fancy. His anger over Gwendolen’s rejection remained unexpressed, unrelieved. He told Lady Mallinger not to speak of it—yet kept thinking: *If this is cruel to the poor girl, the longer she stays ignorant, the better. She’ll learn from Dan himself.*

His imagination had already reconstructed the whole story, piece by piece.

Meanwhile, Sir Hugo’s stay at Diplow allowed him to continue his fatherly attentions to Gwendolen—a role Lady Mallinger, despite her disapproval of widows, was glad to support.

Gwendolen had moved to Offendene, and there she settled with a quietness beyond her mother’s hopes. She was beginning to feel a peaceful melancholy born of surrender—of letting go of self-demand, of accepting kindness as a gift rather than a right. Like one rescued from darkness, she now savored the light, the air, the small joys of daily life. She saw each morning and evening not as routine, but as deliverance. Even the faint glow of compassion from others, or a dog’s loyalty, felt like grace. Those who know themselves deeply—like Hamlet—can understand this constant sense of rescue. And Gwendolen lived it daily, reliving her past struggles: from the false comforts of pride that led her away from conscience, to the final, desperate pull of hatred that threatened to consume her—while she wept and prayed for the very conscience she had abandoned.

She now treasured every word Deronda had spoken about her salvation, every sentence that helped her resist despair.

Yet she was also lifted by the hope of seeing him again. She did not imagine him distant—her need for him blurred the boundaries between their lives. Her world was shaped by his presence, just as ours often are shaped by our deepest attachments—not only to people, but to God. The future she faced was one where she would grow into the image he held before her. Hadn’t he first appeared to her as a corrective force? One she resented at first, then loved completely. She could not imagine ending that reliance—it had become, in her mind, as firm as the earth beneath her feet.

And soon, Deronda came to Diplow, closer to London than the Abbey. He had planned to take Ezra and Mirah to a mild coastal spot while preparing a new home for Mirah as his bride—a place where they could care for her brother. But Ezra begged not to be moved unless it was to join them in the East. The outside world had become a burden; yet the idea of that journey stirred a dreamlike joy within him.

Deronda, meanwhile, wanted to discuss his finances and future with Sir Hugo—especially since he hoped to marry Mirah within months. That gave him reason to visit Diplow. But there was another reason: his promise to Gwendolen. Though he felt blessed in his own life, a quiet ache remained. It may seem paradoxical—the beloved lover called happy, yet happiness often hides sorrow. But human feeling rarely fits neat labels. His joy for Mirah did not exclude care for Gwendolen. True love, after all, is not just joy—it is the embrace of countless burdens, greater than any pleasure outside it.

He visited Diplow twice, saw Gwendolen twice—and left without telling her anything about his new life. He blamed himself. Yet we all delay painful truths, waiting for the right moment, the right mood. The first time, Gwendolen was full of questions—about her own life, her ignorance, how to be kinder, how to make amends. Deronda could not interrupt her with news of his own changes. The second time, she was overwhelmed by dark memories, crying uncontrollably, convinced he would always despise her. All he could do was soothe her. When her tears subsided, leaving her eyes glistening with childlike trust, he could not add another weight.

Still, time passed. He knew he must tell her. Gwendolen had never questioned his affairs—had never wondered why he was in Genoa. Her innocence made the truth harder. If she learned it from someone else, she would feel betrayed. He couldn’t write—his tenderness forbade imagining her reading his farewell alone, perhaps sensing cold joy in his words. So he returned to Diplow, determined to face the risk.

On this third visit, Hans Meyrick was painting the three daughters on a bank—"in the Gainsborough style"—and sketching village children at Pennicote. Hans seemed cheerful, but Deronda sensed a forced brightness, like a lady’s rouge too evenly applied. Though grateful to Deronda, Hans could not help feeling a sting of irritation. He had poured his heart out about Mirah—only to discover Deronda had hidden a rivalry that now defined his life. Poor Hans had always trusted Deronda completely, never expected to be excluded. Now, he realized Deronda was more than he’d known—and that knowledge hurt. Still, Hans had done nothing wrong. He’d stood by his friend. As he told himself: “His metal rang truer than I ever thought possible.” Hans had long said he was a *dilettante* in virtue—loving it in others, failing at it himself. Perhaps that was why he now spoke freely.

“Did you come down recently, Hans?” Deronda asked, joining him in the garden.

“Oh, ten days ago—before Sir Hugo’s date. I came with Rex Gascoigne and stayed at the rectory. I’ve heard all the gossip. Know the wheelwright’s house inside out, even attended an infant school exam. Sister Anna, with her noble upper lip, escorted me—else I’d have been mobbed by three urchins and an idiot. Long hair and odd looks don’t suit Pennicote. The village is idyllic. Only flaw: a gloomy curate with broad shoulders and trousers—should’ve gone into drapery. The Gascoignes are perfect—related to the Vandyke duchess. I glimpsed her in black robes from afar. She doesn’t receive visitors.”

“She wasn’t staying at the rectory?” Deronda asked.

“No—but I went to Offendene. Saw the old house. And the duchess’ family. You’ve been there?”

“Yes,” Deronda replied quietly.

“A fine old place. Perfect for a widow with romantic history. And she’s had several. I think I’ve uncovered a romance between her and your friend Rex.”

“Not long before her marriage?” Deronda asked, genuinely curious. “They’ve only been at Offendene a year. How did you find out?”

“Oh—I know what it’s like to be miserable. I watch for signs of pain in others. Rex never visits Offendene, hasn’t seen the duchess since she returned. And Miss Gascoigne mentioned something during charades—enough to prove Rex once hovered close enough to get burned. I don’t know her side. Maybe the duke stepped in and swept her off. That’s usually how it goes when a worthy man falls in love. Now I understand why Gascoigne says law is his mistress and he’ll remain single. But those are green vows. Since the duke didn’t drown for you, maybe he’ll do it for Rex. Who knows?”

“Is it absolutely necessary that Mrs. Grandcourt remarry?” Deronda asked, ready to say Hans’s guesses weren’t enough to justify new speculation.

“You monster!” Hans laughed. “Do you want her to wear mourning forever? Burn herself alive while you’re alive and happy?”

Deronda said nothing, but his discomfort showed. Hans changed the subject, and later, alone, shrugged slightly—wondering if Deronda truly felt more for Gwendolen than Mirah would like to know. *Why didn’t she fall for me?* he thought, laughing. *I’d have no rivals. No woman ever wanted to debate theology with me.*

No wonder Deronda winced. Such jokes stung—especially when he was already trembling with dread of the pain ahead. Any recognition from another made the fear real.

But he had come to face it. The next day, he rode to Offendene. He’d sent word he’d call, and found Gwendolen waiting in the old drawing-room—where so many turning points in her life had occurred.

She looked less sorrowful than before. No smile, but a quiet strength. She noticed the sadness in Deronda at once. They sat apart, and she spoke first:

“You were afraid to come last time—because I was so broken. But I’m not today. I’ve been sorry ever since. I’ve made it my duty to stay hopeful, so I wouldn’t cause you pain.”

There was a sweetness in her voice that pierced Deronda’s resolve. Yet he knew he must begin.

“I am troubled today,” he said softly, looking at her. “Because I have things to tell you—things you’ll think I’ve withheld. Things that change my life. I feel I’ve failed your trust—never letting you know what’s shaping my future. But when we’ve been together, your struggles have always been more urgent than mine.”

A flicker of surprise passed through her. She didn’t fear—only sensed something profound. Her mind leapt to Sir Hugo’s estate, his legacy.

“You never thought of anything but helping me,” she said gently. “And I was so hard to please. How could you tell me?”

“It may astonish you,” Deronda said, “that I’ve only recently discovered who my parents were.”

She wasn’t astonished. She felt certain now. He continued without hesitation.

“That’s why I was in Italy—to learn the truth. To meet my mother. She raised me in secrecy, by her wish. After my father died, she gave me up as a child. But now she’s dying, and she believes the secret must end. Her main reason? She didn’t want me to know I was a Jew.”

“A Jew?” Gwendolen whispered, stunned, as if poison were spreading through her veins.

Deronda flushed, silent. Gwendolen stared at the floor, searching memory for meaning. Then she looked up, reproachful.

“What difference could that make?”

“It has made a great difference to me,” he said firmly. But he hesitated—the gap between her world and his felt like a language barrier.

She paused, then said, tenderly, “I hope it doesn’t matter to you. You’re still the same—no matter what.”

She meant to assure him: nothing external could change how she saw him. Deronda felt a little comfort in her misunderstanding.

“The discovery wasn’t painful,” he said. “I’d been prepared. I was glad. Through a remarkable Jew—whose ideas have drawn me so deeply that I intend to dedicate much of my life to making them real.”

Again, Gwendolen trembled—this time with alarm. Her lips parted, helpless. She hadn’t yet connected this to Mirah—but the words hinted at vast journeys, unknown worlds. Ideas that once seemed abstract now felt terrifyingly real.

He couldn’t read her thoughts, so he sought the gentlest path.

“That goal will eventually require me to leave England—for years. I must go to the East.”

Now the truth was clearer—and more immediate. Gwendolen’s lips quivered. “But you’ll come back?” she asked, tasting her tears before wiping them.

Deronda rose, leaning against the mantel, facing away. But when she wiped her cheeks and looked up, he turned.

“If I live,” he said, “_some time_.”

They sat in silence. He waited for her question. She waited, too—thinking of what she would say.

“What will you do?” she asked at last, softly. “Can I understand? Or am I too ignorant?”
