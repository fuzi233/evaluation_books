Two months elapsed—a season in Trent's life that he would forever recall with a shudder. He encountered Mrs. Manderson roughly six times, and every encounter, marked by a cool, calculated friendliness hovering precariously between acquaintance and intimacy, served only to baffle and infuriate him. At the opera, to his profound astonishment, he found her seated beside a certain Mrs. Wallace, a lively matron Trent had known since childhood. It transpired that upon returning from Italy, Mrs. Manderson had drifted into social circles to which Trent, by nurture and temperament, naturally belonged. She claimed this occurred because she had pitched her tent within their hunting grounds, bringing several of his acquaintances into proximity. Trent retained a vague, ghastly memory of that specific occasion; he had been entirely unlike himself, ill-at-ease and burning with shame, babbling idiotically of his adventures in the Baltic provinces, only to discover intermittently that he was addressing Mrs. Wallace exclusively. When Trent eventually joined them, the other lady had seemingly lost the slight, agitated demeanor with which she had initially intercepted him in the vestibule. Instead, she greeted him with pleasant inquiries regarding her travels, her settlement in London, and mutual acquaintances.

Throughout the latter half of the opera, which he endured sitting behind them, Trent experienced a singular, trance-like state. He registered nothing but the angle of her cheek, the volume of her hair, the line of her shoulder and arm, and her hand resting heavily upon the cushion. Her black hair appeared to him as a forest, immeasurable, pathless, and enchanted, luring him toward some fatal adventure. By the curtain call, he was pale and subdued, departing from them with a strictly formal courtesy.

On the next occasion, at a country estate where both were guests, and on subsequent meetings, Trent believed he had mastered himself. He mirrored her manner, acquitting himself, in his estimation, with decency. Yet, "decency" was a thin veneer covering an agony of bewilderment, remorse, and longing. He could make absolutely nothing of her attitude. It was undeniable that she had read his manuscript and comprehended the suspicion embedded in his final question to her at White Gables. So, how could she treat him with such frankness and openness, as she did with all the world's men who had caused her no injury?

Yet, his intuition, sharp despite the uniformity of her outward behavior, insisted that an injury *had* been done, and that she felt it acutely. On rare, fleeting moments when they conversed privately, he sensed a warning that she was approaching this subject; each time, driven by fear, he diverted the conversation with ingenuity born of anxiety. He formulated two resolutions. First, upon completing a commission binding him to London, he would depart and remain away indefinitely. The strain was unsustainable. He no longer burned to know the truth; he desired nothing to confirm his fixed internal conviction—that he had blundered, that he had misread the situation, and that he had interpreted her tears as guilt, thereby writing himself down as a slanderous fool. He speculated no further on Marlowe's motives for Manderson's murder. Mr. Cupples returned to London, and Trent asked him nothing. He now knew, with the weight of emphasis in her voice, that "so long as she considered herself bound to him... no power on earth could have persuaded her." He met Mrs. Manderson at dinner at her uncle's vast, tomb-like residence in Bloomsbury, where he spent most of the evening conversing with a German professor of archaeology.

His second resolution was absolute: he would not be alone with her.

However, a few days later, when she penned a note inviting him to visit the following afternoon, he made no effort to decline. It read as a formal challenge. While she presided over the rites of tea, and for some time thereafter, she joined his slightly fevered discourse on current affairs with such natural ease that hope flickered within him. Perhaps she had altered her resolve to corner him and speak gravely. To all appearances, she was now careless, smiling with a radiance that reminded him, not for the first time since that night at the opera, of a Princess of Brunswick: "Her mouth has ten thousand charms that touch the soul." She conducted a tour of the beautiful salon where she had received him, singling out treasures from the spoils of countless bric-à-brac shops, laughing over her quests and bargains. When he asked if she would again delight him with a favorite piece of his composition, which she had played at another house, she consented immediately.

She played with a perfection of execution and feeling that moved him anew, as it had before. "You are a musician born," he murmured quietly when she finished, the last tremors of the music fading. "I knew that before I first heard you."

"I have played a great deal ever since I can remember," she replied simply, turning to him with a gentle smile. "It has been a great comfort to me. When did you first detect music in me?"

"Oh, of course: it was at the opera," he stammered. "But that wouldn't prove much, would it?"

"No," he said abstractedly, his mind still lingering on the melody. "I think I knew it the first time I saw you." Then understanding dawned on his own words, stiffening him. For the first time, the past had been explicitly invoked.

A brief silence fell. Mrs. Manderson looked at Trent, then hastily averted her gaze. Color rose in her cheeks, and she pursed her lips as if preparing to whistle. With a defiant gesture of the shoulders—which he remembered well—she rose abruptly from the piano and seated herself opposite him in a chair.

"That speech of yours will do as well as anything," she began slowly, staring at the point of her shoe, "to bring us to what I wanted to say. I asked you here today, Mr. Trent, precisely because I could bear it no longer. Ever since the day you left me at White Gables, I have told myself that it didn't matter what you thought of me in that affair; that you were certainly not the kind of man to speak to others of what you believed about me, after promising to suppress your manuscript. I asked myself how it could matter. But all along, of course, I knew it did. It mattered horribly. Because what you thought was not true." She raised her eyes and met his gaze calmly. Trent, his face completely expressionless, returned her look.

"Since I began to know you," he said, "I have ceased to think it."

"Thank you," Mrs. Manderson said, blushing suddenly and deeply. Then, playing idly with a glove, she added, "But I want you to know what *was* true."

"I did not know if I should ever see you again," she continued, her voice dropping. "But I felt that if I did, I must speak to you about this. I thought it would not be hard, because you seemed to me an understanding person; and besides, a married woman is not expected to have the same difficulties as a young girl in speaking of such things when necessity demands it. And then we did meet again, and I discovered it was exceedingly difficult. You made it difficult."

"How?" he asked quietly.

"I don't know," the lady admitted. "But yes, I do know. It was simply because you treated me exactly as if you had never thought or imagined anything of that sort about me. I had always supposed that if I saw you again, you would turn on me with that hard, horrible look you wore when you asked me that last question at White Gables—do you remember? Instead, you were just like any other acquaintance. You were just..." She hesitated, spreading her hands. "...nice. You know. After that first time at the opera, when I spoke to you, I went home wondering if you had really recognized me. I thought perhaps you recognized my face without remembering who it was."

A short, involuntary laugh escaped Trent, though he said nothing.

She smiled deprecatingly. "Well, I couldn't remember if you had spoken my name; I thought perhaps you hadn't. But the next time, at the Iretons', you did speak it, so I knew; and a dozen times during those few days I almost brought myself to tell you, but never quite. I began to feel that you wouldn't let me, that you would slip away from the subject if I approached it. Wasn't I right? Tell me, please." He nodded. "But why?" He remained silent.

"Well," she said, "I will finish what I had to say, and then you will tell me, I hope, why you made it so hard. When I began to understand that you wouldn't let me talk of the matter, it made me more determined than ever. I suppose you didn't realize that I would insist on speaking even if you were discouraging. I dare say I couldn't have done it if I had been guilty, as you thought. You walked into my parlour today, never thinking I should dare. Well, now you see."

Mrs. Manderson had shed all air of hesitancy. As she was wont to say, she had talked herself enthusiastic, and in the ardor of her purpose to annihilate the long-troubling misunderstanding, she felt herself mistress of the situation.
