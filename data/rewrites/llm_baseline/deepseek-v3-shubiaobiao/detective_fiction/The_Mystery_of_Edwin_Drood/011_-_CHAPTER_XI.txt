Nestled behind the timeworn stretch of Holborn, where gabled houses of another age still peer mournfully at the bustling street as if searching for the vanished Old Bourne, lies Staple Inn—a quiet retreat of two uneven quadrangles. To step into its confines from the clamor of London is to muffle the world, as though one had stuffed cotton in their ears and slipped on velvet-soled shoes. Here, a handful of soot-stained sparrows chirp in soot-stained trees, pretending at rusticity, while a scant patch of garden and a few yards of gravel grant them the illusion. It is a place of law, too, with its own diminutive hall and a lantern perched atop it—though to what exact purpose, this narrative does not presume to know.

In the days when Cloisterham still shuddered at the distant specter of a railroad, as if the iron tracks might unsettle the sacred British constitution (that peculiar institution equally lamented, feared, and boasted of, no matter what calamity befell the globe), no towering edifices loomed over Staple Inn. The setting sun bathed it in gold, and the south-west wind swept through unhindered.

But neither sun nor wind favored the Inn on a certain December evening near six o'clock, when fog choked its passages and candles glowed weakly through the windows of its occupied chambers. One such set, in the corner of the inner quadrangle, bore above its door the cryptic inscription:

Within, oblivious to the meaning of these letters—save for the occasional idle fancy that they might stand for *Perhaps John Thomas* or *Perhaps Joe Tyler*—sat Mr. Grewgious, writing by his fire.

To look at Mr. Grewgious was to wonder if ambition or disappointment had ever touched him. Trained in the law, he had once set his sights on chamber practice—drafting deeds, conveying property (as Pistol might say, *"convey the wise it call"*). But Conveyancing had spurned him; their union was so barren of mutual affection that they parted as strangers, though they had never truly met.

No, Conveyancing would not be wooed. So Mr. Grewgious turned elsewhere. An arbitration, blown his way by some unaccountable wind, proved his diligence in rooting out justice, and soon a plump Receivership followed, traceable to more tangible breezes. Thus, by chance, he found his niche. Now Receiver and Agent to two wealthy estates, delegating legal matters to solicitors below, he had snuffed out ambition (if ever he had kindled it) and settled beneath the dry vine and fig tree of P. J. T., planter of 1747.

His room was furnished with ledgers, correspondence, and strongboxes—none of which cluttered, so exact was their arrangement. The mere thought of dying with a figure unchecked or a fact unresolved would have struck him dead on the spot. Fidelity was his lifeblood—not the quick, glittering sort, but the steady, unshakable kind.

Luxury was absent; comfort extended only to dryness, warmth, and a faded but snug hearth. The private life of the room centered there, with an easy-chair and an old-fashioned round table, drawn out after business hours from its corner, where it usually stood upturned like a polished mahogany shield. Behind it lay a closet, reliably stocked. An outer room served as his clerk’s domain; his own bedchamber lay across the common stair, and below waited a not-insignificant cellar. Three hundred days a year, he dined at Furnival’s Inn, then returned to his modest kingdom until business called again.

As Mr. Grewgious wrote by his fire that evening, so too did his clerk labor by *his*. This attendant—a pallid, puffy man of thirty, with dull dark eyes and a doughy complexion that seemed to beg for the oven—was an enigma. Some uncanny power bound him to Grewgious, as though summoned by a spell that forgot the dismissal. He clung to his stool despite his employer’s obvious discomfort, yet was treated with inexplicable forbearance.

**"Now, Bazzard,"** said Mr. Grewgious, tidying his papers, **"what news besides fog?"**

**"Mr. Drood,"** said Bazzard.

**"I am doing it,"** said Bazzard.

The visitor entered.

**"Dear me!"** exclaimed Mr. Grewgious, blinking at his candles. **"I thought you’d left your name and gone. How do you do, Mr. Edwin? You sound choked!"**

**"The fog,"** Edwin replied, unwrapping himself. **"It stings like cayenne."**

Edwin, amused by the prospect of an impromptu feast, agreed.

**"And Bazzard,"** added Mr. Grewgious, lowering his voice mischievously, **"you’ll join us—unless you object?"**

**"If invited, I don’t care if I do,"** Bazzard muttered.

Thus arranged, the clerk was dispatched to Furnival’s Inn with orders for soup, a joint, fowl, and whatever else the kitchen might spare. The fog, now an unwelcome guest, swirled in with each opening of the door, only to be devoured by the fire.

When the meal was done and glasses drained, Mr. Grewgious turned to business.

**"You should have acknowledged it,"** chided Grewgious. **"Business is business. But no matter."**

Then, with a sudden shift, he launched into a discourse on love—wooden in delivery yet piercing in insight—painting a portrait of devotion so exact that Edwin flushed and bit his lip.

**"A true lover’s mind,"** declared Grewgious, **"is saturated with the beloved. Her name is sacred, her presence his only solace. He lives a doubled life, halved without her."**

Edwin, squirming, conceded the general truth of it—though protested that lovers might not always wear their hearts openly.

**"Perhaps,"** allowed Grewgious. **"But let him beware trifling with treasure. Woe to him if he does."**

The fire crackled. Bazzard dozed. And Grewgious, after a pause, rapped his knees like a clockwork figure rousing itself.

The bottle was finished, the glasses upended. And in the fog-laden quiet of Staple Inn, under the watchful gaze of P. J. T. 1747, the evening’s true purpose at last emerged.
