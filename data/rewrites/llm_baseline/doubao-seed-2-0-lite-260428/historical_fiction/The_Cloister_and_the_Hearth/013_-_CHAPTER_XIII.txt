Gerard and Margaret walked the road to Sevenbergen light as thistledown, giddy on the first breath of freedom after his narrow escape from the Tergou prison, their recent bold adventure still thrumming warm in their veins. But that bright glow soon faded to heavy, cold dread: Gerard was an escaped fugitive, liable to be dragged back in irons at any moment, no matter that the charge against him was a lie. They would have to part, if only for a little while, and he had already come to loathe the narrow, spiteful bounds of his hometown.

Margaret begged him to flee the country for a spell, but the very thought of him crossing the brutal Alps to Italy made her heart feel as if it would split in two. Gerard, for his part, could only reconcile himself to leaving her by clinging to the dream of that same Italy: a place where his skill as an illuminator and carver would be valued, where he could earn gold and renown fast enough to sweep away every petty obstacle that stood between them and marriage. He told her again all that the noble Van Eyck had counseled him, reminded her the demoiselle’s gold coins had been given him expressly for that Italian journey. This boorish, small-minded village would never nurture his talent, he said. In the south, artists were revered. The unselfish girl gave her consent at last, but not before hot, tender tears streamed down her cheeks. Young and wild with love himself, Gerard wept bitter tears right alongside her, and they clung to each other, over and over asking what wrong they had ever done to earn so many secret enemies, all bent on tearing them apart.

They sat hand in hand long past midnight, now bemoaning their cruel fate, now painting glowing, sunlit pictures of the little home they would share one day, with a wide window for his workbench and a garden full of herbs. Midway through his grandest, most hopeful description of their future, Margaret’s tears would start flowing afresh, and all his grand eloquence would dissolve into a soft, shaking sigh. By dawn they had resigned themselves to parting, but neither had the courage to name the hour. No doubt they would have lingered in that soft, suspended limbo for weeks if fate had not crashed down upon them.

It was three o’clock in the afternoon when Giles came thudding up the lane on horseback, having ridden a dozen miles out of his way to shake off any eyes that might be following him. The pair ran out to meet him, curiosity bright on their faces, but his grim tone struck them cold.
“Brother Gerard,” he boomed, his deep voice rough with urgency, “Kate bids you run for your very life. They have laid a false theft charge at your feet, to give them a pretense to seize you. Do not waste a breath trying to explain. There is no justice for you in Tergou. Those parchments you took from Ghysbrecht? They are but a blind. Kate has seen the murderous look in the men they’ve sent after you. There is a price on your head. Fly! For Margaret’s sake, and for every soul that loves you, do not loiter another second. Fly!”

It hit them like a thunderclap, leaving two white, stunned faces staring first at each other, then at the grim messenger. Up until that moment Giles had only spoken the lines Catherine had bade him carry, but now he added a word of his own, gruff and earnest. “All the constables of Tergou were tramping through our cottage an hour after you vanished, and Dirk Brower at their head. Kate is no fool, Gerard. Listen to her, and run now.”

“Oh yes, Gerard!” Margaret cried, wild with terror. “Fly this instant! Those parchments—my heart misgave me the day you took them, why did I ever let you?”

“Nay, Margaret, Giles says those parchments are nothing but a ruse,” Gerard said firmly. “But that old caitiff Ghysbrecht will never lay eyes on them again. I will not leave until I hide his hoard where he shall never dig it up.” He thanked Giles warmly, bade him farewell, and told him to ride back fast and tell Catherine he was already gone. “For I shall be leagues away before you reach Tergou,” he promised.

He called for old Martin the hunter, told him what had befallen them, and begged him to walk a little way down the Tergou road to keep watch. “Aye,” Martin nodded, testing the tip of his arrow. “If I spot Dirk Brower or any of his men, I will shoot a single arrow straight into the gnarled old oak that grows in your garden. The second you see it, run hard for the forest edge, make for the weird hunter’s spring, and I will guide you through the hidden paths no townsman has ever known.”

Now that the warning had been made plain, Gerard breathed a little easier. He went back out with Margaret, who trembled by the oak tree, every rustle of its leaves making her flinch, while he dug a deep pit in the soft loam under the hawthorn hedge. He tossed the parchments in one by one—most were ancient town charters and burgh records, but one folded deed bore the names of Floris Brandt, Margaret’s father, and Ghysbrecht. “Why, this is as much yours as his,” Gerard said, reaching for it to read.

“Oh not now, Gerard, not now!” Margaret pleaded, clutching his arm. “Every second you waste fills me with fear. See? Heavy drops of rain are starting to fall, and the clouds hang low enough to touch the tops of the trees.” Gerard yielded, tucking that single deed into the breast of his tunic, then shoveled the dirt back over the rest of the parchments and stamped it flat with his boot.

Just then a flash of lightning split the sky, followed by a low roll of distant thunder, and the rain came lashing down hard. Margaret and Gerard ran back to the cottage, and Martin stumbled through the door soon after, his cloak soaked through. “The road is clear for now,” he said, “but a right heavy storm is coming.” His words proved true. The thunder rolled closer and closer until it crashed overhead, lightning flashes coming so fast they looked like the cracks of a whip, and rain falling in torrents that lashed the thatch so hard it sounded like thousands of tiny footsteps. Margaret hid her face against Gerard’s shoulder to avoid seeing the blue-white glare of the lightning. Gerard pushed the rough wooden shutter closed and lit a tallow candle, and the two lovers huddled together, blessing the storm that had stolen them a few more precious hours alone.

The sun set unmarked by either of them, the thunder still pealing, the lightning still flaring, the rain still pouring down. Supper was set on the table, but neither could swallow a bite: the thought that this was the last meal they would share for months, maybe years, choked them. When the storm lulled a little, old Peter retired to his bed. Gerard meant to slip away at peep of day, and neither he nor Margaret would waste a single minute of their remaining time asleep. Martin sat close by the door, fitting a new string to his bow, a task he was always particular about, while the lovers murmured all their sorrows and all their quiet vows over the faint glow of the hearth.

Suddenly the old hunter held up one gnarled hand, signaling for silence. They stilled at once, listening, and for a long moment heard nothing. Then came a faint, soft crunch of autumn leaves strewn across the garden path at the back door: a footstep, trying so hard to be noiseless it made the hair on the back of their necks stand straight up. Martin nocked an arrow to his bowstring in a single fluid motion, and blew the candle out. At that exact moment, they heard more than one set of footsteps creep up to the front of the cottage, not quite as silent as the first, but stealthy as a pack of wolves closing in on prey. Then came a dead, heavy pause.

Their blood turned to ice in their veins.
“Oh Catherine, oh Catherine!” Margaret moaned under her breath, wringing her hands in wild remorse. “You said to fly the instant the warning came.”

“Hush, girl,” Martin hissed in a sharp, stern whisper.

A heavy, booming knock fell on the oak door.
And it hit their terrified hearts harder than a fist.
