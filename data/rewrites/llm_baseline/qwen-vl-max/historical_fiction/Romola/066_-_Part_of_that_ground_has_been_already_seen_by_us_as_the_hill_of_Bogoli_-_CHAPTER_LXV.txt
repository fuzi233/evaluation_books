On the seventh of April, barely a week had passed since the last tremor of civic unrest, and yet Florence stood transformed. The Piazza della Signoria, that proud heart of the Republic, now bore a spectacle more extraordinary than even the infamous Bonfire of Vanities—a gathering not merely of people, but of expectation, dread, and fervent hope. Never before had so many souls crowded into its vast expanse; even on the festival of San Giovanni, when joy ran high and the city burst with color, no such throng had gathered. This was no celebration—it was an oracle in the making.

It was nearing midday, and since dawn the city’s people had been climbing, creeping, clinging to every available perch: rooftops, ledges, iron rods jutting from walls, stone pillars, the backs of weathered statues—anywhere that offered a view. Men sat balanced precariously on architraves, their feet braced against rough stonework, hands gripping iron rings embedded in the ancient façades. They endured cramped limbs, chafed skin, hunger, and cold—not for mere spectacle, but for something greater: a miracle.

And they were not disappointed in their longing. Not entirely. For what they came to see was not just fire, nor just men, but divine judgment made manifest. If the monk did not emerge unharmed from the flames, then at least they would witness his entry—proof enough, they believed, that he was consumed. And if he did walk out whole? Then God Himself had spoken. There could be no doubt: the fire would burn, the monks would step forward. Before them lay the platform—eight feet wide, twenty yards long—piled high with fuel like a forest set ablaze: great oak trunks laid as foundation, thorns piled above, and beneath it all, oiled rags and tow, ready to leap into flame. It stretched from the corner of the marble terrace before the Old Palace, near Marzocco, the stone lion whose stern face seemed to watch over the pyre with ancient disapproval.

Three bodies of armed men stood guard: five hundred soldiers of the Signoria lined the palace steps; five hundred Compagnacci under Dolfo Spini watched from across the square; and three hundred citizens, loyal to Savonarola, stood beneath Orgagna’s Loggia, where the Franciscans and Dominicans would take their places. Money had been spent freely, dignity invoked, and faith stirred. Something immense was about to happen—and if the trial failed, the consequences would be no less profound.

Half the monks were already seated in the Loggia, their grey robes filling one side. The other half remained empty, save for a small altar. The Franciscans had arrived silently, their presence a quiet counterpoint to the coming storm. But then, from across the piazza, a chorus rose—two hundred voices chanting in unison: “Let God arise, and let His enemies be scattered.” The sound swelled, echoing off stone and sky, drawing murmurs from the crowd. Some found it stirring; others, unsettling—an echo of arrogance, perhaps, or a cry meant not to pray, but to intimidate.

At the head of this procession came Fra Domenico, draped in a cope of flame-red velvet, cross in hand, his face uplifted with genuine conviction. He truly believed he would enter the fire for God’s glory and for Savonarola’s truth. Behind him walked Savonarola himself, robed in white, carrying the consecrated Host. He too sang aloud, his voice strong, his gaze fixed ahead. As he stepped onto the Loggia and placed the Sacrament upon the altar, a strange energy seemed to pass through him. The weight of eyes—the scrutiny of thousands—had sharpened his features, enflamed his spirit. And yet, beneath that outward confidence, a deeper current flowed: fear, not of death, but of failure. For Savonarola knew the true cost of this moment. Not martyrdom, but ruin. The trial was not only about divine favor—it was about survival.

And in a window high above, overlooking the chaos, Tito Melema watched with quiet satisfaction. Two evenings prior, the Florentine courier of the Ten had been intercepted—his dispatches seized. Savonarola’s letter, now in the hands of the Duke of Milan, would soon reach the Pope. It was not mere betrayal; it was a catalyst. The tide was turning. And Tito, ever the strategist, felt no malice, only triumph. He had played his part well. Savonarola had been useful—this clever, fanatical preacher, mixing superstition with political insight, a man who could keep secrets and inspire masses. But now, Tito was ready to move on. Let the game end. He would leave Florence soon, stepping from one world into another, cleaner, safer. Today’s spectacle was but a final amusement—something he had helped engineer, though Dolfo Spini, thick-headed and impulsive, could never have managed it alone.

The chanting continued, rising louder, carried by the Piagnoni, those who wore little red crosses as badges of loyalty. They sang not for themselves, but for the prophet, expecting a sign from heaven. Meanwhile, on the opposite side, silence reigned. The Franciscans stood still, cloaked in grey, no music, no color—only resolve. But they had two champions: Fra Giuliano would oppose Fra Domenico, while Fra Francesco challenged Savonarola directly.

“Surely,” muttered the men perched atop rods and pillars, “it must begin now. Why does the chanting not cease? We should see them move toward the platform.”

But they did not move. The Franciscans watched uneasily, their pale faces peering past the dividing boards at the red-robbed Dominican. That color, they feared, might be enchanted—might summon false miracles by dark arts. A man could emerge from fire unscathed, and yet be deceived by the devil.

Then movement began again—between the Loggia and the Palazzo. The debate intensified. The dignitaries appointed to moderate the trial moved back and forth, consulting, arguing. Soon it became clear: Fra Domenico was being led toward the palace. Perhaps the fire had already been lit. Perhaps the miracle was about to unfold.

But no. The red cope vanished into the building. Another Dominican was summoned. The chanting persisted, tiresome, endless. Savonarola stood motionless, the Host still in his hands.

At last, Fra Domenico emerged again—now clothed in plain robes, flanked by two Franciscans, guarding him lest he be enchanted near Savonarola. The crowd understood: he would not enter the fire. It was Savonarola who would go.

But Savonarola did not stir. Instead, he spoke—firmly, boldly, almost with irony. Like Elijah confronting the prophets of Baal, he demanded an end to delays. Yet words were powerless to those hungry, cold, and weary. What need for speech? If the miracle was not to happen, it was Savonarola’s fault. He could end it now—step forward, offer himself.

More movement. More debate. The afternoon slipped away. Clouds gathered, darkening the sky, chilling the air. The chanting faded, replaced by hushed whispers, then shouts—rising in scorn.

“Light the fire! Drive them in!”
“Let us smell roast—we want our dinner!”
“Come, Prophet! Will anything happen before the twenty-four hours are up?”
“What’s your last vision?”
“Oh, he’s got a dozen in his belly—small change for a miracle!”
“Ola, Frate! Where are you? Don’t waste the fuel!”

Still, the monks and officials moved between the Loggia and the Palace, their arguments unheard, their motions meaningless to the crowd. Only Savonarola’s voice broke through—his voice, always the center of attention. It was he who delayed. He was the obstacle.

Then the shouting turned to uproar. Metal clashed. Feet pounded. Dolfo Spini, provoked by the people’s fury, saw his chance. This was no longer a trial—it was a rebellion. The Compagnacci surged forward, pushing through the crowd, driving toward the Loggia. The people recoiled, shoved aside, some falling into the ranks of the Signoria’s soldiers.

For a moment, all breath stopped. The monks froze. The magistrates hesitated. The Loggia, however, was guarded—by Marco Salviati’s men, brave and loyal. The soldiers of the Signoria joined them. The rush was repelled. The Compagnacci retreated, retreating toward the Tetto de’ Pisani.

And then—rain.

It began in drops, then poured. Dark clouds split open, drenching the platform, soaking the fuel, streaming down the edges of the Loggia. The rain fell on the weary, hungry people, soaking their clothes, their spirits. It ran over the altar, splashed the Host, soaked the robes of the monks. And in that instant, everyone knew: the trial was over.

No fire. No miracle. No judgment from heaven.

The Signoria, no doubt, welcomed the rain—as a natural excuse, better than any lie, to declare the event canceled. It was the outcome Savonarola had expected. Yet he was not glad. As the rain fell, he felt not relief, but a deep, cold certainty: his enemies had won. The trial had ended not in fire, but in disgrace. His honor was broken.

He would have to return to San Marco through the angry mob. Friends who once would have died for him now might turn away. And he knew it.

When the rain ceased, he asked for a guard. It was granted. Had he said he was willing to die for his cause? Yes. And he meant it. But to die in shame—mocked as a fraud, a false prophet? That was not martyrdom. That was the erasure of a life devoted to truth.

“O God,” he whispered, “let me die because of what I am, not because of my weakness.”

The light returned, breaking through the clouds. It fell upon Savonarola as he descended the steps of the Loggia, still holding the Host. But there was no glory in it. No divine smile. Only the indifferent sun, illuminating all things—good and bad, noble and base—in the slow unfolding of time.

He heard no blessing. Only taunts. Threats. He knew this was only the beginning of suffering. Yet his courage did not falter. He walked on, steady, silent.

“Well parried, Frate,” said Tito Melema, watching from above. “But I fear your career here is finished. What say you, Niccolò?”

“It is a pity his falsehoods were not all wise,” replied Machiavelli, shrugging. “With the times so much on his side, especially regarding Church matters, he might have done something great.”
