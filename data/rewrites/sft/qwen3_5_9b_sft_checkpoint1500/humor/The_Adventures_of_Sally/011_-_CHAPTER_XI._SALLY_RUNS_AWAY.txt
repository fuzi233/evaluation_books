If Ginger Kemp had been pressed to list his virtues, the resulting inventory would likely have been sparse. He might have begun with "good intentions," though pinning that down would require significant chewing of a pencil. Tact and emotional sensitivity were certainly not on the menu. Yet, for several days following the storm between them, Ginger demonstrated a surprising degree of delicacy by keeping his distance. It wasn't easy; the urge to hover was strong. However, Ginger reasoned that suffering demanded solitude. Drawing from his own past experiences of "nasty jars," he was convinced that the injured party desired nothing more than a quiet corner to brood and fight internal battles.

But by Saturday, the philosophy of solitude began to wear thin. Saturday was a prime day for resuming threads, especially since Ginger didn't have to attend the office and, crucially, had just received his weekly salary. While Mrs. Meecher had deftly clipped a portion of it, enough remained for a princely gesture of consolation. A plan crystallized in his mind: hire a car and whisk Sally away to one of those roadhouses he'd heard rumors about along the Boston Post Road. The scheme looked judicious, and under closer inspection, increasingly brilliant.

This resolve was bolstered by the extraordinary perfection of the weather. For the first time, Ginger had experienced America's "Indian Summer," a velvet wonder that completely overwhelmed him. Standing on the roof of Mrs. Meecher's establishment on a Saturday morning, bathed in sunlight, he felt that an open-air drive with Sally was the only logical way to spend such a day.

From the rooftop of the Maison Meecher, situated on a side-street near the lower end of the avenue, one could survey a panorama of local interest. To the left lay Washington Square, teeming with somnolent Italians and children on roller skates; to the right rose the high smokestacks of a Cunard liner, lumbering slowly down the river and towering above the rooftops as if navigating Ninth Avenue itself. Today, four distinct funnels signaled the *Mauritania*. This vessel held deep sentimental value for Ginger, having been the boat that ferried him across the Atlantic from England. He watched her stately descent until the taller buildings downstream obscured her, then navigated through the hanging laundry to retrieve his hat.

A quarter-hour later, Ginger found himself in the hallway of Sally's apartment house. His gaze landed with immediate, visceral disgust on the back of his cousin, Mr. Carmyle, clad in serge and currently conversing with a man in overalls. No carefree prospector wandering the Mojave Desert who suddenly encountered a rattlesnake could undergo such an abrupt shift in mood as Ginger did at this revolting sight. Even encountering Mr. Carmyle in their native Piccadilly had been unpleasant; seeing him here was nauseating. The only explanation was obvious: Carmyle had come to see Sally. With a sinking heart, Ginger recalled the shiny, expensive automobile idling at the door. Clearly, he was not the sole individual to entertain the notion of taking Sally for a spin on this golden day.

Mr. Carmyle turned with a frown etched onto his dark face, suggesting the janitor's chatter offered no entertainment. Seeing Ginger did little to lift his gloom.
"Hullo!" Carmyle grunted.
"Hullo!" Ginger replied.
An uncomfortable silence stretched between them.
"Have you come to see Miss Nicholas?"
"Why, yes."
"She isn't here," Carmyle said. The fact that he had someone to share the bad news seemed to provide a fleeting spark of comfort.
"Not here?"
"No. Apparently..." Bruce Carmyle's scowl revealed a resentment that a balanced man feels when faced with the unreasonableness of others. "...Apparently, for some extraordinary reason, she has taken it into her head to dash over to England."

Ginger tottered. The blow was crushing in its unexpectedness. He trailed his cousin into the sunshine in a daze. Outside, Carmyle was addressing the driver of the expensive automobile.
"I find I shall not want the car. You can take it back to the garage."
The chauffeur, a moody figure, opened one half-closed eye and spat cautiously—a mannerism reminiscent of Rockefeller facing a delicate financial crisis.
"You'll have to pay just the same," the driver observed, opening his second eye to emphasize the point.
"Of course I shall pay," snapped Mr. Carmyle, irritated. "How much is it?"
Money changed hands, and the car rolled away.
"Gone to England?" Ginger asked, dizzy.
"Yes, gone to England."
"But why?"
"How the devil do I know why?" Bruce Carmyle muttered, wishing his best friend were elsewhere. Gaping Ginger caused him almost physical pain. "All I know is what the janitor told me: she sailed on the *Mauretania* this morning."

The tragic irony struck Ginger with force. He had stood on the roof, calmly watching that very boat disappear down the river...
He nodded absently to Mr. Carmyle and walked off, speechless. The warmth drained from the sunshine, and all interest vanished from his life. He felt dull, listless, untethered. Even the knowledge that his frugal cousin had wasted a day's hire for an unused car offered no solace. Ginger loafed aimlessly through the streets, wandered into the Park, and returned. The Park bored him; the streets bored him; the entire city bored him. A city devoid of Sally was a drab, futile existence, and no amount of solar brilliance could alter that reality.

Night fell, bringing with it a letter—the first genuinely pleasant event of this dreary day. The envelope bore the crest of the *Mauretania*. Ginger snatched it from the rack, clutching it like treasure, and carried it upstairs to his room.

Mrs. Meecher's rooms lacked luxury. She wasn't a fashionable decorator; adding a Morris chair to the essentials of a bedroom represented the pinnacle of pomp for a guest paying seven and a half dollars a week. Usually, the severity of his surroundings induced a gloom in Ginger at bedtime, but tonight, the magic of a letter from the right person lifted his spirits, making him almost gay. Sometimes, even illuminated texts above a washstand cannot quell the human need for connection.

Ginger approached his correspondence with ceremony. Like a child anticipating ice cream, he gloatted beforehand, eager to savor the treat. His first move was to fish the stolen photograph of Sally from his coat pocket. He studied it long and earnestly before propping it up beside his basin for reference. Next, he stripped off his coat, collar, and shoes, filled and lit his pipe, placing the pouch and matches on the Morris chair. He dragged the chair close, settling his feet on the bed, and reignited the pipe. Finally, he took up the letter. He inspected the crest, the handwriting, the postmark, and weighed the bulky paper in his hand.

He retrieved Sally's photograph from the washstand and scrutinized it once more. Then, wriggling deeply into the chair, he tore open the envelope.

"Ginger, dear."

Reading this far, Ginger felt compelled to return to the photograph, studying it with renewed intensity. He gazed at it for many minutes before laying it down and lighting his pipe again. Then he continued.

"Ginger, dear—I'm afraid this address is going to give you a shock, and I'm feeling very guilty. I'm running away, and I haven't even stopped to say good-bye. I can't help it. I know it's weak and cowardly, but I simply can't. I stood it for a day or two, and then I saw it was no good."
(He paused, glancing at the photo again.)
"(Thank you for leaving me alone and not coming around to see me. Nobody else but you would have done that. But then, nobody ever has been—or ever could be—so understanding as you.)"

"There was too much in New York to remind me. That's the worst of being happy in a place. When things go wrong, you find there are too many ghosts about. I just couldn't stand it. I tried, but I couldn't. I'm going away to get cured—if I can. Mr. Faucitt is over in England, and when I went down to Mrs. Meecher for my letters, I found one from him. His brother is dead, you know, and he has inherited, of all things, a fashionable dress-making place in Regent Street. His brother owned *Laurette et Cie*. I suppose he will sell the business later, but just at present, the poor old dear is apparently quite bewildered, and that doesn't seem to have occurred to him. He kept saying in his letter how much he wished I was with him to help him, and I was tempted and ran. Anything to get away from the ghosts and have something to do. I don't suppose I shall feel much better in England, but at least every street corner won't have associations. Don't ever be happy anywhere, Ginger. It's too big a risk, much too big a risk."

"There was a letter from Elsa Doland, too. Bubbling over with affection. We had always been tremendous friends. Of course, she never knew anything about my engagement to Gerald. I lent Fillmore the money to buy that piece, which gave Elsa her first big chance, and so she's very grateful. She says, if ever she gets the opportunity of doing me a good turn..."
