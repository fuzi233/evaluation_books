News of Pen's latest escapade spread through Chatteris with the speed of wildfire, sparking a firestorm of reactions that ranged from genuine fury to delighted amusement. To the good Doctor Portman, Pen's behavior was a personal affront, a breach of decorum that demanded immediate correction. But to Major Pendennis, the whole affair was nothing more than a source of mirth, a charming display of youthful exuberance that confirmed his nephew's robust constitution. Meanwhile, Mrs. Pendennis stood on the precipice of maternal hysteria. Hearing tales of the squabble and her son's "unchristian" conduct, she felt as though the transaction had unleashed a torrent of wretchedness, crime, and sheer annoyance upon her household. Her mind raced with visions of disaster, and she longed with desperate intensity to see Pen whisked away from Chatteris, anywhere but near the woman who, in her frantic imagination, had somehow tangled him in this mess.

When the fond mother remonstrated and the stern Doctor rebuked him for his violence, Pen treated the situation with the grave, self-important air of a young man discovering the weight of honor. He declared himself deeply sorry, insisting the insult had come entirely unprovoked. With a pomposity that belied his age, he vowed that no man could insult him without him vindicating his honor with the utmost dignity, even appealing to his uncle for validation. He asked, with feigned solemnity, whether a gentleman could have acted differently than to offer satisfaction to the chastised party.

"Vous allez trop vite, my good sir," Uncle Pendennis replied, looking slightly puzzled. He had been trying to instill in his nephew some of his own old-world notions of honor—ideas that smelled strongly of camp life and pistol duels rather than the sober sensibilities of the modern age. "Between men of the world, perhaps, I don't say; but between two schoolboys, this sort of thing is ridiculous, my dear boy—perfectly ridiculous."

"It is extremely wicked, and unlike my son," sobbed Mrs. Pendennis, her eyes wet with tears and her mind bewildered by her boy's stubborn obstinacy.

Pen kissed her cheek and declared with theatrical flair, "Women, dear mother, don't understand these matters. I put myself into Foker's hands; I had no other course to pursue."

Major Pendennis grinned and shrugged, observing that the young ones were certainly making great progress in their education. Mrs. Pendennis, however, declared Foker to be a wicked, horrid little wretch, convinced he would lead her dear boy straight into mischief if they attended the same college. "I have a great mind not to let him go at all," she confessed. Only the memory that her husband had always destined him for that specific university prevented her from issuing a total veto.

The decision was made: Pen would go to college in the next October term. Foker had promised to introduce him to the "right set," and the Major laid immense store upon this social engineering. "Mr. Foker knows the very best young men now at the University," the Major boasted. "Pen will form acquaintances there who will be of the greatest advantage through life. The young Marquis of Plinlimmon is there, eldest son of the Duke of Saint David's—Lord Magnus Charters, Lord Runnymede's son, and a first cousin of Mr. Foker... Lady Agnes will certainly invite him to Logwood." He spoke of the intimacy with Foker's son, Lord Runnymede, with such enthusiasm that he dismissed any alarm, viewing it as one of the luckiest breaks that could have happened to Pen.

Helen sighed, assuming the Major knew best. She acknowledged that Mr. Foker had been kind in the wretched business with Miss Costigan, and she was grateful, yet she couldn't shake a dim presentiment of evil. All these quarrels, riots, and displays of worldliness scared her for her boy's future.

Doctor Portman, conversely, was decidedly of the opinion that Pen must go to college. He hoped the lad would read and enjoy a moderate indulgence of the best society, confident that Pen would distinguish himself. Smirke had spoken highly of his proficiency, and the Doctor himself had heard him construe texts, finding him remarkably well-acquitted. Getting him out of Chatteris was a great point in itself. Pen, distracted from his private grief by the various rows swirling around him, gloomily agreed to obey.

August and September brought assizes, races, and a flux of company to Chatteris. Miss Fotheringay continued to act, taking her farewells from the audiences at the Chatteris Theatre. Yet, nobody seemed particularly affected by her presence or her announced departure, save for the few we have named. The polite county folk, who likely admired the Fotheringay prodigiously in London when fashion dictated it, found nothing remarkable in the actress performing on the little Chatteris boards. Many a genius and many a quack had met a similar fate before and since Miss Costigan's time. Meanwhile, this honest woman bore up against public neglect and life's crosses with her usual equanimity, eating, drinking, acting, and sleeping with the regularity and comfort belonging to her temperament. What a deal of grief, care, and harmful excitement a healthy dullness and cheerful insensibility avoids! Nor do I mean to say that Virtue is not Virtue because it is never tempted to go astray; only that dullness is a much finer gift than we give it credit for, and some people are very lucky whom Nature has endowed with that great anodyne.

Pen used to go drearily in and out from the play during this season, proceeding much according to his fancy. His antics tortured his mother, and her anxiety would have led her to interfere often had not the Major constantly checked her, while simultaneously encouraging her. The wily man of the world fancied he saw a favorable turn in Pen's malady. It was the violent efflux of versification, among other symptoms, that gave Pen's guardian and physician satisfaction. He might be heard spouting verses in the shrubbery walks or muttering them between his teeth while sitting with the home party in the evenings. One day, prowling about the house in Pen's absence, the Major found a great book full of verses in the lad's study. They were in English and Latin, with quotations from classic authors given in the scholastic manner in the footnotes. *He can't be very bad,* wisely thought the Pall-Mall Philosopher. He made Pen's mother remark, perhaps without a secret feeling of disappointment for she loved romance like other soft women, that the young gentleman had come home quite hungry to dinner at night and showed a very decent appetite at breakfast. "Gad, I wish I could," said the Major, thinking ruefully of his dinner pills. "The boy begins to sleep well, depend upon that." It was cruel, but it was true.

Having no other soul to confide in—he could not speak to his mother of his loves and disappointments—his uncle treated them in a scornful and worldly tone that, though carefully guarded and polite, jarred greatly on Mr. Pen's feelings. Foker was much too coarse to appreciate those refined sentimental secrets. Consequently, the lad's friendship for the Curate redoubled; he was never tired of having Smirke as a listener on that one subject. What is a lovee without a confidant? Pen employed Mr. Smirke, as Corydon does the elm-tree, to cut out his mistress's name upon. He made him echo with the name of the beautiful Amaryllis. When men have left off playing the tune, they do not care much for the pipe; but Pen thought he had a great friendship for Smirke because he could sigh out his loves and griefs into his tutor's ears. Smirke, for his own reasons, was always ready at the lad's call.

Pen's affection gushed out in a multitude of sonnets to the friend of his heart, which the other received with great sympathy. He plied Smirke with Latin Sapphics and Alcaics. The love-songs multiplied under his fluent pen, and Smirke declared and believed they were beautiful. On the other hand, Pen expressed a boundless gratitude that Heaven should have sent him such a friend at such a moment. He presented his tutor with his best-bound books, his gold guard-chain, and wanted him to take his double-barrelled gun. He went into Chatteris and got a gold pencil-case on credit (for he had no money and was still in debt to Smirke for some of the Fotheringay presents), which he presented to Smirke with an inscription indicative of his unalterable and eternal regard. The Curate, of course, was pleased with every mark of the boy's attachment.

The poor Curate was naturally very much dismayed at the contemplated departure of his pupil. When Arthur should go, Smirke's occupation and delight would go too. What pretext could he find for a daily visit to Fairoaks and that kind word or glance from the lady there, which was as necessary to the Curate as the frugal dinner which Madame Fribsby served him? Arthur gone, he would only be allowed to make visits like any other acquaintance. Little Laura could not accommodate him by learning the Catechism more than once a week. He had curled himself like ivy round Fairoaks; he pined at the thought that he must lose his hold of the place. Should he speak his mind and go down on his knees to the widow? He thought over any indications in her behavior which flattered his hopes. She had praised his sermons three weeks before; she had thanked him exceedingly for his present of a melon, for a small dinner-party which Mrs. Pendennis gave; she said she should always be grateful to him for his kindness to Arthur. When he declared that there were no bounds to his love and affection for that dear boy, she had certainly replied in a romantic manner, indicating her own strong gratitude and regard to all her son's friends. Should he speak out? Or should he delay? If he spoke and she refused him, it was awful to think that the gate of Fairoaks might be shut upon him forever—and within that door lay all the world for Mr. Smirke.

Thus, oh friendly readers, we see how every man in the world has his own private griefs and business, by which he is more cast down or occupied than by the affairs or sorrows of any other person. While Mrs. Pendennis is disquieting herself about losing her son, and that anxious hold she has had of him, as long as he has remained in the mother's nest, whence he is about to take flight into the great world beyond—while the Major's great soul chafes and frets, inwardly vexed as he thinks what great parties are going on in London, and that he might be sunning himself in the glances of Dukes and Duchesses, but for those cursed affairs which keep him in a wretched little country hole—while Pen is tossing between his passion and a more agreeable sensation, unacknowledged yet, but swaying him considerably, namely, his longing to see the world—Mr. Smirke has a private care watching at his bedside, and sitting behind him on his pony; and is no more satisfied than the rest of us. How lonely we are in the world; how selfish and secret, everybody! You and your wife have pressed the same pillow for forty years and fancy yourselves united. Psha, does she cry out when you have the gout, or do you lie awake when she has the toothache? Your artless daughter, seemingly all innocence and devoted to her mamma and her piano-lesson, is thinking of neither, but of the young Lieutenant with whom she danced at the last ball—the honest frank boy just returned from school is secretly speculating upon the money you will give him, and the debts he owes the tart-man. The old grandmother crooning in the corner and bound to another world within a few months, has some business or cares which are quite private and her own—very likely she is thinking of fifty years back, and that night when she made such an impression, and danced a cotillon with the Captain before your father proposed for her: or, what a silly little overrated creature your wife is, and how absurdly you are infatuated about her—and, as for your wife—O philosophic reader, answer and say,—Do you tell her all? Ah, sir—a distinct universe walks about under your hat and under mine—all things in nature are different to each—the woman we look at has not the same features, the dish we eat from has not the same taste to the one and the other—you and I are but a pair of infinite isolations, with some fellow-islands a little more or less near to us. Let us return, however, to the solitary Smirke.

Smirke had one confidante for his passion—that most injudicious woman, Madame Fribsby. How she became Madame Fribsby, nobody knows: she had left Clavering to go to a milliner's in London as Miss Fribsby—she pretended that she had got the rank in Paris during her residence in that city. But how could the French king, were he ever so much disposed, give her any such title? We shall not inquire into this mystery, however. Suffice to say, she went away from home a bouncing young lass; she returned a rather elderly character, with a Madonna front and a melancholy countenance—bought the late Mrs. Harbottle's business for a song—took her elderly mother to live with her; was very good to the poor, was constant at church, and had the best of characters. But there was no one in all Clavering, not Mrs. Portman herself, who read so many novels as Madame Fribsby. She had plenty of time for this amusement, for, in truth, very few people besides the folks at the Rectory and Fairoaks employed her; and by a perpetual perusal of such works (which were by no means so moral or edifying in the days of which we write, as they are at present) she had got to be so absurdly sentimental, that in her eyes life was nothing but an immense love-match; and she never could see two people together, but she fancied they were dying for one another.
