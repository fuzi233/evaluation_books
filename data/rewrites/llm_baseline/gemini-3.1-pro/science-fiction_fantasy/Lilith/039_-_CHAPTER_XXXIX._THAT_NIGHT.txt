The children brought a strange report of the night into the day, their small voices carrying the weight of a multi-generational cycle of night-terrors. Whether the ancient dread of their sleep had seeped into their waking, or their waking fear had sunk into the marrow of their dreams, they were never at rest from it. All night, within the domestic sanctuary of the house, something had been working—something silent, something terrible. The darkness was entirely one with the silence, and that silence was the terror. It was an unnatural, acoustic dead-zone, stripped of the comforting, rhythmic hum of the living earth; an oppressive vacuum that felt less like peace and more like the sterile, suffocating hush of an industrial void. 

Once, they said, a frightful wind had filled the house, shaking its timber bones so that it quivered like a frightened horse. Yet it was a wind completely severed from its sonic landscape—it made not even a moan in their chamber, passing away like a soundless sob, a jarring rupture in the acoustic ecology of the night. They fell asleep, only to wake with a great start, tasting the damp, metallic tang of the water they had been drinking. It swelled up from below until the garret was full to the very roof, but it lacked the natural, resonant *lap* and *sob* that gives living water its soul. When the soundless tide sank away, they slept again, dry and warm. The next time they woke, the air was thick with the musky, dry scent of swarming cats. The children felt claws trying to pierce the nightgowns Lady Mara had woven for them, but not a single scratch was left upon their skin. Through the suffocating dark, the only sound that finally broke the acoustic starvation was the far-off, resonant howl of the huge great-grandmother-cat in the desert—a profound, mythic reverberation echoing across the timelines. She must have been calling her little ones, they thought, for at that ancestral sound, the swarming stopped, and all was still. 

Such was the children’s account. But I was with the veiled woman and the withered princess all through the night, keeping watch by the hearth. As soon as Mara left the room with the children, the sharp scent of fear drew my eyes to the white leopardess, cowering in a corner, terrified of what the night’s recipe would yield. 

When Mara returned, she moved with the grounded, deliberate grace of a traditional agrarian elder tending to a long-simmering root-broth. She drew the settle with Lilith upon it to the middle of the room, then sat opposite me. Between us burned a small, meticulously maintained fire, fueled by ancient peat that filled the room with the evocative, resinous aroma of bruised thyme and deep earth. The hours steeped like bitter tea. A silvery creature, resembling a slowworm, crawled out from the flickering, cloudy presences. Vivid as incandescent silver, the live heart of essential fire, it smelled sharply of struck flint and scorched ozone. It crept across the clay floor toward the hearth. 

Midnight arrived with cyclical precision. Mara rose, unwinding the long swathes that hid her face. She stood behind the princess, her countenance lovely beyond speech—white and sad, seasoned with a sorrow that could never sour into despair. Great tears ran down her cheeks, and she wiped them away, leaving an expression of severe, uncompromising pity. She laid her hands upon the princess's head, an elder assessing the viability of a blighted crop, and breathed onto the sallow brow. 

“Will you turn away from the wicked things you have been doing so long?” Mara asked, her voice carrying the warm, inviting acoustic resonance of a native mother-tongue.

The seeming corpse opened its mouth. The words that emerged appeared to frame themselves of something else than sound, structured in the dominant, unyielding lexicon of an empire she alone inhabited. 

“I will not,” Lilith said. “I will be myself and not another!”

“Alas, you are another now, not yourself! Will you not be your real self?”

“I will be what I mean myself now.”

“If you were restored, would you not make what amends you could for the misery you have caused?”

“I would do after my nature.”

“You do not know it: your nature is good, and you do evil!”

“I will do as my Self pleases—as my Self desires.”

“You have killed your daughter, Lilith!”

“I have killed thousands. She is my own!”

“She was never yours as you are another’s.”

“I am not another’s; I am my own, and my daughter is mine.”

“Then, alas, your hour is come!” 

“I care not. I am what I am; no one can take from me myself!”

“You are not the Self you imagine,” Mara said, her tone steady, diagnosing a palate ruined by the consumption of its own pride. “But another has made you, and can compel you to see what you have made yourself. You will not much longer have satisfaction in the thought of yourself.”

“No one ever made me,” Lilith spat, clinging to the power dynamics embedded in her defiance. “I defy that Power to unmake me from a free woman! You are his slave, and I defy you!”

“I am no slave, for I love that light,” Mara replied. “She alone is free who would make free; she loves not freedom who would enslave. Every life, every will, every heart that came within your ken, you have sought to consume and subdue. You are the slave of every slave you have made. See your own self!”

Mara stepped back. A soundless presence of roaring flame possessed the house. The white-hot worm of essential fire crept up the settle, laying itself at the bare, parchment-like foot of the princess. Slowly, it navigated the folds of her robe until it reached her withered bosom, disappearing into the dark center of her being to pierce the marrow of her intentions. Lilith gave one writhing, contorted shudder. 

“She is seeing herself,” Mara said, drawing me back. 

Suddenly, the princess sprang to the floor, her body arched. Her hair dripped, then stood out, emitting sparks that smelled of singed copper and sour ash, pouring the cold sweat of her torture onto the clay. 

“She is afar in the hell of her self-consciousness,” Mara explained gently. “The central fire of the universe is radiating into her the knowledge of good and evil—the true taste of what she is. She knows that she is herself the fire in which she is burning, but she does not know that the Light of Life is the heart of that fire. Wait and watch.”

At last, Lilith flung herself on her face. “Why did he make me such?” she gasped, her words coming in jagged jets. “I would have made myself—oh, so different! I will not be made any longer!”

“Unmake yourself, then.”

“Alas, I cannot! How often have I not agonized to cease, but the tyrant keeps me being! Now let him kill me!”

“You have made yourself what you are,” Mara said, “but he can remake you.”

“I will not be remade!” she forced the words through clenched teeth.

A wind woke inside the house, blowing without impact, and a silent water began to rise, devoid of the natural acoustic life of a river. Unseen and noiseless, it lifted Lilith and laid her back upon the settle before sinking swiftly away. 

Then began the profound strife of her soul. Her inner queendom, once a monolith, fractured. She began to murmur a tale about herself, and as she spoke, the syllables shifted into a dying linguistic lineage—an ancient, untranslatable dialect whose very phonetics held the lost cultural memory of a primeval world. I could catch only fragments, echoes of a time before the dominant lexicons of power had paved over the intimate expressions of the soul. She spoke of forces and vices I could scarcely digest, her subjective experience fundamentally untranslatable, locked within the phonetic boundaries of a language dying on her tongue.

When she ceased, the horror returned to her hair. “Will you restore that which you have wrongfully taken?” Mara asked.

“I have taken nothing,” Lilith hissed, her palate still clinging to the bitter scraps of her sovereignty, “that I had not the right to take. My power to take manifested my right.”

Gradually, a terrifying Negation infolded her. It was not merely the absence of light, but an absolute acoustic and sensory void—a starvation of the soul. The border of this Life-in-Death touched me, and I felt the horrifying chill of absolute disconnection from the sustaining rhythms of creation. It was the total eradication of the sonic and physical environment, an industrial silence where nothing could grow, nothing could be tasted, and nothing could be known.

With an exceeding great and bitter cry, Lilith dashed herself to the floor. It was the recoil of Being from Annihilation. “For pity’s sake,” she shrieked, “tear my heart out, but let me live!”

A perfect calm, like the resonant stillness of a summer night, fell upon us. She had won the battle against the void. She stood erect, a dareful conqueror, raising her withered arm to sing a pæan of unholy triumph—until her eyes fixed in a ghastly stare. Before her, cast from a heavenly mirror, stood the splendent beauty of what she was intended to be, juxtaposed against the ash-laden reality of what she had made herself. 

She collapsed, the pride draining from her posture. “You have conquered,” she said in the gray dawn. “Let me go into the wilderness and bewail myself.”

“Begin, then, and set right in the place of wrong,” Mara said. “Open thy hand, and let that which is in it go.”

Lilith held out her offending hand, curled into a rigid paw. “I cannot. Open it for me.”

“You must open it yourself. You have often opened it a little way. Gather your strength, and open it wide.”

Defiance flared one last time. “I will not try what I know impossible! I will yet be mistress of myself!”

Then descended the most fearful thing of all—the realization of Life in Death. A creeping chill pervaded the room, stripping away the scent of the hearth, the memory of bread, the hum of the earth. We felt the reflex of her misery; she was consciously a dead thing, locked in an unpalatable, unending consumption of her own decay. She was a dead life, unable to unmake herself, her open eyes staring into the heart of her own indestructible, untranslatable evil.

Yet, without a change of look, she finally walked toward Mara. “I yield,” the princess whispered, the dominant lexicon breaking at last. “I cannot hold out. I am defeated. Not the less, I cannot open my hand.”

“I will take you to my father,” the agrarian elder replied softly. “You have wronged him worst of the created, therefore he best of the created can help you. He will forgive you.”

“Ah, if he would but help me to cease! Let me die.”

“Thou shalt die out of death into life. Now is the Life for, that never was against thee!”

Mara put her arms around Lilith and kissed her forehead. She bore the exhausted woman to her own bed, laying her softly upon the linens. The Lady of Sorrow went to the door and opened it. 

Morn, with the Spring in her arms, waited outside. The morning wind flowed into the room, bringing with it the sweet, vegetative perfume of damp soil and the healing acoustic rhythm of the rain. It was the soft rain that heals the mown grass, a rhythmic downpour that restored the acoustic ecology of the world, filling the room with the hush that lives between music and silence. It bedewed the desert places around the cottage, and the parched, sandy palate of Lilith’s heart heard it, and drank it in. When Mara returned to sit by her bed, Lilith's tears were flowing softer than the rain, and soon, she was fast asleep.
