Struggles with one’s own nature look, to the bystander, like fruitless wrestling in a locked room: the door is shut, the noise muffled, the victory uncertain. Yet they do their quiet work. They tilt the smallest action, as a cook’s thumb turns a knife-blade, so that what is cut falls clean instead of ragged; they give conduct that turn which Reason approves, and Feeling—faithful, vehement Feeling—so often resists. They smooth the outer face of life; they keep the surface water from breaking into perpetual chop, so that the common eye, which never dives, may call the whole sea calm. What lies under—leave it with God. Your equal, frail as you, is no fit judge of what he cannot sound. Carry the secret to the Maker who gave it; show Him the spirit’s bruises; ask how to endure pains appointed; kneel and pray for light where the lamp will not burn, for strength where muscle fails, for patience when need has scraped you to the bone.

For sometimes—at some hour, not perhaps your hour; in some shape, not perhaps the beloved shape you dared to dream—help comes down, and the waters stir. Long are Heaven’s times. An angel’s going and returning may seem to mortal sight a circle wide enough to enring ages; the cycle may clasp unnumbered generations; and meanwhile dust kindles into brief suffering life and, through pain, drops again to dust—perishing out of memory, and then again perishing. To how many maimed and mourning millions is the first and sole messenger the one the East names Azrael?

In the morning I tried to rise; I attempted to dress. The mere act of lifting my arms and fastening a hook cost me as if I were tying knots in wet rope with numb fingers. Between each effort I drank from the carafe on the washstand—cold water, clean and sharp as a blade; I swallowed it for courage, as one takes a mouthful of brine to keep from fainting in a boat. While I stood so, obstinate and shaking, Mrs. Bretton came in upon me.

“Here is an absurdity!” was her greeting; and before I could shape a remonstrance she had me by the shoulders—brisk, prompt, strong in her own decided way, that way once applied to her son and vigorously resisted by him—and in two minutes she had consigned me prisoner to the French bed.

“There you lie till afternoon,” said she. “My boy left orders before he went out that such should be the case; and I can assure you my son is master, and must be obeyed. Presently you shall have breakfast.”

Presently she brought it—brought it herself, not leaving me to any servant. She sat on the bed while I ate, and the room filled with her wholesome presence as a kitchen fills with the warmth of an open oven. There are friends whose eye is a draught in sickness, whose nearness frets; but to me Mrs. Bretton’s look was like a clear window, her voice like a steady clock in the night. What she placed in my hand—tea, bread, a little delicate food—seemed to have, by the very passage through her care, a better savour and a kinder virtue. Her touch communicated reassurance as surely as if it were a spice.

Her lively black eye, her clear brunette cheek, her warm ready hand, her self-reliant mood, her decided bearing, acted on me as the air of some salubrious climate acts on lungs that have been breathing foul rooms. Her son called her “the old lady;” it always surprised me, with pleasure, to note how the alacrity and force of five-and-twenty still breathed from her and around her.

“I would bring my work here,” she said, taking the emptied cup, “and sit with you all day, if that overbearing John Graham had not put his veto on such a proceeding. ‘Now, mamma,’ he said when he went out, ‘take notice, you are not to knock up your god-daughter with gossip,’ and he particularly desired me to keep close to my own quarters and spare you my fine company. He says, Lucy, he thinks you have had a nervous fever, judging from your look—is that so?”

I answered that I hardly knew what to name my ailment, but that I had suffered much, especially in mind. Beyond that I would not go. The details belonged to a part of my existence into which I never expected my godmother to enter. Into what strange region would such a confidence have led that hale, serene nature! The difference between her and me might be figured by that between a stately ship cruising safe on smooth seas, full-manned, full-found, with a captain gay and brave, venturous and provident; and the life-boat that lies most days dry and solitary in an old dark boat-house, only putting to sea when billows run high, when cloud grapples with water, when danger and death divide between them the rule of the deep. Louisa Bretton had never been out of harbour on such a night and in such a scene. Her crew could not conceive it. So the half-drowned life-boat man keeps his own counsel and spins no yarns.

She left me. I lay content. It was good of Graham to remember me before he went out. My day was lonely, but the thought of evening shortened it as surely as a fire shortens a winter twilight. Weakness made rest welcome; and after the morning hours went by—those hours that bring even to the necessarily unoccupied a sense of tasks waiting, of something that ought to be done—when that stirring time was past, and the silent descent of afternoon hushed the housemaid’s steps on stair and landing, I slipped into a dreamy mood, not unpleasant.

My little room seemed like a cave in the sea. Its whiteness and pale green suggested foam and deep water; shell-shaped ornaments blanched on the cornice; the ceiling-angles wore white mouldings like dolphins. Even the one touch of colour, a red satin pin-cushion, looked akin to coral; even the dark shining glass might have reflected a mermaid combing out weed-green hair. When I closed my eyes I heard a gale subside—wind pressing on the house-front like a swell settling on a rock-base; heard it drawn and withdrawn far off like a tide retiring from some upper-world shore, so high above that the rush of its largest waves could sound down in this submarine home only as murmurs and a lullaby.

Evening came. Martha brought a light; with her aid I dressed quickly, stronger now than in the morning, and made my way down to the blue saloon unassisted.

Dr. John had finished his round earlier than usual. As I entered the parlour, his form was the first thing I saw: he stood in the window-recess opposite the door, reading the close type of a newspaper by the dull remainder of day. The fire shone clear, but the lamp on the table was unlit and tea not yet brought up. Mrs. Bretton—my active godmother—lay half-reclined in her deep-cushioned chair, actually lost in a nap; afterwards I learned she had been out in the open air all day, as if the weather were her cordial.

Her son, seeing me, came forward. He trod carefully, that he might not wake the sleeper; and he spoke low—his mellow voice, never sharp, modulated now to soothe rather than startle.

“This is a quiet little château,” he observed, after drawing me to a seat near the casement. “Perhaps you have noticed it in your walks; from the chaussée it is not visible. A mile beyond the Porte de Crécy you turn down a lane, soon an avenue, and that leads you through meadow and shade to the very door. It is not modern—built rather in the old style of the Basse-Ville. A manoir, more than a château. They call it ‘La Terrasse,’ because its front rises from a broad turfed walk, whence steps lead down a grassy slope to the avenue. See—yonder! The moon rises: she looks well through the tree-boles.”

Where, indeed, does the moon not look well? What scene—confined or vast—does her orb not hallow? Rosy, almost fiery, she mounted above a not distant bank; and even while we watched her flushed ascent she cleared into gold, then in a brief space floated up stainless into a now calm sky.

Did moonlight soften or sadden Dr. Bretton? Did it touch him with romance? I think it did. He was not of a sighing mood, yet as he watched, he sighed quietly to himself. I did not need to trace the course of that breath. I knew it was wakened by beauty; I knew it pursued Ginevra.

And because I knew, the thought pressed upon me that it was in some sort my duty to speak the name he meditated. He was ready; his countenance held a teeming plenitude of comment, question, interest, a pressure of language and sentiment checked only—so I thought—by an embarrassment how to begin. To spare him that embarrassment was my best, perhaps my sole use. I had but to utter the idol’s name and love’s tender litany would flow out.

I had found my phrase—“You know that Miss Fanshawe is gone on a tour with the Cholmondeleys”—and was opening my lips, when he scattered my plans by turning to another theme.

“The first thing this morning,” said he—putting sentiment into his pocket, turning from the moon, and sitting down—“I went to the Rue Fossette and told the cuisinière you were safe and in good hands. Do you know I actually found she had not yet discovered your absence from the house? She thought you safe in the great dormitory. With what care you must have been waited on!”

“Oh! all that is very conceivable,” I answered. “Goton could do nothing for me but bring me a little tisane and a crust of bread; and I had rejected both so often during the past week that the good woman grew tired of useless journeys from the dwelling-house kitchen to the school-dormitory, and only came once a day at noon to make my bed. I believe, however, she is good-natured, and would have been delighted to cook me côtelettes de mouton, if I could have eaten them.”

“What did Madame Beck mean by leaving you alone?”

“Madame Beck could not foresee that I should fall ill.”

“Your nervous system bore a good share of the suffering?”

“I am not quite sure what my nervous system is,” said I; “but I was dreadfully low-spirited.”

“Which disables me from helping you by pill or potion. Medicine can give nobody good spirits. My art halts at the threshold of hypochondria; she just looks in and sees a chamber of torture, but can neither say nor do much. Cheerful society would be of use; you should be as little alone as possible; you should take plenty of exercise.”

I assented. The remarks sounded right; they bore the safe sanction of custom and the well-worn stamp of use.

Then he recommenced—my health, nervous system included, being to my relief dismissed. “Miss Snowe, is it permitted me to ask what your religion is? Are you a Catholic?”

I looked up, startled. “A Catholic? No. Why suggest such an idea?”

“The manner in which you were consigned to me last night made me doubt.”

“I consigned to you? But indeed I forget. It yet remains for me to learn how I fell into your hands.”

“Under circumstances that puzzled me. I had been in attendance all day yesterday on a case singularly interesting and critical—the disease rare, and its treatment doubtful. I saw a similar and still finer case in a hospital in Paris; but that will not interest you. At last a mitigation of the patient’s most urgent symptoms—acute pain is one of its accompaniments—liberated me, and I set out homeward. My shortest way lay through the Basse-Ville, and as the night was excessively dark, wild, and wet, I took it. In riding past an old church belonging to a community of Béguines, I saw, by a lamp burning over the porch or deep arch of the entrance, a priest lifting some object in his arms. The lamp was bright enough to show the priest’s features clearly, and I recognised him; he is a man I have often met by sick beds of rich and poor, chiefly the latter. He is, I think, a good old man—better than most of his class here—superior in every way, better informed as well as more devoted. Our eyes met; he called on me to stop: what he supported was a woman, fainting or dying. I alighted.

“‘This person is one of your countrywomen,’ he said: ‘save her, if she is not dead.’

“My countrywoman, on examination, turned out to be the English teacher at Madame Beck’s pensionnat. You were perfectly unconscious, perfectly bloodless, nearly cold.

“‘What does it all mean?’ I asked.

“He communicated a curious account: you had been to him that evening at confessional; your exhausted and suffering appearance, coupled with some things you had said—”

“Things I had said? I wonder what things!”

“Awful crimes, no doubt; but he did not tell me what: the seal of the confessional checked his garrulity and my curiosity. Your confidences, however, had not made an enemy of the good father. He was so struck, so sorry you should be out on such a night alone, that he esteemed it a Christian duty to watch you when you quitted the church, and so to manage as not to lose sight of you till you should have reached home. Perhaps the worthy man might, half unconsciously, have blended in this proceeding a little of the subtlety of his class; it might have been his resolve to learn the locality of your home—did you impart that in your confession?”

“I did not,” I said. “On the contrary, I carefully avoided the shadow of any indication. And as to my confession, Dr. John, you will think me mad for taking such a step, but I could not help it. I suppose it was all the fault of what you call my nervous system. I cannot put the case into words. My days and nights were grown intolerable. A cruel sense of desolation pained my mind: a feeling that would make its way—rush out—or kill me; like”—and here I chose a phrase he would understand—“the current which passes through the heart, and which, if aneurism or any morbid cause obstruct its natural channels, seeks abnormal outlet. I wanted companionship; I wanted friendship; I wanted counsel. I could find none of these in closet or chamber, so I went and sought them in church and confessional. As to what I said, it was no confidence, no narrative. I have done nothing wrong: my life has not been active enough for any dark deed, either of romance or reality. All I poured out was a dreary, desperate complaint.”

“Lucy, you ought to travel for about six months: why, your calm nature is growing quite excitable! Confound Madame Beck! Has the little buxom widow no bowels, to condemn her best teacher to solitary confinement?”

“It was not Madame Beck’s fault,” I replied. “It is no living being’s fault, and I won’t hear any one blamed.”

“Who is in the wrong, then, Lucy?”

“Me—Dr. John—me; and a great abstraction on whose wide shoulders I like to lay the mountains of blame they were sculptured to bear: me and Fate.”

“‘Me’ must take better care in future,” said Dr. John, smiling, I suppose, at my grammar. “Change of air—change of scene; those are my prescriptions. But to return to our muttons. As yet Père Silas, with all his tact—they say he is a Jesuit—is no wiser than you choose him to be; for, instead of returning to the Rue Fossette, your fevered wanderings—there must have been high fever—”

“No, Dr. John: the fever took its turn that night. Now, don’t make out that I was delirious; I know differently.”

“Good! You were as collected as myself at this moment, no doubt. Your wanderings had taken an opposite direction to the pensionnat. Near the Béguinage, amidst the stress of flood and gust, and in the perplexity of darkness, you had swooned and fallen. The priest came to your succour; the physician, as we have seen, supervened. Between us we procured a fiacre and brought you here. Père Silas, old as he is, would have carried you upstairs and laid you on that couch himself. He would certainly have remained with you till suspended animation was restored; and so should I, but at that juncture a hurried messenger arrived from the dying patient I had scarcely left—the last duties were called for: the physician’s last visit and the priest’s last rite; extreme unction could not be deferred. Père Silas and I departed together. My mother was spending the evening abroad. We gave you in charge to Martha, leaving directions, which it seems she followed successfully. Now—are you a Catholic?”

“Not yet,” said I, with a smile. “And never let Père Silas know where I live, or he will try to convert me. But give him my best and truest thanks when you see him; and if ever I get rich I will send him money for his charities. See, Dr. John—your mother wakes; you ought to ring for tea.”

He rang; and as Mrs. Bretton sat up—astonished and indignant at herself for the indulgence to which she had succumbed, fully prepared to deny that she had slept at all—her son came gaily to the attack.

“Hushaby, mamma! Sleep again. You look the picture of innocence in your slumbers.”

“My slumbers, John Graham! What are you talking about? You know I never do sleep by day: it was the slightest doze possible.”

“Exactly! A seraph’s gentle lapse—a fairy’s dream. Mamma, under such circumstances you always remind me of Titania.”

“That is because you yourself are so like Bottom.”

“Miss Snowe—did you ever hear anything like mamma’s wit? She is a most sprightly woman of her size and age.”

“Keep your compliments to yourself, sir, and do not neglect your own size, which seems to me a good deal on the increase. Lucy, has he not rather the air of an incipient John Bull? He used to be slender as an eel, and now I fancy in him a sort of heavy dragoon bent—a beef-eater tendency. Graham, take notice! If you grow fat I disown you.”

“As if you could sooner disown your own personality! I am indispensable to the old lady’s happiness, Lucy. She would pine away in green and yellow melancholy if she had not my six feet of iniquity to scold. It keeps her lively; it maintains the wholesome ferment of her spirits.”

They stood opposite each other, one on each side of the fireplace. Their words were not very tender; their looks atoned. The best treasure of Mrs. Bretton’s life was casketed in her son’s bosom; her dearest pulse throbbed in his heart. As to him—of course another love shared his feelings with filial love; and no doubt, as the new passion was the latest born, he allotted it in his emotions Benjamin’s portion.

Ginevra. Ginevra. Did Mrs. Bretton yet know at whose feet her own young idol had laid his homage? Would she approve? I could not tell; but I could guess: if she knew Miss Fanshawe’s conduct towards Graham—her alternations between coldness and coaxing, repulse and allurement; if she could suspect the pain with which she had tried him; if she could have seen, as I had seen, his fine spirits harassed, his inferior preferred, his subordinate made the instrument of humiliation—then Mrs. Bretton would have pronounced Ginevra imbecile, or perverted, or both. Well—I thought so too.

That second evening passed as sweetly as the first—more sweetly indeed. We enjoyed a smoother interchange of thought; old troubles were not reverted to; acquaintance was better cemented. I felt easier, happier, more at home. And that night, instead of crying myself asleep, I went down to dreamland by a path bordered with pleasant thoughts.
