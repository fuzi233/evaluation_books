The following day, Denys seized a moment to reveal the remainder of the tale to his mother and daughter, carefully excluding Cornelis and Sybrandt from the conversation. "It is not my place to tarnish their names," he explained with his characteristic reserve, "for they are of good stock. Yet Gerard views them as enemies in this affair. As his comrade, I adhere to the soldier's code: never to speak truth to an adversary, but to lie instead."

Catherine sighed deeply but offered no rebuttal.

The adventures Denys recounted stirred a tempest of agitation and sorrow within them; they wept bitterly at the memory of the friends' parting, a scene that still caused Denys to falter in his narration. Yet, eventually, their grief merged into a hopeful anticipation of Gerard's swift return. Denys shared this confidence, though he gently reminded them that Gerard's final wishes must not be neglected. Indeed, if Gerard returned next week to find no Margaret, what manner of figure would he cut?

For the first time, Catherine felt a profound kinship toward the absent Margaret. Her anxiety to locate her and welcome her back before Gerard's arrival matched Denys's fervor, yet she could not agree with his plan to scour the entire province of Holland. "She must have confided in someone where she is going," she reasoned. "These are not dishonest fugitives fleeing debt; they owe not a stiver in Sevenbergen. And, dear heart, Denys, you cannot hunt all of Holland for one girl."

"Can I not?" Denys asked grimly. "We shall see." After a moment's reflection, he proposed a division of labor: she would remain here with eyes and ears wide open, while he would ransack every town in Holland if necessary. "But she will not be many leagues from here," he insisted. "Three do not fly as fast, nor as far, as one."

"That is sensible," Catherine conceded, though she pressed him to visit the demoiselle Van Eyck first. "She and our Margaret were bosom friends. She knows where the girl has gone, if she will but tell us." Eager to depart immediately, Denys was intercepted by Catherine, who, with a deft turn of her hand, smoothed her dress and led him into the house.

They were received graciously by the elderly lady in a richly furnished chamber. As the tapestry slipped from Margaret Van Eyck's hands, she exclaimed, "Gone? Gone from Sevenbergen without telling me? The thankless girl!"

This reaction startled the visitors. "What, you know not? When was she last here?"

"Perhaps ten days ago," the old lady replied. "I had taken out my brushes after so many years to paint her portrait. I did not do it, though; for reasons."

Catherine noted the strangeness of her departure, bag and baggage, without leave or explanation. "Was ever anything so untoward? Just when our hearts are warm toward her, and here comes Gerard's mate from the ends of the earth with comfort, only to find her missing, while Gerard himself is expected. What am I to do? Surely she did not leave without cause. Can you not give us the clue, my good demoiselle? Prithee now."

"I have no clue to give," the elder lady said, her tone becoming peevish.

"Then I can," declared Reicht Heynes, appearing in the doorway with flushed cheeks.

"So you have been listening all this time, have you?"

"What are my ears for, mistress?"

"True. Well, then, shed the light of your wisdom upon this dark matter."

"There is no darkness here," Reicht asserted. "And the clue, if you call it a two-ply twine, has its ends right here in this room. Oh, mistress, I wonder at you pretending."

"Marry, come up," the mistress said, her own cheek flushing nearly as red as the servant's. "So it was I who drove the foolish girl away."

"You did your share, mistress. What greeting did you give her the last time she came? Did she fail to notice it, being all friendless? And you said, 'I have altered my mind about painting you,' turning your nose up at her."

"I did not turn up my nose. It is not shaped like yours for looking heavenward."

"Oh, all our noses can follow our hearts' bent, for that matter. Poor soul. She came to the kitchen to me. 'I am not to be painted now,' she said, tears in her eyes. She said no more. But I knew well what she meant. I had seen you."

"Well," Margaret Van Eyck admitted, "I confess as much and make you the judge, madam. These young girls can do nothing of their own heads; they are most apt to mimic their sweethearts. Your Gerard is reasonably handy at many things, including the illuminator's craft. Margaret is his pupil, a patient one: what marvel? Having a woman's eye for color and a lover to ape. 'Tis a trick I despise at heart; for by it the great art of color, which should be royal, aspiring, and free, becomes a poor slave to the petty crafts of writing and printing, fettered and imprisoned to match the littleness of books, going to church in a rich fool's pocket. Nevertheless, affection rules us all. When the poor wench brought me her thorn leaves, lilies, ivy, dewberries, ladybirds, and butterfly grubs—all the scum of Nature stuck fast in gold-leaf like wasps in a honey-pot—along with her diurnal book showing she had pored over each page for hundreds of hours, I was wroth that an immortal soul and much manual skill should be flung away on Nature's trash. But having bowels, I restrained my better feelings and looked kindly at the work to see how it might be bettered. I said, 'Since Heaven has doomed us to spend time, soul, and color on great letters and little beetles, omitting saints and heroes, why not present the scum naturally?' I told her the grapes I saw hanging in the air did not stick in walls, and even these insects pass their noxious lives at large, not wedged miserably in metal prisons. 'Ah, my dear friend,' she said, 'I see now whither you drive; but this ground is gold; whereon we may not shade.' 'Who said so?' I asked. 'All teachers of this craft,' she replied, and to make an end of me at once, 'Gerard himself!' 'That for Gerard himself,' I said, 'and all the gang; give me a brush!'

"Then I chose to shade her fruit and reptiles with a color false in nature but true relative to that monstrous ground of glaring gold. In five minutes, out came a bunch of raspberries, stalk and all, almost flying into your mouth, and a butterfly grub so truly presented it might turn the stoutest stomach. My lady threw her arms around my neck and said, 'Oh!'"

"Did she now?"

"The little love!" observed Denys, finally managing to wedge in a word.

Margaret Van Eyck stared at him and then smiled. She continued to tell them how she had been led step by step to promise to resume the art she had laid aside with a sigh when her brothers died, and to paint the Madonna once more—with Margaret as the model. Incidentally, she revealed how girls are turned into saints. "Thy hair is adorable," I said. "'Tis red,' she replied. 'Ay,' I said, 'but what a red! How brown! How glossy! Most hair is not worth a straw to us painters; thine is the artist's very hue. But thy violet eyes, which smack of earth, being now languid for lack of one Gerard, now full of fire in hopes of the same Gerard, these will I lift to heaven in fixed and holy meditation. And thy nose, which doth already somewhat aspire that way (though not so piously as Reicht's), will I debase a trifle and somewhat enfeeble thy chin.'"

"Enfeeble her chin? Alack! What may that mean? Ye go beyond me, mistress."

"'Tis a resolute chin. Not a jot too resolute for this wicked world; but when ye come to a Madonna? No thank you."

"Well, I never. A resolute chin."

Denys. "The darling!"

"And now comes the rub. When you told me she was—the way she is, it gave me a shock; I dropped my brushes. Was I going to turn a girl, that couldn't keep her lover at a distance, into the Virgin Mary, at my time of life? I love the poor ninny still. But I adore our blessed Lady. Say you, 'a painter must not be peevish in such matters'? Well, most painters are men; and men are fine fellows. They can do aught. Their saints and virgins are neither more nor less than their lemans, saving your presence. But know that for this very reason half their craft is lost on me, which finds beneath their angels' white wings the very trollops I have seen flaunting it on the streets, bejewelled like Paynim idols, and put on like the queens in a pack of cards. And I am not a fine fellow, but only a woman, and my painting is but one-half craft, and the other half devotion. So now you may read me. 'Twas foolish, maybe, but I could not help it; yet am I sorry." The old lady ended despondently a discourse she had commenced in a mighty defiant tone.

"Well, you know, dame," observed Catherine, "you must think it would go to the poor girl's heart, and she so fond of you?"

Margaret Van Eyck only sighed.

The Frisian girl, after biting her lips impatiently for a moment, turned upon Catherine. "Why, dame, do you think 'twas for that alone that Margaret and Peter left Sevenbergen? Nay."

"For what else, then?"

"What else? Why, because Gerard's people slight her so cruelly. Who would bide among hard-hearted folk that have driven her lad to Italy, and now he is gone, relented not, but face it out, and ne'er come nigh her that is left?"

"Reicht, I was going."

"Oh, ay, going, and going, and going. Ye should have said less or else done more. But with your words you did uplift her heart and let it down with your deeds. 'They have never been,' said the poor thing to me, with such a sigh. Ay, here is one who can feel for her: for I too am far from my friends, and often, when first I came to Holland, I used to take a hearty cry all to myself. But ten times liefer would I be Reicht Heynes with nought but the leagues between me and all my kith, than be as she is in the midst of them that ought to warm to her, and yet fare as lonesome as I."

"Alack, Reicht, I did go but yestreen, and had gone before, but one plaguy thing or t'other did still come and hinder me."

"Mistress, did aught hinder you to eat your dinner any one of those days? I trow not. And had your heart been as good towards your own flesh and blood as 'twas towards your flesher's meat, nought had prevailed to keep you from her that sat lonely, watching the road for you and comfort, with your child's child beating 'neath her bosom."

Here this rude young woman was interrupted by an incident not uncommon in a domestic's bright existence. The Van Eyck had been nettled by the attack on her, but with due tact had gone into ambush. She now sprang out of it. "Since you disrespect my guests, seek another place!"

"With all my heart," said Reicht stoutly.

"Nay, mistress," put in the good-natured Catherine. "True folk will still speak out. Her tongue is a stinger." Tears came into the speaker's eyes by way of confirmation. "But better she said it than thought it. So now 't won't rankle in her. And part with her for me, that shall ye not. Beshrew the wench, she wots she is a good servant, and takes advantage. We poor wretches which keep house must still pay 'em tax for value. I had a good servant once, when I was a young woman. Eh dear, how she did grind me down into the dust. In the end, by Heaven's mercy, she married the baker, and I was my own woman again. 'So,' said I, 'no more good servants shall come hither, a hectoring o' me.' I just get a fool and learn her; and whenever she knoweth her right hand from her left, she sauceth me: then out I bundle her neck and crop, and take another dunce in her place. Dear heart, 'tis wearisome, teaching a string of fools by ones; but there—I am mistress:" here she forgot that she was defending Reicht, and turning rather spitefully upon her, added, "and you be mistress here, I trow."

"No more than that stool," said the Van Eyck loftily. "She is neither mistress nor servant; but Gone. She is dismissed the house, and there's an end of her. What, did ye not hear me turn the saucy baggage off?"

"Ay, ay. We all heard ye," said Reicht, with vast indifference.

"Then hear me!" said Denys solemnly.

They all went round like things on wheels and fastened their eyes on him.

"Ay, let us hear what the man says," urged the hostess. "Men are fine fellows, with their great hoarse voices."

"Mistress Reicht," said Denys, with great dignity and ceremony, indeed so great as to verge on the absurd, "you are turned off. If on a slight acquaintance I might advise, I'd say, since you are a servant no more, be a mistress, a queen."

"Easier said than done," replied Reicht bluntly.
