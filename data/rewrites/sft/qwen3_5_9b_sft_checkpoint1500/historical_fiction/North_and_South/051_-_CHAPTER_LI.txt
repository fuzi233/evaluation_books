The air was heavy with the heat of a late summer afternoon. Edith stepped into the bedchamber, having already removed her outdoor gown for the evening's rest, though she would need it again shortly for dinner. Initially, the room stood empty save for the shadows lengthening across the floor. Moments later, Dixon appeared, arranging a dress on the bed, yet Margaret Hale herself was nowhere to be found. Edith paced, her impatience mounting.

"Oh, Dixon!" Edith cried, her voice tinged with irritation. "Do not lay out those dreadful blue blossoms against that gown of faded gold. It clashes horribly. Wait, please, and I shall fetch some pomegranate blooms instead."

"It is not a dead gold, madam," Dixon protested, her patience worn thin by the mistress's whims. "It is a straw hue. And blue pairs best with straw." Before Dixon could finish her objection, Edith had already returned with a cluster of brilliant scarlet flowers, pinning them to Margaret's dark hair with a flourish.

"Where is Miss Hale?" Edith demanded, testing the arrangement. "I cannot fathom how Aunt allowed her to drift into such erratic habits in Milton! I am constantly fearing she has stumbled upon some horror amidst those desolate streets. I would never venture down them unescorted; they are unfit for a lady."

Dixon, stung by the insult to her aesthetic judgment, replied curtly. "It is no wonder, in my opinion, that the world speaks so much of 'ladies' and treats themselves with such excessive delicacy. If women remain so fragile and dainty, I say there will be no saints left upon earth."

"Oh, Margaret!" Edith exclaimed, turning to find her cousin standing by the window. "Here you are! I have been searching everywhere. Look at you, child! Your cheeks are burning with the heat. Truly, that tiresome Mr. Henry has gone beyond all bounds. Just as I had finalized my guests so carefully, fitting them precisely for Mr. Colthurst, he arrives with an apology, using your name as an excuse, and asks if he may bring Mr. Thornton of Milton—the gentleman who rents from you, I presume—who is currently in London handling some legal affairs. It will ruin my arrangement entirely."

"I do not care for dinner," Margaret said softly, her voice lacking its usual warmth. "Dixon, prepare some tea here. I shall join you in the drawing-room by the time you return upstairs. Honestly, I feel I require lying down."

"No, no!" Edith hurried forward, smoothing the skirts of her dress. "That is absolutely impossible. You look dreadfully pale, yes, but surely it is merely the weather, and we cannot function without you. [Adjusts flowers] Those blooms look like glorious flames framing your black hair, Margaret. Besides, consider the irony: we intended for you to discuss Milton with Mr. Colthurst. Now, behold, a man directly from Milton has arrived. I believe it will prove fortuitous, after all. Mr. Colthurst can interrogate him thoroughly on every topic that interests him, and it will be delightful to trace your past experiences alongside Mr. Thornton's insights for Mr. Colthurst's upcoming speech in the House. I confess, I think Henry's intervention was a stroke of luck. I asked him if he was a man whose acquaintance one would deem shameful, and he retorted, 'Not if you possess any sense within you, my little sister.' Therefore, I assume he possesses the ability to pronounce his 'h's correctly—a rare accomplishment for a native of Darkshire—do you agree, Margaret?"

"Did Mr. Lennox mention why Mr. Thornton has ascended to London?" Margaret asked, her tone tight and controlled. "Is it legal business concerning the estate?"

"Oh! He has failed, or something similar," Edith dismissed lightly, recalling a previous conversation. "Henry mentioned it to you the day you suffered that severe headache—which was it? [To Dixon] There, that is perfect. Miss Hale looks magnificent, does she not? I wish I were as tall as a queen and as tanned as a gypsy, Margaret."

"But regarding Mr. Thornton?"

"Oh! I truly lack the capacity for legal minutiae. Henry delights in recounting every detail. From what I gathered, he appears to be in dire straits, yet a man of considerable respectability, and I was instructed to treat him with the utmost civility. Since I was unsure how to proceed, I sought your counsel. Now, descend with me and recline upon the sofa for fifteen minutes."

Captain Henry Lennox arrived punctually. As he sat, Margaret, flushing slightly, inquired about the circumstances surrounding Mr. Thornton.

"He has come to London regarding the sub-leasing of the property—Marlborough Mills, along with the adjacent houses and grounds. He finds himself unable to maintain the lease, and various deeds and agreements must be reviewed and renegotiated. I trust Edith will receive him with propriety; indeed, she seemed somewhat displeased by my eagerness to secure an invitation for him. However, I believed you would appreciate some attention being shown to him. One must exercise particular scruple in honoring a man who is falling from prosperity." He lowered his voice, addressing Margaret intimately, though as he concluded, he sprang up and presented Mr. Thornton, who had just entered, to Edith and Captain Lennox.

Margaret watched Mr. Thornton with an anxious gaze as he was introduced. It had been well over a year since their last encounter, and time had altered him profoundly. Though his stature remained commanding, elevating him above the average man, and granting him a distinguished bearing derived from the natural grace of his movements, his visage appeared aged and etched with worry. Yet, a noble composure rested upon his features, imparting to observers who had learned of his financial collapse a profound sense of innate dignity and masculine fortitude. Aware from his first survey of the room that Margaret was present—he had noted her intent, occupied expression as she listened to Captain Lennox—he approached her with the measured poise of an old friend. His first, calm words sent a flush of vivid color rushing into her cheeks, which refused to fade throughout the evening. She possessed little to say to him. She disconcerted him by asking, quietly, what seemed to him merely necessary inquiries about her former acquaintances in Milton. Others, more intimate within the household, interjected, pushing him into the background where he conversed intermittently with Captain Lennox.

"You perceive that Miss Hale is in good health, do you not?" Captain Lennox remarked. "Milton did not agree with her constitution, I surmise; when she first arrived in London, I am convinced I had never witnessed anyone so transformed. Tonight, she radiates vitality. She is significantly stronger. Last autumn, she succumbed to exhaustion from a walk of several miles. On Friday evening, we trekked up to Hampstead and returned. Yet, on Saturday, she appeared as robust as she does tonight."

"We?" Who? The two of them alone?

Mr. Colthurst, a man of sharp intellect and rapid rise in Parliament, possessed a keen eye for character. He was struck by a remark Mr. Thornton made during dinner. Curious, he questioned Edith regarding the identity of that gentleman. To Edith's surprise, the tone of Mr. Colthurst's "Indeed!" suggested that Mr. Thornton of Milton was not an obscure figure to him as she had presumed. Edith's dinner proceeded well. Captain Henry was in high spirits, deploying his dry, caustic wit with skill. Mr. Thornton and Mr. Colthurst discovered a few mutual topics of interest, touched upon briefly, reserving deeper discussion for private conversation after meals. Margaret appeared resplendent in her pomegranate flowers; though she leaned back in her chair and spoke sparingly, Edith felt no annoyance, for the conversation flowed smoothly around her absence. Margaret, however, was studying Mr. Thornton's face. He never looked directly at her; thus, she observed him unobserved, noting the subtle changes wrought by this brief interval. Occasionally, prompted by an unexpected word from Captain Lennox, his face flared with the old look of intense enjoyment: the merry brightness returned to his eyes, his lips parted to suggest the brilliant smiles of yesteryears; for an instant, his gaze instinctively sought hers, seeking her sympathy. But when their eyes met, his entire countenance shifted; he became grave and anxious once more, and he resolved to avoid looking near her again during the meal.

Only two ladies accompanied their party, and as these were engaged in conversation with her aunt and Edith, Margaret idly attended to some needlework in the drawing-room. Soon, the gentlemen joined them, Mr. Colthurst and Mr. Thornton deep in discourse. Captain Lennox approached Margaret, whispering, "I genuinely believe Edith owes me gratitude for my contribution to her gathering. You have no concept how agreeable and sensible this tenant of yours is. He has been the ideal interlocutor for coaching Mr. Colthurst on the facts he required. I cannot comprehend how he managed to mismanage his affairs."
