The carnivals of Florence had faded into the gray dust of Lent, leaving Romola with a singular, grinding task: the support of her cousin Brigida, whose failing health mirrored the city's own decay. Yet, beneath this domestic duty, a fever of anticipation burned within her. Bernardo del Nero, now Gonfaloniere, had secured the Medicean victory through sheer force of will, but Romola sensed the trap closing. Every morning, as the pale dawn breached her curtains, the same dread returned—the certainty that her godfather's two-month tenure was merely the incubation period for a catastrophe, whether a glorious restoration or a bloody betrayal. This fear accompanied her through the crowded streets toward the Duomo, only to dissolve into the roar of the crowd, much like the terror of death vanishes amidst the chaos of battle.

Inside the cathedral, the atmosphere shifted from dread to a terrifying, electric intensity. Savonarola preached his final Lenten sermons, aware that excommunication loomed like a storm cloud. He spoke without euphemisms, holding up the rotting body of the Church in a mirror of unflinching truth. He promised a coming revolution, a resurrection of the faithful akin to Lazarus rising from the tomb, though he also foresaw his own martyrdom. To Romola, whose soul resonated with his fervor, this crisis felt personal. His words became the fuel for her daily labors among the starving and the sick, transforming simple pity into a resolve to serve a higher, albeit distant, heroic end.

Yet, the harmony of this mission was fractured by discordant notes. The initial unity of the reform movement had shattered; the dream of French salvation had evaporated, replaced by political intrigue and renewed hostility. Savonarola's sermons grew sharper, laced with defiance and a desperate craving for divine validation. He moved from denying miracles to demanding them, threatening vengeance upon tyrants with a voice thick with personal rage. While Romola recoiled at the fanaticism bleeding into his rhetoric, others embraced it. Baldassarre, driven by a thirst for justice, attended these services specifically to hear the Prophet's threats. He watched Romola closely, timing his approach for a moment when fate—or desperation—would compel her to listen. Twice their gazes locked in the nave; Romola wished to break the connection, yet she lingered, waiting for a sign that never came.

As April drew to a close, the feared coup failed to materialize. Rumors of Piero de' Medici's machinations died with Bernardo's impending departure. Just as Romola began to dismiss her anxieties as futile, a messenger from Camilla Rucellai intercepted her. Camilla, one of Florence's most notorious female prophets, demanded an immediate audience regarding matters of supreme urgency. Though Romola harbored a visceral aversion to the shrill, hysterical nature of these "seers," especially given Camilla's recent attacks on Bernardo, she suppressed her instinct to flee. Habit and a gnawing conscience overrode her impulse to remain silent; she complied, stepping into the trap of her own making.

Camilla awaited her in a chamber stripped bare, resembling a monk's cell. The prophetess clutched Romola's arm with a grip of iron, her voice trembling with manic energy. She claimed a vision from Romola's own Angel, revealing that Romola possessed secret knowledge that could save the Republic. As Camilla escalated her tirade, claiming Christ and the Blessed Infant had commanded her to expel Bernardo del Nero from the Palazzo Vecchio window—a directive allegedly endorsed by Savonarola himself—Romola's composure snapped. With a surge of righteous fury, she tore her arm free, screaming that Camilla was either mad or wicked.

Driven from the room, Romola fled until she reached the quiet sanctuary of the Badia. Collapsing onto the steps before Filippino Lippi's serene depiction of the Virgin, she sought refuge from the clamor of the street and the storm within her mind. Her greatest fear was not the accusation itself, but the implication that Savonarola had sanctioned such barbarity. She knew his teachings were rooted in law and order; he could not condone murder disguised as prophecy. The answer haunted her: Savonarola was paralyzed. Internally, he feared admitting that such visions stemmed from sources other than his own; externally, he dreaded alienating his followers by condemning the very spiritual phenomena that bolstered his authority.

Kneeling there, Romola faced a crushing contradiction. The intellectual allure of Savonarola's grand design clashed violently with the emotional horror of his methods. She loved her godfather, remembered her father, and believed in the necessity of resisting tyranny, yet she could not reconcile these truths with the fanaticism that now defined their cause. The struggle between reason and feeling tore at her, leaving her unable to find solace even in the holy images surrounding her. Rising abruptly, she resolved to return to her suffering neighbors, seeking strength in tangible acts of kindness rather than abstract dogma. But as she turned to leave, the sanctuary doors seemed to part, and she found herself confronting the man she had been avoiding: Baldassarre.
