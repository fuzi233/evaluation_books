The history of mankind, if guilt were weighed solely by the scale of the sin committed, would surely reveal a different order. The architects of greedy wars and the ruthless predators of finance markets would not march in separate ranks from the petty thief and the butcher who slaughters with his own hands. They would all gather in a single procession of self-flagellating penitents. While wickedness does dispense rewards, the victors in this devilish game inevitably prove themselves baser, crueller, and brutish than the fabric of our world permits for the vast majority of humanity. Most humans carry a conscience—a shadow of justice born of fear, and a shadow of love born of pity—that prevents them from achieving the serenity of total depravity, a state too fragile to sustain itself within the complex nature of flesh.

On the twenty-ninth of December, Daniel Deronda knew the Grandcourts had arrived at the Abbey, yet he had not caught a glimpse of them until he retired to dress for dinner. A magnificent snowfall had blanketed the grounds, offering the children a rare joy in snowball fights and castle building. During the Christmas holidays, the Mallinger girls were content with no amusement that "Cousin" Deronda did not join and manage. Following this outdoor exertion, he had played billiards, and the hours slipped by without him dwelling on the prospect of meeting Gwendolen at dinner. Yet, the anticipation remained. As he retired to his room, slightly tired and flushed, he speculated on the influence her marriage to Grandcourt would exert on her. He wondered if there would be discernible shifts in her demeanor compared to her time at Diplow or her first vision at Leubronn.

"I fancy there are some natures one could see growing or degenerating every day, if one watched them closely," he mused. "Some move faster than others. I am sure she retains strong traces of whatever once impressed her deeply. That little affair with the necklace, the scandal regarding her gambling, had clearly bitten into her soul. But such impressibility cuts both ways; it may drive a person to desperation as swiftly as to virtue. Whatever fascinations Grandcourt holds for her capricious tastes—who can believe he possesses the capacity to summon tender affection in daily companionship? One might be tempted to strike him with a whip just to provoke a shred of passion from his face. I fear she married him out of ambition, to escape poverty. But why did she initially avoid him? The poverty came later, though. Poor thing! She may have been urged into it. How can one feel anything but pity for a young creature, full of unused life, ignorantly rash, hanging all her blind expectations on what remains of a human being?"

Deronda acknowledged that applying these meditations to the groom inevitably cast pity onto the bride. His notion of Grandcourt as a "remnant" sprang not from specific knowledge, but from the impression of ordinary polite intercourse that Grandcourt had worn out all natural interest in life. In marriages of consequence, male acquaintances tend to pity the bride, assuming she could do better, while females pity the bridegroom, viewing his situation as retributive justice. Perhaps Deronda was excused for withholding pity for Grandcourt, who had never struck acquaintances as a man likely to suffer more than he inflicted. Conversely, Gwendolen—youthful, headlong, eager for pleasure, fed by flattery that convinced her of a divine right to rule—risked a swift turn from expectancy to a bitter realization of the irremediable. After what he had witnessed, he must have had rather dull feelings not to look forward with interest to her entrance. However, with the honeymoon three weeks past and Gwendolen already enthroned at Ryelands and Diplow, she was likely to compose her countenance, masking her feelings behind a suitable facade.

A various party had gathered in the drawing-room before the new couple appeared. The aristocracy was represented by Lord and Lady Pentreath; the old gentry by the young Mr. and Mrs. Fitzadam; politics and the cider interest by Mr. Fenn and his daughters; Lady Mallinger's family by her brother, Mr. Raymond, and his wife; and the useful bachelor element by Mr. Sinker and Mr. Vandernoodt, whom Sir Hugo had found pleasant at Leubronn. Time passed chiefly in observing the children—various little Raymonds and Lady Mallinger's three girls. The scene was delightful, framed by full-length portraits and illuminated by the red fire of oak-boughs and pale wax-lights, stilled by deep carpets and high English breeding. Lady Mallinger, with her fair matronly roundness and mildly prominent blue eyes, moved about in black velvet, carrying a tiny white dog. The children were scattered among the ladies, while the gentlemen stood aloof, conversing with moderate vivacity. Deronda stood somewhat apart in a dialogue fixed upon him by Mr. Vandernoodt, a man of Dutch blood who was industrious in gathering personal details but silent on philosophy.

"Men who have seen a good deal of life don't always choose their wives well," Mr. Vandernoodt remarked, implying he had learned much about Grandcourt since Leubronn. "He has had rather an anecdotic history... gone deep into pleasures."

"No, really," Deronda replied in an indifferent tone. "I know little more of him than that he is Sir Hugo's nephew."

Just then, the door opened. The scene set off any figure of distinction that entered, and certainly, when Mr. and Mrs. Grandcourt stepped in, their figures commanded attention. The bridegroom wore an easy perfection of costume, his face an impassible mask. It was to be expected that he would accept nothing less than the best in outward equipment, including his wife. The bride was what he might have been expected to choose. "By George, I think she's handsomer, if anything!" said Mr. Vandernoodt. Deronda agreed silently. White silk and diamonds flashed around her neck, ears, and hair, enhancing her new imposingness. Some faces of peculiar beauty are like original artworks, always greeted with questions. At Diplow, Deronda had discerned a tender, appealing charm in Gwendolen, a womanly allure beyond expectation. Was there any new change? Distrusting his impressions, he noted her receiving greetings with a proud, cold quietude and a superficial smile. Within her, the same demonic force seemed to work that had possessed her when she took his resolute glance at the gaming table and turned away a loser. There was no time for further conclusion before the summons to dinner.

Seated not far from her, Deronda could sometimes hear her answers to Sir Hugo, who was at his liveliest. Though he intended to bow, she gave him no opportunity for some time. Finally, Sir Hugo, seemingly unaware they had not spoken, addressed him: "Deronda, you will like to hear what Mrs. Grandcourt tells me about your favorite Klesmer."

Gwendolen's eyelids had lowered. Deronda, already looking at her, thought he discovered a quivering reluctance as she raised them to return his unembarrassed bow and smile, hers being merely a smile of the lip. Sir Hugo continued promptly, "The Arrowpoints have condoned the marriage, and he is spending the Christmas with his bride at Quetcham."

"I suppose he will be glad of it for the sake of his wife, else I dare say he would not have minded keeping at a distance," Deronda said.

"It's a sort of troubadour story," said Lady Pentreath, an easy, deep-voiced old lady. "I'm glad to find a little romance left among us. I think our young people now are getting too worldly wise."

"It shows the Arrowpoints' good sense to adopt the affair after the fuss in the papers," Sir Hugo agreed. "Disowning your own child because of a *mésalliance* is something like disowning your one eye: everybody knows it's yours, and you have no other to make an appearance with."

"As to *mésalliance*, there's no blood on any side," Lady Pentreath countered. "Old Admiral Arrowpoint was one of Nelson's men, a doctor's son. And we all know how the mother's money came."

"If they were any *mésalliance*, I should say it was on Klesmer's side," Deronda interjected.

"Ah, you think it is a case of the immortal marrying the mortal. What is your opinion?" Sir Hugo asked, looking at Gwendolen.

"I have no doubt that Herr Klesmer thinks himself immortal. But I dare say his wife will burn as much incense before him as he requires," Gwendolen replied, recovering her composure.

"Don't you approve of a wife burning incense before her husband?" Sir Hugo asked with jocoseness.

"Oh, yes," said Gwendolen, "if it were only to make others believe in him." She paused, then added with more gaiety, "When Herr Klesmer admires his own genius, it will take off some of the absurdity if his wife says Amen."

"Klesmer is no favorite of yours, I see," Sir Hugo noted.

"I think very highly of him, I assure you," Gwendolen insisted. "His genius is quite above my judgment, and I know him to be exceedingly generous." She spoke with the sudden seriousness often meant to correct an indiscreet sally, harboring a bitterness against Klesmer in her secret soul that she knew she could not justify.
