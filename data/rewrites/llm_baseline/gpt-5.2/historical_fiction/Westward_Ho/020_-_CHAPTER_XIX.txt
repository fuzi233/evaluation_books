The men would willingly have lingered yet a while about Margarita and Cubagua, hoping for another stroke at the pearl-trade; for the thought of those milky beads—wrenched from brown throats by Spanish greed and Indian misery—had set every tongue wagging. But Amyas, who said grimly that he had “fleshed his dogs,” would not hang about the islands after the alarm. So they bore away south-west across the mouth of that vast bay that lies between Paria and Codera, leaving on their right Tortuga, and on their left the meadow-islands of the Piritoos—two long green blades laid flat upon a sea that seemed, in that place, almost tideless and too smooth for its own good.

Yeo and Drew knew every bend of the coast; they had earned that knowledge, as men earn it who trade where trading is half war, and war is never far from hunger. The ship’s world was all homely routine—hands at braces, the wet slap of rope-ends, the perpetual scrape and creak of timber—yet the horizon ahead began to rise into something unhomely: the Caracas mountains, lifting sheer out of the sea, higher and higher, till the very notion of height which most of the crew had carried from Devon and Cornwall was made ridiculous. Frank had seen the Alps; Amyas had looked on the Andes; but Cary’s imagination had been bounded by M’Gillicuddy’s Reeks, and Brimblecombe’s by Exmoor. Brimblecombe, to Cary’s infinite mirth, spent a whole day clinging to the rigging and staring upward with his chin higher than his nose, until his neck stiffened and he groaned as if the mountain had set a hand upon him.

Soon the water grew rough and chopping, though the breeze was fair and gentle. The ship met, as it were, an invisible river running eastward in the sea—one of those winter currents that mock a westering mariner. All night they struggled through broken billows, with the huge wall of Cape Codera a thousand feet above them on the left; and beyond it, bank upon bank of mountains, washed in yellow moonlight. Even the ship’s noises altered under that cliff: the wind sounded thinner, as if drawn through a narrow throat; the surf below the rock had a steady, animal breathing; and the men, hearing those new voices about them, grew strangely quiet between orders.

Morning showed them a large ship that had passed in the night upon the opposite course, and now lay a good ten miles to the eastward. Yeo, with that eager righteousness which made him smell prize as a hound smells blood, would have gone back and taken her; he spoke of the thing as if it were already done, and their guns already telling out a psalm of iron.

“Why, Yeo,” said Amyas, “you told me one day more would bring us to La Guayra.”

“All the more reason, sir,” said Yeo, “to do the Lord’s work thoroughly when He has brought us safely so far.”

“She can pass well enough,” said Amyas. “No loss.”

“Ah, sirs, sirs,” Yeo answered, shaking his head, “she is delivered into your hands, and you will have to give an account of her.”

Frank, whose mind was elsewhere and yet could not bear to have their purpose blurred, took it up quickly. “We shall give account enough of many a tall Spaniard before we return; but La Guayra—and the salvation of one whom we believe dwells there—was our first object.”

Yeo’s eyes had that old, sad look which seemed to listen not to men but to judgment. “A lady brought Captain Oxenham to ruin.”

“You do not dare compare her with this one?” Cary and Frank cried together.

“God forbid,” said Yeo; “yet no venture will prosper unless it have a single eye to the Lord’s work; and that is, to cripple the Spaniard and exalt her majesty. I had thought nothing was dearer than that to Captain Leigh’s heart.”

Amyas stood a moment between two duties, both sharp as points. The queen’s service urged him to turn; his vow drove him on. It sounds a scholar’s dilemma when read in quiet; on deck, with land under the lee and an enemy to windward, it was practical enough.

Frank’s counsel prevailed; they held on for La Guayra. Amyas half hoped the Spaniard would see and come down upon them, so that duty and desire might be made one by a cannon-shot. But the Spaniard went his way eastward; and if he had not, the tale that follows would have had another ending.

About mid-day a canoe—the first they had seen—came staggering towards them under a huge three-cornered sail. She rode the short seas like a gourd, and yet came on with a boldness that made Cary laugh.

“Metal floats in these seas, you see,” said he. “There’s a fresh marvel for you, Frank.”

“Expound,” Frank answered; for he was ready now to swallow marvels as a child swallows sweetmeats.

“Why, how else would those two bronze statues dare go to sea in such a cockle-shell? Have I given you the dor now, master courtier?”

“I am long past dors,” said Frank, smiling in spite of himself. “But what noble creatures—and how fearlessly they come alongside!”

“I suspect they take us for Spaniards and want to sell us cocoa-nuts,” Cary said. “See—she’s laden with vegetables.”

“Aye,” said Amyas; and, because he had learned in these seas that food was more than food—news, peace, bait, friendship—he leaned over the side as the canoe drew near. Baskets lay amidships—plantains, cassava bread wrapped in leaves, gourds of water, and what looked like little brown nuts with pale husks: cacao, perhaps, or some cousin of it, smelling faintly of earth and green bitterness even across the water.

“Hail them, Yeo. You speak the best Spanish, and I want speech of one of them.”

Yeo hailed. The canoe, without more ado, ran alongside and lowered her sail; and a splendid Indian scrambled aboard like a cat—full six feet high, naked save for a girdle, his limbs shining as if oiled, his carriage as proud as any gentleman’s. He smiled at first, showing white teeth; then the smile snapped. His eyes flew round, taking in a dozen small things—the cut of a cap, the fashion of a weapon, the very way a rope was coiled—signs, to such a man, as plain as printed letters. In an instant he sprang to the side and shouted to his comrade in Spanish:

“Treachery! No Spaniard!”

He would have leaped overboard; but a dozen hands caught him. It took trouble to master him, so strong he was, so slippery the naked limbs that twisted like eels. Amyas, between anger at the tumult and pity for the brave fellow, cried to the men to hurt him not, and to the Indian to be quiet and no harm should come. After five minutes’ confusion the stranger gave in sulkily, breathing hard, his chest rising and falling like a bellows.

“Don’t bind him,” said Amyas. “Let him loose, and make a ring round him. Now, my man—there’s a dollar for you.”

The Indian’s eyes glistened at the sight of the coin. He took it, weighing it on his palm as if the coldness spoke.

“All I want,” said Amyas, “is first to tell me what ships are in La Guayra; and next, to go thither aboard of me and show me which is the governor’s house and which the custom-house.”

The Indian set the coin down on the deck as if it were hot, crossed himself, and looked Amyas full in the face.

“No, señor. I am a freeman and a cavalier, a Christian Guayqueria, whose forefathers, first of all the Indians, swore fealty to the King of Spain; and whom he calls to this day, in his proclamations, his most faithful, loyal, and noble Guayquerias. God forbid, therefore, that I should tell aught to his enemies, who are my enemies likewise.”

A growl rose from those who understood him. More than one hinted what a cord round the head or a match between the fingers might do for memory.

“God forbid!” said Amyas, and his voice cut the muttering short. “A brave and loyal man he is, and as such will I treat him. Tell me, my brave fellow—how do you know us to be his Catholic majesty’s enemies?”

The Indian, with a shrewd smile, pointed about the deck at half-a-dozen different objects, saying to each, “Not Spanish.”

“Well—and what of that?”

“None but Spaniards and free Guayquerias have a right to sail these seas.”

Amyas laughed outright. “Thou art a right valiant bit of copper. Pick up thy dollar and go thy way in peace. Make room for him, men. We can learn what we want without his help.”

The Indian paused, incredulous, astonished; courtesy, in an enemy, was a kind of sorcery.

“Overboard with you!” Amyas added. “Don’t you know when you are well off?”

“Most illustrious señor,” began the Indian, falling into that drawling, sententious fashion of his race, “I have been deceived. I heard that you heretics roasted and ate all true Catholics—such as we Guayquerias are—and that all your padres had tails.”

“Plague on you, sirrah!” squeaked Jack Brimblecombe, hopping forward in earnest indignation. “Have I a tail? Look here!”

“¿Quién sabe? Who knows?” said the Indian through his nose, and the men laughed.

“How do you know we are heretics?” Amyas asked.

“Humph! But in repayment for your kindness,” the Indian went on, “I would warn you, illustrious señor, not to go on to La Guayra. There are ships of war there waiting for you; and moreover, the governor, Don Guzman, sailed to the eastward only yesterday to look for you; and I wonder much that you did not meet him.”

“To look for us?” Cary burst out. “Impossible. Lies! This is some trick of the rascal’s to frighten us away.”

Amyas’s face hardened. “Don Guzman came out but yesterday to look for us? Are you sure you speak truth?”

“As I live, señor—he and another ship, for which I took yours.”

Amyas stamped upon the deck. That, then, was the ship they had passed. Fool that he was—duty and opportunity had crossed within a cable’s length, and he had let them go by.

“Off with you,” he said shortly; and the Indian, with a bound, was over the side and into his canoe. They watched him, wondering at the stateliness and courtesy of that bold sea-cavalier, who could be loyal without being servile.

So westward-ho they ran, beneath the mighty northern wall—the highest cliff on earth—some seven thousand feet of rock, parted from the sea by a narrow strip of bright green lowland. Here and there a patch of sugar-cane, or a knot of cocoa-nut trees close to the water’s edge, reminded them that they were in the tropics; but above, all was savage and bare as an Alpine precipice. Deep clefts here and there let the southern sun pour a white blaze down to the very sea-marge; and through them they caught glimpses, far above, of stately trees lining the glens, and of a veil of perpetual mist shrouding the inner summits. Between ship and mountain-side hung white fleecy clouds, motionless in the burning air, as if nailed there; and the silence of that suspended world was broken only by the ship’s own living noises—block and tackle, the slap of sails, a cough, a laugh checked too soon—until even those sounds seemed impertinent against so vast a rest.

“Within those mountains,” said Drew, the master, “three thousand feet above our heads, lies Saint Yago de Leon—the great city the Spaniards founded fifteen years agone.”

“Is it a rich place?” Cary asked, as men ask of towns.

“Very, they say.”

“Is it a strong place?” Amyas asked, as captains must.

“No forts at all, they say. The Spaniards boast that Heaven has made such walls already that man need make none.”

“I don’t know,” said Amyas, looking up. “Lads—could you climb those hills, do you think?”

“Rather higher than Harty Point, sir,” one answered; “but it depends pretty much on what’s behind them.”

Then the last point was rounded, and they were full in sight of the goal they had sailed four thousand miles to reach. A low black cliff crowned by a wall; a battery at either end. Within, a few narrow streets of white houses, running parallel with the sea upon a strip of flat that seemed not two hundred yards broad; behind, the mountain wall, casting the whole place into a shade so deep it looked like ink poured behind the roofs. Drew, who had been off the place before, pointed out a narrow path that wound upward through a glen, seemingly sheer perpendicular: the road to the capital, if any man dared.

Even in shadow the town had a dusty, glaring look, as if the very heat bleached it. The breaths of air off the land were stifling; no wonder, for La Guayra, with that vast fire-brick of heated rock behind it, is one of the hottest spots on earth. The heat came with its own sound—or rather its own lack of sound: the air felt thick, as if it swallowed echoes, and the sea’s voice seemed dulled, as though the shore were a blanket.

Where was the harbour? There was none—only an open roadstead, where five vessels lay tossing at anchor. Two outermost were small merchant caravels. Behind them lay two long, low, ugly craft, and at sight of them Yeo gave a long whew.

“Galleys, as I’m a sinful saint! And what’s that big one inside them, Robert Drew? She has more than hawseholes in her idolatrous black sides, I think.”

“We shall open her astern of the galleys in another minute,” said Amyas. “Look out, Cary; your eyes are better than mine.”

“Six round portholes on the main deck,” Cary answered.

“And I can see the brass patararoes glittering on her poop,” said Amyas. “Will, we’re in for it.”

“In for it we are, captain.

‘Farewell, farewell, my parents dear.
I never shall see you more, I fear.’

Let’s go in, nevertheless, and pound the Don’s ribs, my old lad of Smerwick. Eh? Three to one is fair odds.”

“Not underneath those fort guns, I beg leave to say,” quoth Yeo. “If the Philistines will but come out unto us, we will make them like unto Zeba and Zalmunna.”

“Game-cocks are game-cocks,” Amyas answered, “but reason’s reason.”

“If the Philistines are not coming out, they are going to send a messenger instead,” said Cary. “Look out, all thin skulls!”

As he spoke, a puff of white smoke rolled from the eastern fort; a heavy ball plunged into the water between fort and ship, and the sound came a moment later—flat and brutal, like a door slammed in their faces.

“I don’t altogether like this,” said Amyas. “What do they mean by firing without warning? And what are these ships of war doing here? Drew, you told me the armadas never lay here.”

“No more, I believe they do, sir,” Drew answered, peering, “on account of the anchorage being so bad, as you may see. I’m mortal afeared that rascal’s story was true, and the Dons have got wind of our coming.”

“Run up a white flag, at all events. If they expect us, they must have known it long, or how could they have got their craft hither?”

“True, sir. They must have come from Santa Marta at the least—perhaps Cartagena. That would take a month, going and coming.”

Amyas suddenly remembered Eustace’s threat at the wayside inn, and for one instant his mind tasted the sourness of betrayal. Could he have warned them? It seemed impossible—yet here lay proof in black hulls and portholes.

“Let us hold a council of war, at all events, Frank.”

But Frank was fastened to another sight. Half a mile to the eastward of the town, two or three hundred feet up the steep mountain-side, stood a large low white house, embosomed in trees and gardens. There was no other house of that size near, no place for one; and before it, flapping and snapping in the land-breath, flew the royal flag of Spain. That must be the governor’s house; that must be the abode of the Rose of Torridge. Frank stood devouring it with his eyes until imagination grew cruelly sure, persuading him that he could see a woman’s figure upon the terrace, and that the figure was hers. Amyas could hardly tear him away to the council, which was sad, and only not peevish.

Amyas, Frank, Cary, with Brimblecombe, Yeo, and Drew, withdrew upon the poop; and each looked at the others awhile. Plans and hopes of months were brought to nothing in an hour.

“It is impossible, you see,” said Amyas at last, “to surprise the town by land while these ships are here; for if we land our men we leave our ship without defence.”

“As impossible as to challenge Don Guzman while he is not here,” Cary answered.

“I wonder why the ships have not opened on us already,” said Drew.

“Perhaps they respect our flag of truce,” Cary said. “Why not send in a boat to treat with them, and to inquire for—”

“For her?” Frank broke in sharply. “If we show that we are aware of her existence, her name is blasted in the eyes of those jealous Spaniards.”

“And as for respecting our flag of truce,” said Yeo, “if you will take an old man’s advice, trust them not. They will keep the same faith with us as they kept with Captain Hawkins at San Juan d’Ulloa—when we might have taken the whole plate-fleet, and did not, but asked license to trade like honest men. And yet, after they had granted it, and deceived us by fair speech into landing ourselves and our ordnance, the governor and all the fleet set upon us five to one, and gave no quarter. No, sir; I expect the only reason they don’t attack us is because their crews are not aboard.”

“They will be soon enough, then,” said Amyas. “I can see soldiers coming down the landing-stairs.”

And indeed boats full of armed men began to push off to the ships. Their oars made a soft, busy whisper on the water, like rats in a wall; and the sound, small as it was, set every nerve on edge, for it spoke of preparation, of nets drawing tight.

“We may thank Heaven,” Drew said, “we were not here two hours agone. The sun will be down before they are ready for sea, and the fellows will have no stomach to go looking for us by night.”

“So much the worse for us,” Amyas answered. “If they do that, we may give them the slip, and back again to the town, and there try our luck; for I cannot find it in my heart to leave without one dash at it.”

Yeo shook his head. “There are plenty more towns along the coast more worth trying than this, sir. But Heaven’s will be done.”

As they spoke, the sun plunged into the sea, and all was dark—darkness thickened by the mountain’s shadow, so that the town seemed a low line of lamps pinned under a great black cloth.

At last it was agreed: they would anchor and wait till midnight. If the ships of war came out, they would try to run in past them; and, desperate as it might be, attempt the first plan—land to the westward of the town, take it in flank, plunder the government storehouses close to the landing-place, and fight back to their boats and out of the roadstead. Two hours would suffice if the armada and galleys were but once out of the way.

Amyas went forward, called the men together, and told them. It was not cheerfully received; yet what else was there? The ship’s belly held salted beef and biscuit, the hard bread that smelled of weevils and old flour; but the men’s imaginations already tasted Spanish wine, fresh meat, fruit, and the sweet fatness of loot. Hunger is a counsellor that speaks with a loud voice, and makes gold seem as needful as bread.

They ran down a mile and a half to the westward and anchored.

The night wore on. There was no stir among the shipping; though they could not see the vessels themselves, the lights—easy to distinguish by their heights from those in the town—remained motionless. The men fretted and fumed for weary hours at seeing a rich prize (for of course the town was paved with gold) within arm’s reach and yet, for the moment, impossible. The sea made its own low talk about the cable; from shore came now and then a faint clatter—an oar in a thole-pin, a distant shout—and above all loomed the mountain, holding the sound down as a hand holds down a child.

Let Amyas and his men have patience. Some five years more, and the great Armada will have come and gone; and then that avenging storm—of which they, like Oxenham, Hawkins, and Drake, were but the avant-couriers—will burst upon every Spanish port from Corunna to Cadiz, from the Canaries to Havana; and La Guayra and Saint Yago de Leon will not escape. Captain Amyas Preston and Captain Sommers, the colonist of the Bermudas, will land with a force tiny enough, though larger far than Leigh’s, where Leigh dares not land; and taking the fort of Guayra will find, as Leigh finds, that their coming has been expected, and that the Pass of the Venta, three thousand feet above, has been fortified with huge barricades, abattis, and cannon, making the capital within its ring of mountain-walls impregnable—to all but Englishmen (or Zouaves). For up that seven thousand feet of precipice behind the town those fierce adventurers will climb hand over hand through rain and fog, while men lie down and beg their officers to kill them, for no farther can they go—yet farther they will go, hewing a path with their swords through woods of wild plantain and rhododendron thickets, over—so it seems, however incredible—the very saddle of the Silla, down upon the astonished Mantuanos of Saint Yago, driving all before them; and, having burnt the city in default of ransom, return triumphant by the right road and pass along the coast masters of the deep.

I know not whether any men still live who count their descent from those two valiant captains; but if such there be, let them be sure that the history of the English navy tells no more Titanic victory over nature and man than that now forgotten raid of Amyas Preston and his comrade, in the year of grace 1595.

But though a venture on the town was impossible, there was another venture which Frank could not bear to let slip. A light now shone brightly in one of the governor’s windows—steady as a star set in stone—and it drew his thoughts as a lodestone draws iron. While the ship lay humming softly under the night—timbers answering the swell, blocks ticking, men whispering and shifting like sleepers—Frank sat in the cabin with Amyas, Cary, and Jack, and opened his heart.

“And are we, then,” he asked mournfully, “to go without doing the very thing for which we came?”

All were silent. The air in the cabin held the mixed smells of men and tar and damp wood; some scrap of food—cheese-rind or onion-skin—gave a faint homely sharpness, like a memory of kitchens far away. Outside, the sea’s voice was as constant as breath.

At last John Brimblecombe spoke, small in body but with a will that seemed to ring like metal when struck.

“Show me the way to do it, Mr. Frank, and I will go.”

“My dearest man,” said Amyas, “what would you have? Any attempt to see her—even if she be here—would be all but certain death.”

“And what if it were?” Frank answered, and his voice did not tremble. “What if it were, my brother Amyas? Listen to me. I have long ceased to shrink from Death; but till I came into these magic climes, I never knew the beauty of his face.”

“Of death?” Cary said, half bitter, half wondering. “I should have said, of life. God forgive me; but a man might wish to live forever, if he had such a world as this wherein to live.”

“And do you forget,” Frank went on, “that the more fair this passing world of time, by so much the more fair is that eternal world whereof all here is but a shadow and a dream? by so much the more fair is He before whose throne the four mystic beasts—the substantial ideas of Nature and her powers—stand day and night crying, ‘Holy, holy, holy, Lord God of hosts; Thou hast made all things, and for Thy pleasure they are and were created!’ My friends, if He be so prodigal of His own glory as to have decked these lonely shores—almost unknown since the foundation of the world—with splendours beyond our dreams, what must be the glory of His face itself? I have done with vain shadows. It is better to depart and to be with Him, where shall be neither desire nor anger, self-deception nor pretence, but the eternal fulness of reality and truth. One thing I have to do before I die, for God has laid it on me. Let that be done to-night, and then—farewell!”

“Frank! Frank! remember our mother!” Amyas cried; for that name, in such a cabin at such an hour, was like bread broken to steady the heart.

“I do remember her,” Frank answered. “I have talked over these things with her many a time; and where I would fain be, she would fain be also. She sent me out with my virgin honour, as the Spartan mother did her boy with the shield, saying, ‘Come back either with this, or upon this.’ And one or the other I must do, if I would meet her either in this life or in the next. But do not mistake me: my life is God’s, and I promise not to cast it away rashly.”

“What would you do, then?” Amyas asked, low.

“Go up to that house, Amyas, and speak with her, if Heaven gives me an opportunity—as Heaven, I feel assured, will give.”

“And do you call that no rashness?”

“Is any duty rashness? Is it rash to stand amid flying bullets if your queen has sent you? Is it more rash to seek Christ’s lost lamb if God and your own oath hath sent you? John Brimblecombe answered that question for us long ago.”

“If you go, I go with you!” said all three at once—Amyas, Cary, and Jack.

“No. Amyas, you owe duty to our mother and to your ship. Cary, you are heir to great estates, and are bound thereby to your country and your tenants. John Brimblecombe—”

“Ay!” squeaked Jack, hurt in his pride. “And what have you to say, Mr. Frank, against my going? I, who have neither ship nor estates—except, I suppose, that I am not worthy to travel in such good company?”

“Think of your old parents, John, and all your sisters.”

“I thought of them before I started, sir, as Mr. Cary knows, and you know too. I came here to keep my vow, and I am not going to turn renegade at the very foot of the cross.”

“Some one must go with you, Frank,” said Amyas, and his voice faltered in spite of him; “if it were only to bring back the boat’s crew in case—”

“In case I fall,” Frank said quietly, with a smile that made the cabin seem colder. “I will finish your sentence for you, lad. I am not afraid of it, though you may be for me. Yet some one, I fear, must go. Unhappy me, that I cannot risk my own worthless life without risking your more precious lives!”

“Not so, Mr. Frank!” said John. “Your oath is our oath, and your duty ours. I will tell you what we will do, gentlemen all. We three will draw cuts for the honour of going with him.”

“Lots?” Amyas said. “I don’t like leaving such grave matters to chance, friend John.”

“Chance, sir?” John answered, lifting his small head as if he listened beyond the ship’s walls to something larger than the sea. “When you have used all your own wit, and find it fail you, then what is drawing lots but taking the matter out of your own weak hands, and laying it in God’s strong hands?”
