"The fall was nigh Martinmas,
When nights were long and dark,
That wife's two sons came home again,
And their hats were made of birch."

Thus sang the ballad of Usher's Well, a haunting echo of a mother's longing that lingered in the minds of those who waited by the shore. It was now the evening of February 15th, 1587. At Burrough House, Lady Leigh paced the terrace walk, her gaze fixed upon the winding river and the hazy sandhills beyond, where the western sea stretched into an endless, mist-laden gray. She had performed this vigil every evening for three weary years, rain or shine, searching for a sail that refused to materialize. Though time had passed like water through fingers, leaving no trace of Frank or Amyas, nor of the gallant ship *Rose*, the hollows beneath the loving eyes of Bideford and Appledore deepened daily. They watched with the desperate gaze of the bereaved, much as John Oxenham had vanished into the unknown years prior, leaving behind a silence that echoed louder than any storm.

Hope had flared briefly when Sir Richard Grenville, unknown to his grieving friend, dispatched a rider to Plymouth. News had arrived of Drake, Frobisher, and Carlisle returning triumphant from the Spanish Main—news of sacked cities, relieved colonies, and glorious victories. Yet amidst the clamor of triumph, there was no word of the *Rose*. Lady Leigh had bowed her head, offering silent prayers, whispering, "The Lord gave, and the Lord hath taken away." Her hair, once dark, was now a canopy of silver; her cheeks, once ruddy, were waxen and thin; her steps, once brisk, dragged heavily. She rarely ventured from home, save to the church or the cottages of the poor. She never uttered her sons' names, guarding her lips as if they were sacred altars, yet every high tide compelled her to the terrace. When the red flag signaled water over the bar, she scanned the horizon with a devouring intensity, seeking a specific shape, a known line of canvas, a familiar cut of timber. The great ships entered and departed as of old, their white sails hanging off the bar for hours, days, months, and years. But an instinct, sharp as a sword, told her that none of them were hers. She knew the *Rose* in her mind's eye, every plank, every knot, every fold of sail; she could have identified her from a mile away, yet she never returned. The villagers regarded her with a mixture of awe and pity, seeing a woman who had descended into the fiercest depths of sorrow and walked there unhurt, by the side of a divine presence. Even the bedridden and the lonely found solace in her steady gaze, sensing that she carried a strength born of the fires of suffering.

Yet tonight, Northam was not silent. Below, the pebble ridge thundered with the rolling surf, but the town itself boomed with a noise that drowned the waves. The tower rocked under the pealing bells; the streets swarmed with people shouting, singing around bonfires. They burned the Pope in effigy, drank to the health of the Queen, and chanted, "So perish all her enemies!" The hills glowed red with fire from village to village. Far away, the bells of Bideford answered those of Northam, echoing a joy that had not been felt in seven years. Today, news had broken like lightning: Mary Queen of Scots was beheaded at Fotheringay. England, shaken by seventeen years of terror and treason, leaped up in a tremendous shout of jubilation. The nightmare of the "Daughter of Debate" was over. Elizabeth's prophecy had finally been fulfilled—"The daughter of debate... Hath reap'd no gain where former rule Hath taught still peace to grow." The tyrant who had known no forgiveness, whose tongue spoke only lies, whose heart harbored only hate, was silenced. Her fair face remained a pitiful dream in the memories of those who knew that either she or England must perish, but now the balance was restored.

Lady Leigh, though a staunch Protestant, breathed a prayer for the Queen's soul. "Clear as diamond, and as hard," she had once described herself; might not Mary have learned humility in her seventeen years? She recalled the final scene before the block, knowing it could not have been mere acting. Having served as a courtier, she understood the godless profligacy of the French courts where Mary had been raised, amid the Medici and the Guises. She shuddered, sighing softly to herself, "To whom little is given, of them shall little be required!" But the bells continued their triumphant tolling, refusing to cease.

Then, a sound pierced the twilight—a flash, followed by the thunder of a gun at sea.

Lady Leigh froze. The flash illuminated the space just outside the bar. It could not be a ship in distress; the wind was light and westerly, the tide high. Another flash, another roar. The revelry in Northam halted instantly. The crowd surged toward the churchyard overlooking the flats, drawn by the mystery of the distant report.

There, outside the bar, lay a gallant ship. She ran in with all sails set, a large vessel of nearly a thousand tons, yet she did not fly the English ensign. Could she be a Spanish cruiser seeking reprisal for Drake's raids? Unlikely. The Don favored scientific warfare, bringing fleets of admirals and vice-admirals, not solitary cruisers. If she were a foreigner, she was a daring one indeed, for she had not veiled her topsails—a maneuver every prudent foreign captain observed within sight of an English port to avoid hostilities. History recalled the Spanish Admiral who, in a time of peace, sailed boldly into Plymouth Sound without veil or caution. Upon doing so, the Port Admiral, John Hawkins, had rushed forth with his famous *Jesus of Lübeck*, firing a shot that pierced the admiral's masts, forcing the surrender of the flag and securing an apology. If John Hawkins could defend the Queen's honor in peace, there was no limit to what the old sea dogs of Appledore would do in war.

The strange sail slipped past the hill of Appledore, vanishing momentarily into the gloom. Then, rising from the quiet air, came a cheer—a roar from a hundred throats. Lady Leigh stood still, listening. Another gun thundered among the hills, followed immediately by another cheer.

Twenty minutes later, the vessel rounded the dark rocks of Hubbastone, turning up the Bideford River. Music erupted from her decks—drums, fifes, shawms, and trumpets, their ringing echoes bouncing off the slate roofs and wooded knolls. As she opened fully upon Burrough House, a second, deafening cheer burst from her crew, rolling up the hills from the silver waters below.

Lady Leigh moved quickly toward the house, calling for her maid. "Grace, bring me my hood. Master Amyas is come home!"

"No, surely?" Grace stammered, her voice trembling with joy. "Praised and blessed be the Lord! But, madam, how did you know?"

"I heard his voice on the river," Lady Leigh replied, her heart beating a frantic rhythm. "But I did not hear Mr. Frank's with him."

"Oh, be sure, madam, where the one is, the other is!" Grace insisted, handing her the hood. "They'd never part company. Both come home or neither."

With Grace trailing behind, Lady Leigh hastened toward Bideford. Was it a dream? Had the divine instinct of a mother enabled her to distinguish her child's voice from the cacophony of strangers? Or was her brain, worn thin by three years of waiting, finally fracturing under the strain?

When they reached the quay, the illusion dissolved. Down Bridgeland Street, where tobacco warehouses would rise decades hence, stood a row of rope-walks and sailmakers' shops. Already anchored in the river was the strange ship. Just as they reached the lower end of the street, a great mob swept around the corner—sailors, apprentices, women, hurrahing, weeping, laughing. Lady Leigh stopped; the mob stopped.

"Here she is!" someone shouted. "Here's his mother!"

"His mother? Not *their* mother!" Lady Leigh murmured, turning pale, though her heart was long past breaking.

In that instant, the giant head and shoulders of Amyas swept above the crowd, rounding the corner.

"Make a way! Make room for Madam Leigh!" And Amyas fell to his knees at her feet.

She threw her arms around his neck, burying her face in his shoulder, while sailors and coarse harbor-women fell into a holy silence, forming a protective ring around mother and son.

Lady Leigh asked no questions. She saw immediately that Amyas was alone. Finally, he whispered, "I would have died to save him, mother, if I could."

"You need not tell me that, Amyas Leigh, my son."

Another silence fell. "How did he die?" she whispered.

"He is a martyr. He died in the..." Amyas could not finish.

"The Inquisition?"

"Yes."
