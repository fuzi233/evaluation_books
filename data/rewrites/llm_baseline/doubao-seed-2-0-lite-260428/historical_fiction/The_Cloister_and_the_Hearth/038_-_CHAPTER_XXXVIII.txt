The crisp snow outside the town gate was crisscrossed paw to print by a thousand wolves, every yard of the winding road stitched with their fresh, heavy tracks. “We were wise to heed that old innkeeper’s warning, Denys,” Gerard said, his breath pluming white in the frosty air.

Denys grunted, shifting the weight of his crossbow over his shoulder. “Aye, wise enough. Only last night I heard the beasts scurrying right beneath our inn window, howling and whimpering for warm man’s flesh out in the market square. Not a single fat burgher leaned out to toss the poor vagabones a scrap, more’s the pity.”

Gerard smiled, but his gaze was distant, as if he was looking far past the drifts piling at the roadside. They tramped on in silence for a stretch, their boots crunching through frozen crust.

“What’s got you so deep in thought, lad?” Denys asked at last, prodding him gently in the ribs with an elbow.
“Your goodness,” Gerard said softly.

Denys’s face hardened instantly, all good humour gone in a flash. The veteran could stand a world of playful impertinence, his temper was as warm and easygoing as any man’s, but he would flare up over the silliest, smallest slight no one else even noticed. “A civil question deserves a civil answer,” he said drily, not looking at Gerard.
“Alas, I meant no offence,” Gerard protested.
“Then why pretend you were thinking on my goodness? There’s no such thing under my old leather skin.”
“Had any other man said that, I’d have called him a liar to his face,” Gerard said, half laughing. “But to you I say: you’ve no eye for your own virtues, only for women’s. And I defy your unreasonable choler right now – I was thinking of what you did last night, how you nearly married me off to the Golden Head tavern lass, and sent yourself off alone on the road, so I wouldn’t have to go back to Rome.”

“Ah, so that’s what’s got you moping!” Denys’s good humour flooded back as fast as it had vanished. “Truth be told, that wasn’t goodness, that was plain old friendship, no more. Though my conscience pricks me even now, that I didn’t hamstring you then and there, like a good true friend should. You’d have lain a month nursing the sore leg up at the *Tete d’Or*, that skittish pretty lass would have waited on you hand and foot, and you’d have stayed there fat and happy, no more tramping off to Rome after a girl you haven’t seen in months. I had my blade out all ready to do it, but then I remembered how you hate even the tiniest scratch, and my craven old heart failed me at the last minute.” He wore a look of genuine humble remorse, as if he’d failed a most sacred duty by not maiming his best friend.

Gerard gaped at him, astonished at the monstrous, utterly characteristic admission – but they never got a chance to argue the ethics of whether a true friend ought to cut their companion’s Achilles tendon out of love, for a shout cut across the wintry quiet.
“Look out behind us! Some fool is riding hell for leather on his neighbour’s mule!” Denys yelled.

Gerard spun around. “How can you tell it’s not his own?”
“Blind as a bat! No man rides his own mule like that, half out of his wits.”

Sure enough, a rustic came galloping at them full tilt, and when he reached the two travellers, his eyes went round as saucers, and he hauled so hard on the reins that the mule planted her forelegs and slid forward across the packed snow like a four-legged table on castors.
“Ye’re the two that stayed at the *Tete d’Or* inn, aye?” he panted.
They nodded.
“Which of ye’s the younger one?”
“The one born later,” Denys winked at Gerard.

“Cheers for that, genius,” the rustic said, unimpressed. “Right, then, youngster. You left this behind at the inn, our mistress sent me after you with it.” He held out a crumpled paper packet.
“Nay, good man, I didn’t leave aught behind,” Gerard said, patting his leather pouch.
“D’ye want to call our burgess’s wife a liar?” the man said, reproachful. “And where’s my pourboire? I rode all the way here ventre a terre, stomach to the earth, like.”

Gerard fished out a small copper coin and handed it over. The clown’s face lit up so bright he looked ready to dance. “God bless ye both! The Virgin ride with ye! Come on, Jenny!” He wheeled the mule around and galloped back the way he’d come.

Gerard untied the packet. It was six inches square, and inside was another wrapped packet, then another, then a fourth, until he got so impatient he nearly hurled the whole mess into the snow. Denys snatched it out of the drift and chided him for his petulance.
“O for Herodias’s little daughter’s big toe, that’s no affectation, that’s woman’s wit!” Denys said. “She’d not let her servants see what she’s giving a young unmarried man, out of modesty. Likely it’s your garter she found last night.”
“I don’t wear garters,” Gerard said.
“Then it’s hers! Or a lock of her hair. Wait, what’s this? Raw silk, soft as down. Saints and pikestaves, there’s something hard hiding in here!”

He fished it out: a great gold ring set with a deep purple amethyst, glowing like crushed wine, clear as crystal.
“Lovely,” Gerard said, blushing.
“And there’s a scrap of paper with writing on it! Read it, lad, I can’t read the fancy script when I don’t know what it’s about.”

Gerard took the paper, and his face burned bright red as a girl’s as he read the lines, naive and soft as the girl who wrote them:

“The little dove!” Denys cooed.
“The great silly owl,” Gerard said, shaking his head. “Risking her good name for a foolish prank like this. Thank heaven she gave it to an honest lad who’d never spread word of it to shame her. But why on earth didn’t she bestow this on you? You’re far more ripe for foolish romance than I am.”

Denys sighed and shrugged, he had to admit the boy was right – it was absurd that a pretty lass would pass over a seasoned soldier like him to dote on a green lad barely out of his teens. He handed the ring back to Gerard, insisting he go back to the *Tete d’Or* right that minute to return it, but Gerard refused, saying the tiny pewter ring Margaret had given him was worth more than all the rubies in the world, and he kissed the dented base metal like it was a holy relic. Denys finally convinced him to tuck the amethyst ring inside the lining of his bonnet, where it could act as a protective talisman, as folks said amethysts had power to ward off drunkenness and danger.

Gerard then begged Denys not to talk of women any more: every word of it made him ache so bad for Margaret he could barely stand, it felt like every step they took was pulling him farther and farther away from her. “There’s only room in my heart for one sweetheart, and one friend, Denys. Let me drink our friendship pure, no silly female nonsense to dilute it, while I still have it.”
“Sure thing, honey-pot, sure thing,” Denys said, clapping him on the shoulder. “And forget any idea I’m leaving you at Remiremont! I’ll go with you all the way to Rome. The Pope’s always got a little merry war going, and Burgundian soldiers are always welcome in his ranks. Hand on it, lad!”

Gerard’s heart swelled so full he could barely speak. He ran twice circles around Denys where he walked, danced backwards in front of him, then took his hand, and they tramped on hand in hand like two sweethearts, until they spotted a band of fifty mounted soldiers crested over the brow of the hill, the banner of Burgundy flying bright in the sun.
“Glorious,” Gerard said, staring at their velvet coats, their plumed helmets, their steel breastplates polished like mirrors. But Denys’s smile dropped the second he recognised the commander.
“That’s the Bastard of Burgundy himself! He’s out, which means there’s fighting up north in Flanders. That’s no man to trifle with.”

The troop rode right up to them. “Halt!” a stentorian voice roared.
The Bastard glared at Denys. “Arbalestrier, what are you doing heading south, when every loyal man in the duchy is hurrying north to put down the rebellion?”
Denys bowed respectfully, explained he was on leave after three years of service, on his way home to Remiremont to see his family.
“No leave now. Get on that spare mule, you’re coming north with us to Flanders.”
“Begging your highness’s pardon, I cannot,” Denys said, standing firm. “My home is close by, I haven’t seen my kin in three years, and I am in charge of this young man, I cannot leave him until I see him safe shipped to Rome.”

“You dare bandy words with me?” The Bastard’s face turned purple with rage. “Jarnac! Dismount six archers, shoot this white-livered cur dead for an example! Strip him, put his arms on the spare mule, we’ll find some man worthier to wear them.”
“NO!” Gerard screamed, waking from the stunned stupor the tyrant’s order had thrown him into. “He’ll go with you! I’d lose him for ever before I let a hair on his head be harmed. Just give me one minute to say goodbye, please!”

The Bastard nodded coldly, then rode off with the rest of the troop, leaving Count de Jarnac and six archers behind. The two friends stared at each other, haggard, their faces streaked with tears. “Go to Rotterdam,” Denys gasped. “Margaret is there. I’ll break away at the first chance, meet you there, we’ll all be together.”

The archers hauled Denys up onto the mule, gentle as they could, even rough soldiers had respect for comrades who loved each other that deep. They spurred after the Bastard, and Gerard ran as fast as he could after them, until he saw Denys leaning over the mule’s neck, tearing his hair out in grief, and Gerard’s throat closed up so tight he couldn’t breathe, and he collapsed against a thorn hedge, the thorns piercing his hand unnoticed.

When he got his breath back, he staggered on alone. The whole world was nothing but white snow, silent, desolate, not a living soul in sight. He was friendless now, and soon, a band of servants riding with a wealthy nobleman galloped back, surrounded him, and bade him stand and deliver. The nobleman sat his horse a hundred yards off, watching, too proud to do his own robbing, but happy to take the coin. Gerard didn’t fight, he didn’t want to throw his life away for a few coins, that would break Margaret’s heart. He tossed them his purse, and they rode off laughing.

Penniless, friendless, exhausted to the bone, Gerard stumbled to the edge of a wood. He couldn’t get to Rome now, he couldn’t even get to Strasbourg. The lass’s words echoed in his head: *If the world prove harsh and cold, come back to the Hedde of Gold.* But he’d never go back there as her servant, he was Margaret’s, body and soul. He was so tired, he crawled to the snow at the roadside, stretched out his limbs, and his eyes closed. He’d barely noticed the thorns in his hand, barely felt the cold seep into his bones. The last thing he thought of was Margaret’s face, and Denys’s laugh, before the darkness closed over him, alone in the wintry drift, half way to Rome, with no one to help him.
