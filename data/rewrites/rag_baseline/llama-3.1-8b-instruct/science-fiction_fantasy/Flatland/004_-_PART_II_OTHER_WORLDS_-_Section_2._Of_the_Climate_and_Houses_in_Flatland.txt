In the mist-shrouded city of Flatland, where the relentless drumbeat of the rain echoed through the narrow streets, Lady Ada Wynter navigated the labyrinthine thoroughfares with practiced ease. Her feet moved with a quiet confidence born of generations spent mastering the subtle art of navigating a world without sunlight. For in Flatland, the absence of celestial bodies rendered the traditional methods of orientation useless, and the inhabitants had developed their own unique ways of determining direction.

By a natural law, a constant attraction to the South governed the movements of Flatlanders, a force so slight that even a woman in reasonable health could journey several furlongs northward without undue difficulty. Yet, this gentle pull was sufficient to serve as a compass in most parts of the land, guiding travelers through the winding streets and alleys. The rain, which fell at regular intervals from the North, provided additional aid, its rhythmic patter a soothing accompaniment to the city's daily rhythms. In the towns, the rows of houses, their side walls aligned with the cardinal directions, offered a practical guide, while in the countryside, the trunks of trees served as makeshift pointers.

As Lady Ada walked, her thoughts turned to the peculiarities of Flatland's architecture. The most common form of dwelling was the pentagonal house, its five sides a testament to the ingenuity of the Flatlanders. The two northern sides, constituting the roof, were often doorless, while the eastern wall featured a small entrance for the women, and the western wall a larger door for the men. The southern side, or floor, was usually doorless, a feature that reflected the social hierarchy of Flatland, where men held positions of power and influence.

The prohibition on square and triangular houses dated back to the eleventh century, a law enacted to prevent the dangers posed by the sharp angles of these shapes. The pointed corners of a square or triangle could easily cause harm to an inattentive traveler, and so the Flatlanders had chosen the safer, more rounded forms of the pentagon. As Lady Ada continued her stroll, she pondered the peculiarities of her world, a realm where the boundaries between reality and madness were often blurred. And yet, in this strange and wondrous place, she had found a sense of belonging, a connection to the land and its people that went beyond the mundane concerns of direction and architecture.
