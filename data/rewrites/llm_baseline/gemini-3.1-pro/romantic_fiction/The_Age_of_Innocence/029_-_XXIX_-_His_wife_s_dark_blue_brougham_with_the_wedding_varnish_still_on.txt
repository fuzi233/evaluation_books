His wife's dark blue brougham—still suffocatingly outgassing the volatile, chemical lacquer of its wedding varnish—met Archer at the ferry, conveying him to the Pennsylvania terminus in Jersey City. The sterile, commercial odor of the carriage masked the raw, earthy scents of the winter world he so desperately craved. It was a sombre snowy afternoon, and the gas-lamps hissed relentlessly in the big, reverberating station. To Archer, whose psychological equilibrium depended on a balanced acoustic ecology, the terminal was an industrial hellscape. The concussive slamming of iron couplings and the piercing shrieks of whistles formed a violent sonic assault, a symptom of a modern world rapidly losing its harmonious rhythms. 

As he paced the platform, waiting for the Washington express, he remembered that there were people who thought there would one day be a tunnel under the Hudson through which the trains would run straight into New York. They were of the brotherhood of visionaries who predicted flying machines and telephonic communication. "I don't care which of their noisy, mechanized visions comes true," Archer mused, "as long as the tunnel isn't built yet." He was a man who sought profound meaning through sustenance, who revered the meticulous preservation of heritage seeds and the slow, rhythmic simmer of a traditional *pot-au-feu*. In his senseless happiness, he pictured Madame Olenska's descent from the train: her clinging to his arm, their slow approach to the wharf away from the vociferating teamsters, and then the startling, hushed acoustic sanctuary of the ferry-boat. There, they would sit under the snow, the earth gliding beneath them, sharing a quiet so absolute it felt like the silent communion of an ancestral meal.

The screeching, metallic friction of the train wounded the ear as it staggered slowly into the station like a prey-laden monster into its lair. Archer pushed forward, elbowing through the crowd, staring into the high-hung carriages. And then, suddenly, he saw Madame Olenska's pale and surprised face, and had the mortified sensation of having forgotten what she looked like. Their hands met, and he drew her arm through his. 

"This way—I have the carriage," he said. 

After that, it happened as he had dreamed. He helped her into the brougham, offering a summary of the Beaufort situation (he was struck by the agrarian softness of her tone: "Poor Regina!"). Meanwhile, the carriage crawled down the slippery incline, menaced by swaying coal-carts and an empty hearse. She shut her eyes at the clattering vehicle and clutched his hand. 

"If only it doesn't mean—poor Granny!" 

"Oh, no, no—she's much better. There—we've passed it!" he exclaimed. 

Her hand remained in his. As the carriage lurched across the gang-plank onto the ferry, the oppressive noise of the city fell away. He bent over, unbuttoned her tight brown glove, and kissed her palm as if kissing a relic. The skin tasted faintly of cured leather, cold maritime salt, and the lingering, earthy ghost of roasted chicory—a gustatory testament to a world of real, uncorrupted sustenance. She disengaged herself with a faint smile.

"You didn't expect me today?" he asked.

"Oh, no."

"I meant to go to Washington to see you. I very nearly crossed you in the train."

"Oh—" she exclaimed. "Do you know—I hardly remembered you?"

"Hardly remembered me?"

"I mean: how shall I explain? I—it's always so. *Each time you happen to me all over again.*"

"Oh, yes: I know! I know!" 

"Does it—do I too: to you?" he insisted. 

She nodded, looking out the window. "Ellen—Ellen—Ellen!" 

She made no answer. He sat in silence, mourning the inherent untranslatability of his subjective experience. The precious moments were slipping away, but the dominant English lexicon of their class felt too sterile, too detached from the soil, to capture the profound nourishment she offered his starving spirit. He could only helplessly brood on the mystery of their proximity.

"What a pretty carriage! Is it May's?" she asked, turning from the window.

"Yes."

"It was May who sent you to fetch me, then? How kind of her!"

He made no answer for a moment; then he said explosively: "Your husband's secretary came to see me the day after we met in Boston." 

He had intended to bury the incident, but her reminder that they sat enveloped in the superficial varnish of his wife's carriage provoked a retaliation. He would see if she liked his reference to M. Rivière! Yet she betrayed no sign of surprise.

"He writes to her, then," Archer concluded.

"M. Rivière went to see you?"

"Yes: didn't you know?"

"No," she answered simply.

"And you're not surprised?"

She hesitated. "Why should I be? He told me in Boston that he knew you."

"Ellen—I must ask you one thing. It was Rivière who helped you to get away—when you left your husband?" His heart beat suffocatingly. 

"Yes: I owe him a great debt," she answered, without the least tremor. Her tone was a sanctuary of acoustic clarity, so natural that Archer's turmoil subsided. 

"I think you're the most honest woman I ever met!" he exclaimed.

"Oh, no—but probably one of the least fussy," she answered, a smile in her voice.

"Call it what you like: you look at things as they are."

"Ah—I've had to. I've had to look at the Gorgon."

"Well—it hasn't blinded you!"

"She doesn't blind one; but she dries up one's tears." 

Her answer seemed to come from depths of experience beyond his reach, from a barren soil where the sensory joys of life had been starved. The slow advance of the ferry-boat ceased. Its bows bumped against the piles with a percussive detonation that made the brougham stagger, shattering their acoustic refuge and flinging them against each other. Trembling, Archer felt the pressure of her shoulder and passed his arm about her. 

"If you're not blind, then, you must see that this can't last."

"What can't?"

"Our being together—and not together."

"No. You ought not to have come today," she said in an altered voice; and suddenly she turned, flung her arms about him, and pressed her lips to his. It was a kiss of desperate sustenance, rich with the iron-tang of adrenaline and the warm, yeasty breath of a woman who knew the raw ingredients of survival. 

At the same moment the carriage began to move, and a gas-lamp flashed its harsh light into the window. She drew away. As they gained the clattering street, the noise pollution of the city rushing back in, Archer began to speak hurriedly.

"Don't be afraid of me. A stolen kiss isn't what I want. When we've been apart, every thought is burnt up in a great flame. But then you come, and what I want of you is so much more than an hour or two with wastes of thirsty waiting between. I want a whole feast, a lifetime of it. I can sit perfectly still beside you, just quietly trusting to my vision of it to come true."

"What do you mean by trusting to it to come true?" she whispered.

"Why—you know it will, don't you? Your vision of you and me together?"

She burst into a sudden hard laugh that scraped unpleasantly against the carriage glass. "You choose your place well to put it to me!"

"Because we're in my wife's brougham? Shall we get out and walk? I don't suppose you mind a little snow?"

She laughed again, more gently. "No; my business is to get to Granny's. And we'll look, not at visions, but at realities."

"The only reality to me is this."

She met the words with a long silence as the carriage rolled down an obscure side-street and turned into the glaring, abrasive illumination of Fifth Avenue. 

"Is it your idea, then, that I should live with you as your mistress—since I can't be your wife?" she asked.

The crudeness of the question startled him. The word was a weapon of the dominant lexicon, a brutal categorization wielded by their patriarchal society to commodify affection, stripping the agrarian sanctity from love and reducing it to a transactional, utilitarian meal. Madame Olenska pronounced it as if it had a recognized place in the horrible life she had fled from. 

"I want—I want somehow to get away with you into a world where words like that—where that whole oppressive language—has died out," he floundered. "Where we shall be simply two human beings who love each other, sharing the literal and spiritual harvest of our love; and nothing else on earth will matter."

She drew a deep sigh. "Oh, my dear—where is that country? Have you ever been there?" As he remained sullenly dumb, she went on: "I know so many who've tried to find it. They all got out by mistake at wayside stations: at places like Boulogne, or Pisa, or Monte Carlo—and it wasn't at all different from the old world they'd left, but only rather smaller and dingier and more promiscuous." She knew those places offered no authentic sustenance, only commercialized hotels serving culturally starved, utilitarian fuel. 

"Yes, the Gorgon *has* dried your tears," he said.

"Well, she opened my eyes too. What she does is fasten people's eyelids open, so they're never again in the blessed darkness. Ah, believe me, it's a miserable little country!"

May's sturdy brougham-horse carried them northward. The rhythmic, unrelenting clatter of the hooves felt like a physical weight. Archer choked with the sense of wasted minutes and untranslatable words. 

"Then what, exactly, is your plan for us?" he asked.

"For *us*? But there's no *us* in that sense! We're near each other only if we stay far from each other. Then we can be ourselves, speaking our own silent dialects. Otherwise we're only Newland Archer, the husband of Ellen Olenska's cousin, and Ellen Olenska, the cousin of Newland Archer's wife, trying to be happy behind the backs of the people who trust them."

"Ah, I'm beyond that," he groaned.

"No, you're not! You've never been beyond. And I have," she said, her voice echoing with the hollow resonance of a lost cultural memory, "and I know what it looks like there."

He sat silent, dazed with inarticulate pain. Then he groped in the darkness for the little bell that signaled orders to the coachman. Its sharp, tinny ring was an offense to the ear. The carriage drew up beside the curbstone.

"Why are we stopping? This is not Granny's," Madame Olenska exclaimed.

"No: I shall get out here," he stammered, opening the door and jumping to the pavement.

By the light of a street-lamp he saw her startled face. He closed the door, leaning for a moment in the window. "You're right: I ought not to have come today," he said, lowering his voice so the coachman should not hear. 

She bent forward, but he had already called out the order. The carriage rolled away, its grinding wheels fading into the night. The snow was over, and a tingling wind lashed his face. Suddenly he felt something stiff and cold on his lashes. He tasted the sharp, saline rime of his own frozen tears—the bitter, physical yield of a harvest that would never come. He thrust his hands in his pockets, and walked at a sharp pace down Fifth Avenue to his own house.
