The air in the room was thick with the scent of wax and the sharp, metallic tang of suppressed rage. Madame Olenska entered, a figure composed of shimmering fabric and high-chinned defiance, as if she had been woven from candlelight itself. She challenged the room with her posture, a pretty woman daring a gallery of rivals.

Mrs. Manson rose, her movement arch and theatrical, gesturing to the floral arrangement. "We were saying, my dear, that here was something beautiful to surprise you with."

Olenska halted. Her complexion remained pale, yet a flash of white-hot anger swept across her features, sudden and violent as summer lightning. "Who is ridiculous enough to send me a bouquet?" Her voice was shrill, a tone Archer had never witnessed before. "Why a bouquet? And why tonight of all nights? I am not attending a ball; I am not a girl awaiting marriage. Yet some people remain perpetually ridiculous."

She turned to the door, opening it with a decisive snap. "Nastasia!"

The handmaiden appeared instantly. Olenska spoke in Italian, enunciating each syllable with deliberate slowness, ensuring Archer could parse the words. "Here—discard this into the dustbin."

Nastasia protested. "But no—it is not the fault of the poor flowers."

"Tell the boy to carry them to the house three doors away, the residence of Mr. Winsett, the dark gentleman who dined here. His wife is ill; they may derive pleasure from them."

"The boy is out, you say?"

"Then, my dear one, run yourself. Take my cloak. Fly. I require the object removed from this house immediately! And, as you value your life, do not claim they originated from me!"

She draped the velvet opera cloak over the maid's shoulders and retreated into the drawing-room, closing the door with a sharp click. Her chest rose beneath the lace bodice. For a heartbeat, Archer perceived the tremor of impending tears; then, she erupted into laughter. Turning her gaze alternately to the Marchioness and to him, she asked abruptly, "And you two—have you reconciled yourselves?"

"It is for Mr. Archer to decide, darling; he has waited patiently while you prepared."

"Yes—I granted you sufficient time; my hair refused to settle," Olenska replied, lifting her hand to the piled curls of her chignon. "But this reminds me: Dr. Carver has departed, and you will arrive late at the Blenkers'. Mr. Archer, will you escort my aunt to the carriage?"

She followed the Marchioness into the hall, observing her being stuffed into a chaotic pile of overshoes, shawls, and tippets. From the threshold, she called out, "Ensure the carriage returns for me at ten!" Before disappearing, she returned to the drawing-room. Archer, re-entering, found her standing before the mantelpiece, scrutinizing her reflection. It was uncommon in New York society for a lady to address her parlour-maid as "my dear one" and dispatch her on an errand wrapped in her own cloak; yet, through the layers of his deeper emotions, Archer tasted the exhilarating thrill of inhabiting a world where action followed emotion with Olympian velocity.

Olenska did not shift when he approached from behind. For a fleeting second, their eyes locked in the mirror; then she turned, collapsing onto the corner of the sofa, exhaling a sigh. "There remains time for a cigarette."

He offered the box and ignited a spill for her. As the flame flickered upward, illuminating her face, she glanced at him with laughing eyes. "What impression do you hold of me in a temper?"

Archer paused. Then, with sudden resolve, he answered: "It clarifies what your aunt has been asserting regarding you."

"I knew she had been discussing me. Well?"

"She claimed you were accustomed to various phenomena—splendors, amusements, excitations—that we cannot possibly provide here."

Olenska smiled faintly, the smoke curling around her lips. "Medora is incorrigibly romantic. It has compensated her for so many deficiencies!"

Archer hesitated, then ventured his risk again. "Is your aunt's romanticism invariably consistent with accuracy?"

"Do you inquire whether she speaks the truth?" His niece considered. "Well, I shall inform you: in nearly everything she states, there exists a fragment of truth and a fragment of falsehood. But why do you ask? What has she been recounting to you?"

He looked away into the fire, then back at her radiant presence. His heart constricted with the realization that this was their final evening by that hearth, and that moments hence, the carriage would arrive to transport her away.

"She claims—and pretends—that Count Olenski has requested her to persuade you to return to him."

Olenska offered no response. She sat motionless, clutching her cigarette in a half-raised hand. Her facial expression remained unchanged; Archer recalled his prior observation of her apparent immunity to surprise.

"You knew, then?" he exploded.

She remained silent until the ash detached from her cigarette and fell. She brushed it onto the floor. "She has hinted at a letter: poor darling! Medora's hints—"

"Is it at your husband's request that she has arrived here so suddenly?"

Olenska appeared to weigh this question. "Once again: one cannot determine. She informed me she had received a 'spiritual summons,' however that phrase is defined, from Dr. Carver. I fear she intends to marry Dr. Carver... poor Medora, there is always someone she desires to wed. Perhaps the inhabitants of Cuba grew weary of her! I suspect she served them as a type of paid companion. Truly, I lack knowledge regarding her motives."

"But you do believe she possesses a letter from your husband?"

Once more, Olenska brooded in silence before stating, "After all, it was to be anticipated."

The young man rose and leaned against the fireplace. A sudden restlessness seized him, rendering him tongue-tied by the awareness that their minutes were numbered, and that at any instant he might hear the wheels of the returning carriage.

"You know that your aunt believes you intend to return?"

Olenska lifted her head rapidly. A deep blush surged to her face, spreading across her neck and shoulders. She blushed rarely and painfully, as if the sensation burned her like a cauterization.

"Many cruel things have been believed of me," she stated.

"Oh, Ellen—forget me; I am a fool and a brute!"

She smiled slightly. "You are terribly nervous; you possess your own burdens. I perceive you believe the Wellands are unreasonable concerning your marriage, and naturally, I concur. In Europe, people fail to comprehend our prolonged American engagements; I suppose they are not as tranquil as we are." She pronounced the "we" with a faint emphasis that lent it an ironic timbre.

Archer sensed the irony but dared not seize it. Ultimately, she had perhaps deliberately diverted the conversation from her own affairs, and following the evident pain his previous words had inflicted, he felt compelled to follow her lead. However, the sense of the waning hour rendered him desperate: he could not endure the notion that a barrier of words should descend between them anew.

"Yes," he declared abruptly; "I journeyed south to request May's consent to marry me after Easter. There is no impediment preventing our union then."

"And May adores you—and yet you could not convince her? I deemed her too intelligent to be the slave of such absurd superstitions."

"She _is_ too intelligent—she is not their slave."

Olenska regarded him. "Well, then—I do not comprehend."

Archer flushed and accelerated his speech. "We conducted a candid discourse—almost the initial one. She interprets my impatience as a negative omen."

"Merciful heavens—a negative omen?"

"She believes it signifies that I cannot trust myself to continue caring for her. She concludes, in essence, that I wish to marry her immediately to escape someone whom I—care for more profoundly."

Olenska examined this proposition with curiosity. "But if she holds this belief—why is she not in a hurry as well?"

"Because she is not thus: she is far nobler. She insists all the more upon the extended engagement, to grant me time—"

"Time to relinquish her for the other woman?"

"If I desire it."

Olenska leaned toward the fire, gazing into it with fixed eyes. Down the quiet street, Archer heard the approaching trot of her horses.

"That _is_ noble," she remarked, with a slight fracture in her voice.

"Yes. But it is absurd."

"Absurd? Because you harbor affection for no one else?"

"Because I do not intend to marry anyone else."

"Ah." Another lengthy interval ensued. Finally, she looked up and queried, "This other woman—does she love you?"

"Oh, there is no other woman; I mean, the individual May contemplated was—never—"

"Then, why, ultimately, are you in such haste?"

"There is your carriage," Archer said.

She half-risen and surveyed her surroundings with absent eyes. Her fan and gloves rested on the sofa beside her, which she retrieved mechanically.

"Yes; I presume I must depart."

"You are proceeding to Mrs. Struthers's?"

"Yes." She smiled and added, "I must attend where I am invited, or I shall succumb to excessive loneliness. Why not accompany me?"
