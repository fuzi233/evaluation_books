Why is it that fate so often places me at odds with the world? Propose a law to hand this realm over to the Pretender of Rome or the Grand Turk, and I would surely oppose it—yet find myself outvoted all the same. In Virginia, I was no less at odds with my neighbors. The Patriots branded me a Tory, while the Tories eyed me with suspicion as a dangerous radical. The times were out of joint indeed. *O cursed spite!*

Before a year had passed, I longed for the quiet banks of the Waveney. Yet how could I abandon my mother to the brewing storm? Nor would she leave, nor would any man of spirit. Over his Excellency’s claret, we loyal gentlemen vowed to stand firm—*statue signum, signifer!*—rallying to the cause with brimming glasses and bold words. The Governor, in those moments, spoke like the bravest of Cavaliers.

Of all Virginians, none held to her principles as staunchly as Madam Esmond. When the gentry resolved to host a ball for Lady Dunmore, news arrived of the Boston Port Bill. Swiftly, the House of Burgesses denounced the act and proclaimed a day of fasting and prayer to avert civil war. Yet the ball proceeded—only for sackcloth to follow days later.

"A ball!" cried my mother. "A ball thrown by rebels who will soon insult His Majesty? I would sooner die!" She refused the invitation, only to be hailed as a patriot by cheering crowds the next day. Mistaken for a sympathizer, she thrust her head from her chair and cried, *"God save the King!"*—a cry the people echoed, as all did in those days.

On the night of the ball, Harry, as Burgess and host, wore his old red coat of Wolfe’s (soon to be traded for another hue) and escorted Madam Fanny. My wife and I, as English visitors, attended as well. I danced with the lively Mrs. Washington—a charming partner, though my mother scowled at the praise.

Three days later, when fast-day services were held, Madam Esmond dined in open defiance, lighting her windows while the town stood dark. She bade an aide-de-camp sing *"God save the King,"* then called for *"Britons, strike home!"*—a step too far. A stone crashed through the window, scattering the punch-bowl. Undaunted, she stood framed by starlight, arms outstretched, singing on until the crowd’s jeers were quelled by a familiar voice—*"You mustn’t insult a lady."*

That night, every pane in our house was shattered. *"Britons, strike home!"* had roused more than loyalty.

The colony drilled militia, cast bullets, and defied royal edicts—all while swearing fealty to the Crown. When I warned of England’s might, they scoffed: *"We mean no separation! We are loyal Britons!"* Yet the powder-keg was lit.

Had England fought with honor, not Hessian hirelings and Indian savagery, might the war have ended differently? Long Island should have crushed Washington; Camden should have secured the South. But fate decreed otherwise. The Republic rose, not by luck, but by the unyielding resolve of its leader—Washington, steadfast through hunger, treason, and defeat, magnanimous in victory, and noblest in surrender.

In our own home, division festered. Harry, once my brother in arms, grew rigid in his loyalties, while I—though no radical—saw hypocrisy in denying liberty to those we ruled. When Dunmore promised freedom to slaves who joined the King’s cause, Harry raged, while I asked, *"If we fight for liberty, why not they?"*

Gumbo, freed for his loyalty, now vexed the household with his airs. My mother, ever strict, ordered him whipped—sparking rebellion. Molly, his wife, turned on her mistress, and I took his side. The quarrel passed, but Gumbo had to go.

Theo, heavy with child, sent our little ones to England—her sacrifice made without tears. The ship returned bearing Hagan and Maria, who humbly renounced her title. Maria’s deference won my mother’s heart, and even Hagan’s readings of my *Pocahontas* met approval.

Yet Fanny, sharp-tongued, tormented Maria with sly jabs about age and rank. I pitied Maria’s quiet endurance, while Fanny, once our meek ward, now repaid old wounds with fresh ones.

*"Kindness?"* she sneered when I reproached her. *"No slave was ever treated as I was."*

And I thought of the silent girl who once served us so sweetly—now a viper in my brother’s bed.
