My next task is to provide whatever additional details I possess regarding the Moonstone—or, more accurately, concerning the Indian plot to steal it. Though scant, this information holds significant weight, foreshadowing events yet to unfold. Approximately a week or ten days after Miss Verinder departed, one of my clerks entered my private office holding a visiting card. He announced that a gentleman awaited an audience below. Upon examining the card, I noted a foreign surname that has since faded from my memory. However, beneath it lay a line in English, etched clearly into my recollection: *"Recommended by Mr. Septimus Luker."*

The sheer audacity of a man in Mr. Luker's station to recommend anyone to *me* stunned me into silence. I stared at the card, doubting my own eyesight. The clerk, noticing my confusion, offered his assessment of the stranger waiting downstairs. "He is quite remarkable, sir," he remarked. "His complexion is so dark we initially mistook him for an Indian, or someone of similar origin."

Connecting the clerk's observation with the inscription on the card, I surmised that the Moonstone might be the catalyst behind both Mr. Luker's endorsement and the stranger's visit. To my clerk's astonishment, I immediately granted an audience. Let me justify this lapse in professional decorum, driven purely by curiosity: no living soul in England claims a deeper connection to the romance of the Indian Diamond than I do. I was entrusted with the secret of Colonel Herncastle's escape plan; I received his periodic letters confirming his survival; I drafted his will, bequeathing the Moonstone to Miss Verinder; I convinced his executor to proceed, hoping the jewel would benefit the family; and finally, I dissuaded Mr. Franklin Blake from his scruples, persuading him to transport the diamond to Lady Verinder's residence. If anyone possesses a prescriptive right to the Moonstone and its entanglements, surely it is me.

When my mysterious client was ushered in, I felt an immediate certainty that I faced one of the three Indians—likely their leader. Dressed impeccably in European attire, his swarthy skin, lithe build, and grave, graceful demeanor betrayed his Oriental heritage to any discerning observer. I gestured to a chair and requested to know the nature of his business. After offering a polite apology in flawless English for the intrusion, the Indian presented a small parcel wrapped in cloth of gold. Beneath this, and a second layer of silk, rested a tiny box exquisitely inlaid with jewels upon an ebony base.

"I have come, sir," he stated calmly, "to request a loan. I leave this casket as assurance that my debt shall be repaid."

I pointed to his card. "And you seek funds through Mr. Luker's recommendation?"

The Indian bowed. "How came it that Mr. Luker refused to advance the sum you require?"

"Mr. Luker informed me he possessed no capital to lend."

"And thus, he directed you to me?" The Indian indicated the card again. "It is written there."

Brief, precise, and entirely effective. Should the Moonstone have been in my possession, I knew this Oriental gentleman would have slain me without hesitation. Yet, barring that potential threat, I must admit he was the ideal client. While he showed no reverence for my life, he demonstrated a courtesy toward my time unmatched by any of my compatriots in my years of practice.

"I regret," I began, "the trouble you've taken to visit me. Mr. Luker is mistaken in sending you here. Like others in my profession, I am trusted with lending sums. However, I never lend to strangers, nor on such tenuous security as yours."

Far from pleading my case, the Indian merely bowed again, rewrapped his box, and remained silent. Rising promptly upon my response—the very moment an assassin would strike—he prepared to depart.

"Before I leave," he said, "may your kindness allow me one final question?"

I nodded. One question at parting? The average client asks fifty.

"Supposing," he continued, "that it had been possible—and customary—for *you* to grant me this loan, in what timeframe would it have been standard for *me* to repay it?"

"Following the usual practices in this country," I replied, "a borrower would typically be entitled to repay within one year from the date of disbursement."

The Indian bowed deeply, then exited with sudden, soft agility—a movement so feline and noiseless it startles even the seasoned observer. Once composed, I reached a singular conclusion regarding this enigmatic visitor. His face, voice, and manners during our meeting were controlled with such perfection that scrutiny seemed futile. Yet, he had offered me a glimpse beneath his polished exterior. He displayed no attempt to retain my words until I specified the customary period for early loan repayment. At that precise moment, he locked eyes with me, staring intently. This signaled that his final query served a specific purpose and demanded a specific answer. Reflecting on our exchange, I grew increasingly certain that the presentation of the casket and the request for funds were mere formalities, designed solely to facilitate his concluding inquiry.

Just as I sought to decipher his motives, a letter arrived—from none other than Mr. Septimus Luker himself. Written in terms of sickening subservience, it apologized profusely and requested an immediate interview to clarify matters. Driven by the same unprofessional curiosity, I agreed to meet him the following day.

In stark contrast to the Indian, Mr. Luker was a creature of vulgarity, ugliness, and cringing obsequiousness, utterly unworthy of detailed description. Nevertheless, the substance of his confession was clear. The day prior to the Indian's visit, Luker had encountered the same gentleman. Despite the visitor's European disguise, Luker instantly recognized him as the chief of the three Indians who had previously harassed his home, forcing him to involve the authorities. Convinced he stood in the presence of the very men who had blinded, gagged, and robbed him of his banker's receipt, Luker fell into a state of paralyzed terror, believing his end had arrived.

On his side, the Indian maintained the guise of a complete stranger. He produced the casket and made the identical loan request he later issued to me. Desperate to rid himself of the intruder, Luker immediately claimed he held no funds. When pressed for advice on where else to seek a loan, Luker suggested a reputable solicitor. When asked for a name, he blurted out mine—the only logical choice born of panic, as it was the first legal authority that sprang to his mind.

"The sweat poured off me like rain, sir," Luker confessed, wringing his hands. "I spoke without thinking. Please forgive me, Mr. Bruff, considering I was genuinely terrified out of my wits."

I excused him graciously, eager to dismiss him. Before he departed, I detained him for one final inquiry: Had the Indian said anything notable when leaving Luker's house? Yes. The Indian had posed the exact same question to Luker that he had asked me, receiving the identical answer.

What did this signify? Luker's explanation offered no clarity, and my own ingenuity failed to unravel the puzzle. That evening, burdened by the mystery, I headed upstairs for dinner, unaware that the path to my dressing room would simultaneously lead directly to discovery.
