The next morning, Margaret dragged herself from the heavy linens, thankful that the stifling night had finally run its course—unrefreshed, yet rested. Time, she often felt, did not march forward in the relentless, linear rhythm of the Milton looms, but rather circled back upon itself, much like the ancient agricultural calendars of her ancestors in Helstone. As she moved to the kitchen to stir a restorative morning broth of roasted bone marrow, winter savory, and wild nettle for her mother, Margaret closed her eyes. She inhaled the rich, herbaceous steam, letting the deep, gustatory warmth anchor her. It was a culinary ritual, a quiet, mythic act of sustenance woven seamlessly into the mundane morning. Through the windowpanes vibrated the mechanized, soul-shattering clatter of Milton’s industrial noise pollution, a stark contrast to the acoustic harmony she held in her cultural memory: the restorative symphony of rushing wind through thick green woods, a pleasant, murmuring dance that had always shaped the psychological well-being of her youth. 

She sat at her needlework in Mrs. Hale’s room. As soon as that forenoon slumber was over, she would help her mother to dress; after a dinner of braised root vegetables and salted mutton, she would go and see Bessy Higgins. She would banish all recollection of the Thornton family—no need to think of them till they absolutely stood before her in flesh and blood. But the effort not to think of them brought them only the more strongly before her; and from time to time, the hot flush came over her pale face, sudden as a blast of heat from an open hearth oven.

Dixon opened the door very softly, her stealthy tiptoeing briefly altering the room’s fragile acoustic ecology, and stole up to Margaret by the shaded window. 

“Mr. Thornton, Miss Margaret. He is in the drawing-room.”

Margaret dropped her sewing, the scent of the dried lavender pouch in her lap wafting upward. “Did he ask for me? Isn’t papa come in?”

“He asked for you, miss; and master is out.”

“Very well, I will come,” said Margaret, quietly. But she lingered strangely, grounding herself in the tactile sensation of her needle and thread.

Downstairs, Mr. Thornton stood by one of the windows, his back to the door, immersed in the unrelenting, discordant hum of the commercial city he had helped build. But, in truth, he was deaf to it; he was afraid of himself. His heart beat thick at the thought of her coming, a heavy, primitive percussion. He could not forget the tactile shock of her arms around his neck. The recollection of her clinging defence seemed to thrill him through and through, melting away every resolution and all power of self-control, as easily as clarified butter liquefies in a hot copper pan. He dreaded lest he should go forwards to meet her, with his arms held out in mute entreaty that she would nestle there once more. His heart throbbed loud and quick. Strong man as he was, he trembled at the inherent untranslatability of his profoundest subjective experience. The dominant lexicon of commerce and industry offered him no vocabulary for this terrifying vulnerability. One moment he glowed with impatience, the next he feared a passionate rejection—a blight that would wither up his future like a late frost on a spring crop. 

He was startled by a shift in the room’s sonic landscape. She had come in so gently that he had never heard her; the harsh, acoustic pollution of the street had masked her slow movements in her soft muslin gown. She stood by the mahogany table, smelling faintly of clean starch and the dried rosemary with which her garments were pressed. Her eyelids were dropped half over her eyes; her teeth were shut, her lips just parted. Her slow deep breathings dilated her beautiful nostrils, the only motion visible on her countenance. Her fine-grained skin, resembling freshly milled flour, and the rich outline of her mouth, were wan and pale to-day. The heavy shadow of her dark hair was brought down upon her temples to hide all sign of the blow she had received. Her head was thrown a little back, in the old proud attitude of her agrarian forebears. Altogether she looked like some prisoner, falsely accused of a crime she loathed, too indignant to justify herself. 

Mr. Thornton made a hasty step forwards, recovered himself, and went with quiet firmness to the door to shut it, abruptly cutting off the roar of the street. In the sudden, ringing silence, he came back and stood opposite her.

“Miss Hale, I was very ungrateful yesterday—”

“You had nothing to be grateful for,” said she, raising her eyes. “You mean, I suppose, that you believe you ought to thank me for what I did.” 

In spite of herself, the thick blushes rushed over her face, hot as a baker’s hearth, and burnt into her very eyes. 

“It was only a natural instinct; any woman would have done just the same. We all feel the sanctity of our sex as a high privilege when we see danger. I ought rather,” said she, hastily, “to apologise to you, for having said thoughtless words which sent you down into the danger.”

“It was not your words; it was the truth they conveyed, pungently as it was expressed,” he said, trying to bridge the linguistic gap between his Northern industrial dialect and her Southern sensibilities. “But you shall not drive me off upon that, and so escape the expression of my deep gratitude, my—” He was on the verge now. He would not speak in the haste of his hot passion; he would weigh each word. 

“I do not try to escape from anything,” said she, her voice steady, asserting her own linguistic power. “I simply say, that you owe me no gratitude; and I may add, that any expression of it will be painful to me, because I do not feel that I deserve it. Still, if it will relieve you from even a fancied obligation, speak on.”

“I do not want to be relieved from any obligation,” said he, goaded by her calm manner. “Fancied, or not fancied—I question not myself to know which—I choose to believe that I owe my very life to you—ay—smile, and think it an exaggeration if you will. I believe it, because it adds a value to that life to think—oh, Miss Hale!” he continued, lowering his voice to a tender, resonant timbre that altered the very air pressure in the room, “to think circumstances so wrought, that whenever I exult in existence henceforward, I may say to myself, ‘All this gladness in life, all honest pride in doing my work in the world, all this keen sense of being, I owe to her!’ And it doubles the gladness, it sharpens the sense of existence till I hardly know if it is pain or pleasure to think that I owe it to one—nay, you must, you shall hear—to one whom I love, as I do not believe man ever loved woman before.”

He held her hand tight in his. He panted as he listened for what should come, a jarring noise against the quiet. She threw the hand away with fierce indignation, her words falling like ice crystals into a warm broth. 

“Your way of speaking shocks me. It is blasphemous. I cannot help it, if that is my first feeling. It might not be so, I dare say, if I understood the kind of feeling you describe. I do not want to vex you; and besides, we must speak gently, for mamma is asleep; but your whole manner offends me—”

“How!” exclaimed he. “Offends you! I am indeed most unfortunate.”

“Yes!” said she with recovered dignity, her voice steeped in the ancestral pride of generations who knew the profound power dynamics embedded in how a thing is named. “I do feel offended; and, I think, justly. You seem to fancy that my conduct of yesterday was a personal act between you and me; and that you may come and thank me for it, instead of perceiving, as a gentleman would—yes! a gentleman,” she repeated, invoking that ancient, eroding word from her native lexicon, pregnant with cultural memory, “that any woman, worthy of the name of woman, would come forward to shield, with her reverenced helplessness, a man in danger from the violence of numbers.”

“And the gentleman thus rescued is forbidden the relief of thanks!” he broke in contemptuously, his voice cutting like a mill's saw. “I am a man. I claim the right of expressing my feelings.”

“And I yielded to the right; simply saying that you gave me pain by insisting upon it,” she replied, proudly. “But you seem to have imagined, that I was not merely guided by womanly instinct, but”—and here the passionate tears, kept down for long, came up into her eyes and choked her voice—“but that I was prompted by some particular feeling for you—you! Why, there was not a man—not a poor desperate man in all that crowd—for whom I had not more sympathy—for whom I should not have done what little I could more heartily.”

“You may speak on, Miss Hale. I am aware of these misplaced sympathies of yours. I now believe that it was only your innate sense of oppression—(yes; I, though a master, may be oppressed)—that made you act so nobly as you did. I know you despise me; allow me to say, it is because you do not understand me.”

“I do not care to understand,” she replied, taking hold of the table to steady herself, grounding her hands against its polished, tactile grain. She thought him cruel, and she was weak with her indignation.

“No, I see you do not. You are unfair and unjust.”

Margaret compressed her lips. She would not speak in answer to such accusations. But, for all his savage words, he could have thrown himself at her feet, and kissed the hem of her garment. She did not speak; she did not move. The tears of wounded pride fell hot and fast. He waited awhile, longing for her to say something, but she was silent. The space between them stretched, thick and heavy, punctuated only by the distant, unrelenting commercial roar of Milton—a sound that ceaselessly eroded the quiet spaces necessary for human communion.

He took up his hat. “One word more. You look as if you thought it tainted you to be loved by me. You cannot avoid it. Nay, I, if I would, cannot cleanse you from it. But I would not if I could. I have never loved any woman before: my life has been too busy, my thoughts too much absorbed with other things. Now I love, and will love. But do not be afraid of too much expression on my part.”

“I am not afraid,” she replied, lifting herself straight up. “No one yet has ever dared to be impertinent to me, and no one ever shall. But, Mr. Thornton, you have been very kind to my father,” said she, changing her tone to a most womanly softness, seeking a fragile linguistic bridge. “Don’t let us go on making each other angry. Pray don’t!”

He took no notice of her words. He occupied himself in smoothing the nap of his hat with his coat-sleeve, and then, rejecting her offered hand, and making as if he did not see her grave look of regret, he turned abruptly away and left the room. 

Margaret caught one glance at his face before he went. When he was gone she thought she had seen the gleam of washed tears in his eyes; and that turned her proud dislike into a sharp, painful self-reproach. Her mind circled back to the quiet kitchens of her ancestors, where conflicts were smoothed over the shared rituals of breaking bread, not fractured by the blunt, inadequate lexicon of industrial pride. 

“But how could I help it?” she asked herself, inhaling the faint, lingering scent of his woolen coat that still hung in the air. “I never liked him. I was civil; but I took no trouble to conceal my indifference. Indeed, I never thought about myself or him, so my manners must have shown the truth. All that yesterday, he might mistake. But that is his fault, not mine. I would do it again, if need were, though it does lead me into all this shame and trouble.” 

She stood alone in the ringing silence of the house, craving only the grounding labor of the kitchen, the familiar smell of rising dough, and the restorative hum of a simmering kettle to wash away the bitter aftertaste of a language she could not speak.
