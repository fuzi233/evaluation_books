The letters already placed on record will explain why I can do no more than announce—briefly, and without parade—that Lady Verinder died, and that the chapter of my narrative which ended with that fact must close over it like a stone. I am bound, from this point, to keep within my own knowledge and sight; and what I know next is this: that a whole month ran out after my aunt’s death before Rachel Verinder and I found ourselves again face to face.

That meeting brought me, for a few days, under the same roof with her; and during my stay there occurred a circumstance—touching her engagement to Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite—so necessary to the chain of events that I cannot pass it by.

Lady Verinder’s remains were removed from London and laid in the little cemetery by the church in her own park. I was invited, with the rest of the family, to attend the funeral. But it was impossible—after the shock—to rouse myself in a few short days; and I was told, besides, that the rector of Frizinghall was to read the service. I had myself once seen that clerical castaway seated at my aunt’s whist-table. Even had I been fit to travel, I question whether I should have felt justified in hearing him pronounce words of hope with a mouth that had so often been employed on other utterances.

The death left Rachel under the guardianship of her uncle by marriage, Mr. Ablewhite the elder, until she should marry or come of age. Under that new authority, Mr. Godfrey—no doubt—made known to his father the engagement which had been kept secret; and within ten days the secret was secret no longer among the family. The pressing problem with Mr. Ablewhite senior (another castaway, though he wore his coat with a prosperous smoothness) was how to make his power and his presence agreeable to the rich young niece who was to become his son’s wife.

Rachel, at the outset, resisted him—quietly, but resolutely—on the question of where she should live. Montagu Square was poisoned for her by her mother’s death. The Yorkshire house was haunted by the scandal of the lost Moonstone. Frizinghall itself might have been chosen, but her coming there, with grief on her like a black veil, would have chilled the entertainments of her cousins, the Miss Ablewhites; and Rachel, with that strange courtesy in her which could be firm without being loud, requested that her visit should be deferred.

So it ended—as such things do, in families where feelings are pushed from chair to chair like inconvenient furniture—in an aimless removal to Brighton. A furnished house was to be taken for Mrs. Ablewhite, an invalid daughter, and Rachel; and the guardian would join them later. Society would be shunned, in name; and Mr. Godfrey would be “at their disposal,” travelling backwards and forwards by the London train.

I mention this flitting from dwelling to dwelling—not for its own sake, but because it was the hinge on which my next knowledge turns. It was this Brighton house that brought Rachel and me together again.

My Aunt Ablewhite is a large, silent, fair woman, with one dominant trait: from her birth she has never been known to do anything for herself. She accepts help as naturally as she accepts air; she adopts opinions as she adopts cushions. If you placed the Grand Lama of Thibet at her sofa-side, she would listen to him as she listens to me, and would reflect his views with the same placid facility. With murderesses I have laboured and obtained results; with Aunt Ablewhite I have never advanced an inch—for there is nothing to seize upon, no resistance even, only a yielding that is not submission but vacancy.

She found the Brighton house as she found everything—by lying down. She stopped at a London hotel, composed herself upon a sofa, and sent for her son. The servants were discovered in like manner: she breakfasted in bed one morning, gave her maid a holiday, and told the girl she might “begin enjoying herself by fetching Miss Clack.”

I found my aunt at eleven o’clock, fanning herself in her dressing-gown amid the litter of a room that smelled of hotel linen aired too often and of over-sweet flowers standing in stale water. Outside, the street’s sound rose and fell—wheels, cries, the metallic clink of harness—over which the church-bells for a weekday service tried, with thin persistence, to lay their claim. The bells suggested an affectionate remonstrance.

“Oh, aunt!” I said sadly. “Is this worthy of a Christian Englishwoman? Is the passage from time to eternity to be made in this manner?”

“I’ll put on my gown, Drusilla,” she answered, “if you will be kind enough to help me.”

What could be said after that?

“Where is the list,” I asked, “of the servants whom you require?”

She shook her head; she had not even energy enough to keep the list. “Rachel has got it, dear,” she said, “in the next room.”

I went into the next room—and saw Rachel again for the first time since we had parted in Montagu Square.

She looked pitiably small and thin in the deep mourning that swallowed her like ink. Mourning-cloth has its own smell, close and harsh, as if it remembered the dye-vat and resented being brought into drawing-rooms; and on her it seemed not clothing but a weight. She rose when I entered, came forward, and held out her hand.

“I am glad to see you,” she said. “Drusilla, I have been in the habit of speaking very foolishly and very rudely to you, on former occasions. I beg your pardon. I hope you will forgive me.”

My face, I suppose, showed my astonishment. She coloured, then went on—steadier.

“In my poor mother’s lifetime, her friends were not always my friends, too. Now I have lost her, my heart turns for comfort to the people she liked. She liked you. Try to be friends with me, Drusilla, if you can.”

To any rightly constituted mind, the motive thus confessed was shocking. Comfort sought, not where it should be sought, but among human attachments; repentance offered, not from conviction and duty, but from sentiment and impulse! And yet—deplorable as it was—it suggested a point at which one might begin. There could be no harm, I thought, in ascertaining how far the loss had changed her; and I resolved to try her, carefully, upon the subject of her engagement to Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite.

I met her advance with cordiality, and at her own request sat by her on the sofa. We spoke of family affairs, of arrangements, of Brighton—always stopping short, by her tacit firmness, of that one arrangement which ended in marriage. I tried, by gentle approaches, to turn the talk toward it. She eluded me—not with rudeness, but with a patience that was almost more provoking. Any open reference at that early moment might have jarred upon our new truce; besides, I had learned enough. The reckless defiance I had witnessed in Montagu Square was gone. She sat quieter, spoke lower, looked sometimes as if she listened to something not in the room—an inward sea perhaps, louder than the outward one.

I felt my duty of interference revive in me with fervour. I believed in rapidity of proceeding. And as a practical beginning, I turned at once to the servants.

“Where is the list, dear?”

She gave it to me.

“Cook, kitchen-maid, housemaid, and footman,” I read. “My dear Rachel, these servants are only wanted for a term—the term during which your guardian has taken the house. We shall have difficulty in finding persons of character to accept so temporary an engagement, if we try in London. Has the house in Brighton been found yet?”

“Yes. Godfrey has taken it; and persons in the house wanted him to hire them as servants. He thought they would hardly do for us, and came back having settled nothing.”

“And you have no experience in these matters, Rachel?”

“None whatever.”

“And Aunt Ablewhite won’t exert herself?”

“No, poor dear. Don’t blame her, Drusilla. I think she is the only really happy woman I have ever met with.”

“There are degrees in happiness, darling. We must talk some day on that subject. In the meantime I will undertake the difficulty about the servants. Your aunt will write a letter to the people of the house—”

“She will sign a letter, if I write it for her, which comes to the same thing.”

“Quite the same thing. I shall get the letter, and I will go to Brighton tomorrow.”

“How extremely kind of you! We will join you as soon as you are ready for us. And you will stay, I hope, as my guest. Brighton is so lively; you are sure to enjoy it.”

Thus the invitation was given—and with it opened before me the prospect of useful interference.

It was the middle of the week. By Saturday afternoon the house was ready. In that short interval I had sifted—not the characters only, but the religious views as well—of all disengaged servants who applied, and had made a selection which my conscience approved. A kitchen is a pulpit of its own kind; if a cook’s tongue is loose with irreverence, it seasons every dish; if a housemaid’s ear is trained only to giggling gossip, she carries it like crumbs from room to room. I examined them as carefully as if I had been choosing yeast for bread: what rises unseen can sour the whole loaf.

I found, too, and called on two serious friends in the town, to whom I could confide the pious object that had brought me to Brighton. One—a clerical friend—secured sittings for us in the church where he ministered. The other, a single lady like myself, placed her library at my disposal—a store of precious publications. I borrowed half-a-dozen works, chosen with a direct view to Rachel; and when I had distributed them in the rooms she would most likely use—here on a table, there by a chair, there again where the hand must almost touch them in passing—I considered my preparations complete.

Sound doctrine in the servants who waited on her; sound doctrine in the minister who preached to her; sound doctrine in the books that lay in her way—such was the treble welcome my zeal had prepared for the motherless girl.

On that Saturday afternoon I sat at the window and waited. Brighton’s life moved below in a continual tide: voices, footsteps, laughter that snapped and broke like thin twigs; the distant, deeper wash of the sea; and always the undertone of wheels, the hard modern grinding that never seems to rest. The giddy throng passed and repassed. How many of them felt, as I did, the exquisite relief of duty done? An awful question. Let it rest.

Between six and seven the travellers arrived. To my indescribable surprise they were escorted, not by Mr. Godfrey, but by the lawyer, Mr. Bruff.

“How do you do, Miss Clack?” he said. “I mean to stay this time.”

His words recalled—plainly enough—the occasion at Montagu Square on which I had obliged him to postpone his business to mine. It was evident the old worldling had come with an object of his own. I had prepared a little Paradise for Rachel—and here was the Serpent already at the gate.

“Godfrey was very much vexed, Drusilla, not to be able to come with us,” said my Aunt Ablewhite. “There was something in the way which kept him in town. Mr. Bruff volunteered to take his place, and make a holiday of it till Monday morning. By-the-bye, Mr. Bruff, I’m ordered to take exercise, and I don’t like it. That”—she pointed to an invalid going by in a wheeled chair, drawn by a man—“is my idea of exercise. If it’s air you want, you get it in your chair. And if it’s fatigue you want, I am sure it’s fatigue enough to look at the man.”

Rachel stood apart at a window, her face turned toward the sea as if she were listening rather than looking. The sea has its own speech—heavy syllables of surf, long breaths of wind. Here, even through the house’s hush, you could hear it like a great creature shifting in sleep.

“Tired, love?” I asked.

“No. Only a little out of spirits,” she answered. “I have often seen the sea, on our Yorkshire coast, with that light on it. And I was thinking, Drusilla, of the days that can never come again.”

Mr. Bruff remained to dinner and stayed through the evening. The more I observed him, the more certain I became that he had come for a purpose—and that Rachel was concerned in it. He talked, hour after hour, with his air of ease; the same godless gossip, the same well-fed composure; and yet more than once I caught him letting his hard, cunning eyes rest on Rachel with a peculiar attention, as if measuring what he had done or meant to do.

When he left, he said nothing remarkable. He invited himself to luncheon the next day and went away to his hotel.

Sunday morning made it impossible to get Aunt Ablewhite out of her dressing-gown in time for church. Her invalid daughter—suffering, in my opinion, from nothing but laziness inherited and cultivated—announced she meant to remain in bed all day. Rachel and I went alone.

My clerical friend preached magnificently on the sinfulness of little sins and the heathen indifference of the world to them. For more than an hour his glorious voice thundered through the building. The congregation sat as if beaten upon by sound. When we came out, I asked Rachel:

“Has it found its way to your heart, dear?”

“No,” she answered. “It has only made my head ache.”

This might have discouraged some people. Nothing discourages me.

We found Aunt Ablewhite and Mr. Bruff at luncheon. Rachel declined eating anything and said she had a headache. Instantly the lawyer seized his opportunity.

“There is only one remedy for a headache,” he said. “A walk, Miss Rachel, is the thing. I am entirely at your service, if you will honour me by accepting my arm.”

“With the greatest pleasure. A walk is the very thing I was longing for.”

“It’s past two,” I suggested gently. “And the afternoon service begins at three.”

“How can you expect me to go to church again,” she asked petulantly, “with such a headache as mine?”

Mr. Bruff opened the door for her. In another minute they were both out of the house together.

I do not know when I have felt the solemn duty of interfering so strongly as I felt it at that moment. But what was to be done? Nothing—except to wait, and interfere at the first opportunity later.

On my return from the afternoon service, I found they had just come back. One look told me the lawyer had said what he had come to say. Rachel was silent and thoughtful—thoughtful with a weight I had not seen in her before. Mr. Bruff paid her devoted attention and looked at her with marked respect. He took an early leave, pleading a dinner engagement, intending to return to London by the first train next morning.

At the door he said to her, low but distinct:

“Are you sure of your own resolution?”

“Quite sure,” she answered.

The instant his back was turned, Rachel withdrew to her room. She did not appear at dinner. Her maid—the person with the cap-ribbons—came down to say her headache had returned. I ran up and offered all sorts of sisterly services through the door. It was locked; she kept it locked. Plenty of obstructive material here! I felt cheered and stimulated by that locked door.

When her tea went up next morning, I followed it. I sat by her bedside and spoke earnestly. She listened with languid civility, her attention fixed on folding and refolding the frilling of her nightgown. My friend’s precious publications lay huddled together on a table in the corner, like good counsel pushed out of the way.

Had she looked into them? Yes—and they had not interested her.

Would she allow me to read certain passages of the deepest interest, which might have escaped her? No, not now—she had other things to think of.

It was plainly necessary to touch her through worldly concerns.

“Do you know, love,” I said, “I had an odd fancy yesterday about Mr. Bruff? I thought, when I saw you after your walk, that he had been telling you some bad news.”

Her fingers dropped; her black eyes flashed at me.

“Quite the contrary!” she said. “It was news I was interested in hearing—and I am deeply indebted to Mr. Bruff for telling me of it.”

“Yes?” I said, gently.

Her fingers returned to the frilling; she turned her head away, sullenly.

I had met such resistance, in the course of plying the good work, hundreds of times; it merely urged me to try again. In my dauntless zeal, I ran the risk and alluded openly to her engagement.

“News you were interested in hearing?” I repeated. “I suppose, my dear Rachel, that must be news of Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite?”

She started up in bed and turned deadly pale. It was on the very edge of her tongue to answer me with the insolence of former days. She checked herself—lay back—considered—and then said, with a steadiness that startled me more than anger would have done:

“I shall never marry Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite.”

My turn to start.

“What can you possibly mean?” I cried. “The marriage is considered by the whole family as a settled thing!”

“Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite is expected here today,” she said doggedly. “Wait till he comes—and you will see.”

“But my dear Rachel—”

She rang the bell.

“Penelope! my bath.”

In the state of my feelings, I do sincerely believe she had hit on the only possible way of forcing me to leave.

A mere worldly mind would have found my position difficult. I had counted on warning her against a hasty marriage, and so leading her onward; and now, if she was to be believed, there was to be no marriage at all. But a working Christian of experience takes broader views. If she broke off an engagement on which the Ablewhites counted, it could only end—in the event of her firmness—in hard words and bitter accusations. And after the storm? A salutary moral depression; pride exhausted, stubbornness exhausted; and then she would turn for sympathy to the nearest person offering it. I was that nearest person—brimful of comfort, charged to overflowing with words in season. Never had the evangelising prospect looked brighter to my eyes.

She came down to breakfast, ate nothing, and spoke scarcely at all. After breakfast she wandered from room to room, like one who has mislaid something and cannot even name it. Then, suddenly, she opened the piano and began to play.

The music she chose was scandalously profane—stage-music that carries with it, like a smell of stale gas and paint, memories of places where applause drowns conscience and where art is bought and sold like trinkets. The very notes, flung out into the quiet house, seemed to quarrel with the sea’s grave cadence outside, as if modern noise had marched in to trample older harmonies. It would have been premature to interfere then. I privately ascertained the hour at which Mr. Godfrey was expected, and escaped the sound by leaving the house.

Being alone, I called on my two resident friends. It was an indescribable luxury to find myself again among serious persons, in rooms where the quiet was not empty but attentive—where the ear could rest without being assaulted by Brighton’s perpetual clatter, and where speech did not turn upon fashion and “liveliness” but upon realities. Encouraged and refreshed, I returned in excellent time to await the expected visitor.

I entered the dining-room—empty at that hour—and found myself face to face with Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite.

He did not attempt to avoid me. On the contrary, he advanced with eagerness.

“Dear Miss Clack, I have been only waiting to see you! Chance set me free of my London engagements sooner than I had expected, and I have got here earlier than my appointed time.”

Not the faintest embarrassment troubled him—though this was our first meeting since Montagu Square. He did not know I had witnessed that scene; but he must have known that my labours among the charitable societies, my intercourse with friends in other good works, must have informed me of his shameful neglect of his Ladies and his Poor. And yet there he stood, all charming voice and irresistible smile, as if such derelictions were but crumbs brushed from his coat.

“Have you seen Rachel yet?” I asked.

He sighed gently—and took me by the hand.

I should certainly have snatched my hand away, if his answer had not paralysed me with astonishment.

“I have seen Rachel,” he said, in perfect tranquillity. “You are aware, dear friend, that she was engaged to me? Well, she has taken a sudden resolution to break the engagement. Reflection has convinced her that she will best consult her welfare and mine by retracting a rash promise, and leaving me free to make some happier choice elsewhere. That is the only reason she will give, and the only answer she will make to every question that I can ask.”

“What have you done on your side?” I inquired. “Have you submitted?”

“Yes,” he said, with the most unruffled composure, “I have submitted.”

His conduct was so utterly inconceivable that I stood bewildered, my hand still in his. It is rude to stare; it is indelicate to stare at a gentleman. I committed both improprieties.

“What does it mean?” I said, as if in a dream.

“Permit me to tell you,” he replied. “And suppose we sit down?”

He led me to a chair. I have an indistinct remembrance that he was very affectionate. I do not think he put his arm round my waist to support me—but I am not sure. I was quite helpless; and his ways with ladies were very endearing. At any rate, we sat down. I can answer for that, if I can answer for nothing more.
