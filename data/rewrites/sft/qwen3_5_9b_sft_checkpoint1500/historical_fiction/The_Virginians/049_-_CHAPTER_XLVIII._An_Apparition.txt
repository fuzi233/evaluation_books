Driven from the room by the heat of Mr. Draper's morning reproof, Harry lingered near the threshold, his pride wounded and his spirit heavy. He had intended to call the lawyer back, to plead for a reprieve, but the shame of yielding to necessity—the crushing weight of his poverty—stuck in his throat. To humble himself before a man of law, to beg for delay, felt like a betrayal of every principle he held. "An hour hence will serve," he muttered, turning away with a sullen resignation. He collapsed onto the bed, burying his face in the pillows where bitter tears soon soaked the linen.

In the quiet torment of his isolation, a fresh anguish took root. He despised the marriage arrangement with Maria Esmond. It was not love, but a trap, a cunning maneuver executed by a sister who exploited his boyish innocence as easily as her brother had cheated him at cards. They were his kin, yet they had turned against him, sacrificing the rights of hospitality to feed their own greed. They had made a victim of the trusting youth who sought shelter within their walls. His heart ached with a sorrow so deep it brought him to his knees, his mind drifting to a fantasy of what might have been. *Had they crossed the Atlantic,* he thought, *to Virginia, I would have welcomed them as brothers.*

The somber silence was shattered by the creak of the door. Gumbo stood there, his grin wide and unfeeling, announcing the arrival of a lady. Behind the servant stepped the woman in the capuchin cloak—a vision of sorrow and desperation.

Harry sat upright, his countenance drained of color, his frame haggard. As Gumbo retreated, the lady rushed forward, her breath coming in ragged gasps. With a sob that tore at her throat, she threw her arms around his neck, clutching him with a maternal fervor that transcended mere duty. She buried her face in his shoulder, weeping openly, her lips pressing hot kisses against his pale cheek.

"Oh, my Harry!" she cried, her voice breaking. "Did I ever dream I should see thee here?"

Harry recoiled instinctively, startled by the sudden intimacy, but the woman sank to her knees beside the bed. Seizing his feverish hand, she pressed it to her lips, embracing his legs with a desperate tenderness. Her gaze held a profound, genuine compassion for his plight. The wretched state of his imprisonment, the hollow look in his eyes, filled her soul with a love and pity that stripped away any remaining reserve.

"I—I feared you would not send for me!" Harry whispered, a groan escaping him.

For a moment, she offered nothing but tears, more kisses, and the crushing weight of her embrace. Then, her voice softened. "Oh, my darling! My life! How can I bear to think of thee suffering?"

Though hardened by years of hardship, her heart was far from stone. Her tone surpassed even the devotion of a biological mother. "Some of these debts," she confessed, her voice trembling, "were born of my own folly. Thou hast spent thy gold upon trifles and gems to please me. Oh, how I curse those foolish gifts now! I had no notion I could ever bring myself to do this!"

From within her reticule, she produced a cascade of treasures: brooches, rings, a pocket-watch, and a handful of gold guineas, pouring them into Harry's lap. The sight of the wealth struck him with a jolt, stirring a tide of emotion that threatened to overwhelm him.

"Dearest, kindest cousin!" he sobbed, overwhelmed.

Words failed him, yet his gratitude and affection radiated from his silence. Suddenly, a spark of gaiety ignited within him. Smiling weakly, he tucked the trinkets away, vowing to gift them to Maria, and recounted the perilous bargain he had made to fund Maria's dowry, explaining how the lawyer had mocked him over it. He refused the twenty guineas, insisting he had enough for the present, yet he cherished the gesture as if it were worth a fortune. He promised never to forget her kindness; it had buoyed him when despair nearly crushed him. He vowed to inform his mother of her goodness.

As twilight deepened, the shadows lengthened, yet the gloom of the cell seemed lifted. The world was not devoid of warmth. Here was a creature who loved him truly. Even Castlewood, he reflected, was not entirely lost. Though hopelessly ruined, the Baron had expressed regret, promising to aid if he could. He would seek out Mr. Warrington, who was also detained, and together they would find a path forward. Until the night fully enveloped them, the cousins conversed with a renewed sense of affection.

Finally, Maria sighed, rose, and bid him farewell. As the door clicked shut, cutting off her soothing presence, it swung open again to admit Mr. Draper.

"The honor of your company, sir," the attorney said, his voice jarring against Harry's nerves.

"You waited long enough, sir," Harry replied curtly.

"A lawyer's schedule is rarely his own, sir," Draper retorted, adjusting his coat. "But rest assured, I am at your disposal. Have you packed your trunks? You remain in your dressing gown. Let me descend to arrange matters while you summon your attendant and freshen yourself. I have a carriage ready, and we shall dine with the old lady."

"Will you dine with the Baroness de Bernstein?" Harry asked.

"Not I. I have eaten. It is thou who art bound to dine with thy aunt, I presume?"

"Mr. Draper, you imagine far more than you know," Harry said, standing tall and folding his brocade robe around himself with a fierce dignity.

"What do you mean, sir?" Draper demanded, confused.

"I mean, sir, that I have given my word to a faithful and honorable lady. It becomes me not to retract it."

"For heaven's sake, sir!" Draper shrieked. "I tell you she has lost the bond! There is no obligation binding you! She is old enough to be—"

"That is enough, sir!" Harry stamped his foot, his anger flaring. "You seem to believe you are speaking to a common trickster. Take it as read, Mr. Draper, that you are not accustomed to dealing with men of honor."

"Pettifogger, indeed!" Draper roared, his face flushing with fury. "Men of honor! I assure you, I am as honorable a man as thou. I may not know so many gamblers and horse-jockeys, nor have I squandered my inheritance living like a lord on two hundred a year. I have not bought watches on credit and pawned them!" Springing toward the door, he lunged forward.

"That is the exit, sir," Harry declared, stepping into his path with fists clenched. "Thou cannot pass through the window, for it is barred."

"And my answer to my client is 'No,' then!" Draper screamed, his composure fracturing.

Harry advanced, his voice low and dangerous. "If thou utterest another word..."

The sentence died in his throat as Draper slammed the door and fled, leaving Harry alone in the stifling air. The lawyer had stormed out to Madame de Bernstein, delivering the most favorable account he could devise, yet emerging with no less fiery condemnation from the old woman than he had received from Harry himself.

"What? Shall I trust her, and yet abandon her?" Harry paced his chamber, the silk of his gown rustling loudly. "My dear, faithful, generous mistress! Should I rot in prison for a dozen years, I shall remain true to her."

With the stormy departure of his counsel, the desolate old woman was forced to sit down to the meal she had hoped to share with her nephew. The chair remained empty before her, the silver candlesticks gleaming, untouched. Dish after dish was placed before her by the silent major-domo, tasted, and rejected. Finally, the steward pressed his hand upon her arm. "It is past eight," he murmured. "Thou hast eaten naught today. It would do thee good to eat."

She could not swallow. She required her coffee. "Send Case," she commanded, her voice thin. "Fetch me my coffee."

The servants cleared the table, leaving her seated alone before the vacant chair. Moments later, the old servant returned, carrying no tray, his face etched with terror. "Mistress!" he gasped. "Mr. Warrington!"

The old woman let out a cry, rising from her chair only to sink back down, trembling violently. "So ye have come, sir? Art ye here?" Her voice shook, a fragile thread of sound. "Return the—" And then she screamed, "Good heavens, who is this?"

Her eyes widened, staring wildly, her painted face suddenly ghastly beneath the rouge. Clinging to the arms of her chair for support, she gazed upward as the figure approached.

A gentleman entered, his features and stature mirroring Harry Warrington almost uncannily. His voice, when it spoke, carried a haunting similarity to the prisoner's. He bowed deeply to the Baroness.

"Ye expected my brother, madam?" the stranger said softly. "I have but newly arrived in London. I sought his residence, where I encountered his servant at thy gate, bearing this letter for thee.
