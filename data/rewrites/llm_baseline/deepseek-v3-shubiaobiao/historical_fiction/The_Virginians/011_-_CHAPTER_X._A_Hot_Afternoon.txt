The guests of Castlewood had retired to their chambers, and the two young Warrington brothers found themselves alone in their room, their hearts aflame with indignation. The notion of their mother wedding a man scarcely older than themselves—a colonial surveyor, no less!—was unbearable. The very thought of Castlewood passing into the hands of a stepfather at three-and-twenty struck them as a monstrous betrayal.

Harry, impulsive as ever, was ready to storm their mother’s chamber at once, to denounce the match and swear they would leave her forever if she persisted. But George, ever the strategist, had a different plan.

“Mother cannot marry a man who has fought one of us,” George declared, his pale face flushed with the heat of the evening’s wine. “If we challenge him, if we face him on the field—wound or be wounded—the matter is settled. She would not dare wed our killer.”

Harry, ever the devoted follower, marveled at his brother’s cunning. “You’re right, George! She could never marry a man who’d spilled our blood. Should I send my boy with a challenge at once?”

George, recalling his own duel in Quebec, shook his head with lofty condescension. “We cannot insult a guest beneath our own roof. But once we ride beyond these gates, then we shall speak our minds plainly.”

Harry seized his brother’s hand. “By heaven, we will!”

A shadow crossed Harry’s face then, and he stammered, “George… if it comes to pistols, let it be my turn.”

George waved him off. “The head of the house must lead. If I fall, you may avenge me. Besides,” his voice hardened, “it is I who hate him most. Was it not he who counseled Mother to have that brute Ward lay hands on me?”

Harry, ever the peacemaker, murmured, “Forgive and forget, brother.”

“Forget?” George’s eyes burned. “A wrong does not vanish with time. I will not suffer this man’s arrogance—not then, not now.”

With that, the brothers retired, George sternly commanding Harry to maintain civility toward all guests until their departure.

***

Morning brought the aftermath of the night’s revelry. The English officers stumbled to breakfast, their heads heavy, while Colonel Washington remained composed, refusing the morning’s restorative draught. The British officers jested that he needed proper schooling in their ways, but the Virginian only smiled coolly.

Madam Esmond, though occupied with her duties, noted George’s scrupulous politeness toward Washington—a veneer that masked the storm beneath. Before General Braddock departed, he formally offered George a place in his staff, and the arrangements were swiftly made. The lady of Castlewood, though anxious, bore it with fortitude.

As the great coach rolled away, the boys lingered on the steps. Washington stood with their mother once more, and they heard his solemn vow: *“With my life. You may depend on me.”*

Then came the farewells. Washington clasped Harry’s hand warmly. To George, he said, “No adieu—we meet in three days at the camp.”

But George’s smile was cold. “Stay a moment longer, Colonel. Harry and I will ride with you a little way.”

Mounting their horses, the three set off, their grooms trailing behind. George, unnaturally cheerful, kept the conversation light, while Harry rode in troubled silence. Washington, disarmed by the apparent reconciliation, spoke warmly of the coming campaign, confident in British victory.

At Benson’s tavern, they paused. Halkett’s officers were already there, deep in their cups. George, with deliberate provocation, called for a round of toasts.

“The King!” he proclaimed, fixing Washington with a challenge.

The Colonel stiffened but drank.

“The Duke and the Army!” cried Captain Grace, slurring.

Again, Washington drank, though his face darkened.

“You don’t seem to relish the toast, Colonel,” George taunted.

“I’ve had enough,” Washington growled.

Grace, drunk and belligerent, took offense. “A British officer never refuses a toast!”

Washington’s temper snapped. “You are drunk, sir!”

The room erupted. George, seizing his moment, feigned outrage. “You insult my guest, Colonel!”

Washington, his patience spent, turned on George. “Months of your insolence—were it not for my regard for your mother—”

“What would you do?” George sneered.

“I’d take a cane to you, you impudent pup!”

The words hung in the air like a pistol shot.

Harry, pale but resolute, stepped forward. “That is an insult to us both. You will apologize or give satisfaction.”

Washington, aghast at his own outburst, tried to retreat. “My words were hasty—”

But George was relentless. “You shirk again, Colonel. You have insulted me publicly. I demand reparation.”

Washington, his face a mask of sorrow, bowed. “So be it.”

Grace, swaying, tried to intervene, but the matter was set. Pistols, George insisted—not swords, where Washington’s skill was superior.

As they parted to make preparations, Washington’s voice was heavy. “God forgive you, George. God pardon you, Harry.”

But George’s heart was stone. “This began long ago, Colonel. You thought me a slave then. You shall not think so now.”

Harry, torn but loyal, stood firm beside his brother.

And so the stage was set—not for war against the French, but for a duel that would alter their fates forever.
