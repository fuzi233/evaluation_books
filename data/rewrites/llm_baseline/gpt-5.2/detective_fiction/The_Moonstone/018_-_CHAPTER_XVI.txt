We found my lady in a room that looked, at first glance, as if it had been set for quiet study and nothing more: one reading-lamp burning low, the rest of the candles refused; the shade screwed down so tight that the light fell in a miserly circle—bright on the page, stingy on the face above it. The house beyond her door had its own uneasy music: a distant clink of closing shutters, a floorboard’s complaining creak, the faint, restless shift of wind in the chimneys like a hand rubbing at a sore place.

My lady sat close to the table, not lifting her eyes in her usual frank fashion, but pinning them stubbornly to an open book as if the printed words were a shield she could hold between herself and the rest of us.

“Officer,” she said, without looking up, “is it important to the inquiry you are conducting, to know beforehand if any person now in this house wishes to leave it?”

“Most important, my lady,” answered Sergeant Cuff.

“I have to tell you, then, that Miss Verinder proposes going to stay with her aunt, Mrs. Ablewhite, of Frizinghall. She has arranged to leave us the first thing tomorrow morning.”

The Sergeant’s eyes moved—one quiet glance, and it found me at once. I made a step forward to speak to my mistress; and, in the same instant, felt my courage go like heat out of bread when the oven-door is thrown open too soon. I took a step back again, and said nothing.

“May I ask your ladyship when Miss Verinder informed you that she was going to her aunt’s?” inquired the Sergeant.

“About an hour since,” my mistress answered.

The Sergeant looked at me again. They say old hearts are hard to stir. Mine beat like a kitchen clock that has been knocked out of its case.

“I have no claim, my lady,” he resumed, “to control Miss Verinder’s actions. All I can ask you to do is to put off her departure, if possible, till later in the day. I must go to Frizinghall myself tomorrow morning—and I shall be back by two o’clock, if not before. If Miss Verinder can be kept here till that time, I should wish to say two words to her—unexpectedly—before she goes.”

My lady, still with her eyes on the book, told me to give the coachman her orders: the carriage was not to come for Miss Rachel until two.

When I returned and said it had been done, my mistress asked the Sergeant, “Have you more to say?”

“Only one thing, your ladyship. If Miss Verinder is surprised at this change in the arrangements, please not to mention me as being the cause of putting off her journey.”

At that, my lady lifted her head suddenly, as if something sharp had pricked her under the ribs. Her lips parted. She checked herself by a force that seemed to pinch the breath back down her throat, let her eyes fall again to the open page, and dismissed us with a motion of her hand.

In the hall, where the sounds of the house came clearer—servants moving soft-footed, a latch whispered to, a clock far off counting like a steady knife—Sergeant Cuff spoke low.

“That’s a wonderful woman. But for her self-control, the mystery that puzzles you, Mr. Betteredge, would have been at an end tonight.”

It was as if those words had carried a hidden spice—something bitter and unmistakable—straight to my tongue. The truth rushed at last into my slow old head.

For a moment I believe I went clean out of my senses. I seized the Sergeant by the collar and pinned him against the wall.

“Damn you!” I cried. “There’s something wrong about Miss Rachel—and you have been hiding it from me all this time!”

He lay there flat against the paneling, not raising a hand, not shifting a muscle in that melancholy face of his; and looked up at me as if I had merely spilled a glass of ale.

“Ah,” he said, “you’ve guessed it at last.”

My hand fell away. My head sank. Remember, if you please, I had served the family fifty years. Miss Rachel had climbed upon my knees and pulled my whiskers, many and many a time, when she was a child. Whatever her faults, she was, in my mind, the dearest and prettiest and best young mistress an old servant ever waited on—and loved.

I begged his pardon; but I fear I did it with watery eyes, and in no very becoming way.

“Don’t distress yourself, Mr. Betteredge,” he said, kinder than I had any right to expect. “In my line of life, if we were quick at taking offence, we shouldn’t be worth salt to our porridge. If it’s any comfort to you, collar me again. You don’t in the least know how to do it; but I’ll overlook your awkwardness in consideration of your feelings.”

He curled up the corners of his lips, and in his dreary way seemed pleased with his own joke.

I led him into my little sitting-room and shut the door; and there, though the table was laid for supper and the smell of it rose warm and homely—bread cut, meat waiting, the comfort of a covered dish holding its heat like a living thing—I could not bring myself to taste any of it. Food, that is commonly a mercy, turned to mere matter in my mouth.

“Tell me the truth, Sergeant,” I said. “What do you suspect? It’s no kindness to hide it from me now.”

“I don’t suspect,” he answered. “I know.”

My temper rose again like a pot beginning to boil.

“Do you mean to tell me, in plain English, that Miss Rachel has stolen her own Diamond?”

“Yes,” said the Sergeant. “That is what I mean to tell you, in so many words. Miss Verinder has been in secret possession of the Moonstone from first to last; and she has taken Rosanna Spearman into her confidence, because she has calculated on our suspecting Rosanna Spearman of the theft. There is the whole case in a nutshell. Collar me again, Mr. Betteredge. If it’s any vent to your feelings, collar me again.”

God help me, my feelings were not to be relieved by laying hands on him.

“Give me your reasons!” was all I could manage.

“You shall hear my reasons tomorrow,” he said. “If Miss Verinder refuses to put off her visit to her aunt (which you will find Miss Verinder will do), I shall be obliged to lay the whole case before your mistress tomorrow. And as I don’t know what may come of it, I shall request you to be present, and to hear what passes on both sides. Let the matter rest for tonight. No, Mr. Betteredge—you don’t get a word more on the subject of the Moonstone out of me. There is your table spread for supper. That’s one of the many human infirmities which I always treat tenderly. If you will ring the bell, I’ll say grace. ‘For what we are going to receive—’”

“I wish you a good appetite to it, Sergeant,” I said. “My appetite is gone. I’ll wait and see you served, and then I’ll ask you to excuse me, if I go away and try to get the better of this by myself.”

I saw him served with the best of everything—and I shouldn’t have been sorry if the best of everything had choked him. The clatter of plate and the pour of drink sounded too loud in that little room, as if the house itself had lost the trick of quiet. A bottle of Scotch whisky appeared, and with it the head gardener, Mr. Begbie, bringing in his weekly account; and the Sergeant, as if he had not just turned my world upside down, slipped into talk of roses, grass-walks, and gravel-walks, with the ease of a man tasting a familiar broth.

I left them and went out with a heart that felt heavy as a wet sack. This was the first trouble I remember, for many a year, that wasn’t to be blown off by a whiff of tobacco and that was beyond the reach even of Robinson Crusoe. Restless and miserable, with no room in the house that would hold me comfortably, I took a turn on the terrace.

The night had its own kitchen-work going on: wind kneading the trees, clouds dragged and folded over the moon, the shrubs hissing as if a pot were set too near the fire. In quieter times, those sounds were part of our place’s old language—each corner had its note, each season its tune. Now even the familiar noises seemed to speak in a dialect I could not follow, the same words but altered meaning; and that, to my mind, is how misfortune enters a household first—by changing what the ear has trusted all its life.

It doesn’t much matter what I thought. I felt wretchedly old, worn out, and unfit for my place; and wondered for the first time when it would please God to take me. Yet I held firm to my belief in Miss Rachel. If Sergeant Cuff had been Solomon in all his glory and had told me my young lady had mixed herself up in a mean and guilty plot, I should have answered Solomon, wise as he was, “You don’t know her; and I do.”

Samuel interrupted me. He brought a written message from my mistress. When I went into the house to get a light, he remarked that there seemed a change coming in the weather. My troubled mind had kept me from noticing it; but now, with my attention pricked, I heard the dogs uneasy in their sleep, and the wind moaning low along the eaves like a creature shut out. Looking up, I saw the rack of clouds blackening and hurrying over a watery moon. Wild weather coming—Samuel was right—wild weather coming.

My lady’s message informed me that the magistrate at Frizinghall had written to remind her about the three Indians. Early in the coming week, those men must be released and left free to follow their own devices. If we had more questions to ask, there was no time to lose. She had forgotten to mention this when last she saw Sergeant Cuff and now desired me to supply the omission.

The Indians had gone clean out of my head—as, no doubt, they had gone clean out of yours. I saw little use in stirring that pot again; but I obeyed, as a matter of course.

I found Sergeant Cuff and the gardener with the whisky between them, deep in argument on the growing of roses. The Sergeant was so intent that he held up his hand to silence me as I came in, signing not to interrupt. The question, as far as I could make out, was whether the white moss rose did or did not require budding on the dog-rose to make it grow well. Mr. Begbie said Yes; Sergeant Cuff said No. They turned on me and appealed as hotly as boys over a stolen apple.

Knowing nothing whatever of roses, I steered a middle course—much as her Majesty’s judges do when the scales trouble them by hanging even to a hair.

“Gentlemen,” I said, “there is much to be said on both sides.”

In the lull my impartial sentence produced, I laid my lady’s message on the table under the Sergeant’s eyes.

By this time I had got, as nearly as might be, to hate him; but truth compels me to own he was wonderful in readiness. In half a minute he had read the note, rummaged in his memory for Superintendent Seegrave’s report, picked out the part concerning the Indians, and had his answer ready.

A certain great traveller, who understood the Indians and their language, had been named in the report, hadn’t he? Very well. Did I know the gentleman’s name and address? Very well again. Would I write them on the back of my lady’s message? Much obliged. Sergeant Cuff would look the gentleman up when he went to Frizinghall in the morning.

“Do you expect anything to come of it?” I asked. “Superintendent Seegrave found the Indians as innocent as the babe unborn.”

“Superintendent Seegrave has been proved wrong, up to this time, in all his conclusions,” the Sergeant answered. “It may be worth while to find out tomorrow whether Superintendent Seegrave was wrong about the Indians as well.”

And with that, as if the matter of human guilt and innocence were no more than a change in the weather, he turned straight back to Mr. Begbie, taking up the dispute exactly where it had left off.

“This question between us is a question of soils and seasons, and patience and pains, Mr. Gardener. Now let me put it to you from another point of view. You take your white moss rose—”

I shut the door on them and went out. In the passage I met Penelope hanging about, and asked what she waited for. She was waiting for her young lady’s bell, whenever her young lady chose to call her back to go on with the packing for the next day’s journey.

Further inquiry revealed that Miss Rachel had given, as her reason for wanting to go to her aunt at Frizinghall, that the house was unendurable to her and that she could bear the odious presence of a policeman under the same roof with herself no longer. When she had been told, half an hour since, that her departure would be delayed till two in the afternoon, she had flown into a violent passion. My lady, present at the time, had severely rebuked her, and then—having apparently something to say for her daughter’s private ear—had sent Penelope out.

My girl was in miserably low spirits.

“Nothing goes right, father; nothing is like what it used to be. I feel as if some dreadful misfortune was hanging over us all.”

It was my feeling too; but I put a good face on it before her. Miss Rachel’s bell rang while we spoke. Penelope ran up the back stairs to go on with the packing, and I went the other way towards the hall to see what the glass said about the weather.

As I approached the swing-door leading from the servants’ offices, it was flung open violently from the other side, and Rosanna Spearman ran by me with pain stamped on her face and one hand pressed hard over her heart, as if the pang lived there.

“What’s the matter, my girl?” I called, stopping her. “Are you ill?”

“For God’s sake, don’t speak to me,” she answered; and she twisted out of my hands and fled towards the servants’ staircase.

I called to the cook, within hearing, to look after the poor girl. Two other persons proved to be within hearing as well. Sergeant Cuff darted softly out of my room and asked what was the matter. I answered, “Nothing.”

Mr. Franklin, on the other side, pulled open the swing-door and beckoned me into the hall. His voice had that brittle edge it takes when a man is trying not to show he is frightened.

“Have you seen anything of Rosanna Spearman?” he asked.

“She has just passed me, sir, with a very disturbed face, and in a very odd manner.”

“I am afraid I am innocently the cause of that disturbance, Betteredge.”

“You, sir!”

“I can’t explain it,” said Mr. Franklin; “but if the girl is concerned in the loss of the Diamond, I do really believe she was on the point of confessing everything—to me, of all the people in the world—not two minutes since.”

As he looked towards the swing-door, I fancied I saw it open a little from the inner side. Was there somebody listening? Before I could get to it, the door fell to. When I looked through the moment after, I thought I caught the tails of Sergeant Cuff’s respectable black coat disappearing round the corner of the passage.

He knew, as well as I did, that he could expect no more help from me now that I had found out the direction of his suspicions. Under those circumstances, it was like him to help himself—and to do it by an underground way. Still, not being sure I had really seen him, and not wishing to make needless mischief where Heaven knows there was mischief enough already, I told Mr. Franklin I thought one of the dogs had got into the house; and then begged him to describe what had passed.

“Were you passing through the hall, sir?” I asked. “Did you meet her accidentally, when she spoke to you?”

He pointed to the billiard-table.

“I was knocking the balls about,” he said, “and trying to get this miserable business of the Diamond out of my mind. I happened to look up—and there stood Rosanna Spearman at my side, like a ghost! Her stealing on me in that way was so strange, I hardly knew what to do at first. Seeing a very anxious expression in her face, I asked her if she wished to speak to me. She answered, ‘Yes, if I dare.’

“Knowing what suspicion attaches to her, I could only put one construction on such language. I confess it made me uncomfortable. I had no wish to invite the girl’s confidence. At the same time, in the difficulties that now beset us, I could hardly feel justified in refusing to listen to her, if she was really bent on speaking. It was an awkward position; and I dare say I got out of it awkwardly enough.

“I said, ‘I don’t quite understand you. Is there anything you want me to do?’ Mind, Betteredge, I didn’t speak unkindly. The poor girl can’t help being ugly—I felt that, at the time. The cue was still in my hand, and I went on knocking the balls about, to take off the awkwardness. As it turned out, I only made matters worse. I’m afraid I mortified her without meaning it. She suddenly turned away. ‘He looks at the billiard balls,’ I heard her say. ‘Anything rather than look at me!’ Before I could stop her, she had left the hall.

“I am not quite easy about it, Betteredge. Would you mind telling Rosanna I meant no unkindness? I have been a little hard on her, perhaps, in my own thoughts—I have almost hoped the loss of the Diamond might be traced to her. Not from any ill-will to the poor girl: but—”

He stopped, and returned to the billiard-table, knocking the balls about again—click, click—little sharp sounds that seemed to speak of impatience, not play.

After what had passed between the Sergeant and me, I knew as well as Mr. Franklin what he had left unspoken. Nothing but tracing the Moonstone to our second housemaid could now raise Miss Rachel above the infamous suspicion resting on her in Sergeant Cuff’s mind. It was no longer a question of quieting my young lady’s nerves; it was a question of proving her innocence.

If Rosanna had done nothing to compromise herself, Mr. Franklin’s confessed hope would have been hard enough on her. But she had compromised herself. She had pretended illness and gone secretly to Frizinghall. She had been up all night making something—or destroying something—in private. And she had been at the Shivering Sand that evening under circumstances suspicious, to say the least. For all these reasons, sorry as I was for Rosanna, I could not think Mr. Franklin’s view unnatural in his position.

I said so.

“Yes, yes!” he returned. “But there is just a chance—a very poor one, certainly—that Rosanna’s conduct may admit of some explanation we don’t see yet. I hate hurting a woman’s feelings, Betteredge. Tell the poor creature what I told you to tell her. And if she wants to speak to me—I don’t care whether I get into a scrape or not—send her to me in the library.”

With those words he laid down the cue and left me.

Inquiry at the servants’ offices informed me Rosanna had retired to her room. She declined all offers of assistance with thanks, and asked only to be left to rest in quiet. So there was an end of any confession—if confession there was—for that night. I reported as much to Mr. Franklin; he left the library and went up to bed.

I was putting the lights out and making the windows fast when Samuel came in with news of the two guests I had left in my room. The argument about the white moss rose had apparently come to an end at last. The gardener had gone home. Sergeant Cuff was nowhere to be found below stairs.

I looked into my room. True enough—nothing there but a couple of empty tumblers and a strong smell of hot grog hanging in the air, sweet and harsh at once, like a comfort turned sour.

Had the Sergeant gone to the bedchamber prepared for him? I went upstairs to see.

After reaching the second landing, I thought I heard the sound of quiet, regular breathing on my left-hand side. That way led to the corridor that communicated with Miss Rachel’s room.

I looked in; and there, coiled up on three chairs placed right across the passage—there, with a red handkerchief tied round his grizzled head, his respectable black coat rolled for a pillow—lay Sergeant Cuff, asleep.

He woke instantly and quietly, like a dog the moment you step near.

“Good-night, Mr. Betteredge,” he said. “And mind—if you ever take to growing roses, the white moss rose is all the better for not being budded on the dog-rose, whatever the gardener may say to the contrary!”

“What are you doing here?” I asked. “Why are you not in your proper bed?”

“I am not in my proper bed,” he answered, “because I am one of the many people in this miserable world who can’t earn their money honestly and easily at the same time. There was a coincidence, this evening, between the period of Rosanna Spearman’s return from the Sands and the period when Miss Verinder stated her resolution to leave the house. Whatever Rosanna may have hidden, it’s clear to my mind that your young lady couldn’t go away until she knew that it was hidden. The two must have communicated privately once already tonight. If they try to communicate again when the house is quiet, I want to be in the way and stop it. Don’t blame me for upsetting your sleeping arrangements, Mr. Betteredge—blame the Diamond.”

From somewhere outside, the wind found a keyhole and began to whistle thinly through it—an ugly little tune, like a modern contrivance imitating nature and failing.

“I wish to God the Diamond had never found its way into this house!” I broke out.

Sergeant Cuff looked with a rueful face at the three chairs that were to be his bed.

“So do I,” he said, gravely.
