Ethne sat in the corner of a pew, near the aisle, her hands folded quietly in her lap. Harry Feversham stood beside her, silent and still. The little church was hushed, bathed in the soft glow of afternoon light that streamed through the high windows, painting golden patterns across the stone roof. Outside, the gentle hum of summer—birds singing, leaves rustling, distant voices—drifted in through the open door, as if nature itself were listening.

She spoke first, her voice calm, measured, yet carrying a weight that seemed to fill every quiet corner of the space. “I’m glad you remembered our ride and what we said,” she began. “It matters to me that you do. Because though I’ve brought you back after all these years—after so much pain, so much waiting—I must send you away again. You’ll be one of those absent friends I won’t lose, even when you’re gone.”

Her eyes stayed fixed straight ahead, steady and unshaken. It had been hard to say these words—harder than she’d imagined—but she had thought them through for days, rehearsed them in silence, until they came now like a breath of truth. At first sight of him, returned from exile, she had nearly broken down. But she had gathered herself, stepped back into the church, and waited. She knew he would understand. He would know how deeply this cost her. And she wanted him to know—wanted him to feel the full meaning of it: that none of his six years had been wasted, that every moment of suffering, every silent night, had shaped something real between them.

“It’s not easy,” she continued, “but there’s no other way.” She paused slightly, letting the silence settle around them like dust. “You’re home now. That’s a comfort. But there are greater things to hold onto. Colonel Trench told me enough about your captivity—your courage, your endurance—to know we see the same truth. This parting… it’s nothing compared to the first. That one felt final. Back then, I believed we were forever separated. We wouldn’t meet again—not here, not beyond. But you broke that wall. With everything you endured, with every wound you carried, you made it possible for us to have an ‘afterwards.’ I’m certain of it. And I believe you are too.”

She stopped. For a long while, no sound but the faint creak of the church door and the whisper of wind outside. To Feversham, her words were like water falling on parched earth—life-giving, healing. They lifted him up. The six years of shame, of hiding, of loneliness, of physical torment—all of it faded into insignificance. He had dreamed of hearing such words, lying awake in Suakin, in desert camps, under stars that burned too bright. He had never dared hope they would come. Now they had.

And he stood there, motionless, still hearing her voice even after it had ceased.

He remembered once telling Sutch that he believed he would die with her reproaches echoing in his ears—those cruel words she’d spoken long ago. But now he knew that wasn’t true. The words he would carry to the grave were these—gentle, clear, full of grace.

He had expected her to speak of her engagement. He had heard rumors, and he didn’t argue. But he sensed there was more. And there was.

She hesitated before speaking again, stretching out the moment, prolonging their time together. This was the last time she would see him. Once he left, once his shadow passed through the church door, she would never speak to him again, never look upon him, until her life ended. So she held back, drawing out each word, each pause, as if clinging to seconds that might never return.

“I’m going to be married,” she said at last. “Soon. To Colonel Durrance—someone you know.”

Feversham nodded slowly. “He’s cared for you for a long time. I didn’t realize it until I left, but I suspected. After thinking over everything, I became sure.”

“He’s blind,” Ethne said softly.

“Blind?” Feversham echoed, stunned. “Him? Of all men?”

“Yes,” she replied. “Exactly. His blindness explains everything—why I marry him, why I send you away. It happened after he lost his sight. Before Captain Willoughby brought me the first feather. Between those two events.”

She looked at him then, for the first time since taking her seat. Her eyes were clear, resolute.

“I thought carefully after you left,” she said. “I lay awake many nights, trying to sort things out. I decided no man’s life should be ruined because of me.”

“You weren’t ruined,” Feversham interrupted. “Please believe that.”

“Partly you were,” she said gently. “You wouldn’t admit it—for my sake. But it was true. And I swore no second man would suffer that fate. When Durrance went blind—when I saw what it meant to him, the loss of everything he loved—”

“Except you,” Feversham murmured.

“Except me,” she agreed. “So I promised myself I would be his. But he has grown so sharp, so perceptive. A glance, a tone, and he knows what lies beneath. Right now, he doesn’t know about the four feathers.”

Feversham frowned. “Are you sure?”

“Yes,” she said. “Why?”

“Lieutenant Sutch was at Suakin when I was in Omdurman. He knew I was a prisoner. He tried to help me escape.”

Ethne blinked. “Oh. Then Durrance must have known you were in Omdurman. He saw you in Wadi Halfa. He heard you went south. He was worried. He asked someone to find news—and they told him you were captured. He told me himself. And yes—he said he’d try to arrange your rescue. Probably through Sutch. He’s been in Wiesbaden with an eye doctor—only just returned. Otherwise, he would have told me. He may have sent Sutch to Suakin. But he knows nothing of the feathers. Only that our engagement ended abruptly. He thinks I’ve forgotten you entirely.”

She paused. “But if you came back—if we met, even calmly, even briefly—he would guess. He’s too quick. And he would guess right.”

Feversham watched her face. A flush rose in her cheeks. He did not move. Did not lean in. Did not speak with emotion. He kept his distance, knowing that any sign of feeling would make the farewell harder.

“Yes,” he said quietly. “And he must not guess.”

“No,” she whispered. “He must not. I’m so glad you understand, Harry. The only thing we can do is keep it simple, honest. He must never know. Because—as you said—he has nothing left but me.”

“Is Durrance here?” Feversham asked.

“At the vicarage.”

“Then I’ll go to him,” Feversham said. “I owe it to him. But I want you to know—I never expected you to wait. I never wanted you to. I had no right to expect it. When you gave me that fourth feather in the little room at Ramelton, with music drifting through the door, I understood. It was final. There would be no friendship, no lingering. No half-measures.”

He paused. “That’s why I never told you about my plan—the one that formed the moment you handed me the feathers. I might have failed. I might have died. I might have run. So I waited. If I came back, I would speak then. That was all I hoped for—just to come home, and for you to take back your feather. Not to renew anything here, but to meet again… afterwards.”

Ethne smiled. A small, quiet smile. “Then there will be no parting.”

She said it simply, without sigh or tremor. But her eyes held his, and he saw the depth of what it cost her. And suddenly, with a clarity he’d never known before, he understood—fully, completely—what it had meant six years ago when she was left alone, her heart shattered, her pride wounded. He had always carried guilt, but now he felt its full weight.

“You suffered so much,” he said, voice thick.

“Not alone,” she replied. “I didn’t spend nights in the House of Stone.”

“But it was my fault,” he insisted. “Do you remember what you said when morning came through the blinds? ‘It’s not right that one should suffer so much pain.’ It wasn’t right.”

“I forgot those words,” she said softly. “Until Colonel Trench reminded me. I shouldn’t have said them. I didn’t mean them to live in your memory. I’m sorry.”

“Oh, they were enough,” Feversham said, laughing lightly. “I used to think they’d be the last words I ever heard. But today—you’ve given me others to replace them.”

“Thank you,” she said, quietly.

There was nothing else to say. Feversham wondered why she hadn’t risen. He didn’t ask about his journey, his trials. The moment was too sacred for talk of adventure. They were deciding the shape of their lives—now and beyond.

Yet she lingered. Still seated, still composed, her heart aching with longing. Just a little longer, she thought. The sunlight was fading from the walls. She watched the golden patch shrink, inch by inch. When it vanished, she would let him go.

“I’m glad you escaped Omdurman without help,” she said. “I wanted it to be yours alone—no interference, no rescuers.”

Silence followed. She glanced toward the wall. The light was thinner now.

“You suffered terribly at Dongola,” she whispered. “Colonel Trench told me.”

“What does it matter now?” Feversham replied. “It feels far away.”

“Did you have anything of mine?” she asked.

“My white feather,” he answered.

“Anything else? Something I gave you long ago?”

“Nothing.”

“I kept your photograph,” she said. “I saved it.”

Feversham leaned forward, startled. “You did?”

She nodded. “The moment you left that night, I packed your gifts and sent them to your rooms. But I took your photo first. I kept it. I burned all your letters—after I addressed the parcel and carried it to the hall. I’d just finished when I heard your footsteps outside. But the photo was already safe. I still have it. I’ll keep it with the feathers.”

After a moment, she added, “I wish you’d had something of mine with you.”

“I had no right to anything,” he said.

The last sliver of gold light vanished. The church was filled with cool evening light—clear, but colorless.

“What will you do now?” she asked.

“Go home. See my father. How we meet will decide what comes next.”

“You’ll tell Durrance. I’d like to hear about it.”

“I’ll write.”

They walked out together, side by side. She leaned slightly toward him, their arms brushing. He mounted his horse. As his foot touched the stirrup, she pulled her dog close.

“Good-bye,” she said.

No smile now. Just her hand, offered steadily. He took it, bent low from the saddle, and held it a moment longer than needed. Her eyes met his—brimming with tears, but unwavering.

“Good-bye,” he said.

He released her hand and rode down the hill. He rode a hundred yards, then stopped. Looked back.

She stood where he left her, facing him, unmoving. No wave. No gesture. Just stillness. Then she turned and walked slowly up the village street, alone.

Feversham watched until she reached the gate. By then, she was blurred, fading into the dusk. He saw her enter, but she did not turn again.

He rode on.

The wrong he had done so long ago—though he had labored for six years to atone—could not be erased. Its consequences would live on, bringing sorrow not to himself, but to another. That she bore it with dignity, without complaint, only deepened his remorse. She deserved no pain. Not this. Not ever.

For Ethne was not one to love lightly, nor forget easily. If she loved, she gave her whole heart. And now, though she would marry, she would be alone—truly alone—until the very end.
