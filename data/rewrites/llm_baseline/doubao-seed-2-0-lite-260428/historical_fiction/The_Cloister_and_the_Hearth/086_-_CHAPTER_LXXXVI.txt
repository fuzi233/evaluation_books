The ancient stone church, its walls dappled with shifting sunbeams and its vaulted ceiling streaked with the deep heavenly purple of stained glass, wrapped around Father Clement like a soft, cooling blanket, soothed him exactly as it soothed Margaret lingering at the edges of the congregation. More than that, it lifted his mind straight to the Creator who made every good, pure delight in the world. His gaze drifted to the broad main aisle, packed wall to wall with his country folk, a thousand crisp white linen caps stitched with fine gold filigree bobbing in the pews. In all the hundreds of leagues he had wandered across foreign lands, he had never seen anything that looked quite like them—nothing but fresh fallen snow, drifting soft across field and roof.

That morning, he had mounted the pulpit and thundered of sin and judgment, but this warm drowsy golden afternoon felt entirely out of step with threats. His chest ached with tender yearning for the crowd before him; he had to speak to them of God's quiet, abiding love. For these simple souls heard almost no mention of that gentle love from most pulpits of the day, no more than far-off heathen people who had never heard the name of Christ at all. He told them the glad, soft tidings of salvation, and every last man, woman, and child leaned in, hanging on every word of his earnest, unhurried voice. He was never the sort of preacher who gyrated across the pulpit like a weathercock spinning on a steeple: he stirred hearts far more than he stirred his own body.

Warmed through by the theme, determined that not a single soul in that multitude would miss the joyful news of Christ's love, he turned to face the south aisle. There, framed in a broad slant of sunlight spilling through the high arched window, was the radiant face of Margaret Brandt.

For a long beat he gazed at it without conscious feeling, numb from the crown of his head to the soles of his feet, soul and body both locked in ice. Soon the words died in his throat, and he trembled, fixed fast on that sight. There she stood, her auburn hair bathed in sunbeams, glittering like the halo of a saint, her face glowing doubly bright with her own soft beauty and the golden light that gilded her skin. She leaned very lightly against a smooth white marble column, her lashes fallen tenderly, listening exactly as he had seen her listen to him a hundred times back in Sevenbergen. There was no change in her: this was the blooming Margaret he had left, only a shade riper, sweeter, lovelier than the memory he had carried like a relic across all his years of wandering. He stared at her with wide, unblinking eyes, his cheeks drained of every drop of blood. The thousand people in the pews vanished out of his sight. He heard a faint rustle, the sound of hundreds of parishioners shifting in their seats, as if filtered through a mile of thick wool, but he could not tear his gaze off that warm, living face, that wondrous copper hair glistening gloriously in the sun. He waited for her to vanish, for it to be some trick of the light or ghostly vision. She did not move.

All at once, she lifted her head and looked full at him, straight into his very own soul through those famous violet eyes he had dreamed of every night. He was beside himself, his lips parting to shriek her name across the whole church, when she turned her head swiftly, and slipped back out of the sunbeam. She did not disappear completely before she darted one more glance at him, quick as lightning, that locked with his: a startled, inquiring, nameless, indescribable look, the very last split second before full recognition hits. She staggered, reeling, her mind a whirl of unnameable confusion, while he panted, clutching the edge of the pulpit convulsively so hard his knuckles went white.

He summoned all his strength to mutter a few words no one in the congregation could possibly make sense of, then feebly stumbled back into his sermon. He stammered and babbled on for a while, forcing himself, now that she was out of sight, to frame the sight as a vision from the other world. A strange, unnatural fire of excitement rose in him, and he finished the sermon in a wild flow of eloquence bordering on rhapsody, that electrified every simple soul listening.

When the sermon ended, he sank down onto the little stool inside the pulpit, badly shaken from head to toe. Then a thought, typical of the pious certainties of that age, took hold of him: he had searched Sevenbergen high and low for Margaret's grave in vain. She had been permitted to appear to him now, to show him she was buried here, right by the very pillar where her spirit had revealed itself. That idea settled in his mind as solid and unshakable as a proven fact. He knew he had only to speak to the sexton—whom he had spotted digging in the churchyard mid-sermon, much to his mild displeasure—to learn the exact spot where she was laid to rest.

The church was soon empty of all worshipers. He climbed down from the pulpit, stepped through a low opening in the south wall onto the soft sun-warmed grass, and walked straight over to the sexton. He recognized Jorian in an instant. But Jorian never suspected that the wild young lad whose life he had saved years ago stood before him in the robes of this holy friar. The loss of Gerard's full, shapely beard had altered the whole outline of his face far more than his tonsure, his cropped hair streaked with premature grey, or his cheeks thinned and paled by years of fasts and midnight vigils.

"My son," said Friar Clement softly, "if you keep any memory of the souls you lay in the earth, prithee tell me: is any Christian buried inside the church, near one of the pillars?"

"Nay, father," said Jorian, leaning on his spade, "all who rest here are buried in the churchyard, not under the church stones. Why do you ask?"

"No matter for that," Clement said. "Prithee tell me then where lieth Margaret Brandt."

"Margaret Brandt?" Jorian stared stupidly up at him. "She died some three years ago, aye, and was buried here."

"Oh, that is another matter," said Jorian, shrugging. "That was long before my time. The vicar could tell you, likely—if she was a gentlewoman, or at least rich enough to pay him his grave fee."

"Alas, my son, she was poor," Clement whispered, his head sinking onto his breast, "and paid a heavy penalty for it. But she came of decent folk. Her father Peter was a learned physician; she came hither all the way from Sevenbergen to die." The words barely left his mouth before he went still, no strength or will left to question Jorian further. He was lost entirely in the past, and I doubt he even knew where he stood.

Jorian set his spade down, planted his fists on his hips, and said sulkily, "Are you making a fool of me, holy sir, or has some wag been making a fool of you?" He turned back to digging, flinging dirt with sharp, irritable vigor that made it clear his temper was ruffled.

Clement gazed at him with a puzzled, gently reproachful eye; the tone was rude, and the words made no sense to his foggy, grief-addled mind. Crusty as he was, Jorian was good-natured at heart. He had barely thrown up three spadefuls of earth before he grew ashamed of his outburst. "What a base churl am I to speak thus to thee, holy father, and thee standing there looking at me as mild as a lamb! Aha! I have it. 'Tis Peter Brandt's grave you would fain see, not Margaret's. He does lie here, hard by the west door. There, I'll show you." He laid down his spade, pulled on his doublet and jerkin, and prepared to walk the friar over to the tomb.

He had no idea there was anyone sitting on top of Peter Brandt's grave, still less that she was watching for this very holy friar. Pietro Vanucci and Andrea had not recognized him without his beard, either. The truth is, a beard that has never known a razor grows to become such a distinct, defining feature of a man's face that shaving it off can act as an almost perfect disguise.
