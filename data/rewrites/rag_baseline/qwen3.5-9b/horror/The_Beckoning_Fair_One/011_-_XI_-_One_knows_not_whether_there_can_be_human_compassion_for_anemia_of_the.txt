One knows not whether there can be human compassion for the anemia of the soul. When the pitch of Life is dropped, and the spirit is so thoroughly inverted that only the grotesque remains of what was once sweet and worldly, the human relation dissolves. The sane soul recoils in terror, lest not merely itself, but sanity itself, be consumed. We are not gods; we cannot exorcise demons. Our only imperative is to ensure they do not enter us, even though Love may transfuse us with a divinity we falsely claim. In such states, honor and duty become hollow incantations. A letter dropped within a darkened doorway lies ignored, or if glimpsed for a fleeting second between two unspeakable lapses, is forgotten again. Telegrams go undelivered; the whistling messenger, wiser than he knows, walks away unaware of the drawn blind pushed aside an inch by a trembling finger and then fearfully restored. Let the miserable wrestle with their own shadows; let them, if indeed they be mad, clip and strain and enfold the succubus—but let them do so in a house into which no air of Heaven penetrates, nor does a bright finger of the sun pierce the filthy twilight. The lost must remain lost. Humanity has other business.

For Oleron, the handwriting of the two letters he had seen on the floor within his door that June day held no more meaning than a dim, faraway dream. Stealing noiselessly into his kitchen to clear his sitting-room of an armful of fetid, decaying flowers, he had dismissed them without a glance. At the beating of the telegraph boy's knuckles against the door, mere feet from the bed where he lay, Oleron had gnashed his teeth and stuffed his ears. He had pictured the lad standing just beyond the partition, amidst packets of provisions and bundles of dead blooms. His outer landing was littered with this refuse. Oleron had feared to open the door to admit them. After a week, the errand boys reported a mistake in the order and left nothing behind. Inside, in the red twilight, the old flowers turned brown, fell, and rotted where they lay.

Gradually, his power drained away. The Abomination fastened upon Oleron's vitality. The steady sapping left him for hours of prostration, gazing vacantly up at his red-tinged ceiling, idly suffering fancies that took root in the vacuum. Even his strongest memories held only a precarious grasp on his attention. Sometimes a flitting half-memory of a novel he must write tantalized him before vanishing; sometimes whole novels, perfect and splendid, rose magically before his eyes. Other times, the recollections were absurdly remote and trivial: garrets he had inhabited, lodgings that had sheltered him. Oleron had known much of such things in his time, but all was now past. He had finally found a place he did not intend to leave until they fetched him out—a place some might deem slightly green-sick, others too redolent of long-dead, morbid things for a living man to be mewed up in. Yet it was irresistible, possessing an authority of its own, an associate of its own, and a realm of delights once a man ceased struggling against its inexorable will.

A novel? Someone ought to write a book about a place like that! There must be plenty to uncover if one could but get to the bottom of it! It had probably already been painted by a man named Madley who had lived there... but Oleron had not known this Madley. He had a strong feeling he wouldn't have liked him; he would rather he had lived elsewhere. He hated him, Madley, in fact. (Aha! That was a joke!). Oleron seriously doubted whether the man had led the life he ought; he was in two minds about telling that long-nosed guardian of public morals across the way, but probably the man knew and had made his praying hullabaloo for him too. That was his line. Why, Oleron himself had had a dust-up with him about something—or someone... Elsie Bengough, he remembered.

Oleron felt moments of deep unease regarding Elsie Bengough. Or rather, he was not so much uneasy about her as restless about the things she did. Chief among these was her persistence in thrusting herself into his thoughts. Whenever he was quick enough, he sent her packing the moment she appeared. The truth was she was not merely a bore; she had always been that. Now it had reached the pitch where her very presence in his fancy was inimical to the full enjoyment of certain experiences. She had no tact; she ought to have known people were not at home to everyone's thoughts all the time; she ought, in mere politeness, to allow him certain seasons entirely to himself. She was monstrously ignorant if she did not know, as she appeared not to, that there were special hours when a man's veins ran with fire and daring and power, in which... well, in which he had a reasonable right to treat folk as he had treated that prying Barrett—to shut them out completely.

But no: up she popped, the thought of her, and ruined all. Bright towering fabrics, by the side of which even those perfect, magical novels of which he dreamed were dull and grey, vanished utterly at her intrusion. It was as if a fog should suddenly quench some fair-beaming star, as if at the threshold of some golden portal prepared for Oleron a pit should suddenly gape, as if a bat-like shadow should turn the growing dawn to mire and darkness again. Therefore, Oleron strove to stifle even the nascent thought of her.

Nevertheless, there came an occasion on which this woman Bengough absolutely refused to be suppressed. Oleron could not tell exactly when this happened; he only knew by the glimmer of the street lamp on his blind that it was some time during the night, and that for some time she had not presented herself. He had no warning, none, of her coming; she just came—was there. Strive as he would, he could not shake off the thought of her nor the image of her face. She haunted him. But for her to come at that moment of all moments!... Really, it was past belief! How she could endure it, Oleron could not conceive! Actually, to look on, as it were, at the triumph of a rival.... Good God! It was monstrous! Tact—reticence—he had never credited her with an overwhelming amount of either: but he had never attributed mere... oh, there was no word for it! Monstrous—monstrous! Did she intend thenceforward...? Good God! To look on!...

Oleron felt the blood rush up to the roots of his hair with anger against her. "Damnation take her!" he choked. But the next moment his heat and resentment changed to a cold sweat of cowering fear. Panic-stricken, he strove to comprehend what he had done. For though he knew not what, he knew he had done something, something fatal, irreparable, blasting. Anger he had felt, but not *this* blaze of ire that suddenly flooded the twilight of his consciousness with a white, infernal light. *That* appalling flash was not his—not his *that* open rift of bright and searing Hell—not his, not his! His had been the hand of a child, preparing a puny blow; but what was *this other* horrific hand that was drawn back to strike in the same place? Had *he* set that in motion? Had *he* provided the spark that had touched off the whole accumulated power of that formidable and relentless place? He did not know. He only knew that that poor igniting particle in himself was blown out, that—Oh, impossible!--a clinging kiss (how else to express it?) had changed on his very lips to a gnashing and a removal, and that for very pity of the awful odds he must cry out to her against whom he had lately raged to guard herself... guard herself....

"Look out!" he shrieked aloud.

The revulsion was instant. As if a cold, slow billow had broken over him, he came to find that he was lying in his bed, that the mist and horror that had for so long enwrapped him had departed, that he was Paul Oleron, and that he was sick, naked, helpless, and unutterably abandoned and alone. His faculties, though weak, answered at last to his calls upon them; and he knew that it must have been a hideous nightmare that had left him sweating and shaking thus. Yes, he was himself, Paul Oleron, a tired novelist, already past the summit of his best work, and slipping downhill again empty-handed from it all. He had struck short in his life's aim. He had tried too much, had over-estimated his strength, and was a failure, a failure.... It all came to him in the single word, enwrapped and complete; it needed no sequential thought; he was a failure. He had missed.... And he had missed not one happiness, but two. He had missed the ease of this world, which men love, and he had missed also that other shining prize for which men forgo ease, the snatching and holding and triumphant bearing aloft of which is the only justification of the mad adventurer who hazards the enterprise.
