"What news did you gather regarding my brother's expedition among the Bamangwato?" Sir Henry inquired, pausing as I prepared to pack my pipe before addressing Captain Good.

"I gathered this," I replied, "and until today, I have kept it silent before any living soul. I learned that he is departing for Solomon's Mines."

"Solomon's Mines?" Both listeners exclaimed simultaneously. "Pray, where do they lie?"

"I cannot say with certainty," I admitted. "I know only where they are *said* to be. In my youth, I beheld the peaks bordering them, yet a vast expanse of one hundred and thirty miles of barren desert separated me from that sight. To my knowledge, no white man has ever traversed that wasteland successfully, save for a solitary exception. Nevertheless, perhaps the wisest course is to recount the legend of Solomon's Mines as I understand it, provided you grant me your solemn promise not to divulge a single word of what follows without my explicit consent. Are you bound by this pledge? I ask because my motives are peculiar."

Sir Henry nodded assent, and Captain Good echoed, "Undoubtedly, undoubtedly."

"Very well," I commenced. "As you might surmise, elephant hunters constitute a rugged breed, men who concern themselves chiefly with the grim necessities of survival and the customs of the Kafir tribes. Yet, occasionally, one encounters a soul willing to sift through native oral histories, attempting to reconstruct fragments of this shadowed continent's past. It was such a man who first imparted the legend of Solomon's Mines to me, nearly three decades ago. This occurred during my inaugural elephant hunt in the Matabele lands. His name was Evans, a man whose life ended tragically the following year at the claws of a wounded buffalo; he now rests in the earth near the Zambesi Falls.

"One evening, recalling our conversation, I recounted to Evans some remarkable geological formations I had discovered while tracking koodoo and eland in what is now the Lydenburg district of the Transvaal. I noted that recent prospectors had rediscovered these workings, though I had known of them long prior. A magnificent roadway, hewn directly from solid rock, led to the entrance of a vast gallery. Within its mouth, stacks of gold-bearing quartz stood piled high, awaiting roasting—a testament to workers who fled in haste. Furthermore, approximately twenty paces inward, the tunnel was abruptly sealed by a section of exquisite masonry."

"'Ay,' Evans remarked, 'but I shall weave you a far stranger tale.' Thus began his narrative of a ruined city deep within the interior, which he identified as the biblical Ophir—a claim later validated by scholars long after Evans' death. I listened with rapt attention, for in my youth, the notion of an ancient civilization, capable of extracting immense wealth from a region now sunk into barbarism, seized my imagination with irresistible force. Suddenly, he shifted the topic: 'Lad, have you ever heard of the Suliman Mountains, situated northwest of the Mushakulumbwe territory?' I confessed ignorance. 'Ah, well,' he continued, 'there lies the true location of Solomon's mines—his diamond mines, specifically.'

"'How can you assert this?' I queried.

"'Assert it!' he retorted. 'What is "Suliman" but a corrupted pronunciation of Solomon? Moreover, an elderly Isanusi, a witch-doctoress dwelling in the Manica hinterland, revealed the truth to me. She spoke of the peoples beyond those mountains as a distinct branch of the Zulus, speaking a refined dialect, composed of giants even larger than their kin. Among them reside mighty sorcerers who mastered their arts from white men in an age when the world was shrouded in darkness, possessing the secret key to a miraculous vein of "bright stones."'

"At the time, I scoffed at this fabrication, though it intrigued me, especially given that the Diamond Fields remained undiscovered. Poor Evans departed shortly thereafter and met his end, and for twenty years, the matter faded from my mind. However, precisely twenty years later—a span considerable for an elephant hunter, who rarely survives two decades in the bush—I received more concrete intelligence concerning Suliman's Mountains and the realm beyond. I was stationed beyond the Manica country at a settlement known as Sitanda's Kraal, a desolate outpost where sustenance was scarce and game elusive. Stricken by fever and in a precarious state, I was visited one day by a Portuguese traveler accompanied by a single companion, a half-caste.

"I am intimately acquainted with the low-born Portuguese of Delagoa Bay, creatures often described as devils incarnate, thriving on human suffering and the trade in enslaved flesh. Yet, this visitor differed profoundly from the scoundrels I usually encountered. In appearance, he resembled the noble *doms* of my reading: tall, slender, with piercing dark eyes and curling grey mustaches. We conversed briefly; his English was fractured, and I grasped a portion of his Portuguese. He introduced himself as José Silvestre, proprietor of an establishment near Delagoa Bay. As he prepared to depart the following day with his half-caste companion, he removed his hat in a gesture of old-world courtesy.

"'Farewell, señor,' he intoned. 'Should our paths cross again, I shall stand as the wealthiest man in the world, and I shall ensure you are remembered.' I chuckled faintly, my strength failing me, watching him stride toward the great western desert. I wondered if madness gripped him, or what phantom hope drove him into that void.

"A week elapsed, and the fever receded. One evening, seated upon the dusty ground before my tent, gnawing the final leg of a pitiful bird purchased for a scrap of cloth valued at twenty birds, I stared at the burning red sun descending over the horizon. Suddenly, a figure emerged on the ridge opposite, roughly three hundred yards distant. Clad in a coat, it appeared to be a European. The figure crawled on hands and knees, then struggled upright, staggering a few steps before collapsing and resuming the crawl. Recognizing signs of dire distress, I dispatched one of my hunters. Upon his arrival, the truth became clear.

"'José Silvestre, of course,' Captain Good observed.

"Yes, José Silvestre—or rather, his skeletal remains and a fragment of skin. His visage was a sickly yellow, drained of color by bilious fever, his large dark eyes bulging from sockets emptied of flesh. Only parchment-thin skin, white hair, and protruding gaunt bones remained.

"'Water! For Christ's sake, water!' he whimpered. His lips were fissured, and his tongue, protruding between them, was swollen and black.

"I administered water mixed with a trace of milk. He consumed it greedily, drinking nearly two quarts without pause. I refused further fluid. The fever returned with vengeance, and he collapsed, babbling deliriously of Suliman's Mountains, diamonds, and the desert. I dragged him into the tent, doing what little aid was possible, knowing the outcome was inevitable. Around eleven o'clock, he quieted. Exhausted, I lay down and drifted into slumber.

"At dawn, I awoke to find Silvestre sitting upright, a specter of emaciation, gazing fixedly toward the desert. Soon, the first rays of the sun sliced across the vast plain, illuminating the distant crest of one of the towering Suliman Mountains, over a hundred miles away.

"'There it stands!' the dying man cried in Portuguese, extending his long, thin arm. 'But I shall never reach it. Never. Not a soul shall ever reach it!'

"Suddenly, he halted, seemingly steeling himself. 'Friend,' he whispered, turning his gaze to me, 'do you perceive me? My vision fades.'

"'Yes,' I replied softly. 'Lie down now and rest.'

"'Ay,' he answered. 'Rest awaits me soon. All eternity belongs to it. Listen, I perish! You have shown me kindness. I shall entrust you with this writing. Perhaps you possess the vitality to traverse the desert that claimed my poor servant and me.'

"He rummaged in his tunic and produced what I initially mistook for a Boer tobacco pouch crafted from the skin of the swartvetpens or sable antelope. Secured by a strip of hide, known locally as a *rimpi*, he struggled to loosen it before handing it to me. 'Untie it,' he commanded. I complied, revealing a torn scrap of yellow linen bearing rust-colored inscriptions. Tucked inside was a sheet of paper.

"He grew weaker, his voice fading. 'The paper contains everything written on the linen. It took me years to decipher. Listen: my ancestor, a political exile from Lisbon and among the earliest Portuguese to set foot on these shores, penned this while dying upon those mountains never trodden by white feet before or since. His name was José da Silvestra, living three centuries ago. His slave, waiting on this side of the range, discovered him dead and carried the message home to Delagoa. It has lain dormant in our family until I finally dared to read it. I have paid with my life for this quest, but another may succeed, becoming the richest man in the world—the richest man in the world. Give it to no one, señor; go yourself!'

"He then wandered into delirium, and within an hour, he was gone.

"God rest his soul! He died peacefully, and I interred him deeply beneath massive boulders upon his chest, ensuring the jackals could not disturb his grave. Afterward, I departed."
