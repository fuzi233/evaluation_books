There are lives in which the hardest bondage is not the chain after triumph, but the chain before it—when a hand has almost closed on the narthex of fire and is struck numb by the cold apparatus of “how things are done.” A mind is thrust aside with its unmarketable ardor; a body, pinched by want and illness, becomes the very argument used against it. People pass, their boots beating a steady, indifferent rhythm on the pavement, and do not turn their heads. The visionary—so often mocked as if he were merely a superstition with eyes—may yet be one who thinks in images the way some cook thinks in scents: a future dish appears complete before the first knife touches board; the taste arrives on the tongue before the broth begins to tremble. Such persons are not necessarily less reasoning than the calculators who tally profit; they are often more porous, with a hundred gates for sound, sight, and remembered feeling to pour through. Their danger is not madness, but the loneliness of having too many entrances and too few welcoming rooms.

Mordecai’s figure had lodged itself in Deronda’s mind as a question he meant to return to when he could. Yet Deronda’s interest was still the suspense of a humane observer: the consumptive-looking Jew—fervid, studious, earning his crust by a quiet handicraft—had not, at first, fitted into any of Deronda’s accustomed anticipations of life.

But in Mordecai the meeting had fallen like a spark on hoarded tinder.

For many winters, as he had felt the ebb of physical strength and the widening, draughty solitude of a soul that could not make itself heard, his desire had narrowed and sharpened into a single hunger: to find some young ear into which he might pour his mind as a testament—some spirit kindred enough to receive what his brief, painful life had distilled, and carry it beyond the small room and the dying breath. The hopefulness that illness sometimes lends—an almost childish promise of recovery—had in him been diverted entirely away from his body. He did not imagine lungs newly full of air; he imagined a future in which his thought would walk abroad, no longer coughing in corners, no longer cheapened by the visible stamp of poverty.

At first the need had taken shape by contrast. Mordecai had learned, through rebuff and the coarse economy by which the world prices a man’s ideas according to the cut of his coat and the soundness of his chest, that his own very look—his hollow cheeks, his worn clothing, the labor that kept bread in his mouth—had become a kind of translation error. People read him in the dominant lexicon of the street: “odd Jew,” “poor creature,” “dangerous dreamer,” and the meaning he meant to utter was lost, as if spoken in a dialect that no one had patience to learn.

So his imagination, urgent and practical in its very exaltation, constructed another man: a Jew too, but one who would not be discounted at sight—cultured, morally fervid, capable of sympathy with Mordecai’s inward fire, yet embodied in strength, beauty, refinement; one whose voice would flow without that occasional hoarse catch, one whose circumstances were free from sordid need; one who would glorify the possibilities of his people, not sit as Mordecai did with waning breath and the visible brand of hardship. He needed—he confessed it to himself with a candor that did not soften the necessity—an executive self, a blooming life that could take what was best in his own and give it room to act.

Because he was sensitive to physical types, he had sought them not only in faces but in pictures. In idle hours, when his fingers were not bent over watch-wheels and trinkets, he had wandered through the National Gallery as if searching a larder in time of famine—hoping, among all those canvases, to find one image that would feed his expectation: a face at once young, grand, and beautiful, where melancholy—if it lay there—did not droop like weakness but gathered itself for heroism. He would stand long, in his cloth cap rimmed with black fur, his dark eyes sunk deep as if the sockets were made too large by thought and sickness. The spectators, hearing only the gallery’s hush broken by the faint scuff of shoes and a distant cough, might take him for a Jew who got money out of pictures, or for one of those impoverished souls who linger where they cannot buy. Mordecai knew exactly what he looked like in other people’s language. That knowledge had become a second sound always in his ear, like an unwanted accompaniment.

His mind worked so constantly in images that his coherent trains of thought had the texture of significant dreams—those dreams that waking invention envies, because they break, without apology, across the boundary between what is known and what is longed for. For a long while the Being he needed appeared to him as a figure against a golden sky, distant, turning away, the outline darkened by light behind it. The gold was not arbitrary. Mordecai, despite his cramped life, was keenly alive to certain poetic aspects of London, and his strength, when it allowed him a luxury of air and distance, carried him to the bridges at sunrise or sunset. There the river’s broad, patient movement—sometimes slate-dark, sometimes with a skin of trembling light—opened his thoughts into wide spaces. The massive shapes of buildings, signs of world-commerce, stood dim and solemn, while barges and boats came on from the haze into sound and color: the dip of oars, the creak of timber, the low calls and answers on the water. It was like a great domestic routine of the city, repeated each day with variations of weather and mood; and in it Mordecai could almost believe that the soul, too, had its tides, its returns, its promise of being borne onward rather than shut in.

He needed, too, the soundscape of it—the river’s breathing against stone, the layered hush that even London could still offer when the modern din had not wholly seized the air. In that gentler acoustic, his nerves were less jangled; his thought could rise without being continually startled back into his body. The bridge was his refuge from the harsh percussion of wheels and shouted commerce, from the cheapened, advertised noise that made even art into a barked commodity.

Gradually, in the inevitable progress of imagination toward fuller detail, the figure ceased to turn its back. It advanced; a face became discernible. Youth, beauty, refinement, Jewish birth, noble gravity—these words became, not a list, but color and contour, gathered from faces he had known among the Jews of Holland and Bohemia, and from the paintings that revived those memories. Reverently, it was akin to the young person’s picturing of a future beloved; but Mordecai’s longing—strained by imminent dissolution—was fiercer, not for possession, but for transmission. The visionary form became a companion and auditor, holding its place not only in waking reverie but in those lighter sleeps where the heart seems to keep watch. The trivial story of yesterday would be charged, in those half-dreams, with the impassioned purpose of years.

Lately, the urgency of irremediable time—measured by the gradual choking of life—had turned trust into an agitated watch. Would the bell toll before his mind had found a bearer? Was the sentence already being written? The deliverer’s footstep must be near—the deliverer who would rescue Mordecai’s travail from oblivion and give it an abiding place in the best inheritance of his people.

Many would have called it an insane exaggeration of his own value. Why not say, nobly, “If not I, then another”? But the fuller nature desires not only to look on; it hungers to bless and act. It cannot be content that the heat in it should die unused, like coals quenched in ash. Mordecai could not surrender the moment’s charge without feeling he had betrayed it.

Yet, with that mingling of inconsequence that belongs to us all—and sometimes saves us from the consequences of our own errors—his confidence in the friend to come did not make him passive. He tried, humbly and pathetically, whatever channels were at hand.

It was now two years since he had lived under Ezra Cohen’s roof. There he was regarded with good-will in a composite, half-comic, half-reverent way: workman, dominie, vessel of charity, inspired idiot, man of piety, and—if anyone pressed inquiry—dangerous heretic. Little Jacob, meanwhile, had advanced into knickerbockers and into that quick apprehension which had already shown itself in all matters of hardware and exchange. He had advanced also in attachment to Mordecai, regarding him as an inferior—yet liking him none the worse for that—and taking his services as he might have taken the obedient cleverness of some enslaved spirit.

Mordecai, who had given Jacob his first lessons, found his habitual tenderness easily turning into a teacher’s fatherhood. He knew perfectly the spiritual distance between the parents and himself; he would never have tried to translate his peculiar world into their hearing, aware how the dominant household lexicon would flatten it into nonsense or danger. But the boy moved him with that idealizing affection which sees, in a child, not merely the individual creature with pockets full of treasures, but childhood itself—its glory, its unspent future.

So he began—at first without premeditation, later with conscious purpose—to pour something of himself into Jacob’s ear. No excellent man of business would have heard it without thinking it wild; but none overheard, when Jacob came up to Mordecai’s small upper room on a day with little work, or at an hour when the work was ended. There, after a brief lesson in English reading or numeration, the boy might be coaxed to stay: standing at his teacher’s knees, or jumping astride them, often to the patient fatigue of the wasted limbs. The inducement might be the mending of a toy—Mordecai’s practiced finger-tips were deft in these small salvations—or some little mechanical device that made Jacob’s eyes brighten with greed for trick and power.

With the boy thus tethered, Mordecai would begin to repeat a Hebrew poem of his own, telling Jacob to say the words after him.

“The boy will get them engraved within him,” Mordecai would think. “It is a way of printing.”

Jacob had the lively delight of imitating unintelligible sounds—rolling them in his mouth as he might roll a sweet, savoring the mere physical play. If no opposing diversion occurred, he would keep at it as long as Mordecai’s breath held out. Mordecai, for his part, threw into each repetition the fervor of a sacred occasion, as if the syllables themselves were seed—hard little grains that must be set into the child’s mind, to sprout after many days.

Jacob’s distractions were those of a boy who lives by touch and mischief. He would reach into his pockets, survey their contents like a small merchant; he would draw down the skin of his cheeks to make his eyes look dreadful and roll his head to complete the effect; he would alternately handle his own nose and Mordecai’s as if testing the relation of their masses. Under all this, Mordecai would not pause. He was satisfied if the young organs of speech would submit themselves at all.

More commonly, a sudden impulse would send Jacob leaping away into some antic, and instead of following the recitation he would return on the words already most ready to his tongue, gabbling them with a see-saw motion suited to his limbs—making a verse on which Mordecai had spent his scant heart’s blood into mere accompaniment to play. Yet Mordecai waited with the patience of one who believes in slow fermentations. “My words may rule him some day,” he would say inwardly. “Their meaning may flash out on him. It is so with a nation—after many days.”

Meanwhile, Jacob’s sense of power increased and his time was enlivened by this store of magical articulation. He made the baby crow with it; he drove the large cat into a dark corner; he promised himself he would frighten any incidental Christian of his own years. One week he had seen a street mountebank, and his muscular imitativeness was carried off into that new channel, to Mordecai’s secret vexation.

It was on a day like that, when Mordecai had arrived at a fresh passage—because new combinations of sound generally held Jacob a few minutes longer—that his voice, usually a strong high baritone, let out its Hebrew with more than usual excitement. The hoarseness in it was like haze among illuminations; now and then there was an incipient gasp, as if the words had to be pushed past a narrowing gate. The verses he intoned held a meaning something like this:

“Away from me the garment of forgetfulness,
Withering the heart;
The oil and wine from presses of the Goyim,
Poisoned with scorn.
Solitude is on the sides of Mount Nebo,
In its heart a tomb:
There the buried ark and golden cherubim
Make hidden light:
There the solemn gaze unchanged,
The wings are spread unbroken:
Shut beneath in silent awful speech
The Law lies graven.
Solitude and darkness are my covering,
And my heart a tomb;
Smite and shatter it, O Gabriel!
Shatter it as the clay of the founder
Around the golden image.”

In the absorbed invocation, Mordecai did not notice that Jacob had stopped echoing him. He had started away; and when Mordecai paused, he saw—like a sudden glare—that the boy had thrown himself on his hands with his feet in the air, mountebank fashion, and was picking up with his lips a bright farthing, a favorite among his pocket treasures.

Mordecai had endured many tricks, but this jarred him horribly at that moment, as if a Satanic grin had been set upon his prayer. Something within him—strained, starved, thwarted—broke its composure.

“Child! child!” he cried out, in a tone so strange that Jacob tumbled to his feet in alarm.

Then Mordecai sank backward with a shudder, closing his eyes.

“What?” Jacob said quickly. Not getting an answer, he pressed Mordecai’s knees with a shaking movement, trying to rouse him—as if to insist the familiar, indulgent companion must return.

Mordecai opened his eyes with a fierce expression, leaned forward, grasped the boy’s shoulders, and whispered rapidly, hoarsely, words that seemed to Jacob like thunder spoken into his face:

“A curse is on your generation, child. They will open the mountain and drag forth the golden wings and coin them into money, and the solemn faces they will break up into earrings for wanton women! And they shall get themselves a new name, but the angel of ignominy, with the fiery brand, shall know them, and their heart shall be the tomb of dead desires that turn their life to rottenness.”

The sunken eyes so close, the thin fingers gripping, the hoarse accents freighted with obscure threat—Jacob felt as if the room itself had become unsafe, as if the house were tumbling in and dinner would never come again. He stood trembling; then, when the terrible speech ended and the pinch relaxed, the shock melted into tears. He lifted his small patriarchal countenance and wept aloud.

The sound of that child’s grief brought Mordecai back as quickly as if a hand had soothed his brow. He could not speak again at once, but with a maternal tenderness he drew the curly head to him and pressed it against his breast.

Jacob, sensing danger nearly over, howled more freely; then, in that common transition from impulse into art, began to imitate his own sobbing and improve upon it, as if practicing a performance. The next day he tried to terrify Adelaide Rebekah in the same manner, and succeeded very well.

But Mordecai suffered long from the check within himself. Sane as well as excitable, he judged his own aberration with severity and felt discredited in his own eyes, as if he had used sacred speech like a blunt instrument. He could not bear the sense of having forced his meaning into a child who did not yet possess the language to receive it—of having made his heart’s dialect into mere noise.

All the more his mind strained toward discerning that friend to come, with whom there would be calm certainty of fellowship and understanding—someone who would not turn his words into mountebank tricks, someone whose inner ear could hear the intended music and not only the syllables.

It was then, in his usual midday guardianship of the old book-shop, that Deronda’s appearance struck him like the sudden accord one has waited years to hear. Mordecai’s glance took on an eager interest because the new-comer’s face and frame seemed to realize the long-conceived type. But when Deronda disclaimed Jewish birth, it was as if the vision had been thrust back with double severity: not only disappointment, but the shake to faith itself.

Yet, when Mordecai found Deronda seated at the Cohens’ table, that disclaimer seemed, for a moment, nullified. The impression returned with added force, as if guaranteed by this second meeting under circumstances more peculiar than the first; and when Mordecai asked if Deronda knew Hebrew, he was so possessed by the inrush of belief that he forgot every other condition he had once insisted on.

Deronda’s “No” struck him down again. The frustration was sharper than before.

After he had turned his back on the visitor that Sabbath evening, Mordecai went through days of deep discouragement—like men on a doomed ship who strain their eyes, behold a sail with rejoicing, then watch it never advance and say at last, “Our sick eyes made it.”

But the figure he had long contemplated was not a casual fancy. It was the emotional sequence of his firmest convictions; it had been wrought from the imagery of his most passionate life. Inevitably it reappeared—more specific, more self-asserting. Deronda’s resemblance to the preconceived type was like that of a finely individual portrait to a generalized memory: it corrects even while it delights. And now Deronda’s face met Mordecai’s inward gaze as if it had always belonged there, bringing with it, in imagination, some of the influence that belongs to breathing flesh.

Little by little, discouragement hardened into obstinacy. The ever-recurrent vision took the force of an outward call, commanding him to disregard counter-evidence and keep expectation awake. It was Deronda now who appeared in those night-watches when one thought clutches the mind; Deronda, never with his back turned, in moments of soothed reverie or soothed dozing—painted on the golden sky that was Mordecai’s doubly blessed symbol of advancing day and approaching rest.

Mordecai knew the nameless stranger would come to redeem his ring; and in spite of contrary chances, his wish to see him again grew into a belief that he would see him.

In the January weeks, he felt an increasing agitation—subdued, hidden, like the tremor that keeps nervous people from steady work on the eve of an anticipated change. He could not go on printing Hebrew on little Jacob’s mind, nor keep up attendance at a weekly club which had been another forlorn attempt at utterance. Something else was coming.

The one thing he longed for was to get as far as the river, which he could do but seldom and with difficulty. He yearned, with a poet’s yearning, for the wide sky, the far-reaching vista of bridges, the tender fluctuating lights on the water that seemed to breathe—shiver and mourn, then be comforted and rejoice—while the living city’s sounds rose and fell around it, and the old, truer music of tide and wind struggled to be heard beneath the coarse modern roar.
