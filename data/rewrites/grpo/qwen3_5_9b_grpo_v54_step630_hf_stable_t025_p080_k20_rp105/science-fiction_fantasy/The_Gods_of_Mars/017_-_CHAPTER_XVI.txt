The sight of the magnificent vessel looming before us was enough to stir the blood of every man present, but our awe was short-lived. As Carthoris, Xodar, Tars Tarkas, and I stood transfixed, a second ship crested the distant hills, followed swiftly by a third, gliding into formation with their sister. From the upper decks of the nearest battleship, a squadron of one-man air scouts launched, diving in long, screaming arcs toward the ground until we were instantly encircled by armed sailors.

An officer stepped forward to address us, but his gaze locked onto Carthoris, transforming his stern demeanor into one of startled joy. He rushed to the boy, gripping his shoulder.

"Carthoris, my Prince!" he cried, his voice ringing with relief. "Kaor! Kaor! Hor Vastus greets the son of Dejah Thoris, Princess of Helium, and her husband, John Carter. Where have you been, O my Prince? All Helium has been plunged in sorrow. Terrible calamities have befallen your great-grandfather's mighty nation since the day you vanished from our midst."

"Grieve not, my good Hor Vastus," Carthoris replied, his voice steady. "I do not return alone to cheer my mother's heart and the hearts of my beloved people. I bring back also the one whom all Barsoom loved best—her greatest warrior and her saviour—John Carter, Prince of Helium!"

Hor Vastus turned in the direction Carthoris indicated. When his eyes fell upon me, the nobleman seemed poised to collapse from sheer astonishment.

"John Carter!" he gasped, a troubled shadow crossing his features. "My Prince, where hast thou..." He stopped, the question dying on his lips. The loyal soldier dared not force from me the terrible truth: that I had returned from the bosom of the Iss, the River of Mystery, from the shores of the Lost Sea of Korus, and the Valley Dor.

"Ah, my Prince," he continued, masking his shock with formal greeting, "that you are back is sufficient. Let Hor Vastus' sword have the high honor of being first at thy feet." With these words, the nobleman unbuckled his scabbard and flung his blade onto the ground before me.

To understand the weight of this gesture, one must know the customs of the red Martians. It was an absolute surrender of self. By laying his sword at my feet, Hor Vastus declared that his life, his body, and his soul belonged to me. Until death and beyond, he looked to me alone for authority. Whether I was right or wrong, my word was his only truth; anyone who raised a hand against me would answer to his blade. It was the ultimate oath of fealty, usually reserved for a Jeddak of impeccable character, yet here it was paid to a mere mortal.

There was but one response. I stooped, lifted the sword, kissed the hilt, and stepped forward to buckle the weapon onto Hor Vastus's hip with my own hands.

"Hor Vastus," I said, placing a hand on his shoulder, "you know the promptings of your own heart best. I doubt I shall need your sword, but accept from John Carter, upon his sacred honor, the assurance that I will never call upon you to draw this blade except in the cause of truth, justice, and righteousness."

"That I knew, my Prince," he replied, "before I ever threw my beloved blade at your feet."

As we spoke, more fliers darted between the ground and the battleship. Soon, a larger craft capable of carrying a dozen men dropped lightly near us. As it touched down, an officer leaped from the deck to the ground and advanced to Hor Vastus.

"Kantos Kan desires that this party whom we have rescued be brought immediately to the deck of the *Xavarian*," he reported.

Approaching the small craft, I scanned for my companions and realized with a jolt that Thuvia was missing. Inquiry revealed that no one had seen her since Carthoris had sent her *thoat* galloping wildly toward the hills, hoping to carry her to safety. Immediately, Hor Vastus dispatched a dozen air scouts in every direction. It was impossible for her to have gone far. We boarded the waiting craft and were soon upon the *Xavarian*.

The first to greet me was Kantos Kan himself. My old friend had risen to the highest rank in the Helium navy, yet to me he remained the brave comrade who had shared the privations of a Warhoon dungeon and the horrors of the Great Games. Once an unknown wanderer and a simple *padwar*, he now commanded Helium's aerial terrors, while I was a Prince of the House of Tardos Mors, Jeddak of Helium.

He did not ask where I had been. Like Hor Vastus, he dreaded the truth and refused to wrest a confession from me. He knew it would come eventually, but for now, he was content simply to know I was home. He greeted Carthoris and Tars Tarkas with keen delight, though he asked neither of them where they had been. He could scarcely keep his hands off the boy.

"You do not know, John Carter," he said to me, "how we of Helium love this son of yours. It is as though all the great love we bore his noble father and his poor mother had been centered in him. When it became known that he was lost, ten million people wept."

"What mean you, Kantos Kan," I whispered, "by 'his poor mother'?" The words carried a sinister meaning I could not fathom.

He drew me aside.

"For a year," he explained, "since Carthoris disappeared, Dejah Thoris has grieved and mourned for her lost boy. The blow of years ago, when you did not return from the atmosphere plant, was lessened only by the duties of motherhood, for your son broke his white shell that very night."

"That she suffered terribly then, all Helium knew, for did not all Helium suffer with her the loss of her lord! But with the boy gone, there was nothing left. After expedition upon expedition returned with the same hopeless tale of no clue as to his whereabouts, our beloved Princess drooped lower and lower, until all who saw her felt it was but a matter of days ere she joined her loved ones within the precincts of the Valley Dor."

"As a last resort, Mors Kajak, her father, and Tardos Mors, her grandfather, took command of two mighty expeditions and sailed away a month ago to explore every inch of the northern hemisphere. For two weeks, no word came back. Rumors were rife that they had met a terrible disaster and perished."

"About this time, Zat Arras renewed his importunities for her hand in marriage. He has been after her since you disappeared. She hated him and feared him, but with both her father and grandfather gone, Zat Arras was very powerful, for he remains Jed of Zodanga, to which position Tardos Mors appointed him after you refused the honor."

"He had a secret audience with her six days ago. What transpired none knows, but the next day Dejah Thoris had disappeared, along with a dozen of her household guard and body servants, including Sola, the green woman—Tars Tarkas' daughter, you recall. No word left them of their intentions, but it is always thus with those who go upon the voluntary pilgrimage from which none returns. We cannot think aught but that Dejah Thoris has sought the icy bosom of the Iss, and that her devoted servants chose to accompany her."

"Zat Arras was at Helium when she disappeared. He commands this fleet which has been searching for her. No trace have we found, and I fear it is a futile quest."

While we talked, Hor Vastus' fliers returned to the *Xavarian*, bringing no news of Thuvia. I was depressed by the news of Dejah Thoris's disappearance, and now the burden of apprehension for this girl, whom I believed to be the daughter of a proud Barsoomian house, weighed heavily. I intended to order a further search, but a flier from the flagship arrived with an officer bearing a message from Zat Arras to Kantos Kan.

My friend read the dispatch and turned to me.

"Zat Arras commands me to bring our 'prisoners' before him. There is naught else to do. He is supreme in Helium, yet it would be far more in keeping with chivalry and good taste were he to come hither and greet the savior of Barsoom with the honors due him."

"You know full well, my friend," I said, smiling grimly, "that Zat Arras has good cause to hate me. Nothing would please him better than to humiliate me and then kill me. Now that he has such an excellent excuse, let us go and see if he has the courage to take advantage of it."

Summoning Carthoris, Tars Tarkas, and Xodar, we entered the small flier with Kantos Kan and Zat Arras's officer, and moments later we stepped onto the deck of Zat Arras's flagship.

As we approached the Jed of Zodanga, no sign of greeting or recognition crossed his face; not even to Carthoris did he offer a friendly word. His attitude was cold, haughty, and uncompromising.

"Kaor, Zat Arras," I said in greeting, but he did not respond.

"Why were these prisoners not disarmed?" he asked Kantos Kan.

"They are not prisoners, Zat Arras," the officer replied.

"Two of them are of Helium's noblest family. Tars Tarkas, Jeddak of Thark, is Tardos Mors' best beloved ally. The other is a friend and companion of the Prince of Helium—that is enough for me to know."

"It is not enough for me, however," Zat Arras retorted. "More must I hear from those who have taken the pilgrimage than their names. Where have you been, John Carter?"

"I have just come from the Valley Dor and the Land of the First Born, Zat Arras," I replied.

"Ah!" he exclaimed in evident pleasure. "You do not deny it, then? You have returned from the bosom of the Iss?"

"I have come back from a land of false hope, from a valley of torture and death; with my companions I have escaped from the hideous clutches of lying fiends. I have come back to the Barsoom that I saved from a painless death to again save her, but this time from death in its most frightful form."

"Cease, blasphemer!" cried Zat Arras. "Hope not to save thy cowardly carcass by inventing horrid lies to—" But he got no further. One does not call John Carter "coward" and "liar" lightly, and Zat Arras should have known it. Before a hand could be raised to stop me, I was at his side, one hand grasping his throat.

"Come I from heaven or hell, Zat Arras, you will find me still the same John Carter that I have always been; nor did ever man call me such names and live—without apologizing." With that, I commenced to bend him back across my knee, tightening my grip on his throat.

"Seize him!" cried Zat Arras, and a dozen officers sprang forward to assist him.

Kantos Kan came close and whispered urgently.

"Desist, I beg of you. It will but involve us all, for I cannot see these men lay hands upon you without aiding you. My officers and men will join me, and we shall have a mutiny then that may lead to revolution. For the sake of Tardos Mors and Helium, desist."

At his words, I released Zat Arras and, turning my back upon him, walked toward the ship's rail.

"Come, Kantos Kan," I said, "the Prince of Helium would return to the *Xavarian*."

None interfered. Zat Arras stood white and trembling amidst his officers. Some looked upon him with scorn and drew toward me, while one, a man long in the service and confidence of Tardos Mors, spoke to me in a low tone as I passed.

"You may count my metal among your fighting-men, John Carter," he said.

I thanked him and passed on. In silence we embarked, and shortly after stepped once more upon the deck of the *Xavarian*. Fifteen minutes later, we received orders from the flagship to proceed toward Helium.

Our journey thither was uneventful. Carthoris and I were wrapped in the gloomiest of thoughts. Kantos Kan was sombre, contemplating the further calamity that might fall upon Helium should Zat Arras attempt to follow the age-old precedent that allotted a terrible death to fugitives from the Valley Dor. Tars Tarkas grieved for the loss of his daughter. Xodar alone was care-free—a fugitive and outlaw, he could be no worse off in Helium than elsewhere.

"Let us hope that we may at least go out with good red blood upon our blades," he said. It was a simple wish, and one most likely to be gratified.

Among the officers of the *Xavarian*, I discerned a division into factions before we even reached Helium. Some gathered about Carthoris and myself whenever the opportunity presented, while an equal number held aloof. They offered us only the most courteous treatment but were evidently bound by their superstitious belief in the doctrines of Dor, Iss, and Korus. I could not blame them; I knew how strong a hold a creed, however ridiculous, may gain upon an otherwise intelligent people.

By returning from Dor, we had committed a sacrilege; by recounting our adventures and stating the facts as they existed, we had outraged the religion of their fathers. We were blasphemers—lying heretics. Even those who still clung to us from personal love and loyalty did so in the face of the fact that at heart they questioned our veracity.
