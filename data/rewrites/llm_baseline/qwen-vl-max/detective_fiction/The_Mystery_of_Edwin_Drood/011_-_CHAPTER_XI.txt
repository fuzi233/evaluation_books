Behind the oldest part of Holborn, where ancient gabled houses still stand along the street—like silent witnesses to a past long gone—is a quiet corner known as Staple Inn. It’s a place that feels like stepping out of the city’s noise: a sudden hush, as if the world has been muffled by cotton in the ears and velvet on the soles. Here, a few sparrows hop among smoky trees, chirping as though playing at being country birds. A small patch of soil and gravel gives them just enough space to pretend they’re free. And it’s not only a retreat from the bustle—it’s also a legal nook, with a little hall and a lantern atop its roof, serving some purpose unknown to this story.

In the days when Cloisterham feared the railroad as a threat to its peace—the kind of institution that is always both praised and cursed, depending on what’s happening elsewhere—Staple Inn stood unshaded by modern buildings. The sun lit its walls, and the wind blew through freely. But on this December afternoon, around six o’clock, fog had swallowed everything. Candles flickered weakly behind windows, casting blurred light into the chambers of a corner house in the inner quadrangle. Above its door, in black and white letters, was the enigmatic inscription:

Inside, Mr. Grewgious sat writing by his fire, never giving the sign much thought—though sometimes he wondered if it meant “Perhaps John Thomas” or “Perhaps Joe Tyler.”

Who could tell, looking at Mr. Grewgious, whether he’d ever known ambition—or disappointment? He’d trained for the Bar, aiming to become a conveyancer, to draft deeds, to “convey the wise it call,” as Pistol once said. But Conveyancing had never come to him. She was wooed, but never won. They parted ways without ever truly meeting.

Yet fate sent something else his way—an arbitration case, blown toward him by chance. He handled it with such diligence that he earned a reputation for fairness. Soon after, a lucrative receivership landed in his lap, likely carried by a more predictable breeze. So, by accident, he found his place. Now, Receiver and Agent for two wealthy estates, he handed off their legal matters to a firm below and settled into a life of routine under the dry vine and fig tree planted by P. J. T. in 1747.

His room was filled with ledgers, files, and strongboxes—all arranged with military precision. Nothing was out of place. To leave even one figure incomplete would have struck him dead. Duty was his blood. Not the most exciting kind, perhaps, but the most reliable.

There was no luxury here. Only warmth, dryness, and a faded fireside. His private life centered on the hearth, an easy chair, and a round table brought out after hours from a corner where it stood upright like a polished shield. Behind it, a closet held something good to drink. A clerk worked in the outer room; Mr. Grewgious slept across the stairway and kept a cellar beneath it. Three hundred days a year, he walked to Furnival’s Inn for dinner, then returned to enjoy the simplicity until business resumed.

That evening, as Mr. Grewgious wrote, so did his clerk write by his own fire. A pale, puffy-faced man of thirty, with dark hair and eyes that lacked any spark, Bazzard seemed oddly bound to his master. Like a spirit summoned by magic but unable to be dismissed, he clung to his stool, despite clearly being a burden. Gloomy, tangled-haired, and shadowed by suspicion, he was treated by Mr. Grewgious with inexplicable kindness.

“Now, Bazzard,” said Mr. Grewgious, lifting his head from papers, “what’s the news besides fog?”

“Mr. Drood,” replied Bazzard.

“What about him?”

“He’s called.”

“You might’ve shown him in.”

“I am doing it,” said Bazzard.

The visitor entered.

“Dear me!” said Mr. Grewgious, glancing at the candles. “I thought you’d just left your name and gone. How do you do, Mr. Edwin? Dear me—you’re choking!”

“It’s the fog,” said Edwin. “It stings my eyes like Cayenne pepper.”

“Is it really that bad? Undo your coat. Lucky I’ve got a good fire—but Bazzard’s taken care of me.”

“No, I haven’t,” said Bazzard at the door.

“Ah! Then I must have taken care of myself without realizing it,” said Mr. Grewgious. “Please sit in my chair. No—don’t. Coming from such a fog, you should have it.”

Edwin took the chair. The fog he carried in, and the layers he shed, vanished quickly into the flames.

“I look,” said Edwin, smiling, “as if I’m staying.”

“By the way,” said Mr. Grewgious, interrupting, “do stay. The fog may lift soon. We can get dinner from across Holborn. Better to eat here than outside—please stay and dine.”

“You’re very kind,” said Edwin, scanning the room as if imagining a cozy, impromptu gathering.

“Not at all,” said Mr. Grewgious. “You’re the kind one, joining a bachelor in chambers for potluck. And I’ll ask Bazzard—he might not otherwise agree.—Bazzard!”

Bazzard reappeared.

“Dine with us.”

“If I’m ordered, sir, I will,” he replied grimly.

“Save the man!” said Mr. Grewgious. “You’re not ordered—you’re invited.”

“Thank you, sir,” said Bazzard. “Then I don’t mind.”

“That’s settled. And perhaps you could go to the hotel in Furnival’s and ask them to send over supplies? Let’s have the hottest soup possible, the best made dish, a joint—say, a haunch of mutton—and a goose or turkey if available. In short, whatever they have.”

Mr. Grewgious gave these instructions with the calm precision of someone reciting a list. Bazzard withdrew, pulling out the round table before leaving.

“I was hesitant,” said Mr. Grewgious quietly after the clerk left, “about having him handle the food orders. Because he might not like it.”

“He seems to have his own way, sir,” remarked Edwin.

“His own way?” Mr. Grewgious laughed faintly. “No, poor fellow. If he had his own way, he wouldn’t be here.”

Edwin wondered where he *would* be. But he didn’t say it—Mr. Grewgious had already turned to face the fire, leaning against the mantel, ready to talk.

“I take it,” said Mr. Grewgious, “without claiming prophecy, that you came to tell me you’re going down to Cloisterham—where I can assure you, you’re expected—and to offer any message to my ward, perhaps to give me a hint about any proceedings? Eh, Mr. Edwin?”

“I called, sir, before leaving, as a gesture of respect.”

“Respect!” said Mr. Grewgious. “Ah! Not impatience?”

“Impatience, sir?”

Mr. Grewgious had meant to sound playful—but failed utterly. He leaned too close to the fire, as if trying to burn the humor into himself. But seeing Edwin’s calm expression, he flinched and rubbed his arms.

“I’ve recently been down there,” said Mr. Grewgious, straightening his coat. “That’s why I said you’re expected.”

“Indeed, sir. Yes—I knew Rosa was waiting for me.”

“Do you keep a cat there?” asked Mr. Grewgious.

Edwin flushed slightly. “I call Rosa ‘Pussy.’”

“Oh, really,” said Mr. Grewgious, smoothing his hair. “That’s quite charming.”

Edwin glanced at him, unsure if he was offended. But Mr. Grewgious was unreadable—as steady as a clock face.

“A pet name, sir,” Edwin repeated.

“Umps,” said Mr. Grewgious, nodding. But it was neither full agreement nor refusal—just a strange blend of both. Edwin felt unsettled.

“Did Rosa—” he began.

“PRosa?” echoed Mr. Grewgious.

“I was going to say Pussy, but changed my mind—did she mention the Landlesses?”

“No,” said Mr. Grewgious. “What are the Landlesses? An estate? A villa? A farm?”

“A brother and sister. The sister is at the Nuns’ House and has become a close friend of Rosa’s.”

“She’s strikingly handsome, sir,” said Edwin. “I thought she might have been described to you—or introduced.”

“Neither,” said Mr. Grewgious. “But here comes Bazzard.”

Bazzard returned, followed by two waiters—one slow and rigid, the other swift and frantic. Together, they brought in a fresh wave of fog, which roared through the fire.

The quick waiter carried everything on his shoulders and laid the table with astonishing speed. The slow one stood idle and criticized every move. The fast waiter polished glasses while the slow one inspected them. Then he darted across Holborn for soup, returned, fetched the main course, then the joint and poultry—making multiple trips, each time bringing back fog and breathlessness. The slow waiter scolded him constantly. At the end, he gathered the cloth dramatically, glared at the fast waiter, and pushed him out—sending a clear message: “This reward is mine. That servant claims nothing.”

It was like a miniature satire of bureaucracy—a perfect parody of government inefficiency. One might hang it in the National Gallery.

The fog had sparked this feast, and now it served as seasoning. Hearing clerks sneeze and stamp outside was more thrilling than any gourmet spice. Ordering the exhausted waiter to shut the door before opening it added a deeper flavor than any wine. And note: the young man’s leg, touching the door, showed exquisite timing—always ahead of his body, lingering afterward, like Macbeth’s limb reluctant to leave the stage.

Mr. Grewgious descended to the cellar and returned with bottles of ruby, straw-colored, and golden wines—aged in sunlit lands untouched by fog. They bubbled eagerly, pushing at corks as if eager to escape. If P. J. T. drank such wine in 1747, then surely he was Pretty Jolly Too.

Externally, Mr. Grewgious remained unchanged. The wine might as well have been poured over him—he showed no signs of softening. Yet his eyes watched Edwin closely. When dinner ended and Mr. Grewgious motioned Edwin back to the chair, and Edwin sank into it with minimal protest, Mr. Grewgious smoothed his face and looked at him between his fingers.

“Bazzard!” he called suddenly.

“I follow you, sir,” said Bazzard, who had eaten silently.

“I drink to you, Bazzard. Mr. Edwin, success to Mr. Bazzard!”

“Success to Mr. Bazzard!” echoed Edwin, feigning enthusiasm—though inwardly wondering: *Success in what?*

“And May,” continued Mr. Grewgious, “I cannot be specific—May—my words are so limited that I know I’ll fail—May—this should be poetic, but I lack imagination—May—let me say it plainly: May the thorn of anxiety be removed—May it finally come out!”

Mr. Bazzard frowned slightly, ran a hand through his hair, then into his waistcoat, then his pockets—as if searching for the thorn. Edwin watched, half-expecting to see it appear. But it never did. Bazzard simply said: “I follow you, sir. Thank you.”

“I’m going to toast my ward,” whispered Mr. Grewgious, jingling his glass and leaning close to Edwin. “But I put Bazzard first. He mightn’t like it otherwise.”

He winked—though it was more of a slow blink. Edwin blinked back, clueless.

“And now,” said Mr. Grewgious, “a bumper to Miss Rosa. Bazzard, the fair and fascinating Miss Rosa!”

“I follow you, sir,” said Bazzard, “and I pledge you!”

“So do I!” said Edwin.

“Lord bless me,” exclaimed Mr. Grewgious, breaking the silence. “I’m particularly angular, yet I think—though I have no fancy—I could draw a picture of a true lover’s state of mind tonight.”

“Let us hear it, sir,” said Bazzard.

“Mr. Edwin will correct it where wrong,” added Mr. Grewgious, “and add touches from life. I dare say it’s flawed. I was born a chip—no soft feelings, no soft experiences. Still, I believe the true lover’s mind is fully absorbed by the beloved. Her name is sacred—precious, emotional, never spoken lightly. Any special name he uses for her is kept private. It’s a privilege to whisper it alone with her. To use it elsewhere would be cold, careless, almost dishonest.”

He sat bolt upright, hands on knees, speaking as if reciting a catechism. No emotion stirred—except a faint tingling at the tip of his nose.

“My picture continues,” he said, “the true lover is always eager to be near her. He cares little for others. He seeks her presence—constantly. If I said he seeks her like a bird its nest, I’d make a fool of myself—poetry is beyond me. And I know nothing of birds except those of Staple Inn, who build nests in gutters and chimneys, not by nature’s design. So I skip the bird metaphor. But I do believe the lover lives a doubled and halved life—entwined with her, yet split apart. If I fail to express it, it’s because I lack words—or meaning. Which, I believe, isn’t the case.”

Edwin grew red, then pale. He stared at the fire, biting his lip.

“The speculations of an angular man,” continued Mr. Grewgious, “may be wrong on such a round subject. But I imagine—subject to correction—that a true lover knows no coolness, no indifference, no half-heartedness. Pray, am I near the mark?”

He stopped abruptly, as if mid-speech.

“I should say, sir,” stammered Edwin, “since you ask me—”

“Yes,” said Mr. Grewgious, “I ask you, as an authority.”

“I should say,” continued Edwin, embarrassed, “that your picture is generally accurate. But perhaps you’re a bit harsh on the lover.”

“Likely so,” agreed Mr. Grewgious. “I am hard by nature.”

“He may not show all he feels,” said Edwin, “or he may not—”

He paused, struggling to finish. Mr. Grewgious cut in unexpectedly:

“No, to be sure—he *may* not!”

Silence fell. Bazzard had drifted into sleep.

“The responsibility is great,” said Mr. Grewgious eventually, eyes fixed on the fire.

Edwin nodded, eyes on the flames.

“And let him be sure,” said Mr. Grewgious, “he trifles with no one—neither himself nor another.”

Edwin bit his lip again, staring into the coals.

“He must not treat a treasure as a toy. Woe betide him if he does. Let him remember that.”

He spoke in short bursts, like a child reading scripture. Yet there was a dreamy quality—unusual for such a literal man—as he shook his finger at the embers, then fell silent.

But not for long. Suddenly, he rapped his knees like a statue awakening.

“We must finish this bottle, Mr. Edwin. Let me help you. I’ll help Bazzard too—though he’s asleep. He mightn’t like it otherwise.”

He helped them both, drained his glass, and placed it upside down on the table—like a trap for a bluebottle.

“And now,” he said, wiping his mouth, “to business. You received from me a certified copy of Miss Rosa’s father’s will. You knew its contents, but I sent it as a matter of form. I intended to send it to Mr. Jasper, but Rosa preferred it go directly to you. Did you receive it?”

“Quite safely, sir.”

“You should have acknowledged it. Business is business. But you didn’t.”

“I meant to, sir, when I arrived.”

“Well,” said Mr. Grewgious, “we’ll leave it at that.”
