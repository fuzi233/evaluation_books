On Saturday morning, August 25, 1756, Mr. Warrington wrote from Tunbridge Wells:

“My dear Lord March,

I am pleased to inform you that I have settled all three of our bets. I arrived at Bromley just two minutes before the hour, and my new horses performed admirably. I drove them myself, with the postilion beside me to guide the way, and my black servant inside with Mrs. Betty. I trust they found the journey agreeable. We were not detained on Blackheath, though two men on horseback approached us—yet, upon seeing our faces, they rode off without further trouble. We reached Tunbridge Wells at eleven forty-five, where I completed my business. This settles the score after yesterday’s game of piquet, and I shall be delighted to return your favor soon.

Yours most obliged and faithful servant,

Now, perhaps, the reader can understand how Lady Maria Esmond managed to surprise her dear aunt in bed that very morning—and slip quietly from her house of confinement. She had sent Mrs. Betty to London, expecting no return so soon. As night fell, she and the chaplain played cards after a modest supper provided by the bailiff’s wife. Suddenly, the sound of rolling wheels approached—their carriage was coming!

Lady Maria laid down her cards, her heart pounding. The wheels whirred, the coach stopped at the door. There was a brief exchange at the gate, then Mrs. Betty appeared, her face glowing with joy, though her eyes glistened with tears. And who stepped forward next? Can any reader guess?

The chaplain slammed his cards down with a joyful cry. Lady Maria, turning as pale as death, rose unsteadily, took a few steps forward, and burst into a sobbing cry, throwing herself into her cousin’s arms. How many kisses did he give her? A thousand? A hundred more? Let it pass. He had come to save her. She knew he would. He was her champion, her deliverer from captivity and disgrace. She wept freely upon his shoulder, and as she leaned against him, overwhelmed with emotion, one might say she looked more radiant than at any other moment in this tale. She did not faint this time. Instead, she walked home, leaning gently on his arm, perhaps shedding a few more tears later that night—but Madame Bernstein slept through it all, untouched by sorrow.

“You are both free to go,” Harry said, the moment they were together. “Betty, fetch my lady’s hat and cloak. Chaplain, let us smoke a pipe at my lodgings—it will refresh me after my ride.”

The chaplain, deeply moved, could scarcely contain himself. He seized Harry’s hand, kissed it, and wept openly, praying God to bless his noble, generous young patron. Harry felt a warmth spread through him. There is something noble in helping those in need, in turning grief into joy. Proud and uplifted, he marched beside the woman he had rescued, his hat tilted jauntily. It seemed as though happiness itself danced before him—smiling, singing, scattering flowers and blowing horns of celebration. “Here comes the conqueror!” the world seemed to shout. “Make way for the hero!” They led him triumphantly into the king’s house and seated him on cushions of comfort, as if he had won a battle.

Yet what had he truly done? Only kindness. With a simple gesture, he’d opened the gates of prison and silenced the tyrant who held Lady Maria captive. Let him boast a little tonight—let him swagger with the chaplain, speak of London, of Lord March, of White’s and Almack’s, with the air of a fashionable dandy. We shall not hold it against him.

Sampson, too, was utterly overcome. His emotions ran deep and fast. He was easily stirred—by pain, by joy, by laughter or sorrow. His years as a preacher had sharpened his sensibilities until he lived each feeling fully, as if acting it out. He wept real tears now, not because he needed to, but because he could. He loved sincerely while near you, mourned genuinely beside widows, and laughed uproariously with friends at the tavern. He gave money freely but never repaid debts. That night, he was in a state of rapture, overflowing with gratitude toward Harry Warrings. In all of London, there may have been no more dangerous companion than a man so emotionally charged.

He toasted Harry again and again, praising him as the noblest soul alive. And Harry, the complacent young man, accepted these words as well-earned.

“The younger branch of our family,” Harry declared, with grandeur, “has treated you poorly. But by Jove, Sampson, I’ll stand by you!”

At moments of Burgundian excess, Harry often spoke with great passion about the glory of his lineage.

“I’m glad I could help you,” he said. “Count on me whenever you need me. Did you mention having a sister at boarding school? You’ll need funds. Here—this note may cover her tuition.”

He handed over a banknote. Sampson was struck silent, then burst into tears.

“Mr. Warrington,” he whispered, pushing the note away, “I don’t deserve this kindness—by George, I don’t!” He swore it with fervor.

“Psha!” Harry replied. “I’ve plenty more. That cursed pocket-book I lost last week had no money in it.”

“No, sir,” Sampson murmured, head bowed. “There was no money.”

“Hallo!” Harry exclaimed. “How do you know?”

“I know because I’m a villain,” Sampson confessed. “I found the book that night, when you were drunk at Barbeau’s.”

“And read the letters?” Harry asked sharply, rising and flushing.

“They told me nothing I didn’t already know,” the chaplain replied. “You have spies around you—people you trust, yet suspect not. Young and naive as you are, you cannot keep secrets from such eyes.”

“Are those stories about Lady Fanny, and my cousin Will’s affairs, true?” Harry asked.

“Yes,” the chaplain sighed. “The house of Castlewood has seen better days since the elder branch left it.”

“Sir, you dare not speak ill of my Lady Maria!” Harry snapped.

“Oh, not for worlds!” Sampson replied, glancing at him with an odd expression. “I may think she’s too old for you, that it’s a pity you don’t marry someone your own age—though I admit she looks remarkably youthful, and possesses every virtue.”

“She is too old,” Harry declared, with dignity. “But she has my word—and you see how fond she is of me. Bring me the letters you found. Let me try to forgive you.”

“My benefactor,” Sampson pleaded, “let me try to forgive myself!” He hurried off to his room.

Soon he returned, pale and trembling.

“What happened?” Harry asked, imperious.

The chaplain extended a pocket-book. “With your name in it, sir.”

“My brother’s name,” Harry corrected. “George gave it to me.”

“I kept it locked in a chest this morning, before I was taken,” Sampson said. “Here is the book—but the letters are gone. My trunk and valise have been searched. I am a wretched, guilty man, unable to restore what I owe you.”

He knelt almost, hands clasped, a picture of despair. Who had entered their rooms? The landlady swore no one had come in. Harry’s chamber showed no signs of disturbance. Only Sampson’s papers were missing.

Whose interest was it to take them? Lady Maria’s? Impossible—she had been imprisoned during the theft. She was innocent.

Then why had she been arrested? Sampson knew nothing yet of the letter she had lost. Poor Maria had not shared her secret with him.

As for the stolen papers—Harry, swollen with pride over his winning bets, his rescue, and the sumptuous dinner of partridges and old Burgundy sent by Monsieur Barbeau, dismissed Sampson’s guilt. He accepted the chaplain’s tearful vows of loyalty and forgave him with a noble gesture.

When Sampson swore on his gods that he would die for Harry, the young man replied, “I believe you would, Sampson. I hope you would. Our family—Esmond—has always had faithful friends, and rewarded them. The wine’s yours, Chaplain. What toast do you call?”

“A blessing on the house of Esmond-Warrington!” the chaplain cried, tears in his eyes.

“We are the elder branch,” Harry said, voice noble but slightly blurred. “My grandfather was the Marquis of Esmond.”

“Here’s to you, Chaplain—and I forgive you—and God bless you—and if you’d been taken for three times as much, I’d have paid it. Why—what’s that I see through the shutters? By heavens, the sun’s risen again! No need for candles to go to bed, ha, ha!”

And with another blessing, he retired to sleep.

Around noon, Madame de Bernstein sent a servant to invite her nephew to join her for chocolate. Harry rose and walked to her lodgings.

She noticed a change in his appearance—his clothes sharper, his bearing more confident. He had visited tailors in London, introduced to tradesmen by Lord March.

“My dearest child,” she said, “thank you for your noble, generous care of poor Maria. That seizure in church—how it shocked me! Even worse—that I lost three hundred pounds to Lady Yarmouth Wednesday night. I am quite broke.”

“So Case went to London?” Harry asked.

“Of course. The Baroness de Bernstein cannot afford to be short of funds. Can you lend me some, child?”

“I can give you twenty-two pounds,” Harry said, blushing. “I have only forty-four left until my Virginian remittances arrive. I’ve bought horses, clothes—I’ve been extravagant, aunt.”

“And rescued your poor relations in distress—you prodigal good boy,” she said. “No, child, I don’t want your money. I’ll give you some. Here—a note for fifty pounds, vaurien! Go spend it, be merry! Your mother may repay me, though she does not love me.”

She smiled warmly, offering her hand. Harry kissed it.

“Your mother did not love me,” she continued, “but your mother’s father once did. Remember, sir—come to me when you need me.”

When she chose to charm, Beatrix Bernstein was unmatched in grace and wit.

“I can’t help loving you, child,” she said. “Yet I am so angry with you, I scarce have patience. You’ve actually engaged yourself to poor Maria—she’s as old as your mother! What will Madam Esmond say? She may live three hundred years, and you’ll have nothing to support yourselves.”

“I have ten thousand pounds from my father,” Harry said. “It will help.”

“The interest won’t even cover card games,” she retorted.

“We must give up cards,” Harry insisted. “Maria can’t control herself. She’ll pawn your coat to play. The passion runs in my brother’s blood—in mine too, I confess. I warned you. I begged you not to play with them. And now—a boy of twenty marrying a woman of forty-two—writing letters on his knees, signed with his heart’s blood (which he spells like hartshorn), swearing he’ll marry no one but his beloved cousin, Lady Maria Esmonds. Oh, it’s cruel—cruel!”

“Great heavens, madam!” Harry exclaimed, blushing fiercely. “Who showed you my letter?”

“An accident,” she replied. “She fainted when taken. Brett cut her laces. When she was carried away, we found a sachet on the floor. I opened it—without knowing what was inside. And there was your precious letter. And here—” she held up the case.

A sharp pang pierced Harry’s heart. *Why didn’t she destroy it?*

“I—I’ll give it back to Maria,” he said, reaching for the locket.

“My dear,” she said softly, “I burned the foolish letter. If you choose to betray me, I must bear the consequences. If you write another, I cannot stop you. But if you do, Harry Esmond, I’d rather never see you again. Will you keep my secret? Will you believe an old woman who loves you and knows the world better than you do? I tell you—this promise will bring misery and ruin. What is a lad like you in the hands of a cunning woman who plays with you like a toy? She has trapped you. And I’ve cut the strings. Go back. Betray me if you must.”

“I’m not angry with you, aunt—I wish I were,” Harry said, voice thick with emotion. “I—I won’t repeat what you’ve told me.”

“Maria never will, child—mark my words!” the old lady cried. “She’ll never admit she lost that paper. She’ll lie and say she still has it.”

“But I know she—she loves me,” Harry stammered. “You should have seen her last night.”

“Must I tell more lies about my own flesh and blood?” the Baroness sobbed. “Child, you don’t know her past!”

“And I must not—and I will not!” Harry cried, rising. “Written or spoken—it doesn’t matter. My word is given. Men may break promises in England, but gentlemen of Virginia don’t. If she holds me to it, she shall have me. If we’re miserable—as I fear we will be—I’ll take a firelock and join the King of Prussia—or let a bullet end me.”

“I—have nothing more to say,” she whispered. “Will you ring the bell? I—I bid you good morning, Mr. Warrington.”

She dropped a stately curtsey, rose on her tortoiseshell cane, and turned toward the door. But as she took her first step, she clutched her chest, sank onto the sofa, and shed the first tears Beatrix Esmond had wept in many years.

Harry was deeply moved. He knelt beside her, took her cold hand, and kissed it. He spoke simply, honestly, of how deeply he had felt her love—and how completely he returned it.

“Aunt,” he said, “you don’t know how vile I feel. When you told me the letter was burned—oh, I was ashamed to realize how glad I was.”

He bowed his head over her hand. Hot tears fell upon it.

She had loved this boy. For half a century—perhaps never in her life—had she felt such tender, pure emotion. Her hard heart was broken, softened, undone. She placed both hands on his shoulders and lightly kissed his forehead.

“You will not tell her what I’ve done, child?” she asked.

He swore—never! Never!

And demure Mrs. Brett, entering at her mistress’s call, found the nephew and aunt locked in a quiet, emotional embrace.
