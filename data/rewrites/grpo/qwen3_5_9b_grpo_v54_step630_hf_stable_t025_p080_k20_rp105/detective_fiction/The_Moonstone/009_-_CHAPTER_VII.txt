Lost in a fog of confusion, I desperately needed a moment of solitude to regain my composure. My daughter, Penelope, however, intercepted me with the same relentless energy her late mother once displayed on the staircase. She demanded to know every detail of my recent conference with Mr. Franklin. Under the current circumstances, the only prudent course was to snuff out her curiosity immediately. I deflected her inquiry by claiming that Mr. Franklin and I had engaged in a lengthy debate regarding foreign politics until our tongues were tired, after which we succumbed to exhaustion and fell asleep in the scorching heat of the sun. This tactic, though effective against a persistent spouse or daughter, relies heavily on the natural inclination of women to forgive such evasions with a peck on the cheek at the next opportunity.

As the afternoon dragged on, my wife and Miss Rachel returned. Naturally, they were astounded to learn that Mr. Franklin Blake had arrived and departed on horseback within the span of a few hours. Equally predictable was their barrage of awkward questions; my fabricated tale of political discourse and solar slumber failed to satisfy them a second time. Deprived of further excuses, I attributed his arrival by the early train to one of Mr. Franklin's eccentric whims. When pressed whether his swift departure on horseback was another such whim, I admitted it was, slipping away from the interrogation with what I believed was considerable dexterity.

Once I had appeased the ladies, I returned to my own quarters, only to find Penelope waiting there. With her characteristic blend of affectionate reconciliation and insatiable curiosity, she pressed me for the truth regarding our second housemaid, Rosanna Spearman. Since our encounter at the Shivering Sand, Rosanna had returned to the house in a state of profound disarray. According to Penelope, the girl had shifted through every hue of the rainbow. She oscillated between unexplained mirth and inexplicable sorrow. In one breath, she interrogated me relentlessly about Mr. Franklin Blake; in the next, she flew into a rage at Penelope for suggesting that a stranger could hold any interest for her. She had been seen smiling broadly while scribbling Mr. Franklin's name inside her workbox, only to be overcome with tears while gazing at her deformed shoulder in the mirror. Could she and Mr. Franklin have known one another previously? I assured Penelope it was impossible, citing Mr. Franklin's genuine astonishment at her stare. Yet, Penelope's account of the girl's intense inquisitiveness seemed equally authentic. This circular dialogue grew tiresome until my daughter abruptly concluded it with a hypothesis so preposterous I deemed it the most monstrous notion I had ever encountered.

"Father!" Penelope declared with grave seriousness. "There is only one explanation. Rosanna has fallen in love with Mr. Franklin Blake at first sight!"

You may have heard of beautiful young ladies succumbing to instant passion and considered it natural. But imagine a housemaid emerging from a reformatory, possessing a plain countenance and a hunched shoulder, falling hopelessly in love at first sight with a gentleman visiting her mistress's home. Match that absurdity against any romance novel in Christendom if you dare. I laughed until tears streamed down my cheeks. Penelope, however, resented my merriment in a peculiar manner. "I never knew you cruel before, father," she said softly, before retreating to her room.

Her words struck me like a bucket of icy water. I felt a sudden, savage unease within myself the moment she spoke them, though I could not yet articulate why. Let us shift the subject, for I regret having dwelt on this matter prematurely, as its significance will become apparent shortly.

That evening, the dinner bell rang before Mr. Franklin returned from Frizinghall. I carried his hot water to his chamber, anticipating news of some calamity following his extraordinary delay. To my profound disappointment—and likely yours as well—nothing had occurred. He had not encountered the Indians either on his way to or from Frizinghall. He had safely deposited the Moonstone in the bank, describing it merely as a valuable of immense worth, and secured the receipt in his pocket. Descending the stairs, I felt a sense of anticlimax wash over us after the day's mounting excitement regarding the diamond.

The details of the meeting between Mr. Franklin, his aunt, and his cousin remain beyond my recollection. I would have preferred to wait at table that day, yet in my position within the household, doing so (save for major family festivals) would have compromised my dignity in the eyes of the servants—a lapse my lady already suspected I was prone to, without needing further provocation. The intelligence I gathered from the upper floors that evening came from Penelope and the footman. Penelope noted that she had never seen Miss Rachel so meticulous about her hair, nor so radiant and lovely as she appeared when descending to greet Mr. Franklin in the drawing-room. The footman reported that maintaining a respectful composure in the presence of his superiors while serving Mr. Franklin Blake at dinner represented the greatest challenge to his training in service. Later that night, we heard them singing and playing duets; Mr. Franklin played the pipe in high notes, Miss Rachel matched him with even higher tones, and my lady followed on the piano, weaving a melody over them that sounded wonderfully pleasant through the open terrace windows. Still later, I visited Mr. Franklin in the smoking-room with soda water and brandy, finding that Miss Rachel had completely displaced the diamond from his thoughts. "She is the most charming girl I have seen since returning to England!" was all I could draw from him when I attempted to steer the conversation toward more serious matters.

Approaching midnight, I began the nightly rounds to secure the house, accompanied as usual by Samuel, the footman. Once all doors were locked, save for the side entrance opening onto the terrace, I dismissed Samuel to bed and stepped outside for a breath of fresh air before retiring myself.

The night was still and stifling, with the moon hanging full and bright in the heavens. The silence was so profound that I could occasionally hear the faint, low crash of the sea as the ground-swell washed over the sandbank near the bay's mouth. The terrace side of the house lay in darkness, but the broad moonlight illuminated the gravel walk running along the adjacent side. Gazing upward at the sky, then turning my attention to the path, I perceived the shadow of a figure cast forward from behind the corner of the house.

Being old and shrewd, I refrained from calling out. Unfortunately, my age and weight betrayed me on the gravel. Before I could stealthily circle the corner as I had intended, I heard lighter footsteps—more than one pair, I thought—retreating in haste. By the time I reached the corner, the intruders, whoever they were, had fled into the shrubbery at the far end of the walk, disappearing among the dense trees and bushes. From that thicket, they could easily cross our fence into the road. Had I been forty years younger, I might have caught them before they escaped our grounds. As it was, I returned to summon a younger pair of legs. Samuel and I retrieved two guns and swept the perimeter of the house and the shrubbery. Satisfied that no one was lurking, we turned back. Passing over the walk where I had spotted the shadow, I noticed for the first time a small, bright object lying on the clean gravel beneath the moonlight. Picking it up, I discovered it to be a small bottle containing a thick, sweet-smelling liquid, black as ink.

I said nothing to Samuel. However, recalling Penelope's account of the jugglers and the pouring of the ink pool into the boy's palm, I instantly suspected that I had disturbed three Indians who were lurking about the house, bent in their heathen fashion on discovering the location of the Diamond that very night.
