Major Pendennis, having sauntered homeward with the steady courage of a man who has dined well and intends to make other people indigested, reached the George at last. The inn was settling into its nightly domestic hush: the tap-room’s last laugh dying like a cork popped and forgotten, the stairboards giving their accustomed old cough under a boot, the wind making a thin flute in the keyhole—country music, at least, and not the iron bawling of London streets.

Mr. Morgan, his valet—faithful as a ladle, and just as apt to dip into any pot—stood waiting at the door with that air of respectful omniscience which says, “I know more than you, sir, and I shall tell you as though you had guessed it yourself.”

As the Major was about to take his candle and retire, Morgan stopped him with a little cough that might have been learned in a drawing-room.

“I think, sir, if you would step into the coffee-room, there’s a young gentleman there as you would like to see.”

“What, is Mr. Arthur here?” cried the Major, with the sudden heat of a man who fears he shall find love served up again at midnight.

“No, sir—but his great friend, Mr. Foker, sir. Lady Hagnes Foker’s son, sir. He’s been asleep in the coffee-room since he took his dinner, and has just rung for his coffee, sir. And I think, p’raps, you might like to git into conversation with him,” Morgan added, opening the door as neatly as one uncovers a dish, confident the aroma will do the rest.

Major Pendennis entered; and there indeed sat Mr. Harry Foker, the only occupant of the apartment, placed at a table which looked as though a small siege had been raised and provision-trains captured. Empty decanters stood like slain glass soldiers; crumbs and nut-shells and peelings lay about like the relics of a dessert that had died honourably. The coffee—when it came—sent up its homely brown breath, that bitter, comforting steam which can make even a scandal smell respectable.

Foker was rubbing his eyes, his cheeks still wearing that faint, prosperous flush which wine paints upon youth as a cook paints glaze upon a ham. He had meant, it seemed, to go to the play; but a copious meal had conquered tragedy and comedy alike. He had thrown up his legs on the bench, and napped like a man practising for Parliament.

The Major was turning over, as carefully as a maître d’hôtel considers his sauces, the best mode of address; but Mr. Foker, who was always affable and ready to press his acquaintance as a waiter presses hot rolls, spared him the trouble.

“Like to look at the evening paper, sir?” said Foker, taking up the Globe and offering it with the easy sociability of a gentleman who would offer you his last macaroon if it gave him an excuse to talk.

“I am very much obliged to you,” said the Major, bowing with gratitude as though the paper were a coronet. “If I don’t mistake the family likeness, I have the pleasure of speaking to Mr. Henry Foker—Lady Agnes Foker’s son. I have the happiness to name her ladyship among my acquaintances—and you bear, sir, a Rosherville face.”

“Hullo! I beg your pardon,” said Mr. Foker, who had been going to say, “I took you for a commercial gent,” but swallowed that phrase as a man swallows a fish-bone when a lady looks at him. “To whom have I the pleasure of speaking?”

“To a relative of a friend and schoolfellow of yours—Arthur Pendennis, my nephew, who has often spoken to me of you in terms of great regard. I am Major Pendennis, of whom you may have heard him speak. May I take my soda-water at your table? I have had the pleasure of sitting at your grandfather’s.”

“Sir, you do me proud,” said Foker, moving with alacrity to make room, as though the table were an omnibus and the Major a fare. “And so you are Arthur Pendennis’s uncle, are you?”

“And guardian,” added the Major, dropping that word as one drops a bay-leaf into broth—quietly, but for effect.

“He’s as good a fellow as ever stepped, sir,” said Foker, with the generous warmth of youth, and perhaps with the additional warmth of claret still glowing in him.

“I am glad you think so.”

“And clever, too—I was always a stupid chap, I was—but you see, sir, I know ’em when they are clever, and like ’em of that sort.”

“You show your taste and your modesty, too,” said the Major, smiling as suavely as if he were tasting a well-made sauce. “I have heard Arthur repeatedly speak of you, and he said your talents were very good.”

“I’m not good at the books,” said Mr. Foker, wagging his head—“never could manage that—Pendennis could—he used to do half the chaps’ verses—and yet”—here the candid young gentleman broke out, like a bottle that has been shaken—“you are his guardian; and I hope you will pardon me for saying that I think he’s what we call flat.”

The Major’s heart gave a pleased little leap. A young man fresh from dinner is a natural historian of other people’s secrets.

“And how is Arthur a flat?” asked the Major, with a smile that invited confidence as a warm kitchen invites loitering.

“You know,” answered Foker, winking—he would have winked at the Duke of Wellington with just as little scruple, being in that frank, fearless, foggy state which two bottles of wine sometimes confer—“you know Arthur’s a flat,—about women I mean.”

“He is not the first of us, my dear Mr. Harry,” replied the Major, with a fine air of veteran indulgence. “I have heard something of this—but pray tell me more.”

“Why, sir, you see—it’s partly my fault. He went to the play one night—for you see I’m down here readin’ for my little go during the Long, only I come over from Baymouth pretty often in my drag—well, sir, we went to the play, and Pen was struck all of a heap with Miss Fotheringay—Costigan her real name is—an uncommon fine gal she is too; and the next morning I introduced him to the General, as we call her father—a regular old scamp and such a boy for the whisky-and-water!—and he’s gone on being intimate there. And he’s fallen in love with her—and I’m blessed if he hasn’t proposed to her,” cried Foker, slapping the table until the remnants of dessert jingled like little bells, as if the very raisins were laughing.

“What! you know it too?” asked the Major.

“Know it! don’t I? and many more too. We were talking about it at mess, yesterday, and chaffing Derby Oaks—until he was as mad as a hatter. Know Sir Derby Oaks? We dined together, and he went to the play: we were standing at the door smoking, I remember, when you passed in to dinner.”

“I remember Sir Thomas Oaks, his father, before he was a Baronet or a Knight; he lived in Cavendish-square, and was physician to Queen Charlotte,” said the Major, who could season any modern dish with an antique reference, like a cook dusting an old spice over new meat.

“The young one is making the money spin, I can tell you,” said Mr. Foker.

“And is Sir Derby Oaks,” asked the Major, very softly and very eagerly, “another soupirant?”

“Another what?”

“Another admirer of Miss Fotheringay?”

“Lord bless you! we call him Mondays, Wednesdays, and Fridays, and Pen Tuesdays, Thursdays, and Saturdays. But mind you, nothing wrong! No, no! Miss F. is a deal too wide-awake for that, Major Pendennis. She plays one off against the other. What you call two strings to her bow.”

“I think you seem tolerably wide-awake, too, Mr. Foker,” said the Major, laughing.

“Pretty well, thank you, sir—how are you?” replied Foker, imperturbable, as if their talk were merely a polite exchange about the weather and the roast. “I’m not clever, p’raps: but I am rather downy; and partial friends say I know what’s o’clock tolerably well. Can I tell you the time of day in any way?”

“Upon my word,” said the Major, delighted—delighted as a man is when he finds a sharp knife for a difficult carving—“I think you may be of very great service to me. You are a young man of the world, and with such one likes to deal. And as such I need not inform you that our family is by no means delighted at this absurd intrigue in which Arthur is engaged.”

“I should rather think not,” said Mr. Foker. “Connexion not eligible. Too much beer drunk on the premises. No Irish need apply. That I take to be your meaning.”

The Major declared it was exactly his meaning—though he did not quite know what half those phrases might be in the original tongue; but he caught their flavour, as one catches the flavour of a foreign dish by the look of its grease. He set himself, with patient curiosity, to question his new acquaintance regarding the Costigan household; and Mr. Foker, who had no more notion of locking his tongue than of corking his thoughts, gave him particulars enough to stock a pantry.

To do him justice, the young man spoke favourably of the Costigans’ moral character.

“You see,” said he, “I think the General is fond of the jovial bowl, and if I wanted to be very certain of my money, it isn’t in his pocket I’d invest it—but he has always kept a watchful eye on his daughter, and neither he nor she will stand anything but what’s honourable. Pen’s attentions to her are talked about in the whole Company, and I hear all about them from a young lady who used to be very intimate with her, and with whose family I sometimes take tea in a friendly way. Miss Rouncy says, Sir Derby Oaks has been hanging about Miss Fotheringay ever since his regiment has been down here; but Pen has come in and cut him out lately, which has made the Baronet so mad, that he has been very near on the point of proposing too. Wish he would; and you’d see which of the two Miss Fotheringay would jump at.”

“I thought as much,” said the Major, tasting this intelligence as if it were a particularly promising relish. “You give me a great deal of pleasure, Mr. Foker. I wish I could have seen you before.”

“Didn’t like to put in my oar,” replied the other. “Don’t speak till I’m asked, when, if there’s no objections, I speak pretty freely. Heard your man had been hankering about my servant—didn’t know myself what was going on until Miss Fotheringay and Miss Rouncy had the row about the ostrich feathers, when Miss R. told me everything.”

“Miss Rouncy, I gather, was the confidante of the other.”

“Confidant? I believe you. Why, she’s twice as clever a girl as Fotheringay, and literary and that, while Miss Foth can’t do much more than read.”

“She can write,” said the Major, remembering, with a little internal rattle like a spoon tapping a glass, the letter he had surprised in his nephew’s breast-pocket.

Foker gave a sardonic “He, he!”

“Rouncy writes her letters,” said he; “every one of ’em; and since they’ve quarrelled, she don’t know how the deuce to get on. Miss Rouncy is an uncommon pretty hand, whereas the old one makes dreadful work of the writing and spelling when Bows ain’t by. Rouncy’s been settin’ her copies lately—she writes a beautiful hand, Rouncy does.”

“I suppose you know it pretty well,” said the Major archly.

Mr. Foker winked again, as if winks were the punctuation of his conversation.

“I would give a great deal to have a specimen of her hand-writing,” continued Major Pendennis. “I dare say you could give me one.”

“No, no, that would be too bad,” replied Foker. “Perhaps I oughtn’t to have said as much as I have. Miss F.’s writin’ ain’t so very bad, I dare say; only she got Miss R. to write the first letter, and has gone on ever since. But you mark my word, that till they are friends again the letters will stop.”

“I hope they will never be reconciled,” said the Major, with the sincerity of a man who hopes the cook will never make up with the butcher. “And I can’t tell you how delighted I am to have had the good fortune of making your acquaintance. You must feel, my dear sir, as a man of the world, how fatal to my nephew’s prospects in life is this step which he contemplates, and how eager we all must be to free him from this absurd engagement.”

“He has come out uncommon strong,” said Mr. Foker. “I have seen his verses; Rouncy copied ’em. And I said to myself when I saw ’em, ‘Catch me writin’ verses to a woman,—that’s all.’”

“He has made a fool of himself, as many a good fellow has before him,” said the Major, setting his glass down as carefully as if it were a piece of evidence. “How can we make him see his folly, and cure it? I am sure you will give us what aid you can in extricating a generous young man from such a pair of schemers as this father and daughter seem to be. Love on the lady’s side is out of the question.”

“Love, indeed!” cried Foker. “If Pen hadn’t two thousand a year when he came of age——”

“If Pen hadn’t what?” shouted the Major, starting as if a hot drop of coffee had splashed him.

“Two thousand a year: hasn’t he got two thousand a year?—the General says he has.”

“My dear friend,” cried the Major—shrieked, indeed, with an eagerness which he commonly kept corked down—“thank you! thank you! I begin to see now.—Two thousand a year! Why, his mother has but five hundred a year in the world.—She is likely to live to eighty, and Arthur has not a shilling but what she can allow him.”

“What! he ain’t rich then?” asked Foker, his candour sobering a little, as men sober when the joint they thought was beef proves to be mutton.

“Upon my honour he has no more than what I say.”

“And you ain’t going to leave him anything?”

The Major, who had sunk every shilling he could scrape into an annuity, and was going to leave his nephew nothing but his example and perhaps his shirts, did not choose to put that dish upon the table.

“How much do you think a Major on half-pay can save?” he asked, with a sigh that sounded like a purse-string drawn tight.

“If these people have been looking at him as a fortune,” he continued, “they are utterly mistaken—and—and you have made me the happiest man in the world.”

“Sir to you,” said Mr. Foker, politely, rising; and when they parted for the night they shook hands with the greatest cordiality. The younger gentleman promised not to leave Chatteris without a further conversation in the morning.

So the Major went upstairs to his room with his mind bustling like a kitchen at noon; and Mr. Foker stood and smoked his cigar against the door-pillars of the George, sending out little clouds that melted into the night air as softly as secrets into gossip.

And Pen, very likely, ten miles off, lay in bed kissing a letter from his Emily—pressing paper to his lips as devoutly as a hungry man presses his face to the steam of a newly lifted pie.

In the morning, before Mr. Foker drove off in his drag, the insinuating Major had actually got a letter of Miss Rouncy’s in his own pocket-book. Let it be a lesson to women how they write; for ink, though it seems as innocent as gravy, will leave stains that no hot water of denial can wash out.

With this prize secured, Major Pendennis, in very high spirits, went to call upon Doctor Portman at the Deanery, and related his discoveries with the relish of a man carving up a scandal before a friend who likes the breast. In the Dean’s oak breakfast-parlour they sat in confidential talk, with the solid old wood about them smelling faintly of age, toast, and respectability; and they could look across the lawn and see Captain Costigan’s window, at which poor Pen had been only too visible some three weeks since—like an imprudent moth against a lighted pane.

The Doctor was vehement against Mrs. Creed, the landlady, for her duplicity in concealing Sir Derby Oaks’s constant visits to her lodgers, and threatened to excommunicate her out of the Cathedral, as though ecclesiastical thunder could frighten a woman who made money by shutting doors and opening mouths. But the wary Major thought all things were for the best; and having taken counsel with himself overnight, felt himself strong enough to face Captain Costigan.

“I’m going to fight the dragon,” he said, laughing, to Doctor Portman.

“And I shrive you, sir, and bid good fortune go with you,” answered the Doctor.

Perhaps he and Mrs. Portman and Miss Myra, as they sat with their friend the Dean’s lady in her drawing-room, looked up more than once at the enemy’s window to see if any signs of combat were visible—some flutter of curtain, some retreating shadow, some domestic omen. The house itself had its own soundscape: a distant clink of a spoon, the rustle of a gown, the mild ticking of a clock that seemed to say, in the language of old houses, that time remembers even when people forget.

The Major walked round, found Mrs. Creed’s little door as directed, passed it, and began to ascend to Captain Costigan’s apartment. Before he reached the landing, he heard stamping of feet and a great shouting of “Ha, ha!” within—noise of a very different kind from the Deanery’s measured hush; more like the brutal cheer of a fairground than the clean ring of a chapel bell.

“It’s Sir Derby Oaks taking his fencing lesson,” said the child who piloted Major Pendennis. “He takes it Mondays, Wednesdays, and Fridays.”

So there was the regimen, even in gallantry: days assigned like joints on a menu.

The Major knocked; and at length the door opened, and a tall gentleman came forth with a foil and mask in one hand and a fencing glove on the other—equipped for honour, and looking as if he might be about to carve reputation into slices.

Major Pendennis made him a deferential bow.

“I believe I have the honour of speaking to Captain Costigan—My name is Major Pendennis.”

The Captain brought his weapon up to the salute, and cried, in his rich brogue, as hearty as a dram poured without measure, “Major, the honer is moine; I’m deloighted to see ye.”
