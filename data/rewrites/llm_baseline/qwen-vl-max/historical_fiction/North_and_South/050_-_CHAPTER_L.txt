“Here we go up, up, up;
And here we go down, down, downee!”

In Milton, the chimneys belched smoke into the grey sky, and the ceaseless thunder of machinery echoed through the streets like a never-ending heartbeat. Iron, steam, and wood moved in endless cycles, driven by no purpose but their own mechanical will. Yet beside them stood men and women—strong, restless, and full of intent—each striving with quiet desperation to carve out a place in a world that offered little comfort or certainty. The town hummed with tension, not from industry alone, but from fear. The air was thick with unspoken questions: *Who has failed? Who is next? What will this mean for me?*

There were few who walked without a goal. No idle strollers, no carefree callers. Every face bore the weight of anxiety. In the Exchange and the Mart, men jostled past one another, eyes sharp, voices low, each eager for news that might shift the balance between survival and ruin. Credit had grown brittle; even the most solid businesses now trembled under the shadow of collapse. Though Milton had so far escaped failure, whispers carried from distant ports—the fall of great shipping houses, the crash of American ventures—spreading dread like a contagion. One man’s downfall could pull others into the abyss.

"Thornton is safe," someone muttered, half to himself. "His business grows every year. He's bold, yes—but he's got sense too."

But then, a step aside, a hushed voice: “He’s spent every penny of his profits on expansion. No reserves. New machinery—cost him a fortune. And if the market turns again… well, let’s just say, the wise know what they’re looking at.”

Mr. Harrison, known as a pessimist, spoke these words. Once a modest inheritor of his father’s trade, he’d clung to old ways, fearing change. He resented those who dared more, who reached farther. But truth was, Mr. Thornton was not as secure as he seemed.

He felt it deeply—not in his pocketbook alone, but in his pride. His reputation, built over years of hard work and shrewd decisions, was now under threat. He had believed that commerce was a level field: any honest, brave man could rise, see the game clearly, and command influence across continents. That was the dream his mother once read aloud to him, as if it were prophecy: *“Her merchants be like princes.”* He had taken it to heart. He wanted his name known in foreign lands, his word trusted as gold. He had labored long and silently toward that end—and now, in his own town, among his own people, he saw how much he truly valued what he had built.

Yet until recently, he had lived in parallel with the lives around him—close, yet separate. Then came Higgins. Not as master and worker, but as two men suddenly face to face, stripped of roles. And in that moment, something shifted. They began to recognize the same human heart beating beneath different clothes. It was a small crack in the wall of separation—a wedge that opened slowly, painfully, but irrevocably.

At first, Thornton had resented Higgins. The strike, a year and a half before, had disrupted orders, wasted time, and damaged his standing. He had imported Irish laborers—unskilled, careless—and their work had ruined shipments meant to be flawless. For months, the fallout weighed on him. When his eyes met Higgins’s, anger stirred—though there was no cause. But he fought it. He resolved not to avoid the man. Instead, he allowed Higgins access, listened when he spoke, treated him with fairness. And slowly, resentment gave way to curiosity.

How, he wondered, could two men working the same trade, striving for the same goals, see their duties so differently? From that question arose a new kind of understanding. Not friendship—yet—but respect. A mutual awareness that neither side knew the whole truth. Each had blind spots. Now, both were learning.

Then came the downturn. Markets crashed. Stocks lost half their value. Orders dried up. Capital tied up in machinery and raw cotton could not be liquidated. Payments for completed work dragged. Bills for cotton came due. Money was scarce. Interest rates soared. Borrowing became desperate. And still, expenses rolled on.

Thornton did not break. He worked night after night, poring over ledgers in his private room long after his family slept. He kept his composure. At home, he remained gentle, calm. At the mill, he said little—but the workers noticed. They saw the strain in his eyes, the shortness of breath, the rare sighs caught behind closed doors. And though he had once inspired fear, now some felt pity.

One evening, Higgins lingered after hours, quietly finishing a task left undone. No one knew. Thornton never learned that it was Higgins, not the overlooker, who had done it. But the thought crossed Higgins’s mind: *I reckon I know who’d have been sorry to see our master sitting there like a piece o’ grey calico.* The old parson would have grieved, he mused, seeing such sorrow on a man’s face.

The next day, Higgins stopped Thornton in Marlborough Street.

“Measter,” he said, halting the man mid-stride. “Have yo’ heerd aught of Miss Marget lately?”

“Miss—who?” Thornton replied, startled.

“Miss Marget—Miss Hale—the oud parson’s daughter. Yo’ know who I mean.”

“Oh!” Thornton’s face softened instantly. The cold rigidity melted. A warmth spread across his features. “She’s my landlord now, Higgins. I hear of her through her agent. She’s well. Among friends. Thank you, Higgins.”

That “thank you” hung in the air. It wasn’t just politeness—it carried feeling. Higgins sensed it. He followed it, like a flicker of light in the dark.

“And she’s not getten married, measter?”

“No,” Thornton said, his expression clouding again.

“There’s talk of it—with a connection of the family.”

“Then she’ll not be coming back to Milton?”

“No.”

Higgins stepped closer. “Stop a minute, measter. Is th’ young gentleman cleared?”

He winked—an odd gesture that only deepened the mystery.

“The young gentleman—I mean Master Frederick. Her brother. He was over here, yo’ know.”

“Over here?”

“Ay. At th’ missus’s death. Yo’ need na be feared o’ my telling. Mary and me, we knew all along. We held our peace—got it through Mary working in th’ house.”

“And he was over. It was her brother.”

“Sure enough. And I reckoned yo’ knowed it, or I’d never ha’ let on. Yo’ knowed she had a brother?”

“Yes. I know all about him. And he was over at Mrs. Hale’s death?”

“Nay! I’m not going to tell more. Maybe I’ve already brought trouble. They kept it close. I nobbut wanted to know if they’d getten him cleared?”

“Not that I know of. I know nothing. I only hear of Miss Hale now—through her lawyer.”

Thornton turned away, returning to his urgent tasks. Higgins stood alone, baffled.

*It was her brother,* Thornton thought, walking quickly. *I am glad.* He had never seen her again since the days of the strike, but knowing this—knowing she had not been alone—brought relief. A small thread of hope woven into the dark fabric of his present despair.

Yet the darkness deepened. His agent had trusted a firm in the American trade. That firm collapsed. Others fell with it. Like dominoes. One failure pulled down the next. How could he stand? Could he survive?

Night after night, he worked. Until dawn crept in through the shutters, pale and cold. He had not slept. He sat at his desk, weary beyond measure.

Then the door opened.

His mother stood there, dressed as she had been the day before. She had not slept either.

Their eyes met. Both were pale, drawn, tired.

“Mother,” he said, “why are you not in bed?”

“Son John,” she replied, “do you think I can rest while you sit awake, burdened with care? You haven’t told me what troubles you. But I see it. These many days, you’ve been tormented.”

“Trade is bad.”

“And you dread——”

“I dread nothing,” he said, lifting his head. “I know no man shall suffer because of me. That was my worry.”

“But how do you stand? Will it be a failure?”

Her voice trembled—unusual for her.

“No,” he said. “Not a failure. I must give up the business. But I will pay everyone. I might redeem myself—I am sorely tempted.”

“How?” she asked, stepping forward, laying her hand on his arm. “John, keep your name! Try all risks for that. How would you redeem it?”

“By a speculation—offered to me. Full of risk. But if successful, it would lift me high above water. No one would ever know I was in straits. Still—if it fails—”

“And if it fails,” she whispered, “what then?”

“Honest men are ruined by rogues,” he said grimly. “As I stand now, every farthing of my creditors’ money is safe. But I don’t know where my own is. It may be gone. I may be penniless. So it is their money I would risk.”

“But if it succeeds—no one need know. Is it so desperate?”

“It would make me rich,” he said. “But I would lose my peace of conscience. I would have run the risk of ruining many for my own gain. Mother, I have decided. You won’t grieve much over leaving this house, will you?”

“No,” she said, tears rising. “But to see you not what you are—this will break my heart. What can you do?”

“Be the same John Thornton. Do right. Make mistakes. Then try again. But it is hard, mother. I have worked. I have planned. I have discovered new powers in my position—too late. All is over. I am too old to begin again with the same heart. It is hard.”

He turned away, covering his face.

“I cannot understand,” she said, voice heavy. “Here is my boy—good son, just man, tender heart—and he fails in everything he sets his mind to. Finds a woman to love—and she does not return it. Works hard—and it comes to nothing. Others grow rich and hold their names high. Why? Where is justice?”

“Shame never touched me,” he murmured.

“But God has been very hard on you,” she said, shaking with sobs. “Very hard.”

She fell upon his neck, kissing him through her tears.

“Mother,” he said, holding her gently. “Who sent me this lot in life—both good and evil?”

She shook her head. No religion now. Too much pain.

“Mother,” he continued, “I, too, have rebelled. But I strive no longer. Help me, as you helped me when I was a child. When father died, and we had little, you spoke noble, trusting words. I remember them. Say them again. Let us not believe the world has hardened our hearts. If you speak them now, I will feel the pious simplicity of my childhood. I say them to myself—but they come differently from you.”

“I have had many trials,” she sobbed. “But none so sore as this. To see you cast down from your rightful place! I could say it for myself, John—but not for you. Not for you. God has seen fit to be very hard on you.”

She wept uncontrollably—old grief released.

Then silence.

She looked. He sat by the table, arms across it, head bent.

“Oh, John!” she said, lifting his face.

The pallor, the depth of sorrow—she thought for a moment he was dying. But then the rigidity softened. Color returned. He was alive. Alive.

She thanked God for that—for his simple existence, for the fact that he was still hers.

He rose, opened the shutters. Dawn flooded in—ruddy, cold, bleak. The wind blew from the east. The weather was harsh. There would be no demand for summer goods this year. That last hope—of revival—was dead.

Yet it was a comfort to have spoken with her. To know they understood each other, even if they did not agree. They were not in harmony—but they were not enemies.

Fanny’s husband, angered by Thornton’s refusal to join his speculative venture, withdrew any offer of financial aid. The speculator needed cash—and Thornton could not provide it.

So it came to pass: after weeks of dread, Thornton had to surrender the business he had built with honor and sweat. He would seek a subordinate role.

Marlborough Mills and its dwelling were leased long-term. They must be relet. Offers came swiftly.

Mr. Hamper wished to partner with him—his son was being set up with capital in a neighboring town. But the young man was half-educated, wholly self-serving, brutal in pleasure and pain. Thornton declined. He would rather be a manager—where he could retain some control—than submit to a tyrannical partner whose whims he knew he would oppose within months.

So he waited. Humble. Silent.

And as news swept through the Exchange—of the enormous fortune made by his brother-in-law, Mr. Watson—Thornton stood apart. The world admired Watson. Called him wise, bold, visionary. But Thornton felt nothing but a quiet ache.

Success, he knew, had its price. And sometimes, it was paid in the quiet dignity of those who refused to gamble with others' futures.

He watched the tide turn—and waited.
