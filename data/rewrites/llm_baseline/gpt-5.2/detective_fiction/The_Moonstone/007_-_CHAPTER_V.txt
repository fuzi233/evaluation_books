The first thing I tried, when we found ourselves alone at last, was to get up from my place on the sand—for the third time. The Shivering Sand gives you no honest footing. It sucks at a man’s boots like bad dough at the heel of the hand, and the sea keeps breathing in and out over it with that soft, sly sound—no louder than a cook’s spoon scraping the bottom of a copper pan, and just as steady.

Mr. Franklin stopped me.

“There is one advantage about this horrid place,” he said. “We have got it all to ourselves. Stay where you are, Betteredge; I have something to say to you.”

While he spoke, I took him in—trying, as any man would, to find the boy I remembered inside the gentleman he had turned into. The man put me out completely. Not a bit of the rosy cheeks remained, nor the trim look of him in his little jacket, as if he had stepped out of a picture-frame. His skin had paled; and, what was worse to my old-fashioned taste, the lower part of his face was grown over with curly brown beard and moustachios, like a foreigner’s. His manner had a pleasant, quick touch-and-go to it—light enough to make you feel a room go airy when he entered—but it wasn’t the free-and-easy way of other days. And he had promised, when he went away, to be tall; but he had not kept his promise. Neat he was, and slim, and well made; but by an inch or two he fell short of that middle height which looks right in an English gentleman.

In short, he baffled me—till I came to his eyes. There, in the bright straightforward look, was our nice boy again; and there I stopped my investigation, as I might shut the lid on a spice-box when I have found the true scent.

“Welcome back to the old place, Mr. Franklin,” I said. “All the more welcome, sir, that you have come some hours before we expected you.”

“I have a reason for coming before you expected me,” he answered. “I suspect, Betteredge, that I have been followed and watched in London, for the last three or four days; and I have travelled by the morning instead of the afternoon train, because I wanted to give a certain dark-looking stranger the slip.”

Those words set something stirring in my memory, as quick as a knife-blade under a whetstone. The three jugglers. Penelope’s notion that they meant mischief.

“Who’s watching you, sir—and why?” I asked.

“Tell me about the three Indians you have had at the house today,” says Mr. Franklin, without so much as taking up my question. “It’s just possible, Betteredge, that my stranger and your three jugglers may turn out to be pieces of the same puzzle.”

“How do you come to know about the jugglers, sir?” I said—putting one question on the top of another, which is bad manners, I own; but when the mind is once heated, it pops and hisses like a kettle on a fast fire.

“I saw Penelope at the house,” says he; “and Penelope told me. Your daughter promised to be a pretty girl, Betteredge, and she has kept her promise. Penelope has got a small ear and a small foot. Did the late Mrs. Betteredge possess those inestimable advantages?”

“The late Mrs. Betteredge possessed a good many defects, sir,” says I. “One of them—if you will pardon my mentioning it—was never keeping to the matter in hand. She was more like a fly than a woman: she couldn’t settle on anything.”

“She would just have suited me,” says Mr. Franklin. “I never settle on anything either. Betteredge, your edge is better than ever. Your daughter said as much when I asked for particulars about the jugglers. ‘Father will tell you, sir. He’s a wonderful man for his age; and he expresses himself beautifully.’ Penelope’s own words—blushing divinely. Not even my respect for you prevented me from—never mind; I knew her when she was a child, and she’s none the worse for it. Let’s be serious. What did the jugglers do?”

I was dissatisfied with my daughter—not for letting Mr. Franklin kiss her; he was welcome to that—but for making me tell the story at second hand, like warmed-over broth when the joint is in the house. Still, there was no help for it; so I went through the circumstances from beginning to end.

As I spoke, his merriment dried up, as if a draught had gone through him. He sat knitting his eyebrows and twisting at his beard, listening the way a good carver listens for the bone under the knife. When I had done, he repeated—just as I had given them—two of the questions the chief juggler had put to the boy; repeating them, as it were, to taste them and be sure of the flavour.

“‘Is it on the road to this house, and on no other, that the English gentleman will travel today?’ ‘Has the English gentleman got It about him?’”

He paused; and I heard, underneath our voices, the small noises of the place—the faint lap and draw of the tide, the far cry of a gull that sounded thin in that wide air, and, now and then, the soft shift of sand as it settled. It is an unwholesome music. Give me, for comfort, the honest clatter of plates in a kitchen, and the steady chopping of a cook’s knife, and the friendly talk that rises and falls with the fire. This whispering, empty shore makes a man feel watched—even when he is alone.

“I suspect,” says Mr. Franklin—pulling a little sealed paper parcel from his pocket—“that ‘It’ means this. And ‘this,’ Betteredge, means my uncle Herncastle’s famous Diamond.”

“Good Lord, sir!” I cried. “How do you come to be in charge of the wicked Colonel’s Diamond?”

“The wicked Colonel’s will has left his Diamond as a birthday present to my cousin Rachel,” says Mr. Franklin. “And my father, as the wicked Colonel’s executor, has given it in charge to me to bring down here.”

If the sea, then oozing in smoothly over the Shivering Sand, had turned to dry land before my eyes, I doubt I could have stared harder.

“The Colonel’s Diamond left to Miss Rachel!” says I. “And your father, sir—the Colonel’s executor! Why, I would have laid any bet you like, Mr. Franklin, that your father wouldn’t have touched the Colonel with a pair of tongs!”

“Strong language, Betteredge! What was there against the Colonel? He belonged to your time, not to mine. Tell me what you know about him, and I’ll tell you how my father came to be his executor—and more besides. I have made some discoveries in London about my uncle Herncastle and his Diamond which have rather an ugly look to my eyes; and I want you to confirm them. You called him the ‘wicked Colonel’ just now. Search your memory, my old friend, and tell me why.”

I saw then he was in earnest; and I told him. What follows is the substance of it, written out straight, because if you miss the savour of these particulars now, you will find yourself at sea later—as lost as a man who has swallowed a mouthful of wind and calls it nourishment.

My lady’s father—old lord with the short temper and the long tongue—had five children. Two sons first; then, after a long interval, his lady broke out breeding again, and the three young ladies came briskly one after the other, as fast as nature would permit—my mistress being the youngest and best of them.

Of the sons, the eldest, Arthur, inherited title and estate. The second was the Honourable John; he had a fine fortune left him by a relative, and he went into the army.

It’s an ill bird that fouls its own nest. The Herncastles are my nest; and I take it as a favour not to be pressed for the nastier particulars. But this much I will say plain: the Honourable John was, in my honest belief, one of the greatest blackguards that ever lived. That is the taste he left on all tongues, and no sauce will sweeten it.

He began in the Guards. He had to leave the Guards before he was two-and-twenty—never mind why. The army is strict, and it was too strict for him. He went out to India to see whether they were equally strict there, and to try active service. For courage—if you must give the devil his due—he was a mixture of bull-dog and game-cock, with a dash of savage. He was at the taking of Seringapatam. Afterwards he shifted from one regiment to another, and in the third got his last step as lieutenant-colonel; and, getting that, got also a sunstroke, and came home to England.

He came back with a character that shut the doors of all his family against him—my lady, then just married, taking the lead, and declaring (with Sir John’s approval, of course) that her brother should never enter any house of hers.

There were slurs enough on the Colonel to make decent people shy; but the blot of the Diamond is all I need name here. It was said he had got possession of his Indian jewel by means which, bold as he was, he did not dare acknowledge. He never tried to sell it—not for want of money, for he was not poor, and not, in that way, greedy. He never gave it away. He never even showed it to a living soul. Some said he feared trouble with the military authorities; others—ignorant of the man—said he feared that if he showed it, it would cost him his life. There may have been a grain of truth in that last rumour, only not as they meant it. It was false to say he was afraid; but it was true his life had been twice threatened in India, and it was firmly believed the Moonstone lay at the bottom of it.

When he returned to England and found himself avoided by everybody, the Moonstone was thought to be at the bottom of that too. The mystery that clung to him got in his way and outlawed him among his own. Men would not have him at their clubs. Women—more than one—whom he tried to marry refused him. Friends and relations went suddenly short-sighted in the street.

Another man might have tried to set himself right with the world. But to give in, even when wrong, even with society all against him, was not the Colonel’s way. He had kept the Diamond, in flat defiance of assassination, in India; he kept it, in flat defiance of public opinion, in England. There you have him: a character that braved everything; and a face, handsome as it was, that looked possessed by the devil.

From time to time we heard rumours. Sometimes he was said to be given up to opium-smoking and collecting old books; sometimes to strange chemistry; sometimes seen carousing in the lowest slums of London with the lowest people. Anyhow, a solitary, vicious, underground life—that was his.

Once, and once only, I saw him face to face after his return. It was about two years before the time I now write of, and about a year and a half before his death. The Colonel came unexpectedly to my lady’s house in London. It was the night of Miss Rachel’s birthday—the twenty-first of June—and there was a party in honour of it, as usual.

I received word from the footman that a gentleman wanted me. I went into the hall and found the Colonel there—wasted, worn, old, shabby; as wild and as wicked-looking as ever. The hall was bright with candles, and the sound of company came faintly through the doors—laughter, music, the rustle and stir of a great house in full swing. But he stood in the middle of it like a dark stain on white linen.

“Go up to my sister,” says he, “and say that I have called to wish my niece many happy returns of the day.”

He had tried more than once by letter to be reconciled with my lady—for no other purpose, I am firmly persuaded, than to annoy her. But this was the first time he had come himself. I had it on the tip of my tongue to say there was a party. But the look of him daunted me; it had a kind of listening cruelty in it, as if he heard the smallest weakness in you the way a rat hears bread crumbling.

I went up with his message and left him, by his own desire, waiting in the hall. The servants stared at him from a distance, as if he were a walking engine of destruction, loaded with powder and shot, and likely to go off among them at a moment’s notice.

My lady had a dash—no more—of the family temper.

“Tell Colonel Herncastle,” she said, when I delivered his message, “that Miss Verinder is engaged, and that I decline to see him.”

I tried to plead for a civiller answer, knowing his superiority to restraints that govern gentlemen in general. It was useless. The family temper flashed out at me directly.

“When I want your advice,” says my lady, “you know that I always ask for it. I don’t ask for it now.”

So I went down again with the message; but I took the liberty of putting a better dressing on it, to make it go down if it could.

“My lady and Miss Rachel regret that they are engaged, Colonel,” I said, “and beg to be excused having the honour of seeing you.”

I expected an outbreak, even at that. To my surprise, he took it with an unnatural quiet. His eyes—glittering bright grey—settled on me for a moment; and he laughed, not out of himself like other people, but into himself, softly, chuckling, horridly mischievous.

“Thank you, Betteredge,” he said. “I shall remember my niece’s birthday.”

With that, he turned on his heel and walked out.

The next birthday came round, and we heard he was ill in bed. Six months after that—six months before the time I now write of—a letter came to my lady from a highly respectable clergyman. It told two wonderful things: first, that the Colonel had forgiven his sister on his death-bed; second, that he had forgiven everybody else and made a most edifying end.

I have an unfeigned respect for the Church; but I am firmly persuaded that the devil remained in undisturbed possession of the Honourable John, and that the last abominable act in the life of that abominable man was—saving your presence—to take the clergyman in.

That was all I had to tell Mr. Franklin. And I noticed he listened more and more eagerly as I went on, and that the story of the Colonel being turned away from his sister’s door on Miss Rachel’s birthday struck him like a shot that hits the mark. He did not say so; but his face showed me plainly enough that I had made him uneasy.

“You have said your say, Betteredge,” he remarked. “It’s my turn now. Before, however, I tell you what discoveries I have made in London and how I came to be mixed up in this matter of the Diamond, I want to know one thing. You look, my old friend, as if you didn’t quite understand the object to be answered by this consultation of ours. Do your looks belie you?”

“No, sir,” I said. “My looks, on this occasion at any rate, tell the truth.”

“In that case,” says Mr. Franklin, “suppose I put you up to my point of view before we go any further. I see three very serious questions involved in the Colonel’s birthday-gift to my cousin Rachel. Follow me carefully, Betteredge; and count me off on your fingers, if it will help you.”

He had a certain pleasure in showing how clear-headed he could be, which brought the boy back to me again—like hearing an old tune played on a new instrument.

“Question the first: Was the Colonel’s Diamond the object of a conspiracy in India? Question the second: Has the conspiracy followed the Colonel’s Diamond to England? Question the third: Did the Colonel know the conspiracy followed the Diamond; and has he purposely left a legacy of trouble and danger to his sister, through the innocent medium of his sister’s child? That is what I am driving at, Betteredge. Don’t let me frighten you.”

It was all very well to say it. He had frightened me.

If he was right, our quiet English house—our ordinary life of dinners, and dressing bells, and household keys—was to be invaded by a devilish Indian Diamond, and by a conspiracy of living rogues trailing after it, set loose on us by the vengeance of a dead man. It was as if a strange spice had been shaken into the pot without warning—not a wholesome one that warms, but a hot, bitter dust that stings the nose and will not be named in our plain kitchen language. And what made it worse was the sound of the thing: the thought of stealthy feet, and foreign whispers, and the delicate household noises—our good, known noises—listened to and measured by strangers.

Who ever heard the like of it—in the nineteenth century; in an age of progress; in a country rejoicing in the blessings of the British constitution? Nobody ever heard the like of it; and nobody, therefore, can be expected to believe it.

I shall go on with my story, however, in spite of that.

When you get a sudden alarm of the sort I had got now, nine times out of ten you feel it in your stomach first. And when it catches you there, your attention wanders, and you begin to fidget. I fidgeted silently on the sand. Mr. Franklin noticed me—whether it was a perturbed stomach or a perturbed mind (they mean the same thing in practice)—and, checking himself just as he was starting with his part of the story, said sharply:

“What do you want?”

What did I want? I didn’t tell him; but I will tell you, in confidence. I wanted a whiff of my pipe, and a turn at Robinson Crusoe.
