The air hit me the moment I crossed the threshold, sharp and still as the interior of an ice-house, crystallizing the edge of my coat collar and stinging the tip of my nose. The heavy door clicked shut behind us with no echo, as if the space itself swallowed every sound before it could travel. The sexton muttered something to his wife, and she turned to face us— and the change that had come over her stole my breath. It was as if the blazing splendour of her eyes had overflowed their bounds, sinking deep into every line of her face until she glowed with a loveliness that outstripped even Beatrice’s image blooming in the white rose of the redeemed. Eternal, unshakable life streamed from her skin like unbroken, gentle lightning, and even her hands shone with milky radiance, every curved knuckle gleaming like polished moonstone. Her beauty was so overwhelming I could barely look at her, and I exhaled in relief when she turned that unearthly light away from me.

The candle’s flame stretched only a few feet ahead of us, thin and wavering, and at first I could make out almost nothing of the room around us. Presently, though, its glow caught a faint glimmer raised just above the stone floor. Was that a bed? What living thing could possibly sleep in such mortal, biting cold? No wonder it would never wake of its own accord, I thought. Beyond that first glimmer shone a fainter one, and soon I could see uncertain, pale gleams stretching out on every side.

A few slow paces brought us to the first shape: a human form stretched straight and still under a sheet, its face turned away from the light, so I could not tell if it belonged to man or woman. I soon realized we were walking down an endless aisle of couches, almost every one of them holding a still, sheeted figure laid with its head pointing toward the passage. My throat tightened, and a heavy, silent dread settled over my soul. We wound through aisle after aisle, past couches too numerous to count. Only a handful of them were visible at any one time, but they stretched out on all sides, vanishing into shadow that seemed to stretch to infinity. Was this where I was meant to choose my bed? Must I lie down to sleep among the unwaking, with no one to rouse me? Was this the sexton’s famed library, and were these still sleepers his books? This was no gentle half-way house, this chamber of the half-dead.

“One of the cellars I am placed to watch!” remarked Mr. Raven in a low whisper, as if he feared his voice could rouse even the deepest sleeper. “Much wine is set here to ripen! But it is dark for a stranger,” he added.
“The moon is rising; she will soon be here,” said his wife. Her voice was clear, low, and sweet, ringing with the soft weight of ancient sorrow long since laid to rest.

Even as she spoke, silver light spilled through a narrow opening in the high wall, and a thousand white sheets glowed back to meet her shine. Still, I could not make out the beginning or end of the rows of couches. They stretched on and on, as if they were laid out for every soul in the disparted world to rest upon. Down every narrow, receding aisle, each couch stood alone, holding a single lonely sleeper. I had thought their state was death at first, but soon I saw it was something far deeper, something I had no name for. The moon climbed higher, shining through other openings in the wall, but I could never see enough of the vast space at once to make out its true shape: now it looked like a long, empty cathedral nave, now a huge stone barn repurposed to hold tombs. The moon here was colder than any moon I had ever seen on the frostiest winter night of my old world, casting a bluish, icy gleam over the white sheets and the pallid faces peeking out from beneath them — though it might have been the faces themselves that made the moon feel so cold.

Every sleeper shared the quiet brotherhood of rest, but no two faces bore the same story. Here lay a man who had died — though this was not death, and I had no other word for what I saw — in the full prime of his strength. His dark beard flowed like a liberated stream from the glacier of his frozen, pale countenance, his forehead smooth as polished marble, a faint shadow of pain lingering only at the corners of his lips. On the next couch lay a girl of unearthly beauty. The sadness left on her face by parting had not yet fully melted into perfect peace, but absolute submission softened every placid feature, with no trace of wasting disease, killing care, or grief of heart: if pain had ever touched her, it had long since been charmed asleep, never to wake again. Many of the beautiful lay still there, some of them small children — but I saw no infants among them. The loveliest of all was a stately lady whose white hair was the only marker that she had been old when she first fell asleep. On her face rested not submission, but noble, unshakable acquiescence, a certainty as firm as the foundations of the universe that all was exactly as it should be. On a small handful of faces lingered the almost obliterated scars of old strife, the faint marrings of hopeless loss, the fading shadows of sorrows that had once seemed inconsolable. The aurora of the great new morning had not yet quite melted them away, and every one of those faces seemed to plead, “Pardon me: I died only yesterday!” or “Pardon me: I died but a century ago!” That some had rested here for ages I knew, not only from their unutterable repose, but from some quiet, ancient weight about them for which I had no word or symbol.

At last we came to three empty couches, immediately beyond which lay the form of a beautiful woman just past the prime of life. One of her arms was outside the sheet, her palm turned upward, a dark spot right at its centre. Next to her rested the stalwart figure of a man of middle age. His arm too was out of the sheet, his strong hand half closed as if still clenched around the grip of a sword. I thought he must have been a king who died fighting for the truth.
“Will you hold the candle nearer, wife?” whispered the sexton, bending down to examine the woman’s hand. “It heals well,” he murmured to himself. “The nail found in her nothing to hurt!”

At last I found the courage to speak. “Are they not dead?” I asked softly.
“I cannot answer you,” he replied in a subdued voice. “I almost forget what they mean by DEAD in the old world. If I said a person was dead, my wife would understand one thing, and you would imagine another. This is but one of my treasure vaults,” he went on. “Not all my guests are laid in vaults: out there on the moor they lie thick as the leaves of a forest after the first blast of your winter — thick, let me say rather, as if the great white rose of heaven had shed its petals over it. All night the moon reads their faces, and smiles.”
“But why leave them in the corrupting moonlight?” I asked.
“Our moon,” he answered, “is not like yours — the old cinder of a burnt-out world. Her beams embalm the dead, not corrupt them. You observe that here the sexton lays his dead on the earth; he buries very few under it! In your world he lays huge stones on them, as if to keep them down. I watch for the hour to ring the resurrection-bell, and wake those that are still asleep. Your sexton looks at the clock to know when to ring the dead-alive to church; I hearken for the cock on the spire to crow: ‘AWAKE, THOU THAT SLEEPEST, AND ARISE FROM THE DEAD!’”

I began to conclude the self-styled sexton was in truth an insane parson: the whole thing was too mad to be real. But how was I to escape? I was helpless. In this world of the half-dead, the raven and his wife were the only living souls I had yet encountered. Whither could I turn for help? I was lost in a space larger than imagination; for if here two things, or any parts of them, could occupy the same space, why not twenty or ten thousand? I dared not think further in that direction.
“You seem in your dead to see differences beyond my perception!” I ventured to remark.
“None of those you see,” he answered, “are in truth quite dead yet, and some have but just begun to come alive and die. Others had begun to die, that is to come alive, long before they came to us; and when such are indeed dead, that instant they will wake and leave us. Almost every night some rise and go. But I will not say more, for I find my words only mislead you! This is the couch that has been waiting for you,” he ended, pointing to one of the three empty frames.
“Why just this?” I said, beginning to tremble, stalling for time with parley.
“For reasons which one day you will be glad to know,” he answered.
“Why not know them now?”
“That also you will know when you wake.”
“But these are all dead, and I am alive!” I objected, shuddering.
“Not much,” rejoined the sexton with a smile, “—not nearly enough! Blessed be the true life that the pauses between its throbs are not death!”
“The place is too cold to let one sleep!” I said.
“Do these find it so?” he returned. “They sleep well — or will soon. Of cold they feel not a breath: it heals their wounds. Do not be a coward, Mr. Vane. Turn your back on fear, and your face to whatever may come. Give yourself up to the night, and you will rest indeed. Harm will not come to you, but a good you cannot foreknow.”

The sexton and I stood by the side of the couch, his wife, with the candle in her hand, at the foot of it. Her eyes were full of light, but her face was again of a still whiteness; it was no longer radiant.
“Would they have me make of a charnel-house my bed-chamber?” I cried aloud. “I will not. I will lie abroad on the heath; it cannot be colder there!”
“I have just told you that the dead are there also, ‘Thick as autumnal leaves that strow the brooks In Vallombrosa,’” said the librarian.
“I will NOT,” I cried again; and in the compassing dark, the two figures gleamed out like spectres that waited on the dead. Neither answered me; each stood still and sad, and looked at the other.
“Be of good comfort; we watch the flock of the great shepherd,” said the sexton to his wife. Then he turned to me. “Didst thou not find the air of the place pure and sweet when thou enteredst it?” he asked.
“Yes; but oh, so cold!” I answered.
“Then know,” he returned, and his voice was stern, “that thou who callest thyself alive, hast brought into this chamber the odours of death, and its air will not be wholesome for the sleepers until thou art gone from it!”

They turned and vanished deeper into the great chamber, and I was left alone in the moonlight with thousands of still, quiet forms. I turned to escape. What a long way I found it back through the aisles of the dead! At first I was too angry to be afraid, but as I grew calm, the still shapes loomed terrible at the edge of my vision. At last, with loud, crude offence to the gracious silence, I ran, I fled wildly, and bursting out, flung the door shut behind me. It closed with an awful, perfect silence.

I stood in pitch-darkness. Feeling about me, I found a second door, pulled it open, and was greeted by the dim glow of a familiar lamp. I stood in my own library, hand wrapped around the handle of the masked hidden door. Had I come to myself out of a vision? Or lost myself by stepping back into one? Which was the real — what I now saw, or what I had just ceased to see? Could both be real, interpenetrating yet unmingling? I threw myself on a couch, and fell asleep.

In the library was one small window facing east, through which, at this time of the year, the first rays of the sun shone upon a mirror that cast their reflection straight onto the masked door. When I woke, the beam of light was still there, and it drew my gaze like a magnet. With the sudden, certain feeling that the boundless chamber I had fled lay just behind that door, I sprang to my feet and wrenched it open. The sunlight, like an eager hound released from its leash, shot before me into the closet and pounced upon the gilded edges of a large book.
“What idiot,” I cried, “has put that book on the shelf the wrong way?” But the gilded edges, reflecting the light a second time, flung it onto a half-open nest of drawers tucked in the dark corner, and I spotted the gap in the wood.
“More meddling!” I grumbled, and went to close the drawer. It was stuffed full of old papers, so overfull it would not shut. Taking the topmost sheet out, I perceived that it was written in my father’s hand, a long, dense page. The words my eyes landed on first filled me with sharp, urgent eagerness to read the rest. I carried it to the library, sat down in one of the western windows, and began to read what follows.
