CHAPTER XVI. Marianne had not slept a single moment the night after Willoughby left, and she would have felt deeply ashamed if she had been able to rest. She dreaded facing her family the next morning—her eyes swollen, her voice hoarse, her spirit broken. Yet such feelings were too strong for sleep or silence. The entire night passed in wakefulness, and most of it in tears. When she finally rose, she did so with a pounding headache, unable to speak, unwilling to eat, her presence a constant source of sorrow to her mother and sister. No comfort was offered, nor could any be accepted. Her grief was too profound, too real.

After breakfast, she walked alone through the quiet lanes of Allenham, revisiting every spot where they had once laughed together. Each step stirred memories of joy now twisted into pain. She wandered aimlessly, her heart heavy, her thoughts consumed by what had been and what would never be. The evening brought no relief. At the piano, she played every tune they had shared—the songs he had taught her, the melodies their voices had once harmonized upon. She lingered over each note, tracing his handwriting on the music sheets, her breath catching as emotion overwhelmed her. There was no joy in this sorrow, only the deep, quiet ache of absence.

Day after day, she returned to these rituals: sitting at the instrument, singing until her voice cracked under the weight of her tears; reading books they had read together, lingering over pages that once held warmth but now only echoed loneliness. The intensity of her grief could not last forever. Within days, it softened into a quieter melancholy—but still, whenever she walked alone or sat in silence, the old sorrows resurfaced, as sharp and true as ever.

No letter came from Willoughby. And though her family wondered, Marianne showed no sign of expectation. Mrs. Dashwood found comfort in explanations that pleased her own tender heart. “Remember,” she told Elinor, “Sir John often collects our mail himself. It’s possible their letters pass through his hands, and secrecy must be preserved.” Elinor nodded, though she doubted whether such reasoning truly explained the silence. Still, she tried to believe it.

Yet one idea troubled her deeply. “Why don’t you simply ask Marianne,” she said gently, “whether she is engaged to Willoughby? You are her mother—kind, loving, understanding. Such a question would come naturally from affection, not suspicion. She has always been open with you.”

Mrs. Dashwood recoiled at the thought. “Never! Suppose they are not engaged—what anguish would such a question cause? It would be cruel, ungenerous. I would never earn her trust again if I forced her to confess something meant to remain private. I know my daughter. I know her heart. When the time comes, she will tell me herself. I will not press her. A child’s confidence should not be broken by a parent’s curiosity.”

Elinor sighed. She saw only weakness in such sentimentality, especially when her sister’s youth and suffering demanded clarity. But she could not sway her mother. Reason, care, prudence—all were lost beneath the glow of romantic tenderness.

It was several days before Willoughby’s name was spoken aloud in front of Marianne. Sir John and Mrs. Jennings, however, were less delicate. Their jokes and teasing only added to her pain. But one evening, while Mrs. Dashwood idly picked up a volume of Shakespeare, she said, “We never finished *Hamlet*, Marianne. Willoughby left before we could. Let’s put it aside—we’ll resume when he returns… though it may be months before he does.”

“Months!” Marianne exclaimed, startled. “No—weeks, at most.”

Her mother winced at the slip, but Elinor smiled. For once, Marianne had spoken with certainty—proof, she thought, that her sister still believed in love, in hope, in the return of the man who had promised everything.

A week after Willoughby’s departure, Marianne was persuaded—though reluctantly—to join her sisters on their usual walk. Until then, she had avoided them entirely during their rambles. If they planned to go to the downs, she slipped away toward the lanes; if they chose the valley, she climbed the hills. She disappeared whenever they set off, seeking solitude in the landscape that mirrored her inner world.

But Elinor, concerned for her sister’s isolation, insisted. Together, they walked along the road through the valley, speaking little. Marianne’s mind was elsewhere—too full of memory and longing to focus on anything else. Elinor, content merely to have her beside her, did not push further.

Beyond the valley entrance, the land opened into a broader stretch of countryside. Before them lay the long road they had first traveled when arriving at Barton. As they paused to take in the view—a scene they had never seen from this vantage point—they spotted a rider approaching.

In moments, they recognized him as a gentleman. Then, with sudden joy, Marianne gasped, “It’s him! It’s really him! I know it!”

She started forward, but Elinor called out, “Wait, Marianne—I think you’re mistaken. He’s not tall enough, and he doesn’t carry himself like Willoughby.”

“He does,” Marianne insisted, her eyes shining. “His coat, his horse—everything! I knew he’d come soon.”

She hurried ahead, eager, certain. Elinor quickened her pace to keep up, hoping to shield her sister from disappointment.

At thirty yards, they saw clearly. Marianne looked again—and her face fell. Her heart sank. She turned abruptly, ready to flee, when both sisters called out to stop her. Then another voice joined theirs—one familiar, warm, unmistakable.

She turned around, surprised, and there stood Edward Ferrars.

He was the only person in the world who could forgive himself for not being Willoughby. The only one whose presence could bring even a smile to her tear-streaked face. She wiped her eyes and welcomed him warmly, her own sorrow momentarily eclipsed by her sister’s happiness.

Edward dismounted, handed his horse to his servant, and walked back with them to Barton. He had come expressly to visit.

They greeted him with kindness—especially Marianne, whose warmth surpassed even Elinor’s. To her, however, the meeting between Edward and her sister seemed merely an extension of the distant coldness she had once noticed at Norland. Edward, in particular, gave no sign of affection. He appeared confused, barely showing pleasure at seeing them. He spoke little, answered only when questioned, and offered no special attention to Elinor.

Marianne watched, growing more puzzled. She began to feel a faint dislike for him—though, as always, her emotions quickly led her back to Willoughby, whose manner had been so alive, so passionate, so utterly unlike Edward’s reserve.

After a brief pause, Marianne asked, “Did you come straight from London?”

“No,” Edward replied. “I’ve been in Devonshire for two weeks.”

“Two weeks?” Marianne repeated, astonished that he had stayed so long without visiting Elinorth. “And you didn’t see her?”

He hesitated. “I’ve been staying near Plymouth with some friends.”

“Have you been in Sussex lately?” Elinor asked.

“I was at Norland about a month ago.”

“And how does dear, dear Norland look?” Marianne asked, her voice softening.

“Dear, dear Norland,” Elinor said, “probably looks much as it always does at this time of year—the woods thick with dead leaves.”

“Oh,” Marianne breathed, “how I used to love watching them fall! How the wind would scatter them around me, swirling in gusts! What beauty, what feeling they inspired! Now, no one notices them. They’re just swept away, treated as a nuisance.”

“It’s not everyone,” Elinor said gently, “who shares your passion for dead leaves.”

“No,” Marianne murmured, “my feelings aren’t often understood. But sometimes…” She trailed off, lost in thought.

Then she turned to Edward. “Look here—this is Barton Valley. See those hills? Do you think you’ve seen their equals? To the left, Barton Park, hidden among the trees. You can see the end of the house. And there, beneath that distant hill, rises our cottage.”

“It’s a beautiful place,” Edward said, “but these lowlands must be muddy in winter.”

“How can you think of dirt when such beauty surrounds you?” Elinor asked.

“Because,” he smiled, “among all those lovely sights, I see a very dirty lane.”

Marianne walked on, thinking to herself, *How strange.*

She turned to him. “Is your neighbourhood pleasant? Are the Middletons agreeable?”

“No,” Marianne replied, “we couldn’t be more poorly placed.”

“Marianne!” Elinor protested. “How can you say that? They’ve been kind to us. Don’t you remember all the happy days we’ve spent with them?”

“No,” Marianne whispered, “nor the painful ones.”

Elinor said nothing. Instead, she focused on keeping the conversation going, talking about their home, its comforts, trying to draw Edward into a natural exchange. But his silence and restraint wounded her. She was annoyed, almost angry—but resolved to treat him with fairness, guided by past kindness rather than present indifference.
