The next duty before me is to set down what little I know—additional, but not unimportant—touching the Moonstone; or, to speak with greater accuracy, touching the Indian design for recovering it. The thing itself, like a spice that seems insignificant between the fingers and yet dominates the whole dish, was small in appearance and strong in consequence; and it bore, as I shall hereafter have cause to show, very remarkably on events still to come.

About a week or ten days after Miss Verinder had left us, one of my clerks came into my private room at the office with a card upon his open palm, as if he were offering some delicate morsel which it would be a pity to drop.

“A gentleman is below, sir. He asks to see you.”

I took the card. A foreign name was written on it—one of those combinations of letters which the eye tastes and then forgets. But the line beneath, in plain English, struck like the crack of a spoon on a copper pan:

“Recommended by Mr. Septimus Luker.”

The audacity of a person in Mr. Luker’s position presuming to recommend anybody to me so discomposed my mind that, for an instant, I could do nothing but stare at the words, as though they were a misprint.

My clerk, observing my silence, ventured his own relish of the matter.

“He is rather a remarkable-looking man, sir—so dark that we have all taken him for an Indian, or something of that sort.”

There are times when curiosity is as sudden and irresistible as a scent from a kitchen door in a cold street. The clerk’s impression, combined with that line from Luker, brought the Moonstone instantly into my thoughts. I decided—very much to the young man’s surprise—to see the stranger at once.

If this appears unprofessional, let anyone who reads me remember that no living man in England—at any rate no living man outside of India—can claim to have been so intimately mixed up with the romance of that Diamond as I have been. I was trusted with the secret of Colonel Herncastle’s plan for escaping assassination. I received his letters, written from time to time to report himself still alive. I drew his Will leaving the Moonstone to Miss Verinder. I persuaded his executor to act upon that Will, on the chance that the jewel might prove a valuable acquisition to the family. And lastly, I combated Mr. Franklin Blake’s scruples and induced him to be the means of conveying the Diamond to Lady Verinder’s house. If there is any prescriptive right of interest in the Moonstone, and in everything connected with it, I may surely claim it.

The moment my mysterious client was shown in, I felt an inward conviction that I stood in the presence of one of the three Indians—probably their chief.

He was dressed with care in European clothes; the cut and the cloth were as correct as any tailor in the Strand could desire. But the deep swarthiness of his skin, his long, supple figure, and a gravity of carriage—polite, composed, and strangely elegant—betrayed the Oriental origin of him to any intelligent eye. There was, too, something in his stillness, like a man who keeps his own counsel the way a good cook keeps a fire: steady, unseen, and always ready.

I pointed to a chair and requested him to state his business.

He began by apologising—in excellent English, chosen with that exactness which makes the language itself feel like borrowed property—for the liberty of troubling me. Then, without flourish, he produced a small parcel. The outer covering was cloth of gold. He removed it; beneath was a second wrapping of silk. From these he took a little box and placed it on my table.

The casket was beautiful—ebony for the ground, and jewels set into it with a richness that had the look of something consecrated rather than merely costly. It lay there like a relic, and yet he handled it as a man handles his own knife—confidently, without reverence, as if it served a purpose.

“I have come, sir,” he said, “to ask you to lend me some money. And I leave this as an assurance to you that my debt will be paid back.”

I touched the card with my finger.

“And you apply to me,” I said, “at Mr. Luker’s recommendation?”

He bowed.

“May I ask,” I continued, “how it is that Mr. Luker himself did not advance the money you require?”

“Mr. Luker informed me, sir, that he had no money to lend.”

“And so he recommended you to come to me?”

The Indian, in his turn, indicated the card.

“It is written there,” he said.

Brief; exact; and perfectly to the purpose. If the Moonstone had been in my keeping, I have no doubt this man would have murdered me without a moment’s hesitation. Short of that, I am bound to record that he was the model of a client. He might not have respected my life; but he did what none of my own countrymen ever do, in all my experience—he respected my time.

“I am sorry,” I said, “that you should have had the trouble of coming. Mr. Luker is mistaken in sending you here. I am entrusted, like other men of my profession, with money to lend. But I never lend it to strangers, and I never lend it upon such a security as you have produced.”

Most men, when refused, stir the air with argument, like a bad cook whisking when he should let the sauce settle. This man did nothing of the sort. He made me another bow, wrapped the casket again in its silk and its cloth of gold with hands that wasted no movement, and rose at once to go.

“Will your condescension towards a stranger excuse my asking one question,” he said, “before I take my leave?”

I bowed in return. Only one question at parting! The average, in my experience, was fifty.

“Supposing, sir, it had been possible (and customary) for you to lend me the money,” he proceeded, “in what space of time would it have been possible (and customary) for me to pay it back?”

“According to the usual course pursued in this country,” I answered, “you would have been entitled to pay the money back, if you liked, in one year from the date at which it was first advanced.”

He made a last bow—lower than all the others—and went out.

It was done in a moment: soft, sudden, and noiseless; he slipped away with a supple, cat-like ease that startled me, I own. In the office below there were the ordinary sounds—the faint rasp of pen on paper, the intermittent shuffling of feet, a door somewhere closing with that hard modern smack which reverberates through a building and seems to quarrel with every quiet thought. Yet he made no sound belonging to himself. It was as if he had walked on silence, leaving only the common noises behind him, and taking his own with him.

When I recovered my composure and began to reflect, I came to one clear conclusion about this otherwise incomprehensible visitor. His face, his voice, his manner—while he was in my room—were under such control that scrutiny slid off him as water slides off polished stone. But he had given me one chance, for all that, of seeing beneath his smooth surface.

He had shown no sign whatever of fixing anything I said in his mind—until I came to the customary time allowed for repayment. At that point, for the first time, he looked me straight in the face while I spoke. The inference was plain: he had asked the question with a special purpose, and he had a special interest in the answer.

The more I considered the matter, the more strongly I suspected that the entire production of the casket and the request for a loan had been mere formalities—like a ceremonial washing of hands before a meal whose true flavour is in the one last mouthful—designed to lead naturally to that inquiry at parting.

I had satisfied myself of this, and was attempting to go a step further and penetrate his motive, when a letter was brought in, bearing no less a signature than Mr. Septimus Luker’s.

He begged my pardon in terms of sickening servility, and assured me that he could explain matters to my satisfaction if I would consent to see him. Again I made an unprofessional sacrifice to curiosity, and appointed him to call at my office the next day.

Mr. Luker was, in every respect, so inferior a creature to the Indian—so vulgar, so ugly, so cringing, so tediously prosy—that he is not worthy of being reported at length. The substance of what he told me was this.

On the day before I received the Indian’s visit, Mr. Luker had himself been favoured with a call from that same accomplished gentleman. In spite of the European dress, Luker had instantly identified him as the chief of the three Indians who had formerly annoyed him by loitering about his house, and who had left him no alternative but to consult a magistrate. From that discovery he had rushed at once—naturally enough—to the conclusion that he must now be in the power of one of the three men who had blindfolded him, gagged him, and robbed him of his banker’s receipt. He became, he said, paralysed with terror and believed his last hour had arrived.

The Indian, on his side, preserved the character of a perfect stranger. He produced the little casket and made the very same application for a loan that he afterwards made to me. As the speediest way of getting rid of him, Mr. Luker declared instantly that he had no money to lend.

Thereupon the Indian asked to be informed of the best and safest person to apply to for the loan he wanted. Mr. Luker answered that the best and safest person, in such cases, was usually a respectable solicitor. Pressed to name one, he mentioned me—for no better reason, as he confessed with a candour born of cowardice, than that, in the extremity of his fear, my name was the first that occurred to him.

“The perspiration was pouring off me like rain, sir,” the wretched creature concluded. “I didn’t know what I was talking about. And I hope you’ll look over it, Mr. Bruff, sir, in consideration of my having been really and truly frightened out of my wits.”

I excused him graciously enough; it was the readiest method of releasing myself from his presence. Before he left, however, I detained him with one inquiry.

Had the Indian said anything noticeable at the moment of quitting Mr. Luker’s house?

Yes. He had asked precisely the same question at parting which he had asked of me; and he had received, of course, the same answer.

What did it mean?

Mr. Luker’s explanation gave me no help. Left to myself, my ingenuity proved quite unequal to grapple with the difficulty. I had a dinner engagement that evening, and went upstairs to dress in no very genial frame of mind—little suspecting that, on this particular occasion, the way to my dressing-room and the way to discovery meant one and the same thing.
