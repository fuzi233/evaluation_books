“Mistress, everyone in the village is saying he is dead.”
Margaret’s head snapped up. “Not so. They still feed me soft hope to my face.”
“Ay, to your face, but behind your back every last one of them swears Gerard Eliassoen will never walk these streets again.”

The dam of Margaret’s control broke, and hot tears streamed down her cheeks. Beside her, Luke whimpered in sympathy: big as a ploughman, broad-shouldered and strong, he had the soft, tender heart of a girl who could not bear to see anyone grieve.
“Prithee, do not weep so, sweet mistress,” he stammered. “I would drag him back from the grave itself before I’d see you sob so sore.”
Margaret wiped her eyes, swearing she was crying only out of anger at their two-faced lies, then laid a steady hand on his shoulder. “Luke, he is not dead. You know the old tales that dying men are given sight beyond what we living folk can see? Listen to me. My own father, when he was on his deathbed — I was such a fool, I was humming making a sleeve, convinced he would get completely well — he sat in that very chair you are in right now, and all at once he was bright as a flame, not a dying man at all. He cried out, ‘I see him! Sore disfigured, on a great river, coming this way.’”
Her voice cracked. “Ah, Luke, if you were a woman, and cared for me half as much as you think you do, you would pity me, and go find him. He is the father of my child.”
“Alack, I would if I knew how!” Luke protested. “But how?”
“Nay, of course you cannot,” she said, shaking herself. “I am mad to even speak of it. I only know that if anyone truly cared for me, they would find a way.”

Luke fell quiet, turning the idea over slow in his head, for his thoughts could never gallop the way Margaret’s did. “The old folk do say dying men see more than the rest of us. A great river… the Maas is great, but if he were on the Maas coming this way he would have reached Delfshaven long ago. The Rhine is greater, far longer, stretching all the way up to the German lands. That must be the one.”
“I think so too,” Margaret said, her eyes lighting. “Denys told Gerard he would make his way home down the Rhine. But what if he turns off before he reaches his birthplace? He does not pine for me half as much as I pine for him, that is plain. Luke, do you not think he has deserted me?”
She waited for him to contradict her, but he said bluntly, “It looks very like it. What a fool that man must be.”
“Nay, we know nothing for certain!” she implored him.

“Let me think again,” Luke said, frowning. At last he had it: there was a river station sixty miles up the Rhine, he said, where every public trading boat put in to resupply. He would travel there, linger at the docks, and ask every passenger that stepped off if a man named Gerard Eliassoen was aboard. “I do not know his face, but if you give me a little letter to hand him, no one will doubt I have a real errand from someone who knows him.”
“Good, kind, thoughtful Luke!” Margaret breathed, tears of relief stinging her eyes. “How little I have valued you all these months! Give me until supper, and I will write it.”

At supper she pressed a long, silk-tied, sealed letter into his palm, her cheeks burning bright. Luke weighed it in his hand, his face growing grave. “Tell me, mistress: if your father’s vision was true, if I find this man, if he turns out better than I think him, and comes home to you straight away — what will become of me?”
Margaret’s face flushed all the way to her brow. “Oh, Luke, Heaven will reward you. I will fall on my knees and bless you, and I will love you all my days as a mother loves her son. Trouble ages the heart, and I feel years older than you. You need not go, it is selfish of me — love makes us all think only of ourselves.”
“Humph,” Luke said, not satisfied. “And what if that scapegrace reads your letter, hears me tell him how you are pining away, and is still a traitor, or too much of an idiot to come home? Must I die an old bachelor, and you waste away lonely into your grave, neither maid, wife, nor widow?”

Margaret panted, shaken by his plain, unflinching good sense. At last she faltered out, “If — and may our Lady be merciful and forbid it — if he refuses to come back, then he shall give me our marriage lines, and I shall be an honest woman, even if I am wretched, and our little boy will not be a bastard. And I swear to you, for all the good you have done me this day, I will never be so ungrateful as to walk past your door to another man’s.”
“Ay, but will you walk in at mine?” Luke pressed.
“Oh, do not ask me that! Not yet. Someday, perhaps, when my wounds stop bleeding. I will try. I will try not to throw myself and my child into the Maas. Do not go, Luke! Do not think of this! It is all madness, from first to last.”

But Luke was as slow to abandon an idea as he was to form it. “Madness is better than nothing,” he said. “I am tired of sitting around doing nothing for you. I leave at peep of day. Only — I have no money. My mother keeps all my wages locked away, and I never see a penny of it.”
Margaret pulled out Catherine’s gold angel, the one that had escaped every narrow scrape for months, and pressed it into his hand. At dawn he set off on his wild errand, and in that superstitious age, guided by a dying man’s vision, it did not feel half so mad to him as it does to us.

When Catherine heard what Margaret had done, she was furious. “Sending that poor boy on such a wild-goose chase! You are just like every other girl, mark my words: you will wear that Luke out, make him sick of you as a whipped dog, and then you will suddenly be as fond of him as a cow is of her calf, and by then it will be too late.”

Twelve hours after Luke set off up the Rhine, two friars crossed the southern border into Holland. The wild-goose chase, for all its absurdity, was drawing the two parties closer every hour. Jerome, unable to preach to the Low Dutch-speaking villagers, was desperate to push on to the coast and catch a ship for England as fast as possible, and with the current carrying their boat downstream, they would have slipped right past Luke’s river station without ever seeing him — if not for one small, fateful detour.

Twenty miles above the docks Luke was headed for, Clement (the young friar who was once Gerard Eliassoen) stopped to preach in a large village. Halfway through his sermon, he noticed a grey Franciscan nun weeping openly in the crowd. After the service he went to her gently, and asked what grieved her.
“It is not my own grief that makes me weep,” she told him. “I weep for my dearest friend. Your words reminded me of the sweet girl she was before she was lured away from her vows. You are a Dominican, and I am of St. Francis’s order — but I cannot keep it to myself any longer.”
She told him that the convent’s favourite young nun, Mary, had been tricked into running away, and after a long spiral of sin, was now trapped working in a roadside inn, forced to act as a decoy for wealthy customers. The innkeeper, she said, was a former servant of the local fierce baron, and had the lord’s full protection; if the nuns tried to storm the inn and drag her back, he would burn their convent to the ground.
“Brute force will never save a soul,” Clement said quietly.

He convinced Jerome to stop for the night at the Franciscan convent, but when he announced the next morning that he had promised the abbess he would try to win Mary back, Jerome threw up his hands. It was not their business, he argued, a waste of precious time they could spend making their way to England. But Clement was no longer a docile, obedient pupil. He stood his ground, and at last they struck a deal: Jerome would push ahead to the coast, book their passage on the next outbound English ship, and give Clement a few days to try his well-meaning, seemingly futile experiment.

By ten o’clock that morning, a tall figure in a rough horseman’s cloak, heavy riding boots, and a drooping wide-brimmed felt hat stood frozen outside the roadside inn where Mary was trapped. Clement’s heart was sick with dread, his body cold with sweat; these fiery, ardent souls dream of grand acts of piety, but falter when they come face to face with the ugly, messy reality of sin. He prayed for courage long at the door, then stepped inside, called loudly for a cup of wine, and made a show of drinking it.
“Landlord,” he said, dangling a heavy gold chain across the table, “I hear you have the prettiest chambermaid for miles around.”
The landlord’s eyes lit up at the sight of the gold. He laughed and yelled, “Janet! Come out here, there’s a fine young lover for you, who’d bind you in chains of gold!”
A voice trilled from the back: “Send him in, then. I’m in double luck today.”

Clement rose, shuddering, and followed her into the private back parlour where she was half-heartedly mending a piece of sewing, breaking off every minute to sing a ragged, half-remembered tavern song. He asked if they could go somewhere more private, and she led him to a small, locked back room. Clement softly closed and latched the window shutters.
“What on earth are you doing that for?” Janet asked, frowning.
“Sweetheart,” he whispered, putting on a mysterious air, “so that God may not see us.”
“Madman,” she scoffed. “Do you think a wooden shutter can block His eye? The only way to get through this life is to stop thinking of Him entirely — if you don’t, it poisons every cup you drink.”
Clement waved the heavy gold chain before her. “Feel this. It is pure virgin gold. I will cut two of these heavy links off for you, all yours.”
“Ah, now you are speaking sense,” she said, grabbing for it greedily. “Why, it’s as heavy as the chain around the Virgin’s neck at the conv—”
She cut herself off, horrified.
“Whisht,” Clement breathed. “It *is* the Virgin’s chain. And you shall have your share — if you do not betray me.”
“Monster!” she yelped, jerking back from him. “Rob the Blessed Virgin of her chain to give to a —”
“You are no sinner beyond saving,” he cried triumphantly, dropping his disguise. “Mary!”

She screamed and lunged for the window, fumbling with the shutters. Before she could get them open, Clement kicked off his heavy boots, pulled off his felt hat, and let his horseman’s cloak fall to the floor. He stood before her in his plain black Dominican robes, holding his crucifix aloft, his face calm and solemn, no trace of the roguish horseman left. The sight did not fill her with fear of harm, but crushed her under the weight of long-buried remorse. She cowered back against the wall.
“Mary,” he said gently. “One word. Are you happy?”
“As happy as I will be in hell,” she whimpered.
“The nuns at your convent are not happy, either. They weep for you day and night — most of all, your Sister Ursula.”
“Poor Ursula,” she sobbed, the tears breaking through.
“The angels weep for you too. Will you not dry all their tears, on earth and in heaven, and save your own soul?”
“I would,” she gasped. “But it is too late.”
“Avaunt, Satan!” Clement thundered. “That is your favourite lie! Listen not to him, Mary — I who come to save you am a far greater sinner than you. Sin is not half so sweet as you thought it would be, is it? Eternity is waiting for you, and you do not have to burn for the choices you made.”

He convinced her the convent would welcome her back, that the abbess and the sisters cared far more about her than they did about the Virgin’s golden chain, that he had broken his order’s rules by entering an inn and wearing lay clothes just to come rescue her. When she hesitated, he showed her plainly the dark end of the life she was living, and stoked the long-forgotten, gentle piety she had buried deep months before. At last she knelt sobbing at his feet, swearing she had never known a moment of peace since she broke her vows, that she would come back with him only if he walked beside her — she could never face the abbess and the sisters alone.

As they prepared to leave, a hundred small, silly little obstacles cropped up, silken nets tangled around her resolve, but Clement swept every one aside. He had brought a spare grey Franciscan habit and cowl for her to change into, to hide her fine tavern clothes. When she gathered up all her stolen trinkets and jewels to take back to the convent and sell for the poor, he told her their source was too impure, and bade her fix her eyes on his crucifix and trample them under her feet. She obeyed, then flung the glittering heap out the window onto the nearby dunghill, casting off the wages of sin forever.

They had barely stepped into the kitchen before the landlord realised what was happening. He roared for his men to stop them: “Reuben! Carl! Gavin! Here is a false friar spiriting away our Janet!”
The farmhands ran in, fists raised. Clement strode at them, crucifix held high, and roared in a voice like thunder: “Forbear! She is a holy nun returning to her vows. The first man who lays a hand on her cowl shall have his arm wither, his body lie unburied, cursed by Rome, and his soul shall roast in eternal fire!”
They shrank back as if they had been burned by flame. Clement grabbed the innkeeper by the neck, strong as a lion in his righteous fury, and hurled him across the room, pitching him headfirst onto the stone floor. He wrenched the front door open, and led the trembling nun out onto the high road.
“Mary,” he said, gasping for breath as they walked, “my choler is ungovernable, a wild beast lurks beneath this black gown. If those men had laid a finger on you, I would have beaten their brains out with this crucifix. I know I am a flawed man.”
“Alas, father,” she said, clinging to his arm, “if you had been any less fierce, I would still be trapped in that place. To save me, you needed to be wary as a fox, and bold as a lion.”
Clement crossed himself. “God chooses imperfect instruments to do His perfect work. Glory be to Him.”

When they neared the convent gate, Mary stopped short, too afraid to go in. Clement left her sitting on a stone to calm herself, and went in to tell the abbess her lost nun was waiting outside. The joy of the sisters was boundless. They arranged a gentle welcome for her, no harsh penance on her first day back. When Clement led her around to the side postern, they heard the whole convent singing, music floating out over the garden.
“It is a feast day,” Mary said, confused. “I have come to spoil it.”
“Hardly,” Clement smiled. “You are the queen of the feast. There is more joy in heaven over one sinner who repents than over ninety-nine just people who need no repentance — and today that joy is here, too.”
The postern flew open, and Sister Ursula flung herself at Mary, sobbing and kissing her on the cheek, clinging to her neck as if she would never let go. The abbess followed, tears running down her face. They begged Clement to stay for the celebrations, but he told them he could not. He had already kept Jerome waiting too long, and had to hurry back to the Rhine, to catch the next boat for England. He bade them farewell, and strode briskly down the road towards the river, entirely unaware he was less than a day’s travel from Luke, the man Margaret had sent to find him.

Margaret’s letter, the one she had given Luke to carry, was the kind of soft, tender, utterly unresentful letter only a woman who loves deeply can write. She told him of the birth of their little boy, named Gerard for his father, the comfort he had been through all the long months of silence. She described his every feature, down to the tiny mole on his little finger. “All the mothers on our street envy me my boy, but I envy the wives, for you are not home to us,” she wrote. “Some say you are dead. But if you were dead, how could I still breathe? Others say you are false — but I will believe that from no lips but yours. My father, as he lay dying, said he saw you on a great river, face turned towards me, sore disfigured. If cruel men have scarred your face, if you have lost a limb — why, then you have all the more need of me. My love is not light as a man’s, my dearest Gerard: I will love you more, not less, than I did the day I pricked my arm and shed those few drops of blood for you, remember? I do not reproach you. I only wait.”

She had said nothing of her doubts, nothing of the pain of waiting alone, for fear that if he was still alive, still hurt, her anger would scare him away. She was no angel, she was only a woman in love, and she had sent soft, loyal Luke to carry that letter to him.
