Not even the fresh, pale sprigged wallpaper hung in each of their rooms was enough to reconcile the Hales to their new life in Milton. Far more than pretty decor was needed—far more than they could ever hope to get. The thick, rank yellow fogs of November had settled over the town, swallowing the broad sweep of valley and river plain that would have stretched out below their windows on a clear day; when Mrs. Hale stepped over their threshold for the first time, all she could see was murky, smoky mist pressed right up against the glass, great choking white wreaths of unwholesome damp curling through every open door.

Margaret and Dixon had been unpacking for two full days, but the house still reeked of moving chaos, crates stacked in hallways, furniture half pushed into place, linens stacked on every available surface. “Oh Margaret! Are we truly to live here?” Mrs. Hale asked, her voice thick with blank dismay. Margaret’s own ached in echo of that dreary tone, and she could barely steady herself enough to reply that London fogs were often far worse. “But London had friends behind it,” Mrs. Hale whispered. “Here—we are desolate. Oh Dixon, what a place this is!”

“Indeed ma’am, this damp will be the death of you before the winter’s half out, and then I know exactly who’ll—wait a minute, Miss Hale! That crate is far too heavy for you to be lifting,” Dixon fussed, darting forward to take the weight from Margaret’s arms. “Not at all, thank you,” Margaret replied coolly. “The best thing we can do for Mamma is get her room fully made up for her to rest in, while I run and bring her a cup of hot coffee.”

Mr. Hale was equally low-spirited, and turned to Margaret for comfort as soon as he saw her. “Margaret, I swear this place is unhealthy. Only think if your mother or you fell ill over this muck. I wish I had moved us to some quiet corner of Wales instead—this is dreadful.”

There was no comfort to be given. They were settled in Milton, and they would have to endure the smoke and fog for many months yet. The night before, Mr. Hale had tallied up their moving costs and their fortnight’s stay at the inn in Heston with a sinking heart, and found it had eaten through nearly all his small stock of ready cash. There was nowhere else to go. That heavy smoky curtain of fog was not just weather, Margaret realized late that night—it was a barrier of circumstance that had shut them completely off from the old soft life they had known at Helstone. She sank down onto a wooden crate in her narrow back bedroom, half ready to slump into a stupor of despair. The window looked out onto the blank brick wall of the adjoining house, barely ten feet away, looming through the murk like a solid wall blocking off all hope. The address label stuck to the crate under her fingers was written in the familiar looping hand of the Helstone village grocer, and that tiny detail was almost enough to make her cry.

She forced herself to pull her mind away from the present, and fished out the half-read letter from Edith she had abandoned that morning in the chaos of unpacking. Edith wrote giddily of their arrival in Corfu, of their Mediterranean voyage, the dancing and late-night music on deck, the new villa they shared with another newly married officer’s family, perched high on rocky cliffs looking out over a sea so deep and blue it looked like crushed sapphire. Her days were nothing but boat trips and land picnics, copying the latest popular dance tunes for the regimental bandmaster; her life was a perfect stretch of cloudless blue sky, with not a single shadow of care to mar it. She wrote that she hoped Margaret could come visit them for a long stay if the regiment was posted another year on the island.

Margaret smiled sadly, remembering the rainy day a full year prior that Edith described, when she had refused to wear her new silk gown to a stuffy London dinner, only to meet Captain Lennox there for the very first time. All the easy luxury of the Harley Street drawing rooms, the grand dinners, the quiet ease of her old London life rose up before her, a sharp contrast to the cold damp chaos of her current bedroom. That old smooth world had closed over without her, going on just the same even though Aunt Shaw and Edith were gone, even though she was hundreds of miles north in a smoky mill town. She thought of Henry Lennox, who had once asked her to marry him, and knew he would do all he could to forget her now, given how she had rejected him. She could not help but wonder what life would have been like if she had accepted him—she knew his sharp lawyer’s pride would have chafed terribly at the scandal of her father leaving the church. But in that moment she was almost glad it had not come to pass. They were at the very bottom now, she thought. Things could not get any worse. She undressed slowly, falling asleep with a faint, unspoken hope that some brightness would find them soon. She had no idea just how long she would have to wait for it.

The cold damp settled over the household quickly. Mrs. Hale caught a severe, lingering chest cold, Dixon herself grew worn and haggard with all the extra work, and they could not find a single local girl willing to come work as their maid. All the young women in Milton preferred the higher wages and greater independence of working in the factories, and the few who did apply for the post answered Dixon’s sharp inquiries about their qualifications with blunt, independent cheek, even questioning her right to put on airs as a lady’s maid in a household that lived in a thirty pound a year rental house. Dixon’s standard for a good servant was rooted in the reverent, quiet village girls of Helstone’s Sunday school, who treated the parsonage staff with near awe, and she was utterly scandalized by their rough, unapologetic northern manners. The Hales were forced to hire a charwoman to come in almost every day to help with the heavy work.

Mr. Hale had soon picked up several pupils, recommended to him first by his old college friend Mr. Bell, and then through the influence of the prominent local mill owner Mr. Thornton. Most of them were sons of Milton’s manufacturing families, who held that a boy had to be introduced to the work of the mill or warehouse by the age of fourteen or fifteen if he was ever to become a good tradesman—any boy sent off to an English university would come back too unsettled for commercial life, or so the common wisdom ran. There were a handful of older men, though, past their youth but in their prime, who knew they had missed out on an education in their rush to start work, and came to Mr. Hale for lessons. Mr. Thornton was the oldest of these, and quickly his favourite. Mr. Hale praised his intelligence and good sense so often at the dinner table that it became a quiet family joke that he barely ever did any actual tutoring at their lessons, the two of them spending the whole hour talking. Margaret encouraged that light, teasing tone, because she noticed Mrs. Hale was growing quietly jealous of her husband’s new friend, hurt that he seemed to look forward to their meetings more than spending his evenings with her.

Margaret eventually grew weary of Dixon’s endless tales of insolent potential servants, and offered to go out herself to the butchers and grocers around town to ask after a suitable girl. It was a trial at first, walking the bustling streets of the manufacturing town completely unaccompanied. All her life in London, Aunt Shaw had insisted a footman walk with her and Edith anywhere beyond the immediate bounds of Harley Street, and her long free rambles through the wild Helstone woods, where she could run or stand still for an hour to watch a bird at her own pace, made the slow, decorous pace required of her in the crowded mill streets feel stifling. For the first few weeks she kept accidentally stumbling into the great streams of workers pouring out of the mills at shift change, loud, boisterous, completely unapologetic about their frank curiosity. The mill girls would lean in to touch the soft wool of her shawl, exclaim over her dress, and ask where she had bought such pretty fabric, and Margaret soon found herself smiling back, charmed by their uncomplicated honesty. The mill men flustered her far more, calling out open, blunt compliments on her face and figure, that at first made her cheeks burn hot with indignation—until she realized none of them had any intention of being rude, they simply had no use for the polished false politeness of southern drawing rooms. One ragged, careworn middle-aged worker called out to her once on a grey drizzling day, “Your bonny face, my lass, makes the whole day look brighter,” and Margaret gave him a soft, warm smile back. After that they would nod quietly to each other whenever they passed on the road, never exchanging another word.

One mild early spring day, Margaret walked a little way out of town, past the mills, to the hedgerows at the edge of the fields, gathering small bunches of dog violets and lesser celandines, her heart aching a little for the thick carpets of wild spring flowers that would be blooming in the Hampshire woods that same week. Her father left her to run a quick errand in the town, and on the walk home she ran straight into that quiet nodding acquaintance, Nicholas Higgins, walking with his pale, thin young daughter Bessy, who could barely keep pace on her frail legs. Bessy stared longingly at the posy in Margaret’s hand, and on a sudden impulse Margaret offered it to her. The girl’s pale blue eyes lit right up. Nicholas told her he was from further north, near Burnley, and that it did his heart good to see a southern lady be so kind to a pair of ragged factory folk. Margaret spoke to Bessy, her voice soft with pity, and the girl told her quietly that no spring or summer on earth would ever make her well again, that the mill dust had eaten away at her lungs, but she dreamed of a heavenly spring where she would walk in fields of immortal amaranth flowers, no pain or breathlessness to trouble her.

Margaret was shocked, but not repelled. She asked where they lived, and Nicholas at first grumbled that he did not care for strangers poking around his small cottage, but softened when he saw how sorry Margaret was for his sick daughter, and told her she could call on them any time she liked. As they turned towards their street, Bessy looked over her shoulder and whispered to Margaret not to forget her promise to visit. Nicholas laughed, shaking his head, and said he could read that proud soft face of hers like a book—she would not let them down.

Margaret walked home smiling, and for the first time since they had arrived in Milton, the foggy smoky town did not feel like a cold prison. Brightness had not come with the sun, or some grand lucky turn of fortune. It had come in the shape of two ragged, honest strangers, who had crossed her path on a grey mill road, and given her a real, living human stake in the strange northern town that had once felt so utterly alien.
