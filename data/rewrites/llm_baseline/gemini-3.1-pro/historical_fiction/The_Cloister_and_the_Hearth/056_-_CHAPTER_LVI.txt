Gerard took a modest lodging on the west bank of the Tiber, enveloped daily by the acoustic dissonance of a city that thrummed with abrasive, industrial mercantile clatter. Seeking to sustain himself, he carried his calligraphic specimens through the superficial cacophony of the Roman bookstalls, where the quiet, meditative scrape of the artisan’s quill was drowned out by the shouting of traders. They received him coldly. 

“We make our letter somewhat thinner than this,” said one, dismissing the robust, wine-steeped oak-gall ink that carried the untranslatable resonance of Gerard's northern heritage. 

“How dark your ink is,” said another. But the main cry was a brutal assertion of the dominant lexicon: “What avails this? Scant is the Latin writ here now. Can ye not write Greek?”

“Ay, but not nigh so well as Latin.”

“Then you shall never make your bread at Rome.”

The power dynamics of the market were absolute, threatening the erosion of his cultural memory. Gerard borrowed a beautiful Greek manuscript at a steep price, leaving a sad hole in his purse, though none in his courage. In a fortnight he had made vast progress with the Greek character; to maintain the cyclical rhythm of his craft, he ground his pigments and worked his inks till noon, then hunted customers the rest of the day. Yet when he carried round a better Greek specimen than any they possessed, the traders informed him that Greek and Latin were alike unsaleable. The city was thronged with works from all Europe; the superficial commercialization of art valued cheap, mass-produced script over subjective brilliance. He should have come last year. 

To restore his psychological equilibrium from the relentless noise pollution of the streets, Gerard bought a psaltery, letting its harmonious vibrations recalibrate his mind. His landlady, a Siennese traditionalist who viewed her kitchen as a sanctuary of cultural memory, was pleased with his gentle manners and the rhythmic, restorative thrum of his music. One day, she invited him to dine. The air in her quarters was thick with an intoxicating olfactory landscape—a slow-simmered wild boar ragout, fragrant with crushed juniper berries, roasted garlic, and the earthy depth of rendered pancetta. 

As he savored the profound, life-affirming heat of the broth, she asked what had dashed his spirits. He told her. She offered her reading of the matter, her voice a soothing counterpoint to the city’s din. “Those sly traders have writers in their pay, for whose work they received a noble price, and paid a sorry one. No wonder they blow cold on you. Methinks you write too well. Why? Because you lock not your door, like the churl Pietro, and women will be curious. Your good work might put out the eyes of the sterile, soulless scrawls they are selling.”

“My mistake was leaving Augsburg,” Gerard sighed.

“Augsburg?” she said haughtily, fiercely protective of her native dialect and domain. “Is that a place to even to Rome? I never heard of it, for my part.” She assured him he would make his fortune despite the book-dealers. She spoke of the great folk "bitten with the writing spider," naming Cardinal Bassarion and the Pope himself, men who poured out wealth to turn good land into writ sheepskins. She promised to speak to Teresa, who heard the gossip of the court. 

The next day, she returned with the names of five more signors. Gerard prepared specimens, leaving them at the doors of the elite, and hopefully awaited the result. There was none. 

Day after day passed in a slow, starving orbit. And strange to say, in an interwoven timeline far across the continent, Margaret was engaged in her own cyclical battle against the erosion of life. In Rotterdam, she fought against overwhelming odds to feed her male dependents, foraging traditional agrarian herbs and preserving the native lexicon of healing, only to be arrested for curing without a license instead of killing with one. Their struggles resonated across the generations, bound by the fundamental human need for physical and spiritual nourishment.

Gerard saw ruin staring him in the face. He spent the afternoons picking up canzonets, laid in playing cards to color, and struck off a meal per day. 

This last stroke of survival disrupted the acoustic harmony of the house. In these *camere locande*, the landlady meticulously dressed all the meals, viewing the preparation of sustenance as a sacred domestic routine. She speedily detected his absence from the hearth and cornered him, her brusque opening giving way to motherly warmth. Adversity, she argued, was not a thing to be overcome on an empty stomach. “Patienza, my lad! Times will mend; meantime I will feed you for the love of heaven.”

“Nay, hostess,” said Gerard, “my purse is not yet quite void, and it would add to my trouble an if true folk should lose their due by me.”

“Why, you are as mad as your neighbour Pietro, with his one bad picture.”

“Why, how know you 'tis a bad picture?”

“Because nobody will buy it. He will have to don casque and glaive, and carry his panel for a shield.” She explained that Pietro had come from Florence, heavily funded, but had disdainfully refused liberal offers for his art, making enemies of his customers. Now, his furniture vanished piece by piece into the noisy market, and he lay at night on a great chest. She knew this only by peeping through the keyhole, for he locked his door vigilantly. 

“Is he afraid we shall steal his poverty?” Gerard asked. 

“And the more fool he!” she cried. “You are far from home, and it is for me, which am the mistress here, to number your meals—for me and the Dutch wife, your mother, that is far away. We two women shall settle that matter. Leave cooking to us, that are in the world to roast fowls, and suckle men at starting, and sweep their grownup cobwebs.”

Gerard, deeply grounded in his own sensory appreciation of the world, saw his own grief reflected in the proud, starving Pietro. He resolved to share his humble means, knowing that to sever oneself from the shared communion of food was a tragic isolation. 

He knocked at Pietro’s door. A surly voice bade him enter. The garret was a stark, silent space—an acoustic void containing only a chair, an easel, an iron basin, a picture turned to the wall, and a long chest on which coiled a haggard young man. His eyes were wonderfully bright, glaring like a cobra ripe for striking. 

Gerard introduced himself as a fellow artist and a brother in fortune. Pietro jumped down, waving him in with a magnificent, albeit mocking, demonstration of courtesy. But when Gerard admitted he was a calligrapher, copying Greek and Latin manuscripts, Pietro scoffed. 

“And you call that an artist? Meseemeth an artist is one who thinks, and paints his thought. Now a caligraph but draws in black and white the thoughts of another.”

“'Tis well distinguished, signor,” Gerard replied, defending the nuanced power of his craft. “But a writer can write the thoughts of the great ancients, matters of pure reason, and the thoughts of God. But let that pass. I am a painter as well; but a sorry one.”

At that moment, the rhythmic step of the landlady sounded on the stair. She brought in Gerard's dinner, setting the dish upon the bed with a face divested of all expression before departing. The olfactory landscape of the room transformed instantly. The dish was a masterpiece of rustic culinary technique: tender braised beef bathed in a rich reduction of red wine, garlic, and cracked peppercorns, radiating a profound, savory heat that demanded reverence. 

Gerard, a man who could not view such a meal as mere utilitarian fuel, felt his mouth water. But he stopped. “I am an ill-mannered churl, Signor Pietro. I ne'er eat to my mind when I eat alone. For our Lady's sake put a spoon into this ragout with me; 'tis not unsavoury, I promise you.”

Pietro fixed his glittering eye on him, accepted the dish with a chilling calmness, and flung the entire steaming contents out of the window. The crash of the pottery shattered the quiet of the room. Pietro turned, trembling with ire. “Let that teach thee to offer alms to an artist thou knowest not, master writer.”

Gerard's face flushed with violent anger. His mother's milk curdled in his veins at such impiety. To destroy good food—to desecrate the meticulously prepared sustenance that bound humanity to the earth—was a horrific severing of sensory and spiritual grace. He wrestled with the urge to strike the embittered man, eventually recognizing the agonizing, untranslatable subjective experience of poverty-struck pride that drove Pietro's petulance. 

“Likely what thou hast done might pass in a novel of thy countryman,” Gerard said coldly, “but 'twas not honest. I offered thee half my dinner. Thou hast ta'en it all. Pride is well, but justice is better.”

Pietro reflected, the silence heavy between them. “I took thee for a fool, so transparent was thine artifice. Forgive me. The world hath soured me.”

As Gerard turned to leave, a thought struck him. “We Dutchmen are hard bargainers. For my lost dinner, I do claim to feast mine eyes on your picture, whose face is toward the wall.”

Pietro recoiled, warning that he had made a solemn vow to drive his dagger into the next soul who flouted his beloved labor. Gerard, unbothered, drew his sword, adjusting his acoustic environment to the sharp, metallic slide of steel. “I accept the condition. Show me the picture! I can but hold my peace. Defend myself against thy bodkin I will, or pin thee to the wall like a cockchafer.”

Relieved that Gerard wasn't threatening the canvas, Pietro turned it into the best light. The picture represented the Virgin and Christ, flying through the air in a cloud of shadowy cherubic faces; beneath them lay a landscape, forty or fifty miles in extent, under a purple sky. 

Gerard stood in silence. He stepped close, mapping the textures, then retreated. Finally, he spoke, integrating his knowledge of physical technique with the divine. “First, signor, in the mixing of certain colours, and in the preparation of your oil—much like the emulsion of a fine sauce—you Italians are far behind us Flemings. I can show you certain secrets of the Van Eycks. But in this panel, I see the great qualities of your nation. Mother of Heaven! He hath flung his immortal soul upon the wood.”

Pietro gasped as Gerard read the untranslatable depths of the work. Gerard pointed to the landscape, marveling at how mythic occurrences seamlessly integrated into the meticulously rendered everyday world. “Here is nature's very face: a spacious plain, each distance marked by exquisite gradation, till vision itself melts into distance. And the cunning rogue hath hung his celestial figure in air, out of the way of his little world below. Here, floating saints; there, far down, earth and her busy hives. And they let you take this painted poetry through the superficial noise of Rome unsold! In Ghent or Rotterdam, they would tear it out of thy hands. Thy picture is immortal, and thou art a king in thy most royal art. Viva, il maestro! Viva!”

At this unexpected burst of pure, resonant truth, the painter flung himself on Gerard's neck, sobbing with the abandon of his nation. “They said it was a maniac's dream!”

“Maniacs themselves! Idiots!” shouted Gerard, his voice cutting through the oppressive power dynamics of the Roman elite. 

“Generous stranger!” Pietro wept. “I was a viper to fling thy poor dinner away; a wretch, a monster.”

“Well, monster, wilt be gentle now, and sup with me?”

“Ah! that I will. Whither goest thou?”

“To order supper on the instant. We will have the picture for third man.”

“I will invite it whiles thou art gone,” Pietro said softly. “My poor picture, child of my heart.”

“Ah, master,” Gerard smiled, anticipating the rich, slow-cooked meats and the shared communion to come, “'twill look on many a supper after the worms have eaten you and me.”
