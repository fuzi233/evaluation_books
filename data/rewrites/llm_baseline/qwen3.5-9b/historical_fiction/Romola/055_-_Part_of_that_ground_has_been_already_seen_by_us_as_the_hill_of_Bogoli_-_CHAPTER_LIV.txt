A singular purpose drove Romola forward, a resolve that had coalesced throughout the afternoon like a tributary swelling into the main stream of her existence. It felt less like a choice than an imperative of her soul. Ignoring the encroaching twilight and refusing Maso's offer of his cautious escort, she crossed the bridge where the river churned black beneath the fading crimson of the sunset. Taking the shortest path, she rushed toward the Old Palace. Perhaps she would find her husband there; it mattered not. Probability offered no comfort; she needed only to release the weight of her heart.

She moved blindly through the pillared courtyard and up the broad staircase, unaware of the frescoes lining the walls or the flicker of newly lit candles until the door swung open. Bernardo del Nero entered, his white hair held high above his silk *lucco*.

"Romola, my child, what is this?" he exclaimed, his voice tight with anxious surprise as he shut the door behind them.

She had removed her veil and stepped toward him in silence. He placed a hand on her shoulder, pulling her slightly closer to examine her features. Her face was gaunt, carved by exhaustion and prolonged turmoil; her hair hung loose and disordered. Yet, her eyes burned with a fierce excitement that seemed to have conquered her physical weariness.

"What has he done?" Bernardo demanded abruptly. "Speak, child. Tell me everything. Cast aside your pride; I am your father."

"It concerns not myself—not at all," Romola replied quickly. "Dearest godfather, it concerns you. I have heard whispers—some I dare not speak aloud. But you are in peril within these walls, in peril everywhere. Fanatics seek to harm you, and there are traitors among you. Trust no one. To trust is to invite betrayal."

Bernardo smiled faintly. "Have you driven yourself to such a feverish state, my poor child, to repeat such ancient truths to an old man like me?"

"Oh, no! These are not old truths," she insisted, clutching her hands together as if to stifle the confession trapped within them. "These are fresh revelations I possess but cannot fully articulate. Dearest godfather, you know I am not foolish. Why should I come to you without cause? Is it too late to warn you against every shadow that pretends to stand by your side? Is it too late to urge you to flee to your villa, to hide in the countryside once these final three days of office end? Oh, God! Perhaps it is too late! And if harm befalls you, it will feel as though I have caused it!"

The final words escaped her involuntarily, a sudden rupture of a long-suppressed dread. Startled by her own vehemence, she faltered.

"I mean," she added, her voice trembling, "that I know nothing with certainty. Only fear fills me."

"Poor child!" Bernardo said, regarding her with a gaze of quiet depth for a moment. Then he spoke softly, "Go, Romola. Return home and rest. Your fears may merely be monstrous shadows cast by something trivial and harmless. Even traitors require motive; the rats scurry only where they smell the cheese, and we do not yet know which direction the wind blows."

He paused, turning his eyes away with an air of abstract resignation, then shrugged slowly. "Warnings avail me nothing, child. I enter no conspiracies, nor do I ever abandon my colors. If I march alongside stubborn men rushing to meet guns and pikes, I must share their fate. Let us say no more. I have few years left in my sack for them to steal. Go, child; return home and rest."

He touched her head again, affectionately. She could not resist leaning into his arm, pressing her forehead against his shoulder. This caress felt like the final remnant of that youthful filial bond, which now appeared so radiant to her even amidst its sorrows, for those sorrows remained pure, untouched by malice.

"Is silence best, my Romola?" the old man asked.

"Yes, for now; but I cannot promise it will remain so," she answered hesitantly, lifting her head with a pleading expression.

"Well, while I draw breath, you have a father's ear and a father's home," he declared, lifting the heavy black drapery to wrap around her head. "Remember that." Opening the door, he urged, "Hasten away. You look like a specter; you will find safety enough outside."

That night, when sleep finally claimed Romola, it was deep and profound. Her agitation had reached its zenith; she required strength before she could endure further suffering. Against her usual rigidity, she slept well past sunrise.

When dawn broke, it shattered with the roar of cannon fire. From Maso, accompanied by numerous dubious details, Romola learned that Piero de' Medici stood before the gate facing Rome with thirteen hundred men. A countryman had alerted the Signoria just before daylight; otherwise, the city would have fallen unawares. The master of the informant was absent, having been summoned to the Palazzo long ago.

Romola dispatched the old servant to gather intelligence while she ascended to the loggia, scanning the horizon for signs of the dreaded entry or evidence of a successful defense. Maso returned with grim tidings: the great Piazza teemed with armed men, and many prominent citizens suspected of sympathizing with the Medici had been summoned and detained. Some of the populace seemed indifferent to Piero's arrival, even suggesting the Signoria itself had invited him; yet, regardless of intent, the soldiers from Pisa were arrayed against him.

In the chaotic memory of those morning hours, few external events stood out clearly above the surging tide of retrospection and anticipation. She recalled walking to the Badia precisely as scheduled, despite the street alarms, and waiting there in vain. The scene that haunted her most vividly occurred as she emerged from the church, standing on the steps while the doors were barred for the afternoon recess.

There was a palpable shift in the expressions and tones of both armed and unarmed passersby. The cannons fired again, yet this time the sound elicited only laughter. She soon understood the cause. Piero de' Medici and his cavalry had turned their backs on Florence, galloping furiously down the road to Siena.

She learned this from a sturdy shopkeeper-Piagnone who had not yet lowered his pike.

"It is true," he concluded, his voice tinged with bitter emphasis. "Piero is gone, but those who conspired with his arrival remain here—we all know that. If the new Signoria fulfills its duty, we shall soon learn who they are."

The words struck Romola like a sharp spasm. Yet, the evil they foretold was not yet immediate. As she returned to her home, her most urgent anxiety shifted to the terrifying possibility that she had lost sight of Baldassarre for a long time.
