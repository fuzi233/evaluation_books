Matrons who have tended households through fevers, frosts, and half a dozen labours of their own never screech at the sight of a fainting woman, nor leap to blame flighty whimsy for the fall. They know the body fails far faster than the mind, far more often, than any giddy fit of pique. So Catherine moved with brisk, unhurried purpose the second the young girl crumpled: she laid her flat on the cold earthen floor, no pillow under her head to choke her breath, threw the casement wide to let sharp autumn air pour in, and fumbled open the tight laces of her bodice before she even called for aid. Only when every sensible, proven remedy was applied did she step to the doorway, and call out loud enough for neighbours half a street away to catch the tone: “Come in here, if you please.”

Margaret Van Eyck and Reicht hurried through, to find the girl still laid flat, Catherine patting her wrists in steady, rhythmic little taps to rouse her. “Oh my poor girl! What harm have you done her?” the old painter cried, white with distress.
“Me?” Catherine glared, her hands never pausing. “Madam, I have done nothing but tend her. No harm befell her but what is natural to the state she is in.”

Margaret Van Eyck flushed bright with ire. “You speak very cool of it, when you are the very cause of that state! You and your husband schemed to tear the two apart, and now Gerard has run off all alone to Italy. You have broken this poor child’s heart between you.”
“That I have not,” Catherine answered blunt as a blacksmith’s hammer. “Nor any woman alive. Wait—you cannot mean this lass is my Gerard’s? That can’t be. The fur trim on her kirtle alone cost no less than five crowns an ell, and besides, this young gentlewoman is a wife, or by every right under God’s sky ought to be one.”
“Ought to be!” Margaret Van Eyck snapped. “And who is the reason she is not? Who stepped between them at the very altar to halt their vows?”
“God forgive whoever did that,” Catherine said gravely, “but it was not me, nor my man. Not a chance.”

The older woman softened a little, her anger seeping out of her shoulders. “Well, you have seen her laid low here now. Perhaps you will not bear her that bitter grudge any longer. Thank Heaven, she is fluttering back to her senses.”
“Me bear her a grudge?” Catherine blinked, bewildered. “That is long over and done with. Poor soul, she has trouble behind her, and worse still waiting ahead of her, and to think I made her, of all women in the world, read Gerard’s letter out loud to me! That’s what knocked her clean out, I’ll wager a silver penny on it. There now, sweetheart, don’t you be afraid. None but friends are here.”

They lifted her into a cushioned oak easy chair, the colour creeping slow and faint back to her pale lips. Catherine tugged Margaret Van Eyck aside by the sleeve. “She is not planning to trudge back to Sevenbergen tonight, is she?”
“She swears she will go, no matter what any of us say.”
“Then you will not let her. You are older and wiser than her, you can overrule her. Look, she is starting to notice our talk now.”

Catherine leaned in then, her mouth pressed so close to the old painter’s ear their warm breath mingled. It was barely a heartbeat, too little time to whisper a full word, let alone a whole sentence. But women who have shared the unspoken secrets of childbed, household illness, and quiet hardship speak that lightning-fast, wordless language of shared womanly knowledge in a single flash. Margaret Van Eyck reeled back, eyes wide and horrified, and whispered back:
“It is false! A calumny! Monstrous! Look at her face. It is blasphemy to lay such an accusation at the door of a girl with that look in her eyes.”
“Tut, tut, tut!” Catherine huffed. “You might as well argue this is not my own hand. I have borne seven children, I know what I see, and I tell you it is so.”

To Margaret Van Eyck’s utter shock, Catherine stepped right over to the girl, wrapped both arms around her, and kissed her warm and fierce on the forehead. “I grieved for Gerard these past months, and I hear you shed your own blood for him too. The very words you read out of my boy’s letter showed me how much wrong I did you. I held aloof from you all this time, but no more. From this hour, you are my own daughter.” Another tight, crushing embrace sealed that impulsive promise, and the warm, hot-tempered matron was gone off to rummage in her larder for spiced wine to heat for her new child.

Margaret sank back in the chair, a wobbly, faint smile stealing over her face. Gerard’s own mother had kissed her, and called her daughter. But the next moment she saw her old friend staring at her, her face tight with vexation.
“I cannot believe you let that woman kiss you.”
“His mother,” Margaret murmured, half reproachful.
“Mother or no mother, you would never have let her lay a finger on you if you heard the vile lie she whispered in my ear about you—you, whom she had never even laid eyes on before today.”

The old lady was just gathering her words, hesitant and careful, to make Margaret share her indignation, when an unlooked-for interruption closed her lips. The young woman slid from her chair straight to her knees, and began to beg piteously for pardon, clinging to the hem of her old friend’s gown. From the ragged, broken words of her penitence, any onlooker would have sworn she had inflicted some cruel, unforgivable insult on the venerable woman who had cared for her all her life.
