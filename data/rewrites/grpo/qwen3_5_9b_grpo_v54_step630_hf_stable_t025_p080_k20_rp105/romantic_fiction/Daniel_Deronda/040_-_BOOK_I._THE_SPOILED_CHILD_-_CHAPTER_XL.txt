"Within the soul a faculty abides,
That with interpositions, which would hide
And darken, so can deal, that they become
Contingencies of pomp; and serve to exalt
Her native brightness, as the ample moon,
In the deep stillness of a summer even,
Rising behind a thick and lofty grove,
Burns, like an unconsuming fire of light,
In the green trees; and, kindling on all sides
Their leafy umbrage, turns the dusky veil
Into a substance glorious as her own,
Yea, with her own incorporated, by power
Capacious and serene."
—WORDSWORTH: *Excursion*, B. IV.

Daniel Deronda stepped out of the narrow Chelsea house with a restless spirit, craving physical exertion to burn off the heat rising in his chest. He intended to walk toward the city, but the sight of the Chelsea Stairs and the waiting boats instantly resolved his plan. He would avoid the passive confinement of a carriage; instead, he hailed a wherry and took an oar himself.

His destination was Ram's bookshop. He had arrived there the previous day too late for Mordecai's midday vigil and had been informed that the man invariably returned between five and six. Deronda felt a pressing need to deepen his acquaintance with this enigmatic inhabitant of the Cohen household. It was a prerequisite for redeeming his ring; he feared that their conversations would once again end abruptly, leaving him stranded like a recruit dismissed before the battle began. As he warmed to the rhythm of the oar, fixing his mind on the errand and his desire to aid Mirah, a shift occurred within him. He mentally stepped out of his own shoes and viewed the situation through Mordecai's eyes. He chided himself for resembling a recruiting sergeant who cares only for the numbers he gathers, ignoring the human drama that brings them.

"I suppose if I secure the information I seek," Deronda mused, "I shall be content if he offers nothing more of himself, or explains why he seems to expect something from me that has gone unfulfilled. My curiosity might fade, yet perhaps we have passed each other like ships in the night, each carrying an exile who would have recognized the other had they looked face to face. There is little chance of a special bond between us, for his voyage, I suspect, is nearly over. Still, I wonder if there is a profound mutual absence between strangers who exchange blank glances, or even those who long for one another's departure in a crowded room. Yet, by adopting the recruiting sergeant's strategy, one creates opportunities for such meetings."

As the wherry approached Blackfriars Bridge, where Deronda planned to land, the clock struck half-past four. The gray afternoon was dying gloriously; the western clouds had shattered into narrowing purple strata before a vast, saffron-clear sky that held a monumental calm. On the river, however, this light danced as a luminous movement—the flashing ripples, the sudden glow of a brown sail, the transition of laden barges from shadow to color—creating an active response to the brooding glory above.

Feeling sufficiently heated, Deronda set down the oar and pulled his Inverness cape around him. As he fastened the top button, his gaze lifted and caught a familiar face framed against the parapet of the bridge. The western light rendered it startlingly distinct, an illuminated emblem of bodily emaciation and spiritual urgency. It was Mordecai. The invalid had also spotted the approaching boat, holding his gaze fixed upon it. First, he watched simply because it was moving; then, a shiver of presentiment ran through him until the nearing figure raised its face toward him—the face of his visions. Immediately, Mordecai raised a white, trembling hand and beckoned repeatedly.

Deronda, eager for recognition, had signaled without delay, and the answer came instantly. Mordecai lifted his cap and waved it, feeling in that instant that his inner prophecy was fulfilled. Obstacles, contradictions, all dissolved into a sense of completion flooding his soul. His exultation mirrored that of an experimenter witnessing the first stirrings of a change he had foreseen in concentrated vision. The prefigured friend emerged from the golden background and signaled; this was reality; the rest was merely to be lived.

In three minutes, Deronda had landed, paid the boatman, and joined Mordecai, who stood perfectly still, waiting.

"I was very glad to see you standing here," Deronda said. "I intended to go to the bookshop and look for you again. I was there yesterday—perhaps they mentioned it?"

"Yes," Mordecai replied with simple gravity. "That was the reason I came to the bridge."

This answer struck Deronda as mysteriously cryptic. Were the peculiarities of this man truly linked to some form of mental alienation, as Cohen had hinted?

"You knew nothing of my being at Chelsea?" Deronda asked after a pause.

"No; but I expected you to come down the river. I have been waiting for you these five years." Mordecai's deep-sunk eyes were fixed on Deronda's with a look of affectionate dependence, both pathetic and solemn. Deronda's sensitivity responded fully, even though he could not help believing this strangely disclosed relationship was founded on an illusion.

"It will be a satisfaction to me if I can be of any real use to you," Deronda answered earnestly. "Shall we take a cab and drive wherever you wish? You have probably had enough walking with your short breath."

"Let us go to the bookshop. It will soon be time for me to be there. But now look up the river," Mordecai said, turning back toward the water. He spoke in undertones of an excited calm, so absorbed by a sense of fulfillment that he felt no barrier to a complete understanding with Deronda. "See the sky, how it is slowly fading. I have always loved this bridge; I stood on it when I was a little boy. It is a meeting-place for spiritual messengers. It is true what the Masters said—that each order of things has its angel: that means the full message of each from afar. Here I have listened to the messages of earth and sky. When I was stronger, I used to stay and watch for the stars in the deep heavens. But this time, just about sunset, was what I loved best. It sank into me and dwelt with me—fading, slowly fading: it was my own decline. It paused, it waited, till at last it brought me my new life—my new self, who will live when this breath is all breathed out."

Deronda remained silent, strangely affected. The first suspicion that Mordecai might be hallucinating, a monomaniac strained by a diseased organism, gave way to a more submissive expectancy. His nature was too broad, too ready to conceive regions beyond his own experience, to settle immediately on the easy explanation of "madness" whenever a consciousness showed fullness and conviction where his own was blank. It suited his disposition to meet rather than resist claims arising from another's need. This claim brought a sense of solemnity that seemed to radiate from Mordecai, nullifying his outward poverty and lifting him into authority, as if he were that preternatural guide from universal legend who suddenly drops his mean disguise to stand as a manifest Power. This impression was sanctioned by a resolved quietude in Mordecai's manner. After a moment of silence, he said, "Let us go now," and when they were in the cab, added, "We will get down at the end of the street and walk to the shop. You can look at the books, and Mr. Ram will be going away directly and leave us alone."

It seemed this enthusiast was just as cautious, just as alive to the judgments of others, as if he were the antipode of all enthusiasm, a "man of the world."

While they rattled along in the cab, Mirah was still present in Deronda's mind amidst this strange experience, but he foresaw that the conversation would be dictated by Mordecai, not himself. He was no longer confident of the questions he could ask. Reacting to his own mood, he thought inwardly, "I suppose I am in a state of complete superstition, awaiting a destiny that could interpret an oracle. Yet, some strong relation must exist between me and this man, since he feels it so strongly. Great heaven! What relation has proved more potent in the world than faith even when mistaken—than expectation even when perpetually disappointed? Is my side of the relation to be disappointing or fulfilling? Well, if it is ever possible for me to fulfill, I will not disappoint."

In ten minutes, the two men, with an intensity as if they were undeclared lovers, found themselves alone in the small, gas-lit bookshop. They turned face to face, each bare-headed from an instinctive desire to see the other fully. Mordecai leaned his back against the little counter, while Deronda stood against the opposite wall, hardly four feet away. One wishes one could perpetuate those two faces as Titian's "Tribute Money" has perpetuated two contrasting types. Imagine—the pathetic stamp of consumption, with its brilliance of glance, set upon a sharply defined structure of features that reminds one of a forsaken temple, giving a far-off look of one unwillingly withdrawing. Imagine it on a Jewish face, naturally accentuated for the expression of an eager mind—the face of a man little above thirty, yet bearing the age of time lengthened by suffering. His black hair and beard throw out the yellow pallor of his skin; his difficult breathing marks the mobile nostril; his wasted yellow hands are conspicuous on his folded arms. Give to this yearning consumptive glance the look of a slowly dying mother when her one loved son visits her bedside, and the flickering power of gladness leaps out as she says, "My boy!"—for the sense of spiritual perpetuation in another resembles that maternal transference of self. Seeing such a portrait, you would see Mordecai.

Opposite to him stood a face not more distinctly Oriental than many types seen among the Latin races. It was rich in youthful health, possessing a forcible masculine gravity in its repose that gave value to the judgment with which he met the gaze of this mysterious son of poverty who claimed him as a long-expected friend. The exquisite quality of Deronda's nature—that keenly perceptive sympathetic emotiveness running alongside his speculative tendency—was never more thoroughly tested. He felt nothing that could be called belief in the validity of Mordecai's impressions concerning him or in the probability of any greatly effective issue. What he felt was a profound sensibility to a cry from the depths of another, accompanied by a summons to be receptive rather than superciliously prejudging. Receptiveness is a rare and massive power, like fortitude; and this state of mind gave Deronda's face its utmost expression of calm, benignant force—an expression that nourished Mordecai's confidence and made an open way before him.

"You cannot know what has guided me to you and brought us together at this moment," Mordecai began. "You are wondering."

"I am not impatient," Deronda said. "I am ready to listen to whatever you may wish to disclose."

"You see some of the reasons why I needed you," Mordecai said quietly, as if reserving his strength. "You see that I am dying. You see that I am as one shut up behind bars by the wayside, who if he spoke to any would be met only by head-shaking and pity. The day is closing—the light is fading—soon we should not have been able to discern each other. But you have come in time."

"I rejoice that I am come in time," Deronda said feelingly. He would not say, "I hope you are not mistaken in me"; the very word "mistaken," he thought, would be a cruelty at that moment.

"But the hidden reasons why I need you began afar off," Mordecai continued. "They began in my early years when I was studying in another land. Then ideas, beloved ideas, came to me, because I was a Jew. They were a trust to fulfill, because I was a Jew. They were an inspiration, because I was a Jew, and felt the heart of my race beating within me. They were my life; I was not fully born till then. I counted this heart, and this breath, and this right hand"—Mordecai pathetically pressed his hand upon his breast, then stretched its wasted fingers out before him—"I counted my sleep and my waking, and the work I fed my body with, and the sights that fed my eyes—I counted them but as fuel to the divine flame. But I had done as one who wanders and engraves his thought in rocky solitudes, and before I could change my course came care and labor and disease, and blocked the way before me, and bound me with the iron that eats itself into the soul. Then I said, 'How shall I save the life within me from being stifled with this stifled breath?'"

Mordecai paused to rest that poor breath, taxed by the rising excitement of his speech. He also wished to check that excitement. Deronda dared not speak; the silence in the narrow space seemed alive with mingled awe and compassion before this struggling fervor. Presently, Mordecai went on:

"But you may misunderstand me. I speak not as an ignorant dreamer—as one bred up in the inland valleys, thinking ancient thoughts anew, and not knowing them ancient, never having stood by the great waters where the world's knowledge passes to and fro. English is my mother-tongue; England is the native land of this body, which is but as a breaking pot of earth around the fruit-bearing tree, whose seed might make the desert rejoice. But my true life was nourished in Holland at the feet of my mother's brother, a Rabbi skilled in special learning: and when he died I went to Hamburg to study, and afterwards to Göttingen, that I might take a larger outlook on my people, and on the Gentile world, and drank knowledge at all sources. I was a youth; I felt free; I saw our chief seats in Germany; I was not then in utter poverty. And I had possessed myself of a handicraft.
