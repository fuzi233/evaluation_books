The Piazza della Signoria, usually a stage for civic pride, transformed into a theater of suspense on the seventh of April. A crowd, denser than any gathered for the Feast of San Giovanni, pressed against every available inch of the square. Men hung precariously from iron rods jutting from walls, wedged themselves between rough stone pillars, and balanced atop the necks of statues, their limbs cramped and skin chafed, yet utterly unmindful of discomfort. They had come to witness a miracle. While humanity often hears of wonders and accepts them with varying degrees of skepticism, the Florentines were about to see one unfold before their eyes. At the very least, they would witness the descent; if the monk emerged unscathed, it would be a divine sign; if he perished, the verdict would be equally clear.

Before them stood the long platform, eight feet wide and stretching twenty yards across the marble terrace near the Old Palace, flanked by the frowning gaze of the stone lion, the Marzocco. Upon it lay a terrifying grove of fuel: dry oak branches, crackling thorns, and tow soaked in oil, prepared to roar into a consuming flame. To ensure the spectacle's gravity, the Signoria had deployed five hundred hired soldiers before the palace, while five hundred Compagnacci under Dolfo Spini waited on the opposite side. Between them, three hundred armed citizens loyal to Savonarola, led by Marco Salviati, stood guard near Orgagna's Loggia, where the Franciscans and Dominicans were to face each other.

The air buzzed with anticipation as the Franciscans took their silent, grey-robed places on one side of the loggia. From the other side, a deafening chant rose from two hundred voices: "Let God arise, and let His enemies be scattered." The leader, Fra Domenico, wore a cope of flame-colored velvet, his simple mind exalted by faith and his genuine intent to walk into the fire for the glory of God and his rival, Fra Girolamo. Behind him walked Savonarola in white vestments, carrying the consecrated Host. He, too, projected an aura of unshakeable confidence, his face flashing with energy under the scrutiny of thousands.

Yet, beneath this public performance, a profound internal struggle tore at Savonarola. He was not merely acting the part of a prophet facing martyrdom; he was performing a desperate charade to avoid impending ruin. The weight of foreboding pressed upon his heart, heavier than the leaden sky above. Two evenings prior, the courier of the Ten had been arrested, his dispatches stolen. Savonarola's private letters were now in the hands of the Duke of Milan and soon the Pope, providing the very justification for the extreme measures currently being plotted against him.

Watching this drama from a window of the Old Palace was Tito Melema. His satisfaction was not born of malice, but of the mild self-gratification of a gambler who had finally won. To Tito, the complex web of human nature, molded into the figure of a plain-faced friar, was merely a puzzle solved. Fra Girolamo was an immensely clever man, capable of keeping secrets and mixing absurd superstition with shrewd political notions, but he was also a tool Tito had used to climb the ladder. Now, with the trap sprung, Tito intended to leave Florence entirely. The "Trial by Fire" was merely the final act of a comedy written by Tito, one that the thick-headed Dolfo Spini could never have orchestrated alone.

As the chanting swelled into a roar, joined by the Piagnoni waving their red crosses, the tension in the square reached a fever pitch. The Franciscans, however, remained silent, their grey robes a stark contrast to the fiery colors of their opponents. They had two champions: Fra Giuliano paired with Fra Domenico, while the original challenger, Fra Francesco, reserved his dispute solely for Savonarola.

The crowd grew restless. "All must be ready now," they thought, watching the flame-colored cope. But the Dominicans did not move. Instead, figures passed to and fro between the loggia and the palace. Dignitaries debated with the Franciscans, and eventually, Fra Domenico was summoned inside. The crowd assumed the fire had been lit and the miracle was imminent. But the cope vanished, only to return moments later with another Dominican, guarded by Franciscans lest he be "enchanted."

The realization dawned slowly: Fra Domenico was not entering the fire. The focus shifted to Fra Girolamo. He stood firm, delivering a bold, ironic speech demanding the cessation of delays, reminiscent of Elijah challenging the priests of Baal. But words were useless to a crowd hungry for action and trapped in uncomfortable positions. If the miracle did not begin, the blame would fall squarely on Fra Girolamo for failing to offer himself when the fuel was ready.

Time slipped away as clouds gathered, darkening the light and sending a chill through the hungry, impatient multitude. Suddenly, the dynamic shifted. Fra Domenico insisted on carrying a crucifix into the fire, an object the Franciscans deemed too sacred to profane. Reluctantly yielding, Savonarola handed him the vessel containing the Host instead. A theological debate erupted instantly. Was it impious to burn the Sacrament? Could the "Accidents" of the Eucharist survive the flames while the "Substance" remained? The question was elastic, impossible to settle by fire, yet both sides dug in their heels.

The sky darkened further, and the air grew colder. The chanting faded into confused murmurs and rising shouts of scorn. "Light the fire!" "Drive them in!" "What is your last vision?" The crowd's patience evaporated, replaced by a chaotic uproar of clashing metal and trampling feet. Dolfo Spini's Compagnacci surged forward, eager to seize the "arch-hypocrite," pushing the civilians aside and threatening the loggia.

But the guard under Marco Salviati held firm, repulsing the rush. In that moment of utter confusion, the heavens opened. Rain began to fall, initially in scattered drops, then with violent force. It soaked the dry fuel on the platform, turning the potential inferno into a damp mess. Streams of water ran off the stones, drenching the weary, hungry crowd. The rain washed away the dust of the square and extinguished the possibility of the trial before it could truly begin.

The Signoria, relieved to have an excuse to disperse the dangerous gathering, declared the event over. Savonarola stepped away from the loggia, the rain plastering his white robes to his frame. He knew the design of his enemies was fulfilled; his honor was not saved. He would have to face the enraged mob again, knowing that friends who once defended him with their lives would now turn against him.

He asked for a guard from the Signoria, which was granted. He had been willing to die for his cause, but to die in dishonor, branded a false prophet? "O God! That is not martyrdom," he thought, his courage mounting despite the moral assault. "It is the blotting out of a life that has been a protest against wrong. Let me die because of the worth that is in me, not because of my weakness."

As the rain ceased and the breaking clouds cast a pale, impartial light upon him, Savonarola walked away with the Sacrament in his hand. There was no glory in the light, no smile from heaven. Only the cold, steady illumination that justifies or condemns by simply revealing the truth of things. He heard no blessings, only taunts and threats. He knew this was a foretaste of the bitterness to come, yet his spirit remained unbroken.

"Well parried, Frate!" Tito Melema called out as Savonarola descended the steps. "But I fear your career at Florence is ended. What say you, my Niccolò?"

"It is a pity his falsehoods were not all of a wise sort," Machiavelli replied, shaking his head with a melancholy shrug. "With the times so much on his side as they are about Church affairs, he might have done something great."
