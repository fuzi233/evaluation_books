Colonel Henry Esmond, a veteran who had borne the rank of Colonel through the wars of Queen Anne's reign, found himself, at the close of that turbulent era, entangled in plots to restore the Stuart line to the English throne. Fortunately for the stability of the realm, the nation chose a different dynasty; yet, among the few who opposed the House of Hanover, Colonel Esmond was numbered. Advised by friends to seek safety abroad, he initially felt a deep regret for his political involvement. However, recognizing the pacific nature of the new Prince Regnant, his allies swiftly secured his pardon.

It is recorded that Colonel Esmond hailed from the noble English line of Castlewood in Hants. It was widely whispered that King James II and his son had once offered him and his father the title of Marquis, and that the Irish peerage held by his family might have been assumed had he not chosen to overlook a certain informality. Wearied by the ceaseless political strife and annoyed by family troubles in Europe, he elected to settle in Virginia, where he took possession of a vast estate originally granted by King Charles I to his ancestor. There, his daughter and grandsons were born, and his wife passed away. This lady, upon her marriage, was the widow of the Colonel's kinsman—the unfortunate Viscount Castlewood, who had fallen in a duel with Lord Mohun at the end of King William's reign.

Colonel Esmond named his American residence Castlewood, mirroring the ancestral home across the ocean. Indeed, the entire fabric of Virginia life was lovingly modeled upon English customs. It was a fiercely loyal colony, boasting that King Charles II had ruled in Virginia before he did in England. The English monarch and the Church were honored with equal devotion. The resident gentry, allied with distinguished English families, held their heads high above the Dutch merchants of New York and the mercantile Roundheads of Pennsylvania and New England. Fewer people were truly republican than those of this great province, which was destined to lead the memorable revolt against the British Crown.

The Virginia gentry lived upon their great estates in a manner almost patriarchal. Each property employed a multitude of hands—purchased and assigned servants—who were subject to the master's command. The land provided their food, livestock, and game; the great rivers teemed with fish. From their banks, the journey home was clear. Ships loaded tobacco from private wharves on the Potomac or James River, carrying it to London or Bristol in exchange for English goods and manufactured articles, the sole produce the Virginian gentry deemed worth cultivating. Their hospitality was boundless; no stranger was ever turned away. The gentry received one another and traveled between homes in a state nearly feudal.

The question of slavery was not yet fully formed in the minds of the people at this time. To own black servants did not shock the sensibilities of any Virginian gentleman, nor was the despotism exercised over the negro race generally savage. Food was plentiful, and the poor black people were lazy and not unhappy. One might preach emancipation to Madam Esmond of Castlewood as easily as telling her to let her horses run loose; she possessed no doubt that the whip and corn-bag were beneficial for both.

Her father may have harbored different thoughts, possessing a skeptical turn of mind on many points, but his doubts never broke forth into active denial. Rather, he appeared disaffected than rebellious. Once, he had participated in active life at home and might have been eager to share its rewards, but in later years, he seemed indifferent to them. Something had occurred in his life that cast a melancholy tinge over his entire existence. He was not unhappy—he was kind to those around him, affectionate, and even obsequious to the women of his family, whom he rarely contradicted—but he suffered from a bankruptcy of the heart that his spirit never recovered. He submitted to life rather than enjoying it, and he was never in better spirits than in his final hours as he prepared to lay it down.

Having lost his wife, his daughter assumed the management of the Colonel and his affairs. He handed them over to her charge with entire acquiescence. Provided he had his books and his quiet, he cared for nothing else. When company arrived at Castlewood, he entertained them handsomely, displaying a pleasant, sarcastic turn of wit. He was not in the least sorry when they departed.

"My love, I shall not be sorry to go myself," he told his daughter. "And you, though the most affectionate of daughters, will console yourself after a while. Why should I, who am so old, be romantic? You may, who are still a young creature." He spoke these words without meaning them entirely, for the lady he addressed was a matter-of-fact little person with very little romance in her nature.

After fifteen years of residence upon his great Virginian estate, affairs prospered so well with the worthy proprietor that he acquiesced in his daughter's plans to build a mansion much grander and more durable than the plain wooden edifice in which he had been content to live, ensuring his heirs had a habitation worthy of their noble name. Several of Madam Warrington's neighbors had built handsome houses for themselves; perhaps it was her ambition to take rank in the country that inspired this desire for improved quarters. Colonel Esmond, of Castlewood, cared neither for quarters nor quarterings. But his daughter held a very high opinion of the merit and antiquity of her lineage. Her sire, growing exquisitely calm and good-natured in his serene, declining years, humored his child's peculiarities in an easy, bantering way, nay, even assisted her with his antiquarian learning, which was considerable, and with his skill in painting, in which he was proficient.

A knowledge of heraldry, a hundred years prior, formed part of the education of most noble ladies and gentlemen. During her visit to Europe, Miss Esmond had eagerly studied family histories and pedigrees, returning to Virginia with a store of documents regarding her family, which she relied upon with implicit gravity. Alongside these were the most edifying volumes then published in France and England concerning the noble science. These works satisfied her perfectly that the Esmonds were descended not only from noble Norman warriors who came to England with their victorious chief but also from native English of royal dignity. Two magnificent heraldic trees, cunningly painted by the Colonel's hand, represented the family springing from the Emperor Charlemagne on one side—drawn in plate-armour with his imperial mantle and diadem—and from Queen Boadicea on the other, whom the Colonel insisted upon painting in the light costume of an ancient British queen, wearing a prodigious gilded crown, a trifling mantle of furs, and a lovely symmetrical person tastefully tattooed with figures of a brilliant blue tint. From these two illustrious stocks, the family tree rose until it united in the thirteenth century in the person of the fortunate Esmond who claimed descent from both.

Of the Warrington family into which she married, good Madam Rachel thought but little. She wrote herself Esmond Warrington but was universally called Madam Esmond of Castlewood after her father's death, when she came to rule over that domain. It is even to be feared that quarrels for precedence in colonial society occasionally disturbed her temper. Though her father had possessed a marquis's patent from King James, which he had burned and disowned, she frequently acted as if that document existed and was in full force. She considered the English Esmonds of inferior dignity to her own branch, and regarding the colonial aristocracy, she made no scruple of asserting her superiority over the whole body of them. Hence arose quarrels, angry words, and even a scuffle or two, as we gather from her notes, at the Governor's assemblies in Jamestown. Why recall the memory of these squabbles? Are not the persons who engaged in them beyond the reach of quarrels now, and has not the Republic put an end to these social inequalities? Before the establishment of Independence, there was no more aristocratic country in the world than Virginia; so the Virginians, whose history we have to narrate, were bred to have the fullest respect for the institutions of home, and the rightful king had not two more faithful little subjects than the young twins of Castlewood.

When the boys' grandfather died, their mother, in great state, proclaimed her eldest son, George, her successor and heir to the estate. Harry, George's younger brother by half an hour, was always enjoined to respect his senior. The whole household was equally instructed to pay him honor: the negroes, of whom there was a large and happy family, and the assigned servants from Europe, whose lot was made as bearable as it might be under the government of the lady of Castlewood. In the whole family, there was scarcely a rebel save Mrs. Esmond's faithful friend and companion, Madam Mountain, and Harry's foster-mother, a faithful negro woman who could never understand why her child should not be first, who was handsomer, stronger, and cleverer than his brother, as she vowed. Though in truth there was scarcely any difference in beauty, strength, or stature between the twins, in disposition they were exceedingly unlike. Yet in feature, they resembled each other so closely that but for the color of their hair, it would have been difficult to distinguish them. In their beds, and when their heads were covered with those vast ribboned nightcaps worn by our great and little ancestors, it was scarcely possible for anyone but a nurse or mother to tell one from the other.

However, alike in form, we have said they differed in temper. The elder was peaceful, studious, and silent; the younger was warlike and noisy. He was quick at learning once he began, but very slow to start. No threats of the ferule would provoke Harry to learn in an idle fit, nor would they prevent George from helping his brother with his lessons. Harry possessed a strong military turn; he drilled the little negroes on the estate and caned them like a corporal, having many good boxing matches with them, never bearing malice if he was worsted. George, conversely, was sparing of blows and gentle with all about him.

As was the custom in all families, each boy had a special little servant assigned to him. It was a known fact that George, finding his little wretch of a blackamoor asleep on his master's bed, sat down beside it and brushed the flies off the child with a feather fan. This act filled old Gumbo, the child's father, with horror as he found his young master so engaged, and provoked the indignation of Madam Esmond, who ordered the young negro off to the proper officer for a whipping. In vain George implored and entreated, bursting into passionate tears and beseeching a remission of the sentence. His mother was inflexible regarding the young rebel's punishment, and the little negro went off beseeching his young master not to cry.

A fierce quarrel ensued between mother and son out of this event. Her son would not be pacified. He declared the punishment a shame—a shame—that he was the master of the boy and that no one, not even his mother, had a right to touch him. He acknowledged she might order him to be corrected and that he would suffer the punishment, as he and Harry often had, but no one should lay a hand on his boy. Trembling with passionate rebellion against what he conceived to be injustice, he vowed—actually shrieking out an oath that shocked his fond mother and governor, who had never heard such language from the usually gentle child—that on the day he came of age, he would set young Gumbo free. He went to visit the child in the slaves' quarters and gave him one of his own toys.

The young black martyr was an impudent, lazy, saucy little personage who would be none the worse for a whipping, as the Colonel no doubt thought. He acquiesced in the child's punishment when Madam Esmond insisted upon it, laughing in his good-natured way when his indignant grandson called out, "You let mamma rule you in everything, grandpapa."

"Why, so I do," said grandpapa. "Rachel, my love, the way in which I am petticoat-ridden is so evident that even this baby has found it out."

"Then why don't you stand up like a man?" said little Harry, who was always ready to abet his brother.

Grandpapa looked queerly.

"Because I like sitting down best, my dear," he replied. "I am an old gentleman, and standing fatigues me."

On account of a certain apish drollery and humor which exhibited itself in the lad, and a liking for some of the old man's pursuits, the first of the twins became the grandfather's favorite and companion. He would laugh and talk out all his infantine heart to the old gentleman, to whom the younger had seldom a word to say. George was a demure, studious boy, and his senses seemed to brighten in the library, where his brother was so gloomy. He knew the books before he could well-nigh carry them and read in them long before he could understand them. Harry, on the other hand, was all alive in the stables or in the wood, eager for all parties of hunting and fishing, promising to be a good sportsman from a very early age.

Their grandfather's ship sailed for Europe once when the boys were children, and they were asked what present Captain Franks should bring them back. George was divided between books and a fiddle; Harry instantly declared for a little gun. Madam Warrington (as she then was called) was hurt that her elder boy should have low tastes and applauded the younger's choice as more worthy of his name and lineage. "Books, papa, I can fancy to be a good choice," she replied to her father, who tried to convince her that George had a right to his opinion, "though I am sure you must have pretty nigh all the books in the world already. But I never can desire—I may be wrong, but I never can desire—that my son, and the grandson of the Marquis of Esmond, should be a fiddler."

"Should be a fiddlestick, my dear," the old Colonel answered.
