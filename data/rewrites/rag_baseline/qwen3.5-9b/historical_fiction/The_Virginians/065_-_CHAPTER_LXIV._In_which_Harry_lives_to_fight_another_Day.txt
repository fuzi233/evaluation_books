The silence in the kitchen was heavier than the grief Gumbo allowed himself to wear. To the outside observer, the negro's reaction to Harry's enlistment was theatrical—a performance of tears and howls designed to convince anyone listening that his heart had physically shattered at the prospect of separation. Yet, beneath the melodrama lay a desperate, singular need: proximity to female attention. Gumbo retreated to the quarters of the Lambert household, seeking solace in the presence of the maid-servants. There, amidst the soft curves of their bosoms, he found a sanctuary where his dark complexion ceased to be a mark of difference and became merely a feature, as unremarkable as Othello's to Desdemona. Europe, it seemed, possessed a peculiar capacity for squeamishness regarding Africa, a bias that vanished instantly once the context shifted to romance. Even the Chevalier de St. Georges, a figure of aristocratic renown, had proven himself a favorite of the fair sex, suggesting that the barrier was not skin, but circumstance.

In this humbler theater, Gumbo flourished. The servants wept openly in his company, elevating him from a mere attendant to a brotherhood of shared sorrow. Hetty, usually a creature of sharp wit and pert laughter, found herself transformed. Witnessing Gumbo roar over the injustice of his master's departure—his refusal to take the boy along despite promises of saving Harry's life at any cost, even if it meant being cut into twenty thousand pieces—triggered a shift in Hetty's demeanor. She laughed, but it was a brittle sound, quickly swallowed by a newfound humility. She moved through the house like a ghost of her former self, meek and silent. To her mother, she was submissive; to John and Betty, grateful; to Polly, enduring the pain of her hair being combed; to Charley, accepting the crushing of her toes and the disarray of her workbox without complaint. Even in Papa's presence, the girl who once wagged her tongue with impudence now offered only a curtsey and a whispered, "Yes, if you please, dear papa!"

The contrast was stark. What sort of meal was a leg of mutton compared to the rations of a volunteer facing cannonballs? What purpose did church serve when the prayer was for survival? Hetty knelt, her knees knocking together, hiding her face as the parson read the commandment against murder, convinced he was staring directly at her sin. Her mind raced through the geography of danger, tracking travelers by land and sea, scanning newspapers for whispers of the Expedition. Every glance at her father returning from the Ordnance Office was a forensic examination of his face, searching for the signs of a general's promotion or the tragedy of a prisoner's wound. She imagined the pensioner in Chelsea Garden, whose legs had been severed by a single shot, and resolved that she would remain on her knees forever unless Harry walked through that door intact. "Haven't you heard of people, Theo," she asked, her voice trembling, "whose hair turns grey in a single night?" She checked the mirror, searching for the phantom silver strands, terrified that the war had already aged her soul.

"Yet you were not so nervous when Papa was away in Minorca," Theo observed gently.

"Ah, Theo," Hetty replied, her cheeks flushing with a mixture of shame and relief. "One sees clearly enough that George is not with the army, but safe at home."

George blushed, looking pensive. If Harry had been in the ranks, the dynamic of the family would have shifted irrevocably; another pair of boots would have been added to the ledger of loss. But for now, the narrative held a temporary peace. Harry Warrington was, for the moment, as secure as any officer stationed at Regent's Park Barracks.

His first engagement, however, offered no such security. It was a campaign devoid of glory, a series of maneuvers that felt less like war and more like a fable of a lion chasing a goose. The British Lion, emboldened by the absence of a worthy adversary, found itself confronting nothing but empty forests and retreating shadows. Imagine a beast charging into the wild, only to discover six tigers emerging from the brush. The rational choice, the only choice for a creature of instinct, is to tuck the tail between the legs and retreat to the den. To fight six tigers would be to admit oneself an ass.

Harry's experience mirrored this absurdity. On June 1st, he embarked with thirteen thousand men from the Isle of Wight. By dawn on the fifth, the fleet anchored in the Bay of Cancale, Brittany. For hours, the volunteers idled, peering at the French coast, watching cattle graze and dragoons trot away into the distance. A small fort, displaying a reckless audacity, fired upon the cutter carrying Marlborough and the Commodore. By two o'clock, the fleet was settled, and the grenadiers prepared to disembark in flat-bottomed boats. Lord George Sackville and General Dury commanded the landing party, including the gentlemen volunteers.

There was no heroism to be written here. The French, anticipating a trap or simply unwilling to engage, melted away. The frigates silenced the lone fort that had dared to interrupt the reconnaissance, and the army marched forward to capture the garrison. The entire force was taken prisoner, save for one old gentleman who, firing his two remaining cannons, declared that if every Frenchman had acted with such gallantry, the British would never have landed. He was shot in the leg, a casualty of his own stubborn pride.

The volunteers were mocked by their comrades for their eagerness to collect scalps, but none fell to Harry's tomahawk. The only death recorded was that of a French gentleman riding with his servant, foolishly refusing quarter from Lord Downe. Both men and their horses were dispatched instantly.

The following day, the force advanced from Cancale to St. Malo. The villages were stripped bare, the roads narrowed to single file by the sheer volume of men, creating a procession vulnerable to ambush from behind the tall hedges. Though no enemy awaited them, the tension of potential slaughter hung in the air. At nightfall, the army reached St. Malo, greeted by a barrage of artillery that did little damage in the dark. Seizing the opportunity, the British ignited the harbor, setting ablaze ships, wooden structures, and magazines of pitch and tar. The resulting inferno consumed the night.

Yet, the French, confident in their unseen strength within the town, did not emerge to stop the destruction. Marlborough and Sackville, satisfied with the display of power, decided not to besiege the city. Instead, they marched back to Cancale and boarded their ships. The expedition concluded not with a triumph, but with a return to Portsmouth exactly four weeks after departure.

George Warrington, receiving the news, burst into the Lambert dining room with exaggerated enthusiasm. "Glorious news, ladies!" he cried. "Our champion returns. He has faced dragons—upon my word, he says so."

"Dragons?" Hetty asked, her anxiety spiking.

"But not killed any," George clarified, reading the letter aloud. "'Dearest Brother—I think you will be glad to hear that I am returned, without any commission as yet; without any wounds or glory; but, at any rate, alive and hearty.' He describes the ship as crowded as the Black Hole of Calcutta, the weather rough, and the food inedible. 'My gentlemen's stomachs are dainty,' he writes, complaining of plain sailor's rum and biscuit after the delicacies of Braund and White. 'But I, who have been at sea before, took my rations and can of flip very contentedly... showing that a Virginia gentleman is as good as the best of 'em.'"

He continued, recounting the journey to Cancale Bay, the sighting of "a few dragons on a hill" who rode away without engaging, the sleeping under canvas, and the burning of privateers at St. Malo. "Better luck next time," Harry concluded. "This hasn't been very much nor particularly glorious: but I have liked it for my part. I have smelt powder, besides a good deal of rosin and pitch we burned. I've seen the enemy; have slept under canvas, and been dreadfully crowded and sick at sea. I like it."

The mention of dragons sent Hetty into fresh distress, imagining the seven champions and the fate of Andromeda. Papa dismissed the fear, attributing the creatures to the imagination of the Seven Champions, noting that seeing St. George's flag must have caused them to slink off. The little boy from Chartreux solemnly confirmed the lore, recalling the monster guarding the apple tree.

Despite the lack of actual combat, the atmosphere in the Lambert household shifted. Harry's return, though marked by boredom rather than bloodshed, served to quell Hetty's qualms. His announcement that he was unhurt and alive provided a temporary respite. Later, during an official visit to the troops at Portsmouth, George accompanied his father and found Harry transformed. The listless, moping youth of London coffee houses was gone, replaced by a figure brimming with energy and health. The excitement of the campaign had forged a new vigor in him. He was a ready pupil in the open-air school of war, eager to learn his new duties.

George watched his brother pace the streets of the warlike city, arm-in-arm, marveling at the scale of the army compared to the disastrous force he had witnessed two years prior.
