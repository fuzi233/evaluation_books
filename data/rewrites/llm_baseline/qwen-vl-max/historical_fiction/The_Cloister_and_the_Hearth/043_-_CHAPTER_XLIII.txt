A quiet transformation had come over Margaret Brandt. She moved through her daily tasks as though walking in a dream, her movements slow and distant. If Peter spoke to her with any haste, she would start violently, her eyes wide with fear, fixed upon him as if he were a stranger. She visited her dear friend Margaret Van Eyck less often now, and when she did, she seemed uneasy, almost afraid of the warmth that once welcomed her. Where before she had accepted her old friend’s tender embraces with open affection, she now received them with trembling passivity, sometimes even flinching away.

But the most striking change was her refusal to leave her home during daylight hours. When she did go to Tergou, it was always after nightfall, and she returned before dawn. She no longer attended matins, though she had once done so regularly. Peter, who was not one to notice subtle shifts in mood or behavior, finally noticed this strange withdrawal. One evening, he asked her why she avoided going out.

“People look at me,” she whispered, her voice barely above a breath.

Later, Margaret Van Eyck, concerned for her friend, gently questioned her. A sudden flash of terror crossed Margaret’s face, followed by a flood of silent tears. She could say nothing more. The elder woman, ever kind and patient, pressed softly. “What, my dear, are you afraid to confide your sorrow to me?”

“I have no sorrow,” Margaret replied, “only one I’ve brought upon myself. I am treated far better than I deserve—especially here.”

“And yet you come so rarely?” the old lady said, puzzled.

“I come more often than I deserve,” she sighed, her voice heavy with regret.

At that moment, Reicht called from the kitchen. “Reicht is bawling for you,” Margaret Van Eyck said, turning toward the door. “Go, child—what on earth can be troubling you?”

As she walked home, the old woman mulled over the possibilities. Perhaps Margaret felt humiliated by the way Gerard’s family treated her. That thought stirred her indignation. “I shall speak plainly to them,” she decided, “at least to those among them who are women.” And so, the very next day, she wrapped herself in her cloak and hood, took Reicht with her, and made her way to the hosier’s house.

Catherine greeted her with respectful deference, her eyes glistening with gratitude. “Thank you, madam,” she said, her voice thick with emotion, “for all your kindness to Gerard.”

Encouraged, Margaret Van Eyck ventured into the subject of Margaret Brandt. But the moment her name was mentioned, Catherine’s expression changed. Her eyes hardened, her lips thinned, and her face took on the stubborn, closed-off look of someone unwilling to listen or understand. It was a mask worn only by those whose minds were set like stone. Margaret Van Eyck tried to reason with her, but her words fell on deaf ears. Frustrated, and unused to being denied, she rose abruptly and left, offering a curtsey so abrupt and incomplete it seemed more like an insult than a courtesy. Catherine returned it with a haughty tilt of the head, as if daring her to challenge her further.

Outside the gate, Margaret Van Eyck found Reicht speaking with a pale girl leaning on crutches. As she passed them, her cheeks flushed, and she lifted her chin in scornful dismissal—meant, perhaps, for the whole household. But suddenly, a small, delicate hand slipped quietly into hers. Turning, she saw two gentle, dove-like eyes filled with tears, looking up at her with both gratitude and quiet pleading.

The old woman understood instantly. Her anger vanished. She stopped, knelt down, and kissed the girl’s forehead. “I see,” she murmured. “And mind this: I leave it to you.”

Back at her own home, she told Reicht, “Today I went to a house where I saw two things—one common, one rare. I saw a foolish, stubborn woman—and I saw an angel in flesh, with a face so pure and sweet that if I had it before me now, I’d pick up my brushes again just to try and capture it.”

Kate, true to the impression formed, waited for the right moment and then told her mother what Reicht Heynes had shared—how Margaret had given her own blood to save Gerard in the forest. “See, mother,” she said softly, “how deeply she loves him.”

“Who wouldn’t love such a man?” her mother replied.

“Oh, mother,” Kate whispered, “think of it. Poor thing.”

“Ay, wench,” the mother said, “she has her own troubles, as we do. I can’t bear the sight of blood—let alone my own.”

It was a step forward. But when Kate tried to press further, she was met with silence.

About a month later, a soldier from the Dalgetty clan returned from service in Burgundy and arrived at the hosier’s house one evening. The father was away on business, but the rest of the family sat together at supper. The soldier placed a letter on the table beside Catherine and, without accepting any reward, departed for Sevenbergen.

The letter was unfolded and laid flat. Strangely enough, though none of them could read, they all recognized Gerard’s handwriting at once. “Your father must be away,” Catherine exclaimed. “Are you not ashamed? Not one of you can read your brother’s letter!”

Though the words were meaningless to them—like hieroglyphs to us—there was something in the letter they could understand. There exists a language beyond words, unbound by letters or grammar, that speaks directly to the heart. It flows across borders, crosses seas, and needs no interpreter. It is universal.

And so, when they saw the drawing Gerard had sketched between two short paragraphs—a simple image of two hands clasped together—they understood. They recognized his style in every line, every curve. In that instant, Gerard stood before them, bidding farewell.

They wept until the image blurred before their eyes. Giles murmured, “Poor Gerard,” his voice lower than anyone expected from him. Even Cornelis and Sybrandt sat in silence, their usual bravado replaced by a fleeting sense of guilt.

But how could they learn what the letter said? They were reluctant to reveal their ignorance—or their grief—to a stranger.

“The Dame Van Eyck?” Kate suggested timidly.

“And so I will,” Catherine said, her voice firming. “She has a good heart. She loves Gerard too. She’ll be glad to hear from him. I was harsh with her when she came here—but I’ll make amends. Then she’ll tell me what my poor boy writes.”

Soon she arrived at Margaret Van Eyck’s house. Reicht led her into a room where a young woman sat quietly by the stove. She rose and courteously stepped aside. “Thank you, young lady,” Catherine said. “These winter nights are cold, and your stove is a comfort.”

As she warmed her hands, Catherine studied her companion from head to foot. The young woman wore a plain wimple, but her gown was trimmed with fur—an indication of rank or wealth in those days. Yet what struck Catherine most was the honesty and gentleness in her face. She felt sure this girl would understand her pain.

“Now, what brings me here, young lady?” Catherine began. “A letter! A letter from my poor boy, far off in some wild land. And I’m ashamed to say none of us can read it. I wonder—can you?”

“Yes,” the girl answered softly.

“Can you, now? That’s a fine thing, my dear. I dare say the dame won’t be long. But every minute feels like an hour to a mother longing for news.”

“I’ll read it to you,” the girl offered.

“Bless you, my dear—bless you!” Catherine said, so eager she didn’t notice the quiet intensity with which the girl reached for the letter. She didn’t see the slight tremor in her fingers as they closed around it.

“Come then,” Catherine urged. “Read it to me. I’m dying to know.”

“The first words are: *‘To my honoured parents.’*”

“Ay!” Catherine breathed. “He always did honour us, poor soul.”

“*‘God and the saints keep you in His holy care, and bless you by night and by day. Your one harsh deed is forgotten; your years of love remembered.’*”

Catherine placed her hand over her heart and sank back into her chair, overcome by a long, shuddering sob.

“Then comes this,” the girl continued, “a sketch—*‘a long farewell.’*”

“Ay, go on,” Catherine said, wiping her eyes. “Bless you, girl—you give me sorrowful comfort. Still, it’s comfort.”

“*‘To my brothers Cornelis and Sybrandt—Be content; you will see me no more!’*”

“What does that mean?” Catherine gasped. “Ah!”

“*‘To my sister Kate—little angel of my father’s house. Be kind to her—’* Ah!”

“That is Margaret Brandt,” Catherine whispered. “His sweetheart, poor soul. I’ve not been kind to her, my dear. Forgive me, Gerard!”

“*‘—for poor Gerard’s sake, since grief to her is death to me.’*”

And then, nature, weary of holding back, gave way. The young woman collapsed from her chair, unconscious, the letter still clutched in her hand, her head resting on Catherine’s knees.
