Throughout history, love has often been described as a force beyond reason—a kind of fate that overwhelms the mind, rendering logic and resolve powerless. Think of Daphnis, swept away by passion despite his understanding of Heraclitus’s wisdom; or Tristan, whose heart was undone not by philosophy but by a potion no scholar could resist; or Romeo, who in the moonlight beneath Juliet’s balcony forgot all argument against Ptolemy’s cosmos. Yet not all love is so blind. Even when powerful, it may coexist with thought, imagination, and moral conviction. It can be shaped by dreams of distant stars, stirred by visions of what once was and what might yet be—drawing strength from memory and hope alike.

When Deronda returned to London, he told Sir Hugo he had planted a clear idea in Grandcourt’s mind: that fifty thousand pounds could be gained by relinquishing a future claim, uncertain though it was. But beyond this, there was no sign of Grandcourt’s true feelings—only a continued willingness to keep their acquaintance warm.

“And what did you think of the bride-to-be upon closer inspection?” asked Sir Hugo.

“I liked her better than I did in Leubronn,” Deronda replied. “Roulette brought out something harsh in her—almost a shadow. But at Diplow, she seemed softer, more truly feminine. Her eyes and mouth carried a gentler expression.”

“Don’t flirt too much with her, Dan,” Sir Hugo said, aiming for lightness. “If you make Grandcourt furious when they come to the Abbey at Christmas, it’ll spoil my plans.”

“I can stay in town, sir.”

“No, no,” Sir Hugo insisted. “Lady Mallinger and the children need you. Just don’t cause trouble—unless you can manage a duel and shoot Grandcourt. That might be worth the hassle.”

“I don’t recall ever flirting,” Deronda said, unamused.

“Oh, haven’t I?” Sir Hugo teased. “You always look at women with such tenderness, talk to them like a Jesuit. You’re dangerous—you’re the kind of man who makes Clarissas chase after you instead of the other way around.”

What good came of being irritated by a tasteless joke? Only irritation comes first—the reflection on usefulness follows later. Few compliments are more irritating than being told we seem to do things we never intend. Sir Hugo’s idea of flirting was clearly peculiar. For Deronda’s part, he was certain he had never flirted. Still, he was relieved the baronet knew nothing about his secret purchase of Gwendolen’s necklace—no chance of that becoming fodder for teasing.

He would be cautious moving forward—especially at Mrs. Meyrick’s house, where he was soon to visit for the first time since returning from Leubronn. Mirah was a woman who inspired quiet affection simply through her presence and speech. It was hard not to feel drawn to her.

Mrs. Meyrick had already written him a letter full of warmth about Mirah’s life in their home. “We grow fonder of her every day,” she wrote. “At breakfast, we all turn toward the door, waiting for her to appear. We watch her, listen to her—as if she were a visitor from another world. Not a word she’s spoken has given me cause for doubt. She is content, deeply grateful. My daughters are learning from her, and hope to find her more pupils. She refuses to live idle—she wants to work, just as my girls do. Mab says our life feels like a fairy tale, and all she fears is that Mirah will turn into a nightingale again and fly away. Her voice is perfect—not loud, but deep and tender, like thoughts of what once was. That’s how an old soul like mine feels about beauty in song.”

But Mrs. Meyrick had left out the details that might have made her hesitant. Amy and Mab, who had accompanied Mirah to synagogue, found Jewish customs harder to accept than they had expected—especially compared to Scott’s idealized Rebecca. They kept silent, out of respect for Mirah, whose faith was too personal to question lightly. But eventually, Amy—practical and reform-minded—could no longer hold back.

“Excuse me, Mirah,” she ventured, “but does it seem right to you that women sit apart, behind rails in a gallery?”

“Yes, I’ve never thought otherwise,” Mirah answered, gently surprised.

“And you prefer seeing men wear their hats?” Mab asked cautiously, offering the smallest difference.

“Oh, yes,” Mirah smiled. “I like what I’ve always seen—it brings back feelings I wouldn’t trade for anything.”

After that, any criticism—of belief or practice—would have felt cruel to these gentle souls. Mirah’s religion wasn’t a set of doctrines; it was woven into her very emotions, inseparable from her sense of self.

“She says she’s a poor Jewess,” Amy whispered later, after Mirah had gone to bed. “She doesn’t even know half her people’s traditions. Maybe if she loves us enough, and never finds her mother, she’ll slowly drift into Christianity—like most of the world does. It’s strange to belong to the Jews now.”

“Oh, oh, oh!” Mab exclaimed. “I wish I weren’t such a hideous Christian. How can an ugly Christian, always dropping her knitting, convert a beautiful Jewess who has no flaw?”

“It might be wrong of me,” Kate said shrewdly, “but I can’t help hoping her mother isn’t found. There might be something unpleasant.”

“I don’t think so,” Mrs. Meyrick replied. “I believe Mirah is made in her mother’s image. And imagine the joy if such a daughter were returned! But a mother’s feelings aren’t always rational” (she gave her daughters a playful glance). “And is a dead mother worth more than a living one?”

“Well, perhaps,” Kate said softly, “but we’d rather have you alive, little mother.”

Not only the Meyricks, whose knowledge had come from scattered reading and curious minds, but Deronda himself—well-educated, worldly, and grounded—had been shaken by Mirah’s presence. He realized how little he knew of modern Judaism, of the inner life of the Jewish people. The Chosen People had long been seen as chosen for others’ sake, their beliefs treated as outdated or exotic, fit only for scholars. Deronda, like many of his class, had assumed Judaism was a relic—something to ignore unless studying it academically. But Mirah—fleeing one parent, yearning for the other—had shown him a truth he hadn’t known: Judaism still lived in human hearts, still shaped lives, still offered meaning in a broken world.

On the journey back with Sir Hugo, he began noticing synagogues, searching book titles about Jews. This awakening—from indifference to curiosity—was a cure for boredom, though not one easily prescribed. Deronda carried it with him, making his idle weeks bearable. It was during this trip that he entered a Jewish synagogue for the first time—in Frankfurt, on a Friday evening.

Walking through the Juden-gasse, familiar from earlier visits, he remembered its old houses, but now his eyes lingered on the people—their faces, their bearing, their quiet dignity. His mind raced, connecting them to centuries past, stirring a deep sympathy with history. This sensitivity had shaped parts of his character—traits important to understand if one wished to follow his future.

Though young, well-born, and well-mannered, Deronda was not the kind of man others typically examined closely. He didn’t provoke questions about his beliefs or tastes. He could pass as a pleasant, clever fellow without scrutiny. But later, when he aged and softened, his quirks became clearer—and it was mercy if they weren’t offensive.

To grasp how later events affected him, one must see more than what he showed in conversation. His vivid impressions often made him seem mysterious, even contradictory. From early youth, he had developed a deep sensibility and reflective nature, which grew into a wide-ranging empathy. This empathy threatened to paralyze action—whenever he opposed something, even in thought, he felt he was attacking kin, things he loved. His imagination taught him to see the world through others’ eyes, so strong partisanship—except against injustice—felt like hypocrisy.

His sympathy was vast, flexible, yet entangled with analytical thinking that weakened emotion. Few men avoided vice more than he, yet he disliked it mildly, seeing it not as abstract sin, but as part of complex human lives shaped by history and pain. He understood and pitied.

This balance made him fiercely democratic in spirit, yet deeply conservative in feeling—drawn to tradition, haunted by memory. He hungered for ideas about government and religion, yet clung to ancient forms that held meaning for him, forms no argument could destroy. He leaned toward causes that lost—perhaps because he felt a kinship with those who suffered. Martyrdom changed sides, and he feared he might change with it, repelled by the idea of chasing success, which the world often calls duty.

Yet he also feared blind hatred. He sympathized with privileged heirs, recoiled from bitterness, and disliked the tone of angry reformers. Too much reflection risked paralyzing his moral courage—his anger at injustice, his choice of companionship. In recent years, he had grown acutely aware of this danger. What he craved was either a sudden event or a moment of insight that would pull him into a clear path, channeling his restless energy.

He no longer cared much for knowledge—he had no ambition for achievement—unless both could merge with his emotions. He dreaded the cold, empty culture that reduced everything to analysis, knowing everything *about* things but missing the thing itself—like knowing all about violets except their scent.

How would this turning point come? What force would justify loyalty, make him part of society instead of a wandering spirit, stirred by vague passions but without place or purpose? To make some difference—for the better—was not something he could live without. But how?

He saw part of the fault in his upbringing—no demands placed on him, no fixed role, only a relationship of uncertain nature. But he admitted he had slipped into a meditative numbness, drifting farther from the active, passionate life he believed was best—indeed, the only life worth living.

He wanted to keep emotion alive, strong, in the face of overthinking. To grind sentiment into dust while keeping it vibrant—like making cannon: first shape a round hole, then encase it in iron. Hold fast to your core.

Yet how distinguish what should be preserved whole from what is merely preserved as relic?

Such thoughts ran beneath his surface as he read law or listened politely. He had not pursued any single task with passion or consistency. Not a model life, certainly—but a struggle before dawn, one many young men have faced, bruised but not broken.

Under his calm exterior burned a quiet fire, making him sensitive to poetry in ordinary things. The Juden-gasse stirred in him a sense of connection to the distant past, evoking two poetic elements of history: the fragile beginnings of faiths and institutions, and their slow decay. The dust and remnants covering them only deepened their power—like twin green leaves destined to become a sheltering tree, or a legacy turned to sorrowful memory.

As he stepped out of the Juden-gasse and wandered in the evening air, heading for the synagogue, this reflection softened the sting of petty scenes along the way. At a dusty bookshop, he asked for the service time and was warmly guided by a sharp-eyed Jewish boy who enthusiastically directed him to the old Rabbinical school rather than the new Reformed building. Then, with charming politeness, overcharged him for a rare book—“nicht so leicht zu bekommen,” he explained.

Across the counter, a gruff, deaf shopkeeper glared at cards combining business and ritual, while a haggard man in a tall coat shouted in Yiddish, offering a bag. Moments later, another man of the same sort emerged from the shadows and repeated the cry. Deronda saw several odd-looking Jews—not wholly innocent, but indistinguishable from similarly flawed Christians. Recently, worried about Mirah’s family, he had viewed vulgar Jews with personal alarm. But comparison helped. Seeing similar flaws in others lessened surprise. He paid too much, but not with regret—his interest in Hebrew destiny remained intact.

He reached the Rabbinische Schule at sunset, entering with a modest congregation.

He took a seat near an elderly man with a white beard and felt hat—his profile striking, equally fitting for an Italian nobleman or a Hebrew sage. Their eyes met briefly, awkwardly. Deronda looked away—then felt a prayer book pushed toward him. He bowed thanks.

The service began. The reader climbed the platform, chanting Psalms and Old Testament phrases. Deronda, reading the German translation, let go of words and surrendered to the music—its rhythm, its chant, its shared devotion. Liturgy, at its strongest, transcends language. It becomes a plea not for specific gain, but for escape from weakness, an invitation to Good to enter and abide. Or it becomes pure joy—an exaltation that Good exists. Both emotions, amplified by communion across generations, struck him deeply.

The Hebrew liturgy moved through litanies, hymns, proclamations, blessings. Tonight, for Deronda, it was all one: the reader’s soaring voice, the boys’ sweet chorus, the swaying of worshippers, the humble surroundings—all blended into a single expression of history—tragic, enduring, glorious.

He was overwhelmed. The feeling seemed too intense for the moment—like divine presence in darkness before vision arrives.

Then, as the sounds faded and indifferent faces passed before him, a cold thought crept in: perhaps he was alone in this feeling. Perhaps he was the only one for whom the service meant more than routine.

Just as he bowed to his neighbor and turned to leave, a hand gripped his arm. Startled, he turned to see the white-bearded man close by.

“Pardon me, young gentleman,” the man said in German. “May I ask—what is your parentage? Your mother’s family name? Her maiden name?”

Deronda felt a surge of resistance. He wanted to shake off the touch, but managed to withdraw it calmly. “I am English,” he said coolly.

The man studied him a moment, then lifted his hat slightly and walked away. Whether he regretted the question or felt rebuffed, Deronda couldn’t tell.

On the walk back, he tried to dismiss the incident. He could not have acted differently. How could he reveal a name he didn’t know—to a stranger who had overstepped? The man likely mistook resemblance for kinship—common, meaningless.

The event was trivial, yet his instinctive recoil had been strong. He was glad he had ended it quickly.

It was also a reason not to mention the synagogue to the Mallingers—beyond his usual reluctance to speak of things Sir Hugo might call “Quixotic.” Sir Hugo was kind-hearted, especially to women, and sometimes did romantic deeds. But he never saw them as romantic. He laughed at grand motives, far-off reasons. This was the key difference between him and Deronda, who often spent breakfast lost in silent speculation about how to fill his days as others did.

Their stop in Frankfurt was brief, on the way home. But the impressions lingered—especially because of Mirah. That question about his mother’s family—if he hadn’t dismissed it as insignificant, it might have felt like a threat, not a promise. It deepened his anxiety about finding Mirah’s relatives and reinforced his caution. If he discovered something painful, was he bound to tell her? Might it bring new suffering?

He had written to Mrs. Meyrick to announce his visit at four. When he arrived, Mirah sat quietly working, surrounded by Mrs. Meyrick, Mab, the open piano, and the glowing engravings on the walls. Her hair was neatly done, her dress delicate. A peaceful glow lit her face—so different from the ragged figure by the river. A painter could have used her without changing a line, placing her before a choir singing “peace on earth, goodwill to men.”

Mirah noticed the contrast too. As they greeted each other, she said:

“See how different I am from the wretched creature by the river—because you found me and brought me to the very best.”

“It was luck,” Deronda said. “Any man would have done the same.”

“That’s not how I see it,” Mirah said firmly, shaking her head. “I think of what really happened. It was you—no one else—who found me and were kind to me.”
