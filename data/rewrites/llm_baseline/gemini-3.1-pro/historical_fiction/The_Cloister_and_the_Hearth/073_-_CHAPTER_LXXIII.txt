Catherine swept into Margaret’s dwelling not merely as a mother-in-law, but as a meticulous guardian of the hearth’s sacred ecology. The governing power ran strong in her blood. Almost immediately upon her crossing the threshold, the cast-iron skillets seemed to cast off their dullness, the hearth fires breathed with an optimized, rhythmic crackle, and the fragrant, earthy scent of dried juniper and hanging sage was coaxed back to life. But Catherine was a conductor who demanded absolute acoustic and physical control over her domain. She possessed no patience for the discordant elements of the home. She rearranged the heavy oaken chairs and the grinding mortars to better suit the daily choreography of sustenance, righting the flow of the room. But she could not stop at the inanimate fixtures; she soon turned her critical ear to the living inhabitants.

As Margaret’s time of confinement drew near, Catherine perceived the heavy, martial presence of Denys and Martin as a jarring disruption to the home's delicate sonic balance. The unyielding thump of their leather boots shattered the quiet, agrarian rhythms of the household, introducing a clattering, abrasive noise that grated against Catherine’s nerves like the unnatural din of a blacksmith's commercial forge. When the two simple-minded soldiers earnestly asked how they might serve, Catherine deployed a barrage of sharp, vernacular rebukes—a dominant lexicon of domestic authority—advising them that they might make themselves most useful by stepping out for a walk, preferably one that extended well into the middle of the following week. 

Under the yoke of this matriarchal tyranny, the men shrank. They slipped out at the break of dawn and stole back in at nightfall, moving with the agonizing caution of cats navigating a wall topped with shattered glass. Their hushed, awe-struck murmurs were an uncomfortable attempt to minimize their auditory footprint, but their very existence in a house preparing for the profound, mythic arrival of new life remained an intolerable dissonance. Catherine ran infinite pins and needles of sharp dialect into them, expertly utilizing the power dynamics of her native tongue to drive the depopulators from the sanctuary. 

When Margaret finally descended from her birthing chamber, the kitchen smelled deeply of blooming yeast and slow-roasted root vegetables. But she found her sanctuary swept completely clean of its heroes. Old Martin, his joints stiff as winter branches, had retreated to the street, carrying his wooden stool into the sunlight, seeking the acoustic harmony of the open air over the oppressive clatter of Catherine's culinary dominion. Margaret, her eyes wide and tender as bruised violets, looked toward the warming hearth and asked for Denys, anticipating his gentle, familiar presence.

Catherine, vigorously scrubbing a wooden bowl with a fragrant paste of salt and coarse fat, did not break her rhythm. She declared that Denys had returned to his friends in Burgundy. 

"What, gone without bidding me farewell?" Margaret whispered. The sudden, permanent absence of Denys's boisterous Burgundian dialect left a void in the room's auditory tapestry, an untranslatable silence that pressed heavily on her. A tear gathered and fell, carrying the raw, saline tang of grief.

Catherine’s cheeks reddened. Pricked by a sudden, sharp pang of conscience, she masked her guilt with a dismissive toss of her head, claiming Margaret had been asleep and she dared not disrupt her rest. Yet, slipping out the door when Margaret wasn't looking, Catherine attempted to lavish a compensatory feast of hospitality upon the surviving soldier. She approached Martin with the offer of rich, marrow-thickened sustenance. But the old man, anchored in his sensory exile, refused her. "No, thank you, dame," he murmured. "I have got used to out o' doors. I meddle wi' nobody here, and nobody meddles wi' me."

Stung by his rejection, Catherine’s tone shifted instantly from the sweet, maternal purr of dark honey to the sharp, acidic bite of fermented vinegar. She screamed a curse at the old wretch and flounced back indoors, her heavy skirts disrupting the air. After a moment of calm reflection, accompanied by the quiet shedding of her own tears, she absolved herself. She would simply provide such boundless nourishment to Margaret that the young mother would never miss the abrasive clatter of soldiers.

To banish the last of her unease, Catherine initiated a ritual as ancient and circular as the turning of the agrarian seasons: the washing of the newborn. She filled a deep copper basin with tepid water. The room blossomed with an olfactory landscape of pure comfort as the steam exhaled the rich, grounding scent of rendered tallow soap and crushed herbs. In this celestial ceremony, the wrongs of banished heroes dissolved like dust in an ocean of warmth and lather.

Margaret hovered, unable to distance herself from the tactile pull of the ritual. She had to place her own fingers into the aromatic water, intertwining her touch with the generational chore. As she kneeled opposite little Gerard, she began to murmur to her son. She did not use the structured, dominant grammar of the adult world; instead, she spoke in a soft, rhythmic babble—an intimate, pre-verbal dialect of maternal devotion, perfectly attuned to the infant's ear, defying the rigid lexicons that sought to categorize and control human experience.

"I wish you could wash out that," Margaret said, fixing her eyes on the infant's wet hand. 

"What?"

"What, have you not noticed? On his little finger."

Catherine leaned in, and there, stark against the pale, soapy skin, was a tiny, earth-brown mole. "Eh, but this is wonderful!" the elder woman cried, recognizing the mythic echo written in the flesh. "Nature, my lass, y'are strong; and meddlesome to boot. Hast noticed such a mark on some one else? Tell the truth, girl!"

"What, on him? Nay, mother, not I."

"Well then he has; and on the very spot," Catherine said, her voice swelling with the gravity of a meticulous historian preserving a vital lineage. The cyclical pacing of time collapsed inward for her. "I have had him hundreds o' times on my knees, the same as this, and washed him from top to toe in lukewarm water. 'Oh, fie! here's a foul blot,' quo' I; and scrubbed away at it I did till I made the poor wight cry. And now says you to me, 'do try and wash you out o' my Gerard's finger.' Think on't!"

The erosion of memory had been thwarted by a single mark of pigment. "Wash it out?" Margaret cried, leaning down to breathe in the sweet, milky scent of her baby's skin. "I wouldn't for all the world. Why, it is the sweetest bit in his little darling body." She pressed her lips to the tiny speck, a desperate, tactile feast, as if through the sheer sensory consumption of his skin she could summon the father home. She kissed the mole, and then, pulling the sprawling infant into her lap, devoured his back with kisses, worrying him as a wolf does a lamb, while Catherine smiled at the savage, beautiful onslaught. 

In the months that followed, Margaret’s life was a continuous, interwoven timeline of sensory nourishment, a vibrant tapestry of domestic myth measured out in the rhythmic grinding of spices, the bubbling of rich stews, and the profound acoustic warmth of her firstborn's laughter. 

In the valley of Grindelwald, a traveler might stand suspended between two starkly contrasting realities: on one side, the perpendicular Alps—an icy, lifeless expanse of rock and everlasting snow that starves the senses and deafens the ear with howling, sterile winds; on the other, the vibrant, emerald slopes, teeming with the sounds of grazing life and the savory smoke of cottage hearths. Such are the two halves of this interwoven saga. On the Italian side, the father endures the unnatural, hollow echoes of the cloister—a realm of spiritual starvation where the vibrant vernaculars of his youth are systemically erased by the dominant, rigid lexicon of monastic Latin, and where sustenance is viewed merely as utilitarian ash, devoid of earthly joy. On the Dutch side, a humble Hearth thrives, green and fragrant, lush with the untranslatable language of maternity. And as a narrow pathway parts the icy, silent towers of Grindelwald from its sunlit braes, so too does a single page separate the sterile chill of the Cloister from the sustaining, aromatic warmth of the Hearth.
