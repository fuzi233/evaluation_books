For all that valet Morgan fancied himself the most well-informed man in London about everyone else’s bank balances, half of Mayfair knew more about Lady Clavering’s alleged vast fortune than he did. The gossip mill was running so hot by the time the Claverings rolled into polite society that folks were swearing her wealth was basically infinite: indigo factories spitting out profit day and night, opium clippers cutting through the ocean with holds full of cash, London banks overflowing with rupees, piles of stolen native prince jewels, and stacks of interest payments from Indian royals who’d borrowed fortunes from her dad three generations back. Her banker’s account number had so many zeros appended to it that every listener’s jaw dropped to match, and everyone whispered that there was even a special envoy from the Nawab of Lucknow — Colonel Altamont, that wild adventurer who’d supposedly converted to Islam and survived a dozen near-death scrapes — over in London trying to sell Lady Clavering (aka the Begum Clavering, as society had already dubbed her) the Nawab’s legendary 200-carat nose ring diamond, “The Light of the Dewan.”

Historians of the British constitution love to brag that any low-born bloke with enough merit can waltz right into the House of Lords and sit next to a Cavendish or a Stanley, and it’s equally true that London high society, for all its haughty sniffing and gatekeeping, will drop every last one of its barriers the second someone shows up with enough cash. That’s not snobbery, that’s good old British honesty: our fancy aristocrats aren’t the stuck-up prigs the unwashed masses think they are! No, if you’re rolling in money, they’ll shake your hand, eat your truffle suppers, dance at your balls, marry your daughters, or hand over their own perfect heiress children to your sons, just as affably as any common tradesman would.

Chevalier Strong, who’d already overseen the kitting out of Clavering’s country estate, was only too happy to lend his exquisite, extremely self-serving taste to the London upholsterers decking out their Grosvenor Place townhouse. He fussed over hanging and rehanging every painting, tested every sofa for ideal napping placement, schmoozed wine merchants and caterers for the new household… and also used the opportunity to kit out his own cozy Shepherd’s Inn chambers and stock his tiny cellar with perfectly drinkable claret. His friends complimented his very neat new digs, and the select few who stopped by for a lamb cutlet with Strong now got to wash it down with something far better than the swill he’d been serving six months prior. The Chevalier was, as he put it, “in clover,” waited on by an old comrade from the Spanish Legion he’d last seen half-dead in the breach of a fort, and then stumbled across sweeping a crossing in Tottenham Court Road. This ex-legionary also looked after Strong’s current lodger, who was none other than that famous Nawab of Lucknow favourite, the valiant Colonel Altamont.

Ned Strong was the least curious (or, more accurately, the most discreet) man alive, and he didn’t poke around at the weird, clearly blackmail-adjacent dynamic that had popped up between Sir Francis Clavering and Altamont ever since their first meeting at Baymouth. It was obvious the Colonel held some very juicy dirt on the weak-willed Baronet, and Strong, who already knew Clavering’s youth had been extremely wild and his Indian army career far less than glorious, assumed Altamont had caught Clavering out in some forgery or dodgy scheme back in Calcutta that he could ruin him for.

One day after a very heated meeting, Altamont crumpled up a note Clavering had sent via Strong, snarling that a hundred quid was nowhere near enough. “Tell Clavering I need a thousand, or by Jove I spill every last secret and blow his whole life to smithereens,” he’d roared. “Give me the money by next Friday noon, and I’ll ship off abroad and leave him alone for a full year. If it’s not here? I’m running a gossip paragraph in the local paper Saturday, and the whole world will know everything by the end of the week.”

Strong delivered the message, and the very next Friday at noon he was at Altamont’s Baymouth hotel with the full thousand. Altamont behaved like the perfect gentleman, settled his bill, announced he was off on a foreign tour, and Strong even saw him set sail at Dover. “Must be a dodgy forged bill he’s got Clavering over a barrel for,” Strong thought.

But less than a year later, Altamont was back, grumbling that a completely unheard-of run of fourteen reds in a row at Baden-Baden roulette had cleaned him out. No man could survive that streak, he insisted, no matter how lucky he was. He’d been forced to draft a bill on Clavering to pay for his passage home, and Clavering, who was already hemorrhaging cash on election expenses, the country house, and the new London townhouse, was absolutely furious, cursing that he wished the Colonel could rot in a German debtor’s prison for the rest of his days so he’d never have to see his face again.

He couldn’t ask his wife for the money, though. Lady Clavering was more than generous with household spending, but she’d inherited her father Snell’s sharp business brain alongside his massive fortune, and only gave her husband a very handsome, but firmly capped, gentleman’s allowance. She’d occasionally spot him extra cash or pay off a gambling debt, but she always demanded a full itemized account for every penny, so Clavering couldn’t possibly tell her about the thousands he was shovelling to Altamont.

Strong’s new unofficial job was sourcing all that hidden cash for his patron. Back in his Shepherd’s Inn chambers, a parade of money lenders popped in to meet Clavering, passing over bank notes and stamped promissory notes back and forth like they were handing out tea biscuits. When a man gets used to running up debts and trading 12-month IOU’s for ready cash from the time he’s a teen, no windfall of fortune ever actually fixes the habit. Strong, who didn’t own a penny to his own name, ended up signing as the drawer on almost every single one of Clavering’s bills, haggling with the holders when they came due, negotiating instalments, and drawing up new bills to extend the deadlines. Hundreds of these gallant London souls wander around town cheerfully, eating well and sleeping soundly, even with a bailiff lingering around the corner and a whole noose of debt wrapped around their necks, just like the garrison at Comorn who put on plays and danced balls even as the enemy was at the gates and a gallows waited for them if they lost. Old soldier Ned Strong bore that little inconvenience like a total champ.

But before we go poking around the rest of Shepherd’s Inn’s colourful residents, we’ve left Lady Clavering and her guests loitering on the Grosvenor Place doorstep far too long.

First they piled into the grand dining room, which was kitted out in heavy middle-aged oak for no reason Lady Clavering could name. “Unless it’s ‘cause me and Clavering are actually middle-aged,” she laughed, and offered them the leftovers of the luncheon she and Blanche had just finished. Everyone had assumed Blanche, the little Sylphide, only ate six grains of rice a day like Amina, the Ghoul’s friend in the Arabian Nights — but out of sight of the servants, she’d dug into three very substantial mutton cutlets, a bit of fashionable hypocrisy that every other debutante in London was also very familiar with. Pen and his uncle politely declined the leftovers, and admired the room with all the correct, required compliments, calling it “exquisitely chaste,” which was the mandatory buzzword for any fancy rich person’s decor. There were clunky 17th century Dutch high-backed chairs, a 16th century carved buffet, a sideboard made out of stolen choir stalls from a Low Countries church, a giant brass cathedral lamp hanging over the round oak table, fake old family portraits bought off Wardour Street, secondhand French tapestries, and full sets of cardboard armour and battleaxes that wouldn’t have hurt a toddler. Nothing, the whole party agreed, could possibly be more chaste.

The library behind it was equally solemn: matching sets of books no one would ever read, huge overstuffed easy chairs, and severe bronze statues, guarded by double doors so Sir Francis could smoke his cigars, read *Bell’s Life in London*, and fall asleep after dinner in perfect peace when he wasn’t off playing billiards at his club or punting at the Saint James’s gambling houses.

But none of that could hold a candle to the drawing rooms. The carpets were so thick and fluffy your foot made no more noise on them than your shadow, dotted with roses and tulips printed as big as warming pans. There were high chairs, low chairs, bandy-legged chairs, chairs so spindly it was a wonder anything heavier than a fairy could sit on them, marquetterie tables heaped with cheap fancy trinkets, china from every country on earth, gilt daggers, Books of Beauty, Turkish slippers, and open boxes of Parisian bonbons. Dresden shepherds and shepherdesses sat on every end table alongside porcelain blue poodles and squawking chickens, there were Boucher nudes and Greuze shepherdesses (all extremely chaste, of course), layers of muslin and brocade curtains, gilded birdcages full of lovebirds, and two cockatoos screaming at each other so loud you could hear them down the block. One clock played silly little tunes on the console, another boomed the hours like Great Tom of Oxford over the mantel. It was perfect, the peak of modern high society taste — the decadent Romans of the late Empire, or the fancy courtesans of Louis XV, couldn’t have done better. Everyone who stepped through the doors agreed Lady Clavering’s reception rooms were far more chaste and elegant than even Lady Harley Quin’s, or the railroad millionaire’s wife Mrs. Hodge-Podgson’s.

Poor Lady Clavering herself didn’t care two figs about any of it. “All I know is the whole thing cost a fortune, Major,” she told her guest, “and I’d strongly advise you not to sit on one of those flimsy gilt chairs. I fell straight through one the second night we threw a dinner party. Why didn’t you lot come visit us sooner? We’d have invited you.”

“You’d have loved seeing Mamma crash through a chair, wouldn’t you Mr. Pendennis?” Blanche snarked, the little Sylphide annoyed that Pen was getting on so well with her mother.

“I’d have been very happy to offer Lady Clavering my arm to catch her, if she’d needed it,” Pen replied, blushing a little as he bowed.

“ Quel preux Chevalier!” Blanche huffed, tossing her tiny head.

“I’ve got a lot of sympathy for people who fall over, remember,” Pen grinned. “I made a total fool of myself falling once not long ago.”

“And you ran straight home to Laura so she could console you,” Blanche shot back. Pen winced. He hated that his humiliating rejection by Laura was now common gossip, so he immediately pivoted to gushing about how stunning all the furniture was, to change the subject.

“No, don’t praise me,” honest Lady Clavering waved him off. “The upholsterer and Captain Strong did it all while we were up at the country park. Lady Rockminster came round the other day and said the salons are very nice, so that must be good.”

“Lady Rockminster is my cousin Laura’s patron,” Pen said.

“The *Dowager* Lady Rockminster,” Major Pendennis corrected, his voice dropping to a reverent hush like he was in church the second the most famous name in all of fashionable society was mentioned. “If you have her approval, Lady Clavering, you cannot possibly go wrong. She’s the absolute centre of taste and fashion, everyone knows that. These rooms are magnificent.” He looked around the drawing rooms with the sort of awed reverence he usually reserved for royal coronations.

“Yes, she’s took us up,” Lady Clavering said.

“*Taken* us up, Mamma,” Blanche yelped, sharp as a tack.

“Taken us up, then,” Lady Clavering laughed. “It’s very kind of her, but it feels a bit odd at first, being taken up like a parcel. She wants to arrange all our balls and write all our dinner party invites for us, but I told her no way. I want to invite my own old friends, not have her sit at the head of my own table like it’s her house. You two must come over on the 14th, Arthur and Major — it’s not one of the huge fancy dinners, Blanche,” she added, noticing her daughter was biting her lips and frowning.

The Major smiled and bowed, saying he far preferred small quiet gatherings to stuffy big dinners, he’d had more than enough of those in his decades of social climbing.

“I always think a dinner tastes best the second day,” Lady Clavering added helpfully, trying to fix her phrasing — and making it way, way worse. Blanche clasped her hands in despair, and groaned “O mamma, vous etes incorrigible!” The Major swore he loved small snug dinners more than anything in the world, even as he mentally noted he could easily dump the Claverings if a better invitation popped up before the 14th. Pen, meanwhile, was far too new to the dinner-party circuit to turn down the chance at a fancy feast in a fancy house, and accepted immediately.

Once they were walking down the street, the Major turned to Pen. “What was that little spat you two were having with Miss Amory? I thought you two were getting on famously not long ago.”

“‘Used to’ is a very vague word for where a woman’s concerned,” Pen said, striking a dandified pose. “Was and is are two completely different things, especially when it comes to ladies’ hearts.”

“Egad, they change faster than the weather,” the Major laughed. “When we were stationed at the Cape, there was a lady who swore she’d poison herself over me — and three months later she ran off with some other man, leaving her husband behind. Don’t get tangled up with that Miss Amory, Arthur. She’s forward, affected, and not nearly as well-bred as she pretends, and she’s only got ten thousand pounds to her name. That’s nothing! I could barely pay her milliner’s bills with the interest on that sum.”

“Bit of a connoisseur of millinery, are we, Uncle?” Pen teased.

“I was, sir, I was!” the old man winked, giving a very over-the-hill flirtatious little bow to a fancy carriage rolling past them into Hyde Park. “That’s Lady Catherine Martingale’s carriage — her daughters are very pretty, but their mother was a thousand times more beautiful back in the day. Listen to me, Arthur, with your good looks and your prospects, you could make an excellent marriage someday. Don’t go telling anyone back at Fairoaks this, but a reputation for being a little bit wicked, a dangerous homme dangereux, does no harm at all with the ladies. They hate milksops, they love a bit of rogue in a young man. But when it comes to actual marriage? That’s a different matter entirely. Marry a woman with money. I’ve told you before, it’s just as easy to marry a rich girl as a poor one, and infinitely more comfortable to sit down to a fancy well-cooked dinner with three courses of entrees than a cold leg of mutton with a wife who can’t afford anything better.”

Pen quoted the old hackneyed ballad back at him: “It has to be a cottage with a double coach-house, a cottage of gentility, sir,” and the Major, who had no idea what poem he was referencing, carried on with his very cynical life advice, delighted his nephew was such a quick study. Grumpy Warrington was still back at their lodgings complaining that Pen was turning into such a insufferable little puppy no one would be able to stand him soon — but the truth was, Warrington loved seeing his young friend bright, healthy, and full of hope, the way a grown man long past pantomimes still gets a kick out of watching a kid gape at clowns and harlequins. Pen’s old sullen moods had vanished now that fortune was finally smiling on him, and he was blooming like a rose in summer sun.
