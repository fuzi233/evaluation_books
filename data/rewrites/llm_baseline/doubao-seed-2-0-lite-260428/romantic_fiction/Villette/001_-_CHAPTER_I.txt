My godmother’s handsome, sunlit home stood on a quiet cobbled lane in Bretton: a spotless, centuries-old market town where her late husband’s line had resided for so many generations the family bore the very name of their birthplace—Bretton of Bretton, folks called them. Whether that odd quirk came down to random coincidence, or some far-flung, prominent ancestor grand enough to lend his name to the whole surrounding parish, I never learned for certain.

As a young girl, I visited Bretton twice a year, and I cherished every hour of those stays. The house and everyone in it were made perfectly for my quiet tastes: large peaceful rooms lined with well-polished furniture, clear wide windows framing the cobbled high street below, and a small balcony that looked down over a stretch of old stone homes where Sundays and holidays seemed to linger year-round, so still was the air, so spotless the paved paths. In a house full of grown-ups, one small child tends to be cosseted, and I was no exception. Mrs. Bretton had been left a widow young, with only her son to care for, long before I met her; her husband, a well-loved local physician, had passed when she was barely thirty, still in the full bloom of her youth and good looks. By the time I knew her, she was no longer young, but she remained striking: tall, sturdily built, dark-complexioned for an Englishwoman, with the bright glow of perfect health on her cheeks and a pair of warm, crinkling black eyes that seemed to hold a joke just for you. Everyone who knew the family thought it a crying shame her son had not inherited her dark coloring: John Graham Bretton’s eyes were bright sharp blue, even as a schoolboy, and the long soft fall of his hair was a shade no one could properly name save when the sun caught it, and they called it golden. Still, he got her strong, handsome features, her straight perfect teeth, her height (he was not yet fully grown, but he already promised to match her tall frame), and best of all, that unshakable, even-tempered good humor that is worth more to a person than any vast fortune.

It was the autumn of an unremarkable year when my godmother came in person to fetch me from the distant relatives I was living with permanently, and brought me back to Bretton. I think she already saw trouble brewing on the horizon then, shadows of events I could barely even guess at—though the faint, unnameable hunch that something was about to go very wrong hung over me with a soft, unsettling sadness, and I was gladder than I could say to trade my old home for her quiet warmth. Time at Mrs. Bretton’s side never rushed, never surged or raged: it moved soft and slow, like a full, deep river gliding smooth through flat, green meadows. My stays there felt like the stop Christian and Hopeful made beside that blessed stream in Pilgrim’s Progress, the banks lined with green trees and meadows full of lilies that bloomed all year round. There was no wild novelty, no exciting twist or unexpected incident—but I loved peace so deeply, and craved excitement so little, that when disruption did come, it felt like a rude, jarring disturbance I wished would stay far away.

One afternoon a letter arrived whose contents left Mrs. Bretton clearly surprised, a line of faint concern furrowing her brow. My heart leaped straight to my throat at first, certain it was bad news from my old home, waiting for some terrible announcement that would tear me away from all the calm I loved. But she never spoke of it to me, and after a day the small shadow seemed to pass. The next day, I came back from a long rambling country walk, pushed open my bedroom door, and stopped dead. Next to my own French bed in its shaded nook, there sat a tiny white-draped crib in the far corner, and beside my big mahogany chest of drawers stood a miniature rosewood case. I stood still and stared. A second guest is coming, I realized immediately. Mrs. Bretton is expecting someone new to join us.

At dinner, she explained it all. A little girl would be arriving very soon, she said: the daughter of a dear friend and distant relation of Dr. Bretton’s. She had just lost her mother, she went on, and though the loss was a heavy one, it was less tragic than it might have been: Mrs. Home, the little girl’s mother, had been pretty, giddy, and careless, who had neglected her child and left her gentle, quiet husband disappointed and heartsore. The pair had never been suited to one another at all, and had separated amicably by mutual agreement, no messy legal proceedings involved. Not long after, the young woman had danced too hard at a ball, caught a cold that turned to fever, and died very suddenly. Her husband, a man of soft, sensitive feelings, had been so shattered by the shock of the news that he had convinced himself his own lack of patience, his quiet coldness to his frivolous wife, had helped hasten her death. He brooded over the idea until his spirits were so broken his doctors ordered him to travel abroad to recover, and Mrs. Bretton had stepped in to offer a home to his little girl until he was well enough to come for her. “I only hope the child doesn’t take after her mother,” my godmother added, shaking her head. “Silliest, most frivolous little flirt that ever a sensible man was fool enough to marry. Mr. Home is a clever man, in his way, though he’s hopelessly impractical: spends half his life locked in his laboratory running science experiments, a thing that butterfly wife of his could never understand or bear. To tell the truth, I shouldn’t have liked it much myself.” She went on to tell me that Mr. Home was of mixed Scottish and French descent, his scientific streak inherited from a French savant uncle, and he had distant noble relatives in France who wrote “de” before their names.

That same evening at nine o’clock, our servant Warren was sent down to the coach stop to meet the little girl’s carriage. John Graham was away visiting a school friend who lived out in the country, so it was just me and Mrs. Bretton in the drawing room, she reading her evening paper, I plying my needle over a piece of sewing. It was a wild wet night, rain lashing hard against the windowpanes, the wind howling sharp and restless down the old streets. “Poor child,” Mrs. Bretton murmured every few minutes. “What a terrible night for a little one to travel. I wish she were safe here by the fire.”

Just before ten, the doorbell rang. I ran straight down to the hall before Warren could even shut the front door. There was her small trunk and bandboxes stacked by the wall, her nurse-girl huddled beside them, and Warren holding a tiny shawled bundle tight in his arms. “Is that the child?” I asked. “Yes, miss.” I tried to peek at her face, but she twisted it fast away from me, tucking it tight against Warren’s shoulder. “Put me down, please,” a tiny muffled voice said the second he stepped across the drawing room threshold. She fumbled with the pin fastening the big heavy shawl around her, yanked it out herself, and shrugged the clumsy wrapping off in a rush, then made a deft little attempt to fold it—though the fabric was far too big and heavy for her small hands to manage. “Give that to Harriet, please,” she directed, “and she can put it away.” Then she turned and fixed her big eyes straight on Mrs. Bretton.

“Come here, little dear,” my godmother said, opening her arms. “Come let me see if your hands are cold and damp, come warm yourself by the fire.” The child stepped forward promptly. Dressed in her neat little traveling gown, she was so small she looked no bigger than a porcelain doll: her neck as delicate as wax, her head full of silky light curls, making the resemblance even stronger. Seated on Mrs. Bretton’s ample lap, she was tiny enough to be held all in one arm. Mrs. Bretton cooed soft little fond phrases as she rubbed warmth back into the child’s cold hands and feet, and after a few quiet, wistful moments of staring, the little girl smiled back. Mrs. Bretton was never an overly sentimental, caressing woman—even with her beloved son John Graham, she rarely gushed or fussed—but when the small stranger smiled at her, she kissed her soft forehead, and asked what her name was. “Missy,” the child said, then added, “Papa calls me Polly.” “Will Polly be happy to live with me for a little while?” “Not always,” she said, shaking her head very seriously. “Till Papa comes home. Papa’s gone away. He will come back, or send for me.” Her eyes filled over before she could finish the sentence. She slipped off Mrs. Bretton’s lap before anyone could stop her, dragged a footstool over to the darkest corner of the room, and sat there curled up on it, only the tiny, almost silent occasional sniffle giving away that she was crying, no loud, unashamed wails like other grieving small children would make. Mrs. Bretton didn’t notice, which was for the best. After a little while, a small voice called out from the corner: “May we ring for Harriet?”

I rang the bell, and the nurse came. “Harriet, I must be put to bed,” her little mistress told her. “Ask where my bed is, please.” Harriet said she already knew. “Ask if you sleep with me, Harriet.” “No, Missy,” the nurse said. “You’ll be sharing a room with this young lady here,” she said, nodding at me. Polly didn’t move from her corner, but I saw her eyes dart over to study me, silent, for several long minutes. Then she stepped out of the shadows. “Good night, ma’am,” she said to Mrs. Bretton, then walked straight past me without a word. “Good night, Polly,” I said. “No need to say good night, since we sleep in the same chamber,” she replied, and vanished out of the drawing room. We heard Harriet offer to carry her up the stairs, but the little girl refused again and again, “No need, no need,” and we listened to her small, slow step trudge all the way up the staircase by herself.

An hour later, when I went up to bed, she was still wide awake, propped straight up on her pillows, her small hands folded one over the other on the sheet, with a strange old-fashioned calm no child her age should have. I didn’t speak to her for a long while, but just before I blew out the candle, I told her she ought to lie down before she caught cold. “By and by,” she said. She grabbed a tiny scrap of shawl off the chair by her crib and wrapped it tight around her shoulders, and I let her do as she pleased. When the room went dark, I listened for a long time, and heard her still crying, soft, holding the sobs in so no one would stumble on her grief unawares.

When I woke at dawn, I heard the soft trickle of water. She was already up, climbed on a stool by the washstand, struggling to tip the big heavy ewer she could not possibly lift high enough to pour water into the basin. She washed and dressed all by herself, fumbling with the tiny buttons, strings, and hooks, tackling every difficulty with a quiet stubborn persistence that was lovely to watch. She folded her nightgown perfectly, smoothed the sheets of her little crib till it looked untouched, then slipped behind the fall of the white curtain where I could not see her, and went still. I peeked over the edge of my bed, and found her on her knees, forehead pressed to her hands, praying very softly.

Harriet tapped at the door a moment later, and she jumped to her feet. “I am dressed, Harriet,” she whispered. “I dressed myself, but I don’t feel neat. Make me neat, please.” “Why did you dress yourself, Missy?” “Hush, speak very low,” she said. “You’ll wake the girl.” She nodded at me, where I was pretending to be fast asleep. “I dressed myself to learn, against the time you leave me.” “Do you want me to go?” “When you are cross, I have many times wanted you to go. But not now. Tie my sash straight. Make my hair smooth.” And so she fussed, correcting every crooked part of her dress, till Harriet laughed and said what a particular little thing she was. The pair walked to the door, and Polly stopped, pressing a small fist to her chest. “Oh Harriet, I wish this was papa’s house. I don’t know these people.” “Be a good child, Missy.” “I am good,” she said, her voice trembling, “but I ache here. Papa, papa.”

I roused myself then, and got up, to head the little crisis off before it could get worse. Harriet told her to say good morning to me, and she mumbled the words, then followed the nurse out of the room. Harriet left later that same day to visit her own family who lived nearby. When I went down to the breakfast parlor, I found Paulina Mary—they called her Polly for short—sitting quiet by Mrs. Bretton’s side, a mug of warm milk set in front of her, a piece of bread in her small hand that lay perfectly still on the tablecloth, untouched. She hadn’t eaten a bite, Mrs. Bretton said, and by the dark circles under her eyes, she hadn’t slept either. “I don’t know how on earth we’re going to win this little creature over,” she said to me. I told her time and a little kindness would work its magic. “She’ll settle right in the moment she takes a fancy to someone in this house,” Mrs. Bretton replied. “But not a minute before.”
