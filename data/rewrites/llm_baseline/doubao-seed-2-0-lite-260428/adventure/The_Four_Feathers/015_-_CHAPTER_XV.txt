"I won’t interrupt you," Ethne promised as Willoughby settled into the seat beside her. He had barely gotten two dozen words out before she broke that vow.

"I’m Deputy-Governor of Suakin," he began. "My chief was back in England on leave that May. You’ve never had the misfortune to visit Suakin, Miss Eustace, and you’re lucky for it – least of all in May. No white woman can survive that town long. It brews a sodden, unbreathable heat all its own, the air thick with brine so heavy you can’t draw a full breath, not even to sleep at night. I was sitting on the palace’s first-floor veranda around ten o’clock that night, staring out over the harbour and the distillation works, wondering if it was even worth dragging myself to bed, when a servant told me a man who refused to give his name was desperate to see me. That man was Feversham. There was only one single veranda lamp burning, the night was pitch dark, and I didn’t recognise him until he was standing right at my elbow."

Ethne cut in at once. "How did he look?"

Willoughby frowned, blinked, and hesitated. "Honestly? I can’t rightly say. Much the same as any man who’s spent a year or two in the Soudan, I suppose – a touch gaunt, over-hardened from the sun and the work, that sort of thing."

"Never mind," Ethne sighed, heavy with disappointment. Five whole years had passed since she’d heard a single word of Harry Feversham. She ached for any scrap of news: the turn of a phrase she knew by heart, the flick of a familiar hand gesture, proof he was whole and well. She ached to know if his face had changed, his shoulders broadened, but one glance at Willoughby’s blunt, unobservant face told her she’d get none of those small, precious details. "I beg your pardon. Go on, please."

"I asked him what he wanted, why he’d hidden his name from the gatehouse," Willoughby resumed. "‘You never would have seen me if I’d given it,’ he said, and pulled a packet of letters out of his pocket. Those letters, Miss Eustace, were written years before by General Gordon in Khartum. They’d been carried down the Nile as far as Berber – but the day after the messenger got there, Berber surrendered to the Mahdists. That messenger, Abou Fatma, hid the letters in the inner wall of a house belonging to Yusef, a rock-salt seller in the market. Abou was thrown in prison on suspicion not long after, only escaped to Suakin months later. The letters stayed hidden in that mud wall until Feversham tracked them down. I flipped through them, saw they held no military value at all, and asked him straight out: why would a man who’d run rather than march with his regiment on active service risk torture and certain death to dig some old useless letters out of a Berber wall?"

Standing on that Suakin veranda, the quiet harbour pool glinting before him, Feversham told his story plainly, no dramatics. He spoke of meeting Abou Fatma in the Suakin bazaar, plotting the recovery of the letters, travelling with the messenger all the way to Obak before Abou dared not go a step further, then mounting his scruffy grey donkey and riding on alone for Berber. He did not hide that first hot rush of blind panic that turned his joints to water when he first spotted the low brown walls of the town and the towering date palms lining the Nile, the way he’d broken into a run across the open sun-scorched desert, a shaking, marrowless thing made only of fear. He left one thing unsaid, though: the hours he’d spent curled under his coat draped over his head, staring at the imagined face of a woman thousands of miles away, clinging to the look of quiet sorrow on her features to steel himself to go on.

"He walked straight into Berber as the sun went down," Willoughby said. It was a spare line, but it was more than enough for Ethne Eustace. She drew a deep, shuddering breath of relief, her face softening, a light breaking in her grey eyes, a small smile tugging at her lips.

"He walked into Berber," she repeated softly.

"And found the entire old town had been razed to the ground by the Emir’s order," Willoughby continued. "A new Berber was being built on its southern edge, every last landmark Feversham had memorised gone. Roofs torn off, walls toppled, front facades carted away for building material – narrow alleys of crumbled, roofless mud courts, just like Feversham described them, stretching on for acres, gaped open straight to the stars. A single broken tower jutted up here and there, the last remnant of some wealthy man’s home. There wasn’t a single sign left to mark where Yusef’s little salt seller’s hut had stood. Foxes were already burrowing in the rubble."

The smile faded from Ethne’s face, but she curled her fingers tighter around the white feather resting in her palm, and laughed with deep, quiet contentment. It was dust-yellowed from the desert. It was proof there would be no talk of failure here. "Go on," she said.

Willoughby told of how Feversham sent his negro guide with the donkey back to fetch Abou Fatma at the Wells of Obak. "Feversham stayed in Berber a full fortnight," he went on. "One week he came out to the outer well every dawn to wait for his man to return from Obak, the next week he waited while that guide hunted every corner of the new town for Yusef. I doubt you can even begin to picture what those two weeks must have cost him – the constant gnawing anxiety, the ever-present danger, the split-second expectation that a voice would shout for him to halt, a hand land heavy on his shoulder. He knew if that hand fell, death would be the merciful part of the punishment. I can picture that town: low houses, sand streets pocked with pits where locals dug mud for new building work, no shade anywhere under that blistering, sun-scorched sky. No dark corner to hide, not one. All day the crowds jostled and shouted up and down those streets – that was the Mahdist policy, pack the towns tight so every man spied on his neighbour. Feversham dared not sleep under a roof at night, for fear his British accent would give him away if anyone spoke to him. He could buy bread and dates at the market stalls each day, but he never dared linger to chat. He slept hidden in the crumbled ruins of the old town after dark. For the same reason he couldn’t loiter in the back alleys by day lest a passerby stop to question his business, couldn’t hang around listening for Yusef’s name in the market lest loiterers drag him into their conversation. He couldn’t even creep through the ruins in daylight. From sunrise to sunset he had to march those hot sand streets at a fast clip, like a man on urgent business that could not wait. Two whole weeks of that, Miss Eustace. A wearing, brutal life, is it not? I wish I could tell you it half as vividly as he told me that night on the Suakin palace veranda."

Ethne ached to hear every last detail. Harry Feversham had made that story burn so bright in Willoughby’s mind, that even fifteen months later, this unimaginative, matter-of-fact man was painfully aware how poorly he was reciting it. "The quiet Red Sea harbour stretched below us, African stars hung sharp overhead. Feversham spoke in the quietest tone imaginable, but slow, deliberate, his eyes never leaving my face, like he was forcing me to feel every step he’d taken, understand every fear. Even when he lit the cigar I handed him, he never looked away. That was the first time in four years he’d sat as an equal with a fellow Englishman, he told me. I wish I could remember every single word he said."

Willoughby paused, frowning hard to dredge up more memory, then gave up with a helpless huff. "Well – after two weeks of that cat-and-mouse life in Berber, the guide finally found Yusef. He wasn’t selling salt any more, he was tending a small dhurra crop down by the Nile bank. Yusef gave Feversham the exact directions he needed to find that house in the old ruins, where the letters were hidden in the inner wall. But Yusef couldn’t be trusted any more. Maybe Feversham’s accent slipped. More likely Yusef decided he was a British spy, and thought it wise to confess to Emir Mohammed-el-Kheir before anything went wrong. Whatever the reason, Feversham knew he’d been made the second the man left him. That same night, he went alone into the old ruins."

"Alone?" Ethne breathed. "Go on."

"He found the house six down the narrow alley, just as Yusef had said. The front wall was gone, but the side and back walls still stood. Three feet off the ground, two feet in from the right corner, the letters were buried in that mud brick. Feversham dug at it with his knife, carved a hole big enough to slip his hand inside. The wall was thick, he dug deep, pausing every few seconds to feel for the packet. At last his fingers closed around the wrapped letters, he stuffed them inside the fold of his jibbeh – and a lantern light blazed full on his back."

Ethne jolted in her seat, as if she herself had been trapped there in that dark ruin. The acres of roofless mud courts, the single broken tower jutting against the starry sky, the silent alleys, the distant drumbeats and shouts from new Berber, Harry Feversham alone, his half-redemption in his pocket, and that sudden lantern glow out of the dark – she could see it all so clearly, the feather still warm in her palm, that she could barely believe he’d gotten out alive.

"What happened next?" she whispered.

"He was facing the wall, the light coming at him from the alley behind. He didn’t turn, but out of the corner of his eye he saw the motionless fold of a white Arab robe. He carefully tucked the packet away, his hands steady enough to surprise even himself in that moment. The shock had strung his mind so tight, so clear, he’d never thought faster. His fingers shook as he tied the knots on his jibbeh fold, but it was from sharp, bright excitement, not panic. Every single thought was deliberate, cool, unhurried. He had his knife in his hand. He spun around and ran straight for the man holding the lantern. There were two of them waiting. He ducked the spear jabbed at his chest, beat the spear shaft aside with his left arm, lunged forward and drove his knife home. The Arab fell dead at his feet, the lantern clattered to the ground and went out. Feversham leapt over the body and ran east, straight for the open desert. And he was never once out of control. He’d seen the second soldier chasing him, counted on it. He rounded a corner, crouched behind a half-broken wall, and the second he heard the Arab’s feet pounding past him, he launched himself onto the man’s shoulders and struck again."

Willoughby paused, turning to Ethne, his face perplexed, awed. "The strangest thing about those few wild, brutal minutes – Feversham said he felt no fear at all. I can’t make sense of that, can you? This is the man who was so terrified of the chance of cowardice in battle that he sent his commission in rather than face his regiment. And here he was, staring death right in the face, no fear anywhere."

Willoughby could not understand, but Harry Feversham had. He’d sat on that Suakin veranda, whittling away at the edge of Willoughby’s table with the very knife he’d used to dig the letters out of the wall, the same blade that had tasted Arab blood that night. He’d kept it uncleaned, the rust of dried caked brown over its whole length, clutched it to his chest all through the 240-mile flight across the desert back to Suakin, more precious to him than anything else he owned save for the white feathers. Not just for proof of what he’d done, but to remind himself it had actually happened. When he’d gotten lost in the sandhills outside Obak, all his dates and water gone, parched and half-starved, stumbling only a mile from the Wells, he’d pulled that knife out of his tunic, run his fingers over the rough rust, and it had been a talisman, a reminder that he had not broken when the test came. Long before he spotted the white roofs of Suakin, though, the giddy high of that moment faded, and the knife became an emblem of all the stupid, boyish fears that had led him to resign his commission. He remembered Lieutenant Sutch’s words, spoken years before in the grill room of London’s Criterion Restaurant, quoting Hamlet: "The thing he sees, the thing he thinks over, imagines every step and consequence of – that is what he shrinks from. But when the sharp, sudden moment of action comes, does he fail?" He’d sat there on the Suakin veranda that May night, whittling at the table, his voice bitter, "It was all an illusion. A stupid illusion that hurt the woman I would have given anything to keep from suffering. And I paid for it, didn’t I? It wrecked my own life too."

Willoughby could not parse those words, no more than Feversham’s old general father could have – but Ethne had grown five years older in grief since the night Harry had confessed to her at Lennon House, of his strict military upbringing, of his dead mother, of the dread of disgrace that had haunted his every waking hour.

"I understand," she cried, tears stinging her eyes. "I should have understood years ago, but I was too proud, too narrow, too sure of what cowardice was. It was never the pain he feared. It was the possibility of cowardice, the endless waiting for it, that he ran from. If I’d been a little kinder, a little less quick to judge –" Remorse crashed over her, that sharp, familiar guilt for the fourth feather she had added to the three that had been sent him, the one no one else knew about. She fell quiet, crushed under the weight of it.

Willoughby, stubborn, could not bear to think he’d been wrong. "All those fine distinctions are too vague for the real world," he said. "You can’t run a regiment on fine shades of feeling. The three of us who sent those feathers did no wrong, and if we didn’t, you’ve got no reason to blame yourself."

Ethne flared hot with sudden anger. She’d been too wrapped up in the story to notice the man telling it, but now she looked him over, all his smug obstinate stupidity plain to see. How dare he sit in judgment on Harry Feversham? And then she remembered – she herself had agreed with his judgment, she’d handed Harry that fourth feather. She set her jaw, watching him from the corner of her eye, ready to snap the second he spoke a single condescending word about Feversham again. "Which of you three first thought of sending the feathers?" she demanded, sharp as a knife. "Was it you?"

"Not me. I think it was Trench," he said.

"Trench!" Ethne’s free hand, the one not holding the feather, curled into a fist and slammed into her other palm. "I will remember that name."

"But I take equal responsibility," Willoughby assured her. "I regret we caused you pain, but I don’t admit we were wrong. I’m taking my feather back now, rescinding my accusation. All thanks to you, that is."

"Me?" Ethne blinked. "What do you mean?"

Willoughby looked at her, surprised she didn’t see it. "Any man who’s lived in the Soudan knows women have that great gift of faith and forgiveness. You gave the feathers back to Feversham to give him a reason, a quest to go on and redeem his honour. That’s obvious."

Ethne was on her feet before he finished speaking, standing a pace away, her face turned away, perfectly still. Willoughby, the blunt unthinking man, had struck a sharper, truer point than he ever could have reached using his wits, pointing out a path she could have taken, and had not – a path her remorse told her she ought to have taken. But she would not lie. She would not let him keep that wrong idea. She had failed at charity, at justice, five years before – and that failure had given Harry the chance to do all this great, brave work on his own.

"Say that again," she said, low. "Slowly, please."

"You gave the feathers back to Feversham –"

"He told you that himself?"

"No," Willoughby said. "I guessed. His disgrace was impossible to retrieve, on the face of it. The chance to earn that redemption would almost certainly never come. Even then he waited three full years in the Suakin bazaar for it. It had to be a woman’s faith that dreamed up that plan, a woman’s encouragement that kept him going."

Ethne laughed, turning back to him. Her face was soft with bright, unshakable pride, that light in her eyes making her look almost transfigured. "No," she said, happy and sure. "The plan was all his, every last part. I never encouraged him. Until this very day, I’ve not heard a single word from Harry Feversham since that night of the dance in Donegal. I did not give him the three feathers back. I told him they were his to keep, because I agreed entirely with the accusations they stood for. And worse – I plucked a fourth white feather from my own fan and gave that to him too. I wanted to burn every bridge between us. No more friendly greetings, no more fond memories, nothing. We were to be strangers, for good."

Her voice dropped to a whisper on the last words, ones only Harry and Lieutenant Sutch would ever truly understand. "I grieved for it after I did it.
