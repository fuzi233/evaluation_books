"That doubt and trouble, fear and pain,
And anguish, all, are shadows vain,
That death itself shall not remain;
That weary deserts we may tread,
A dreary labyrinth may thread.
Thro' dark ways underground be led;
Yet, if we will one Guide obey,
The dreariest path, the darkest way
Shall issue out in heavenly day;
And we, on divers shores now cast,
Shall meet, our perilous voyage past,
All in our Father's house at last!"
—R. C. Trench.

As soon as their visitors departed, Margaret hastened upstairs, donning her bonnet and shawl with eager haste. Her destination was clear: she intended to inquire after Betsy Higgins and spend as much time as possible with her before dinner.

Stepping out onto the street, Margaret found the narrow thoroughfares teeming with life. To her, the bustling crowd held a newfound significance, a tangible connection forged simply by learning to care for those who called such cramped quarters home. Inside the Higgins household, Mary Higgins—the younger sister, whose appearance was perpetually untidy—had managed to prepare the room for the anticipated arrival. Despite the efforts, the house bore the marks of its industrial surroundings; the floor remained rough-stone in the center, while the flagstones beneath the chairs and tables, as well as those lining the walls, retained their grimy, unwashed darkness.

Though the day was swelteringly hot, a large fire burned brightly within the grate, transforming the entire room into an oven. Margaret failed to recognize the lavish consumption of coal as a gesture of warm hospitality from Mary; instead, she assumed the stifling heat was medically necessary for Bessy's condition.

Bessy lay upon a small sofa positioned beneath the window. She appeared significantly weaker than the previous day, exhausted from repeatedly rising to peer outside, hoping against hope to spot Margaret's approach. Now that her friend had arrived and seated herself beside her, Bessy sank back into silence, her gaze fixed adoringly on Margaret's face. With childlike fascination, she traced the textures of Margaret's clothing, marveling at their refinement.

"I never understood why the folks in the Bible spoke so highly of soft garments," Bessy remarked. "It must be delightful to walk dressed as you do. It feels entirely different from the ordinary. Most fine clothes tire my eyes with their glaring colors, but somehow, yours bring me peace. Where did you acquire this frock?"

"In London," Margaret replied, amused by the question.

"London! Have you actually been there?"

"Yes. I lived there for several years. Though my true home lies elsewhere—in the countryside, amidst the forests."

"Tell me about it," Bessy urged, leaning back and closing her eyes, crossing her hands over her chest as if ready to absorb every word. "I love hearing about the country, the trees, and such things."

Margaret hesitated. Since leaving Helstone, she had rarely spoken of the village save for incidental mentions. Yet, in her dreams, Helstone appeared more vivid than reality itself. As sleep claimed her at night, her memories drifted effortlessly through its cherished landscapes. Seeing the trust in Bessy's eyes, her heart softened.

"Oh, Bessy, I loved the home we left so dearly! I wish you could see it. I cannot describe half its beauty. Great trees stand everywhere, their branches stretching long and level, casting a deep, cooling shade even at midday. Yet, though every leaf seems still, there is a constant, rushing sound of movement all around—not close at hand. Sometimes the turf is as soft and fine as velvet; other times, it is lush with the perpetual moisture of a tiny, hidden brook nearby, tinkling softly. In other areas, vast stretches of billowy fern rise up—some in green shadow, others bathed in long streaks of golden sunlight, resembling the sea."

"I have never seen the sea," Bessy murmured. "But please, go on."

"And here and there lie wide commons, elevated as if above the very tops of the trees—"

"That is wonderful," Bessy interrupted eagerly. "I felt suffocated down below. Whenever I ventured out, I always craved climbing high to see far away, to take a deep, fulfilling breath of fresh air. I become so smothered in Milton that I need to escape. I imagine the eternal rustling you described among the trees would send me dizzy; it was precisely that sound that gave me a headache in the mill. On these commons, however, there must be little noise?"

"No," Margaret assured her. "Nothing but occasionally a lark singing high overhead. Sometimes I'd hear a farmer shouting sharply at his servants, but it was so distant that it merely brought a pleasant reminder that others were hard at work elsewhere, while I sat on the heather doing nothing."

"I once thought," Bessy continued, her voice trembling slightly, "that if I could have a single day of pure idleness, resting in a quiet place like the one you describe, it might restore me. But now, after many days of forced leisure, I am just as weary of them as I was of my labor. Sometimes I am so utterly drained that I fear I cannot enjoy heaven without a period of earthly rest first. I am somewhat afraid of going straight there without a good sleep in the grave to strengthen me."

"Do not be afraid, Bessy," Margaret said gently, placing her hand over the girl's. "God can grant you a perfection of rest that neither earthly idleness nor the dead sleep of the grave can provide."

Bessy shifted uncomfortably. "I wish Father would not speak to me as he does. He means well, as I told you yesterday, and I tell you again. But you see, though I do not believe him during the day, by night—when I am burning with fever, half-asleep and half-awake—it returns to haunt me. Oh, so terribly! And I think, if this is truly the end of everything, and if all I was born for was to wear out my heart and life, to sicken in this dreadful place with those mill-noises ringing in my ears forever until I scream to be silenced, and to choke on the dust until I thirst for one long, deep breath of the clear air you speak of... and with my mother gone, unable to tell her again how much I loved her, and with all my troubles unresolved..."

Her voice broke, and she sat up abruptly, seizing Margaret's hand with violent, almost fierce intensity.

"You wench, you!" she cried. "If this life is the end, and there is no God to wipe away tears from all eyes—you, I could go mad, and kill you, I could!"

She collapsed back onto the sofa, completely spent by the surge of her passion.

Immediately, Margaret knelt beside her. "Bessy, we have a Father in Heaven."

"I know it! I know it!" Bessy moaned, turning her head restlessly from side to side. "I am very wicked. I have spoken very wickedly. Oh, do not be frightened of me, and never come again. I would not harm a hair of your head. And," she opened her eyes, staring earnestly at Margaret, "I believe, perhaps more than you do, in what is to come. I have memorized the Book of Revelation, and whenever I am awake and in my right mind, I never doubt the glory awaiting me."

"Let us not discuss the fancies that enter your fevered head," Margaret advised kindly. "I would prefer to hear about what you used to do when you were well."

"I think I was well when Mother died," Bessy admitted. "But I have never been truly strong since sometime around that time. I began working in a carding-room shortly thereafter, and the fluff entered my lungs and poisoned me."

"Fluff?" Margaret asked, puzzled.

"Fluff," Bessy repeated. "Tiny bits that fly off the cotton during carding, filling the air until it looks like fine white dust. They say it coils around the lungs and tightens them. Many who work in carding-rooms end up wasting away, coughing and spitting blood, simply poisoned by the fluff."

"But can it not be prevented?" Margaret questioned.

"I do not know," Bessy sighed. "Some factories have a great wheel at one end to create a draft and carry the dust away. But that wheel costs a fortune—perhaps five or six hundred pounds—and yields no profit. Only a few masters dare install them. Moreover, I've heard workers complain that they dislike working where such a wheel exists. After being accustomed to swallowing the dust, they claim the clean air makes them hungry, insisting their wages should be raised to compensate for the hardship of breathing freely. Thus, between the masters and the men, the wheels never get installed. I know I wished ours had one."

"Did your father not know about it?"

"He did," Bessy confirmed. "He was sorry. But our factory was generally a good one, staffed by steady, reliable people. My father feared sending me to a strange place.
