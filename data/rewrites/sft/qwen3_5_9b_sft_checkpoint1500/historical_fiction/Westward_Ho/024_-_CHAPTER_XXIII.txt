Souls that have toil'd, and wrought, and thought with
me—Death closes all: but something ere the end,
Some work of noble note, may yet be done,
Not unbecoming men that strove with gods!"

TENNYSON'S Ulysses.

Nearly three years had elapsed since that devoted handful knelt at vespers beneath the towering tree of Guayra—a span of time seemingly void, traceable only by fragmented notes and misspelled names. Across an expanse of eight hundred miles by four hundred, through uncharted hills and dense forests, they had pursued the Golden City in vain. They had traversed the timbered shores of the Orinoco, crossed the foaming chaos of Maypures, and ascended the upper reaches of the mighty Amazon. They had climbed into Peru itself, trampling the cinchona groves of Loxa, unaware as the world remained of their healing powers. They had gazed upon the virgin snows of Chimborazo, piercing the thunderclouds, and watched the volcanic fury of Cotopaxi spew molten streams before turning eastward. From the Alpine heights of the Andes, they had plunged into "the green and misty ocean of the Montana." Slowly, painfully, they had worked their way northward along the eastern foothills of the inland Cordillera, now bivouacked upon a feeder of the Meta, descending from the Suma Paz into the forest-clad plains. Here sat Amyas and Cary, Brimblecombe, Yeo, and the loyal Indian lad who had shadowed them through all their wanderings—alive and well, yet as distant from Manoa, with its fairy lakes and golden palaces, as ever. Repeated rumors of its existence had sparked fresh expeditions, only to yield fresh disappointments, a hope deferred that gnaws the heart.

Here they sat at last—forty-four men of the original eighty-four who departed Guayra: where were the others?

"Their bones are scatter'd far and wide,
By mount, by stream, and sea."

Drew, the master, lay upon the banks of the Rio Negro, accompanied by five valiant companions slain in combat by poisoned Indian arrows during a futile attempt to breach the Parima mountain gorges. Two more perished in the Andean valleys, frozen by hailstones driven from the condor's eyrie; four succumbed to the rapids of the Orinoco; five or six others, wounded, were abandoned among friendly tribes to be retrieved if fortune should smile—a probability fading with time. Fever, serpents, jaguars, alligators, cannibal fish, and electric eels had whittled their ranks month by month. Of their march through the primordial wilderness, no trail remained save these solitary graves.

Beside the silent stream, beneath the tropic moon, the survivors sat. Sun-burnt and gaunt, yet robust and bold, the quiet fire of English courage burned undimmed in every eye, and genial mirth lingered on every lip. They mocked danger and treated toil as sport, as cheerfully as when sailing the bar of Bideford in a past life. Beards grew down upon their chests; long hair, knotted like women's tresses, shielded them from the blazing sun. Leggings of the delicate Guazu-puti deer, shirts patched with Indian cotton, and trophies of jaguar, puma, and ape adorned their shoulders. Ammunition was exhausted, muskets ruined by the perpetual steam of the woods and left behind near an Orinoco cataract. Yet their swords shone bright and terrible, bows bent to a strength no Indian arm could match, tipped with remnants of armor. Many carried the *pocuna* or blowgun of the natives—far deadlier, and far quieter, than the firearms they had discarded. Thus they wandered, lords of the forest and its beasts, terrifying to hostile Indians, yet kind, just, and generous to those dealing faithfully. Countless Caribs, Atures, Solimos, and Guahibas recounted with wonder the righteousness of these bearded heroes, who declared themselves foes of the faithless Spaniard and messengers of a great and good queen beyond the seas, promised to deliver and avenge the oppressed.

Sleeping among the trees, some on the ground, some in hammocks strung between trunks, all lay silent save for the heavy plunge of a tapir tearing up weeds for his night meal. Occasionally, a jaguar climbing from bough to bough awakened the clustered monkeys, which in turn roused the birds. For ten minutes, unearthly roars, howls, shrieks, and cacklings rang the forest as if pandemonium had broken loose; yet this quickly subsided. Even amidst the din, it served to disturb neither the sleepers nor the council of war proceeding beside the watch-fire between the three adventurers and the faithful Yeo. A hundred times they had convened thus, in vain; this meeting, too, might prove fruitless. Yet it was more solemn than usual: two years, the agreed limit for searching for Manoa, had passed, and a new destination must be chosen unless they intended to spend their remaining days in this verdant wilderness.

"Well," said Will Cary, removing his cigar from his mouth, "at least we have gained something from those last Indians. It is a comfort to puff at tobacco again after three weeks' fasting."

"For me," said Jack Brimblecombe, "Heaven forgive me! when the magical leaf returns to my teeth, I am tempted to sit as still as a chimney and smoke till my dying day, unmoving."

"Then I shall forbid you tobacco, Master Parson," Amyas replied. "We must rise and depart tomorrow. We have idled here three mortal days, accomplishing nothing."

"Shall we ever achieve anything?" Cary asked. "I fear the gold of Manoa resembles the gold where the rainbow touches the earth—a field perpetually beyond reach."

Amyas fell silent, as did the others. Denial was impossible; their hopes were nearly extinguished. Within the immense circle they had traced, they had encountered nothing but disillusionment.

"There is but one final chance," Amyas finally declared. "The mountains east of the Orinoco, where we failed initially. Perhaps the Incas moved on to them upon escaping."

"Why not?" Cary countered. "They would interpose all the forests, the Llanos, and half a dozen great rivers between themselves and those dogs of Spaniards."

"Shall we attempt it once more?" Amyas pressed. "This river must feed the Orinoco; once there, we stand again at the foot of the mountains. What say you, Yeo?"

"I cannot but remind you, your worship," Yeo said, "that when we ascended the Orinoco, the Indians told terrible tales of those mountains—their vast stretch, the difficulty of crossing due to precipitous cliffs and thick valley forests. Have we not already lost five good men there?"

"What care we?" Amyas retorted. "No forests can be denser than those we have already pierced. If one had but a monkey's tail for an extra limb, one might travel a hundred miles along the tree-tops, finding it far pleasanter than wading through vines and being consumed by crawling horrors from morn to night."

"But recall," Jack interjected, "the warnings regarding the Amazons."

"What, Jack, afraid of a group of women?"

"Why not?" Jack insisted. "I would not flee from a man, as you know; but a woman—it is unnatural. They must be witches or devils. Did they not speak of men without necks, with eyes in their breasts? How could a Christian confront such monstrous creatures?"

"He could not sever their heads, certainly; but a rib-cage thrust would suffice for them as for their neighbors."

"Well," Jack conceded, "if I fight, let me fight honest flesh and blood. None of these outlandish monsters. How do you know they are not invulnerable by sorcery?"

"How do you know they are not? And concerning the Amazons," Cary added, "they are women, pure and simple, everywhere. I wager you could wheedle them around with a compliment or two, just as if they were burghers' wives. Pity I lack a court suit and a Spanish hat. I would take an orange in one hand and a handkerchief in the other, go alone as an ambassador, and within a week be as great with Queen Blackfacealinda as ever Raleigh was at Whitehall."

"Gentlemen!" Yeo interrupted. "Where you go, I go; and not only I, but every man of us, I doubt not. Yet we have lost half our company, expended our ammunition, and are no better men than these naked heathens surrounding us, save for our swords. As you know, it was the wonder and noise of their ordnance—and their horses, a break-neck beast I hold no faith in—that enabled both Cortez and Pizarro, those imps of Satan, to secure their golden conquests. If we could have astounded the people of Manoa with similar terrors—"

"Having first found the said people," Amyas laughed. "It echoes the old fable. Every craftsman believes his own trade is the sole pillar of the commonwealth."

"Well! Your worship," Yeo continued, "perhaps as a gunner I overvalue arms. But it requires no slate or pencil to solve this equation: Are forty men without shot as good as eighty with?"
