Jeeves—my man, you understand—is the sort of fellow who makes you feel that Providence, having scattered brains about the globe with a careless hand, afterward sat down and concentrated the remainder into one silent, perfectly brushed individual who knows where everything is, what everything means, and how everything will end.

It’s like this. You meet some chap in the street wearing a suit so dashed correct that you hear harps. You say to yourself, “That’s the suit. That suit is destiny.” You rush home, send for tailors, and, a week later, the thing arrives and you put it on—only to discover you resemble a music-hall turn who has been told to “do sporty” on short notice. Jeeves, meanwhile, has watched the whole operation from the beginning with the calm of a man listening to a kettle that has already begun to sing in a key he dislikes.

“Injudicious, sir,” he says, before the first stitch is sewn.

“Rot,” you say, for you are young and buoyant and do not yet realize you are often wrong on matters of cloth.

Then you catch yourself in the mirror and understand that Jeeves has, once again, been entirely right, and that Life is a mystery, but Jeeves is not.

It’s the same with everything else. Take the time I had what I believed to be a tip about the “Lincolnshire.” It came to me hot, rich, and certain—like a steak that arrives at the table still hissing and telling you it will change your future. The horse was Wonderchild. The name alone sounded like a winner. I wanted to do the decent thing by Jeeves.

“Jeeves,” I said, “if you feel like picking up a little money, have something on Wonderchild.”

He shook his head as if I’d offered him a plate of fish that had been left standing in the sun.

“I’d rather not, sir.”

“But it’s solid. I’m putting my shirt on him.”

“I do not recommend it, sir. The animal is not intended to win. Second place is what the stable is after.”

Which sounded to me like the sort of thing said by pessimists and other blighters who have never really lived. But it happened exactly as he said. Wonderchild led, he flashed past the stands, he had the race in his pocket, he was practically shaking hands with the winning-post—then Banana Fritter came sliding up like a well-timed cream puff and nosed him out.

I went home, called Jeeves, and formally abdicated.

“After this,” I said, “not another step without your advice. Consider yourself the brains of the establishment.”

“Very good, sir. I shall endeavour to give satisfaction.”

He has. And that’s why, when my pal Bruce Corcoran—Corky—came to me one afternoon with his heart in his boots and a girl in tow, my first instinct was not to think, but to ring.

“Leave it to Jeeves,” I said. Because if you’ve got a man who can hear the future the way other fellows hear the ice-man, it’s unsporting not to use him.

I’d known Corky since my first season in New York. I’d stayed on in America for reasons involving my Aunt Agatha and a family tangle with the stage—details of which are best swallowed quickly, like an unwise oyster. New York is a topping place to be exiled in, and I fell in among two sets: the chaps who have houses up by the Park and money that comes in like an elevator, and the chaps down Washington Square who have ideas, beards, and the gas turned down.

Corky belonged to the second brigade. He was an artist—portrait-painter, technically—though, so far as I could make out, portraits were shy game and refused to come within range of his brush. He scraped along doing comic-paper drawings and bits for advertisements—chairs, bedsteads, and those smiling families who look as if they’ve just swallowed the product and can no longer communicate in ordinary human speech.

His main supply line, however, was a rich uncle: Alexander Worple, a jute magnate. Jute, to my mind, sounds like something one removes from a dog’s paw, but it had apparently built this old boy a fortune large enough to make you blink. Corky’s idea was that a rich uncle ought to be a sort of pleasant agricultural elder: one who sits on a porch somewhere, tasting peaches, telling you how your grandfather used to salt pork, and then pressing money into your palm with a tear in his eye. But Alexander Worple was not that type. He was robust. He was fifty-one and looked the sort of man who would live to ninety and sue the undertaker for libel.

Worse, he was convinced Corky had no talent and that Art was a disease caught by sitting in draughts. He wanted Corky in jute—starting at the bottom. Corky, whose imagination supplied horrors whenever anybody said “the bottom,” fought a steady, three-monthly campaign for a modest allowance, using the one lever that worked.

Mr. Worple’s hobby was ornithology.

Now, I’ve noticed the American captain of industry, as a rule, stops existing when business hours end. He locks the office, becomes an empty shell, and is rebooted next morning by the sight of a ledger. But not old Worple. In his spare time he studied birds. He had written a book called American Birds and was working on another called More American Birds, with every sign of continuing until there were no American birds left unclassified and complimented. Corky’s quarterly allowance depended on sitting with him while he talked about warblers and cuckoos until the room seemed full of wings and the air full of chirruping.

Birds, Corky said, bored him stiff—except, naturally, when broiled and accompanied by a cold bottle. But he endured them because he needed rent and paint.

Old Worple had, in addition, one of those tempers that sound like a factory whistle—sudden, deafening, and liable to go off whenever you think you’re safe. His general belief was that Corky was an idiot, and that any independent action on Corky’s part proved it. It struck me that Jeeves likely held the same view of me, only with better manners.

So when Corky turned up at my place that afternoon, shepherding a girl ahead of him like a man bringing home something fragile and expensive, and said:

“Bertie, I want you to meet my fiancée, Miss Singer,”

the first thing out of my mouth was not congratulations. It was:

“Corky—how about your uncle?”

Corky gave a laugh with no mirth in it. He looked like a chap who has successfully committed the crime but is now worrying about the suitcase.

“We’re so scared, Mr. Wooster,” said the girl. “We were hoping you might suggest a way of breaking it to him.”

Muriel Singer was the quiet sort, with big eyes that make a fellow feel like a bulldog that has accidentally sat on a kitten. She sat there as if she expected the furniture to bite her. And you know the type: she doesn’t need to raise her voice. She just looks at you and suddenly you’re prepared to rescue villages, fight duels, and buy inconveniently expensive things. It’s like certain American drinks—pleasant, innocent-tasting concoctions that creep into you gently and then, without warning, you find yourself challenging a large man in a corner because you disapprove of his attitude to life.

“I don’t see why your uncle shouldn’t be bucked,” I said to Corky. “He’ll think Miss Singer the ideal wife.”

Corky didn’t brighten.

“You don’t know him. Even if he liked Muriel, he wouldn’t admit it. He’d kick on principle. All he’d see is that I’ve taken an important step without asking him. That’s enough to make him raise Cain.”

I concentrated hard enough to smell burning.

“Well,” I said, “you want him to meet Miss Singer without knowing you know her. Then you come along afterward and spring the thing—”

“But how can I arrange that?”

Quite. That was the snag, like finding the perfect recipe and discovering the market’s out of your key ingredient.

“There’s only one thing to do,” I said.

“What’s that?”

“Leave it to Jeeves.”

And I rang.

“Sir?” said Jeeves, arriving in that soundless way of his. You rarely see Jeeves enter a room. One moment there’s empty space and your own ignorance; the next moment there’s Jeeves, as if he has condensed out of the very atmosphere. My cousin who’s a Theosophist once told me men can do it by spiritual training and a strict diet. He may be right, though in his case the strict diet appears to have consisted of pie.

The instant Jeeves was in the room, my mind stopped skidding about and settled. It’s like hearing a steady, well-judged household sound—the low tick of a good clock, the soft sigh of curtains in a sane breeze—after spending the day among street-drills and motor horns. Order returned.

“Jeeves, we want your advice.”

“Very good, sir.”

I outlined Corky’s trouble: uncle, temper, jute, birds, engagement, terror.

“So you see what it amounts to, Jeeves. We want Mr. Worple to make Miss Singer’s acquaintance without twigging that Corky already knows her.”

“Perfectly, sir.”

“Well, think of something.”

“I have thought of something already, sir.”

“You have!”

“The scheme I would suggest cannot fail of success, but it has what may seem a drawback, sir, in that it requires a certain financial outlay.”

“He means,” I translated for Corky, “he’s got a rattling notion, but it costs.”

Corky’s face drooped. The poor chap always lived a bit close to the bone.

“You can count on me, Corky,” I said. The girl’s eyes were still doing that thing. “Glad to help. Carry on, Jeeves.”

“I would suggest, sir, that Mr. Corcoran take advantage of Mr. Worple’s attachment to ornithology.”

“How on earth did you know he was fond of birds?” I said.

“It is the way these New York apartments are constructed, sir. The partitions are of a flimsy nature. Without wishing to overhear, I have sometimes heard Mr. Corcoran expressing himself with a generous strength on the subject.”

“Oh. Yes. Well?”

“Why should not the young lady write a small volume, to be entitled—let us say—The Children’s Book of American Birds, and dedicate it to Mr. Worple? A limited edition could be published at your expense, sir. A considerable portion of the text would be given over to eulogistic remarks concerning Mr. Worple’s own larger treatise. A presentation copy could be dispatched to him, accompanied by a letter in which the young lady asks permission to make the acquaintance of one to whom she owes so much.”

You could have knocked me down with a feather—appropriate, considering. The man was a marvel. He’d taken the raw ingredients of Corky’s misery, added tact, stirred in flattery, and produced a soufflé that looked as if it would rise to the ceiling.

“Jeeves,” I said, “that is absolutely ripping. One of your best.”

“Thank you, sir.”

Muriel looked distressed.

“But I’m sure I couldn’t write a book about anything. I can’t even write good letters.”

“Muriel’s talents,” said Corky, with a cough, “run more to the drama, Bertie. She’s in the chorus of Choose your Exit at the Manhattan. And that’s another reason we’re nervous. Uncles—especially my uncle—don’t like the stage.”

I understood. In my own family there’d been enough fuss when I once tried to marry into musical comedy. And uncles and aunts, as a class, treat the theatre as if it were a contagious disease carried on greasepaint.

But Jeeves, of course, had thought of the rest.

“I fancy it would be a simple matter, sir, to find some impecunious author who would be glad to do the actual composition for a small fee. It is only necessary that the young lady’s name should appear on the title page.”

“That’s true,” said Corky, reviving. “Sam Patterson would do it for a hundred dollars. He turns out a novelette, three short stories, and a chunk of serial under different names every month. This would be nothing.”

And that was that. Jeeves withdrew, Corky departed, and I—by a natural process—became a publisher. I used to imagine publishers were men of massive intellect, with minds like steel traps and hearts like ink bottles. It appears, on the contrary, that the essential publisher is a chap with a fountain-pen who writes cheques while other fellows do the work.

In due time the book arrived.

I happened to be at Corky’s place when the first copies of The Children’s Book of American Birds were delivered with a bang at the door, as if the parcel were trying to escape. Muriel was there. Corky tore the wrapping like a man opening his own verdict.

It was a shiny, respectable little volume, with a red cover and a bird on it—some confident, well-fed specimen that looked as if it had never had to earn an allowance by listening to lectures. Muriel Singer’s name shone in gold.

I opened at random. The pages smelled of fresh ink and paper—newly born book, as crisp as toast.

“Often of a spring morning,” it said, “as you wander through the fields, you will hear the sweet-toned, carelessly flowing warble of the purple finch linnet. When you are older you must read all about him in Mr. Alexander Worple’s wonderful book—American Birds.”

It went on like that—little domestic scenes with birds piping away in clean air, and then, like a refrain in an old family song, Mr. Alexander Worple’s name, Mr. Alexander Worple’s authority, Mr. Alexander Worple’s wonderful book. Every few pages the old boy was being crowned afresh, garlanded with adjectives, and set on a pedestal next to the yellow-billed cuckoo.

“It’s a cert,” I said.

“An absolute cinch,” said Corky.

And it was. A day or two later Corky breezed up to my apartment looking as if somebody had removed a piano from his chest. The uncle had written Muriel a letter so warmly human Corky said he’d have suspected forgery if he hadn’t known the handwriting. Any time Miss Singer cared to call, Mr. Worple would be delighted to meet her.

Soon after that, I went out of town—country-house visits, good fellows, and so forth—and it wasn’t for several months that I settled back in New York.

Naturally, I’d wondered how the whole thing had turned out. So, my first evening back, I went to a quiet little restaurant I sometimes use when I’m not in the mood for places where the band tries to outshout the cutlery. There, by the door, I saw Muriel Singer sitting alone.

I approached.

“Well, well, well, what?” I said.

“Why, Mr. Wooster! How do you do?”

“Corky around?”

“I beg your pardon?”

“You’re waiting for Corky, aren’t you?”

“Oh—no. I’m not waiting for him.”

There was something in her voice. A sort of—thing. Like when a familiar tune is played in a different key and you can’t place why it makes you uneasy.

“I say—have you had a row with Corky?”

“A row?”

“A spat. A little misunderstanding. Faults on both sides. That sort of thing.”

“Why, whatever makes you think that?”

“Well—I thought you usually dined with him before the theatre.”

“I’ve left the stage now.”

It all came over me.

“Why, of course! You’re married!”

“Yes.”

“How perfectly topping! I wish you all kinds of happiness.”

“Thank you so much. Oh, Alexander,” she said, looking past me, “this is a friend of mine—Mr. Wooster.”

I turned.

There stood a formidable chap with stiff grey hair and a red, healthy, vigorous face—the sort of face that looks as if it enjoys early mornings and disapproves of nonsense. He seemed peaceful enough at the moment, but I could imagine him, if crossed, producing a noise like a freight elevator giving up.

“I want you to meet my husband, Mr. Wooster. Mr. Wooster is a friend of Bruce’s, Alexander.”

The old boy took my hand warmly. It was the only thing that kept me upright.

“So you know my nephew, Mr. Wooster,” he said. “I wish you would try to knock a little sense into him and make him quit this playing at painting. But I have an idea he is steadying down. I noticed it first that night he came to dinner with us, my dear, to be introduced to you. He seemed altogether quieter and more serious. Something appeared to have sobered him. Perhaps you will give us the pleasure of your company at dinner to-night, Mr. Wooster? Or have you dined?”

I said I had dined. What I wanted was not food but oxygen and open space in which to pace like a man who has just been struck by a falling safe.

I went straight home. Somewhere inside, Jeeves was moving quietly—tidying the universe.

“Jeeves,” I said, “now is the time for all good men to come to the aid of the party. A stiff b. and s. first, and then I’ve news for you.”

He returned with a tray and a long glass. I took a pull like a man trying to refill his courage.

“Better have one yourself, Jeeves. You’ll need it.”

“Later on, perhaps, thank you, sir.”

“All right. But you’re going to get a shock. You remember Mr. Corcoran?”

“Yes, sir.”

“And the girl who was going to slide into his uncle’s esteem by writing the bird book?”

“Perfectly, sir.”

“Well, she’s slid. She’s married the uncle.”

Jeeves received this as he receives everything: without a blink, without a gasp, without so much as a ruffle in the mental nap of his mind.

“That was always a development to be feared, sir.”

“You don’t mean to tell me you expected it?”

“It crossed my mind as a possibility.”

“By Jove! You might have warned us.”

“I hardly liked to take the liberty, sir.”

After I’d had something to eat and my nerves stopped behaving like a kitchen full of pans, I could see it wasn’t my fault. How was I to foresee that a plan, sound as a well-made gravy, would go wrong because the ingredients developed independent passions? But all the same, the thought of meeting Corky again made me squirm. I avoided Washington Square entirely for months—gave it the complete miss-in-baulk—until, just as I began to think I might safely risk showing my face among the arty coves, Fate went and over-seasoned the dish.

I opened the paper one morning and read that Mrs. Alexander Worple had presented her husband with a son and heir.

I was so dashed sorry for poor old Corky I couldn’t touch my breakfast. I told Jeeves to drink it himself. I felt as if the universe had sat down, cleared its throat, and made a noise at Corky.

For a while I wavered between charging down to his studio to grip his hand in manly silence and staying away because I hadn’t the nerve to watch his face. I tried absent treatment—sent sympathy in waves. But after a month or so, it struck me that this was poor form. A chap wants his pals when the world has handed him one in the solar plexus. I pictured Corky alone in his studio with nothing but paint, dust, and his thoughts, and the picture got at me.

So I bounded into a taxi and told the driver to go like a steam-engine for Corky’s place.

I rushed in without warning, full of brave comradeship—and stopped short.

Corky was hunched at his easel, painting for all he was worth. And on the model-throne sat a severe middle-aged woman holding a baby.

You have to be ready for these things.

“Oh—ah!” I said, backing out.

Corky looked over his shoulder.

“Halloa, Bertie. Don’t go. We’re just finishing for the day. That will be all this afternoon,” he added to the woman.

She rose, took the baby with the efficiency of a woman who can carry an heir as easily as a basket of laundry, and decanted it into a perambulator standing in the fairway.

“At the same hour to-morrow, Mr. Corcoran?”

“Yes, please.”

“Good afternoon.”

“Good afternoon.”

Corky watched the door close, then turned to me. His face had that set look of a man who has stored up a great deal and is about to begin ladling it out. He seemed to assume I knew everything—which, thanks to newspapers and my own idiotic innocence, I did.

And he began to get it off his chest.

I’m a bit hazy on my authorities—Shakespeare, or possibly one of those other fellows who wrote so much they must have done it standing up—but I’m positive somebody with a large brain once observed that Fate never waits till you’re merely comfortable. It waits till you’re feeling offensively on top of things, then steps out from behind the nearest potted plant and wallops you with something solid.

That, if you want to know, was precisely my position on the morning Lady Malvern arrived.

The day had started in the kind of brilliant, rinsed-blue fashion that makes a man forgive the existence of motor-buses. I had come out from under a cold shower feeling as if my system had been polished with a wire brush and tuned like a violin. I was towelling off and humming—very softly, because I don’t like to frighten the plumbing—when the sense of wellbeing was sharpened by another item: the night before I had asserted myself with Jeeves.

Absolutely asserted myself.

The man had been managing me like a benevolent despot. I hadn’t greatly minded when he turned down one of my newer suits—his eye for cloth is, dash it, uncanny—but the moment he began taking a strong line about my cloth-topped boots (which I loved like brothers, and which had never harmed anybody), I began to see I was sliding into something serf-like. And then came the Hat Question. Jeeves wanted me in a Longacre, as worn by John Drew. I had set my heart on the Country Gentleman, as worn by another actor chap. There was a scene. There were moments when I thought we might have to call in the League of Nations. In the end I bought the Country Gentleman. Victory. Freedom. A man among men.

So there I was, towelling the good old spine and wondering with a glow what Jeeves had lined up for breakfast—because, whatever else you can say about Jeeves, the man understands a morning. He can make a kidney behave as if it has dreams and a future. He can send up toast that cracks like autumn leaves. Even the coffee at his hands comes out smelling like dark, civilized forests, and it makes the spoon ring in a pleasing, domestic sort of way against the cup—none of your modern, shrieking, machinery racket.

I was thinking of that agreeable clink, in fact, when there came a tap at the bathroom door.

I stopped the song and opened the door an inch.

“What ho without there!”

“Lady Malvern wishes to see you, sir,” said Jeeves.

“Eh?”

“Lady Malvern, sir. She is waiting in the sitting-room.”

I gave him the look.

“Pull yourself together, Jeeves, my man. You know perfectly well there’s no one waiting for me in the sitting-room. How could there be when it’s barely ten o’clock yet?”

“I gathered from her ladyship, sir, that she had landed from an ocean liner at an early hour this morning.”

That did make it sound a shade less like delirium. I remembered my own arrival in America, when they shot us out of the ship at an hour when a decent person is still asleep and the sparrows are only just clearing their throats.

“Who the deuce is Lady Malvern, Jeeves?”

“Her ladyship did not confide in me, sir.”

“Is she alone?”

“Her ladyship is accompanied by a Lord Pershore, sir. I fancy that his lordship would be her ladyship’s son.”

“Oh, well—put out rich raiment of sorts, and I’ll be dressing.”

“Our heather-mixture lounge is in readiness, sir.”

“Then lead me to it.”

While I was getting into the shirt and feeling about for studs, it began to come back to me, like a tune you don’t want.

“I’ve placed her, Jeeves,” I said. “She’s a pal of my Aunt Agatha.”

“Indeed, sir?”

“Yes. Met her at lunch one Sunday before I left London. Vicious specimen. Writes books. She wrote a book on social conditions in India when she came back from the Durbar.”

“Yes, sir? Pardon me, sir, but not that tie.”

“Eh?”

“Not that tie with the heather-mixture lounge, sir.”

It hit me like a cold sausage. I had supposed the man chastened by last night’s Hat Affair, but here he was, back in the saddle, giving my cheerful pink tie the sort of tone a man reserves for a suspicious oyster.

It was a solemn moment. If I weakened now, all my heroism would crumble like pastry in hot soup.

“What’s wrong with this tie?” I demanded. “I’ve seen you give it a nasty look before. Speak out like a man! What’s the matter with it?”

“Too ornate, sir.”

“Nonsense. A cheerful pink. Nothing more.”

“Unsuitable, sir.”

“Jeeves, this is the tie I wear.”

“Very good, sir.”

That “Very good, sir” had the chill of a shut larder. I could see he was wounded. Still, I was firm. I tied the tie, got into coat and waistcoat, and went in to face Fate and Lady Malvern.

“Halloa! Halloa! Halloa!” I said, because one needs something to cling to. “What?”

“Ah! How do you do, Mr. Wooster? You have never met my son, Wilmot, I think? Motty, darling, this is Mr. Wooster.”

Lady Malvern was one of those hearty, happy, healthy women who give you the feeling they were built in a factory to withstand naval gunfire. Not so very tall, but broad in a way that suggested she might have been measured with a tape used on prize cattle. She filled my largest arm-chair with the air of someone who had always intended to. Bright, bulging eyes; a mass of yellow hair; and when she smiled she showed enough front teeth to make you wonder if she had been on the stage at one time as a special effect.

She made me feel ten years old and newly washed.

Motty, the son, was about twenty-three, tall and thin and meek-looking, with the same yellow hair plastered down and parted in the middle as if it had been ironed. His eyes bulged too, but without the liveliness—dull grey, pink-rimmed, like a tired fish. His chin seemed to have abandoned the enterprise half-way. He had no eyelashes to speak of. He sat there sucking the knob of his stick with a sort of furtive resignation.

“Awfully glad to see you,” I said. “So you’ve popped over, eh? Making a long stay in America?”

“About a month. Your aunt gave me your address and told me to be sure and call on you.”

That sounded promising, because Aunt Agatha and I had not parted on a note of harmony. About a year before, she had sent me to New York to rescue my Cousin Gussie from a girl on the music-hall stage, and by the time I had finished, Gussie had married the girl and gone on the stage himself. Doing well. Aunt Agatha had not seen the humour. I had consequently remained in America with the caution of a man avoiding a certain kind of umbrella.

So hearing that she was telling her friends to call on me made me believe time had softened her, and England might one day be navigable again.

“Your aunt said,” Lady Malvern proceeded, “that you would do anything that was in your power to be of assistance to us.”

“Rather! Oh, rather! Absolutely!”

“Thank you so much. I want you to put dear Motty up for a little while.”

I didn’t grasp this at once.

“Put him up? For my clubs?”

“No, no! Darling Motty is essentially a home bird. Aren’t you, Motty darling?”

Motty uncorked himself from the stick.

“Yes, mother,” he said, and corked himself up again.

“I should not like him to belong to clubs. I mean put him up here. Have him to live with you while I am away.”

The words flowed out as sweetly as jam and had about the same effect if you tried to breathe them. It took a moment for the full horror to get around my system.

The woman didn’t seem to see anything the matter. She rolled on, placid as a steamroller.

“I am leaving New York by the midday train, as I have to pay a visit to Sing-Sing prison. I am extremely interested in prison conditions in America. After that I work my way gradually across to the coast, visiting the points of interest on the journey. You see, Mr. Wooster, I am in America principally on business. No doubt you read my book, India and the Indians? My publishers are anxious for me to write a companion volume on the United States. I shall not be able to spend more than a month in the country, as I have to get back for the season, but a month should be ample. I was less than a month in India, and my dear friend Sir Roger Cremorne wrote his America from Within after a stay of only two weeks. I should love to take dear Motty with me, but the poor boy gets so sick when he travels by train. I shall have to pick him up on my return.”

From where I was sitting I could see Jeeves in the dining-room laying the breakfast-table with the quiet precision of a man arranging artifacts for posterity. I yearned to get him alone for ten seconds. Jeeves, if properly approached, can often produce a solution like a hot consommé: clear, final, and leaving you warmer. But Lady Malvern gave no gaps.

“It will be such a relief to know Motty is safe with you, Mr. Wooster. I know what the temptations of a great city are. Hitherto dear Motty has been sheltered from them. He has lived quietly with me in the country. I know that you will look after him carefully, Mr. Wooster. He will give very little trouble.” She discussed him as if he were a parcel being forwarded. Motty gazed at the wall, mouth slightly open. “He is a vegetarian and a teetotaller and is devoted to reading. Give him a nice book and he will be quite contented.”

She rose. The chair sighed as if relieved.

“Thank you so much, Mr. Wooster! I don’t know what I should have done without your help. Come, Motty! We have just time to see a few of the sights before my train goes. But I shall have to rely on you for most of my information about New York, darling. Be sure to keep your eyes open and take notes of your impressions! It will be such a help. Good-bye, Mr. Wooster. I will send Motty back early in the afternoon.”

They went out. The door shut with a sound I shall always associate with doom. I howled for Jeeves.

“Jeeves! What about it?”

“Sir?”

“What’s to be done? You heard it all, didn’t you? You were in the dining-room most of the time. That pill is coming to stay here.”

“Pill, sir?”

“The excrescence.”

“I beg your pardon, sir?”

I looked at him sharply. It wasn’t like Jeeves to pretend not to understand simple English. Then it came to me. The tie. The man was still in mourning for the Longacre.

“Lord Pershore will be staying here from to-night, Jeeves,” I said coldly.

“Very good, sir. Breakfast is ready, sir.”

I could have sobbed into the bacon and eggs. There are bacons in this world so nobly fried, eggs so sunny-side-up and optimistic, that it seems a sin to damp them with human tears—but I could have done it. Jeeves was offering not one ounce of pity, and it was that which gave the thing its final sting.

Still, I didn’t capitulate. I ate. The fork scraped the plate with a brave little domestic sound, and the coffee smelt magnificent and uncaring. Outside, New York roared as usual: the far-off grind and shriek of modern life, like somebody trying to play a symphony on a set of loose gates. One does sometimes feel there ought to be laws about noise—real laws, not the sort that say “Honk if you feel artistic.” There’s something in the quiet crackle of toast and the gentle clink of crockery that keeps the soul from going savage.

But my soul, I regret to say, was going savage anyway.

By noon Motty’s luggage arrived, and shortly after that a large parcel of what I assumed to be the “nice books.” It was massive, the sort of parcel that suggests whole shelves of improving literature and a youth safely muzzled for months. That cheered me. I felt almost jaunty, put on the Country Gentleman hat with a defiant tilt, gave the pink tie a twist, and went out to lunch with one or two of the lads at a neighbouring place.

The lunch was sound—excellent browsing and sluicing and cheery conversation. There are certain grilled things, done properly, that can make a man forget he’s been sentenced. When I got back to my own doorstep, by dinner-time I had almost erased Motty from my mind.

I dined at the club and looked in at a show afterwards, and it wasn’t till fairly late that I returned to the flat. No sign of Motty. I assumed he had gone to bed with his books and his principles.

Then I noticed the parcel of “nice books” was still lying there, unopened, in its string and paper. That looked rummy. It suggested Motty, after seeing his mother off, had decided not to sully the day with reading.

Jeeves came in with the nightly whisky-and-soda. His manner indicated continued frost.

“Lord Pershore gone to bed, Jeeves?” I asked, with reserved hauteur.

“No, sir. His lordship has not yet returned.”

“Not returned? What do you mean?”

“His lordship came in shortly after six-thirty, and, having dressed, went out again.”

At that moment there was a noise outside the front door—a scrabbling, pawing sound, as if somebody were trying to burrow through civilisation. Then a thud.

“Better go and see what that is, Jeeves.”

“Very good, sir.”

He went out, and came back with the calm of a man returning from inspecting the weather.

“If you would not mind stepping this way, sir, I think we might be able to carry him in.”

“Carry him in?”

“His lordship is lying on the mat, sir.”

I went to the front door. There, huddled like a discarded coat, was Motty. He was moaning.

“He’s had some sort of dashed fit,” I said. Then, as a bright idea struck me: “Jeeves! Someone’s been feeding him meat!”

“Sir?”

“He’s a vegetarian, you know. He must have been digging into a steak or something. Call up a doctor!”

“I hardly think it will be necessary, sir. If you would take his lordship’s legs, while I—”

“Great Scot, Jeeves!” I said, as the true explanation began to unfold like a cheap map. “You don’t think—he can’t be—”

“I am inclined to think so, sir.”

And by Jove, he was right. Once you’ve seen it, you can’t mistake it. Motty was under the surface.

It was the deuce of a shock.

“You never can tell, Jeeves!”

“Very seldom, sir.”

“Remove the eye of authority and where are you?”

“Precisely, sir.”

“Where is my wandering boy to-night and all that sort of thing, what?”

“It would seem so, sir.”

“Well, we had better bring him in, eh?”

“Yes, sir.”

So we lugged him in. Jeeves put him to bed with the efficiency of a field-hospital and without so much as a raised eyebrow. I lit a cigarette and sat down to think, with the gloomy certainty that I had stepped onto a moving staircase that only went down.

Next morning, after a thoughtful cup of tea—tea is a sound thing in crises; it has a quiet hiss as it pours, like a sensible person saying “steady”—I went into Motty’s room to investigate.

I expected wreckage. Instead, there was Motty sitting up in bed, chirpy as a sparrow, reading gingery stories as if he’d been born in a smoking-room.

“What ho!” I said.

“What ho!” said Motty.

“What ho! What ho!”

“What ho! What ho! What ho!”

After that, conversation was, as you might say, in need of a cue.

“How are you feeling this morning?” I asked.

“Topping!” said Motty, beaming. “I say, you know, that fellow of yours—Jeeves, you know—is a corker. I had a most frightful headache when I woke up, and he brought me a sort of rummy dark drink, and it put me right again at once. Said it was his own invention. I must see more of that lad. He seems to me distinctly one of the ones!”

I stared. This was not the same chap who yesterday had sat sucking his stick like a nervous cow.

“You ate something that disagreed with you last night, didn’t you?” I said tactfully, giving him a sporting chance to slide out.

“No!” said Motty, with firmness. “I didn’t do anything of the kind. I drank too much! Much too much. Lots and lots too much! And, what’s more, I’m going to do it again! I’m going to do it every night. If ever you see me sober, old top,” he added with a kind of holy glow, “tap me on the shoulder and say, ‘Tut! Tut!’ and I’ll apologize and remedy the defect.”

“But I say, you know—what about me?”

“What about you?”

“Well, I’m—so to speak—as it were—kind of responsible for you. If you go doing this sort of thing I’m apt to get in the soup somewhat.”

“I can’t help your troubles,” said Motty. “Listen to me, old thing: this is the first time in my life I’ve had a real chance to yield to the temptations of a great city. What’s the use of a great city having temptations if fellows don’t yield to them? Makes it so bally discouraging for a great city. Besides, mother told me to keep my eyes open and collect impressions.”

I sat on the edge of the bed. The room felt as if it had begun to spin very slowly.

“I know just how you feel, old dear,” said Motty kindly. “And if my principles would permit it, I would simmer down for your sake. But duty first! This is the first time I’ve been let out alone, and I mean to make the most of it. We’re only young once. Why interfere with life’s morning? Young man, rejoice in thy youth! Tra-la! What ho!”

Put like that, it did sound, in a way, almost reasonable—like hearing a man explain why he must set fire to his own trousers.

“All my bally life,” Motty went on, “I’ve been cooped up in the ancestral home at Much Middlefold, in Shropshire, and till you’ve been cooped up in Much Middlefold you don’t know what cooping is! The only time we get any excitement is when one of the choir-boys is caught sucking chocolate during the sermon. When that happens, we talk about it for days. I’ve got about a month of New York, and I mean to store up a few happy memories for the long winter evenings. This is my only chance to collect a past, and I’m going to do it. Now tell me, old sport, as man to man, how does one get in touch with that very decent chappie Jeeves? Does one ring a bell or shout a bit? I should like to discuss the subject of a good stiff b.-and-s. with him!”

I had entertained a vague idea that if I stuck close to Motty and escorted him about, I might act as a sort of moral sandbag. I thought if, in the middle of revelry, he caught my reproving eye, he might ease up.

So next night I took him to supper with me.

It was the last time. I’m a quiet, peaceable chappie, raised among London habits, and I can’t stand the pace these rural sportsmen set when they break loose. I’m all for rational enjoyment, but a fellow makes himself conspicuous when he throws soft-boiled eggs at an electric fan. And I don’t object to decent mirth, but I do bar dancing on tables and having to dodge waiters, managers, and chuckers-out just when a man’s system is trying to concentrate on digesting.

When I managed to detach myself and get home, I made up my mind that I was jolly well done with accompanying Motty on his cultural tours. After that, the only time I met him late at night was once when I passed the door of a fairly low-down restaurant and had to step aside to dodge him as he sailed through the air en route for the opposite pavement, with a muscular-looking chappie peering out after him with a kind of sombre satisfaction.

In a way, one couldn’t help sympathizing. The poor blighter had four weeks to compress what ought to have been ten years of harmless dissipation. Still, with Lady Malvern and Aunt Agatha looming in the background like a pair of ancient family curses, I couldn’t get rid of the feeling that sooner or later I was the one scheduled to get it behind the ear.

So there I sat, night after night, listening for the familiar footstep, becoming jumpy at sudden noises. Modern New York has plenty of those. Even at midnight you get the long-drawn wail of distant traffic, the clatter of somebody’s careless footsteps on stone, a taxi horn that sounds like a dying sea-beast. In quieter places you can measure a life by the sounds it makes—kettle, knife on board, a chair drawn back, a friendly murmur. Here everything was clang and shriek and commercial insistence, as if the city was forever trying to sell itself to your ears. It does something to a man’s nerves.

And Jeeves offered no sympathy whatever. The man was still sore about the hat and tie, and refused to rally round.

One morning I wanted comforting so much I swallowed the Wooster pride and appealed direct.

“Jeeves,” I said, “this is getting a bit thick!”

“Sir?” He was business and cold respectfulness.

“You know what I mean. This lad seems to have chucked all the principles of a well-spent boyhood. He has got it up his nose!”

“Yes, sir.”

“Well, I shall get blamed, don’t you know. You know what my Aunt Agatha is!”

“Yes, sir.”

“Very well, then.”

I waited. He didn’t unbend an eyelash.

“Jeeves,” I said, “haven’t you any scheme up your sleeve for coping with this blighter?”

“No, sir.”

And he shimmered off to his lair. Obstinate devil. All because of a perfectly priceless Country Gentleman hat and a tie that cheered the day like salmon.

It was shortly after this that Motty got the idea of bringing pals back in the small hours to continue the revels in the home, which is where I began to crack. You see, the part of town where I lived wasn’t the right place for that sort of thing. Down Washington Square way they start evenings at about two in the morning—artists and writers and what-not—frolicking till the milk arrives. There the neighbours can’t sleep unless someone is practising Hawaiian dances overhead.

But on Fifty-seventh Street the atmosphere wasn’t built for it. When Motty turned up at three with a collection of hearty lads, who stopped singing their college song only to begin “The Old Oaken Bucket,” there was marked peevishness among the old settlers. The management was extremely terse over the telephone at breakfast and required soothing.

The next night I came home early after a lonely dinner—chosen for its low probability of Motty—and found the sitting-room dark. I was moving to switch on the light when there was a sudden sort of explosion and something collared my trouser-leg.

Living with Motty had reduced me so that I was incapable of coping with surprises. I jumped back with a yell and tumbled into the hall just as Jeeves emerged.

“Did you call, sir?”

“Jeeves! There’s something in there that grabs you by the leg!”

“That would be Rollo, sir.”

“Eh?”

“I would have warned you of his presence, but I did not hear you come in. His temper is a little uncertain at present, as he has not yet settled down.”

“Who the deuce is Rollo?”

“His lordship’s bull-terrier, sir. His lordship won him in a raffle, and tied him to the leg of the table. If you will allow me, sir, I will go in and switch on the light.”

There is nobody like Jeeves. He walked into that sitting-room as calmly as if he were stepping into a pantry, which, considering the noises, was the biggest feat since Daniel and the lions. And what’s more, the animal—this Rollo—didn’t so much as look at his legs. Instead, it calmed down as if someone had turned off a gramophone in its head and gave a little wriggle, rolling over with all four paws in the air, devoted to Jeeves at first sight.

Then it caught sight of me again and became excited all over, with the one clear idea of resuming operations where it had left off.

There are mornings when a man lies among his pillows, letting the first cup of tea wash the night’s dust off his tongue, and watches his valet drift about the room like a priest in a well-run temple, setting out the day’s clothes with the calm of one who knows exactly where every stud ought to go and why. On those mornings it has occurred to me, with a coldness at the pit of the stomach, what I should do if Jeeves ever took it into his head to leave.

New York, of course, is a place where valets are not so continuously hunted by predatory acquaintances—London is far worse. In London there were actual raids. Men I had called friends would come into my rooms and look at Jeeves the way a hungry dog looks at a chop. Reggie Foljambe, for instance, tried to buy him, and Alistair Bingham-Reeves—whose own valet had once pressed his trousers sideways, like some kind of modernist statement—used to sit and glitter at Jeeves as if measuring him for a sack.

The explanation is simple: Jeeves is so disgustingly competent. Even in the small matter of shirt-studs there is a kind of quiet genius about him, as though the very click of metal into linen were part of a larger, private science.

And not only does he save me from myself in crises; he will, if asked, extend that saving hand to any friend of mine who appears to be drowning in soup. Case in point: the rather curious business of dear old Bicky Bickersteth and his uncle, the hard-boiled egg.

It began after I’d been in America a few months. I came back to the flat late one night—New York at that hour humming like a boiler-room, taxis hooting, elevated trains clattering overhead, the whole city making the sort of noise that would drive a nightingale to drink. Jeeves brought in the final restorative, and said, as if remarking on the weather:

“Mr. Bickersteth called to see you this evening, sir, while you were out.”

“Oh?” I said.

“Twice, sir. He appeared a trifle agitated.”

“What, pipped?”

“He gave that impression, sir.”

I was sorry if Bicky was in a stew, but I was also rather glad to have something neutral to discuss with Jeeves. Things had been strained between us—strained in the way a string is strained just before it snaps and takes your eye out—because I had decided to grow a moustache.

You would have thought, to hear Jeeves breathe in when he first saw it, that I had nailed a dead haddock to my face. The thing cut him to the quick. Ever since, he had moved about the flat in an atmosphere of silent reproach, like a man compelled to serve in a house where somebody insists on playing a gramophone at breakfast. It was getting on my nerves. I’m not unreasonable, and Heaven knows I have surrendered plenty of ties and suits when Jeeves has gently suggested that they might “not be entirely suitable, sir.” But a chap must draw the line somewhere. One cannot have a valet editing one’s upper lip.

“He said that he would call again later, sir.”

“Something must be up, Jeeves.”

“Yes, sir.”

I gave the moustache a meditative twirl—one of the few pleasures it afforded—and then, because the thought of Jeeves suffering behind his impassive mask annoyed me, I stopped.

“I see by the paper, sir,” Jeeves went on, “that Mr. Bickersteth’s uncle is arriving on the Carmantic.”

“Yes?”

“His Grace the Duke of Chiswick, sir.”

That was news. I had known Bicky chiefly as a fellow who was always hard up and yet oddly noble about it, refusing on principle to borrow from friends and preferring to starve with good manners. I had met him at a sort of arty beano in Washington Square—sculptors and poets and girls who looked as if they had been dressed by instinct rather than by shops—and I had taken to him because he was English, had been up at Oxford with me, and could do a perfectly gifted imitation of a bull-terrier chasing a cat up a tree. But I had never suspected he had a duke for an uncle.

“If the Duke of Chiswick is his uncle,” I said, “why hasn’t Bicky got a title? Why isn’t he Lord Something?”

“Mr. Bickersteth is the son of his grace’s late sister, sir, who married Captain Rollo Bickersteth of the Coldstream Guards.”

Jeeves knows everything in the way that libraries know things, only quicker and with better timing.

“Is Bicky’s father dead too?”

“Yes, sir.”

“Leave any money?”

“No, sir.”

That accounted for much. To the innocent mind a duke for an uncle sounds like a perpetual Christmas stocking. But Chiswick was the sort of duke who owned half a map and spent as if he were renting it by the inch. A wealthy old bird, yes—counties, London property, the works—but famous for being prudence in human form. An aristocratic hard-boiled egg, with the shell of economy and the unyielding interior.

At that moment the bell rang. Jeeves went to answer it, and I heard him in the passage, smooth as cream:

“Yes, sir. Mr. Wooster has just returned.”

And Bicky came in, looking as if someone had taken his nerves and put them through a wringer.

“Halloa, Bicky!” I said. “Jeeves told me you’d been trying to get me. Jeeves, bring another glass. What’s on your mind?”

“I’m in a hole, Bertie. I want your advice.”

“Out with it, old lad.”

“My uncle’s turning up to-morrow.”

“So Jeeves said.”

“The Duke of Chiswick.”

“So Jeeves said.”

Bicky blinked at him.

“Jeeves seems to know everything.”

“Oddly enough, Bicky, I was thinking precisely that just now.”

“Well,” said Bicky, very gloomily, “I wish he knew a way to get me out of this.”

Jeeves came in with the glass and placed it down with that neat, noiseless accuracy of his, as if he were laying a sacrament on an altar.

“Mr. Bickersteth is in a hole, Jeeves,” I said. “Rally round.”

“Very good, sir.”

Bicky looked dubious.

“This is rather private, Bertie.”

“Oh, don’t fuss. I bet Jeeves knows it already. Don’t you, Jeeves?”

“Yes, sir.”

Bicky went pale in a sort of slow-motion way.

“Eh?”

“I am open to correction, sir,” said Jeeves, “but is not your dilemma due to the fact that you are at a loss to explain to his grace why you are in New York instead of in Colorado?”

Bicky wobbled like a blancmange in a gale.

“How in Heaven’s name—?”

“I chanced to meet his grace’s butler before we left England, sir. He informed me that he overheard his grace speaking to you on the matter as he passed the library door.”

Bicky made a hollow laugh.

“Well, if everyone knows, no use pretending. The old boy turfed me out because he said I was a brainless nincompoop. He’d agreed to send me a remittance on condition I went to some blasted place called Colorado and learned ranching or farming or something—ride horses, chase cows, all that. I hate horses. They bite. I couldn’t face it. But I needed the money.”

“I follow you, dear boy.”

“So I got to New York and thought it looked a decent place to stay. I cabled him I’d dropped into a business opening and wanted to scrap the ranch idea. He wired back that it was all right. He thinks I’m doing well. I never dreamed he’d come out. Now he’s wirelessed he’s coming to stay with me. I’ve always given him the impression I’m living in style. I can’t have him at my boarding-house, Bertie.”

I turned to Jeeves.

“What’s to be done?”

Jeeves considered it with the air of a man tasting a sauce and deciding what it lacks.

“To what extent, sir, if the question is not delicate, are you prepared to assist Mr. Bickersteth?”

“I’ll do anything I can, of course.”

Jeeves began, “Then you might lend Mr. Bickersteth—”

“No, by Jove!” said Bicky, firm as a rock. “I’ve never borrowed from you, Bertie, and I’m not starting now. I may be a chump, but I don’t owe a penny to a soul—apart from tradesmen.”

“I was about to suggest, sir,” said Jeeves, “that you might lend Mr. Bickersteth this flat. Mr. Bickersteth could give his grace the impression that he is the owner. With your permission I could convey the notion that I am in Mr. Bickersteth’s employment. You would be residing here temporarily as Mr. Bickersteth’s guest. His grace would occupy the second spare bedroom.”

Bicky stopped wobbling and stared, reverent, as if a miracle had occurred in the drawing-room.

“I would advocate dispatching a wireless message to his grace on board the vessel, notifying him of the change of address,” Jeeves concluded. “Mr. Bickersteth could meet his grace at the dock and proceed directly here. Will that meet the situation, sir?”

“Absolutely.”

“Thank you, sir.”

When Jeeves had glided out, Bicky continued to stare at the doorway, like a man who has just heard an angel sing.

“How does he do it, Bertie?” he said. “I believe it’s something to do with the shape of his head. Have you ever noticed his head? It sort of sticks out at the back!”

I was up early next morning to witness the arrival of the hard-boiled egg in person. Ocean liners have a way of docking at hours intended to test the virtue of saints. By a little after nine I had dressed, swallowed tea, and was leaning out of the window. The morning had that rare, washed-clean quality—sunlight in the street, a faint smell of baking from somewhere, the city briefly holding its breath between the night’s racket and the day’s. I was just beginning to feel as though I might possess a soul after all, when the peace was torn open by a row below.

A taxi had drawn up. Out got an elderly party in a top hat, vibrating with indignation. He was arguing with the cabman in a tone that suggested he had been born to have people agree with him. From what I could gather he was trying to persuade the driver to accept London prices. The cabman, who seemed to regard London as a rumour invented to swindle Americans, declined.

“In London,” the old boy was saying, “this would have cost eightpence!”

The cabman replied in a dialect of Manhattan which did not appear to have an eightpence in it.

I called to Jeeves.

“The duke has arrived, Jeeves.”

“Yes, sir?”

“That’ll be him at the door.”

Jeeves opened it, and the old boy came in looking as if the Atlantic crossing and the taxi fare had combined to crush him flat. He sank into a chair, chest heaving with the effort of restraint, and looked about him like a man inspecting a burglar’s haul.

“How do you do, sir?” I said, playing host. “Your nephew went down to meet you, but you must have missed him. My name’s Wooster. Great pal of Bicky’s. I’m staying with him. Would you care for a cup of tea? Jeeves, tea.”

Old Chiswick stared at the room—the rugs, the furniture, the general air of comfort that says money has been spent without apology.

“Does this luxurious flat belong to my nephew Francis?”

“Absolutely.”

“It must be terribly expensive.”

“Pretty well, of course. Everything costs a lot over here.”

He gave a small groan. Jeeves brought the tea. The duke drank, as if tea were the one decent sound in a world of honking taxis, and gave a grim nod.

“A terrible country, Mr. Wooster! A terrible country! Nearly eight shillings for a short cab drive! Iniquitous!” He looked about again, his face tightening as if each cushion were a personal affront. “Have you any idea what my nephew pays for this flat?”

“About two hundred dollars a month, I believe.”

“What! Forty pounds a month!”

One could see the calculations whirring behind the forehead: Bicky, whom he had presumably filed away as a permanent human expense, apparently thriving. It was not fitting his mental ledgers.

“It seems rummy,” I said hastily, “but New York often bucks a chap up. Something in the air. It develops people. Bicky may have been a bit of a chump in the past—no offence intended—but it’s quite different now. Devilish efficient. Looked on in commercial circles as quite the nib.”

He stared.

“I am amazed. What is the nature of my nephew’s business, Mr. Wooster?”

“Oh, just business, you know. The sort of thing Carnegie and Rockefeller do.”

I edged toward the door, feeling my way through the conversation like a man trying not to step on crockery.

“Awfully sorry to leave you, but I’m due to meet some fellows.”

In the lift, coming down, I met Bicky on his way up, wearing an expression of eager hope.

“Halloa, Bertie! I missed him. Has he arrived?”

“Upstairs, having tea.”

“What does he think?”

“He’s absolutely rattled.”

“Ripping! I’ll toddle up. Toodle-oo, Bertie.”

“Pip-pip.”

He trotted off with far too much cheer for a man whose future depended on an elderly miser’s digestion, and I went to the club and sat by the window watching the traffic stream by, the city’s endless roar rolling like surf. It struck me, not for the last time, that human peace is partly a matter of what you can hear. Give me the muted clink of a teaspoon against china rather than New York’s brass-band of engines.

It was late evening when I returned to dress for dinner. The flat was oddly quiet—no Bicky, no duke, no Jeeves moving about with the gentle whisper of starched linen.

“Where’s everybody, Jeeves?” I asked, when he appeared.

“His grace desired to see some of the sights of the city, sir. Mr. Bickersteth is acting as his escort. I fancy their immediate objective was Grant’s Tomb.”

“I suppose Bicky’s fairly full of beans about the way it’s going, what?”

“Not altogether, sir.”

“What’s the trouble now?”

“The scheme which I took the liberty of suggesting has, unfortunately, not answered entirely satisfactorily.”

My spirits sank.

“Surely the duke believes Bicky’s doing well and all that?”

“Precisely, sir. With the result that his grace has decided to cancel Mr. Bickersteth’s monthly allowance, on the ground that, as Mr. Bickersteth is doing so well on his own account, he no longer requires pecuniary assistance.”

“Great Scot, Jeeves!”

“Somewhat disturbing, sir.”

“I never expected anything like this.”

“I confess I scarcely anticipated the contingency myself, sir.”

Poor old Bicky. The thing had been meant to save him, and it had cut off his supplies like a knife slipping and hitting the artery.

“We must do something, Jeeves.”

“Yes, sir.”

“Can you think of anything?”

“Not at the moment, sir.”

“There must be some way.”

“It was a maxim of one of my former employers, sir—Lord Bridgnorth—that there is always a way. His lordship, when a patent hair-restorer failed to attract the public, placed it on the market under another name as a depilatory and amassed a substantial fortune. I have generally found his lordship’s aphorism sound. No doubt we shall discover a solution for Mr. Bickersteth, sir.”

“Well, do.”

“I will spare no pains, sir.”

I dressed miserably. It shows how upset I was that I nearly put a white tie on with a dinner-jacket—a thing that would have made Jeeves emit one of those silences of his that weigh like lead. I went out and ate something merely to keep the clock moving, though the food tasted like paste. Somewhere, Bicky was sliding toward the breadline, and I was ordering courses as if nothing had happened.

When I got back the duke had gone to bed, but Bicky was in the sitting-room hunched in an arm-chair, a cigarette drooping from the corner of his mouth, staring into space as if it had personally betrayed him. There was a glass in his hand. He drained it with desperate intent, forgetting it was empty.

“This is a bit thick,” I said.

“I’m done, Bertie,” he said.

He tried the glass again, as if hoping the laws of matter had changed.

“If only it had happened a week later! My next month’s money was due Saturday. I could have worked a wheeze I’ve been reading about—magazine advertisements. It seems you can make a dashed amount if you collect a few dollars and start a chicken farm. Sound scheme! You buy a hen. It lays an egg every day. You sell seven eggs for twenty-five cents. Keep costs nothing. Profit twenty-five cents on seven eggs. Or take a dozen hens. Each has a dozen chickens. The chickens have more chickens. In no time the place is knee-deep in hens and eggs at twenty-five cents per seven. You’d make a fortune. Jolly life too, keeping hens!” His eyes lit for a moment with the warm, absurd romance of it: sunrise on a little farm, the soft mutter of poultry, a world of honest clucks instead of New York’s metallic scream.

Then the light went out again.

“But it’s no good,” he said. “I haven’t the cash.”

“You’ve only to say the word,” I told him.

“Thanks awfully, but I’m not going to sponge on you.”

That is always the way. The fellows you’d gladly lend money to refuse as if it were poison, while the ones you’d rather not finance at all will chase you down Piccadilly with the hot breath of the toucher on your neck. I’ve spent half my life scattering largesse to blighters I didn’t care for; and now, when I was dripping with doubloons and desperate to help, here was Bicky—poor fish—flat broke and too decent to take a penny.

“Well,” I said, “there’s only one hope, then.”

“What’s that?”

“Jeeves.”

“Sir?”

He was suddenly there behind me, as noiseless as a fish in water. In the matter of entering rooms the man is uncanny. Poor old Bicky shot up like a startled pheasant.

“Did you call, sir?”

“Oh—there you are, Jeeves!”

“Precisely, sir.”

“Bicky’s still up the pole. Any ideas?”

“Why, yes, sir. Since our recent conversation, I fancy I have found what may prove a solution. I do not wish to appear to be taking a liberty, but I think we have overlooked his grace’s potentialities as a source of revenue.”

Bicky made a sound that might have been laughter if laughter had died of thirst.

“I do not allude,” Jeeves continued, “to inducing his grace to part with money. I am taking the liberty of regarding his grace in the light of an at present—if I may say so—useless property capable of being developed.”

We stared.

“Make it easier, Jeeves.”

“In a nutshell, sir: his grace is, in a sense, a prominent personage. The inhabitants of this country are peculiarly addicted to shaking hands with prominent personages. It occurred to me that Mr. Bickersteth or yourself might know persons willing to pay a small fee—two dollars or three—for the privilege of an introduction, including handshake, to his grace.”

Bicky blinked.

“You mean someone would actually pay cash just to shake hands with my uncle?”

“I have an aunt, sir,” said Jeeves, “who paid five shillings to a young gentleman for bringing a moving-picture actor to tea on a Sunday. It gave her social standing among the neighbours.”

Bicky hesitated, then began to waver toward hope.

“If you think it could be done…”

“I feel convinced of it, sir.”

“What do you think, Bertie?”

“I’m for it absolutely. A very brainy wheeze.”

“Thank you, sir. Will there be anything further? Good night, sir.”

And he flowed out, leaving us to plan the marketing of the Duke of Chiswick like a new breakfast cereal.

Until we began trying to sell introductions, I had never realized what a thoroughly foul time stockbrokers must have when the public refuses to bite. The “market,” as they say, opened quietly for us. It opened so quietly you could have heard a mouse sneeze. By the end of the week the only name on our list was a delicatessen-store keeper down in Bicky’s neighbourhood, and he wanted to pay in sliced ham rather than cash, which didn’t exactly help with rent.

There was a brief gleam when the brother of Bicky’s pawnbroker offered ten dollars, money down, for an introduction. Then it turned out he was an anarchist and intended to kick the duke instead of shaking hands. It took me ages to persuade Bicky not to pocket the ten and let the thing proceed. In his present state he regarded any man with ten dollars as a sportsman and a benefactor of mankind.

The whole enterprise would have died there, I fancy, if Jeeves hadn’t taken command. That’s the thing about the man: when everybody else is standing in the kitchen staring at an empty larder, he is already planning what can be coaxed out of a bone, a carrot, and a pinch of salt, and he will do it without raising his voice or clattering a pan. He came into my room one morning with tea, the smell of it clean and steady amid the city’s outside din, and said:

“Might I speak to you with regard to that matter of his grace, sir?”

“It’s all off,” I said. “We’ve decided to chuck it. Nobody will come.”

“Sir?”

“It won’t work.”

“I fancy I can arrange that aspect of the matter, sir.”

“You mean you’ve got somebody?”

“Yes, sir. Eighty-seven gentlemen from Birdsburg, sir.”

I sat up so fast I slopped the tea.

“Birdsburg?”

“Birdsburg, Missouri, sir.”

“How did you get them?”

I daresay you’ve heard of Bobbie Cardew. If you haven’t, you’ve missed a domestic phenomenon—one of those quiet, kitchen-table catastrophes that start with nothing more than a forgotten date and end with a marriage looking as if it’s been left too long in a hot oven.

Because, to begin with, Bobbie wasn’t what you’d call a man with his senses switched on. In the old days—when I first knew him—he drifted through London the way a leaf drifts along a gutter after rain: vaguely, charmingly, and with absolutely no intention of arriving anywhere in particular.

I’ve known people accuse me, Reggie Pepper, of being a bit of a fool, but Bobbie was in a higher culinary class of idiocy altogether—one of those rich, well-fed, traditional British puddings that wobble if you so much as breathe at them. If I wanted him to dine, I had to treat him the way a conscientious cook treats a soufflé: constant attention, no sudden noises, no draughts, and for heaven’s sake don’t take your eyes off it. A letter posted at the start of the week. A telegram the day before. A telephone call on the day. And half an hour before, a messenger in a taxi to put him into his clothes and send him out like a roast to table, address pinned on.

And the peculiar thing was: he wasn’t entirely brainless. Somewhere deep in him—under the froth and the cigarette-ash and the “Good Lord, is it Tuesday?”—there was a compact little layer of sense, like a kernel of nut in a suet dumpling. You just couldn’t get at it without explosives.

I used to think dynamite was required. But it turns out there’s a domestic equivalent that does the job more quietly and with less official paperwork.

Marriage.

Marriage—being the dynamite of the soul—went off under Bobbie as neat as a kitchen match under a pan. He fell in love, dashed into matrimony with a whoop as if it were a jaunt to the theatre, and only afterwards discovered that the thing has consequences.

His wife was Mary Anthony—Mary Cardew once the registrar had finished with them. Not the sort of girl you’d have expected Bobbie to pick, and perhaps that was part of the attraction. She worked for her living; and there’s a romantic glamour, to a man who’s never worked a day in his life, in a woman who knows what it means to earn her bread honestly and butter it herself.

Mary was a hospital nurse. Five foot six, a mass of red-gold hair like a sunset caught and pinned up, grey eyes that could go very soft or very steel, and a chin that suggested, not unkindly, that if a man insisted on being a fool she would at least insist on his being a punctual fool.

They met when Bobbie smashed himself at polo and was put under her care. She smoothed his brow, rallied round with cooling unguents and that sort of thing; and scarcely had the bandages come off before he married her. Quite the romance—like a patient falling in love with the nurse because she holds the basin with conviction.

He told me at the club one evening and introduced me next day. I admired her. I’ve never had to earn my living—uncle Edward, Pepper, Wells and Co., collieries, left me enough to keep me in tobacco and errors—but I respect people who can face the world and still manage to get dinner. Mary had had a rough time—an orphan, built her life on her own—so there was a kind of steadiness about her, like an old family recipe that survives because it’s worth surviving.

At first everything looked smooth enough. They took a flat; I was in and out; and if this was marriage, I couldn’t see why fellows treated it as if it were an operation without anaesthetic. Bobbie seemed happy. Mary seemed—well, deeply in love, to judge by the way she looked at him when she thought nobody was watching.

Then we come to the incident of the quiet Dinner, and that is where the domestic stew began to simmer.

I met Bobbie in Piccadilly and he asked me back to dine at the flat. I went—like a man volunteering to taste soup he can see is curdling.

When we arrived, there was Mary, and the sight of her fairly knocked the breath out of me. Her hair—usually sensible and nurse-like—was piled in waves and clever little crinkles, with diamonds stuck in as if someone had sprinkled the whole business with sugar crystals. She wore a dress that looked as if it had been engineered by angels with a practical knowledge of the female form. If she dressed like that for an ordinary night at home, I understood perfectly why Bobbie didn’t fancy the club.

Bobbie, utterly unconscious, beamed.

“Here’s old Reggie, dear,” he said. “I’ve brought him home to have a bit of dinner. I’ll phone down to the kitchen and ask them to send it up now—what?”

She stared at him as if he’d arrived from a different century. Then she turned scarlet. Then she went white. Then she gave a little laugh that sounded like a spoon tapping a glass too hard. It was fascinating in the way a saucepan boiling over is fascinating when it isn’t your stove.

“I am so glad you were able to come, Mr. Pepper,” she said, smiling at me.

After that she behaved as if nothing had happened. She talked at dinner, chaffed Bobbie, played ragtime afterwards—cheerful, bright, hostess-like. But I’d seen her face at the start. I knew she was holding herself in hand with both fists, the way you hold a lid on a pot that wants to blow. There was a scream in her—one good scream—that she was swallowing down like a mouthful of scalding tea.

The flat, meanwhile, had its own noises: the faint clink of cutlery, the soft hum of London through the windows, the domestic hush that ought to feel comforting. Yet under Mary’s laughter there was a different sound altogether—the silence that comes when someone has been struck and is determined not to cry out. I’ve always thought silence can be the worst noise in a room. Modern London never shuts up—motors, buses, horns, a constant metallic racket like a factory—but there are moments in a marriage when the loudest thing is what isn’t being said.

I escaped at the earliest possible moment.

Next day I met Bobbie at the club. He looked like a lonely gum-drop at an Eskimo tea-party.

He began at once, glad of a witness.

“Do you know how long I’ve been married?” he said.

“About a year, isn’t it?”

“Not about a year,” he said mournfully. “Exactly a year—yesterday!”

Then I saw it: the dinner, the dress, the diamonds—Mary had dressed to go out. Bobbie had blithely brought home Reggie Pepper for a quiet bite.

“Yesterday was—”

“The anniversary,” he said. “I’d arranged to take Mary to the Savoy, and on to Covent Garden. She particularly wanted to hear Caruso. I had the ticket for the box in my pocket. Do you know, all through dinner I had a kind of rummy idea there was something I’d forgotten, but I couldn’t think what?”

“Till your wife mentioned it?” I said, because I like living dangerously.

He nodded.

“She—mentioned it,” he said thoughtfully.

I didn’t ask how. A woman with Mary’s chin doesn’t do things by halves.

“To be absolutely frank, old top,” said Bobbie, “my stock’s pretty low at home.”

He went out after a bit. I watched him from the upper smoking-room window—Piccadilly below with its rolling tide of buses and engines, the city’s constant din rising like an ugly hymn of petrol—and saw him pause, walk on, pause again, then turn into a jeweller’s. Which was an example of that buried stratum of sense: when he did feel a blow, he tried to plaster it over with gold.

From then on I was interested. Not in the way one is idly interested in a friend’s marriage, the way one is interested in a neighbour’s new curtains. This was different. You had Bobbie—easy-going, forgetful, strolling through life like a man who thinks dinner will simply happen because dinner has always happened. And you had Mary, determined that it shouldn’t. It was the immovable object and the irresistible force, only staged in a flat with cutlery.

Nature was on Bobbie’s side, too. When Nature makes a chump she’s proud of her work and doesn’t want anyone tampering with it. So she armours him. And the armour, in Bobbie’s case, was memory like a sieve. It protected him from improvement. Advice slid off him the way gravy slides off a plate you forgot to warm.

For a week or so after the anniversary disaster, the shock kept him alert. He remembered things as if he’d turned into an elephant. But it only dented the armour; it didn’t pierce it. Soon he was back to forgetting whatever Mary asked him to bring home—books, parcels, messages—anything that required him to carry a thought from morning to evening.

It was rather pathetic. Mary loved him and was frightened. She saw the thin edge of the wedge. If a man forgets his wedding anniversary at one year, what happens at four? At ten? Eventually he’ll come in one evening, take his hat off, and ask whose flat this is.

One afternoon Bobbie was pouring out troubles to me. He’d forgotten something Mary had asked for—might have been a book.

“It’s such a little thing to make a fuss about,” he said. “She knows it’s simply because I’ve got such an infernal memory. I can’t remember anything. Never could.”

He was leaving when he produced two sovereigns.

“Oh, by the way,” he said.

“What’s this for?” I asked, though I knew.

“I owe it you.”

“How’s that?”

“That bet on Tuesday. Billiard-room. Murray and Brown. I gave you two to one Brown would win, and Murray beat him by twenty odd.”

“So you do remember some things?” I said.

He got quite heated—said it was rotten of me to think he’d forget a debt, and so on.

“Subside,” I said. “Now listen.”

And I spoke to him with the slow patience of a cook explaining to a child why you don’t leave the milk out in the sun.

“You’ve got to pull yourself together, and quick. This money proves you can remember when it matters to you. Now make it matter when it matters to Mary. Put anniversaries and birthdays in that same category. It may feel like brain-surgery, but you can’t wriggle out.”

“I suppose you’re right,” he said gloomily. “But why does she think such a lot of these rotten little dates? What’s it matter if I forget what day we were married on or what day she was born on or what day the cat had the measles? She knows I love her just as much as if I were a memorizing freak at the halls.”

“That isn’t enough for a woman,” I said. “They want proof. They want you to speak their language. Forget it and you’ll get trouble.”

He chewed the knob of his stick.

“Women are frightfully rummy,” he said.

“You should have thought of that before you married one,” I said.

After that I stopped lecturing. You can’t reform a man who treats advice like an optional garnish.

Months passed. Now and then he’d appear at the club looking clouded, and I’d know there had been domestic thunder. But it wasn’t until spring that the lightning struck properly—straight through the ribs.

I was at the window one morning, smoking and watching Piccadilly’s racket—the buses grinding past with that thick, joyless roar that makes you wonder how anyone ever heard birds in London—when Bobbie rushed in, eyes bulging, face the colour of an oyster, waving a piece of paper.

“Reggie,” he gasped. “She’s gone!”

“Gone!” I said. “Who?”

“Mary! Gone! Left me!”

“Where?”

He nearly foamed.

“Where? How should I know where? Read this.”

It was a letter. Not long—Mary wasn’t a woman who wasted words when there were lives to be run and patients to be fed.

“MY DEAR BOBBIE,—I am going away. When you care enough about me to remember to wish me many happy returns on my birthday, I will come back. My address will be Box 341, London Morning News.”

I read it twice.

“Well,” I said, “why don’t you?”

“Why don’t I what?”

“Wish her many happy returns. It doesn’t seem much.”

“But she says on her birthday.”

“Well, when’s her birthday?”

He stared at me as if I’d asked him to recite the entire Domesday Book.

“I’ve forgotten,” he said.

“Forgotten?”

“Yes,” said Bobbie. “Forgotten.”

“How do you mean—forgotten whether it’s the twentieth or the twenty-first?”

“I know it came somewhere between the first of January and the thirty-first of December. That’s how near I get.”

I rang for restoratives. One doesn’t interrogate a man in that condition without fortifying him first.

“Well, Bobbie,” I said, “this is a stiffish case to spring on an amateur. Suppose someone went to Sherlock Holmes and said, ‘Mr. Holmes, when is my wife’s birthday?’ It would give him a jolt. But Holmes always asks for clues, so cough up something. What was the weather like last time?”

Bobbie shook his head.

“Just ordinary weather.”

“Warm?”

“Warmish.”

“Or cold?”

“Fairly cold, perhaps. I can’t remember.”

I ordered more restoratives. He was the least helpful assistant I’d ever had; the sort of fellow who turns up in the kitchen to “help” and then forgets where the knives are.

Then Bobbie brightened suddenly.

“I’ve got it,” he said. “I gave her a present on her last birthday. All we do is go to the shop, find the date it was bought, and that gives the day.”

“Excellent,” I said. “What did you give her?”

His face fell again, like a soufflé remembering gravity.

“I can’t remember,” he said.

You might think that would finish him. But ideas, like truffles, sometimes appear where you least expect them. Bobbie, having no practice at thinking, was producing thoughts the way a startled hen produces eggs—astonished each time.

He remembered those little books, When were you Born? one for each month, telling you your character and talents. His plan was to buy all twelve and find the month that described Mary best.

For Bobbie it was a brilliant notion. We bought the set and sat down to it like two men tasting sauces.

And found the flaw at once. Every month described Mary.

December people “keep their own secrets” and are “extensive travellers.” Mary had certainly kept her secret, and as for travelling, she’d travelled quite extensively enough for Bobbie’s comfort.

October people had “original ideas” and “loved moving.” That summed up her departure beautifully.

February people had “wonderful memories”—which was Mary’s one tyrannical virtue.

We tried again. Bobbie went for May because it said May women were “capricious, which is always a barrier to a happy married life.” I favoured February, because February women “are unusually determined to have their own way, are very earnest, and expect a full return in their companions.” Which sounded as if the author had met Mary and survived.

In the end Bobbie tore the books up, stamped on them, burnt them, and went home.

The next days changed him. It was as if the shock had finally pierced that armour. He began to think—properly think—wearing his brain down like a pestle grinding spices. He came to me constantly. I sympathised, but I also kept handing him the moral lesson whenever he was weak enough to take it.

Then one day he arrived looking brighter.

“Reggie,” he said, “I’m on the trail. I’ve remembered something vital.”

“Yes?”

“On Mary’s last birthday we went to the Coliseum. I remember it distinctly.”

“It’s a good memory,” I said, “but how does it help?”

“They change the programme every week,” he said triumphantly.

“Ah,” I said. “Now you’re cooking.”

“And the week we went, one of the turns was Professor Somebody’s Terpsichorean Cats. I remember them. Now I’m going to the Coliseum this minute and I’ll dig out the date of those cats if I have to use a crowbar.”

So off we went. The management treated us like brothers, produced archives, and ran fingers over old programmes till they treed the Terpsichorean Cats in the middle of May.

“I told you it was May,” said Bobbie.

“If you’ve any sense,” I said, “there won’t be another time.”

And he said there wouldn’t.

Once a memory starts running, it begins to enjoy itself. That night, just as I’d got to sleep, the telephone bell rang. Bobbie. He didn’t apologize.

“Reggie,” he said, “I’ve got it nearer. We saw those Terpsichorean Cats at a matinee.”

“Yes?” I said, feet freezing on the bedroom floor.

“Well, that brings it down to two days. It must have been either Wednesday the seventh or Saturday the tenth.”

“Yes,” I said, “if they didn’t have daily matinees at the Coliseum.”

He gave a howl like a man whose sauce has split at the last second.

“Bobbie,” I said, because I am fond of him and because my feet were turning to ice, “I’ve remembered something too. The day you went to the Coliseum I lunched with you both at the Ritz. You’d forgotten to bring money, so you wrote a cheque.”

“But I’m always writing cheques!”

“Yes. But this was for ten pounds, payable to the hotel. Look in your cheque-book. See how many ten-pound cheques to the Ritz you wrote between May fifth and May tenth.”

A gulp.

“Reggie,” he said, “you’re a genius. Hold the line.”

He came back presently.

“Halloa!” he said.

“I’m here.”

“It was the eighth. Reggie, old man, I—”

“Topping,” I said. “Good night.”

But it was well into the small hours and I thought I might as well finish the business while my circulation still existed. I rang an hotel near the Strand.

“Put me through to Mrs. Cardew,” I said.

“It’s late,” said the man.

“And getting later every minute,” I said. “Buck along.”

After some delay, Mary’s voice came—sharp with sleep and instantly alert, like a nurse woken by a wrong note in a ward.

“What is the matter?” she said.

“My feet are cold,” I said. “But that isn’t the main item. I’ve been talking to Bobbie.”

“Oh! Mr. Pepper?”

“Yes. He’s remembered it, Mrs. Cardew.”

There was a little scream—half shock, half relief. One forgets how dramatic telephones can be; somewhere in the exchange a girl with a headset was probably collecting the whole thing like a food historian collecting recipes, storing up the sounds of human life the way older cultures stored up words. London’s wires carry a lot more than voices; they carry the exact pitch of panic, the particular hush of hope.

“He’s remembered it!” Mary gasped. “Did you tell him?”

“No,” I said. I hadn’t.

“Mr. Pepper.”

“Yes?”

“Was he—has he been—was he very worried?”

I chuckled, because this was where I was meant to be brilliant.

“Worried? He was about the most worried man between here and Edinburgh. He has been worrying as if the nation paid him a salary for it. He’s started after breakfast and kept it up—”

If there is one thing a man learns, sooner or later, it is that sorrow travels on its stomach. It does not care for noble scenery or bracing air; it wants something hot and sustaining, and if it cannot get that it settles down in a corner and goes on being miserable with a steady professional grip.

So when I came into the club one morning and found Freddie Meadowes hunched in the shade like a damp dish-cloth, staring at the carpet as if it had personally offended him, I knew he had not been fed properly—either in the literal sense or the emotional. Freddie, as a rule, is a fellow who can make a room sound brighter simply by being in it; even his laugh seems to have been trained on open country. Now he looked like a man who had been locked all night in a pantry with nothing but cold mutton and his thoughts.

Jimmy Pinkerton was with me. Jimmy writes plays and has a way of observing human misery as if it were a promising first act. Between us we prised it out of Freddie at last. Girl, of course. Angela West. Quarrel. Engagement broken. Letters returned unopened; telephone treated as a hostile instrument. The works.

“Change of scene,” I said. “Come down with me to Marvis Bay. I’ve got a cottage. Jimmy’s coming on the twenty-fourth. Sea air. Quiet. A few simple meals. The sound of the tide in place of the London din. You’ll mend.”

Jimmy supported this with one of his cheerful little anecdotes about a man who went abroad after a rebuff and then couldn’t remember the lady’s surname when she wired him. Freddie did not brighten. He merely looked as if he had been forced to listen to “The Dead March” played on a spoon.

But he agreed to come. He said it didn’t matter where he was.

Marvis Bay is in Dorsetshire, and if you are the sort of person who requires brass bands and motor horns to feel alive, you would die there of starvation inside a week. It is mostly sand, sea, wind, and the honest noises of nature—waves grinding pebbles like a slow mill, gulls quarrelling like fishwives, and, in the evening, the persistent high violin-note of gnats, which is Nature’s way of telling you she disapproves of your ankles.

Freddie took to the place in a bleak fashion. Once the moon rose and the water began its steady breathing, you couldn’t keep him off the beach. The gnats took to him even more. They treated him as a sort of public refreshment room, and I believe perfectly good strollers were passed over just that the insects might be in prime condition for Freddie’s arrival.

But a man in love is a poor companion when he has decided to be tragic. By the end of the first week I would have welcomed Jimmy a day early with a brass band of my own. Freddie alternated between chewing at an unlit pipe and scowling at the carpet, and sitting down at the piano to play “The Rosary” with one finger. He could play nothing else, and he could not really play “The Rosary” either. Somewhere about the third bar his courage gave out and he went back to the beginning, so the thing was not music but a kind of circular sentence without punctuation.

He was at it one morning when I came in from bathing, smelling of salt and feeling as if my blood had been scrubbed clean.

“Reggie,” he said, hollowly, looking up, “I’ve seen her.”

“Seen who?”

“Angela. At the post office. We met in the doorway. She cut me.”

He struck at “The Rosary” again and slid sideways out of the second bar as if the notes had greased themselves.

“Reggie,” he said, “you ought never to have brought me here. I must go away.”

“Go away? Don’t be an ass. This is exactly where you begin to score.”

“She looked clean through me!”

“Of course she did. That’s part of the ritual. Now listen. What you need is to put her under an obligation. Get her timidly thanking you. Gratitude’s a lever. Gratitude—”

“But what’s she going to thank me timidly for?”

I considered. The breakfast problem was simple that morning—eggs, bacon, and a pot of jam with a smell of strawberries strong enough to make you believe in childhood again—but the Freddie problem required invention.

“Save her from drowning,” I said.

“I can’t swim,” said Freddie.

That was Freddie. A dear old chap, capable of imitating cats fighting in a backyard so that you could see the moonlit dust and hear the offended yowls of generations, but with a genius for being useless at the critical instant.

He began “The Rosary” yet again, and I went out to the sands to do the thinking for both of us.

I had just rounded some rocks, with my mind whirring, when I saw a blue dress. It was Angela West. I had never met her, but Freddie had sixteen photographs of her arranged round his bedroom as if he were conducting a private museum, so I could have identified her at a hundred yards through fog.

She sat on the sand helping a small, fat child build a castle. Close by, on a chair, an elderly lady read a novel and was being called “aunt,” which made it seem likely that the child was a cousin. Freddie would have been able to get sentiment out of this, no doubt. I could not. The child was one of those round, bulging specimens who look as if they have been kneaded by a careless baker and left to rise.

When the castle was finished the child began to whimper, the way they do when life fails to provide immediate entertainment. Angela took him to a sweet-stall, and I walked on.

It was on my way back that the inspiration hit me. I saw the fat child alone, smacking a jelly-fish with a spade in the absorbed, cold-blooded manner of youth. No Angela. No aunt. Nobody in sight at all.

And suddenly, don’t you know, the plan formed itself—complete, shining, and reckless, like one of those sauces that come together in the pan when you have stopped fussing and simply let butter and heat do their work. If Angela was fond of the child—if he was her cousin, as seemed likely—then a brief disappearance, followed by Freddie arriving with him, explaining that he had found the young blighter wandering about and had practically saved his life, must melt the ice. Gratitude, again. Tears. Reconciliation. Possibly a handshake. At least a cessation of cutting.

So I kidnapped the child.

I don’t defend it on legal grounds; I merely state it as a fact. I gathered him in under my arm and made off with him, composing Freddie’s triumphal scene all the way home. I could see it so clearly that it gave me a lump in my throat, like swallowing a badly chewed crust.

Freddie was slow to appreciate art.

When I came in carrying the child and dumped him in our sitting-room, Freddie did not leap up crying, “By Jove, Reggie, you are a genius!” He stared. The child, who had decided that the world was ending, began to bellow. Freddie, already delicate, winced as if his soul had been pinched.

“Stop it!” he cried. “Do you think nobody’s got any troubles except you? What the devil is all this, Reggie?”

The yell came back at him with enough force to rattle the window. I dashed to the kitchen and returned with a jar of honey. Honey, I have discovered, is the nearest thing we possess to a universal peace treaty. The child stopped bellowing and began to smear his face with it, looking like a small pagan idol being prepared for ceremony.

“Well?” said Freddie, when the noise died down.

I explained the plan. Gradually it worked its way through.

“You’re not such a fool as you look, sometimes, Reggie,” he said, and disentangled the child from the jar with a certain grim resolve. Then he took him out to scour the beach for Angela.

I sat on the veranda and smoked, feeling almost noble. The sea made its steady, civilised sound beyond the dunes. A blackbird in the hedge rehearsed three notes, paused, and then tried them again, as if it too was labouring to get something perfect. I told myself that soon Freddie would be laughing, Angela would be softened, and we would all be sitting down to a decent lunch with the kind of appetite that comes when one has saved a romance.

Then I saw Freddie coming back up the road.

The child was still with him.

Freddie looked as if his last friend had died in a railway accident.

“Hello,” I said, rising. “Couldn’t you find her?”

“Yes,” he said, and laughed bitterly in a way that made the hedges seem suddenly hostile. “I found her.”

“Well then—?”

He dropped into a chair and groaned.

“This isn’t her cousin, you idiot,” he said.

“What?”

“He’s no relation at all. Just a kid she happened to meet on the beach. She’d never seen him before in her life.”

It takes a lot to remove the breath from a man who has been bathing and is feeling generally resilient, but this did it.

“Then who is he?”

“I don’t know. But I’ve had a time. She listened while I told my story and then—well, she didn’t actually call me a liar, but she made me feel as if, if I ever met Dr. Cook and we swapped experiences, it would be the biggest duel on record. I crawled away with this infant like a whipped dog.”

He glared at me.

“And mind, this is your affair. If you want to avoid Dartmoor you’d better find his parents and return him before the police come for you.”

It is extraordinary, when you come to think of it, how difficult it is to restore a child to its proper owners. You would imagine anxious parents hovering in every doorway, wailing and wringing their hands, but not a bit of it. I tramped Marvis Bay like a bloodhound with this small lump of humanity in tow, and nobody so much as said, “That’s mine.” The child might have been an independent lodger. It wasn’t until, struck by a rare flash of sense, I questioned the man at the sweet-stall that I learned the child’s name was Medwin, and that his people were staying at Ocean Rest in Beach Road.

I raced there and knocked. Nothing. Knocked again. Movements inside, but no opening. I was about to treat the knocker as if it were responsible for the situation when a voice from above shouted, “Hi!”

I looked up. An upper window framed a round, pink face with grey whiskers jutting east and west like two startled mice.

“Hi!” it shouted again.

“What the deuce do you mean by ‘Hi’?” I called.

“You can’t come in,” said the face. “Hello! Is that Tootles?”

“My name is not Tootles, and I don’t want to come in,” I said. “Are you Mr. Medwin? I’ve brought back your son.”

“I see him. Peep-bo, Tootles! Dadda can see ’oo!”

The face vanished. There were muffled voices. It reappeared.

“Hi!”

I stamped on the gravel until it crackled.

“Do you live here?” he said.

“I’m staying here for a few weeks.”

“What’s your name?”

“Pepper. But—”

“Pepper? Any relation to Edward Pepper, the colliery owner?”

“My uncle. But—”

“I used to know him well. Dear old Edward Pepper! I wish I was with him now.”

“I wish you were,” I said.

He beamed.

“This is most fortunate,” he said. “We were wondering what to do with Tootles. We have the mumps here. My daughter Bootles has developed mumps. Tootles must not be exposed. We could not think what to do with him. It was most fortunate your finding him. He strayed from his nurse. I would hesitate to trust him to a stranger, but any nephew of Edward Pepper’s has my implicit confidence. You must take Tootles to your house. Ideal arrangement. I have written to my brother in London to fetch him. He may be here in a few days.”

“May!”

“A busy man. But certainly within a week. Till then Tootles can stop with you. Excellent plan. Very much obliged. Your wife will like Tootles.”

“I haven’t got a wife!” I yelled, but the window slammed as if he had spotted a germ attempting to escape and had cut it off with a snap.

I stood there, breathing hard, wiping my forehead. In the kitchen of Ocean Rest, no doubt, some wholesome domestic broth was being prepared to comfort the mumps-stricken Bootles, and here was I, in the open air, being handed a child like an unwanted parcel.

The window flew up again.

“Hi!”

A package weighing about a ton hit me on the head and burst, scattering its contents with a sound like a sack of pebbles meeting justice.

“Did you catch it?” said the face. “Dear me, you missed it! Never mind, you can get it at the grocer’s. Ask for Bailey’s Granulated Breakfast Chips. Tootles takes them for breakfast with a little milk. Be certain to get Bailey’s.”

My spirit, if you know what I mean, folded neatly and lay down. I took Tootles by the hand and trudged away. Napoleon leaving Moscow had a less trying march.

As we turned up the road we met Angela West.

The sight of her acted on the child like a trumpet. He pointed at her and said, “Wah!”

Angela stopped and smiled. I loosed him, and he ran straight to her with the confidence of a creature who has never yet had to earn his own lunch.

“Well, baby?” she said, bending to him. “So father found you again, did he?”

Then to me, pleasantly, as if this were the most natural thing in the world:

“Your little son and I made friends on the beach this morning.”

It was the absolute limit. Coming on top of the whiskered lunatic and his mumps and his Bailey’s Breakfast Chips, it struck me such a blow that before I could deny paternity she had nodded good-bye and was half-way down the road.

Freddie, when he heard, behaved less like a man of the world than like a man being introduced to execution. He leaped up, stared at the child, clutched his head, and remained silent for a time—during which I could see phrases forming, like storm-clouds. When he began, he did not stop for quite a while. I had not known he possessed so rich a vocabulary.

When he had at last paused to draw breath, he said:

“Well? Say something! Heavens, man, why don’t you say something?”

“You don’t give me a chance,” I said soothingly.

“What are you going to do about it?”

“What can we do about it?”

“We can’t spend our time acting as nurses to this—this exhibit.”

He got up.

“I’m going back to London.”

“Freddie!” I cried. My voice actually shook, because one feels abandoned when faced with a child and the prospect of breakfast chips. “Would you desert a pal at a time like this?”

“I would. This is your business.”

“Freddie,” I said, “you must stand by me. Do you realise this child has to be undressed and bathed and dressed again? You wouldn’t leave me to do all that single-handed? Freddie, we were at school together. Your mother likes me. You owe me a tenner.”

He sat down.

“Oh, well,” he said, in the tone of a man who has consented to be buried alive.

“Besides,” I said, “I did it for your sake.”

He looked at me oddly.

“Reggie,” he said, “I’ll stand a good deal, but I won’t stand for being expected to be grateful.”

Looking back, I see that what saved me from an asylum was sweets. I bought up most of the contents of the local sweet-shop. Acid drops, toffees, little chocolate things with a smell of vanilla and cheap cocoa, all of it. By keeping Tootles in a steady state of sticky bliss we managed to crawl through the day. At eight he fell asleep in a chair. Undressing him was easy: we unbuttoned every button in sight and, where there were no buttons, pulled until something gave. Then we carried him to bed.

Freddie stood over the heap of garments on the floor and I knew what he was thinking. Getting him out of his clothes had been brute force. Getting him back in was another matter. There was a long linen arrangement which might have been anything from a sail to a vestment. There was also a strip of pink flannel that looked as if it belonged to no known civilisation. We smiled at each other wanly, like two explorers who have reached a map’s blank edge.

In the morning I remembered there were children at the next bungalow but one. We went there before breakfast and borrowed their nurse. Women are extraordinary. In eight minutes she had that child dressed, scrubbed, and ready to face public life. She did it with the swift competence of somebody whisking flour into hot butter, not even pausing to wonder why the result is smooth when any man would have produced lumps. I showered her with money. She promised to come morning and evening.

I sat down to breakfast almost cheerful. The smell of bacon filled the cottage with hope; toast crackled; the kettle sang a mild domestic tune which, compared with London’s grinding noise, seemed like a language all of its own—one you understand in the bones.

“And after all,” I said, “there’s something to be said for having a child about the house. Makes it cosy. Domestic. Sort of—”

At that moment Tootles upset the milk over Freddie’s trousers. When Freddie returned, having changed, he remarked that King Herod was a much-maligned man, and that the more he saw of Tootles the less surprised he was at impulsive opinions on infanticide.

Two days later Jimmy Pinkerton arrived. He took one look at the child—who happened to be howling at the moment with a noise that belonged in a factory—and picked up his portmanteau.

“For me,” he said, “the hotel. I can’t write dialogue with that going on. Which of you adopted this little treasure?”

I told him about Medwin, the mumps, and Ocean Rest. Jimmy became interested, as a man does when he smells material.

“I might work this up for the stage,” he said. “It wouldn’t make a bad situation for act two of a farce.”

“Farce!” snarled Freddie, from somewhere near the edge of madness.

Jimmy ignored him and made a little picture in the sand with stones and seaweed as if Marvis Bay itself were his rehearsal room.

“It’s the old ‘Tiny Hand’ business,” he said. “Always safe. Parted lovers. Lisping child. Reconciliation over the little cradle. Big. Child centre. Girl left. Freddie up-stage by the piano. Can Freddie play?”

“He can play a little of ‘The Rosary’ with one finger.”

Jimmy shook his head.

“No. Cut the music. But the rest—perfect. We want the kid’s line. Something short, crisp. ‘Boofer lady, does ’oo love dadda?’—not definite enough. We want—ah! ‘Kiss Freddie.’ That’s it. Punch.”

“But, Jimmy,” I said, “how do we get Angela to the cottage? She cuts him. She won’t come within a mile.”

Jimmy frowned.

“Exterior set,” he said. “We corner her on the beach. Meanwhile, rehearsal. We must get the kid letter-perfect. First rehearsal for lines and business eleven sharp to-morrow.”

Freddie was too gloomy to be told. We concentrated on Tootles. It quickly became clear that the only way to rouse his intelligence was to connect the line with sweets, as one introduces a stock to persuade a sauce to behave.

“The chief difficulty,” said Jimmy at the end of the first rehearsal, “is establishing in his mind that those two words, clearly spoken, result automatically in acid drops. Once he grasps that, we have success.”

Coaching the kid was like trying to teach a seagull to sing hymns. Some days he seemed to have it—“Kiss Freddie” came out almost like a professional’s, and we congratulated each other. Then next day he would collapse and produce gibberish. Time flew.

“We must hurry,” I said. “His uncle may arrive any day.”

“And we haven’t an understudy,” said Jimmy. “Work!”

Tootles, to do him justice, was a trier. Whenever sweets appeared he had a dash at his line, and if he didn’t get it right he said something else, and if that failed he said more, until he was rewarded. His only fault was uncertainty.

Personally I would have risked it and staged the performance at the first chance, but Jimmy would not.

“We’re not nearly ready,” he said. “Today he said ‘Kick Freddie.’ That won’t win any girl’s heart. And she might do it. No—postpone production.”

But we didn’t. Fate ran the theatre.

The very next afternoon Freddie had settled at the piano—“The Rosary” beginning its endless circuit once more—and I was leading Tootles out for exercise when Angela came along the road towards the beach. The child yelled in welcome. She stopped at the foot of the steps.

“Hello, baby!” she said. “Good morning,” she said to me. “May I come up?”

She didn’t wait. She simply came, as if she had always done precisely what she chose. She stepped onto the veranda, began fussing over Tootles, and there, six feet away, in the sitting-room, Freddie smiting the piano with one finger as if trying to extract pity from it.

I attempted to break up the scene.

“We were just going down to the beach,” I said.

“Yes?” said Angela, vaguely, listening. The piano had ceased for a moment, then began again, and every note sounded like a warning bell. “So you’re having your piano tuned?” she said. “My aunt has been trying to find a tuner for ours. Do you mind if I go in and tell this man to come on to us when he’s finished here?”

“Er—not yet!” I said. “Not yet, if you don’t mind. He can’t bear to be disturbed when he’s working. Artistic temperament. I’ll tell him later.”

“Very well,” she said, getting up. “Ask him to call at Pine Bungalow. West is the name. Oh—he seems to have stopped. I suppose he’ll be out in a minute. I’ll wait.”

“Don’t you think—shouldn’t we be going on to the beach?” I said.

She had begun talking to Tootles and did not hear. She was feeling in her pocket for something.

“The beach,” I babbled.

People talk about Monte Carlo as if it were all moonlight on roulette wheels and the soft purr of money sliding into the pocket. That is the advertisement. The reality, at least on the trip I’m thinking of, was more like being chased round a floating drawing-room by fate, bad timing, and the sort of misunderstanding that sticks to you like fishbone.

We had drifted into Monaco Harbour on Marshall’s yacht, the _Circe_. Marshall was one of those elderly sportsmen who look as if they’ve been bred expressly to say “Ha!” at breakfast and “Tut!” at dinner. With him there were Mrs. Vanderley and her daughter Stella, Mrs. Vanderley’s maid Pilbeam, my own man Voules, and George Lattaker—dear old George, whom I had personally engineered into the party because a fellow never suspects that the simple act of inviting a friend to a pleasant yachting holiday can become accessory to lunacy.

George had come for business. His Uncle Augustus Arbutht, trustee of a legacy left by one of George’s aunts, was in Monte Carlo and due to hand over the proceeds now that George had reached the dignified milestone of twenty-five. George had an income already, but an income is a mild, well-bred animal; a legacy is a rhinoceros, and men naturally dream of rhinoceroses.

The day trouble truly got its knife into us began at seven in the morning, when I came out of a sound sleep into a world of sobbing and melodrama right outside my cabin door. There was a female voice, wet as a sponge: “Oh, Harold!” and a male voice—raised. Raised, mind you, by Voules.

In public Voules spoke the way you’d expect a marble bust to speak if someone fitted it with a tongue: calm, correct, and a little chilly. Hearing him in private sounding like a man whose tea has been poisoned was startling.

“Voules!” I yelled.

Silence fell as if someone had dropped a curtain. The sobbing retreated down the passage like a tide going out. Then a discreet tap, and Voules entered wearing his usual expression of dignified vacancy, as if he had just been thinking noble thoughts about crease-lines.

“I say, Voules,” I said. “Are you under the impression I’m to be crowned Queen of the May? It’s seven o’clock.”

“I understood you to summon me, sir.”

“I summoned you because I could hear you conducting a small civil war outside my door. What was that about?”

“I owe you an apology, sir. In the heat of the moment I raised my voice.”

“It’s a wonder you didn’t raise the ship. Who was the lady?”

“Miss Pilbeam, sir. Mrs. Vanderley’s maid.”

“And why was she sobbing ‘Oh, Harold!’ as if she were auditioning for tragedy?”

“I was breaking our engagement, sir.”

That took me squarely between the eyes. Voules engaged to Pilbeam! It was like hearing that Westminster Abbey had eloped with a gramophone. But you can’t pry into a man’s tender life—at least not without being hit with something hard—so I changed tack.

“Bring me breakfast,” I said. “I can’t wait for the rest.”

So I ate alone, and afterward went on deck with a cigarette and tried to recover the notion that the world was sane. The harbour lay bright and blue; the Casino glinted on shore like a confectioner’s dream; gulls made clean noises overhead, and the general sound of the morning had that pleasant, rinsed quality—ropes creaking softly, water licking the hull, distant footsteps on the quay. It was a relief after the thin, ceaseless grind of Monte Carlo itself, where even silence seems to advertise something.

The others began appearing presently. Stella Vanderley came up early. She looked pale and strained, as if she had been up all night listening to some wrong note in the world.

“Bad night?” I asked.

“I did not sleep well,” she said.

“Seen George?”

Something in her face tightened, like a hand on a curtain-cord.

“I have not seen Mr. Lattaker.”

This surprised me, because all through the voyage she and George had been thick as thieves, and I had been expecting, any hour, to receive the confidential murmur: “Reggie, old chap—she’s said yes.”

Before I could pursue the matter, the morning papers arrived. Mrs. Vanderley opened hers and emitted a scream of the sort women reserve for fallen princes and missing pearls.

“The poor, dear Prince!” she cried.

“What a shocking thing!” said Marshall.

“I knew him in Vienna,” said Mrs. Vanderley, warming to the subject with that quick sympathy the well-fed can produce for royalty in distress. “He waltzed divinely.”

I found the item. His Serene Highness the Prince of Saxburg-Leignitz—“Serene” always makes me feel as if they’ve been left out in the sun too long—had been assaulted late the previous night in a dark street on his way back from the Casino to his yacht. It seemed he had been strolling about without an escort (as you do when you’re a Serene Highness and the world is obliged to behave), and somebody had taken advantage of this to lay him out with zeal. He’d been found unconscious by a passer-by and carried to his yacht, where he still lay insensible.

“This will do someone no good,” I said. “What do you get for slugging a Serene Highness? They’ll have the chap’s head on a plate.”

Marshall read further. “‘Later, the pedestrian who discovered His Serene Highness proves to have been Mr. Denman Sturgis, the eminent private investigator. Mr. Sturgis has offered his services to the police, and is understood to be in possession of a most important clue.’ That fellow had charge of that kidnapping case in Chicago. If anyone can catch the man, he can.”

About five minutes after this comforting announcement, a boat hailed the _Circe_ and came alongside. A tall, thin man climbed the gangway and looked us over with the expression of one who has never in his life been charmed by anything. His eyes were the sort that seem to click.

“Good morning,” he said. “I believe you have a Mr. George Lattaker on board?”

“Yes,” said Marshall. “He’s down below. Want to see him? What name?”

“He would not know my name. I should like to see him on urgent business.”

Marshall motioned to me. “Reggie, my boy, go and hurry him up.”

I went down to George’s cabin and called him. No answer. I opened the door. The room was empty, and what’s more, the bunk looked as if no one had so much as sat on it. I went back on deck.

“He isn’t there,” I said.

“Not there?” said Marshall. “Perhaps he’s gone ashore. He’ll be back for breakfast. The gentleman can wait. Have you breakfasted? Then come down.”

The tall thin man said he would, and they all went below with the cheerful unconcern of people who haven’t yet had their day ruined.

I stayed on deck, smoking and listening to the cleaner sounds of the harbour—the water, the ropes, the far-off call of gulls—trying to convince myself that nothing worse could happen than a prince getting thumped.

Then I heard my name, breathed rather than spoken.

“Reggie!”

I turned. At the top of the gangway stood George—wearing evening dress, dusty to the eyebrows, hatless, and looking like a man who has been used as a mop.

“Great Scot!” I said.

“Sh!” he whispered. “Anybody about?”

“They’re all at breakfast.”

He exhaled like a diver reaching the surface, sank into my chair, and closed his eyes. He looked wretched. His hair had that battered look hair gets after a night of arguments with the universe.

“I say,” I said, touching his shoulder.

He sprang up with a smothered yelp.

“Did you do that?” he snapped. “What did you do it for? What’s the sense in touching a fellow on the shoulder? My nerves are sticking a yard out of my body this morning!”

“All right, old boy. Steady.”

He swallowed. His eyes had the wild shine of a man who has had too little sleep and too much life.

“I did a murder last night,” he said.

“What?”

“It’s the sort of thing that might happen to anybody. Directly Stella Vanderley broke off our engagement I—”

“Broke off your engagement? When did you get engaged?”

“About two minutes ago. It may have been less; I hadn’t a stopwatch. I proposed to her at ten last night in the saloon. She accepted. I was just going to kiss her when we heard someone coming. I stepped out into the corridor, and there was that maid—Pilbeam—coming along.”

He paused, and gave me a look of deep moral exhaustion.

“Have you ever been accepted by the girl you love, Reggie?”

“Never. I’ve been refused all over the place.”

“Then you won’t understand. I was off my head with joy. I felt I had to kiss something immediately. Anything. It might have been the ship’s cat. It wasn’t. It was Pilbeam.”

“You kissed Pilbeam?”

“I kissed her. And at that exact moment the saloon door opened and out came Stella.”

“Great Scott!”

“Precisely what I said. Stella, poor darling, not knowing the circumstances, took it the wrong way. She broke off the engagement. So I got out the dinghy and rowed off. I was mad. I didn’t care what happened. I only wanted to forget. I went ashore. I—well, it’s just on the cards I may have drowned my sorrows a bit. I don’t remember much except having the deuce of a scrap with somebody in a dark street, somebody falling, myself falling, and then me legging it for all I was worth. I woke up this morning in the Casino gardens. I’ve lost my hat.”

A cold, neat little click sounded in my head, like a latch being lifted. I snatched up the paper.

“Read this,” I said.

He read. His face altered in stages—blank disbelief, dawning horror, then something like a man watching his own funeral.

“Good heavens,” he said.

“You didn’t… you didn’t do that to His Serene Nibs, did you?”

“Reggie,” he said, in a hollow voice, “this is awful.”

“Cheer up. They say he’ll recover.”

“That doesn’t matter.”

“It matters to him.”

He read again, slower. His finger stopped.

“It says they have a clue.”

“They always say that.”

“But—my hat!”

“Yes?”

“My hat. I must have dropped it during the scrap. And this man—Denman Sturgis—must have found it. It had my name in it.”

At that moment the world turned itself over and showed the next unpleasant fact, as casually as a waiter presenting a bill.

“George,” I said slowly, “there’s a tall, thin chap aboard. Came just before you did. Wouldn’t give his name. Said he wanted to see you urgently. He’s down there now, eating breakfast.”

George went the colour of a damp napkin.

“No.”

“I don’t like the look of him. Eye like a gimlet. I’d lay odds it’s this Sturgis.”

“He had… he had a hat?”

“Of course he had a hat.”

“I mean mine. Was he carrying a parcel?”

“By Jove—yes. He was.”

George made a small strangled sound, the way a kettle begins to complain.

“Reggie, old man—lend me a tenner. I’ve got to get over the frontier into Italy. I’ll wire my uncle to meet me in—”

“Look out!” I hissed. “Someone’s coming.”

George dived out of sight just as Voules appeared at the top of the companion-way, bearing a letter on a tray as if letters were sacred objects.

“I beg your pardon, sir,” said Voules. “I thought I heard Mr. Lattaker’s voice. A letter has arrived for him.”

“He isn’t here,” I said quickly.

“Shall I remove the letter, sir?”

“No. Give it to me. I’ll pass it on.”

“Very good, sir.”

“Oh—Voules. Are they still breakfasting? The gentleman who came to see Mr. Lattaker?”

“He is at present occupied with a kippered herring, sir.”

“Kippered herring,” I repeated, as if this were a strategy.

“Yes, sir.”

“Right.”

Voules withdrew with the air of a man whose inner life consists of folded shirts. I called George back. He emerged, twitchy as a cat in a room full of rocking-chairs.

“It was only Voules,” I said. “Here’s a letter for you. The sleuth is still at it—busy with kippers.”

“That’ll hold him,” said George feverishly. “Full of bones.”

He tore open the letter, read a few lines, and made a sound like a man discovering his bath is full of ice.

“Well, I’m hanged,” he said.

He handed it to me. It was from Uncle Augustus, and it ran:

“My dear George—I shall be seeing you to-morrow, I hope; but I think it is better, before we meet, to prepare you for a curious situation that has arisen in connection with the legacy which your father inherited from your Aunt Emily… You have doubtless heard your father speak of your twin-brother Alfred, who was lost or kidnapped… Yesterday, however, I received a letter purporting that he has been living all this time in Buenos Ayres… He states that he is on his way to meet me and will arrive any day now… It will be necessary to examine credentials…—Your affectionate uncle, AUGUSTUS ARBUTT.”

I read it twice. Then, on the second reading, a notion came to me, bright and sudden as the smell of coffee drifting into a room where everybody has been brooding.

“George,” I said, “this lets you out.”

“Lets me out of half the money, you mean,” he said bitterly. “If this Alfred’s genuine, we split. The will left it to my father, or failing him his ‘offspring.’ I thought that meant me. Now it appears it means a crowd. I call it rotten—springing unexpected offspring at the eleventh hour.”

“You chump,” I said. “It saves your neck. Stay here and be Alfred.”

He stared as if I had suggested he become a seaweed.

“You ought to be in some sort of home, Reggie.”

“Ass! Twins aren’t exactly alike only in novels where authors need them to be. Who’s to say you aren’t Alfred if you swear you are? Your uncle’s letter backs the idea that there is an Alfred. All you’ve got to do is keep it up long enough to get this Sturgis fellow off the yacht. We sail for England tomorrow.”

His face shifted. Hope, that reckless thing, crept back.

“Why—by Jove—it might work,” he said.

“Of course it will.”

“And as Alfred I’d get a chance to talk to Stella and make things right for George.” He brightened further. “Reggie, you’re a genius.”

“No, no.”

“You are.”

“Well—only by fits.”

At that moment there was a gentle cough behind us. We spun round. Voules stood there, immaculate and deadly calm.

“What the devil are you doing here, Voules?” I said.

“I beg your pardon, sir. I have heard all.”

There was a silence in which you could hear the harbour water fretting softly against the hull, and, farther away, the thin metallic fuss of the shore—motor launches, distant horns, the beginnings of that modern racket which makes even sunlight feel commercial.

“Voules,” I said, going warm and earnest, “Voules is all right. You wouldn’t give us away, would you?”

“Yes, sir.”

“Yes? You would?”

“Yes, sir.”

“But, Voules,” I said, “be reasonable. What do you gain by it?”

“Financially, sir, nothing.”

“And by keeping quiet,” I said, tapping him lightly on the chest as if bribery were a medical treatment, “you might gain a very considerable sum.”

“Am I to understand, sir, that because you are rich and I am poor you think you can buy my self-respect?”

“Oh, come!” I said.

“How much?” said Voules.

We came to terms. I wish I could say he behaved nobly, but he haggled like a fishmonger. It was a hundred down and another hundred later before he would consent to let us continue living. After that George went below to change into something more respectably twin-like.

He had hardly vanished when the breakfast party returned to the deck, looking benevolent and unaware that we were juggling prisons and princes between the deck-chairs.

“Did you meet him?” I asked Marshall.

“Meet whom?”

“George’s twin brother Alfred.”

Marshall blinked. “I didn’t know George had a brother.”

“Nor did he till yesterday. Kidnapped in infancy, everybody thought dead. He got a letter from his uncle. I shouldn’t wonder if George has gone ashore to see him. Meanwhile Alfred’s turned up. He’s down in George’s cabin smartening up. The likeness is extraordinary. Look—here he comes now.”

George appeared, freshly brushed, in an ordinary yachting suit, looking for all the world like a man who had never hit a prince in his life. There was a moment of communal staring—the sort of pause you get when the orchestra misses a beat.

“Mr. Pepper tells me my brother is not on board,” said George, smiling pleasantly.

“It’s an amazing likeness,” said Marshall.

“Is my brother like me?” asked George.

“No one could tell you apart,” I said.

“I suppose twins always are,” said George. “But if there were ever a question of identification, there would be one way of distinguishing us. Do you know George well, Mr. Pepper?”

“He’s a dear old pal.”

“You’ve been swimming with him perhaps?”

“Every day last August.”

“Then you would have noticed if he had a mole like this on the back of his neck.”

He turned, stooped, and showed the mole, which usually lay hidden under his collar. I had seen it a hundred times at bathing.

“Has George a mole like that?” he asked.

“No,” I said firmly. “Oh, no.”

“You would have noticed if he had?”

“Yes. Oh, yes.”

“I’m glad,” said George. “It would be a nuisance not to be able to prove one’s own identity.”

That mollified them. A mole is a wonderful thing; it is like a seal on a document. From then on the imposture—if imposture is the word when a man is saving civilization—ran smoothly. George ate his lunch with appetite, talked with ease, and began, disconcertingly, to enjoy being Alfred.

Things held together until lunch-time, when I found him in the shade on the foredeck with Stella. They were deep in talk, and Stella’s face, which had been pinched that morning, had softened, as if some knot in her mind had begun to loosen.

When the gong sounded and the others moved below, George caught my arm.

“It’s all right,” he whispered, beaming. “What did I tell you?”

“What did you tell me?”

“About Stella. Alfred’s fixed it for George. I told her she looked worried, got her to tell me what was wrong, and then I explained that George is a pretty decent chap and oughtn’t to be turned down for what was clearly temporary insanity. She saw it.”

“Everything’s patched up?”

“Absolutely—if only we can produce George. Now if this infernal sleuth would stop hanging about like a bad smell in a pantry—he seems to have taken root.”

“I suppose he thinks you’re bound to come back,” I said. “He’s waiting.”

“He’s a nuisance,” said George.

We were heading for lunch when a boat hailed us. We leaned over the side.

“It’s my uncle,” said George.

A stout man came up the gangway with the confidence of someone who expects to be obeyed by the universe.

“Halloa, George!” he said. “Get my letter?”

“I think you are mistaking me for my brother,” said George smoothly. “My name is Alfred Lattaker.”

“What’s that?”

“I am George’s brother Alfred. Are you my Uncle Augustus?”

Uncle Augustus stared.

“You’re devilish like George,” he said.

“So everyone tells me.”

“And you’re really Alfred?”

“I am.”

“I’d like a word with you. Business.”

He flicked an eye at me, so I did the tactful thing and sidled away below.

At the foot of the steps I ran into Voules.

“I beg your pardon, sir,” he said. “If it would be convenient, I should be glad to have the afternoon off.”

His manner was beautifully normal, as if he hadn’t just sold his silence for two hundred pounds. I granted it.

I had lunch—George didn’t appear—and as I was leaving the saloon I was pounced upon by Pilbeam. Her eyes were red; she looked as if she had been rinsed in sorrow.

“I beg your pardon, sir, but did Mr. Voules ask you for the afternoon?”

I couldn’t see what it had to do with her, but she was so agitated that I told her yes.

“I’ve given him the afternoon off.”

She collapsed. Not theatrically, either—properly. It was beastly. I’m helpless with crying women. I tried “There, there,” and discovered it has the same effect as blowing on a fire.

“He—he said he was going to the tables to gamble away all his savings,” she sobbed, “and then shoot himself, because he had nothing left to live for.”

The earlier corridor scene clicked into place. I loathe mysteries, and I couldn’t have Voules—the finest trouser-crease artist in Europe—going about shooting himself. I pressed Pilbeam. Gently at first, then with firmness when she continued to dissolve.

At last the facts came out in broken pieces: Voules had seen George kiss her the night before.

Everything, in that instant, made a dreadful sort of sense—the engagement broken, the dawn quarrel, Voules’s sudden need for melodrama. I decided George—persuasive Alfred—must soothe Voules as he had soothed Stella. A man who can calm a woman’s wounded pride in two hours can surely talk a valet out of suicide.

I went to find George on the foredeck. He was alone, and if ever a face was “sicklied o’er with the pale cast of care,” George’s was. He looked green—like a man who has eaten something that fought back.

“Finished with your uncle?” I asked.

He gave a smile that wouldn’t have deceived a child.

“There isn’t any uncle,” he said. “There isn’t any Alfred. And there isn’t any money.”

I stared.

“Explain yourself, old top.”

“It’s simple. The old crook—Augustus—has spent every penny of the trust money. He’s been at it for years, ever since I was a kid. When the time came to cough up, he went to the tables hoping for a miracle and lost the last scrap. Then he had to delay me while he got away, so he invented this twin-brother business. He knew I’d find out sooner or later, but it bought him time to bolt. He’s on his way to South America now.”

“You let him go?” I said.

“What could I do? With Sturgis on board I can’t afford a fuss. I can’t prove there’s no Alfred when my only chance of keeping out of prison is to be Alfred.”

I tried to give him something to hold on to.

“Well,” I said, “you’ve made things right with Stella, anyway.”

“What’s the good of that now?” said George bitterly. “I’ve hardly any money and no prospects. How can I marry her?”

I looked out across the harbour. The water made its small honest noises against the hull, but from the shore came the harder sounds—engines, shouting, the metallic clatter of a town built to eat men’s luck. It struck me that we were, all of us, marooned between two kinds of racket: the clean, old music of the sea, and the modern din that turns everything—even a prince in a dark street—into a headline and a clue.

“It looks to me,” I said at last, “as if things are in a bit of a mess.”

“You’ve guessed it,” said poor old George.

Has it ever struck you—properly struck you, I mean, not the sort of airy, after-dinner notion that floats past between the cheese and the port—what a clean, shining nerve Woman possesses when it comes to arranging other people’s lives as if they were place-settings at her table? I’ve brooded on it a good deal, and not in an abstract way either, but with the raw, immediate intimacy of a man who has been invited down to the country on the strength of fresh eggs and golf, only to discover that he has been booked to do burglary for the comfort of an artistic temperament. The thing reached its summit in the matter of the Yeardsley “Venus.”

To make the stew intelligible, I must give you the ingredients in their proper order, as any decent cook-book historian would insist. When I first knew her she was Elizabeth Shoolbred: old Worcestershire stock, money like autumn apples, and one of those tall, calm girls with a face that looks as if it was born under a good light and never once had to bargain for it.

Her brother Bill was at Oxford with me. Through Bill I met Elizabeth; through Elizabeth I lost, for a brief week, the easy digestion of the bachelor. I loved her. I loved her in the full-bodied, unanalysed way—like a man who has never had to translate desire into another man’s dialect. We were engaged for about seven days, during which I began to view furniture catalogues with the sombre reverence usually reserved for prayer-books, and whenever an orchestra struck up “The Wedding Glide” my spine did a slow, devotional shiver.

Then she broke it off.

A month later she married Clarence Yeardsley, an artist.

The shock wore off, as shocks will when one is soaked daily in golf, billiards, racing, and the club’s consoling noise—the affectionate clangour of fellows who rally round and drown your thoughts like a brass band stationed inside your skull. By the time I’d finished being tragic about it, I was, to be perfectly frank, vastly relieved. I had come to believe that the dead past had been decently composted and that, like last year’s potato peelings, it could do no further harm.

Then one morning, when I came down to breakfast with a head that wanted buttered toast and silence, there arrived a letter like a flash of lemon on a cut.

“MY DEAR OLD REGGIE,—What ages it seems since I saw anything of you. How are you? We have settled down here in the most perfect old house, with a lovely garden, in the middle of delightful country. Couldn’t you run down here for a few days? Clarence and I would be so glad to see you. Bill is here, and is most anxious to meet you again. He was speaking of you only this morning. Do come. Wire your train, and I will send the car to meet you.

—Yours most sincerely, ELIZABETH YEARDSLEY.

“P.S.—We can give you new milk and fresh eggs. Think of that!

“P.P.S.—Bill says our billiard-table is one of the best he has ever played on.

“P.P.S.S.—We are only half a mile from a golf course. Bill says it is better than St. Andrews.

“P.P.S.S.S.—You must come!”

A fellow isn’t made of stone. New milk has a smell like warm grass and clean pails; fresh eggs have that shy, farmyard bloom which makes breakfast feel like a sacrament. And golf—well, golf is a religion with me, and Elizabeth knew it. That St. Andrews remark—she had flung it in like a pinch of paprika, and it caught.

So I went.

Bill met me at the station with the car, looking as if the rural air had done everything for his cheeks and nothing for his spirit. He gripped my hand as if I were an emergency ration.

“Thank goodness you’ve come,” he said, as we bumped off along lanes that smelt of hedge-mint and damp earth. The acoustic world out here was in excellent order: birds gossiping in the trees, tyres whispering over gravel, the engine purring instead of roaring—none of your London din that saws at the nerves like cheap music in a shop. “I was just about at my last grip.”

“What’s the trouble, old scout?”

“If I had the artistic what’s-its-name,” he said, “if the mere mention of pictures didn’t give me the pip, I dare say it wouldn’t be so bad. As it is, it’s rotten!”

“Pictures?”

“Pictures. Nothing else is mentioned in this household. Clarence is an artist. So is his father. And you know what Elizabeth is like when you give her her head.”

That brought it back: long marches through galleries in the old days, with Elizabeth moving from frame to frame as if canvases were loaves and she were feeding on them, while I followed like a dog that has been told, in a language it doesn’t speak, that this is good for it. I’d somehow assumed marriage to an artist would cure her of it by overdose. Bill’s tone suggested otherwise.

“They talk pictures at every meal,” he said. “Not as if it were seasoning—no, the whole dish. It makes a chap feel like he’s eating sawdust while they discuss the brushwork. How long are you down for?”

“A few days.”

“Take my tip, and let me send you a wire from London. I go there to-morrow. I promised to play against the Scottish. The idea was that I was to come back after the match. But you couldn’t get me back with a lasso.”

I tried to salvage the trip.

“But, Bill, old scout, your sister says there’s a most corking links near here.”

He turned his head and stared at me so hard the car nearly took a bite out of the bank.

“You don’t mean honestly she said that?”

“She said you said it was better than St. Andrews.”

“So I did. Was that all she said I said?”

“Well, wasn’t it enough?”

“She didn’t happen to mention that I added the words ‘I don’t think’?”

“No, she forgot to tell me that.”

“It’s the worst course in Great Britain.”

I felt as if somebody had promised me a soufflé and delivered a cold suet pudding. I can’t do without my daily golf—bad habit or no. Still, I tried again.

“We’ll have to take it out in billiards. I’m glad the table’s good.”

“It depends what you call good. It’s half-size, and there’s a seven-inch cut just out of baulk where Clarence’s cue slipped. Elizabeth has mended it with pink silk. Very smart and dressy it looks, but it doesn’t improve the thing as a billiard-table.”

“But she said you said—”

“Must have been pulling your leg.”

We turned in at the gates of a good-sized house set back from the road, crouching among trees. In the dusk it looked black and thoughtful, as if it had opinions about trespassers. There was still that healthy country hush—no factory throb, no tram shriek—just the soft breath of leaves and somewhere, far off, a cow expressing itself in a low, contented vowel.

And then, like a sensible man, I began to feel uneasy. Elizabeth had dangled golf with a cynical knowledge of my weakness. Why? A sudden chill took me: perhaps she had some girl down here and meant to marry me off, the way young married women do with the brisk zeal of people arranging flowers. She’d said the house contained only Clarence, herself, Bill, and Clarence’s father, but a woman who could invoke St. Andrews so casually might lie about a parlourmaid.

“Bill,” I said, “there aren’t any frightful girls or any rot of that sort stopping here, are there?”

“Wish there were,” he said. “No such luck.”

As we drew up, the front door opened and a woman’s figure appeared in the light, and her voice came out over the gravel with an odd, dramatic relish.

“Have you got him, Bill?” she said.

In my frame of mind it sounded less like hospitality than like Lady Macbeth checking the inventory.

“Do you mean me?” I said.

She stepped down into the light. It was Elizabeth—exactly as she had been, as if time had been afraid to touch her.

“Is that you, Reggie? I’m so glad you were able to come. I was afraid you might have forgotten all about it. You know what you are. Come along in and have some tea.”

Now, tea in a country house ought to be a kindly thing: the clink of china, the domestic little sigh a teapot makes, the smell of hot water meeting leaves. But I was still braced for ambush. And then Clarence arrived.

If you’ve ever been turned down by a girl and later introduced to the fellow she married, you’ll recognize the sensation: the mind, like an astonished cook, refusing to believe the ingredients could make that particular dish. You think, “There must be some mistake. She can’t have preferred this to me.”

Clarence was a little, thin, nervous-looking chap of about thirty-five, with grey at the temples and hair that had lost its discipline. He wore pince-nez and a drooping moustache, and the whole effect was of a man who had been over-boiled by life and served without sauce. In front of him I felt, if not a hero, at least like something that had been properly roasted.

“How do you do, Mr. Pepper? Hark! Can you hear a mewing cat?” said Clarence, all in one rush.

“Eh?” I said.

“A mewing cat. I feel sure I hear a mewing cat. Listen!”

We listened. The house had its own soundscape: distant clocks doing their careful arithmetic, a fire breathing softly, a faint murmur from the servants’ regions like the sea heard through a wall. Then the door opened and an old, white-haired gentleman came in—Clarence’s father, cut from the same pattern but in an earlier edition.

“Father,” said Clarence, “did you meet a mewing cat outside? I feel positive I heard a cat mewing.”

“No,” said the father, with a slow shake; “no mewing cat.”

“I can’t bear mewing cats,” said Clarence. “A mewing cat gets on my nerves!”

“A mewing cat is so trying,” said Elizabeth.

“I dislike mewing cats,” said old Mr. Yeardsley.

That disposed of the cat, for the moment, like a topic that has been properly carved and served. They moved on to pictures.

They talked pictures steadily till it was time to dress for dinner. They did, I mean; I sat there like a man at a banquet where everybody else is eating a language he can’t digest. Eventually somebody mentioned picture-robberies, and the “Monna Lisa,” and it reminded me of a paragraph I’d seen in the evening paper on the train—some chap had had a valuable painting pinched the night before. At last, I thought, something with a bit of narrative to it. The paper was in my overcoat in the hall; I fetched it.

“Here it is,” I said. “A Romney belonging to Sir Bellamy Palmer—”

They all shouted “What!” at the same instant, like a chorus that has been rehearsing.

Elizabeth snatched the paper. “Let me look! Yes. ‘Late last night burglars entered the residence of Sir Bellamy Palmer, Dryden Park, Midford, Hants—’”

“Why, that’s near here,” I said. “I passed through Midford—”

“Dryden Park is only two miles from this house,” said Elizabeth, and I noticed her eyes sparkle in a way that was not, in my opinion, the normal feminine response to burglary. “Only two miles! It might have been us! It might have been the ‘Venus’!”

Old Mr. Yeardsley bounded in his chair.

“The ‘Venus’!” he cried, as if someone had announced an heir.

They were excited—genuinely, almost musically excited—like people tasting a new flavour they’ve been told is rare. My news had landed as neatly as a soufflé.

It wasn’t until after dinner that I saw the Yeardsley “Venus.” That sounds incredible, because when Elizabeth showed it to me it dominated the wall with the confident nakedness of a thing that assumes attention. But at meals my senses are engaged in more practical worship: the smell of gravy, the crackle of roast skin, the butter’s faint sweetness melting into hot potatoes. If there had been a small earthquake behind my chair I might have attributed it to indigestion and carried on.

After dinner we were alone in the drawing-room. Old Mr. Yeardsley was writing letters in the morning-room; Bill and Clarence were playing billiards on the half-size table with the pink silk surgery. The house had settled into that pleasant nighttime domestic rhythm: distant footsteps, the quiet clink of glass, the fire making that steady, reassuring sound of work being done.

Elizabeth had been silent a while, as if reducing something. Then she leaned toward me.

“Reggie.”

The way she said it—soft, precise—gave me that premonitory feeling you get when you smell something burning and realize it may be your own dinner.

“What-o?” I said, nervously.

“Reggie, I want to ask a great favour of you.”

“Yes?”

She stooped and put a log on the fire, and went on with her back to me, as if the flames needed managing and I didn’t.

“Do you remember, Reggie, once saying you would do anything in the world for me?”

There it was. The pure, undiluted cheek of Woman. After the way she had handed me the push and then married Clarence, you’d have thought she’d let that remark die quietly. It had been a pre-Clarence declaration—made in a dialect of devotion that no longer served as legal tender.

Still, I had said it.

“Oh, yes,” I managed.

“There’s something you can do for me now, which will make me everlastingly grateful.”

“Yes,” I said, like a man hearing the waiter describe a dish and not yet realizing it contains anchovies.

“Do you know, Reggie,” she said suddenly, “that only a few months ago Clarence was very fond of cats?”

“Eh! Well, he still seems—er—interested in them, what?”

“Now they get on his nerves. Everything gets on his nerves.”

“Some fellows swear by that stuff you see advertised all over the—”

“No, that wouldn’t help him. He doesn’t need to take anything. He wants to get rid of something.”

“I don’t quite follow. Get rid of something?”

“The ‘Venus,’” said Elizabeth.

I blinked.

“You saw the ‘Venus,’” she said.

“Not that I remember.”

“Well, come into the dining-room.”

We went in and she switched on the lights. The dining-room had that particular smell a good one always has: polished wood, faint cold ashes, and the memory of food—like the last notes of a song lingering after the orchestra stops.

“There,” she said.

On the wall near the door was a large oil painting. Classical, I suppose. What I mean is—you know what I mean. It was the sort of picture that makes you wonder how the sitter kept warm. It was also the sort of picture that would make you choke on your soup if you were constitutionally inclined to choke.

“Is that the ‘Venus’?” I said.

She nodded.

“How would you like to have to look at that every time you sat down to a meal?”

“Well, I don’t know. I don’t think it would affect me much. I’d worry through all right.”

She gave an impatient little jerk of the head.

“But you’re not an artist,” she said. “Clarence is.”

Now I began to see a slit of daylight. Artistic Temperament—A.T.—will account for any behaviour short of cannibalism. It’s the universal sauce: pour it over insanity and it becomes culture.

She told me the story. Old Mr. Yeardsley, it appeared, was an amateur painter; this “Venus” was his masterpiece. He said so himself, and, being the cook, he had a right to praise his own broth. When Clarence married, his father gave him the “Venus” as a wedding present and hung it in place with his own hands. Then the professional artist, Clarence, saw flaws in it—drawing, expression, colouring. Looking at it made him ill, and yet he couldn’t bear to hurt his father by taking it down. So he endured it three times a day at meals until the strain began to work on his nerves. Elizabeth, like a practical woman tasting the salt and deciding something must be done, had resolved to remove the offending dish.

“Now you see,” she said.

“In a way,” I said. “But don’t you think it’s making rather heavy weather over a trifle?”

“Oh, can’t you understand? Look!” Her voice dropped, as if we were in a church. She switched on another light, illuminating the picture next to the “Venus.” “There! Clarence painted that!”

She looked at me expectantly, like someone offering you a spoonful and waiting for ecstasy. I studied Clarence’s work. It was another classical scene. To me it looked very much like the other one. But then, I’m not a food historian of art; I can’t tell one sauce from another by its pedigree.

Some criticism was expected, so I hazarded one.

“Er—‘Venus’?” I said.

“No. ‘Jocund Spring,’” she snapped, switching off the light again. “I see you don’t understand even now. You never had any taste about pictures. When we used to go to the galleries together, you would far rather have been at your club.”

This was so accurate it left me without defence. She came closer and put her hand on my arm.

“I’m sorry, Reggie. I didn’t mean to be cross. Only I do want to make you understand that Clarence is suffering. Suppose—suppose—well, let us take the case of a great musician. Suppose a great musician had to sit and listen to a cheap vulgar tune—the same tune—day after day, day after day, wouldn’t you expect his nerves to break! Well, it’s just like that with Clarence. Now you see?”

“Yes, but—”

“But what? Surely I’ve put it plainly enough?”

“Yes. But what I mean is, where do I come in? What do you want me to do?”

“I want you to steal the ‘Venus.’”

I stared at her.

“You want me to—?”

“Steal it. Reggie!” Her eyes shone with excitement, the way a cook’s eyes shine when she has invented a solution that uses nothing but what’s already in the larder. “Don’t you see? It’s Providence. When I asked you to come here, I had just got the idea. I knew I could rely on you. And then by a miracle this robbery of the Romney takes place at a house not two miles away. It removes the last chance of the poor old man suspecting anything and having his feelings hurt. Why, it’s the most wonderful compliment to him. Think! One night thieves steal a splendid Romney; the next the same gang take his ‘Venus.’ It will be the proudest moment of his life. Do it to-night, Reggie. I’ll give you a sharp knife. You simply cut the canvas out of the frame, and it’s done.”

“But one moment,” I said. “I’d be delighted to be of any use to you, but in a purely family affair like this, wouldn’t it be better—how about tackling old Bill?”

“I have asked Bill already. Yesterday. He refused.”

“But if I’m caught?”

“You can’t be. All you have to do is to take the picture, open one of the windows, leave it open, and go back to your room.”

It did sound simple, the way recipes do when they say “take a little stock” and omit the part where you must first raise and slaughter the chicken.

“And as to the picture itself—when I’ve got it?”

“Burn it. I’ll see that you have a good fire in your room.”

“But—”

She looked at me. And Elizabeth’s eyes, I regret to say, were a dialect I still understood perfectly, however much time and Clarence and Worcestershire had intervened.

“Reggie,” she said—just that. “Reggie.”

Well, there are certain sounds—the crackle of a fire, the low hush of a good house at night, the remembered lilt of “Auld Lang Syne”—that loosen a man’s sense like warm butter. The past, though supposedly dead, can still send up a scent.

“All right,” I said. “I’ll do it.”

I don’t know if you’re one of those hardened types who commit crimes as casually as ordering a chop. If you’re not, you’ll understand that the business felt less like a romantic caper when I sat in my room later, waiting for one o’clock, than it had when I agreed to it in the bright dining-room. On paper it had been easy. In the dark it developed corners.

Time crawled. The house’s sounds grew larger: a board settling with a small complaint, the wind rubbing leaves together, a distant clock striking with maddening calm. At a quarter to one I could stand it no longer. I lit the lantern I had borrowed from Bill’s bicycle, took my knife, and slunk downstairs, trying to move in harmony with the house rather than against it.

In the dining-room I opened the window. For a moment I was tempted to smash a pane to give the affair local colour—burglary ought to have a bit of theatre—but the noise would have been industrial, obscene in that quiet rural acoustic, and I desisted. I set the lantern on the table and reached for the picture—

And then something hit me under the chin as if the entire British Empire had decided to express itself through a fist. There was a burst of sparks inside my skull; the world turned into a saucepan being vigorously stirred. Next thing I knew, something cold and wet splashed my face, and a voice—Bill’s—said, “Feeling better now?”

I sat up. The lights were on. I was on the floor. Bill was kneeling beside me with a soda siphon, like a waiter reviving a difficult customer.

“What happened?” I said, cautiously testing my jaw as one tests a cracked eggshell.

“I’m awfully sorry, old man,” he said. “I hadn’t a notion it was you. I came in here and saw a lantern on the table, and the window open, and a chap with a knife in his hand, so I didn’t stop to make inquiries. I just let go at his jaw for all I was worth. What on earth do you think you’re doing? Were you walking in your sleep?”

“It was Elizabeth,” I said, spitting soda-water and indignation in equal parts. “Why, you know all about it. She said she had told you.”

“You don’t mean—”

“The picture. You refused to take it on, so she asked me.”

“Reggie, old man,” he said, “I’ll never believe what they say about repentance again. It’s a fool’s trick and upsets everything. If I hadn’t repented, and thought it was rather rough on Elizabeth not to do a little thing like that for her, and come down here to do it after all, you wouldn’t have stopped that sleep-producer with your chin. I’m sorry.”

“Me, too,” I said, giving my head another shake to make sure it was still properly attached.

“Are you feeling better now?”

“Better than I was. But that’s not saying much.”

“Would you like some more soda-water? No? Well, how about getting this job finished and going to bed? And let’s be quick about it too. You made a noise like a ton of bricks when you went down just now, and it’s on the cards some of the servants may have heard. Toss you who carves.”

“Heads.”

“Tails it is,” he said, uncovering the coin. “Up you get. I’ll hold the light. Don’t spike yourself on that sword of yours.”

It was as easy as Elizabeth had promised. Four quick cuts, the knife slipping through canvas with a faint, intimate rasp—the sound of a fabric giving up. The “Venus” came out of its frame like an oyster. I rolled it up. Bill set the lantern on the floor and went to the sideboard, collecting whisky, soda, and glasses with the brisk competence of a man provisioning for a siege.

“We’ve got a long evening before us,” he said. “You can’t burn a picture of that size in one chunk. You’d set the chimney on fire. Let’s do the thing comfortably. Clarence can’t grudge us the stuff. We’ve done him a bit of good this trip. To-morrow’ll be the maddest, merriest day of Clarence’s glad New Year. On we go.”

We went up to my room, and the fire was waiting, bright and well-fed, making that cheerful, stomach-warming sound which is half comfort and half appetite. Smoke and whisky have a way of settling a man’s nerves. We sat, smoking and yarning, the glasses clinking softly, and every now and then we cut a slice off the rolled canvas and fed it to the flames.

Burning a painting is a curious business. The oil and pigment give off a rich, acrid smell—like scorched nuts and old varnish—nothing like the clean sweetness of burning wood. The colours curl and blacken; the canvas stiffens, then collapses into ash that rises in delicate, defeated flakes. Piece by piece the Yeardsley “Venus” went the way of forgotten recipes and dead dialects—things that once meant the world to somebody, now reduced to smoke no one can translate.

And yet, with the cosiness of the room, and the good blaze, and the glow that comes of doing good by stealth, I don’t know when I’d had a jollier time. It reminded me of school nights long ago when we used to brew in my study, the world outside irrelevant, the present full of small, companionable sounds.

We had just put the last slice on the fire when Bill sat up suddenly and gripped my arm.

“I heard something,” he said.

I listened. My room was just over the dining-room, and the sound came up clearly through the floor: stealthy footsteps, by George, and then the sudden ugly clatter of a chair going over.

“There’s somebody in the dining-room,” I whispered.

Now, I’m the peaceful sort of cove who will gladly persuade himself that a noise is only the house settling, or a mouse practising scales. Bill, on the other hand, is the type who considers trouble a recreational sport. Burglars, to him, were not danger; they were entertainment.

He was out of his chair in a single jump.

“Come on,” he said. “Bring the poker.”

I brought the tongs as well. I felt like it. Bill pocketed the knife. We crept downstairs.

“We’ll fling the door open and make a rush,” said Bill.

“Supposing they shoot, old scout?”

“Burglars never shoot,” said Bill.

Which was comforting, provided the burglars were gentlemen who observed the same rules.

Bill gripped the handle, turned it sharply, and in we went.

And then we pulled up short, staring.

Looking back, I can own up to it: there was a stretch in the Rockmetteller Todd business when I seriously suspected Jeeves—Jeeves!—might be stumped. The man wore the faintly clouded expression of a butler who has opened an oyster and found, not a pearl, but a small note reading: Good luck.

Which, for the record, is not an expression you ever expect to see on Jeeves. Officially, he presses my clothes and keeps my ties in militant discipline; unofficially, he’s the sort of support system other fellows have to hire committees for. When an emergency turns up, I don’t telephone the police—I ring for Jeeves.

So when Rocky Todd began waving his troubles about, I naturally assumed Jeeves would simply take the universe by the scruff and shake a solution out of it.

It all started at an hour when even the milk bottles on the doorstep have a hunted, half-asleep look. I was in bed, engaging in that sound, nutritious kind of slumber that feels like being wrapped in warm toast, when the door burst open and somebody jabbed me between the lower ribs and the moral sense, then shook the bedclothes as if trying to beat crumbs from a tablecloth.

I blinked, focused, and there was Rocky.

Now this was wrong in several important particulars. Rocky lived out on Long Island, not in my bedroom. Also Rocky had a religion, and its central doctrine was that no human being should rise before noon unless there was a fire, an earthquake, or an editor at the foot of the stairs with a cheque. Rocky’s whole philosophy was built on the proposition that effort is vulgar. He wrote poetry—by which I mean that once a month he would sit up for three days, manufacture a few exhortations to the youth of America to go out and wrestle destiny, and then collapse for the remaining three hundred-odd days in a trance of righteous fatigue.

I once saw one of his things. It didn’t so much rhyme as shout at you in free verse, like a brass band trying to recruit you:

Be! Be! The past is dead.  
To-morrow is not born.  
Be to-day! To-day!  
Be with every nerve,  
With every muscle,  
With every drop of your red blood!  
Be!

It was printed in a magazine opposite a picture of a nearly nude fellow with the kind of muscles you get in Greek statues and advertisements for breakfast foods. The editors, Rocky said, paid him a hundred dollars for it. He celebrated by remaining in bed till four for the better part of a month, which struck me as an unusually spirited interpretation of “Be.”

However, Rocky had always been a safe bird for the long run, because he possessed an aunt in Illinois with money and a conscience. He had been named Rockmetteller after her, and as only nephew he was, so to speak, the heirloom. In his daydreams he planned to inherit and do absolutely nothing forever, except perhaps write an occasional ode encouraging young men, with life opening out before them and all its splendid possibilities, to pull up a chair, light a pipe, and put their feet on the mantelpiece.

And here he was, in the grey dawn, prodding me like a woodpecker with a grievance.

“Read this, Bertie!” he said, waving a letter in my face as if it were a summons from the Last Judgment. “Wake up and read this!”

“I can’t read before I’ve had tea and a cigarette,” I said, with dignity. I groped for the bell.

Jeeves appeared, looking absurdly fresh—like a man who has been up since five, strolling through meadows to collect dew in a thimble.

“Tea, Jeeves.”

“Very good, sir.”

He flowed out—Jeeves doesn’t walk so much as pour—and Rocky surged back with the letter.

“What is it?” I said. “What’s the trouble?”

“Read it!”

“I haven’t had my tea.”

“Then listen. It’s from my aunt.”

At this point I drifted off again briefly, only to wake to Rocky’s voice saying, in the tones of a man peering over a cliff at the wreckage of his future:

“So what on earth am I to do?”

Jeeves returned with the tray, and with him came sanity: the clink of china, the fragrant steam of tea, the comforting smell of toast—an aroma as English as apologies and as steadying. I sat up, lit a cigarette, and felt my soul re-enter my body.

“Read it again, Rocky, old top,” I said. “I want Jeeves to hear it. Mr. Todd’s aunt has written a rather rummy letter, Jeeves, and we require your counsel.”

“Very good, sir.”

Jeeves stood there as if he’d been carved by an artist who specialized in discreet competence. Rocky began:

“‘MY DEAR ROCKMETTELLER.—I have been thinking things over for a long while, and I have come to the conclusion that I have been very thoughtless to wait so long before doing what I have made up my mind to do now.’”

“What do you make of that, Jeeves?” I asked, buttering toast.

“It is not yet fully explicit, sir, though it may clarify at a later point.”

“It clarifies like pea soup,” said Rocky, with feeling.

“Proceed,” I said, crunching along.

“‘You know how all my life I have longed to visit New York and see for myself the wonderful gay life of which I have read so much. I fear that now it will be impossible for me to fulfil my dream. I am old and worn out. I seem to have no strength left in me.’”

“Sad, Jeeves, what?”

“Extremely, sir.”

“Sad nothing,” said Rocky. “It’s a pose. Last Christmas she could have wrestled a plough. The doctor says she’s perfectly sound, but she insists she’s a wreck and he has to agree or she’ll fire him. She’s got it fixed in her head that New York would kill her, so she stays in Illinois longing dramatically.”

“A bit like the fellow with his heart in the Highlands, Jeeves?”

“The parallel is not unsound, sir.”

Rocky slogged on:

“‘So I have decided that, if I cannot enjoy all the marvels of the city myself, I can at least enjoy them through you. I suddenly thought of this yesterday after reading a beautiful poem in the Sunday paper about a young man who had longed all his life for a certain thing and won it in the end only when he was too old to enjoy it. It was very sad, and it touched me.’”

“A thing,” said Rocky, with bitterness, “that my poetry hasn’t managed to do in ten years.”

“‘As you know, you will have my money when I am gone; but until now I have never been able to see my way to giving you an allowance. I have now decided to do so—on one condition. I have written to a firm of lawyers in New York, giving them instructions to pay you quite a substantial sum each month. My one condition is that you live in New York and enjoy yourself as I have always wished to do. I want you to be my representative, to spend this money for me as I should do myself. I want you to plunge into the gay, prismatic life of New York. I want you to be the life and soul of brilliant supper parties. Above all, I want you—indeed, I insist on this—to write me letters at least once a week giving me a full description of all you are doing and all that is going on in the city, so that I may enjoy at second-hand what my wretched health prevents my enjoying for myself. Remember that I shall expect full details, and that no detail is too trivial to interest.—Your affectionate Aunt, ISABEL ROCKMETTELLER.’”

He lowered the letter as if it weighed a hundred pounds. “Well?”

“Well?” I said. “Well, you lucky devil.”

“Yes,” he said wildly. “Lucky. What am I going to do?”

It took me a moment to grasp the full weirdness of the situation. Here was a man who had just been presented with a monthly allowance on condition he amused himself in New York—one of those burdens people usually pray for—and he looked as if the hangman had mailed him a timetable.

“Aren’t you bucked?” I asked.

“Bucked!”

“My dear chap, if I were you I’d be singing. This is pretty soft.”

He emitted a noise like a tortured harmonica and then began a speech on New York that made reformers sound like travel agents.

“Soft? To have to live in New York? To leave my cottage and take some overheated, smelly apartment in this festering Gehenna? To spend night after night with people who think a good time means enough noise for six and enough drink for ten? I loathe New York. I only come near it to see editors. It’s got a blight on it. The whole place is moral delirium tremens. I can’t stand it. The very thought of living here makes me sick. And you call it soft!”

He went on, warming to it.

“To share air with six million people! To wear stiff collars and decent clothes all the time! To—good Lord, I suppose I’d have to dress for dinner!”

This last horror seemed to strike him like a brick.

I was shocked to the marrow.

“My dear chap!” I said.

“Do you dress for dinner every night, Bertie?”

I turned slightly.

“Jeeves,” I said, icily, “how many suits of evening clothes have I?”

“We have three suits of full evening dress, sir; two dinner jackets—”

“Three.”

“For practical purposes, sir, two only. If you recall, we cannot wear the third. We have also seven white waistcoats.”

“And shirts?”

“Four dozen, sir.”

“And white ties?”

“The first two shallow shelves in the chest of drawers are completely filled with our white ties, sir.”

I looked at Rocky. “You see?”

Rocky writhed as if a wire had been attached to him and somebody had turned the current on.

“I won’t do it! I can’t! Most days I don’t get out of my pyjamas till five, and then I just put on an old sweater.”

Jeeves actually winced. You could see his spirit recoil, the way a chef recoils when someone proposes boiling truffles.

“Then what are you going to do?” I asked.

“That’s what I came to find out.”

“You might write and explain to your aunt.”

“I might—if I wanted her to leap to her lawyers and cut me out of her will.”

I saw the snag.

“What do you suggest, Jeeves?” I said.

Jeeves cleared his throat.

“It would appear, sir, that Mr. Todd is obliged—by the conditions under which the funds are advanced—to write Miss Rockmetteller long and detailed letters concerning his life in New York. If Mr. Todd remains in the country, the only means of complying would be for him to enlist a second party to acquire the experiences Miss Rockmetteller wishes described, and to convey them to Mr. Todd in the form of a report, upon which Mr. Todd might, with the assistance of his imagination, base the correspondence.”

Having delivered this in a voice as calm as simmering stock, Jeeves became silent.

Rocky stared. “Could he put it clearer, Bertie? It started as if it might make sense, then went a bit flickery.”

“Perfectly simple,” I said. “You get somebody to go round New York for you, noticing things, and then you write your aunt letters as if you’d been there yourself. That’s it, Jeeves?”

“Precisely, sir.”

Hope leaped into Rocky’s eyes.

“But who would do it?” he said. “It would have to be someone who notices things.”

“Jeeves!” I said. “Let Jeeves do it.”

Rocky looked at Jeeves as if he had just learned that butlers are a separate, superior species.

“But would he?”

“You would, wouldn’t you, Jeeves?”

For the first time in our long partnership, I saw Jeeves come within measurable distance of a smile. One corner of his mouth curved perhaps a quarter of an inch.

“I should be delighted to oblige, sir. As a matter of fact, I have already visited certain places of interest on my evening out, and it would be most enjoyable to make a practice of the pursuit.”

“Fine!” I said. “First stop, then—Reigelheimer’s, Forty-second Street.”

Jeeves shook his head with quiet finality.

“Pardon me, sir. People are no longer going to Reigelheimer’s. The place at the moment is Frolics on the Roof.”

I turned to Rocky. “You see? Leave it to Jeeves.”

And for a while, everything ran like a well-oiled frying pan.

Jeeves was happy because he loves using his brain; and also, I fancy, because he found New York’s night-life an interesting change from the steady domestic music of the apartment—the respectable hiss of the kettle, the gentle click of cutlery, the soft acoustics of good manners. He began returning from his evenings out trailing a faint bouquet of cigar smoke and expensive cologne, like a sauce reduced to a glossy finish. Once I saw him at the Midnight Revels, sitting near the dance floor with a cigar the size of a small baton and a bottle of the best, making notes in a small book. The band crashed and wailed, the dancers thumped, the whole place rang like a factory that had swallowed an orchestra—yet Jeeves sat there in the middle of it all, listening as though he could separate every note, every voice, every little human sound from the industrial din. His expression was one of austere benevolence, like a priest blessing a particularly sinful soufflé.

I was happy because I like Rocky and enjoyed doing him a good turn. Rocky was happy because he could remain in the country, in pyjamas, engaged in his favourite sport of watching worms and contemplating existence without moving his spine. And Aunt Isabel—Aunt Isabel was delighted, because she was receiving Broadway in weekly instalments.

Rocky’s letters, based on Jeeves’s notes, were marvellous. It was extraordinary. When I write to friends in London I tend to do it as if I were sending a telegram and paying by the word:

“DEAR FREDDIE,—Well, here I am in New York. It’s not a bad place. I’m not having a bad time. The cabarets aren’t bad. Don’t know when I shall be back. How’s everybody? Cheer-o!—Yours, BERTIE.  
PS.—Seen old Ted lately?”

I don’t care two straws about Ted, but if I didn’t add him I couldn’t get the thing onto page two.

Rocky, on the other hand—guided by Jeeves, who appeared to have the appetite of a food historian for detail—produced epics.

“DEAREST AUNT ISABEL,—How can I ever thank you enough for giving me the opportunity to live in this astounding city! New York seems more wonderful every day. Fifth Avenue is at its best, of course, just now. The dresses are magnificent!”

Pages about dresses. I hadn’t known Jeeves possessed such an encyclopaedic eye for fabrics, cuts, textures—the way satin drinks light, the way sequins chatter softly with movement, like rain in a tin pail. And then:

“I was out with some of the crowd at the Midnight Revels the other night. We took in a show first, after a little dinner at a new place on Forty-third Street.”

He described the dinner with a reverence that made it sound as if they had attended a sacred rite rather than eaten. The smell of hot rolls, the gleam of butter, the peppery tickle of something in a sauce—Jeeves must have observed it all, storing it away like recipes in a family book. Rocky’s letters went on:

“We were quite a gay party. Georgie Cohan looked in about midnight and got off a good one about Willie Collier. Fred Stone could only stay a minute, but Doug. Fairbanks did all sorts of stunts and made us roar. Diamond Jim Brady was there, as usual, and Laurette Taylor showed up with a party. The show at the Revels is quite good. I am enclosing a programme. Last night a few of us went round to Frolics on the Roof—”

And so on, yards of it, as though New York were a pantry and he was listing every spice.

I called Jeeves in.

“Jeeves, you’re a wonder.”

“Thank you, sir.”

“How you notice everything at these places beats me. I couldn’t tell anyone a thing except that I’ve had a good time.”

“It is a knack, sir.”

“Well, Mr. Todd’s letters ought to brace Miss Rockmetteller, what?”

“Undoubtedly, sir.”

And by Jove, they did. They braced her so completely that about a month after we started, I was sitting in the apartment one afternoon, smoking and letting my mind idle, when the door opened and Jeeves’s voice broke the air like a dropped plate.

He doesn’t speak loudly. Jeeves’s voice usually drifts about the place as soothing as the sound of a kettle preparing to sing. But what he said made me leap.

“Miss Rockmetteller!”

And in came a large, solid woman.

I don’t mind admitting I was floored. Hamlet, when the ghost turned up, must have felt much as I did. In my mind, Rocky’s aunt had become a permanent fixture of Illinois—like a respectable old orchard tree that could never possibly uproot itself and stride into Manhattan. Yet here she was, planted firmly on my carpet.

She looked less like an invalid than anyone I have ever seen, except my Aunt Agatha. In fact, she had a good deal of Aunt Agatha about her—an air of health that could be used as a weapon. She had the kind of face that suggests she might enjoy a brisk argument before breakfast, and win it.

“Good afternoon,” I said, because one has to say something.

“How do you do?” she replied briskly. “Mr. Cohan?”

“Er—no.”

“Mr. Fred Stone?”

“Not precisely. My name is Wooster—Bertie Wooster.”

This clearly disappointed her. The name Wooster meant nothing to her, which is always a blow.

“Isn’t Rockmetteller home?” she said. “Where is he?”

That pinned me to the wall. I couldn’t very well tell her he was in the country, wearing pyjamas and studying worms like an agrarian elder considering the prospects of spring.

There was a small sound behind me: Jeeves’s respectful cough—the one he uses when he is about to speak without being summoned, which always means he has spotted the exact moment a man is about to fall into the soup.

“If you remember, sir,” said Jeeves, “Mr. Todd went out in the automobile with a party in the afternoon.”

“So he did,” I said, clutching at this as a drowning man clutches at driftwood. “So he did. Did he say when he’d be back?”

“He gave me to understand, sir, that he would be somewhat late in returning.”

Jeeves vanished, leaving me alone with Aunt Isabel, who sat down without being asked and remained bolt upright on the edge of the chair, gripping her umbrella like a woman prepared to nail down the furniture if it tried to escape.

She looked at me in a most unpleasant manner—like something she had found in the bread bin.

“You seem very much at home here, young man,” she said. “Are you a great friend of Rockmetteller’s?”

“Oh, rather!”

She frowned as if she had expected better taste from her nephew.

“Well, you need to be,” she said, “the way you treat his flat as your own!”

That slam knocked the words out of me. I had been imagining myself the genial host, and suddenly she was speaking as if I were an intruder—part burglar, part plumber’s assistant who had wandered into the drawing-room with muddy boots and a wrench. It stung.

Conversation now hovered over an abyss. I grabbed at the one dependable life-raft known to man.

“Would you care for a cup of tea?” I said.

“Tea?”

She said it as if it were an exotic weed recommended by charlatans.

“Nothing like a cup after a journey,” I urged. “Restores you. Sort of puts you right.”

I tottered down the passage to Jeeves’s domain. He was reading the evening paper as calmly as if my world had not just been struck by a meteor.

“Jeeves,” I said, “we want some tea.”

“Very good, sir.”

“I say, Jeeves—this is a bit thick, what?”

“Indeed, sir?”

“She’s got the idea this place belongs to Mr. Todd. What put that into her head?”

“No doubt Mr. Todd’s letters, sir. It was my suggestion, if you recall, that they should be addressed from this apartment in order that Mr. Todd might appear to possess a central residence in the city.”

I remembered. We had all admired it at the time.

“Well, it’s bally awkward. She looks on me as if I’m some chap who hangs about touching Mr. Todd for meals and borrowing his shirts.”

“Yes, sir.”

“It’s rotten.”

“Most disturbing, sir.”

“And what about Mr. Todd? We’ve got to get him up here. After you’ve brought the tea you’d better send him a telegram—tell him to come by the next train.”

“I have already done so, sir. I took the liberty of writing the message and dispatching it by the lift attendant.”

“By Jove, you think of everything, Jeeves.”

“Thank you, sir. A little buttered toast with the tea?”

“Right,” I said. “Thanks.”

I returned to the sitting-room. She hadn’t altered her posture by so much as a molecule. She gave me another of those looks—cold, measuring, faintly wounded that I continued to exist.

“This is a surprise, what?” I said, after a silence that had developed its own climate.

“What is a surprise?”

“Your coming here, don’t you know. And so on.”

Her eyebrows rose.

“Why is it surprising that I should visit my only nephew?” she said.

Put that way, it did seem idiotic.

“Oh, rather. Of course. Certainly. What I mean is—”

Jeeves arrived with the tea, a tray bearing the familiar comforts: pot, cups, milk, and the toast—golden at the edges, butter ready to melt into it as if eager to be useful. The smell alone was a small, civilized hymn.

With something to do—something to pour—I felt less like a man being examined by a hostile committee.

“Tea, tea, tea—what? What?” I said, bustling.

It wasn’t my intended remark, but it covered the crisis. I poured her a cup.

She sipped. Then she set it down with a shudder of profound disapproval.

“Do you mean to say,” she demanded frostily, “that you expect me to drink this stuff?”

“Rather! Bucks you up, you know.”

“What do you mean by the expression ‘Bucks you up’?”

“Well—makes you full of beans. Makes you fizz.”

“I don’t understand a word you say.” Her gaze sharpened. “You’re English, aren’t you?”
