His Excellency, the Commander-in-Chief, set out to visit Madam Esmond with all the grandeur befitting the most distinguished figure in His Majesty’s North American colonies. A troop of dragoons led the procession from Williamsburg, their horses clattering through the dusty streets amid cheers and shouts from a loyal crowd—mostly enslaved people who lined the road to witness the spectacle. The General rode in his own ornate coach, flanked by Captain Talmadge, his Master of the Horse, who remained at the carriage door before riding alongside during the journey. Major Danvers, aide-de-camp, sat in the front seat beside Mr. Benjamin Franklin, the postmaster from Philadelphia—a man once a printer’s apprentice but now renowned for his sharp wit and wide-ranging knowledge of both the colonies and England, where he had traveled more than once.

“Truly remarkable,” the General remarked, tipping his hat with a gracious bow, “how a man of such modest beginnings could acquire so much learning—and yet carry himself with such refined manners. Mr. Franklin, you surprise me!”

The postmaster bowed low. “It has been my fortune,” he replied, “to meet gentlemen like yourself, and I’ve done my best to learn from them. My father was poor, and schooling in New England was scarce. But I’ve gathered what I could—though nothing, I assure you, compares to what one finds in England.”

General Braddock laughed heartily. “Ah, education! There are officers in the army, by George, who can’t tell whether to spell *bull* with one or two *b’s*. I’ve heard the Duke of Marlborough wasn’t much of a penman either. I never served under him—he was before my time—but he gave the French a sound beating, scholar or not.”

Mr. Franklin nodded. “I’m aware of that.”

“And my Duke?” Braddock continued, eyes twinkling. “No scholar, eh? Aha, Mr. Postmaster—I see your wink says it all.”

Franklin immediately dropped his expression, turning his gaze to the General’s jolly, round face with an innocence as pure as a child’s. “He may not be a scholar,” he said, “but no French general ever matched him. He saved the crown for the finest king in Christendom—our beloved King George.”

Off came Franklin’s hat, and from beneath his powdered wig, a halo of white dust floated into the air.

“He is the soldier’s true friend,” Braddock declared. “And the fiercest foe to those beggarly red-coated Scots rebels and scheming Jesuits who’d steal our liberty and religion. His Royal Highness—the Prince of Wales—is no scholar either, but he’s one of the noblest gentlemen alive.”

“I’ve seen him on horseback,” said Franklin, “at a review in Hyde Park. The Duke is indeed a magnificent rider.”

“You’ll drink to him tonight,” Braddock announced. “He’s the best master, the best friend, the best son any king could have—and the finest gentleman who ever wore an epaulet.”

“Epaulets are quite beyond my calling,” Franklin chuckled. “You know I live in a Quaker city.”

“Of course they are,” Braddock replied, good-naturedly. “To each his trade. You, sir, to your books—we welcome that. We, to fight and govern. Hey, gentlemen? Look at these roads! This cursed coach bounces like a buck!” He spotted a rider ahead, accompanied by two negro boys in livery. “Who’s that on the fine gelding?”

“Mr. Washington,” said the aide-de-camp.

“I’d take him for a corporal in the Horse Grenadiers,” Braddock declared. “He carries himself well. Knows this country, too, Mr. Franklin.”

“Yes, indeed.”

“And a remarkably polished young man—considering his upbringing. I’d have thought he’d have the grace of Europe about him.”

“He does his best,” Franklin said, glancing at the General—this towering embodiment of English elegance, swaying side to side in the carriage, face flushed as crimson as his coat, swearing at nearly every word, ignorant of everything outside military matters—except perhaps wine and women. Not born to nobility, yet fiercely proud of his lack of pedigree; brave as a bulldog, fierce, lustful, generous, prodigal, gentle in mood, quick to laugh, dull of wit, utterly unlearned, yet certain his country was the greatest in the world—and himself as noble a gentleman as any in it.

“Yes,” Franklin added, “he’s quite impressive for a provincial. He was beaten at Fort What-d’ye-call-um last year, near the Thingamy River. What was its name again, Talmadge?”

“The Lord knows, sir,” Talmadge answered, grinning. “And I dare say the Postmaster does too—judging by that wink.”

“Oh, Captain!”

“It was a trap,” said the General. “Only militia and Indians with him. Good day, Mr. Washington. Fine horse, sir. That was your first action?”

“That at Fort Necessity?” Washington replied, saluting gravely as he approached, followed by two smartly dressed negro grooms. “Yes, sir. I began poorly—never having faced battle until that unfortunate day.”

“You were raw levies,” Braddock said. “You should have seen our militia flee from the Scotch—curse them! You needed regular troops.”

“It is my deepest wish,” Washington said, “to serve with such men.”

“By George, we shall try to grant you that,” Braddock declared, letting loose another thunderous oath. The heavy coach rolled onward toward Castlewood, while Washington asked permission to ride ahead and inform Madam Esmond of the General’s imminent arrival.

The Commander-in-Chief moved so slowly that several lesser guests, invited to meet him, overtook the procession. Unwilling to pass the great man, they formed a long, dusty train behind the carriage. First came Mr. Dinwiddie, the Lieutenant-Governor, attended by his servants and accompanied by Parson Broadbent, the jovial chaplain of Williamsburg. Soon after came little Mr. Dempster, the schoolmaster, wearing his grand Ramillies wig reserved for formal occasions. Then appeared Mr. Laws, the judge, with his wife seated behind him on a pillion, and their negro servant carrying a box with her cap, mounted on a mule. The sight was so absurd that Major Danvers and Mr. Franklin burst into laughter—though quietly, so as not to disturb the General, who by now was fast asleep. Braddock, roused only slightly, ordered the odd rearguard to move forward, leaving him and his dragoons to proceed at their own pace. There would be room for all at Castlewood when they arrived.

There was food, drink, and tobacco for the soldiers of His Majesty; laughter and music for the enslaved; and a warm, generous welcome for their masters.

The General required constant help at table, holding out his glass repeatedly, demanding more wine, more meat, more ale. Nathan’s sangaree, a sweetened rum punch, he declared excellent, and drank deeply of it even before dinner. Cider, brandy, ale, and fine Bordeaux wine flowed freely—some of it brought personally by Colonel Esmond from England, which little Mr. Dempster claimed was fit for the Pope’s table, winking slyly at Parson Broadbent. The clergyman returned the wink, though he didn’t understand Latin—nor did he care. Broadbent was a roistering divine, fond of gambling, cockfighting, and drinking, who had spent time in Fleet Prison and frequented Newmarket and Hockley-in-the-Hole. Sent to Virginia by Lord Cingbars—whose mother, incidentally, was Broadbent’s own mother—his reputation was mixed. He had hunted birds, fish, and game with young George Esmond, and fought cocks with him. Some whispered that Broadbent was the poacher whom Washington once caught and caned by the river at Mount Vernon. He escaped into the dark, but bore the marks of a black eye and a torn collar when he returned weeks later. The boys had played cards, hunted, fished, and shared many adventures together—though Harry, the younger brother, often stayed behind to study with other tutors, while George read his own books, keeping his secrets close.

At the feast, every bird that flew and every fish that swam in Virginia waters found its way to Madam Esmond’s table. The gumbo was praised as perfection—so much so that young George’s black servant, discovered eating from a bowl behind the door by the late Colonel, was humorously christened “Gumbo” on the spot. The shad were rich and fresh, the stewed terrapins worthy of London aldermen—so good, in fact, that Braddock offered them to the Duke himself. Stewed terrapin, indeed, was fit for any duke—or emperor. The enslaved cooks at Castlewood were masterful, trained under the watchful eyes of both the late and present Madam Esmond. Certain dishes—especially the sweets and flans—Madam Esmond prepared herself, carving with dexterity, rolling up her lace-trimmed sleeves to reveal delicate arms and small hands, performing the ancient ritual of hospitality with grace and charm.

In those days, it was customary for the mistress of the house to preside over the meal with warmth and attention—encouraging guests, knowing how to carve, offering new courses, and cheering others on. She leaned toward Braddock: “I’ve kept the jowl of this salmon for you, Excellency—no refusal, please.” To Franklin: “You drink only water, sir—yet our cellar holds wines that give no headache.” To Judge Laws: “You love woodcock pie?”

“Because I know who makes the pastry,” the judge replied, bowing deeply. “I wish my wife had Madam Esmond’s skill. I often say to her, ‘My dear, I wish you had Madam Esmond’s hand.’”

“It is a very pretty hand,” said Franklin, “and I suspect others might admire it too.”

Mr. Esmond glanced uneasily at the remark.

“A lovely hand for light pie-crust,” the judge continued, “and my compliments, madam.” He believed such praise would please her. She simply replied that she had learned cooking in England, under her mother’s tutelage, and made dishes her father and sons enjoyed. If they liked them, she was glad.

More conversation followed, more dishes, ten times more meat than needed. Washington spoke little, but he, Talmadge, Danvers, and Franklin discussed roads, rivers, supply trains, artillery, and the challenges of colonial militias. On the table, Washington placed bits of bread to mark key points—then a servant wiped the Potomac away with a napkin and swept the Ohio into a spoon.

After dinner, Parson Broadbent rose and walked behind the Lieutenant-Governor’s chair to say grace, then resumed his place. Then came the desserts—puddings, cakes, custards, and preserves—so numerous and elaborate that few modern readers would care to list them. But Madam Esmond had prepared them all with care and pride.

When the table was cleared, Nathan, her chief steward, placed a glass before each guest and filled his mistress’s. Bowing to the company, she raised her glass. “I offer only one toast,” she said, “but I know you will join me heartily.” Then, she called out, “His Majesty!” Braddock and the others echoed the name with loyalty. After a toast and a salute, she withdrew between rows of enslaved servants, curtsying deeply at the door.

Madam Esmond carried herself with admirable spirit throughout the evening—gay, elegant, and full of cheer. The few ladies present admired her grace and the splendor of the feast. But scarcely had they entered the drawing-room when her composure cracked. Seated beside Mrs. Laws, she suddenly broke down, weeping into her handkerchief mid-compliment. “Ah, madam,” she sobbed, “it may be an honor to host the King’s representative—our family has welcomed greater men than Mr. Braddock. But he comes to take one of my sons away. Who knows if he’ll return? Last night, I dreamed he lay wounded, pale, blood streaming from his side. I wouldn’t let my grief show before the gentlemen—but you, Mrs. Justice, who’ve lost children, who know a mother’s heart—wouldn’t think less of me if I wept tonight.”

The ladies offered comfort. She tried to regain control, remembering her duties. “It will be time enough to grieve when he’s gone,” she said. “My boy must not see me weak. Our parting must not be made worse by my sorrow. It is right for men of his rank to answer their country’s call. That has always been the Esmond way. The same Power that preserved my father through twenty battles in Queen Anne’s time—may it now protect my son.”

Then, instead of mourning, she joined her friends at a card game and a cup of coffee, while the gentlemen lingered in the next room, drinking and toasting.

When one lady suggested they were staying too long, Madam Esmond replied: “Let them stay. It’s rare for young men here to be in such company. Though their talk may be bold, gentlemen of fashion will respect youth and speak nothing unsuitable.”

Clearly, the English visitors enjoyed the feast. As the ladies played, Nathan slipped into the room and whispered to Mrs. Mountain. “No more!” she cried at first. “They can have the common Bordeaux, but not the Colonel’s special claret—already drunk, along with ale, cider, Burgundy, Lisbon, and Madeira!”

But Madam Esmond insisted on generosity. Mrs. Mountain reluctantly fetched the keys and hurried to the vault where the Colonel’s prized Bordeaux still rested—surviving its owner, who had long since passed into the earth.

On the way, she asked if anyone had overindulged. Nathan said Mister Broadbent was tipsy—as usual. The General, too, seemed affected. And Master George? “A lilly drunk,” Nathan insisted.

“Master George?” Mrs. Mountain exclaimed. “He hasn’t touched a drop in days!”

Yet Nathan stood firm. George had been filling his glass, talking, singing, making jokes—especially at Mr. Washington’s expense, which had angered the young colonel.

“Well,” Mrs. Mountain said, “a gentleman ought to enjoy himself among friends. Let him pass the bottle.”

She trotted off to the cellar with renewed speed.

Young George Esmond’s recent behavior—his teasing, his mockery—had deeply offended Mr. Washington. Though only a few years older than the Castlewood twins, Washington was known for his maturity and restraint. The boys had long looked up to him as mentor, guide, and friend—almost everyone did. He demanded seriousness from others, and his presence silenced frivolity. To him, jests seemed misplaced. He understood them slowly, and they faded in his presence.

“He always seemed great to me,” wrote Harry Warrington years later. “Like a hero. When he taught us surveying, or rode to hounds—it felt like leading an army. If he fired, the bird fell. If he cast a net, the largest fish were caught. His words were few, but wise—never idle, always grave, strong, ready to do duty. Even my brother, who disliked him, respected him—more than any man alive.”

Mr. Washington was the first to leave the gathering. Young George had spoken freely, taunting him with remarks he couldn’t answer. Angered beyond endurance, he walked out through the open windows onto the broad veranda.

Madam Esmond saw him pacing there, and, the evening being warm, she left her cards and joined him.

“I see the gentlemen linger over their wine,” she said gently.

“Drinking makes good soldiers,” he replied. “Some here are doing their duty.”

“And the General leads?”

“No doubt,” Washington said softly. “But he is the General. I cannot comment on his conduct. Perhaps British officers are different from us. Our sun is hot—we don’t need wine to heat our blood. Toasts seem a matter of honor to them. Talmadge told me just now that an officer cannot refuse a toast any more than a challenge. He hated it at first, but learned to endure it—with great resolve.”

“What do you talk about for so long?” she asked.

“I cannot tell you all,” he said. “We discussed the war, Contrecoeur’s forces, how we might reach him. The General plans to command from his coach—lightly dismissing the enemy. But if we meet them, I trust we’ll prevail.”

“How can there be doubt?” she asked—her father had served under Marlborough.

“Mr. Franklin,” Washington continued, “spoke wisely. He would have said more, but the English gentlemen dismissed him as a raw provincial. They insist we don’t know what disciplined troops can do. Why not build turnpikes and inns for his Excellency? ‘There are some inns,’ says Danvers, ‘not as comfortable as ours.’ ‘No,’ says Franklin, ‘you can’t expect that.’ He drinks water, laughs at them—but I wonder if it’s fair for a water-drinker to sit and judge men over their wine.”

“And my boys?” she asked, laying a hand on his arm. “Harry promised me—when he gives his word, I trust him. George is always moderate. Why do you look so grave?”

He hesitated, unable to explain.
