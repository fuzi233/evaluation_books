Monna Brigida's conversion was precipitated not by a sudden epiphany, but by the crushing weight of her own reflection. When Romola spoke of expecting someone else, she referred to her cousin, yet Brigida remained oblivious to the depth of her kin's necessity. As they walked toward the Piazza, the two women encountered a cluster of youths halting before Tessa. Upon seeing the simple peasant girl's distress, Romola stepped forward, intending to intervene. "Wait for me a moment, cousin," she called out.

Brigida, however, recoiled instantly. "Ah, I will not go on: come for me to Boni's shop—I shall go back there."

Her retreat was driven by a fractured psyche. On one hand, she clung to the "vanities" pinned to her person; on the other, a paralyzing dread seized her that the Piagnoni were correct—that rouge, false hair, and pearl embroidery were instruments of damnation. The air itself seemed thick with their dogma, distorting the flavor of reality until nothing appeared as it once had. Here stood Romola, youthful and radiant, living a life that demanded rigorous asceticism. For a widow of fifty-five whose identity was constructed entirely upon the adoration of her own image, maintaining her spirit required immense imaginative fortitude. Brigida found herself battling her morning routine. Was it truly worth the effort to apply makeup if her soul required purification? Yet, when she lifted her hand-mirror, the illusion shattered. She saw a sallow visage, cheeks sagging, lined with crows' feet that no amount of lip-smoothing could conceal. When she parted her grey hair to arrange it in the plain style of the Piagnoni, her courage evaporated.

Monna Berta, though only fifty-two, possessed a sharp tongue and a superior bearing that would reduce Brigida to ridicule, calling her an old hag. Any woman outside the sect would shrug at her appearance; any man would address her as if she were his grandmother. At fifty-five, she reasoned, she was not ancient—merely requiring a touch of artifice. Thus, the rouge, the braids, and the embroidered *berretta* remained. Today, however, as she prepared for the Piagnone Carnival, the internal conflict became unbearable. Fearing the collectors of the Anathema without Romola's protection, she attempted to flee to the druggist's shop. But her retreat was too slow.

Before she could turn, she was spotted by the white-robed boys trailing behind the group approaching Tessa. Their eagerness to claim her was fueled by the knowledge that part of their troop had already cornered a figure bearing the visible mark of the Anathema. Several of these children were in their earliest years of strict training, their imaginations inflamed by a terrifying reverence for divine consequence mixed with a mischievous desire for dominance. A small boy of ten, wearing an olive wreath over cherubic cheeks and wide brown eyes, was the first to intercept her. His gaze held a solemn awe, convinced that every action carried eternal weight, yet his instincts ran toward mischief.

He blocked her path. Brigida felt a surge of anger, searching frantically for an exit, but none existed. It was not the small boy who spoke first, but a youth of fifteen gripping the handles of a wide basket.

"Venerable mother!" he declared. "The blessed Jesus commands you to surrender the Anathema you carry upon you. That cap embroidered with pearls, those jewels securing your false hair—give them up and sell them for the poor. Cast the hair itself away, for it is a lie fit only for the fire. Doubtless, beneath your silk mantle, you harbor other treasures."

"Yes, lady," chimed in the youth at the opposite handle, reciting Fra Girolamo's sermons with practiced fluency. "They are too heavy for you. Heavier than a millstone, weighing you down toward perdition. Will you adorn yourself with the hunger of the poor and proudly bear God's curse?"

"In truth, you are old, *buona madre*," the small boy added in a sweet soprano. "You appear frightfully ugly with the red on your cheeks and that black, glistening hair, along with those fine things. Only Satan delights in such a sight. Your Angel feels sorrow. He wishes you to wipe away the red."

Snatching a soft silk scarf from the basket, he held it out for her to cleanse her face as her guardian angel commanded. Brigida's anger and humiliation rapidly curdled into spiritual terror. Monna Berta and the crowd of fashionable onlookers had vanished from her awareness. Instead, she was encircled by young monitors in white robes, adorned with wreaths and red crosses, radiating a dreadful, unnatural candor. Her Franciscan confessor, Fra Cristoforo, was absent to bolster her skepticism of Dominican teachings. Helpless, gripped by the sensation of a supreme warning, she accepted the scarf. Trembling with submission, she wiped her cheeks.

"It is well, madonna," the second youth affirmed. "This is a holy beginning. Once you remove these vanities from your head, the dew of heavenly grace will descend."

A wave of mischief surged through the group. Reaching for one of the jeweled pins anchoring her braids to the *berretta*, the older boy pulled it free. The heavy black plait fell forward, dragging the entire headgear down over Brigida's face. This provided the final catalyst. She raised her hands hastily, undoing the remaining fastenings, and tossed her beloved crimson-velvet *berretta*, with its intricate embroidery of seed-pearls, into the basket of doom. She stood revealed as an unrouged woman, grey hair pushed back from a face where the deep lines of age had decisively conquered her former plumpness.

Yet the *berretta* was not permitted to rest. With impish fervor, the youngsters lifted it, holding it aloft pitilessly, the false hair dangling limply.

"See, venerable mother," the taller youth proclaimed, "what ugly lies you have liberated yourselves from! And now you resemble the blessed Saint Anna, mother of the Holy Virgin."

Thoughts of immediate entry into a convent, of vanishing from the world forever, raced through Brigida's mind. Nothing remained but the salvation of her soul. Of course, spectators laughed; she needed no confirmation. Perhaps being forced to contemplate Paradise was preferable. But the realization that her accustomed world was no longer within her grasp brought hard tears to her elderly eyes. Through the mist of emotion, she saw a large, rough hand thrusting a red cross before her, obscuring the boys' shoulders. A strong, guttural voice boomed:

"Only four *quattrini*, madonna, blessing included! Buy it. You'll find comfort in it now that your wig is gone. *Deh!* What are we sinners doing all our lives? Making soup in a basket and receiving only the dregs for our stomachs. Better buy a blessing, madonna! Only four *quattrini*; the profit is less than the scent of a single coin, and it goes to the poor."

Blinded by confusion, Brigida moved toward the final act of submission, reaching for money hidden in her embroidered pouch beneath her silk mantle. Suddenly, the circle around her parted. A figure emerged, as welcome as an angel breaking prison bars.

"Romola, look at me!" Brigida cried in a piteous tone, extending both hands.

The white-robed troop began to drift away, sensing their zeal regarding the headgear had exceeded bounds and warranting a reprieve from demanding penitential offerings.

"Dear cousin, do not be distressed," Romola said, struck with pity yet unable to suppress a smile at the sudden transformation of her kinswoman into a figure of genuine, natural simplicity, sharply contrasting with all her previous memories. She removed the black drapery from her own head and draped it over Brigida's shoulders.

"There," she soothed. "No one will notice you now. Let us turn down the Via del Palagio and proceed directly home."

They hurried away, Brigida clutching Romola's hand tightly, seeking tangible proof of her presence. "Ah, my Romola, my dear child!" the short, stout woman exclaimed, hurrying with frequent steps to match the majestic stride of her relative. "What an old scarecrow I am! I must be good—I mean to be good!"

"Yes, yes; buy a cross!" the guttural voice insisted. The rough hand thrust the red cross forward once more. Bratti, undeterred by Romola's presence and unwilling to lose a potential customer, had quietly trailed their retreat.

"Only four *quattrini*, blessing and all—and if there is any profit, it will all go to the poor."

Brigida would have paused even in a less submissive state. She raised one hand deprecatingly to stop Romola's protest, then extended a *grosso*, worth many white *quattrini*, in an entreating tone.

"Take it, good man, and begone."

"You're in the right, madonna," Bratti replied, snatching the coin and shoving the cross into her palm. "I shall not offer you change, for I might as well rob you of a mass. What! We must all be scorched a little, but you'll emerge the easier. Better to fall from the window than the roof. A good Easter and a good year to you!"
