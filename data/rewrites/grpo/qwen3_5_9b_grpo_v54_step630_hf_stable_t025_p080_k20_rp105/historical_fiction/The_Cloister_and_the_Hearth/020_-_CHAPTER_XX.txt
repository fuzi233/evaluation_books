Courage in the common man is a narrow river; push it an inch from its bed, and it dries up. Martin's fortitude was iron against the familiar terrors of his rough life, yet here, confronted by a bloodhound's unerring instinct, it cracked. The supernatural chill of the hunt combined with the novelty of the beast to paralyze him. He leaned heavily on his bow, his spirit leaking away like water through a sieve. Gerard, seeing the danger only as a distant shadow, urged them to flee.

"What good is flight?" Martin sighed, his voice heavy with resignation. "If we escape the wood, we die cheap. But here, close at hand, I know a place where we may die with honor."

"Despair not, good Martin!" Gerard cried, though his own resolve wavered. "There must be a way."

"Oh, Martin!" Margaret interjected, her voice trembling with a fierce, desperate logic. "What if we part ways? Gerard's life is forfeit alone. Is there no way to draw the hound upon us two, letting him pass safe?"

"Girl, you know not the nature of this beast," Martin snapped, his head sinking low. "It does not track a man; it tracks blood. They have taken him to where Ghysbrecht fell, and from that dead man's stain, the hound will lead them straight to the killer, whether Gerard runs through an army or swims the Meuse."

As he spoke, the hound's mellow, haunting cry echoed through the trees—a sound so tunable it seemed to mock the horror of the moment. It was a paradox of beauty and death, like the eye of a constrictor that fascinates before it strikes. To Martin, standing bewildered and unnerved, the sound was a physical weight. Gerard, too, felt the paralysis set in; he had struck a man and spilled his blood, and now the four-legged avenger hunted him. Was this not the finger of God?

While the men were numbed by dread, Margaret's mind raced with activity. Her love for the soldier burned brighter than their fear.

"Lend me your knife," she commanded Martin.

"It will be little use in your hands," he replied.

With a sly, swift motion, Margaret stepped behind Gerard. Drawing the blade, she cut her own arm, letting the blood flow freely. She stooped, smearing her hose and shoes until they were dark with crimson, doing so with such adroitness that neither man noticed. Then she seized Gerard's arm.

"Come, be a man!" she urged. "Let this end. Take us to a thick place where numbers cannot help our foes."

"I am going," Martin grumbled, resigned to his fate. "Hurry serves no purpose; we cannot outrun the hound, and the place is near." Turning left, he led them forward like men walking to execution.

He guided them to a dense hazel coppice, similar to the one that had aided their escape hours prior.

"There," Martin said, pointing to the thicket. "It is but a furlong wide, but it will serve."

"What do we do?"

"Pass through and wait on the other side. As they emerge, I will shoot three, knock two on the head, and the rest will kill us."

"That is all you can think of?" Gerard asked, his patience fraying.

"That is all."

"Then, Martin Wittenhaagen, I take the lead, for you have lost your head. Can you obey a young man like me?"

"Oh, yes, Martin," Margaret cried, seizing the moment. "Do not gainsay Gerard! He is wiser than his years."

Martin gave a sullen assent.

"Do as you see me do," Gerard ordered. Drawing his massive knife, he hacked at hazel shoots, twisting them breast-high behind him to create a barrier. Martin followed, moving with a dogged, hopeless air. As they labored through the brush, the hound's bay grew louder, less melodic, and sterner.

Margaret trembled. Martin dropped to his stomach to listen.

"I hear horse's feet," he whispered.

"No," Gerard corrected grimly. "It is a mule. That cursed Ghysbrecht is still alive; none else would follow so bitterly."

"Never strike your enemy unless you mean to slay him," Martin said gloomily.

"I shall hit harder this time, if Heaven grants me the chance," Gerard vowed.

Finally, they broke through the coppice into an open wood. The trees were large but sparse, offering no cover. Soon, the hound's cry mingled with a dozen voices hooping and hallooing.

"The whole village is out after us," Martin declared.

"I care not," Gerard said calmly. "Listen. I have smoothed the path for the dog but roughened it for the men, so we may deal with them separately. The hound will gain on the men; as soon as he emerges, we kill him."

"The hound? There are more than one."

"I hear but one."

"Ay, but one speaks while the others run silent. If the leader loses the scent, another will take up the cry. There are at least two dogs, or devils in dog's skins."

"Then we must kill two. The moment they fall, we retreat into the coppice and go right back."

"That is a good thought, Gerard," Martin said, a spark of hope returning to his eyes.

"Hush! The men are in the wood."

Gerard whispered his plan. "Stand with your bow by the side of the coppice, in the ditch. I will move a few yards to that oak and hide behind it. The dogs will follow me; you shoot as many as you can, and I will brain the rest as they circle the tree."

Martin's eyes flashed with renewed purpose. They took their positions.

The hooping grew closer, the rustling of the undergrowth intensifying until the inevitable struggle for life hung in the air. Suddenly, a trembling hand touched Gerard's shoulder, causing him to start violently.

"Martin says if we are forced to part, make for the high ash-tree we entered by," Margaret whispered urgently.

"Yes! Yes! But go back! For Heaven's sake, do not come here, all out in the open!"

She turned to run back to Martin, but before she could reach him, a huge dog burst from the coppice, standing erect for a heartbeat. Margaret cowered, but the beast ignored her entirely; scent was his world. He lowered his nose, then, with an awful yell, sprang straight for Gerard's hiding spot. Before he could reach the tree, Martin loosed an arrow. The shaft found its mark, and the hound rolled head-over-heels, dead as a stone, spitted by the bowstring's twang.

Simultaneously, a second hound emerged, smelling his fallen comrade. Gerard rushed out with his cudgel, but before he could strike, a streak of white lightning seemed to flash across the beast, leaving him groveling in the dust, desperately wounded and howling piteously, but not dead.

Gerard had no time to finish the job; the coppice rustled dangerously close, seeming to breathe. Pointing wildly for Martin to retreat, Gerard ran a few yards right and crawled into the thick brush just as three men burst forth. These had gained on their comrades; the rest followed at varying distances. Gerard crawled back on all fours, and instinct taught Martin and Margaret to do the same along their line of retreat. Within yards, pursuers and pursued passed each other on opposite tracks.

A loud cry announced the discovery of the dead and wounded dogs. Voices swelled as fresh hunters arrived. The hunters wasted time in surprise; the hunted used it to escape.

"I hear no more hounds," Martin whispered to Margaret, his fear subsiding.

Now it was Margaret's turn to tremble.

"Oh, why did we leave Gerard? They will kill my Gerard, and I will not be near him."

"Nay, nay! The head to catch him is not on their shoulders. You bade him meet us at the ash-tree?"

"And so I did. Bless you, Martin, for thinking of that. To the ash-tree!"

"Ay, but with less noise."

They reached the coppice's edge when suddenly, hooping and hallooing erupted behind them. The men had realized the fugitives were inside and were beating back into the thicket.

"No matter," Martin whispered. "We have time to win clear and slip back by hard running. Ah!"

He stooped suddenly. Just as he prepared to burst from the brush, his eye caught a figure keeping sentinel. It was Ghysbrecht Van Swieten, seated on his mule. A bloody bandage crossed his broken nose, yet his eyes peered keenly through the gore, having heard the fugitives' rustling. Martin muttered a terrible oath, cautiously strung his bow, and fitted his final arrow. Margaret covered her face, her heart pounding against her throat. She knew this man must die or Gerard must.

The bow rose, the arrow drawn to its head, when an active figure leaped upon Ghysbrecht from behind with the speed of a hawk on a pigeon. A kerchief flew over the burgomaster's head, muffling his cries as he was whirled from his seat and fell heavily, groaning in terror. Gerard jumped down after him.

"Hist, Martin! Martin!"

Martin and Margaret emerged, the former shouting, "Now fly! Fly! While they are all in the thicket; we are saved!"

At this critical moment, when safety seemed within grasp, Margaret, who had borne so bravely, began to succumb, partly from the loss of blood.

"Oh, my beloved, fly!" she gasped. "Leave me, for I am faint."

"No! No!" Gerard cried. "Death together, or safety. Ah! The mule! Mount her, you, and I will run by your side."

In an instant, Martin mounted Ghysbrecht's mule. Gerard lifted the fainting girl into his arms and placed her on the saddle, relieving Martin of his bow.

"Help! Treason! Murder! Murder!" shrieked Ghysbrecht, suddenly rising on his hamstrings.

"Silence, cur!" Gerard roared, trampling the burgomaster down by the throat as one crushes an adder.

"Now, have you secured her? Then fly! For our lives! For our lives!"

But even as the mule, spurred by Martin's heel, scattered the flints with his hind hooves before reaching a canter, and even as Gerard withdrew his foot from Ghysbrecht's throat to run, Dierich Brower and his five men, who had returned for orders and heard the burgomaster's cries, burst roaring out of the coppice upon them.
