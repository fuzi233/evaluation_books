Young Harry Warrington’s act of open revolt hit Madame de Bernstein so suddenly that the only response she could muster was the fiery burst of anger we left her in the throes of at the close of the last chapter. As she stormed from the room, she shot two blazing, furious glares at Lady Fanny and her mother, but passed over Lady Maria entirely, who sat bent low over her tambour embroidery frame. Maria scarcely lifted her head to watch her aunt retreat, or to catch the sharp, silent looks the Countess and her daughter exchanged across the parlour.

“So after all our warnings, you dare pursue him, madam?” the Countess’s glare seemed to say.
“Dare what?” Lady Fanny’s wide, confused eyes pleaded back.
Her innocent look did her no good. She was not half so convincing a picture of unknowing purity as Maria, who never moved a stitch. Had Fanny been guilty of encouraging Harry, she would have played the innocent far more artfully, rehearsing and perfecting a demure, unassuming face to suit her alibi long before the moment came. Whatever her eyes claimed, her mother glared at her so fiercely she looked ready to tear the very orbs out of their sockets. But Lady Castlewood had no mind to stage the barbarous eye-gouging from *King Lear* in front of guests; when she did tear her daughter down, she would do it all smiling, with an arm wrapped affectionately around her “dear child’s” waist, and gouge her cruelties out in private.

“So you have no taste for running off to Tunbridge Wells with the old lady, then?” she purred to Cousin Warrington, a perfectly polished, well-bred simper stretched across her face.
“Small blame to the lad, too!” my Lord Castlewood interposed. (The face over the tambour frame flickered upward for a single fleeting instant.) “A young fellow ought not to spend all his days in idling and holiday. Let him mix useful learning with his pleasures, and visit Tunbridge or Bath’s fiddles and pump rooms later on. Mr. Warrington is set to inherit and run a great estate in America: it is only right he sees how our English properties are managed. Will has already shown him the kennels, the stables, and all the popular games of the day—games I must say, cousin, you play nearly as well as your tutors. After harvest, we will take him out for a little English fowling and shooting; come winter, we shall ride out together a-hunting. Though there has been a coolness between our side of the family and our Virginia kinsfolk, we share the same blood. Before we send our cousin back to his mother, let him see what a proper English gentleman’s life at home looks like. I should like to read with him as well as hunt with him, that is why I have been pressing him so hard lately to stay and keep me company.”

My lord spoke with such unfeigned, frank openness that his mother, half-brother, and sister all found themselves staring, wondering what hidden scheme he could possibly be hatching. The three of them often held quiet little conspiracies together, gathering to grumble and plot against the head of the house. When Lord Castlewood adopted that particular frank tone, no one could fathom his designs: more often than not, his true purpose would not come to light for months on end. He did not say “this is the truth,” but rather “this story will be accepted and believed across my household.” And so it became a settled, agreed-upon fiction that my Lord Castlewood felt a laudable urge to nurture family affection, educate, amuse, and improve his young relative, that he had taken a great fancy to the lad, and wished Harry to stay near his estate for months to come.

“What game is Castlewood playing now?” William asked his mother and sister once they had slipped away into the empty corridors. He paused, and snapped his fingers. “By George, I have it!”
“What is it, William?”
“He means to lure him into gambling, and win the Virginia estate right out from under him. That’s the whole of it!”
“But the lad has no actual claim on the Virginia estate right now to pay his losses if he loses,” the Countess pointed out.
“If my brother hasn’t some sly scheme in mind, may I be struck dead—”
“Hush! Of course he has a scheme. But what, exactly?”
“He can’t mean to marry him to Maria. Maria is nearly as old as Harry’s own mother,” William mused.
“Pooh! With her old face, sandy hair, and freckled skin? Impossible!” Lady Fanny exclaimed, heaving a little sigh despite herself.
“Of course your ladyship fancied the Iroquois prince, too!” her mother snapped.
“I trust I know my station and my duty better than that, madam!” Fanny protested. “Even if I did like him, that is no reason I should throw my lot in with him and marry him. Your ladyship has taught me that much a hundred times over.”
“My Lady Fanny!”
“Surely you never loved our papa before you married him! You have told me so a thousand times!”
“And you certainly never fell in love with him afterwards, did you?” William cut in with a laugh. “Fan and I remember all the dreadful rows our honoured parents used to have. Don’t we, Fan? And our brother Esmond was the only one who could keep the peace between them.”
“Don’t drag up those dreadful, vulgar old scenes, William!” his mother cried. “When your father drank too much, he was as mad as a Bedlamite, and his conduct should be a warning to you, sir, who are far too fond of the same horrid habit.”
“I am sure, madam, you were never any happier for marrying a man you did not like, and your ladyship’s title brings barely a penny to it,” Fanny whimpered. “What is the use of a coronet when your jointure is less than a tradesman’s wife? Half the shopkeepers’ wives in town are richer than we are. Only lately a grocer’s widow from London Bridge moved into our Kensington Square, and her daughters have three gowns apiece to my one. Even though they only keep a single man-servant and two maids, I know for a fact they eat and drink a thousand times better than we do, picking at our cold scraps of meat while our great lurching, impudent, lazy footmen loll about doing nothing.”
“Ha! Ha! Thank goodness I dine at the palace, not at home!” William cackled. He owed his little post as Deputy Powder, one of the dozens of tiny court sinecures, to his aunt’s favour with Count Puffendorff, Groom of the Royal (and Serene Electoral) Powder-Closet.
“Why should I not be happy without a title, just as many other people are?” Lady Fanny went on. “I dare say they are even perfectly happy out in America.”
“Happy? With a mother-in-law who is a perfect Turk and Tartar, by all accounts? With Indian war-whoops howling all around you, and the constant risk of losing your scalp, or being eaten by a wild beast every single time you set foot outside to go to church?”
“Then I would not go to church,” Fanny said.
“You’d go with anyone who asked you, Fan!” William roared, “and so would old Maria, and so would any woman, that’s the plain truth!” He laughed loud at his own wit.

“Pray, what is all this merriment about?” Madame de Bernstein asked, peeping in on her relatives from the tapestried door that opened onto the gallery where they had been talking.
William told her his mother and sister had fallen to bickering, which was no novelty to Madame de Bernstein, because Fanny was set on marrying their cousin, the wild “Indian” from the colonies, and the Countess would not hear of it. Fanny protested vehemently against the lie. She said since the very first day her mother had told her not to speak to the young gentleman, she had barely exchanged two words with him. She knew her station far too well to run off to the wilderness to get scalped by savages or eaten by bears.
Madame de Bernstein looked puzzled. “If he is not staying here for you, then who is he staying for? At every house he has been taken to visit, you have all made certain he never meets a woman who is not either a fright or still in the nursery, and I think the boy is far too proud to fall in love with a dairymaid, Will.”
“Humph! That is a matter of taste, ma’am,” William shrugged. “Mr. William Esmond’s taste, maybe—but not that boy’s. The Esmonds raised in his grandfather’s household would never go courting in the kitchen,” she retorted.
“Well, ma’am, every man to his own taste, I say it again. A fellow could go farther and fare worse than my brother’s servants’ hall. Besides Fan, there’s only the maids or old Maria for him to choose from.”
“Maria? Impossible!” The words had barely left her mouth before a sudden sharp thought struck Madame de Bernstein: this aging, quiet Calypso might well have lured her unsuspecting young Telemachus into her web. She recalled half a dozen instances from her own long experience of young men utterly infatuated with far older women. She remembered how often Harry had slipped away unaccountably of late—absences she had always written off as his love of field sports. She remembered how often, when Harry was gone, Maria Esmond had vanished too. Walks down cool, leafy avenues, quiet whispers in garden temples, or behind neatly clipped hedges, casual squeezes of the hand in dim twilight corridors, soft lingering glances stolen on the stairs—her lively fancy, her intimate knowledge of the world, and no small amount of personal romantic history of her own, laid every possible scene out before her, even as she had just declared such a match impossible.

“Impossible, ma’am? I don’t know about that,” William went on. “My mother warned Fan off him, didn’t she?”
“Oh, your mother warned Fanny off, did she?”
“Certainly, my dear Baroness! Didn’t she pinch Fanny’s arm until it was black and blue? Didn’t they fight and scrap about it?”
“Nonsense, William! For shame, William!” both the women caught up in the scandal cried out in unison.
“And now that we have all heard how rich he is, it’s nothing but sour grapes, that’s all. Now that he’s been warned off the young bird, maybe he’s hunting the old one instead. Why impossible? You know old Lady Suffolk, ma’am?”
“William, how dare you speak of Lady Suffolk to your aunt?”
A broad grin spread across the young gentleman’s face. “Only because Lady Suffolk was such a favourite at Court! Well, other ladies have succeeded her, haven’t they?”
“Sir!” Madame de Bernstein cried, looking properly offended.
“So they have, I say! Who else is my Lady Yarmouth now, if not? And didn’t old Lady Suffolk fall madly in love with George Berkeley and marry him when she was well past middle age? Nay, ma’am, if I remember right—and we hear no end of town gossip at our dinner table—Harry Estridge went completely mad for your ladyship when you were barely past twenty, and would have married you a third time if you had only let him.”
This allusion to a well-known adventure from her own younger days did not anger Madame de Bernstein the way Will’s jabs about court favourites had; instead, it put her in excellent humour. “Au fait,” she murmured, musing as she tapped a neat little hand on the table, no doubt thinking of mad, impulsive Harry Estridge. “It is not impossible, William, for old folks and young folks alike to play the fool.”
“But I still can’t wrap my head around a young fellow being in love with Maria,” William went on, “however he might be smitten with you, ma’am. That’s *autre chose*, as our old French tutor used to say. You remember that Count, ma’am? He! he! Maria remembers him too!”
“William!”
“And I dare say the Count remembers the beating Castlewood had him given, the impudent French dancing-master calling himself a nobleman and daring to fall in love with a daughter of our family! Whenever I want to be particularly annoying to old Maria, I just rattle off a few bits of broken French to her. She knows exactly what I mean.”
“Have you been speaking ill of her to your cousin Harry Warrington?” Madame de Bernstein asked sharply.
“Well, she is always speaking ill of me, isn’t she?” William said defensively. “So I said my piece about her, that’s all.”
“Oh, you absolute idiot!” the old lady cried. “What fool ever speaks ill of a woman to her sweetheart? He will tell her every word, and the pair of them will hate you forever.”
“Exactly the point, ma’am!” William burst out laughing. “I had a little suspicion, you see. Two days ago, when we were out riding together, I told Harry Warrington exactly what I thought of Maria. Why shouldn’t I? She’s always at my throat, isn’t she, Fan? And your favourite cousin turned as red as my plush waistcoat. He stared at me, trembling all over with rage, and said no true Esmond would ever malign his own flesh and blood the way I did.”
“Why did you not chastise him then, sir, as my lord did the dancing-master?” Lady Castlewood demanded.
“Well mother, you see that quarter-staff is a sport that uses two sticks,” William replied. “And it is my firm opinion that Harry Warrington can guard his own head uncommonly well. That is one very good reason I did not offer to cane my cousin. And now you mention it, I know he told Maria every word. She has been looking daggers at me, as if she wants to murder me, ever since. All which shows—” he turned to his aunt, grinning.
“Shows what?”
“That we are well and truly on the right scent. We have tracked down the old fox at last!” The ingenuous young man clapped a hand over his mouth and whooped aloud.

The whole question now was how far this ridiculous little intrigue had already gone. Will said that at her age, Maria would be in no mood to waste time drawing things out—she did not have years to spare to wait for her happy ending. There was no love lost between Will and his half-sister. “Scolding either of them will get us nowhere. Threats only make people twice as stubborn in these sorts of cases. I was only ever in real danger once when I was young,” Madame de Bernstein said, “and that was because my poor mother insisted on contradicting me at every turn. If this boy is anything like the rest of his family, the more we oppose him, the more headstrong and determined he will be. We will never get him out of this scrape that way.”
“Faith, ma’am, why not just leave him in it?” Will grumbled. “Old Maria and I don’t care for each other much, that’s true—but an English earl’s daughter is good enough for an American tobacco planter, at the end of the day.”
His mother and sister both cried out in protest, refusing to hear of such a union. Will laughed back at them: “You are acting like the dog in the manger. You don’t want the man for yourself, Fanny—”
“As if I ever wanted him!” Fanny tossed her head.
“Then why grudge him to Maria? I think Castlewood wants her to have him, anyway.”
“Why grudge him to Maria, sir?” Madame de Bernstein snapped, suddenly fiery with energy. “Do you forget who this poor boy is, and what your entire family owes to his? His grandfather was the dearest, best friend your father ever had. He gave up this estate, this title, this very castle you are all conspiring in, so that you and yours could profit from it. And the reward for all that kindness is that you barely let the child in the door when he knocks, and talk casually of trapping him into marrying a silly elderly creature old enough to be his mother! He shall not marry her.”
“That is exactly what we have been saying all along, my dear Baroness!” Lady Castlewood cut in smoothly. “Our side of the family has no eagerness for such a match, though my lord and Maria may be plotting it between them.”
“You would like him for yourselves, now that you hear he is rich—and may be far richer still, young women, mark my words,” the Baroness said, turning on the two of them.
“Mr. Warrington may be ever so rich, madam,” Lady Castlewood retorted with some spirit, “but there is no need for your ladyship to perpetually remind us all how poor we are. At the very least, there is barely any age gap between Fanny and Mr. Harry. Surely you will not be the one to say that a lady of our name and family is not good enough for any gentleman born in Virginia or anywhere else.”
“Let Fanny marry an English gentleman, Countess, not an American. With her name, my support, and all her good looks and accomplishments, she cannot fail to find a husband worthy of her. But from what I know of the daughters of this house, and what I can guess of our young cousin’s character, no happy match could ever be made between either of you and him.”
“What do you know about me?” Lady Fanny asked, flushing bright red.
“Only your temper, my dear. You do not suppose I believe all the idle tittle-tattle and scandal one cannot help hearing around town? But that temper, and that gilded London upbringing, are enough on their own. Imagine one of you forced to leave St. James’s and the Mall behind, to go and live on a remote plantation surrounded by nothing but wilderness and savages! You would die of ennui within a month, or nag your husband to death with your ill humour. You ladies were born to grace royal courts—not wigwams. Let this lad go back to his wilderness with a wife who is suited to his life there.”
The two other ladies assured her in unison that they wanted nothing better. After a few more trivial words, they took their leave, while Madame de Bernstein pulled the tapestried door closed behind her and retired to her own private chamber.
