Miss Butterworth had been raised on a code of manners so rigid that fidgets struck her as a moral failing as much as a social one. When she sat, her spine stayed straight; when she moved, it was with quick, purposeful steps that never stirred a curtain or jostled a candlestick. She had spent fifty years scolding her flightier younger cousins for their petty nervous tics, so it was a shock to find herself, left alone after their long interview, barely able to resist the urge to pluck at her glove seam or tap her foot in tiny, restless rhythms. She was steeped in unease, and when Mr. Gryce finally stepped back into the room, smiling almost cheerily, it was an unmistakeable relief.

“That’s all sorted out,” he said. “All we have to do now is wait for results. Madam, have you any other thoughts to share? I’d be glad of the benefit of them.”

Her self-possession snapped right back into place. “Would you?” she said, eyeing him a touch doubtfully. “I’d rather be sure the theory I already gave you holds water before I venture another. Let’s compare notes instead—let’s start by saying why neither of us thinks Bartow the mute is the killer.”

“I thought we’d exhausted that topic,” Gryce said. “Your suspicions were roused by the young couple you saw fleeing the house, and mine? Well, to you I can admit it: something in the mute’s pantomime makes it clear he’s rehearsing a scene he witnessed, not one he committed. I’ve seen no reason yet to doubt that impression.”

“I felt the exact same way,” she agreed, “even before my suspicions turned elsewhere. Besides, a man is far more likely to be driven mad by shock at another man’s crime than guilt over his own—if he loved the victim.”

“Wait, but Bartow doesn’t mourn Mr. Adams,” Gryce pointed out. “And look at that infernal spark of joy that lights his face at the end of his whole reenactment. It’s as if he’s rejoicing over the deed, or drinking in some enormous satisfaction from it.”

“Maybe he’s copying what he saw the murderer feel,” Miss Butterworth countered, then shook her head. “No. There was no joy on the face of that young man I saw rushing away from the violence. Quite the opposite. Mr. Gryce, we’re in deep water here. I feel half-submerged already.”

“Hold your head up,” Gryce chided, half-teasing. “Every flood has an ebb. If you go under, what will become of me?”

“You’re being humorous,” she said, brightening. “That’s a good sign. You’re never playful when you’re stumped. You must have spotted a crack of daylight somewhere.”

They turned back to dissect Bartow’s gestures line by line, until Miss Butterworth brought up a point that made Gryce laugh outright, caught unawares: the mirror effect. If Bartow stood face to face with the killer, his reenactment would be a perfect mirrored copy. The doctors had confirmed Mr. Adams was killed with a left-handed blow, so if Bartow mimicked that movement with his right, it meant the killer was actually right-handed—and had been positioned *behind* Bartow, not across from him. It was a small, critical flaw in their whole working theory, and Miss Butterworth grew bolder, suggesting they return to the sealed study after the funeral to observe Bartow at his ease, to map the exact shape of the murder scene properly.

It was Gryce’s turn to look thoughtful. “That can be arranged,” he said. “Once the funeral’s over and the swarm of officials clears out. By the way—are you worried the coroner will summon you to testify?”

The question made her fidget, even with all her poise. She admitted she had been, and that there was a whole piece of her adventure in the dead man’s house she had not yet told him. She hadn’t only seen the young couple. A third person had arrived at the door while she was still there: an elderly gentleman, well-dressed, hale, looking almost gay with expectation as he reached for the doorbell, there for a pre-arranged appointment with Mr. Adams in the study.

She had not turned him away. She had ushered him down the hall, to spare him the shock of stumbling on the scene alone. And that shock had been far stranger than anything she could have predicted: he had not just been horrified to find Adams dead on the floor under the cross. His expression shifted, fast and unreadable: from surprise, to curiosity, to blazing fleeting hate, to terror, ending in a wild, uncanny near-laugh that would have broken out loud if the parrot in its high cage had not woken up at that exact second, shrilling the words she would never forget: “Remember Evelyn!”

The squall made the old man jump back, fist raised like he was fending off an attacker. His eyes landed on the portrait hanging over the antechamber, and he cringed so hard it was painful to watch. He stared back at Adams’s body on the floor, muttered a line that sent a chill down her spine: *“It’s Amos’s son, not Amos! Is it fatality, or did he plan this meeting, thinking—”* That was when he spotted her lurking in the antechamber. He smoothed his demeanor back to polished ease, bowed, and fled without a word, letting out a raucous, uncontrolled cackle on his way out the front door. By the time the latch clicked shut, he was wearing the same easy, self-satisfied smile he had arrived with.

“Remarkable,” Gryce breathed, leaning forward. “There’s a whole secret history buried there. ‘Amos’s son’—Adams’s first name was Felix. Felix Amos’s son. That’s an uncommon name. We can run it in the papers, it’s bound to turn up a lead. You certainly got more out of that house than any of the officers did, Miss Butterworth.”

By then the hour was late, and she rose to take her leave. But Gryce stopped her before she could reach the door, one last question on his mind: had she worn her jet bonnet with the long feather boa the day before, when she was in the study?

Confused, she said yes, the boa had been draped around her neck that afternoon.

“Then the faint streaks of blood we found smudged on the edge of the study table are yours,” Gryce said. “If the tips of that boa are stained even faintly, we’ll know you leaned over the body close enough for it to brush the wood. If they’re clean? Then we know that young lady in the dove-colored silk was the one who stood there, not the figure we saw carried out unconscious later.”

Miss Butterworth flushed, half-amused, half-mortified. “So my little secret search for clues left my own trace behind. I’ve proven the fallibility of the inquisitive woman, haven’t I?” She took her leave then, with a show of humility that was not entirely sincere.

Mr. Gryce made no move to stop her. He had extracted every scrap of useful information she held, and his conscience—well, Mr. Gryce’s detective conscience had grown nicely calloused in certain spots, after years of solving murders. He felt no qualms at all.
