Jeeves—my valet and occasional savior—is a marvel of efficiency. The man knows everything. If you asked him the capital of Patagonia or the best way to remove red wine from a white waistcoat, he’d have the answer before you finished the question. He’s like one of those railway information chaps who can rattle off train schedules while half-asleep.

Take the time I spotted my pal Monty Byng strolling down Bond Street in a sharp grey check suit. Envy hit me like a ton of bricks. I cornered him for the tailor’s details and had my own version underway within the hour.

That evening, I announced my sartorial triumph to Jeeves.

“I’m getting a check suit like Monty’s,” I declared.

“Ill-advised, sir,” he replied. “It will not suit you.”

“Nonsense! It’s the pinnacle of fashion.”

“Unsuitable, sir.”

The suit arrived. I put it on. One glance in the mirror nearly knocked me flat. Jeeves was right—I looked like a circus clown who’d lost a bet. Yet Monty had looked impeccable in the same fabric. Life’s unfair like that.

But Jeeves’s genius extends beyond fashion. Once, I stumbled upon a surefire tip for the Lincolnshire race.

“Jeeves,” I said, eager to share the wealth, “if you want to make a quick buck, back Wonderchild.”

He shook his head. “I would refrain, sir.”

“Why? It’s a guaranteed winner!”

“The horse is intended to place second, sir.”

I dismissed his warning as drivel. Naturally, Wonderchild led until the final stretch, only to be pipped by Banana Fritter. I slunk home, humbled.

“From now on,” I vowed, “I consult you before making any decisions.”

“Very good, sir.”

And so, when my friend Corky Corcoran turned up in my flat, dragging a doe-eyed girl named Muriel Singer behind him, my first instinct was to summon Jeeves.

“We’re engaged,” Corky blurted, “but my uncle will murder me if he finds out.”

Muriel gazed at me with pleading eyes. “We were hoping you might help us break the news gently.”

Corky’s uncle, Alexander Worple, was a tyrant with two passions: jute (whatever that is) and birds. Corky survived on a meager allowance by pretending to share his uncle’s ornithological obsession. The mere idea of Corky marrying an actress—Muriel was in the chorus of *Choose Your Exit*—would send Worple into orbit.

I pondered. Then it hit me.

“Leave it to Jeeves,” I said, ringing the bell.

Jeeves materialized, as always, at the perfect moment.

“Sir?”

“We need a scheme.” I outlined Corky’s predicament.

Jeeves steepled his fingers. “I propose Miss Singer authors a volume titled *The Children’s Book of American Birds*, dedicating it to Mr. Worple. A limited edition, privately published, with generous references to his own work. Upon receipt, he will be so charmed, he’ll insist on meeting her.”

Corky paled. “But Muriel can’t write!”

“A ghostwriter can be engaged for a modest fee,” Jeeves assured.

The plan was set in motion. A struggling scribe named Sam Patterson was hired, and soon, a glossy book arrived. It was a masterpiece of flattery, praising Worple’s expertise on everything from finches to cuckoos.

A few days later, Corky burst in, waving a letter. Worple was *delighted* to make Miss Singer’s acquaintance!

Months passed. I returned to New York and spotted Muriel dining alone.

“Where’s Corky?” I asked.

She blinked. “Oh, we’re not together anymore.”

“Had a row?”

“No, I’m married.”

Before I could react, a booming voice cut in. “Muriel, darling!”

I turned. There stood Alexander Worple, beaming.

“Meet my husband,” Muriel said.

I nearly fainted.

Back at my flat, I poured myself a stiff drink.

“Jeeves,” I groaned, “Muriel married the *uncle*.”

He didn’t bat an eye. “A foreseeable outcome, sir.”

“Foreseeable? You might have warned us!”

“I hesitated to overstep, sir.”

I avoided Corky for months, guilt-ridden. When I finally worked up the nerve to visit, I found him painting a severe-looking woman holding a baby.

“Ah—” I stammered, backing toward the door.

Corky waved me in. “Just finishing up.” He dismissed the nanny, then sighed.

“Life’s full of surprises, Bertie.”

I nodded. Some surprises, it seemed, were best left to Jeeves.
