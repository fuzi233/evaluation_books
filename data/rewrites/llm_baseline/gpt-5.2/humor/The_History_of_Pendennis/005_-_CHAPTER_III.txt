Arthur Pendennis was about sixteen when Providence, with the easy carelessness it shows in such matters, handed him the sceptre of Fairoaks and said, “There: reign.” He reigned, as young kings do, chiefly by being looked at.

In figure he was what his friends, in their brutal candour, called dumpy, and what his mamma, in her maternal jurisprudence, registered as “a neat little figure.” Nature had given him hair of that wholesome brown which turns to gold whenever the sun condescends to be civil; a round, rosy, freckled face, set in an expression of such ready good-humour that people forgave him his freckles as they forgive pepper in a sauce, because it gives the whole dish a better relish. His honest blue eyes laughed first and asked questions afterwards. When, later, those whiskers for which he sighed (as if they were a degree at Oxford) at last arrived, they were found to be decidedly red, as if they had been lightly toasted. Between sixteen and eighteen he shot up from five feet six to five feet eight, and then stopped with the obstinacy of a pudding that will not rise another inch though you coax it with more fire. His mother stared at that extra altitude as if it were an act of filial rebellion: three inches taller than Mr. Pendennis! Was there ever such an outrage upon established measures?

Of course he never went back to school. Greyfriars’ discipline did not suit him; it was like being forced to swallow physic through a funnel. At home there were hedges to jump, books to skim, and a mother to adore him at leisure. The question was debated, solemnly, like a matter of State. His uncle wanted him sent back. The Doctor wrote that Arthur’s future depended upon knowing a Greek play thoroughly, as if a man might be saved or damned by Euripides. But Pen, with the dexterity of youth, dropped a few artless hints about the dangers of Greyfriars—what wild chaps there were, what tempests of vice blew through dormitories—and his mother’s timid heart took alarm as readily as a soufflé takes air. The boy remained at home; and the whole Greek drama was spared the indignity of being learned.

Then Major Pendennis offered to procure him a commission in the Foot Guards, being himself acquainted with His Royal Highness the Commander-in-Chief, and having that great man’s kindness in his pocket like a visiting card. Pen’s heart leaped. He had heard the band at St. James’s on a Sunday—trumpets blaring, cymbals clashing, drums beating as if to drown thought itself—and he had seen, in the middle of that glittering racket, Tom Ricketts, once the butt and cockshy of the fourth form, the same Tom whose tight jacket and trousers had made him look like a boiled sausage and invited the lash of older wit. Pen had once cut him over the back with a hockey-stick; and now there he was—crimson and gold, bear-skin cap, staggering under the colours, nodding patronisingly as though he had invented Waterloo, talking familiarly to enormous men with tufts on their chins and medals on their breasts. What would not Pen have given to wear such epaulettes, to be swallowed up in that scarlet splendour, and to be deafened daily by music and martial uproar?

But Mrs. Pendennis, when her son ventured to propose it, put on a face as if he had suggested becoming a highwayman. She did not quarrel with those who thought otherwise (she was always charitable to the brink of being terrible), but in her opinion a Christian had no right to make the army a profession. Mr. Pendennis never, never would have permitted it. She should be very unhappy if Arthur thought of it.

Now, Pen would sooner have cut off his own nose and ears—deliberately, and of aforethought malice—than cause his mother one hour’s unhappiness. He was of that generous order which gives away its own best toys to keep peace in the nursery. So he made her an immediate present of his red coat, his epaulettes, his bear-skin cap, and the whole boiling stew of military glory. Mrs. Pendennis, naturally, thought him the noblest creature alive. Major Pendennis, when the commission was acknowledged and refused, wrote back curtly, with a certain angry dryness, and privately entered “spooney” against his nephew’s name.

Still, the Major was mollified at Christmas, when he came down to Fairoaks and saw the boy out hunting. Pen had a very good mare and rode her with uncommon pluck and grace; he took his fences coolly, yet with judgment, and without that bragging flourish which is the horsemanship of asses. He wrote to the chaps at school about his top-boots and his feats across country, as a young gentleman will write about boots and feats when he has nobody at hand to admire them daily. He began to think seriously of a scarlet coat again—one is always thinking seriously of scarlet at sixteen—and his mother owned, with a sigh and an inward prayer, that it would become him remarkably well, though she passed whole hours in anguish during his absence and daily expected to see him brought home on a shutter, like a joint of mutton.

With so many pleasures, it must not be supposed he neglected study altogether. His appetite for reading was excellent—only it was an appetite that refused the school’s bill of fare. It was when people tried to force his head into the waters of knowledge that he declined to drink. Left to himself, he devoured everything in the house, from Inchbald’s Theatre to White’s Farriery, and ransacked neighbouring book-cases like a boy robbing orchards. At Clavering he found a cargo of old French novels and read them with all his might, as if each volume were a secret pie cooling under somebody else’s window. He would perch for hours on the topmost bar of Doctor Portman’s library steps with a folio on his knees—Hakluyt’s Travels, Hobbes’s Leviathan, Augustini Opera, Chaucer’s Poems—turning pages with the pleased absorption of a young animal gnawing whatever is put before it.

He and the Vicar were excellent friends; and from his Reverence Pen learned that honest taste for port wine which distinguished him through life—a lesson taught as many great lessons are, not by precept but by practice, with the decanter making a soft, persuasive music against the glass. The house had its own acoustic ecology: the hush of the library broken by the crisp rasp of paper, the tender clink of glasses, the distant Sunday bells floating over fields as if the air itself were being stirred in a great invisible bowl. It was the sort of sound-world in which a boy’s hopes ripen quietly, like fruit in a warm pantry. Mrs. Portman, that dear good woman, was not jealous though her Doctor avowed himself in love with Mrs. Pendennis and pronounced her by far the finest lady in the county. Mrs. Portman’s only grief, as she looked up fondly at Pen on the book-ladder, was that her daughter Minny was too old for him—as indeed she was—Miss Myra Portman being only two years younger than Pen’s mother, and weighing as much as Pen and Mrs. Pendennis together.

If these details seem insipid, remember your own youth and ask whether it was made of fireworks or of bread-and-butter. There is a particular sweetness in the well-nurtured boy—brave and gentle, warm-hearted and loving—who looks the world in the face with kind honest eyes, as if it were a table laid for him, full of bright colours and innocent flavours. One does not know those years while one is eating them; only later, when the plate is cleared, does one remember how good they were.

To keep Mr. Pen from that idleness of which his friend the Doctor of the Cistercians had prophesied such dreadful consequences, Mr. Smirke, Dr. Portman’s curate, was engaged at a liberal salary to walk or ride over from Clavering and pass several hours daily with the young gentleman. Smirke was perfectly faultless at a tea-table; he wore a curl on his fair forehead as carefully as a garnish on a dish, and tied his neck-cloth with a melancholy grace, as if each fold were a line of elegy. He was a decent scholar and mathematician, and taught Pen as much as Pen was ever disposed to learn, which was not much. Pen soon took the measure of his tutor, and as the lad possessed a sense of humour, he found it impossible to respect a man who rode into the court-yard at Fairoaks on a pony turning out his toes so absurdly and leaving such a gap between his knees and the saddle that the creature seemed to be riding with an invisible barrel between his legs.

Pen nearly killed him with terror by putting him on his mare, Rebecca—named after Pen’s favourite heroine, the daughter of Isaac of York—and taking him for a ride over a common where the county fox-hounds (then hunted by that staunch old sportsman, Mr. Hardhead of Dumplingbeare) happened to meet. Mr. Smirke, on Rebecca, astonished the hounds as much as he disgusted the huntsman, laming one of the former by persisting in riding amongst the pack and receiving from the latter a speech more remarkable for energy of language than any oration he had ever heard since he left the bargemen on the banks of Isis. The whole scene was a symphony of rural sound—the horn, the yelp of hounds, the thud of hooves, the huntsman’s thunder—into which poor Smirke’s terrified little squeaks fell like cracked notes from a cheap fiddle.

Smirke confided his poems, Latin and English, to his pupil and presented Mrs. Pendennis with a volume of the latter, printed at Clapham, his native place. They read the ancient poets together and rattled through them at a pleasant canter, very different from the steady grubbing pace of the Cistercians, who scented out each word as they went like truffle-pigs, digging up every root in the way. Pen hated halting; if he was at fault he made his tutor construe and so galloped through the Iliad and Odyssey, the tragic writers, and the charming wicked Aristophanes (whom he vowed to be the greatest poet of all). He went at such a pace that, though he certainly traversed a great extent of the ancient country, he forgot it afterwards, retaining only that vague memory which some men in the House of Commons keep—two or three quotations for decency’s sake—or which a reviewer hints at, to show he has once heard of Greek. We are the most prosaic people in the world and the most faithful; and with a curious reverence we transmit, from generation to generation, the superstition of what we call the education of a gentleman.

Besides the ancients, Pen read the English with gusto. Smirke sighed and shook his head sadly about Byron and Moore; but Pen was a sworn fire-worshipper and a Corsair. He had them by heart and used to take little Laura into the window and say, “Zuleika, I am not thy brother,” in tones so tragical that the solemn child opened her great eyes wider, and stitched on, and understood not one word of what he read. He read Shakespeare to his mother (which she said she liked, but didn’t), and Byron, and Pope, and his favourite Lalla Rookh, which pleased her indifferently. But Bishop Heber—and Mrs. Hemans above all—melted her away. She was absorbed into her pocket-handkerchief when Pen read them in his kind boyish voice, as if each sentimental verse were a warm broth poured directly into her heart.

The Christian Year appeared about this time. Mother and son whispered it to each other with awe; faint—very faint—and seldom in after-life did Pendennis hear that solemn church-music, but he always loved the remembrance of it, and of the times when it struck on his heart and he walked over the fields full of hope and void of doubt, while the Sunday bells rang and rang—bells whose mellow, patient sound belonged to the place like its trees and its bread-ovens, a sound that made even a restless boy keep time inwardly for a moment, as if his life had a rhythm older than his own desires. How unlike the brass-and-cymbal clangour of St. James’s—how clean the country’s music was, before railways and iron shrieks and the modern passion for noise began to trample every delicate silence into the road.

It was at this period he broke out in the Poets’ Corner of the County Chronicle with verses with which he was perfectly satisfied. The pieces signed “NEP.” were his: “To a Tear;” “On the Anniversary of the Battle of Waterloo;” “To Madame Caradori singing at the Assize Meetings;” “On Saint Bartholomew’s Day” (a tremendous denunciation of Popery, and a solemn warning to England against emancipating the Roman Catholics), and so on—masterpieces which Mrs. Pendennis no doubt keeps to this day with his first socks, the first cutting of his hair, his bottle, and other relics of infancy, labelled and cherished as if posterity would one day demand them for a museum. He used to gallop Rebecca over Dumpling Downs or into the county town—call it Chatteris—spouting his own poems, full of Byronic afflatus, and believing, as youth will, that his private emotions were a public event.

His genius then was of a gloomy cast. He brought his mother a tragedy in which he killed sixteen people before the second act; it made her laugh so that, in a pet, he thrust the masterpiece into the fire—thus performing the only execution in the play that gave anybody real satisfaction. He projected an epic in blank verse, Cortez, or the Conqueror of Mexico, and the Inca’s Daughter. He wrote part of Seneca, or the Fatal Bath, and Ariadne in Naxos, with choruses and strophes and antistrophes that puzzled poor Mrs. Pendennis, who could follow a recipe better than a Greek chorus. He began a History of the Jesuits in which he lashed that Order with tremendous severity, warning his Protestant fellow-countrymen against their machinations. His loyalty delighted his mother: he was staunch Church-and-King in those days, and at the election, when Sir Giles Beanfield stood on the Blue interest against Lord Trehawk, Lord Eyrie’s son—a Whig and a friend of Popery—Arthur Pendennis, with an immense bow for himself (made by his mother) and a blue ribbon for Rebecca, rode alongside the Reverend Doctor Portman on his grey mare Dowdy, and at the head of the Clavering voters whom the Doctor brought up to plump for the Protestant Champion.

On that day Pen made his first speech at the Blue Hotel; and also, it appears, for the first time in his life took a little more wine than was good for him. Mercy, what a scene at Fairoaks when he rode back at ever so much o’clock at night! What moving of lanterns in the courtyard and stables though the moon shone bright; what a gathering of servants; what clattering over the bridge and up the stable-yard, with half a score of Clavering voters yelling after him the Blue song of the election—voices thick with patriotism and liquor, stamping their tune into the quiet country night as if to prove that enthusiasm is only another sort of noise.

Pen wanted them all to come in and have some wine—some very good Madeira—some capital Madeira. “John, go and get some Madeira!” he cried, with the lavishness of a young gentleman spending his mother’s cellar as though it were the national treasury. There is no knowing what the farmers might have done had not Madam Pendennis appeared in a white wrapper with a candle, her pale handsome face striking the revel like a sudden bell in church. She frightened those zealous Blues so by mere virtue and candlelight that they touched their hats and rode off, leaving the yard to the moon and the uneasy stamping of Rebecca.

Among these amusements there was one which forms, the poets assure us, the main business and pleasure of youth. Pen, always studying poets, was not likely to escape the passion they prescribe; and his heart, being ardent and his imagination eager, soon began to hunger for Love as naturally as it hungered for praise and poetry. He sighed for it first in secret and, like Ovid’s love-sick swain, opened his breast and said, “Aura, veni.” What generous youth has not courted some such windy mistress? It costs nothing and seems for a time to fill the room.

Yes, Pen began to feel the necessity of a first love—of a consuming passion—of an object upon which to concentrate all those vague floating fancies under which he sweetly suffered. He wanted a young lady to whom he could really make verses, whom he could set up and adore in place of those unsubstantial Ianthes and Zuleikas. He read his favourite poems over and over; he called upon Alma Venus; he translated Anacreon’s odes; he picked out passages suitable to his complaint from Waller, Dryden, Prior. Smirke and he were never weary, in their interviews, of discoursing about love. The faithless tutor entertained him with sentimental conversations instead of algebra and Greek—for Smirke was in love too.

Who could help it, being in daily intercourse with such a woman? Smirke was madly in love—so far as such a mild flame may be called madness—with Mrs. Pendennis. That honest lady, downstairs teaching little Laura the piano, or devising flannel petticoats for the poor, or moving through the calm routine of her modest spotless Christian life, never suspected the storms upstairs in the study—storms which consisted chiefly of sighs, glances, and the occasional misquotation. There sat Pen in his shooting-jacket, elbows on the green study-table, hands clutching his curly brown hair, Homer under his nose; and there sat Smirke reading with him and suffering gently.

They would talk about Helen and Andromache. “Andromache’s like my mother,” Pen would avouch; “but I say, Smirke, by Jove I’d cut off my nose to see Helen,” and he would spout favourite lines. He drew portraits of her—straight noses, enormous eyes—with “Arthur Pendennis delineavit et pinxit” gallantly written underneath, as if Latin could make the thing truer. Smirke naturally preferred Andromache, and in consequence he was uncommonly kind to Pen. He gave him his Elzevir Horace, and his little Greek Testament his own mamma at Clapham had bought for him; he bought him a silver pencil-case; and, in learning, let him do as much or as little as he pleased. Smirke always seemed on the point of unbosoming himself. He confessed he had an attachment—an ardently cherished attachment—about which Pendennis longed to hear.

“Tell us, old chap,” Pen said; “is she handsome? has she got blue eyes or black?”

Mr. Smirke heaved a gentle sigh, cast up his eyes to the ceiling, and faintly begged him to change the conversation.

Poor Smirke! He invited Pen to dine at his lodgings over Madame Fribsby’s, the milliner’s, in Clavering; and once, when it was raining and Mrs. Pendennis had driven in her pony-chaise to Clavering on some arrangement—about leaving off mourning, probably—she was prevailed on to enter the curate’s apartments. Smirke instantly sent out for pound-cakes, as if pastry might express what language dared not. The room smelt of damp great-coats, weak tea, and that faint powdery perfume milliners trade in; and Mrs. Pendennis, drying her gloves, brought with her the clean country air and the mild authority of domestic virtue. The sofa on which she sat became sacred to him from that day; and he kept flowers in the glass from which she drank ever after, as though he could preserve a sip of her presence the way one keeps herbs between paper to remember summer.

As Mrs. Pendennis was never tired of hearing praise of her son, Mr. Smirke neglected no opportunity of conversing with her on that subject. It might bore him to hear stories about Pen’s generosity, bravery in fighting the big naughty boy, his fun and jokes, his prodigious skill in Latin, music, riding, and so forth; but what price would he not pay for the privilege of sitting in her company while she served up these maternal dishes again and again? The widow, after such conversations, thought Mr. Smirke a very pleasing and well-informed man. As for her son, she had not settled whether he was to be Senior Wrangler and Archbishop of Canterbury, or Double First Class at Oxford and Lord Chancellor; but that all England did not possess his peer was a point, in her mind, beyond dispute.

A simple person of inexpensive habits, she began forthwith to save—perhaps to be a little parsimonious—in favour of her boy. There were no entertainments at Fairoaks during the year of her weeds. Nor did the Doctor’s silver dish-covers, of which he was so proud—flourished all over with Pendennis arms and surmounted with the crest—come out of the plate-chests again for long years. The household was diminished; expenses curtailed; the table grew plainer. When Pen dined from home there was a blank anchorite repast. He himself headed a remonstrance from the kitchen regarding the deteriorated quality of the Fairoaks beer. She was becoming miserly for Pen. Indeed, who ever accused women of being just? They are always sacrificing themselves—or somebody—for somebody else.

There happened to be no young woman in the widow’s small circle whom Pendennis could possibly endow with the inestimable treasure of a heart he was longing to give away. Some young fellows in such want bestow their affections upon Dolly the dairymaid or cast tender eyes on Molly the blacksmith’s daughter. Pen thought a Pendennis too grand to stoop so low. He was too high-minded for a vulgar intrigue; and had the notion of seduction ever crossed him, his heart would have revolted as from baseness and dishonour.

Miss Minny Portman was too old, too large, and too fond of reading Rollin’s Ancient History. The Miss Boardbacks, Admiral Boardback’s daughters of St. Vincent’s (or Fourth of June House), disgusted Pen with the London airs they brought into the country from Gloucester Place, where they passed the season; they looked down on Pen as a chit, and a chit feels those things as keenly as a barometer. Captain Glanders’s three girls (H.P., 50th Dragoon Guards) were still in brown-holland pinafores, with the ends of their hair-plaits tied up in dirty pink ribbon. Not having acquired the art of dancing, the youth avoided such chances as he might have had at the Chatteris Assemblies. In fine, he was not in love because there was nobody at hand to fall in love with.

So the young monkey rode out day after day in quest of Dulcinea and peeped into pony-chaises and gentlefolks’ carriages along the broad turnpike roads, with a heart beating within him, a secret tremor and hope that she might be in the yellow postchaise swinging up the hill, or one of the three girls in beaver bonnets in the back seat of the double gig driven at four miles an hour by a fat old gentleman in black. But the postchaise contained a snuffy old dowager of seventy with a maid her contemporary; and the three girls in beaver bonnets were no handsomer than the turnips skirting the roadside. Do as he might, ride where he would, the fairy princess he was to rescue and win had not yet appeared to honest Pen.

Upon these points he did not discourse to his mother. He had a world of his own. What generous ardent imaginative soul has not its secret pleasure-place? Let no clumsy prying try to disturb it in children. Actaeon was a brute for pushing in where Diana was bathing. Leave him occasionally alone, my good madam, if you have a poet for a child. Even your admirable advice may bore sometimes. You may be faultless; it does not follow everybody in your family must think exactly like you. That little child yonder may have thoughts too deep even for your great mind, and fancies so coy they will not bare themselves when your ladyship sits by. And there are feelings—like tastes and smells and the music of bells over fields—which cannot be translated into another person’s language without losing half their savour; the heart keeps its own dialect for such matters, and is shy of the dominant lexicon even at home.

Helen Pendennis, by sheer love, divined a great number of her son’s secrets. But she kept them in her heart and did not speak of them. Besides, she had made up her mind that he was to marry little Laura, who would be eighteen when Pen was six-and-twenty—after he had finished college, made his grand tour, and settled either in London astonishing the metropolis with his learning and eloquence at the bar, or, better still, in a sweet country parsonage surrounded with hollyhocks and roses, close to a delightful romantic ivy-covered church, from whose pulpit Pen would utter the most beautiful sermons ever preached.

While these natural sentiments waged their pleasant war in Pen’s bosom, it chanced one day he rode into Chatteris carrying to the County Chronicle a tremendous thrilling poem for next week’s paper. He put up his horse, as usual, at the stables of the George Hotel, and stood talking with the hostler about Rebecca—her coat, her temper, her feed, the whole sacred ritual by which horseflesh becomes companionship—when a grand black tandem with scarlet wheels came rattling into the inn-yard. The driver’s voice called out, loud and patronising, “Hallo, Pendennis, is that you?”

Pen had difficulty recognising, under the broad-brimmed hat and vast great-coats and neckcloths, the person and figure of his quondam schoolfellow, Mr. Foker.
