I had kept the pony-chaise standing ready from the moment the notion of a night train had been spoken of—as you keep a kettle near the hob when storms are coming on and you distrust the temper of the fire. A household is much the same; you learn to lay your hand on the right thing before the need declares itself. So when the luggage showed first—those dumb, square-mouthed boxes that always look as if they are about to swallow up comfort—and Mr. Franklin followed it down the stairs, I knew he had at last done what he so seldom did: held fast to a resolution.

“So you have really made up your mind, sir?” I said, meeting him in the hall. “Why not wait a day or two longer, and give Miss Rachel another chance?”

The foreign polish was off him now. He did not answer in words. He put a letter into my hand—my lady’s letter—folded with that hard, careful neatness people use when they are trying to keep their feelings from running over.

Most of it was the same as what I had already had from her: the Diamond lost; the house turned inside out by a mystery that would not show its face; the police dismissed because their very name was poison to Miss Rachel. But at the end there was a piece about my young lady—one of those late sentences, slipped in like the last pinch of salt that changes the taste of the whole dish.

“You will wonder,” my lady wrote, “at my allowing my own daughter to keep me perfectly in the dark. A Diamond worth twenty thousand pounds has been lost—and I am left to infer that the mystery of its disappearance is no mystery to Rachel, and that some incomprehensible obligation of silence has been laid on her, by some person or persons utterly unknown to me, with some object in view at which I cannot even guess. Is it conceivable that I should allow myself to be trifled with in this way? It is quite conceivable, in Rachel’s present state. She is in a condition of nervous agitation pitiable to see. I dare not approach the subject of the Moonstone again until time has done something to quiet her. To help this end, I have not hesitated to dismiss the police-officer. The mystery which baffles us, baffles him too. This is not a matter in which any stranger can help us. He adds to what I have to suffer; and he maddens Rachel if she only hears his name.

“My plans for the future are as well settled as they can be. My present idea is to take Rachel to London—partly to relieve her mind by a complete change, partly to try what may be done by consulting the best medical advice. Can I ask you to meet us in town? My dear Franklin, you, in your way, must imitate my patience, and wait, as I do, for a fitter time. The valuable assistance which you rendered to the inquiry after the lost jewel is still an unpardoned offence, in the present dreadful state of Rachel’s mind. Moving blindfold in this matter, you have added to the burden of anxiety which she has had to bear, by innocently threatening her secret with discovery, through your exertions. It is impossible for me to excuse the perversity that holds you responsible for consequences which neither you nor I could imagine or foresee. She is not to be reasoned with—she can only be pitied. I am grieved to have to say it, but for the present, you and Rachel are better apart. The only advice I can offer you is, to give her time.”

I gave the letter back, and I was honestly sorry for him. There are pains that come with a noise—doors slammed, voices raised; and there are pains that come as quiet as a knife going in. This was one of the quiet ones.

“You know the proverb, sir,” I said, because it is a poor old servant’s trade to hunt up proverbs the way a cook hunts in her drawer for a warming-pan. “When things are at the worst, they’re sure to mend. Things can’t be much worse, Mr. Franklin, than they are now.”

He folded my lady’s letter as if it were a thing too hot to hold.

“When I came here from London with that horrible Diamond,” he said, “I don’t believe there was a happier household in England than this. Look at the household now! Scattered, disunited—the very air of the place poisoned with mystery and suspicion! Do you remember that morning at the Shivering Sand, when we talked about my uncle Herncastle, and his birthday gift? The Moonstone has served the Colonel’s vengeance, Betteredge, by means which the Colonel himself never dreamt of!”

Then he shook my hand and went out to the chaise.

I followed him down the steps. The evening lay long and bright on the old place, and yet it was as if some sound had been taken out of the air—the steady domestic hum that makes a house feel lived in. Penelope, poor girl, came crying to say good-bye. He kissed her. The other maids peeped and drew back again, as women do; for he was one of those men whom they all like, and that liking is a kind of weather in a servants’ hall—you can feel it shift without being told.

At the last moment I laid my hand on the chaise and stopped it.

“Just let us hear from you by letter, sir,” I said. “Tell us where you’re going to.”

He did not heed me at first. He was looking at the windows, the sweep of the gravel, the line of trees—the small familiar things a man takes in his eyes when his heart is not fit to speak. Then, all at once, he pulled his hat down over his face.

“Going?” he said, echoing me. “I am going to the devil!”

The pony started at the word, as if it had a Christian horror of it. I had only time to call after him, “God bless you, sir, go where you may!” before he was out of sight.

A sweet and pleasant gentleman—sweet, in spite of faults and follies. And he left a gap behind him, as a good fire leaves a chill when it goes out.

That Saturday night closed in dull and heavy. Summer can do that: hang a warm scent of mown grass and dust on the air, and yet make the heart feel cold. I kept myself from sinking by sticking to my pipe and my Robinson Crusoe. The women, except Penelope, kept the hours moving by talking of Rosanna’s suicide—turning it over and over as if it were a tough bit of meat that would not come right in the teeth.

They were obstinate: Rosanna had stolen the Moonstone; Rosanna had destroyed herself for terror of being found out. Penelope privately held to what she had said all along. But, oddly enough, her notion failed at the same point where my young lady’s assertion of Rosanna’s innocence failed: it left Rosanna’s secret journey to Frizinghall, and the business of the nightgown, like two ingredients in a recipe with no dish to belong to.

There was no use pressing that on Penelope. An objection slid off her mind like rain off a waterproof coat. My daughter, in that respect, inherits my superiority to reason—and has got ahead of her father.

Sunday brought back the close carriage from Mr. Ablewhite’s empty, with a message to me and written instructions for my lady’s maid and for Penelope.

My mistress had determined to take Miss Rachel to her house in London on Monday. The instructions set down what clothes were wanted, and directed the two maids to meet their mistresses in town at a given hour. Most of the servants were to follow. My lady had found Miss Rachel so unwilling to return to our house after what had happened in it that she would go to London direct from Frizinghall. I was to remain in the country, until further orders, to look after the house and grounds. The servants left with me were to be put upon board wages.

Reading that, I heard again Mr. Franklin’s words—“scattered, disunited”—as plainly as if they had been spoken in my ear. And thinking of him led me to uneasiness. His temper was not of the sort that sits down and cools like a stew left off the fire; it runs away like milk if you are not watching. By Sunday’s post I wrote to Mr. Jeffco, his father’s valet—an old acquaintance of mine—to beg he would let me know what Mr. Franklin had settled to do on arriving in London.

Sunday evening was duller, if possible, than Saturday. We ended our day of rest as hundreds of thousands end it: we anticipated bedtime and fell asleep in our chairs—letting the house noises die down one by one until even the clock seemed tired of striking.

What Monday did to the rest of the household I can’t say. It gave me a shake-up. The first of Sergeant Cuff’s prophecies—that I should hear from the Yollands—came true that day.

I had seen Penelope and my lady’s maid off by railway with the luggage for London, and was pottering about the grounds when I heard my name called. I turned, and there stood the fisherman’s daughter, Limping Lucy.

Take away the lame foot and the leanness—which, in my opinion, is a cruel drawback in a woman—and she had her merits: a dark, keen, clever face; a clear voice that cut through the outdoor hush like a gull’s cry; and a beautiful brown head of hair. A crutch was one of her misfortunes. A temper was her chief defect.

“Well, my dear,” I said, “what do you want with me?”

“Where’s the man you call Franklin Blake?” she asked, fixing me with a look that was like being pinned.

“That’s not a respectful way to speak of any gentleman,” I answered. “If you want my lady’s nephew, you will mention him as Mr. Franklin Blake.”

She limped nearer and looked as if she could have eaten me alive.

“Mr. Franklin Blake?” she repeated. “Murderer Franklin Blake would be a fitter name for him.”

My practice with the late Mrs. Betteredge served me. When a woman tries to put you out of temper, you put her out of temper instead. One word does it—and I said it pleasantly.

“Pooh!”

Her temper flamed up like dry thatch. She poised on her sound foot, lifted the crutch, and beat the ground three times so that the blows went up through the soles into my bones.

“He’s a murderer! he’s a murderer! he’s a murderer! He has been the death of Rosanna Spearman!”

She screamed it. One or two of the men working near us looked up, saw it was Limping Lucy, and looked away again, as people do when they know a squall is passing and there’s no use throwing yourself into it.

“He has been the death of Rosanna Spearman?” I said. “What makes you say that, Lucy?”

“What do you care? What does any man care? Oh, if she had only thought of the men as I think, she might have been living now!”

“She always thought kindly of me, poor soul,” I said, “and, to the best of my ability, I always tried to act kindly by her.”

I spoke gentle, because I saw the wretchedness under the insolence. Misery often talks impudent in humble life; it is the only way it can keep itself from breaking down.

My tone melted her. She bent her head and rested it on the top of her crutch, like a tired child.

“I loved her,” she said softly. “She had lived a miserable life, Mr. Betteredge—vile people had ill-treated her and led her wrong—and it hadn’t spoiled her sweet temper. She was an angel. She might have been happy with me. I had a plan for our going to London together like sisters, and living by our needles. That man came here, and spoilt it all. He bewitched her. Don’t tell me he didn’t mean it, and didn’t know it. He ought to have known it. He ought to have taken pity on her. ‘I can’t live without him—and, oh, Lucy, he never even looks at me.’ That’s what she said. Cruel, cruel, cruel.

“I said, ‘No man is worth fretting for in that way.’ And she said, ‘There are men worth dying for, Lucy, and he is one of them.’ I had saved up a little money. I had settled things with father and mother. I meant to take her away from the mortification she was suffering here. We should have had a little lodging in London, and lived together like sisters. She had a good education, sir, as you know, and she wrote a good hand. She was quick at her needle. I have a good education, and I write a good hand. I am not as quick at my needle as she was—but I could have done. We might have got our living nicely.

“And, oh, what happens this morning? what happens this morning? Her letter comes and tells me she has done with the burden of her life. Her letter comes, and bids me good-bye for ever.”

Then she lifted her head, and through tears the old flame sprang up again.

“Where is he? Where’s this gentleman I mustn’t speak of except with respect? Ha, Mr. Betteredge, the day is not far off when the poor will rise against the rich. I pray Heaven they may begin with him. I pray Heaven they may begin with him.”

Here was your average good Christian, and here was what comes of pushing average Christianity past endurance. The parson himself could hardly have done anything with her at that pitch. All I attempted was to keep her to the point, because the one thing worth anything in this world of ours is a fact.

“What do you want with Mr. Franklin Blake?” I asked.

“I want to see him.”

“For anything particular?”

“I have got a letter to give him.”

“From Rosanna Spearman?”

“Yes.”

“Sent to you in your own letter?”

“Yes.”

At that, I felt the air change about me. Sergeant Cuff had left his infection behind him. The detective-fever began to set in—like a sudden appetite, or a thirst, that makes you restless until it is satisfied.

“You can’t see Mr. Franklin,” I said.

“I must, and will, see him.”

“He went to London last night.”

She searched my face, and found the truth there. Without another word she turned instantly away toward Cobb’s Hole.

“Stop!” I said. “I expect news of Mr. Franklin Blake tomorrow. Give me your letter, and I’ll send it on to him by the post.”

She steadied herself on the crutch and looked back over her shoulder.

“I am to give it from my hands into his hands,” she said. “And I am to give it to him in no other way.”

“Shall I write and tell him what you have said?”

“Tell him I hate him. And you will tell him the truth.”

“Yes, yes. But about the letter—”

“If he wants the letter, he must come back here, and get it from me.”

With that she limped off.

All my dignity burnt away on the spot, and I followed her, trying to make her talk. It was all in vain. It is my misfortune to be a man, and Limping Lucy enjoyed disappointing me. Later I tried her mother. Good Mrs. Yolland could only cry and recommend a drop of comfort out of the Dutch bottle—like a woman who has nothing left to offer but a smell of gin and the sound of weeping. I found the fisherman on the beach; he said it was “a bad job,” and went on mending his net, the sea making its steady, unanswering noise behind him. Neither father nor mother knew more than I did.

So there was nothing for it but to wait for morning and the chance of writing to Mr. Franklin.

You may imagine how I watched for the postman on Tuesday. He brought two letters.

One, from Penelope—which I had scarcely patience to read—announced that my lady and Miss Rachel were safely established in London.

The other, from Mr. Jeffco, informed me that his master’s son had left England already.

On reaching London, Mr. Franklin had gone straight to his father’s house. He arrived at an awkward time. Mr. Blake the elder was up to his eyes in House of Commons business, and amusing himself at home with that parliamentary plaything they call a private bill. Mr. Jeffco showed Mr. Franklin into his father’s study.

“My dear Franklin! why do you surprise me in this way? Anything wrong?”

“Yes; something wrong with Rachel; I am dreadfully distressed about it.”

“Grieved to hear it. But I can’t listen to you now.”

“When can you listen?”

“My dear boy! I won’t deceive you. I can listen at the end of the session, not a moment before. Good-night.”

“Thank you, sir. Good-night.”

That was the conversation inside. The conversation outside was shorter.

“Jeffco, see what time the tidal train starts tomorrow morning.”

“At six-forty, Mr. Franklin.”

“Have me called at five.”

“Going abroad, sir?”

“Going, Jeffco, wherever the railway chooses to take me.”

“Shall I tell your father, sir?”

“Yes; tell him at the end of the session.”

Next morning he started for foreign parts. Where, nobody could guess. Europe, Asia, Africa, America—Mr. Jeffco thought the chances as equally divided as might be, and I had no better reckoning.

That news shut the door on my one hope of bringing Limping Lucy and Mr. Franklin together. Penelope’s belief—that Rosanna had destroyed herself through love for Mr. Franklin Blake—stood confirmed, and that was all. Whether Rosanna’s letter contained a confession, as Mr. Franklin had suspected she wanted to make while living, nobody could say. It might be only a farewell and nothing more; or it might tell the whole truth about those strange proceedings Sergeant Cuff had detected, from the time the Moonstone was missed to the time she ran to her death at the Shivering Sand.

A sealed letter had been placed in Limping Lucy’s hand; a sealed letter it remained. We suspected she had been in Rosanna’s confidence; we tried to make her speak; we failed. Now one servant, now another—still clinging to the notion that Rosanna had stolen the Diamond and hidden it—went peering and poking about the rocks where she had been traced, and peering and poking in vain. The tide went out with its long sucking hush; the tide came in with its heavier, grinding return. Summer ran on; autumn came. The Quicksand that hid her body hid her secret too.

The news of Mr. Franklin’s departure on Sunday morning, and my lady’s arrival in London with Miss Rachel on Monday afternoon, had reached me by Tuesday’s post. Wednesday brought nothing. Thursday produced another budget from Penelope.

A great London doctor had been consulted and had earned his guinea by saying Miss Rachel had better be amused. Flower-shows, operas, balls—there was a whole round of gaieties before her; and, to her mother’s astonishment, Miss Rachel eagerly took to it all. Mr. Godfrey had called—sweet as ever on his cousin in spite of the birthday reception—and, to Penelope’s great regret, had been most graciously received, adding Miss Rachel’s name to one of his Ladies’ Charities on the spot. My mistress was out of spirits, and had held two long interviews with her lawyer. Penelope’s speculations followed, touching a poor relation of the family—Miss Clack, she who had sat next to Mr. Godfrey at the birthday dinner and had a pretty taste in champagne. Penelope was astonished Miss Clack had not called yet; she would surely not be long before she fastened herself on my lady, and so forth—women’s pin-pricks travelling as well by ink as by tongue. This would not be worth noting, except that you may be turned over to Miss Clack after parting with me; and if so, I beg you not to believe a word she says if she speaks of your humble servant.

Friday, nothing happened—except that one of the dogs showed signs of a breaking-out behind the ears. I gave him syrup of buckthorn and put him on pot-liquor and vegetables. Pass it over, if you please. The creature deserved proper physicking.

Saturday—the last day of the week—was the last day of my narrative.

The morning post brought a surprise: a London newspaper. The handwriting on the direction puzzled me until I compared it with the money-lender’s name and address in my pocket-book. It was Sergeant Cuff’s hand.

I looked through the paper and found an ink-mark drawn round one of the police reports. Here it is. Read it as I read it, and set what value you please on the Sergeant’s attention in sending me the news of the day:

“LAMBETH—Shortly before the closing of the court, Mr. Septimus Luker, the well-known dealer in ancient gems, carvings, intagli, &c., &c., applied to the sitting magistrate for advice. The applicant stated that he had been annoyed, at intervals throughout the day, by the proceedings of some of those strolling Indians who infest the streets. The persons complained of were three in number. After having been sent away by the police, they had returned again and again, and had attempted to enter the house on pretence of asking for charity. Warned off in the front, they had been discovered again at the back of the premises. Besides the annoyance complained of, Mr. Luker expressed himself as being under some apprehension that robbery might be contemplated. His collection contained many unique gems, both classical and Oriental, of the highest value. He had only the day before been compelled to dismiss a skilled workman in ivory carving from his employment (a native of India, as we understood), on suspicion of attempted theft; and he felt by no means sure that this man and the street jugglers of whom he complained, might not be acting in concert. It might be their object to collect a crowd, and create a disturbance in the street, and, in the confusion thus caused, to obtain access to the house. In reply to the magistrate, Mr. Luker admitted that he had no evidence to produce of any attempt at robbery being in contemplation. He could speak positively to the annoyance and interruption caused by the Indians, but not to anything else. The magistrate remarked that, if the annoyance were repeated, the applicant could summon the Indians to that court, where they might easily be dealt with under the Act. As to the valuables in Mr. Luker’s possession, Mr. Luker himself must take the best measures for their safe custody. He would do well perhaps to communicate with the police, and to adopt such additional precautions as their experience might suggest. The applicant thanked his worship, and withdrew.”

One of the wise ancients—on what occasion I forget—recommended us to look to the end. Looking to the end of these pages and wondering how I should bring them to it, I find the plain facts do it for me.

We have gone on, in this business of the Moonstone, from marvel to marvel; and here we end with the greatest marvel of all: Sergeant Cuff’s three predictions accomplished in less than a week from the time he made them.
