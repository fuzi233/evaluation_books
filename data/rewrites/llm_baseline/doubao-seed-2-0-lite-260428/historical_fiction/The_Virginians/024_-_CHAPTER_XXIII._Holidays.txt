Mrs. Lambert’s soft little daydream had dissolved like frost on a spring windowpane. She and Theo both knew in their quiet hearts that the dashing hero they’d spun out of thin air the day Harry Warrington stumbled wounded through their gate was no grand otherworldly prince—only a flesh-and-blood young mortal, ordinary enough in his ways. They spoke scarcely a word of the disappointment aloud, but as with all people who love one another dearly, each could read the other’s thoughts plain as print. The mother’s way of consoling her daughter was to wrap her in extra soft caresses, her lips brushing Theo’s cheek to whisper, “Never mind, my sweet. Our supposed hero has turned out no great shakes, and none such is half good enough for my Theo. You shall have a real husband ere long, if there’s a worthy one to be found in all England. Why, I was barely fifteen when your father spotted me at the Bury Assembly, and while I was still at boarding school I swore I’d never take any other man. If Heaven saw fit to bless me with the very best husband in the whole kingdom, surely it will smile on my child just as fairly—you deserve a king, if that’s what your heart fancies!”

Indeed, I would not put it past Mrs. Lambert, who kept close track of the young Prince of Wales’s age and all the tales of his handsome, generous nature, to half-expect that the royal heir himself might come cantering past their little gate any day, take a tumble from his horse right on their threshold, be carried inside to be nursed back to health, prevail on his royal grandfather to give Colonel Martin Lambert a full regiment, and promptly fall head over heels in love with her Theo.

The Colonel and his second daughter Hetty, for their part, took the whole affair as a fine joke, laughing at all the silly sentimental match-making going on under their own roof. Mrs. Lambert, you must know, was exceedingly fond of novels, and would cry her eyes out over their tragic pages with the greatest assiduity. No stagecoach ever rumbled past their gate that she did not lean over the half-door, half-expecting a wealthy, titled husband for one of her girls to step down and ring their bell. As for Hetty, her tongue grew saucier than ever, and she dropped a hundred sly, teasing allusions to their unexpected guest. She dragged Prussia and Persia into every conversation with outrageous pertness, asking if the King of Prussia was properly called the Shaw or the Sophy, and how many miles lay between Ispahan and the Saxony border he was currently invading, the campaign that kept her father poring over maps and newspapers late into every evening. She sneaked the *Persian Tales* down from her mother’s book closet and left it slily on the parlour table where the whole family could see. For her own part, she declared, she would never marry a Persian prince: she vastly preferred a gentleman who was limited to one wife at a time. She teased Theo unceasingly about “her Virginian gentleman” and “her prince,” and then asked her mother if she would like Hetty to claim their other visitor, Harry’s dark-skinned servant, for her own “black prince.” She rallied both her mother and sister over their romantic fancies until they were cross, at which point she would burst into tears, kiss them both soundly one after the other, and coax them back into good humour.

Simple, open-hearted Harry Warrington never had the faintest inkling of all the jokes, tears, petty quarrels, reconciliations, and secret wedding plans he had unwittingly sparked. A hundred jabs about Shahs and Sophys were shot at him like Parthian arrows, and not one so much as pricked his skin. A Sophy? Very likely he assumed it was a lady’s name, and thought it the height of absurdity that a man with a great bushy black beard would answer to such a title. We step unawares into the midst of quiet families all the time, dropping like a cool, compact stone into a still pond—little do we know the flutter and commotion we stir up, disturbing the fish, scaring the ducks, sending ripples dancing all across the water’s surface. How could Harry guess the effect his sudden arrival had made in this small, sentimental household? He thought well enough of himself on many scores, but he was still shy around women, at that age when young gentlemen need gentle encouragement to come out of their shells, and raised back home in Virginia with very modest, old-fashioned manners towards the other sex. So Hetty’s teasing buzzed around him like summer gnats, and he paid it no more mind than he would have paid a cloud of midges. He was not stupid, as Hetty insisted he was: he was merely simple, far too occupied with his own honest, straightforward affairs to guess at the secret byplay going on around him. Think of all the tragedies, comedies, intrigues, and farces playing out under our very noses in the drawing rooms we visit every week, and we remain entirely blind, self-satisfied, and ignorant of every bit of it. When the two sisters sat of an evening combing out their long flowing ringlets, or whispered to each other in the great shared bed they slept in as was the custom of the day, how could Harry guess he occupied such a large share of their jokes, their laughter, and their quiet thoughts?

Three days after his arrival, his new hospitable hosts took him wandering the wide, green expanse of my Lord Wrotham’s private park, where the Lamberts had permission to roam at will. By the park’s small lake they came upon a flock of swans, which the young ladies were in the habit of feeding with stale bread. As the great white birds glided closer to the shore, Hetty darted a mischievous glance at her mother and sister, then over at her father, who stood by smiling in his bright red waistcoat. “Mamma’s swans are very like these, are they not, papa?”

“What swans, my dear?” her mother asked, confused.

“Not quite so grand, though. They’ve got shorter necks than these, and scores of them are wandering on our village common,” Hetty went on. “I saw Betty plucking one in the kitchen this morning. We shall have it for dinner, with apple sauce and—”

“Don’t be a silly little goose, Hetty,” Theo interrupted, blushing.

“—and sage and onions,” Hetty finished, unperturbed. “Do you care for roast swan, Mr. Warrington?”

“I shot three of them last winter on our river back home in Virginia,” Harry answered easily. “Ours aren’t half such dazzling white birds as these, but they eat very well for all that.”

The poor simple youth had not the slightest clue he was the subject of Hetty’s fable that very minute, that he was the swan being allegorized for his dinner plate. Some very obscure old Latin text I once read tells me that long before Virginia was ever discovered, plenty of other folks were just as slow to catch on to veiled jokes.

That little tender flutter of Theo’s heart, which we have already noted stirred in her breast the first moment she saw Harry stumble to their gate, handsome, pale, and bleeding? It was a premature sentiment, no great grand passion like the one she knew her heart was capable of holding. Like a foolish lark that wakes at false dawn, it had begun to sing long before the sun was up. Hop back to your perch, little trembling creature, tuck your head under your wing! The real day has not yet broken. Roosting is far better than singing for now. By and by the true dawn will come, the whole sky will flush red and gold, and you shall soar up into the light to salute the rising sun with all your music.

You may have noticed, careful reader, that line a few paragraphs back, noting Harry was walking about freely just three days after his arrival. If his shoulder was fully mended, and he could walk with perfect ease, what possible business had him lingering at Oakhurst? Ought he not to have posted straight off to Tunbridge Wells to meet Lady Maria Esmond on the Pantiles, the lady who was waiting for him? His shoulder was healed, his health was fully restored, he did not even own a change of coats, having borrowed all his present clothes from the Colonel. Every duty, every tie of affection, every bond of relationship should have sent him to Tunbridge without delay—for the sake of the gentle lady pining for him there, even for the sake of the washerwoman who had his linen waiting for her to wash. Why did he tarry, unless he was falling in love with one of the two Lambert girls (and I assure you he was not)? Could it possibly be that he did not want to go?

Hath the gracious reader grasped the meaning of the mystic illustration opening our last chapter, of Sinbad the Sailor bowed down under the weight of that hateful Old Man of the Sea clamped around his neck? What if Harry Warrington was Sinbad, and the old man throttling him was his fate? What if for the past two days he had felt those bony knees digging tight into his throat? What if his venomous old aunt the Baroness’s wicked work was fully done, and his once fond love for Lady Maria was as dead as poor fair Rosamond, poisoned by her royal rival? Is Lady Maria lighting the lamp and laying the supper for her Leander at Tunbridge, while Leander sits comfortably by another hearth, never once thinking of swimming across the water to her?

Since that cowardly, sneaking blow was struck to Lady Maria’s reputation by her own kinswoman, all kind hearts must pity her. I know she has her faults, yes—she wears false hair, and no doubt a dozen other little artificial aids to beauty. But a woman in distress, shall we not pity her? A lady of a certain age, are we to laugh at her merely because of the number of years she has seen? Trapped between her tyrannical old aunt and her own foolish delusion, you may be sure Lady Maria Esmond was having no pleasant time of it at Tunbridge Wells. There was no one there to protect her. Madam Beatrix the Baroness had her entirely to herself. Lady Maria was poor, and depended on her aunt for her allowance. The old woman held more than one secret of hers over her head, brandishing it as a threat whenever Lady Maria dared to rebel. For my own part, my heart softens completely when I think of her: picture that antique Andromeda, with all her rich flowing hair falling loose over her shoulders, chained to a rock on Mount Ephraim, delivered over to the dragon that is Baroness Bernstein. Succour, Perseus! Come quickly on your winged feet, with your flashing falchion! But Perseus is not in the least hurry. The dragon feasts on her day after day, unchallenged.

Harry Warrington, who would not have let his half-healed shoulder stop him from going out hunting, lingered day after day at Oakhurst, and every day he liked the kind souls who welcomed him more and more. He could not remember, since his grandfather died, when he had been in such good company. Back home in Virginia his life had been spent amongst fox-hunting squires, whose society he had enjoyed well enough, riding their horses, sharing their punch bowls, living their rough, hearty lives. The ladies he knew from home were no doubt well-bred, decorous, and pious, but they were narrow-minded for all that. His home was a tiny, insular place, full of stiff little pomposities, petty etiquettes, small flatteries, small conversations, and small scandals. He did not realize until long after he left it how confined and narrow his whole life there had been. He was free enough in his movements, to be sure: he had hounds and horses, and could ride and hunt for scores of miles in every direction around his estate. But his mother reigned supreme over the household, and when he was at home he had to breathe the air she dictated.

Here at the Lamberts’ he found himself in a world that was gayer, brighter, and freer than any he had ever known. The man and woman who owned this quiet home had seen the world, even if they chose to live retired from it. From their earliest youth they had enjoyed access not only to good books, but to good company—the living, breathing books that are such pleasant, profitable reading. Society at its best does this much good: it cures you of your conceit, teaches you how small you are, and introduces you to people better than yourself. If you are a young person reading this, take my word for it: nothing is more wholesome than to associate with those who are your superiors. I would never wish my own son Thomas to be first prize boy in every Greek and Latin class, stroke oar on the rowing team, and cock of the school. It would be far better for his body and soul to take a respectable middle rank, to have a fair set of competitors around him, and to get a good sound thrashing now and then, followed by a hearty shake of the hand of the boy who beat him. What honest man who could choose his lot would choose to be a prince, with everyone walking backwards before him, only obsequious courtiers to talk to, no man daring to speak his mind unless your high mightiness gave him permission?

One of the greatest gifts Harry Warrington got from the family Fate had dropped him amongst was the slow realization that he was a profoundly ignorant young man, and there were hundreds of people in the world far better than he had ever imagined possible. A little arrogant around people he considered his equals, in the company of those wiser and better than himself he was docile, generous, and eager to learn. We have already seen how deeply he admired his own brother back home, and his gallant young friend Colonel Washington at Mount Vernon. Towards the gentleman kinsmen at Castlewood he had always felt he was at least their equal. But in Colonel Lambert he found a man who had read books far beyond Harry’s own small knowledge, who had travelled the world and come through its temptations unscathed, the same way he had come through all the battles and dangers he had faced. Kindness and honesty were written plain on the Colonel’s face, and breathed in every word he spoke—qualities our brave young lad had always loved and instinctively trusted.

As for the women of the house, they were the kindest, merriest, most agreeable Harry had ever met. They were far pleasanter than the parson’s black-eyed daughter back home, whose laugh could be heard half a mile off. They were just as well-bred as the fine ladies of Castlewood, all save Madam Beatrix, who could be as grand as an empress whenever the fancy took her. But after an hour’s conversation with Madam Beatrix, no matter how amusing her stories were, Harry would walk away with a bitter taste in his mouth, feeling as if the whole world was wicked and corrupt. The Lamberts were nothing like that: they laughed over the racier pages of Mr. Fielding, and cried over every volume of Mr. Richardson, even the bits that would make straitlaced Mrs. Grundy’s hair stand on end, yet their merry chatter left no sourness behind it. Their gossip about their neighbours was droll, never malicious. The little curtsies and cheerful greetings the villagers gave them when they walked into town were warm, not formal. Their charity was generous and cordial, never done for show. To be in the company of truly good people is a blessing beyond price, though Harry did not realize it fully at the time, until later experience showed him far less pleasant contrasts, and made him feel gratitude for those quiet days. This was a sunshiny, tranquil little patch of peace in a life that was destined to be full of storm and upheaval—an hour or two of sweet rest he would remember for years. Not much happened in that quiet stretch of time. It was only pleasant waking, friendly welcomes, serene, unhurried afternoons. The old house’s gates seemed to shut all the wickedness of the world outside, and the people who lived within seemed better, purer, kinder than other folk.

Harry was not in love, no—certainly not with saucy Hetty, or gentle generous Theodosia. But when the time came for him to leave, he squeezed both girls’ hands hard, and felt a warm, immense fondness for them both. He thought he would dearly love to meet their two brothers, who must be fine, honest fellows if they were raised in such a home. As for Mrs. Lambert, I swear she was as teary and sentimental at his departure as if he had just been the final volume of *Clarissa Harlowe*.

“He is so kind and honest,” Theo said gravely, as the three of them stood on the terrace watching Harry ride off down the Westerham road with the Colonel and two of the servants.

“I don’t think he’s stupid at all, not anymore,” Hetty admitted. “And mamma? I think he really is very like a swan indeed.”

“It felt just like one of our own boys leaving to go back to school,” Mrs. Lambert said softly.

“Just like it,” Theo echoed, a little sadly.

“I’m so glad papa is riding part of the way with him to Westerham,” Hetty went on. “And that he bought Farmer Briggs’s horse. I don’t like the idea of him going to stay with those Castlewood people. I’m sure that Madame Bernstein is a wicked old woman. I half expected to see her ride away on her crooked walking stick.”

“Hush, Hetty,” Theo chided her.

“Do you think she would float if we tried her in the village pond, the way they tested poor old Mother Hely at Elmhurst?” Hetty went on, ignoring her. “That other older lady looked fond of him, you know—the one with the fair hair. She looked very sad when she left, but Madame Bernstein whisked her off with her crutch, and she had no choice but to go. I don’t care, Theo. I know for a fact that woman is wicked. You think everyone is good, because you’ve never done anything wrong yourself.”

“My Theo is a good, sweet girl,” their mother said, looking fondly from one daughter to the other.

“Then why do we call her a miserable sinner in church?” Hetty demanded.

“We are all sinners, my love,” Mrs. Lambert said.

“What? Even papa too? You know you don’t truly believe that,” Hetty cried, and Mrs. Lambert had to fudge a little, for she could never bring herself to say a single unkind word against her beloved Colonel. “By the way, what was it you told John to give to Mr. Warrington’s black servant?”
