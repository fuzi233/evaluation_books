Land—land—land.

It lay far off to the south-west, a long indigo stroke between a sea turned wine-red by the sinking sun and a sky burnished like heated brass. After weeks of bilge-stink, sweat-sour hammocks, and the medicinal reek of tar and salt pork, the very thought of earth had a taste: cool water that did not come from a cask, fruit that bled sweetness instead of brine, shade that did not smell of damp canvas. Men who had been counting their ribs in the dark crawled up as if dragged by a rope of hope; men still strong enough to stand dropped to their knees and gave thanks, their voices breaking in the wind.

That wind—steady, warm, faithful—came stroking along the sails and whispering through rigging and block like a hand over strings. It was not silence they had lived with at sea, but one long monotone: the hush and slap of water, the creak of timbers, the constant grind of rope. Now the trade-wind had a different note, a promise in it, as if it had learned the human word for mercy.

Frank, leaning forward where the bowsprit thrust toward that blue bar, mouthed his own prayer into the salt air.

“Blow freshly… freshlier yet… Save her. Land me at her feet—if only to call the lost lamb home, and die.”

The sunset set fire to his thin cheeks; the bright fever-spot on either side looked like a cruel mockery of health. His eyes were too large in his wasted face, and they clung to the land as if it were not an island at all, but the one fixed point in a world that had become all water.

A few minutes, and the west’s jewel-box snapped shut. Emerald and ruby and amethyst melted into one pearl-gray band; overhead, the deepened sky took on that hard, clean blue of polished stone, and the Moon and Venus hung above the sea like two lamps in a chapel.

“That should be Barbados, your worship,” said Drew the master, with the quiet pride of a man whose figures have not betrayed him. “Unless my reckoning’s far out—which, Heaven knows, it has no right to be after such a passage. God be praised.”

“Barbados?” Amyas turned, shading his eyes. “I never heard of it.”

“Very like, sir. But Yeo and I were here with Captain Drake—and I was here after, too, with poor Captain Barlow. There’s good harborage on the south and west, as I mind.”

“And neither Spaniard, cannibal, nor other evil beast,” said Yeo, dry as ship’s biscuit. “A very garden of the Lord, hid away in the seas for an inheritance to those who love Him. I heard Captain Drake talk once of planting it, if ever he had a chance.”

Amyas’s face softened. “I mind now Sir Humphrey speaking of an island here. Would God he had gone thither instead of Newfoundland.”

“Nay then,” said Yeo, not unkindly. “He is in bliss now with the Lord; and you would not have kept him from that, sir?”

Amyas sighed and shook his head, as if the wind could blow a dead man back. Then he looked over his company—scurvy-swollen gums, hollow eyes, men shuffling like old men though beardless—and the captain’s mind did its plain arithmetic.

“What say you, my masters? How can we do better than spend a few days here, to set our sick on their legs, before we make the Main and set to our work?”

All approved at once, a murmur that grew toward cheer; only Frank said nothing, as if any delay were a theft.

“Come, fellow-adventurer,” Cary said, clapping him lightly. “We must have your voice too.”

“To my impatience,” Frank answered low, so that only Cary heard, “there is but one place on earth. I am all day longing for wings to fly thither. But the counsel is right. I approve it.”

So it was settled. The decision ran through the ship like a draught of fresh water. Men who had been too weak to swear found strength enough to shout; and long before morning they had slid along the island’s southern shoulder and were feeling their way into the bay where Bridgetown would one day stand, though there was not yet so much as a cut wharf or a torch to tell of human hands.

The land slept in moonlight—low wooded hills, the palms standing like dark plumes against the pale sky. Fireflies stitched the shadows with dancing sparks, as if the ground itself were spangled with stars fallen and still restless. The air came off the shore thick with scent: crushed leaf, warm resin, something like honey bruised with green, and beneath it all the clean wet smell of soil. Nostrils that had lived on salted wind drank greedily.

And the sound—oh, the sound. The sea’s eternal whisper was not gone, but it was no longer alone. From the land came a swelling chorus: insects clicking and whirring, tree-toads snoring in comic solemnity, shore-fowl crying their plaintive notes across the shallows. The night had a body now, made of voices. Every man on deck listened as if his own mind were being tuned again after weeks of one harsh note.

At last the ship stopped. The cable rattled through the hawsehole with a music of iron; the anchor took hold; and, careless for the moment of Spaniard or Carib, a cheer burst from every throat.

Amyas had much to do to keep them aboard. They were like starving men sniffing bread through a door.

“Two hours of day, lads,” he cried. “Two hours, and then ashore. Will you go breaking your legs on coral in the dark?”

“Never were two such long hours,” complained a young lad, pacing like a caged thing.

“You never were in the Inquisition,” Yeo answered, sharp as vinegar. “Or you’d know better how slow time can run. Stand still, and give God thanks you’re where you are.”

“I say, Gunner,” piped another, eyes bright in a hollow face. “Be there goold to that island?”

“Never heard of none,” said Yeo. “And so much the better for it.”

A cripple with scurvy-swollen joints licked cracked lips. “But… be there oranges and limmons there?”

“Not of my seeing,” Yeo said, softening. “But plenty of good fruit down to the beach, thank the Lord. There comes the dawn at last.”

Up flushed the rose. The sun came in a single stride, and the day, sudden as a struck match, set everything glittering. Level light ran along smooth palm-stems, turned the fringe of reef-foam into broken rainbows, and gilded lonely uplands far inland—quiet now, though in another age they would be cut with roads, pricked with engine-houses, and filled with the harsh iron complaint of mills. For the moment there was no man-made din—only the sea, the birds, and the wind dying off into a glassy calm.

Long lines of pelicans went clanging out to sea, their wings beating the air with a heavy, workmanlike rhythm. The night’s insect-hum hushed as if someone had shut a door, and then a thousand birds burst into song—notes flung out bright and clean, each species with its own dialect of joy, none translated, none needing to be.

Boat-load after boat-load went over the side. The sick were lifted as gently as one might carry bread just risen and too delicate to jar. They were landed on the beach and laid in the shade of palms, where the sand was pale and warm and the air smelled of green life. In half an hour the shore was scattered with Englishmen like castaways in a painting—some sprawled, some sitting, some weeping outright for no reason they could have named but relief.

And then, as if the body’s oldest hunger spoke first and loudest: “Fruit! fruit! fruit!”

Men who could barely walk crawled from plant to plant. They tore at violet grapes of a creeping shore-vine, staining their mouths purple as if they had been drinking wine at a feast. They gulped down prickly pears, blistering lips and tongues in their greed, thorns sticking like needles in their fingers despite Yeo’s curt warnings.

Some of the healthier set to hewing at cocoa-nut trees, confident as fools that they could beat a palm into surrender with a ship’s hatchet. They blunted their edges and sweated, and got little for it but curses.

Yeo and Drew, having gathered half-a-dozen men with sense enough to listen, went inland with their knives and their eyes. They returned in an hour like messengers from a pantry no one had dared imagine: acid junipa-apples that made the mouth pucker and then beg for more; guavas heavy with perfume, their skins yielding under the thumb; and crowned ananas—pineapples—prickly as a mailed fist, but promising, even before the knife went in, a fragrance like spiced honey and sun-warmed straw.

There on sandy turf they made their banquet. No table but the ground, no cloth but the shade; and yet it was a kind of sacrament after the ship’s salted misery. Knives flashed; skins fell away; fibers parted under the blade with that clean tearing sound ripe fruit makes. The pineapple, when cut, gave off a sharp, intoxicating sweetness that seemed to clear the head like a hymn. Men chewed with their eyes shut, as if to taste more fully; juice ran down wrists and dripped into the sand; and laughter came easier, the way it does when the mouth is no longer dry with despair.

Around them the island watched. Old blue land-crabs sat in the doors of their burrows and brandished their fists, absurdly brave. Cranes stood solemn in the shallows, heads cocked, considering how long it had been since they had seen featherless bipeds break the solitude.

Frank wandered apart, not gloomy so much as struck dumb by the strangeness—by the way even the smallest fly and leaf seemed to speak a different mother-tongue than the hedges of Devon. He looked, and looked again, as if he could force these foreign forms into the words he carried.

“New,” he said at last, softly. “New—everything new. Oh, awful feeling! All things changed around us, even to the tiniest fly and flower; yet we the same—the same forever.”

Amyas, mouth full of junipa-apple, followed him with the air of a cheerful showman who has seen wonders before and refuses to be overawed.

“Look, Frank—that’s a colibri. You’ve heard of colibris?”

A humming-bird hung over a bloom like a living jewel—its throat flashing green, then copper, then fire, as the light shifted. The air around it trembled with its wings’ fierce music, a high, insistent hum like a tiny spinning wheel. It darted away, then back again, and another joined it; together they whirred and danced round the flower-starred bush, their flight a bright argument no human grammar could quite catch.

Frank watched long, solemn as a priest before an altar. Then he breathed, half to himself, in the learned tongue that came to him when English failed.

“Qualis Natura formatrix, si talis formata? Oh my God, how fair must be Thy real world, if even Thy phantoms are so fair!”

“Phantoms?” Amyas frowned, uneasy at the word. “That’s no ghost, Frank, but a jolly little honey-sucker, with a wee wife, and children no bigger than peas—solid greedy little fellows enough, I’ll warrant.”

“Not phantoms in thy sense,” Frank answered, gentler than his first cry, “but in the sense of those who know the worthlessness of all below.”

Amyas halted and looked at him squarely. “I’ll tell you what, brother Frank—you’re a great deal wiser than me, I know; but I can’t abide to see you turn up your nose at God’s good earth. See now: God made all these things; and never a man, maybe, set eyes on them till fifty years agone; and yet they were as pretty as they are now, ever since the making of the world. And why do you think God could have put them here, then, but to please Himself—” and Amyas took off his hat, sudden reverent “—with the sight of them? What’s good enough to please God is good enough to please you and me.”

Frank’s face softened; the hard, feverish brightness in his eyes dimmed for a moment into something like humility.

“Your rebuke is just, dear old simple-hearted fellow. God forgive me, if with all my learning—which has brought me no profit—and my longings—which have brought me no peace—I presume at moments, sinner that I am, to be more dainty than the Lord Himself. He walked in Paradise among the trees of the garden, Amyas; and so will we, and be content with what He sends. Why should we long for the next world, before we are fit even for this one?”

“And in the meanwhile,” Amyas said, recovering his grin, “this earth’s quite good enough—at least here in Barbados.”

Frank, as if to turn the talk away from his own heart, listened to the island’s noises again—the layered chorus, each creature keeping its own hour and pitch. The ship behind them creaked faintly; the sea sighed; the palms hissed when the breeze stirred. It was a kind of music that belonged to the place, not made for sale or show, and for that reason truer.

“Do you believe,” he asked, “in those tales of the Spaniards—that Sirens and Tritons are heard singing in these seas?”

Amyas shrugged. “I can’t tell. There’s more fish in the water than ever came out of it, and more wonders in the world than we ever dreamt of. I was never in these parts before; and in the South Sea, I must say, I never came across any—though Yeo says he has heard fair music at night up in the Gulf, far away from land.”

“The Spaniards report,” Frank went on, warming to his own learning as if it were a hearth, “that at certain seasons choirs of these nymphs assemble in the sea, and with ravishing music sing their watery loves. It may be so. For Nature, which has peopled the land with rational souls, may not have left the sea altogether barren of them; above all, when we remember that the ocean is as it were the very fount of all fertility, and its slime—so the most learned hold with Thales of Miletus—that prima materia out of which all things were one by one concocted. Therefore the ancients feigned wisely that Venus—the mother of all living things, whereby they designed the plastic force of nature—was born of the sea-foam, and rising from the deep, floated ashore upon the isles of Greece.”

“I don’t know what plastic force is,” Amyas said, laughing, “but I wish I’d had the luck to be by when the pretty poppet came up. The nearest thing I ever saw to that was maidens swimming alongside us in the South Seas, and would have come aboard too; but Drake sent them off again for a lot of naughty packs—and I verily believe they were no better. Look at the butterflies now! Don’t you wish you were a boy again, and not too proud to go catching them in your cap?”

So they wandered together through the tropic wood—through leaves that shone like polished leather, past blooms that smelled of spice and rot and honey all at once, past ants marching in dark processions, past lizards that clicked away into shade. The island spoke continuously: the scratch of a crab, the clack of a beak, the sudden dry rattle of seed-pods, the far boom of surf on reef. Frank listened as if he might learn the place’s language by ear alone; Amyas only drank it in, content, as he had said, with what God sent.

They returned to the beach to find the sick already changed. Men who at dawn could not lift themselves from their hammocks were now pacing under palms, slow but stubborn, gaining strength with each step. The fruit had done more than fill bellies; it had put color into faces, and hope into eyes.

“Well done, lads!” Amyas shouted. “Keep a cheerful mind. We’ll have music ashore after dinner—for want of mermaids to sing to us. And those that can dance may.”

And so four days passed.

They did not forget their duties: they washed foul clothes in fresh water till the fabric smelled less of ship and more of sun; they filled casks from streams; they gathered fruit enough to keep a while. Yet, like schoolboys on holiday, they gave themselves up to simple merriment. At first they went hunting for gold with the folly of men who think riches grow like berries—ranging inland, prying under roots, staring into gullies, convinced each glint in sand must be treasure, in spite of Yeo’s steady reminder that none had ever been heard of there.

When no gold came, they were fain to lounge about like full-grown babies. They picked up shells and sea-fans to carry home to sweethearts, holding them to the ear to hear the sea’s imprisoned whisper. They smoked agoutis out of hollow trees with whoops and laughter. They tormented every living thing they could catch or corner—till not a land-crab dared show his claws from his hole, nor an armadillo unroll himself, until the invaders were gone.

Then, the ship watered and victualed with what the island would give, they weighed anchor. The bay took them back with no complaint but the chain’s rattle and the canvas filling. The palm-fringed shore slid away, returning to its own voices.

They left Barbados as they had found it—no men therein—never guessing, in their hunger and their play, that they had been the unknowing forerunners of a future thick with noise and trade and engines; that this quiet garden, where the night had been all insects and frogs and shore-birds, would one day ring with other sounds and other tongues, and be called, in a world grown busy, the richest gem of the tropic seas.
