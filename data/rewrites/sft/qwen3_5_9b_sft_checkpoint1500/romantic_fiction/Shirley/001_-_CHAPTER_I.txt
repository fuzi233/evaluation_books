In recent times, a deluge of curates has poured down upon the northern counties of England. They blanket the hills, thick as moss, numbering several in almost every parish. Young, vigorous, and seemingly destined for immense spiritual good, they form a striking contrast to the era from which we now retreat. We leave behind the present, with its dust, sunburn, and arid heat, to drift backward into a slumber from which we dream of a cooler dawn.

Do not let this prelude mislead you into expecting a conventional romance. Seek not sentiment, poetry, or reverie; seek not the electric shocks of melodrama. Calm your hopes and lower your standards to a humbling baseline. What awaits you is something real, cool, and solid—a narrative as unromantic as a Monday morning when the working class rises with the grim knowledge that duty calls. It is not promised that you will encounter excitement, perhaps later in the feast, but the first course laid before you is one that a devout Catholic—nay, an Anglo-Catholic—might consume on Good Friday during Passion Week: cold lentils steeped in vinegar, unleavened bread with bitter herbs, and no roast lamb.

Indeed, in those distant years, the "abundant shower" of curates had not yet fallen. In 1811–1812, the priesthood was scarce. There existed no Pastoral Aid, no Additional Curates' Society to bolster worn-out rectors or fund a fresh recruit from Oxford or Cambridge. The successors of the Apostles, disciples of Dr. Pusey and instruments of Propaganda, were then merely being hatched under cradle blankets or regenerated via nursery baptisms in washbasins. One could not have gazed upon any of them and foreseen that the Italian-ironed double frills of their net caps concealed the brows of future saints; nor could one have predicted from the folds of their long night-gowns the white surplices in which they would later exercise the souls of their parishioners or nonplus their old-fashioned vicars by flourishing aloft in the pulpit garments never before raised higher than the reading desk.

Yet, even in those lean times, curates existed. This particular branch of the divine plant was rare, but it could be found. A favored district in the West Riding of Yorkshire could boast three rods of Aaron blossoming within a circle of twenty miles. Behold them, reader. Step into this neat garden-house perched on the skirts of Whinbury, and enter the little parlour. There, seated around the table, they are at dinner. Permit me to introduce them: Mr. Donne, curate of Whinbury; Mr. Malone, curate of Briarfield; and Mr. Sweeting, curate of Nunnely. These are Mr. Donne's lodgings, inhabited by John Gale, a modest clothier. Mr. Donne has graciously invited his brothers to share a repast. Join us, observe what is seen, and hear what is spoken. For the moment, however, they are only eating, so let us converse aside.

These gentlemen are in the prime of youth, possessing an energy that their morose, elderly vicars desperately wish to channel into pastoral duties—into the diligent supervision of schools and frequent visits to the sick. Yet the youthful Levites find this tedious. They prefer to lavish their energies on a regimen that, to outside observers, appears heavy with ennui and cursed with monotony, more burdensome than the toil of a weaver at his loom. To them, however, it yields an unfailing supply of enjoyment and occupation.

I refer to their frantic triangulation between their respective lodgings. Not a circle, but a triangle of visits, they maintain throughout the year, indifferent to season or weather. With a zeal that defies explanation, they brave snow, hail, wind, rain, mire, and dust to dine, drink tea, or sup with one another. What attracts them remains difficult to pinpoint. It is certainly not friendship, for whenever they meet, they quarrel. It is not religion—the word is never uttered among them, though they may occasionally debate theology; piety, alas, is absent. Nor is it the love of food and drink: each might enjoy a superior joint and pudding, tea as potent, and toast as succulent at his own dwelling as is served to him at his brother's. Mrs. Gale, Mrs. Hogg, and Mrs. Whipp—their respective landladies—firmly assert that "it is just for naught else but to give folk trouble." By "folk," the virtuous ladies naturally mean themselves, for indeed, they are kept in a perpetual "fry" by this system of mutual invasion.

Mr. Donne and his guests are currently engaged in dinner. Mrs. Gale attends to them, though a spark of the hot kitchen fire burns in her eye. She considers the privilege of inviting a friend to a meal occasionally, without extra charge—a benefit included in the terms of her letting—to have been exercised quite sufficiently of late. The week is but Thursday, yet on Monday Mr. Malone of Briarfield came to breakfast and stayed until dinner; on Tuesday, Mr. Malone and Mr. Sweeting of Nunnely came to tea, lingered till supper, occupied the spare bed, and kept her company to breakfast on Wednesday morning; now, on Thursday, they are both here at dinner, and she is nearly certain they will remain all night. "C'en est trop," she would mutter if she spoke French.

Mr. Sweeting is carefully mincing a slice of roast beef on his plate, complaining that it is excessively tough; Mr. Donne remarks that the beer is flat. Ah, that is the crux of the matter: if they would only be civil, Mrs. Gale would not mind so much; if they would only seem satisfied with what they receive, she would not care. But "these young parsons are so high and so scornful, setting everyone beneath their fit." They treat her with scant courtesy precisely because she does not keep a servant but performs the household labor herself, as her mother did before her. Furthermore, they constantly speak against Yorkshire ways and Yorkshire folk; by that very token, Mrs. Gale refuses to believe one of them to be a genuine gentleman or of gentle kin. "The old parsons are worth the whole lump of college lads," she observes, "they know what belongs to good manners, and are kind to high and low."

"More bread!" cries Mr. Malone, in a tone which, though prolonged to only two syllables, instantly identifies him as a native of the land of shamrocks and potatoes. Mrs. Gale hates Mr. Malone more than either of the other two, yet she fears him as well. He is a tall, powerfully built figure, possessing real Irish legs and arms, and a face that is genuinely national—not the Milesian countenance, nor the style of Daniel O'Connell, but the high-featured, North-American Indian sort of visage belonging to a certain class of the Irish gentry. It possesses a petrified, proud look, better suited to the owner of a plantation of slaves than to the landlord of a free peasantry. Mr. Malone's father called himself a gentleman; he was poor, deeply in debt, and besottedly arrogant, and his son was exactly like him.

Mrs. Gale offers the loaf.

"Cut it, woman," commands her guest. And the "woman" cuts it accordingly. Had she followed her inclinations, she would have cut the parson as well; her Yorkshire soul revolts absolutely from his manner of command.

The curates have hearty appetites. Though the beef is "tough," they devour a great quantity of it. They swallow a tolerable allowance of the "flat beer," while a dish of Yorkshire pudding and two tureens of vegetables disappear like leaves before locusts. The cheese receives distinguished marks of their attention, and a "spice-cake," served by way of dessert, vanishes like a vision, leaving no trace. Its elegy is chanted in the kitchen by Abraham, Mrs. Gale's son and heir, a youth of six summers. Having reckoned upon the reversion thereof, when his mother brings down the empty platter, he lifts up his voice and weeps sore.

Meanwhile, the curates sit and sip their wine, a liquor of unpretending vintage, moderately enjoyed. Mr. Malone, indeed, would much prefer whisky, but Mr. Donne, being an Englishman, keeps no such beverage. As they sip, they argue—not on politics, nor philosophy, nor literature (topics now, as ever, totally devoid of interest)—nor even on theology, practical or doctrinal, but on minute points of ecclesiastical discipline, frivolities that seem empty as bubbles to anyone but themselves. Mr. Malone, contriving to secure two glasses of wine while his brethren content themselves with one, gradually becomes hilarious in his own peculiar fashion; that is, he grows slightly insolent, speaks rude things in a hectoring tone, and laughs clamorously at his own brilliance.

Each companion becomes, in turn, his butt. Malone possesses a stock of jokes which he dispenses regularly on convivial occasions like the present, seldom varying his wit, for which there is no necessity, as he never considers himself monotonous and cares little for others' thoughts.
