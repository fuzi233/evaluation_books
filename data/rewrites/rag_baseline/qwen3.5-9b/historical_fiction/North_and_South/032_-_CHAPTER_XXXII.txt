The rain lashed against the leaded glass of the library windows, a rhythmic drumming that felt, to Lady Violet Lebas, like the heartbeat of London itself—frantic, restless, and thoroughly indifferent to the quiet suffocations of its women. She sat at her mahogany desk, the heavy scent of beeswax and damp wool filling the room, her gaze fixed upon the thick sheaf of letters that had arrived by the morning post. They were from Mr. Andrew Lang, his sprawling, confident script detailing his latest thoughts on literature.

Violet sighed, tracing the edge of the parchment with a silver letter-opener. Lang was a man of undeniable erudition, yet his letters were suffocated by the very patriarchal complacency she so deeply despised. He wrote to her of Virgil, of modern English poetry, of Fielding and Rochefoucauld, positioning himself as the benevolent instructor to her "undefeated resolution to admire only the right things."

"The right things," she murmured aloud, the words tasting like ash. What he meant, of course, was the established canon—the texts elevated by centuries of wealthy, privileged men who saw their own reflections in the exploits of conquerors and the musings of comfortable philosophers.

She turned to his pages on Fielding. Lang excused the novelist's coarse morality, arguing that men must be pardoned the "vices of young blood" so long as they were brave and generous. He painted a nostalgic picture of a "lusty old militant world," entirely blind to the fact that this merry England was built upon the bruised backs of women and the indentured silence of the poor. He dismissed the systemic inequalities that left women like Sophia Western with no recourse but to forgive the betrayals of their husbands.

But tonight, the ink of Lang's dismissal was irrelevant. The storm outside mirrored the tempest within the North. Here, in the shadow of the industrial sprawl, the stakes were not literary debates but the very survival of souls.

In the dim glow of the parlor, three figures huddled together, a fragile triangle of grief and desperate hope. Mr. Hale sat rigid, his posture a coiled spring of terror. His wife was gone, taken by the cold indifference of fate, and now his son, Frederick, stood on the precipice of exile. The boy's initial devastation had receded, replaced by a stoic shame; he refused to speak of his mother, burying the depth of his sorrow beneath a mask of hardened resolve. Yet, it was Margaret who bore the weight of the silence. Her tears were frequent, her voice softened by a mournful tenderness that deepened whenever her eyes met Frederick's, haunted by the knowledge of his imminent departure.

"You will go with Frederick to the station, Margaret?" Mr. Hale asked suddenly, his voice cracking under the strain. "I shall want to know he is safely off. You will bring me word that he is clear of Milton, at any rate?"

"Certainly," Margaret replied, her composure slipping just enough to reveal the woman beneath. "I shall like it, if you won't be lonely without me, papa."

"No, no!" Hale waved a frantic hand, his eyes darting to the door as if expecting an intruder. "I should always be fancying some one had known him, and that he had been stopped, unless you could tell me you had seen him off. And go to the Outwood station. It is quite as near, and not so many people about. Take a cab there. There is less risk of his being seen. What time is your train, Fred?"

"Ten minutes past six; very nearly dark."

"So what will you do, Margaret?"

"Oh, I can manage," she lied, forcing a bravado that tasted bitter on her tongue. "I am getting very brave and very hard. It is a well-lighted road all the way home, if it should be dark. But I was out last week much later."

It was a lie born of necessity, a shield against the crushing anxiety that paralyzed her father. When the parting finally came, Margaret moved with mechanical efficiency, hurrying Frederick into the cab to shorten a scene that tore at her father's heart. They reached Outwood with twenty minutes to spare, a luxury that quickly evaporated into confusion. The booking office was closed, the gates barred against the night. With nowhere to stand, they descended the stone steps to the cinder path cutting diagonally across the field beside the tracks.

Margaret's hand lay in Frederick's arm, a lifeline in the darkness. He squeezed it tightly, his grip fierce with a desperation that belied his earlier calm.

"Margaret! I am going to consult Mr. Lennox as to the chance of exculpating myself so that I may return to England whenever I choose, more for your sake than for the sake of anyone else," he whispered, the wind stealing the words from his lips. "I can't bear to think of your lonely position if anything should happen to my father. He looks sadly changed—terribly shaken. I wish you could get him to think of the Cadiz plan, for many reasons. What could you do if he were taken away? You have no friend near. We are curiously bare of relations."

Margaret fought back the tears threatening to spill. The cares of the last few months had carved hollows in her father's face, turning him into a stranger to himself. Yet she rallied, summoning the strength to meet his gaze.

"There have been such strange unexpected changes in my life during these last two years," she said, her voice steady despite the tremor in her hands, "that I feel more than ever that it is not worth while to calculate too closely what I should do if any future event took place. I try to think only upon the present."

They stood silent for a moment, the setting sun casting long, distorted shadows across the field. Frederick held her hand, searching her face for answers she could not give, reading there a depth of care and trouble she struggled to conceal.

"We shall write often to one another," she promised, seeing the relief flicker in his eyes. "And I will promise—for I see it will set your mind at ease—to tell you every worry I have. Papa is..." She hesitated, a barely perceptible start jerking her shoulder.

Freeland felt the sudden tension in her arm and turned his full face toward the road. A horseman rode slowly along the lane, just passing the stile where they stood.

"Who is that?" Frederick demanded, almost before the rider was out of earshot.

Margaret's face flushed, a mixture of embarrassment and defiance coloring her features. "Mr. Thornton; you saw him before, you know."

"Only his back. He is an unprepossessing-looking fellow. What a scowl he has!"

"Something has happened to vex him," Margaret apologized softly. "You would not have thought him unprepossessing if you had seen him with mamma."

"I fancy it must be time to go and take my ticket. If I had known how dark it would be, we wouldn't have sent back the cab, Margaret."

"Oh, don't fidget about that. I can take a cab here, if I like; or go back by the rail-road, when I should have shops and people and lamps all the way from the Milton station-house. Don't think of me; take care of yourself. I am sick with the thought that Leonards may be in the same train with you. Look well into the carriage before you get in."

They returned to the station, the air thick with the smell of coal dust and impending doom. Margaret insisted on entering the booking office, stepping into the harsh glare of the flaring gas lamps. Inside, several idle-looking young men lounged near the station-master, their expressions bored and predatory. One of them caught her eye, his gaze lingering with an undisguised admiration that made her feel exposed, vulnerable. She returned the look with a pride that bordered on arrogance, a defense mechanism honed by years of navigating a world that sought to diminish her.

She moved swiftly to her brother, who waited outside, clutching his bag. "Let us walk about here on the platform," she urged, her voice slightly flurried. The thought of being left alone so soon caused her courage to leak away faster than she cared to admit.

A footstep followed them along the flagstones, stopping abruptly as they halted, both listening intently to the distant whistle of the approaching train. Neither spoke; their hearts were too full, too fractured for words. Another moment, and the train would be here; a minute more, and he would be gone forever.

Regret struck Margaret like a physical blow. She almost wished she had urged him to sail for Spain via Liverpool instead. Two or three hours, and he would be beyond reach, safe from the net closing around them.

Suddenly, Frederick turned, facing the bright lamp where the gas flame danced in vivid anticipation of the arrival. A figure emerged from the gloom—a man dressed in the ragged uniform of a railway porter, but with the swagger of a brute. His eyes were clouded with alcohol, yet his movements were precise, deadly.

"By your leave, miss!" he barked, shoving Margaret roughly aside and grabbing Frederick by the collar. "Your name is Hale, I believe?"

In an instant, the world dissolved into a kaleidoscope of motion. How Margaret reacted, she did not see; her vision blurred, everything dancing before her eyes.
