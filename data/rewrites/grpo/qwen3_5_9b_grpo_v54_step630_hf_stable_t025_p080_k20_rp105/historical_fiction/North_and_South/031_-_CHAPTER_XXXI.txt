The October morning arrived, not with the gentle, silvery mist of the countryside that clears before the sun to reveal vibrant colors, but with the heavy, oppressive fog of Milton. The sun struggled to pierce the gloom, casting only long, dusky shadows across the streets. Inside the Hale household, the air was thick with a different kind of chill. Margaret moved through the rooms with a languid, mechanical grace, assisting Dixon in preparing the house for the coming days. Tears constantly blurred her vision, yet she found no time to weep openly. Her father and brother were consumed by grief; she was the anchor required to keep them afloat. Every detail of the funeral—from the arrangements to the guests—fell upon her shoulders.

As the fire crackled brightly and the tea-kettle sang its familiar song, Margaret paused to survey the room one last time before summoning Mr. Hale and Frederick. She insisted on a cheerful appearance, a stark contrast to the desolation in her heart. Kneeling by the sofa, she hid her face in the cushions, muffling her sobs until Dixon's hand touched her shoulder.

"Come, Miss Hale," Dixon urged, her voice unusually tender. "You must not give way. Who else is fit to direct things? The funeral arrangements, the guests, the location—it is all to be settled. Master Frederick is beside himself with weeping, and Master Hale is lost in his sorrow. Death comes to us all, my dear, but you are fortunate not to have lost a friend until now."

Perhaps Dixon was right, yet this loss felt unique, incomparable to any other tragedy. Finding no comfort in the servant's words, Margaret was nonetheless moved by Dixon's unexpected kindness. Rising with a forced smile, she thanked her and went to announce that breakfast was ready.

Mr. Hale entered as if in a trance, his movements unconscious, his mind elsewhere. Frederick followed, feigning cheerfulness until he grasped Margaret's hand and broke down in tears. Throughout the meal, Margaret spoke of trivialities, desperate to distract them from the memory of their last shared meal, which had been haunted by the strained silence of waiting for news from the sickroom.

Afterward, Margaret approached her father regarding the funeral plans. He nodded vacantly, agreeing to contradictory suggestions, offering no real guidance. As she prepared to leave the room to consult Dixon, Mr. Hale stopped her.

"Ask Mr. Bell," he said, his voice hollow. "Mr. Bell of Oxford?"

"Yes," Margaret replied, recognizing the connection. "He was your groom's-man."

"I will write today," she promised. Mr. Hale slumped back into his listlessness.

By evening, Dixon returned with news that sent a fresh wave of anxiety through Margaret. She had managed to rouse her master, Mr. Hale, from his stupor by informing him that it was unsafe for Frederick to remain. She had encountered a man named Young Leonards, a former draper's son from Southampton known for his reckless behavior and a stint aboard the *Orion* during the mutiny.

"He was a scamp," Dixon recounted, her voice rising with indignation. "I called out his name, and he recognized me. He mocked me, asking if I was still 'Miss Dixon.' I told him I preferred to be addressed as a married woman, or at least one who had better prospects than helping old Mr. Leonards give George a good rating. He turned the conversation to Frederick, calling him a disgrace and mentioning the hundred-pound reward for his capture. He implied he could offer Frederick patronage if he wished to turn his life around, a suggestion that made my blood boil."

"But you didn't tell him where we are?" Margaret asked, relieved.

"No," Dixon admitted. "But he threatened to trap Lieutenant Hale and offered to share the reward if I helped him. He jumped on a bus, leering at me with a wicked smile, thinking he had the last word."

Margaret felt a surge of dread. "I must tell Frederick. What did he look like?"

"A bad-looking fellow," Dixon described. "Red whiskers, dressed in fustian like a laborer, despite claiming a confidential situation."

Frederick soon entered, his brightness dimmed but the violent edge of his grief subsiding. He kissed Margaret's forehead and urged her to rest on the sofa. As she lay down, covered by a shawl, her brother sat beside her, and they spoke in hushed tones. Margaret relayed Dixon's account. Frederick's reaction was one of stunned dismay.

"I'd rather anyone had the hundred pounds than that rascal," he declared. "If he knew I was within twenty miles, he'd ferret me out to settle old scores. I wish Dixon could be given a pension; she deserves it."

Mr. Hale, overhearing their conversation, stepped forward, trembling. "My boy, you must go. It is very bad, but you must. You have done all you could."

"Oh, papa, must he go?" Margaret pleaded, though she knew the necessity.

"I declare, I have a mind to face it out," Frederick said, his voice bitter. "If I could only gather my evidence! I cannot endure being in the power of such a blackguard. It has a certain charm, this stolen visit, like the forbidden pleasures the Frenchwomen speak of."

Margaret recalled his childhood disgrace for stealing apples, noting that his feelings had not changed much since then. Mr. Hale confirmed Frederick's departure was mandatory.

Before leaving, Frederick revealed a secret that shifted the atmosphere. He spoke of Dolores Barbour, a Roman Catholic girl of nineteen whom he intended to marry. He confessed that he himself was inclined toward Catholicism, a fact that explained his earlier silence regarding his father's religious struggles. He proposed that if they could not clear his name, they might flee to Spain.

"No more removals," Mr. Hale stated firmly. "One has cost me my wife. She will be here, and here I will stay."

Margaret, however, seized upon the opportunity to help. "For Dolores' sake, Fred, you must try to clear yourself. Even if the charge of mutiny is true, you must show that your disobedience was an act of heroism, not crime. People need to know the truth."

Frederick argued that a court-martial was merely a display of authority, where evidence counted for little. Yet, Margaret persisted, suggesting they consult a lawyer. She named Mr. Henry Lennox, the husband of her friend Edith.

"He must go to London tomorrow," Margaret decided. "I will write a note to Mr. Lennox at his chambers in the Temple. You can leave a list of names from the *Orion* with him."

Frederick agreed, though he groaned at the thought of parting. He assured her he could sail from anywhere and would not stay in London long. As Margaret wrote the note, Frederick watched over her shoulder. Before she could finish reviewing it, he snatched the paper, tucked it into his pocket-book, and produced a lock of black hair that had fallen from it. His eyes glowed with pleasure as he showed it to her.

"Now you would like to see that, wouldn't you?" he teased. "No! You must wait until you see her herself. She is too perfect to be known by fragments. No mean brick shall be a specimen of the building of my palace."
