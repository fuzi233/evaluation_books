The fund prospered.

Not by miracle, unless one counted as miracle the stubborn, daily labour of three rectors who would not be gainsaid; the shining example of Miss Keeldar, whose purse opened with a frankness that made parsimony blush; and the noiseless, diligent soldiership of their two aides—Mary Ann Ainley and Margaret Hall—both of them women who could contrive relief with the same neat economy wherewith they pinned on caps, mended cuffs, and kept accounts in a hand as fine as stitch-work.

A handsome sum was gathered; it was managed with prudence; and, for the present, hunger was kept from certain doors. In consequence, the neighbourhood wore, for a little space, a quieter face. For a fortnight no cloth had been torn in rage; no mill-window dashed in; no midnight threatening of mansion or manufactory carried into act. Shirley—who liked action, but not blood—was sanguine. She said the worst of the storm was over; that summer would mend trade as surely as it dried the lanes; that war must end, because even war was mortal; and that peace, once returned, would pour a new life into commerce.

Such was commonly the tenor of her observations to her tenant, Gérard Moore, whenever they fell together in those chance intervals where there was room for a word aside.

Moore listened—quietly; too quietly to content her. She would turn on him an impatient glance, demanding, by mere look, that he should say more—should explain himself, should contradict her, should at least add some single spark to her bright heap of hope. Then he would smile in his way—the mouth strangely sweet under the grave brow—and answer, in substance, that he too trusted in the finite nature of war; that upon that limit his anchor was fixed; that his present course depended on it.

“For you are aware,” he would go on, “that I now work Hollow’s Mill entirely on speculation. I sell nothing; there is no market. I manufacture for a future day—I make myself ready to seize the first opening. Three months ago this was impossible: credit and capital were exhausted. You well know who came to my rescue, from what hand I received the loan which saved me. It is on the strength of that loan I can continue a bold game which, a while since, I feared I should never play again. Total ruin will follow loss—I know it; gain is doubtful; yet I am cheerful. So long as I can be active—so long as I can strive—so long, in short, as my hands are not tied, I cannot be depressed. One year—nay, six months—of the reign of the olive, and I am safe; for, as you say, peace will give an impulse to commerce. In that you are right. But as to the restored tranquillity here—as to the permanent good effect of your charitable fund—I doubt. Eleemosynary relief never yet tranquillized the working-classes; it never made them grateful; it is not in human nature. Were all things ordered aright, they ought not to be forced into need of that humiliating relief—and that is what they feel. We should feel it in their place. Besides, to whom should they be grateful? To you, to the clergy perhaps; but not to us mill-owners. They hate us worse than ever. The disaffected here correspond with the disaffected elsewhere: Nottingham is a head-quarters, Manchester another, Birmingham a third. The subalterns take orders from chiefs; they are disciplined; no blow is struck without mature deliberation. In sultry weather you have watched the sky threaten thunder day by day, and yet night after night the clouds have cleared and the sun set quietly; but danger was not gone—only delayed. The long-threatening storm will break at last. There is analogy between the moral and physical atmosphere.”

“Well, Mr. Moore,”—and so these conferences always ended—“take care of yourself. If you think I have ever done you any good, reward me by promising to take care of yourself.”

“I do; I will take close and watchful care. I wish to live, not to die. The future opens like Eden before me; and still, when I look deep into the shades of my paradise, I see a vision I like better than seraph or cherub glide across remote vistas.”

“Do you? Pray, what vision?”

“I see—”

The maid came bustling in with the tea-things, and the vision was thrust back, like a word in a native dialect that has no safe place in company—something too intimate, too perilously untranslatable for the common ear.

The early part of May had been fine; the middle wet; and in the last week, at change of moon, it cleared again. A fresh wind went over the country like a broom with a clean purpose, sweeping off the silver-white, deep-piled rain-clouds and bearing them—mass on mass—eastward, till they dwindled on the horizon and slid behind its rim. The whole blue vault was left rinsed and ready for summer. On Whitsuntide the sun rose broad; Whit-Tuesday, which was the great day of the schools’ gathering, wore splendour like a holiday garment.

Briarfield, by old custom and by Mr. Helstone’s will (which in that district passed for law), was the point of rendezvous. The two large schoolrooms, built chiefly at the present rector’s expense, had been cleaned out, whitewashed, repainted, and dressed with flowers and evergreens—some from the rectory garden; two cartloads from Fieldhead; and a wheel-barrowful from the more grudging domain of De Walden, where Mr. Wynne dwelt. Twenty long tables—each to hold twenty guests—were laid with white cloths and flanked with benches. Above them, by a fancy local and dear to Mr. Helstone’s clerk, hung some twenty cages of canaries. The clerk delighted in their piercing song, and knew that amidst confusion of tongues they always carolled loudest: a small, wild music persistently keeping its own language in a room where human voices, polite and rude, struggled for precedence.

These tables were not for the twelve hundred scholars, but for patrons and teachers. The children’s feast would be spread out of doors, where grass and sunlight made a cleaner board than any carpenter. At one the troops were to come in; at two they were to be marshalled; till four they were to parade the parish; then the open-air meal; afterwards the meeting in church with music and speechifying.

Why Briarfield had the lead was not because it was the biggest—Whinbury surpassed it—nor the oldest—Nunnely’s mossy parsonage and low-roofed temple, buried in Nunnwood oaks, were older still. It was because Mr. Helstone willed it. Boultby could not dispute precedence; Hall would not. They let their resolute brother lead and rule.

This anniversary had always been a trying day to Caroline Helstone. It dragged her into public; it compelled her to face what was wealthy, respectable, influential in the neighbourhood; and, but for Mr. Hall’s kind countenance, she would have appeared unsupported. She must be conspicuous—must walk at the head of her regiment as the rector’s niece and first teacher of the first class—must make tea at the first table for a mixed multitude of ladies and gentlemen—all without mother, aunt, or other chaperon. Being nervous, and mortally afraid of publicity, she trembled at the approach of Whitsuntide.

But this year Shirley was to be with her, and the very thought changed the trial into something like pleasure. Miss Keeldar in her single self was better than a host of ordinary friends. She moved with ease; she was self-possessed, spirited, never presuming on her importance and yet perfectly conscious of it. Courage might be borrowed by merely looking at her.

The one fear was punctuality. Shirley had a careless way of lingering behind time; and Caroline knew her uncle would not wait a second for any one. At the moment the church clock tolled two, the bells would clash and the march begin. Shirley must be secured.

Whit-Tuesday saw Caroline rise almost with the sun. She, Fanny, and Eliza made busy hands all the morning, setting the rectory parlours in first-rate company order, arranging the chairs, throwing open the windows to admit air that smelled of washed earth and lilac, and setting out a collation of cooling refreshments—wine, fruit, cakes—upon the dining-room sideboard. There was a domestic confidence in such preparations: the order of cups and plates, the pale gleam of glass, the sugared promise of confection, all set against the more anxious order of the world outside, where men argued wages and threatened mills.

Then Caroline dressed—in her freshest white muslin; the fineness of the day and the solemnity of the occasion warranted it. Her new sash—Margaret Hall’s birthday gift, which she had reason to believe Cyril himself had bought—was tied by Fanny’s dexterous fingers; and in return Caroline had indeed given Cyril a set of cambric bands in a handsome case. Her simple bonnet was trimmed to answer the sash; her scarf of white crape suited her gown. When ready, she formed a picture not dazzling, but delicately pleasing; not richly coloured, but full of purity and grace. In her brown eye and clear forehead there was the same gentle harmony that lay in the folds of her dress—a mind modest, tender, pensive, yet not discordant with the bright day.

She was, after all, a faulty human being—fair, and anxious to do right—yet, as Cyril Hall had said, neither so good nor so great as withered Miss Ainley, now putting on her best black gown and Quaker-drab shawl and bonnet in her narrow cottage chamber, as one who asked no praise and expected no recompense.

Caroline went out and took the shortest, most hidden way to Fieldhead—across sequestered fields and through lanes so screened with hedge and young leaf that the world seemed reduced to birds, grass, and her own light footfall. After the late rains all was clean, and under the present sun all was dry. No dust rose to stain the hem of her white garment; no wet clung to her sandal. The country, washed and warmed, had a smell of new bread made from earth and sun.

She reached Fieldhead and penetrated to Miss Keeldar’s dressing-room.

It was well she came. Shirley, instead of making ready, lay stretched on a couch, absorbed in reading, as if time were her servant. Mrs. Pryor stood near, urging in vain that she should rise and dress.

Caroline wasted no words. She took the book from Shirley’s hand, and with her own began the business of disrobing and re-robing her. Shirley, indolent with heat and gay with youth, wanted to talk, laugh, linger. Caroline, intent on time, persevered—fastening strings, setting pins, smoothing folds. At last, as she united the final row of hooks and eyes, she found leisure to chide her—called her naughty, incorrigible, careless. And Shirley did look the picture of carelessness—yet a very lovely picture.

She contrasted wholly with Caroline. There was style in every fold of her dress, in every line of her figure. The rich silk suited her better than simplicity; the deep embroidered scarf became her, worn negligently but with grace. The wreath on her bonnet sat like a crown. Fashion and ornament, in her case, seemed not vanity but natural expression—like the frank light in her eyes, the rallying smile about her lips, her straight carriage and lightsome step.

Caroline caught her hand; hurried her downstairs and out; and together they sped through the fields laughing, looking like a snow-white dove and a gem-tinted bird of paradise joined in social flight.

Thanks to Miss Helstone’s promptitude, they arrived in good time. While trees still hid the church, they heard the bell toll—a measured, urgent summons. There was the trooping of numbers, the trampling of many feet, the low tide-sound of voices. From a rising ground they saw, on the Whinbury road, the Whinbury school approaching—five hundred souls. Rector and curate headed it: Boultby and Donne. Boultby loomed large in full canonicals, walking under the canopy of a shovel-hat, supported by the stoutest of gold-headed canes, and clothed in the squarest, vastest black coat. He flourished his cane now and then, and inclined his hat with a dogmatical wag to his aide-de-camp. Donne, narrow compared with his principal, contrived nevertheless to look every inch a curate: self-complacent from turned-up nose and elevated chin down to black gaiters, somewhat short strapless trousers, and square-toed shoes.

Walk on, Mr. Donne; you had undergone scrutiny. Whether the white and purple figures watching from the hill thought you well set forth was another question.

Those figures—Caroline and Shirley—came running down when the regiment had marched by. The churchyard was full of children and teachers in their best holiday attire; and, distressed as the district was, bad as the times were, it was wonderful to see how respectably, how handsomely even, they had contrived to clothe themselves. That British love of decency worked miracles. Poverty which would reduce an Irish girl to rags was impotent to rob an English girl of neatness, because neatness was part of her self-respect. Besides, the lady of the manor—Shirley, now gazing with pleasure—had indeed done them good. Her seasonable bounty had consoled many a poor family against the coming holiday, and supplied many a child with a new frock or bonnet. She knew it, and it elated her—not with sanctimony, but with a bright, bodily relief, as if she had fed some hunger she could not otherwise quiet within herself. She could not be charitable like Miss Ainley; it was not in her nature. It soothed her to see there were other ways of giving—ways practicable to other characters.

Caroline too was pleased, for she had done a little good in her own narrow measure—robbed herself of more than one dress, ribbon, collar she could ill spare, to help outfit the scholars of her class; and because she could not give money she had followed Miss Ainley in giving time and industry, sewing till her fingers ached, then sewing again.

Not only was the churchyard full; the rectory garden was thronged. Ladies and gentlemen walked among waving lilacs and laburnums; the house also was occupied; at the wide-open parlour windows gay groups stood. These were patrons and teachers who would swell the procession. In the parson’s croft behind the rectory stood the musicians of the three parish bands with their instruments; and Fanny and Eliza, in the smartest caps and gowns and the whitest aprons, moved amongst them serving out quarts of ale—sound and strong, brewed some weeks since under the rector’s special superintendence. Whatever Mr. Helstone touched must be done handsomely; shabby doings of any description were not endured under his sanction. From the building of school or church to the ordering of a dinner, he advocated the lordly, liberal, effective. Miss Keeldar resembled him in this; they mutually approved each other’s arrangements.

Caroline and Shirley were soon amid the company. Caroline met it more easily than ever she had done. Instead of stealing away to her own room till the procession should be marshalled, she moved through the parlours, conversed and smiled, even spoke once or twice before she was spoken to. She seemed a new creature; and it was Shirley’s presence that transformed her. Shirley had no fear of her kind, no instinct to shrink or avoid. Men, women, children—unless rendered positively offensive by low breeding or coarse presumption—were welcome enough to her. This disposition made her a general favourite. It robbed her raillery of sting and gave her serious talk a charm; yet it did not cheapen her intimate friendship, which was a separate, rarer thing depending on another part of her character. Miss Helstone was the choice of her affection and intellect; the Misses Pearson, Sykes, Wynne, and their like were merely the profiteers of her good-nature and vivacity.

Donne came into the drawing-room while Shirley, seated on the sofa, was the centre of a wide circle. She had already forgotten her exasperation against him; she bowed and smiled good-humouredly. The disposition of the man was seen at once. He neither declined the advance with dignity, as one whose pride is justly wounded, nor met it with frankness, as one glad to forget. His punishment had impressed him with no shame, and he did not feel it now, facing his chastiser. He was not vigorous enough in evil to be actively malignant; he only passed by sheepishly with a rated, scowling look. Nothing could again reconcile him to his enemy; yet no passion of resentment could his lymphatic nature fully know.

“He was not worth a scene,” said Shirley to Caroline. “What a fool I was! To revenge on poor Donne his silly spite at Yorkshire is like crushing a gnat for attacking the hide of a rhinoceros. Had I been a gentleman, I believe I should have helped him off the premises by dint of physical force. I am glad I only employed the moral weapon. But he must come near me no more. I don’t like him. He irritates me. There’s not even amusement to be had out of him. Malone is better sport.”

It seemed Malone wished to justify the preference; for scarcely had she spoken when Peter Augustus came up—all in grand tenue, gloved and scented, hair oiled and brushed to perfection, bearing in one hand a huge bunch of cabbage-roses, five or six in full blow. He presented them to the heiress with a gravity and flourish no pencil could adequately render.

Who after this should dare say Peter was not a lady’s man? He had gathered and he had given flowers; he had offered a sentimental tribute at the shrine of Love—or Mammon. Hercules with the distaff was but a faint type of Peter with his roses. He seemed amazed at himself; he backed without a word, going away with a husky chuckle of self-satisfaction; then bethought him to stop and turn, as if to certify by sight that he really had done this gallant thing.

Yes: there were the six red cabbages on the purple satin lap; a very white hand with gold rings slightly held them; and streaming ringlets, half hiding a laughing face, drooped over them. Only half hiding. Peter saw the laugh; unmistakable. He was made a joke of—his chivalry the subject of a jest for one petticoat, for two: Miss Helstone too smiled. Moreover, he felt himself seen through; and Peter grew black as a thunder-cloud. When Shirley looked up, a fell eye was fastened on her. Malone, at least, had energy enough in hate. She read it in his glance.

“Peter is worth a scene,” she whispered to Caroline, “and shall have it, if he likes, one day.”

Now, solemn and sombre in colour though bland enough in face, appeared at the dining-room door the three rectors. They had been busy in the church; they came now to take a little refreshment ere the march commenced. The great morocco-covered easy-chair had been left vacant for Dr. Boultby; he was placed in it. Caroline, prompted by Shirley—who told her this was the time to play hostess—hastened to hand to her uncle’s vast, revered, and, on the whole, worthy friend a glass of wine and a plate of macaroons.

Boultby’s churchwardens—patrons, as he insisted on their being—were already beside him. Mrs. Sykes and other ladies hovered right and left, expressing hopes he was not fatigued and fears the day would be too warm. Mrs. Boultby, who held that when her lord dropped asleep after a good dinner his face became as the face of an angel, bent over him and wiped some perspiration, real or imaginary, from his brow. Boultby rumbled out thanks in a round voix de poitrine and assurances of tolerable health. Of Caroline he took no notice beyond accepting what she offered. He did not see her—he never did see her; he hardly knew such a person existed. He saw the macaroons, however, and being fond of sweets, possessed himself of a small handful. The wine Mrs. Boultby insisted on mingling with hot water, qualifying with sugar and nutmeg.

Mr. Hall stood near an open window, breathing fresh air and the scent of flowers, talking like a brother to Miss Ainley. To him Caroline turned with pleasure; she must serve him, not let him help himself. She brought a little salver with variety. Margaret Hall joined them; so did Miss Keeldar. The four ladies stood about their favourite pastor, thinking, in their way, that they looked upon an earthly angel. Cyril Hall was their pope, infallible to them as Dr. Thomas Boultby to his admirers.

A throng enclosed the rector of Briarfield too. Twenty pressed round old Helstone; no parson was ever more potent in a circle. The curates, herding together after their manner, made a constellation of three lesser planets. Young ladies watched them from afar, but ventured not nigh.

Mr. Helstone produced his watch.

“Ten minutes to two,” he announced aloud. “Time for all to fall into line. Come.”

He seized his shovel-hat and marched away. All rose and followed en masse.

The twelve hundred children were drawn up in three bodies of four hundred souls each; at the rear of each regiment stood a band; between every twenty there was an interval where Helstone posted teachers in pairs. To the van of the armies he summoned—

“Grace Boultby and Mary Sykes lead out Whinbury.

“Margaret Hall and Mary Ann Ainley conduct Nunnely.

“Caroline Helstone and Shirley Keeldar head Briarfield.”
