We came, in good time, out from that sorrowful inward place of the great Mountains—the throat and gullet of stone that I had named the Upward Gorge—and halted, soon after, between the feet of the vast hills, beyond the mouth of it. The air here lay wider, and there was a lightness upon the land, though ever under the great roof of the Night; yet it was as though some hidden hand had swept away a closeness from the lungs.

Naani looked every way at once, as one that hath been long shut within a narrow house and is brought sudden into a broad field. She drew breath quick, and then deeper, and her eyes shone with a bright wonder; and the freedom itself seemed to make her tremble, as if the very wind were a thing to be learned.

She turned, and looked upward into the dark gap whence we had come, spying upon the great mouth of the Gorge. Fear touched her then, and she ran a little way downward toward the open country of the Seas, and stopped, and looked backward again, with awe and a soul relieved; and then she looked outward, and outward, upon the spread land and the far Sea, and she near laughed and wept in the same breath, amazed and glad. She did never cease from looking, nor from those deep breathings of the wide air; for never in all her life had she been in so broad a place of lightness, as you shall perceive.

And we, both, felt that we had no more need to speak husht, as we had done alway in the gloom and narrowness of the Gorge. Surely she cried out, like a child that trieth an echo; and her voice went very prettily into the distance and seemed to be lost.

Yet lo, in a moment, there came back an answering from the dark Mountains behind us; and we turned very sudden. Whether it was true echo, or some strangeness making answer, or some unnatural call creeping downward from the Gorge, we could not be sure. Therefore we went down, a while more, until our breaths were steadier and our minds felt free of that mouth of horror that lay upward in the stone.

Then we sought a flat rock for our use, and found one that stood a little above the land; and we climbed upon it and sat there to eat and drink.

We ate close and happy; yet I kept a wise looking about, for fear of the Humpt Men, or other dangers. And alway the Maid questioned, and stared far over the Country, and her shining joy at the Sea stirred her so that sudden memories came upon her—vague, sharp as a spice upon the tongue, yet strange as dreams—mingled all with pleasure and pain. Once she broke to weeping and must be in mine arms until she knew herself again; and then she came back to her dear, natural joy.

Oft she spoke upon the clear wonder of the air, that seemed to her as a new drink. And it did seem so to me likewise, that had lived also in a Dark Land. But even as she was in her rapture, she would break sudden, and set back her speech an Eternity with olden words and half-remembered turns of phrase, englamoured and unsteady—like moonlight that once hath shone upon a table where folk did eat and talk. Then, in a moment, she would be forward again into her far-future speech, and her being close unto me; and oft we fell into a solemn silence of the heart.

The greatness of the Sea called to her with some old voice, half waking her; and I, by her stirring, was half-wakened also. Not so as on my outward way, when I had only hardness and haste; but now her awakenings pulled mine old thoughts and memory-dreams afresh upon my spirit. So we sat there shaken with dreamings of the old World, that lay, as it were, above us in that dreadful Night that roofed the Country. I have no speech to make known all the troublings and stirrings of our spirits at that time; for there are stirrings that die when you put them into words, and there are words that cannot bear them.

Far off, by miles, beyond the feet of the Mountain, the shore of the Sea went upon our left; and there lay a great mist and steam, rising and rolling. This was that same steam I had come through on mine outward journey. Naani asked of it, and I told her what I knew; and how we must pass through it.

She wondered also at the volcanoes that burned in the Sea and here and there upon the wide Country; the height and grandness exalted her and yet made her strangely humble. So, presently, I took her into mine arms and kissed her, because she was so utter a sweet Maid, and lovely with her naturalness and quick interest. Truly she kissed me in turn; and between her kissings she questioned me still—yearning for more knowing of the Country, and, as I think, being sweetly impudent in her joy.

Presently she kissed me thrice, very warm upon the mouth; and then she set her small hands upon my shoulders—very pretty, laid upon my broadness and the metal of mine armor—and strove to shake me to speedier answering, full of dear naughtiness, and needing that I should kiss her hard. I answered her, but after my fashion: a word between each kiss. She, quick and naughty, put her hand between our lips; and I kissed her palm, small and soft. Then, when I had no thought, she opened her fingers quick and kissed me through between them, and shut them again, stopping me, and laughed with her eyes.

Afterward I made her stand upon the rock. I loosed her hair down over her shoulders, and took the boots from her, so that her little feet showed bare and pretty. At first she half refused, and then stood dear and obedient that I should have my way, a little shy, the more pretty for her blushings. When I had her to my liking I stepped back a pace and looked at her. She looked again at me, quaint and naughty; then turned about very grave, pretending to be a dummy figure. And when she was come round and faced me with a sedate look, she stretched out her pretty foot all sudden and put her pink toes upon my lips. I was so surprised that I had no wit to do aught ere she snatched them back. Then she sprang into mine arms and would be hugged, and loved very great. I laughed, all tender; for I loved her utterly. I told her, as a man in love will tell his Maid, that I needed a pocket sufficient to carry her always nigh to my heart. She laughed mischievous, and said she would tickle me if I carried her thatwise—aye, and pinch me too. I had no answer but to shake her gently; yet in the midst she kissed me, very naughty upon the mouth. What shall a man do with such an one?

Then she would be more sedate and be set down upon the rock, and made me turn so she might come at the pouch upon my back. She took thence the comb and combed her hair, and I sat and talked with her and jested, my heart light as it had not been for long. Though I dreaded the Humpt Men and the monstrous beasts of that Country, yet I had no abiding horror, for there was a naturalness in those things, and no sense of an Evil Force, such as the dark places carry. Presently she bound up her hair; but I begged her to leave it upon her shoulders because it was so pretty, and she smiled and did it, happy to my pleasuring.

We grew, after that, truly sedate, and set our gear together. I put the boots again upon the Maid, and we began once more to journey downward into the Country of the Seas.

We went at a good pace, yet not to weariness; for my intent was that we should rest for our slumber upon this side of the place of steam and boilings near the shore, as you shall mind. We passed along the feet of the Mountains toward that steam, and went so for six good hours; yet still were a great hour from it, because we went not so fierce as on my outward way, when necessity drove me hard.

So, when we had walked six hours, we were somewhat beyond the eighteenth hour of that day’s journeying, and ready for sleep. We found a tall rock, hard to climb, with a flat top twice my length every way. It served well. When we were safe upon it we ate and drank, and presently slept, with the cloak under us, as was the Maid’s will; for the Country was warm and kind, and we had no need of covering.

We waked, both, after seven good hours. We sat up and looked newly each at the other, as though in that good lightness we saw each other anew; and there was fresh joy in it. She came into mine arms and kissed and needed to be kissed; and we had our need, both, and were the more hungry of one another for the having.

Then Mine Own made our breakfast. The water fizzed very strong and surprising, as it did ever in that place—like a pot beginning to sing upon a hearth—though there was no hearth save the hidden hotness of the Earth. We ate and drank and were utter happy, talking upon this and that. The Maid looked about as she ate, her eyes forever travelling to the wonders and newness; but I looked near, lest any danger be anigh.

In a while she drew my gaze to the Mountains through which the Gorge came. Now, in ease, I perceived with her how monstrous they were—like an endless wall, going upward until it was lost out of the lightness of that Country into the dark Night of the Upper World, gone beyond thought. On my outward way I had had but a vague knowing; now, being rested and having the Maid to speak with, I noted odd matters more. I tell you this little thing that you may see how restfulness lay upon me, by compare with my outward going.

We were slow with talk and eating, conscious of leaning to slackness; therefore we hurried somewhat, came down from the rock, and went forward again at a good pace.

After we had gone a while, we began to hear, far off, the hissing of the steam and the noise of upward burstings of boiling water. The sound was strange—at times like kettles upon kettles, at times like some great breath escaping—yet it troubled me less than the Maid, for I had heard it before. I assured her; and she came nigh to me; and so we entered presently into the steam.

We went more than three hours within that thickness. I had the Maid upon my back that I might go first, lest she step into a boiling pool in the mazingness of the steam that lay everywhere. I guided myself somewhat by the shore of the Sea upon our left; though we could see neither sea nor aught else, save when we went so close that the wetness was at our feet.

Truly the Sea seemed to boil in parts, and there were hot pools in all places, so that it was constant puzzle whether we went by a great pool or by the true sea. Therefore we went utter wary.

About us, from every part, came strange burstings and shriekings and whistlings where the waters broke upward from the deep world. Odd whiles the sounds were as of great monsters; and the earth shook under us. Other whiles there was a hush, and only the steam about us, and somewhere uncertain a low piping from some steam-cranny—thin, lonely, and mournful to hear.

When it was somewhere nigh upon the fourth hour, we came out of the thick steam. The pipings and roarings fell to our rearward; the steam thinned to a mist; the noises seemed far off. Presently we were clear into the air of that Country.

Now the Maid perceived the trees, in great forests upon our right hand, while the shore of the Sea ran upon our left. She was utter in wonder of the trees, needing to pluck branches, smell them, and look at each leaf. The scent of their green life—sharp, resinous, and clean—stirred her; for never in all her life had she seen such a matter as those great trees. Yet even that stirred her with vague memories, no more than dreams; and if you think but a moment, you will perceive how it was with her, and how a small thing can wake a corner of the heart.

When the sixth hour was full come, we halted in a wise place and took our tablets and water. After, the Maid bade me take her to a warm pool near by. She asked that I turn from her, yet be anigh as her Protector, as I was ever. So she washed and made herself happy with that sweet cleanness proper to her; and the warm air carried up a faint mineral smell from the pool, like wet stone heated. After she was done, she watched while I did mine, and helped me in all matters she was able; and truly I was glad that she had delight to attend upon me, treating me mother-wise, yet with her maid-heart not hid, as you have perceived.

So we went onward again, talking upon this thing and that; I watchful, bidding her keep her eyes wary, yet not be in unease.

When we had gone thus for seven good hours, we were come nigh opposed to the bright-burning fire-hill that lay offward in the Sea, which had given me a warm light when I slept in the tree on my outward way. We had been past seven hours from the place where the steam was, though I had gone that space faster alone; but I could not set so great a pace to the Maid, save odd whiles. Keep this always in mind, that you understand why parts of the homeward journey were long, by compare with mine outward going.

I set our hour for food because I saw we came nigh the place where the tree stood; and I knew the Maid would like to eat there, where I had slept. I took her to the tree, and when I told her, she begged that I indulge her and that we go upward to the branch where I had lain, and there eat our tablets.

I was willing. There was no danger in the climb; and I went alway below her, to be surety for her safeness. We came up to the great branch. She chose how we should sit, and I showed just where I had lain. She looked very close and saw how the hardness of mine armor had marked the bark under my weight. Then she sat upon that branch and ate and drank, looking outward at the light from the fire-hill, husht and thinking; and I did not disturb her.

When she was done she climbed down a little and kissed the place where I had lain. Then a thought came to her; she drew her knife and cut out a small piece of bark, and put it into her breast for a keepsake; and so seemed contented.

After we were down upon the earth again, I told her of the great beast. She cried out, and showed me the mark yet upon the ground where its belly had brushed as it ran, and the broken places of the foot-marks. By this she saw how great it had been—though it was a little thing beside the Slug—being horned and hard-skinned.

Her keen observings brought it home to me that but seventeen days had passed since I went onward from this place; and it seemed strange and scarce credible, for it felt as a great time—because it had been marked by stress of mind and great happenings, as you will agree. Yet also those times I call days did hold oft the hours of two days, and mayhap three, as you remember.

We went onward, and after the Maid had walked twelve hours I made to carry her, as ever—though she had walked thirteen that time. She said she would go upon her own feet through the next six hours, to ease me; but I knew she would be spent in a day or two if she did so, and we would make better speed if I kept to my way: that she should walk twelve hours of every journey, and afterward come into mine arms. For she was bred less hardy than I, and was yet weakened, I thought, by that dreadful month of lonesomeness and escaping before I came to succor her.

As I carried her, she spoke of her wonder at me—that I was so hard of body and set in determination. Truly I was exceeding strong, and my will likewise; else I think I had never borne to come to Mine Own through so much desolation. I smiled very happy, loving that I was strong, and in delight that Mine Own took gladness in it; for you will mind how you were in the love-days, and understand my human pride.

The Maid nestled unto me as she talked—somewise as a child to the mother, yet also as a Maid that loveth to be nigh her Man. I lifted her nearer to my lips; but she refused to kiss me, being a sweet impertinence in mine arms. Yet when I made to lower her to the easier carry, she slipped her pretty face snug under my chin and kissed me there, after her own fashion; and then was content to be as usual.

As I set her comfortable, it seemed to me she was tender. Sudden it came that mayhap my armor was hard and painful to her. I asked; she saw I would not be put off, and told me true. I was utter angered with myself—and somewise with her also, that she had not waked my unthinkingness to it.

I set her instant to the earth and made her bare her shoulders. Truly they were much bruised where she had lain so oft in mine arms against the hardness of the metal. Anger rose so that I near shook her; and she, seeing it, and being nigh to be shaken, put up her lips sudden, with a strange naughtiness, to tempt me from mine anger—an anger that she half liked. In verity I near slapped her upon her pretty shoulders, but that she ceased from tempting; instead she turned her shoulders to me, even as a child, that I might button her garment.

When I had buttoned her, she came round and closed her hand into a little fist—so small beside mine—and slipped it into my palm. I let it bide there, quiet, and made not to close upon it, as I was wont. She moved her hand within mine, to make me take notice, to grasp her; but I stayed stern, neither putting it away nor holding it—only letting it bide. Yet even then I was stirred in a secret place by her pretty ways.

After a little she drew her hand away, and had daring to be cold to me. My anger renewed quaintly, and I thought she well needed to be whipped. She made a naughty, foolish impudence upon what I said; and I told her she needed such as would teach her manners. Yet, as you will conceive, inwardly all that while her naughtiness did not stir me to proper anger, but made me masterful, wanting that she should know I was her Master, and in the same moment touched me deep, where love dwelleth and troubleth the heart with strange actings.

She asked me, in a saucy fashion meant to anger, what I meant she needed. I said she went the way to earn that she be flogged like any boy; and I meant it actual then, which amazeth me now. Yet even as I said it I was stirred by her beloved quaintness, so dainty even when she would anger me.

Lo, when I told her she went to earn a sharp reproof, she turned quick and came close, all tender and small, needing to be nigh. She slipped both her closed hands—two little fists—into my one hand. Then, because I could be no more stern with Mine Own, I put mine arm about her; and she nestled so that all my being wanted to be a shield about her.
