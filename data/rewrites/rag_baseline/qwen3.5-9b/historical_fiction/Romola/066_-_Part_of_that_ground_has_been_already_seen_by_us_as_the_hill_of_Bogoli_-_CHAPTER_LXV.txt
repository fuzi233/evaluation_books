The Piazza della Signoria, seven days later on the seventh of April, staged a spectacle that dwarfed the infamous Bonfire of the Vanities in sheer audacity. A crowd larger than any previous gathering, even surpassing the throngs of San Giovanni, squeezed into every available inch of the vast square. By midday, the space was a chaotic mosaic of desperate humanity clinging to the city's architecture: men wedged themselves onto iron rods jutting sharply from walls, draped their limbs over slender pillars, balanced precariously atop the necks of rough-hewn statues guarding noble entrances, and found purchase on mere palm-widths of architrave or rusticated stone. They gripped iron rings and staples driven into the masonry, their bodies contorted, their flesh abraded, yet seemingly oblivious to the pain. They had come to witness a Miracle. While mankind generally hears of miracles with skeptical detachment, the Florentines were prepared to see one unfold before their eyes. At minimum, they would observe the entry; if the monk emerged unscathed, the proof would be absolute. If he failed, the inference would be equally damning: he had been consumed in the middle.

There was no ambiguity regarding the mechanics of the event. Before them lay a long platform, eight feet wide and twenty yards long, constructed against the marble terrace of the Old Palace near the stone lion, the Marzocco. Its gaze seemed to frown upon the terrifying grove of fuel stacked diagonally across the square: a foundation of dry oak branches, topped with crackling thorns, and finally, the well-anointed tow and rags renowned for producing fierce flames in Florentine illuminations. Flanking this stage were three distinct bodies of armed force. Five hundred mercenaries of the Signoria stood guard before the palace; five hundred *Compagnacci* commanded by Dolfo Spini occupied the opposite side of the square; and three hundred citizen-soldiers led by Marco Salviati, a trusted ally of Savonarola, held position before Orgagna's Loggia, where the Franciscans and Dominicans awaited placement alongside their champions.

Significant capital and labor had been expended to orchestrate this drama, involving high dignitaries. The stakes were clear: if the two monks were simply incinerated, God would have spoken plainly, declaring Fra Girolamo not His prophet. Time was short. Half the monks were already in position, filling the section of the Loggia facing the palace with grey habits, while the opposing half remained empty save for a small altar. The Franciscans had entered in silence. From the other side of the piazza, however, a deafening chant rose from two hundred voices, signaling the arrival of the Dominicans. The repetitive invocation—"Let God arise, and let His enemies be scattered"—was grating to the impartial ear, sounding less like piety and more like a calculated attempt to project confidence and induce panic. Leading this procession was Fra Domenico, clad in a blaze of flame-colored velvet, holding the cross. His simple mind was genuinely exalted by faith, intent on entering the flames for the glory of God and Fra Girolamo. Following him walked Savonarola in white priestly vestments, carrying a vessel containing the consecrated Host. Both men chanted loudly, projecting an image of unshakeable firmness. As the crowd's gaze shifted anxiously, curiously, or malignantly toward them from the moment of entry until Savonarola ascended the Loggia steps to deposit the Sacrament, a flash of intensified energy lit his countenance, responding to the scrutiny.

Human nature dictates that the false pretense we dread in private solitude often becomes our instinctive posture under the pressure of expectant eyes. The stimulus of the multitude transforms hesitation into performance. For Savonarola, accustomed to wielding immense influence, this need to dominate had become imperative, especially as life's complexities fused his selfhood with a non-selfish purpose. On the day of the Trial by Fire, the duplicity inherent in public careers—whether priest, orator, or statesman—reached its zenith in his consciousness. He was not merely struggling against impending martyrdom; he was fighting against impending ruin.

Consequently, he projected total confidence while a leaden weight of foreboding crushed his heart. This anxiety stemmed not only from the probable outcome of the trial but from a separate catastrophe already unfolding. Two evenings prior, news had reached Florence that the courier of the Ten had been arrested and stripped of his dispatches. Savonarola's letter was now in the hands of the Duke of Milan and imminent delivery to the Pope—a development that would heighten rage and justify extreme measures. Watching this turn of events from a window of the Old Palace was Tito Melema. His satisfaction was devoid of malice; it was the quiet gratification of a strategist who had won a game requiring hypothetical skill rather than brute force. To Tito, the bundle of desires and contrivances known as human nature, molded into the form of a plain-faced friar and a man of absurd superstition, was not a pathetic object but a puzzle solved. Yet, Fra Girolamo was an immensely clever operator, blending fabrication with remarkably acute notions of governance. Tito harbored no spite for him; rather, Fra Girolamo's existence had been highly convenient, providing Tito with a rung on the ladder from which he intended to leap to a smoother, more advantageous footing. With preparations complete, Tito awaited the setting sun to execute his departure from Florence. He had been so industrious that he now possessed the leisure to enjoy today's comedy, a feat the thick-headed Dolfo Spini could never have achieved without his intervention.

The chanting swelled to a roar, amplified by the *Piagnoni*, who carried their red crosses as badges of office. Most chanted the prayer for the confusion of God's enemies, expecting an answer mediated through a figure more signal than Fra Domenico. The good friar, in his flamboyant cope, knelt before the altar where the Sacrament rested, awaiting his summons. Across the Loggia, the atmosphere was starkly different: silence and grey. The Franciscans possessed two champions: Fra Giuliano paired with Fra Domenico, while the original champion, Fra Francesco, reserved his challenge solely for Savonarola.

"Surely," thought the men perched uneasily on rods and pillars, "all must be ready now. This chanting must cease, and we shall see the Frati move toward the platform."

Yet the monks did not stir. Pale Franciscan faces peered over the boarding at the flame-colored cope with suspicion. It bore an evil aspect; perhaps it was enchanted, capable of working a false miracle by magic. One could emerge whole from the fire, and yet it might be the work of the Devil.

Movement began between the Loggia and the marble terrace of the Palazzo. The roaring chanting softened slightly as distant observers watched more intently. However, this movement proved to be an obstacle rather than a commencement. Dignitaries appointed to moderate the affair entered and exited the Palace, engaging in heated debate with the Franciscans. Eventually, it became clear that Fra Domenico, conspicuous in his bright robes, was being summoned toward the Palace. Presumably, the fire had been kindled, though visibility was poor, and the miracle was about to commence.

This was a deception. The flame-colored cope vanished inside the Palace, followed shortly by another Dominican being withdrawn. For a prolonged interval, the scene reverted to its former state: the tedious chanting, devoid of the miraculous, and Fra Girolamo in his white vestment standing motionless. Finally, a shift occurred. Fra Domenico emerged from the Palace, returned to his brethren, and had completely exchanged garments with a fellow monk. He was flanked on each side by a Franciscan guard, lest proximity to Savonarola allow him to be enchanted anew.

"Aha," reasoned the distant spectators, their discomfort momentarily forgotten, "Fra Domenico is not entering the fire. It is Fra Girolamo who offers himself after all. We shall see him move presently, and if he emerges unburnt, we shall have a magnificent view!"

But Fra Girolamo did not move beyond the ordinary gestures accompanying speech. His words were bold, firm, and perhaps ironically admonitory, reminiscent of Elijah confronting the priests of Baal, demanding the cessation of trivial delays. Yet speech is the most irritating form of argument for those who are out of hearing range, cramped in their limbs, and hollow in their stomachs. Why argue? If the miracle did not proceed, the blame fell squarely on Fra Girolamo, who could resolve all difficulties by offering himself to the fire now that it was ready, just as he had been eager to do when no fuel was visible.

Further movement, further debate. The afternoon slipped away, accelerated by gathering clouds that altered the light and sent a chilling wind through the spectators, who were starving both physically and spiritually.

Now, the focus shifted. Fra Domenico insisted on carrying a crucifix into the fire, an act forbidden by the Franciscans who deemed it a profanation. After minor resistance, Savonarola conceded, gaining the strategic advantage of appearing compliant. Immediately, however, he placed the vessel containing the consecrated Host into Fra Domenico's hands. The notion that the presence of the sacred mystery might avert the ordinary effects of fire in the worst-case scenario lingered in his mind, but his primary calculation was more pragmatic. Accepting the Host, he spoke quietly, as if fulfilling a predetermined script: "Since they are unwilling that you should enter with the crucifix, my brother, enter simply with the Sacrament."
