As soon as her reply was sent, Mrs. Dashwood delighted in sharing the news with her son-in-law and daughter-in-law: she had secured a new residence and would not trouble them for long until her new home was ready. They listened with astonishment. Mrs. John Dashwood remained silent, while her husband politely expressed hope that she would not settle too far from Norland. With great satisfaction, she replied that she was moving to Devonshire.

Upon hearing this, Edward turned quickly toward her. His voice, filled with surprise and deep concern, needed no translation for her ears as he asked, "Devonshire! Are you truly going there? That is so distant! And to which part?" She explained the location: just four miles north of Exeter. "It is merely a cottage," she continued, "yet I hope to welcome many friends there. A couple of rooms can easily be added, and if my friends find no hardship traveling so far to visit me, I am certain I shall find none in hosting them." She ended with a warm invitation for both Mr. and Mrs. John Dashwood to come stay at Barton, extending one even more tender specifically to Edward. Although her recent talk with her daughter-in-law had resolved her to leave Norland as soon as necessary, it did not alter her feelings regarding the matter that mattered most. Separating Edward and Elinor was never her intention; instead, she wanted to demonstrate to Mrs. John Dashwood, through this pointed invitation to her brother, how completely she ignored her disapproval of the marriage.

Mr. John Dashwood repeatedly told his mother how deeply sorry he was that she had chosen a house so remote that it prevented him from assisting with the move of her belongings. He genuinely felt a pang of conscience about the situation, for the very effort he had promised his father to undertake was now rendered impossible by these arrangements. Their furniture was transported entirely by water, consisting mainly of household linens, silverware, china, books, and a fine piano belonging to Marianne. As the packages departed, Mrs. John Dashwood sighed; she could not suppress the thought that it was unfair for Mrs. Dashwood's modest income to allow her to keep such elegant items compared to their own wealth.

Mrs. Dashwood rented the cottage for a year; it was fully furnished, allowing her immediate occupancy. There was no friction between the parties regarding the terms. She simply awaited the clearing out of Norland and the organization of her new household before departing for the west. This process, undertaken with remarkable speed given her intense interest, was completed swiftly. The carriage her husband had left her had been sold shortly after his passing, and seeing an opportunity to dispose of it, she agreed to sell it as well, following the earnest counsel of her eldest daughter. Had she acted solely on her own desires for her children's comfort, she would have retained it, but Elinor's prudence prevailed. Furthermore, Elinor wisely restricted their staff to three people: two maids and a man, whom they quickly hired from among those who had previously served at Norland.

The man and one maid were dispatched immediately to Devonshire to prepare the house for their mistress's arrival. Since Lady Middleton was entirely unfamiliar to Mrs. Dashwood, she preferred traveling straight to the cottage rather than stopping as a guest at Barton Park. Relying unquestioningly on Sir John's description of the property, she felt no urge to inspect it personally until she stepped inside as its owner. Her eagerness to leave Norland was undiminished by the visible relief shown by her daughter-in-law at the prospect of the move—a relief that was only weakly masked behind a chilly suggestion that Mrs. Dashwood delay her departure.

Now was the moment when her son-in-law's promise to his father could rightfully be honored. Having neglected to fulfill it upon first taking up residence at the estate, their imminent departure seemed the perfect occasion to make good on it. However, Mrs. Dashwood soon abandoned any hope of receiving such aid. From the general tenor of his conversations, she became convinced that his assistance would extend no further than providing maintenance for six months at Norland. He spoke constantly of rising household expenses and the endless demands placed on the purse of any man of standing, suggesting he stood in need of funds himself rather than having designs on giving them away.

Within a few weeks of receiving Sir John Middleton's first letter, everything was arranged for their new life, enabling Mrs. Dashwood and her daughters to begin their journey. Many tears were shed during their final farewells to a place so dearly loved. "Dear, dear Norland!" cried Marianne as she wandered alone before the house on the last evening of their stay. "When shall I cease to regret you? When shall I learn to feel at home elsewhere? Oh, happy house, could you know the suffering I endure now viewing you from this spot, from where I may never look upon you again! And you, ye familiar trees! Yet you will remain unchanged. No leaf will wither because we depart, nor any branch grow still though we can no longer watch them. You will persist, unaware of the joy or sorrow you inspire, indifferent to the changes in those who once walked beneath your shade! But who will remain to cherish you?"
