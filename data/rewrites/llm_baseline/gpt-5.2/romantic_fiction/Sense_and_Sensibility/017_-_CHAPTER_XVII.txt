Mrs. Dashwood was startled only for the space of a breath when Edward Ferrars was shown into the little parlour at Barton; astonishment melted at once into that frank, maternal joy which made every guest feel, not admitted, but claimed. The room itself seemed to soften to her welcome: the fire spoke in small, sap-sweet cracklings; the kettle gave its thin, companionable song; and beyond the windows the late air moved through bare branches with a hush like distant surf. Whatever shyness or chill he had brought with him—whatever stiffness learned in richer rooms and under a colder roof—could not stand long against such warmth.

Elinor, watching as she always watched—quietly, with a patience that took note of everything—had the comfort of seeing him become, by degrees, more like the Edward she had known before: his face less guarded, his attention more natural, his kindness less an effort. His affections, which had seemed of late to sleep under some weight, revived towards them all; he asked after their comfort, admired the prospect, praised with real feeling the plain convenience of their home. Yet his praise had not the easy gaiety of content. He spoke, he smiled when he must, but his spirits did not rise with the fire.

They all perceived it. Mrs. Dashwood, who could never see a young man depressed without instantly imagining the parent who had done it, sat down to dinner with indignation already prepared against selfish mothers. The meal, though simple, had in it that domestic care which turns necessity into pleasure: broth clear as amber, bread that still carried the nutty scent of its crust, a joint carved with tenderness as much as skill, and, after, a small dish of preserved fruit whose clove and sugar seemed to gather summer into one spoonful. Even Edward’s appetite, which politeness could not entirely counterfeit, appeared restrained; he tasted rather than ate, as if his mind stood between him and the plate.

When dinner was over, and they had drawn round the fire while the servants cleared away and the last scents of roast and spice slowly cooled into the air, Mrs. Dashwood leaned forward with that affectionate frankness which made questions feel like kindness.

“What are Mrs. Ferrars’s views for you at present, Edward?” said she. “Are you still to be a great orator in spite of yourself?”

“No,” he answered, and the word came out like a relief. “I hope my mother is now convinced that I have no more talents than inclination for a public life!”

“But how is your fame to be established? for famous you must be to satisfy all your family; and with no inclination for expense, no affection for strangers, no profession, and no assurance, you may find it a difficult matter.”

“I shall not attempt it. I have no wish to be distinguished; and have every reason to hope I never shall. Thank Heaven! I cannot be forced into genius and eloquence.”

“You have no ambition, I well know. Your wishes are all moderate.”

“As moderate as those of the rest of the world, I believe. I wish as well as every body else to be perfectly happy; but, like every body else, it must be in my own way. Greatness will not make me so.”

“Strange that it would!” cried Marianne, whose whole being, even at rest, seemed tuned like an instrument ready to answer any touch. “What have wealth or grandeur to do with happiness?”

“Grandeur has but little,” said Elinor, in her composed manner; “but wealth has much to do with it.”

“Elinor, for shame!” Marianne’s colour rose as quickly as flame catches kindling. “Money can only give happiness where there is nothing else to give it. Beyond a competence, it can afford no real satisfaction, as far as mere self is concerned.”

“Perhaps,” said Elinor, smiling, “we may come to the same point. Your competence and my wealth are very much alike, I dare say; and without them, as the world goes now, we shall both agree that every kind of external comfort must be wanting. Your ideas are only more noble than mine. Come, what is your competence?”

“About eighteen hundred or two thousand a year; not more than that.”

Elinor could not help laughing; it was not unkindness, only the clear sight of a sister’s splendid imagination setting up housekeeping as easily as it composed a sonnet. “Two thousand a year! One is my wealth! I guessed how it would end.”

“And yet two thousand a-year is a very moderate income,” Marianne insisted, serious as if stating a household account. “A family cannot well be maintained on a smaller. I am sure I am not extravagant in my demands. A proper establishment of servants, a carriage, perhaps two, and hunters, cannot be supported on less.”

Elinor smiled again, for the picture was drawn with such exactness that one might almost hear the wheels of those future carriages on an unseen gravel drive, and the impatient pawing of the hunters in their stalls at Combe Magna.

“Hunt—ers!” Edward repeated, and there was in his tone both amusement and honest perplexity. “But why must you have hunters? Every body does not hunt.”

Marianne coloured, and her reply came with that proud quickness which betrayed how much she took the customs of her own imagined circle for the laws of nature. “But most people do.”

Margaret, who had been listening with the eager eye of childhood that turns talk into game, struck out a thought with the boldness of a wish. “I wish that somebody would give us all a large fortune apiece!”

“Oh that they would!” cried Marianne; her eyes brightened, her cheeks glowed, and for a moment the room seemed fuller of light, as if the very fire leapt in agreement with so intoxicating an idea.

“We are all unanimous in that wish, I suppose,” said Elinor, “in spite of the insufficiency of wealth.”

“Oh dear!” Margaret cried, already half-drunk on possibility. “How happy I should be! I wonder what I should do with it!”

Marianne looked as though she had never doubted what she would do: purchase beauty, reward genius, and make the world echo with music.

“I should be puzzled to spend so large a fortune myself,” said Mrs. Dashwood, with a laugh that carried no bitterness, “if my children were all to be rich without my help.”

“You must begin your improvements on this house,” observed Elinor; “and your difficulties will soon vanish.”

“What magnificent orders would travel from this family to London,” said Edward, and for a moment he rallied, his fancy warming like hands held to flame. “What a happy day for booksellers, music-sellers, and print-shops! You, Miss Dashwood, would give a general commission for every new print of merit to be sent you—and as for Marianne, I know her greatness of soul, there would not be music enough in London to content her. And books!—Thomson, Cowper, Scott—she would buy them all over and over again: she would buy up every copy, I believe, to prevent their falling into unworthy hands; and she would have every book that tells her how to admire an old twisted tree. Should not you, Marianne? Forgive me, if I am very saucy. But I was willing to show you that I had not forgot our old disputes.”

“I love to be reminded of the past, Edward—whether it be melancholy or gay, I love to recall it—and you will never offend me by talking of former times.” Her voice, softened by sincerity, seemed to settle the room into an attentive quiet; even the wind outside sounded, for a moment, like it was listening. “You are very right in supposing how my money would be spent—some of it, at least—my loose cash would certainly be employed in improving my collection of music and books.”

“And the bulk of your fortune would be laid out in annuities on the authors or their heirs.”

“No, Edward, I should have something else to do with it.”

“Perhaps, then, you would bestow it as a reward on that person who wrote the ablest defence of your favourite maxim, that no one can ever be in love more than once in their life—your opinion on that point is unchanged, I presume?”

“Undoubtedly. At my time of life opinions are tolerably fixed. It is not likely that I should now see or hear any thing to change them.”

“Marianne is as steadfast as ever, you see,” said Elinor; and though she spoke lightly, there was in her look a meaning that did not ask to be read aloud. “She is not at all altered.”

“She is only grown a little more grave than she was.”

“Nay, Edward,” said Marianne, turning upon him with a kind of fearless justice, “you need not reproach me. You are not very gay yourself.”

“Why should you think so!” he replied, and a sigh escaped him, as involuntary as steam from a kettle-lid. “But gaiety never was a part of my character.”

“Nor do I think it a part of Marianne’s,” said Elinor. “I should hardly call her a lively girl—she is very earnest, very eager in all she does—sometimes talks a great deal and always with animation—but she is not often really merry.”

“I believe you are right,” he returned; “and yet I have always set her down as a lively girl.”

“I have frequently detected myself in such kind of mistakes,” said Elinor, and her tone, though gentle, carried the precision of an inward habit—of weighing, naming, and keeping truth clean. “In a total misapprehension of character in some point or other: fancying people so much more gay or grave, or ingenious or stupid than they really are, and I can hardly tell why or in what the deception originated. Sometimes one is guided by what they say of themselves, and very frequently by what other people say of them, without giving oneself time to deliberate and judge.”

“But I thought it was right, Elinor,” said Marianne, her irony as bright as a sharp knife, “to be guided wholly by the opinion of other people. I thought our judgments were given us merely to be subservient to those of neighbours. This has always been your doctrine, I am sure.”

“No, Marianne, never. My doctrine has never aimed at the subjection of the understanding. All I have ever attempted to influence has been the behaviour. You must not confound my meaning. I am guilty, I confess, of having often wished you to treat our acquaintance in general with greater attention; but when have I advised you to adopt their sentiments or to conform to their judgment in serious matters?”

“You have not been able to bring your sister over to your plan of general civility,” said Edward to Elinor. “Do you gain no ground?”

“Quite the contrary,” replied Elinor, and her look at Marianne was expressive enough to make Margaret giggle and then check herself, as if the sound might be too loud.

“My judgment,” he returned, “is all on your side of the question; but I am afraid my practice is much more on your sister’s. I never wish to offend, but I am so foolishly shy, that I often seem negligent, when I am only kept back by my natural awkwardness. I have frequently thought that I must have been intended by nature to be fond of low company, I am so little at my ease among strangers of gentility!”

“Marianne has not shyness to excuse any inattention of hers,” said Elinor.

“She knows her own worth too well for false shame,” replied Edward. “Shyness is only the effect of a sense of inferiority in some way or other. If I could persuade myself that my manners were perfectly easy and graceful, I should not be shy.”

“But you would still be reserved,” said Marianne; and the words fell into the room with a weight quite disproportionate to their softness.

Edward started, as if she had touched a bruise. “Reserved! Am I reserved, Marianne?”

“Yes, very.”

“I do not understand you,” he replied, colouring. “Reserved!—how, in what manner? What am I to tell you? What can you suppose?”

Elinor looked surprised at his emotion; and, anxious to draw the sting from what had been said, tried to laugh it away. “Do not you know my sister well enough to understand what she means? Do not you know she calls every one reserved who does not talk as fast, and admire what she admires as rapturously as herself?”

Edward made no answer. The brief flicker of ease went out; his gravity returned upon him in its fullest extent, as surely as embers sink when no one stirs the fire. He sat for some time silent and dull, while the little domestic sounds—the settling of the hearth, the faint clink of a spoon forgotten on a saucer, the wind’s patient music outside—seemed to fill the spaces his words would not.
