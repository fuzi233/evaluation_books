Imagine a rambling, patchy house, the finest parts built of gray stone and red tile, with a round tower jutting from one corner. Its conical roof, dark and mellow, is topped by a weather-vane that makes a pleasing silhouette against the summer greens or the winter snows. The grounds are shaded by spreading trees; on one side, a great tree flourishes near broken banks where Scotch firs stand with naked roots, and beyond lies a rookery. On the other, a pool hangs beneath overhanging bushes, where waterfowl flutter and scream. Surrounding it all is a vast meadow, effectively a park, bordered by an old plantation and guarded by stone lodges that resemble tiny prisons. Beyond the gate, the countryside, once purely rural and lovely, is now blackened by coal mines, chiefly inhabited by men and brothers wearing candles in their hats—their diabolish complexions making them peculiarly suspect in the eyes of the children at Gadsmere. These are Mrs. Glasher's four beautiful children, who have lived there for about three years.

Now, in November, with flower beds empty, trees leafless, and the pool shivering blackly, the place seems somberly in keeping with the black roads and black mounds that seem to mourn the district—except when the children play on the gravel with the dogs for company. Yet Mrs. Glasher, under her present circumstances, likes Gadsmere as well as any other abode. The complete seclusion, secured by the land's unattractiveness, suits her perfectly. When she drives her two ponies with a waggonet full of children, she meets no gentry in carriages, only men of business in gigs. At church, there are no eyes she needs to avoid; the curate and his wife are either ignorant of anything to her disadvantage or simply ignore it. To them, she is merely a widow lady, the tenant of Gadsmere; and the name of Grandcourt holds little interest in that district compared to the names of Fletcher and Gawcome, the lessees of the collieries.

It has been ten years since the elopement of an Irish officer's beautiful wife with young Grandcourt, followed by a duel where bullets wounded only the air. Those who remember the affair now wonder what became of Mrs. Glasher, whose beauty and brilliance made her conspicuous in foreign lands where she was known to live with young Grandcourt. That he should have disentangled himself seemed natural and desirable. As for her, it was thought a woman who had forsaken her child along with her husband had probably sunk lower. Grandcourt, of course, grew weary of her. Given to the pursuit of women, a man in his position would now desire a suitable marriage with the fair daughter of a noble house. No one spoke of Mrs. Glasher anymore, any more than they spoke of the victim in a manslaughter trial a decade prior; she was a lost vessel after whom no expedition would be sent, while Grandcourt was seen in harbor with his colors flying, registered as seaworthy as ever.

Yet, in truth, Grandcourt had never truly disentangled himself from Mrs. Glasher. His passion for her had been the strongest and most lasting he had ever known. Though it was now as dead as the music of a cracked flute, it left a certain dull disposition. Upon the death of her husband three years prior, this prompted in him a vacillating notion of marrying her, in accordance with understandings often expressed during his first ardor. At that early time, Grandcourt would have willingly paid for the freedom won by a divorce, but the husband refused, unwilling to be remarried himself or to have his domestic habits printed in evidence.

The altered poise of the years had changed Mrs. Glasher completely. Initially, she was comparatively careless about marriage. Escaping a disagreeable husband to find bliss with a lover who completely fascinated her—young, handsome, amorous, and living in the best style—was enough. She was an impassioned, vivacious woman, fond of adoration and exasperated by five years of marital rudeness. The sense of release was so strong it stilled anxiety for more than she actually enjoyed. An equivocal position mattered little to her then; she envied no dull, disregarded wife. The only spot spoiling her vision of her new pleasant world was the knowledge that she had left her three-year-old boy, who died two years later. His first tones saying "mamma" retained a difference from those of the children who came after. But now, years had brought many changes beyond the contour of her cheek and throat. That Grandcourt should marry her had become her dominant desire. The equivocal position she had not minded for herself now told upon her through her children, whom she loved with a devotion charged with the added passion of atonement. She had no repentance except in this direction. If Grandcourt married her, the children would be none the worse off; they would see their mother in a dignified position, face no disadvantage, and her son could be made his father's heir. It was this yearning that gave supreme importance to Grandcourt's feeling for her. Her love for him had long resolved itself into anxiety that he should give her the unique, permanent claim of a wife. She expected no other happiness in marriage than the satisfaction of her maternal love and pride—including her pride for herself in the presence of her children. For this result, she was prepared to endure anything quietly in marriage with tragic firmness. She possessed the acuteness to cherish Grandcourt's flickering purpose negatively, avoiding passionate appeals and scene-making. In her, as in everyone else who wanted something from him, his incalculable turns and tendency to harden under beseeching had created a reasonable dread—a slow discovery born of no presentiment in the bearing of a youthful lover with a fine line of face and the softest manners. But reticence had necessarily cost something to this impassioned woman, and she was the bitterer for it. There is no quailing, even that forced on the helpless and injured, which lacks an ugly obverse: the withheld sting was gathering venom.

She was absolutely dependent on Grandcourt. Though he had always been liberal with expenses, he kept everything voluntary on his part. With the goal of marriage before her, she would ask for nothing less. He had said he would never settle anything except by will. When thinking of alternatives, it often occurred to her that even if she did not become Grandcourt's wife, he might never have a son with a legitimate claim, and the end might be that her son was made heir to the best part of his estates. No son at that early age could promise to have more of his father's physique. Yet her becoming Grandcourt's wife was so far from an extravagant notion that even Lush had entertained it, saying he would as soon bet on it as any other likelihood regarding his familiar companion. Lush, indeed, upon inferring that Grandcourt had a preconception of using his residence at Diplow to win Miss Arrowpoint, thought it well to fan that project, taking it as a tacit renunciation of the marriage with Mrs. Glasher, which had long been a mark for the hovering and wheeling of Grandcourt's caprice. But both prospects were negatived by Gwendolen's appearance on the scene. It was natural enough for Mrs. Glasher to enter with eagerness into Lush's plan of hindering that new danger by setting up a barrier in the mind of the girl being sought as a bride. She entered into it with an eagerness containing passion as well as purpose, some of the stored-up venom delivering itself in that way.

Afterward, she heard from Lush of Gwendolen's departure and the probability that all danger from her was gone, but there had been no letter to tell her the danger had returned and become a certainty. Since then, she had written to Grandcourt as she did habitually, and he had been longer than usual in answering. She inferred he might intend coming to Gadsmere at the time he was actually on his way, and she was not without hope—what construction of another's mind is not strong wishing equal to?—that a certain sickening from that frustrated courtship might dispose him to slip more easily into the old track of intention.

Grandcourt had two grave purposes in coming to Gadsmere: to convey the news of his approaching marriage in person, to make this first difficulty final; and to obtain from Lydia his mother's diamonds, which he had long ago confided to her and wished her to wear. Her person suited diamonds, making them appear worth the money given for them. These particular diamonds were not mountains of light—mere peas and haricots for the ears, neck, and hair—but they were worth thousands, and Grandcourt necessarily wished to have them for his wife. Formerly, when he asked Lydia to put them into his keeping again, simply on the ground that they would be safer and ought to be deposited at the bank, she had quietly but absolutely refused, declaring they were quite safe. Finally, she had said, "If you ever marry another woman I will give them up to her: are you going to marry another woman?" At that time, Grandcourt had no motive urging him to persist, and he possessed a grace in him: the disposition to exercise power either by cowing or disappointing others, or exciting in them a rage they dared not express—a disposition active in him as other propensities became languid—had always been in abeyance before Lydia. A severe interpreter might say that the mere facts of their relation, the melancholy position of this woman who depended on his will, made a standing banquet for his delight in dominating. But there was something else in his forbearance toward her: the surviving though metamorphosed effect of the power she had had over him. It was this effect, the fitful dull lapse toward solicitations that once had the zest now missing from life, which had again and again inclined him to espouse a familiar past rather than rouse himself to the expectation of novelty. But now novelty had taken hold of him and urged him to make the most of it.

Mrs. Glasher was seated in the pleasant room where she habitually passed her mornings with her children round her. It had a square projecting window looking out onto broad gravel and grass sloping toward a little brook entering the pool. The top of a low black cabinet, the old oak table, and the chairs in tawny leather were littered with the children's toys, books, and garden garments, at which a maternal lady in pastel looked down from the walls with smiling indulgence. The children were all there. The three girls, seated round their mother near the window, were miniature portraits of her—dark-eyed, delicate-featured brunettes with a rich bloom on their cheeks, their little nostrils and eyebrows singularly finished as if they were tiny women, the eldest being barely nine. The boy sat on the carpet at some distance, bending his blonde head over the animals from a Noah's ark, admonishing them separately in a voice of threatening command, and occasionally licking the spotted ones to see if the colors would hold. Josephine, the eldest, was having her French lesson; the others, with dolls on their laps, sat demurely enough for images of the Madonna. Mrs. Glasher's toilet had been made very carefully—each day now she told herself that Grandcourt might come. Her head, which, despite emaciation, possessed an ineffaceable beauty in the fine profile, crisp curves of hair, and clearly marked eyebrows, rose impressively above her bronze-colored silk and velvet, and the gold necklace which Grandcourt had first clasped round her neck years ago. Not that she derived pleasure from her toilet; her chief thought of herself seen in the glass was, "How changed!"—but such good in life as remained to her she would keep. If her chief wish were fulfilled, she could imagine herself attaining the comeliness of a matron fit for the highest rank. The little faces beside her, almost exact reductions of her own, seemed to tell of the blooming curves which had once been where now was sunken pallor. But the children kissed the pale cheeks and never found them deficient. That love was now the one end of her life.

Suddenly, Mrs. Glasher turned her head from Josephine's book and listened. "Hush, dear! I think someone is coming."

Henleigh, the boy, jumped up. "Mamma, is it the miller with my donkey?"

He received no answer and, going up to his mother's knee, repeated his question in an insistent tone. But the door opened, and the servant announced Mr. Grandcourt. Mrs. Glasher rose in some agitation. Henleigh frowned at him in disgust at his not being the miller, and the three little girls lifted their dark eyes to him timidly. None of them had any particular liking for this friend of mamma's—in fact, when he had taken Mrs. Glasher's hand and then turned to put his other hand on Henleigh's head, that energetic scion began beating the friend's arm away with his fists. The little girls submitted bashfully to be patted under the chin and kissed, but on the whole, it seemed better to send them into the garden, where they were presently dancing and chatting with the dogs on the gravel.

"How far are you come?" said Mrs. Glasher as Grandcourt put away his hat and overcoat.

"From Diplow," he answered slowly, seating himself opposite her and looking at her with an unnoting gaze which she noted.

"You are tired, then."

"No, I rested at the Junction—a hideous hole. These railway journeys are always a confounded bore. But I had coffee and smoked."

Grandcourt drew out his handkerchief, rubbed his face, and in returning it to his pocket looked at his crossed knee and blameless boot, as if any stranger were opposite him, instead of a woman quivering with a suspense which every word and look of his was to incline toward hope or dread. But he was really occupied with their interview and what it was likely to include. Imagine the difference in the rate of emotion between this woman whom the years had worn to a more conscious dependence and sharper eagerness, and this man whom they were dulling into a more neutral obstinacy.
