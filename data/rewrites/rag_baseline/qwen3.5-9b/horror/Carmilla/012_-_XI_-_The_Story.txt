"With all my heart," the General rasped, the words tearing from his throat with visible effort. After a brief, agonizing pause to rearrange the fractured pieces of his narrative, he launched into one of the strangest tales I have ever heard. "My dearest child was looking forward with immense anticipation to the visit you had so graciously arranged for her to meet your charming daughter." He offered me a bow that was simultaneously gallant and steeped in a profound melancholy. "Meanwhile, we received an invitation from my old friend, Count Carlsfeld, whose castle lies roughly six leagues beyond Karnstein. We were summoned to witness the series of festivities he hosted in honor of his illustrious guest, the Grand Duke Charles."

"Yes; and I believe they were truly splendid," my father interjected. "Princely! Yet his hospitality is quite regal. He possesses something akin to Aladdin's lamp."

"The night from which my sorrow originated was consumed by a magnificent masquerade," the General continued, his voice dropping to a reverent whisper. "The grounds were cast open, the trees draped in cascades of colored lanterns. There was a display of fireworks that Paris itself had never witnessed. And the music... music, you understand, is my greatest weakness. Such ravishing melodies! The finest instrumental ensemble the world could possibly muster, alongside the most exquisite singers gathered from the great opera houses of Europe. As you wandered through these fantastically illuminated grounds, the moonlight filtering through the chateau's long rows of windows casting a rosy glow, you would suddenly hear those ravishing voices stealing from the silence of hidden groves or rising from the water upon the lake. In that moment, looking and listening, I felt myself transported back into the romance and poetry of my earliest youth."

"When the fireworks faded and the ball began, we retreated to the noble suites of rooms thrown open to the dancers. A masked ball is a beautiful sight in itself, but I had never before beheld such a brilliant spectacle. It was an assembly of pure aristocracy; I was nearly the only 'nobody' present. My dear child looked radiant. She wore no mask. Her excitement and delight added an indescribable charm to her features, which were already lovely. I noticed a young lady dressed magnificently, yet concealed behind a mask, who appeared to be observing my ward with extraordinary intensity. I had seen her earlier in the great hall, and again, for a few moments, walking near us beneath the castle windows, similarly engrossed. Accompanying her was another lady, also masked, richly and gravely attired, possessing a stately air reminiscent of high rank, serving as her chaperone. Had the young lady not worn her veil, I could certainly have been far more certain regarding whether she was truly watching my poor darling. Now, I am fully assured that she was."

"We had moved into one of the salons. My poor child, having danced extensively, rested briefly in a chair near the entrance; I stood nearby. The two ladies I had described approached, and the younger one took the seat directly beside my ward, while her companion remained standing beside me, addressing her charge in a hushed tone. Availing herself of the anonymity granted by her mask, she turned to me, adopting the tone of an old friend and addressing me by name. This opened a conversation that piqued my curiosity immensely. She referenced numerous scenes where we had crossed paths—at Court, at distinguished households. She alluded to trivial incidents I had long ceased to recall, yet which, I discovered, had merely lain dormant in my memory, springing instantly to life at her touch.

"I grew increasingly desperate to ascertain her identity, every passing moment. She deflected my attempts to uncover her secret with remarkable adroitness and pleasantness. The knowledge she displayed regarding various passages of my life seemed almost inexplicable, and she appeared to derive a natural, almost malicious pleasure in frustrating my curiosity, watching me flounder from one conjecture to another in my eager perplexity. Meanwhile, the young lady, whom her mother occasionally addressed by the peculiar name of Millarca, engaged my ward with the same ease and grace. She introduced herself by claiming her mother was a very old acquaintance of mine. She spoke of the agreeable audacity that masks render possible; she conversed like a confidante, admiring her dress and insinuating, with charming subtlety, her admiration of her beauty. She amused her with witty critiques of the crowd flooding the ballroom and laughed at my poor child's jests. When she chose, she was incredibly witty and lively. Eventually, they became fast friends, and the young stranger lowered her mask, revealing a face of remarkable loveliness. Neither I nor my dear child had ever seen it before. Though unfamiliar to us, the features possessed such an engaging radiance that it was impossible not to feel the attraction powerfully. My poor girl fell for her instantly. I have rarely witnessed anyone so captivated at first sight, unless perhaps it was the stranger herself, who seemed to have completely lost her heart to her."

"In the interim, availing myself of the liberties of a masquerade, I directed several questions to the elder lady. 'You have utterly puzzled me,' I said, laughing. 'Is that not enough? Will you not now consent to stand on equal footing and do me the kindness of removing your mask?'

'Can any request be more unreasonable?' she replied coolly. 'To ask a lady to yield an advantage! Besides, how do you know you would recognize me? Years bring change.'

'As you see,' I answered with a bow and a rather melancholy little laugh.

'As philosophers tell us,' she countered. 'And how do you know that a glimpse of my face would assist you?'

'I should take my chances for that,' I responded. 'It is futile to try to pass yourself off as an old woman; your figure betrays you.'

'Nevertheless, years have passed since I last saw you, and rather since you saw me, for that is what concerns me. Millarca there is my daughter; I cannot therefore be considered young, even in the eyes of those whom time has taught to be indulgent. Moreover, I may not relish being compared to what you remember me to be. You possess no mask to remove. You can offer me nothing in exchange.'

'My petition appeals to your pity; remove it.'

'And mine appeals to yours; let it remain where it is,' she retorted.

'Well, then, at least you will tell me if you are French or German; you command both languages with perfection.'

'I do not think I shall disclose that, General. You intend a surprise and are meditating a specific point of attack.'

'At all events, you will not deny this,' I pressed, 'that being honored by your permission to converse, I ought to know how to address you. Shall I say Madame la Comtesse?'

She laughed, and undoubtedly would have met me with another evasion—if, indeed, I can regard any occurrence in an interview, every circumstance of which I now believe was prearranged with the profoundest cunning, as liable to modification by mere accident.

'As to that,' she began, but was cut off almost immediately by a gentleman dressed in black. He looked particularly elegant and distinguished, save for one drawback: his face was the most deadly pale I had ever witnessed, save perhaps in death. He wore no costume, only the plain evening attire of a gentleman. Without a smile, yet with a courtly and unusually low bow, he declared: 'Will Madame la Comtesse permit me to say a very few words which may interest her?'

The lady turned swiftly to him, touching her lip in a token of silence. Then she said to me, 'Keep my place for me, General; I shall return when I have spoken a few words.'

With this playful injunction, she stepped aside with the gentleman in black, talking for several minutes with apparent earnestness. They then walked slowly away together into the throng, and I lost sight of them for a few moments.

I spent the interval beating my brains against the wall of mystery concerning the identity of the lady who remembered me so kindly. I contemplated turning to join the conversation between my pretty ward and the Countess's daughter, attempting to gather her name, title, chateau, and estates by the time she returned, thereby securing a surprise for her. But just then, she reappeared, accompanied by the pale man in black, who stated: 'I shall return and inform Madame la Comtesse when her carriage arrives at the door.' He withdrew with a bow."
