The contrast between Daniel Deronda's departure from England and his return was as stark as the shift from winter snow to spring bloom. He had sailed for Genoa with a heart shrouded in fog, unsure if the currents of his destiny would carry him away from the familiar shores of his upbringing or pull him deeper into the unknown tides of Jewish heritage. He returned, however, not merely free, but anchored. In his possession lay a charter of sorts, validating the ambitions that had stirred within him, a bond forged by experience that he welcomed with a humble grace despite the lack of promises regarding a secret, sleeping passion. Yet, something vital had shifted within him. For the first time, he dared to acknowledge the silent, steady presence of Mirah in his soul. Since his departure from Chelsea, where Mirah's farewell gaze had struck a chord of profound, protective tenderness, her influence had grown stronger, not as a rival to Gwendolen, but as a counterweight that softened the edges of his admiration for her. Where once his pity for Gwendolen had been fueled by a martyr-like self-sacrifice, it now flowed toward a gentler, more internalized compassion. Furthermore, his growing kinship with Mordecai had drawn Mirah closer to his thoughts, binding her fate to his moral struggles, particularly those surrounding her brother's honor. This connection had not slipped past his consciousness; indeed, it had become a source of inner turmoil, a "jealous fire" sparked by Hans's attentions. When his mother had accused him of loving a Jewess, evasion had felt like betrayal. Now, standing on the threshold of a new understanding, compelled by the urgency of Hans's actions and the weight of his mother's probing, Deronda felt a sudden, liberating clarity. The exhausting burden of self-suppression melted away, replaced by a fierce, impulsive drive. As he approached England from Mainz, the journey itself felt like an obstacle, a delay he could no longer afford. It was as if discovering his ancestry had granted him a second soul, transforming his impartial sympathy into a focused, practical loyalty. He sought no longer the detached wisdom of the observer, but the shared warmth of fellowship with those of his blood. He craved to be near Mordecai, to finally align his will with theirs, and to view Mirah through the lens of a new, open possibility, interpreting her every gesture with a fresh, hopeful intent. He trusted that Hans's advances would fail to sway Mirah, sensing that her feelings for him, whatever they were, resided in a channel that would not divert. To shock a woman who viewed him only as a Lord Chancellor into becoming his lover was a transition he feared, preferring a path of ease and mutual growth.

Consequently, why linger? Why hesitate at the London terminus? Deronda resolved to proceed directly from the station to the modest lodging in Brompton Square. Time was not to be lost. He had promised to visit Lady Mallinger at the Abbey the following day, and now twilight was descending. Above all, he needed to entrust the precious chest containing his grandfather's manuscripts to Mordecai. He desired that Mordecai study these documents both alone and in his company, and the promptness of this delivery would bring a profound satisfaction to Mordecai's heart. Thus, it filled Deronda's own heart with a deep contentment. The strongest currents of his nature rushed forward in unison: the fervent affection that delighted in fulfilling the desires of those near him, and the imaginative hunger for a relationship broad enough to illuminate the horizon of his daily existence. One must admit that in this classical, romantic, and historically charged position, as if emerging from hiding with his hereditary armor, he wore the somber, drab costume common to his contemporaries. He did not stop to consider if these muted tones suited him, for he rarely indulged in such introspection. Yet, his innate depth of character lent a radiant glow to his features, a flickering light in his skin and eyes as he stepped inside, wondering what secrets awaited. He entered the house with a silence that spoke of reverence.

That evening, mere hours after Mirah's painful interview with her father, the atmosphere within the lodgings remained heavy with unspoken sorrow. Mordecai, deeply moved by her grief and haunted by the tragic memories the incident had awakened, had abandoned his task of sorting through the scattered papers. Some lay strewn across the floor, untouched since the first paroxysms of anxiety. Neither Mordecai nor Mirah had moved to restore order. They sat frozen in a tableau of despair, unaware of the passing minutes, as the clock on the mantelpiece marked the slow fade of daylight. Mirah, forgetting the meals she had neglected, remained seated beside her brother, her hand clutched in his, while he leaned back with closed eyes and labored breath, appearing to Mirah as one whose soul could no longer inhabit its confined vessel. The thought of imminent death constantly visited her whenever she gazed upon his pale, motionless face. Now, to her overwhelming grief, was added the bitter regret that she could not have restrained the violent emotional storm that had shaken him. She watched him, her complexion pale, her eyes shimmering with the residue of recent tears, her hair in a disarray akin to a child's after waking, staring at that emaciated countenance as if it were a portrait of a dead joy that sustained her life through sorrow. Life, at that moment, stretched before Mirah not as a continuation, but as a terrifying repetition of sadness, shadowed by the presence of a father who lived as if already dead, creating a double bereavement.

Suddenly, the door opened. Though no one entered visibly, a familiar voice echoed softly, "Daniel Deronda—may he come in?"

"Come! Come!" cried Mordecai, rising instantly with a face irradiated by light and eyes wide open. He appeared scarcely surprised, as if he had anticipated the visitor since morning, while Mirah sprang up, her face flushing with a mixture of confusion and alarm.

Yet, when Deronda crossed the threshold, the air seemed to clear, like sunlight breaking through rain. No cloud could obstruct the benevolent beam of that moment. As he extended his right hand to Mirah, who stood near her brother's left, he placed his other hand gently upon Mordecai's shoulder. Standing thus, holding both of them, he uttered no word, reading their expressions until he asked anxiously of Mirah, "Has anything happened? Any trouble?"

"Do not speak of trouble now," Mordecai replied swiftly, sparing her the necessity of an answer. "There is joy in your face—let the joy be ours."

Mirah thought, *It is for something he cannot tell us.* They all sat down, with Deronda drawing a chair close before Mordecai.

"That is true," Deronda said, his voice emphatic. "I have a joy that will endure for us, even in the midst of the greatest troubles. I did not reveal the purpose of my journey abroad, Mordecai, because... never mind. I went to discover my parentage. And you were right. I am a Jew."

The two men clasped hands in a movement that seemed to flash through Mirah like an electric shock. But Deronda continued without pausing, speaking with the conviction of Mordecai's own mind as much as his own: "We share the same people. Our souls share the same vocation. Life nor death shall separate us."

Mordecai's answer burst forth in Hebrew, barely a whisper above a murmur, yet carrying the weight of liturgical bond: "Our God and the God of our fathers."

The weight of emotion pressed too heavily upon the speech, rendering it slower than usual, devoid of its customary rapid adaptation to shifting fervor.

Mirah sank to her knees beside her brother, gazing at his now illuminated face, which had moments prior appeared deathly. This action was an inevitable outlet for the violent reversal from despondency to a solemn gladness that washed over her, as if she were witnessing a sacred rite. For the moment, her thoughts of her own future were eclipsed entirely by the effect upon her brother.

"And it is not only that I am a Jew," Deronda continued, experiencing one of those rare moments where yearning and action became wholly unified, and reality aligned with ideal goodness. "But I descend from a strain that has ardently maintained the fellowship of our race—a lineage of Spanish Jews that produced many scholars and men of practical power. And I possess that which grants us a communion with them. My grandfather, Daniel Charisi, preserved manuscripts and family records stretching far back, hoping they would eventually reach the hands of his grandson. And now, his hope is fulfilled, despite the attempts to hide my parentage from me. I possess the chest containing them, along with his own papers, and it lies down below in this house. I intend to leave it with you, Mordecai, that you may assist me in studying the manuscripts. Some I can read with ease—those in Spanish and Italian. Others are in Hebrew, and I believe Arabic; there seem to be Latin translations as well. I was only able to examine them cursorily during my stay in Mainz. We shall study them together."

Deronda concluded with a bright smile, emanating from the habitual gravity of his countenance, seeming a revelation—a light that dispelled the shadows of doubt rather than the artificial smile that often discredits all expression.
