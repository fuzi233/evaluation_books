Once the distinguished guests of Castlewood had been escorted to their respective quarters, the young masters retreated to their own room to discuss the monumental events of the day. Their consensus was swift and unanimous: such a marriage was unthinkable. Could the heir to the Marquis of Esmond's fortune truly wed the younger son of a colonial family, a man raised merely as a land surveyor? To hand over the stewardship of Castlewood, and the boys themselves at the tender age of nineteen, to the mercy of a stepfather barely twenty-three? It was monstrous.

Harry immediately proposed storming his mother's bedroom—where her dark-skinned maidservants were currently removing the heavy jewels and finery she had worn for the feast—to protest the abhorrent match. He declared they would return home, live off their modest estate, and abandon her forever if this unnatural union proceeded.

George, however, advocated a different strategy, outlining his plan to his awestruck brother. "Our mother cannot marry a man with whom one or both of us have fought on the field," George reasoned. "She cannot wed a man who has wounded us, killed us, or whom we have wounded or killed. We must summon him, Harry."

Harry recognized the profound logic in his brother's words and admired his superior sagacity. "No, George," he agreed. "Mother cannot marry our murderer; she wouldn't stoop so low. And if we challenge him, he is finished. *Cadit quaestio*, as Mr. Dempster used to say. Shall I dispatch my page to issue a challenge to Colonel Washington immediately?"

"My dear Harry," the elder brother replied, reflecting with some pride on his own affair of honor at Quebec, "you are not yet accustomed to matters of this gravity."

"No," admitted Harry, sighing with envy and reverence for his senior.

"We cannot insult a gentleman within our own walls," George continued, speaking with majestic authority. "The laws of honor forbid such inhospitable treatment. However, we may ride out with him. Once the park gates are shut, we shall make our intentions perfectly clear."

"That we can, by George!" Harry cried, seizing his brother's hand. "And we shall, too. Say, Georgy..." Suddenly, the lad's face turned crimson, and his brother asked what he intended to propose.

"This is my turn, brother," Harry pleaded earnestly. "If you undertake the campaign, I deserve the other affair. Indeed, indeed, I do." He prayed fervently for this promotion.

"Again, the head of the house must take the lead, my dear," George said with a superb air. "Should I fall, my Harry will avenge me. But I must fight George Washington, Hal. And it is best that I do; for, in truth, I hate him the most. Was it not he who counseled my mother to order that wretch, Ward, to seize me?"

"Ah, George," interjected the more pacific younger brother, "you ought to forget and forgive."

"Forget? Never, sir, as long as I remember. One cannot command memory to vanish from a man's mind; a wrong committed yesterday remains a wrong tomorrow. I have never harmed anyone knowingly, and I shall never allow harm to befall me if I can prevent it. I think poorly of Mr. Ward, but not so poorly as to believe he will ever forgive you for that blow with the ruler. Colonel Washington is our enemy, mine especially. He has advised one injustice against me and plots a greater. Tell you what, brother: we must punish him."

The old Bordeaux wine had set George's usually pale complexion ablaze. Harry, his brother's devoted worshipper, could not help but admire George's haughty demeanor and rapid eloquence. Prepared to follow his leader with his usual docility, the boys retired to their beds. Before retiring, George issued special instructions to his junior to remain civil to all the guests while they remained under their mother's roof the following day.

Decent manners and a reluctance to spread gossip prevented us from naming which of Madame Esmond's guests was the first to succumb to the weight of her hospitality. The respectable descendants of Messrs. Talmadge and Danvers, aides-de-camp to His Excellency, might prefer not to recall how their ancestors were intoxicated a century prior; yet, the gentlemen themselves bore no shame for the fact, and there is little doubt they or their companions were tipsy two or three times a week. Imagine them stumbling to bed, supported by sympathetic negroes; imagine their vinous General, too stout a drinker to resist six bottles of Bordeaux, led to his chamber by the young gentlemen of the house, and quickly falling asleep to the slumber bestowed by friendly Bacchus.

Madame Esmond observed the state of her guests without surprise or horror. She rose early the next morning, arranging cooling drinks for their overheated throats, which the servants delivered to their rooms. At breakfast, one of the English officers teased Mr. Franklin, who consumed no alcohol whatsoever, refusing the morning draught of toddy. The officer pointed out that the Philadelphia gentleman had lost two pleasures: the drink itself and the toddy. The young man retorted that the disease was pleasant and the remedy delicious, laughing as he proposed to repeat both.

Colonel Washington, the General's new American aide-de-camp, remained quite sober and serene. The British officers vowed they must intervene and teach him the ways of the English army, but the Virginian gentleman gravely stated he had no desire to learn that aspect of English military education.

The widow, preoccupied with the cares of a grand dinner followed by a substantial breakfast, had little leisure to scrutinize her sons' behavior closely. Yet, she noted that George was scrupulously polite to her favorite, Colonel Washington, as he was to all other guests.

Before departing, General Braddock secured a private audience with Madame Esmond. His Excellency formally offered to adopt her son into his family. When arrangements for George's departure were finalized between his mother and future commander, Madame Esmond, though aware of the perils, showed no squeamish terror regarding the dangers of the bottle—a hazard she knew would be among the severest her son faced. She understood her boy must take his part in the world and endure his share of evil and good. "Mr. Braddock is a perfect fine gentleman in the morning," she told her aide-de-camp, Mrs. Mountain, stoutly. "Though my father did not drink, it is certain that many of the finest companies in England do."

The jolly General shook hands warmly with George, who presented himself to His Excellency after the maternal interview concluded. The General welcomed him and instructed him to attend at Frederick in three days, shortly after which the expedition would set forth.

Now, the great carriage was requisitioned again. The General's escort pranced around it; the other guests mounted their horses. Madame Esmond accompanied Her Excellency to the steps of the veranda. The young gentlemen followed, standing on either side of the coach door. The guard trumpeter blew a shrill blast, and the negroes shouted, "Huzzah, and God save the King!" as Mr. Braddock graciously took leave of his hospitable hosts and rolled away toward headquarters.

As the boys ascended the steps, the Colonel was once more bidding farewell to their mother. No doubt she had been urging George into his namesake's care once more. Colonel Washington assured her, "With my life. You may depend on me," as the lads returned to their mother and the few remaining guests in the porch. The Colonel was fully booted and ready to depart. "Farewell, my dear Harry," he said. "With you, George, it is no adieu. We shall meet in three days at the camp."

Both young men were heading toward danger, perhaps even death. Colonel Washington was leaving, and she would not see him again before the campaign began. No wonder the widow was deeply moved.

George Warrington watched his mother's emotion and interpreted it with a pang of malignant scorn. "Stay yet a moment to console our mother," he said with a steady countenance. "Only the time to get ourselves booted, and my brother and I will ride with you a little way, George." George had already ordered his horses. The three young men sped off, their negro grooms trailing behind. Mrs. Mountain, knowing she had sown discord between them and trembling for the outcome, felt a vast relief that Mr. Washington departed without a quarrel with the brothers, and certainly without any open declaration of love to their mother.

No man could be more courteous in demeanor than George Warrington toward his neighbor and namesake, the Colonel. The latter was pleased and surprised by his young friend's altered behavior. The shared sense of impending danger, the necessity of future fellowship, the softening influence of the long friendship binding him to the Esmond family, and the tender farewells exchanged with the mistress of Castlewood inclined the Colonel to forget the unpleasantness of recent days. He became unusually friendly with his young companion. George was quite gay and easy; it was Harry who was now melancholy. He rode silently and wistfully beside his brother, keeping his distance from Colonel Washington, to whose side he had previously pressed eagerly. If the honest Colonel noticed his young friend's conduct, he likely attributed it to Harry's known affection for his brother and his natural anxiety to stay close to George as the day of parting approached.
