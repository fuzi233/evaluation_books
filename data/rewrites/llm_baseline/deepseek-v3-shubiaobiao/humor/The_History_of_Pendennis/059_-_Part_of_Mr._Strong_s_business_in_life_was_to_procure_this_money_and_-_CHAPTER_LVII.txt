Major Pendennis, ever the man of his word (provided it cost him nothing), had dutifully assured Warrington that young Arthur’s unfortunate entanglement with the gatekeeper’s daughter was well and truly severed. Pen himself, after a brief sulk over the blow to his vanity, consoled himself with the knowledge that Miss Fanny was unlikely to perish of a broken heart, sparing him any messy consequences.

Thus liberated, the entire party embarked on their grand Continental tour—Arthur Pendennis, gentleman of leisure, traveling with Madame Pendennis and Mademoiselle Bell, while George Warrington, Esq. (aged 32, height 6 feet, hair black, beard equally so, etc.), secured the necessary passports and shepherded the group from Dover to Ostend. From there, they meandered through Bruges and Ghent before descending upon Brussels and eventually the Rhine.

Now, we shall not bore the reader with the usual travelogue—Laura’s wide-eyed rapture at the Gothic splendor of cathedrals, Helen’s horrified fascination with Catholic excess (barefoot friars! jeweled saints! people *dancing* on Sundays!), or Pen’s general air of detachment, as if the whole affair were a mildly tedious dream. Suffice it to say, the widow clutched her prayer book like a shield against popery, while Laura, ever observant, noted the growing shadows in Helen’s eyes. The poor woman was tormented—had she lost her son’s heart? Was there some secret chamber within him now locked against her?

Meanwhile, Warrington—ever the self-sacrificing bachelor—played courier, translator, and general factotum with surprising enthusiasm. He fetched *Galignani’s*, arranged donkey rides, and even endured the Kurhaus band’s relentless waltzes, all while stealing glances at Laura, who remained oblivious to his silent pining. (A tragic figure, our George, doomed to admire from afar while Pen, the oblivious oaf, lounged about recovering his appetite and his waistline.)

Enter Major Pendennis, rejuvenated by a stay at Lord Steyne’s estate (where he had regaled the company with scandalous anecdotes and made himself indispensable). Having exhausted the pheasant population, he now descended upon Rosenbad, impeccably dressed and trailing his long-suffering valet, Morgan. The Major, ever the social strategist, lost no time in introducing Pen to a Russian princess (whose past was as colorful as her rouge) and other assorted nobility, while Helen shuddered at the gambling tables and extracted a promise from her son never to play. (Pen, to his credit, kept his word—though the Major sighed at such provincial morality.)

Then came the bombshell—a letter from Fanny Bolton, discovered among Pen’s forwarded mail. The poor girl, heartbroken and bewildered, pleaded for some token of remembrance, some shred of kindness. Pen, incensed, confronted his uncle, who denied all involvement with the air of a man who very much *had* been involved. Realizing the culprit could only be his mother, Pen stormed out, leaving the Major to mutter about "fat in the fire" and Helen’s impending meltdown.

Back at their lodgings, Pen raged to Warrington, vowing vengeance on whoever had driven Fanny away. Warrington, ever the voice of reason (or at least restraint), pointed out that attacking one’s own mother was poor form—even if she *had* behaved abominably. But Pen, in high dudgeon, was beyond listening. The stage was set for a spectacular family row, and the curtain was about to rise.
