It was Palm Sunday—known across the olive-cloaked hills of Valdarno far more often as Olive Sunday—and the morning sun blazed with a rare, honeyed clarity, washed clean by the rain that had fallen the day before. Once again Girolamo Savonarola climbed the worn stone steps of San Marco’s pulpit, and looked out over a throng whose faith in him had not yet wavered. That morning, he spoke not with fiery rhetoric, but with quiet, sad sincerity: he told the crowd he was ready to die, that he could see his own doom standing clear before him, sharper than any prophetic vision. He spoke the final benediction, and looked out over the faces of the men and women who lifted their gazes to him, bright with reverent, loving devotion. Then he stepped down from the pulpit, and turned away from that sight for the last time.

Before the sun dipped below Florence’s red-tiled roofs, the whole city was in uproar. Passions that had flared the day before had smouldered low through the soft quiet morning, and now they burst forth with a brutal fury that was no accident: it had been deliberately stoked, and carried the quiet connivance of the city’s officials. The riot first broke out at the Duomo, when a band of Compagnacci, the young aristocratic street thugs, moved to break up the evening sermon the Piagnoni, Savonarola’s followers, had gathered to hear. No sooner had blood been drawn and the shoving turned to a full brawl, than the cry rose up, sharp and ragged, from a hundred throats: “To San Marco! Burn San Marco to the ground!”

Long before the last of the daylight faded, the great church and its attached Dominican convent were under siege from a swelling, screaming mob, numbering thousands. The defenders did not yield easily. The monks, who had long known the hostility outside their walls was growing, had hidden arms stowed away, and many fought just as fiercely in their flowing white tunics as any Knight Templar. Even Savonarola’s command to lay down their arms could not still the rush to self-defence in bodies hard and muscular beneath the rough Dominican serge. Lay supporters who had refused to flee fought alongside them: muskets blazed from the high altar, feet from the great carved crucifix, stones and scalding hot embers were poured down on the assailants from the convent roof, and swords clashed in vicious, close-quarters fighting through the cloisters. For all the weight of the mob’s numbers, the attack dragged on deep into the night.

The city government’s sympathies lay entirely with the besiegers. Early in the fighting, guards were dispatched not to disperse the rioters, but to order every person inside the convent to lay down their weapons, all lay supporters to leave, and for Savonarola himself to quit Florentine territory within twelve hours. Had Savonarola stepped outside the convent walls then, he would almost certainly have been torn to pieces by the waiting crowd. He was willing to surrender, but his friends held him back. Even for high-ranking Piagnoni nobles, slipping out through the unguarded garden wall was a terrible risk, but Francesco Valori, the fiery former statesman, made the attempt, hoping to raise a rescue party in the city.

By the time midnight had fallen, the defenders could not hold out much longer. The Compagnacci were moments away from breaking through to the library, where Savonarola knelt in prayer with the brothers who had refused to take up arms, or laid them down at his order, when a second detachment of guards arrived, sent by the ruling Signoria to demand the surrender of Fra Girolamo, and his two closest coadjutors, Fra Domenico and Fra Salvestro.

A roar of triumphant, hateful jeers went up from the crowd when the lantern light glinted off the monks’ white habits as Savonarola stepped through the convent doors, surrounded by guards who offered no promise of safety beyond the walls of the city prison. The crowd surged to press as close as they could, shoving and fighting for a spot in the narrow street leading to the Palazzo Vecchio, eager to see the Prophet dragged back in ignominy to the very public square where he had defied the city’s authorities only days before. They shouted insults at him, spat, and the armed Compagnacci, allowed to mix freely with the guards, struck him with their fists and kicked at his legs.

As Savonarola was dragged and jostled through that hooting throng, as lanterns were held up to his face to show him grinning, mocking faces, as the insults and blows rained down on him, he thought he had reached the bitterest point of suffering a man could know. If the city had judged him guilty and was determined to take his life, only death lay ahead of him. But the bitterest drop of all is never forced into a man’s mouth from outside: the lowest depth of true resignation is not found in martyrdom, but in the quiet, private moment when you wrap your cloak around your head in silence, and think *I am not worthy to be a martyr. The Truth will outlive me, but it will not triumph through my hands.*

That brief, vicious triumph of jeering at the captured monk, before he was hurried through the doors of the Old Palace to the cells beneath, only whetted the mob’s appetite the way a taste of blood rouses a tiger. Were not the houses of the hypocrite’s closest friends ripe for sacking? Half the armed crowd, too far back to take part in the siege of San Marco, had already set to the more profitable work of raiding the homes of wealthy Piagnoni. This was no random, chaotic plunder: the rioters targeted the houses of Savonarola’s most prominent supporters with sharp, deliberate care, proof that the riot was carefully orchestrated, the rabble armed with clubs and staves well led by sword-carrying Compagnacci.

Next after the Frate himself, there was the ambitious Francesco Valori, whom the rioters accused of plotting to make himself lifelong Doge or Gonfaloniere with Savonarola’s backing. The grey-haired noble, who only eight months before had thundered for the execution of five of his fellow citizens, escaped San Marco only to face what his enemies called justice: he found his house surrounded by the angry, greedy mob, saw his wife shot dead through the doorway by an archer’s arrow, and was himself hacked to death on the road to the Palazzo, where he had been summoned to answer false charges, by the swords of two noblemen, Ridolfi and Tornabuoni.

And so that grim masquerade of the Furies, the riot, raged across Florence through the dark hours of the night and the first pale light of dawn. The man who directed it all never showed his face in the streets: he had good reason to issue his orders from a hidden private lodging, his name far too high-profile to have his red-plumed cap seen bobbing above the crowd while all this bloody work played out before sunrise.

That lodging was the very same quiet house, in the street between Santa Croce and San Marco, where we once saw Tito Melema pay a secret late-night visit to Dolfo Spini. There the Captain of the Compagnacci sat through that long, historic night, receiving messengers who came and went in a constant stream. Some were armed Compagnacci, others dressed in plain, unremarkable clothes with no visible weapons. A huge cask of wine stood on the table, with cups set out for every new arrival. Spini was careful not to drink so much he lost his wits, but he sipped steadily enough to stoke the wild excitement of the constant, fresh reports being brought to him.

Of all the plainly dressed visitors, Ser Ceccone the notary was one of the most frequent, and as the dark hours wore on towards dawn, he stayed as Spini’s constant companion, alongside Francesco Cei, the disgraced poet who was hiding out in Florence, waiting for Savonarola’s fall to have his banishment revoked. The tallow candles had burned down to lumpy, misshapen stubs, and the faint grey pre-dawn light was just beginning to show through the cracks in the shuttered windows, when Spini, who had been pacing up and down, face flushed dark with rage after a heated conversation with his two non-military companions, burst out in a snarl.

“Devil take the Greek! He’ll pay for this, I swear. Ha! We’ll have the claws sunk in him when he least expects it. All this time he pretended to toss every scrap of profit my way, as if I was some blind beggar, and all the while he’s winking and shoving every florin into his own purse. I’d like to wrap him in rags hung with raw meat and set my hunting hounds on him! And he’s got that fine ruby of mine, the one I was fool enough to give him the day before yesterday. Curse him! He’s been laughing at me behind my back for two whole years, ruining the best laid plan I ever came up with. What a fool I was to trust a rogue who twists his schemes into knots no one but himself can ever untangle.”

“A Greek, no less, who washed up in Florence with his pockets stuffed full of stolen gems,” said Francesco Cei, a faint amused smile on his face as he watched Spini fume. “You did not pick your confidant very wisely, my Dolfo.”

“He’s a damned sight cleverer than you, Francesco, and handsomer too!” Spini snapped, rounding on his associate, in the surly mood that made him lash out at anyone handy.

“If I may say so humbly,” Ser Ceccone put in, rubbing his palms together in his usual obsequious way, “Messer Francesco’s poetic genius far outweighs—”

“Ugh, stop rubbing your hands like you’re about to forge a fake will! I hate that notary trick of yours,” Spini interrupted. His idea of patronage was mostly this rough, frank bullying. “Wait, that’s Taddeo at the door! Now’s the time. What news?” he called out, as two Compagnacci burst into the room, faces flushed and sweating.

“Bad news,” one of them said. “The mob was dead set on sacking Soderini’s house, and now they’ve been blocked from doing it, they’re going to turn on us next if we don’t give them something new to chew on. I swear there are Medicean agents mixed in with them, stirring up trouble. They’ll be marching on your palace across the bridge before long, if we don’t find them a new bait to chase.”

“I’ve got it!” Spini shouted, grabbing Taddeo by the belt and yanking him aside to whisper new orders in his ear, while the other messenger explained to Cei that the Signoria had stepped in at the last minute to block the sacking of Soderini’s home.

“Done!” Spini exclaimed a minute later, giving Taddeo a hard shove towards the door. “Go. Make it fast, no delays.”
