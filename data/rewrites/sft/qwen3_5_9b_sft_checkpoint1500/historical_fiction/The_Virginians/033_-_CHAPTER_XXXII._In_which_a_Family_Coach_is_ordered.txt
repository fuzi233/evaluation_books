The secret whispered by Mr. Lambert into his wife's ear at the close of the previous chapter, and the subsequent joy it wrought throughout the Oakhurst household, is now revealed to us. With the hay harvest complete and the corn fields untouched, the farm horses were finally free from labor. "Why," asked Colonel Lambert, "should we not harness them to the coach, and pay a visit to Tunbridge Wells, taking friend Wolfe at Westerham along the way?"

Mamma, the children, and the honest Colonel alike embraced the proposal with delight. Instantly, the girls descended to gather their finest attire—calamancoes, paduasoes, falbalas, furbelows, capes, cardinals, sacks, negligées, solitaires, caps, ribbons, mantuas, clocked stockings, and high-heeled shoes, alongside countless other articles of toilette. Mamma's most prized robes, usually confined to the presses for rare, solemn occasions, were retrieved and retired immediately afterward to lavender and privacy. The brave Colonel donned his laced hat, waistcoat, and silver-hilted hanger; Charley, delighted, wore a *rasee* holiday suit formerly belonging to his father, which the Colonel had worn at his wedding—a garment Mrs. Lambert cut up with genuine sorrow. Even Ball and Dumpling, the family ponies, had their tails and manes tied with ribbons, while Chump, the ancient white cart-horse, was appointed as a unicorn leader to assist the carriage horses up the first five hilly miles from Oakhurst to Westerham.

The vehicle itself was an antique, rumored to have served in the procession that brought George I. from Greenwich to London upon his accession, having belonged to Mr. Lambert's father and been regarded by the family as one of the most splendid coaches in the three kingdoms. Brian, coachman and ploughman, occupied the box alongside Mr. Charley. The precious garments were secured in imperial bags atop the roof, the Colonel's pistols tucked into the carriage pockets, and a blunderbuss hung behind the box within easy reach of Brian, a veteran soldier. Fortunately, no highwayman molested the convoy, nor did any innkeeper levy contributions from the Colonel, whose slender purse and large family rendered him immune to depredators on the King's highway. A modest, reasonably priced lodging had been arranged for them by young Colonel Wolfe at his usual haunt, where he himself accompanied them on horseback.

These lodgings stood directly opposite Madame Bernstein's establishment. Upon reaching their quarters on a Saturday evening, the Oakhursts observed chair after chair discharging powdered beaux and patched beauties at the Baroness's door, where she was hosting one of her numerous card parties. Though the sun had not yet set—for their ancestors began their dissipation early and continued until late—the young country ladies and their mother watched the influx of guests through their window. Colonel Wolfe identified the arrivals for the girls, turning the viewing into a delightful spectacle comparable to attending the party itself. Hetty and Theo marveled at the scene, able not only to see the guests arrive but to peer through the Baroness's open windows and observe them inside.

Among the distinguished figures passing by was the Duchess of Queensberry. When Colonel Wolfe explained her identity, Martin Lambert promptly recited a score of lines from his favorite poet, Mat Prior: *"Kitty, beautiful and young."*

"Think that old lady was once like you, girls!" cried the Colonel.

"Like us, papa? Well, certainly we never set up for being beauties!" replied Miss Hetty, tossing up her little head.

"Yes, like you, you little baggage! Like you at this moment, wanting to go to that drum yonder—" quoting Prior again, *"Inflamed with rage at sad restraint / Which wise mamma ordained..."*

"We were never invited, papa," said the satirical Martin, "and I am sure if there is no beauty more worth seeing than that, the wit cannot be much worth the hearing."

"Oh, but he is a rare poet, Mat Prior!" continued the Colonel, urging caution. "Though, mind you, girls, you will skip over all the poems I have marked with a cross. A rare poet! To think you should see one of his heroines! 'Fondness prevailed, mamma gave way...' (she always will, Mrs. Lambert!)"

"I am sure it must have been very inflammable," said Mamma.

"So it was, my dear, twenty years ago, much more inflammable than it is now," remarked the Colonel.

"Nonsense, Mr. Lambert," answered Mamma.

Suddenly, Hetty ran forward, pointing to the little square and the covered gallery leading to Madame Bernstein's apartments, where a crowd of street urchins, idlers, and yokels watched the company.

"It's Harry Warrington!" exclaimed Theo, waving a handkerchief. Yet, the young Virginian did not see Miss Lambert. Walking arm-in-arm with a portly clergyman in a crisp, rustling silk gown, the two entered Madame de Bernstein's door.

"I heard him preach a most admirable sermon here last Sunday," said Mr. Wolfe, "a little theatrical, but most striking and eloquent."

"You seem to be here most Sundays, James," observed Mrs. Lambert.

"And Monday, and soon till Saturday," added the Colonel. "See, Harry has beautified himself already; his hair is in buckles, and I have no doubt he is going to the drum too."

"I had rather sit quietly generally of a Saturday evening," said the sober Mr. Wolfe, "at any rate, away from card-playing and scandal; but I own, dear Mrs. Lambert, I am under orders. Shall I go across and send Mr. Warrington to you?"

"No, let him have his sport. We shall see him tomorrow. He won't care to be disturbed among his fine folks by us country-people," said meek Mrs. Lambert.

"I am glad he is with a clergyman who preaches so well," said Theo softly, her eyes suggesting, *You see, good people, he is not so bad as you thought.* "The clergyman has a very kind, handsome face."

"Here comes a greater clergyman!" cried Mr. Wolfe. "It is my Lord of Salisbury, with his blue ribbon, and a chaplain behind him."

"And whom, mercy's name, have we here?" broke in Mrs. Lambert as a sedan-chair, covered in gilding and topped with no fewer than five earl's coronets, approached, borne by men in richly laced clothes and preceded by three footmen in splendid liveries. The Bishop, about to enter, stopped and ran back with respectful bows and curtsies to the sedan-chair, offering his hand to the lady who stepped forth.

"Who on earth is this?" asked Mrs. Lambert.

"Sprechen sie Deutsch? Ja, meinherr. Nichts verstand," quipped the waggish Colonel.

"Pooh, Martin."

"Well, if you can't understand High Dutch, my love, how can I help it? Your education was neglected at school. Can you understand heraldry? I know you can."

"I make," cried Charley, reciting the shield, "three merions on a field or, with an earl's coronet."

"A countess's coronet, my son. The Countess of Yarmouth, my son."

"And pray who is she?"

"It hath ever been the custom of our sovereigns to advance persons of distinction to honour," continued the Colonel gravely, "and this eminent lady hath been so promoted by our gracious monarch to the rank of Countess of this kingdom."

"But why, papa?" asked the daughters together.

"Never mind, girls!" said Mamma.

But the incorrigible Colonel pressed on. "Y, my children, is one of the last and most awkward letters of the whole alphabet. When I tell you stories, you are always saying 'Why.' Why should my Lord Bishop be cringing to that lady? Look at him rubbing his fat hands together, smiling into her face! It is not a handsome face any longer. It is all painted red and white like Scaramouch's in the pantomime. See, there comes another blue-ribbon, as I live. My Lord Bamborough. The descendant of the Hotspurs. The proudest man in England. He stops, he bows, he smiles; he is hat-in-hand, too. See, she taps him with her fan. Get away, you crowd of little blackguard boys, and don't tread on the robe of the lady whom the King delights to honour."

"But why does the King honour her?" the girls asked again.

"There goes that odious last letter but one! Did you ever hear of Her Grace the Duchess of Kendal? No. Of the Duchess of Portsmouth? Non plus. Of the Duchess of La Vallière? Of Fair Rosamond, then?"

"Hush, papa! There is no need to bring blushes on the cheeks of my dear ones, Martin Lambert!" said the mother, placing her finger to her husband's lips.

"'Tis not I; it is their Sacred Majesties who are the cause of the shame," cried the son of the old republican. "Think of the bishops of the Church and the proudest nobility of the world cringing and bowing before that painted High Dutch Jezebel. Oh, it's a shame! A shame!"
