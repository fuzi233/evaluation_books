Mrs. Dashwood and her daughters had never anticipated upon arriving in Devonshire that their quiet retreat would so quickly transform into a whirlwind of social obligations. Instead of finding solitude, they were immediately swept up in a tide of engagements, constant invitations, and ceaseless visits that left them with precious little time for reflection or serious pursuits. Once Marianne recovered from her initial disappointment, Sir John's long-planned schemes for amusement were set in motion with vigor. Private balls erupted within the park grounds, and outings on the water became a weekly ritual, weather permitting.

Willoughby was invariably included in these gatherings. The relaxed atmosphere of these events fostered an intimacy between him and the Dashwood sisters that grew rapidly. It offered him perfect opportunities to observe Marianne's virtues, to express his fervent admiration, and to receive unmistakable signs of her affection in return. Elinor, unsurprised by the depth of their connection, simply hoped it were displayed with greater discretion. On rare occasions, she gently urged Marianne toward self-restraint, but Marianne recoiled at the suggestion. To her, concealing genuine feeling was not just unnecessary but a shameful submission of reason to petty convention. Willoughby shared this sentiment entirely. Their behavior became a living testament to their beliefs: whenever he was near, Marianne saw no one else. Everything he did was flawless; everything he said was witty. If card games ended the evening, he would cheat himself and everyone else solely to ensure she held the winning hand. If dancing took precedence, they were partners for half the night, and when forced apart, they stood close together, ignoring all other company.

Such conduct inevitably drew laughter, yet ridicule failed to shame or deter them. Mrs. Dashwood embraced their passion with such warmth that she felt no impulse to curb it, viewing it merely as the natural expression of a strong heart in a youthful spirit. For Marianne, this was a season of profound joy. Her devotion to Willoughby was absolute, and the lingering attachment she carried for Norland began to soften under the enchanting influence of her new surroundings.

Elinor's happiness, however, was far more muted. Her peace of mind was unsettled, and the amusements offered her no solace for the life she had left behind. Neither Lady Middleton nor Mrs. Jennings could fill the void left by her old friends. Although Mrs. Jennings talked incessantly and treated Elinor with boundless kindness, her stories were repetitive, and her knowledge of family history seemed endless. Lady Middleton, while quieter, possessed a reserve that was merely a lack of wit rather than true dignity; she remained distant and unengaging toward both her husband and mother. Elinor soon realized that Lady Middleton's silence was a shield against conversation, not a sign of grace. There was nothing new in her speech, and her spirits remained perpetually flat. Though she tolerated the parties organized by her husband if they maintained a certain style and her eldest children were present, she derived no genuine pleasure from them. Her presence added little to the room's ambiance, often serving only as a reminder of her concern for her "troublesome" sons.

In contrast to the superficiality of her new acquaintances, Elinor found a singular figure worthy of her respect and friendship: Colonel Brandon. While Willoughby's attention was entirely consumed by Marianne, leaving no room for anyone else, Colonel Brandon found unexpected comfort in Elinor's company. He possessed the intellect to command her respect and the gentleness to inspire her affection. Elinor's sympathy for him deepened as she suspected he bore the scars of a thwarted love. One evening, while the others danced and the pair sat quietly nearby, the truth nearly slipped from his lips. With a faint smile, he remarked, "Your sister, I understand, does not approve of second attachments."

"No," Elinor replied softly, "her opinions are purely romantic."

"Or, perhaps more accurately," he continued, "she believes them impossible to exist."

"I believe she does," Elinor admitted. "Though I wonder how she manages to hold such views without considering her own father, who married twice. Perhaps a few years will bring her back to the sensible foundation of common observation, making her stance easier to defend."

"That is likely," Brandon agreed, adding, "Yet there is something so charming in the prejudices of youth that one regrets seeing them yield to mundane realities."

"I cannot agree with you there," Elinor countered firmly. "The dangers inherent in such feelings outweigh all the allure of enthusiasm and ignorance. Her system tends to dismiss propriety entirely. What she needs most is a deeper understanding of the world."

Brandon paused before asking, "Does she make exceptions? Is a second attachment equally unforgivable regardless of circumstance?"

"Elinor shook her head. 'I am unaware of the nuances of her principles, but I have never heard her excuse a single instance.'"

"This cannot be true forever," Brandon insisted. "A complete change of heart... No, do not wish for that. When youthful idealism crumbles, it is often replaced by opinions that are far too common and perilous. I speak from experience. I once knew a woman whose temperament and judgment mirrored your sister perfectly. But due to changed circumstances..." He stopped abruptly, realizing he had revealed too much. His sudden silence and troubled expression confirmed Elinor's fears. The resemblance was undeniable, and the story of lost love hung heavy in the air. Elinor tried to suppress the revelation, but Marianne, had she been present, would have instantly constructed the tragic narrative in her mind, confirming every detail of a disaster born of misplaced affection.
