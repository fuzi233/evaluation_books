It is a universally admitted truth among the literati that all great writers possess a profound sense of shyness. I, for one, am no exception, although acquaintances often remark that my reserve is hardly perceptible to the casual observer.

I am relieved by this observation. My former shyness was, however, a formidable obstacle, causing me significant distress and rendering my interactions with others—particularly my female companions—a source of considerable friction. They complained most vehemently. Indeed, the destiny of a shy man is rarely enviable. Men tend to disdain him, women seem to pity him with a mixture of contempt, and he, naturally, despises himself. Time offers no remedy, and habit provides no relief, save perhaps for a singular piece of advice I once stumbled upon in a modest weekly journal within the "Answers to Correspondents" column. It read: "Adopt an easy and pleasing manner, especially toward ladies."

Alas for the ill-fated soul who took this counsel seriously! Imagine the ironic smile with which he likely devoured those words. "Adopt a pleasing manner"? Nay, my dear friend, do not attempt to manufacture a disposition. To force oneself into artificial ease inevitably results in a performance that is either gushingly insincere or offensively familiar. The path of least resistance is to remain one's authentic self; at worst, one will be perceived merely as surly and dull.

Yet, the shy man does wield a peculiar form of vengeance against the society that torments him. He communicates his misery with such intensity that he frightens others nearly as much as they frighten him. He acts as a dampener upon the collective spirit of any gathering; even the most boisterous spirits find themselves rendered depressed and jittery in his presence.

Much of this stems from a fundamental misunderstanding. Society frequently mistakes the shy man's timidity for arrogant aloofness, bowing down in awe of what they perceive as superiority. His awkwardness is interpreted as insolent carelessness. When, seized by terror at the mere sound of a greeting, blood rushes to his brow and his tongue ties itself into knots, he is viewed as a grotesque example of someone overcome by passion.

To be misunderstood is the shy man's eternal lot; whatever impression he intends to convey, the world invariably receives its inverse. If he attempts a jest, it is dismissed as a fabrication, and his honesty is called into question. If he employs sarcasm, it is taken literally, branding him a fool. Should he try to ingratiate himself with a hint of flattery, it is construed as biting satire, ensuring his eternal hatred by the recipient.

While these tribulations provide endless amusement to onlookers and have served as fertile ground for comedy since antiquity, a closer inspection reveals a deeply tragic dimension. A shy man is, by definition, a lonely man severed from the tapestry of human connection. He traverses the world yet remains untethered to it. An invisible, impenetrable wall separates him from his fellow creatures, bruising his efforts to climb over it. He witnesses joyful expressions and melodious voices from the other side, but his hand cannot cross the divide to grasp another. Standing amidst merry crowds, he yearns to join them, to claim kinship, yet they glide past him, oblivious. He reaches out, only to find his prison walls shifting with his steps, hemming him in tighter. Whether in bustling streets, crowded halls, or the clamor of industry, wherever humanity congregates, the shy man stands apart, shunned and solitary like a leper. His soul brims with affection and desire, yet the world remains unaware. The iron mask of shyness is fastened tightly over his features, hiding the man beneath. Words of warmth and greeting rise to his lips only to dissolve into silent, unheard whispers behind steel bars. His heart aches for the weary traveler, but his sympathy remains mute. Indignation at injustice swells in his chest, but lacking a safety valve, it turns inward, poisoning his spirit. Consequently, the deep reservoirs of love, hate, and scorn that curse the sensitive nature of the shy man festering internally transform him into a misanthrope and a cynic.

Indeed, shy men, much like women considered physically unattractive, fare poorly in this world. Enduring life with any degree of comfort requires the skin of a rhinoceros. This thick dermis serves as our necessary moral clothing; without it, we are unfit for polite society. A pale, gasping figure, flushed and trembling with shaking limbs, presents a painful spectacle, and if the ailment proves incurable, it is perhaps best that the afflicted individual departs swiftly.

Fortunately, this malady can be cured. I speak from personal conviction. Though I usually avoid discussing my private affairs, I shall break my silence for the sake of humanity. I confess that at one juncture, I was, as the ancient ballad describes, "the shyest of the shy." Whenever introduced to a charming maiden, my knees knocked together as if struck by fear. Yet, I did the deed—nay, I *did* the deed—alone and entirely on my own initiative. I ventured into the lair of a railway refreshment-room waitress. I rebuked her for her apparent callousness and lack of courtesy, insisting firmly yet politely on the deference due to a traveling Briton. Finally, I met her gaze squarely.

Truth be told, I departed the room immediately thereafter, appearing somewhat precipitous and without consuming refreshment. However, this departure was driven by a change of heart, not by cowardice, as you may rightly infer.

One solace available to the shy is that their condition is no indicator of intellectual inferiority. While bull-headed clowns may sneer at nerves, the loftiest souls are not necessarily those possessing the thickest hides. The horse is not inferior to the sparrow, nor the forest stag to the pig. Shyness signifies extreme sensibility and bears no relation to self-consciousness or vanity, despite the incessant assertions of the philistine school of philosophy regarding these connections.

Vanity, ironically, is the swiftest antidote. Once a man realizes he is vastly superior to everyone else, bashfulness vanishes. Surveying a room, he perceives every other occupant as a child of intellect compared to himself, feeling no hesitation toward them as he would not toward a flock of magpies.

Vanity constitutes the finest armor a man can don. Its smooth, impervious surface causes the petty thrusts of spite and envy to glance harmlessly away. Without this breastplate, the sword of talent cannot penetrate the fray of life, for it must endure blows as well as deliver them. I refer, of course, not to the vanity displayed by an elevated nose and a falsetto voice—that is merely play-acting, akin to children pretending to be monarchs. Genuine vanity renders a man neither objectionable nor difficult. On the contrary, it fosters geniality, kindness, and simplicity. Such a man feels no need for affectation, for he is utterly satisfied with his own character. His pride is so deeply rooted that it never surfaces externally. Indifferent to praise or censure, he can afford to be truthful. Believing himself far above the trivial distinctions of mortals, he is equally comfortable with a duke or a costermonger. Valuing only his own standards, he is never tempted to offer the miserable pretenses that lesser souls sacrifice hourly to the god of public opinion.

Conversely, the shy man is humble, modest in his judgment, and excessively concerned with the opinions of others. For a youth, this is perhaps appropriate, as his character is still coalescing from a chaotic void of doubt. As insight and experience grow, this diffidence recedes. Rarely does a man carry his shyness past adolescence. Unless internal strength fails to shed it, the abrasions of worldly contact generally smooth it away. One seldom encounters a truly shy adult, unless in fiction or on the stage, where, by the way, he is highly revered, particularly by the female sex.

In that ethereal realm, the shy hero appears as a fair-haired, saintly youth, for fair hair and goodness are inseparable in theatrical lore. No discerning audience could accept a villain without this aesthetic. I recall an actor who misplaced his wig and proceeded in his natural jet-black hair, prompting the gallery to roar at his noble sentiments, mistaking him for the antagonist. This shy young man loves the heroine with a devotion expressed only in asides, for he dares not speak it aloud. He is portrayed as noble and selfless, speaking softly and being tender toward his mother. The villains mock and jeer, yet he accepts their taunts with gentle grace. Ultimately, it is revealed that he is a man of immense intelligence, unknown to all, and the heroine declares her love, leaving him astonished and blissful. Everyone adores him, begging forgiveness, which he grants with a few chosen, sarcastic words, blessing them. It seems he enjoys such a delightful existence that all non-shy lads dream of emulating him. But the genuinely shy man knows better. He understands that reality lacks the charm of fiction. He is slightly clumsier, stupider, less devoted, and possesses much darker hair—an alteration that significantly modifies the prognosis.

Where the shy man resembles his fictional ideal is in his fidelity. I grant him this virtue: he is constant in love. The reason is not distant; it exhausts his entire supply of courage to confront a single woman, let alone endure the ordeal twice. He stands in such dread of the entire female sex that he finds it impossible to court many. One is sufficient for him.
