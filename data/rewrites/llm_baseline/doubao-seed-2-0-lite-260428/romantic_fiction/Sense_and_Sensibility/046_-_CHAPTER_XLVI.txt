Marianne’s illness, for all its cruel weakening grip, had not lasted long enough to drag her recovery out into months of tedium. Buoyed by the fire of youth, her naturally strong constitution, and the comfort of her mother’s presence by her bedside, she mended so smoothly that just four days after Mrs. Dashwood’s arrival at Cleveland, she was well enough to be helped across to settle in Mrs. Palmer’s sunlit dressing room. There, at her own urgent request—she could not bear to wait another hour to pour out her gratitude to the man who had ridden through the dark, stormy night to fetch her mother—Colonel Brandon was invited to come see her.

When he stepped through the door and laid eyes on her altered, gaunt face, and took the pale, small hand she stretched out to him at once, the raw emotion shaking his frame struck Elinor as something far deeper than simple fondness for Marianne, or even awareness that his attachment to her was known to the family. She soon spotted the melancholy lingering in his eyes, the faint shifts of color across his cheeks as he gazed at her sister, and guessed the ghosts of old, unspoken miseries were crowding back to him. Marianne, hollow-eyed, pale, reclining weakly, so clearly clinging to him as her saviour now, bore an even stronger resemblance to the lost Eliza he had grieved for so many years.

Mrs. Dashwood, no less watchful of the scene unfolding before her than Elinor, read it through a far softer, far more hopeful lens. She saw nothing in the Colonel’s demeanor but the sweet, uncomplicated stirrings of a quiet, loyal heart, and every soft word and grateful glance from Marianne she chose to interpret as the first dawn of something far more than gratitude between them.

A day or two later, when Marianne grew visibly stronger with every passing hour, Mrs. Dashwood, spurred on by her own eagerness to get her daughter home and Marianne’s quiet yearning for Barton, began to make plans for their departure. Mrs. Jennings could not in good conscience leave Cleveland while the Dashwoods were still her guests, so the Colonel, at their combined gentle urging, quickly agreed he too would stay on at the estate a little longer. In return, he and Mrs. Jennings persuaded Mrs. Dashwood to take his carriage for the journey back, to spare Marianne every possible jolt and discomfort on the road; and when Mrs. Dashwood and Mrs. Jennings jointly invited him to visit Barton Cottage in a few weeks’ time, he accepted the offer with unmasked, quiet joy.

The day of their departure came at last. Marianne bade Mrs. Jennings a long, tender, tearful goodbye, full of earnest gratitude and earnest apologies for all the unthinking, ungrateful coldness she had shown the older woman in the throes of her grief. To Colonel Brandon, she said farewell with the warm, unreserved cordiality of an old, dear friend, and he was the one to carefully help her climb into the carriage, fidgeting slightly to make sure she had at least half the space to herself to rest comfortably. Mrs. Dashwood and Elinor climbed in after her, and the remaining three were left standing in the drive, talking of the travellers and already feeling the dull quiet their absence left behind, until Mrs. Jennings was called off to her own carriage to be cheered by her maid’s endless gossip, and Colonel Brandon turned his horse’s head to ride alone back to his empty home at Delaford.

The Dashwoods travelled for two days to reach Barton, and Marianne bore every mile far better than anyone had dared hope. Elinor and her mother tended to her every need with a love so zealous it bordered on vigilance, and both were rewarded by her steady physical ease and the new, soft calm settled over her spirits. For Elinor, that quiet composure was a gift beyond price: after weeks of watching Marianne suffer in secret, crushed under a heartbreak she had neither the courage to speak of nor the strength to hide, she now watched her with a joy no one else in the world could match, certain that this new calm, born of hard-won serious reflection, would soon bloom into lasting contentment.

As they drew close to Barton, though, every field and every familiar tree lining the road stirred a dozen sharp, old memories, and Marianne fell silent and thoughtful, turning her face away from them to gaze intently out the carriage window. Elinor did not wonder, did not blame her at all; when she helped Marianne down from the carriage and saw her cheeks streaked with fresh tears, she felt nothing but tender pity for an emotion so entirely natural, so unobtrusive and gentle that it deserved nothing less than praise.

Every moment after they stepped inside the cottage showed Elinor that Marianne’s mind had turned itself to steady, reasonable exertion. No sooner had they entered their shared sitting room than Marianne looked slowly around every corner of it, her jaw set with resolute firmness, as if determined to accustom herself all at once to every object that still held a memory of Willoughby. She said little, but every word she spoke was aimed at cheer, and though a sigh slipped past her lips now and then, she always followed it quickly with a small, determined smile. After dinner, she insisted on trying to play her piano. She walked over to it, but the first sheet of music her eyes fell on was the opera Willoughby had brought her once, filled with the duets they had sung together, her name scrawled on the cover in his bold handwriting. She shook her head, set it aside, ran her fingers over the keys for a minute, complained that her fingers were still too weak to play, and closed the instrument again—though not before stating firmly that she would begin practicing regularly very soon.

The next morning brought no fading of those hopeful signs. Rest had strengthened both her body and her mind, and she looked and spoke with bright, genuine spirit, chattering happily about Margaret’s imminent return, about the dear little family circle they would soon have restored, about the long walks they would take and the quiet, cheerful days ahead that were all she could wish for happiness.
“When the weather warms and I have all my strength back,” she said, “we will walk for hours every day. We will go down to the farm on the edge of the down to check on the little children, we will visit Sir John’s new plantations at Barton Cross and the old abbey lands, we will hike up to the Priory ruins and trace out every line of its old foundations, just as the villagers say they once stood. I know this summer will be happy. I will never get up later than six o’clock, and from then until dinner I will divide every hour between music and reading. I’ve made my plan, and I’m going to spend my days on serious, worthy study. Our little home library is already far too familiar to me to give me anything but idle amusement, but there are dozens of excellent books at the Park, and plenty of new works I know Colonel Brandon will lend me. If I read only six hours a day, I will gain all the knowledge I now feel I am missing by the end of the year.”

Elinor honored the noble, earnest spirit that had birthed this plan, even as she smiled softly to see that same eager, impulsive fancy that had once led Marianne to wallow in languid indolence and selfish repining now pushing her to throw herself into virtuous self-improvement with equal excess. But her smile soon faded into a sigh. She remembered that Willoughby’s full, damning confession was still unshared, and feared that the news might shatter Marianne’s new hard-won peace, ruin this bright, hopeful prospect of quiet, busy tranquility, at least for a little while. She resolved to delay telling her the truth, to wait until Marianne’s health was more fully mended.

But that resolution would not hold. It was two or three days before the weather cleared enough for Marianne, still so frail, to venture outside the cottage. Then a soft, sun-warmed morning dawned, mild enough to tempt Marianne’s restlessness and earn her mother’s full confidence, and Marianne, leaning on Elinor’s arm, was allowed to walk along the lane in front of the house for as long as she could go without tiring.

The sisters set off at a slow, gentle pace, slow enough to match Marianne’s still-shaky strength after so long confined to bed. They had only walked far enough from the house to get a full view of the steep hill behind the cottage when Marianne paused, turning her eyes toward it, and spoke softly, calmly:
“There, right there,” she said, pointing with one hand to a small grassy mound jutting out from the slope, “that is where I fell. That is where I first saw Willoughby.” Her voice dropped almost to a whisper, but after a breath she lifted it again. “I’m so thankful that I can look at that spot now and feel so little pain. Elinor… shall we ever talk about him properly? Is that wrong? I think I can talk of it now, the way I ought to.”

Elinor pulled her close, and told her tenderly that she could speak of anything she wanted.
“As for regret,” Marianne said, “I’ve laid all that aside, at least where he is concerned. I don’t want to tell you all about how I felt for him back then. I want to talk about how I feel now. Right now, I would be completely at peace if I could be sure of just one thing: that he wasn’t always pretending, that he wasn’t always lying to me. More than anything, I need to know he was never as wicked as I feared he was, after I heard the story about that poor unfortunate girl.” She stopped short, her throat tight.

Elinor’s heart leaped with quiet joy, and she answered gently: “If you were sure of that, you think you could rest easy?”
“Yes. My peace of mind matters doubly here. It is not just horrifying to imagine that someone I once loved so deeply could be capable of such cruelty—it makes me ashamed of myself, Elinor. What but a foolish, unguarded, reckless affection could have led me to expose myself to all that pain?”
“Then how do you explain his behaviour, all those strange choices he made?” Elinor asked her.
“I would rather believe he was only fickle,” Marianne said, her voice soft. “Oh, how gladly I would believe he was just very, very fickle.”

Elinor held her tongue for a minute, debating whether to tell her the full truth right then, or wait until she was stronger. They walked on in silence for a few minutes.
“I don’t wish him any harm,” Marianne sighed at last. “I only hope his own private thoughts are no more unpleasant than mine. He will suffer enough in them, I think.”
“Are you comparing your conduct to his?” Elinor asked.
“No,” Marianne shook her head. “I am comparing my conduct to what it ought to have been. I am comparing it to yours.”
“Our situations were nothing alike,” Elinor said.
“They were far more alike than our behaviour,” Marianne said, squeezing her sister’s hand. “Don’t, my dearest Elinor. Don’t be kind enough to defend the mistakes I know your judgment must condemn. My illness changed me. It gave me quiet, calm leisure to think back on everything. Long before I was well enough to speak, I was well enough to reflect. I saw that every choice I made, ever since we met Willoughby last autumn, was nothing but imprudence that hurt me, and cruelty that hurt everyone around me. My own wild, ungoverned feelings brought all that suffering down on my own head, and my refusal to bear that pain with fortitude almost drove me to my grave. I brought this illness on myself, through my own reckless disregard for my health, even when I knew I was being foolish. If I had died, it would have been self-destruction. I never knew how close I came until the danger was past. And with all these thoughts weighing on me, I wonder that I recovered at all. I wonder that my desperate desire to live long enough to make amends to God, and to all of you, did not kill me on the spot. If I had died, what agony I would have left you in—my Elinor, who nursed me through every fretful, selfish day of my grief, who knew every miserable murmur of my heart! How would you have borne the memory of me? And our mother, how would you ever have consoled her? I cannot tell you how much I loathe the foolish, selfish girl I was. Everywhere I look back, I see some duty I neglected, some small failing I indulged. I was ungrateful to Mrs. Jennings, whose kindness never wavered. I was insolent and unjust to the Middletons, the Palmers, the Steeles, even every casual acquaintance. I hardened my heart against their kindness, and let my temper snap at them for paying me any attention at all. Even to John and Fanny, I gave less than even they deserved. But you—you above everyone, even above my own mother—were the one I wronged most. I, and only I, knew how heavy your own heart was, how much sorrow you carried. But did that make me kinder to you? Did it make me take example from your forbearance, ease your burdens, share in all the small courteous duties you had been carrying alone? No. No, not even when I knew you were unhappy did I ever lift a finger to help. I refused to see that anyone else could grieve, I mourned only for the heart that had deserted me, and left you—who I claimed to love more than anything—to grieve for me.”

She stopped, breathless, her tears spilling over, and Elinor pulled her close to soothe her, praising her frankness and her contrition with all the honesty that sisterly love could offer. Marianne squeezed her hand back.
“You are so good to me,” she whispered. “The future is all the proof I can give you now. I have made my plans, and if I can stick to them, I will govern my feelings, I will soften my temper. I will never again be the sort of person who torments herself and worries everyone she loves. I will live only for my family. You, Mama, Margaret—you will be my whole world, and my love will belong entirely to you three. I will never want to leave my home again, and if I do go out into other company, it will only be to show that my spirit is humbled, my heart is mended, that I can practice all the small gentle civilities of life with kindness and forbearance. As for Willoughby… it would be foolish to say I will ever forget him. No change of circumstances, no new opinions, can ever erase that memory. But I will govern it. I will keep it in check with my faith, my reason, and the steady work that fills my days.” She paused, and added very softly, “If I could only know his heart, though… everything would be easy.”

Elinor, who had been teetering on the edge of the decision for days, heard those words and made up her mind. There was no more waiting. She wove her story gently, carefully, preparing Marianne for every new piece of news, relating Willoughby’s full apology and confession simply and honestly, doing justice to the sincerity of his repentance, only softening the parts where he still swore he loved Marianne.

Marianne did not speak a single word. She trembled all over, her eyes fixed to the ground, her lips paler than even her illness had made them. A thousand questions rose in her throat, but she dared not utter a single one. She hung on every syllable Elinor spoke, her hand clutching her sister’s so tight her knuckles went white, and tears streamed steadily down her cheeks.

Fearing she would tire herself out, Elinor led her slowly back toward the cottage, talking all the while of Willoughby, of all the small, tender details of his confession, careful to share every safe, gentle memory she could. The moment they stepped through the front door, Marianne kissed Elinor’s cheek, and breathed two words almost too quiet to hear through her tears: “Tell Mama.” Then she pulled away, and walked slowly up the stairs to the quiet solitude of her room.

Elinor did not dare disturb her. Her mind was already turning over what she would say when Marianne came back down, already resolved to revisit the subject gently if her sister did not bring it up first, as she turned toward the parlour to fulfill the last request her sister had whispered to her.
