The signing of the will was over much sooner than I had expected—hurried, almost rushed, in a way that felt disrespectful. Samuel, the footman, was summoned to serve as second witness, and without delay, the pen was placed in my aunt’s hand. A moment passed before I could gather myself to offer a few suitable words on such a solemn occasion. But Mr. Bruff’s expression warned me against it—he seemed to expect silence, and I wisely held back.

In less than two minutes, it was done. Samuel, untouched by any spiritual reflection I might have offered, retreated downstairs. Mr. Bruff folded the will and glanced my way, clearly wondering whether I intended to leave him alone with my aunt. But I had my mission—and my bag, filled with carefully chosen books, rested securely on my lap. He might as well have tried to move St. Paul’s Cathedral with his gaze as hope to move me from my purpose.

There was one thing I could not deny about him—his sharpness. He saw through things quickly, no doubt shaped by years of worldly experience. And just as he did, so too did the cabman earlier: both reacted with a muttered oath and withdrew swiftly, leaving me in full command of the situation.

As soon as we were alone, my aunt settled onto the sofa and, with some hesitation, brought up the subject of her will. “I hope you won’t feel neglected, Drusilla,” she said gently. “I mean to give you your little legacy myself—personally.”

Ah! The perfect moment had arrived. I seized it at once. Without hesitation, I opened my bag and pulled out the top volume—an early edition, only the twenty-fifth printing—of the renowned anonymous work attributed to Miss Bellows, titled *The Serpent at Home*. For those unfamiliar, the book’s central theme is how evil lurks in the most innocent daily routines. Among its most compelling chapters are “Satan in the Hair Brush,” “Satan behind the Looking Glass,” “Satan under the Tea Table,” “Satan out of the Window”—and many others.

“Please pay attention, dear aunt,” I said, handing her the open book to a marked passage—“Satan among the Sofa Cushions.” It was a powerful, impassioned section, bursting with truth.

Lady Verinder glanced at it briefly, then handed it back, more troubled than before. “I’m afraid, Drusilla,” she said, “I must wait until I’m feeling better before reading anything like that. The doctor…”

At the mention of the doctor, I knew what would follow. In all my past efforts to reach souls lost in darkness, I had seen this pattern repeated—doctors, steeped in materialism, always stepping in between me and my duty, claiming the patient needed rest, that no disturbing influence—especially mine—was allowed. Now, this same blind medical authority threatened to rob me of my only claim to ownership—my spiritual claim upon my dying aunt.

“The doctor says I’m not well today,” she continued. “He forbids strangers, and insists that if I read at all, it must be light, amusing fare. ‘Do nothing to weary your head or quicken your pulse,’ he told me when he left.”

There was no choice but to yield—for now. To openly challenge the doctor’s authority would only provoke him to threaten withdrawal from her care. But I knew there were other ways to plant the seed of truth. And I was well practiced in them.

“You may feel stronger in an hour or two,” I suggested. “Or perhaps tomorrow morning, you’ll wake with a sense of something missing—and even this humble volume might fill it. May I leave it here? The doctor can hardly object to that.”

I slipped the book beneath the sofa cushions, half in, half out, near her handkerchief and perfume bottle. Every time her hand reached for either, it would brush against the book. And who could say when that touch might lead to a deeper awakening?

After making this arrangement, I decided it was wise to withdraw. “I’ll leave you to rest, dear aunt. I’ll call again tomorrow.”

As I spoke, I glanced toward the window—full of flowers in pots and boxes. Lady Verinder adored these living things, often rising to admire their fragrance. A new idea struck me. “Oh! May I take a flower?” I asked—and used the moment to slip unnoticed to the window.

Instead of taking one, I added another—placing a second book from my bag among the geraniums and roses, hidden where it might surprise her later.

Then came the thought: Why not do the same in every room she enters?

I bid farewell and crossed the hall into the library. Samuel, assuming I’d already left, went down without seeing me. On the table lay two of the “light” books the doctor had approved. I covered them immediately with two of my own.

In the breakfast-room, I found the canary singing in its cage—my aunt’s favorite bird, which she fed herself. Groundsel was scattered on the table beneath. I tucked a book among the seeds.

In the drawing-room, music sheets lay open on the piano. I slipped in two more volumes between the pages.

Another book found its place in the back drawing-room, tucked beside unfinished embroidery I knew she worked on.

A small third room opened off the back drawing-room, separated only by curtains. Her plain fan rested on the mantel. I opened my ninth book to a particularly moving passage and placed the fan inside as a marker.

Now came the question: Should I go higher? Risk being discovered in her bedroom, especially if the maid with cap-ribbons was above? But what of that? A true Christian fears no insult.

I ascended, ready for whatever might come. All was quiet—the servants likely enjoying tea. My aunt’s room was at the front. Opposite the bed hung the miniature of my late uncle, Sir John. It seemed to smile, as if whispering, “Drusilla, leave a book.”

On either side of the bed stood tables. She slept poorly and often needed things during the night. I placed a book near the matches on one side, another beneath the box of chocolate drops on the other. Whether she reached for light or sweetness, a precious volume would meet her hand—or her eye—with silent urgency: “Try me. Try me.”

Only one book remained. One room unexplored—the bathroom, connected to the bedroom. I peered in. Then, a voice within me whispered: “You’ve met her everywhere else. Meet her at the bath—and the work is complete.”

I saw a dressing-gown draped over a chair. It had a pocket. Into that pocket I placed my final book.

Can words convey the joy I felt as I slipped out unseen, bag empty, walking into the street? Oh, my friends who chase pleasure through the tangled paths of sin—how simple happiness is, if only you choose goodness!

That night, folding my belongings, I reflected on the real wealth I had distributed—from the highest room to the lowest corner of my aunt’s home. I felt lighter than ever—free from anxiety, as though reborn. So joyful was I that I sang a verse of the Evening Hymn. Then I fell asleep before I could sing another. Like a child again. Quite like a child again.

The next morning, I awoke feeling astonishingly young—though I made no comment on my appearance, for such matters are fleeting.

Around luncheon time, I prepared to visit Montagu Square—not for comfort, but to see my aunt. As I put on my bonnet, the maid at my lodgings appeared. “Lady Verinder’s servant to see Miss Clack.”

I lived on the parlour floor—a small, low-ceilinged room, poorly furnished, yet spotless. I stepped into the hall to greet the visitor. It was Samuel, the young footman—fresh-faced, courteous, with a look of openness. I had long felt a spiritual connection with him and wished to speak seriously with him.

I invited him in. He entered, carrying a large parcel. When he set it down, he visibly flinched. “My lady’s love, Miss. And I was to say you’d find a letter inside.”

Having delivered his message, he looked as if he wanted to flee. I detained him with kind questions. Could I see my aunt? No—she had gone out driving. Miss Rachel and Mr. Ablewhite were with her.

I paused. Mr. Godfrey’s charitable duties were far behind. Why was he spending the day in idle leisure?

I stopped him at the door. Miss Rachel was going to a ball that evening. Mr. Ablewhite was to join her for coffee. A concert was advertised for the next day, and Samuel was to secure tickets for a large party—including Mr. Ablewhite.

“All the tickets may be gone, Miss,” he said, “if I don’t run right away!”

And off he went. I was left alone, thoughts swirling.

That evening, our Mothers’-Small-Clothes-Conversion Society had a special meeting, called specifically to seek Mr. Godfrey’s help. Instead, he planned to attend a ball and a concert—amusements utterly contrary to his role as our spiritual leader.

What did it mean? Alas—it meant I would see him in a new light, linked forevermore to one of the most profound spiritual failures of our age.

But let us return to the events of that day.

Alone in my room, I turned to the parcel Samuel had left. Had my aunt sent my promised legacy? Perhaps old clothes, worn silver, outdated jewelry? I accepted anything with grace. I opened it—and stared.

Inside were the twelve books I had placed throughout her house the previous day—returned, all of them, by the doctor’s orders.

No wonder Samuel had trembled. No wonder he fled after delivering it.

The letter from my aunt was brief: she dared not disobey her physician.

What now?

With my principles and training, I never hesitated. Once committed to a mission, a true Christian does not yield. Taxation, riots, wars—none can deter us. We act beyond reason, beyond ridicule. We see, hear, feel only through our own convictions.

Glorious privilege—earned only by those who are always right.

For my aunt, the next step was clear. Clerical preparation failed due to her reluctance. Books failed due to the doctor’s interference. So—what remained?

Preparation by Little Notes.

That is: extracts from my books, copied by different hands, addressed as letters to my aunt—some to be mailed, others placed around the house as I had done before. As letters, they would arouse no suspicion. They would be opened. And once opened, perhaps read.

Some I wrote myself:
“Dear Aunt, may I ask your attention to a few lines?”
“Dear Aunt, I was reading last night and came across this passage…”

Others were penned by my fellow workers at the Mothers’-Small-Clothes Society:
“Dear Madam, pardon the interest of a humble, true friend.”
“Dear Madam, may a serious person surprise you with a few cheering words?”

By evening, I had twelve new letters ready—each a spark of truth, disguised as correspondence. Six I arranged to send by post; six I kept for personal delivery the next day.

Shortly after two o’clock, I returned to Montagu Square. I greeted Samuel again, asking after my aunt. She had endured a restless night and was resting on the sofa in the same room where the will was signed.

I said I would wait in the library, hoping to see her.

In my zeal, I didn’t inquire about Rachel. The house was quiet. The concert had begun hours ago. I assumed Rachel and her companions—Mr. Godfrey included—were already immersed in music and merriment. I focused entirely on my task.

On the library table lay my aunt’s morning mail—including the six letters I had posted. She had not opened them—perhaps overwhelmed by the volume. I placed one of my remaining letters on the chimney-piece, isolated from the rest, to draw her curiosity.

Another, I left deliberately on the floor in the breakfast-room. The first servant entering would assume it had fallen and ensure it reached her.

With the lower floors sown, I moved upstairs to scatter the rest in the drawing-room.

Just as I entered, I heard a soft, hesitant knock at the front door—gentle, polite, almost apologetic.

Before I could retreat to the library (where I was supposed to be), Samuel answered. It mattered little—I assumed visitors were not admitted given my aunt’s condition.

To my horror, the knock was answered.

Samuel’s voice echoed below: “Upstairs, if you please, sir.”

Footsteps followed—male footsteps—approaching the drawing-room.

Who could this privileged visitor be?

Almost instantly, I knew: the doctor?

If it were anyone else, I would have stayed. But I could not face the man who had returned my books.

I slipped into the small third room, closed the curtains, and waited.

Minutes passed. I heard the visitor pacing, speaking to himself. I thought I recognized the voice.

Was it not the doctor? Or someone else—Mr. Bruff?

No. An instinct told me otherwise.

He continued talking. I parted the curtains slightly and listened.

The words: “I’ll do it today.”

And the voice—clear, unmistakable—was Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite’s.
