The silence that followed the tragedy at the Hoog Straet was heavier than the storm that had battered the house. For Catherine, usually a whirlwind of activity, the quiet felt like a tomb. With eyes swollen from weeping, she ventured to the convent the following afternoon, hoping to find Gerard and begin the arduous path toward reconciliation. Yet, the gatekeeper offered only a grim report: Gerard had departed the previous night under the cover of darkness and showed no sign of returning soon.

Defeated, Catherine returned home to find Margaret sitting in a stupor, clutching their young son Elias so tightly it seemed she feared losing him to the world itself. Life had lost its flavor for Margaret, who sat like a statue carved from grief. Persuading her weary companion to visit Gerard proved difficult, but eventually, Margaret yielded. "If you think I can do any good," she murmured, "I will come."

Upon reaching the carpenter's yard, they discovered a new name board being installed where Cornelis and Sybrandt's had stood. To their shock, the name read *Margaret Brandt*. Though Catherine harbored deep love for her stepmother, the sight cut her deeply. "The bane of one is another's meat," she whispered bitterly. Margaret, however, remained cold and defensive, asking if Catherine believed she squandered funds unjustly. "You are a good soul," Catherine admitted, noting how the truth often favored the strongest.

Later, Giles arrived, eager to champion their side. He listened to Catherine's tale of woe—how they were anathematized by their brother and cast adrift by their father—but his own prejudices ran counter to hers. He scoffed at the idea that the culprits had escaped punishment lightly, crowing about his influence at court and promising to use it to bring justice back to the Brandts. "I'll show him what it means to have a brother with a heart to serve a friend," Giles declared, his spirit buoyed by the prospect of revenge.

Just as the mood settled back into somber silence, a well-dressed stranger entered, asking quietly for Margaret Brandt. Catherine, her gossip instincts instantly ignited, announced loudly that Margaret was wanted. A pale, frail elderly man was ushered inside: Ghysbrecht Van Swieten. At the sight of him, Catherine shrieked and covered her face, while Margaret turned away in horror, trembling violently.

From the shadows, a weak voice emerged. "Good people, a dying man hath come to ask your forgiveness."

"It is to look upon your work," Catherine retorted, dropping her apron as fresh sobs racked her frame. "She is fainting!" Eli stepped forward, his voice shaking with a mix of anger and shame. "Burgomaster, 'tis not our wont to leave a visitor standing... but man, man, you have wrought us too much ill."

Amidst the tension, little Kate stepped forward. "Father, mother," she pleaded gently, "Death squares all accounts. See you not death in his face? I shall not live long, and his time is shorter still." Her wisdom softened the atmosphere. Eli quickly arranged a chair, and Ghysbrecht's attendants helped him sit. Two wooden boxes were brought forward, leaving the intruder alone in the midst of those he had cruelly injured.

With unsteady fingers, Ghysbrecht opened the lids. From the first, he produced the title deeds to property in Tergou. "These lands and houses belonged to Floris Brandt," he confessed, tears streaming down his cheeks. "They were usurped for a debt long since paid with interest. I restore them to their rightful owner, his granddaughter, with penitent tears." He then revealed a second box containing three hundred and forty golden angels—the rents and fines he had collected, which he had meticulously accounted for, intending justice despite his nature. "Avarice withheld me," he sobbed, "against temptation! I was not born dishonest, yet you see."

"Well, to be sure!" Catherine exclaimed, her greed momentarily overriding her sorrow. "And you the burgomaster! Whipped many a thief in your day. 'Tis better late than never. What, Margaret, art thou deaf? The good man hath brought thee back thine own. Thou art a rich woman!"

"But bid him keep the land and gold," Margaret interjected, her voice hollow, "and give me back my Gerard, whom he stole from me with treason."

"Alas!" Ghysbrecht cried, "Would I could! What I can I have done. It cost me a sore struggle; I rose from my last bed to do this myself, lest some mischance prevent her from receiving her rights."

"Old man," Margaret said, her gaze fixed on the floor, "since thou, whose idol is wealth, hast done this, may God and the saints forgive thee. As for me, I am neither saint nor angel, but a poor woman whose heart thou hast broken. Speak to him, Kate, for I am like the dead."

Kate, the family oracle, meditated silently before speaking. Her soft, silvery voice flowed like a soothing melody. "My poor sister hath a sorrow that riches cannot heal. Give her time, Ghysbrecht. 'Tis not in nature she should forgive thee all. Her boy is fatherless, and she is neither maid, wife, nor widow. The blow fell but two days since, laying her heart a-bleeding."

A single, heavy sob echoed from Margaret, acknowledging the truth. Kate continued, "Therefore, give her time! Ere thou diest, she will forgive thee all, yea, even to please me, who may not long survive thee. Meanwhile, we, whose wounds are sore but not so deep as hers, do pardon thee, a penitent and a dying man. I, for one, will pray for thee henceforth. Go in peace!"

Her words were sufficient. Even Eli, usually stern, invited Ghysbrecht to share bread and wine to strengthen him for his journey. But Ghysbrecht declined. "What I have done is a cordial to me," he explained, his eyes clear with relief. "This land I clung to so dearly was a bed of nettles all along. 'Tis gone, and I feel happier and livelier for the loss."

As Ghysbrecht was carried away on a litter, Catherine gazed upon the pile of gold with undisguised joy. She had never seen so much wealth at once and chided Margaret for sitting like an image, refusing to acknowledge the treasure. Despite Kate's gentle plea that Margaret's heart was like lead, Catherine persisted until Margaret finally approached the money.

Margaret stared down at the wealth with wide, wondering eyes. Suddenly, she wrung her hands, her composure shattering into piercing anguish. "Too late! Too late!" she cried, collapsing into hysterics. The gold and land, though rightfully hers, arrived far too late to alter the course of her life. If only a fraction of this fortune, or the land itself, had been hers a year or two prior, Gerard might never have abandoned her side to seek adventure in Italy. Instead, the riches stood as a monument to a love lost forever, echoing the tragic refrain: "Too late! Too late!"
