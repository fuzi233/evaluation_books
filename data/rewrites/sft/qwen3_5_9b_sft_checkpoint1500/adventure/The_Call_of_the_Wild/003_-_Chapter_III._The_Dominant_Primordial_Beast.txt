Within Buck, a primal force surged, swelling with the harsh realities of the trail. It was a secret growth, invisible to others, forging a cunning poise that replaced his initial confusion with a deliberate, terrifying control. He learned quickly to avoid conflict, sensing the danger of rashness. Yet, standing opposite him was Spitz, a rival who sensed the threat and refused to let go. Where Buck practiced restraint, Spitz offered only teeth, bullying relentlessly and seeking the fatal collision that would decide their fate. For weeks, this tension simmered, interrupted only by the whims of fortune.

That change arrived on a bleak, freezing night by Lake Le Barge. A driving wind, sharp as a white-hot blade, and a perpendicular wall of rock had forced Perrault and François to abandon their tent, setting camp directly on the ice of the frozen lake. Their fire, built from scant driftwood, melted holes through the ice beneath them, leaving them to eat supper in the dark. Close to the sheltering rock, Buck had constructed a nest, a sanctuary of warmth he was reluctant to leave. But when he returned, the intruder had claimed his space. A warning snarl revealed the occupant to be Spitz.

For the first time, Buck's patience shattered. The dormant beast within him roared, and he lunged at Spitz with a fury that startled even his enemies. Spitz, accustomed to seeing Buck as a timid giant who survived only on bulk, recoiled in shock. François, witnessing the chaos erupt from the disrupted nest, cried out to Buck, "Give it to him!" The atmosphere shifted instantly. Both rivals circled, eager and cautious, waiting for the opening. But before the strike could fall, pandemonium broke out.

A crash of a club against bone and a shrill yelp signaled the arrival of scores of starving huskies, scavengers who had tracked the scent of food from a distant Indian village. While Buck and Spitz fought, the intruders had slipped in unnoticed. Now awakened, the men swarmed the camp with clubs, beating back the frenzied, hollow-eyed brutes who were driven mad by hunger. The team-dogs, bursting from their nests, were immediately assailed by this horde of skeletal monsters. Never had Buck seen such creatures; they were loose skin stretched over bone, their eyes blazing with a supernatural, ravenous intensity.

Swept back against the cliff, the team was overwhelmed. Buck was beset by three, his head and shoulders ripped and slashed in moments. The din was horrific. Billee, usually gentle, screamed in terror; Dave and Sol-leks fought with bloody jaws; Joe snapped like a demon, crushing a foreleg through bone. Pike, the malingerer, leaped onto a crippled attacker and broke its neck. Buck, locked in a grapple, tasted the warm blood of a jugular slash, a flavor that fueled a new, darker fierceness. Just as he threw himself onto another, teeth sank into his own throat. It was Spitz, attacking from the flank.

Perrault and François, having cleared the immediate area, rushed to save their sled-team. The wild wave of beasts rolled back before them, and Buck finally shook himself free. But the reprieve lasted only seconds. Compelled to return to the grub box, the men left the huskies to resume their assault. Terrified into a frenzy, Billee fled over the ice, followed by Pike and Dub, dragging the rest of the team in a chaotic rout. As Buck rallied to join the flight, he saw Spitz rush him, intent on toppling him into the mass of attackers. Bracing for the impact, Buck absorbed Spitz's charge and bolted, joining the desperate scramble across the frozen lake.

Later, the survivors limped into the forest, a sorry procession of wounded dogs. Every single one bore multiple injuries, some grievous. Dub's hind leg was shattered; Dolly's throat was torn; Joe had lost an eye; Billee's ear hung in ribbons. By daybreak, they shuffled back to camp to find the marauders gone and the men in a foul mood. Half their provisions were gone, eaten along with leather traces, moccasins, and the tip of François's whip. The men cursed, realizing the cost of the raid, but two hours of frantic labor restored the harnesses, and the suffering team trudged forward.

The terrain ahead was the Thirty Mile River, a stretch of wild, unfrozen water that defied the frost, save for the quiet eddies where ice clung. For six days, Perrault and François pushed the team through this nightmare, risking life with every step. The thermometer registered fifty degrees below zero. Perrault, a man of iron nerve, broke thin ice bridges repeatedly, using his long pole to stay aloft, often stopping to build fires to dry his clothes and prevent hypothermia. The sled broke through once, plunging Dave and Buck into icy water until they were nearly drowned, requiring hours of sweating over a fire to thaw. Again, Spitz dragged the team through a breach, forcing Buck to strain with all his strength as Dave and François pulled with cracking tendons. Finally, the rim ice collapsed completely. Perrault climbed the vertical cliff by a miracle, François followed, and they hoisted the sled and dogs up one by one, stranded on the river with only a quarter-mile credit for the day.

By the time they reached the Hootalinqua and found solid ice, Buck was utterly exhausted. The remaining dogs were similarly spent, yet Perrault pushed them harder to recover lost time. They covered thirty-five miles to the Big Salmon, then another thirty-five to the Little Salmon, and forty miles more toward the Five Fingers. Here, the toll on Buck became apparent. Unlike the hard-footed huskies, Buck's paws had softened over generations of domestication. Limping in agony all day, he collapsed at camp, refusing his ration of fish. Each night, François spent half an hour rubbing Buck's feet, sacrificing the tops of his own moccasins to fashion four new ones for Buck. This kindness brought relief, even making Perrault smile when François forgot the moccasins and Buck waved his feet in helpless appeal.

At the Pelly, disaster struck anew. As they harnessed up, Dolly, a dog previously inconspicuous, went suddenly mad. A heartbreaking howl sent the team bristling, and she charged straight at Buck. Panic seized him; he had never seen madness before, yet he recognized the horror and fled. Dolly pursued him, panting and frothing, unable to catch him due to his terror, nor could he escape her due to her insanity. He raced through the woods, down to the river, across frozen channels, and back to the main stream, all while hearing her snarl one leap behind. François, holding his axe poised, watched as Buck shot past. The axe crashed down on mad Dolly's head.

Buck staggered against the sled, sobbing for breath, helpless. This was Spitz's moment. He sprang upon Buck, tearing flesh to bone twice before François's lash descended. Buck watched with satisfaction as Spitz received the worst whipping yet administered. "One devil, dat Spitz," Perrault observed. "Some dam day heem keel dat Buck." "Dat Buck two devils," François replied confidently. "Lissen: some dam fine day heem get mad lak hell an' den heem chew dat Spitz all up."

From then on, it was open war. Spitz, the acknowledged leader, felt his supremacy threatened by this strange, enduring Southern dog. Buck was different from the soft Southland dogs Spitz had known; he endured the toil, the frost, and starvation, matching the husky in strength and savagery. What made Buck truly dangerous was not his blind pluck, but the cunning instilled by the club of the man in the red sweater. He could wait, biding his time with a patience that was almost primitive.

The clash for leadership became inevitable. Buck desired it because it was his nature, bound by the ancient pride of the trail—the pride that holds dogs to the last gasp, that lures them to die joyfully in the harness, and breaks their hearts if they are cut out. This pride transformed them from sullen brutes at break of camp into straining, eager creatures. It was the pride that drove Spitz to thrash the slackers and made him fear Buck as a potential usurper.

Buck openly challenged Spitz's authority, stepping between the leader and the guilty dogs. One night, following a heavy snowfall, Pike the malingerer did not appear. Hidden under a foot of snow, he trembled until Spitz, wild with wrath, began sniffing and digging everywhere, snarling so fiercely that Pike shivered. When Pike was finally unearthed and Spitz flew at him to punish him, Buck intervened. So shrewdly managed was the maneuver that Spitz was hurled backward. Pike, emboldened by this open mutiny, sprang upon his overthrown leader. Buck, forgetting any code of fair play, jumped on Spitz as well. But François, chuckling at the incident, brought his lash down on Buck with all his might. The blow stunned Buck, knocking him backward, and the lash laid upon him again and again while Spitz soundly punished Pike.
