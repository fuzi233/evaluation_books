The afternoon sun poured through the windows of the Rectory, illuminating dust motes that danced like tiny, chaotic spirits. Inside Pen's study, the Curate, Mr. Smirke, sat pretending to read with his pupil, while outside, the air was thick with the scent of blooming flowers and the promise of a delightful walk. Mrs. Portman, having finished her business with Mrs. Pybus, decided the weather was simply too fine to ignore. She strolled toward Fairoaks to visit her dear friend, carrying with her a secret that would soon unravel the delicate social fabric of the neighborhood.

In the course of their conversation, Mrs. Portman revealed a scandalous truth about Mr. Smirke: he harbored an ancient, hidden attachment. "And on whom is it that Mr. Smirke has bestowed his heart?" Mrs. Pendennis asked, her face a mask of superior composure that barely hid her internal alarm.

"Why, my dear," Mrs. Portman replied, leaning in conspiratorially, "when he first arrived, everyone whispered he was destined for Myra. We were forced to withdraw our inquiries. Then rumors swirled of another interest, but I always contradicted them, insisting it was you—"

"That I!" Mrs. Pendennis cried, feigning outrage. "People are dreadfully impertinent. Mr. Smirke came here solely as Arthur's tutor."

"Pon my soul, it is a little too much," the Major interjected, lowering his newspaper and double eyeglass. "I've no patience with Mrs. Pybus," Helen added indignantly.

Mrs. Portman confirmed the gossip: Mr. Smirke had been secretly engaged to Miss Thompson of Clapham Common for years. She expressed delight, noting that an unmarried curate was often an awkward presence in a household, and promised strict confidentiality.

Mrs. Pendennis, with perfect sincerity, professed her gladness for the news, hoping the kind man would find a deserving wife. Once the visitor departed, Helen and her brother discussed the matter with satisfaction. Helen chastised herself for her recent haughty behavior toward the Curate, regretting that she hadn't appreciated his constant kindness to Arthur.

"Gratitude to this sort of person is well enough," the Major grumbled, "but familiarity breeds contempt. This gentleman teaches and takes his money like any other master. There must be distinctions in ranks."

Helen, however, felt differently. Now that Arthur was leaving, she recalled how polite Mr. Smirke had been—bringing books, copying music, teaching Laura, and offering thoughtful gifts. Her heart ached with guilt. When the Curate emerged from the study, looking eager to leave, she summoned him to her drawing-room, a place he rarely visited. With a slightly flushed face, she invited him to dinner, a summons he accepted with boundless joy.

During the meal, Helen was exceptionally gracious, perhaps overcompensating because the Major treated his nephew's tutor with icy disdain, addressing him as if he were a sovereign speaking to a lowly retainer. Pen laughed at his uncle's condescension, though he was equally prone to his own youthful arrogance. Smirke, oblivious to the Major's impertinence, charmed Helen with a blend of clerical wisdom and worldly wit, discussing bazaars, missionary meetings, and the latest novels. He employed every art to please the object of his affection, proving himself a man of sprightly talent and susceptible heart.

Soon after, the Major yawned out of the dining room. "What an unsufferable bore that man is," he complained. "He talks endlessly."

"He has been very good to Arthur," Mrs. Pendennis noted, wondering who Miss Thompson was.

"I always thought he was looking elsewhere," the Major muttered.

"Where?" Mrs. Pendennis asked innocently. "Toward Myra Portman?"

"Toward Helen Pendennis, if you must know," her brother-in-law replied bluntly.

"Himself! Impossible!" Helen exclaimed, knowing full well the truth. "His marriage will be a happy thing. I hope Arthur won't drink too much."

Arthur, flushed with the pride of managing the cellar, had stocked the table with generous supplies of claret. As the elders retired with little Laura, Pen and Smirke began to drink freely. One bottle vanished quickly; another lost half its contents before an hour had passed. Pen, with a hollow laugh, toasted the falsehood of women, declaring that wine was a mistress who never deceived. Smirke countered that some women were all truth and tenderness, sighing dramatically as he drained his glass.

Pen then recited verses he had composed that morning, lamenting a woman who had slighted his passion and vowing to become a man worthy of his race. His voice trembled with emotion, his honest grey eyes shining with genuine sensitivity. Smirke, moved by his young friend's sentimentality, gripped Pen's hand across the dessert dishes. "You are a poet, Arthur," he declared. "Go on and prosper. When you are famous, will you remember your old tutor?"

"I shall never forget," Pen swore, raising his glass. "You are my best friend."

"Am I, really, Arthur?" Smirke asked, his heart pounding audibly against his ribs.

"My best friend, my friend for ever," Pen replied, finishing the second bottle. "We'll have another! By Jove, we will. My uncle says Sheridan drank five bottles at Brookes's. This is the finest wine in England." Pen began to sing a drinking song from *Der Freischuetz*, his voice ringing through the open windows. Outside, Helen paced the lawn, her heart swelling with pride as she heard her son's sweet notes.

"You are taking too much wine, Arthur," Smirke warned softly.

"No," Pen insisted, "women give headaches, but this doesn't. Tell us about the lady, Smirke. I've seen you sighing for her."

Smirke's cambric shirtfront heaved with emotion. "Here's her health," Pen shouted, forcing a toast. "May she soon be Mrs. Smirke!"

"Do you say so?" Smirke trembled. "Do you really say so, Arthur?"

"Of course! Here's Mrs. Smirke's good health!" Pen cheered, waving his glass overhead until his mother and Laura wondered what was happening.

"I accept the omen," Smirke gasped, blushing furiously. "Oh, my dear Arthur, you—you know her?"

"What—Myra Portman? I wish you joy; she has a devilish large waist, but I wish you joy, old fellow."

"Oh, Arthur!" Smirke groaned, nodding speechlessly.

"Sorry I offended you—but she has a large waist," Pen continued, the third bottle clearly affecting him.

"It's not Miss Portman," Smirke cried in agony.

"Is it someone at Chatteris? Madame Fribsby?" Pen guessed, laughing hysterically.

The Curate could contain no more. "O Pen, how can you suppose any of those beings could influence this heart when I contemplate perfection? For two years, my heart has known no other idol. Haven't I loved you as a son, Arthur?"

"Yes, old boy, you've been very good," Pen said, though his liking was not truly filial.

"My means are limited," Smirke rushed on, "but whatever I inherit—five hundred a year at least—will be settled upon her and you at my death."

"What the deuce do you mean?" Pen asked, puzzled.

"Arthur, you say I am your dearest friend—Let me be more. Oh, can't you see that the angelic being I love is no other than your dear, dear angel of a mother?"

"My mother!" Pen jumped up, sobering instantly. "Pooh! Damn it, Smirke, you must be mad—she's seven or eight years older than you."

"Did you find that any objection?" Smirke wailed.

"The cases are not similar," Pen said, blushing red. "Our positions are very different."

"How do you mean?" Smirke asked sadly.

"I mean what I say," Arthur declared. "My tutor has no right to ask a lady of my mother's rank to marry him. It's a breach of confidence. It's a liberty you take, Smirke!"

"O Arthur!" Smirke cried, clasping his hands, as Arthur stamped his foot and rang the bell for coffee. "Don't let's have any more of this."

John, the butler, entered to serve coffee, glancing in wonder at the three empty claret bottles. Smirke declined to go into the drawing-room. Arthur, with majestic air, dismissed him and ordered his horse. Smirke, knowing the way, fetched his own pony from the stable.

Pen followed him out, holding his hand. "Forgive me if I have said anything harsh," he said in an agitated voice. "But it can't be, old fellow. Be a man. God bless you."

Smirke nodded silently and rode out of the lodge gate. Pen watched until the pony's hooves faded into the distance. Helen lingered on the lawn, brushing hair from her son's forehead and kissing it. "Smirke is unwell," Pen said with a laugh, his usual cheerfulness returning. He put his arm around her waist, walking her up and down the path as Laura laughed from the window.

Later that night, Pen recounted the absurd scene to his mother, describing the dismal yet ludicrous encounter. Helen blushed deeply, while Arthur enjoyed the roguish perplexity of the moment. "Confound the fellow's impudence," the Major muttered as he took his candle. That night, Pen and his mother talked long and deeply, filled with love and laughter, and the boy slept soundly for the first time in months.

Meanwhile, Captain Costigan secured a splendid engagement for his daughter, Miss Fotheringay, and settled his debts with Mr. Dolphin. The Buccaneer club of Chatteris, usually a riotous gathering of local gentlemen, threw a magnificent farewell banquet for the Captain. After speeches of exaggerated patriotism and heartfelt gratitude, the Captain sang his favorite songs, and the evening ended in triumph. The next day, Miss Costigan left a necklace and a white satin gown for her friend Miss Rouncy, and she and her father departed in the Competitor coach, never to be seen by Pendennis again.

Pen learned of their departure only through the county papers. Rushing to Chatteris, he found a "Lodgings to let" card in the familiar window where he and Emily had once gazed at the Dean's garden. He wandered into her empty bedroom, burying his face in the vacant pillow, overwhelmed by a sense of finality and loss. He distributed coins to the servants who had cared for the Costigans, feeling the weight of years passing in mere months.

The entire household felt the shift. Smirke wanted to flee the sight of the "siren widow"; Foker dreamed of supper-parties in Saint Boniface; the Major longed for pheasant shooting; and Helen and Laura nervously packed Pen's trunks. They wrote cards with his name, their tearful eyes reflecting their wistful goodbye.

Days later, the mail coach arrived at Fairoaks, its lamps blazing against the night. Helen and Laura stood by the shrubbery as the carriage wheels crunched over the gravel. In an instant, the lights disappeared, and Helen's prayers followed the departing boy. The house seemed lonelier without him, the empty study echoing with his absence. Laura slept in her mother's room, while Helen knelt by Pen's vacant bed, praying for her son as mothers alone know how to plead. She knew his pure blessings were following him, miles away, as he tried his restless wings in the vast world beyond.
