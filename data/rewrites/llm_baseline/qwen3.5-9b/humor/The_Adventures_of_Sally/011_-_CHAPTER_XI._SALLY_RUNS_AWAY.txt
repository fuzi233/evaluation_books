If Ginger Kemp had been asked to list his virtues, he likely would have produced a list shorter than a grocery receipt. He might have begun with "good intentions," though pinning that down would require chewing a pencil until the wood splintered. Beyond that, the catalog would remain sparse; certainly, "tact" and "delicacy of feeling" were not items on the menu. Yet, by avoiding Sally for several days, he demonstrated a surprising degree of both. Staying away was no small feat, but Ginger forced himself to do it. He reasoned from his own perspective: in times of turmoil, solitude was the sufferer's greatest need. Having endured his own rough patches, he believed the proper remedy was to sit quietly, brood, and fight one's internal battles alone.

By Saturday, however, Ginger concluded that mere avoidance was no longer sufficient. Saturday was an auspicious day to resume contact. With no office obligations and, crucially, having just received his weekly paycheck, he possessed the means to attempt a grand gesture. Although Mrs. Meecher had wisely deducted a portion of his earnings, enough remained to fund a princely consolation tour. The plan crystallized instantly: hire a car and whisk Sally away to dinner at one of the trendy roadhouses along the Boston Post Road. The more he contemplated the scheme, the more perfect it seemed. This conviction was bolstered by the extraordinary beauty of the weather. Late autumn had revealed America's "Indian Summer" to Ginger, a phenomenon that had completely captivated him. Standing on the roof of Mrs. Meecher's establishment that Saturday morning, intoxicated by the velvet glow of the sunlight, he decided there was only one logical way to spend such a day: take Sally for a drive in an open car.

The Maison Meecher was a towering structure on a side street near the bottom of the avenue. Its rooftop offered a panoramic view once one navigated the grove of laundry hanging limply from lines below. To the left lay Washington Square, teeming with sleepy Italians and children roller-skating; to the right stood a sight that never failed to fascinate Ginger—the towering smokestacks of a Cunard liner drifting slowly down the river, rising above the rooftops as if the vessel were traveling down Ninth Avenue. Today, four funnels signaled the *Mauritania*. Since Ginger had crossed the Atlantic aboard this very ship, it held a deep sentimental value for him. He watched her stately descent until distant buildings obscured her, then wove his way through the drying clothes to retrieve his hat.

A quarter-hour later, Ginger found himself in the hallway of Sally's apartment building, staring with undisguised revulsion at the back of his cousin, Mr. Carmyle, dressed in serge and conversing with a man in overalls. No carefree prospector singing through the Mojave Desert could have suffered such an abrupt mood shift as Ginger did upon witnessing this revoltable scene. Even in their native Piccadilly, encountering Mr. Carmyle had been unpleasant; seeing him here was nauseating. There was only one explanation: he had come to see Sally. With a sinking heart, Ginger recalled the gleaming, expensive automobile idling at the door. Clearly, he was not the only one who had conceived the idea of driving Sally on this golden day.

He remained frozen when Mr. Carmyle turned, his dark face furrowed in a frown suggesting the janitor's chat had been tedious. Seeing Ginger clearly did nothing to lift his gloom.
"Hullo!" said Carmyle.
"Hullo!" replied Ginger.
An uncomfortable silence stretched between them.
"Have you come to see Miss Nicholas?"
"Why, yes."
"She isn't here," said Mr. Carmyle. The relief of sharing the bad news seemed to brighten his demeanor slightly.
"Not here?"
"No. Apparently..." Bruce Carmyle's scowl betrayed the resentment a balanced man feels toward the unpredictability of others. "...Apparently, for some extraordinary reason, she has decided to dash off to England."
Ginger staggered. The blow was crushing. He trailed his cousin into the sunlight in a daze.
"I find I shall not need the car. You may return it to the garage," Carmyle told the driver of the expensive vehicle.
The chauffeur, a sullen figure, cracked one eye open and spat cautiously—a maneuver reminiscent of Rockefeller facing a delicate financial crisis.
"You'll have to pay just the same," he noted, opening his second eye for emphasis.
"Of course I shall pay," snapped Mr. Carmyle irritably. "How much is it?"
Money changed hands, and the car rolled away.
"Gone to England?" Ginger asked, dizzy.
"Yes, gone to England."
"But why?"
"How the devil do I know why?" Bruce Carmyle would have struggled to find a better friend at this moment. Gaping Ginger caused him almost physical pain. "All I know is what the janitor told me: she sailed on the *Mauretania* this morning."
The tragic irony hit Ginger hard. He had stood on the roof, calmly watching the boat depart... He nodded blankly to his cousin and walked away. He had no words left. The warmth had vanished from the sunshine, and all interest had drained from his life. He felt dull, listless, untethered. Even the thought that his frugal cousin had wasted a day's hire on an unused car brought no comfort.

Ginger loafed aimlessly through the streets. He wandered into the Park and back out again. The Park bored him; the streets bored him; the entire city bored him. A city without Sally was a drab, futile place, and no amount of solar brilliance could alter that. Night finally arrived, bringing with it a letter. It was the first genuinely pleasant event of this dreary, unproductive day: the envelope bore the crest of the good ship *Mauretania*. Snatching it from the rack, he carried it upstairs to his room.

Few rooms at Mrs. Meecher's boardinghouse exuded luxury. Mrs. Meecher was not a decorator; she believed adding a Morris chair to the basics constituted the pinnacle of pomp for a guest paying seven-and-a-half dollars a night. Usually, the austerity of his surroundings depressed Ginger, but tonight—thanks to the magic of a letter from the right person—he felt uplifted, almost cheerful. There are moments when even illuminated texts fail to soothe us. Ginger approached his correspondence with ceremony. He behaved like a child anticipating ice cream, lingering before diving into the treat to prolong the pleasure.

His first act was to fish the stolen photograph of Sally from his pocket. He studied it long and earnestly before propping it against his washbasin for reference. He stripped off his coat, collar, and shoes, filled and lit his pipe, set his pouch and matches on the Morris chair, and settled in with his feet on the bed. Once comfortable, he relit his pipe and picked up the letter. He examined the crest, the handwriting, and the postmark. It was a heavy envelope. He retrieved Sally's photo again, scrutinizing it, then wriggled deeper into the chair and tore open the envelope.

"Ginger, dear."

Reading this far, Ginger felt compelled to pick up the photograph again, studying it with renewed intensity. He gazed at it for minutes before laying it down and lighting his pipe once more. Then he continued.

"Ginger, dear—I'm afraid this address is going to shock you, and I feel terribly guilty. I'm running away, and I haven't even stopped to say goodbye. I can't help it. I know it's weak and cowardly, but I simply can't help it. I managed for a day or two, then realized it was useless. (Thank you for leaving me alone and not coming around. Nobody else would have done that. But then, nobody has ever been—or could be—as understanding as you.)"

At this point, Ginger felt forced to look at the photograph again.

"There was too much in New York to remind me. That's the worst part of happiness in a place: when things go wrong, there are too many ghosts. I just couldn't stand it. I tried, but I couldn't. I'm going away to get cured—if I can. Mr. Faucitt is in England. When I went to Mrs. Meecher for my mail, I found a letter from him. His brother has died, and he has inherited, of all things, a fashionable dressmaking shop on Regent Street called *Laurette et Cie*. I suppose he'll sell it later, but right now, the poor old dear seems bewildered, and that hasn't occurred to him. He wrote how much he wished I were there to help, and I was tempted—and I ran. Anything to escape the ghosts and have something to do. I doubt I'll feel much better in England, but at least every street corner won't hold associations. Don't ever be happy anywhere, Ginger. It's too big a risk, much too big a risk."

"There was also a letter from Elsa Doland, bubbling with affection. We've always been tremendous friends. Of course, she didn't know about my engagement to Gerald. I lent Fillmore the money to buy that picture, giving Elsa her first big break, so she's very grateful. She says if she ever gets a chance to do me a good turn..."

"Aren't things muddled?"
