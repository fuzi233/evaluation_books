Margaret walked swiftly to the Higgins’s house that afternoon. Mary waited for her with anxious eyes, half-fearful of what news she might bring. A reassuring smile from Margaret softened the girl’s apprehension, and they passed quickly through the dim house-place, up the narrow stairs, into the quiet chamber where death had laid its claim.

The sight struck Margaret deeply. The face that had once been worn with pain and restless thought now bore the faint, untroubled smile of eternal rest. Tears welled in Margaret’s eyes, yet a strange calm settled over her soul. *This* was death—more peaceful than life itself. Scripture whispered in her mind: *“They rest from their labors… The weary are at rest… He giveth His beloved sleep.”*

Slowly, she turned away. Mary wept quietly behind her. Not a word passed between them as they descended.

Below, Nicholas Higgins stood motionless, his hand braced against the table, his face rigid with shock. His eyes were dry but fierce, grappling with the truth—his daughter was gone. He had clung to hope, convinced she would recover, but death had stolen her while he was away.

Margaret hesitated, feeling an intruder upon his grief. She moved to slip past him, but his fingers clamped around her arm with sudden force. His voice, when it came, was rough and broken:

**“No,”** Margaret answered gently, holding still beneath his grip.

He released her after a moment, his breath uneven. **“All men must die,”** he muttered, as if testing the words. **“But she were younger than me.”** His gaze sharpened, searching Margaret’s face. **“Ye’re certain she’s dead? Not just faintin’? She’s done that before.”**

**“She is gone,”** Margaret said softly.

A shudder ran through him. Then, with a violent sob, he collapsed against the table, shaking it with the force of his anguish. Mary rushed forward, but he flung out an arm. **“Away wi’ ye! What do I care for ye now?”**

Margaret took Mary’s trembling hand. Higgins’s grief was terrible—raw and unbridled. He tore at his hair, struck his head against the wood, then lay spent, his fury dissolving into exhaustion.

At last, he pushed himself upright. His face was swollen, his eyes bloodshot. Without a word, he turned toward the door.

Mary caught his sleeve. **“Father, don’t go out—not tonight!”**

Margaret stepped into his path. **“Come with me,”** she urged. **“Come and see her once more.”**

Her voice carried quiet command. He wavered, then followed her back upstairs.

Beside the still form, Margaret whispered, **“Her last words were for you: ‘Keep my father from drink.’”**

Higgins let out a hoarse laugh. **“What does it matter now? Nothin’ can touch her.”** But his voice cracked. **“She suffered enough in life—hard work, sickness—never a day o’ joy. And now…”**

Margaret pressed gently, **“You’ve been grieving all day. Where have you been?”**

**“Nowhere worth mentionin’,”** he rasped. **“Committee meetings. Useless talk. Then Boucher’s wife screamin’ for her fool husband. Walked till my feet bled, tryin’ to make men listen. And all the while, she lay here—”** His throat closed.

Margaret hesitated, then took the risk. **“Come home with me. You need food, rest.”**

His eyes narrowed. **“Yer father’s a parson, ain’t he?”**

**“He was,”** Margaret admitted.

**“Then I’ll go,”** Higgins decided abruptly. **“Got questions for a man like him.”**

Margaret’s pulse quickened. Her father, unprepared for such a guest—her mother ill—it was a gamble. But if she refused now, Higgins would vanish into the nearest tavern.

Downstairs, Mary clung to him, weeping. **“Don’t leave me alone wi’ this!”**

**“Ye’ve neighbors,”** he said gruffly, though his hand lingered on her hair. **“I’ll return.”**

Outside, Higgins walked stiffly beside Margaret, his face shadowed by his cap. He glanced at his grimy hands and worn clothes. **“Should’ve cleaned up,”** he muttered.

Margaret shook her head. **“No matter. You’re welcome as you are.”**

At the Hale residence, she hurried ahead to warn her father. She found him in the study, startled by her breathless account.

**“A grieving man, yes—but drunk?”** Mr. Hale frowned.

**“Not now,”** Margaret assured him. **“He’s sober, only heartbroken. Please—he needs kindness.”**

Mr. Hale sighed but nodded. **“Very well. See to your mother, and join us when you can.”**

Upstairs, Mrs. Hale fretted over Frederick’s impending visit. **“What if he’s recognized? What if he’s caught?”**

Margaret soothed her, though her own fears prickled. **“We’ll be careful. Dixon will guard the door.”**

Dixon sniffed. **“Aye, let them try gettin’ past me.”**

By the time Margaret returned to the study, the atmosphere had shifted. Higgins, washed and composed, sat across from Mr. Hale, their conversation earnest but civil.

**“Ye say faith’s the answer,”** Higgins argued, **“but I’ve seen too many pious men grindin’ the poor underfoot. If God’s so mighty, why let it happen?”**

Mr. Hale listened patiently. **“I don’t claim to have all answers. But I believe truth prevails—in time.”**

Higgins scoffed—then faltered. **“Then tell me this: if there’s a life after, why’d she suffer so in this one?”** His voice roughened. **“My Bessy never had a chance.”**

Margaret intervened softly. **“You *do* believe, Nicholas. Else why does her loss wound you so?”**

He turned to her, his defiance crumbling. **“Aye,”** he whispered. **“I reckon I do.”**

For the first time that day, his shoulders sagged not in rage, but in surrender to sorrow—and the fragile comfort of shared grief.
