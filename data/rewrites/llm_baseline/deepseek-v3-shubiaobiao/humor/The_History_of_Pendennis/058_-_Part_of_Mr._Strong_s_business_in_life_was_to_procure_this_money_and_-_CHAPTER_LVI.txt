Helen Pendennis had developed a new, entirely scientific theory: the moment her son Arthur regained his strength, his unfortunate infatuation with Fanny Bolton would flare up like a poorly managed grease fire. Though she hadn’t uttered the girl’s name since her conversation with the Major, Helen’s surveillance of Arthur had reached levels that would make a private investigator blush. Under the flimsy pretext of his "delicate health," she scarcely let him out of her sight, intercepting his mail with the efficiency of a seasoned spy.

Arthur, meanwhile, eyed each letter at the breakfast table with the nervous energy of a man expecting a bomb. His mother’s gaze never wavered from her teacup, but he could *feel* it—like a hawk tracking a particularly dim-witted rabbit. Would today be the day Fanny’s handwriting appeared? His heart performed acrobatics at the mere thought. And yet, day after day, no letter came. Was he relieved? Annoyed? It was hard to say. Breaking things off cleanly might have been the sensible thing, but since when has love (or whatever this was) ever been sensible? No, the human heart prefers a messy, drawn-out collapse—like a slow-motion carriage wreck.

Pen, still too weak to walk half a mile without resembling a wilted lettuce, was nonetheless deemed unfit for unsupervised carriage rides. Helen, however, had no authority over George Warrington, who one morning announced he had business in London. She *knew*. Of course she knew. Warrington was going to sniff out news of Fanny like a bloodhound with a vendetta.

And so he was. Arthur had confessed the whole sordid tale—his near downfall, his miraculous escape, and his lingering sense of obligation. Warrington, who had seen enough of life’s disasters to last three lifetimes, nodded gravely. "It’s hard to struggle, Arthur, and easy to fall," he said. "The best courage is to run away."

"Spoken like a man who’s run from something himself," Pen observed.

Warrington’s face darkened. "Let’s just say I know what wreckage looks like."

Meanwhile, in the labyrinthine underbelly of London, another drama unfolded. Mr. Huxter, aspiring surgeon and amateur mimic, had become a regular at the Back Kitchen, where he entertained his cronies with spot-on impressions of one Captain Costigan—a man whose dignity was inversely proportional to his blood-alcohol level. The Captain, blissfully unaware of his role as comic relief, held court with tales of his daughter’s triumphs and his own military (and alcoholic) exploits. Huxter, ever the opportunist, milked the act for all it was worth.

One morning, Huxter—fresh from a night of revelry at Vauxhall—spotted the Captain swaying down Henrietta Street like a ship in a storm, pursued by a pack of jeering street urchins. Seizing the chance for both charity and entertainment, Huxter swooped in, offering his arm. The Captain, after some slurred protests, finally accepted, and Huxter guided him through the maze of alleys to Shepherd’s Inn, where Costigan promptly tripped on an orange peel and went down like a felled oak.

Huxter, now in full medical mode, diagnosed a compound fracture (with possible whisky complications) and installed himself as the Captain’s physician. This gave him a splendid excuse to loiter around the Inn, where he soon ingratiated himself with the Boltons—particularly Fanny, who looked as though she’d been through a wringer. Huxter, oblivious to the finer points of her distress, showered her with jests, toys for her siblings, and an alarming amount of unsolicited cheer.

Mrs. Bolton, never one to keep a scandal to herself, soon filled Huxter in on *the incident*—painting Pen as a villain of Shakespearean proportions. Huxter, already nursing a grudge against Arthur, was all too eager to believe it. That is, until Bows set him straight with the *actual* story, at which point Huxter sheepishly retracted his threats to punch Pen’s head.

Not that it mattered. Fanny, lost in her own private misery, barely noticed Huxter’s antics. Bows, ever the tragic spectator, watched with a bitterness only years of unrequited affection can cultivate. "Of course," he muttered to himself. "The moment I care for someone, someone younger and handsomer comes along."

When Warrington arrived as Pen’s emissary, Bows greeted him with the warmth of a man who’d long ago given up on pleasant surprises. "Ah," he said dryly. "Let me guess—you’re here about Fanny. Tell me, does the great man wish to toss his handkerchief her way again? Or has his family decided she’s suffered enough?"

Warrington, to his credit, didn’t flinch. He delivered his message—thanks for the aid during Pen’s illness—and endured Bows’ sardonic retorts with the patience of a saint. Or at least a man who’d dealt with worse.

And so the wheels of fate turned, grinding hopes and hearts alike. Somewhere in the mess, Fanny stirred her gruel, Bows nursed his grievances, and Pen—blissfully unaware of the chaos in his wake—pondered the mysteries of life, love, and why his mother still wouldn’t let him check his own mail.
