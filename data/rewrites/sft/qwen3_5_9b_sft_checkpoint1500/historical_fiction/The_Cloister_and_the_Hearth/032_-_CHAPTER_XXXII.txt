Within the quiet sanctity of the refectory, conversation drifted inevitably toward the grave, specifically regarding the sudden demise of the monk tasked with transcribing the monastery's charters and rules. Seizing the moment with cautious humility, Gerard proffered his services. A hesitation rippled through the table, a subtle pause he misinterpreted as skepticism. "Nay, let it not be for hire, my lords," he hastened to assure them, "but for love alone. As a trifling return for countless nights of shelter bestowed upon this poor wayfarer by the brethren of your order."

A monk, pleased by the sentiment, smiled but gently interjected that the departed brother was a master of the pen, a craftsman whose skill could not simply be inherited or imitated by a novice. Undeterred, though his heart beat faster, Gerard produced from his tunic a vellum deed. He had carefully cleaned its reverse side and used it as a canvas for a sample of his script. When the monk laid eyes upon it, he leapt to his feet with a start, rushing up the long hall to the high table. Bending low, he touched the hem of his robe to the fifth step, then the tenth, presenting the document to the Prior with breathless enthusiasm.

Instantly, a dozen discerning eyes locked onto the page, and a hum of appreciation rose from the assembly. Soon, Gerard saw the Prior nodding repeatedly, pointing out features of the writing. The monk returned, his face glowing with pride, carrying a savory *crustade royale*—a game pie heavy with spices—and a silver grace cup brimming with rich pimentum for Gerard. Accepting the offerings, Gerard bowed deeply, first to the distant Prior, then to his companions, raised the cup, and circulated it around the table.

Before the liquid had settled, the entire table hailed him as a brother. "Art convent-bred?" they asked in unison. "Deny it not!" Gerard, relieved, acknowledged the truth, offering thanks to Heaven. Without this lineage, he felt he would have been as coarse and ignorant as Sybrandt and Cornelis. "But 'tis passing strange how you could know," one monk remarked. Two others spoke simultaneously, explaining the trick: "You drank with the cup in both hands."

Their voices, previously subdued, swelled into a roar of mirth so obstreperous and prolonged that the Prior dispatched the Sub-Prior down the hall to quell the disturbance and impose penance on the riotous monks. Gerard's cheeks burned with shame; amidst the chaotic laughter, his ear had caught the distinct word "courage!" echoing alongside the trumpet-like tones of Denys of Burgundy.

Later, Gerard was installed in Feu Werter's cell, illuminated by wax lights, equipped with a tilting frame and a full array of caligraphic tools. Yet the work proved too vast for a single evening. The dilemma arose: how to ask Denys, the sworn enemy of monks, to remain longer? Gerard approached him, submitting to whatever decision Denys wished. To his delight, Denys replied graciously, "A day's rest will harm neither of us. Thou shalt write, and I will pass the time as I may."

Gerard's labor was vastly admired. They concurred that the monastery's records had been grievously impoverished by poor Werter's untimely death. The Sub-Prior pressed a rix-dollar into Gerard's hand, along with several brushes and colors from the convent's ample stock. Warm-hearted and buoyed by this omen, Gerard resolved that his pen alone must forge his fortune and help him recover his beloved. "Come, Denys," he said, his tone good-natured, "see what the good monks have given me. Now, strive to be fairer to them. For, to speak plainly, it chilled my friendship for a fleeting moment to hear thee call my benefactors 'hypocrites.'"

"I recant," Denys declared instantly.

"Thank you! Thank you, good Denys!" Gerard exclaimed.

"I was a scurrilous vagabond."

"Nay, nay, say not so!" Gerard protested warmly.

"But we soldiers are rude and hasty. I give myself the lie, and I offer those I misunderstood all my esteem. 'Tis unjust that thousands should be defamed for the hypocrisy of a few."

"Now thou art reasonable," Gerard observed. "Thou hast pondered what I said?"

"Nay, it is their own doing," Denys shrugged.

Gerard chuckled, for men delight in being proven right, and listened intently as Denys recounted the genesis of his conversion. "Well then, at dinner on the first day, a young monk beside me opened his jaws and laughed right out, a musical, boisterous sound. 'Good,' said I, 'at last I have fallen on a man and not a shorn ape.' To test him further, I slapped his broad back and delivered my salute. 'Heaven forbid!' cries he. I stared. For the fellow looked as sad as Solomon; a better mime hath never been seen, even at a Mystery play. 'I see war is no sharpener of the wits,' said he. 'What are the clergy for but to fight the foul fiend? And what else are the monks for?'

'The fiend being dead,
The friars are sped.'

'Thou mayst plough up the convents,' he continued, 'and we poor monks shall have naught to do—but turn soldiers, and so bring him to life again.' Then there was a great laugh at my expense. 'Well, thou art the monk for me,' said I. 'And thou art the crossbowman for me,' quoth he. 'And I'll be bound thou couldst tell us tales of the war that should make our hair stand on end.' 'Excusez! The barber hath put that out of the question,' quoth I, and then I had the laugh."

"What wretched ribaldry!" Gerard murmured pensively.

The candid Denys immediately admitted he had witnessed merrier jests hatched with less cacophony. "'Twas a great matter to have got rid of hypocrisy," he recalled. "'So,' said I, 'I can give you the chair of the rooster, if that may content thee.' 'That we will see,' was the cry, and a signal went round."

Denys then burst forth, glee shining in his eyes, describing how at bedtime he had been ushered not into the great dormitory but into a private cell, strictly forbidden to sleep. To aid his vigil, a book of pictures depicting a hundred merry adventures of monks pursuing the female laity was lent to him. In due course, he was led barefoot down to the parlour, where a supper fit for a Duke awaited—a feast of twelve jolly friars, the roaringest youths he had ever met in peace or war. How the stories, toasts, jests, and wine cups circulated! Some played cards with a gorgeous pack wherein Saint Theresa and Saint Catherine, adorned in gold, served as the four queens; black, white, grey, and crutched friars acted as the four knaves. They staked their very rosaries, swearing like troopers when they lost.

At midnight, a sly monk stole out, but he was cunningly followed by others into the garden, where he was seen thrusting his hand into the ivy and drawing out a rope-ladder. With this, he scaled the ten-foot wall, aided by a russet kirtle popping up from the outside world as quickly as he ascended. Thus began billing and cooing—a situation Denys found far more feline than ecclesiastical, eliciting from him the appropriate comment of a "mew!" The monks joined the musical chorus, and the lay visitor shrieked, greatly discomforted; but Abelard merely cried, "What, are ye there, ye jealous miauling knaves? Ye shall caterwaul to some tune tomorrow night. I'll fit every man-jack of ye with a fardingale!" It was this brutal threat that reconciled Denys to staying another day—at Gerard's request.

Gerard groaned.

Meanwhile, unable to disconcert such a brazen monk, and the damsel beginning to whimper, they danced caterwauling in a circle, bestowing a solemn benediction on the two wall-flowers before retreating to the parlour. There they found a pair lying dead drunk, and two others affectionate to tears. They promptly carried off the inanimate ones, dragged the loving and lachrymose ones kicking and screaming into their cells.

"And so, shut up in measureless content," Denys concluded.

Gerard was disgusted and voiced his displeasure.

Denys chuckled and proceeded to recount how the following day he and the young monks had drained the fish ponds, secreting immense quantities of pike, carp, tench, and eel for their own use. Furthermore, in the dead of night, he had been taken shoeless by crooked paths into the chapel, a ghost-like, dark place, and then down steps into a crypt beneath the floor. Suddenly, paradise had burst upon him.

"'Tis there the holy fathers retire to pray," Gerard inserted solemnly.
