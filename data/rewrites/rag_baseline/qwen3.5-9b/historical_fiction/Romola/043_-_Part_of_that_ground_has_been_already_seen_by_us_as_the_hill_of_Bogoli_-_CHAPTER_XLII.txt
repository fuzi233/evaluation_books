October 30th, 1496. The sky offered a deceptive clarity, the air crisp with an autumnal bite that the Florentines ignored. Their attention was fixed elsewhere, on the gales battering the Ligurian coast, a natural phenomenon seemingly conspiring to dismantle Fra' Girolamo's theological assurances that Providence watched over Florence.

Those storms had repelled vessels from Marseille laden with grain and mercenaries. The city faced a dual crisis: starvation and a vacuum of manpower. Famine stalked the streets, and the borders were compromised. The French King, the "new Charlemagne," had withdrawn after his effortless conquest of Naples, reportedly paralyzed by the grief of a lost heir, leaving the region vulnerable. In response, a Holy League had coalesced under Pope Borgia to expel the French, a maneuver that smelled less of liberation and more of wolfish politics—an agreement among predators to eliminate rivals before dividing the carcass. Florence, refusing to join this coalition and clinging to the French alliance, had chosen isolation at a perilous cost.

Now, Pisa, bolstered by Venetian, Milanese, and Imperial forces, besieged Leghorn. If that maritime artery severed, Florence, hemmed in by papal animosity and regional envy, would suffocate. The Signoria responded with vigor, funding armies and fortifications, yet also adhering to the archaic Italian tactic of conciliation. Against precedent, they opened their gates to the starving peasantry and displaced mendicants fleeing other cities.

This humanitarian stance drew sharp criticism from the disaffected. They argued Florence should align with the League to avoid hostility, and that feeding strangers while citizens starved was madness. Yet, the distress intensified daily. Even as Fra' Girolamo remained silent in obedience to Rome, the arrival of news regarding the failed grain shipment forced the Signoria's hand. Two days prior, Savonarola had returned to the Duomo, delivering a sermon of such audacity that he wagered his habit on the city's salvation. He demanded only that Florence fulfill its civic and religious duties; if God did not intervene, he would strip his robes.

By this morning, however, no divine intervention was visible. Hope rested solely on the *Tabernacolo* of the Madonna dell'Impruneta. For generations, this sacred image had delivered Florence from plague, flood, and war. Its patronage, the Buondelmonti family, guarded it with swords; wealth flowed to its shrines. The populace believed the Madonna would bend to their will. Yesterday evening, the tabernacle had departed L'Impruneta, escorted by high dignitaries, destined for a solemn procession through the city gates.

But as dawn broke, the tabernacle remained outside. Pestilence trailed the famine. Hospitals overflowed; private courtyards served as triage centers, yet the streets remained littered with the unsheltered. Early that morning, the confraternities began their grim rounds, carrying the bodies of the dead. Among them moved Romola de' Bardi. Dressed in simple black serge, her head veiled to conceal her hair save for the golden bands framing her brow, she advanced from the Ponte Vecchio toward the Porta Santa Maria.

Her path was blocked by a bier borne by the Company of San Jacopo del Popolo. The brothers paused to inspect a corpse, surrounded by a cluster of idlers whose faces were hollowed by hunger.

"He's taken!" one shouted. "Messer Domeneddio loves him enough to claim him."

"Better stretched out than standing upright with a belly like stone," another muttered. "Death gets a poor bargain for an old fellow."

"No Florentine, ten to one," a third observed. "A beggar expelled from Siena. May San Giovanni protect us from sending soldiers against an army of starving men."

"This one is different," someone corrected. "From the Stinche. Look at the grey patch where the prison mark was."

"Hush! Help them lift him!"

"It's possible he's alive," a brother noted, lifting the body onto the stretcher. "Just collapsed from lack of food."

Romola stepped forward. She loosened a small flask at her belt and, with practiced efficiency, used an ivory implement to pour a few drops of wine into the man's mouth. The liquid worked; the head twitched, eyes fluttering open with a vacant, returning awareness.

Recognition struck Romola instantly. The wild, dark eyes, the sallow, lined face, the long white beard—it was Baldassarre, the escaped prisoner she had glimpsed in the Duomo when Tito first donned armor, the man whose mere presence had terrified the boy. A tremor seized her. Was this the moment she learned the bitter secrets of her husband's past? She felt the urge to flee, yet a stronger compulsion anchored her beside the dying man. Her hands trembled, but muscle memory guided them; her right hand hovered to offer more wine, her left supporting his neck.

Baldassarre looked at her for the first time. Weeks of seclusion since her flight and his arrest had erased the vivid descriptions of the golden-haired wife circulating in the city. All he saw now was the woman before him.

"Shall we carry him to the steps of San Stefano?" Romola asked. "We'll clear the street, and you may proceed."

They moved thirty yards until the church doorway. By then, Baldassarre managed to prop himself against the stone. The confratelli departed, but the crowd of onlookers swelled. The atmosphere shifted; knowing the man was alive bred unease, though Romola's presence imposed a somber respect.

"They fed him daily in the Stinche," a voice grumbled. "No wonder he crumbles without it."

"The Magnificent Eight released prisoners to make room for honest folk," another retorted. "If thieves get wine and bread, we Ciompi might as well drink from the Arno."

Romola sat beside Baldassarre. "Can you eat a crust? I've promised to visit the hospital, but I will return if you wait. I will take you to shelter. Do you understand?"

He stared blankly, repeating the word "back." She opened her basket, filled with soft bread, and placed a piece in his hand.

"Do you hoard bread for those who cannot swallow?" a rough voice cut in. A man in a red nightcap shoved through the crowd.

"If anyone isn't hungry," another interjected, "leave him be. He's luckier than those with gnawing stomachs and no meal."

"Yes, if a man intends to die, don't revive him against his will. Dead men require no trencher."

"Oh, you misunderstand the Friar's charity," a youth in fine cloth countered, his face untouched by want. "The Friar preaches to birds, telling hawks to feed sparrows. Every good citizen feeds six starving beggars from Arezzo or Bologna. Madonna, you're a pious Piagnone; don't waste your bread on honest folks who have the Friar's prophecies to digest."

"Come, Madonna," the red-capped man urged. "The old thief won't eat. Try us. We fast so much we're half-saints."

The circle tightened, the gaunt men crowding Romola until there was no space to breathe. She held out a horn cup, moistening a piece of bread with wine, ignoring their advances. Then she rose, scanning the faces. Instinctively, the nearest men recoiled, as if their proximity was an insult.

Romola extended the basket to the man in the nightcap. Her gaze was direct, devoid of reproach.

"Hunger is a cruel master," she said. "You hold the power to take this bread. It was reserved for the sick and the weak. You are strong; if you choose not to suffer, you can take everything from the powerless. Take the bread from this basket, but know this: I stand guard over this old man. I will resist you taking his sustenance."

Silence fell, heavy and absolute. Romola's pale face bore the deepening sockets of habitual fasting, her hazel eyes burning with intensity. The man in the nightcap looked foolish, backing away and jostling his neighbor with a gesture of moral superiority. The retreat was universal; everyone claimed to have been pushed forward unwillingly. The youth in the fine tunic vanished.

Just then, the armed servitors of the Signoria arrived to clear the obstruction for the approaching procession. The man identified as Cecco retreated up the church steps, bowing respectfully to Romola.

"Madonna, if you must continue your errands, I shall tend to the old man."
