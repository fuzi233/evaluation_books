It was, as far as I can ascertain, in September of 1811 that a post-chaise pulled up before the grand door of Aswarby Hall, deep in Lincolnshire. The only passenger—a small boy—leapt out the moment it stopped, his eyes wide with curiosity as he waited for the bell to summon the house. Before him stood a tall, square red-brick mansion built during Queen Anne’s reign, its façade adorned with a classical stone porch added in 1790. The windows were long and narrow, their small panes glowing like embers under the fading light. A pediment crowned the front, pierced by a round window, while wings on either side—connected by glass-enclosed galleries supported by columns—held the stables and servants’ quarters. Each wing rose to an ornamental cupola topped with a gilded weather-vane. The evening sun lit the whole structure, turning the glass into flickering fires.

Beyond the hall stretched a flat park dotted with oaks and lined with firs, silhouetted against the sky. From the church tower, half-hidden among trees at the edge of the grounds, the golden rooster caught the light as the clock struck six. Its chime drifted gently through the air, soft and mournful.

For the boy standing in the porch, it was a scene both beautiful and strange—peaceful yet tinged with autumnal sorrow. He had come from Warwickshire, where he’d been left orphaned six months earlier. Now, thanks to the unexpected kindness of his elderly cousin, Mr. Abney, he was to live at Aswarby Hall.

Few expected this. Mr. Abney was known as a solitary man, stern and reserved, deeply absorbed in ancient lore. He was said to know more about pagan beliefs than even the Cambridge professor of Greek. His library held every available text on the Mysteries, Orphic poetry, Mithraism, and Neoplatonism. In the marble hall stood a magnificent statue of Mithras slaying a bull, shipped from the Levant at great cost. He had written articles for *The Gentleman’s Magazine* and *The Critical Museum*, detailing Roman superstitions of the late Empire. To most, he was a man lost in books, so isolated that no one believed he would ever hear of his orphaned cousin—let alone invite him into his home.

Yet when the door opened, Mr. Abney emerged from his study, hands clasped together with unmistakable warmth. “How are you, my boy?” he asked, beaming. “How old are you? And are you not too tired to eat supper?”

“No, sir,” replied Stephen Elliott. “I’m quite well.”

“That’s good,” said Abney. “And how old are you?”

He asked twice—strangely, almost urgently.

“Twelve next birthday, sir.”

“Ah! Eleventh of September? That’s very convenient. Nearly a year from now. I like—ha ha—to record such things in my book. Are you certain?”

“Quite sure, sir.”

“Well then,” he said, turning to the butler, Mr. Parkes, “take him to Mrs. Bunch’s room and let him have tea—supper—whatever it is.”

Parkes nodded and led the boy downstairs.

Mrs. Bunch was the heart of the household—kind, practical, and full of stories. Within minutes, Stephen felt at home. She had lived near Aswarby since she was five, and had served the family for twenty years. If anyone knew the secrets of the house and the land, it was her.

Stephen, curious and bold, soon began peppering her with questions: Who built the temple at the end of the laurel walk? Who was the old man in the portrait on the staircase, seated at a table with a skull beneath his hand?

She answered most with ease. But some questions drew silence or uneasy glances.

One November evening, sitting by the fire in the housekeeper’s room, Stephen suddenly asked, “Is Mr. Abney a good man? Will he go to heaven?”

Mrs. Bunch looked startled. “Good? Bless the child! He’s as kind-hearted as any soul I’ve ever met. Didn’t I tell you about the little girl he brought in seven years ago? And the boy two years after I came here?”

“No!” Stephen insisted. “Tell me now!”

“Well,” she sighed, “the girl—I don’t remember much. Master found her wandering one day and brought her in. Mrs. Ellis, the former housekeeper, was told to care for her. She had no family—she told me herself. She stayed three weeks, quiet as a mouse, always helping around the house. Then one morning, she vanished. Not a trace. No footprints, nothing. We searched the ponds, dragged them all. But there was singing around the house that night—lasting nearly an hour—and Parkes swore he heard voices calling in the woods. I believe gypsies took her. Such a gentle child… I still miss her.”

“And the boy?” Stephen asked.

“Oh, that poor boy,” she whispered. “He was foreign—called himself Giovanni. Came playing a hurdy-gurdy one winter day. Master brought him inside right away, asked him everything—where he came from, his age, his family—so kindly. But just like the girl, he disappeared. One morning, gone. Left his hurdy-gurdy behind. Never taken it. It sits on the shelf to this day.”

That night, Stephen dreamed.

He stood at the top of the house, looking down the passage to a locked bathroom—its upper half glazed, its muslin curtains long gone. Through the glass, he saw the lead-lined tub fixed to the wall, facing the window. The moonlight poured in. And in the tub lay a figure—thin, pale, wrapped in a shroud, its lips curled in a faint, terrible smile. Its hands pressed tight over its chest. Then, a low moan escaped its mouth. The arms stirred.

Stephen recoiled, waking with a gasp—standing on the cold floor, the moonlight flooding the hallway.

With trembling courage, he approached the bathroom door. Nothing. Only darkness. He returned to bed, shaken.

Next morning, Mrs. Bunch listened in horror to his tale. She immediately replaced the muslin curtain.

At breakfast, Stephen told Mr. Abney. The man listened intently, made notes in what he called “my book,” and said nothing more.

As spring approached, Abney reminded Stephen often of the equinox—how the ancients believed it a time of spiritual danger. “You must be careful,” he warned. “Shut your bedroom window at night. Censorinus wrote much on this.”

Then came two unsettling events.

First, Stephen woke from a restless night with no memory of dreaming. The next evening, Mrs. Bunch mended his nightgown.

“Gracious me!” she exclaimed. “Look at this! How do you tear your clothes so?”

There were long, parallel cuts along the left side of the chest—deep enough to split the linen, but not quite through. They matched the scratches on the outside of his bedroom door—too high for animals, too precise for rats.

“I didn’t do it,” Stephen said. “And those marks on the door—they’re exactly like these.”

Mrs. Bunch stared, then grabbed a candle and rushed upstairs. She returned shortly.

“They’re real,” she whispered. “Too high for cats or dogs. Look like Chinese fingernails—my uncle in the tea trade used to say they could scratch like that. Don’t tell Master. Just lock your door when you go to bed.”

“I always do,” Stephen said. “After prayers.”

“That’s right,” she murmured. “Always pray. Then no harm can touch you.”

On Friday, March 23, 1812, the wind howled through the gardens. Stephen stood at the fence, watching the park. He felt as though invisible figures swept past on the breeze—drifting, helpless, trying to grasp something solid, to return to life.

After lunch, Abney said, “Stephen, come to my study tonight—at eleven. I have something important to show you. Do not speak of it to anyone. Go to bed as usual.”

Stephen’s heart leapt. Eleven o’clock! He could stay up late!

That evening, he passed the library. The brazier had been moved before the fire. On the table sat a silver-gilt cup filled with red wine, papers beside it. Abney sprinkled incense from a silver box, unaware of the boy.

By ten, Stephen stood at his open window. The night was still. But from across the mere came eerie cries—like lost souls in torment. Owls? Waterfowl? Not quite. They grew louder, closer. Soon they seemed to drift through the shrubbery.

Then silence.

But then—he saw them.

Two figures on the gravel terrace: a boy and a girl, standing side by side, staring up at the windows.

The girl was pale, smiling faintly, hands clasped over her heart. Her form reminded Stephen of the dream—of the figure in the bath.

But the boy…

He was thin, ragged, black-haired. He raised his arms, claw-like hands reaching toward the sky. The moonlight passed through his fingers—long, sharp nails, translucent. And on his chest—on the left side—was a gaping wound, dark and hollow.

A cry—not heard, but felt—exploded in Stephen’s mind. The same sound from the woods.

Then they vanished.

Frightened, Stephen grabbed his candle and ran to the study.

The door was unlocked. He knocked. No answer.

Then—voices. A choked scream. A struggle.

He pushed open the door.

Mr. Abney sat in his chair, head thrown back, face twisted in agony. His left side was torn open—heart exposed. No blood on his hands. A knife lay on the table—clean.

The window was open.

The coroner declared it a wild animal attack.

But Stephen read the papers left on the table—written in Abney’s own hand:

> “It was a belief held by the ancients—that by absorbing the personalities of others, a man may attain mastery over the unseen forces of nature. Simon Magus flew, became invisible, assumed forms—by the soul of a murdered boy. Hermes Trismegistus wrote that three hearts—taken from those under twenty-one—could grant immense power.
>
> I have spent twenty years testing this. First: Phoebe Stanley, a gipsy girl, removed March 24, 1792. Second: Giovanni Paoli, an Italian wanderer, March 23, 1805. The final victim—my cousin Stephen Elliott—must be claimed on March 24, 1812.
>
> The method: remove the heart from the living, burn it, mix ashes with red wine. Conceal the remains—bathroom or cellar. Ghosts may haunt you, but the philosopher need fear them not. I anticipate freedom—immortality—even beyond death itself.”

Stephen understood.

The dreams. The scratches. The singing. The children.

They were not ghosts.

They were echoes—spirits of the stolen, bound to the place by the ritual. The girl who vanished. The boy who fled with his hurdy-gurdy.

And now, the third.

But Abney had failed.

The spirits had returned—not to punish, but to reclaim.

Stephen closed the papers.

He locked his door.

And prayed.

Not for safety.

But for mercy.
