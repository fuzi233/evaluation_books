If Ginger Kemp had ever been compelled—at gunpoint, say, by a stern food-historian with a notebook—to list his virtues, he would have produced a page as sparsely garnished as Mrs. Meecher’s boarding-house porridge. He might have managed “means well,” after which he would have sat chewing the pencil as if it were an obstinate bit of gristle. Tact, delicacy, and that soft-fingered perception which knows when to pat and when to vanish would certainly not have appeared on his menu.

And yet, during the next few days, he did something that looked uncommonly like delicacy. He stayed away from Sally.

It was not easy. Staying away from Sally was like walking past an open bakery with your pockets full of money and your soul full of hunger. But he did it, partly because he argued from his own tastes (always a dangerous habit in any cook, lover, or amateur philosopher), and was firmly convinced that in times of trouble a person wants solitude—wants to sit in a quiet room and reduce the problem slowly, like stock, skimming off the foam of emotion until something clear remains at the bottom.

Ginger had, in his time, had what he called nasty jars—moments when life went crack in the hand like a plate dropped on tiles—and on those occasions all he had wanted was to be left alone with his thoughts, to fight it out like a man in a pantry with a wasp. So he left Sally alone, though it went against him, and he did it with a sort of clumsy chivalry: the chivalry of a man who can’t tie a bow but will carry the parcel all the way home.

By Saturday, however, he decided the broth had simmered long enough. Action, he felt, could now be applied.

Saturday was a capital day for gathering up life’s dropped threads. He didn’t have to go to the office, and—more to the point—he had just drawn his week’s salary. Mrs. Meecher, with the deftness of an agrarian elder harvesting by moonlight, had taken a portion off him at the source, but enough remained to let him attempt consolation on a scale that might be described, by a man not actually paying for it, as princely.

He conceived a plan. He would hire a car and take Sally out to dinner at one of the road-houses up the Boston Post Road—places he had heard of in the same tone men use for secret fisheries and miraculous pies. The idea improved as he contemplated it. It was helped, too, by the weather.

The weather had been a revelation to Ginger. It was his first American Indian Summer, and it had behaved like a conspirator. The sun didn’t blaze; it upholstered. It laid velvet over the city’s harsh angles and made even dust seem romantic. As Ginger stood on the roof of Mrs. Meecher’s establishment that Saturday morning, thrilled by the soft brilliance and the gentler noises it coaxed out of the town—the distant wheels no longer snarling, the river’s breath audible between buildings—he felt that the only sane way to spend such a day was in an open car with Sally beside him and the air tasting faintly of leaves.

The Maison Meecher was a lofty building on a side street at the lower end of the avenue. From its roof, after you had navigated the hanging jungles of washing limp on lines—great domestic flags of surrender—you could see many things. To the left lay Washington Square, full of somnolent Italians and children who made the peculiar scraping music of roller-skates: a metallic whispering that, in quieter moments, sounded almost like rain on a tin roof. To the right was a sight that never failed to intrigue Ginger: the high funnels of a Cunard liner moving slowly down the river, apparently sailing, by some mythic misprint, along Ninth Avenue. Today there were four funnels, and Ginger deduced the Mauretania.

As the boat on which he had come over from England, the Mauretania had a sentimental interest for him. He stood watching her stately progress until taller buildings farther down-town shut her away, then picked his way through the washing and went down to his room to fetch his hat.

A quarter of an hour later, he was in the hallway of Sally’s apartment-house, and the day’s velvet promptly snagged.

He was gazing—gazing with the sort of disgust a man feels when he lifts a saucepan-lid and discovers that somebody has used his stew as an ashtray—at the serge-clad back of his cousin, Mr. Bruce Carmyle. Mr. Carmyle was engaged in conversation with a gentleman in overalls.

No care-free prospector, singing his way through the Mojave Desert and suddenly finding himself nose to nose with a rattlesnake, could have experienced a sharper change of mood than did Ginger at this spectacle. Even in their native Piccadilly it had been unpleasant to collide with Bruce Carmyle. To find him here, now, in the very doorway of Sally’s life, was nauseating.

Only one thing could have brought him. Obviously he had come to see Sally—and with a sinking of the heart Ginger remembered the shiny, expensive automobile waiting at the curb. It was clear that Ginger’s plan of open-car sunshine had occurred to other minds as well, though in Bruce Carmyle’s head even sunshine probably arrived with an invoice attached.

Ginger was still standing there when Mr. Carmyle swung round. His dark face wore the scowl of a man who has been forced into conversation with a janitor and found the janitor insufficiently awed.

The sight of Ginger did not improve him.

“Hullo!” said Mr. Carmyle.

“Hullo!” said Ginger.

A silence followed. It had the awkward density of undercooked dumplings.

“Have you come to see Miss Nicholas?” said Mr. Carmyle at last.

“Why, yes.”

“She isn’t here,” said Mr. Carmyle, and the discovery that he had found someone to share the bad news seemed to cheer him, as shared misfortune will cheer certain temperaments—particularly those that like to watch other people choke on it.

“Not here?”

“No. Apparently…” Mr. Carmyle’s scowl deepened; it was the expression of a well-balanced man encountering another person’s unreasonable behavior. “…apparently, for some extraordinary reason, she has taken it into her head to dash over to England.”

Ginger tottered. The blow was so unexpected it seemed to knock the sound out of the hallway: even the ordinary building noises—the elevator’s groan, a door shutting, distant plumbing—went thin and far away.

He followed his cousin out into the sunshine in a kind of dream. Bruce Carmyle was speaking to the chauffeur of the expensive automobile.

“I find I shall not want the car. You can take it back to the garage.”

The chauffeur, a moody man, opened one half-closed eye and spat cautiously. It was the sort of careful, considered spitting a financier might employ when approaching the crisis of a delicate negotiation.

“You’ll have to pay just the same,” he observed, opening his other eye to lend emphasis.

“Of course I shall pay,” snapped Mr. Carmyle. “How much is it?”

Money passed. The car rolled off with the soft purr of something well-fed.

“Gone to England?” said Ginger. His voice sounded to him like somebody else’s, like a line misquoted in a foreign play.

“Yes. Gone to England.”

“But why?”

“How the devil do I know why?” said Mr. Carmyle, with the air of a man whose patience has been forced through a mincer. “All I know is what the janitor told me—that she sailed on the Mauretania this morning.”

The tragic irony of it came down on Ginger like a lid. He had stood on the roof watching that very ship glide away, content as a man anticipating dinner, never dreaming that his whole heart was sitting somewhere on that deck, pulling away from him with each quiet turn of the propellers.

He nodded absently to Mr. Carmyle and walked off. There was nothing to say. Words were cheap and he felt suddenly bankrupt.

The warmth went out of the sunshine. The day’s sweetness, which had been like ripe fruit, turned flavorless in his mouth. He wandered with no plan, loafing through streets that now seemed made of cardboard scenery. He went into the Park and out again. The Park bored him. The streets bored him. The whole city bored him—its clanging, its tire-hiss, its rude modern music of engines and construction that never stopped. Without Sally, New York became all industrial racket and no meaning: a kitchen full of noise where nobody is cooking.

Not even the thought that his cousin—a careful man with his money—had paid a day’s hire for a car he couldn’t use brought Ginger any balm.

Night came at last, and with it a letter.

It was the first even passably pleasant thing that had happened all day, because the envelope bore the crest of the good ship Mauretania. Ginger snatched it from the letter-rack as if it were a last biscuit from a tin in a famine, and carried it upstairs to his room.

Very few rooms at Mrs. Meecher’s boarding-house struck any note of luxury. Mrs. Meecher was not one of your fashionable interior decorators. She was of the opinion that when you had provided a bed, a wash-stand, and a Morris chair, you had mounted as high toward splendor as any guest at seven-and-a-half dollars a week had a right to climb. As a rule, the severity of these surroundings afflicted Ginger with gloom when he went to bed; but tonight—such is the magic of a letter from the right person—he was uplifted and almost gay. There are moments when even illuminated texts over the wash-stand cannot wholly quell a man.

There was nothing of haste, and much of ceremony, in Ginger’s method of approaching correspondence. He behaved like a small boy with unexpected ice-cream, lingering over it before taking the first spoonful, anxious to make it last. His first move was to feel in the breast-pocket of his coat and produce the photograph of Sally which he had feloniously removed from her apartment. He looked long and earnestly at it, then propped it against the basin, within easy reach, for reference.

He took off his coat, collar, and shoes; filled and lit a pipe; placed pouch and matches on the Morris chair’s arm; and drew the chair up so that he could sit with his feet on the bed. Having manoeuvred himself into comfort, he lit his pipe again and took up the letter.

He looked at the crest, at Sally’s handwriting, at the postmark. He weighed it. It was bulky. He took up the photograph and scrutinized it once more, as if her face might dictate the proper way to begin reading. Then he lit his pipe again, wriggled deeper into the chair, and opened the envelope.

“Ginger, dear.”

Having read so far, Ginger found it necessary to take up the photograph and stare at it with even greater intentness. He gazed at it for several minutes, then laid it down and lit his pipe again. Then he went on.

“Ginger, dear—I’m afraid this address is going to give you rather a shock, and I’m feeling very guilty. I’m running away, and I haven’t even stopped to say good-bye. I can’t help it. I know it’s weak and cowardly, but I simply can’t help it. I stood it for a day or two, and then I saw that it was no good. (Thank you for leaving me alone and not coming round to see me. Nobody else but you would have done that. But then, nobody ever has been or ever could be so understanding as you.)”

Ginger, at this point, was compelled to look at the photograph again.

“There was too much in New York to remind me. That’s the worst of being happy in a place. When things go wrong you find there are too many ghosts about. I just couldn’t stand it. I tried, but I couldn’t. I’m going away to get cured—if I can.

“Mr. Faucitt is over in England, and when I went down to Mrs. Meecher for my letters, I found one from him. His brother is dead, you know, and he has inherited, of all things, a fashionable dress-making place in Regent Street. His brother was Laurette et Cie. I suppose he will sell the business later on, but, just at present, the poor old dear is apparently quite bewildered and that doesn’t seem to have occurred to him. He kept saying in his letter how much he wished I was with him, to help him, and I was tempted and ran. Anything to get away from the ghosts and have something to do. I don’t suppose I shall feel much better in England, but, at least, every street corner won’t have associations. Don’t ever be happy anywhere, Ginger. It’s too big a risk, much too big a risk.

“There was a letter from Elsa Doland, too. Bubbling over with affection. We had always been tremendous friends. Of course, she never knew anything about my being engaged to Gerald. I lent Fillmore the money to buy that piece, which gave Elsa her first big chance, and so she’s very grateful. She says, if ever she gets the opportunity of doing me a good turn… Aren’t things muddled?

“And there was a letter from Gerald. I was expecting one, of course, but… what would you have done, Ginger? Would you have read it? I sat with it in front of me for an hour, I should think, just looking at the envelope, and then… You see, what was the use? I could guess exactly the sort of thing that would be in it, and reading it would only have hurt a lot more. The thing was done, so why bother about explanations? What good are explanations, anyway? They don’t help. They don’t do anything…

“I burned it, Ginger. The last letter I shall ever get from him. I made a bonfire on the bathroom floor, and it smouldered and went brown, and then flared a little, and every now and then I lit another match and kept it burning, and at last it was just black ashes and a stain on the tiles. Just a mess!

“Ginger, burn this letter, too. I’m pouring out all the poison to you, hoping it will make me feel better. You don’t mind, do you? But I know you don’t. If ever anybody had a real pal…

“It’s a dreadful thing, fascination, Ginger. It grips you and you are helpless. One can be so sensible and reasonable about other people’s love affairs. When I was working at the dance place I told you about there was a girl who fell in love with the most awful little beast. He had a mean mouth and shiny black hair brushed straight back, and anybody would have seen what he was. But this girl wouldn’t listen to a word. I talked to her by the hour. It makes me smile now when I think how sensible and level-headed I was. But she wouldn’t listen. In some mysterious way this was the man she wanted, and, of course, everything happened that one knew would happen.

“If one could manage one’s own life as well as one can manage other people’s! If all this wretched thing of mine had happened to some other girl, how beautifully I could have proved that it was the best thing that could have happened, and that a man who could behave as Gerald has done wasn’t worth worrying about. I can just hear myself. But, you see, whatever he has done, Gerald is still Gerald and Sally is still Sally and, however much I argue, I can’t get away from that. All I can do is to come howling to my redheaded pal, when I know just as well as he does that a girl of any spirit would be dignified and keep her troubles to herself and be much too proud to let anyone know that she was hurt.

“Proud! That’s the real trouble, Ginger. My pride has been battered and chopped up and broken into as many pieces as you broke Mr. Scrymgeour’s stick! What pitiful creatures we are. Girls, I mean. At least, I suppose a good many girls are like me. If Gerald had died and I had lost him that way, I know quite well I shouldn’t be feeling as I do now. I should have been broken-hearted, but it wouldn’t have been the same. It’s my pride that is hurt. I have always been a bossy, cocksure little creature, swaggering about the world like an English sparrow; and now I’m paying for it! Oh, Ginger, I’m paying for it! I wonder if running away is going to do me any good at all. Perhaps, if Mr. Faucitt has some real hard work for me to do…

“Of course, I know exactly how all this has come about. Elsa’s pretty and attractive. But the point is that she is a success, and as a success she appeals to Gerald’s weakest side. He worships success. She is going to have a marvellous career, and she can help Gerald on in his. He can write plays for her to star in. What have I to offer against that? Yes, I know it’s grovelling and contemptible of me to say that, Ginger. I ought to be above it, oughtn’t I—talking as if I were competing for some prize… But I haven’t any pride left. Oh, well!

“There! I’ve poured it all out and I really do feel a little better just for the moment. It won’t last, of course, but even a minute is something. Ginger, dear, I shan’t see you for ever so long, even if we ever do meet again, but you’ll try to remember that I’m thinking of you a whole lot, won’t you? I feel responsible for you. You’re my baby. You’ve got started now and you’ve only to stick to it. Please, please, please don’t ‘make a hash of it’! Good-bye. I never did find that photograph of me that we were looking for that afternoon in the apartment, or I would send it to you. Then you could have kept it on your mantelpiece, and whenever you felt inclined to make a hash of anything I would have caught your eye sternly and you would have pulled up.

“Good-bye, Ginger. I shall have to stop now. The mail is just closing.

“Always your pal, wherever I am.—SALLY.”

Ginger laid the letter down, and a small sound escaped him that was half sigh and half oath. He sat listening to the boarding-house at night: a cough in a distant room, the thin complaint of pipes, the street’s perpetual mechanical mutter. The whole soundscape felt suddenly hostile, as if modern life, with its engines and hiss and clank, had conspired to drown out the one voice he needed.

He wondered whether even now some desirable end might not be achieved by going to Chicago and breaking Gerald Foster’s neck. Abandoning this scheme as impracticable, and being unable to think of anything else to do, he re-lit his pipe and started to read the letter again.
