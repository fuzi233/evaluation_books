Gerard was nearly as eager for the promised land of Burgundy as Denys himself. The soldier never tired of singing its praises, and at every minor irritation he would remind Gerard how much better things were done in that refined region. Above all, he dwelled on the promise of clean linens at the inns—something Gerard had longed for since leaving home. “I ask no more,” the Hollander would say, with a weary sigh. “To think I’ve not lain in a bare bed once since I left my house! When I see their linen, instead of taking off my clothes, it’s my eyes and nose I’d gladly shut away.”

Denys carried his affection for his homeland so far that he walked twenty leagues in shoes that had burst apart rather than buy new ones from a German merchant—whose rudeness, delays, and general ill-manners made him seem less a trader than an enemy. At sunset, the two travelers found themselves equally distant from a small town and a monastery, though the latter lay off the main road. Denys insisted on the inn; Gerard, on the convent. In the end, Denys gave in—but only after extracting a promise that once they reached Burgundy, they would always choose inns over monasteries. Gerard agreed readily, for his map of religious houses ended here.

They turned aside from the path.

Now, Gerard asked with quiet surprise why this sudden distaste for places that had often fed and sheltered them without charge. Denys hesitated at first, clearing his throat and shifting his weight, but then his pent-up grievances erupted.

“It’s not sudden,” he said at last. “It’s been there all along—a deep-rooted horror I’ve kept quiet out of politeness. But now, since we’re near the end, I’ll speak plainly. I saw they’d put powder in your drink,” he added, “so I held my tongue. But now, being the last, why not let it all out? Know this: I’ve felt like a fish out of water in every one of those great stone prisons. You vanish straightaway with some old monk, and I’m left to endure my purgatory alone.”

“Forgive me,” said Gerard softly. “I’ve been selfish.”

“Aye, aye,” replied Denys, “I forgive you, little one. It’s not your fault—you’re not the first fool to be priest-rid and monk-hit. But I’ll never forgive *them* for my misery.”

Then, as if recalling a trial before Henry VIII’s commissioners, he launched into his indictment.

These gloomy towers were all built alike. Inns varied, but monasteries offered nothing but repetition. A great gate, a smaller one, a flight of steps, and then a shadowy cloister. There, the dortour—the sleeping hall. Elsewhere, the cold, vast refectory, where silence was enforced—or at least whispers. “And who can eat in silence?” he demanded. “Who wants to sit mute while his belly grumbles? Or worse—when tinkle, tinkle, tinkle goes the bell, and you must rise for church, even if your soul is too numb for prayer. I am not a pig at worship, I tell you.”

“And then,” he continued, “you’re supposed to go to bed with your eyes open. By the time you manage to close them, some restless spirit has pulled the rope again—tinkle, tinkle—and now you must recite a prayer in the dark, whether you know one or not. If they heard the prayers I mutter when they wake me up like that—well, they’d have cause to shudder!”

He paused, then added: “You fall asleep again, get a few hours’ rest—then it’s tinkle, tinkle again, for matins.”

“And the only clapper you truly love,” Gerard interjected half-jokingly, “is a woman’s.”

“Because there’s music in that—even when she scolds,” came the firm reply.

“And then,” Denys went on, “the endless checks. I touch the salt cellar with my finger—‘Have you no knife?’ they cry. I wipe my knife on the cloth to save time—‘Wipe it dirty on the bread, and clean upon the cloth!’ Oh, petty souls! These tiny, priggish rules chill good fellowship like icicles melting from straw roofs.”

“I hold cleanliness no pedantry,” said Gerard firmly. “You should learn better manners once and for all.”

“Nay,” retorted Denys. “It’s they who lack manners. They silence a man at every word.”

“At every other word,” Gerard corrected, “only when it’s obscene or blasphemous.”

“Exaggerator!” Denys snapped. “Why, at the very last of these dungeons, I found poor travelers huddled round a monk, chilled and silent as criminals waiting for execution. To cheer them, I shouted, ‘Courage, tout le monde, le dia—’”

“Connu! What happened?” Gerard interrupted.

“Well,” said Denys, “‘Blaspheme not!’ said the fellow. ‘Plait-il,’ I replied. Doesn’t he wheel about and snap at me in a kind of Alsatian French, turning every P into a B. I could barely keep from laughing in his face.”

“Being yourself unable to speak ten words of his language without error,” Gerard pointed out.

“True,” Denys admitted. “But everyone ought to speak French. What use are so many tongues, except to draw a wall between men’s hearts?”

“But what did he say?” Gerard pressed.

“What matters what a fool says?” Denys countered.

“Oh, not all a fool’s words are folly,” Gerard replied. “Or I wouldn’t listen to you.”

“Well,” Denys said, “he said, ‘Those who begin by flouting the Devil’s name will end by doing the same to all the names in heaven.’ I said, ‘Father, I am a soldier—this is merely my “consigne,” my watchword.’ He asked, ‘Oh, so it’s just a custom?’ I didn’t understand the old fox, thinking to clear myself, so I said, ‘Yes, it was.’ Then he said, ‘Ah, that’s ten times worse. It will bring him down on you one day. He still comes where his name is often called.’”

“And yet,” Gerard observed, “he showed no gratitude for news neither his missals nor breviary had ever revealed.”

“No,” Denys agreed. “Then he told me soldiers usually commit the crimes others are broken on the wheel for—murder, rape, pillage.”

“And is that not true?” Gerard asked.

“True or not,” Denys replied cautiously, “it was ill mannered.”

“And so,” Gerard persisted, “this courteous host says, ‘Since you are enemies of mankind, why make enemies of good spirits too, by shouting the names of evil ones?’ And a lot more nonsense.”

“Well,” Gerard said gently, “whether you heed his advice or not, why blame someone for raising his voice to save your soul?”

“How can his voice save my soul,” Denys shot back, “when he keeps turning his P’s into B’s?”

Gerard was stunned. Before he could recover from this thunderbolt of Gallicism, Denys charged ahead, declaring all monks hypocrites.

“Look at them,” he declared. “They slink about, unable to meet a man’s eye like honest folk.”

“Nay,” Gerard said eagerly, “that modest glance downward is part of their discipline—it’s *custodia oculorum*.”

“Cursed toads eating hoc hac horum?” Denys scoffed. “No such thing. That’s how a cutpurse looks. Can’t meet a true man’s eye. Doff the cowl—monk; behold, a thief. Don the cowl—thief; lo, a monk. Tell me, how can they ever look God Almighty in the face, when they can’t even face a man here below?”

Ah, here it is—black as ink! Into the well we go, comrade. Misericorde—there goes the tinkle already. ’Tis the best tinkle of all—for dinner. Wait, listen! I knew it: the wolf in my stomach cried ‘Amen!’”

He confirmed this with two hearty oaths and marched into the convent like a triumphant gamecock, convinced he had won the argument. He did not suspect how deeply he had offended Gerard—with his crude mockery, his vulgar comparisons, and above all, his irreverent tone. Yet Gerard said nothing. Not a word.
