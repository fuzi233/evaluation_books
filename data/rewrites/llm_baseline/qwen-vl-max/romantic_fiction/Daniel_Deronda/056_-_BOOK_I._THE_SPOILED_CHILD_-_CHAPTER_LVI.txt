“The pang, the curse with which they died,
Had never passed away: I could not draw my eyes from theirs,
Nor lift them up to pray.” —COLERIDGE.

That night, Deronda did not undress. Gwendolen, after insisting on seeing him one last time before she would allow herself to be left alone, had been quiet—too quiet. Her voice, when she spoke, was barely a whisper, trembling with an urgency she could not hide. She asked only that he promise to come to her when she sent for him in the morning. Yet even as he stood there, watching her, he felt a deep unease. The possibility of a sudden change, the fear of fever or delirium, and the lingering dread that something in the catastrophe had stirred a hidden turmoil within her—all weighed heavily upon his mind. He told her attendant he would remain close by, ready at any moment if her condition worsened. He made it clear to all present that he was in touch with her family in England and that he bore responsibility for her care—a role he could assume easily enough, since he was known to Grandcourt’s valet, the only old servant who had accompanied them on the voyage.

But when exhaustion finally overtook him, Deronda slept—not peacefully, but tangled in dreams that mirrored the chaos of the day. They came like a storm of fragmented images: the sea, the boat, the cry, the silence. And then, waking suddenly, he saw it—the face of Grandcourt, dead and staring, imprinted in his mind by the weight of her unspoken guilt.

Morning came without summons. A small comfort. As he dressed, he allowed himself a moment of hope. It was still early—no message yet. Later, he learned that she had not slept well, but had remained calm, no violent signs of distress. She had finally drifted into rest. He marveled at the strength within her—how she could endure such terror and still keep her composure. He sensed, too, that beneath the physical shock, she was holding something back, concealing a deeper wound. For himself, he felt strangely detached. His own recent encounter with his mother had worn down his emotions; he seemed now to act out of duty rather than passion. His heart, once so open to suffering, felt numb. He was living through another kind of sorrow, and her pain, though sharp, felt like a memory he had not yet fully returned to.

Still, he went about his duties. He secured a formal statement from the fishermen who had rescued Gwendolen. Details were scarce. The boat Grandcourt had taken was found adrift, sail loose. The men believed he had been thrown overboard by the flapping canvas while turning the vessel, and that he could not swim. But their attention had first been drawn by a man’s cry—then, swiftly, by a woman’s scream. They saw her leap into the water.

Back at the hotel, he was told Gwendolen had risen and wished to see him. He was led into a dim room, curtains drawn tight, where she sat wrapped in a white shawl, her long hair carefully coiled, her earrings still in place—those blue stars glinting faintly in the shadow. When he entered, she rose abruptly, her face pale as snow, except for the purple shadows beneath her eyes, her lips parted in a look of helpless accusation. She seemed like a ghost of the proud, defiant Gwendolen Harleth he had once known—the woman who walked away from her losses at the gaming table with cold dignity. Now, she was broken, and the sight pierced him with a grief he could not name.

“Please,” he said softly, stepping forward, “rest. Do not stand.”

She obeyed, sinking back into the chair.

“Will you sit near me?” she whispered. “I must speak very quietly.”

He drew a small chair beside her. The gesture seemed to stir her deeply. Turning her pale face toward his, she leaned in, her voice barely audible:

“You know I am a guilty woman?”

Deronda turned paler. “I know nothing,” he replied, unable to say more.

“He is dead,” she murmured, with a quiet firmness.

“Yes,” he answered, voice thick with sorrow, reluctant to speak further.

“His face will never rise above the water again,” she continued, hands clenched tightly. “Not for anyone else—only for me. A dead face. I shall never escape it.”

Her words came from a place of desperate repression. She stared past him, into the distance, as if reliving every second. Was she delirious? Was she trying to conceal even from herself what she had done? Deronda’s mind flickered with these questions—half-hope, half-dread. But he could not speak. She was confessing. And he feared what she might reveal. He wanted to turn away, to let her bear her burden alone. But he was not a priest. He could not simply accept this soul laid bare upon him. Still, he could not deny her.

“You will not say I should tell the world?” she pressed, looking up at him. “You will not say I should be disgraced? I cannot. I could not bear it. Not my mother. Never. I must tell you—but you will not make me tell anyone else.”

“I can say nothing,” Deronda replied, voice low and heavy, “except that I wish to help you.”

“I told you from the start,” she whispered. “I told you I was afraid of myself.” There was a pleading ache in her tone, almost childlike. “I felt a hatred inside me—like a spirit of evil—always plotting. Everything I could do to free myself came to mind. And it grew worse. That’s why I asked you to come to me in town. I thought I would tell you everything. I tried. But I couldn’t. Then—he came in.”

She paused. A shudder ran through her.

“I will tell you everything now. Do you think a woman who wept, who prayed, who fought to save herself from herself—could be a murderer?”

“God!” Deronda breathed, voice shaking. “Don’t torture me. You didn’t kill him. You jumped into the water to save him. That’s what happened. This was an accident. One you could not prevent.”

“Don’t be impatient,” she said, trembling. The plea in her voice forced him to meet her eyes. “You used to say—you cared more for those who had done wrong and suffered. You said they might be redeemed, scourged into better things. If you hadn’t said that—if I hadn’t remembered your words—everything would have been worse. I *did* remember. It came to me at the end—that’s why I—But now, if you cannot bear to hear me—if you turn away from me—what shall I do? Am I worse than when you first found me? All the wrong I’ve done was already in me—more than I knew. If you had not come, if you had not been patient—would I be worse? And now—will you forsake me?”

Her hands, clenched moments before, now lay limp and trembling on the armrest. Her lips parted, breathless. Deronda could not answer. He looked away. Then, slowly, he took one of her hands—soft, cold—and clasped it as if they were two children walking together. It was the only way he could say, “I will not leave you.” And all the while, he felt as if he were signing a blank contract, one that might be filled with horror.

This touch was new to Gwendolen. No man had ever offered her tenderness she truly needed. And in that moment, she read his silence as a vow of endless patience, of unwavering constancy. Strength surged through her. She could go on.

“All sorts of thoughts,” she began, voice drifting, “contrivances—so hard. I fought against them. I was terrified. I saw his face—dead.” Her voice dropped to a whisper, almost touching his ear. “Long ago, I saw it. I wished him dead. But it frightened me. I was two people. I wanted to kill—like thirst. And then—immediately—I felt I had done something terrible, irreversible. Something that would make me a monster. And it came. It came.”

She fell silent, lost in memory.

“It was all in my mind when I first spoke to you—at the Abbey. I had done something then. I couldn’t tell you. It was the only thing I did toward carrying out my thoughts. They haunted everything—but only one became real. I did one thing. And I never undone it. It remains. Back when we were at Ryelands. In my boudoir—among the toys—something small and sharp, like a willow leaf in silver. I locked it in my dressing-case drawer. I kept thinking of it. I imagined putting it under my pillow. But I never did. I never opened the drawer again. I dared not. It had its own key. And not long ago, on the yacht—I dropped the key into the deep water. I meant to free myself. After that, I thought how to open it without the key. When we came to Genoa, I planned to have it opened secretly at the hotel. But then—on the stairs—I met you. I thought I’d talk to you—tell you everything I couldn’t in town. And then I was forced into the boat.”

A sob escaped her. She sank back, overwhelmed by the memory of that moment.

“And it has all stayed in my imagination,” Deronda said gently, not looking at her. “Only in your mind. To the very end, you resisted the temptation?”

Silence. Tears traced paths down her cheeks. She pressed her handkerchief to her face, straightening her posture. She was gathering herself again. Then, leaning toward him, she whispered:

“No. I will tell you the truth—exactly as God knows it. I will not lie. What else could I do? I used to think I could never be wicked. Wickedness seemed far away. But I have been. I have felt it. And everything has punished me. The things I once desired—now they burn. Even daylight feels like punishment. Because—you know—I should not have married. That was the beginning. I wronged someone. I broke my word. I sought pleasure for myself—and it turned to misery. I wanted to profit from another’s loss—you remember? Like roulette. And the money burned into me. I couldn’t complain. It was as if I had prayed for others to lose and me to win. And I won. I knew it. I knew I was guilty. On the sea, lying awake in the cabin, I sometimes felt everything I had done was revealed—no excuses—nothing hidden. How could anything be known only to me? It wasn’t mine—it was God’s knowledge entering me. Even the silence punished me. Everything but you. I always thought you wouldn’t want me punished. You would try to help me become better. Only thinking of that helped me. Will you change now? Will you want to punish me?”

Another sob.

“God forbid,” Deronda groaned. But he sat still.

The confession unfolded in fragments, each memory a wound reopened. She skipped over some details, blurred others, confused the line between thought and deed. But her voice, though low, carried the weight of truth.

“That’s why it was so hard when I was forced into the boat. Because when I saw you, it was joy—unexpected. I thought I could tell you everything—the locked drawer, what I hadn’t said before. If I had told you, if it were in your mind, it would have less power over me. I hoped in that. But after all my struggles, my tears, my rage—the hatred, the longing, the thirst—came back. And that disappointment—being shut out, driven into the boat—brought it all back. As if I were locked in prison with it, no escape. Oh, it seems so long ago now—when I stepped into that boat! I would have given everything to have lightning strike him dead.”

There was a fierce edge in her voice, born of memory.

“If he were here now—what would I do? I cannot wish him here. Yet I cannot bear his dead face. I was a coward. I should have borne scorn. I should have fled—wandered like a beggar—rather than stay and feel like a fiend. But wherever I turned, there was something I couldn’t bear. Sometimes I thought he would kill me if I resisted. But now—his dead face is there. And I cannot bear it.”

Suddenly, she pulled her hand from his, stood up, arms raised high, and moaned:

“I have been cruel! What can I do but cry for help? I am sinking. Die—die—you are forsaken. Go down, go down into darkness. Forsaken—no pity—*I* shall be forsaken.”

She collapsed into the chair, sobbing uncontrollably. Even Deronda was gone from her awareness. He was shattered. He had expected his recent trials to dull his feelings—but instead, the agony of this young woman’s fall from innocence into remorse struck him with unbearable force. Her swift journey from bright girlhood to this torment, witnessed by him in helpless silence, pierced him deeper because it followed so closely on the heels of his own spiritual revelation. He found himself standing in one of those rare moments when pity becomes so intense it makes one choose to renounce joy, to live only for the suffering.

He had risen from his seat during her outburst—her confession spoken in whispers, even in her madness, as if to a secret God. At last, he turned his back and walked away.

Then, silence. She realized he was gone. When he turned back, he saw her face tilted toward him—eyes wide, lips parted. She was a picture of timid, desperate appeal—too afraid to speak, too afraid to ask. Was he leaving her? Was she abandoned?

But his eyes met hers—full, sorrowful, unflinching. And in that gaze, she read: *I know. But I will not abandon you.*

He sat beside her again—without speaking, without touching her.

Gwendolen felt it again—the same compunction that had moved her at the Abbey. A love deeper than guilt. She said, softly, with regret:

“I make you very unhappy.”

Deronda gave a faint “Oh,” shrinking slightly. Then, gathering himself, he said clearly: “It does not matter whether I am happy or not. What matters is what will help you most. Tell me everything you need to say.”

His devotion was noble—but it created distance. She felt him beyond reach, compassion wrapped in a halo of superiority. She longed to kneel—to humble herself completely. But her mind held back. She remained silent, motionless, caught between conflicting desires.

At last, he said, “Perhaps you are too tired. Shall I go? I will return whenever you wish.”

“No,” she whispered, the fear of his departure restoring her voice. “I must tell you—what happened in the boat. I was full of rage—forced to go. I could do nothing but sit like a slave. Then we left the harbor—into the deep. Everything was still. We didn’t look at each other. He gave orders. And the light—it seemed to hold me prisoner. I remembered as a child imagining sailing away to a world where no one was forced to live with whom they didn’t like. I didn’t want my father-in-law to come home. And now—just the opposite. I had stepped into a boat. My life was sailing—gliding—away—alone with *him*, from salvation. And because I felt helpless, my thoughts turned darker. I longed for worse things. I had cruel wishes. I imagined impossible ways—yet I didn’t want to die. I feared drowning us both. If it had helped, I would have prayed—for something to happen to him. That he would sink and leave me alone. I knew no way to kill him there—but I *did* kill him in my thoughts.”

She paused, submerged.

“But still, I felt I was growing more wicked. And what you once said came back—about fearing to increase your sin and remorse—then you would hope for nothing. It was like fire written inside me. Becoming wicked was misery—being cut off forever from knowing what you—what better lives were. That had always returned to me—but now with despair. Evil wishes were too strong. I remember letting go the tiller and saying, ‘God help me!’ But then I had to take it again. And the evil prayers came back—blotting everything—until, in the midst of them—I don’t know how—he was turning the sail. A gust. He was struck. I know nothing. I only know—I saw my wish outside me.”

Her voice quickened, dropping lower.

“I saw him sink. My heart leapt—as if it were leaving my body. I think I didn’t move. I kept my hands tight. Long enough to be glad—and yet know it was no use—he would come up again. And he *was* up—farther off. The boat had moved. It was lightning. ‘The rope!’ he called—not his voice. I hear it now. I bent for the rope. I felt I must. I was sure he could swim. I dreaded him coming back. But he sank again. I had the rope in my hand—no, there he was—face above water—calling again—and I held my hand—and my heart said, ‘Die!’—and he sank. And I felt, ‘It is done. I am wicked. I am lost!’—and I had the rope in my hand—I don’t know what I thought—I was leaping away from myself. I would have saved him then. I was fleeing from my crime—and there it was—close to me as I fell—there was the dead face—dead, dead. It can never be changed. That is what happened. That is what I did. You know it all. It can never be altered.”
