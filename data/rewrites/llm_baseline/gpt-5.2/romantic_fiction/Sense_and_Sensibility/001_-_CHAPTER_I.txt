The Dashwoods had been rooted in Sussex so long that the very hedgerows seemed to remember their name. Norland Park sat in the middle of its acres like a well-kept hearthstone—broad lawns, old trees, and, in the seasons of fruit, an air that carried sweetness from orchard to pantry. For generations the house had been lived in with such steady decency that the neighboring families spoke of it as they spoke of a familiar tune: not startling, not new, but comforting in its repetitions.

The late master of Norland had been a bachelor, and for years his household had moved to the quiet rhythm kept by his sister—his companion, his housekeeper, the one who knew which linen wanted airing and which preserves needed turning. When she died, ten years before him, the rooms altered at once; not in their furniture, but in their sound. A single footstep echoes differently when it is no longer answered by another. To fill that long hush, he invited into Norland the family of his nephew, Mr. Henry Dashwood—the legal inheritor of the estate, and the man the old gentleman meant to favor.

So Mr. Henry Dashwood came with his wife and daughters, and with him came the domestic music of a younger household: children’s voices in the passages, quick laughter from the shrubbery, the scrape of chairs at supper, the clink of spoons in bowls of warm milk. In time even the old man’s breathing seemed easier within such company. Mr. and Mrs. Henry Dashwood attended him with a constancy that was not merely dutiful but kind; they learned the small preferences of age—when the fire should be made up, how the tea should be drawn, what hour best suited a walk beneath the cedars. Their goodness gave him a solid comfort, and the children—by their very being alive and hungry and in motion—gave relish to days that might otherwise have tasted thin.

Mr. Henry Dashwood had a son by a former marriage, and three daughters by his present lady. The son, John, was a steady, respectable young man, made secure by his mother’s fortune, half of which became his on coming of age. His own marriage soon after enlarged his means again. The Norland succession, then, was never so necessary to him as it was to his sisters; for their portion, without their father’s inheritance, must be small indeed. Their mother had brought nothing; their father had only seven thousand pounds at his own disposal. The remainder of his first wife’s fortune was tied to her child, and he himself held only a life-interest in it—like a man permitted the scent of a dish but denied the right to carve.

Then the old gentleman died. The will was read, and, as wills so often do, it managed to distribute disappointment as carefully as property. He was not so unjust, nor so ungrateful, as to cut off his nephew; but he left Norland in such a manner as to diminish, by a single legal turn, half the value of the inheritance. Mr. Dashwood had wished for the estate far more on account of his wife and daughters than for himself or his son; yet it was secured not to him freely, but to his son—and after him to his son’s son, a child of four. The whole was tied up for the benefit of that small heir in a way that left Mr. Henry Dashwood without power to provide for those dearest to him, who most needed it: no charge could be laid on the estate, no sale made of those valuable woods whose timber, standing straight and ancient, seemed to promise rescue.

The old gentleman had meant no unkindness. It was only that, in those occasional visits when John brought his wife and little boy to Norland, the child had gained upon his great-uncle’s affections with the common arts of infancy—half-formed words, a determined insistence on his own way, cunning tricks performed for praise, and a great deal of noise. Against that lively clamor, the years of considerate attention from Mr. Dashwood’s wife and daughters counted for less than they ought. Still, as a mark of affection, he left each of the three girls a thousand pounds.

Mr. Henry Dashwood’s first shock was severe. Yet his temper was cheerful and hopeful; he could imagine years ahead, and the possibility—by living economically—of laying by a considerable sum from an estate already large, and capable of almost immediate improvement. He began, in his mind, to measure out economies as a cook measures flour: not starving, only careful; not mean, only exact. But the fortune that had come so tardily was his for only one twelvemonth. He survived his uncle no longer. When he died, ten thousand pounds—his own seven thousand, with the three legacies—was all that remained for his widow and daughters.

His son was sent for as soon as his danger was known. The sickroom at Norland had its own low soundscape: the soft fall of a log settling in the grate, a whispered consultation at the door, the careful pouring of broth so it would not splash and startle. With what strength illness could command, Mr. Dashwood pressed John to protect the interest of his stepmother and sisters. John did not have the warm, overflowing feelings of the rest of them; but a recommendation made at such a time, in such a voice, could move even a cooler heart. He promised to do everything in his power to make them comfortable.

His father was eased by that assurance. And once the funeral was over, once the heavy black draperies were taken down and the visitors’ carriages ceased to crunch on the drive, Mr. John Dashwood had leisure to consider what, prudently, might be in his power to do.

He was not ill-disposed—unless coldness and selfishness deserve that name. In general he was well respected, for he behaved with propriety in the common discharge of his duties. Had he married a more amiable woman, he might have been made still more respectable; he might, perhaps, even have been made amiable himself, for he was very young when he married and very fond of his wife. But Mrs. John Dashwood was a strong caricature of him: narrower, sharper, more resolutely turned toward her own advantage.

When John gave his promise, he had it in his mind to increase the fortunes of his sisters by a gift of a thousand pounds apiece. At that moment he truly believed himself equal to it. The prospect of four thousand a year, in addition to his present income, besides the remaining half of his own mother’s fortune, warmed him into something like generosity. In the days that followed, he seasoned the thought as one seasons a dish—adding, tasting, admiring his own liberality.

“Yes,” he told himself, “I will give them three thousand pounds. It will be liberal and handsome. It will make them completely easy. Three thousand pounds! I can spare so considerable a sum with little inconvenience.”

He dwelt on it not only for a day, but for many days successively, and did not repent of his intention.

Yet no sooner was his father’s funeral concluded than Mrs. John Dashwood arrived—without sending the smallest notice to her mother-in-law—bringing her child and a train of attendants. No one could dispute her right; from the moment of Mr. Dashwood’s death the house was her husband’s. But the indelicacy of coming so, of taking possession as if grief had no claim to privacy, was the greater for being lawful. To a woman in Mrs. Dashwood’s situation—newly widowed, uncertain in her future, still raw with sorrow—it must, with only common feelings, have been highly unpleasing. In her mind, where honor was keen and generosity romantic, any offense of this kind—given or received—became a source of fixed disgust.

Mrs. John Dashwood had never been a favorite with any of her husband’s family; but until now she had not had occasion to show, at full length, how little regard she could pay to the comfort of other people when it suited her to act. Mrs. Dashwood felt the ungracious behavior acutely, and despised her daughter-in-law with such earnestness that she would have quitted Norland forever on the very day of their arrival, had not her eldest girl restrained her—first by entreaty to consider what propriety required, and afterward by appealing to that tender love which made Mrs. Dashwood endure much for the sake of her children and avoid, for them, an open breach with their brother.

Elinor, the eldest, whose counsel proved so effectual, possessed strength of understanding and coolness of judgment which qualified her, though only nineteen, to advise her mother. Again and again she had counteracted—quietly, firmly, to the advantage of them all—that eagerness of mind in Mrs. Dashwood which would otherwise have led to imprudence. Elinor’s heart was excellent. Her disposition was affectionate; her feelings were strong; but she knew how to govern them—knowledge her mother had yet to learn, and which one of her sisters had resolved never to be taught.

Marianne’s abilities were, in many respects, quite equal to Elinor’s. She was sensible and clever, but eager in everything. Her sorrows, her joys, could have no moderation. Generous, amiable, interesting—she was everything but prudent. The resemblance between her and her mother was striking. Elinor saw, with concern, the excess of her sister’s sensibility; but Mrs. Dashwood valued it and cherished it, as if intensity were proof of truth. Together now, mother and daughter encouraged each other in the violence of their affliction. The first agony of grief, overwhelming as it was, they did not allow to soften with time; they voluntarily renewed it, sought it, created it again and again. They gave themselves up wholly to sorrow, searching every recollection for whatever could deepen wretchedness, and resolving against ever admitting consolation.

Elinor, too, was deeply afflicted; but she could struggle and exert herself. She could consult with her brother, could receive her sister-in-law on her arrival and treat her with proper attention, and could strive to rouse her mother to similar exertion, to similar forbearance. She bore, in her very composure, the quiet strain of holding a household together when the world’s law and language favored another branch of the family—when the estate spoke, in its cold legal terms, of heirs and entailments, and could not be persuaded to pronounce the warmer, more human words: wife, daughters, home.

Margaret, the youngest, was good-humored and well disposed; but she had already imbibed a good deal of Marianne’s romance without having much of her sense. At thirteen she did not, as yet, bid fair to equal her sisters at a more advanced age.
