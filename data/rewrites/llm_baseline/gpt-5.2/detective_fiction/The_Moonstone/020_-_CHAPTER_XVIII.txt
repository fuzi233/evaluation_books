I went down to the front door, and found Sergeant Cuff on the steps, as quietly planted there as if the house had grown him like an iron gate-post.

After what had passed between us, it went sorely against my grain to seem eager in his affairs; but curiosity is like the smell of onions in a warm kitchen—once it gets into you, it clings. My dignity slipped on the wet flags, and out came my question before I could salt it with reserve.

“What news from Frizinghall?”

“I have seen the Indians,” he said. “And I have found out what Rosanna bought privately in the town, on Thursday last. The Indians will be set free on Wednesday next week. There isn’t a doubt on my mind, and there isn’t a doubt on Mr. Murthwaite’s mind, that they came here to steal the Moonstone. Their calculations were all thrown out by what happened in the house on Wednesday night; and they have no more to do with the actual loss of the jewel than you have. But I can tell you one thing, Mr. Betteredge—if we don’t find the Moonstone, they will. You have not heard the last of the three jugglers yet.”

As he said those startling words, Mr. Franklin came back from his walk. He managed his curiosity better than I had managed mine; passed us without a syllable; and went straight in, as if he had left his mind somewhere out on the gravel and meant to fetch it later.

I had already made a sacrifice of my pride; I resolved to dine on the whole dish.

“So much for the Indians,” I said. “What about Rosanna next?”

He shook his head, like a man smelling a pot that wouldn’t declare itself. “The mystery in that quarter is thicker than ever,” he answered. “I have traced her to a shop at Frizinghall, kept by a linen-draper named Maltby. She bought nothing at any other draper’s, or at any milliner’s, or tailor’s; and she bought nothing at Maltby’s but a piece of long cloth. She was very particular in choosing a certain quality. As to quantity, she bought enough to make a nightgown.”

“Whose nightgown?” I asked.

“Her own, to be sure. Between twelve and three on the Thursday morning, she must have slipped down to your young lady’s room, to settle the hiding of the Moonstone while all the rest of you were in bed. In going back to her own room, her nightgown must have brushed the wet paint on the door. She couldn’t wash out the stain; and she couldn’t safely destroy the nightgown without first providing another like it, to make the inventory of her linen complete.”

“What proves it was Rosanna’s nightgown?” I objected. (A man may be cornered by facts, and still feel stubborn, as a cook does when told the fault is in the seasoning and not in the fire.)

“The material she bought for making the substitute,” he said. “If it had been Miss Verinder’s nightgown, she would have had to buy lace, and frilling, and Lord knows what besides; and she wouldn’t have had time to make it in one night. Plain long cloth means a plain servant’s nightgown. No, no, Mr. Betteredge—all that is clear enough. The pinch of the question is—why, after having provided the substitute, does she hide the smeared nightgown instead of destroying it? If the girl won’t speak out, there is only one way of settling the difficulty. The hiding-place at the Shivering Sand must be searched—and the true state of the case will be discovered there.”

“How are you to find the place?” I asked.

“I am sorry to disappoint you,” he said, in that mild, dreary way of his which gives a chill, “but that’s a secret which I mean to keep to myself.”

“Now, Mr. Betteredge,” he went on, “suppose we drop speculation, and get to business. I told Joyce to have an eye on Rosanna. Where is Joyce?”

Joyce was the Frizinghall policeman left at his disposal. The clock struck two; and, punctual as the kitchen clock when the joint is ready, the carriage came round to take Miss Rachel to her aunt’s.

“One thing at a time,” said the Sergeant, stopping me as I was about to send to look for Joyce. “I must attend to Miss Verinder first.”

The rain was still threatening; so it was the close carriage that had been appointed. Sergeant Cuff beckoned Samuel down from the rumble.

“You will see a friend of mine waiting among the trees, on this side of the lodge-gate,” he said. “My friend, without stopping the carriage, will get up into the rumble with you. You have nothing to do but to hold your tongue, and shut your eyes. Otherwise, you will get into trouble.”

He sent Samuel back, and there was no more to be said. But in my own mind the kettle began to boil over. A watch set on my young lady! A spy behind her in her mother’s carriage! The very notion jarred in my ears as if somebody had brought a factory hammer into the garden—smashing the proper quiet of the place where you ought to hear birds, rain, and the soft talk of leaves. I could have bitten my own tongue clean off for speaking to Sergeant Cuff at all.

My lady was the first to come out. She stood aside on the top step, not saying a word either to him or to me. Lips closed; arms folded under her light garden cloak; still as a statue, waiting.

Then Miss Rachel came down—very nicely dressed in soft yellow stuff that set off her dark complexion, clipped tight at the waist like a little jacket. A smart straw hat; a white veil twisted round it; primrose gloves fitting her hands like a second skin. Her black hair lay smooth as satin; her ears were rosy shells with a pearl dangling from each. She moved swiftly—straight as a lily on its stem, lithe as a young cat. Nothing altered in her pretty face that I could see, but her eyes and her lips. Her eyes were brighter and fiercer than I liked; her lips had lost their colour and their smile so completely that I hardly knew them.

She kissed her mother, sudden and hasty, on the cheek.

“Try to forgive me, mamma,” she said—then pulled down her veil so vehemently she tore it. Next moment she ran down the steps and rushed into the carriage as if it was a hiding-place.

Sergeant Cuff was as quick. He put Samuel back, and stood before Miss Rachel with the carriage-door open in his hand, just as she had settled herself.

“What do you want?” says Miss Rachel, from behind her veil.

“I want to say one word to you, miss,” he answered. “I can’t presume to stop your paying a visit to your aunt. I can only venture to say that your leaving us, as things are now, puts an obstacle in the way of my recovering your Diamond. Please to understand that; and now decide for yourself whether you go or stay.”

Miss Rachel never answered. “Drive on, James!” she called out.

Without another word, the Sergeant shut the door.

Just as it closed, Mr. Franklin came running down the steps.

“Good-bye, Rachel,” he said, holding out his hand.

“Drive on!” cried Miss Rachel, louder than ever, and took no more notice of him than she had taken of Sergeant Cuff.

Mr. Franklin stepped back thunderstruck. The coachman looked towards my lady. My lady—anger, sorrow, and shame all struggling together in her face—made him a sign to start the horses; then turned and went back hastily into the house.

Mr. Franklin found his voice again and called after her, as the carriage drove off, “Aunt! you were quite right. Accept my thanks for all your kindness—and let me go.”

My lady turned as though to speak; then, as if distrusting herself, waved her hand kindly.

“Let me see you, before you leave us, Franklin,” she said, in a broken voice—and went to her room.

“Do me a last favour, Betteredge,” Mr. Franklin said to me, tears in his eyes. “Get me away to the train as soon as you can!”

And he too went in. Miss Rachel had unmanned him utterly; and if that doesn’t tell you how fond he was of her, nothing will.

Sergeant Cuff and I were left face to face at the foot of the steps. He stood with his face set towards a gap in the trees, commanding a view of one of the windings of the drive; hands in pockets; softly whistling “The Last Rose of Summer” to himself, as if the tune were the only thing in the world that still had its right notes.

“There’s a time for everything,” I said, savage enough. “This isn’t a time for whistling.”

At that moment the carriage showed through the gap, winding towards the lodge-gate. Another man besides Samuel was plainly visible in the rumble behind.

“All right!” said the Sergeant to himself. He turned to me. “It’s no time for whistling, Mr. Betteredge, as you say. It’s time to take this business in hand, now, without sparing anybody. We’ll begin with Rosanna Spearman. Where is Joyce?”

We both called; no answer. I sent one of the stable-boys to look for him.

“You heard what I said to Miss Verinder?” the Sergeant remarked, while we waited. “And you saw how she received it? I tell her plainly that her leaving us will be an obstacle in the way of my recovering her Diamond—and she leaves, in the face of that statement! Your young lady has got a travelling companion in her mother’s carriage, Mr. Betteredge—and the name of it is, the Moonstone.”

I said nothing. I held on to my belief in Miss Rachel as a man holds on to the taste of home bread when he’s forced to swallow strange fare: whatever else is taken, that must remain.

The stable-boy came back, with Joyce behind him—very unwillingly, as it seemed to me.

“Where is Rosanna Spearman?” asked Sergeant Cuff.

“I can’t account for it, sir,” Joyce began; “and I am very sorry. But somehow or other—”

“Before I went to Frizinghall,” said the Sergeant, cutting him short, “I told you to keep your eyes on Rosanna Spearman, without allowing her to discover that she was being watched. Do you mean to tell me you have let her give you the slip?”

“I am afraid, sir,” says Joyce, beginning to tremble, “that I was perhaps a little too careful not to let her discover me. There are such a many passages in the lower parts of this house—”

“How long is it since you missed her?”

“Nigh on an hour since, sir.”

“You can go back to your regular business at Frizinghall,” said the Sergeant, composed as ever, quiet and dreary as a winter drizzle. “I don’t think your talents are at all in our line, Mr. Joyce. Your present form of employment is a trifle beyond you. Good morning.”

The man slunk off.

How I was affected by finding Rosanna missing is beyond me to put neatly. I was in fifty different minds at once. I stood staring at Sergeant Cuff, and language went out of me like a fire going down for want of air.

“No, Mr. Betteredge,” he said, as if he could pick my thoughts like currants from a cake. “Your young friend Rosanna won’t slip through my fingers so easy as you think. As long as I know where Miss Verinder is, I have the means at my disposal of tracing Miss Verinder’s accomplice. I prevented them from communicating last night. Very good. They will get together at Frizinghall, instead of getting together here. The present inquiry must be simply shifted (rather sooner than I had anticipated) from this house to the house at which Miss Verinder is visiting. In the meantime, I’m afraid I must trouble you to call the servants together again.”

It is disgraceful, but true: those words gave me another attack of the detective-fever. I forgot I hated him. I seized him confidentially by the arm.

“For goodness’ sake, tell us what you are going to do with the servants now?”

Sergeant Cuff stood stock-still, and addressed himself in a kind of melancholy rapture to the empty air.

“If this man,” said the Sergeant (meaning me, as plain as day), “only understood the growing of roses he would be the most completely perfect character on the face of creation!”

After that strong outburst, he sighed, and put his arm through mine as if we were old friends walking out to inspect a bed of blooms.

“This is how it stands,” he said, dropping down again to business. “Rosanna has done one of two things. She has either gone direct to Frizinghall (before I can get there), or she has gone first to visit her hiding-place at the Shivering Sand. The first thing to find out is, which of the servants saw the last of her before she left the house.”

We instituted the inquiry. It turned out Nancy, the kitchenmaid, had seen the last of Rosanna. Nancy had seen her slip out with a letter in her hand and stop the butcher’s man, who had just been delivering some meat at the back door—raw joints in their brown paper, smelling of rain and blood and the road; the sort of smell that clings under the nails even after scrubbing.

Nancy had heard Rosanna ask him to post the letter when he got back to Frizinghall. The man had looked at the address and said it was a roundabout way of delivering a letter directed to Cobb’s Hole, to post it at Frizinghall—and that, moreover, on a Saturday, which would prevent the letter getting to its destination until Monday morning. Rosanna had answered that the delay till Monday was of no importance. The only thing she wished to be sure of was that he would do what she told him. The man had promised, and driven away. Nancy had been called back to her work in the kitchen; and no other person had seen anything afterwards of Rosanna Spearman.

“Well?” I asked, when we were alone again.

“Well,” says the Sergeant, “I must go to Frizinghall.”

“About the letter, sir?”

“Yes. The memorandum of the hiding-place is in that letter. I must see the address at the post-office. If it is the address I suspect, I shall pay our friend Mrs. Yolland another visit on Monday next.”

I went with him to order the pony-chaise. In the stable-yard we got a new light thrown on the missing girl.
