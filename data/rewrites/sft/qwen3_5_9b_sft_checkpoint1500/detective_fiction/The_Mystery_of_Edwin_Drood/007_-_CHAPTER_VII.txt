"I know very little of that gentleman, sir," Neville stated, turning back toward the road as they walked together.

"You know very little of your guardian?" the Minor Canon asked, echoing the sentiment.

"Almost nothing!" Neville replied.

"How did he—"

"To become my guardian? Allow me to explain, sir. Presumably, you are aware that my sister and I hail from Ceylon?"

"Not at all," the Canon admitted.

"I find that surprising. We were raised by a stepfather there. Our mother perished in infancy, leaving us orphaned and destitute. Life was a wretched existence for us. Upon her passing, she appointed this man as our guardian. His motive, as far as I could discern, was merely a connection—a friendship, perhaps, or a business tie, for his name appeared frequently in print, catching the eye of the local community."

"That must have been recently, I assume?"

"Indeed, sir. Very recently. That stepfather was a cruel brute, a grinder of souls. It is fortunate he died when he did; otherwise, I suspect I might have taken matters into my own hands."

Mr. Crisparkle halted abruptly in the moonlight, staring at his young charge with a mixture of alarm and confusion.

"Do you surprise me, sir?" Neville asked, instantly shifting his demeanor to one of humble submission.

"You shock me. Unspeakably, you shock me."

Neville lowered his gaze, walking silently for a moment before speaking again. "You never witnessed him beating your sister, did you? I have seen him strike mine, more than once or twice, and the image remains etched in my mind forever."

"Nothing," Mr. Crisparkle murmured, his severity waning as a surge of righteous indignation rose within him, "not even the tears of a beloved and beautiful sister under such dastardly abuse, could ever justify the horrible terms you employed."

"I regret those words, especially directed at you, sir. I beg to retract them. However, permit me to clarify a point. You mentioned my sister's tears. If she were truly capable of shedding a tear under his tyranny, she would have allowed him to tear her limb from limb long before."

Mr. Crisparkle paused to consider these mental notes. Neither the revelation surprised him nor did it incite a desire to question its validity further.

"Perhaps it seems strange, sir," Neville ventured, his voice hesitant, "that I should so quickly request permission to confide in you, to ask for your ear and a hearing for my defense?"

"Defense?" Mr. Crisparkle repeated. "You are not on your trial, Mr. Neville."

"I believe I am, sir. Or at least, I know I ought to be, if you fully understood my nature."

"Well, Mr. Neville," the Canon responded gently. "What if I simply wait and discover it for myself?"

"If that pleases you, sir," Neville answered, his countenance darkening slightly into sulky disappointment, "if it is your wish to curb my impulse, I shall submit."

A certain tone lingered in those brief words, unsettling the conscientious man to whom they were addressed. It suggested that, perhaps unintentionally, Mr. Crisparkle might be deflecting a trustworthiness vital to a malformed young mind, potentially hindering his own capacity to guide and improve the boy. They approached the glow of the windows, and the Canon stopped.

"Let us return and pace up and down a bit longer, Mr. Neville. You may not have time to conclude what you wished to say to me. You are hasty in assuming I intend to stop you. On the contrary, I invite your confidence."

"You have invited it, sir, without realizing it, ever since I arrived. I say 'ever since,' as though I have been here a week. The truth is, my sister and I came here intending to quarrel with you, to affront you, and to depart again."

"Really?" Mr. Crisparkle found himself at a loss for words.

"You see, we could not possibly know what you were beforehand, sir, could we?"

"Clearly not," agreed the Canon.

"And having disapproved of everyone else with whom we have encountered, we resolved not to like you."

"Really?" said Mr. Crisparkle again.

"But now we do like you, sir. We perceive an undeniable distinction between your household and your reception of us compared to anything else we have known. This, combined with finding myself alone with you, and the surrounding atmosphere appearing so serene and peaceful following Mr. Honeythunder's departure—all of Cloisterham seeming so ancient, solemn, and beautiful under the moonlight—prompted me to open my heart."

"I quite understand, Mr. Neville. And it is wholesome to listen to such influences."

"In describing my own flaws, sir, I implore you not to mistake them for my sister's shortcomings. She has emerged from our miserable upbringing vastly superior to me, much as that Cathedral tower rises above the surrounding chimneys."

In his own breast, Mr. Crisparkle harbored doubts regarding this assessment.

"For as long as I can remember, sir," Neville continued, "I have had to stifle a deadly and bitter hatred. This has rendered me secretive and vengeful. I have been constantly held down by a strong hand, driving me, in my weakness, toward deceit and baseness. I have been denied education, liberty, money, clothing, the necessities of life, the simple pleasures of childhood, and the common possessions of youth. Consequently, I lack, I know not what emotions, memories, or virtuous instincts—you see, I cannot even name the thing you have cultivated in other youths to whom you have been accustomed!"

"This is evidently true," Mr. Crisparkle thought internally, "yet it is not particularly encouraging."

"And finally, sir," Neville concluded, "I was raised among abject and servile dependents of an inferior race, and I may easily have contracted some affinity with them. Sometimes, I do not know whether it may be a drop of what is tigerish in their blood."

*"As in the case of that remark just now,"* Mr. Crisparkle reflected.

"On a final note concerning my sister, sir—we are twins—you must know, to her honor, that nothing in our misery ever subdued her, although it often cowed me. When we fled from it (we ran away four times in six years, only to be captured and cruelly punished), the escape was invariably planned and led by her. Each time, she dressed as a boy, displaying the daring of a man. I take it we were seven when we first departed; however, I clearly remember losing the pocket-knife intended for cutting her hair short, watching desperately as she attempted to tear it from her scalp or bite it off. I have nothing further to say, sir, save that I hope you will bear with me and grant allowance for my circumstances."

"As to that, Mr. Neville, you may be assured," returned the Minor Canon. "I preach no more than I must, and I will not repay your confidence with a sermon. Nevertheless, I earnestly entreat you to keep steadily in mind that if I am to benefit you, it can only be with your own assistance; and that you can only render that aid effectively by seeking help from Heaven."

"I will endeavor to do my part, sir."

"And, Mr. Neville, I will strive to do mine. Here is my hand upon it. May God bless our endeavors!"

They now stood before the door, where a cheerful murmur of voices and laughter drifted outward.

"We shall take one more circuit before entering," Mr. Crisparkle proposed. "For I wish to ask you a question. When you declared your mind changed regarding me, you spoke not only for yourself but for your sister as well?"

"Undoubtedly, sir," Neville affirmed.

"Forgive me, Mr. Neville, but I believe you have had no opportunity to communicate with your sister since meeting me. Mr. Honeythunder was remarkably eloquent; perhaps, without malice, I may suggest that he rather monopolized the occasion. Might you not have answered for your sister without sufficient warrant?"

Neville shook his head with a proud smile.

"You do not yet know, sir, the extent of the understanding that exists between my sister and me. Though no spoken word—perhaps not even a glance—has passed between us, she feels exactly as I have described, and she is perfectly aware that I am seizing this opportunity to speak for both of us."

Mr. Crisparkle scrutinized the boy's face with a degree of incredulity. Yet, Neville's expression radiated such absolute and firm conviction in the truth of his statement that the Canon cast his gaze downward onto the pavement, pondering silently until they reached the threshold again.

"I shall request one more turn, sir, this time," the young man said, a flush rising in his cheeks. "But for Mr. Honeythunder's—I think you termed it eloquence, sir? (with a sly nod)—"

"Yes, I called it eloquence," the Canon conceded.

"But absent Mr. Honeythunder's eloquence, I might not have needed to inquire of you what follows. This gentleman, Mr. Edwin Drood, sir—is that the name?"

"Quite correct," said Mr. Crisparkle. "D-r-double-o-d."
