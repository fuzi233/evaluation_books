On the twenty-seventh of June, I received orders from Sergeant Cuff to pursue three individuals suspected of murder and identified as "Indians." They had been spotted earlier that morning at Tower Wharf boarding a steamer destined for Rotterdam. Departing London via a rival line on the morning of Thursday, the twenty-eighth, I arrived in Rotterdam and located the captain of the vessel that had departed on the previous Wednesday.

He confirmed that the three men had indeed been passengers, though only as far as Gravesend. Beyond that point, one of the trio had asked the time of arrival in Calais. Upon learning the ship was bound for Rotterdam instead, the group's spokesperson expressed profound shock and distress at their error. They offered to forfeit their entire fare if the captain would allow them to disembark. Viewing them as helpless foreigners stranded in a foreign land with no apparent cause for detention, the captain signaled for a lifeboat. The three men transferred to it and went ashore.

It was evident that this maneuver had been premeditated specifically to evade detection. Without delay, I returned to England. Leaving the steamer at Gravesend, I tracked the men back to London, and subsequently followed their trail to Plymouth. Local inquiries revealed that they had departed forty-eight hours prior aboard the *Bewley Castle*, an East Indiaman sailing directly to Bombay. Acting on this intelligence, Sergeant Cuff instructed the authorities in Bombay to intercept the vessel upon its arrival, ensuring the police could board it immediately. Having secured this outcome, my involvement in the case concluded. I have heard nothing further regarding the matter since.

**II. The Statement of the Captain of the *Bewley Castle* (1849)**

At the request of Sergeant Cuff, I commit to writing the following account concerning three passengers, believed to be Hindus, who traveled under my command last summer on the *Bewley Castle* en route to Bombay. These men boarded at Plymouth. Throughout the outward voyage, I received no complaints regarding their behavior; they were accommodated in the forward section of the ship. Personally, I observed them rarely.

Towards the end of the journey, we encountered severe bad luck, remaining becalmed off the Indian coast for three full days and nights. Lacking the ship's logbook to verify exact coordinates, I can only recall generally that the currents drifted us closer to land. Once the wind returned, we reached port within twenty-four hours.

As any sailor knows, prolonged calms inevitably relax shipboard discipline. This occurred on my vessel. Several passenger gentlemen lowered the smaller boats and amused themselves rowing and swimming whenever the evening air cooled sufficiently. Protocol dictated these boats be hoisted back into their davits afterward, yet they were left moored alongside. Amidst the oppressive heat and the frustration of the stillness, neither officers nor crew seemed inclined toward their duties.

On the third night, the watch reported nothing unusual. However, when dawn broke, the smallest boat was missing, and shortly thereafter, reports surfaced that the three Hindu passengers were also absent. Had they stolen the boat just after dark—as I am convinced they did—we were too close to shore for a pursuit to be viable once the loss was discovered in the morning. I am certain they landed during the calm before daylight, accounting for potential exhaustion and clumsy navigation.

Upon arriving at port, I learned for the first time why these passengers sought to escape. I conveyed only what I have stated here to the local authorities. They deemed me culpable for permitting the relaxation of discipline. I have expressed my regrets to both the officials and the shipowners. Since that time, no further information has come to my knowledge regarding the three men.

Do you retain any memory, my dear sir, of a somewhat uncouth gentleman you encountered at dinner in London during the autumn of 1848? His name was Murthwaite, and we engaged in a lengthy conversation following the meal. Our discussion centered on an Indian diamond known as the Moonstone and a contemporary conspiracy to seize the gem.

Since then, I have wandered through Central Asia and recently revisited the northern and northwestern regions of India where I once adventured. Approximately two weeks ago, I found myself in a remote province known as Kattiawar. Here, an event transpired—incredible as it seems—that involves you personally.

Kattiawar is a rugged territory; even the farmers till the soil armed to the teeth. The populace remains fanatically devoted to ancient Hinduism, worshipping Brahma and Vishnu. The sparse Muslim families scattered in the interior dare not eat meat. Should a Muslim be suspected of harming the sacred cow, he is summarily executed by the surrounding devout neighbors. To bolster this fervor, two of Hinduism's most renowned pilgrimage sites lie within Kattiawar's borders: Dwarka, the birthplace of Krishna, and the holy city of Somnath, sacked and destroyed in the eleventh century by the conqueror Mahmud of Ghazni.

Finding myself in these romantic lands for the second time, I resolved not to depart Kattiawar without witnessing once more the magnificent desolation of Somnath. Traveling on foot toward the sacred city, I calculated I was roughly three days' journey away. Soon, I noticed others traveling in my direction, appearing singly and in small groups. To those who addressed me, I claimed to be a Hindu-Buddhist from a distant province on pilgrimage. Dressed appropriately, speaking the language fluently, and possessing the lean, tanned complexion of a local, I easily blended in—not as a native, but as a traveler from afar.

By the second day, dozens and then hundreds of Hindus were moving in my direction. By the third day, the throng swelled to thousands, all converging slowly toward Somnath. A minor favor I rendered to a fellow pilgrim on the third day introduced me to members of the higher castes. Through them, I learned the multitude was heading to a grand religious ceremony on a nearby hill, scheduled for that very night, honoring the God of the Moon.

As we approached the site, the crowd detained us. By the time we reached the hill, the moon hung high. My Hindu guides possessed special privileges granting access to the shrine, and they graciously permitted me to join them. Upon arrival, the shrine was concealed behind a curtain strung between two majestic trees. Beneath these trees, a flat rock formation projected outward, creating a natural platform. Standing below with my guides, I gazed upward.

Looking back down the slope, I beheld the most spectacular union of nature and humanity I have ever witnessed. The lower hills faded imperceptibly into a grassy plain where three rivers converged. On one side, the graceful curves of the water snaked away, alternating between visibility and concealment beneath the trees until the horizon. On the other, the ocean lay motionless in the night's calm.

Imagine this serene landscape populated by tens of thousands of humans, all clad in white, cascading down the hillside, spilling onto the plain, and lining the riverbanks. Illuminate this tableau with the wild red glow of cressets and torches flickering intermittently throughout the vast crowd. Envision the unclouded eastern moonlight pouring its glory over everything. Such was the vision that greeted me from the hilltop.

A mournful melody played on strings and flutes drew my attention to the hidden shrine. Turning, I saw three figures on the rocky platform. In the center stood the man I had spoken with in England when the "Indians" appeared on the terrace at Lady Verinder's house. His two companions from that occasion were undoubtedly present here as well.

One spectator near me noticed my startle. Whispering, he explained the significance of the three figures. They were Brahmins who had forfeited their caste in service to the god. The deity had decreed their purification must be achieved through pilgrimage. That night, the three men were to part ways forever. Setting out in three distinct directions as pilgrims to various shrines across India, they were never to see each other's faces again, nor rest from their wanderings from the day of their separation until the day of their death.

As these words were whispered, the mournful music ceased. The three men prostrated themselves on the rock before the curtained shrine. Rising, they looked upon one another, embraced, and then descended separately into the crowd. The people parted for them in absolute silence. In three different directions, the masses split simultaneously. Slowly, the great white sea of humanity closed back together, erasing the tracks of the doomed men. We saw them no more.

Then, a new strain of music, loud and jubilant, erupted from the hidden shrine. The crowd around me shuddered and pressed tighter. The curtain between the trees was drawn aside, revealing the shrine. There, seated high on a throne atop his symbolic antelope, with four arms stretching toward the corners of the earth, loomed the God of the Moon, dark and awe-inspiring in the mystical heavenly light. And there, in the forehead of the deity, gleamed the yellow diamond whose brilliance had last illuminated me in England, emerging from the bosom of a woman's dress!

Yes! After eight centuries, the Moonstone shines forth once more over the walls of the sacred city where its tale began.
