As the sun dipped below the Tuscan horizon, casting a warm orange glow over the rolling hills, Romola navigated the winding streets of Florence, her heart heavy with the weight of the day's revelations. The Trial by Fire had been a catalyst for her return, and now she found herself drawn into the midst of the city's fervent emotions. Amidst the joyful exclamations and mournful wails, she sat quietly in Monna Brigida's modest home, listening intently to the older woman's tales of the city's turmoil.

Monna Brigida, once a devout follower of Savonarola, had since backslid into false hair, but now donned a somber expression, her voice laced with a mixture of sorrow and curiosity. As she spoke of Tito's tragic demise, Romola's thoughts turned to the young widow and her two children, whose fate remained a mystery. Her godfather's confessor, a monk from San Spirito, had spoken of Tito's murder on the banks of the Arno, but Romola knew the true extent of the catastrophe. Savonarola's confession of duplicity under torture weighed heavily on her mind, leaving her little room for the trivial gossip that swirled around her.

Romola's gaze fell upon Monna Brigida, her eyes pleading for information. "Tell me, dear cousin," she asked abruptly, "has anyone spoken of a young woman with two little children since Tito's passing?" Monna Brigida's expression changed, her eyes rounding in surprise, as she lifted her hands in a gesture of shock. "Cristo! no," she exclaimed. "What! was he so bad as that, my poor child? Ah, then, that was why you went away, and left me word only that you went of your own free will."

Romola's interruption was swift and gentle, as she sought to redirect the conversation. "No, dear cousin, please do not speak so. I wish above all things to find that young woman and her children, and to take care of them. They are quite helpless. Say nothing against it; that is the thing I shall do first of all." Monna Brigida's expression softened, her voice dropping to a whisper as she shrugged her shoulders and murmured, "If that's being a Piagnone, I've been taking peas for paternosters. Why, Fra Girolamo said as good as that widows ought not to marry again.
