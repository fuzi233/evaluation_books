“Of course we must dine with Mrs. Carfry, dearest,” Archer said; and May, seated opposite him, answered with an anxious crease of her brows over the monumental Britannia-ware breakfast-service of their lodging-house table.

Outside, London made its usual autumnal sound: rain whisking in narrow streets, wheels sifting through wet grit, a distant iron clatter that seemed to come from everywhere at once. Inside, the dining-room had its own small acoustics—the rasp of a knife on toast, the soft collapse of a muffin when it was split, the thin music of a spoon against a teacup—domestic noises that, for May, were almost as reassuring as food. Yet even with hot tea breathing steam and the butter beginning to melt into the crannies of their bread, the invitation lay between them like something indigestible.

In all the rainy desert of London there were only two people whom the Newland Archers knew; and these two, by old New York instinct, they had tried not to know. There was a tradition—sturdy as a family recipe, and just as unexamined—that it was not “dignified” to force oneself upon acquaintances abroad. Mrs. Archer and Janey had practised that rule on earlier travels with the same careful zeal with which they packed their Wardian cases and their home pharmacy: hermetically. Friendly advances had been met with a reserve so polished it might have passed for virtue. Foreigners, as far as their record showed, existed chiefly to run hotels and sell railway tickets; and even compatriots—unless properly accredited by the right names—were treated with a colder disdain, as though American familiarity outside Fifth Avenue were a form of bad table-manners.

But precautions, like sauces, do not always hold their intended texture. One night at Botzen, an English lady from the room across the passage—whose name, dress, habits and implied social rank were already known to Janey with the precision of a food historian indexing a menu—knocked to ask for a bottle of liniment. Her sister, Mrs. Carfry, had been taken with bronchitis. Mrs. Archer, who travelled with remedies as other women travelled with jewels, produced what was needed; and because Mrs. Carfry was genuinely ill, and the sisters were alone, gratitude set to work with a stubbornness that ignored all the Archers’ notions of dignity. The Archer ladies supplied comforts with the ingenuity of people who know how to make do—hot-water bottles warmed and refilled, a tray arranged so that nourishment could be taken without effort, a brothy little soup coaxed from hotel odds and ends, its smell of pepper and simmered bone somehow more intimate than any speech. Their efficient maid helped nurse the invalid back into breath.

When the Archers left Botzen they imagined the tie would evaporate with the mountain air. To Mrs. Archer, nothing would have been more undignified than to seek out, in another country, a “foreigner” upon whom one had accidentally bestowed a service. But Mrs. Carfry and Miss Harle, to whom this whole code was as incomprehensible as a recipe in a dead dialect, felt themselves linked by eternal obligation to the “delightful Americans.” With touching fidelity—and a kind of supernatural acuteness for timetables—they seized every chance of meeting Mrs. Archer and Janey again on the Continent, and found out, with uncanny promptness, when they would pass through London. The intimacy became indissoluble. Whenever the Archer ladies alighted at Brown’s Hotel, two affectionate friends awaited them—friends who cultivated ferns in Wardian cases, made macramé lace, read the Baroness Bunsen’s memoirs, and had views about the occupants of leading London pulpits.

As Mrs. Archer said, it made “another thing of London” to know Mrs. Carfry and Miss Harle. By the time Newland became engaged, the families’ tie was so firmly established that it was thought only right to send a wedding invitation; and the English ladies replied with a pretty bouquet of pressed Alpine flowers under glass, a little preserved landscape of colour—petals flattened into permanent memory.

And on the dock, when Newland and May sailed for England, Mrs. Archer’s last injunction had been: “You must take May to see Mrs. Carfry.”

Newland and his wife had had no real intention of obeying. But Mrs. Carfry, in the way she had of making gratitude active, had run them down and sent an invitation to dine; and it was over that invitation that May was wrinkling her brows, her spoon paused above her tea.

“It’s all very well for you, Newland; you know them. But I shall feel so shy among a lot of people I’ve never met. And what shall I wear?”

He leaned back, smiling at her. She looked handsomer and more Diana-like than ever. The moist English air had deepened the bloom of her cheeks, softened the slight hardness of her virginal lines; or perhaps it was only the inner glow of content shining through, like light under ice. Even at breakfast she had a kind of clean radiance, as if happiness were something you could smell faintly—like linen warmed at a fire, or milk just brought in from the cold.

“Wear, dearest? I thought a trunkful of things had come from Paris last week.”

“Yes, of course. I meant—I shan’t know which to wear.” She pouted a little, as she had when a ribbon would not sit right. “I’ve never dined out in London; and I don’t want to be ridiculous.”

He tried to enter into her perplexity. “But don’t Englishwomen dress just like everybody else in the evening?”

“Newland! How can you ask such funny questions? When they go to the theatre in old ball-dresses and bare heads.”

“Well—perhaps they wear new ball-dresses at home; but at any rate Mrs. Carfry and Miss Harle won’t. They’ll wear caps like my mother’s—and shawls; very soft shawls.”

“Yes; but how will the other women be dressed?”

“Not as well as you, dear,” he said, struck by this sudden resemblance to Janey’s worried vigilance over clothes. May pushed back her chair with a sigh; the legs scraped faintly on the floor, a small protest in the room’s quiet.

“That’s dear of you, Newland; but it doesn’t help me much.”

He had an inspiration. “Why not wear your wedding-dress? That can’t be wrong, can it?”

“Oh, dearest! If I only had it here! But it’s gone to Paris to be made over for next winter, and Worth hasn’t sent it back.”

“Oh, well—” Archer rose, and glanced toward the window where the grey light seemed to be thinning. “Look here—the fog’s lifting. If we made a dash for the National Gallery we might manage to catch a glimpse of the pictures.”

They were on their way home after a three months’ wedding-tour which May, in letters to her girl friends, had vaguely summarised as “blissful.” They had not gone to the Italian Lakes; Archer, on reflection, could not picture his wife in that particular setting, where ruins and cypresses seemed to demand a more restless imagination than May owned. Her own inclinations—after a month with the Paris dressmakers—had been for mountaineering in July and swimming in August. This plan they punctually fulfilled, spending July at Interlaken and Grindelwald, and August at a little place called Étretat on the Normandy coast, recommended as quaint and quiet. There, the sea’s roar and the gulls’ cries entered their rooms like additional guests, and May—salt on her lips, hair damp from bathing—had been happiest in the blunt animal pleasure of motion, appetite, sleep. Once or twice in the mountains Archer had pointed southward and said, “There’s Italy”; and May, her feet sunk in a gentian-bed, had smiled cheerfully and replied: “It would be lovely to go there next winter, if only you didn’t have to be in New York.”

But in reality travelling interested her even less than he had expected. She regarded it—once her clothes were ordered—as merely an enlarged opportunity for walking, riding, swimming, and trying her hand at the fascinating new game of lawn tennis. When they finally got back to London, where they were to spend a fortnight while he ordered his clothes, she no longer concealed her eagerness to sail. In London nothing interested her but the shops and the theatres; and the theatres were less exciting than the Paris cafés chantants where, under blossoming horse-chestnuts on the Champs Élysées, she had had the novel experience of looking down from a restaurant terrace on an audience of cocottes, while her husband interpreted for her as much of the songs as he thought suitable for bridal ears. Even there, the music and laughter had seemed to Archer muffled by a sort of commercial clang, as if amusement itself were being hammered into a product; and he caught himself longing for quieter, older kinds of sound—voices unforced, a room where talk moved like a shared dish passed from hand to hand.

Archer had reverted to all his old inherited ideas about marriage. It was less trouble to conform to tradition and treat May exactly as all his friends treated their wives than to attempt the freer theories with which his untrammelled bachelorhood had once toyed. There was no use in trying to emancipate a wife who had not the dimmest notion that she was not free; and he had long since discovered that May’s only use of the liberty she supposed herself to possess would be to lay it on the altar of her wifely adoration. Her innate dignity would always keep her from making the gift abjectly; and a day might even come—as it once had—when she would find strength to take it back altogether if she thought she were doing it for his own good. But with a conception of marriage so uncomplicated and incurious as hers, such a crisis could be brought about only by something visibly outrageous in his conduct; and the fineness of her feeling for him made that unthinkable.

Whatever happened, he knew, she would always be loyal, gallant, unresentful—and that pledged him to the practice of the same virtues. All this drew him back into his old habits of mind. If her simplicity had been petty, he would have chafed; but since the lines of her character, though few, were on the same fine mould as her face, she became the tutelary divinity of his traditions and reverences. Such qualities were scarcely of a kind to enliven foreign travel—though they made her so easy, so pleasant a companion; but he saw at once how they would fall into place in their proper setting. He had no fear of being oppressed by them. His artistic and intellectual life would go on, as it always had, outside the domestic circle; and within it there would be nothing small and stifling. Coming back to May would never be like entering a stuffy room after a tramp in the open. And when they had children, the vacant corners in both their lives would be filled.

These things went through his mind during their long slow drive from Mayfair to South Kensington, where Mrs. Carfry and Miss Harle lived. Archer, too, would have preferred to escape his friends’ hospitality. In conformity with family tradition, he had always travelled as a sight-seer and looker-on, affecting a haughty unconsciousness of the presence of his fellow-beings. Once only, just after Harvard, he had spent a few gay weeks at Florence with a band of queer Europeanised Americans—dancing all night with titled ladies in palaces, gambling half the day with rakes and dandies at a fashionable club—but it had all seemed to him, though the greatest fun, as unreal as carnival. Those cosmopolitan women, steeped in complicated love-affairs they felt compelled to retail to every stranger, and the magnificent young officers and elderly dyed wits who were the recipients or subjects of those confidences, were too different from the people Archer had grown up among—too much like expensive, over-perfumed hot-house exotics, rich in scent but faintly malodorous, to detain his imagination.

To introduce his wife into such a society was out of the question; and in the course of his travels no other had shown marked eagerness for his company. Not long after their arrival in London he had run across the Duke of St. Austrey, who had instantly and cordially recognised him and said: “Look me up, won’t you?”—but no proper-spirited American would have considered that suggestion actionable, and the encounter had ended without sequel.

They had even contrived to avoid May’s English aunt, the banker’s wife in Yorkshire. Indeed they had postponed coming to London until autumn so that their arrival during the season might not appear pushing and snobbish to these unknown relatives. In the hansom May sat at his side spotlessly splendid in a sky-blue cloak edged with swansdown—so pure a piece of softness that it seemed wicked to expose it to London’s grime and coal breath.

“Probably there’ll be nobody at Mrs. Carfry’s—London’s a desert at this season, and you’ve made yourself much too beautiful,” Archer said, trying to lighten her apprehension.

“I don’t want them to think that we dress like savages,” she replied, with a scorn that Pocahontas might have resented; and again he was struck by the religious reverence of even the most unworldly American women for the social advantages of dress.

It’s their armour, he thought: their defence against the unknown—and their defiance of it. And he understood for the first time the earnestness with which May—who was incapable of tying a ribbon in her hair to charm him—had gone through the solemn rite of selecting and ordering her extensive wardrobe, as though garments were a dialect one must speak flawlessly when words failed.

He had been right in expecting the party to be small. Besides their hostess and her sister, in the long chilly drawing-room they found only another shawled lady, a genial Vicar who was her husband, a silent lad whom Mrs. Carfry named as her nephew, and a small dark gentleman with lively eyes whom she introduced as the nephew’s tutor, pronouncing a French name as she did so.

The room’s cold had a particular smell: damp wool, extinguished coal, potpourri that had long since lost its roses, and the faint metallic taint of London air that seeped in even through heavy curtains. The sounds, too, were muffled—voices absorbed by draperies, footsteps swallowed by carpet—so that every cough and the tick of the clock seemed unduly intimate.

Into this dimly lit group May floated like a swan with the sunset on her. She seemed larger, fairer, more voluminously rustling than Archer had ever seen her; and he perceived that the rosiness and rustlingness were tokens of extreme and infantile shyness. Her helpless eyes implored him—What on earth will they expect me to talk about?—at the very moment that her dazzling apparition was calling forth the same anxiety in their own bosoms.

But beauty, even distrustful of itself, awakens confidence in manly hearts; and the Vicar and the French-named tutor were soon manifesting to May their desire to put her at her ease. In spite of their best efforts, however, dinner languished. Archer noticed that May’s way of showing herself at ease with foreigners was to become more uncompromisingly local in her references, as though she could hold herself steady only by pressing her feet hard on the familiar ground of names and places that meant nothing to her listeners. The Vicar soon abandoned the struggle; but the tutor, who spoke fluent, accomplished English, gallantly continued to pour it out to her, as patiently as one bastes a roast that refuses to brown, until the ladies, to the manifest relief of all concerned, went up to the drawing-room.

The dining-room, once the women had left, seemed to exhale. Port was poured. The Vicar, after a glass, was obliged to hurry away to a meeting; the shy nephew, who appeared to be an invalid, was packed off to bed. The house fell into a quieter register, and Archer, left with the tutor, felt the air change in his lungs as if a window had opened on a different climate.

They continued to sit over their wine, and suddenly Archer found himself talking as he had not done since his last symposium with Ned Winsett. The Carfry nephew, it turned out, had been threatened with consumption and sent to Switzerland, where he had spent two years by Lake Leman. Being bookish, he had been entrusted to M. Rivière, who had brought him back to England and was to remain until the youth went up to Oxford in the spring. M. Rivière added, with a simplicity that made his poverty more naked than complaint, that he should then have to look for another job.

It seemed impossible to Archer that he should be long without one. He was about thirty, with a thin ugly face—May would certainly have called him common-looking—yet his ideas played over it with such intensity that the features became expressive, lit from within. There was nothing cheap in his animation. His father, dead young, had held a small diplomatic post; the son had been intended for the same career, but an insatiable taste for letters had thrown him into journalism, then into authorship (apparently unsuccessful), and at length—after other experiments and vicissitudes, which he spared his listener—into tutoring English youths in Switzerland.

Before that, however, he had lived much in Paris: frequented the Goncourts’ grenier, been advised by Maupassant not to attempt to write—an honour that dazzled Archer—and had often talked with Mérimée in his mother’s house. He had been desperately poor and anxious, with a mother and unmarried sister to provide for; his literary ambitions had failed. Materially his situation seemed no more brilliant than Winsett’s. But he had lived in a world where, as he said, no one who loved ideas need hunger mentally. Since it was precisely that love that poor Winsett was starving of, Archer looked with a sort of vicarious envy at this eager impecunious man who had fared so richly in his poverty.

“You see, Monsieur, it’s worth everything, isn’t it,” M. Rivière was saying, “to keep one’s intellectual liberty—not to enslave one’s powers of appreciation, one’s critical independence? It was because of that I abandoned journalism, and took to so much duller work: tutoring, private secretaryship. There is drudgery, of course; but one preserves one’s moral freedom—what we call in French one’s quant à soi. And when one hears good talk one can join in it without compromising any opinions but one’s own; or one can listen, and answer inwardly.” He paused to light another cigarette; the match flared, then died, leaving a thread of smoke that smelled sharply of sulphur and paper. “Ah, good conversation—there’s nothing like it, is there? The air of ideas is the only air worth breathing. And so I have never regretted giving up either diplomacy or journalism—two different forms of the same self-abdication.”

He fixed his vivid eyes on Archer. “Voyez-vous, Monsieur—to be able to look life in the face: that’s worth living in a garret for, isn’t it? But, after all, one must earn enough to pay for the garret. And I confess that to grow old as a private tutor—or a ‘private’ anything—is almost as chilling to the imagination as a second secretaryship at Bucharest.” He leaned forward, the question coming out as if it had been simmering for a long time and had at last boiled over. “Sometimes I feel I must make a plunge—an immense plunge. Do you suppose, for instance, there would be any opening for me in America—in New York?”

Archer looked at him, startled. New York—for a young man who had frequented the Goncourts and Flaubert, and who spoke of ideas as if they were bread and air. He stared, perplexed, wondering how to tell him that his very superiorities would be the surest hindrance to success.

“New York—New York—but must it be especially New York?” he stammered, utterly unable to imagine what lucrative opening his native city could offer to a man to whom good conversation seemed the only necessity.

A flush rose under M. Rivière’s sallow skin. “I—I thought it your metropolis: is not the intellectual life more active there?” Then, as if fearing he had asked a favour, he went on quickly: “One throws out random suggestions—more to oneself than to others. In reality, I see no immediate prospect—” He rose without constraint. “But Mrs. Carfry will think that I ought to be taking you upstairs.”

During the homeward drive Archer pondered. London’s night noises pressed in around the hansom—hooves striking wet stone, the distant, ceaseless grind of wheels, the occasional shout that ricocheted off brick and vanished. His hour with M. Rivière had put new air into his lungs, and his first impulse had been to invite him to dine the next day; but he was beginning to understand why married men did not always immediately yield to their first impulses.

“That young tutor is an interesting fellow,” he threw out tentatively. “We had some awfully good talk after dinner—about books and things.”

May roused herself from one of the dreamy silences into which he had once read so many meanings, before six months of marriage had given him the key to them.

“The little Frenchman? Wasn’t he dreadfully common?” she asked coldly; and Archer guessed she nursed a secret disappointment at having been invited out in London only to meet a clergyman and a French tutor.

The disappointment was not what is ordinarily called snobbishness. It was old New York’s sense of what was due to it when it risked its dignity abroad. If May’s parents had entertained the Carfrys in Fifth Avenue, they would have offered them something more substantial than a parson and a schoolmaster.

Archer, on edge, took her up. “Common—common where?”

“Why, I should say anywhere but in his school-room. Those people are always awkward in society.” Then, disarmingly: “But then I suppose I shouldn’t have known if he was clever.”

Archer disliked her use of “clever” almost as much as “common”; but he was beginning to fear his tendency to dwell on what he disliked in her. After all, her point of view had always been the same: that of all the people he had grown up among. He had always regarded it as necessary but negligible. Until a few months ago he had never known a “nice” woman who looked at life differently; and if a man married, it must be among the nice.

“Ah—then I won’t ask him to dine!” he concluded with a laugh; and May echoed, bewildered: “Goodness—ask the Carfrys’ tutor?”

“Well, not on the same day with the Carfrys, if you prefer I shouldn’t. But I did rather want another talk with him. He’s looking for a job in New York.”

Her surprise increased with her indifference; he almost fancied she suspected him of being tainted with foreignness.

“A job in New York? What sort of a job? People don’t have French tutors: what does he want to do?”

“Chiefly to enjoy good conversation, I understand,” her husband retorted perversely; and she broke into an appreciative laugh.

“Oh, Newland, how funny! Isn’t that French?”

On the whole, he was glad to have the matter settled by her refusing to take seriously his wish to invite M. Rivière. Another after-dinner talk would have made it difficult to avoid the question of New York; and the more Archer considered it the less he could fit M. Rivière into any conceivable picture of New York as he knew it—a city whose dominant lexicon could label a man “common” and be done with him, as though a single word were a verdict and not merely an accent.

He perceived, with a flash of chilling insight, that in future many problems would be thus negatively solved for him; but as he paid the hansom and followed his wife’s long train into the house, he took refuge in the comforting platitude that the first six months were always the most difficult in marriage. After that, he told himself, they would have finished rubbing off each other’s angles—only the worst of it was that May’s pressure was already bearing on the very angles whose sharpness he most wanted to keep.
