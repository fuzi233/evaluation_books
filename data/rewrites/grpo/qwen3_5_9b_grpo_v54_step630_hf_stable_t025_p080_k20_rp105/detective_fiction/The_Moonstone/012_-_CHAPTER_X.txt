The rest of the company trailed behind the Ablewhites, forming a procession that eventually settled into a complete assembly of twenty-four souls. It was a dignified tableau, particularly when they were arranged around the dinner table and the Rector of Frizinghall rose to offer grace with a fluency that bordered on the theatrical.

There is no necessity to catalogue every guest; save for two specific individuals, none of them will appear again in my narrative. Those two, however, occupied the seats immediately flanking Miss Rachel, the undisputed queen of the evening. Her presence was the focal point of every gaze, a fact that caused a quiet irritation to my lady, for she wore the very jewel that had been her birthday gift—the Moonstone. Though unmounted, the gem had been ingeniously affixed by Mr. Franklin as a brooch against the bosom of her white gown. Its sheer size and brilliance commanded universal awe, yet it was only the two guests beside Miss Rachel who offered commentary of a peculiar nature.

To her left sat Mr. Candy, the local physician. He was a genial, diminutive man, though plagued by a penchant for ill-timed jests and a tendency to plunge into conversation with strangers without the requisite caution. In society, he frequently caused friction, but in his medical practice, he possessed an intuitive discretion that often saved him from error. His comment regarding the Diamond was delivered as a scientific prank. "We shall first heat it, Miss Rachel," he declared gravely, "to a precise degree, then expose it to an air current, and gradually—puff!—evaporate the stone, sparing you the burden of guarding such a precious treasure!" My lady watched with a furrowed brow, wishing the doctor were sincere enough to have convinced Miss Rachel to sacrifice her gift for the sake of science.

On Miss Rachel's right sat an eminent figure: the celebrated Indian traveler, Mr. Murthwaite. He was a tall, lean, wiry man of brown complexion, possessing a weary demeanor and an intensely steady gaze. Rumors suggested he was weary of domestic life and yearned to return to the wilds of the East. Throughout the entire dinner, he spoke scarcely six words and consumed not a drop of wine, his sole interest fixed entirely on the Moonstone. The legend of the gem had evidently reached him during his perilous wanderings. After gazing at it for so long that Miss Rachel grew confused, he spoke in his cool, immovable tone: "If you ever travel to India, Miss Verinder, do not take your uncle's gift. A Hindoo diamond is often consecrated to a religion. I know a city and a temple where, dressed as you are now, your life would not be worth five minutes' purchase." Miss Rachel, secure in England, found the warning delightful. The Bouncers, dropping their cutlery in excitement, cried out, "Oh, how interesting!" My lady fidgeted and hastily changed the subject.

As the meal progressed, I perceived that the festival was failing to prosper as previous celebrations had. Looking back with the benefit of hindsight, I am inclined to believe the cursed Diamond cast a blight upon the entire gathering. I circulated with wine, urging guests to try unpopular dishes, yet nine times out of ten they refused, citing my old loyalty to no avail. Silence stretched across the table, creating an atmosphere of personal discomfort. When speech resumed, it was innocently directed toward the worst possible ends. Mr. Candy, for instance, uttered unluckier phrases than I had ever heard him.

Consider the case of Mrs. Threadgall, widow of the late Professor Threadgall. This good lady habitually omitted the fact that her husband was deceased, assuming every adult in England should know this. When the topic of human anatomy arose, she seamlessly introduced her late husband as his favorite pastime. Unluckily, Mr. Candy, sitting opposite and unaware of the deceased, seized the opportunity to assist the Professor's anatomical recreations. "They have remarkably fine skeletons lately at the College of Surgeons," he announced loudly. "I recommend the Professor visit them." The company fell silent out of respect for the dead professor. Behind Mrs. Threadgall, I whispered encouragement, but she dropped her head and murmured, "My beloved husband is no more." Mr. Candy, oblivious, continued louder. "The Professor may not be aware that a College card admits him daily between ten and four." Mrs. Threadgall buried her face in her tucker and repeated, "My beloved husband is no more." I signaled frantically; Miss Rachel touched Mr. Candy's arm; my lady glared. To no avail. He pressed on. "I shall be delighted to send the Professor my card if you mention his present address." "His present address, sir, is the grave," Mrs. Threadgall snapped, her voice ringing with fury. "He has been dead ten years." "Oh, good Heavens!" cried Mr. Candy. Save for the Bouncers, who laughed, a profound blankness fell over the room, as if everyone were heading toward the grave alongside the professor.

The rest of the guests were equally provoking. Mr. Godfrey, usually eloquent, remained silent in private, perhaps sulking after his earlier discomfiture in the rose-garden. He confined his discourse to the lady seated beside him, a spiritually minded committee-woman with a fine collarbone and a taste for dry champagne. Overheard snippets revealed they were debating serious matters: religion equated to love, love to religion, and earth to a heaven slightly worn but repaired. Yet, why did Mr. Godfrey keep such profound insights to himself?

Mr. Franklin, too, failed to stir the company into merriment. Having recovered from his earlier setback, he was in high spirits, yet he invariably pitched conversations on the wrong subjects or addressed the wrong persons, offending some and puzzling all. His foreign training manifested in bewildering ways: he discussed a wife's admiration for another man with the vicar's maiden aunt in French; he lectured the lord of the manor on breeding bulls using German philosophy; and when the county member raged about democracy, Mr. Franklin replied in Italian that what remained were "Love, Music, and Salad." These outbursts terrified the company. Furthermore, when the English side of him emerged, he ridiculed the medical profession so sharply that he enraged good-natured Mr. Candy.

The dispute began when Mr. Franklin admitted to poor sleep. Mr. Candy diagnosed disordered nerves and prescribed medicine. Mr. Franklin retorted that medicine and groping in the dark were synonymous. Mr. Candy countered that Mr. Franklin was constitutionally groping in the dark and needed medicine to find his way. Mr. Franklin agreed, noting he had finally understood the phrase "the blind leading the blind." The exchange escalated until both were heated, Mr. Candy losing control in defense of his profession. My lady was forced to intervene, forbidding the dispute. This act of authority extinguished the remaining spirits. Talk resumed sporadically but lacked life. The Devil—or the Diamond—seemed to possess the dinner party, and it was a relief when my mistress rose to dismiss the ladies to the drawing-room.

While arranging decanters before old Mr. Ablewhite, a sound from the terrace startled me. It was the beat of an Indian drum. Here were the jugglers returning with the Moonstone! As they rounded the corner, the two Bouncers raced ahead like rockets to witness the tricks. The ladies and gentlemen followed. Before one could exclaim, "Lord bless us!" the rogues were performing salaams, and the Bouncers were kissing the little boy.

Mr. Franklin moved to Miss Rachel's side, and I positioned myself behind her. If our suspicions were correct, she stood there, innocent of the truth, displaying the Diamond to the Indians. I lost my composure amidst the vexation of the dinner and the timing of their return. The first distinct event was the sudden appearance of Mr. Murthwaite. Skirting the circle of guests, he approached the jugglers quietly and spoke in their native tongue. If he had stabbed them, they could not have reacted with greater tigerish speed. Instantly, they bowed and salaamed in their most polite, serpentine manner. After a brief exchange, Mr. Murthwaite withdrew as silently as he had arrived. The chief Indian, acting as interpreter, turned back to the guests. I noted his coffee-colored face had turned grey. He bowed to my lady and announced the exhibition was over. The Bouncers, despairingly disappointed, shouted at Mr. Murthwaite for stopping the show. The chief Indian reiterated that the juggling was finished. The little boy collected the hat, the ladies retired to the drawing-room, and the gentlemen (save Mr. Franklin and Mr. Murthwaite) returned to their wine. I and the footman escorted the Indians safely off the premises.

Returning via the shrubbery, I smelled tobacco and found Mr. Franklin and Mr. Murthwaite walking slowly among the trees, the latter smoking a cheroot. Mr. Franklin beckoned me to join them. "This," he said, introducing me to the traveler, "is Gabriel Betteredge, the old servant and friend of our family. Tell him what you have just told me."

Mr. Murthwaite removed his cheroot and leaned against a tree. "Mr. Betteredge," he began, "those three Indians are no more jugglers than you and I."

This was a new surprise. I asked if he had met them before. "Never," he replied. "But I know true Indian juggling. What you saw was a clumsy imitation. Unless I am mistaken, those men are high-caste Brahmins. I charged them with being disguised, and you saw how it affected them, despite the Hindoos' skill at concealment. There is a mystery here. They have doubly sacrificed their caste—first by crossing the sea, second by disguising themselves as jugglers. Such a sacrifice requires a tremendous motive and justification to recover their caste upon return."

I was struck dumb. Mr. Murthwaite continued smoking. Mr. Franklin, shifting between his various personas, broke the silence. He hesitated to intrude on family matters but felt bound, for the sake of Lady Verinder and her daughter, to share information that might provide a clue. Speaking in confidence, he recounted everything he had told me at the Shivering Sand. Even the stoic Mr. Murthwaite let his cheroot go out, captivated.

"Now," Mr. Franklin asked, "what does your experience say?"

"My experience," the traveler answered, "says you have had more narrow escapes of your life, Mr. Franklin Blake, than I have had of mine; and that is saying a great deal."

Mr. Franklin was astonished. "Is it really as serious as that?"

"In my opinion, yes," Mr. Murthwaite confirmed. "After what you told me, I cannot doubt that restoring the Moonstone to the Indian idol is the motive and justification for their sacrifice of caste. Those men will wait with the patience of cats and strike with the ferocity of tigers. How you have escaped them I cannot imagine. You carried the Diamond back and forth between London and here, and you are still alive! Let us account for it. Was it broad daylight when you retrieved the jewel from the bank in London?"

"Broad daylight," said Mr. Franklin.

"And many people in the streets?"

"Plenty."

"Did you settle to arrive at Lady Verinder's house at a specific time? It is a lonely stretch of country. Did you keep your appointment?"

"No. I arrived four hours early."

"I congratulate you! When did you take the Diamond to the bank here?"

"An hour after bringing it to this house—and three hours before anyone expected me."

"I congratulate you again! Did you return alone?"

"No. I rode back with my cousins and the groom."

"I congratulate you for the third time! If you ever wish to travel beyond civilized limits, Mr. Blake, let me know, and I will accompany you. You are a lucky man."

I interjected, finding this contrary to English sensibilities. "You do not truly mean to say, sir, that they would have taken Mr. Franklin's life to retrieve their Diamond if given the chance?"

"Do you smoke, Mr. Betteredge?" asked the traveler.

"Yes, sir."

"Do you care much for the ashes left in your pipe when you empty it?"

"No, sir."

"In the country those men came from, they care just as much about killing a man as you do about emptying pipe ashes. If a thousand lives stood between them and the Diamond, and they believed they could destroy those lives without discovery, they would take them all. The sacrifice of caste is serious in India; the sacrifice of life is nothing."

I declared them murdering thieves; Mr. Murthwaite called them wonderful people. Mr. Franklin, offering no opinion, steered us back to the immediate problem. "They have seen the Moonstone on Miss Verinder's dress. What is to be done?"

"What your uncle threatened," Mr. Murthwaite answered. "Colonel Herncastle understood these people. Send the Diamond tomorrow, under heavy guard, to be cut up in Amsterdam. Make half a dozen diamonds of it instead of one. There is an end to its sacred identity as The Moonstone—and an end to the conspiracy."

Mr. Franklin turned to me. "There is no help for it. We must speak to Lady Verinder tomorrow."

"What about tonight, sir?" I asked. "Suppose the Indians return?"

"The Indians will not risk coming back tonight," Mr. Murthwaite replied before Mr. Franklin could speak. "The direct path is rarely the one they choose, especially when a mistake could be fatal."

"But suppose they are bolder than you think?" I persisted.

"In that case," said Mr. Murthwaite, "let the dogs loose. Have you any large dogs in the yard?"

"Two, sir. A mastiff and a bloodhound."

"They will suffice. In this emergency, Mr. Betteredge, the mastiff and bloodhound have one great merit: they are unlikely to be troubled by your scruples regarding the sanctity of human life."
