“Ah,” purred Zat Arras, “to what kindly circumstance am I indebted for the pleasure of this unexpected visit from the Prince of Helium?”

A guard’s fingers yanked the gag away. My jaw ached where the straps had bitten. I gave the Jed of Zodanga nothing in return—not even the courtesy of breath. I merely stood, tasting the stale air of his chamber—hot metal, oiled leather, the faint peppery tang of radium lamps—and held his gaze as one might hold a knife over a board: steady, unmoved.

The others in the room watched our silence thicken. It was like a kitchen gone wrong, when a pot boils over and everyone hears it, yet no one dares reach for it. Color climbed Zat Arras’ cheeks, slow and poisonous.

“You may go,” he snapped at those who had brought me. When they were gone and only his two companions remained, he addressed me again, each word measured as if he were shaving slivers from ice.

“John Carter,” he said, “by the edict of custom, by the law of our religion, and by the verdict of an impartial court, you are condemned to die. The people cannot save you—I alone may accomplish that. You are absolutely in my power to do with as I wish—I may kill you, or I may free you, and should I elect to kill you, none would be the wiser.

“Should you go free in Helium for a year, in accordance with the conditions of your reprieve, there is little fear that the people would ever insist upon the execution of the sentence imposed upon you.

“You may go free within two minutes, upon one condition. Tardos Mors will never return to Helium. Neither will Mors Kajak, nor Dejah Thoris. Helium must select a new Jeddak within the year. Zat Arras would be Jeddak of Helium. Say that you will espouse my cause. This is the price of your freedom. I am done.”

He offered it like a platter set down with flourish—yet the smell of it was rancid. I knew what lay under his polished phrasing: my death in darkness, Helium steered by a traitor, my Princess flung to the obscene rites of Issus. And, for a heartbeat, the arithmetic of it clawed at me. If I died, how might our friends execute the rescue? If I lived, I could search. If I refused, might I seal Dejah Thoris’ fate by my own pride?

But there are vows that cannot be seasoned into palatability. Dejah Thoris—daughter of a thousand Jeddaks—would rather die with clean hands than live fat on treason. I could do no less.

I turned my contempt upon him like a cold blade.

“There can be no alliance,” I said, “between a traitor to Helium and a prince of the House of Tardos Mors. I do not believe, Zat Arras, that the great Jeddak is dead.”

He shrugged as if my words were smoke.

“It will not be long, John Carter,” he replied, “that your opinions will be of interest even to yourself, so make the best of them while you can. Zat Arras will permit you in due time to reflect further upon the magnanimous offer he has made you. Into the silence and darkness of the pits you will enter upon your reflection this night with the knowledge that should you fail within a reasonable time to agree to the alternative which has been offered you, never shall you emerge from the darkness and the silence again. Nor shall you know at what minute the hand will reach out through the darkness and the silence with the keen dagger that shall rob you of your last chance to win again the warmth and the freedom and joyousness of the outer world.”

He clapped his hands—sharp, flat, like the slap of meat against stone. Guards returned.

“To the pits,” he said, as though ordering a dish sent away untouched.

A radium hand-light was thrust ahead of us, its glow a thin broth of greenish luminance, and we went down—ever down—through tunnels that stole the air from your lungs. Above lay Helium, with its gardens and fountains, its voices, its music of water and wind through scarlet trees. Below was only the dead acoustics of rock: our footfalls swallowed, our breath muffled, the faint clink of metal too loud because nothing else lived to counter it.

At last they halted in a chamber where the walls were studded with iron rings. Chains ran from them like black vines. At the ends of many chains were what remained of men—skeletal ankles still hugged by iron bands, ribs like broken basketry, skulls turned toward nothing. One skeleton was kicked aside with the casualness of sweeping crumbs from a floor. The lock of its ankle-chain was opened, and the same band was snapped about my own leg.

Then they took the light away.

Darkness, complete and thick, dropped on me as though poured. For a few minutes I could still hear the retreating clank of their harness and weapons, the fading rasp of sandals on stone. Then even that dissolved, until silence took over—so absolute that it became its own kind of sound, a pressure against the ears. In that silence I understood why sane men fear prisons more than blades: not for the pain, but for the way quiet can erase you, day by day, until you no longer remember the shape of your own name.

I stood a time, listening to nothing, with the bones of other men for company, and at last slid down to the hard floor. The rock was cold against my shoulder. I slept because there was nothing else to do.

When I woke, a young man stood before me. In one hand he carried a light; in the other, a receptacle of Barsoomian prison fare—a gray, gruel-like mixture with the flat odor of boiled grain and old water, the kind of sustenance that keeps flesh moving but offers no comfort, no memory, no home.

“Zat Arras sends you greetings,” he said, “and commands me to inform you that though he is fully advised of the plot to make you Jeddak of Helium, he is, however, not inclined to withdraw the offer which he has made you. To gain your freedom you have but to request me to advise Zat Arras that you accept the terms of his proposition.”

I shook my head once.

He set the food beside me, said nothing further, and took the light away as he departed—leaving me again with the soundless weight of earth above and bones around.

Twice each day he came, always with the same message, always with the same bowl of indifferent gruel. The routine took on the circular certainty of a household duty—like bread kneaded at dawn, like water hauled at dusk—except here the duty was despair, served hot and brought away cold. I tried at first to draw him into talk—of Helium, of the outside world, of anything that had texture and color—but he would not be coaxed. His silence was practiced, disciplined, like a priest’s.

Days became weeks, weeks months. In the half-light of each visit I studied him as one studies ingredients by smell and hand when sight is uncertain. He was handsome, about Carthoris’ age and size. His trappings were poor, rusted and patched, yet he carried himself with a dignity that did not match his station. There was something in his bearing—an inherited straightness—that reminded me of old agrarian elders who can walk into any market and, without raising their voice, command respect simply by the way they hold their shoulders.

All the while my mind churned on one question: what of our plans? What of Dejah Thoris? What of Carthoris and Kantos Kan and Tars Tarkas and Hor Vastus and Xodar? Were they free? Dead? In these pits as I was?

I scraped at a single link of my chain for months, patient as a cook grinding spice with a dull stone, knowing that only repetition yields powder. The metal resisted. My hands bled and healed and bled again. I measured time by pain and by the youth’s footsteps.

At last I resolved to gamble with speech.

On his next visit, when he set down the bowl, I said, “You have been very kind to me during my imprisonment here. As I feel that I have at best but a very short time to live, I wish, ere it is too late, to furnish substantial testimony of my appreciation.”

He watched me, cautious.

“Promptly you have brought my food each day, seeing that it was pure and of sufficient quantity. Never by word or deed have you attempted to take advantage of my defenceless condition to insult or torture me. You have been uniformly courteous and considerate. It is this which prompts my feeling of gratitude.”

His eyes flicked, despite himself, to my own accoutrements—still fine, though dulled by dirt and time—then down to his rusty harness.

“In the guard-room of my palace,” I went on, “are many fine trappings. Go thou there and select the harness which most pleases you—it shall be yours. All I ask is that you wear it, that I may know that my wish has been realized. Tell me that you will do it.”

Pleasure lit him, quickly banked.

“And I went to the palace of the Prince of Helium with any such demand,” he said at last, “they would laugh at me and, into the bargain, would more than likely throw me headforemost into the avenue. No, it cannot be, though I thank you for the offer. Why, if Zat Arras even dreamed that I contemplated such a thing he would have my heart cut out of me.”

“There can be no harm in it,” I urged, keeping my voice gentle. “By night you may go to my palace with a note from me to Carthoris, my son. You may read the note before you deliver it, that you may know it contains nothing harmful to Zat Arras. My son will be discreet; so none but us three need know.”

He stood in thought so long that the silence became again a third presence between us.

“And,” I added, as one adds the final fragrance that tips desire into action, “there is a jewelled short-sword which I took from the body of a northern Jeddak. When you get the harness, see that Carthoris gives you that also. With it and the harness there will be no more handsomely accoutred warrior in all Zodanga.”

He turned and left without speaking. For hours afterward I sat with my back against stone, listening to the tunnels as if they might deliver an answer on the breath of air.

When he came again, I heard his steps and the faint creak of leather. He set down my food—and beside it, writing materials.

For a moment I feigned surprise, then let comprehension dawn across my face as though it were reluctant. Inside, my heart surged so violently I feared it would betray me.

I wrote a brief order to Carthoris: deliver to Parthak a harness of his selection from the guard-room and the jewelled short-sword I described. Nothing more. But in such places, where language is rationed and watched, a few words can be a whole life—can be proof of breath, proof of loyalty, proof that the world above has not yet forgotten your name.

I laid the note open on the floor. Parthak took it and left without a word.

By my reckoning I had been in the pits three hundred days. If Dejah Thoris still lived among the First Born, her year was nearly spent. The rites of Issus were not an abstraction. I had seen too much of Barsoom’s pageantry—the way cruelty dresses itself in silk and music—to believe any mercy would intervene for her.

When footsteps came again, I strained to hear whether Parthak’s gait returned, whether fine harness might squeak, whether the small bright chime of a jewelled hilt might speak against stone.

The man who entered was not Parthak.

“What has become of Parthak?” I demanded.

He did not answer. He set down the bowl and left, soundless as a machine.

Days passed. Always the same mute jailer. Always the same thin gruel, its smell a daily insult. My hopes, which had risen like bread in warmth, collapsed again into sour heaviness. Parthak’s removal could mean only one thing: my note had been discovered, or Parthak had betrayed me—or Parthak had vanished into some deeper darkness. And if he had carried my message to Zat Arras instead of to Carthoris, then all my careful sparing of words had been merely seasoning on my own doom.

Thirty days crawled by from the time I had given him the note. Three hundred and thirty days since my chains had closed on my ankle. By my count, perhaps thirty days remained before Dejah Thoris would be ordered to the arena.

The picture rose in my mind with obscene clarity: the white apes, their huge bodies stinking of old blood and musky fur; the sand of the arena; the black nobles’ laughter; the scrape of claws on stone; and, afterward, the sacrilege of flesh made feast. Barsoom has always known hunger, and with hunger comes the darkest bargains—yet nothing in all my wanderings had prepared me for the thought of my Princess reduced to spectacle and meat.

I buried my face in my arms and fought to keep tears from burning out of me. The pits were not only dark; they were hungry for sound, eager to drink sobs as readily as silence.

Then I heard my jailer coming.

The approach of footsteps—however faint—was like a hand slapped sharply on a table: a summons back from madness. A grim clarity seized me. I would try once. One superhuman effort. I would kill the jailer by ruse, take his light, and trust blind fate to lead me through the tunnels to air and sky.

I threw myself to the floor near the wall and contorted my body as though dead in spasm. I gripped the slack of my chain in my right hand so I could swing it like a weighted club. My plan was simple: when he bent close, I would seize his throat with my left hand and strike with the chain. Quick. Final.

Nearer came the steps. He paused. I heard a muttered exclamation. Then a shift of weight as he knelt beside me.

I tightened my grip. I must open my eyes, find his throat, strike—all in one breath.

The ruse worked too well.

The instant my eyes opened I saw a face so close that it filled my world—and recognized it as that of my son, Carthoris.

The chain was already falling.

In that heartbeat between recognition and impact, my mind screamed to stop, but the body had begun what the will had ordered. Fate, malignant and precise, had set my own hand against my own blood. The blow landed—yet mercy, though late, somehow softened it. My vision blurred as if the radium light had turned to water. I felt myself pitching forward, and the world went out.

When I returned to consciousness, a cool, firm hand pressed my forehead. For a moment I kept my eyes closed, trying to gather thoughts that scattered like spilled grain. Then memory returned, sharp as a knife: the strike, the face, my son.

I did not dare look.

But at last I forced my eyes open.

Carthoris leaned above me, bruised across the forehead where the chain had struck, yet alive. No companion stood behind him—only the narrow chamber and the dead and the chains.

I reached for him and drew him into my arms with a strength that surprised even me, as though love could be a bandage pressed hard enough to stop any bleeding the universe might attempt. In that underground silence my gratitude rose like a prayer—fervent, wordless, beyond any dialect I knew.

“The brief instant in which I recognized you,” I whispered, “must have been enough to check the force. Thank the Eternal Mystery.”

He laughed softly, though pain tightened his eyes. “It was enough. I lay unconscious for a time. I do not know how long.”

“How came you here at all?” I demanded, still stunned that he had found me in this labyrinth.

“It was by your wit,” he said, “in apprising me of your existence and imprisonment through the youth, Parthak. Until he came for his harness and his sword, we had thought you dead. When I read your note, I did as you bid: I gave Parthak his choice among the harnesses in the guard-room, and later I brought him the jewelled short-sword. But the moment I had fulfilled the promise you had made him, my obligation ceased. Then I questioned him—but he would give no information. He was intensely loyal to Zat Arras.

“Finally I gave him a choice between freedom and the pits beneath the palace—the price of freedom to be full information as to where you were imprisoned and directions to reach you. Still he held to his stubborn partisanship. Despairing, I had him removed to the pits, where he still is.

“No threats of torture or death, no bribes, however fabulous, would move him. His only reply was that whenever Parthak died—were it to-morrow or a thousand years hence—no man could truly say, ‘A traitor is gone to his deserts.’

“At last Xodar—fiend for subtle craftiness—evolved a plan. I caused Hor Vastus to be harnessed in the metal of a Zodangan soldier and chained in Parthak’s cell beside him. For fifteen days Hor Vastus has languished in the darkness. But not in vain. Little by little he won Parthak’s confidence, until to-day Parthak, thinking he spoke to a countryman and a friend, revealed the exact cell where you lay.

“I found the plans of the pits among official papers. To come to you was harder. Though the pits connect beneath the city, there are single entrances between sections at the upper level. Those openings are always guarded. I reached the entrance beneath the palace Zat Arras occupies and found a Zodangan soldier on guard. I left him there when I passed—but his soul was no longer with him.

“And here I am,” he finished, “just in time to be nearly killed by you.”

As he spoke, he worked at the lock that held my fetters. The metal clicked, stubborn and then yielding. The chain fell away with a sound like a pot-lid set down too hard. I stood—stiff, sore, unsteady—yet freer than I had been in almost a year. He handed me a long-sword and a dagger; their familiar weight returned to my hands like a remembered tool.

We made our way back through the tunnels. At the point where we entered Zat Arras’ pits we found the guard Carthoris had slain. The body had not yet been discovered. To delay pursuit and confound their search, we carried it a short distance and hid it in a tiny cell off the main corridor beneath an adjoining estate—tucking it away as one hides evidence of a spoiled dish, though no concealment could change what it was.

Half an hour later we reached the pits beneath our own palace. We emerged into the audience chamber, stepping from underworld darkness into lamplight that tasted of oil and warmed stone.

Kantos Kan, Tars Tarkas, Hor Vastus, and Xodar were there, waiting with the taut impatience of men who have listened too long for a signal. The room felt suddenly alive with sound—the rustle of harness, the low creak of leather, the subtle chorus of breathing. Even that ordinary music struck me as precious; in the pits, silence had been a tyrant, here the smallest noise was proof of comradeship.

There was no time for recounting my suffering. I wanted only the state of our preparations.

“It has taken much longer than we had expected,” said Kantos Kan. “The need for utter secrecy has handicapped us terribly. Zat Arras’ spies are everywhere. Yet, to the best of my knowledge, no word of our real plans has reached the villain’s ear.

“To-night there lies about the great docks at Hastor a fleet of a thousand of the mightiest battleships that ever sailed above Barsoom—each equipped to navigate the air of Omean and the waters of Omean itself. Upon each battleship there are five ten-man cruisers, and ten five-man scouts, and a hundred one-man scouts; in all, one hundred and sixteen thousand craft fitted with both air and water propellers.

“At Thark lie the transports for the green warriors of Tars Tarkas—nine hundred large troopships, and with them their convoys. Seven days ago all was in readiness, but we waited in the hope your rescue might be accomplished in time for you to command the expedition. It is well we waited, my Prince.”

“How is it, Tars Tarkas,” I asked, “that the men of Thark take not the accustomed action against one who returns from the bosom of Iss?”

“They sent a council of fifty chieftains to talk with me here,” replied the Thark, his great voice like distant drums. “We are a just people. When I told them the entire story they agreed their action toward me would be guided by Helium’s action toward John Carter. In the meantime, at their request, I resumed my throne as Jeddak of Thark, that I might negotiate with neighboring hordes for warriors for the land forces. I have done that which I agreed.

“Two hundred and fifty thousand fighting men—from the ice cap at the north to the ice cap at the south, representing a thousand communities from a hundred wild hordes—fill the great city of Thark to-night. They are ready to sail for the Land of the First Born when I give the word and fight there until I bid them stop. All they ask is the loot they take and transportation home when the fighting and looting are over. I am done.”

“And thou, Hor Vastus,” I said, “what has been thy success?”

“A million veteran fighting-men from Helium’s thin waterways man the battleships, the transports, and the convoys,” he answered. “Each is sworn to loyalty and secrecy, nor were enough recruited from a single district to cause suspicion.”

“Good,” I said. The word tasted like clean water. “Each has done his duty. Now, Kantos Kan—may we not repair at once to Hastor and get under way before to-morrow’s sun?”

“We should lose no time, Prince,” Kantos Kan replied. “Already the people of Hastor question the purpose of so great a fleet fully manned. I wonder that word has not reached Zat Arras. A cruiser awaits above at your own dock; let us leave at—”

A fusillade of shots cut him off—sharp cracks from the palace gardens, a sudden tearing of the night’s fabric. Sound, again, as ruler: how quickly violence announces itself by noise, how quickly peace is measured by the return of natural quiet.

We rushed to the balcony in time to see a dozen of my palace guard vanish into shrub-shadow, pursuing a fleeing figure. Below, on the scarlet sward, a handful of guards bent over a still, prostrate form.

At my command they bore the body into the audience chamber and laid it at our feet. It was a red man in the prime of life. His metal was plain—the harness of a common soldier, or of one who wished his name and rank to taste like nothing at all.

“Another of Zat Arras’ spies,” said Hor Vastus.

“So it would seem,” I replied, and turned to the guard. “You may remove the body.”

“Wait,” said Xodar. His thin face sharpened with intent, as though he were about to dissect a fish for hidden bones. “If you will, Prince, ask that a cloth and a little thoat oil be brought.”
