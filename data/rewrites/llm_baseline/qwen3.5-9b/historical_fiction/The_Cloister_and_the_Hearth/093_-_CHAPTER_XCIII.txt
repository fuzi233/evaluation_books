One day, while lying there sighing and groaning—prayerless, tuneless, and utterly hopeless—a sudden thought pierced his mind. He realized what he had done for the poor and the wayfarer, he would now do for himself. He resolved to fill his den of despair with the Name of God, the sacred words of Scripture, and the pious consolations of the Church.

Then, like Christian standing at the feet of Apollyon, he suddenly reached out his hand. He grasped not his sword, for he possessed none, but the humble weapon of peaceful labor: his chisel. With a fervor as though his very soul depended upon the movement of his arm, he began to work.

It is said that Michelangelo, in the generation following, carved statues unlike the timid sculptors of his day who modeled works in clay before handing them to mechanics. Instead, Michelangelo would seize the block, conceive the image instantly, and with mallet and steel cause the marble chips to fly wildly around him until the mass sprouted into form. So too did Clement draw no preliminary lines to guide his hand. Turning to his memory for the gracious words, he dashed at his work, eagerly carving them into the soft stone between moments of labor and combat.

He begged his visitors for candle ends and rancid oil. "Anything is good enough for me," he declared, "if it will but burn." Thus, at night, the cave glowed afar off like a blacksmith's forge, visible through the window and the gaps in the rough stone door. Rustics beholding the light crossed themselves, suspecting devilries, while within, holy talismans appeared one after another upon the walls. Sparks and chips flew day and night, night and day, as the soldier of Solitude and the Church wielded his bloodless weapon, sighing and groaning, between working and fighting.

*Kyrie Eleison.* *Christe Eleison.* *(Ton Satanan suntripson upo tous pothas ymwn)* — Beat down Satan under our feet.
*Sursum Corda.* — Lift up your hearts.
*Deus Refugium nostrum et virtus.* — O God, our refuge and strength.
*Agnus Dei, qui tollis peccata mundi, miserere mihi.* — O Lamb of God, who taketh away the sins of the world, have mercy on me.
*Sancta Trinitas unus Deus, miserere nobis.* — O Holy Trinity, one God, have mercy on us.
*Ab infestationibus Daemonum, a ventura ira, a damnatione perpetua. Libera nos Domine.* — From the assaults of demons, from the wrath to come, from everlasting damnation, deliver us, O Lord.
*Quem quaerimus adjutorem nisi te Domine, qui pro peccatis nostris juste irascaris?* — Of whom may we seek succour but of Thee, O Lord, who for our sins art justly displeased?
*Sancte Deus, Sancte fortis, Sancte et misericors Salvator, amarae morti ne tradas nos.* — O Holy God, O Holy Strong, O Holy and Merciful Savior, deliver us not into bitter death.

And beneath the great crucifix, fastened to the wall, he carved these words from Augustine:
*"O anima Christiana, respice vulnera patientis, sanguinem morientis, pretium redemptionis. Haec quanta sint cogitate, et in statera mentis vestrae appendite, ut totus vobis figatur in corde, qui pro vobis totus fixus est in cruce. Nam si passio Christi ad memoriam revocetur, nihil est tam durum quod non aequo animo toleretur."*

Which may be thus rendered:
*"O Christian soul, look on the wounds of the suffering One, the blood of the dying One, the price paid for our redemption! Think how great these things are, and weigh them in the balance of thy mind, that He may be wholly nailed to thy heart, who for thee was all nailed unto the cross. For if thou dost recall the sufferings of Christ, there is naught on earth too hard to endure with composure."*

Soothed a little, a very little, by the sweet and pious words he raised all around him, yet weighed down by watching and working night and day, Clement one morning sank prostrate with fatigue, and a deep sleep overpowered him for many hours.

Awakening quietly, he heard a tiny chirp. Opening his eyes, behold! Upon his breviary, resting on a low stool near his feet, perched a robin redbreast. With a single pull, the bird ruffled all its feathers, smoothed them instantly, and cocked its bill this way and that with a vast display of cunning purely imaginary. Clement held his breath, half closing his eyes lest he frighten the airy guest.

Down came the robin onto the floor. There, it enacted its pantomime of astuteness; then *pim, pim, pim*, with three stiff little hops, like a ball of worsted on vertical wires, it landed squarely on the hermit's bare foot. On this eminence, it swelled and contracted again, with the ebb and flow of its feathers. But Clement lost interest, for he quite closed his eyes, scarcely drawing breath in fear of frightening and losing his visitor. He was content merely to feel the minute claw upon his foot. He could barely feel it, aided only by knowing it was there.

Presently, a little flutter of two small wings, and the feathered busybody returned to the breviary. Then Clement determined to try feeding this pretty little fidget without scaring it away. It proved very difficult. He had a piece of bread within reach, but how to get at it? He spent five minutes creeping his hand toward the bread, realizing he must not move his arm once there. Slyly, he got a crumb between finger and thumb and shot it forward, as boys shoot marbles, keeping his hand perfectly still.

Cock-robin saw the crumb fall nearby and showed sagacity, yet remained motionless. When another followed, and then another, the bird popped down, caught up one of the crumbs, but not fully understanding this mystery, fled with it to a higher eminence—for greater security—to wit, the hermit's knee. Thus the game proceeded until a much larger fragment than usual rolled along. Here was a prize! Cock-robin pounced upon it, bore it aloft, and fled so swiftly into the world that the cave resounded with the buffeted air.

"Now, bless thee, sweet bird!" sighed the stricken solitary. "Thy wings are music, and thou, a feathered ray, hast come to lighten my darkened soul."

From that moment onward, during his orisons, and then returning to his tools with a little renewed courage, this became his day's work:
*"Veni, Creator Spiritus, Mentes tuorum visita, Imple superna gratia, Quae tu creasti pectora. Accende lumen sensibus, Mentes tuorum visita, Infirma nostri corporis, Virtute firmans perpeti."*

Thus the days rolled on. The weather grew colder, but Clement's heart grew warmer, and despondency rolled away. By and by, somehow or another, it was gone entirely. He had outlived it. It had come like a cloud, and departed like one. Presently, all was reversed; his cell seemed illuminated with joy. His work pleased him; his prayers were full of unction; his psalms were songs of praise.

Hosts of little birds followed their crimson leader, flying from the snow. A parish full of Cains made friends one after another with Abels—fast friends indeed. And on one keen frosty night, as he sang the praises of God to his tuneful psaltery, causing his hollow cave to ring forth with holy psalmody—as if the cave itself were Tubal's surrounding shell or David's harp—he heard a clear whine, not unmelodious. It grew louder and less in tune. Peeping through the chinks of his rude door, he saw a great red wolf moaning melodiously, its nose held high in the air.

Clement was rejoiced. "My sins are going," he cried, "and the creatures of God are owning me, one after another." In a burst of enthusiasm, he struck up the laud: *"Praise Him, all ye creatures of His! Let everything that hath breath praise the Lord."* All the time he sang, the wolf bayed at intervals.

Above all, he seemed now drawing nearer to that celestial intercourse which was the sign and bliss of the true hermit. He had dreams of saints and angels so vivid they were more like visions. He saw bright figures clad in woven snow. They bent upon him eyes lovelier than those of the antelope he had seen at Rome, fanning him with broad wings hued like the rainbow. Their gentle voices bade him speed upon his course.

He had not long enjoyed this felicity when his dreams began to take another, strange complexion. He wandered with Fra Colonna over the relics of antique nations. The friar was lame, leaning on a staff, which he waved over mighty ruins. Were they Egyptian, Greek, or Roman, straightway the temples and palaces, whose wrecks they were, rose again like an exhalation, thronged with the famous dead.
