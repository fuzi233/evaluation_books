Philip Trent, a painter and the son of a painter, had secured a notable reputation within the English art world before reaching his twenties. His works sold readily, driven by an original, forceful talent and a working style that blended leisurely consistency with bursts of intense creative fervor. While his father's name and a substantial inheritance spared him the stigma of the struggling artist, Trent's greatest asset was an unconscious charm that endeared him to everyone. Possessed of good spirits and a lively, humorous wit, he combined these traits with a genuine, penetrating interest in other people. Though his judgment of character was sharp, it remained private; outwardly, he projected an image of perpetual enjoyment. Even when deeply engrossed in labor or indulging in whimsical nonsense, his face retained a spark of contained vitality. At thirty-two, he had not yet succumbed to the solemnities of age.

His meteoric rise to fame, vastly disproportionate to his artistic output, stemmed from a fleeting impulse. One day, reading a newspaper, he encountered a report of a murder committed aboard a railway train—a crime of a rarity unknown in England. The circumstances were baffling, involving two suspects. Intrigued by a novelty, Trent, who rarely engaged with such matters, began to pore over reports in various journals. His imagination, dormant in daily life, ignited; facts began to weave themselves into patterns familiar only from his artistic inspirations or personal adventures. By day's end, he had penned and mailed a lengthy letter to the editor of *The Record*, selected specifically for its comprehensive and intelligent coverage of the incident.

In this correspondence, Trent mirrored Edgar Allan Poe's approach to the Mary Rogers case. Relying solely on newspaper accounts, he highlighted the significance of seemingly trivial details and organized the evidence to cast severe doubt upon a key witness. Sir James Molloy, the editor, printed Trent's letter in bold typeface. Later that evening, *The Sun* announced the arrest and full confession of the accused man.

Sir James, a man acquainted with every stratum of London society, wasted no time in establishing contact with Trent. The two hit it off immediately; Trent possessed a native tact that effectively erased generational gaps. The great rotary presses in the basement of the *Record* building inspired a new enthusiasm in him. He had even painted there—a "machinery-scape" in the manner of Heinrich Kley—which Sir James purchased instantly.

Months later, the Ilkley Mystery arrived. Following an inviting dinner hosted by Sir James, the editor offered Trent a fantastically large sum to serve temporarily as his special correspondent in Ilkley.

"You can handle this," the editor urged. "You write well, you know how to converse with people, and I can teach you the technicalities of reporting in half an hour. You possess a head for mysteries, combining imagination with cool judgment. Imagine the satisfaction of pulling this off!"

Trent admitted it sounded like a grand adventure. After smoking and frowning, he convinced himself that only the fear of the unfamiliar held him back. Reacting against fear had become a fixed moral habit; thus, he accepted the offer.

He succeeded brilliantly. For the second time, he outpaced the authorities, earning a name spoken on every tongue. He returned to painting, feeling no pull toward journalism. True to his word, Sir James, knowing Trent's value in the art world, refrained from offering a lucrative salary—a courtesy extended by no other editor. Nevertheless, over the following years, Trent received approximately thirty requests for assistance in unraveling domestic and international puzzles. Sometimes, occupied by his own work, he declined; other times, he was beaten to the truth. Regardless, his sporadic collaboration with *The Record* cemented his status as one of England's most renowned figures. Characteristically, however, the public knew almost nothing of him personally. Trent had imposed strict silence regarding his identity upon the Molloy papers, and his colleagues wisely avoided advertising their own staff.

As he walked briskly up the sloping road toward White Gables, Trent reflected that the Manderson case might prove deceptively simple. Cupples, the hotel manager, was a wise man, yet likely biased regarding his niece. However, the manager's description of the victim was telling. Though not a poet, he had conveyed a clear impression: Mrs. Manderson was universally beloved. "There isn't a child or adult around here who doesn't brighten at the sound of her voice," the manager had said. "She possesses backbone, pluck, and sheer go. Everyone in Marlstone feels sorry for her plight, though some may privately think she is fortunate in the end." Trent yearned to meet her.

Beyond a vast lawn and shrubbery, he spotted the house: a two-story structure of dull red brick, named for its twin gables. Built roughly a decade prior, it exuded an air of opulent peace typical of wealthy English estates. Before it, rich meadows descended to the cliff's edge; behind, a wooded valley stretched toward the moors. The notion that such a tranquil, orderly scene could harbor a violent crime seemed absurd, yet just beyond the garden hedge stood the gardener's toolshed. Near it, against the wooden wall, lay the body.

Trent passed the main gate and proceeded along the road until facing the shed. Forty yards further, the road curved sharply between dense plantations, marking the boundary of the estate where a small white gate sat nestled in the hedge. This gate, intended for gardeners and service personnel, swung open effortlessly. He stepped onto a path leading to the rear of the house, flanked by an outer hedge and a towering wall of rhododendrons. Through a break in the foliage, a narrow track revealed the neat wooden shed, situated among trees overlooking a corner of the front garden. The body had lain on the side facing away from the house; perhaps a servant, peering from a nearby window earlier that morning, had glanced indifferently at the hut, wondering what it was like to possess such wealth.

Trent examined the site meticulously, searching the interior of the hut and the surrounding ground. He found nothing but the trampled grass where the body had rested. Crouching low, he combed the earth with keen eyes and sensitive fingers, but his search yielded no results.

The solitude was shattered by the sound of a front door slamming shut inside the house. Trent straightened and moved to the edge of the drive. A man was hurrying away from the house toward the main gate.

Startled by the crunch of gravel, the man spun around, his gaze locking onto Trent with nervous intensity. His face was ghastly pale and worn, yet undeniably youthful. There were no wrinkles on his haggard blue eyes, despite the evident strain and exhaustion etched into his features. As they closed the distance between them, Trent admired the man's broad shoulders and lithe, powerful build. Despite the stiffness of weariness, his handsome, regular features and short, smooth yellow hair suggested rigorous training.

"If you are Mr. Trent," the young man said pleasantly, "you are expected. Mr. Cupples telephoned from the hotel. My name is Marlowe."

"You were secretary to Mr. Manderson, I presume?" Trent asked, finding himself drawn to the youth. Despite appearing on the verge of collapse, Marlowe radiated the clean-living vigor characteristic of his social class. Yet, there was a challenge in his tired eyes—an habitual expression of deep contemplation, weighing invisible burdens. It was a look too intelligent and purposeful to be merely dreamy. "It is a terrible ordeal for all of you," Trent continued. "I fear it has shaken you completely, Mr. Marlowe."

"A slight limp, that's all," Marlowe replied wearily. "I drove the car all Sunday night and most of yesterday, and couldn't sleep after hearing the news. Who could? But I have an appointment now with the doctor to arrange the inquest, likely for tomorrow. If you'll go upstairs and ask for Mr. Bunner, you'll find him waiting. He's the other secretary—an American and a fine fellow—and he'll orient you. Also, there is a detective here, Inspector Murch from Scotland Yard. He arrived yesterday."

"Murch!" Trent exclaimed. "We are old friends. How did he get here so quickly?"

"I have no idea," Marlowe answered. "He was here last evening before I returned from Southampton, interviewing everyone, and has been active here since eight this morning. He's currently in the library—you can see the open French window at the far end of the house. Would you care to join him?"

"I shall," Trent agreed. Marlowe nodded and departed. The thick turf of the lawn allowed Trent to move silently, like a cat. Moments later, he peered through the open leaves of the southern window. Inside, a broad back and a bent head covered in short, grizzled hair caught his eye. The man was stooping over papers spread across a table.

"'Twas ever thus," Trent murmured in a melancholy tone.

The man within spun around with startling speed. "From childhood's hour I've seen my fondest hopes decay," he quoted. "I thought I was ahead of Scotland Yard this time, and now the highest officer in the Metropolitan Force occupies the field."

The detective smiled grimly and approached the window. "I was expecting you, Mr. Trent. This is precisely the sort of case you enjoy."
