Louis Moore was accustomed to a quiet existence. Possessing a vast inner world within his own thoughts and heart, he bore the confinement of a small, still corner of the real world with a patience that few others could match. How silent Fieldhead is tonight! Everyone—except Moore himself, Miss Keeldar, the entire Sympson family, and even the boyish Henry—has departed for Nunnely. Sir Philip, the kind-hearted baronet, insisted on their attendance, wishing to introduce them to his mother and sisters, who are currently staying at the Priory. He even extended the invitation to the tutor. Yet, Louis Moore would far sooner schedule a meeting with the ghost of the Earl of Huntingdon beneath the canopy of the densest, darkest, oldest oak in Nunnely Forest, surrounded by the spectral ring of his Merry Men. He would prefer to arrange a rendezvous with a phantom abbess or a mist-pale nun amidst the damp, overgrown ruins of their ancient sanctuary, rotting deep within the forest's heart.

Tonight, Louis Moore desires company, but not the boy-baronet, nor his benevolent yet stern mother, nor his aristocratic sisters, nor any member of the Sympson clan. This night holds no calm; the equinox still wrestles with its storms. The wild rains of the day have subsided; the great singular cloud disperses and rolls away from the heavens, not leaving behind a sea of sapphire, but tossing buoyantly before a continuous, long-echoing tempest of high-rushing moonlight. The moon reigns gloriously, seemingly delighted by the gale, as joyful as if she offered herself to its fierce embrace with love. No Endymion watches for his goddess tonight. There are no flocks grazing on the mountains, and this is fortunate, for tonight she welcomes Æolus.

Sitting in the schoolroom, Louis hears the storm roar around the other gable and along the front of the hall. This end of the house is sheltered. He wants no shelter; he desires no muted sounds or screened positions. "All the parlours are empty," he remarks. "I am sick at heart of this cell."

He leaves the room and moves toward the casements, which are larger and freer than the branch-screened lattice of his own apartment, admitting an unobstructed view of the dark-blue, silver-fleeced, swirling autumn sky. He carries no candle; a lamp or fire is unnecessary. Though crossed by clouds and fluctuating, the broad, clear beam of the moon shines upon every floor and wall. Moore wanders through the rooms, appearing to follow a phantom from parlor to parlor. In the Oak Room, he pauses. This room is not cold, polished, and fireless like the salon. The hearth burns hot and ruddy; the cinders tinkle in the intense heat of their clear glow. Near the rug sits a small worktable, a desk upon it, and a chair beside it. One might assume the vision Moore has been tracking occupies that chair; indeed, if you could see him, he stands before it. There is as much interest in his eyes and as much significance in his face as if, in this household solitude, he had discovered a living companion and intended to speak to it.

He makes discoveries. A small satin bag hangs from the chair-back. The desk is open, the keys remaining in the lock. A pretty seal, a silver pen, a couple of crimson berries resting on a green leaf, and a small, clean, delicate glove scatter the surface. These trifles both decorate and disorder the stand. Order forbids such details in a painting, yet details provide charm.

Moore speaks aloud. "Her mark," he says. "Here she has been—a careless, attractive creature—called away in haste, doubtless, and forgetting to tidy up. Why does she leave fascination in her footprints? Where did she acquire the gift to be heedless without offending? There is always something to chide in her, yet the reprimand never settles in displeasure on the heart. Instead, for her lover or husband, after trickling through words, it naturally melts from his lips into a kiss. It is better to spend half an hour remonstrating with her than a day admiring or praising any other woman alive. Am I muttering? Soliloquizing? Stop that."

He does stop. Standing in thought, he then arranges for his evening's comfort. He drops the curtains over the broad window, banishing the regal moon and its sovereign, court, and starry armies. He adds fuel to the hot but rapidly wasting fire, lights a candle from the pair on the table, places another chair opposite the one near the workstand, and finally sits down.

His next move is to retrieve a small, thick book of blank paper from his pocket, produce a pencil, and begin writing in a cramped, compact hand.

Come close, reader. Be bold. Stoop over his shoulder fearlessly and read as he scribbles.

"It is nine o'clock; the carriage will not return before eleven, I am certain. Freedom is mine until then. Until then, I may occupy her room, sit opposite her chair, rest my elbow on her table, and have her little mementoes surrounding me.

'I used rather to like Solitude—to fancy her a somewhat quiet and serious, yet fair nymph; an Oread descending to me from lone mountain passes, possessing something of the blue mist of hills in her attire and their chill breeze in her breath, but also much of their solemn beauty in her demeanor. Once, I could court her serenely and imagine my heart lighter when I held her to it—all mute, yet majestic.

'Since that day I summoned S. to me in the schoolroom, and she came and sat so near my side; since she opened the troubles of her mind to me, asked my protection, and appealed to my strength—from that hour, I have come to abhor Solitude. Cold abstraction, fleshless skeleton, daughter, mother, and mate of Death!

'It is pleasant to write about what is near and dear as the core of my heart. Nothing can deprive me of this little book, and through this pencil, I can say to it whatever I will—say what I dare utter to nothing living, say what I dare not think aloud.

'We have scarcely encountered each other since that evening. Once, when I was alone in the drawing-room seeking a book of Henry's, she entered, dressed for a concert at Stilbro'. Shyness—*her* shyness, not mine—drew a silver veil between us. I have heard and read much cant regarding 'maiden modesty,' but properly used, and not hackneyed, the words are good and appropriate. As she passed to the window, after tacitly but gracefully acknowledging me, I could call her nothing in my own mind save 'stainless virgin.' To my perception, a delicate splendor robed her, and the modesty of girlhood was her halo. I may be the most foolish, as I am one of the plainest, of men, but in truth, that shyness of hers touched me exquisitely; it flattered my finest sensations. I likely appeared a stupid block, I dare say. I was alive with a life of Paradise as she turned *her* glance from *my* glance, and softly averted her head to hide the flush of her cheek.

'I know this is the talk of a dreamer—of a rapt, romantic lunatic. I *do* dream. I *will* dream now and then; and if she has inspired romance into my prosaic composition, how can I help it?

'What a child she is sometimes! What an unsophisticated, untaught thing! I see her now looking up into my face, entreating me to prevent them from smothering her, and to ensure she receives a strong narcotic. I see her confessing that she was not so self-sufficient, so independent of sympathy, as people thought. I see the secret tear drop quietly from her eyelash. She said I thought her childish, and I did. She imagined I despised her. Despised her! It was unutterably sweet to feel myself at once near her and above her—to be conscious of a natural right and power to sustain her, as a husband should sustain his wife.

'I worship her perfections; but it is her faults, or at least her foibles, that bring her near to me, that nestle her to my heart, that fold her about with my love, and that, for a most selfish but deeply natural reason, bind me to her. These faults are the steps by which I ascend to dominance over her. If she were a trimmed, artificial mound, without inequality, what vantage would she offer the foot? It is the natural hill, with its mossy breaks and hollows, whose slope invites ascent, whose summit it is pleasure to gain.

'To leave metaphors. It delights my eye to look upon her. She suits me. If I were a king and she the housemaid who swept my palace stairs, across all that space between us, my eye would recognize her qualities; a true pulse would beat for her in my heart, though an unspanned gulf made acquaintance impossible. If I were a gentleman and she waited on me as a servant, I could not help liking that Shirley.
