I trundled down the front drive steps only to run straight into Sergeant Cuff, lingering on the threshold. After all the sharp exchanges we’d had lately, my pride rebelled at letting him see I cared a fig for what he was up to — but curiosity burned hot enough to drown out all my good sense. Before I could clamp my mouth shut, the words were out: “Any news from Frizinghall?”

“I’ve questioned the Indian jugglers,” Cuff said, “and I’ve tracked down what Rosanna Spearman bought in town last Thursday, behind everyone’s back. The three of them walk free next Wednesday. Both Mr. Murthwaite and I are in full agreement: they traveled here for no other reason than to steal the Moonstone. Their whole scheme got blown to bits, of course, when the jewel vanished from the house that Wednesday night. They had no hand in the actual theft, no more than you did, Mr. Betteredge. But I can tell you this — if we don’t find that diamond first, they will. You haven’t seen the last of those three charlatans.”

Franklin Blake came back from his stroll just as the Sergeant spoke those startling lines. He mastered his curiosity far better than I had, brushed past us without a single word, and slipped straight into the house. Having already tossed my dignity to the dirt, I decided to get every penny’s worth out of the sacrifice. “That’s the Indians sorted,” I said. “What about Rosanna?”

Sergeant Cuff shook his head. “That mystery’s thicker than ever. I traced her steps to a linen draper’s shop in Frizinghall run by a man named Maltby. She didn’t spend a penny at any other draper, milliner, or tailor in town — all she bought at Maltby’s was a length of plain long cloth, carefully picked to an exact weight and weave, enough fabric to stitch a whole servant’s nightgown.

“My working theory?” he went on. “She snuck down to Miss Verinder’s room between midnight and three a.m. that Thursday, to hide the Moonstone while the whole house slept. On her way back to her own quarters, her nightgown brushed the still-wet fresh white paint on that bedroom door. She couldn’t scrub the stain out, and she couldn’t burn the spoiled gown without drawing attention to the gap in her personal linen inventory — so she bought the cloth to sew a replacement. If she’d been making a new nightgown for Miss Rachel, she’d have needed yards of lace, frills, and trim, and there’s no way she could have finished it all in one night. Plain long cloth means plain servant’s nightgown, no two ways about it. The only puzzle left now is why she hid the stained gown instead of burning it to ash. That secret’s buried at her favorite hiding spot out at the Shivering Sand. Search that stretch of beach, and we’ll have the full truth.”

I asked how he intended to pinpoint her hidden cache. He smiled, a thin, secretive little twitch of the mouth. “Sorry to disappoint, Mr. Betteredge — that’s a secret I’m keeping to myself.” (To spare you the same gnawing frustration I felt, I’ll note here he’d picked up a search warrant in Frizinghall that morning. His years of experience told him Rosanna almost certainly scribbled the exact location of her hiding spot on a scrap of paper, to guide her back even after shifting tides and time had erased her usual landmarks. Get that scrap, and he’d have all he needed.)

“Now, let’s drop speculation and get to work,” he said. “I told Joyce — the Frizinghall policeman Superintendent Seegrave assigned to me — to keep a constant eye on Rosanna. Where is he?”

The hall clock struck two right on cue. The closed carriage meant to take Miss Rachel to her aunt’s house, summoned to beat the incoming rain, rounded the drive right as he spoke. “One thing at a time,” Cuff said, stopping me before I could dash off to find Joyce. “Miss Verinder gets my full attention first.” He waved footman Samuel down from the rumble at the back of the carriage. “You’ll spot a friend of mine waiting in the trees just before the lodge gate,” he told him. “He’ll hop up into the rumble beside you without stopping the carriage. All you have to do is keep your mouth shut and pretend he isn’t there. Step out of line, and you’ll land in hot water.”

I felt my stomach twist right over. They were putting a spy on my young lady! A hidden shadow trailing her in the rumble of her own mother’s carriage. I could have gnawed my own tongue clean off for ever lowering myself to chat with Sergeant Cuff in the first place.

Lady Verinder was already waiting at the top of the steps, arms folded tight in her light garden cloak, not a single word for either of us, standing as still as a marble statue, lips pressed thin, waiting for her daughter. A minute later, Miss Rachel came down the stairs: soft butter-yellow silk jacket that hugged her narrow waist, a neat little straw hat twisted with a white veil, primrose gloves that fit her hands like a second skin, her black hair smooth as satin under the brim, two pearl drops glinting like dew on her shell-pink ears. She moved as straight as a lily stem, as lithe as a half-grown cat — but her face was nothing like the Rachel I knew. Her eyes were bright, sharp, fiercer than I’d ever seen them, her lips bleached completely pale, no trace of her usual smile left. She kissed her mother’s cheek quick, almost roughly, mumbled “Try to forgive me, mamma,” then wrenched her veil down so hard she tore the fabric, bolted down the steps, and threw herself into the carriage like it was a safe hiding place.

Sergeant Cuff moved just as fast. He stepped in front of the open carriage door before she could slam it shut. “What do you want?” Rachel snapped, voice sharp, from behind her veil.

“I only need one word with you before you leave, miss,” Cuff said. “I have no right to stop you visiting your aunt. But I have to tell you plainly: your leaving this house right now is a direct obstacle to me recovering your stolen Moonstone. You know that now. Decide for yourself if you go or stay.”

Rachel didn’t so much as pause to answer. “Drive on, James!” she called to the coachman. Cuff shut the carriage door without another word. Just as the latch clicked, Franklin came sprinting down the steps, hand outstretched. “Goodbye, Rachel!” he called.

“Drive on!” Rachel yelled even louder, ignoring him entirely, same as she’d ignored the Sergeant. Franklin stumbled back, thunderstruck — as well he might. The coachman glanced over at Lady Verinder, who stood on the top step with anger, grief, and raw shame warring openly on her face, and she gave him a sharp nod to start the horses. She turned and hurried back inside before the carriage had even cleared the steps. Franklin called after her, voice raw, that he was leaving too, thanked her for all her kindness. Lady Verinder half-turned to speak to him, then thought better of it, waved a gentle hand. “Come see me before you go, Franklin,” she said, voice breaking, and vanished down the hall to her rooms.

Franklin turned to me, eyes glistening. “Do me one last favor, Betteredge. Get me to the nearest train station as fast as you can.” He walked back into the house, completely unmanned by Rachel’s rejection — proof of just how deeply the boy loved her.

That left Sergeant Cuff and I alone at the foot of the steps. He was staring at the gap in the trees that framed a winding stretch of the carriage drive, hands stuffed in his pockets, softly whistling *The Last Rose of Summer*. “There’s a time and place for everything,” I snapped at him. “This isn’t the time for whistling.”

Right then the carriage came into view through the gap, heading for the lodge. There was a second man in the rumble behind Samuel, plain as day. “All right,” Cuff muttered to himself. He turned back to me. “You’re right. No more whistling. It’s time to press on with this investigation, no soft treatment for anyone. We start with Rosanna Spearman. Where is Joyce?”

We called for him, got no answer. I sent a stable boy to hunt him down. “You heard what I said to Miss Verinder,” Cuff noted while we waited. “You saw how she reacted. I told her leaving would stop me getting her diamond back — and she left anyway. Your young lady’s got a traveling companion riding in that carriage with her, Mr. Betteredge. And its name is the Moonstone.”

I said nothing. I’d have died before I doubted Rachel’s innocence for a second. The stable boy came back, dragging a very pale, trembling Joyce behind him. “Where is Rosanna Spearman?” Cuff asked.

“I can’t explain it, sir — I’m terribly sorry,” Joyce stammered. “I was so careful not to let her spot me following her, and there’s so many winding passages in the lower part of the house —”

“Are you telling me you let her slip right past you?” Cuff cut him off, as calm and flat-voiced as ever.

“I lost track of her nearly an hour ago, sir.”

“Go back to your regular beat in Frizinghall,” Cuff said. “This line of work’s a little too far above your pay grade, Mr. Joyce. Good morning.” The man slunk off, shoulders hunched.

I could barely wrap my head around Rosanna vanishing. My mind was spinning in fifty different directions at once, and I couldn’t find the words to say a single thing. “No, Mr. Betteredge,” Cuff said, like he’d read the exact thought bouncing around in my skull. “Rosanna’s not slipping through my fingers that easy. As long as I know where Miss Verinder is, I can track her accomplice. I stopped them from meeting last night, fine — now they’ll just meet up at her aunt’s house in Frizinghall instead. The investigation’s just moving a little earlier than I planned, from this estate to that house. Right now, I need you to round up all the servants for me.”

The old detective fever hit me like a flash flood. I forgot I hated Sergeant Cuff, grabbed his arm all conspiratorial. “For heaven’s sake, what are you going to do with the servants now?”

The great Cuff froze dead in his tracks, tilted his head up at the sky in a kind of mournful rapture. “If this man only knew how to grow roses,” he said, apparently talking about me, “he’d be the most perfect human being on the face of the earth.” He sighed, tucked his arm through mine, and got back to business. “Rosanna did one of two things: she either headed straight for Frizinghall to meet up with Rachel before I can get there, or she detoured first to the Shivering Sand to dig up her hidden cache. First we need to find out who saw her last before she left the house.”

We questioned every servant we could round up, and found the last person to spot her was Nancy the kitchen maid. Nancy saw Rosanna slip out the back door clutching a letter, stop the butcher’s delivery man just as he dropped off his order, and ask him to post it for her when he got back to Frizinghall. The delivery man pointed out that the address on the envelope, Cobb’s Hole, was far closer than Frizinghall — posting it there meant it wouldn’t be delivered until Monday, since Saturday’s last post run had already gone. Rosanna told him the delay was exactly what she wanted, and made him swear he’d post it anyway. No one in the house saw her after that.

“Well?” I asked, when we were alone again.

“Well,” Cuff said, “I’m heading to Frizinghall. I’m going straight to the post office to check that address. If it’s the one I suspect, I’ll be paying Mrs. Yolland — Rosanna’s old friend who lives right by the Shivering Sand — a visit first thing Monday.”

We headed for the stable yard to order the pony-chaise harnessed. And that’s where a whole new, unforeseen clue about the missing Rosanna Spearman fell right into our laps.
