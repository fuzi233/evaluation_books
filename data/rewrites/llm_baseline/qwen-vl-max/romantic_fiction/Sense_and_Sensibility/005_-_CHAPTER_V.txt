No sooner had Mrs. Dashwood sent off her reply than she allowed herself the joy of sharing the news with her son-in-law and his wife: she had secured a home, and would not burden them any longer than necessary—only until everything was ready for her to take up residence. The announcement met with surprise. Mrs. John Dashwood remained silent, but her husband responded with polite concern, expressing hope that she wouldn’t settle too far from Norland.

Mrs. Dashwood smiled with quiet satisfaction as she revealed her destination: Devonshire.

Edward turned sharply toward her at the mention of the name, his voice laced with genuine astonishment and worry. “Devonshire?” he echoed. “Are you truly going there? So far away? And which part, pray?”

She explained it was just four miles north of Exeter—a modest cottage, though one she hoped would soon become a gathering place for friends. “A few extra rooms could easily be added,” she said, “and if my friends are willing to make the journey to see me, I shall certainly do all I can to welcome them.” She then extended a warm invitation to Mr. and Mrs. John Dashwood to visit her at Barton, and to Edward, she offered one with even deeper affection.

Though her recent conversation with her daughter-in-law had solidified her decision to leave Norland without delay, it had not swayed her in the matter that mattered most: she still intended to keep Edward and Elinor together. Far from wanting to separate them, she wished to make clear to Mrs. John Dashwood—through this deliberate gesture of inviting Edward—that she cared nothing for her disapproval of their connection.

Mr. John Dashwood repeated again and again how deeply sorry he was that his mother’s new home lay so far from Norland, rendering him unable to assist with the removal of her belongings. He truly felt troubled by it; the very effort he had promised himself—limited to helping with her needs—was now impossible under these circumstances.

The household goods were shipped by water, including linen, silver, china, books, and Marianne’s elegant pianoforte. As the packages departed, Mrs. John Dashwood watched with a quiet sigh. It pained her to see such fine items go to someone whose income was so much smaller than theirs.

Mrs. Dashwood had taken the cottage for a year—it came furnished, and she could move in immediately. There were no complications in the arrangement, and she waited only for the final sorting of her possessions at Norland and the settling of her household staff before setting out westward. Thanks to her swift decisiveness when matters stirred her heart, everything was arranged quickly.

Her late husband’s horses had been sold shortly after his death, and now, with an opportunity to dispose of her carriage as well, she agreed to sell it—at the earnest urging of her eldest daughter. Though keeping it would have brought comfort to her children, Elinor’s judgment prevailed. Her wisdom also limited their domestic staff to three: two maids and one man, all chosen from among those who had served them at Norland.

The man and one maid were sent ahead to prepare the cottage for their arrival. Mrs. Dashwood preferred to go straight to Barton rather than stay as a guest at Barton Park, especially since Lady Middleton was entirely unknown to her. She trusted Sir John’s glowing description of the house so completely that she felt no need to inspect it beforehand—she would judge it only when she stepped inside as its mistress.

Her eagerness to leave Norland only grew stronger, fueled by the unmistakable relief she sensed in her daughter-in-law’s demeanor. That relief was barely masked beneath a cold suggestion that she might consider delaying her departure.

Now, more than ever, was the moment for Mr. John Dashwood to fulfill his father’s dying request. Since he had failed to act upon it when he first inherited the estate, his family’s departure seemed the perfect time to honor the promise. But Mrs. Dashwood soon gave up hope. From the tone and direction of his conversations, it became clear that his generosity extended only to maintaining them at Norland for six months.

He spoke often of rising household expenses and the endless financial demands placed upon a man of standing in society—so frequently, in fact, that he seemed less like a benefactor and more like someone in urgent need of funds himself.

Within weeks of Sir John Middleton’s first letter reaching Norland, all preparations were complete. Mrs. Dashwood and her daughters could finally begin their journey.

Farewells were bitter, filled with tears. Each goodbye carried the weight of a life left behind. “Dear, dear Norland!” Marianne whispered, wandering alone before the house on their last evening there. “When will I stop missing you? When will I learn to call another place home? Oh, happy house—if only you knew the sorrow I feel in seeing you now, perhaps for the last time! And you, beloved trees—how unchanged you remain. No leaf will fall because we’ve gone, no branch grow still just because we won’t watch you anymore. You’ll stay the same, unaware of the joy or pain you inspire, blind to the changes in those who once walked beneath your shade. But who will walk here now?”
