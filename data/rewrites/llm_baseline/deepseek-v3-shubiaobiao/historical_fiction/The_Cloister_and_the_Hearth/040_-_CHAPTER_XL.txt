Some hearts grow old at forty; others stay forever young at eighty. Margaret Van Eyck’s was of the latter kind—an evergreen, vibrant with life. Her affection for young Margaret Brandt burned with the ardor of youth, yet it was no fleeting fancy. The elder Margaret possessed a keen eye for character, and in the younger she recognized something rare in their sex: genuineness. But beyond the girl’s own virtues, another force worked in the old artist’s heart—human nature itself.

Strange as it may seem to the unobservant, the heart warms more readily to those we have helped than to those who help us. The Greeks noted it; poets have sung of it.

> *"I heard, and thought how side by side
> We two had stemmed the battle’s tide..."*

The verse spoke of bonds forged not by favors received, but by favors given.

And Margaret Van Eyck had given much. She had broken her own habits to visit the ailing girl, nursed her, soothed her, petted her, and done more for her recovery than all the medicines in the world. Now her heart swelled with tenderness for the recipient of her kindness, and she loved her more deeply than she had ever loved Gerard—though it was for Gerard’s sake she had first sought her out.

When she saw the roses return to Margaret’s cheeks and read the parchment that had brought them there, she yielded without protest.

"Sweetheart," she said, "I had hoped he might stay in Italy five or six years, return wealthy, and above all, a master of his craft. But your happiness comes first. If you cannot live without him, then we must bring him home as swiftly as may be."

"Ah, madam! You see my very thoughts." The young woman blushed and lowered her eyes. "But how to reach him? That puzzles me. He is gone to Italy—but where? He spoke of Florence, of Rome..."

"Come to the right place at the right time," the elder Margaret said with a smile. "Hans Memling was here today. He leaves for Italy next week—'to improve his hand,' he says. Though Heaven knows it needs more than improvement."

"But how will he find Gerard?"

"He knows Gerard well enough. They supped here together more than once, thick as thieves. His path will cross Gerard’s sooner or later—artists haunt the same cities. Write your letter, copy the pardon into it, and I’ll see it safely delivered. In six months at most, Gerard will hold it in his hands. And when he does—" She chuckled. "He’ll kiss it, tuck it in his bosom, and come flying home. What are you smiling at? And why so rosy all of a sudden? Ah, happy days are coming, my little pearl."

Meanwhile, Martin sat in the kitchen with a black-jack of ale before him, Reicht Heynes spinning nearby. And how she plied him with questions that night!

Hans Memling had been a pupil of Jan Van Eyck and his sister. A painter of skill, if not of fame, he was a good-natured soul with one flaw: a fondness for the bottle that kept him ever lean of purse. It also ensured he remained a frequent guest at Margaret Van Eyck’s table—and thus bound to her service.

The letter was written, the pardon transcribed, and all entrusted to the elder Margaret. The following week, true to his word, Hans returned from Flanders. She gave him the letter and a gold piece for his journey. He seemed in haste to depart.

"All the better," she remarked. "He’ll reach Italy the sooner."

But like a horse that strains to gallop only to flag after the first strides, Hans’s zeal cooled the moment he reached Tergou’s principal inn and spied two boon companions in the bay window. He stopped for a parting drink—one that his friends insisted on paying, then another, and another, until his tongue loosened like a wine-skin’s stopper. He boasted of teaching the Italians to paint, of his exploits in battle, of conquests amorous and martial, until at last, brimming with loose words, he let slip his mission: a letter to one Gerard, a townsman of theirs.

"A good fellow!" he proclaimed, slapping Sybrandt so heartily on the back that the wind left him.

Sybrandt, wheezing, edged around the table but listened keenly. Here was news: Gerard had gone to Italy. Affecting doubt, he challenged it.

"My brother Gerard? Never."

"Liar!" roared Hans, his mood shifting with the drink. "He’s in Italy, ten times your brother! See here—read it yourself!" He flung the letter onto the table.

Sybrandt took it up, pretending to study it before admitting he could not read. But another drinker, proud of his rare skill, took it and read aloud:

"Fine writing," the man observed, tracing the letters.

"Ay," Hans declared grandly, "and small wonder—’tis the hand of Margaret Van Eyck herself! A dear friend of mine, Margaret Van Eyck."

Sybrandt slipped away to find Cornelis.

The brothers put their heads together. Italy was far—could they keep him there?

"Keep him? Nothing would hold him long from his Margaret."

"Curse her!" Sybrandt spat. "Why didn’t she die when she had the chance?"

"She’d outlive the plague just to spite us."

Thus festering, their hearts conceived a plan: keep Gerard in Italy forever, and claim his share of their father’s wealth.

But execution required wit, and here they faltered—until, as if the Devil whispered in both ears at once, they cried as one:

"The burgomaster!"

Ghysbrecht Van Swieten received them at once. Suspense gnawed at him; certainty, even bitter, was better than doubt.

"You have news of Gerard?"

They told of the letter and Hans Memling. His eyes gleamed. "Who wrote it?"

"Margaret Van Eyck."

"Are you certain?" He fetched a sample of her writing. "Like this?"

"Ay, the same."

"Good. And what would you have of me?" His tone was careless, but his pulse raced.

They stammered, circling their intent before blurting it: a false letter, one to replace the true in Hans’s wallet, with words to keep Gerard in Italy.

Ghysbrecht’s mind warred—vengeance urged Gerard’s return to face the law; prudence whispered to keep him an ocean away. But darker still was his fear: Gerard knew a secret that could undo him.

"My duty is to aid citizens who cannot write," he said at last. "But the matter is yours, not mine. What shall I pen?"

"Tell him Margaret is dead."

"Dead? At her age?"

"She nearly was. It’s no outright lie."

"And you think this will keep him abroad?"

"Ay," Cornelis said bitterly. "Italy was his dream. He’d return for her, but not for us. He cares naught for his own kin."

Ghysbrecht’s lips curled. "A bitter draught for him."

"For his own good," Sybrandt said smoothly.

"A lie breaks no bones," Cornelis added with a smirk.

Sybrandt’s gaze flicked to Ghysbrecht’s broken nose. "Nor leaves marks like a cudgel."

The burgomaster’s face darkened. That venomous glance struck home. Hate flared.

"Very well. Dictate, and I shall write. But remember—the guilt lies not with the hand that writes, but the tongue that guides it."

The brothers assented, sneering inwardly. Ghysbrecht drew his inkwell close, studying Margaret Van Eyck’s script, when an interruption came—Jorian Ketel burst in, urgency in his face.

"I bring news—not for all ears."

Ghysbrecht drew him aside. A whispered exchange, then Jorian was sent to saddle his mule. The burgomaster turned back, suddenly cold.

"The peace of households is precious. This must not be rushed. We shall see."

"But the man will be gone!"

"Where is he?"

"At the inn, drinking."

"Keep him there, then."

And with that, he dismissed them, baffled.

Earlier that morning, Margaret Brandt had met Jorian near her door. He scowled at her, but she stopped him.

"You saved him once. Why have you stayed away? Did you not believe in the hundred crowns?"

Jorian, disarmed by her candor, confessed his earlier rebuff. She led him to the garden, showing where the parchments lay buried.

"None have touched them but you may take them now."

Jorian, exultant, raced to the Stadthouse.

Now, as Ghysbrecht rode toward Sevenbergen, Jorian strode beside him, boasting that within the hour the missing deeds would be in hand.

"Lucky it was no thief took them," Jorian mused.

"No thief? What then?"

"A jackdaw. Mischief, not malice. Like my old chough—stealing what he least needed and hiding it. This Gerard’s the same. The parchments are no use to him; he hid them out of whim."

Ghysbrecht’s tension eased. "Perhaps you’re right."

At Peter’s gate, hesitation struck him.

"I wish it were anywhere but here."

Jorian reassured him. "The girl is honest. She had no part in it."

In the garden, loose soil marked the spot. Jorian dug, uncovering a parchment. Ghysbrecht snatched it up, trembling, tossing out one after another—until his face fell.

"Where is the other? The one worth all these?"

Jorian, suspicious, accused him of cheating.

A calm voice cut through.

"There was another—not a town record, but a private deed between you and my grandfather."

"Where is it?"

"Gerard took it with him."

"Whither?"

She turned away. "Ask me no more. The sight of you is more than I can bear."

Ghysbrecht stood stricken—remorse, fear, greed warring within. Then Martin Wittenhaagen’s mocking laugh rang out.

"Ho! Arrest him, thief!" Ghysbrecht snarled.

Martin thrust the duke’s pardon under his nose.

"The duke sends a message," he said coolly. "He’ll not have burghers playing kings. Be humble—or he’ll hang you at your own door, as he did the last one."

The insult, the impotence, drove Ghysbrecht to fury. He spat on Martin’s coat.

Martin laughed. "A venomous old toad! Not worth my boot."

Ghysbrecht mounted his mule, shaking with rage. "I’ll make their hearts ache. All of them!"

Back in Tergou, the black sheep brooded over Hans’s wallet. If only they could swap the letter! But they could not write.

Then Dierich Brower summoned them to the burgomaster.

Ghysbrecht, pale and tense, had forged a letter. "Now, the superscription—what were the words?"

Sybrandt recalled: *"To Gerard Eliassoen, these by the hand of the trusty Hans Memling, with all speed."*

"Good. Now, the size? The fold?"

They described it.

"Go. Give him this in place of the true one."

But when they reached the inn, Hans was gone.

Dierich hurried them back.

Ghysbrecht’s eyes gleamed. "Is he mounted?"

"No."

"Then catch him. Must I spell it out? Must every move be made with wine and trickery?"

A dark glance passed between Ghysbrecht and Dierich. The brothers paled, their zeal evaporating.

But Ghysbrecht was not done.

"Return the true letter to me—by any means."

And so the trap was set.
