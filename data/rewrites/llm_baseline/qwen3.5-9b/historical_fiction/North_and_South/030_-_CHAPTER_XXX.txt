The following morning, Mrs. Thornton visited Mrs. Hale. The invalid's condition had deteriorated sharply overnight; a sudden, terrifying step toward death had been taken in the space of twelve hours, leaving her family stunned by the gray, sunken hollows that had appeared in her features. Mrs. Thornton, who had not seen her for weeks, felt a sudden softening within her own hardened heart. Though she had arrived because her son requested it as a personal favor, her pride and bitterness remained firmly entrenched against the Hale family, of whom Margaret was a member. She questioned the reality of Mrs. Hale's illness, suspecting it was merely a fleeting whim designed to excuse the lady from her usual duties.

"I wish we had never come near this place," she declared to her son, "that you had never gotten acquainted with them. There have never been such useless languages as Latin and Greek invented."

Her son endured this tirade in silence. Only when she finished her invective against the dead tongues did he calmly reiterate his wish for her to visit Mrs. Hale at the appointed hour, noting it was the most convenient time for the invalid. Mrs. Thornton submitted with the worst grace imaginable, yet secretly admired her son for his persistence in maintaining contact with the Hales.

To her, his goodness bordered on weakness, while her own contempt for Mr. and Mrs. Hale and her outright dislike of Margaret filled her thoughts until she was overwhelmed by the dark shadow of the Angel of Death. There lay Mrs. Hale—a mother, like herself, yet much younger—stiff and still on a bed offering no sign of hope. The room was devoid of light and shade; there was no power of action, only faint whispers alternating with studied silence. Yet, this monotony seemed almost too much to bear.

When Mrs. Thornton entered, strong and vibrant with life, Mrs. Hale lay motionless, though clearly conscious of her visitor's presence. She did not open her eyes for a moment or two. Heavy tears glistened on her lashes before she finally looked up. Reaching feebly beneath the covers, she sought the touch of Mrs. Thornton's large, firm fingers and whispered, barely audible:

"Margaret—you have a daughter—my sister is in Italy. My child will be without a mother; in a strange place—if I die—will you..."

Her filmy, wandering eyes fixed themselves on Mrs. Thornton's face with an intensity of longing. For a minute, Mrs. Thornton's features remained rigid, stern, and unmoved. Had the sick woman's vision not been dimming with gathering tears, she might have seen a dark cloud cross the cold features of the visitor. It was not a thought of her son or her living daughter, Fanny, that finally stirred Mrs. Thornton's heart, but a sudden recollection triggered by the room's arrangement: of a little daughter, dead in infancy long ago. Like a sudden sunbeam, this memory melted the icy crust behind which lay a real, tender woman.

"You wish me to be a friend to Miss Hale?" Mrs. Thornton asked, her voice measured and clear, refusing to soften despite her heart's turmoil.

Mrs. Hale pressed the hand lying below hers on the coverlet, unable to speak further. Mrs. Thornton sighed. "I will be a true friend, if circumstances require it. Not a tender friend. That I cannot be." She paused, tempted to add "to her," but relented at the sight of the poor, anxious face. "It is not my nature to show affection even where I feel it, nor do I volunteer advice in general. Still, at your request—and if it will be any comfort to you—I will promise."

A long pause followed. Conscientious to a fault, Mrs. Thornton refused to promise what she did not intend to perform. Performing acts of kindness regarding Margaret, whom she currently despised, was nearly impossible.

"I promise," she said with grave severity, a tone that somehow inspired the dying woman with faith more stable than the flickering, wavering life around them. "I promise that in any difficulty in which Miss Hale—"

"Call her Margaret!" gasped Mrs. Hale.

"In which she comes to me for help, I will assist her with every power I possess, as if she were my own daughter. I also promise that if ever I see her doing what I believe to be wrong—such wrong not touching me or mine, where I might be suspected of having an interested motive—I will tell her of it faithfully and plainly, as I should wish my own daughter to be told."

Another long silence ensued. Mrs. Hale sensed the promise excluded everything, yet it was significant. Its reservations were unclear to her weakened mind, but she was too dizzy and exhausted to dwell on them. Meanwhile, Mrs. Thornton reviewed the probable scenarios in which she had bound herself. She derived a fierce pleasure from the prospect of delivering unwelcome truths to Margaret in the name of duty.

"I thank you," Mrs. Hale began, her voice fading. "I pray God to bless you. I shall never see you again in this world. But my last words are, I thank you for your promise of kindness to my child."

"Not kindness!" Mrs. Thornton corrected, ungraciously truthful to the end. Having soothed her conscience with the words, she was glad they went unheard. She pressed Mrs. Hale's soft, languid hand, then rose and departed from the house unseen by anyone.

While this somber interview unfolded, Margaret and Dixon were huddled together, plotting how to keep Frederick's impending arrival a profound secret from everyone outside the house. A letter from him could arrive any day, followed swiftly by his physical presence. Martha was to be sent away on holiday immediately; Dixon would maintain a stern guard at the front door, admitting only essential visitors to Mr. Hale's room downstairs—an excuse easily made by Mrs. Hale's critical illness. If Mary Higgins needed assistance in the kitchen, she was to hear and see as little of Frederick as possible, referring to him only as Mr. Dickinson if necessary. Her sluggish, indifferent nature served as the greatest safeguard of all.

They resolved that Martha would leave that very afternoon for her mother's house. Margaret regretted not sending her earlier, fearing it might seem odd to grant a servant a holiday when the household required constant attention.

Poor Margaret spent the entire afternoon playing the role of a Roman daughter, draining her own scanty reserves of strength to support her father. Mr. Hale oscillated between hope and despair amidst his wife's attacks, buoying himself up during brief respites from her pain, convinced that recovery was imminent. Consequently, when the paroxysms returned, each more severe than the last, they brought fresh agony and crushing disappointment.

That afternoon, he sat in the drawing-room, unable to bear the solitude of his study or engage in any activity. He buried his head in his arms, folded on the table. Margaret's heart ached witnessing his state, yet, since he offered no speech, she hesitated to offer unsolicited comfort. Martha was gone; Dixon sat with Mrs. Hale while she slept. The house was profoundly still and quiet, and darkness fell without anyone moving to fetch candles. Margaret sat by the window, staring out at the streetlamps and the road, seeing nothing but alive to her father's heavy sighs. She dreaded going down for lights, fearing that her withdrawal might trigger a violent emotional collapse in her father without her being nearby to soothe him. Just as she considered attending to the kitchen fire, which was solely her responsibility, the muffled doorbell rang with such violent force that the wires jingled throughout the house, though the actual sound was muted.

She jumped up, passed her father—who had not stirred at the dull, veiled noise—and returned to kiss him tenderly. He remained immovable, taking no notice of her embrace. She descended softly into the dark hallway. Dixon would have engaged the chain before opening the door, but Margaret, her mind preoccupied, felt no fear. A tall male figure stood between her and the luminous street. He was looking away, but turned quickly at the sound of the latch.

"Is this Mr. Hale's?" he asked in a clear, full, delicate voice.

Margaret trembled from head to toe. Initially, she did not answer. Then, in a moment, she sighed out, "Frederick!" stretching out both hands to catch his and draw him inside.

"Oh, Margaret!" he exclaimed, holding her gently by the shoulders after their kiss, as if even in the darkness he could see her face and read a quicker answer to his question than words could provide. "My mother! Is she alive?"

"Yes, she is alive, dear, dear brother! She—as ill as she can be—is; but alive! She is alive!"

"Thank God!" he breathed.

"Papa is utterly prostrate with this great grief."

"You expect me, don't you?"

"No, we received no letter."

"Then I have come before it. But my mother knows I am coming."

"Oh! We all knew you would come. But wait a little! Step in here. Give me your hand. What is this? Oh! Your carpet-bag. Dixon has shut the shutters; but this is Papa's study, and I can take you to a chair to rest for a few minutes while I go and tell him."
