Happy is the man who holds two chains: one of merit, the other of women. Oh, that I possessed, like Gerard, a *chaine des dames* to hoist me upward by! With such an anchor, I should become a prose laureate, a professor of the spasmodic, or some other dignitary in no time at all. Until then, let me sketch the portrait of Fra Colonna.

The true resurrectors of ancient learning and philosophy were two fictional writers—Petrarch and Boccaccio. Their labors were not crowned with immediate, vast, or public triumph; yet they sowed the good seed, which never perished but quickened in the soil, waiting for the sun. From their day forward, Italy knew no lack of a native scholar or two versed in Greek, and every learned Greek who landed there was received with fraternal warmth. Before the fourteenth century closed, it saw the births of Poggio, Valla, and the elder Guarino; and early in the fifteenth, Florence under Cosimo de' Medici became a nest of Platonists. Headed by Gemistus Pletho, a born Greek, these scholars began around 1440 to transcribe Aristotle. For few minds are large enough to render justice to Great A without becoming unjust toward Capital B.

Theodore Gaza defended that great man with moderation; George of Trebizond attacked him with acerbity, retorting sharply against Plato. Then Cardinal Bessarion, another born Greek, rose to resist George and his idol in a tract titled *"Adversus calumniatorem Platonis."* Whether wise or not, pugnacity is a form of vitality. Born without controversial bile in so zealous an epoch, Francesco Colonna, a young Florentine nobleman, lived solely for the arts. At twenty, he turned Dominican friar. His aim was quiet study. He retreated from idle company and faction fights—the humming and stinging of the human hive—to St. Dominic and the Nine Muses.

An eager student of languages, pictures, statues, chronology, coins, and monumental inscriptions, these latter studies loosened his faith in popular histories. He traveled many years in the East, returning laden with spoils: master of several choice manuscripts, versed in Greek, Latin, Hebrew, and Syriac. He found his homeland had not stood still. Besides Cosimo, other lettered princes had risen: Alfonso, King of Naples; Niccolò d'Este; Lionel d'Este. Above all, his old friend Thomas of Sarzana had been made Pope, lending a mighty impulse to letters. He had amassed five thousand manuscripts in the Vatican library, setting Poggio to translate Diodorus Siculus and Xenophon's *Cyropaedia*, Laurentius Valla to translate Herodotus and Thucydides, Theodore Gaza to translate Theophrastus, and George of Trebizond to tackle Eusebius and various treatises of Plato.

The monk found Plato and Aristotle under truce, but Poggio and Valla at loggerheads over verbs and nouns, burning with philological hatred. All this was heaven, and he settled down in his native land, his life a rosy dream. None are so happy as the versatile, provided they need not earn their bread by it. And Fra Colonna was versatility. He knew seven or eight languages and a touch of mathematics; he could write, paint, model, sing, and strum, and he could relish superior excellence in all these branches. For this last trait, he deserved to be as happy as he was. Gauge the intellects of your acquaintances, and you will find few whose minds are neither deaf, nor blind, nor dead to some great art or science—"And wisdom at one entrance quite shut out." And such of them as are conceited as well as stupid shall parade instead of blushing for the holes in their intellects.

A zealot in art, the friar was a skeptic in religion. In every age, there are a few men who hold the opinions of another age, past or future. Being a lump of simplicity, his skepticism was as naive as his enthusiasm. He affected to view the religious ceremonies of his day as mere models, while heathen philosophers regarded the worship of gods and departed heroes as "mummeries" fit only for the populace. Yet here his mind drew an unconscious, droll distinction. Whatever Christian ceremony his learning taught him was of purely pagan origin; therefore, he respected it out of reverence for antiquity, though had he, with his turn of mind, been a pagan contemporary, he would have scorned it from his philosophic heights.

Fra Colonna was charmed with his new artist. Having access to half the palaces in Rome, he sounded Gerard's praises until the young man was soon summoned to resign him. "But I am so happy with you, Father," objected Gerard. "Fiddlesticks about being happy with me!" said Fra Colonna. "You must not be happy; you must be a man of the world. The grand lesson I impress on youth is: be a man of the world. Now these Montesini can pay you three times as much as I can, and they shall too—by Jupiter!"

And the friar slapped a terrific price on Gerard's pen. It was acceded to without a murmur. Much higher prices were being paid for copying than authorship had ever obtained for centuries under the printing press. Gerard received three hundred crowns for Aristotle's treatise on rhetoric. The great are mighty sweet upon all their pets while the fancy lasts; and in the fever for Greek manuscripts, the handsome writer soon became a pet, caressed by nobles of both sexes like a lapdog.

It might have turned a vain fellow's head, but the canny Dutchman saw the steel hand beneath the velvet glove and did not presume. Nevertheless, it was a proud day when he found himself seated with Fra Colonna at the table of his present employer, Cardinal Bessarion. They were perhaps a mile below the summit of that table, but never mind—they were there, and Gerard enjoyed the advantage of seeing roast pheasants served with all their feathers intact, as if they had just flown out of a coppice rather than off the spit, alongside chickens cooked in bottles, tender as peaches. But the grand novelty was the napkins, surpassingly fine and folded into cocked hats, bird's wings, fans, and other intricate shapes, instead of lying flat. This electrified Gerard, though readers may have witnessed the dazzling phenomenon without tumbling backward, chair and all.

After dinner, the tables were splintered and carried away, revealing another table spread with sweetmeats beneath. The *signore* and *signorine* fell upon them and gorged themselves, while the *signori* eyed them with reasonable suspicion. "But, dear Father," objected Gerard, "I see not the bifurcate daggers with which men say His Excellency arms the left hand of a guest." "Nay, 'twas Cardinal Orsini who invented that peevish instrument for guests to fumble their meat withal," replied the friar. "One, being in haste, did skewer his tongue to his palate with it, I hear; *O tempora, O mores!* How would the ancients, reclining godlike at their feasts, have spurned such pedantries?"

As soon as the ladies had disported themselves among the sugar-plums, the tables were suddenly removed, and the guests sat in a row against the wall. Then entered, ducking and scraping, two ecclesiastics with lutes, kneeling at the cardinal's feet to sing the service of the day before retiring with a deep obeisance. In answer, the cardinal fingered his skullcap as our late Iron Duke did his hat; the company dispersed, and Gerard had dined with a cardinal and one who had thrice almost become pope.

But greater honor was in store. One day the cardinal sent for him, praised the beauty of his work, and took him in his coach to the Vatican. Up a private stair, they reached a luxurious little room with a great oriel window. There were inkstands, sloping frames for writing, and all the instruments of art. The cardinal whispered to a courtier, and presently the Pope's private secretary appeared with a glorious, grimy old manuscript of Plutarch's *Lives*. Soon Gerard was seated alone, copying it, awe-struck yet half-delighted at the thought that His Holiness would handle his work and read it.

The papal inkstands were glorious externally, but within the ink was vile. Gerard, however, always carried good, home-made ink in a dirty little inkhorn. Praying on his knees for a firm and skillful hand, he set to work. One side of his room was nearly occupied by a massive curtain divided in the center, but its ample folds overlapped. After a while, Gerard felt drawn to peek through it. He resisted the impulse. It returned. It overpowered him. Leaving Plutarch, he stole across the matted floor, took the folds of the curtain, and gently gathered them up with his fingers. Putting his nose through the chink, he ran it against a cold steel halbert. Two soldiers, armed cap-a-pie, held their glittering weapons crossed in a triangle. Gerard drew swiftly back, but in that instant he heard the soft murmur of voices and saw a group of persons cringing before some hidden figure.
