**PAUL, FALLING IN WITH A GOODLY COMPANY OF PILGRIMS, LEARNS OF THEM THE ROAD THAT HE MUST TRAVEL. AND MEETS THE PRINCESS OF THE GOLDEN LOCKS.**

The East India Dock Road, in those ancient days, was a thoroughfare of profound silence, a stark contrast to the chaotic symphony of trams and omnibuses that now rattles over its pavements. It was a lonely lane, hemmed in on one side by the towering, echoing walls of the docks and on the other by scattered cottages rising from a landscape of market gardens and drying grounds. Of all that vanished, only one relic remained—or at least, it did last time I walked this path—and that was the single-storeyed brick cottage at the start of the bridge, once the toll-house. It held a peculiar power for me, for it was within its shadow that my childhood slipped away, leaving me standing there, small and terrified, gazing at a vast, unfamiliar world beyond.

I cannot articulate it better. That afternoon, I had ventured to Plaistow to visit the family dentist. It was a remote spot, yet it possessed compensating virtues. "Have the half-crown in your hand," my mother would instruct, ensuring the coin sat safely at the bottom of my knickerbocker pocket, "but if he refuses it, return it." I suspect he was a distant relation, a kindly soul indeed. Seated in his velvet throne, he would unroll his terrifying case of instruments, asking me to choose while praising the deadliest-looking ones with affectionate hyperbole. But when I opened my mouth to discuss the gruesome possibilities, *lo!* a pair would shoot from under his sleeve, and before I knew what was happening, the ordeal was over. Then, tea. He was an old bachelor living in a magnificent garden amidst Plaistow's picturesque pastures, and his housekeeper produced jams and jellies of legendary quality. Our conversations usually revolved around my mother—portrayed by him as a prankish, wilful little girl, though when I recounted these tales, she would laugh, claiming no memory of such a child, and sternly declare that we ought to gossip about something more sensible. Yet, inevitably, her next question would be, "And what else did he say?" implying I hadn't mentioned anything about *her*.

After clearing the tea things, he would produce his great microscope. To me, it was a portal to a fairy realm of dragons and monsters, cementing his status as a harmless magician. Looking back, I associate his microscopic obsession with the fact that he was an exceptionally tiny man, yet possessed the biggest heart I ever knew. On departure, I would formally hand over my half-crown "with mamma's compliments," and he would formally accept it. However, stepping outside the gate, I would invariably find it missing from my jacket pocket. Returning it unblushing, he would deny all knowledge, suggesting it must be another half-crown lost in the shuffle. "Slippery things, half-crowns," he would muse.

That specific day, returning home, I paused on the bridge to watch the lazy barges weave between the piers. It was a hushed summer evening, the air of the grim city filled with whispering voices. As I passed through the white toll-gate, I felt a sensation of leaving myself behind on the bridge. The impression was so vivid I looked back, half-expecting to see my younger self leaning over the iron parapet, gazing into the sunlit water. It sounds foolish, but I wonder if others have ever experienced this shedding. The little boy never returned. He passed away from me as a man's body might pass away, leaving only remembrance and regret. For a time, I tried to play his games, to dream his dreams, but the substance was missing. I was merely a thin ghost, making believe.

This loss troubled me deeply, even to tears. I did not want to grow up; the dark road of maturity frightened me. Why must I move forward? Could I not stay forever playing, dreaming? Gradually, over long months and years, a new consciousness emerged—a different rhythm, new throbbings rooted in a changing world. Little Paul, the narrator of my previous adventures, began to fade. Thus, I must let him fade from this book with sorrow, but before I depart completely, let me recall what else I remember of him.

The most vivid image is of my Aunt Fan, crouching over the kitchen fire. Her skirt and crinoline were rolled up to her waist, sacrificing modesty to custom, leaving only her petticoat exposed. Rhythmic motions swayed her body as her hands stroked her knees affectionately. Opposite her stood I, armed with a paper knife as a sword or a broomstick as a horse. Sometimes I was a knight and she a wicked ogre; she would roar and swear until I slew her, instantly transforming her into a beautiful princess I carried away on my prancing broomstick. While the princess conversed sweetly from her high window, the scene was realistic enough, but carrying her away required pure make-believe, for Aunt Fan refused to leave her chair before the fire.

Other scenes involved the meat-chopper. I might be an Indian brave, she "Laughing Water" or "Singing Sunshine," and we would go out scalping together. Or, in quieter moods, I was the Fairy Prince and she the Sleeping Beauty. Though she excelled in these roles, her favorite performance was when seated in the center of an upturned table, I as Captain Cook and she as the Cannibal Chief. "I shall skin him and hang him in the larder till Sunday week," she would announce, smacking her lips, "then he'll be perfectly tender—not too tough, not too high." My Aunt Fan was a master of detail.

While I wished her no ill, I am convinced her compliance was not entirely self-sacrificing. Often she suggested the game and the theme, invariably casting herself as the heavy lead—the dragon, the wicked uncle, the fussy necromancer. As the author of a hypothetical cookbook for Giant-Land, she would surely have succeeded. Recipes were rarely monotonous: "Boil him," "Roast her for supper." No, Aunt Fan introduced variety and daintiness to the domestic economy of Ogredom. "Stuffed with chestnuts and served on toast," she would direct, "don't forget the giblets; they make excellent sauce." Regarding imprisoned maidens, her advice was precise: "Not too much fish—it spoils the flesh for roasting."

She delighted in transforming beautiful damsels into Glyptodons or Hippocrepians, warriors into plaice, and queens into Brussels sprouts. With gusto, she planned complex slaughters, ensuring knives were sharp and mops ready for the cleanup. She followed the realistic school of writing.

Our plays invariably concluded with her death. Her groans and writhings sometimes frightened me, but I always welcomed the final gurgle. Immediately after, she would lower her skirt, signaling the fall of the curtain, and set to work preparing tea.

Another recurring vision is of myself, wearing a glazed-peaked cap, explaining the world to a smaller figure with curly hair and wide-open eyes. Every now and then, she would dash ahead to turn and gaze admiringly into my face, captivated by my praise. I valued her company for reasons she likely didn't understand. First, she protected me from my baser instincts; with her beside me, I dared not flee from sudden foes. Together, we courted adventure. Standing hazards added a charm to outdoor exercise that older, safer folks missed. To me, a walk was perhaps a bit tiresome, dictated by contemplation of my waistband, but to her, it was an expedition fraught with peril. "Will the gentleman asleep on The Chequer's doorstep greet me with a sniff or try to bite my head off? Shall we pass the cross-eyed loafer with a casual 'how-do' or engage in a life-and-death struggle?" Her trusting face nerved me, and in lonely places, I allowed her to hold my hand.

Second, her presence ensured I wasn't trampled or swept aside into doorways. She possessed a winsome face and a formidable vocabulary. When a blind idiot, male or female, attempted to walk *through* us rather than around, she would intervene with shrill trebles. "Now, then, where yer coming to, old glass-eye? We ain't spirits!" If they attempted to reply, her voice would rise higher. "Garn! They'd run out of heads when they're making you! That's only a turnip stuck on top of yer!" Personal chastisement was futile; an irate adult attempting to reprimand her was like an hippopotamus trying to lecture a terrier. The result was comedy for the entire street.

Though I observed her prowess with admiration, she remained meek and almost irritatingly submissive to me. She found out where I lived and would wait for hours, her little face pressed against the iron railings, until I either came out or shook my head from my bedroom window. When she ran off, the fading silence of her pattering feet left me a little sad. I cared for her, yet she never entered my daydreams; she existed only in the outer world of shadows, not in my inner life.

I also thought she was unwise for introducing me to the shop, for children and dogs seem to share a snobbery. If her father had been a firewood dealer, I could have soothed myself by imagining mistakes. Fairies, the best intentioned, were often muddlers.
