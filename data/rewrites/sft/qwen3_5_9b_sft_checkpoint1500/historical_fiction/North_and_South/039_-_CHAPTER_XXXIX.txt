Margaret withdrew to her chamber, leaving Mrs. Thornton behind. Within the confines of her small room, she paced back and forth, a physical manifestation of her inner turmoil, the familiar rhythm of her agitation. Yet, acutely aware that the thin walls of this slightly built dwelling carried every footstep from room to room, she eventually sat down, waiting in silence until she was certain Mrs. Thornton had departed the house.

Forced into introspection, she began to reconstruct the conversation, parsing every utterance, every implication, as if examining evidence under a microscope. When the replay ended, she rose, her voice heavy with a melancholy resignation. "In any case, her words do not strike me; they slide off. For I am innocent of the motives she imputes. Yet, it remains a bitter truth that any woman can so readily believe such calumny of another. It is a heavy, sad burden. Where I have erred, she offers no accusation; she knows nothing. And he... he never revealed anything. I should have suspected his silence!"

She raised her chin, feigning a pride in whatever subtle decency Mr. Thornton might have displayed. But then, a fresh, stinging thought seized her, pressing her hands together so tightly her knuckles whitened. "He, too, must attribute some affection to poor Frederick." A flush warmed her cheeks as the word lingered. "I see it now. It is not simply that he knows of my deceit; he believes another woman holds sway over me; and that *I*..." A sigh escaped her lips. "Oh, deary me! What am I to do? What does this madness portend? Why do I care so deeply for his judgment, beyond the simple loss of his trust in my veracity? I cannot fathom it. Yet, misery consumes me. How tragically short this last year has felt! I have leapt from childhood to a premature old age. I have known no youth, no true womanhood; the dreams of marriage have closed forever before me. I anticipate the cares and sorrows of an elderly widow with a tear-stained spirit. I am wearied by the constant demand for fortitude. I could endure hardship for Papa; it is a natural, holy duty. Perhaps I could summon the energy to resent Mrs. Thornton's unjust, impertinent suspicions. But it is devastating to realize how utterly misunderstood I am. What has altered me today? I know not. I simply cannot stop it. Sometimes I must yield. 'No,' she declared, springing upright, steeling herself. "'I shall not—I *will not* dwell on myself or my station. I shall not dissect my own feelings. It serves no purpose now.' She paused, wiping her eyes with impatient gestures, knowing the tears would return regardless of her resolve. 'Perhaps many a woman makes a far sadder error and pays the price only when it is too late. How proudly, how sharply I spoke to him that day! Yet, I was ignorant. This realization crept upon me gradually, and I know not whence it sprang. Now, I shall hold fast. It will be arduous to treat him as before, carrying this crushing conscience, but I shall remain composed, quiet, and sparing in my words. To be sure, he avoids me; he keeps his distance, seemingly intent on evading our paths. That would be worse than all. And yet, no wonder he shuns me, believing what he must.'"

Stepping outside, Margaret moved swiftly toward the countryside, attempting to drown the churning reflections within her mind by the sheer velocity of her steps.

Upon her return, standing on the threshold, her father approached. "A good girl," he remarked warmly. "You've been to Mrs. Boucher's. I was just intending to pay a call there before dinner, if I had time."

"No, Father," Margaret replied, her face flushing with embarrassment. "I never considered it. However, I shall go immediately after dinner; I will visit while you are resting."

True to her word, Margaret proceeded. Mrs. Boucher lay gravely ill, truly dying, not merely ailing. The kind, sensible neighbor who had visited recently appeared to have assumed total charge of the household. Several children had been sent to neighbors; Mary Higgins had fetched the three youngest at dinner, and subsequently, Nicholas had ventured out to seek the doctor. The physician had not yet arrived. There was nothing to do but wait. Margaret, however, hoped to consult the doctor's opinion and reasoned that visiting the Higginses in the interim offered the best chance of learning whether Nicholas had successfully petitioned Mr. Thornton.

Inside the Higgins home, she found Nicholas busily fashioning a penny-spin on the dresser, entertaining three tiny children who clung to him with fearless adoration. He, along with the children, smiled at the long, smooth rotation of the coin. Margaret observed that this look of absorbed joy in his occupation was a promising sign. When the penny finally halted, "little Johnnie" broke into tears.

"Come here," Margaret said, lifting the child from the dresser and cradling him. She held her watch to his ear, simultaneously asking Nicholas if he had encountered Mr. Thornton.

Nicholas's expression shifted instantly, darkening with a sudden gravity. "Ay!" he confirmed. "I've seen and heard too much concerning him."

"He refused you, then?" Margaret asked, her voice filled with sorrow.

"To be sure. I knew he'd do it all along. It's no good expecting mercy from the hands of those masters. You're a stranger and a foreigner, unlikely to understand their ways; but I knew it."

"I regret having asked you. Was he angry? Did he not address you as Hamper did?"

"He wasn't overly civil!" Nicholas admitted, resuming the spinning of the penny, this time for his own amusement as well as the children's. "Do not fret. I'm exactly where I was. I'll resume tramping tomorrow. I returned his courtesy in kind. I told him I didn't possess the high regard for him that would compel me to return voluntarily; but you advised me to go, and I am beholden to you."

"You informed him you were sent by me?"

"I don't recall calling you by your name. I don't think I did. I mentioned a woman who knew no better had advised me to investigate if there was a soft spot in his heart."

"And he—?" Margaret pressed.

"Said I was to tell you to mind your own business. That's the longest spin yet, my lads. Those are civilized words compared to what he used toward me. But never mind. We stand where we stood. I'll break stones on the road before I let these little ones freeze."

Margaret placed the squirming Johnnie back onto the dresser. "I apologize for asking you to approach Mr. Thornton. I am disappointed in him."

A faint noise echoed behind her. Both she and Nicholas turned simultaneously to reveal Mr. Thornton standing there, his countenance marked by displeased surprise. Acting on an instant impulse, Margaret stepped past him, offering no words save for a deep, low bow designed to conceal the sudden pallor rising in her cheeks. He bowed equally low in return, then closed the door after her. As she hastened toward Mrs. Boucher's, she heard the sharp clang of the latch, a sound that seemed to complete the measure of her humiliation. He, too, was annoyed to discover her presence.

Yet, beneath his stern exterior, Mr. Thornton harbored a tender sentiment—a "soft place," as Nicholas Higgins had termed it. He possessed a profound pride in guarding it, keeping it sacred and secure, and remaining fiercely jealous of any circumstance threatening its intrusion. While he dreaded exposing this vulnerability, he was equally driven by a desire for all men to recognize his justice. He felt a gnawing guilt for having accorded such a scornful hearing to anyone who had waited, with humble patience, five hours to speak to him. Whether the man had spoken saucily or not mattered little to Mr. Thornton; he actually admired Higgins's boldness and was conscious of his own irritable temper at the time, likely rendering them even-handed. It was the five hours of waiting that struck him most deeply. He had not five hours to spare himself, yet he had sacrificed an hour or two of his rigorous intellectual and physical labor to gather evidence regarding the truth of Higgins's story, the nature of his character, and the tenor of his life. Though he strove to suppress it, he was convinced that everything Higgins had said rang true. Then, conviction descended like a spell, touching the latent tenderness of his heart. The man's patience, the simple generosity of his motive (for he now understood the quarrel between Boucher and Higgins), caused him to forget entirely the cold reasoning of justice and leap over it via a higher instinct. He intended to offer Higgins employment, and he was more irritated by finding Margaret there than by hearing her final words. For then he realized she was the woman who had urged Higgins to approach him, and he dreaded admitting any thought of her as a motive for an action undertaken solely because it was right.

"So that was the lady you spoke of as a woman?" he asked indignantly to Higgins. "You might have told me who she was."
