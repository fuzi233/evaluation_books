Humboldt tells, somewhere in his travels, of stumbling on a knot of Indians huddled round a fire, their skins clotted with grease and paint, crunching ants and kneading clay between their teeth as if it were bread. He confesses—almost in spite of himself—that but for the lesson-book of science, which bade him call them the raw beginning of mankind, he should sooner have believed them the last poor shreds of a nobler people, fallen and perishing. It was an honest temptation; and had he yielded to it outright, he would have spared himself a doctrine which, for all its boldness, has often less fact than fancy, and must shut its ears to a thousand plain witnesses in order to keep its story straight.

Amyas and his comrades were not of the sort to trouble their heads with such new theories. They took the old tale as they found it, and saw in these woodland folk not clever beasts on an upward ladder, but immortal souls astray—men and women, as themselves, yet “taken captive,” and likely enough to be lost, in this world and the next, if no hand brought them light.

And the place they came to seemed at first to plead against despair. Their canoes grounded on a bank where the forest opened into a scattered village—palm-leaf sheds, hammocks slung from trunk to trunk, and, in little clearings, patches of cassava and indigo showing a careful hand. There was a homely air of order: the kind of comfort that comes of knowing where one’s food grows, and tending it day by day in the same damp heat, under the same bird-cry and insect-whirr.

Then the sound began.

On either side of the landing stood four or five stout fellows, each planted behind his instrument: tall drums and long earthen trumpets that swelled into hollow bulbs along their length. The first foot of an Englishman on shore was the signal. Out burst a din of thumps and groans, of hoots and howls, a din that slapped the ear like a wet cloth and made the very leaves seem to shake with pain. The cataract’s own roar had a steadiness and majesty in it; this was the noise of something mean and eager, as if it fed on confusion.

“They be calling on their devil, sir,” muttered Yeo between his teeth.

Cary, wincing, had still wit enough to answer, “Then they’re like to get him; for none else would ever come to listen to such a racket.”

“And mark you,” said Yeo, looking past the instruments, “there’s some feast or sacrifice toward. I’m not overconfident yet.”

“Nonsense,” Amyas returned, with the stubborn cheer of a man who has been hungry and wet too long to be frightened by mere ceremony. “We could kill every soul of them in half an hour, and they know it as well as we.”

Yet there was no denying that something solemn—by their measure of solemnity—was forward. The people were drawn up in two lines along an open space: men in front, women behind, all bedizened as bravely as they could manage with arnotto and indigo, strings of seeds, and feathers that flashed red, blue, and oily green where the sun broke through.

With a yell that seemed to tear itself out of their throats, a figure sprang into the midst—jaguar-skin on his shoulders, tail dragging, teeth grinning, horns set above his brow, black-and-yellow plumes nodding, and in his hand a great rattle.

“Here’s the Piache, the rascal,” Amyas said.

“Ay,” Yeo answered, “in Satan’s livery; and I’ll warrant his work matches his coat.”

“Don’t be afeard, Jack,” Cary called, edging behind Brimblecombe with mock concern. “It’s your trade to tackle him. Go at him boldly and he’ll run.”

The men laughed, and the Piache—who had dressed to strike dread—hung for a moment, as if his own ear misgave him. But impudence was his bread. He strode forward, cracked his rattle over one of the musicians’ heads to buy himself silence, and launched into a long harangue, while Amyas listened with a cigar between his lips as though he were hearing a plea in some Devonshire court.

“What’s the howl about?” Amyas asked their boy-interpreter.

“He asks if you have seen Amalivaca beyond the great water.”

Amyas had been asked that question up and down the rivers—after the mythic civilizer who, so these folk said, had carved the strange marks upon inland cliffs and then gone home again over the sea. As usual, Amyas answered by praising Queen Elizabeth, setting her up as the wonder of the world and the true lady of the sunrise.

The Piache replied at once that she must be one of Amalivaca’s seven daughters—those he carried away, while he broke the legs of the rest so they should not run after him, and left them to people the woods.

“My queen’s legs are not broken,” Amyas returned, amused, “for she is a pattern of grace and activity, and the best dancer in all her realm. But tell your master this: will you give us cassava bread, and let us rest peaceably on this island awhile, before we go on to fight the clothed men beyond the mountains?”

At that the Piache began capering and turning head over heels with much howling, then beckoned them to follow. The Indians were unarmed and in high good humour; so Amyas went on, his men at his heels, their ears still buzzing from the racket.

The Piache stopped at a hut whose door had been carefully closed. He dropped to all fours like a beaten cur, crawled up, and began whining to someone within.

“Ask what he’s at, boy,” Amyas said.

The lad questioned the old cacique who had come with them, and got for answer that the Piache was consulting the Daughter of the Sun.

“Here’s our mare’s nest at last,” Cary whispered, as the Piache’s whines rose to screams and wild gestures, then to violent convulsions—foam at the mouth, eyes rolling, his whole body jerking as if a cord pulled it—till he suddenly sank and lay as one dead.

“As good as a stage play,” Cary added.

“The devil’s played his part,” Jack Brimblecombe said gravely; “and now, by all play-rules, Vice should come on.”

“And a fair Vice it’ll be, I warrant,” Cary murmured, leaning his head to listen. “A right sweet Iniquity, Jack. Hark!”

From the hut’s darkness floated a low, sweet song. At once the village bowed, faces to earth or hands, as if the very air had grown holy. The Englishmen, too, fell still—astonished—because the voice was not shrill and broken like an Indian’s, but round and clear and rich as a European’s; and it moved with such compass and surety that more than one of those rough wanderers, who could hold his part in a psalm at home, felt his throat tighten. The melody rose and fell like a bird’s call across water—almost without words, yet perfect in its turnings. It did not fight the cataract’s thunder; it threaded through it, and made the roar seem for a moment like the deep drone beneath a choir.

At last came one triumphant burst, sharp enough to make every ear ring—and then dead silence.

The Piache sprang up as if he had never been still, and began preaching again at Amyas.

“Tell the howling villain to make an end,” Amyas said. “His tune won’t hold after that last note.”

The boy translated, grinning: the Piache declared that the Daughter of the Sun accepted the strangers as friends; her friends were theirs, her foes theirs.

A scream of delight went up, and Amyas, rolling a fresh tobacco leaf in a strip of plantain with the neat fingers of habit, answered as if he were bargaining for ale at an inn.

“Then let her give us cassava,” he said, and lit his cigar.

The hut-door opened. The Indians flung themselves flat.

Out came the fair apparition the English had seen before upon the island—only now clothed in feather-robes and plumes of every hue the forest can lend: scarlet like fresh arnotto, blue like indigo stain, yellow as maize, black with green sheen, and white like her own strange skin. She walked slowly, stately as one who has never been contradicted, her gaze sweeping over her prostrate adorers with a cold pride; then she pointed, with arms that moved as gracefully as reeds in a stream, toward trees and gardens and huts, making plain without a syllable that all they had was at Amyas’s service. Taking his hand, she lifted it gently to her forehead.

At that sign a shout of rapture broke out, and as she withdrew again into her hut the crowd pressed round the English, laughing, touching, staring—at swords, at bows and blow-guns, at the wild-beast trophies on their shoulders. Women hurried off and returned with fruit and flowers, with cassava cakes hot from the ashes—white bread of the roots they had grated, washed, pressed of their bitter juice, and baked thin upon flat stones—and, to Amyas’s great anxiety, calabashes of a drink that smelled sweet and sour together, like over-ripe fruit warmed in sun. They sat under the trees and feasted, while the drums and trumpets resumed their hideous din, and lithe youths and girls danced uncouth dances whose stamping and shrill cries set Brimblecombe’s teeth on edge and made Yeo’s brow darker every moment.

Between bites Amyas could not help tasting the cassava as a sailor tastes bread after long want—dry, a little smoky, with that faint, clean bitterness which tells of the poison washed away by patience. He watched the hands that had made it: the women’s fingers moving quick and certain, as if kneading and pressing were prayers; the smell of warm root and crushed leaf mixing with sweat, damp earth, and river-mist. Yet the drink, and the noise, and the leering dance set him on edge. He was willing enough to get back to his island while his men were still sober, and before the village’s good humour curdled.

So there were many leave-takings and promises of return on the morrow, and the English paddled back to their island-fortress, racking their wits as to who and what the mysterious maid might be.

Amyas, lying afterwards in his hammock, had shaped his own answer: she must be of the lost Inca blood—perhaps descended from that fair girl, wife of the Inca Manco, whom Pizarro had once stripped, scourged, and shot with arrows, not to win a war but to torture a fugitive king’s heart. The thought clung like smoke in the nostrils.

That evening, as always, they gathered for prayer—hardly a day had passed since they left England when they had not done so. The service ended, they must needs sing a psalm, and then a catch or two, as Englishmen will when food is in the belly and danger for the hour seems asleep. Till the moon climbed high, twenty mellow voices rang above the cataract’s unwearied thunder. The island’s small noises—crickets, frogs, the occasional slap of a fish—were lost under the great continual sound; yet to ears that had lived with it for weeks, the torrent was like a steady bass, and anything new cut through it at once.

Once or twice, in the midst of a tune, they fancied an answer out of the darkness, but took little heed until Cary, who had gone apart, came back and touched Amyas’s arm.

“The sweet Iniquity is mimicking us, lad.”

They went to the river-brink. Across the black water the forest stood up like a wall. There, plain now, floated that same clear voice, repeating snatches of the airs they had sung—true and sure, as if the singer had learned them long ago and only waited to show her skill. Strange and solemn it was: the men’s deep notes on the island answered from the unseen wood by sweet treble, as if the dark itself had a tongue. Amyas and Cary stood a long while, listening, staring out across eddies that swirled down golden in the moonlight; but they saw nothing but shadow, and the steady shine where water turned.

After a while the voice ceased. They went back to sleep, and dreamed of Incas and nightingales.

They visited the village again next day, and every day for a week and more. The maiden appeared rarely, and when she did she kept her distance—haughty as a queen, granting favour by the very act of withholding it.

As soon as Amyas could make himself understood a little better, he questioned the cacique about her. The old man made an owl’s face at the name, and shook his head with mysterious warning. She was a child of the Sun; it was enough. The less said, the safer.

“Tell him,” Cary whispered wickedly to the boy, “that we are children of the Sun by his first wife, and sent to see how they’ve used our step-sister; for the Sun can’t spy all their tricks down here, the trees be so thick. If he won’t tell, all their cassava shall be blighted.”

“Will, Will, don’t play with lying,” Amyas said sharply; yet the threat had its effect. The cacique, frightened, took them in his canoe a full mile down-stream, as if afraid the maiden might overhear through leaf and water, and there, in a kind of rhythmic chant—like a tale that must be remembered by sound because words shift and die—he told them what he knew.

Many moons ago—how many he could not tell—his tribe had been a mighty people and dwelt in Papamene, until the Spaniards drove them forth. Wandering northward, far away under the mountain spurs where Cotopaxi burned like a lamp against the sky, they found this fair creature alone in the forest, no bigger than a child of seven. Her skin was white; her limbs delicate; her eyes had a look that did not belong to the woods. The simple folk took her for a god and led her with trembling joy. When they found she was human, their wonder hardly lessened. How could such a tender thing live among jaguar and snake? She must be protected; she must be of the Sun; she must belong to that mighty Inca race whose dreadful fall had travelled even into these green solitudes. The elders had heard that many Incas had haunted for years the eastern slopes of the Andes—about Ucalayi and Maranon—and that one day they would rise again, when bearded white men should come over the sea to restore them to their throne.

So, by the conjuror’s command, they tended her with royal honours to win the Sun’s favour, and the Inca’s favour, in that day of coming glory. As she grew, she became to them not only a fetish to be feared and adored, but a prophetess: more prudent in council than their elders, valiant in war, cunning in the chase. Her songs—those strange sweet songs—were full, they said, of wisdom about birds and beasts and flowers and rivers, taught her by the Sun and the Good Spirit from above. Unmarried she remained, partly from scorn of all Indian youths, but chiefly because the conjuror had declared it profane to mingle with the Sun’s race. He had given her a hut near his own, where she was served in state, and where she gave oracular answers to the questions he put to her—answers which, no doubt, kept his cassava-bin and coca-pouch pleasantly full.

Cary, when the tale was done, said drily what any man might have thought: that the conjuror “made a very good thing of it.” But Amyas was silent, his mind running, like the river itself, back into the south—back to rumours of the Inca remnant, to the vexatious thought that he might have been close to them and yet could not follow with his diminished band. If only he could speak with the maiden herself. If only he could carry home some hint that would be worth gold to a future venture. Manoa had not yet let go its hold upon him. If he got safe home, there were gallant gentlemen enough—Raleigh’s face rose at once before him—who would try again, not by these upper waters, but by the mouth of the Orinoco.

They paddled back while the cacique begged them, in his poor trembling way, to tell the Sun in their daily prayers how well the Omaguas had treated his descendant, and not to take her away, lest the Sun forget them and their manioc and fruit ripen no more.

Amyas had no wish to linger longer than needful to bring up the sick men left behind toward the Orinoco. That, he knew, would take months and carry danger in every mile.

Cary offered at once to go, if he could have half a dozen men and an Indian crew to help work the canoe. The last was hardest. The Omaguas feared the fierce Guahibas whose country must be passed; and every tribe, as Amyas had learned, looked on any tribe of another tongue as natural enemies—hated, and made only to be destroyed when met. The English, in their fashion, called this too the work of the devil, the divider and accuser; and perhaps they were right—only, as any man should, Amyas might do well to watch that, in hunting the devil in others, he did not make room for him in his own breast.

Whether pride or shyness had kept the maiden aloof, she conquered it after a while—perhaps from mere woman’s curiosity, perhaps from sheer hunger for something new in a life so narrow as a forest village, where the days were measured by pounding roots and the nights by the same drum-thump and frog-cry. She made it plain, however, that though all the bearded men might be great, none should be her companion but Amyas.

Before a month was past she was often hunting with him in the neighbouring woods, with a train of chosen nymphs—girls who, emboldened by her, had begun to spurn the dusky suitors round them. To the English it was proof that they were near the land of the famous Amazons, of whom Indians told such tales. Amyas, for his part, was sure that in her he saw the tradition of the Virgins of the Sun, and the austere rule of Peruvian superstition. Had not George of Spires and Jeronimo Ortal, fifty years before, found convents of the Sun upon these very waters?

So a friendship grew—harmless enough at first—and soon turned to practical use. No sooner did she understand that Amyas needed a crew than she went to the Piache, called the tribe together, retired to her hut, and began that singing which held them like a spell. When she came out, it was given out (unless the Piache lied) that the song had been a command: young men must go with Cary, under pain of the sovereign displeasure of an evil spirit whose name was not to be spoken. The argument, being of the sort that bites quickest into fear, succeeded at once, and the canoe set out on its perilous errand.

John Brimblecombe doubted much whether any venture begun under such patronage could prosper. Amyas too, though he used what came to hand, disliked the jugglery, and told Ayacanora it had been better to say the deed was good, and pleasing to the Good Spirit.

“Ah!” she answered, with a child’s simplicity and a woman’s shrewdness. “They know better than that. The Good Spirit—he big and lazy. He smiles, and takes no trouble. But the little bad spirit—he so busy—here, and there, and everywhere.” And she fluttered her pretty hands up and down among the leaves. “He the useful one to have for friend.”

The Piache approved mightily—as became his trade. Once he told Brimblecombe, with a sneer sharp as a thorn, that he was a meddlesome fellow to teach the Indians that the Good Spirit cared for them. “For if they ask the Good Spirit for what they want, who bring me cassava and coca to keep the bad spirit quiet?”

The argument—felt by devil’s priests in all ages to be very forcible—did not stop Jack’s preaching; and still less the morning and evening service in the island camp. That service, with its ordered psalmody, drew Indians in such numbers, lured by the strange clean harmonies cutting through the cataract’s perpetual roar, that the Piache saw his offerings dwindle. The forest folk, who lived by sound as much as sight, seemed soothed by the English voices—notes that did not shriek and pound like the trumpets, but moved in measured courses like water in a channel. The conjuror raged, and vowed to end Jack’s Gospel with a poisoned arrow.

Blinded (as Jack would have phrased it) by his master, he even took it into his head to tell Ayacanora—thinking her still the partner of his tithes—of his plan. He was amazed to receive a box on the ear and a storm of abuse. After which Ayacanora went straight to Amyas, told him all, and proposed that the Piache should be thrown to the alligators, and Jack set up in his place; declaring that whatever the bearded men said must be true, and whoever plotted against them should die.

Jack, however, forgave his foe with a steadiness that was more than pride, and preached on—with fresh zeal, but with little success. For the conjuror, though his chief treasure had slipped him, had still a reserve: a certain holy trumpet hidden in a cave on the neighbouring hills, which no woman might look on under pain of death. It was known—had been known for generations—that unless that trumpet, after fastings and flagellations and solemn rites, was blown by night through the woods, the palm-trees would bear no fruit. So great was its fame that neighbouring tribes sent, in season, to hire both trumpet and blower, paying in precious trumpery, that they might share in its supposed power to fatten and ripen.

One day the Piache proclaimed aloud that, because of the Omaguas’ impiety, he would go to a neighbouring tribe of more religious mind; and taking with him the precious instrument, he would leave their palms to blight and themselves to the evil spirit.

Then the village’s mood turned as swiftly as milk in heat. There was wailing—women’s cries rising above the river—and wrath. Jack’s words were good words, they allowed; but what was Gospel compared to a trumpet that promised fruit? The rascal saw his advantage and poured out a furious harangue against the heretic strangers. As he maddened, his hearers maddened. The savage nature, capricious as a child’s, flashed out into suspicion and rage. Women screamed; men scowled and ran to their huts for bows and blow-guns.

The case grew sudden and deadly. Amyas had with him no more than a dozen men, and they carried only their swords. The Indian men could muster near a hundred. Amyas forbade his men either to draw or to retreat; but poisoned arrows are weapons before which the boldest may well quail, and more than one cheek whitened that had seldom been pale in England or on the Spanish Main.

“It is God’s quarrel, sirs all,” Jack Brimblecombe said, his voice steady in the thickening tumult. “Let Him defend the right.”

As he spoke, from Ayacanora’s hut rose her magic song—low at first, then gathering strength—quivering aloft among the green heights of the forest, threading through drum-thump, cataract-roar, and angry cries like a bright blade drawn in the dark.
