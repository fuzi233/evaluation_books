Despite my godmother's natural cheerfulness and her determined effort to entertain us, the evening at La Terrasse lacked true joy until the wild howl of the winter night was pierced by the signals of arrival. Often, when women and girls sit warm by cozy firesides, their hearts and imaginations are forcibly divorced from their physical comfort. They are driven out into the dark, forced to brave the elements, to stand at lonely gates amidst blinding storms, waiting anxiously for fathers, sons, or husbands to return home.

At last, Father and Son arrived at the château: Count de Bassompierre that night accompanied Dr. Bretton. I cannot say which of the three heard the horses first; the severity of the weather compelled us to rush downstairs to meet them. However, they warned us to keep our distance. Both riders were encased in snow—"two mountains of white." Upon seeing their condition, Mrs. Bretton immediately ordered them to the kitchen, strictly forbidding them under penalty of danger from stepping onto her carpeted stairs until they had shed their "Old Christmas" disguises. Yet, drawn by curiosity, we followed them into the kitchen. It was a large, old Dutch kitchen, picturesque and pleasant.

There, the little white Countess danced in circles around her equally white sire, clapping her hands and crying, "Papa, papa, you look like an enormous Polar bear!"

The bear shook himself, and the little sprite fled from the frozen spray. She quickly returned, laughing and eager to help remove the arctic disguise. Finally emerging from his "dreadnought," the Count threatened to overwhelm her with it as if it were an avalanche.

"Come, then," she said, bending to invite the fall. When he playfully raised it above her head, she bounded out of reach like a little chamois. Her movements possessed a supple softness and velvet grace akin to a kitten; her laugh was clearer than the ring of silver and crystal. As she took her sire's cold hands to rub them and stood on tiptoe to kiss his lips, a halo of loving delight seemed to shine around her. The grave and reverend seigneur looked down upon her as men look upon the apple of their eye.

"Mrs. Bretton," he said, "what am I to do with this daughter or daughterling of mine? She neither grows in wisdom nor in stature. Do you not find her nearly as much a child as she was ten years ago?"

"She cannot be more the child than this great boy of mine," replied Mrs. Bretton, who was currently in conflict with her son regarding a change of dress he resolutely opposed. He leaned against the Dutch dresser, laughing and keeping her at arm's length.

"Come, mamma," he said, "as a compromise to secure both inward and outward warmth, let us have a Christmas wassail-cup and toast Old England right here on the hearth."

While the Count stood by the fire and Paulina Mary danced happily within the spacious kitchen, Mrs. Bretton instructed Martha to spice and heat the wassail bowl. Pouring the drink into a Bretton flagon, it was served piping hot via a small silver vessel I recognized as Graham's christening cup.

"Here's to Auld Lang Syne!" cried the Count, holding the gleaming cup aloft. Turning to Mrs. Bretton, he began:

"We twa ha' paidlet i' the burn
Fra morning sun till dine,
But seas between us braid ha' roared
Sin' auld lang syne.

"And surely ye'll be your pint-stoup,
And surely I'll be mine;
And we'll taste a cup o' kindness yet
For auld lang syne."

"Scotch! Scotch!" cried Paulina. "Papa is talking Scotch; and Scotch he is, partly. We are Home and de Bassompierre, Caledonian and Gallic."

"And is that a Scotch reel you are dancing, you Highland fairy?" asked her father. "Mrs. Bretton, a green ring will soon be growing in the middle of your kitchen. I would not wager that she is entirely *can*ny; she is a strange little mortal."

"Tell Lucy to dance with me, papa; there is Lucy Snowe."

Mr. Home—still possessing as much of the plain Mr. Home as of the proud Count de Bassompierre—held out his hand to me kindly. "He remembers me well," he said. "Even if his own memory were less trustworthy, my name is so often on his daughter's lips, and he has listened to so many long tales about me, that I should seem like an old acquaintance."

Everyone had tasted the wassail except Paulina, whose *pas de fée*, or fantasy dance, no one dared interrupt to offer such a profane draught. Yet she was not to be overlooked.

"Let me taste," she told Graham as he placed the cup on the dresser shelf out of her reach.

Mrs. Bretton and Mr. Home were now conversing. Dr. John had not failed to observe the fairy's dance; he had watched it and liked it. Beyond the softness and beauty of her movements, which appealed greatly to his love of grace, the ease she displayed in his mother's house charmed him, setting him at ease himself. Again, she seemed a child to him; again, almost his playmate. I wondered how he would address her; I had not yet seen him speak to her. His first words revealed that the old days of "little Polly" had been recalled by the evening's childlike light-heartedness.

"Your ladyship wishes for the tankard?"

"I think I said so. I think I intimated as much."

"Couldn't consent to a step of the kind on any account. Sorry for it, but couldn't do it."

"Why? I am quite well now; it can't break my collar-bone again, or dislocate my shoulder. Is it wine?"

"No; nor dew."

"I don't want dew; I don't like dew: but what is it?"

"Ale—strong ale—old October; brewed, perhaps, when I was born."

"It must be curious: is it good?"

"Excessively good."

He took it down, administered a second dose of this mighty elixir to himself, expressed extreme contentment in his mischievous eyes, and solemnly replaced the cup.

"I should like a little," said Paulina, looking up. "I never had any 'old October'; is it sweet?"

"Perilously sweet," said Graham.

She continued to look up with the countenance of a child longing for some prohibited dainty. Finally, the Doctor relented, took the cup down, and indulged her by letting her taste from his hand. His eyes, always expressive of pleasurable feelings, luminously and smilingly acknowledged the gratification, prolonging the moment by regulating the cup's position so that only a drop at a time reached her rosy, sipping lips.

"A little more—a little more," she said petulantly, touching his hand with her forefinger to make him incline the cup more generously. "It smells of spice and sugar, but I can't taste it; your wrist is so stiff, and you are so stingy."

He indulged her, whispering gravely: "Don't tell my mother or Lucy; they wouldn't approve."

"Nor do I," she said, shifting her tone and manner the instant she had fairly assayed the beverage, as if it had acted like a disenchanting draught undoing a wizard's spell. "I find it anything but sweet; it is bitter and hot, and takes away my breath. Your old October was only desirable while forbidden. Thank you, no more."

With a slight, careless yet graceful bend, she glided away from him and rejoined her father.

I believe she spoke the truth: the child of seven was in the girl of seventeen.

Graham looked after her somewhat baffled and puzzled. His eye remained on her for the rest of the evening, but she seemed not to notice him.

As we ascended to the drawing-room for tea, she took her father's arm; her natural place appeared to be at his side. Her eyes and ears were dedicated to him. He and Mrs. Bretton were the chief talkers of our little party, and Paulina was their best listener, attending closely to everything, prompting the repetition of traits and adventures.

"And where were you at such a time, papa? And what did you say then? And tell Mrs. Bretton what happened on that occasion." Thus she drew him out.

She did not again yield to any effervescence of glee; the infantine sparkle was exhaled for the night. She was soft, thoughtful, and docile. It was pretty to see her bid goodnight; her manner toward Graham was touched with dignity. In her very slight smile and quiet bow spoke the Countess, and Graham could not but look grave and bend responsively. I saw he hardly knew how to blend in his mind the image of the dancing fairy and the delicate dame.

The next day, when we were all assembled round the breakfast table, shivering and fresh from the morning's cold ablutions, Mrs. Bretton decreed that no one should leave the house unless forced by dire necessity. Indeed, egress seemed next to impossible; the drift darkened the lower panes of the windows, and looking out, one saw the sky and air vexed and dim, the wind and snow in angry conflict.
