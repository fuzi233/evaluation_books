I lay beneath a tree as the children, one by one or in small clusters, climbed to their nests for the night. Though weary myself, I remained awake, certain Lona would come—she had not yet bid me goodnight.

When I first saw her again, I was struck by her resemblance to the princess, and I knew at once she was the daughter Adam had spoken of. Yet where Lilith’s beauty was harsh and dazzling, Lona’s was softened by innocence, deepened by a mother’s tenderness. *She must be with the child,* I thought—the one belonging to the woman I had met fleeing, the woman Lona had already told me was *not half mother enough.*

At last she came, settling beside me with a quiet joy, stroking my face and hands before she began to recount all that had happened since I left. Moonlight flickered through the leaves, illuminating her face—so full of thought, of care that only made her love more radiant. How such a child could have sprung from such a mother was beyond me—but then, she had not one parent, nor two, but *three.*

She drew me to her by the part of me most like herself, and I loved her as one who, no matter how she grew, would only ever become more of a child. I realized now that I had loved her even as I left her, and the hope of seeing her again had been my greatest comfort. Every word she spoke went straight to my heart, purifying it like truth itself.

She told me that after my departure, the giants had grown more hostile, half-convinced of the Little Ones’ existence but still blind to their presence. Sometimes the giants would stamp and rage, sensing—or imagining—some sign of them, while the children stood nearby, laughing silently at their fury.

But soon the giants’ anger took a darker turn: they began destroying the fruit trees the Little Ones depended on. This forced their mother to act. She set the sharpest-eared among them to listen at night and learned that the giants believed *I* was hiding nearby, waiting to strike them down in their sleep.

So she devised a plan. The Little Ones would leave the valley, retreating into the forest—far enough to escape the giants’ notice, but close enough to return for fruit under cover of darkness. The forest had little undergrowth for shelter, but if birds could live there, why not the Little Ones? They could learn from the wild creatures, take refuge in the treetops, build nests where the earth offered no hollows.

She proposed it, and they agreed eagerly. Already they could climb like squirrels; they had watched birds build nests countless times. The forest trees, though vast, did not frighten them—they stretched toward the sky, their branches like open arms, inviting. Perhaps, in the highest boughs, they might even find the bird that laid baby-eggs, the one that tumbled them down when they were ripe!

And so they became creatures of the canopy, as at home among the leaves as the birds themselves. The giants, finding no trace of them, decided they had fled—and soon forgot them entirely.

Lona asked if I had noticed how many of the children had grown. I admitted I had not, but I could believe it. She said their minds had grown too—a comfort, for at first their increasing size had alarmed her.

They gathered fruit in the twilight or under the moon—hours the giants feared, as if darkness and moonlight were poison to them. But soon the Little Ones learned that night-picked fruit spoiled faster, and they began to wonder: why not drive the giants away instead?

Already they knew the forest’s animals—had made friends with horses, elephants, even bears, addressing each as *brother* or *sister.* (Only caterpillars were left alone—until they emerged as butterflies, at which point the children greeted them as *Sister Butterfly,* as if their transformation were a kind of repentance.)

One evening, as they went to gather fruit, they stumbled upon a woman seated on the ground, a baby in her lap—the same woman I had met on the road to Bulika. Mistaking her for a giantess who had stolen one of their own, they swarmed her in a whirl of small fists and feet. But when she surrendered the child without resistance—kissing her as tenderly as they would have—they realized she was no enemy. They brought her fruit, offered her kindness.

The woman, afraid to return to Bulika (where the princess would surely punish her for the leopardess’s injury), chose to stay. Here, at least, her child would be safe.

The woman knew the legend—that the princess feared a child destined to destroy her. The people of Bulika knew it too, though none guessed the horrors she committed to preserve her youth. The woman saw an opportunity: if she could rouse the Little Ones to attack Bulika, she might slip away in the chaos, return to her husband, and vanish.

She began by teaching the children to throw stones—a game they took to with surprising skill. Soon they were pelting targets with deadly accuracy.

Then, while gathering fruit, they found me—asleep, guarded by giants. They rushed home, plotted, returned with elephants and horses, overwhelmed my captors, and carried me off.

That night, Lona and I spoke for hours—of the children’s growth, of what it might mean. Her wisdom astonished me; had I been more of a child myself, perhaps it would not have.

Near dawn, a fluttering stirred me awake. The Little Ones were descending from their nests, scattering into the forest.

*“They’re gathering stones,”* Lona explained. *“If the giants come, they’ll rain them down from the trees.”*

She spoke with pride. *“Wait until you see them throw.”*

Then she told me of the night before last—of two leopardesses, one white, one spotted, locked in battle beneath the trees. The children, thinking it play, had rushed to pet them—until the white one roared, scattering them like birds. By morning, only Whitey remained.

*“I know them both,”* I said. *“Spotty hates the children. Whitey loves them—she only scared them to keep them safe.”*

The Little Ones returned, noisy with triumph, lugging stones. They climbed back to their nests, clumsy with their burdens, and slept at once.

Lona withdrew to her tree. I stayed where I was, comforted by the thought that Whitey might still linger nearby.

At sunrise, the giants came—straggling in small groups until over a hundred had gathered. The children slept on, and I dared not wake them.

But then a giant stumbled over me. I struck him, and at once a storm of stones hailed down—every one missing me, every one striking true. The giants fell, roaring, until the forest floor was littered with them.

The children descended like monkeys, mounted the waiting elephants, and rode over the groaning giants. When their noise grew tiresome, the elephants silenced them with a few swift blows of their trunks.

By the next morning, the giants were gone—vanished. They never returned to the forest. The Little Ones moved deeper into the orchard valley, and the war was over.
