The twilight of that evening descended upon my lodgings with a heaviness that seemed to press against the glass, breaking the solitude I had cultivated. It was then that Mr. Bruff arrived, a visitor whose presence usually carried the weight of legal certainty, yet tonight, the air around him felt thin, stripped of its customary vigor. The lawyer's demeanor had undergone a subtle but profound metamorphosis; the confident stride of the advocate was replaced by a tentative gait, and the sharp edge of his wit dulled into a somber resignation. When he extended his hand to shake mine—a gesture unprecedented in our history—it was done in a silence so thick it seemed to swallow the ambient noise of the room.

"Are you returning to Hampstead?" I ventured, offering a fragment of speech to bridge the chasm of his hesitation.

"I have just departed Hampstead," he replied, his voice low and measured. "Mr. Franklin, I acknowledge that you have finally pierced the veil of mystery. Yet, if I had possessed the foresight to calculate the toll exacted by this revelation, I would have chosen to keep you in the shadows rather than drag you into this light."

"Have you seen Rachel?"

"I returned her to Portland Place shortly after arriving here. It was inconceivable to allow her to traverse the streets alone in a carriage following such an encounter. While I cannot hold you accountable—given that you witnessed her under my roof and with my explicit consent—for the visceral shock this ill-fated meeting has wrought upon her, I must insist on preventing a recurrence of such trauma. She is young, possessing a spirit of iron resolve, and with time and rest, she will endure. However, I require your solemn assurance that you will not impede her recuperation. Can I trust that you will make no further attempts to seek her out without my express sanction?"

"After the suffering she has endured, and the agony I have known," I stated, meeting his gaze, "you may rely upon my word."

"Do you give me your promise?"

"You have my promise."

Relief washed over Mr. Bruff's features, softening the lines of anxiety etched into his brow. He removed his hat, placing it on the table, and drew his chair closer, invading my personal space with a newfound intimacy born of desperation.

"That is settled," he murmured. "Now, let us address the horizon—your future, specifically. In my assessment, the extraordinary trajectory this affair has taken coalesces into a singular reality. First, we are certain that Rachel disclosed the entire truth to you, as clearly as language permits. Second, although we acknowledge that a catastrophic error exists somewhere in the tapestry of events, we cannot fault her for believing you guilty based on the evidence of her senses. Her perception was reinforced by circumstances that, on their surface, pointed with undeniable force against you."

At this juncture, I interjected. "I do not blame Rachel," I clarified. "I merely lament that she could not summon the courage to articulate her suspicions more plainly at the critical moment."

"You might as well mourn that Rachel is not someone else," Mr. Bruff countered sharply. "Even then, I doubt if a woman of refinement, whose heart was fixed on marriage to you, could bring herself to accuse you openly of theft. Regardless, it was not in Rachel's nature to do so. In matters entirely distinct from your own plight—which nonetheless placed her in a predicament strikingly analogous to hers regarding you—I am privy to knowledge that she was driven by a motive identical to the one governing her actions in your case. Furthermore, as she confessed to me during our journey to town this evening, had she spoken plainly, she would have disbelieved your denial then with the same certainty she holds today. What response can you offer to that? There is none. Come, Mr. Franklin! I admit my initial interpretation of the case was flawed, yet under current conditions, my counsel retains value. I warn you plainly: we squander our vitality and fracture our minds in vain if we attempt to retrace and untangle this horrific complexity from the beginning. Let us shut our eyes resolutely to the events of last year at Lady Verinder's estate; instead, let us focus on what we *can* uncover in the future, rather than dwelling on what remains hidden in the past."

"Surely you overlook," I argued, "that the entirety of this ordeal is fundamentally rooted in the past, particularly concerning me?"

"Answer this," Mr. Bruff retorted, his tone sharpening. "Is the Moonstone the catalyst for all this misery—or is it not?"

"It is—naturally."

"Agreed. What do we posit occurred to the Moonstone when it was transported to London?"

"It was pledged to Mr. Luker."

"We know you are not the individual who pledged it. Do we know who did?"

"No."

"Where do we currently locate the Moonstone?"

"Safekeeping with Mr. Luker's bankers."

"Precisely. Observe closely. We are now in June. By the month's end—a span of approximately a year from the date of the pledge—the person who pawned the gem may be prepared to redeem it. Should redemption occur, Mr. Luker, bound by the terms of his agreement, must retrieve the diamond from the bankers' custody. Under these specific circumstances, I propose stationing a watcher at the bank as the month concludes, identifying the individual to whom Mr. Luker returns the Moonstone. Do you perceive the logic?"

I conceded, albeit with reluctance, that the concept was novel, at the very least.

"It is Mr. Murthwaite's conception as much as my own," Mr. Bruff noted. "It never would have crossed my mind absent a conversation we shared some time ago. If Mr. Murthwaite is correct, the Indians will likely be vigilant at the bank near the month's end, and grave consequences may ensue. Whether those consequences affect us personally is secondary; what matters is that they aid us in capturing the enigmatic figure who pawned the diamond. That individual, regardless of the mechanism, bears sole responsibility for your precarious standing; only they can restore your honor in Rachel's eyes."

"I cannot deny," I admitted, "that your proposed strategy addresses the dilemma with audacity, ingenuity, and freshness. However—"

"But you harbor an objection?"

"Yes. My grievance is that your proposal necessitates waiting."

"Granted. According to my calculations, it demands patience for approximately two weeks, plus or minus. Is that duration insurmountable?"

"To me, Mr. Bruff, in my current predicament, it feels like an eternity. My existence becomes unbearable unless I take immediate action to vindicate my character."

"Well, well, I comprehend your sentiment. Have you considered what steps you might take?"

"I have contemplated consulting Sergeant Cuff."

"He has retired from the police force. Expecting his assistance is futile."

"I know precisely where to locate him; I intend to try."

"Try," Mr. Bruff said after a pause, weighing the option. "Given the extraordinary dimensions the case has assumed since Sergeant Cuff's tenure, you *might* reignite his interest in the inquiry. Attempt it, and inform me of the outcome. Meanwhile," he continued, rising to his feet, "if you fail to make discoveries between now and the end of the month, am I authorized to attempt, on my own behalf, surveillance at the bank?"

"Certainly," I answered—"unless I absolve you of the necessity to conduct this experiment during the interim."

Mr. Bruff smiled, a fleeting expression of camaraderie, and picked up his hat.

"Instruct Sergeant Cuff," he said, "that *I* declare the discovery of the truth hinges upon the identification of the person who pawned the Diamond. Let me know what the Sergeant's experience dictates regarding this premise."

Thus, our paths diverged.

Dawn broke gray and chill as I departed for the small town of Dorking, the destination of Sergeant Cuff's retirement, as directed by Betteredge. At the local hotel, I secured the precise instructions for locating the Sergeant's cottage. It lay accessible via a tranquil by-road, situated a short distance from the town center, nestled securely within its own private domain of garden land. A sturdy brick wall fortified the rear and flanks, while a tall, dense quickset hedge shielded the front. The gate, adorned above with intricately painted trellis work, was fastened tight. After ringing the bell, I pressed my eye against the lattice, greeted immediately by a riot of blooms—Cuff's favorite flowers flourishing wildly, spilling over the threshold and peering in through the windows. Removed from the grim crimes and labyrinthine mysteries of the metropolis, the renowned thief-taker appeared to be languishing in the final, indulgent years of his life, suffocating beneath a canopy of roses!

An elderly woman, respectable in appearance, opened the gate to receive me, and in doing so, extinguished every hope I had nurtured regarding Sergeant Cuff's availability. He had departed mere hours prior, embarking on a journey to Ireland.

"Did he travel there for business?" I inquired.
