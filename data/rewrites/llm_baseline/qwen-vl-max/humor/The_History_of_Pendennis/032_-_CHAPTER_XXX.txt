Let’s talk about the Inns of Court—those ancient, creaking fortresses of legal learning where time seems to have taken a holiday and left behind only dust, damp, and the faint echo of forgotten arguments. They’re like museums of old customs, where men still wear wigs in court (though not always on their heads), and where the very air smells of parchment, pipe smoke, and mild regret.

Now, don’t get me wrong: I’m not saying these places are modern marvels. On the contrary, they’re architectural relics—buildings that were erected before indoor plumbing was invented, before anyone thought cleanliness might be next to godliness, and certainly before anyone dreamed of shower-baths. Imagine this: you're a barrister, earning your living by interpreting statutes written in Latin by people who probably never took a bath themselves. And yet, you live in a room so small it could fit inside a wardrobe, paying rent that would buy you a decent cottage with a garden and a goat.

Meanwhile, down in Spitalfields, a poor mechanic has running water, a cistern, and a sink—things that seem almost scandalous when compared to the noble lawyer’s daily ritual of fetching water from a jug carried up by a laundress who also happens to be responsible for mopping the stairs and occasionally spilling her bucket over the head of Mr. Grump, a gentleman of more than half a century who lives directly beneath Warrington and Pendennis.

Mr. Grump is a man of principle—though perhaps not much else. He believes shower-baths are “an absurd, newfangled, dandified folly,” especially since one of them, installed by Warrington and Pendennis, leaks through the ceiling into his chambers like a disgruntled ghost. Every morning he wakes to the sound of water gushing above him, as if some divine punishment were being administered via hot tap. He curses the laundress, the builders, the entire notion of hygiene, and once said publicly that if he’d known how wet things would get, he’d have stayed in the country and become a shepherd.

But let’s not dwell too long on Mr. Grump. Let’s look around the Temple itself—the Upper Temple, to be precise—where history hangs thick in the air like bad breath after a feast. The walls are adorned with coats of arms belonging to knights, baronets, lords, and gentlemen whose names now mean nothing to anyone except those who study heraldry or enjoy confusing genealogies. You’d think one of them, at least, might have had the decency to install a proper water system for future generations. But no. Instead, we have Pump Court and Fountain Court—names that suggest refreshment and joy—but where no bencher has ever been seen diving into the fountain, nor any junior counsel cooling off during a particularly dull case.

Still, there’s something oddly charming about the place. It’s like an old boarding house run by ghosts. The students come and go, the benchers sit at high table, and everyone eats soup, meat, tarts, and port wine under portraits of judges who probably never ate anything but cold beef and black coffee.

And here’s where our hero, Arthur Pendennis, enters the scene—not as a scholar, nor a saint, but as a young man trying to find his way in life without quite knowing what direction he’s heading. He’s not exactly studying law with religious devotion—he’s reading, yes, but mostly because it keeps him out of trouble. His uncle thinks he’s turning over a new leaf, writing home proudly that “the lad has sown his wild oats and is becoming quite steady.” Which is true—except that the oats he’s sowing are mostly in taverns, and the steadiness comes from having a good friend named Warrington.

Warrington is the kind of fellow who makes you feel warm just looking at him. He wears clothes that look like they’ve survived several wars, but he’s got a smile that could melt ice. He’s a baronet’s son, which gives him a certain social standing, but he doesn’t care about it. He prefers smoking pipes, telling stories, and laughing at the absurdities of human nature. When he first meets Pendennis in Hall, they recognize each other instantly—not because they’ve met before, but because they both went to Boniface College and thrashed the same bargeman named Bill Simes.

“Stunning Warrington,” says Pendennis, delighted.
“Yes,” replies Warrington, “I recollect you in your freshman’s term. But you appear to have cut me out.”

They become fast friends immediately. And soon enough, Pendennis moves into Lamb Court, sharing rooms with Warrington, leaving behind the sterile elegance of St. James’s and embracing the rougher pleasures of bachelor life: cheap dinners, late-night talks, and occasional visits to the opera—though usually only if someone else pays.

Now, about dinner at the Temple Hall: it’s a spectacle. Students sit in messes of four, each with a joint of meat, a pie, and a bottle of port. But clever fellows like Lowton know tricks. For instance, wait until the benchers finish their meal—there are only three of them, but ten dishes—and then sneak over to see what’s left. “Wait a bit,” says Lowton, pulling Pendennis’s sleeve. “The side-tables are full, and there’s only three benchers to eat ten dishes—if we wait, perhaps we shall get something from their table.”

And sure enough, sometimes they do. The benchers often send down their fish—trout, salmon, even turtle—to the senior table. Lowton is a master strategist. He wants to be captain of the mess so he can claim the thirteenth glass of port—a tradition so sacred it borders on religion.

“Ah, Mr. Lowton,” says Pendennis, “you’re a sly fellow.”
“Well,” replies Lowton modestly, “I’ve lived in London most of my life. Of course I’ve got my eyes about me.”

He points out the notable guests: Boosey the bencher, Balls the King’s Counsel, Swettenham, and Old Grump, who’s been dining there for forty years. Then there are the swells—Trail, Ringwood, Bob Suckling—who don’t actually eat anything. They’re merely pretending to dine, so they can later boast they were at the Temple Hall while really feasting at a club or a grand dinner party. Their names appear in the *Morning Post*, but their stomachs remain empty. That’s why Lowton likes dining with them—they don’t drink, so the port goes to whoever’s willing.

“You’re green,” says Lowton, shaking his head. “You don’t understand the ways of the world.”

Pendennis learns quickly. Especially when Warrington arrives, dressed in ragged clothes but radiating charm. He sits down at the table, smiles at Pendennis, and says, “We ought to know each other. We’re both Boniface men. My name’s Warrington.”

And thus begins a friendship that will carry them through many adventures, legal mishaps, and questionable choices.

After dinner, Warrington invites Pendennis back to his rooms for a pipe. “Come on,” he says. “It’s short. We’ll talk about old times.”

And so they do. They talk about college, about girls, about dreams, about the fact that neither of them is likely to make it to the Bench or the Woolsack anytime soon. But they don’t mind. Life is better when shared with a friend who laughs at the same jokes and understands the value of a quiet evening with tobacco and conversation.

Later, Pendennis gives up his lodgings in St. James’s and moves in with Warrington. Pidgeon, the servant boy, and Flanagan, the laundress, now split their duties between the two friends. It’s simpler, cheaper, and infinitely more fun.

So there you have it—the Temple, the lawyers, the customs, the quirks, and the friendships formed in dimly lit chambers and noisy halls. A place where history lingers, where progress is slow, and where the greatest achievement may be simply surviving dinner without being caught stealing a piece of trout from the benchers’ table.

And if you ever pass by the Temple Garden, keep your eyes open—you might just catch sight of Sir Roger de Coverley strolling with Mr. Spectator, discussing the latest fashion in hoops and patches, or Harry Fielding scribbling away at midnight, ink on his ruffles, towel on his head, chasing fame one article at a time.

But for now, let’s leave them to their dreams—and return to our own, which, like Pendennis’s, may not be perfect, but are certainly more enjoyable when shared with a good friend and a pipe.
