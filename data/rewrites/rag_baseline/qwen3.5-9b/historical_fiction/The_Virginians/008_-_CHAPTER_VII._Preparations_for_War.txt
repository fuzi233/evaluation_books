The air inside Mr. Washington's room had thickened, becoming a solid weight that pressed against the ribs. It was no longer simply a room; it was a vessel containing the entire trajectory of a family's future, suspended in a state of high-tension equilibrium. Outside, the world was shifting gears. The quarrel between the French and the English, once a distant rumble in the colonial periphery, had erupted into a national roar. Reinforcements poured from France into Canada; the expectation of English troops in Virginia hung over the landscape like a storm front.

Madame Esmond moved through the house with a frantic energy, her praise for her son Mr. Washington rising in volumes that filled the corridors. She contrasted his sterling qualities with the vacillating nature of Harry, the wild boy, and the listless musings of George. Yet, George, standing amidst this manufactured admiration, felt no warmth. Instead, he watched the performance with a detached, saturnine humor. He offered jokes so dense with irony that his mother, bewildered by their depth, sat silent, unable to parse the meaning of her own son's moody silence. He was a pest to himself, a creature of internal friction, watching the external machinery grind forward.

Public events dictated the rhythm of private lives. The conflict had expanded, demanding resources and blood. Letters arrived from Quebec, written by Madame la Presidente de Mouchy, describing a destiny that seemed cruelly inverted. She spoke of George quitting the embrace of an adored mother to throw himself into the arms of Bellona. She hesitated to wish victory upon the French lilies when she saw her friend ranged under the banners of the Leopard. Her tone was one of affectionate sorrow, mingled with the political necessity of the moment. She mentioned the Chevalier de la Jabotiere, burning to meet his rival on the field of Mars, and the cold Hippolyte, preferring the chase to the soft conversation of ladies. These were the threads of a tapestry being woven in blood and ink.

The banner of the Leopard was unfurled. Regiments were raised, fleets dispatched. In February 1755, Commodore Keppel anchored in Hampton Roads with the Centurion, carrying General Braddock and his staff. The Duke of Cumberland, a figure of complex estimation, had appointed the General. A fleet of transports followed, laden with stores, men, and money. The great man landed at Alexandria and proceeded to Annapolis, summoning the governors of the colonies to a council of war.

The arrival of the General ignited a frenzy throughout the provinces, particularly at Castlewood. Harry was the first to move, rushing to see the troops encamped at Alexandria. The sight of the lines, the inspiring music of fifes and drums, captivated him. He sought out the officers, eager to join the expedition, becoming a welcome guest at their mess. Madame Esmond viewed this association with satisfaction, believing the English gentlemen of good fashion were refining her sons, distancing them from the horse-racing Virginian squires and the petty lawyers of Williamsburg. She possessed a sharp eye for flattery and a low tolerance for the squabbles of officialdom.

Harry and George smiled politely at their mother's compliments regarding the elegance of the camp. They did not know that the surface of propriety masked a rotting core. The officers swaggered, frightening the peaceful villagers with their riot. The General raged against the disorder of his troops and the niggardliness of the provincials. The soldiers treated the land as conquered territory, insulting the wives of their Indian allies. The General was forced to forbid Indian women from his camp; their husbands, amazed and outraged, withdrew their support, depriving the army of crucial aid just months later.

Yet, Madame Esmond refused to hear these stories. Those who visited Castlewood were polite gentlemen. Soon, the General himself sent messages. His father had served under Marlborough alongside Colonel Esmond. With permission, he wished to pay his respects to the daughter of such a meritorious officer. If Madame Esmond had known the source of this politeness, the charm might have faded. The Commander-in-Chief held levees at Alexandria. Among the gentry who paid their respects were the twins of Castlewood, riding in state on their best nags, accompanied by their negro-boys in smart liveries.

Braddock was sulky, angry with the provincial gentry, and barely noticed the young gentlemen, asking casually of his aide-de-camp who the young Squire Gawkeys were in their blue and gold waistcoats. Mr. Dinwiddie, the Lieutenant-Governor, and a Philadelphia gentleman were dining with him. Dinwiddie identified the boys as the sons of the Princess Pocahontas. Enraged, Braddock demanded to know who she was. Dinwiddie, harboring resentment against the imperious little lady, launched into a disrespectful account of Madam Esmond, mocking her pomposity and pretensions until the General fell asleep.

When he awoke, Dinwiddie was gone, but the Philadelphia gentleman remained. The General resumed the conversation, speaking of Madam Esmond in curt, soldierly terms, asking again for the name of the old fool. He vented his contempt for the gentry and the country. The Philadelphia gentleman, Mr. Franklin, repeated the widow's name but offered a radically different assessment. He knew her estates, her cattle, her horses, her stores—all assets valuable in the current juncture. He advised the General to conciliate her. Having already decided Franklin was shrewd, the General graciously invited the two young men to dine the following day.

They arrived, behaving with the modesty and good-breeding expected of their station. They returned home delighted, and so did their mother. In reply to Braddock's message, Madam Esmond penned a billet in her finest style, fixing the time for his reception at Castlewood.

The campaign became the central obsession of the household. For Harry, it was the dearest wish of his life. He dreamed only of war, scouring and polishing every gun and sword in the house, organizing the negroes under arms. His mother, possessing a gallant spirit herself, knew the time had come for one of her boys to leave. She dreaded deciding which. She admired the elder, but she loved the younger with a passion that terrified her.

Harry, eager as he was, dared not broach the subject directly. When he finally did, George's countenance wore an ominous look. Harry worshipped his elder brother, giving way to him in all things. This obedience terrified him further as he realized George was also consumed by military matters. George had the wars of Eugene and Marlborough memorized, the military books of his grandfather, and the warlike lives of Plutarch. He practiced fencing with Dempster, the old Scotchman, an adept in the martial arts though shy of revealing his origins.

Madam Esmond entrusted her boys with the reply to the General's message, attaching substantial presents for the staff and officers. The General thanked Mr. Franklin repeatedly for securing this alliance. "Would not one of the young gentlemen like to see the campaign?" he asked. A friend, Mr. Washington, had already promised to join as aide-de-camp; the General would gladly take another Virginian. Harry's eyes brightened, his face flushing. "He would like with all his heart to go!" he cried. George, looking intently at his younger brother, stated that one would be proud to attend, while the other's duty was to remain and care for their mother. Harry yielded. His will was still obedient to George's. Despite his longing, the desire itself made him timid. He dared not speak openly as they rode home, silencing themselves for miles, each aware of the other's turmoil, afraid to force the issue.

At home, they told their mother of the offer. "I knew it must happen," she said. "At such a crisis, our family must come forward. Have you settled which of you is to leave me?" She looked anxiously from one to the other.

"The youngest ought to go, mother; of course I ought to go!" cried Harry, turning red.

"Of course he ought," said Mrs. Mountain, present at the talk.

"There! Mountain says so! I told you so!" Harry exclaimed, shooting a sidelong glance at George.

"The head of the family ought to go, mother," said George, sadly.

"No! No! You are ill, and have never recovered your fever. Ought he to go, Mountain?"

"You would make the best soldier, I know that, dearest Hal. You and George Washington are great friends, and could travel well together. He does not care for me, nor I for him, however much he is admired in the family. But, you see, 'tis the law of Honour, my Harry." Mrs. Mountain spoke to her brother with extraordinary kindness. "The grief I have had is that I must refuse thee. I must go. Had Fate granted you that extra half-hour of life I possess, it would have been your lot. You would have claimed your right to go first, you know you would."

"Yes, George," said poor Harry, "I own I should."

"You will stay at home, and take care of Castlewood and our mother. If anything happens to me, you are here to fill my place. I would like to give way, my dear, as you, I know, would lay down your life to serve me. But each of us must do his duty. What would our grandfather say if he were here?"
