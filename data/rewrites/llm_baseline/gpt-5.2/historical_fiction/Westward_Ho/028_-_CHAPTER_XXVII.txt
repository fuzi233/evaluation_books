And so Ayacanora came to lodge in Lucy’s narrow cabin as though she had always belonged there—as duly shipped, as truly part of the company as any man who ate the ship’s biscuit and answered the boatswain’s call.

Yet she proved the most perilous of all their prizes.

For when those who have lived by hunting and hunger come first among folk who can draw sweetness from a chest with a key, there falls upon them a kind of sickness—of the spirit first, and then of the body. The old labors which had kept the limbs honest are gone; the old pride which had braced the heart is bruised by new craft and stronger tools. What was once won by patient tracking is now had for the snatching; and so the hand learns to take before the tongue has learned to ask. Amyas, watching her, felt that he held a soul on the edge of a knife; and had he been less steady, less simple in his steadfastness, the poor wild creature might have ended in a misery such as men afterward told of in docks and garrets—one more marvel broken and mislaid among the refuse of London.

At first it truly seemed she was slipping fast.

The warrior-prophetess of the Omaguas dwindled into a wayward child. After her first wonder at the great floating house—after she had ranged from deck to deck, nosed into every locker, tried every latch with fingers quick as a crab’s—she began to steal and hoard any trifle that glimmered and pleased her. She was too proud, or too shy, to beg; and so she took. When Amyas forbade it, she flung back her head with that sudden, savage royalty of hers, threatened to drown herself, and shut herself away sulking for hours, as if the very planks had offended her.

Still, she obeyed him—except where sweetness was concerned.

Perhaps her body, used to plantains and forest fruit, craved what seemed to her nearest kin: the preserved guavas and candied peels in the bishop’s store, the sticky comfits, the dried figs, the honeyed dainties. Those dwindled with a speed that made the purser’s face lengthen. Worse, the sweet Spanish wine which Amyas had set apart for Lucy’s daily cordial began to sink in the bottle as if the sea itself drank it.

Amyas’s rebuke then was sharp enough to cut. He told her—plainly, as he would have told a cabin-boy—that to rob a sick woman was mean; that it was the act of a coward, not of a brave. Ayacanora’s lip quivered; her eyes flashed; she cried out she would drown herself, sprang for the deck, and would have cast her bright head over the side then and there, had not Amyas caught her by the wrist and drawn her back with a strength that was gentleness only because he willed it so.

Afterward came storms of weeping and a flood of penitence; promises poured out in broken English and swift Indian speech. And a week later Amyas found she had slipped past her own conscience by bullying the Portuguese steward into giving her other wine instead. The thing might have gone worse; for she drank one evening, and the drink made her foolish and restless, laughing too loudly, stirring from place to place as if the very air pricked her. In the morning her head ached and her eyes were dull; and Amyas saw his warning proved before him like a lesson written in pain.

The steward, hauled up, swore in his terror that she had threatened to kill him. Amyas, hot with wrath, ordered him a sound flogging. But Ayacanora, with a pride that had its own rude honor, flung herself between the man and punishment, begging him off, offering herself instead, confessing that she had indeed frightened him into obedience.

Her headache and Amyas’s cold looks taught her more than stripes could have done. She made one more rush toward drowning herself, as if it were her last refuge from shame; then, like a creature that has found the fire burns, she shrank from the thing thereafter. From that day forward—to her dying day, it was said—she could hardly be persuaded to touch fermented drink.

Yet Amyas lay awake more than once, beating his brains. How was he to govern a girl who flew to suicide whenever crossed? To punish her he dared not, even if he had the heart; to put her ashore was impossible. There was in him an instinct—no idle superstition—that her strange clinging to the English was not mere caprice, but some charge laid upon him; and he prayed, with that downrightness of his which made prayer a kind of work, to be shown what he should do before she harmed herself—or, what seemed as likely some days, harmed a man who vexed her.

At last he thought it best to set Parson Jack to teach her the beginnings of Christianity, that she might be baptized when they came home.

But here rose a new trouble. She would learn of none but Amyas. Parson Jack, with all his honest zeal, was to her as a crow to a hawk. Amyas himself had reasons enough for refusing the office of schoolmaster; yet for a week or two she remained untaught in that matter, though in English she made astonishing speed, catching words as a cook catches scents—by instinct, by hunger, by daily nearness. She learned the names of things first—bread, knife, salt, fire—things that could be held and tasted and felt; and with the words came a sharper desire to be understood.

Then, as if her ill-humors must needs spend themselves on persons, she took a violent dislike to John Brimblecombe, mimicking his walk and his voice upon deck to the men’s delight; and she hated Will Cary too, never suffering him to approach or speak to her. Whether jealousy of his intimacy with Amyas, or the subtle woman’s knowledge that Cary alone might dare to make love to her, no one could tell; for Cary, to do him justice, was as innocent of that intention as Amyas himself. When remonstrated with, her only reason was childishly imperial: Cary, she said, was a cacique as well as Amyas, and there ought not to be two caciques. Once, in sober earnest, she proposed that Amyas should kill him and take the ship for himself; and when Amyas, amid shouts of laughter, repeated her counsel to its intended victim, she sulked for days as though betrayed.

The negroes fared worst of all. At first she hid from them as from some unclean beast; after, she flung at them whatever lay to hand—bits of rope’s-end, small tools, once a belaying-pin—till the poor Quashies complained to Amyas that their skulls would be cracked for no fault save their skin.

Over the common sailors she reigned like a petted princess with a knife in her sleeve—calling them from their work to run her errands, make her toys, twist her bright little fancies into cord or wood; enforcing her commands now and then with a shrewd box on the ear. Yet the good fellows, especially old Yeo, bore it and even fed it, as sailors will—humoring her much as they might humor a tamed leopard whose claws might show at any moment. She amused them, and she amused Amyas too; and the constant fret of watching her kept his mind from settling too heavily on what waited him in England—his mother’s face, and the dark tale he must tell her, which otherwise would have sat like lead upon his triumph and deliverance.

At last matters came—like most matters—to a head.

The ship had slipped along for many days under a favoring breeze. She had passed the ring of West Indian islands and crawled, safe from pursuit, through the great weed-beds of the Sargasso Sea, where the ocean lies thick and still as a meadow flooded. There, for the first time, they judged it safe to relax the discipline which had held them tight as a clenched fist, and to rummage their noble prize.

What they found of gold and silver, jewels and merchandise, need not be told. There was enough, when all claims were paid—Cary’s and the crew’s, and Mr. Salterne’s third as owner—to make Amyas rich for life. But in the captain’s cabin they discovered two chests: one stuffed with gorgeous Mexican feather-work, and the other with Spanish and East Indian finery that had come by way of Havana and Cartagena, bound, it seemed, to some señora at Caracas.

At Cary’s proposal, and amid the acclamations of the men, both chests were voted to Ayacanora as her share of the pillage—in reward, they said, of her Amazonian prowess and service.

She seized upon them with a greedy joy, had them carried into Lucy’s cabin, and knelt before them for hours, stroking and fingering as if the silks were living things. The Mexican feather dresses she sniffed at as savage; but the Spanish gowns—ah! those were enchantment. For two or three days she walked the quarter-deck in Seville mantillas and Madrid hats, in brocaded farthingales stiff as armor, in glittering trinkets hung about her throat and ears; and heaven knows what else, and heaven knows how wrongly worn. She spread herself in the sun like a peacock, turning for Amyas’s eye, hungry for praise.

The crew tittered. Amyas felt more inclined to weep.

There is something more pitiful than hunger in a child: vanity—that bright, trusting hunger to be admired. And most pitiful of all is the moment when it finds itself laughed at instead. Amyas would have spoken, but feared to crush her; and the thing healed itself as illnesses sometimes do—by breaking.

On the third day, unable to hold in her triumph longer, she came straight to Amyas.

“Ayacanora quite English girl now; is she not?”

A titter rose behind her. She turned, saw a dozen honest faces in broad grin, understood in a flash, and her pride—so tender under all its fierceness—bled at once. She darted down the companion, vanished below.

Amyas, thinking she would throw herself overboard, followed. But she had locked herself in with Lucy. Through the door he heard her sobbing, great convulsive sobs; and Lucy’s faint voice, pleading to know what was wrong.

He knocked. She would not open. All day she kept the door fast; and toward evening they were forced to break it, lest Lucy be starved.

There sat Ayacanora amid the wreck of her finery—half torn from her in spite, scattered across the floor like shed feathers—crying as if her heart would crack. Lucy cried too, half from fright and hunger, half because another’s grief is contagious in a close cabin where two women breathe the same thin air.

Amyas tried to comfort her, promised the men should not laugh again; but then, stumbling over the right words, he added, “You must not be so—so—”

“So what?” she demanded, and cried harder.

“So like a wild girl, Ayacanora.”

At that, her hands dropped to her knees. A spasm shook her throat and breast. She slid down, not in rage now but as if struck through, and knelt before him, looking up with an imploring face that made him feel as though he had wounded a child with a sword.

“Yes,” she whispered, “wild girl—poor, bad wild girl. But I will be English girl now!”

“Fine clothes will never make you English, my child,” Amyas said, very gently.

“No! Not English clothes—English heart! Good heart, like yours! Yes, I will be good, and Sir John shall teach me!”

“That’s my good maid,” Amyas answered, and his own throat tightened. “Sir John shall begin to-morrow.”

“No! Now—now! Ayacanora cannot wait. She will drown herself if she is bad another day! Come, now!”

And she made him fetch Brimblecombe then and there. The honest fellow, though still sore in dignity from her mockeries, bore himself patiently for an hour or more; and afterward she told Lucy, that very night, all that he had said—fumbling for words, translating into her own strange sense what she could not yet wholly understand.

From that day, when Jack went in to read and pray with the poor sufferer, Ayacanora no longer fled on deck. She stood by, trying to make it all out. She knelt when he knelt; she tried to pray too—that she might have an English heart. And if the prayer was dumb, it was not therefore unheard.

So they went on for a few days, more hopefully, with no great outbreak—until one morning, just after they had cleared the Sargasso-beds.

The ship was taking care of herself. The men sat under the awning mending and cobbling, their tools clicking softly—needle through canvas, awl into leather, hammer tapping metal—so that the whole deck had a homely, workshop music to it: the scrape of a knife on a spar, the creak of rope, the low talk, the occasional laughter. The sea’s hush around them held it all the more plainly, as a chapel holds a psalm. Even the wind seemed to walk on tiptoe; and Amyas, hearing how cleanly the little sounds rose and fell, thought how a ship at peace has its own kind of sanity—how different from the racket of guns and the shriek of iron, that modern men, he fancied, might one day call glory.

Brimblecombe was catechising his pupil below. Amyas and Cary, cigars in mouth, lounged and talked of all heaven and earth, and chiefly of how best to set on foot a fresh adventure against the Spaniards so soon as they reached England. And as they spoke, Amyas found himself pouring out to Will that dark hatred of the whole nation—his purpose of revenge for his brother and for Rose—which had settled like a murky cloud into every corner of his heart.

Suddenly a noise broke up from below—a scuffle, a shout—and both men sprang to their feet.

Up on deck rushed Jack Brimblecombe, holding his head with both hands as if to keep it from falling off.

“Save me! save me from that she-fiend! She is possessed with a legion! She hath broken my nose—torn out half my hair!—and I am sure I have none to spare! Here she comes! Stand by me, gentlemen both! Satanas, I defy thee!”

And Jack, with a courage that knew how to take cover, planted himself behind Amyas and Cary, while Ayacanora whirled upon deck like a very Maenad; then, seeing Amyas, stopped short, chest heaving, eyes bright with wrath.

“If you had defied Satan down below,” Cary said with a laugh, “I suspect he would not have broken out on you so boldly, Master Jack.”

“I am innocent—innocent as the babe unborn! Oh, Mr. Cary, this is too bad of you, sir!” Jack cried, indignant, while Amyas asked, sharp and anxious, what had happened.

“He looked at me,” Ayacanora said sturdily.

“Well,” said Amyas, “a cat may look at a king.”

“But he shall not look at Ayacanora. Nobody shall but you, or I will kill him!”

Jack protested, spluttering, that he had not so much as lifted an eye. The fancy—if fancy it was—had seized her like a fit. She refused to return below to her lesson. Jack went off grumbling, minus some hair, and wore a black eye for a week.

“At all events,” Cary said, relighting his cigar, “it is a fault on the right side.”

“God give me grace,” Amyas muttered, “or it may prove one on the wrong side for me.”

“He will, old heart-of-oak,” said Cary, and laid an arm about Amyas’s neck—an easy comradeship that made Ayacanora’s nostrils flare with visible disgust. She stalked away to the ship’s side, found a fishing-line, and began amusing herself, laughing and clapping her hands when flying-fish skittered from the bonitos, their silver bodies hissing through the air like thrown knives.

At last, weary of idleness, she went forward to the poop-rail and listened to John Squire the armorer, who sat tinkering a headpiece—metal ringing lightly under his hammer—and humming a song, changing the name in it to suit his own home:

“Oh, Bideford is a pleasant place, it shines where it stands,
And the more I look upon it, the more my heart it warms;
For there are fair young lasses, in rows upon the quay,
To welcome gallant mariners, when they come home from say.”

“’Tis Sunderland, John Squire, to the song, and not Bidevor,” said his mate.

“Well, Bidevor’s so good as Sunderland any day,” John Squire answered, “for all there’s no say-coals there blacking a place about; and makes just so good harmonies, Tommy Hamblyn—

“Oh, if I was a herring, to swim the ocean o’er,
Or if I was a say-dove, to fly unto the shoor,
To fly unto my true love, a waiting at the door,
To wed her with a goold ring, and plough the main no moor.”

Here old Yeo broke in, as if the very tune were a temptation.

“Aren’t you ashamed, John Squire, to your years, singing such carnal vanities, after all the providences you have seen? Let the songs of Zion be in your mouth, man, if you must needs keep a caterwauling all day like that.”

“You sing ’em yourself then, gunner.”

“Well,” said Yeo, “and why not?” And out came his psalm-book, and he began in that deep, twanging voice of his, which seemed made to carry above wind and water:

“Such as in ships and brittle barks
Into the seas descend,
Their merchandise through fearful floods
To compass and to end;
There men are forced to behold
The Lord’s works what they be;
And in the dreadful deep the same,
Most marvellous they see.”

“Humph!” said John Squire. “Very good and godly: but still I du like a merry catch now and then, I du. Wouldn’t you let a body sing ‘Rumbelow’—even when he’s heaving of the anchor?”

“Well, I don’t know,” Yeo answered; “but the Lord’s people had better praise the Lord then too, and pray for a good voyage, instead of howling about—

‘A randy, dandy, dandy O,
A whet of ale and brandy O,
With a rumbelow and a Westward-ho!
And heave, my mariners all, O!’”

“Is that fit talk for immortal souls? How does that child’s-trade sound beside the Psalms, John Squire?”

Now it befell that Salvation Yeo, for the very purpose of holding up that time-honored melody to ridicule, put on the true nasal twang and rang it out as merrily as ever he had done, perhaps twelve years before, when he got up John Oxenham’s anchor in Plymouth Sound.

And it befell also that Ayacanora, standing near Amyas, heard it and started as if stung. Then, half to herself, she took up the strain, and sang it over again—word for word—in the very same tune and tone.

Salvation Yeo started in his turn and turned deadly pale.

“Who sung that?” he asked quickly.

“The little maid here,” said Amyas. “She’s coming on nicely in her English.”

“The little maid?” Yeo whispered, paler still. “Why do you go about to scare an old servant, by talking of little maids, Captain Amyas? Well—” and then he spoke aloud to himself, as men do when their hearts run away with them—“as I am a sinful saint, if I hadn’t seen where the voice came from, I could have sworn it was her; just as we taught her to sing it by the river there, I and William Penberthy of Marazion, my good comrade. The Lord have mercy on me!”

A silence fell. All knew that whenever Yeo, by chance or by pain, came near any remembrance of that lost child, something in him shook like a mast in a squall. Only Ayacanora, pleased with Amyas’s praise and not knowing why men stared, went humming on, light as a bird:

“And heave, my mariners all, O!”

Yeo leapt up from the gun where he sat.

“I can’t abear it! As I live, I can’t! You, Indian maiden—where did you learn to sing that there?”

Ayacanora looked at him, half frightened by his vehemence; then at Amyas, to see if she had done wrong; and then, taking refuge in sauciness, turned away, stared over the side, and hummed again, provoking as a child can be when it is cornered.

“Ask her, for mercy’s sake—ask her, Captain Leigh!” Yeo cried, trembling.

“My child,” said Amyas, speaking in Indian, “how is it you sing that so much better than any other English? Did you ever hear it before?”

Ayacanora looked up, puzzled, and shook her head.

“If you tell Indian to Ayacanora, she dumb,” she said sharply. “She must be English girl now, like poor Lucy.”

“Well then,” said Amyas, coaxing her as he would coax a skittish horse, “do you recollect—do you recollect—anything that happened when you were a little girl?”

She paused, and lifted her hands above her head, as if the memory were a roof of leaves.

“Trees—great trees like the Magdalena—always nothing but trees—wild and bad everything. Ayacanora won’t talk about that.”

“Do you mind anything that grew on those trees?” Yeo asked eagerly.

She laughed. “Silly! Flowers and fruit, and nuts—grow on all trees, and monkey-cups too. Ayacanora climbed up after them—when she was wild. I won’t tell any more.”

“But who taught you to call them monkey-cups?” Yeo asked, his voice breaking with hope.

“Monkey’s drink; mono drink.”

“Mono?” Yeo repeated, baffled, and tried another path. “How did you know the beasts were called monos?”

“She might have heard it coming down with us,” Cary put in, joining them.

“Ay, monos,” she said, defending herself. “Faces like little men, and tails. And one very dirty black one, with a beard, say Amen in a tree to all the other monkeys, just like Sir John on Sunday.”

That thrust at Brimblecombe and the preaching ape made the sailors grin; but it did not move old Yeo at all.

“But don’t you recollect any Christians?—white people?” he pressed.

Ayacanora fell silent.

“Don’t you mind a white lady?” Yeo asked, and his hands worked as if kneading air.

“Um?” she said, half unwilling, half straining.

“A woman—a very pretty woman—with hair like his?” and he pointed to Amyas.

“No.”

“What do you mind, then, beside those Indians?” Yeo asked in despair.

She turned her back peevishly, tired of being hunted through her own thoughts.

“Do try to remember,” Amyas said, softly; and at his tone she made the effort, as if his voice were a leash that held her.

“Ayacanora mind great monkeys—black, oh, so high,” and she lifted her hand above her head and made a gesture of loathing.

“Monkeys? What, with tails?” asked Amyas.

“No, like man. Ah! yes—just like Cooky there—dirty Cooky!”

And that hapless son of Ham, crossing the main-deck at the wrong instant, saw a marlingspike—unlucky that one lay at hand—whistle past his ears.

“Ayacanora, if you heave any more things at Cooky, I must have you whipped,” Amyas said sternly, though there was no such intent in his heart.

“I’ll kill you then,” she answered, in the most matter-of-fact tone, as if it were only another choice of conduct.

“She must mean negurs,” Yeo muttered. “I wonder where she saw them now. What if it were they Cimaroons?”

“But why should any one who had seen whites forget them, and yet remember negroes?” Cary asked.

“Let us try again,” Amyas said. “Do you mind no great monkeys but those black ones?”

“Yes,” she said at last, after turning it over as if the thought were bitter on her tongue—“devil.”

“Devil?” all three echoed, for none of them were free of the belief that the fiend might take shapes and show himself to heathen conjurers.

“Ay, him Sir John tell about on Sundays,” she said.

“Save and help us!” Yeo groaned. “And what was he like unto?”

She made signs for a monkey’s face, and a gray beard like Yeo’s. That was plain enough. But then she began a series of motions about her pretty little neck—fingering and spreading, as if she wore a stiff ring there—which set them all at fault.

Cary burst out laughing.

“I know! Sir Urian had a ruff on, as I live! Trunk-hose too, my fair dame? Stop—I’ll make sure. Was his neck like the señor commandant’s—the Spaniard’s?”

Ayacanora clapped her hands, delighted to be understood, and nodded eagerly.

“The ‘devil’ appeared like a monkey,” Cary said, still half laughing, “with a gray beard, in a ruff—humph!”

“Ay!” Ayacanora cried, and in good enough Spanish now, as if the right word had been kept in that tongue all along—“Mono de Panama; viejo diablo de Panama.”

Yeo flung up his hands with a shriek.

“Oh Lord of all mercies! Those were the last words of Mr. John Oxenham! Ay—and the devil is surely none other than the devil Don Francisco Xararte! Oh dear! oh dear! oh dear! my sweet young lady! my pretty little maid! And don’t you know me? Don’t you know Salvation Yeo, that carried you over the mountains, and used to climb for the monkey-cups for you, my dear young lady? And William Penberthy too, that used to get you flowers; and your poor dear father, that was just like Mr. Cary there, only he had a black beard, and black curls, and swore terribly in his speech, like a Spaniard, my dear young lady?”

And the honest fellow dropped on his knees and covered Ayacanora’s hands with kisses, babbling as if words could build a bridge back over twelve lost years. The crew, seeing him on his knees to the savage girl, and hearing his cries, fancied him suddenly mad and crowded aft.

“Steady, men, and don’t vex him!” Amyas called. “He thinks he has found his little maid at last.”

“And so do I, Amyas, as I live,” Cary said under his breath.

“Steady, steady, my masters all!” Amyas added. “If this turn out a wrong scent after all, his wits will crack. Mr. Yeo—can’t you think of any other token?”

Yeo stamped like a man whose heart is too full for patience.

“What need then? ’Tis her, I tell ye, and that’s enough! What a beauty she’s grown! Oh dear! where were my eyes all this time, to behold her, and not to see her! ’Tis her very mortal self, it is! And don’t you mind me, my dear, now? Don’t you mind Salvation Yeo, that taught you to sing ‘Heave my mariners all, O!’ a-sitting on a log by the boat upon the sand, and there was a sight of red lilies grew on it in the moss, dear, now, wasn’t there? and we made posies of them to put in your hair, now?”

He ran on, pleading and suggesting, as if he could coax her memory into the shape he needed—into the child he had lost.

Ayacanora watched him—first angry, then amused, then attentive; and at last with such fierce earnestness that her face seemed to change before them. Suddenly she grew crimson. She snatched her hands away from the old man’s kisses, hid her face in them, and stood rigid as if the deck had turned to ice beneath her bare feet.

“Do you remember anything of all this, my child?” Amyas asked, very gently.
