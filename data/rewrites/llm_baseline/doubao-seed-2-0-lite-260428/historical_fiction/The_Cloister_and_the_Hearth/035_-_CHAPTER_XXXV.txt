Gerard stirred at last, slow to drag his eyes open after a night that had unspooled into blood and terror before they found the rough, reed-thatched hovel that had sheltered them till dawn. Denys the archer crouched beside him, his weathered face lined with uncharacteristic worry, a half-carved arrow shaft idle in his calloused, bow-string hardened hand.
“By all the saints, you can sleep,” Denys chided, nodding toward the slant of honeyed noon light spilling through the cracks in the hut’s mud wall. “It is full midday, man. The town criers have done three rounds of the streets already.”
Gerard drew a long, deep breath, his chest still tight with the half-memory of glinting daggers and the roar of bandits in the dark treeline. “That sleep was a blessed gift sent from Heaven,” he murmured softly. “It hangs like a soft, kind veil between me and that awful night. To think we sit here whole, unharmed—half the time I can almost convince myself the whole bloody struggle was nothing more than a fever dream.”
“Aye, lad, that’s the only sensible way to look on such things once they are done,” Denys grunted, clapping him gently on the shoulder, hard enough to make the young man wince faintly at the bruise hidden under his tunic. “They’re shadows, no more, once the sun’s up. Eat a bite to break your fast, and you’ll scarce think of it again. Besides, I swore I’d have you into the town by noon, to stand before his worship the alderman as we are bound to.”
Gerard reached for the hunk of hard rye bread they’d stashed in their pack the day before, sopping the crust deep in the sour, spiced red wine they’d scavenged from a roadside tavern the prior afternoon, and ate slowly, his gaze drifting now and then to the dark treeline beyond the hut’s door. When he was done, the two men tramped back out to the stretch of forest clearing where the fight had unfolded, and Gerard flinched, shuddering, at the sight of discarded daggers, torn scraps of bandit tunic, and dark, crusted stains seeping into the fallen autumn leaves. He did not linger there, hurrying after Denys as they took the rutted road toward the small walled town, his eyes darting between every hedge and thicket, half-expecting a hidden attacker to leap out at them with every step.
They passed through the town gates at last, and Denys led him straight to *The White Hart*, a stout timber inn with a swinging sign painted with the white hart of Burgundy’s royal arms. “No cut-throats will touch you here,” Denys promised, shoving the door open to the warm, yeasty smell of spiced ale. “I’ve known the landlord nigh on ten years. He’s a burgess of good standing, and halfway to being named bailiff by the town council. This was where I was heading for yestereve, but we lost too much time fending off the rogues, and night overtook us before we could make the walls, and—”
“And you would not listen to the woman at the cottage door, would you?” Gerard put in, a faint, wobbly laugh breaking through his frayed nerves. “You thought you knew better than old Jeanneton, who warned us the woods were full of thieves.”
Denys huffed, not affronted. “Aye, and who was it that saved our lives in the end, if not a woman? Risked her own skin to slip out in the dark and carry word of the ambush right to the alderman, so the patrol came in time to haul those villains off before they cut our throats.”
“That is the very truth of it,” Gerard said warmly, his face softening. “Women have never been aught to me, not since—” He paused, thinking of Margaret far away in their native Holland, and his voice steadied. “But I would give half the coins in my purse right now to find that lass, to thank her, to reward her for what she did. Do you not feel the same?”
“Parbleu, of course,” Denys nodded.
“Where are we to find her, then?” Gerard asked.
“The alderman will surely know where she lives,” Denys told him. “We have to go and speak to him first, anyway.”
When they were shown into the alderman’s panelled parlour, the town worthy regarded them for a long, strange, unreadable moment, his bushy brows drawn together. Then a grim, slow smile spread across his face, and he leaned forward to fire question after question at Gerard, scribbling down every word of their account of the ambush with a sharp goose quill. When he had finished, he set down the quill and fixed them with a stern look.
“You two will remain in this town until the bandits are brought to trial,” he said, “and you will be on hand to give your testimony. Fail to appear, and you face heavy fines, even a turn in the town jail.”
Both men’s faces fell, their shoulders slumping.
“Do not look so glum,” the alderman added. “It will not be long. The rogues were taken red-handed, still with their daggers drawn, no way to deny their crimes. And besides—” he added, with a thin little smile “—you could not leave this town at all this week, in any case.”
Denys blinked, confused, but Gerard almost laughed, thinking the old man must be daft to imagine a tiny provincial Burgundy town could hold him back from Rome, from the Margaret he ached to return to. He leaned forward, speaking before either of them could press the alderman on his odd remark.
“Your worship,” he said, “the thing that weighs most on both our hearts right now—we cannot find the lass who saved our lives anywhere in the town.”
The alderman tilted his head. “Your benefactress? Who do you mean?”
“Why, the good girl who slipped out in the dead of night to come to you, who risked being caught by the bandits to carry word that we were about to be murdered out on the west road,” Gerard said, his voice earnest and warm. “Sir, our hearts burn within us to thank her, to bless her, to make sure she is repaid for her kindness. Where is she?”
