ON BEING IN LOVE. Of course, you've been in love! If not, it's coming. Love is like the measles; we all have to go through it. Like the measles, too, we catch it only once. Never fear catching it a second time. The man who has had it can wander into the most dangerous places and perform the most foolhardy tricks with absolute safety. He can picnic in shady woods, stroll through leafy aisles, and linger on mossy seats watching the sunset. He fears a quiet country house no more than his own club. He can join a family trip down the Rhine. He can, to bid a friend farewell, venture straight into the jaws of the marriage ceremony itself. He can keep his head during the whirl of a ravishing waltz and rest afterward in a dark conservatory, catching nothing worse than a cold. He can brave a moonlit walk down sweet-scented lanes or a twilight trek among somber rushes. He can climb a stile without danger, scramble through a tangled hedge without getting caught, and descend a slippery path without falling. He can look into sunny eyes without being dazzled. He hears the siren voices yet steers with an unwavering helm. He clasps white hands in his, but no electric, *Lulu*-like force binds him to their dainty pressure.

No, we never sicken with love twice. Cupid spends no second arrow on the same heart. Love's handmaids—respect, admiration, and affection—are our lifelong friends. Our doors may always remain open to them, but their great celestial master, in his royal progress, pays but one visit and then departs. We like, we cherish, we are very, very fond of—but we never love again. A man's heart is a firework that, once in its time, flashes heavenward. Meteor-like, it blazes for a moment and lights the whole world beneath with its glory. Then the night of our sordid, commonplace life closes in around it, and the burned-out casing falls back to earth, lying useless and uncared for, slowly smoldering into ashes.

Once, breaking free from our prison bonds, we dare, as mighty old Prometheus dared, to scale the Olympian mount and snatch from Phoebus' chariot the fire of the gods. Happy those who, hastening down before it dies out, can kindle their earthly altars at its flame. Love is too pure a light to burn long among the noxious gases we breathe, but before it is choked out, we may use it as a torch to ignite the cozy fire of affection. After all, that warming glow is more suited to our cold little back-parlor of a world than the burning spirit of love itself. Love should be the Vestal fire of some mighty temple—a vast, dim fane whose organ music is the rolling of the spheres. Affection will burn cheerfully when the white flame of love flickers out. Affection is a fire that can be fed day by day, piled higher as the wintry years approach. Old men and women can sit by it with their thin hands clasped; little children can nestle in front; friends and neighbors have their welcome corners by its side; and even shaggy Fido and sleek Titty can toast their noses at the bars. Let us heap the coals of kindness upon that fire. Throw on your pleasant words, your gentle hand-pressures, your thoughtful and selfless deeds. Fan it with good humor, patience, and forbearance. Then you can let the wind blow and the rain fall unheeded, for your hearth will be warm and bright, and the faces gathered round it will make sunshine despite the clouds without.

I am afraid, dear Edwin and Angelina, you expect too much from love. You think there is enough of your little hearts to feed this fierce, devouring passion for all your long lives. Ah, young folk! Don't rely too heavily on that unsteady flicker. It will dwindle and dwindle as the months roll on, and there is no replenishing the fuel. You will watch it die out in anger and disappointment. To each, it will seem that the other is growing colder. Edwin sees with bitterness that Angelina no longer runs to the gate to meet him, all smiles and blushes; and when he has a cough now, she doesn't begin to cry and put her arms round his neck, declaring she cannot live without him. The most she will likely do is suggest a lozenge, and even that in a tone implying she is merely annoyed by the noise. Poor little Angelina, too, sheds silent tears because Edwin has stopped carrying her old handkerchief in the inside pocket of his waistcoat. Both are astonished at the decline in the other, but neither sees their own change. If they did, they would not suffer as they do. They would look for the cause in the right quarter—in the littleness of poor human nature—join hands over their common failing, and start building their house anew on a more earthly and enduring foundation. But we are so blind to our own shortcomings, so wide awake to those of others. Everything that happens to us is always the other person's fault. Angelina would have loved Edwin forever and ever and ever if only Edwin hadn't grown so strange and different. Edwin would have adored Angelina through eternity if she had only remained the same as when he first adored her.

It is a cheerless hour for you both when the lamp of love has gone out and the fire of affection is not yet lit, and you must grope about in the cold, raw dawn of life to kindle it. God grant it catches light before the day is too far spent. Many sit shivering by the dead coals until night falls. But, there, of what use is it to preach? Who, feeling the rush of young love through his veins, can think it will ever flow feeble and slow? To the boy of twenty, it seems impossible that he won't love as wildly at sixty as he does now. He cannot recall any middle-aged or elderly gentleman known for exhibiting symptoms of frantic attachment, yet this doesn't stop him from believing in himself. His love will never fall, whoever else's may. Nobody ever loved as he loves, so naturally, the rest of the world's experience is irrelevant to his case. Alas! Alas! Ere thirty, he joins the ranks of the sneerers. It isn't his fault. Our passions, both good and bad, cease with our blushes. We do not hate, nor grieve, nor rejoice, nor despair in our thirties as we did in our teens. Disappointment doesn't suggest suicide, and we quaff success without intoxication. We take all things in a minor key as we grow older. There are few majestic passages in the later acts of life's opera. Ambition aims less ambitiously. Honor becomes more reasonable and conveniently adapts itself to circumstances. And love—love dies.

"Irreverence for the dreams of youth" soon creeps like a killing frost upon our hearts. The tender shoots and expanding flowers are nipped and withered, and of a vine that yearned to stretch its tendrils round the world, there remains but a sapless stump. My fair friends will deem all this rank heresy, I know. Far from a man not loving after passing boyhood, it is not until there is a good deal of gray in his hair that they think his protestations worthy of attention. Young ladies take their notions of our sex from novels written by their own, and compared with the monstrosities masquerading as men in that nightmare literature, Pythagoras' plucked bird and Frankenstein's demon were fair average specimens of humanity. In these so-called books, the chief lover—or Greek god, as he is admiringly referred to (by the way, they don't specify which "Greek god"; it might be hump-backed Vulcan, double-faced Janus, or even driveling Silenus, the god of abstruse mysteries)—resembles the whole family. However, he shares with them the trait of being a blackguard, and perhaps that is what is meant. To even the little manliness his classical prototypes possessed, he lays no claim whatsoever, being a listless, effeminate noodle on the shady side of forty. But oh! the depth and strength of this elderly party's emotion for some bread-and-butter schoolgirl! Hide your heads, ye young Romeos and Leanders! This *blase* old beau loves with an hysterical fervor requiring four adjectives for every noun to properly describe.

It is well, dear ladies, for us old sinners that you study only books. Did you read mankind, you would know that the lad's shy stammering tells a truer tale than our bold eloquence. A boy's love comes from a full heart; a man's is more often the result of a full stomach. Indeed, a man's sluggish current may not be called love compared with the rushing fountain that wells up when a boy's heart is struck by the heavenly rod. If you would taste love, drink of the pure stream that youth pours out at your feet. Do not wait until it has become a muddy river before stooping to catch its waves. Or is it that you like its bitter flavor—that the clear, limpid water is insipid to your palate and that the pollution of its after-course gives it a relish to your lips? Must we believe those who tell us that a hand foul with the filth of a shameful life is the only one a young girl cares to be caressed by?
