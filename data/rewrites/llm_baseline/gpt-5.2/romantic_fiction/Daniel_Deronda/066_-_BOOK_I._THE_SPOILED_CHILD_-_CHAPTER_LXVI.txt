“One day still fierce ’mid many a day struck calm.”

The day after he had stood on Mirah’s threshold and taken her purse as if it were a daughter’s natural tribute, Lapidoth let the thought of her fade behind the louder, rasping summons of chance. Money in his hand was never money for bread first: it was an entrance-fee to that heated chapel of hazard where every cough, every clink, every whispered “Try again” had the authority of a priest. He had walked away with thirty shillings that were meant to keep a modest household sweet and steady, and before the smell of Mirah’s room had left his coat—lavender, warm wood, the faint metallic breath of the piano wires—he was already hunting the sharper perfume of stale cigars and sour spirits that hung about the place with “PYRAMIDS” painted on the blind.

There, amidst the chafing murmur of men who had learned to keep their hope quiet, he doubled, trebled, and then watched the whole little store slide away as if a hand had brushed crumbs from a table. The coin had had a brief, bright ring; then only the duller world remained—hunger not as a bodily pang so much as a humiliating necessity that interfered with the true appetite. Lapidoth’s craving was not for meat or ale, but for the poised second before the ball fell, for the suspended breath of possibility. That longing, grown absolute, made other excitements—shame, affection, remorse—thin and easily blown out, like a candle in a draught.

Still, a man cannot gamble on empty stomach forever, and Lapidoth’s vagrant life required ready money even for the minimum that kept him from collapse. When he came out at last, Mirah’s purse lay flat in his pocket, its little clasp pressing a reproach into his thigh at every step. He stood a moment on the pavement balancing his next move with a practised quickness. He could pawn the purse; he could return it with a bold face and a tale about paying a score for food and lodging. In him there survived a sharp sensibility to his own “claims.” Children’s earnings, children’s shelter, children’s pity—these were, to his mind, only delayed repayments for what he had once been pleased to call devotion.

And yet he had shrunk, at first, from the thought of Ezra. Ezra’s very existence had the unpleasantness of a narrow place where one must hold one’s breath. But as the hours passed and the pocket grew light, Lapidoth’s dread altered its object: the possible opening of supplies—some generous friend of Mirah’s, some soft-hearted benefactor—began to shine in his imagination brighter than any fear of a son’s contempt. Luck had been against him lately. Luck must turn. Why should not the turn begin at his daughter’s door?

So he went, toward the little square in Brompton, with the plan of making his reappearance easy—watching for Mirah on the steps, catching her as she came or went, taking shelter in her startled tenderness before Ezra could be summoned. But evening had come down, and with it a hush that made the house-sounds clearer. As he drew near, he did not need to ask whether Mirah was within. Her voice floated out—warm, tremulous, alive with a young heart’s insistence—singing “Herz, mein Herz.” The song’s German syllables, with their intimate vowels and softened consonants, carried a whole old world in them, a world Lapidoth had used like a stage and then deserted. The melody sat on the air like a hand on a brow; it quieted the square’s small noises—distant wheels, a door-latch, the faint bark of a dog—and for an instant, even in him, it woke a confused sense of having once belonged to something tender.

Within, Mirah sat at the piano, her fingers resting after a phrase, while Ezra listened with his eyes shut, as if the sound were the one safe element left to him. Mrs. Adam opened the door and came in with a face of troubled respectability.

“A gentleman below says he is your father, miss.”

Mirah started up as if the words were a knife. “I will go down to him,” she said, and her eyes flew to Ezra.

“No, Mirah, not so,” Ezra answered, the decision in his voice like iron. “Let him come up, Mrs. Adam.”

Mirah stood, her hands twisting together until her knuckles whitened. The room, with its accustomed quiet—polished wood, the faint smell of lamp-oil and the clean starch of Mrs. Adam’s curtains—seemed suddenly too small for what was coming. Ezra had risen too, and Mirah saw in his face something she had never seen: his brow knit, his lips hardened, and in his eyes a severity that did not belong to illness but to judgment.

When Mrs. Adam admitted the visitor, she could not help one glance at the three, and the likeness that leapt out—shape and line—made her inwardly mutter, “Father, sure enough.” Yet it was as if the same mould had been used for two utterly different castings: the elder with his thin, restless elegance turned shabby; the younger with a worn intensity that made him look as if some inner fire had burnt away the ordinary coverings of comfort.

Lapidoth had arranged his face for sorrow before he entered, and he began with it. But there was a real twitch in him when he met Ezra’s gaze.

“Well, Ezra, my boy, you hardly know me after so many years.”

“I know you—too well—father,” Ezra said slowly, the word father pronounced as if it were an accusation.

Lapidoth’s mouth tightened, then loosened into the glibness that speech always restored in him. “Ah, you are not pleased with me. I don’t wonder at it. Appearances have been against me. When a man gets into straits he can’t do just as he would by himself or anybody else. I’ve suffered enough, I know.” He turned quickly toward Mirah, drawing her little purse from his pocket as if it were a prop in a play, and held it out. “Here’s your little purse, my dear. I thought you’d be anxious about it because of that bit of writing. I’ve emptied it—you’ll see—for I had a score to pay for food and lodging. I knew you would like me to clear myself, and here I stand—without a single farthing in my pocket—at the mercy of my children. You can turn me out if you like, without getting a policeman. Say the word, Mirah; say, ‘Father, I’ve had enough of you; you made a pet of me, and spent your all on me, when I couldn’t have done without you; but I can do better without you now,’—say that, and I’m gone out like a spark. I shan’t spoil your pleasure again.”

The practiced tears were already in his voice—those tears he could summon as other men might summon a bow.

“You know I could never say it, father,” Mirah answered. Her voice shook, not with persuasion but with anguish, for she felt the falseness in his speech like grit between her teeth, and yet could not wrench herself free of the old tenderness that had grown into her like scar tissue.

Mirah moved a step nearer Ezra, as if physical closeness might steady both of them. But Ezra’s pain had found its own hard line.

“Mirah, my sister, leave us!” he said, and the authority startled her.

She looked at him, faltering, beseeching. Awe held her, and yet she could not go without flinging one small shield between Ezra and the father. She came close, slipped her hand into Ezra’s thin trembling one, and whispered—low, but not so low that Lapidoth could not catch it—“Remember, Ezra—you said my mother would not have shut him out.”

“Trust me, and go,” Ezra returned.

Mirah obeyed, but she could not descend fully. A few steps up the staircase, she sat, heart beating so loudly she feared it would be heard, listening for sounds below—listening as if the fate of all their years would declare itself in the tone of a voice, the scrape of a chair, the sudden hush. The house itself became an instrument: boards creaked in their old speech; the distant street carried its soft, living rustle—footsteps that belonged to nobody, the far-off wheel on wet stone. Compared with the brutal roar of public rooms, those domestic sounds were mercy. She clung to them, as if they might keep violence out.

Lapidoth, left alone with his son, tried to adjust himself to the coming ordeal. He had long trained himself into a kind of spiritual callousness: the ability to see suffering as incongruous, as something that might be evaded by a laugh or dissolved by tears. Ezra looked like a man speaking out of a grave, and Lapidoth tried to treat it as one treats a gloomy sermon heard by chance—endurable because one can wait it out. But he had not guessed what sort of voice lived in Ezra, nor how long it had been gathering strength in silence.

“This home that we have here,” Ezra began, and his words came with measured force, as if every syllable cost breath, “is maintained partly by the generosity of a beloved friend who supports me, and partly by the labors of my sister, who supports herself. While we have a home we will not shut you out from it. We will not cast you out to the mercy of your vices. For you are our father, and though you have broken your bond, we acknowledge ours.”

Lapidoth made a slight movement, as if to seize on that concession, but Ezra went on, and the air in the room seemed to grow sharper.

“But I will never trust you. You absconded with money, leaving your debts unpaid; you forsook my mother; you robbed her of her little child and broke her heart; you have become a gambler, and where shame and conscience were there sits an insatiable desire; you were ready to sell my sister—you had sold her, but the price was denied you.”

Each charge fell not like a shouted insult but like a hard object placed deliberately on the table. Ezra’s voice did not rise; its restraint made it crueller.

“The man who has done these things must never expect to be trusted any more. We will share our food with you—you shall have a bed, and clothing. We will do this duty to you, because you are our father. But you will never be trusted. You are an evil man: you made the misery of our mother. That such a man is our father is a brand on our flesh which will not cease smarting. But the Eternal has laid it upon us; and though human justice were to flog you for crimes, and your body fell helpless before the public scorn, we would still say, ‘This is our father; make way, that we may carry him out of your sight.’”

Lapidoth had prepared himself for rebuke; he had not prepared himself for this—this voice that seemed to come from a depth he had never entered, and to strike, not around him, but through him. Something in him—some spring of hysterical excitability—was touched into violent action. He flung himself into a chair and cried aloud, burying his face against the table. It was, as ever with him, both involuntary reaction and conscious resource; tears had long been one of his easiest currencies.

Ezra sat down again and said nothing. The effort of speaking had shaken him. His thin hands trembled on the arms of the chair, and his face had the look of a man who has moved too near the edge of his strength. He stared before him without seeing, as if his own words had opened a door on a cold place and the draught was still passing over him.

Up on the stairs, Mirah heard the sobbing and could endure it no longer. The sound—raw, broken, too familiar—pulled her like a hook. She opened the door and came in. At once her fear turned toward Ezra: she went straight to him, took his trembling hand in both of hers, and he pressed it as if it were a support. He did not speak, scarcely even looked at her, but his fingers clung.

Lapidoth, face still hidden, knew she had entered by the faint rustle of her dress and the altered hush of the room’s breathing. After a moment he lifted his head, pressed his handkerchief to his eyes, and put out his hand toward her.

“Good-bye, Mirah,” he said hoarsely, with the plaintive tone that had so often served him. “Your father will not trouble you again. He deserves to die like a dog by the roadside, and he will. If your mother had lived, she would have forgiven me—thirty-four years ago I put the ring on her finger under the Chuppa, and we were made one. She would have forgiven me, and we should have spent our old age together. But I haven’t deserved it. Good-bye.”

He rose as he said the last word, as if the theatre required an exit. But Mirah caught his hand and held it fast. She was not weeping; she looked frightened, awe-struck, as if she had seen the blade of something that could not be blunted.

“No, father, no!” she cried. Then, turning to Ezra with a helpless urgency, “Ezra, you have not forbidden him?—Stay, father, and leave off wrong things. Ezra, I cannot bear it. How can I say to my father, ‘Go and die!’”

“I have not said it,” Ezra answered, and the effort to speak again drained him. “I have said, stay and be sheltered.”

“Then you will stay, father—and be taken care of—and come with me,” Mirah said. She drew Lapidoth toward the door as if to prevent any further clash of words, any further thunder that might split what little peace their house still held.

This, in truth, was what Lapidoth wanted: not repentance, but safety; not amendment, but warmth, and a roof without payment. For the moment, as Mirah’s dutiful hand guided him, a kind of comfort softened him, and he could almost imagine a different habit of life—almost, as one imagines a tune one will never learn.

Mirah led him downstairs to her own parlor. The room held the gentler odors of a woman’s care—clean linen, beeswax on furniture, the faint sweetness of something once stewed and now cooled, as if apples and clove had long ago breathed into the air and stayed there. Outside, the street murmured in its evening key: the distant drip from an eave, a carriage passing with a brief rush like wind over grain, then quiet again.

“This is my sitting-room when I am not with Ezra,” she said, “and there is a bedroom behind which shall be yours. You will stay and be good, father. Think that you are come back to my mother, and that she has forgiven you—she speaks to you through me.”

Her tones implored him, but she could not add the old caresses; something in her had learned fear. Lapidoth, quick to recover his balance when the pressure lifted, began to talk of her voice—how it had improved, how it carried, how such singing was not to be wasted. When Mrs. Adam entered to lay out his supper, he turned his attention to her too, speaking with a certain airy politeness meant to show that, whatever his clothes declared, he was not a common man.

Food was set before him—plain, warming, the sort of fare that makes a small room feel safer: bread with a firm crust, a modest portion of something hot whose steam rose as a comfort, and the domestic clatter of spoon against plate. Lapidoth ate moderately, as he always did; even his vices were not supported by hearty appetite. He accepted the shelter as if it were his due, and the house—its quiet, its soft lamp-light, its little careful economies—closed around him like a garment he had once worn and had the shamelessness to put on again.

But when night came, wakefulness brought back the stronger visions that ruled him. While the household settled into the low, breathing quiet of sleep—stairs creaking once, a door softly shutting, then only the faint, humane noises of a house at rest—Lapidoth lay and wondered what money Mirah might still have. He went back, not to the sound of Ezra’s terrible judgment, but to the remembered acoustics of roulette: the dry tick of the ball, the mutter of numbers, the soft scrape of coin, the thin laughter that snapped like grease-fire at a table edge. England, he thought, was a cursed country for most things. Ezra did pass across the gaming-table in his mind, and Ezra’s words were audible—yet he passed like a ghost, insubstantial, and the words had their heart eaten out by the glittering arithmetic of chance, by the imagined movements that seemed to weave themselves into the very tissue of Lapidoth’s consciousness.
