Mrs. Lambert and her daughters stood at the gate of Oakhurst, their handkerchiefs fluttering like pale blossoms in the breeze as they waved farewell to the departing riders. The gentlemen trotted leisurely down the lane, their figures growing smaller and fainter until the bend of the road, where a cluster of limes stood sentinel, swallowed them from view. That very spot had witnessed many such leave-takings—husbands riding off to war, sons setting out for school—each vanishing behind those same trees, returning only when time and fate permitted.

The quiet rhythm of their lives, the hours spent in waiting and remembrance, had nurtured in these women a tenderness as deep as it was enduring. With the men gone, the house seemed to echo with absence. There was the empty chair at the table, the whip hanging idly in the hall, the fishing rod left propped in the corner—each a silent testament to the one who had just departed. At dinner, the cherry tart sat half-eaten, a bittersweet reminder of the boy who had forced himself to swallow a few bites despite his sorrow. The evening prayers were spoken without his voice in response. Night fell, and the mother lay awake, listening to the unfamiliar stillness of a house missing one of its own.

Beyond those rustling limes lay the world—a world from which the ladies of Oakhurst were, for the moment, shielded. Mrs. Lambert often grew pensive when she walked that way in the absence of her men. She spoke once of carving their names into the silvered bark, entwined with lovers’ knots, as was the gentle custom of the time. Miss Theo, whose talent for verse was as refined as her manners, composed a poem about the trees, which her proud mother promptly sent to a fashionable magazine.

“Now we are out of sight,” said Colonel Lambert, lifting his hat as they passed the limes. “My wife never fails to watch until we round this corner. I hope, Mr. Warrington, that you will return to see Oakhurst again—perhaps when my boys are home, and the hunting is better.”

“I have been happier here than I can say,” replied Harry. “You have made me feel as though I were leaving old friends behind.”

“The friend whose house will shelter us tonight has a son who is an old friend of our family,” said the Colonel. “My wife, who fancies herself a matchmaker, once hoped to pair him with one of my girls—until the young man chose to fall in love elsewhere.”

“Ah!” sighed Harry.

“Other hearts have been lost before his,” remarked the Colonel lightly.

“I beg your pardon—the gentleman’s name was—?”

“James Wolfe,” said the Colonel, smiling. “A young man still, though he has seen more of war than most men twice his age. He is the youngest lieutenant-colonel in the army—unless you count the scores of noblemen who take precedence by birth rather than merit.”

“Naturally,” said Harry, with the automatic deference of a colonial raised to respect rank.

“Yet he has commanded men old enough to be his father, and none resent it, for he has earned his place. He is loved by his regiment, fluent in languages, and as much a scholar as a soldier.”

Harry sighed again. “I feel the lack of my own education keenly. Had my brother lived, he would have represented our name far better. George was a scholar, a musician—he could hold his own among the learned men of our colony. I fear I am but a poor substitute.”

“Knowing your ignorance is the beginning of wisdom,” said the Colonel kindly. “At home, I thought myself a fine fellow, no doubt flattered by those around me. Now I see more clearly—though perhaps I am only boasting again.” He chuckled. “Our Virginia gentry know more of horses and hounds than books. I wish I knew less of the former and more of the latter.”

“Horses and hounds are not the worst tutors,” said the Colonel. “Not every man is made for learning, yet he may still be a worthy gentleman. What need have we all to be first in the world? His Royal Highness commands, I am a colonel, and Jack Hunt behind us was once a private soldier and is now an honest groom. Each has his place. Who is to say which is highest? When I was young, I dreamed of glory. Now I think of duty—and of those we left behind.”

At Westerham, they were welcomed by Colonel Wolfe’s parents—a stately matron and an old soldier whose recollections stretched back forty years—and by the Colonel himself, home briefly from his post at Maidstone. Harry studied the man with keen interest. Wolfe was not handsome—pale, lean, with high cheekbones and red hair—but his manner was warm, his conversation lively, and his respect for his elders unwavering.

At supper, the elder Mrs. Wolfe whispered to Colonel Lambert, her eyes alight with matchmaking schemes. Glasses were raised to “Miss Lowther,” and the young Colonel drank deeply, his homely face alight with devotion.

“If she were the daughter of King Charlemagne, she would still be worthy of him,” declared the old soldier stoutly.

“Oh, if you knew her, you would say no less!” laughed Wolfe.

The evening passed in easy camaraderie, and the next morning, Colonel Lambert turned his horse toward home, leaving Harry in Wolfe’s charge as they rode on to Tunbridge.

Wolfe spoke earnestly of his longing for quiet joys—a fireside, a loving wife, children at his knee. Harry listened politely, though his own heart yearned for adventure and renown.

At Tunbridge, Harry found his aunt, Madame de Bernstein, holding court in lavish lodgings. She introduced him with pride as the heir to a Virginia fortune vast as Kent and far more fertile.

“You must prepare to be flattered,” she warned him. “The world will treat you as a prince. Do not let it turn your head.”

“But why should I be thought so rich?” Harry asked.

“Because I have said so,” she replied archly. “And because a reputation for wealth opens doors. You need not spend a penny—let them think you hoard your gold. The mothers will smile, the daughters will curtsey. When I was young, I sought advancement too. Now I am old, and cards are my only diversion. You will see high play tonight—but do not look shocked. I cannot live without it.”

Harry, remembering the crude gaming at Castlewood, felt a pang of distaste. But before he could speak, Lady Maria entered, and the Baroness’s sharp eyes darted between them, as if measuring secrets yet unspoken.
