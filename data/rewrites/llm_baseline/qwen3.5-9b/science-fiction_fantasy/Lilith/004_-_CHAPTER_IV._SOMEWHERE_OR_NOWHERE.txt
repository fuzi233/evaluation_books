The sun burned with an intensity that felt unnatural, casting a harsh glare across the landscape. Yet, I suspected the fair weather would not hold. I lifted my hand to inspect the milky sapphire ring upon my finger, checking the tiny star embedded within it. To my disappointment, the celestial body inside seemed even more indistinct than usual, blurred as if viewed through frosted glass.

Leaving the breakfast table, I moved to the window to examine the stone again. Outside, the lawn was slick with the remnants of a heavy night's rain. A thrush struggled near a discarded snail shell, its movements frantic against the damp earth. Turning the ring in my fingers to align the gem with the sun, expecting a sharp reflection, I froze.

Gazing back at me from the milky blue depths of the sapphire was a keen, black eye.

Startled, I dropped the ring. When I bent to retrieve it, the eye was gone. Simultaneously, the sun vanished behind a thick, dark vapor. Within moments, the entire sky churned gray. The air grew stiflingly hot, then a sudden gust tore through the room. Lightning flashed, followed by a singular, sharp crack of thunder. Rain began to fall in torrential sheets.

I had thrown open the window, standing exposed to the driving storm, when I spotted a figure approaching over the sodden grass. It was a raven, moving with a solemn, deliberate gait, utterly indifferent to the deluge. Recognizing him instantly, I breathed a sigh of relief that I remained safely on the ground floor. Yet, a gnawing conviction settled in my gut: *if I am not careful, disaster will strike.*

He drew closer, executed a profound bow, and then, with a sudden, powerful leap, landed squarely on the windowsill. Without hesitation, he stepped over the ledge, dropped into the room, and walked calmly toward the door. Assuming he intended for the library, I prepared to follow, vowing not to take a single step unless necessary. Instead, he turned—not toward the stairs or the library—but toward a small, forgotten door leading to a grassy patch tucked between two sections of the rambling old house.

Hastening to open it, I watched him step out onto the creeper-covered porch. He paused, staring up at the rain, which cascaded like a massive, translucent curtain. Standing in the doorway behind him, I waited. Another flash of lightning illuminated the scene, followed by a rolling boom of distant thunder. The raven turned his head over his shoulder, locking eyes with me—a silent question: *"Did you hear that?"*—before swiveling back to contemplate the storm with apparent approval.

His posture was so remarkably human, his mannerisms so deliberate, that the words escaped me before I could check them. "Fine weather for the worms, Mr. Raven!" I blurted out.

"Yes," he croaked, his voice familiar and gravelly. "The ground will be lovely for them to emerge from and retreat into! It must be a magnificent time on the steppes of Uranus," he added, tilting his head upward. "I believe it is raining there too; it has been doing so all week!"

"Why would that make it a grand time?" I asked.

"Because the creatures there are all burrowers," he explained. "Like the field mice and moles here. They will thrive for ages to come."

"How do you know that, if I may ask?" I pressed.

"As anyone would who has witnessed it firsthand," he replied. "It is a spectacular sight, though difficult to acclimate to, when the earth heaves and a beast emerges. You might mistake it for a hairy elephant or a deinotherium, but none of these animals resemble anything we have known here. I was nearly terrified the first time I saw the dry-bog serpent wallow out—what a head, what a mane! And such eyes! ... But the shower is nearly over. It will cease directly after the next clap of thunder. Here it comes!"

A flash lit up the sky, followed swiftly by the rumble. Instantly, the rain stopped.

"Now we must depart!" the raven announced, stepping to the front of the porch.

"Depart where?" I asked.

"To where we must go," he answered firmly. "Did you truly believe you had arrived home? I warned you: there is no coming and going at leisure until you are actually *home*!"

"I do not wish to go," I stated flatly.

"That makes no difference—at least, not much," he countered. "This is the path!"

"I am perfectly content exactly where I am."

"You think so, but you do not. Come along."

He hopped from the porch onto the grass and turned, waiting for me. With stubborn resolve, I refused. "I will not leave the house today."

"You will enter the garden!" he insisted.

"I yield for the moment," I relented, stepping off the porch.

Sunlight pierced the breaking clouds, causing the raindrops on the grass to sparkle like diamonds. The raven walked ahead.

"You'll soak your feet!" I cried.

"And mud my beak," he retorted, immediately plunging his bill deep into the sod. He withdrew it, dragging out a large, wriggling red worm. He flung his head back, tossing the worm high into the air. It unfurled great wings, resplendent in crimson and black, soaring effortlessly upward.

"Tut! Tut!" I exclaimed. "You are mistaken, Mr. Raven! Worms are not the larvae of butterflies!"

"Never mind," he croaked. "It serves for once! I am not a scholar currently, but a sexton at... at a certain cemetery—more properly speaking—in... at... no matter where!"

"I see! You cannot keep your spade idle; when there is nothing to bury, you must excavate something! Only, you ought to consider what you unearth before sending it flying! No creature should be permitted to forget its origins!"

"Why?" the raven asked.

"Because pride will swell, and it will cease to recognize its superiors."

No one realizes they are acting foolishly while they do it.

"Where *do* the worms originate?" the raven asked suddenly, his curiosity piqued.

"From the earth, as you have just observed!" I answered.

"Yes, last!" he replied. "But they could not have originated from it first—for that would never return to it!" he added, gazing upward.

I looked up as well, seeing only a small, dark cloud whose edges glowed red, reminiscent of a sunset.

"The sun is not setting!" I exclaimed, amazed.

"Oh, no!" the raven corrected. "That red hue belongs to the worm."

"You see the result of making creatures forget their beginnings!" I cried, warming to the subject.

"It is well, surely, if it allows them to rise higher and grow larger!" he returned. "But truthfully, I merely teach them to locate it!"

"Do you intend to fill the air with worms?"

"That is the duty of a sexton. If only the rest of the clergy understood it as well!"

His beak plunged into the soft turf again, and out tumbled another wriggling worm. He tossed it aloft, and it flew away. I glanced behind me and let out a cry of dismay. I had only moments prior declared I would not leave the house, and now I was a stranger in a strange land!

"What right do you have to treat me thus, Mr. Raven?" I demanded, deeply offended. "Am I, or am I not, a free agent?"

"A man is as free as he chooses to make himself, never an atom freer," the raven answered. "You have no right to compel me to act against my will!"

"When you possess a will, you will discover that no one can control you."

"You wrong me in the very essence of my individuality!" I persisted.

"If you were an individual, I could not harm you; therefore, since you are not, I do not hesitate."

Surrounding me was a dense pine forest, and my eyes scanned the trees desperately, hoping to spot a familiar glimmer that might guide me home. Alas, how could I possibly call that house *HOME*, where every door and window opened outward into the void, and even the garden offered no sanctuary? I suppose my expression betrayed my confusion.

"Perhaps it may comfort you," the raven said gently, "to be told that you have not yet left your house, nor has your house abandoned you. At the same time, it cannot contain you, nor do you inhabit it!"

"I do not understand you," I replied. "Where am I?"

"In the region of the seven dimensions," he answered, emitting a strange, vibrating noise from his throat accompanied by a flicker of his tail feathers. "You had best follow me carefully for a moment, lest you injure someone!"

"There is no one to hurt but yourself, Mr. Raven! I confess I would prefer to hurt you!"

"That you perceive no one is precisely where the danger lies. However, do you see that large tree to your left, approximately thirty yards away?"

"Of course I do: why shouldn't I?" I answered testily.

"Ten minutes ago, you did not see it, and now you do not know where it stands!"

"I do."

"Where do you suppose it stands?"

"Why *there*, where you know it is!"
