Good Helen Pendennis, ever since her son Arthur came staggering back from the brink of a fever-induced delirium, had executed a full, no-nonsense domestic coup over every square inch of his life. She had claimed his drawers, his closets, every half-buttoned shirt and laddered stocking within them, and naturally, the random letters tucked in amongst his socks and cravats—someone had to sort those out, obviously, when the poor boy was too wobbly to stand, let alone answer post. She had been nursing a very specific, unspoken anxiety about the dreadful Fanny Bolton business the whole time, too, too polite to badger her recovering son about it but fretting so hard over it at 2 a.m. she could barely sleep.

The woman even unscrewed the brass front-door knocker off Arthur’s Temple chambers, arguing that the postman’s thundering double rap would jolt the patient right out of his much-needed rest. She screened every single piece of mail that came through the door, no exceptions: angry bootmakers chasing payment for dancing boots Pen swore he’d bought for a single party, hatters begging him to settle his bill before Saturday, the whole gamut of reminders that Arthur was the sort of nice careless boy who forgot money existed until someone waved a receipt in his face. Helen, thrifty to the point of near-parsimony, had a little nest egg stashed away, and she gleefully poured chunks of it into wiping out his tiny pile of debts. After all, any boy who gets his mom to sort his mail and pay his bills while he recovers is already winning at life, and there’s no truer test of a man’s unburdened conscience than how happy he is to hear the postman knock. The good folks cheer for the rat-tat; the rascals dive under the sofa cushions. Helen was only being extra kind, after all, sparing Pen all that tedious hassle while he healed.

There was no damning evidence to be found in his drawers, though, no clear answers about the Fanny Bolton mess. So Helen dragged her brother-in-law Major Pendennis off for a private huddle one sunny day at Richmond, while Pen and Warrington loitered on the terrace bench watching stray dogs wander past. She spilled all her jumbled terrors to him, holding back a small handful of her most mortifying worries, the way every spendthrift hides a few bills from their parents and every society wife omits a couple of silk gowns from her dressmaker’s accounts.

When she asked the Major what on earth to do about this horrid, dreadful affair, and if he knew anything about it, the old gentleman screwed his face up so tight you couldn’t tell if he was suppressing a cackle or had just tasted a very sour plum, squinted at her out of his tiny little eyes, then dropped his gaze back to the carpet. “My dear good creature, I know nothing about it, and I’ve no desire to. If you ask my opinion? You’d best know nothing about it either. Young men will be young men, begad, and if you think our boy is some sort of Jo—”

“Pray spare me that talk,” Helen cut in, standing so straight she looked like a teetotal church steeple.

“My dear, I didn’t start this conversation, if you’ll permit me to say,” the Major replied, bowing so blandly you’d never guess he’d got up to ten times worse than anything Fanny Bolton could possibly be involved in back in his army days.

“I can’t bear to hear such a dreadful sin spoken of so lightly,” the widow said, tears pricking the corners of her eyes. “I can’t stand the thought my boy would do something so shameful. I almost wish he’d died before he ever acted so out of honour. My little Arthur, who was always so sweet and good as a small child! How could he fall so low as to—to—”

“As to flirt with a nice little working-class girl?” the Major snickered. “Egad, if every mother in England broke their heart over that, we’d have no mothers left! Don’t cry, my dear, I hate seeing a woman cry, never could stand it. How do we even know anything serious happened? Has Arthur said a word about it?”

“His silence proves it!” Helen sobbed into her handkerchief.

“Nonsense. There are subjects a young man simply cannot bring himself to blab to his mamma about, you know,” the Major hinted, leaning back like a man who knew every single one of those subjects by heart.

“She wrote to him!” Helen gasped from behind her cambric mask.

“What, before he got sick? Nothing more natural.”

“No—after,” the poor lady wheezed. “I don’t think it came before, that is, I—”

“Only since. And I suppose you took charge of his post while he was too ill to read it, didn’t you?”

“I am the unhappiest mother in the whole world!” Helen wailed.

“The unhappiest because your son is a grown man and not a hermit?” the Major huffed. “Careful, my dear. If you’ve been hiding letters from him, you might do far more damage than the little trumpery drama you’re panicking over. Arthur’s got a stubborn, proud streak—you go rifling his mail and hiding notes from him, and you’ll drive a wedge between the two of you that you’ll regret for the rest of your life, mark my words.”

“There was only one letter!” Helen burst out. “Just a tiny one, only a few words. Look—how can you speak so lightly of this?”

The Major almost burst out laughing when she said “only a tiny one”, he was so tickled he could barely speak, even while he felt real pity for the poor distraught widow in front of him. Their moral worldviews were about as different as a temperance tract and a club bar bill, after all.

“I’d recommend you,” he said, trying to sound serious, “seal that letter back up—those cheap wafer seals are a doddle to fake—and tuck it in with the rest of his post, give it to him when he asks for his old correspondence. If the seal won’t stick? Say you mistook it for a tradesman’s bill, simple as that.”

“I cannot lie to my son,” Helen said, all rigid piety. She’d hidden that letter in her bag two days before they left the Temple, after watching the post slot for it every single day since Pen fell ill, rummaging through all his other letters to make sure she got her hands on that exact one. She pulled it out and passed it to the Major.

“Arthur Pendennis, Esq.,” he read, squinting at the wobbly, scrawled handwriting, a small smirk on his face. “No, no, I won’t read the rest. You’ve already told me it’s just bad-spelt prayers for his health and a note begging to see him? Well, that’s no sin. And since you’re asking for advice? My man Morgan dug around a little, and Doctor Goodenough looked into it too. Turns out this girl was absolutely smitten with Arthur, he paid for her trip to Vauxhall Gardens—Morgan heard that from that Irish chap Flanagan, the laundress’s acquaintance. The girl’s dad’s a violent old drunk who beats her mum, who swears up and down her daughter’s completely innocent to her husband, but then told Goodenough Arthur treated her baby terribly. Whole thing’s still a total mystery. Do you want me to badger Pen until he tells me the whole truth? He’s as honourable a lad as ever lived, he’d spill it immediately.”

“Honour?” Helen scoffed, bitter as a very sour lemon. “Oh brother, what sort of honour are you talking about? If my boy’s guilty, he has to marry her. I’d get down on my knees and beg him to do it.”

“Good God, are you mad?” the Major squawked so loud the sparrows on the terrace took off in a panic. He suddenly remembered exactly how stubborn Arthur was: if his mamma begged him to marry the girl out of honour, the fool would absolutely do it, just for the dramatic principle of the thing. “My dear sister, have you lost your senses?” he said, softening his tone after a dramatic pause. “What right do we have to assume anything untoward happened between them? Look at this letter—she’s sad at home, her dad’s mean, she says she was his nurse, all the spelling’s wrong, that’s it. That’s not evidence of anything except a silly little girl pining after him. She didn’t even step foot in his rooms until he was so delirious he couldn’t tell his own laundress apart from the Archbishop of Canterbury, Mrs. Flanagan told Morgan that herself. She turned up with old Mr. Bows, who was kind enough to come drag me out of Stillbrook that night, actually—never even paid his cab fare, come to think of it, what a nice old chap. There’s nothing to this story at all.”

“You really think so? Thank Heaven, thank God!” Helen cried, jumping up. “I’m going right now to ask Arthur, look at him over there on the terrace with Mr. Warrington, talking to those little kids. He always loved children. He’s innocent, he must be! Let me go ask him.”

The Major’s private opinion was very different, of course—he was judging Arthur by what he himself would have done at 22, after all. If she runs and asks him, he’ll blab the whole stupid truth, and all our plans are ruined, he thought. He made one last desperate effort to stop her.

“My dear good soul,” he said, patting her hand, “think for a second. He never told you about this affair, right? What right do you have to dig it up unprompted? You say you believe he’s a man of honour—why are you doubting that honour over nothing? Who’s even accusing him? Some anonymous busybody, no one’s brought any actual specific charges. If something terrible had happened, the girl’s parents would be banging on our door screaming for compensation, wouldn’t they? You don’t have to entertain some random anonymous rumour, and Pen doesn’t have to prove he’s innocent of it. At that rate, you might as well make him marry that gin-soaked old Irish laundress Mrs. Flanagan, just because she popped in to change his bed sheets when he was sick.”

Helen burst out laughing through her tears, and the Major knew he’d won.

“Marry Mrs. Flanagan, begad!” he went on, tapping her thin little hand. “No. The boy’s told you nothing, you know nothing, he’s innocent, obviously. Now what’s our next move? Suppose he did happen to be a little sweet on this girl? We’ll stick him out at Fairoaks all by his lonesome, with nothing to do all day but moon over that little crush, and a silly little infatuation will turn into a full-blown tragic romance before you can blink. Lonely country houses are the worst places in the world for that sort of nonsense, nothing to distract a fellow from his own moping. We need to occupy him! Amuse him! Take him abroad! He’s only ever been to Paris for a long weekend lark, he’s never done proper travelling. Goodenough says he barely escaped death with that fever, so he’s going to need careful nursing—you can come and watch over him, of course, bring Miss Laura Bell along, and I’ll invite Warrington too. Arthur’s absolutely besotted with Warrington, can’t stand to be apart from him. He’s one of the best young fellows I ever met, family’s old as the hills, perfect company.”

“Does Mr. Warrington know anything about this affair?” Helen asked. “He was away for two months before it all happened, Pen wrote me that much.”

“Not a word! I pumped him myself, never heard a single mention of it, I swear to you,” the Major yelped, suddenly alarmed at the thought of Warrington spilling the beans. “And my dear, you’d best not say a word to him about it either, it’s far too delicate and painful a subject.”

The simple widow squeezed her brother-in-law’s hand. “Thank you, brother. You’ve been so kind, you’ve given me so much peace. I’m going to my room to rest and thank God my boy is innocent. He is innocent, isn’t he?”

“Yes, my dearest creature, absolutely,” the old man said, kissing her hand, so touched by her tender innocence he was almost softhearted. He watched her walk away, fondness warring with amused exasperation in his chest. “Innocent!” he muttered to himself. “I’d swear that until my face turned black rather than make that poor good soul sad.”

Victory secured, the old warrior flopped down on the sofa, draped his yellow silk handkerchief over his face, and settled in for a cozy little nap, snoring so steadily it sounded like a very rickety bagpipe solo. Down on the terrace, the two young men dawdled away the sunny hours, Pen chattering nonstop about his brilliant plans for a new novel and even a brand new tragedy, the lines of which he started declaiming at the top of his lungs while Warrington snickered and pretended to be deeply impressed by his dramatic deliveries.

The Major’s nap got interrupted by Laura Bell walking in. She’d been visiting her old friend Lady Rockminster, who had rented a summer villa nearby, and the old lady had sent over armfuls of grapes, partridges, and other little treats for Arthur the moment she heard he was sick. She’d tried to convince Laura to come stay with her, but Laura wouldn’t leave Helen, who’d run herself so ragged nursing Arthur that Doctor Goodenough had ended up prescribing rest for her too.

The Major jumped awake immediately, ever the gallant old charmer. “Wherever did you find those roses blooming on your cheeks?” he trilled. “I can’t think of a more delightful way to wake up from a nap than the sight of you!” Laura had plenty of good humour and common sense, which made her rather enjoy teasing the old club habitué about his worldliness and silly stories about aristocrats, but today she was in no mood to joke. She’d just been with Helen, she said, and the poor woman looked completely worn out, she was very worried about her health. The Major hinted that Arthur’s illness and other recent anxieties had worn her down, and Laura blushed bright red, catching the allusion, but said nothing. The old diplomat pitched his perfect plan: they’d all go abroad for the autumn, to a quiet little spa town on the Rhine, where Helen could rest and recover, and Arthur could shake off any leftover silly infatuations and become a new man. Naturally Laura wouldn’t leave her dear Helen, of course not, she agreed instantly.

Helen, meanwhile, had sat in her room for an hour turning the whole plan over in her head, and was now as giddy as a schoolboy who’s just opened a book of travel adventures. The farther they went the better, she thought, somewhere so far away not even a sad little letter from Fanny could find them. She fumbled open her desk, pulled out her bank book, and counted all her little savings. If they needed more, she could sell her diamond cross, or borrow a little extra from Laura. “Let’s go, let’s go as soon as he can stand the journey,” she thought, praying Doctor Goodenough would hurry up and give them permission to quit England.

The good doctor drove over to dine with them that very day. “If you keep agitating yourself like this,” he scolded Helen, “you’re going to end up the one laid up in bed, then Laura will have to nurse you, then she’ll get sick too, and I’ll be running back and forth here for free so often my wife will divorce me. Mrs. Goodenough is already jealous of all of you, swears I’m half in love with my patients. Get out of the country as soon as you can, so I can have a little peace at home.”

When they told Arthur the plan to go abroad, he was over the moon with excitement. He immediately decided to start growing a moustache that very second, to get his mouth all trained for perfect French and German pronunciation, only to be mortified when the new hair came in a very bright, very noticeable shade of red. He’d been dreading the thought of three boring months at Fairoaks, listening to old Portman’s long sermons and the local squire’s never-ending Peninsular War stories, with none of the Claverings around for company. “I want to get out of the country,” he confessed to Warrington. “While you were away I ran into a very stupid temptation, and I’m so glad my fever put a stop to it before I made a total fool of myself.” He spilled the whole messy Vauxhall story to his friend, who listened very gravely.

“Not only would you have made your whole life miserable,” Warrington said, “it would have absolutely ruined that poor girl’s life too. And think of the pain it would have caused your mother and all your friends.”

“Not a word to my mother, I beg you!” Pen yelped in alarm. “A scandal like that would kill her, I truly believe it. And the best trick for dodging that sort of danger, I’ve figured out, is not to stand around facing it. Turn your back and run for the hills!”

“Were you very much smitten with her?” Warrington asked.

“Hm!” Pen said, affecting the world-weary air of a veteran rake. “She dropped all her h’s, but she was a dear little girl.”

All you poor silly sweet young maidens out there, take note! If you could hear the man you fancy bragging about you over cigars and coffee in his club, passing your crumpled little love notes around to all his friends to snicker at, you’d never pick up a pen to write him a secret note again. There’s a sort of misdeed that isn’t even complete until the rogue brags about it afterwards, and the man who betrays your honour once will absolutely betray your secret too.

“It’s easy to fall when the temptation’s there, and hard to fight it,” Warrington said gloomily. “And you’re right. The very best thing to do when that sort of danger looms is turn your back and run.”

They got back to talking about the travel plans, and Arthur begged Warrington to come along, insisting he’d have no fun at all without his best friend there. But George shook his head. He couldn’t go, he had engagements, the truth was he simply didn’t have the money to travel, end of story.
