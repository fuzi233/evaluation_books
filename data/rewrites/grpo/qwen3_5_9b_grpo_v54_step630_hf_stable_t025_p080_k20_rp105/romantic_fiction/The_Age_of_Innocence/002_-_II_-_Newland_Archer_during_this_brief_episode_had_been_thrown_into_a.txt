Newland Archer found himself trapped in a peculiar state of social vertigo. It was mortifying that the box commanding the undivided attention of the male half of New York should be occupied by his betrothed, seated between her mother and aunt. For a fleeting second, he failed to recognize the lady in the Empire gown, unable to fathom why her presence sparked such a tempest among the initiated. Then, clarity struck, accompanied by a sudden surge of indignation. Indeed, no one would have suspected the Mingotts of attempting such a maneuver.

Yet, they had. The hushed whispers drifting from the rear seats confirmed it beyond doubt: the young woman was May Welland's cousin, the relative universally whispered about as "poor Ellen Olenska." Newland knew she had arrived from Europe only days prior; he had even received a note from Miss Welland—delivered without disapproval—confirming that she had visited Ellen, who was currently residing with the elderly Mrs. Mingott. Newland possessed a generous heart; he admired the Mingott family's fierce loyalty to their few "black sheep" and believed that true nobility lay in kindness, even in private. However, receiving the Countess in the domestic sphere was one thing, but parading her publicly at the Opera, specifically in the very box where his own engagement to May was to be announced within weeks, was a different matter entirely. He felt exactly as Old Sillerton Jackson did: he could not believe the Mingotts would dare.

Of course, Newland knew that whatever a man dared within the bounds of Fifth Avenue, Old Mrs. Manson Mingott, the matriarch, would dare. He had always held her in high regard. Despite her origins as Catherine Spicer of Staten Island—a name marred by a father mysteriously discredited and lacking the wealth or status to erase the stigma—she had allied herself with the head of the Mingott fortune. She had married two daughters to "foreigners," an Italian marquis and an English banker, and capped her audacity by constructing a vast house of pale cream-colored stone in an inaccessible wilderness near Central Park, defying the era's preference for brown sandstone.

Her foreign daughters had become legends, never returning to visit their mother. Sedentary and corpulent in her habits, the old lady had philosophically remained at home, ruling from her cream-colored palace—a structure rumored to be modeled after Parisian aristocratic hotels—as if living above Thirty-fourth Street or possessing French doors that swung open rather than sliding up were entirely normal. Everyone, including Mr. Sillerton Jackson, agreed that Old Catherine lacked beauty, a gift that in New York justified every triumph and excused every failure. Uncharitable observers claimed she had risen through sheer will and a hardness of heart, a haughty effrontery somehow sanctified by the extreme decency of her private life. Her husband, Mr. Manson Mingott, had died young, leaving her with a fortune tied up with the caution of the Spicers, but his bold widow had gone forth fearlessly. She mingled in foreign circles, married her daughters into questionable fashion, dined with Dukes and Ambassadors, and befriended Papists and opera singers alike. Yet, as Sillerton Jackson was the first to proclaim, her reputation remained untarnished, the only distinction being her lack of the earlier Catherine's beauty.

Mrs. Mingott had successfully untied her husband's fortune and lived in affluence for half a century, yet memories of her early struggles rendered her excessively thrifty. While she insisted on the finest fabrics and furniture, she refused to spend lavishly on transient pleasures. Consequently, her table was as meager as Mrs. Archer's, and her wines offered no redemption. Her relatives deemed this penury a discredit to the Mingott name, traditionally synonymous with fine living, yet guests continued to flock to her despite the "made dishes" and flat champagne. In response to her son Lovell's attempts to restore family credit by hiring the city's best chef, she would laugh, "What's the use of two good cooks in one family, now that I've married the girls and can't eat sauces?"

As Newland mused on these complexities, he turned his gaze back to the Mingott box. He observed that Mrs. Welland and her sister-in-law faced their semicircle of critics with the Mingottian *aplomb* instilled by Old Catherine, though May Welland betrayed a heightened flush—perhaps because she knew he was watching her—with a sense of the situation's gravity. As for the cause of the commotion, she sat gracefully in her corner, eyes fixed on the stage, revealing, as she leaned forward, a glimpse of shoulder and bosom that New York was not accustomed to seeing in ladies who wished to remain unnoticed.

To Newland, nothing seemed more abhorrent than an offense against "Taste," that distant divinity whose mere visible representative was "Form." Madame Olenska's pale, serious face seemed appropriate to her tragic situation, but the way her dress, devoid of a tucker, sloped away from her thin shoulders shocked and troubled him. He dreaded the thought of May Welland being influenced by a young woman so careless of the dictates of Taste.

"After all," he heard a younger man begin behind him as the Mephistopheles-and-Martha scenes unfolded, "just *what* happened?"

"Well—she left him; nobody attempts to deny that."

"He's an awful brute, isn't he?" continued the young inquirer, a candid Thorley, clearly preparing to champion the lady's cause.

"The very worst; I knew him at Nice," said Lawrence Lefferts with authority. "A half-paralysed white sneering fellow—rather handsome head, but eyes with a lot of lashes. Well, I'll tell you the sort: when he wasn't with women he was collecting china. Paying any price for both, I understand."

A general laugh rippled through the box, and the young champion pressed, "Well, then----?"

"Well, then; she bolted with his secretary."

"Oh, I see." The champion's face fell.

"It didn't last long, though: I heard of her a few months later living alone in Venice. I believe Lovell Mingott went out to get her. He said she was desperately unhappy. That's all right—but this parading her at the Opera's another thing."

"Perhaps," young Thorley hazarded, "she's too unhappy to be left at home."

This was greeted with an irreverent laugh, and the youth blushed deeply, trying to feign an air of having intended a "_double entendre_."

"Well—it's queer to have brought Miss Welland, anyhow," someone murmured in a low tone, casting a sidelong glance at Archer.

"Oh, that's part of the campaign: Granny's orders, no doubt," Lefferts laughed. "When the old lady does a thing, she does it thoroughly."

The act was concluding, and a general stir erupted in the box. Suddenly, Newland Archer felt compelled to decisive action. The desire to be the first to enter Mrs. Mingott's box, to proclaim to the waiting world his engagement to May Welland, and to stand by her through whatever difficulties her cousin's anomalous situation might entail, had abruptly overruled all scruples and hesitations. He hurried through the red corridors to the farther side of the house.

Upon entering the box, his eyes met Miss Welland's. She had instantly understood his motive, though the family dignity both considered a supreme virtue prevented her from voicing it. The inhabitants of their world lived in an atmosphere of faint implications and pale delicacies, and the fact that they understood each other without a word seemed to draw them closer than any explanation could. Her eyes said, "You see why Mamma brought me," and his answered, "I would not for the world have had you stay away."

"You know my niece Countess Olenska?" Mrs. Welland enquired as she shook hands with her future son-in-law. Archer bowed without extending his hand, adhering to the custom of introduction to a lady; Ellen Olenska bent her head slightly, keeping her pale-gloved hands clasped on her huge fan of eagle feathers. After greeting Mrs. Lovell Mingott, a large blonde lady in creaking satin, Newland sat beside his betrothed. In a low tone, he said, "I hope you've told Madame Olenska that we're engaged? I want everybody to know—I want you to let me announce it this evening at the ball."

Miss Welland's face flushed like the dawn, and she looked at him with radiant eyes. "If you can persuade Mamma," she said. "But why should we change what is already settled?" He offered no verbal answer, only the silent affirmation of his gaze, and she added, smiling more confidently, "Tell my cousin yourself: I give you leave. She says she used to play with you when you were children."

She made way for him by pushing back her chair, and promptly, with a touch of ostentation designed for the whole house to witness his action, Newland seated himself at the Countess Olenska's side.

"We *did* use to play together, didn't we?" she asked, turning her grave eyes to his. "You were a horrid boy, and kissed me once behind a door; but it was your cousin Vandie Newland, who never looked at me, that I was in love with." Her glance swept the horseshoe curve of boxes. "Ah, how this brings it all back to me—I see everybody here in knickerbockers and pantalettes," she said, her slightly foreign accent trailing softly, her eyes returning to his face.

Though agreeable, the young man was shocked that they should reflect such an unseemly picture of the august tribunal before which, at that very moment, her case was being tried. Nothing could be in worse taste than misplaced flippancy. He answered somewhat stiffly, "Yes, you have been away a very long time."

"Oh, centuries and centuries; so long," she replied, "that I'm sure I'm dead and buried, and this dear old place is heaven;" which, for reasons he could not define, struck Newland Archer as an even more disrespectful way of describing New York society.
