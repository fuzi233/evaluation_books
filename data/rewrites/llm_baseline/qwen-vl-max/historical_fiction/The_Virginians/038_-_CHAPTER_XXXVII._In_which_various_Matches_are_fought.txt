Reading the *London Advertiser* with his morning coffee, Mr. Warrington came across an announcement that stirred his interest: a grand contest of skill between two of England’s most celebrated pugilists, Sutton and Figg. The event promised to be a spectacle of manly prowess, and without hesitation he decided to attend. That very day, he set off in the phaeton he had purchased the previous afternoon, driving himself from Covent Garden toward Marybone Fields, where the famed Wooden House stood.

The young Virginian was not entirely familiar with the roads, and his journey proved somewhat circuitous. He wandered through quiet lanes behind Mr. Whitfield’s round Tabernacle on Tottenham Road, then lost his way among the open fields near Middlesex Hospital. Yet eventually, he arrived at the arena, finding it already thronged with eager spectators. A motley crowd gathered outside—louts and ruffians, beggars and cripples, all clamoring for attention or alms. Shoeblack boys tumbled over one another to polish his boots; flower girls offered nosegays; fruit-sellers called out their wares; piemen, tramps, and strolling players filled the air with noise and movement. A flag fluttered atop the building, and from the stage, a manager accompanied by a drummer and horn-blower announced repeatedly that the noble English pastime was about to begin.

Mr. Warrington paid his admission and took a seat in the gallery, where he had a clear view of the platform below. His servant, Gumbo, sat in the amphitheatre beneath, occasionally slipping out to enjoy a pint of ale or play cards with other coachmen waiting beside their masters’ carriages. The scene was alive with the bustle of servants—footmen, lacqueys, liveried men—who swarmed around every great house, chapel, and theatre. They were everywhere: in anterooms, on staircases, in halls, feasting, gambling, and demanding tips. Once a proud and dignified class, they now seemed like relics of a vanishing age—tall, melancholy figures in powdered wigs and buckled shoes, still visible on formal occasions, but doomed, like the Red Indians of America, to fade into history.

Before the main combatants appeared, lesser contests entertained the crowd. A boxing match followed, but the fighters lacked spirit, and neither suffered serious harm. The audience grew restless. Then came cudgel-play, but the injuries were minor, and the crowd began to grumble. “The masters! The masters!” they roared. At last, the champions emerged.

First came Sutton, sword in hand, who saluted the crowd with a flourish and bowed deeply to a stout gentleman seated in a private box. Shortly after, Figg entered, greeted with approval by the same man. Both fighters wore only their shirts, their heads shaved, bearing scars from countless battles. Each wore an armiger—a colored ribbon—on his sword arm. They shook hands, and as a contemporary poet once wrote, “The word it was bilboe.”

The duel began. Figg struck with such force that had it connected with Sutton’s head, it would have severed it clean as a carrot cut by a knife. But Sutton parried the blow with his own blade, and the impact was so great that Figg’s weapon shattered in his grasp. New swords were brought forth. Blood soon flowed—Sutton drew first blood from Figg’s side, sparking cheers from his supporters. But Figg appealed to the audience, particularly to the stout gentleman in the box, explaining that the wound had come from the broken sword, not from any fault in his defense. While the crowd debated this, Mr. Warrington noticed a man in riding clothes and a plain wig enter the private box. To his surprise, he recognized Lord March and Ruglen, his friend from Tunbridge Wells.

Lord March, though not overly effusive, showed marked respect to the stout gentleman, receiving banknotes with a deep bow. When he spotted Mr. Warrington, he finished his business and crossed to the young Virginian’s gallery, greeting him warmly. They watched the duel unfold—both men fought with remarkable skill and courage. After swords, they took up quarter-staves, and the battle continued long and fiercely. In the end, victory belonged to Figg, the elder champion.

As the fight raged, a thunderstorm broke overhead. Mr. Warrington gladly accepted a ride back to town in Lord March’s carriage, leaving his own phaeton for Gumbo to drive home.

The experience had delighted Harry. “This,” he declared, “is sport indeed—the finest I’ve seen since arriving in England.” As was his habit, he paused, then sighed, “I wish…”—but stopped himself. “No, I don’t,” he added.

“What do you wish?” asked Lord March.

“I was thinking of my elder brother,” said Harry. “I wished he’d been here. We used to talk of doing things together—fights, hunts, adventures. But he wouldn’t have liked this rough sort of thing. He didn’t care for fighting, though he was the bravest lad alive.”

“Was he?” asked Lord March, leaning back and studying his companion. “You should have seen him in a quarrel with a gallant officer—our mutual friend. It was absurd, but George couldn’t be held back. I’ve never seen anyone so cool—and yet so fierce. God help me, I wish he could have been here instead of you, to show us what a real Virginian gentleman is.”

“Nay, sir,” replied Harry, “I’ll do well enough. I can ride, and I think I shoot better than George. But he had the mind—oh, the head!” he said, tapping his own skull. “He read every book ever written, played fiddle and harpsichord, composed poetry and sermons. What can I do? Ride, drink Burgundy, play cards—that’s all I’m good for.”

“And yet,” said Lord March, amused, “you do those things as well as most men.”

“You’re right,” said Harry, smiling. “And I’ll back myself against any man in London.”

Lord March chuckled. “By Gad, Mr. Warrington, you ought to be put on display at Exeter ‘Change. A Virginian gentleman who genuinely misses his brother—such a breed isn’t known in this country. I believe you’d actually want him back.”

“Believe?” Harry flushed. “You mean you believe I believe I’d want him back. But you’re wrong. It’s not in human nature—not as I understand it.”

They passed through Cavendish Square, where Harry lamented the unsightly boards fencing off empty land. “There’s Montagu House turned into a museum—books, birds, rhinoceroses! And they’ve cut a road through Bedford House Gardens—New Road they call it—ruining the Duke’s pleasure, though perhaps they’ll compensate him in gold.”

“Where shall we go?” asked Lord March. “Tyburn Road and the Park? Or Swallow Street?”

“Pall Mall for dinner,” said Harry. “Or if you prefer, your place. Then we can spend the evening however you like—with the Queen of Spades, or…”

“With the Queen of Spades,” Harry agreed, blushing.

They arrived at Harry’s hotel in Covent Garden. The landlord, recognizing Lord March, bowed low, nearly touching his wig to the nobleman’s boots.

After dinner, wine flowed freely—fine vintage, even for a connoisseur like the earl. They discussed going to Vauxhall for fireworks, but Harry, who had never seen anything beyond a few squibs at Williamsburg on November 5th (which he considered sublime), yielded to Lord March’s preference for piquet.

Harry won at first, but luck soon turned. Lord March gained the upper hand, growing testy when Mr. Draper, Harry’s business agent, entered the room and was invited to sit and drink. Harry always welcomed guests warmly—sharing his bread, his meat, his water, his wine. Such generosity, he believed, was rare in London’s selfish world. Though many gave grand dinners, few possessed true kindness.

Draper, though not fond of wine, was thrilled to be in the company of nobility. He praised the game, sitting undisturbed by Lord March’s scowls.

But the game was interrupted again. A commotion erupted outside—muffled voices, then a woman’s cry. Suddenly, Mrs. Betty, Lady Maria’s maid, burst into the room, sobbing. Behind her stood Gumbo, tears streaking his face.

“Help! Help, sir!” she cried, falling to her knees.

“Help whom?” Harry asked.

“Gumbo! You scoundrel!” shouted Harry, turning sharply. “Have you done something to Mrs. Betty?”

Gumbo stepped back, placing a hand on his chest. “No, sir. Nothing happened between me and this lady.”

“It’s my mistress!” cried Betty. “She’s been taken! Here’s the letter!”

Lord March, annoyed, dismissed her. “Is it only old Molly Esmond? She’s bankrupt, no doubt. Dry your eyes, Mrs. Betty, and let us continue our game.”

But Betty refused to stop. “Oh, Mr. Harry! Your honour must come! My lady calls for you!”

“Confound you!” snapped Lord March. “Can’t you keep quiet?”

Yet Betty wept on. The game ended.

Harry rose. “My dear lord, we must cease for tonight. My relative writes in distress. I must go to her.”

“Curse her! Why couldn’t she wait till tomorrow?” growled Lord March.

Harry ordered a postchaise at once. His horses would take him to Bromley.

“Bet you won’t make it in an hour!” laughed Lord March. “Bet you won’t reach Tunbridge Wells before midnight! Bet you’ll be robbed on Blackheath!”

“Done!” said Harry.

Lord March carefully noted the wager in his pocket-book.

Lady Maria’s letter read:

> I am fallen into a trap, the work of villains. I am imprisoned. Betty will tell you all. Ah, my Henrico! Come to the rescue of your MOLLY.*

Within half an hour, Mr. Warrington was in his postchaise, crossing Westminster Bridge, galloping toward Tunbridge Wells to save his kinswoman.
