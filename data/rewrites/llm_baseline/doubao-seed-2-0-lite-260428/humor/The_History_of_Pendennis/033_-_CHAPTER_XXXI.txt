Arthur Pendennis was absolutely buzzing with the thrill of “experiencing real life” his first few months loose in London, bouncing between every weird dive and hole-in-the-wall the city had to offer like a golden retriever who’d just escaped the garden gate. He loved to imagine he was mixing with every strata of society, gawking at coal heavers in their taprooms, bare-knuckle boxers in their pub parlours, and suburban day-trippers messing about on the Thames. He’d have gladly clinked ale mugs with a famous pickpocket or sat down for a pint with a crew of burglars if the chance ever popped up, he was that determined to collect “authentic” characters.

His deadpan, unflappable flatmate Warrington was his perfect tour guide, it turned out. Arthur could barely keep a straight face watching Warrington lean in all earnest to chat boxing stats with the Tutbury Pet at the Champion’s Arms, or swap shift gossip with the coal heavers camped out at the Fox-under-the-Hill. Warrington knew every pub within 10 miles of central London, was on first name terms with every landlord and landlady, and got free run of the bar just as easily as he got a seat at their fancy club back in the Temple. He swore up and down he liked pub company better than his own stuffy upper-class crowd. “At fancy society do’s everyone’s identical,” he’d grumble. “Same suit, same boring small talk, same overpriced claret. One dandy at a club is a carbon copy of the next, one debutante at a ball is indistinguishable from the rest. Here? Everyone’s got actual personality. I’d rather drink gin and water than claret any day, sit on a wooden bench on a sanded Carnaby Market floor than a fancy sofa on a chalked carpet in Mayfair. I’ll say it loud: I prefer hanging out with unapologetic snobs to boring posers.” This guy was a full-on social republican, and never once thought he was better than the working lads he drank with — though he definitely secretly enjoyed how deferential they all were to him.

Pen followed him everywhere, grinning from ear to ear, full of his own silly 20-something pomposity. He fancied himself a prince in disguise, slumming it among the loyal subjects of his father’s non-existent vast kingdom. The pub crew all thought he was a swell high chap, a nice generous young toff, and his natural air of easy, imperious good humour sold the bit perfectly — even though all he was actually heir to was tuppence halfpenny, barely two generations removed from a shopkeeper selling medicine pots. Pen’s whole “oh so gracious condescension” schtick at this point in his life was equal parts ridiculous and charming, the kind of silly innocent conceit that only fades once you hit 30 and realise no one’s actually impressed that you went to university.

Their routine was reliably great: they’d pretend to study law for half the morning, then get distracted reading political pamphlets, novels, and the newspaper until dinnertime rolled around, then charge out onto the town with the kind of energy only a 24-year-old with zero hangovers and no real responsibilities can pull off. Those are the golden days, aren’t they? When every part of you feels sharp and healthy, the whole world feels brand new, and you can drink half a dozen pints and still get up the next day without feeling like you got run over by a cart. The old tunes you hum when you’re grey are the ones you learned back then, the lads you got drunk with then are the only ones who still make you feel 20 again — even if now the pleasure gardens are run down, the old garlands are tattered, half the lights have gone out, and the only thing that greets you when you wake up at dawn is a headache instead of the sunrise over the Thames.

Pen’s mum Helen and his guardian Laura would have had a collective heart attack if they’d seen the pair of them rattling over the Temple flagstones at 5am, still half tipsy, after one of their wild nights out. But the nights weren’t nearly as wicked as they could have been: Warrington was a self-declared woman-hater, and Pen was too much of a silly romantic to mess around with any lady who wasn’t a literal goddess in his head. He could barely speak to a barmaid without being unfailingly polite, and cringed away from any coarse joke like he’d been burned. Guys only treat women like royalty when they’re kneeling, after all — the second they stand up, they leave.

That’s exactly what an old friend of Pen’s told him one night, over a frothing pint. It was none other than good old Mr. Bows, formerly of the Chatteris Theatre, now working as the resident piano man at the Back Kitchen, a tiny, raucous club tucked away in the back of the Fielding’s Head pub in Covent Garden.

Half of Pen’s casual London mates were regulars there. The Fielding’s Head had been a famous boozer ever since Henry Fielding himself was the local Bow Street magistrate back in the 1700s, and they still pointed out his old supposedly original chair that the night’s host sat in. That host was almost always landlord Cutts, unless he was laid up with gout. He was a big jolly guy with a booming baritone who sang nonstop during their weekly harmonic meetings, all hits from the British Brandy-and-Water school of ballads: *The Good Old English Gentleman*, *Dear Tom This Brown Jug*, all the songs about patriotism, good beer, and dying happy with your mates around you. He could work the whole room up crying over a song about a dying old soldier, then before the applause had even died down, he was shoving a menu at you going “C’mon lads, place your orders! John, get that gentleman his sausages and mash, Mr Green’s got his champagne cup over there!”

“And a glass of punch for me too, John, and make sure the water’s boiling!” a familiar voice would yell across the room, making Pen jump and blush the first time he heard it. It was the one and only Captain Costigan, now based full time in London, and one of the Back Kitchen’s most beloved regulars.

The Captain’s chaotic stories drew dozens of young lads to the pub to hang around him. He was a local celebrity almost overnight, ever since his daughter Emily married the super rich Sir Charles Mirabel. He’d corner every stranger drinking next to him to ramble about “me daughther”: her fancy carriages, how madly Sir Charles adored her, how he had full permission to withdraw a hundred pounds from his son-in-law’s bank whenever he wanted. Then he’d casually add that he planned to “draw the money next Saturday, I give ye me word of honour, lads, right at Coutts the second I show the cheque” — and immediately follow that up by begging the guy next to him for half a crown “to tide me over till then, I’ll pay ye back the second the cheque clears.”

Sir Charles, for the record, wasn’t nearly as fond of his father-in-law as Costigan claimed — though on nights he was extra sloshed, Costigan would sob to everyone around him about his ungrateful daughter and her stingy rich husband. They were decent enough to him, though, settling a small regular pension on him that the Captain cashed before the week was even half over. On pay day, he’d storm straight into the Back Kitchen waving the banknote, yelling at the top of his lungs for the landlord to change it before he even sat down. “That note’s good for the Bank of England, Cutts me boy!” he’d roar. “Bows, get yourself a drink, no skimping tonight! A glass of punch’ll make you play all the more spirited!” He was lavishly generous with his money when he had it, and only ever buttoned his breeches pocket the second the last penny was gone, or a creditor wandered through the door.

It was on one of these pay day highs that Pen spotted him, swaggering over at the singers’ table handing out free brandy and water to every mate that walked in. Warrington, who was already mates with the pub’s bass singer, steered Pen straight over that way.

Pen’s face lit up. He’d just run into Emily Mirabel at a fancy party the night before, the first time he’d seen her in years, back when she was the absolute centre of his whole stupid teenage world. He ran over with his hand outstretched, ready to greet the old man like a long lost relative. The Captain, whose grip had gone wobbly from decades of lifting pints of punch, shook his hand hard, stared right at Pen’s face, and slurred: “Good heavens, is that you? Me dear boy, me dear old friend — wait. I know yer face. Bedad, I can’t for the life of me remember yer name.” Five years of nonstop punch had addled his memory, and Arthur had changed a lot since they last met, so the guy had a pretty good excuse.

Pen laughed, a little bit mortified, but not offended. “It’s me, Captain! Arthur Pendennis, from Chatteris!”

The sound of his name snapped Costigan’s foggy brain right back into focus, and he exploded in a volley of over-the-top greetings. Pen was his dearest gallant young friend, he’d kept him close in his heart all these years, how was his — wait, his father? No, his mother, his guardian the Major? “I can tell by yer fancy getup ye’ve come into yer inheritance, haven’t ye? And bedad ye’ll spend it like a proper gentleman, I’ll bet. No? Not yet? Well look, if ye need a few quid, I’ve got a couple of guineas right here, me dear boy, you’ll never want for anything as long as old Jack Costigan’s around! John! Get this gentleman a glass of punch, on me! And yer friend there, I’ve seen him before, sir! Allow me to introduce meself, have a glass of punch on the house!”

Pen made a mental note that he definitely did not envy Sir Charles Mirabel his father-in-law. “Where’s me old friend Mr Bows, Captain?” he asked. “Have you seen him lately?”

“Sure he’s around,” Costigan said, jingling his money and whistling his famous party tune *The Little Doodeen*. “Me dear boy — wait I forgot yer name again, but it’s Costigan, Jack Costigan! Drink as many punch tumblers in my name as ye want, I’m not ashamed of it.” And he wandered off, waffling to the next guy at the bar.

“Pay day for the old General,” the bass singer Hodgen whispered to Warrington. “He’s three sheets to the wind. He already butchered that Little Doodeen number ten minutes ago right before I sang King Death. Hey, you heard my new hit *The Body Snatcher*? Wrote it special, first performed at St Bartholomew’s the other week. You two want a copy? John, hand over one of the song sheets — there’s a portrait of me on the front in costume, sitting on a coffin with a spade, everyone says it’s a perfect likeness.”

“Thanks,” Warrington said dryly. “Heard it nine times already. Know it by heart.”

Just then the piano started up, and Pen looked over to see that exact Mr Bows he’d been asking about, sitting in front of a beat-up old piano that sounded hoarse and wobbly from decades of late nights. Bows had spotted Pen the second he walked in, and noticed how warmly he’d greeted the sloshed Captain. He struck up an old familiar tune, the one that used to play in the Chatteris Theatre right before Emily’s big entrance in *The Stranger*. Pen’s chest got all tight — he could remember exactly how his heart used to pound hearing that exact music, waiting for her to walk on stage. No one else in the raucous pub noticed the tune, though, not over the clatter of cutlery, people yelling for poached eggs and kidneys, and waiters running back and forth.

Pen walked over at the end of the song and shook the old man’s hand. “You haven’t forgotten the old tune, have you Mr Pendennis?” Bows said, grinning. “I knew you’d remember. That was the first fancy theatre tune you ever heard, wasn’t it? You were just a little kid back then. The Captain’s proper wasted tonight, pay day madness, I’m gonna have to drag him home later. We still share a flat, you know — still running the old partnership, even if Miss Em — Lady Mirabel — left the firm. You remember those old days, huh? She was a right beauty, wasn’t she? Your very good health, sir.” He took a long sip of the porter sitting next to his piano.

Pen popped back to visit them both again and again after that, rebuilding his old friendship with the old musician and the chaotic Captain. The Back Kitchen always had a rotating cast of the most random people you could imagine: country farmers in from the villages on business, young apprentices sneaking out after they locked their shop shutters, scruffy loud medical students yelling along to the songs, posh university boys with that weird unplaceable Alma Mater smug smirk, handsome guards officers, St James’s Street bucks, even Irish and English senators, and a couple of actual members of the House of Lords.

Hodgen’s *The Body Snatcher* was the biggest hit in the whole city, and everyone was turning up just to see him perform. He’d pull back the little curtain, sit on a prop coffin with a gin flask, a spade, and a candle stuck in a human skull, and belt out the song in a deep rumbly voice that made everyone’s skin prickle, before slamming his spade on the table and letting out a demon cackle so loud all the glasses on every table rattled. Even landlord Cutts himself admitted he couldn’t compete, so he’d sneak off to the bar or his private rooms before the song started. After Hodgen finished, most of the crowd cleared out, leaving only the hardest partiers still there to listen to Costigan mumble his *Little Doodeen* for the two or three drunks left who still cared.

One night, well after midnight, two familiar regulars wandered in almost at the same time. “Mr Hoolan and Mr Doolan,” Warrington whispered to Pen. Pen recognised Doolan right away — that was the guy from the Alacrity coach, who’d turned down Pen’s dinner invitation because he said he could never make Friday dinners for work reasons.

Doolan’s newspaper *The Dawn* was splayed on the table, stained with porter, right next to Hoolan’s rival paper *The Day*. *The Dawn* was rabidly Liberal, *The Day* ultra-Conservative. Half of London’s newspapers back then were staffed by Irish lads, who’d show up to work for whatever side paid them best, then meet up at the pub after to drink pints like best mates no matter what they wrote that day.

“Kidneys John, and a glass of stout!” Hoolan yelled. “How are you Morgan? How’s Mrs Doolan?”

“Grand, thanks Mick,” Doolan said. “She’s used to it by now. I might pop over to Kilburn on Sunday, we’ll have a glass of punch over at yours.”

“Don’t bring your kid Patsy, our Georgy’s got the measles,” Hoolan said, and then the pair dived straight into shop talk: foreign mail schedules, who the Paris correspondent was, how much the rival morning paper was wasting on couriers, who had the biggest circulation.

Warrington laughed, picked up *The Dawn*, and pointed at a scathing leading article that started off by calling their rival *The Day* a gang of assassins who hired thugs to ruin innocent people’s reputations. “How’d you lot like that article at your place, Mick?” he asked. “Your Captain Shandon wrote that in two hours, sitting in the pub while the errand boy waited outside.”

“Our governor says no one cares about newspaper catfights anymore,” Hoolan said. “Told Dr Boyne not to even bother writing a reply. The Doc was gutted, he says writing mean insults is the easiest work you can do, no need to do any research at all.”

“Eloquence is dead these days,” Doolan sighed.

“Sure is,” Hoolan agreed. “Remember back when the Doc wrote for *The Phoenix* and him and Condy Roony wrote page long insults at each other every single day?”

“Didn’t they duel twice too?” Doolan laughed. “Roony winged the Doc the second time.”

“They’re talking about Dr Boyne and Captain Shandon,” Warrington explained to Pen. “The two top writers for the rival papers, who are actually best mates in real life. They’ll write 10,000 words a day calling each other traitors, then go out drinking together after work.”

“Your servant Mr Warrington, Mr Pendennis!” Doolan said, bowing very formally. “I’ve thought about that lovely night we spent on top of the Alacrity coach a hundred times, and told my wife all about it. I’ve even seen your friend Mr Foker in here a few times! Back when we met I was working on the *Tom and Jerry Weekly*, but now I’m sub-editor of *The Dawn* — one of the best written newspapers in the whole empire,” he added, with a polite little nod to Warrington, his tone suddenly all formal and posh, nothing like the rough casual brogue he used with his fellow Irishman.
