A sleek, dark-blue brougham, polished to a mirror shine that still bore the ghost of its wedding-day varnish, waited at the ferry landing for Archer. Inside, the air smelled faintly of fresh leather and the crisp chill of the winter outside. The vehicle carried him with luxurious ease to the Pennsylvania Station in Jersey City, a sprawling cathedral of iron and glass where gas lamps flickered against the gloom of a somber, snow-blanketed afternoon.

As Archer paced the vast, echoing platform, waiting for the Washington Express, his thoughts drifted to the future. People spoke of tunnels boring beneath the Hudson, allowing trains to sprint directly into Manhattan, and of ships cutting the Atlantic in mere days, or machines that could fly. These were the dreams of visionaries, architects of impossible tomorrows.

"I don't care if any of these fantasies come true," Archer murmured to himself, his heart swelling with a reckless, boyish joy. "Only so long as the tunnel remains unburied."

In this haze of anticipation, he constructed a perfect scene in his mind: Madame Olenska stepping from the train, her pale face illuminated by the gaslight, lost momentarily in the sea of indifferent travelers until he found her. He imagined guiding her arm through the crush of the crowd, leading her swiftly to the carriage, navigating the chaotic wharf where horses slipped and carters shouted, before settling into the quiet sanctuary of the brougham. They would sit side by side, wrapped in snow, as the earth seemed to roll away beneath them, transporting them to a world beyond the sun. It was a dream so vivid it felt real. *How many things,* he thought, *how many beautiful, urgent things I need to tell her.* The words formed in his mouth, arranging themselves in an eloquence he barely possessed, burning bright in his chest.

Then, the clatter and groan of the approaching train grew louder. It lumbered into the station like a beast returning to its den. Archer pushed forward, jostling through the throng, peering blindly into the windows of the high cars. And then, suddenly, there she was. Madame Olenska's face, pale and startled, appeared inches from his view, striking him anew with the shock of forgetting what she truly looked like.

They met. Their hands clasped, and he drew her arm securely through his. "This way—I have the carriage," he said, his voice steady despite the storm inside him.

Everything unfolded exactly as he had dreamed. He assisted her into the brougham, helping her settle her bags. Somewhere in the blur of movement, he vaguely recalled reassuring her about her grandmother and giving her a quick summary of the scandal surrounding Lady Beaufort, hearing her soft exclamation, "Poor Regina!" The carriage wound its way out of the station's tangle, creeping down the slick incline toward the wharf. They navigated past swaying coal-carts, confused horses, and disheveled wagons. And then, looming out of the mist, it appeared: an empty hearse.

Archer watched her reaction closely. She shut her eyes tightly, clutching his hand with a sudden, desperate grip.

"If only it doesn't mean... poor Granny!" she whispered.

"Oh, no, no! She is fine, truly. She's recovered completely." He spoke with an urgency that sounded hollow, hoping the lie might bridge the gap. "There—we've passed it!" he declared, as if pointing out the danger rendered it harmless.

Her hand remained interlaced with his. As the carriage lurched across the gangplank onto the ferry, Archer leaned over, unbuttoning her tight brown glove with trembling fingers. He kissed her palm, treating it with the reverence due to a sacred relic. She pulled away with a faint, wry smile.

"You didn't expect me today?" he asked softly.

"Oh, no."

"I intended to go to Washington to see you. I had arranged everything..." He trailed off, remembering the narrow miss. "I very nearly crossed paths with you on the train."

"Oh—" she breathed, as though the thought of missing each other entirely caused genuine terror.

"Do you know," he continued, searching her eyes, "I hardly recognized you?"

"Hardly recognize me?"

"How do you explain it? I... it happens every time. *Each time you catch me all over again.*"

"Oh, yes," she said, glancing out the window at the passing gray world. "I know. I know."

"But do I?" he pressed, needing the truth. "Do I affect you the same way?"

She nodded silently, her gaze fixed on the snow-streaked dusk beyond the glass.

"Ellen—Ellen—Ellen!" he cried, the name escaping him in a rush of emotion.

She offered no response. He fell into a heavy silence, watching her profile dissolve into the twilight. Four long months lay between them, a chasm of time that threatened to swallow them both. How little they truly knew of one another! The precious moments were slipping away, and yet, paralyzed by the paradox of their intimacy and distance, he could only brood. They sat so close, bodies touching, yet their faces remained invisible to each other, trapped in a private fog.

Suddenly, she turned from the window. "What a lovely carriage! Is it May's?"

"Yes."

"So May sent you to fetch me? How kind of her!"

Archer paused, the wheels of his mind turning. Then, with an explosive honesty, he blurted out, "Your husband's secretary visited me the day after we met in Boston."

He had intended to keep that meeting buried, a secret shame. But the knowledge that they rode in his wife's carriage sparked a retaliatory impulse. If she mentioned May, surely he could mention Rivière. Yet, when he looked at her, expecting a flush or a stumble, she showed none. She did not betray surprise.

"He writes to her, then," Archer concluded, the realization settling coldly over him.

"M. Rivière went to see you?"

"Yes. Didn't you know?"

"No," she answered simply, her voice devoid of judgment.

"And you aren't surprised?"

She hesitated. "Why should I be? He told me in Boston that he knew you; that he met you in England, I think."

"Ellen, I must ask you one thing."

"Yes."

"I wanted to wait until I saw you, but I couldn't write it. Was it M. Rivière who helped you leave your husband?"

His heart hammered a frantic rhythm against his ribs. *Would she meet this with the same calm?*

"Yes," she replied, her voice quiet and steady. "I owe him a great debt."

Her tone was so natural, so almost indifferent, that Archer's turmoil subsided instantly. Again, her simple directness had reduced him to a state of clumsy conventionality, making him feel small and bound by rules he sought to break.

"I think you are the most honest woman I have ever known," he exclaimed, the words tearing from his throat.

"Oh, no—but perhaps one of the least fussily concerned," she answered, a gentle smile playing on her lips.

"Call it what you wish: you see things as they are."

"Ah," she said, her expression growing serious. "I have had to. I have had to look at the Gorgon."

"Well, she hasn't blinded you! You've seen clearly enough that she is merely an old bogie-man, like all the others."

"She does not blind one," she corrected softly. "She only dries up one's tears."

The words stopped Archer dead. They spoke of a depth of suffering he could not possibly comprehend, a reservoir of experience far beyond his reach. The ferry boat finally halted, its bow bumping violently against the wooden piles of the slip. The impact threw the carriage sideways, sending Archer and Madame Olenska colliding against each other. Trembling, he felt the press of her shoulder and instinctively draped his arm around her.

"If you are not blind," he said, his voice thick with emotion, "then you must see that this cannot last."

"What cannot?"

"Our being together—and not together."

"No," she said, her voice altering, dropping an octave. "You ought not to have come today."

And suddenly, she turned. Flinging her arms around him, she pressed her lips to his.

At that exact moment, the carriage began to move. A gas lamp at the head of the slip flared, casting a harsh beam into the window. She drew away, and they sat in a stunned, motionless silence as the brougham struggled through the clogged traffic of the ferry landing.

Once they gained the street, Archer began to speak rapidly, trying to reason with the chaos within. "Do not be afraid of me. You need not shrink back into your corner like that. A stolen kiss is not what I seek. Look: I am not even attempting to touch the sleeve of your jacket. Do not imagine that I do not understand why you refuse to let this feeling between us dwindle into some ordinary, secret affair. I could not have spoken thus yesterday, for when we are apart, and I look forward to seeing you, every thought burns in a great flame. But then you arrive, and you are so much more than I remembered, and what I desire of you is so much more than an hour or two now and then, separated by wastes of thirsty waiting... that I can sit perfectly still beside you, like this, holding that other vision in my mind, quietly trusting it to become real."
