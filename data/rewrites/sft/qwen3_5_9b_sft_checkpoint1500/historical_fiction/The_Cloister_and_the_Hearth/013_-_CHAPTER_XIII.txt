Gerard and Margaret hastened to Sevenbergen with a buoyant spirit, buoyed by the fresh air of regained freedom and the thrill of their recent escapades. Yet, this exhilaration quickly curdled into somber reflections. Gerard, though free, remained a fugitive, perpetually under the shadow of recapture and punishment. Consequently, separation became inevitable. Furthermore, Gerard had cultivated a profound loathing for his homeland. Margaret desired his departure, yet the mere mention of Italy caused her heart to falter. Conversely, Gerard's willingness to abandon her stemmed solely from his fervent wish to journey southward; he was convinced that Italy offered the wealth, renown, and legal sanctuary necessary to overcome every barrier standing between them and marriage. Having confided in Margaret the warnings of the demoiselle Van Eyck, he reiterated that the gold pieces bestowed upon him were specifically intended for this Italian venture. As a craftsman and artist stifled in this uncouth town, he believed his talents would finally be recognized across the sea. It was on this selfless, pragmatic ground that the gentle girl granted her consent, though her acquiescence was accompanied by a torrent of tears. Young and loving as she was, Gerard wept bitterly alongside her, frequently questioning what sin had forged such a united front of enemies against them, seemingly conspiring to tear them apart.

Hand in hand, they sat until the clock struck midnight, oscillating between lamentations of their cruel destiny and painting luminous, hopeful visions of a future reunited in foreign lands. Amidst these reveries, Margaret's sorrow would well up anew, causing poor Gerard's eloquent comfort to dissolve into a weary sigh. By dawn, they had reached a state of resignation regarding their imminent parting, though neither possessed the courage to specify the hour; indeed, it is doubtful whether the moment of separation would ever have arrived without external intervention.

However, around three o'clock in the afternoon, Giles returned, having traversed miles on circuitous paths to evade detection. Both lovers rushed to the threshold, driven by intense curiosity. "Brother Gerard!" Giles cried, his voice booming with its customary intensity. "Lady Kate commands you to flee for your life! They accuse you of theft; you have handed them evidence. Do not attempt explanation; seek no justice in Tergou. The parchments you seized were merely a ruse. She has seen the verdict in the eyes of the men—a price hangs over your head. Fly! For Margaret's sake, and for every soul who loves you, do not waste a single moment of life, but run!"

It was a thunderclap that left two pale, terrified faces staring at one another, fixed upon the ominous messenger. Then, Giles, breaking from his script of Catherine's orders, spoke a word of his own accord. "All the constables descended upon our dwelling in search of you, and so did Dirk Brower himself. Kate is astute, Gerard. Listen to her counsel and flee!"

"Oh, yes, Gerard!" cried Margaret in a frenzy. "Escape immediately! Ah, those parchments! My conscience warned me; why did I permit you to take them?"

"Margaret, they are but a deception," Giles insisted. "Let that pass. That wretch shall never behold them again. I will not depart until I have concealed his treasure in a place where he can never discover it." After offering Giles a warm thanksgiving, Gerard bade him farewell, instructing him to return and tell Kate that he was already gone—"For I shall be gone before you reach home," he declared. He then shouted for Martin, relaying the dire news and begging the young archer to travel a short distance toward Tergou and keep vigil along the road.

"Aye!" replied Martin. "Should I sight Dirk Brower or any of his men, I shall shoot an arrow into the oak tree within our garden. Upon seeing it, you must dash into the nearby forest and meet me at the Hunter's Spring. There, I shall guide you safely through the woods."

With this precaution in place, Gerard drew a breath he had not known he needed. Accompanied by Margaret, while she stood guard near the oak tree, trembling lest an arrow strike the branches, Gerard began digging a deep pit to inter the stolen documents. One by one, he cast them into the earth. Most were charters and records of the burgh, but one document appeared to be a private indenture between Floris Brandt, Peter's father, and Ghysbrecht.

"Why, this belongs just as much to you as to him," Gerard observed, reaching to read it. "Let me examine this."

"Oh, not now, Gerard, not now!" Margaret implored, panic rising in her voice. "Every second you delay fills me with dread; look! Large drops of rain begin to fall, and the clouds darken ominously."

Yielding to her urgent plea, Gerard withdrew the deed, tucking it securely into his bosom. He then covered the remaining parchments with soil, trampling the earth down firmly. As he worked, a jagged bolt of lightning pierced the sky, followed instantly by a roll of distant thunder, and soon the heavens opened in a deluge. Margaret and Gerard fled indoors, swiftly joined by Martin.

"The road ahead is clear," Martin reported, "and a violent storm is approaching." His prophecy proved accurate. The thunder grew louder, crashing overhead like a giant's drum, while flashes of lightning strobed continuously, resembling the strokes of a whip, as rain poured down in torrents. Margaret buried her face, unable to bear the spectacle of the lightning. Inside, Gerard raised the rough shutters and lit a candle. The lovers conferred quietly, and Gerard offered thanks to the tempest, blessing the storm for granting them a few fleeting, precious hours together. Unnoticed by the world outside, the sun dipped below the horizon, yet the thunder continued to rumble and the lightning to flash, drenching the land. Supper was laid out, yet Gerard and Margaret could not eat; the knowledge that this might be their final supper together choked them with grief.

As the storm gradually subsided, Peter retired to his chamber for rest. However, Gerard was to depart at first light, and neither lover could afford even an hour of slumber. Martin lingered for a while, carefully fitting a new string to his bow, a task requiring his utmost precision. Beside him, the lovers whispered their sorrows and their enduring love.

Suddenly, the old man raised a hand, signaling silence. The room fell quiet, and they strained their ears, hearing nothing at first. But moments later, a soft, dry *crack* resonated—the sound of footsteps crunching faintly upon the autumn leaves scattered across the garden behind the house. To those living without enemies, such a step might have been ignored; but to those hunted, it was a terrifying signal. It was a foot attempting to move silently.

Martin swiftly nocked an arrow to his string and extinguished the candle with a sharp blow of his hand. Immediately, to their mounting horror, they heard additional footsteps approaching the side door of the cottage. These steps were less silent than the first, yet executed with equal stealth—and then, a dead, agonizing pause.

Their blood seemed to freeze within their veins.

"Oh, Kate, oh, Kate!" Margaret sobbed, wringing her hands in anguish, terror, and wild remorse for having delayed Gerard's escape. "You said to fly at once!"

"Hush, girl!" Martin whispered, his tone stern and commanding.

A heavy, authoritative knock shook the doorframe, sending a tremor through the hearts of those within.
