After the bitter departure of their chaplain, Madam Esmond and her elder son, George, reached an uneasy truce. Though neither spoke of their quarrel, the weight of it pressed upon the boy’s mind so heavily that he fell into a fever, his restless nights haunted by delirious cries of *“Broken! Broken! It can never be mended!”*—words that sent a chill through his mother’s heart as she watched over him.

Her remedies proved useless. Pride swallowed, she rode through the snow to the door of little Dr. Dempster, the very man she had once dismissed. *“I shall bury my resentment, madam,”* he said, *“as you have buried your pride. Let us hope it is not too late to save my young pupil.”* With that, he gathered his instruments and returned to Castlewood. For days, death hovered near, and Harry stood on the brink of inheritance—until the fever broke. Weak but alive, George recovered under Dempster’s care, though the physician urged a change of air, even a voyage to England. Yet old grudges barred that path. Instead, the brothers, with Dempster as tutor and a pair of servants, journeyed north: to New York, up the Hudson’s broad currents to Albany, and beyond into the French territories, where letters of introduction won them welcome among the colonial gentry.

Harry, ever the adventurer, took to the wilderness—hunting bears, trading furs, and bedding down beside campfires with the Indians. George, his health still delicate, found his place in drawing rooms. His French, already polished, became flawless; his manners charmed; his violin sang with the latest airs from Paris. He even fought a duel—a swift, graceful affair that left the Chevalier de la Jabotiere with a pierced shoulder and a newfound respect. *“A mother is blessed to have such a son,”* wrote Madame de Mouchy to Madam Esmond. (One wonders what the pious Mr. Whitfield might have thought of the widow’s pride in her son’s *prowess*.)

Ten months later, the brothers returned, taller, altered. George, once slight, now matched Harry in height. With powdered wigs, they were near indistinguishable; without them, George’s raven-black hair contrasted sharply with Harry’s sunlit curls. Yet beneath the surface, George’s temperament remained unchanged—proud, sensitive, quick to resent. He had left Virginia nursing a grudge against a certain gentleman, and he returned with it intact.

That gentleman was Mr. Washington. Though barely of age, Washington carried himself with the gravity of a man twice his years. Trusted by neighbors, respected for his judgment, he seemed to George a figure of unbearable virtue—especially when Madam Esmond held him up as a model. *“Ah, Harry! You waste your days on cockfights, while the Major guards our borders! George, is Latin worthier than service?”*

Mrs. Mountain, the widow’s sharp-eyed companion, fed George’s suspicions with whispers. *“Mark my words—he wants more than her counsel. A rich widow, a young officer—why, it’s plain as day!”* George, flushing, ordered her to silence. Yet the evidence gnawed at him: Washington’s constant presence, his own room at Castlewood, his name ever on his mother’s lips.

Then came news that shook George’s jealousy into something darker. Washington, commissioned to warn the French from encroaching on Virginia’s lands, had returned with defiance—and a militia regiment was mustering. *“An Esmond serves only under the king’s commission,”* Madam declared, spurning the thought of her son enlisting under Washington or begging favor from Governor Dinwiddie.

War loomed. French troops, outnumbering the British, seized a half-built fort in Pennsylvania, christening it Fort Duquesne. When Washington’s small force clashed with them, the first shots of the war were fired—though the skirmish ended in retreat. Humiliated, his regiment disbanded, Washington returned to Castlewood.

*“I was angry when we parted,”* he said to George, offering his hand. *“I thought you scorned me. I boasted too much of what we’d do.”*

*“You did your duty,”* George replied, the old envy fading. *“No one questions that.”*

But Madam Esmond, ever sharp, could not resist a barb. *“Kind of us to receive you? Colonel, I had not thought misfortune made men less welcome here.”* Her curtsey was flawless—and George, watching, felt the old bitterness rise anew.

The world had widened for the Virginians. Yet some wounds, it seemed, would not heal.
