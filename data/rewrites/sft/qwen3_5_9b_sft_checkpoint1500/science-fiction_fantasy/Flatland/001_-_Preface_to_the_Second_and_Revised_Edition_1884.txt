If my friend from Flatland still possessed the sharp, unclouded intellect he held when he first penned these memoirs, I would have no need to speak for him here. He wishes, above all, to thank the inhabitants of Spaceland—their critics, their patrons, and their readers—who, with startling speed, demanded a second printing of his work. He also seeks to apologize for certain errors and misprints, though he insists he bears little blame for them, and finally, he aims to clarify a few misunderstandings. Yet, he is not the Square he once was. Seven years of brutal imprisonment, compounded by the crushing weight of universal skepticism and ridicule, have worked in tandem with the inevitable fading of old age to strip away many of the concepts and terms he learned during his brief sojourn in Spaceland. Consequently, I have been tasked with answering on his behalf two specific objections: one intellectual, the other deeply moral.

The first objection arises from a simple observation: when a Flatlander looks upon a Line, they see something that must possess *thickness* to be seen by the eye, not merely length. Therefore, the argument goes, they ought to admit that their people are not simply long and broad, but also—perhaps in a negligible degree—*thick* or *high*. To the citizens of Spaceland, this logic is nearly irrefutable, and I must confess that when I first heard it, I was at a loss for a rebuttal. However, my aged friend’s response strikes me as perfectly sound.

"I concede," he told me when I raised this point, "the validity of the critic's observations, yet I reject his conclusions. It is undeniable that within Flatland exists a Third Unrecognized Dimension, which we might term 'height,' just as it is true that within Spaceland lurks a Fourth Unrecognized Dimension, currently unnamed, which I shall call 'super-height.' Yet, just as we cannot perceive our own height, you cannot perceive your super-height. Even I, who spent twenty-four hours in Spaceland and understood the concept of height, find myself unable to truly grasp it now, nor visualize it through sight or pure reason. I can only hold onto it by faith alone.

"The explanation is plain. A dimension requires direction, measurement, and the capacity for comparison between more and less. In Flatland, however, every line is *equally* and *infinitesimally* thick—or high, if you prefer. There is no variation to guide our minds toward conceiving this dimension. No 'delicate micrometer,' as suggested by a rash Spaceland critic, would help us; for we would not know *what* to measure, nor *where* to look. When we behold a Line, we see something that is long and *bright*; indeed, brightness, alongside length, is essential to a Line's existence—if the light fades, the Line vanishes. Thus, whenever I speak to my compatriots about this unseen dimension somehow visible in a Line, they respond, 'Ah, you mean BRIGHTNESS.' And when I correct them, saying, 'No, I mean a genuine Dimension,' they immediately counter, 'Then measure it, or tell us its direction!' And I am silenced, for I cannot do either.

"Only yesterday, the Great Circle—our High Priest—visited the State Prison to conduct his seventh annual inspection. For the seventh time, he asked me, 'Are you any better?' I attempted to demonstrate to him that he, too, possesses 'height,' extending beyond mere length and breadth, though he remains unaware. His reply? 'You claim I am "high." Measure my "high-ness," and I will believe you.' Stunned, helpless, I was crushed, and he departed victorious.

"Do you still find this perplexing? Imagine instead a visitor from the Fourth Dimension, kindly descending to pay you a visit. They would tell you: 'Every time you open your eyes, you witness a Plane—a realm of Two Dimensions—and you infer a Solid, a realm of Three. Yet, in truth, you also see (though you fail to recognize) a Fourth Dimension. It is not color, nor brightness, nor any sensory quality you know; it is a true Dimension. Though I cannot point out its direction, nor allow you to measure it.' What would you say to such a guest? Would you not have them imprisoned? Well, that is my fate. And just as it is natural for us Flatlanders to imprison a Square for preaching the Third Dimension, so it is natural for you Spacelanders to imprison a Cube for preaching the Fourth. Alas, how profound the kinship among blinded and persecuted souls across all dimensions! Regarding this point, the Square’s defense appears impregnable. I wish I could say his answer to the second, and far graver, objection was equally lucid. Some have charged him with misogyny. Since this accusation has been vigorously pressed by that segment of the Spaceland race destined by Nature to form the larger half of the population, I desire to address it, to the extent of my honesty. However, the Square is so unaccustomed to the nuanced moral vocabulary of Spaceland that a literal transcription of his defense would likely misrepresent him. As his interpreter and summarizer, I gather that during his seven-year incarceration, he has significantly altered his personal views regarding both Women and the Isosceles, or Lower Classes. Personally, he now leans toward the Sphere’s perspective: that straight lines, in many crucial aspects, surpass circles.

Yet, writing as a historian, he has aligned himself—perhaps too closely—with the prevailing views of Flatland, and indeed, of historians in Spaceland until very recently. In their chronicles, the fates of women and the masses have rarely been deigned mention, let alone careful consideration. Furthermore, in a passage of great obscurity, he now seeks to distance himself from the circular or aristocratic leanings that critics have naturally attributed to him. While acknowledging the immense intellectual power wielded by a select few Circles over countless generations of subjects, he believes the raw facts of Flatland, standing silent without his commentary, reveal that revolutions cannot always be quelled by bloodshed. Moreover, Nature itself, in sentencing the Circles to sterility, has doomed them to ultimate failure—"and herein," he observes, "I perceive the fulfillment of the supreme law governing all universes: where human wisdom believes it is forging one path, the wisdom of Nature compels it to forge another, and a vastly superior one."

Finally, he implores his readers not to assume that every trivial detail of daily life in Flatland must mirror some counterpart in Spaceland. Nevertheless, he harbors the hope that, viewed as a cohesive whole, his work will prove both thought-provoking and entertaining to those Spacelanders of moderate and humble minds who, when contemplating matters of supreme import that lie beyond empirical experience, refuse to dismiss them with the phrase, "This can never be," nor accept them blindly with the assertion, "It must needs be precisely thus, and we know all about it."
