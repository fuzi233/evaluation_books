Evening crept in on a slow, smoky tide, and with it the smell of supper—those bears’ paws laid to stew, giving up their rich, dark savour as patiently as a stockpot gives up marrow. The fire snapped and settled; fat hissed when it met the coals. We sat close, talking low, yet our ears kept ranging outward, as if we could pull our boys home by listening hard enough. Beyond the circle of light the island held its own music: wind combing the grass, distant surf working like a great mill, and—nearer—those small domestic sounds that had become our measure of safety: the cow’s occasional breathy low, the uneasy shifting of penned creatures, the dogs’ nails clicking and then stopping as they, too, listened.

At last, the sound we wanted broke through everything else: hoofbeats—first a faint drumming stitched into the night, then the unmistakable tramp and beat, quick and confident across open ground. We were on our feet at once. A shrill, ringing cheer came ahead of them like a thrown spark, and then the little troop appeared, cutting across the darkened plain at a sharp trot, the horses’ breaths visible in the firelight as they drew near.

They sprang down, laughing and talking all at once. The animals were loosed to crop and drink, and the riders crowded in, eager to show what they had dragged out of the day.

Franz and Jack looked as if they had dressed themselves in mischief: each had a young kid slung on his back, the four legs tied together so that the small hooves stuck out under their chins like ridiculous ornaments.

Fritz’s game-bag was queerer still—rounded lumps and sharp points, and now and then a distinct little movement that made the leather bulge.

“Hurrah, for the chase, father!” Jack shouted, his voice as loud as the dogs. “Nothing like real hunting after all. And just to see Storm and Grumble go along over a grassy plain! It is perfectly splendid! We soon tired out the little antelopes, and were able to catch them.”

“Yes, father,” Franz added, eager to keep pace with Jack’s triumphs, “and Fritz has two angora rabbits in his bag, and we wanted to bring you some honey. Only think! Such a clever bird—a cuckoo—showed us where it was!”

“My brothers forget the chief thing,” Fritz said, with that calm pride of his that always sounded like a plan already half-built. “We have driven a little herd of antelopes right through the Gap into our territory; and there they are, all ready for us to hunt when we like—or to catch and tame!”

“Well done!” I cried, and meant it with my whole heart. Yet gratitude rose first, before any delight in meat or trophies. “Here is indeed a list of achievements. But to your mother and me, the chief thing of all, is God’s goodness in bringing you safe back to us. Now, let us hear the whole story, that we may have a definite idea of your performances.”

Fritz began at once, orderly as a journal. They had ridden down Glen Verdant to the defile in our rocky barrier; the morning had been cool enough that the horses took the bit and wanted to fly. Beyond the Gap, they slowed and watched for game. From a grassy hill they had seen two herds grazing by a stream, not knowing at first whether they were goats, gazelles, or antelopes.

“We were about to gallop down and try to get a shot,” Fritz said, “when it struck me it would be wiser to drive the whole herd through the Gap into our own domain, where they would be shut up, as it were, in a park—free and yet within reach.”

He told it cleanly: the hard rush down the hill, the semicircle formed behind the larger herd, the dogs aiding with their sharp barks and quick feints. Shouts, cries, and the pounding of hooves kept the antelopes streaming along the water toward the narrow opening. Near the Gap they balked, bunching like sheep pressed toward a yard they did not trust.

“It was all we could do to prevent them from bolting past us,” Fritz said. “But at length one made a rush at the opening, and the rest following, they were soon all on the other side of the frontier—and inhabitants of New Switzerland.”

“Capital,” I said, clapping his shoulder. “Capital, my boy! But I don’t see what is to make them remain inhabitants of our domain, or to prevent them from returning through the Gap whenever they feel inclined.”

“Stop, father—you interrupted me too soon.” His eyes shone. “We thought of that possibility too, and provided against it. We stretched a long line right across the defile and strung on it feathers and rags and all sorts of other things. They danced and fluttered in the wind and looked so strange that I am perfectly certain the herd will never attempt to pass it. Levaillant says—”

He quoted from memory, pleased to prove that reading could be as useful as riding, and I praised him for it. Yet when the rabbits came out—two soft angoras, long-haired and trembling, their noses working fast at our smells—I could not keep from warning him.

“The antelopes are welcome,” I said, “but I cannot say the same for the rabbits you have there. They increase so rapidly that if you establish a colony of the little wretches your next difficulty will be to get rid of them.”

“True,” he admitted, “but my idea was to place them upon Whale Island, where they would find abundant food and in no way trouble us. May I not establish a warren there? It would be so useful. And my eagle caught them—three. I rewarded him with the one he killed, and these two I kept unhurt.”

Jack, who could never bear to be second in any telling, broke in. “Now, father, do listen to me and hear my story, or else Fritz will begin upon my adventures and tire you out with his rigmarole descriptions.”

“Certainly, Jack,” I said. “First and foremostly, how did you bring down those beautiful little animals you have there?”

“Oh, we galloped them down,” he said, as if that explained everything that ever needed doing. While Fritz was away after rabbits, the dogs had sniffed in the grass; then the little fawns sprang up and fled—bounding, skipping, swift as thrown stones. Storm and Grumble chased; the dogs soon fell behind; the boys pressed close on their horses until the fawns, terrified and exhausted, dropped into the grass and lay still.

“So we shouted to Fritz, and—”

“My dear boy,” I said, unable not to measure his numbers, “according to your statement Fritz must have been seven miles and a half off.”

Jack’s cheeks reddened. “Oh, well, father, perhaps we did not ride for quite a quarter of an hour, and of course I can’t say exactly how fast we were going; and then, you see, the fawns did not run in a straight line. At any rate Fritz heard us. And then we saw a wretch of a cuckoo, who led us ever so far out of our course by cuckooing and making faces at us and then hopping away. Franz declared it must be an enchanted princess, and so I thought I would rid it of its spell—but Fritz stopped me shooting it. He said it was a ‘Honey Indicator,’ leading us probably to a bees’ byke, so we spared its life. And presently—sure enough—it stopped close by a bees’ nest in a hollow tree.”

Jack’s face took on the greedy brightness of any boy promised sweetness. “We were in a great hurry to taste the honey, so I threw in a lot of lighted lucifer matches, but somehow it did not kill the bees at all—only made them awfully angry. They flew out in a body and stung me all over. I rushed to Storm and sprang on his back, but though I galloped away for bare life, it was an age before I got rid of the little wretches, and now my face is in a perfect fever. I think I will get mother to bathe it for me.”

Off he ran, still talking as he went, leaving Fritz and me to settle the fawns, examine the rabbits, and consider what our new stores might mean for the winter.

With the rabbits I determined to do as Fritz proposed: Whale Island would take them without letting them take us. The thought fitted another I had been turning over like a seed in the hand—that Whale Island might serve as a refuge, a kind of fortress if ever we were forced to quit our home in haste, and that in such a retreat we should be grateful for a steady supply of animal food.

I tried to interest the boys in the block of talc I had discovered, thinking of stoves and heat and future contrivances, but at that moment my wife’s voice called us to supper, and the smell from the pot silenced every other ambition.

The bears’ paws lay there, steaming, the skin and sinew softened to a gelatinous tenderness, and the broth around them glossy with fat. The odour was deep and hearty—woodsmoke, meat, salt, and that wild, almost sweet note that clings to game. They were so like human hands in shape that Jack could not resist a roguish “Fee-fo-fum,” but no one’s appetite suffered for the jest. We ate with the steady pleasure of bodies that have earned their food, and even as we chewed we listened—because here, sound was never mere background. A quiet night, a clean chorus of crickets, the surf’s regular breathing: these were as comforting as a full dish. Noise without meaning—sudden, metallic, furious—was what we feared, for it belonged to danger and to the old world we had escaped.

We fed the fire for the watch, crept into the tent, and slept soundly.

We worked hard in the days that followed: the bear’s meat was smoked, the fat melted and stored, bamboos gathered in quantity. Yet I wanted one more excursion before we settled into the nearer routines, and at early dawn I roused the boys.

Fritz mounted the mule; I rode Lightfoot; Jack and Franz took their usual steeds. The dogs bounded ahead, and we went first to the euphorbia to collect its gum, then to see whether the ostrich had deserted her eggs in the sand.

Ernest watched us go without the slightest look of regret—book and quiet suited him as much as chase suited Jack. He turned back to the tent to help his mother and to read, as if ink and paper were also provisions to be stored.

We rode down the Green Valley quickly, following our former track. We reached Turtle Marsh, filled our flasks, and came to the rising ground where Fritz had once seen mounted strangers. Today there was only wind, and the wind’s voice lay clean over the land.

Jack and Franz begged for a gallop. I let them press ahead, while Fritz and I turned to the euphorbia trees. From the incisions I had made, red gum had exuded and then hardened in the sun into resinous tears. I rolled the coagulated drops into little balls and stored them in a bamboo jar.

Fritz watched me closely. “Did you not tell me that the juice of that tree was poisonous, father? Why have you collected such a quantity?”

“I did indeed say so,” I answered. “It is a most deadly poison. The inhabitants of the Cape use it to poison springs where wild animals assemble to drink, and so slaughter them for their hides. I intend, however, to use it to destroy the apes should they again commit depredations, and also in preparing skins to protect them from insects.”

We rode on after the boys. They were still some distance away when suddenly four magnificent ostriches rose from the sand as if the earth itself had thrown them up. The sound of their rising was like heavy cloth snapped in the air—the rush of wings, the thud of feet, the startled snort of horses.

Jack and Franz saw them and, with shouts, drove them toward us. The male ran in front—shining black feathers, great tail plume waving; three females followed, ash-grey and swift. Their speed was astonishing. They were within gunshot before they noticed us.

Fritz had had the forethought to bind his eagle’s beak, so that if it struck the bird down it could not tear it. Now he unhooded and cast the falcon upward. The eagle climbed, circled, then dropped like a thrown knife onto the head of the foremost ostrich. The great bird, confused and terrified, faltered—just enough.

Jack was on him in an instant. He flung his lasso; the coils caught wing and leg; the ostrich crashed down in a storm of sand and feather.

The other three ostriches vanished into distance, and we leaped down to approach our prize. Even bound, it fought with such strength—kicking right and left with those terrible legs—that for a moment I nearly despaired of getting it home alive.

Then I remembered what darkness had done to it before. I seized my coat and hunting bag and flung them over its head, shutting out the light.

Its fury fell away as if someone had turned a key. The struggling stopped, and we could come close. We buckled a broad strip of sealskin around its body and fixed stout cords on either side for leading. Another cord looped its legs to prevent it from breaking into a full gallop. Then we freed it from the lasso.

“Do you know,” I asked the boys, “how the natives of India secure a newly captured elephant?”

“Oh, yes!” Fritz said at once. “They fasten him between two tame elephants.”

“We’ll do that to this fine fellow,” Jack added, delighted with the image, “and tame him double quick.”

“The only difficulty,” Jack went on, “is that we have no tame ostriches. However, I daresay Storm and Grumble will have no objection to perform their part.”

So we led Storm and Grumble up on either side of the recumbent bird and secured the cords to their girths. Jack and Franz mounted, each with a stout whip. We pulled away the covering from the ostrich’s eyes and stood ready.

For a few moments, as sight returned, it lay perfectly still. Then it sprang up and tried to dash forward. The thongs held. It was brought to its knees. It struggled, found the resistance unyielding, and at last seemed to accept the new law of its body. It set off at a sharp trot. The boys’ merry shouts made the air ring and echoed off the sand, an exuberant racket that would have been unbearable had it been the harsh, purposeless clangour of machines. This was living noise—breath, hooves, laughter, the honest uproar of effort—and it carried the island’s wild heart in it.

Still, even the ostrich could not run forever with two horses as anchors. Storm and Grumble refused at last to continue such a race across loose sand, and the bird came to a stand.

The boys had enjoyed their long run. I told them to walk the prisoner home slowly, while Fritz and I went back to the nest. The eggs were warm; the mother had left them only recently. We took about half, leaving the rest, and I packed the chosen eggs carefully in a large bag and slung it before me.

We soon overtook our advance guard and reached the tent without further incident.

My wife’s face, when she saw us, held astonishment and dismay together like two jars on one shelf.

“My dear husband,” she exclaimed, “do you think our provisions so abundant that you must scour the deserts to find some great beast to assist us to devour them? You must discover an iron mine next, for iron is what ostriches chiefly live on, is it not? Oh, I do wish you would be content with the menagerie you have already collected, instead of bringing in a specimen of every beast you come across. And this is such a useless monster!”

“Useless! Mother,” Jack cried, affronted on the ostrich’s behalf. “You would not say so had you seen him run. Why, he will be the fleetest courser in our stables. I am going to make a saddle and bridle for him, and in future he shall be my only steed. Then as for his appetite, father declares it is most delicate—he only wants a little fruit and grass, and a few stones and tenpenny nails to help his digestion.”

Jack’s instant claim of ownership stirred his brothers.

“Very well,” he said, quick as a knife. “Let us each take possession of the part of the ostrich we captured. Your bird, Fritz, seized the head; keep that. Father shall have the body. I’ll have the legs, and Franz a couple of feathers from the tail.”

“Come, come,” I said, amused despite myself. “Jack has a very good right to the ostrich, seeing that he brought it to the ground. If he succeeds in taming it and converting it into a saddle-horse it shall be his. From this time, therefore, he is responsible for its training.”

The day was too far advanced to think of setting out for Rockburg. We fastened the ostrich between two trees and spent the remainder of the evening preparing for our departure.

At early dawn our picturesque caravan moved homewards. The ostrich was still so refractory that we again marched him between Storm and Grumble. Since our best steeds were thus employed, the cow was harnessed to the cart laden with our treasures. Room was left for my wife in the cart. Jack and Franz rode Storm and Grumble. I rode Lightfoot, and Fritz brought up the rear on Swift.

At the mouth of the Gap we halted. The boys’ line of feathers and rags had served its purpose, but I replaced it with a stout palisade of bamboos. I also collected pipe-clay there, thinking ahead to winter work and the slow, satisfying labour of shaping vessels—pots that would one day hold grain, broth, and whatever sauces my wife could coax from our herbs.

When we reached the sugar-cane grove, we stopped to collect the peccary hams we had left to smoke. The meat had taken on that firm, deep colour that comes when smoke and time have done their quiet work, and its smell—sweet, resinous, meaty—clung to our fingers. My wife asked me to gather seeds of an aromatic plant in the neighbourhood that smelled of vanilla. I collected a good supply, already imagining those seeds dried and guarded like a small library of flavour, each one a word in a language the tongue remembers even when the world forgets.

We pushed on toward Woodlands, intending to rest there for the night. The tent went up; we lay down on beds of cotton and slept like people who have learned to be grateful for stillness.

In the morning we examined our farmyard. Everything looked prosperous: our domestic creatures multiplied, busy and content. The sight made us long more than ever for Rockburg, and we decided to hasten there with all possible speed. The number of pigs, goats, and poultry had increased greatly since our last visit; my wife wished especially to take two fine broods of chickens.

We found, too, that the herd of antelopes Fritz and Jack had driven through the Gap had settled nearby. Several times we saw the graceful animals browsing among the trees, their presence like a promise that our island could be both pantry and pasture if we treated it wisely.

While at the farm, we repaired stalls and our dwelling-room—making the former safer against wild beasts and the latter fit for our use when we returned. When all was arranged, we rested, and early the next morning completed our journey to Rockburg.

By midday we were home. We threw open doors and windows to let in fresh air; the animals were settled into their places; the cart’s mixed cargo unloaded and put away. The familiar sounds returned—our own threshold’s creak, the cluck and scratch of hens, the steady, comforting tap of tools against bamboo—each noise a mark of possession and peace.

As much time as I could spare, I gave to the ostrich. For the present we fastened him between two bamboo posts in front of the dwelling.

Then I turned to the eggs. I determined, if possible, to hatch them by artificial heat. I arranged a stove and kept it at a uniform temperature, placing the eggs upon it carefully wrapped in cotton wool, like treasures in a padded chest. Each day I listened—almost foolishly—for some change: a shift, a faint tapping, anything that might translate the silent future inside those shells into a sound my heart could understand.

Next morning Fritz and I took the boat first to Whale Island to establish the angora rabbits, and then to Shark Island, where we placed the dainty little antelopes. We left them in liberty and abundance, and returned quickly to cure the bears’ skins and add our provisions to the stores in the cellar.

On the way back we met Jack, making toward Rockburg in great glee with a basket that seemed too heavy for his pride alone. Inside lay an immense eel, sleek and muscular.

Ernest had set two lines the previous night; one had been dragged away, but the other held this splendid fellow. We ate half at dinner—firm white flesh, rich and sweet when properly cooked—and salted the remainder for storage. Even here, on a shore that had never heard a market bell, we kept the old human rhythm: eat, preserve, sow, and remember.

For a little while we returned to duties about the house. Thinking our verandah would be improved by creepers, I sowed around the foot of each bamboo pillar vanilla and pepper-seed, with others that would climb and spread. They would shade the house in summer and make it pleasanter to look upon—usefulness and beauty braided together, as they ought to be.

I made hen-coops for the hens and chicks we had brought from Woodlands, for I knew Knips and Fangs—those inquisitive rascals—might be tempted to make anatomical experiments detrimental to the youngsters’ welfare.

Ernest’s rat-skins were declared a nuisance indoors and hung outside. The odour was so powerful that even then Jack pretended to faint every time he passed them, making Franz shriek with laughter and my wife shake her head, half-scolding, half-amused.

The museum received additions: the condor and vulture were placed there to be stuffed when the rainy season gave us leisure. The mica and asbestos were brought in as well—not to lie idle, but to wait until I could use them for china-making and lamp-wicks.

After two days thus spent, we faced the larger tasks ahead: cultivating fields of wheat, barley, and maize; managing the ostrich eggs; taming the captives.

Agriculture, though least to our taste, was most important. We began there. The animals drew the plough, but digging and hoeing taxed us to the utmost. We worked two hours in the morning and two in the evening, and the words of Scripture proved themselves on our backs and in our hands: “In the sweat of thy face shalt thou eat bread.” Yet even that sweat had its own honest salt; it tasted of continuance.

In the intervals we devoted ourselves to the ostrich. But our efforts seemed vain. He remained untameable, wild-eyed and stubborn. I decided to try the plan that had subdued the refractory eagle: tobacco fumes.

The effect almost alarmed me. The ostrich sank to the ground and lay motionless. Slowly, at length, he rose and paced between the bamboo posts—subdued, but to my dismay he refused all food. Three days he pined, weaker each day, and the sight of so large a creature fading before us was like watching a fire go out when there is no wood left to add.

“Food he must have!” I cried. “Food he must have!”

My wife, practical where I was anxious, attempted an experiment. She mixed maize flour with butter and rolled it into balls. One she placed within the bird’s beak. He swallowed, stretched out his long neck, and looked for more as if the world had suddenly become edible again. A second, third, and fourth ball followed. His appetite returned; his strength came back like colour returning to a cheek.

The wildness did not return with it. His fierce nature seemed to have drained away, leaving a creature that could learn. He ate rice, guavas, maize, and corn readily, “washing it down,” as Jack said, with small pebbles—an action that astonished Franz until I explained that birds require such stones to grind their food within them, as smaller birds take gravel.

After a month of careful training, the ostrich would trot and gallop, obey the sound of our voice, and feed from our hand. He grew docile—so docile that our minds, always forced into invention, were taxed again: how does one saddle and bridle a bird?

A bit for his beak was impossible. I tried idea after idea and rejected them all. Then I remembered the power of light and its absence over him: how his fury had ceased when his eyes were covered, and how, with sight restored, strength returned.

I made a leathern hood reaching from neck to beak, with holes for eyes and ears. Over the eye-holes I contrived square flaps—blinkers—arranged with whalebone springs so they closed tightly of themselves. The reins connected to these blinkers, so the rider could raise them or let them fall.

When both blinkers were open, the ostrich galloped straight ahead. Close the right eye, and he turned left. Close the left, and he turned right. Shut both, and he stood stock still—as if darkness itself were a command.

Proud as I was of the contrivance, I could not test it fully until I had made a saddle. After several failures I succeeded in manufacturing one to my liking, something like an old-fashioned trooper’s saddle, peaked before and behind—for my greatest fear was always that the boys would fall. I set it on the shoulders near the neck and secured it with strong girths round the wings and across the breast, so it could not slip down the bird’s sloping back.

Thus, through smoke, sweat, seeds, and steady practice, we drew the wild into the circle of our domestic life, and found again—as if in an old story told in a new land—that survival is not merely taking, but learning how to keep, how to name, how to remember, and how to feed those we love.
