The sun, our Lord of Day, was sinking toward the horizon, his golden face obscured by the choking, black smoke of a burning mountain. Phorenice wrung the water from her soaked garments, her teeth chattering in the sudden chill. "Row harder, Deucalion," she commanded, her voice sharp despite the shiver. "Take me through the water-gate and restore me to my comforts. That merchant will rue the day he saw how his fine silks were ruined, and I, too, lament the loss. As a woman, I have no reserve stock of garments to replace these." She glanced sideways at me, tossing back her short, fiery red hair. "Tell me, what is your judgment on my wisdom in bringing you here without an escort?"

"The Empress can do no wrong," I recited the ancient formula, forcing a smile.

"At least I have proven I can fight," she continued, her eyes gleaming with a mixture of triumph and challenge. "I caught you looking at me with approval more than once. You were a difficult man to thaw, Deucalion, but you warm perceptibly the closer you stay to me. La, sir, we shall be a pair of rustic sweethearts before long if this continues. I am glad I devised this trip near those smelly fishermen."

She had taken me out in the litter solely to invite a confrontation, to display her martial prowess, and perhaps to observe my own bearing under pressure firsthand. Well, if we were to share a life as husband and wife, it was prudent that each knew the other's strengths intimately. Furthermore, I was a veteran of battle, too enamored with the glorious handling of arms to quarrel deeply with anyone who offered a worthy fight. Still, I could not help but compare Phorenice to another woman. Given the same opportunity, Nais would have spared me the struggle out of sheer pity for the squalid rebels who refused to acknowledge her as their chieftain. Here, however, was this Empress frittering away twenty of her hardest subjects merely to gratify a whim.

Yet, bound by my vows as a priest and the commands of the High Council on the Sacred Mountain, I suppressed these wayward thoughts. As I rowed across the swaying waves toward the harbor forts, I prayed to the High Gods for the dexterity of speech, a power they granted me through their love for Atlantis and the harassed people I sought to serve. Phorenice's eyes glowed with delight as I spoke.

The beach where we had fought was protected from external ships by a jagged chain of rocks rising from the deep, running from the harbor wall to the city's edge. The fishermen knew the passes, often slipping through without touching a stone, or leaping out to carry their light boats until the water floated them again. But I possessed neither the local knowledge nor the dexterity required. I thought to the High Gods: *Show finally if You wish this woman, who has defiled You, to reign in Atlantis, and if You wish me to serve as her husband.*

I cried these prayers inwardly, awaiting an omen. There was no half-answer. A great wave rose in the lagoon behind us, a surge capable only of an earthquake, and on its sleek back, we were hurled forward, cleared of the reefs and their clinging seaweed without so much as grazing a stone. Bowing my head as I rowed toward the harbor forts, I realized the High Gods had not yet taken vengeance for the insults this lovely woman had offered Them.

Inside the forts, sentries beat drums in their rotation, paying little heed to our fishing boat clamoring for the chain to be lowered. Fortunately, two officers were airing themselves in a turret of the nearer fort; they recognized our shouts. They silenced the drums, lowered torches to inspect our faces, and then, with a splash, dropped the great chain into the water.

A galley lay moored against the harbor wall. Ropes were lowered, and a crew descended to man the oars. We were given customary changes of dry clothing as gifts from the officers, donning them in the galley's cabin to replace our sodden rags. Fevers could be caught by carrying wet clothes after sunset, though I knew from experience they could be warded off with drugs. I noticed with grim amusement that the Empress possessed so little of the Goddess within her that she feared the ailments of frail humanity almost as much as I did.

The galley sped across the calm harbor waters and made fast to the gold rings of the royal quay. While we waited for litters, I watched a lantern light in the boat guarding Phorenice's mammoth. The huge red beast stood shoulder-deep in the harbor water, trunk upturned. It was tamed now, the lantern's light rippling across its trembling skin. Yet, I chose not to intercede for it. If the mammoth sank into the harbor mud and was swallowed, I could bear the loss with equanimity.

Truth be told, that ride on the great beast's back had impressed me unfavorably. It instilled in me a sense of helplessness nearly intolerable. Perhaps circumstances had made me unduly self-reliant, but I preferred walking on my own proper feet, as the Gods intended. On my own feet, I could guard my own head and neck, a feat I had accomplished on four continents throughout a long, active life. On the back of that detestable mammoth, however, I grew as nervous as a child or a coward.

Having little leisure for personal fears, I listened as Phorenice questioned the port-captain about Nais's fate. He reported that she had been locked in a dungeon beneath the royal pyramid, with a guard officer bonding for her safety.

"It is to be hoped he understands his work," the Empress said coldly. "That pretty Nais knows the pyramid better than most; he may be sent to the tormentors for placing her in a cell with a secret outlet. Would you feel pleasure if the girl escaped, Deucalion?"

"Assuredly," I replied, knowing secrecy was useless. "I hold no enmity against Nais."

"But I do," she said viciously. "And I am still minded to lock your faith to me with the wedding gift you know of."

"The thing shall be done," I said. "First, before all else, the Empress of Atlantis."

"Poof! Deucalion, you are too stiff and formal. You ought to be honored that I condescend to be jealous of your favors. Your hand, sir, to help me into the litter. Come in beside me and keep me warm against the night air. Ho, you guards with the torches! Keep back against the street walls; the perfume you burn stifles me."

That night, another feast was held in the royal banqueting hall. I sat beside Phorenice on the raised dais beneath the symbols of the snake and the outstretched hand. What had been implied before was now proclaimed openly; the Empress acknowledged me as her future husband, and the curled, jeweled throng of courtiers hailed me as superior to themselves by virtue of her choice. There was method in their salutations. Rumors of my preference for older, simpler habits must have circulated, for no wine was drunk to my health in the new, impertinent manner. Decorously, each lord and lady approached, spilling a goblet at my feet. When I called anyone up to receive tit-bits from my platter, they ate simply and thankfully, without extravagant kissing or pocketing.

The flaring jets of earth-breath revealed a plainer habit of dress and a more sober demeanor among this thoughtless mob. Phorenice, leaning over until her ruddy curls brushed my face, chided me playfully for usurping her authority.

"Oh, sir," she pleaded mockingly, "do not make your rule over us too ascetic. I have given no orders for this change. Tonight, there are no perfumes; the food is so plain I half intend to burn the cook. As for the clothes and gauds of these diners, by my face! they might have come straight from the old King's reign before I stepped in to show how tasteful colors could be on a robe. It is done by no orders of mine, Deucalion. They have swung to this change by sheer courtier instinct. Look at the beards of the men! There is not half the curl about many of them today that they showed with such exquisiteness yesterday. By my face! I believe they'd reap their chins tomorrow as smooth as yours if you continue setting the fashions at this prodigious rate and I do not interfere."

"Why hinder them if they feel more cleanly shaven?"

"No, sir. There shall be only one clean chin where a beard can grow in all Atlantis, and that shall be carried by the man who is husband to the Empress. Why, my Deucalion, would you have no sumptuary laws? Would you have these good folk here and the common people outside imitate us in every cut of hair and fold of garment which pleases us? Come, sir, if you and I chose to say that our sovereignty is marked only by superior strength of arm and wit, they would hate us for our arrogance. Whereas, if we keep apart to ourselves a few mere personal decorations, these become just objects to admire and pleasantly envy."

"You show me there is more in the office of a ruler than meets the eye."

"And yet they tell me, and indeed show me, that you have ruled with some success."

"I employed the older method. It requires a Phorenice to invent these nicer flights."

"Flatterer!" she said, smiting me playfully with the back of her little fingers. "You are becoming as great a courtier as any of them. You make me blush with your fine pleasantries, Deucalion, and there is no fan-girl here tonight to cool my cheek. I must choose another. But it shall not be Ylga. Ylga seems to have more kindness for you than I like. If she is wise, she will go live in her palace at the other side of the city and occupy herself with ordering her slaves and making embroideries. I shall not be hard on Ylga unless she forces me, but I will have no woman in this kingdom treat you with undue civility."

"And how am I to act," I asked, falling in with her mood, "when I see and hear all the men of Atlantis making their protestations before you? By your own confession, they all love you as ardently as they seem to have loved you hopelessly."

"Ah, now," she said. "You must not ask me to do impossibilities. I am powerful if you will. But I have no force to govern the hearts of these poor fellows on matters such as that. But if you choose, make proclamation that I am given body and inwards to you. If they continue to offend your pride, you may take your culprits and give them over to the tormentors. Indeed, Deucalion, I think it would be a pretty attention to me if you arranged such a ceremony. It seems to me a present," she added with a frown, "that the jealousy is too much on one side."

"You must not expect that a man who has been divorced from love for a busy life can learn all its niceties in an instant. With any other schoolmistress than you, Phorenice, I should not be so forward. In fact, judging by my past record, I should not have begun to learn at all."

"I suppose you think I should be satisfied with that? Well, I am not. I can be finely greedy over some matters."

The banquet did not extend to inordinate length. Phorenice had undergone much since her last sleep. Though she had declared herself Goddess in the meantime, her body remained mortal. Black rings of weariness had grown under her wondrous eyes. When dancers came to posture before us, she threw a jewel at them and bade them begone before completing half their performance. The poet, a silly swelling fellow who came to sing the deeds of the day, she would not hear at all.

"To-morrow," she said wearily, "but for now grant me peace. My Lord Deucalion has given me much food for thought this day. Presently, I go to my chamber to muse over the future policies of this State throughout the night. To-morrow come to me again, and if your poetry is good and short, I will pay you surprisingly. But see to it that you are not long-winded. If there are superfluous words, I will pay you for those with the stick."

She rose, and when the banqueters had saluted, I led her away from the hall down passages with secret doors leading to her private chambers. She clung to my arm, and once when we halted as a great stone block swung slowly ajar, she drooped her head against my shoulder. Her breath came warm against my cheek, and the loveliness of her face so close surpassed the description of words. I think it was in her mind that I should kiss the red lips held so near to mine, but willing though I was to play the appointed part, I could not bring myself to it. So, when the stone block swung shut, she drew away with a sigh, and we proceeded without further speech.

"May the High Gods treat you tenderly," I said at the door of her bed-chamber.

"I am my own God," she replied, "in all things but one. By my face! you are a tardy wooer, Deucalion. Where do you go now?"

"To my own chamber."

"Oh, go then, go."

"Is there anything more I could do?"

"Nothing that your wit or will would prompt you to. Yes, indeed, you are finely decorous, Deucalion, in your old-fashioned way, but you are a mighty poor wooer. Don't you know, my man, that a woman esteems some things the more highly if they are taken from her by rude force?"

"It seems I know little enough about women."
