The sun stretched its golden fingers across the days, elongating them without delivering the answer Romola craved or the terror she feared. There was no word from Baldassarre, and despite the Government's vigilant eyes, the shadow of the suspected conspiracy remained unbroken. Yet, in this suspended silence, other forces surged forward, pressing close against her heart and weaving a bridge between the phantom-laden corridors of her anxiety and the tangible trials of the present. These forces were the spreading Plague and the thunderous Excommunication of Savonarola.

Both calamities served to halt her drifting away from the Frate, anchoring her once more to the man who had unveiled for her a life of sacred duty. Now, as he appeared to falter in his struggle against the tide of profligacy, Romola found she could not carry the sublime, ecstatic joy of her own existence into the hallowed halls of pestilence and suffering without acknowledging the debt she owed to Fra Girolamo. How could she witness the silencing of a soul whose greatness lay not in heresy or superstition, but in the fierce energy with which he forged the Christian life into reality, without feeling pulled irresistibly to his defense?

Deep into the scorching days of June, the decree from Rome arrived and was solemnly proclaimed within the vast expanse of the Duomo. Romola attended, seeking to fortify the sympathy she felt for Savonarola, a wellspring of her own strength. The scene stood in stark, memorable contrast to the vibrant gatherings she had known before. Gone were the upturned faces of citizens, rising in amphitheater-like tiers toward the walls to form a garland of hope around the elders; gone was the mighty voice that thrilled the heart with visions of visible and invisible struggles. Instead, the evening air grew heavy, the bare walls darkened by the flickering glow of tapers. A black and grey flock of monks and secular clergy stood with bent, expectant faces, listening to the occasional tinkling of small bells interrupting a monotonous voice that read a sentence already hanging in the air for weeks. Finally, the lights died, leaving only the slow, shuffling tread of monkish feet retreating into a dim, suffocating silence.

Romola's fervor for the Frate was doubled by the gleeful triumph etched onto the hard, coarse faces of the crowd, and by the fear-stricken confusion visible in the expressions of many of his staunchest allies. The boundary between the duty of obedience and the right of resistance was never easy to define, but here it was rendered overwhelmingly difficult by a conviction that the Church was not merely a political compromise, but a living organism infused with divine power to bless and to curse. For most pious Florentines, this belief was not a chosen opinion but an inalienable impression, as undeniable as the curve of the sky. Thus, Savonarola's bold arguments—that the Excommunication was unjust and therefore void—did not reassure them; instead, they trembled more deeply, viewing his defiance as a sacrilege cast against a mystic image whose power knew no defense.

Romola, however, had never drawn her early nourishment from the traditional currents of the Christian community where her father had lived apart. Her relation to the Church flowed solely through Savonarola; his moral force was the only authority she had ever bowed to. In his excommunication, she saw only the menace of hostile vice. On one side stood a man devoted to public virtue and spiritual purity; on the other, the assault of alarmed selfishness, led by a lustful, greedy, lying, and murderous old man—Rodrigo Borgia, now elevated to infamy as Pope Alexander the Sixth. The finer shades of truth that soften such stark antitheses are rarely seen by those not distressed by the folly of martyrs or the prudence of their burners. But Romola needed a strength that neutrality could not provide. This Excommunication, by simplifying and ennobling Savonarola's resistance, seemed to offer her a rescue from the threatening isolation of doubt. The Frate was no longer trapped in a petty antagonism with Florentine enemies; he stood simply as a standard-bearer leaping into the breach against a vicious exercise of ecclesiastical power. Life seemed clearest when the heart beat faster at the sight of such generous self-sacrifice. In that moment, there was no doubt of the highest prize the soul could win, and a renewed current of enthusiasm carried her through these difficult summer days.

She had said nothing to Tito regarding her recent interview with Baldassarre or the revelation he had made. What agitating words could she possibly extract from him? Only a cool sarcasm about her sympathy with his assassin, or perhaps nothing at all. Baldassarre was evidently helpless; the true danger was not that he would harm Tito, but that Tito, tracking him down, might devise a new scheme to rid himself of the injured man who haunted his conscience. Romola felt she could take no decisive action until she saw Baldassarre again and learned the full truth about that "other wife"—whether she was the woman to whom Tito was first bound.

The possibilities surrounding that other wife, which struck the deepest wound to her hereditary pride, mingled with the earliest memories of her illusory love. These suspicions ate away at the lingering tenderness of her past with her husband, shrinking her from Tito with a horror so profound it might have spurred a passionate outburst had he not been unusually absent from home. Like many wealthy citizens during the plague, Tito spent his leisure hours in the countryside. The agreeable Melema was welcomed at many villas, and since Romola had refused to leave the city, he had no need to maintain a country residence of his own.

Finally, in the later days of July, the alleviation of these public troubles left Romola to face the raw weight of her personal lot. The Plague had nearly vanished, and Savonarola's position had grown hopeful thanks to a favorable magistracy writing urgent vindicatory letters to Rome, entreating the withdrawal of the Excommunication. Romola's healthy frame, exhausted by continuous excitement and over-exertion, began to succumb to the inevitable languor of reaction. Yet, her mental restlessness refused to let her remain idle. During the sultry hours, she stayed indoors, but in the cool of the mornings and evenings, she walked constantly, varying her direction with a vague, desperate hope. If Baldassarre still lived, perhaps she might encounter him. Maybe some illness had paralyzed his memory, causing him to forget where she lived, or even her existence entirely. That was her most sanguine explanation for his silence. But the explanation she felt was most probable was far sadder: that he had died of the Plague.
