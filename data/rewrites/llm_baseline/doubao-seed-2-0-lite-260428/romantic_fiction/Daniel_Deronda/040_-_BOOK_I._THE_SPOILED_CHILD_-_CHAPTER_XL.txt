Daniel Deronda stepped out of the narrow Chelsea house with a restlessness coiled tight in his chest, craving hard, physical exertion to burn off the fuming, unnameable temper he could not quite shake. He was bound for the City, and the sight of the Chelsea Stairs, with their line of waiting wherries, settled his decision at once: he would not suffer the jolting, frustrating inaction of a cab ride, but hire a boat and pull the oar himself.

His errand was Ram’s bookshop. He had arrived too late to meet Mordecai at his midday post there the day before, and had been told the quiet, remarkable Jewish man never failed to return between five and six. Deronda ached to deepen their strange, fleeting acquaintance before he redeemed his ring, desperate to avoid the sharp, sudden drop of Mordecai’s interest that had felt like a drawbridge slamming shut on their last meeting, threatening to seal off all easy future connection for Mirah’s sake. As the work of the oar warmed his muscles, he fixed his thoughts on the purpose ahead, and as was his wont, his perspective shifted gently: he stopped seeing Mordecai only as a source of useful information, and chided himself softly for being little better than a recruiting sergeant, blind to the full, complicated inner lives of the people he sought help from.

“If I got every piece of information I most want from him,” he thought, “I daresay I’d be perfectly content to never hear more of his story, never learn why he looked at me as if he had a long-held expectation that I had disappointed. That strange, sharp curiosity he stirs in me would die out. And yet what if we were two passing ships, each carrying an exile who would have recognized the other at once, if only they’d leaned over their rails at the same moment? There’s no great chance of some peculiar tie between that poor, ailing man and me — his voyage can’t last much longer, I’d wager. But I wonder how many thousands of momentous, missed connections happen every day between strangers who only glance blankly at each other, or even hurry to get out of each other’s way in a crowd. And all because people go about their business like that recruiting sergeant, never stopping to see the full soul beside them.”

The wherry was gliding toward Blackfriars Bridge, where Deronda planned to land, when the clock struck half past four. The gray day was dying gloriously, western clouds streaked into narrowing purple strata beneath a wide, soft saffron sky that held the quiet grandeur of a carved monument. On the river, that light danced and shifted, gilding every ripple, turning the brown sails of passing craft to sudden flame, leading laden barges out of shadow and into honeyed color as if the whole waterway was singing a response to the sinking sun’s brooding glory.

Thoroughly warm now, Deronda set down the oar and pulled his Inverness cape back over his shoulders. As he lifted his head to fasten the top button, his eyes caught a face he had never been able to stop thinking of, looking straight toward him over the bridge parapet. Lit by that western glow, it blazed with startling, unearthly distinctness: a portrait of bodily emaciation burning with spiritual eagerness. It was Mordecai. He, too, had been watching the river westward, and had caught sight of the approaching boat, held it fast in his gaze, first out of idle curiosity, then with a rush of returning memory that made him quiver as if caught in a premonition — until the figure on the boat lifted its face to his, the face of all his long-held visions, and lifted a white, outstretched hand to wave at him again and again.

Deronda, desperate that Mordecai see him and wait, had signaled at once. The answer came immediately: Mordecai tore off his cap and waved it, every part of him thrumming with the certainty that his years-long inner prophecy was fulfilled. All obstacles, all absurd, unlikely coincidences, melted away in the flood of completion that swelled his soul. His exultation was no different from that of a scientist watching the first stirrings of a long-experimented reaction he has dreamed of for years. The friend he had prefigured for so long had come from out of that golden sunset light, and signaled to him. It was real. The rest would follow.

In three minutes Deronda had landed, paid his boatman, and was striding to join Mordecai, who stood perfectly still, rooted to the spot by instinct, waiting for him.

“I was so glad to see you standing here,” Deronda said. “I was heading straight for the bookshop to look for you. I was there yesterday — I expect they mentioned it to you?”

“Yes,” Mordecai said, simple and grave. “That is why I came to the bridge.”

The answer was startlingly mysterious. For a split second Deronda wondered if Cohen’s hint of mental unbalance in the strange scholar was true. “You had no way of knowing I was at Chelsea, did you?” he asked after a pause.

“No. But I expected you to come down the river. I have been waiting for you these five years.” His deep, sunken eyes locked on Deronda’s with a look of affectionate, solemn dependence that was at once heartbreaking and awe-inspiring. Deronda’s heart softened completely, even as he could not help but assume this fated connection Mordecai spoke of was built on illusion.

“It would make me happier than I can say if I can be of any real use to you,” he said earnestly. “Shall we take a cab to wherever you wish to go? Your breath is short — you must have tired yourself out walking already.”

“Let us go to the bookshop. It will soon be time for me to be there. But first look up the river,” Mordecai said, turning back toward the water, his voice low and thrumming with a kind of excited calm, so wrapped up in the sense that his long wait was over he could not imagine any barrier to perfect, total understanding between him and Deronda. “See how the sky is fading slow. I have always loved this bridge. I stood on it when I was a little boy. It is a meeting place for spiritual messengers. What the old masters said is true — every order of things has its angel, its full message from far away. Here I have listened to the messages of earth and sky. When I was stronger I would stay here to watch the stars come out in the dark heavens. But this time of day, just before sunset, was always my favorite. It sank into me and lived with me — fading, slow fading, it was the mirror of my own decline. It paused, it waited, until at last it brought me my new life — my new self, who will live on long after this breath of mine is all breathed out.”

Deronda did not speak. He felt strangely, powerfully moved. His first, easy suspicion that Mordecai was delusional, a monomaniac driven mad by disease, melted away into quiet, submissive expectancy. His nature was too wide, too ready to imagine worlds beyond his own narrow experience, to fall back on the lazy, cruel label of “madness” whenever he met a soul whose full conviction fell somewhere where his own was blank. It was in his nature to meet, rather than resist, any claim of another’s need on him — and this claim wrapped him in a solemn hush that made Mordecai’s ragged poverty vanish completely, lifting him into something like authority, as if the mean disguise of a wandering poor scholar had fallen away to reveal a sacred, preordained guide.

“Let us go now,” Mordecai said after a long, silent pause, and once they were settled in the rattling cab he added, “We will get down at the end of the street and walk the rest of the way to the shop. You can browse the books, and Mr. Ram will leave soon, and we will be alone.” The man who had seemed so wrapped up in his visions was as cautious, as attentive to the judgment of other minds, as any worldly, practical man of business could be.

Mirah was still at the back of Deronda’s mind through every strange beat of this experience, but he knew at once that the conversation from here on would be steered by Mordecai, not himself. He was no longer certain what questions he would be able to ask, and he thought to himself, half laughing at his own awestruck mood: “I suppose I am completely superstitious now, waiting for an oracle to reveal my destiny. But what greater, more powerful force has ever moved the world than faith, even when it is misplaced? Than long-held expectation, even when it has been disappointed a thousand times? If it is ever in my power to fulfill what this man expects of me, I will not let him down.”

Ten minutes later, the two men, thrumming with the same intense, unspoken awareness as two unconfessed lovers, found themselves alone in the small, gas-lit bookshop, turning face to face. By instinct they both bared their heads, wanting to see each other fully, no shadows, no pretense. Mordecai leaned his back against the little counter, and Deronda stood against the opposite wall barely four feet away. There was no denying the contrast between them: Mordecai’s face, sharp and gaunt as an abandoned temple, the mark of consumption softening his features but making his eyes burn brighter, the black hair and beard throwing the yellow pallor of his skin into stark relief, his wasted hands folded across his chest. His gaze held all the yearning of a dying mother looking at the only son she knows will carry her memory forward, that same flicker of fragile, bright gladness.

Across from him, Deronda’s face held the rich, unspent health of youth, his masculine gravity lending a quiet weight to the reverence with which he met Mordecai’s gaze. His rare, exquisite sympathy, his instinct to meet another’s deepest feeling without pre-judgment, was never more fully tested. He did not truly believe Mordecai’s conviction that they were fated to meet was rooted in fact, but he heard the cry from the very depths of the other man’s soul, and felt the clear summons to be receptive, rather than proud and dismissive. That receptiveness gave his face an expression of calm, benignant force that nourished Mordecai’s confidence, and cleared the path for him to speak.

“You cannot know what has guided me to you, to bring us together at this moment. You are wondering.”

“I am not impatient,” Deronda said. “I am ready to listen to whatever you wish to tell me.”

“You see some of the reasons why I needed you,” Mordecai said, quiet, careful not to waste his remaining strength. “You see I am dying. You see I am like a man locked behind bars by the side of the road, who if he speaks to passersby will only get head shakes and pity. The day is closing, the light is fading — soon we would not have been able to make each other out. But you have come in time.”

“I rejoice that I am here in time,” Deronda said, thick with feeling. He would not say I hope you are not mistaken in me — the word “mistaken” would have been unforgivably cruel in that moment.

“But the hidden reasons I needed you began long, long ago,” Mordecai went on. “They began in my youth, when I studied in a different land. Beloved ideas came to me, because I was a Jew. They were a sacred trust I had to fulfill, because I was a Jew. They were an inspiration, because I was a Jew, and I felt the great heart of my people beating inside my chest. They were my whole life. I was not fully born until that moment. I counted every breath, every waking moment, every bite of food I ate to keep my body going, every sight I saw, nothing but fuel for that divine flame. But I was like a man who wanders into rocky solitudes to carve his thoughts into stone, before he can make his way back, care, labor, and disease block his path, and bind him with chains that eat into the soul. And then I asked myself: how do I save the life inside me, before it is stifled along with my failing breath?”

He paused to rest his ragged breath, and to calm his rising excitement. Deronda dared not speak: the silence in that narrow little shop hummed with awe and compassion, wrapped around Mordecai’s fervor.

“You may misunderstand me,” Mordecai said after a moment. “I am no ignorant dreamer, reared in inland valleys, thinking ancient thoughts new and not knowing they are old, who never stood by the great ports where the world’s knowledge passes to and fro. English is my mother tongue, England is the native land of this body, which is nothing but a crumbling pot of earth around a fruit tree whose seed could make the desert bloom. But my true life was nourished in Holland, at the feet of my mother’s brother, a Rabbi of great learning. When he died I went to Hamburg to study, then to Göttingen, to take a wider view of my people, and of the Gentile world, drinking knowledge from every source. I was young then, free, I traveled to all the great Jewish communities in Germany, and I was not in utter poverty. I learned a handicraft too. I told myself, it does not matter if my lot is like that of Joshua ben Chananja: after the great destruction he earned his bread making needles, but in his youth he had sung on the steps of the Temple, and carried the memory of the glory before it departed. I chose that fate knowingly. People called me a man who feeds on visions, and I did not deny it — for visions are the creators and feeders of the whole world. I see the world exactly as it is, and the vision will remake it. You are not listening to a madman who raves apart from the lives of his fellow men.”

Mordecai paused, and Deronda, sensing he waited for a response, said, “Do not think I would ever call your words raving. I listen to know, without prejudice. I have known enough myself to feel deep interest in the story of a spiritual destiny embraced willingly, in youth.”

“A spiritual destiny embraced willingly in youth?” Mordecai corrected gently. “It was my soul being born fully, when I was still a boy. It brought its own world — a medieval world, where men made the ancient Hebrew language live again in new psalms of exile. They wove Gentile philosophy into Jewish faith, and still yearned for a center to gather our scattered people. One of their souls awoke in me, and traveled through their world, into Spain and Provence, debating with Aben-Ezra, sailing with Jehuda ha-Levi, hearing the roar of the Crusaders and the shrieks of tortured Israel. And when its tongue was loosed, it spoke that old language, made alive again with the blood of their ardor, their sorrow, their martyred trust. While it is imprisoned in me, it will never learn another.”

“Have you written entirely in Hebrew, then?” Deronda asked, thinking back to that first meeting, when Mordecai had asked if he knew the tongue.

“Yes,” Mordecai said, his voice heavy with sadness. “In my youth I wandered into that solitude, not knowing it was a solitude. I had all the great dead around me, the martyrs gathering to listen. But soon I found the living were deaf to me. At first I saw my life stretching long before me — I thought part of my Jewish heritage is unbreakable patience, the skill to find a foothold even where all others say nothing can grow. But then new messengers came to me from the Eternal. I had to bow under the same yoke that weighs on every person born of woman: family troubles called me away, I had to work, to care for others, not just for myself. When I was left alone again, the angel of death had already turned to me, and I felt his robes brushing my heels every step I took. I never stopped trying. I begged for help and understanding, went to every man of my people with influence, or wealth, or learning. But none would listen. I was rebuked for error, offered a few coins in charity. No wonder. I looked poor, carried a bundle of Hebrew manuscripts, and said our leading teachers are misleading the hope of our whole race. Scholar and merchant were both too busy to listen. Scorn stood between me and every one of them. One man said the Book of Mormon would never work in Hebrew, and that it was absurd to think a ragged stranger like me could teach the learned men of our people anything. He was not entirely wrong.”

“Even if you wrote in Hebrew for years,” Deronda said gently, trying to offer him comfort, “you surely command English better than almost anyone.”

Mordecai shook his head slowly. “Too late. Too late. I can write no more. My writing now would be as ragged as this gasping breath. If I wrote in English now, I would be like a man beating on a wooden board to summon people who have only ever answered the ringing of a bell. My soul knows the flaws of its own speech. Any new writing from me would be just like this body — it might hold the divine breath of thought, but people would smile and say ‘only a poor Jew’ and the cruellest smilers would be my own people.”

His head sank, despondency wrapping around him like a dark cloud for a moment, and Deronda leaned forward, his voice clear and warm, a comfort all its own. “I feel with you. I feel very strongly with you. But forgive me for speaking too fast — nothing you have written has to be buried forever. The means to publish it are well within your reach. If you trust me, I can make sure everything you need to see it done is provided for.”

“That is not enough,” Mordecai said, looking up at once, the light of his confidence blazing back. “That is not all I trust you for.
