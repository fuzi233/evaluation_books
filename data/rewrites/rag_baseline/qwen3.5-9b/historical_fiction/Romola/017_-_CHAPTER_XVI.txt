"Full up. No room."

The door slammed with the dull, decisive thud of bureaucratic finality.

Tito stood in the chill of the early morning, calculating the precise cost of his failure. To have walked the streets of Florence seeking employment, then begging for a night's rest in the casual ward, was a tactical error already committed. To be denied entry to that sanctuary of the destitute was a strategic defeat that threatened his entire campaign. He had lost his confidence in Fortune; the feverish excitement of his earlier alarms had curdled into a dull, regretful lassitude. He realized now that he had valued the fragile approval of his peers above the harsh necessities of survival, risking ignominy to avoid what they called obligation.

But actions, once taken, possess an indestructible vitality. Like children born to a parent, they live and act apart from the will of their creator. Deeds cannot be strangled; they persist in consciousness and beyond, pressing upon the actor with relentless force. For the first time, this dreadful vitality of his past deeds was crushing Tito.

He turned toward his lodgings in the Piazza di San Giovanni, deliberately avoiding the Mercato Vecchio—the shortest route—to evade the possibility of seeing Tessa. He was not in the mood to seek anything; he could only await the first sign of his altered lot.

The piazza was bathed in the warm sunlight of a festival morning, where autumn dew still lingered, inviting an idleness undulled by fatigue. Around the octagonal Baptistery, stalls were being spread with fruit and flowers, while laden mules stood quietly absorbed in their nose-bags, their drivers perhaps already kneeling before the Blessed Virgin within the sacred doors. On the broad marble steps of the Duomo, scattered groups of beggars and gossipers formed a chaotic tableau: an old crone with white hair encouraged a round-capped baby to test its tiny bare feet on the warmed stone, while a nearby dog sniffed the performance with suspicion; shaggy-headed boys watched a small, pale cripple carving a face onto a cherry-stone; and above them, on the wide platform, men practiced changing knots in desultory laughter or gesticulated eagerly in close pairs.

Yet the largest and most significant assembly of loungers awaited Tito. It was peak business hours for Nello, and in this warm season, clients preferred the shade of the pretty red-and-white awning outside the shop to the cramped interior. Sitting with a lathered chin thrown backward, allowing one's nose to serve as a handle, was not a noble posture, yet it was a fashion of Florentine respectability. Men looked grave and serious while undergoing this ritual, united by the shared absurdity of the moment. It was also the hour when yesterday's gossip was freshest, and the barber's tongue found its greatest glory alongside the razor. The deft activity of those two instruments seemed driven by a common spring. Tito foresaw that escape was impossible; he would have to smile, retort, and appear perfectly at ease. It was merely the ordeal of swallowing bread and cheese pills after all. The man who let the mere anticipation of discovery choke him possessed weak nerves.

Just then, a hand landed on his shoulder. No amount of prior resolution could prevent the unpleasant jolt of that sudden contact. As Tito turned, his face betrayed an inward shock, but the owner of the hand broke into a light laugh. He was a young man of Tito's own age, with keen features, a small, close-clipped head, and a closely shaven lip and chin, suggesting a mind stripped of unnecessary material. His eyes were bright with hope and friendliness, qualities that often vanished later in bitterness and disappointment. At this moment, however, there were only pleasant predictions surrounding Niccolò Machiavelli, a man of promise expected to restore the fortunes of his ancient family.

"Why, Melema," Machiavelli said, "what evil dream did you have last night that you mistook my light grasp for that of an *sbirro* or something worse?"

"Ah, Messer Niccolò!" Tito recovered instantly. "It must have been an excess of dulness in my veins this morning that shuddered at the approach of your wit. But the fact is, I have had a bad night."

"That is unfortunate, for you are expected to shine today in the Rucellai Gardens without any obstructing fog. I assume you are attending."

"Messer Bernardo invited me," Tito replied, "but I shall be engaged elsewhere."

"Ah! I remember—you are in love," Machiavelli shrugged. "Otherwise, you would never have such inconvenient engagements. We are to feast on peacock and ortolans under the loggia among Bernardo Rucellai's rare trees. There will be the finest spirits and wines in Florence. Only, since Piero de' Medici will be present, the choice spirits may be drowned in the capricious verses of impromptu poetry. I detest that game; it is a device for the triumph of small wits, who are most inspired by the smallest occasions."

"What are you saying about Piero de' Medici and small wits, Messer Niccolò?" asked Nello, whose slender figure currently dominated the Herculean frame of Niccolò Caparra.

The famous ironworker, seen earlier in the Mercato Vecchio with bared muscles and a leather apron, wore a holiday suit this morning. As he sat submissively while Nello skipped around him, applying lather, seizing his nose, and scraping with magical quickness, he resembled a lion donned in linen and tunic, preparing to enter society.

"A private secretary will never rise in the world if he couples great and small in that manner," Nello continued. "When great men are not permitted to marry their sons and daughters as they wish, small men must not expect to marry their words as they please. Have you heard the news Domenico Cennini was sharing? That Pagolantonio Soderini struck Ser Piero da Bibbiena across the ear for instigating Piero de' Medici to interfere with the marriage between young Tommaso Soderini and Fiammetta Strozzi, and is to be exiled as ambassador to Venice as punishment?"

"I do not know which I envy him most," Machiavelli remarked. "The offense or the punishment. The offense will make him the most popular man in all Florence, while the punishment will send him among the few in Italy who truly understand how to manage their own affairs."

"Yes, provided Soderini remains in Venice long enough," Cennini added. "He may adopt the Venetian style and bring it home. The Soderinis have long been allies of the Medici, but this event may finally open Pagolantonio's eyes to the utility of our old Florentine trick of swapping harnesses when the old one galls us—if we have not entirely lost that trick in the last fifty years."

"Not we," declared Niccolò Caparra, rejoicing in the free movement of his lips again. "Eat eggs during Lent and the snow will melt. That is what I tell our people when they grow noisy over their cups at San Gallo, discussing insurrection. I say: never plan a *romor*. You might as well try to fill the Arno with buckets. When there is sufficient water, the river will be full; but that will not occur until the torrent is ready."

"Caparra, that oracular speech is due to my excellent shaving," Nello quipped. "You could never have produced it with that dark rust on your chin. *Ecco*, Messer Domenico, I am ready for you now. By the way, my learned friend," he continued as he noticed Tito moving toward the door, "old Maso has been seeking you, but your nest was empty. He will return shortly. The old man looked mournful and seemed in haste. I trust there is no trouble in the Via de' Bardi."

"Doubtless Messer Tito knows that Bardo's son is dead," said Cronaca, who had just joined them.

Tito's heart leapt—had the death occurred before Romola saw him?

"No, I had not heard," he replied, maintaining composure equal to the occasion. He turned and leaned against the doorpost, feigning reluctance to depart. "I knew his sister went to see him. Did he die before her arrival?"

"No," Cronaca stated. "I was in San Marco at the time and saw her emerge from the chapter-house with Fra Girolamo. He told us the dying man's breath was preserved by a miracle, solely so he might disclose his secrets to his sister."

Tito felt his fate sealed. His mind raced over the circumstances of his departure from Florence, and he formulated a plan to retrieve his money from Cennini before the disclosure became public knowledge. If he secured his funds, he need not endure the scorching stares and biting words any longer. He would wait, accompany Cennini, and extract the payment immediately. Holding this project in his mind, he stood motionless, hands in his belt, eyes fixed absently on the ground.

Nello glanced at him, convinced Tito was consumed by anxiety regarding Romola. Finding such a pretty image of self-forgetful sadness, he subtly pointed his razor at Tito and shot a challenging look at Piero di Cosimo, whom he had never forgiven for refusing to acknowledge any prognostications of character in his favorite's handsome face. Piero, leaning against the opposite doorpost close to Tito, shrugged his shoulders. The frequent recurrence of such challenges from Nello had shifted the painter's initial neutrality into a positive inclination to believe ill of the much-praised Greek.
