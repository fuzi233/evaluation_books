The road from Oakhurst to Tunbridge was measured not in miles, but in the slow erosion of presence. Mrs. Lambert and her daughters stood beneath the limes, watching the figures shrink until the green canopy swallowed them whole. This was the familiar geometry of departure: husband to war, sons to school, the rhythmic subtraction of bodies from the domestic equation. The empty room retained the phantom weight of the sleeping boy; the hallway held the ghost of the whip; the dinner table remembered the cherry tart eaten in silence. Evening prayer fell flat without the scholar's voice; midnight brought a silence that felt heavier than noise. The limes served as a barrier between the world and the women left behind, a screen where Mrs. Lambert carved invisible names into the bark, seeking permanence in wood that offered none.

Colonel Lambert tipped his hat as the gentlemen passed the trees. "We are out of sight," he said, the gesture final. "My wife watches until we round this corner. Perhaps, Mr. Warrington, seeing the house again will bring better sport."

"I leave old friends," Warrington replied, the sentiment feeling heavy in the cool air.

"The host at Westerham has a son, James Wolfe," Lambert added, his tone shifting to the casual delivery of gossip. "An old friend of ours. My wife, a notorious matchmaker, would have paired him with one of my girls, but fate intervened."

"Ah!" Warrington sighed, the sound involuntary.

"Brave fellows existed before Agamemnon," Lambert mused.

"Were his name Aga...?" Warrington asked, meekly searching for the syllable.

"No, James Wolfe," Lambert corrected, smiling. "Scarce thirty. The youngest lieutenant-colonel in the army, barring the nobility who hold rank by birthright rather than merit."

His companion nodded with the automatic deference of a colonial aristocrat.

Lambert continued, describing a man who commanded captains older and more experienced than himself, yet possessed neither their seniority nor their luck. Wolfe was beloved, a scholar fluent in multiple tongues, a consummate soldier. Warrington listened, feeling the sharp edge of his own inadequacy. "I feel I have neglected my youth," he admitted. "George, my brother, would have represented our name better. He was a scholar, a musician. I am glad to be home, if only to learn how ignorant I am."

"If you know that well, 'tis a gain," Lambert said.

Warrington confessed that recently, bereft of his brother, he had believed himself a fine fellow, flattered by the locals who cared only for dogs, horses, and betting. Now, he wished to know more about books and less about them.

"Dogs and horses are books in their own right," Lambert countered. "Some men are not made for scholarship. Jack Hunt, riding behind, was a private soldier and is now a worthy groom. Does it matter if one holds a baton or a currycomb? When I began, I dreamed of success and honor. Now, I think of duty and the folks we left. Let us trot on; Westerham awaits."

At Westerham, they were greeted by a stately matron and her son, the young Lieutenant-Colonel of Kingsley's regiment. Wolfe was lean, pale, with high cheekbones and red hair, lacking conventional beauty but possessing a courtesy that disarmed observers. Supper revealed the mechanics of the matchmaking machine. The hostess whispered to Lambert about a connection to the Lowther family of the North.

"If she were the daughter of Charlemagne," Lambert laughed, "she is not too good for James Wolfe."

"She is the priceless pearl," the mother insisted, dismissing Wolfe's protestations of unworthiness. "We tend our children through fever and smallpox, break our hearts at parting, and then a girl arrives with bright eyes, and our boy leaves, caring nothing for us."

"How did you marry James's father?" Wolfe asked gently.

"Because he was gouty and needed care," the lady answered simply. "Not because I liked him."

The evening dissolved into easy talk, the friction of separation smoothed by the warmth of shared hospitality.

Next morning, Lambert handed Warrington over to Wolfe's charge. As Lambert rode home, Warrington's thoughts lingered on the parlour at Oakhurst, the three days of calm that now seemed distant. Wolfe, however, spoke of the Lambert household with reverence. "To have such a name, to live such a life," he said, "seems the height of human ambition."

"And glory and honour?" Warrington pressed. "Are those nothing?"

"They were my dreams once," Wolfe answered, his voice quieter now. "Now, my desires are tranquil. I have followed arms since fourteen, seen every duty, served in every garrison. I have done nearly everything a soldier does, except command an army. Now, I seek quiet, books, a wife to love me, children to dandle. True love is better than glory. A tranquil fireside, with the woman of your heart beside it—that is the greatest good the gods can send."

Warrington assented, but his face betrayed a lack of cheer. "You say 'yes' as if a fireside were not to your taste."

"There are other things a young fellow enjoys," Warrington replied. "After sixteen years of the world, I am still tied to my mother's apron-strings. After campaigns, after distinction, after making the world talk of me, then I may retire."

Wolfe responded with a rhapsody on marriage and the beauties of his mistress, a theme that resonated deeply with him but struck Warrington as melancholy. Their ride ended without incident, save for a moment of confusion near Tunbridge Wells where Warrington drew his pistol at innkeepers' agents, mistaking them for highwaymen. Wolfe laughed, calming him, as Gumbo's runaway horse was eventually tamed.

They parted ways at the inn, each heading to the ladies they sought.

Warrington found his aunt in handsome lodgings, attended by a retinue of lacqueys. Her cousin, Lady Maria, was absent. Instead, he encountered a beau in a rich suit and two dowagers with voluminous hoops. The Baroness introduced him as the young Croesus from Virginia, an estate larger than Kent, infinitely fruitful, populated by armies of negroes and gold mines. She spoke of his mother as a sovereign princess, a tale spun with such confidence that the modest youth blushed under the weight of invented grandeur.

"You could not have come at a better moment," the Baroness said as the visitors departed. "They will trumpet your virtues in every company. I have lied about the geography of Virginia to fill out your estate with diamonds and spices. Your income is a mystery, but undoubtedly vast. Prepare to be flattered as you never have been."

"To what end?" Warrington asked, skeptical.

"In the first place, do not contradict your aunt," she replied firmly. "As for your reputation, a London newspaper has already claimed you are immensely wealthy. You cannot help yourself. Tomorrow, the congregation will turn from their prayers to worship the golden calf in your person. Would you have me undeceive them and speak ill of my own flesh and blood?"
