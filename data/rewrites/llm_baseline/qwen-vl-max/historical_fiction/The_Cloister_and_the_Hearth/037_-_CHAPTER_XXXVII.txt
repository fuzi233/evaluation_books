The condemned men were ordered to stand bound in the marketplace for two hours, so that anyone who recognized them—or knew of other crimes they might have committed—could come forward and testify at their trial. But throughout the entire time, no one stepped forward with any new accusation. This was not surprising, for these men were known as night predators, creatures of darkness who preyed upon weary travelers, mostly strangers passing through.

Just as the guards began to unfasten them, a piercing scream tore through the crowd. A woman pointed wildly at one of the prisoners, her eyes wide with terror—and before she could speak, she collapsed unconscious.

Instantly, people rushed around her, some to help, others out of curiosity. As she stirred and began to regain her senses, voices rose in debate.

“It was him she pointed at.”

“No, it was this one!”

“Nay,” another insisted, “it was that sullen fellow with the golden hair hanging about his neck.”

Before more could be said, the woman sprang up like a lioness defending her young. “My child! Man! Man! Give me back my child!” She seized the glossy golden locks the officers had fastened around the prisoner’s neck, tore them from his throat, and pressed them to her face, kissing them feverishly. Then, as clarity returned, she realized what the hair meant—her daughter was dead. With a heart-wrenching cry, she fell to her knees, sobbing over the lock of hair. The crowd roared in fury, surging toward the accused man as if to tear him apart. In those days, every man carried death at his belt. His life would have ended then and there, but Denys drew his sword without hesitation, shouting, “A moi, camarades!” He held the mob back, shielding the prisoner. “Who touches him dies!”

Other archers joined him, and with difficulty they kept the crowd at bay while Denys appealed to the angry throng.

“What kind of justice is this? Would you rob the wheel and give the villain an easy death?”

The mob seethed, but the steel of the archers held them at a distance. The municipal officials, seeing the danger, gathered around and formed a guard, prudently escorting the prisoners back to prison.

The crowd hooted at both the officers and the accused alike. Denys saw the prisoners safely inside, then made his way to “The White Hart,” where he expected to find Gerard.

On the way, he passed a first-floor window where two girls were working. He greeted them. They smiled. Conversation followed. Their manners were open and confident; their complexions bright and healthy.

He invited them to dinner at the inn. They hesitated. He accepted their refusal. Then they agreed. And in their pleasant company, he forgot all about poor Gerard—who, meanwhile, had been taken into custody. On the way, however, Gerard regained his wits and demanded to know by whose authority he was being arrested.

“By the vice-bailiff’s,” replied the constable.

“The vice-bailiff? Alas, what have I, a stranger, done to offend such a man? This charge of sorcery must be a cover. I am no sorcerer—I am only a poor lad far from home.”

The officer was irritated. “Show him the capias, Jacques.”

Jacques held the writ aloft, nearly a yard from Gerard’s eye, while the large constable suddenly pinned him down—both fearing he might snatch the document, which they regarded with superstitious dread.

But Gerard had no such thought. Would he have touched it with tongs? He simply leaned forward and read it. To his astonishment, he saw the name of the vice-bailiff who had signed it: the very alderman who had welcomed him just days before.

He gained courage. “There must be some mistake,” he said. “I ask to be brought before him.”

“What say you to that, Jacques?”

“Impossible. We have no orders to take him before his worship. Read the writ!”

“Nay, but good fellows,” Gerard pleaded, “what harm can it do? I’ll give each of you an ecu.”

“Jacques, what say you now?”

“Humph. I say we have no orders *not* to take him to his worship. Read the writ.”

“Then let us take him to prison—but by way of his worship.”

They agreed. They collected the silver and told Gerard they were doing him a favor. He saw they wanted gratitude as well as gold. He tried to offer it, but words failed him. Feigning was never his strength.

He entered the alderman’s presence with his heart pounding. “Sir,” he stammered, “I beg to know what I have done to displease you since I left your house with Manon and Denys?”

“Nothing that I know of,” the alder人 replied.

When shown the writ, he admitted he had signed it at dawn. “I grow old, and my memory fails me. I had forgotten your own offense—but now I recall. All is well. You are the one I committed for sorcery. Wait—before you go to gaol, you shall hear what your accuser says. Run and fetch him.”

The man could not locate the accuser at once. Impatient, the alderman turned to Gerard. “The main charge is that you set a dead body on fire with a diabolical flame—one that burned but did not consume. If true, young man, I’m sorry for you. For you will surely burn at the stake in Neufchasteau.”

“Oh, sir, let me speak with the cure!”

The alderman shook his head. “The Church is harsher than the town when it comes to sorcerers.”

“But I am innocent,” Gerard insisted, voice trembling between defiance and pleading.

“If you think so,” said the alderman, “officer, take him to the cure—but see he does not escape. Innocent, quotha?”

They found the cure in his doublet, mending a wheelbarrow. Gerard told his story and begged mercy. “They’re going to hang me for using phosphorus to defend myself against thieves!”

It was fortunate that he had already confessed everything to the cure, for now the priest had the full picture. The corporation clutched the stick by the handle; the cure, by the end. He looked solemn and said, “I must question you privately on this matter.” He led Gerard into a small room and ordered the officer to wait outside, guarding the door and ready to enter if called. The big constable stood rigid, expecting the room to vanish in a blast of brimstone.

As soon as they were alone, the cure relaxed. “Show me the trick,” he said, eager.

“I cannot unless the room is dark.”

The cure quickly shut out the light with a wooden shutter. “Now.”

“But what shall I use?” Gerard asked. “It was a dead face that made it look so terrible.”

The cure felt about the room. “Ah—here’s an image. It’s my patron saint.”

“Heaven forbid! That would be profanation.”

“Pshaw! It’ll rub off, won’t it?”

“Aye, but I’d feel guilty taking liberties with a saint,” Gerard objected.

“Fiddlestick!” said the divine.

“To be sure—by placing it on the saint, you show me it’s no Satanic art.”

“Perhaps that was why I suggested it,” the cure replied, with a subtle smile.

Encouraged, Gerard lit the eyes and nostrils of the image, making the cure jump. Then he ignited patches of hair and set the whole face glowing like a firefly.

“By Our Lady!” shouted the cure. “That’s strange—no wonder they took you for a magician. Now, come with me!”

He put on his grey gown and hat, and in moments they stood before the alderman. By his side stood the accuser—a striking figure in red hose and shoes, a black gown with blue trim, and a cocked hat.

After greeting the alderman, the cure turned to the man and said cheerfully, “So, Mangis, back at your trade again, accusing honest men? Come, your worship, this is the same old tale—two of a trade never agree. Here is Mangis, who claims to be a sorcerer, yet sells himself to Satan tonight—though Satan is too wise to buy what he can get for free. This Mangis, who pretends to be a sorcerer, but is merely a quacksalver, accuses a true lad who used a known chemical secret for self-defense—a secret familiar to me and all churchmen.”

“But he is no churchman,” objected the alderman, “to dabble in such mysteries.”

“He is more churchman than layman,” the cure replied swiftly. “Brought up in a convent, in the lesser orders. Therefore, sorcerer, withdraw your complaint.”

“That I will not, your reverence,” Mangis replied firmly. “I *am* a sorcerer—but a white one, not a black. I make no pact with Satan. On the contrary, I fight him with lawful arts. I never profane the sacraments, as black sorcerers do. I do not turn into cats or suck infants’ blood. I simply tell peasants when their livestock are possessed, when to plant rye, which days are lucky for courting or selling oxen. Above all, I detect black magicians—like that whole tribe burned at Dol last year.”

“Ay, Mangis. And what became of that famous fire your tongue lit?”

“Their ashes were scattered to the wind.”

“Ay. But the true end of your comedy is this: The parliament of Dijon has examined the case and found they were not sorcerers, but peaceful citizens. Last week, they ordered masses for their souls and expiatory plays in seven towns of Burgundy. Yet none of that can bring them back. In our land, when we slay the innocent by listening to false knaves like you, we do not blame our credulous ears—but the lying tongue that deceived them. Think, then, that at a word from me to my lord bishop, you will smell burning pine closer than any rogue has ever smelled it—and travel on a smoky cloud to the one whose heart you bear (for ‘devil’ in Latin means ‘false accuser’), and whose livery you wear.”

And the cure pointed at Mangis with his staff.

“That is true, i’fegs,” said the alderman. “For red and black are the devil’s colors.”

Mangis’s face had gone as pale as his clothes were fiery. The contrast was almost comical. He stammered, “I respect Holy Church and her will. Let him burn the churchyard and all within it for me. I withdraw my complaint.”

“Then withdraw yourself,” said the vice-bailiff.

The moment he was gone, the cure resumed his conversational tone. “Your worship,” he said courteously, “the accused received the chemical substance from Holy Church and returned it—giving it all to me.”

“Then it’s in good hands,” replied the alderman. “Young man, you are free. Let me have your prayers.”

“Doubt it not. Humph. Vice-bailiff, the town owes me four silver franks—three months overdue.”

“They shall be paid, cure—within the week.”

Thus, Church and State parted in harmony. As soon as he stepped outside, Gerard seized the priest’s hand and kissed it.

“Oh, sir! Your reverence! You’ve saved me from the fiery stake. What can I say? What can I do?”

“Nought, foolish lad. Bounty rewards itself. Though—humph—I wish I had done it without lying. It ill becomes my office to speak falsehoods.”

“Falsehood, sir?” Gerard was confused.

“Did you not hear me say you gave me the phosphorus? That little lie will cost me a fortnight’s penance.”

The cure sighed, but his eye twinkled.

“Nay, nay,” Gerard protested eagerly. “That was no lie, father. You knew the phosphorus was yours—it still is.” He thrust the bottle into the cure’s hand. “But alas, it’s too poor a gift. Will you not take something from my purse for Holy Church?” He held out his purse, eyes glistening.

“Nay,” the priest said sharply, quickly putting his hands behind him. “Not a doit. Fie! Fie! You are pauper et exul. Come daily at noon and dine with me—for my heart warms to you.” And he walked off abruptly, hands clasped behind him.

They itched.

But they itched in vain.

Where there’s a heart, there’s a Rubicon.

Gerard hurried to the inn to relieve Denys of the anxiety caused by his long absence. He found him seated comfortably, playing dice with two young women—mannered, bold, and fair of complexion.

Gerard was wounded. “N’oubliez point la Jeanneton!” he said, blushing.

“What of her?” Denys asked, rattling the dice cheerfully.

“She said, ‘Le peu que sont les femmes.’”

“Oh? And what do you say to that, mesdemoiselles?”

“We say no man speaks ill of women except those too old, too ugly, or too dull to please them.”

“Witless, quotha?” Gerard retorted loftily. “Even wise men lack folly enough to please them, nor madness enough to desire to please them. But I speak to my comrade—not to you, you brazen toads, so free with a man at first sight.”

“Preach away, comrade. Fling bywords at our heads. Know, girls, he’s a Solomon for proverbs. Methinks he was raised on them.”

“Let your friendship be a byword!” Gerard snapped. “One that melts to nothing at the sight of a farthingale.”

“Malheureux!” cried Denys. “I speak but pellets, and you answer daggers.”

“Would I could,” Gerard replied. “Adieu.”

“What a little savage!” said one of the girls.

Gerard stuck his head back in the door. “I’ve thought of a byword,” he said spitefully—

“Qui hante femmes et dez
Il mourra en pauvretez.”

“There.” And slamming the door violently, he left before they could reply.

Exhausted by his ordeal, Gerard went to the bar for bread and wine. The landlord offered only a pint bottle. Well then, he’d take it. But when he compared the contents to the size, he noticed a discrepancy. He examined the bottle closely and found the glass thin where it tapered, but unnaturally thick near the bottom.

He pointed it out at once.

The landlord answered haughtily, “I don’t make bottles. I’m not responsible for their shape.”

“That we shall see,” Gerard said. “I’ll take this pint to the vice-bailiff.”

“Nay, nay, for Heaven’s sake!” the landlord exclaimed, changing tone. “I love to please my guests. If this pint is short, we’ll charge both this and its mate three sous instead of two.”

“So be it. But I marvel that you, host of such a fine inn, should cheat so. The wine also tastes strongly of spring water.”

“Young sir,” the landlord replied, “we cut no throats here, unlike most inns. ‘The White Hart’ is no lion or bear. Whatever robbery is done here, it’s done to the poor host. How else could I survive if I dealt honestly with the few who pay?”

Gerard objected to the system entirely. Honest trade meant small profits, quick returns—neither cheating nor being cheated.

The landlord sighed. “So might one keep an inn in heaven, but not in Burgundy. When foot soldiers are quartered on me, how can I profit? Two sous per day is their pay, and they eat two sous’ worth, and drink besides. Pardoners are my friends, but palmers and pilgrims—what do I gain? A loss. Minstrels and jongleurs draw customers and claim no fee, except for liquor. Secular monks neither gain nor lose, but the black and grey friars vow poverty, not hunger—they eat like wolves and give nothing but prayers—perhaps not even those. In my father’s day, we hosted weddings. Now, the great families lend their houses, plates, mugs, spoons to any honest couple wanting to wed. The mechanics go there with their brides. They don’t come to us—we couldn’t seat them. Once, the nobles sold wine by the barrel. Now, they sell it by the galopin in the market. How can we compete? They grow it. We buy it. The coroner’s quests still bring profit—but the meat is always gone before the mouths are full.”

“You should stock better,” Gerard suggested.

“The law forbids it. We may not enter the market for the first hour. By the time we arrive, the burghers have bought all but the refuse. And we may not buy more than three bushels of meal at a time—yet market day comes only once a week. The butchers won’t kill for us unless we bribe them.”

“Courage!” Gerard said kindly. “Every trader feels the pinch somewhere.”

“Ay—but not as we do. Our shoe is trod flat on one side *and* pinches us lame. A savoir: if we fail to pay merchants for meal, meat, wine, they can throw us in prison till we pay or die. But we cannot imprison those who buy from us. A traveler’s horse we may hold for debt—but where? In our stable, eating at our expense. We may detain the traveler himself—but where? Not in gaol. In our own house—where we must feed and lodge him gratis. So fling good silver after bad? Merci, no. Let him go with a wanion. Our best customers are thieves. Would God there were more. They don’t inspect the canakin’s shape or the bill. With them, it’s lightly come, lightly go. They spend freely—knowing each carouse may be their last. But thief-takers, instead of learning from this example, rob the poor host. When noble or honest travelers arrive, the provost’s men pretend to suspect them and demand to search them. To avoid offense, the host must feed and wine them. Then come the excise—town, royal, parliamentary—swarms of them, each with a wolf in the stomach and a sponge in the gullet. Monks, friars, pilgrims, soldiers, excisemen, provost-marshals, and bad debtors—how can ‘The White Hart’ withstand them all? We cut no throats in self-defense, unlike your ‘Swans,’ ‘Roses,’ ‘Boar’s Heads.’ How can we give a pint of wine for a pint? Everything has risen so high. Not a pound of bread I sell costs me three copper deniers—twelve to the sou. Each pint of wine, bought by the tun, costs me four deniers. A sack of charcoal—two sous, gone in a day. A pair of partridges—five sous. Can you believe it? Five sous for two bones and feathers? A pair of pigeons—thirty deniers. Ruination! We may not raise prices with the market. Oh, no. I tell you, the shoe is trod flat on one side *and* pinches us dry. We may charge nothing for mustard, pepper, salt, or firewood. Do you think we get them for nothing? Candle—sou per pound. Salt—five sous per stone. Pepper—four sous per pound. Mustard—twenty deniers per pint. Raw meat—does it dwindle on the spit? No cost to me—only loss of weight? Then what do I pay my cook? But you’ll never guess. A HUNDRED SOUS A YEAR AS I AM A LIVING SINNER.

“And my waiter—thirty sous, plus perquisites. He’s richer than I. And then to be insulted as well as plundered. Last Sunday I went to church—rarely. Didn’t the cure lash hotel-keepers? He hit all trades, except the clergy—the one famed for looseness, pride, sloth. But he spared them. He beat us with a neat’s pizzle: godless this, godless that, most godless of all for opening doors during mass. But the law forces us to open at all hours for travelers from elsewhere. They can fine us if we refuse—mass or no mass. If a townsman sneaks in with them, are we to blame? They all swear they’re tired wayfarers. Can I know every face in this city? So if we obey the law, our souls suffer. If we don’t, our purses bleed—fine and lost business.”

A man speaking of himself is “a babbling brook”; of his wrongs, “a shining river.”

“Labitur et labetur in omne volubilis aevum.”

Luckily for readers, though not for the speaker, this eloquent orator was interrupted mid-sentence. Another man burst in, fresh from his own grievance—hot and furious. It was Denys, cursing and swearing, shouting he had been robbed.

“Did those hussies pass this way? Who are they? Where do they live? They’ve stolen my purse and fifteen golden pieces! Raise the hue and cry! Ah! traitresses! vipers! These inns are all guet-apens!”

“There now,” the landlord said to Gerard.

Gerard urged calm. “Tell me how it happened.”

“First one left on some pretext. Then the other went to fetch her. Neither returned. I reached for my purse—and it was empty. The ungrateful creatures—I was letting them win it slowly. But loaded dice weren’t fast enough. They had to take it all at once.”

Gerard insisted on going to the alderman and sending officers after them.

“Not I,” Denys snapped. “I hate the law. No—let it go as it came.”

Gerard refused to abandon it.

At the landlord’s suggestion, he dragged Denys to the provost-marshal. The official shook his head. “We have no clue to occasional thieves who work honestly at their needles—until some fool tempts them with easy prey, and then they strike.”

“Come away!” Denys cried. “I knew what use a bourgeois would be in a crisis.” He stormed off in rage.

“They’re clear of town by now,” Gerard said.

“Say no more if you value my friendship. I have five pieces with the bailiff, ten with Manon—luckily. Or these traitresses would have feathered their nest with my last plume. What are you gaping at? Nay, I do ill to vent my anger on you. I’ll tell you all. You’re wiser than I. What did you say at the door? Never mind. Well then—I did offer marriage to Manon.”

Gerard was stunned.

“What? You offered her what?”

“Marriage. Is that so strange to offer a girl?”

“It’s strange to offer it to a strange girl in passing.”

“Nay, I’m not such a fool as you think. I saw the corn in the chaff. I knew I couldn’t win her fairly, so I tried foul play. ‘Mademoiselle,’ I said, ‘marriage is not my habit, but struck by your qualities, I make an exception. Deign to bestow this hand on me.’”

“And she bestowed it on your ear.”
