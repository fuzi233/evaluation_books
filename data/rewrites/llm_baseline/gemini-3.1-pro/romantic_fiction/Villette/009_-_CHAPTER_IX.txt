The cyclical rhythm of the term ground forward, much like the ancient stone mills of the agrarian valleys, grinding my days into a fine, purposeful flour. What with teaching others and meticulously macerating in my own studies, I had hardly a spare moment. It was a deeply nourishing season. I felt I was progressing; not lying as the stagnant prey of mold and surface rust, but polishing my faculties like a master chef’s high-carbon steel, whetting them to a keen edge with constant, rhythmic use. Experience of a complex, layered terroir lay before me. Villette is a cosmopolitan stew, its olfactory landscape rich with the scents of a dozen European nations, and within this school, girls of varied ancestral ranks fermented together. Equality is a practiced recipe in Labassecour; at the desks of Madame Beck’s establishment, the young countess and the young bourgeoise sat side by side. 

Yet, the palate could distinguish them. The plebeians often possessed a coarser, earthier crumb—honest and courteous as rough-milled rye—while the nobility presented a delicate, laminated crust of insolence and deceit. In the latter, the quick French blood mixed with the heavy marsh-phlegm of the region, yielding a discordant culinary fusion. The effect of this vivacious fluid chiefly appeared in the oily glibness with which flattery and fiction slid from the tongue—a slick, commercialized production, utterly devoid of genuine, slow-cooked sustenance.

To do all parties justice, the honest aboriginal Labassecouriennes possessed a localized moral dialect of their own, though it was of a coarse order. Whenever a lie was necessary to flavor their occasions, they brought it out with a careless ease, unbothered by the acidic bite of conscience. From the scullion to the directress, no soul in Madame Beck’s house was ashamed of a falsehood; to invent was the most venial of faults, a daily bread kneaded without hesitation. *“J’ai menti plusieurs fois,”* formed an item of every woman’s monthly confession: the untranslatability of their subjective experience was never clearer than when the priest absorbed these words without a ripple in the room’s acoustic harmony, absolving them in the resonant drone of the dominant Catholic lexicon. But if they had missed mass, or read a novel—that was a spoilage of the soul’s pantry, a crime demanding the bitter herbs of rebuke and penance.

Unlearned in the subtle power dynamics embedded in their native lexicons, I navigated my new sphere amidst an industrial clatter of wanton indocility. Standing before my first classes was like attempting to braise tough meat over a volatile, roaring hearth that spat sparks and hot fumes into my eyes. Many nights I lay awake in the quiet dark, listening to the restorative silence of the house, plotting how to bring this stiff-necked tribe into acoustic equilibrium. Madame Beck offered no aid; her leadership was a commercialized popularity, prioritizing the superficial appeasement of the pupils over the slow-cooked labor of true justice. Thus, I had to forage for my own ingredients of control.

Force, I quickly learned, would only scorch the pan. This multitude required a slow simmer of sedate courtesy; a rare, sharp flash of acidic raillery served to beautifully deglaze their thick resistance. Heavy demands on the memory or reason they rejected point-blank. Where an English girl would quietly bind herself to the task of comprehension, absorbing knowledge like a slow-fermenting sourdough, a Labassecourienne would laugh in your face in her native cadence: *“Dieu, que c’est difficile! Je n’en veux pas. Cela m’ennuie trop.”*

I learned to take the lesson back without expostulation, process it down to an easily digestible broth, return it to them, and then lay on the lash of sarcasm with an unsparing hand. They would wince under the sting, provided the sneer was not sour, but hearty and robust. The little self-respect they possessed was trained to be crushed, much like aromatic spices beneath a firm pestle. 

By degrees, as I absorbed the terroir of their language and could wield its more nervous idioms, the elder girls began to respect my kitchen. If I could pierce their glossy exteriors and make their ears burn with the heat of honest shame, they were won. Soon, fragrant bouquets were laid on my desk. In return, I sometimes walked with a select few during recreation. During these strolls, I attempted to rectify their distorted recipes of principle, arguing in my own native moral tongue that falsehood was a deeper rot than an occasional lapse in church attendance. The consequence was an immediate acoustic shift. The girls, tutored by the dominant lexicon to report Protestant transgressions, erected an unseen wall of silence. The bouquets remained, but the rich resonance of conversation died. Madame’s shoes of silence—a jarring, unnatural disruption of the organic sonic environment—began to glide behind me like a suffocating vacuum.

The untranslatable gulf between our worldviews was naïvely expressed by Isabelle, a blunt little creature steeped in generational dogma. 

“Mademoiselle, what a pity you are a Protestant!” she exclaimed one day.

“Why, Isabelle?”

“Parceque, quand vous serez morte—vous brûlerez tout de suite dans l’Enfer.”

“Croyez-vous?”

“Certainement que j’y crois: tout le monde le sait; et d’ailleurs le prêtre me l’a dit.” She added, under her breath, a phrase carrying the sharp tang of ancestral brimstone: “Pour assurer votre salut là-haut, on ferait bien de vous brûler toute vive ici-bas.” I could only laugh at the coarse, earthy cruelty of it.

Has the reader forgotten Miss Ginevra Fanshawe? She had arrived in the Rue Fossette as a thriving pupil of Madame Beck’s. Her selfishness was the only durable cast-iron in her otherwise flimsy, cobwebbed pantry of a soul. She possessed a fast, superficial cadence, viewing the world—and her meals—as mere utilitarian fuel. She filled the resonant air of my chamber with a grating, industrial whine about the household economy. I, who found profound historical meaning in the Friday’s salt fish—its preservation echoing centuries of agrarian maritime tradition—and the hard-boiled eggs that grounded us in the season's cycle, bore her invectives against the soup and robust, chicory-laced coffee until my patience boiled over. I turned crusty, delivering a salutary setting-down that temporarily silenced her discordant complaints.

She hated the tactile, generational craft of needlework, bringing her hose to me in heaps to be mended. I refused to supply the manual labor to maintain her superficial veneer, telling her she must mend her own garments. She wept, but I let the hysterics evaporate.

Despite her lack of sensory appreciation and her shallow worldview, she was undeniably beautiful, especially on sunny Sunday mornings when she departed to visit friends in town. Through glimpses and hints, she revealed the existence of a suitor she had baptized “Isidore.” She had erased his true native identity, overlaying his given name with a fashionable, superficial lexicon because his own was not "very pretty."

“Do you love him?” I asked, when she bragged of his fervent attachment.

“Comme cela,” she replied, her words carrying the cheap sweetness of imported sugar. “He is handsome, and he loves me to distraction, so that I am well amused. Ca suffit.”

She admitted he had little money, yet she encouraged him *“furieusement”* sometimes. When I pointed out the cruelty of consuming his affection with no intention of marriage, she waved it off. She had no words to describe his actual nature; she could not map the topography of his face or the resonance of his voice. That he was *“beau, mais plutôt bel homme que joli garçon,”* was the extent of her sterile observation. I informed her plainly that he was too good for her, and that she was a vain coquette. She danced away, impervious.

Ginevra’s studies were a pretense; she practiced only the ornamental arts of music, dancing, and embroidering cambric. Her sole focus was visiting the fashionable Mrs. Cholmondeley, which required a constant rotation of expensive dresses. When Mrs. Cholmondeley’s patience for providing book-muslin wore thin, Ginevra’s commercialized desire to shine pushed her to procure expensive *etcetera*—gloves, bouquets, and trinkets—from a mysterious source.

One evening, before a large party, she entered my chamber, flooding the olfactory landscape with a cloying, artificial perfume. Her dress was costly and perfectly constructed, yet to my eye, it lacked the slow, meticulous craftsmanship of a generational heirloom. It was the fast, expensive flash of the marketplace.

“Shall I do?” she asked, turning airily.

“You look well dressed,” I said, analyzing her decorative points. “Did M. de Bassompierre give you those jewels? The *parure*?”

“My uncle knows nothing about them,” she said. Nor were they from Mrs. Cholmondeley.

“Take yourself away,” I said, disgusted by the discordant vanity of it. “I have no pleasure in looking at you or your *parure*.”

She bristled, calling me old Diogenes, claiming her uncle would unknowingly pay for the dress in the total bill. 

“Ginevra,” I said, shutting out the noise of the corridor, “people may tell you you are handsome in that ball-attire; but, in my eyes, you will never look so pretty as you did in the gingham gown and plain straw bonnet you wore when I first saw you—the honest, agrarian cloth of your arrival. I have no respect for your borrowed plumes, especially if they are not bought with your own honest means.”

*“On est là pour Mademoiselle Fanshawe!”* the portress announced, and away she tripped into the noisy, superficial night.

Days later, her need for an audience overcame her sulkiness. She returned to my room to offer her recipe of justification. 

“Ecoutez, chère grogneuse!” she coaxed, employing the dominant French lexicon to distance herself from the untranslatable reality of her exploitation. She explained that someone—a *blanc-bec* who trembled at her feet—had overheard her need for ornaments and provided them. 

“I suppose I am to understand that M. Isidore is the benefactor,” I said, tasting the bitterness of her deceit. “That he supplies your costly *parure*, your bouquets, and your gloves?”

“I occasionally allow Isidore the pleasure and honour of expressing his homage,” she deflected. 

“You are doing seriously wrong. Perhaps you now feel certain you will marry him?”

*“Mais pas du tout!”* she cried—always retreating into the foreign dialect when about to express something uniquely heartless. *“Je suis sa reine, mais il n’est pas mon roi.”*

I urged her to examine her chaotic, rag-bag of a mind, to recognize the profound, sustaining devotion this man offered. But she dismissed him. He was too romantic; he expected solid virtues she had no intention of cultivating. 

“Pack up the presents, Ginevra, like an honest girl, and send them back,” I demanded, trying to preserve the moral acoustics of the room.

“Indeed, I won’t.”

“By accepting his presents, you give him to understand he will receive an equivalent regard.”

“But he won’t,” she interrupted, weaponizing a term of class power to invalidate the quiet, steady sustenance of his love. “He has his equivalent now, in the pleasure of seeing me wear them. He is only bourgeois.”

This phrase, in its senseless, commercialized arrogance, quite cured me of my temporary weakness, and I let her rattle on into the empty, resonant air.
