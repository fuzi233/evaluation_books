If the weight of a guilty conscience were perfectly proportional to the magnitude of the crime committed, the pages of human history would read quite differently. We might expect to find the architects of greedy wars and the ruthless predators of the financial markets marching shoulder-to-shoulder with petty thieves, pickpockets, and murderers who stain their hands with small-scale brutality, all united in a single regiment of self-flagellating penitents. Yet, wickedness does dispense its rewards; however, those who win in this diabolical game inevitably become baser, crueller, and more brutish than the average human being—the vast majority of women's offspring—is capable of sustaining. Most carry within them a form of conscience: a fear that serves as the shadow of justice, and a pity that acts as the shadow of love. These shadows prevent the attainment of "serene wickedness," a state of being that is notoriously difficult to maintain in our complex, dual-natured flesh.

On the twenty-ninth of December, Deronda learned that the Grandcourts had arrived at the Abbey, though he had not caught a glimpse of them until he prepared for dinner. A magnificent snowfall had recently occurred, granting the children the rare joy of snowball fights and snow fortifications. During the Christmas holidays, the Mallinger girls were satisfied with no amusement unless it involved "Cousin"—the name they had always bestowed upon Deronda—and required his direction. Following this outdoor exertion, Deronda had played billiards, allowing the hours to pass without his mind dwelling on the impending dinner encounter with Gwendolen. Nevertheless, the prospect held a distinct interest for him. As he retired to his room, slightly weary and flushed from his games, before the half-hour bell rang, he began to speculate on the influence her marriage to Grandcourt would exert upon her. He considered the likelihood of discernible changes in her demeanor compared to his previous encounters with her at Diplow, and even earlier, at Leubronn.

"I suspect there are certain natures that one could watch grow or degenerate daily if one kept close observation," Deronda mused. "Some of us progress faster than others. I am certain she retains strong traces of whatever once impressed her deeply. That incident involving the necklace, and the notion that someone deemed her gambling improper, clearly left a bite in her. However, such susceptibility works both ways: it can drive one toward desperation just as readily as toward improvement. Whatever fascinations Grandcourt holds for her capricious tastes—who can truly believe he possesses the power to summon tender affection through daily companionship? One might be tempted to strike him for the mere spectacle of eliciting emotion from his face and words. I fear she married him out of ambition, perhaps to escape poverty. But why did she initially avoid him? The poverty came later, though. Poor thing! She may have been pressured into it. How can one feel anything but pity for a young creature, brimming with untapped vitality, recklessly ignorant, hanging all her blind hopes on the remains of a human being?"

Naturally, the terms Deronda applied to the bridegroom were less flattering than the excuses and pity used to describe the bride. His view of Grandcourt as a "remnant" stemmed not from specific knowledge, but from the general impression of polite interaction that suggested Grandcourt had exhausted all his natural, healthy interest in the world. Generally speaking, whenever a notable marriage occurs, male acquaintances tend to pity the bride while female acquaintances pity the groom; each feels the other might have done better. This sentiment is especially prevalent when the bride is charming and young; gentlemen present often conclude she lacks genuine attachment to a husband so uninteresting to them, assuming she married on other grounds. Under such circumstances, who pities the husband? Even his female friends often deem his situation retributive, believing he should have chosen differently. Perhaps Deronda deserves exemption here, as he felt no pity for Grandcourt, who had never struck observers as a man likely to suffer more than he inflicted. Conversely, regarding Gwendolen—young, impulsive, eager for pleasure, and fed by flattery that convinces a lovely girl of her divine right to rule—one wonders how quickly life might shift from expectation to a bitter realization of the irreparable. Given what he had witnessed of her, Deronda surely possessed duller feelings than necessary not to look forward with some curiosity to her entry into the dining room. Still, since the honeymoon was already three weeks distant, and Gwendolen had been enthroned not only at Ryelands but also at Diplow, she was likely to have composed her countenance, manifesting or concealing her emotions suitably. She was not a woman inclined to expose her feelings helplessly to the curious gaze.

A diverse party had gathered to welcome the new couple. The old aristocracy was represented by Lord and Lady Pentreath; the established gentry by young Mr. and Mrs. Fitzadam of the Worcestershire branch; politics and the public good, specifically regarding the cider industry, by Mr. Fenn, Member for West Orchards, accompanied by his two daughters; Lady Mallinger's family by her brother, Mr. Raymond, and his wife; and the useful bachelor element by Mr. Sinker, the eminent counsel, and Mr. Vandernoodt, whose acquaintance Sir Hugo had found pleasant enough at Leubronn to warrant his adoption in England.

Everyone had assembled in the drawing-room before the new couple appeared. In the interim, time was spent primarily observing the children—various little Raymonds, nephews and nieces of Lady Mallinger, along with her own three girls, who were invariably permitted to appear at this hour. The scene was genuinely delightful, enhanced by full-length portraits with deep backgrounds set into the cedar paneling, surmounted by a ceiling glowing with the rich colors of coats of arms arranged between the sockets. The room was illuminated nearly as much by the red fire of oak boughs as by pale wax candles, softened by the deep-piled carpet and the high English breeding that subdued all voices. The mixture of ages, ranging from the white-haired Lord and Lady Pentreath to the four-year-old Edgar Raymond, lent a varied charm to the living groups. Lady Mallinger, possessing a fair, matronly roundness and mildly prominent blue eyes, moved about in black velvet, carrying a tiny white dog on her arm as a finishing touch to her attire. The children were scattered among the ladies, while most gentlemen stood somewhat apart, conversing with a moderate vivacity typical of the long minutes preceding dinner. Deronda stood slightly outside the main circle, engaged in a dialogue fixed upon him by Mr. Vandernoodt, a man of the finest Dutch blood imported during the Revolution. For the rest, he was one of those commodious figures in society who possess nothing remarkable themselves but are believed to be acquainted with the best in every department: close-clipped, pale-eyed, nonchalant, serving as an excellent foil to the intense coloring and vivid gravity of Deronda.

They discussed the bride and bridegroom, whose arrival was awaited. Mr. Vandernoodt was an industrious collector of personal details, capable of recounting everything about a great philosopher or physicist save for their theories or discoveries. Now, he implied he had learned numerous facts about Grandcourt since meeting him at Leubronn.

"Men who have seen a great deal of life do not always end by choosing their wives wisely. He has rather an anecdotal history—I fancy he plunged quite deep into pleasures, considering how lazy he is. But, of course, you know all about him."

"No, really," Deronda replied in an indifferent tone. "I know little more of him than that he is Sir Hugo's nephew."

Just then, the door opened, deferring any further satisfaction of Mr. Vandernoodt's communicativeness.

The scene was designed to highlight any figure of distinction entering it, and certainly, when Mr. and Mrs. Grandcourt appeared, no observer could deny their figures possessed distinction. The bridegroom wore neither more nor less perfect ease of costume, nor more nor less impassive composure of face than before his marriage. It was assumed he would accept nothing less than the best in outward equipment, including his wife; and the bride was precisely what one might expect him to choose. "By George, I think she's handsomer, if anything!" exclaimed Mr. Vandernoodt. Deronda agreed but remained silent. Her white silk and diamonds—though it may seem odd, she did wear diamonds around her neck, in her ears, and in her hair—may have contributed to the new imposingness of her beauty. This beauty flashed upon him as more unquestionable, if not entirely more satisfying, than when he had first seen her at the gaming table. Some faces, peculiar in their beauty, resemble original works of art; they are almost always met with skepticism at first. However, upon seeing Gwendolen at Diplow, Deronda had discerned in her more than he had anticipated of that tender, appealing charm known as womanliness. Was there any new change since then? He distrusted his own impressions; yet, as he saw her receive greetings with what appeared to be proud, cold quietude and a superficial smile, it seemed that the same demonic force active when she had taken him in her resolute glance and turned away a losing player from the gaming table was still at work within her. There was no time for further conclusion, nor even for him to offer his greeting before the summons to dinner.
