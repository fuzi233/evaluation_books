Love is often spoken of as if it were a lightning-struck fate, severed from all habit and judgment—a madness that would laugh at doctrines and proofs, whether the lover had Heraclitus on his tongue, or scholastic subtleties in his head, or a map of the heavens in his pocket. Yet there is another kind of potent love, no less consuming, that does not isolate itself like fever, but enters every chamber of the mind and makes common days shine with borrowed meaning. It can take the most ordinary act—an offered book, a door held open, a voice heard through thin walls—and set it ringing with larger histories, as if the heart had suddenly learned to keep time with remoter ages.

When Daniel Deronda came back to town, he could at least give Sir Hugo an intelligible account of what had been done. Grandcourt, he believed, now understood distinctly that he might secure fifty thousand pounds by relinquishing a prospect that was both distant and uncertain; but beyond that there was no further sign, except the colder sign of a man choosing to keep the channel open—friendly words, future convenience, no promise.

“And what did you think of the future bride on a nearer survey?” Sir Hugo asked, with that air of a man tasting gossip as lightly as he tasted a good claret.

“I thought better of her than I did in Leubronn,” Deronda said. “Roulette was a bad setting; it brought out something—something demoniac. At Diplow she seemed more womanly—more attractive; less hard, less self-possessed. Her mouth and eyes had another expression.”

“Don’t flirt with her too much, Dan,” Sir Hugo returned, amused with his own warning. “If you make Grandcourt savage when they come to the Abbey at Christmas, it will interfere with my affairs.”

“I can stay in town, sir.”

“No, no. Lady Mallinger and the children can’t do without you at Christmas. Only don’t make mischief—unless you can get up a duel, and manage to shoot Grandcourt, which might be worth a little inconvenience.”

“I don’t think you ever saw me flirt,” Deronda said, with a quietness that was not the same thing as being pleased.

“Oh, haven’t I, though?” Sir Hugo persisted, enjoying his own perversity. “You’re always looking tenderly at the women, and talking to them in a Jesuitical way. You are a dangerous young fellow—a kind of Lovelace who’ll make the Clarissas run after you, instead of you running after them.”

There are jokes that leave a smear, however lightly they are thrown, because they inform you—confidently—of a habit you do not possess and would not choose. Deronda, who had never knowingly flirted, felt the familiar irritation come first and the uselessness afterward. He was thankful, at least, that Sir Hugo knew nothing of the necklace he had repurchased for Gwendolen; the baronet would have seasoned that act with precisely this kind of raillery, and it would have soured the memory of it.

So he resolved to be watchful of himself—especially at the Meyricks’, where he was going for the first time since his return from Leubronn; for Mirah, by the mere fineness of her gratitude and the quiet tremor of her feeling, made it difficult not to show tenderness even in the simplest words.

Mrs. Meyrick had already written to him, and her letter, with its homely warmth, had been like a hearth-scent after travel. “We are getting fonder of her every day,” she said. “At breakfast-time we all look toward the door with expectation to see her come in; and we watch her and listen to her as if she were a native from a new country. I have not heard a word from her lips that gives me a doubt about her. She is quite contented and full of gratitude. My daughters are learning from her, and they hope to get her other pupils; for she is anxious not to eat the bread of idleness, but to work, like my girls. Mab says our life has become like a fairy tale, and all she is afraid of is that Mirah will turn into a nightingale again and fly away from us. Her voice is just perfect: not loud and strong, but searching and melting, like the thoughts of what has been. That is the way old people like me feel a beautiful voice.”

Mrs. Meyrick, with her delicacy, did not enlarge upon the little shocks Amy and Mab had received on accompanying Mirah to the synagogue—shocks not of piety, but of arrangement and custom. They were too loyal to Mirah to handle her faith as if it were a specimen to be criticized; still, curiosity, that innocent cousin of sympathy, will sometimes push a question between people who mean only kindness.

“Excuse me, Mirah,” Amy had ventured at last, “but does it seem quite right to you that the women should sit behind rails in a gallery apart?”

Mirah’s mild surprise was like a soft light thrown on their ignorance. “Yes—I never thought of anything else.”

“And you like better to see the men with their hats on?” Mab asked, with the caution of one touching a sacred thing by the smallest edge.

“Oh, yes. I like what I have always seen there, because it brings back to me the same feelings—the feelings I would not part with for anything else in the world.”

After that, criticism—of doctrine or practice—would have seemed to those generous girls like thrusting a cold hand into a warm nest. Mirah’s religion was not an argument she possessed: it was a texture of memory and love, and any rougher handling would have frayed it.

“She says herself she is a very bad Jewess, and does not half know her people’s religion,” Amy said one night, when Mirah had gone to bed. “Perhaps it would gradually melt away from her, and she would pass into Christianity like the rest of the world, if she got to love us very much, and never found her mother. It is so strange to be of the Jews’ religion now.”

“Oh, oh, oh!” Mab cried. “I wish I were not such a hideous Christian. How can an ugly Christian, who is always dropping her work, convert a beautiful Jewess, who has not a fault?”

“It may be wicked of me,” Kate said, with her sharper seeing, “but I cannot help wishing that her mother may not be found. There might be something unpleasant.”

“I don’t think it, my dear,” said Mrs. Meyrick. “I believe Mirah is cut out after the pattern of her mother. And what a joy it would be to her to have such a daughter brought back again! But a mother’s feelings are not worth reckoning, I suppose”—she threw a mischievous glance at her daughters—“and a dead mother is worth more than a living one?”

“Well, and so she may be, little mother,” said Kate; “but we would rather hold you cheaper, and have you alive.”

Not only the Meyricks—clever girls who had gathered knowledge as they could, by irregular foraging—but Deronda himself, with his masculine instruction and his calm competence, had been startled by Mirah into the sense of knowing almost nothing about living Judaism, about the inward story still moving under the surface of modern streets. It had been convenient, in the common way, to treat the Jews as a people “chosen” for the sake of someone else’s lesson; to suppose their thought was an error that ought to have died. To Deronda, as to most of his neighbors, Judaism had been an eccentric fossil; a thing for specialists, not for an accomplished man to trouble his mind with.

Then Mirah—her terrified flight from one parent, her yearning for the other—made the fossil throb. It was not a relic in a cabinet: it was a vesture of the world for actual human lives. And on the idle excursion that followed with Sir Hugo, Deronda found himself looking, with a new kind of attention, for synagogue fronts and for book-titles about Jewish history, as a man begins to listen for a language he has discovered he ought to understand. That sudden shift—from supposing we have right opinions because we have cared little, to caring and finding our opinions were ignorance—can cure ennui better than any physician; Deronda carried that cure with him through weeks that might else have been mere lounging.

It was on that journey that he first entered a synagogue—at Frankfort, where the party rested on a Friday. He had been in the Juden-gasse long before, and had remembered chiefly the picturesque houses. Now his eyes rested on the human types, and his mind connected them with past phases of their race; the old fibre of historic sympathy in him began to stir as if it had heard its name called.

There is a kind of young man whom society lets pass without inquiry: if he has a fine person, no odd manners, the education of a gentleman, and an income, people do not pry into his tastes and thoughts. He may be accepted as agreeable and clever without being examined. Later, when he grows portly and a little slovenly, his peculiarities are easier to see; and it is taken for a mercy if they are not offensive. But anyone who would understand what later events might do with Deronda must know something of what he was at five-and-twenty, which ordinary conversation did not reveal.

The vividness of his impressions had often made him seem enigmatical. His early sensibility and reflectiveness had opened into a many-sided sympathy that threatened to hinder any persistent course: as soon as he took up an antagonism, even in thought, he felt like a man striking at his own flesh. His imagination so habitually showed him how things looked to others that strong partisanship—unless against an immediate oppression—became insincerity for him. Sympathy flowed into analysis, and analysis neutralized sympathy.

He kept himself clear of vices; yet he hated vices mildly, because he was used to tracing them not as abstractions but as parts of mixed natures with individual histories. He was fervidly democratic in his feeling for the multitude, and yet, by affection and imagination, intensely conservative; eager for speculations on government and religion, and yet reluctant to part with long-sanctioned forms that were, for him, quick with memories no argument could kill. He suspected himself of loving too well the losing causes of the world; and since martyrdom changes sides, he was in danger of changing with it—repelled by the clue of success which the order of the world forces on us, and makes it treason against common good to reject. Yet he feared narrow hatred; he apologized for heirs of privilege; he shrank from the bitter tone of the unaccepted innovator.

In him, a too reflective, too diffusive sympathy threatened to paralyze indignation against wrong and the selectness of fellowship which give moral force. Of late he had grown painfully aware of this, and what he longed for—more than knowledge, more than practice—was some outward event or inward light that would urge him into a definite line of action and compress his wandering energy into one channel with his emotions. He dreaded that dead anatomy of culture which makes the universe a ceaseless answer to queries and knows everything about everything—except the scent itself of violets, for which it has no nostril. He wanted his feelings—the true savours of life—to remain substantial against a reflectiveness that would turn all differences into dust.

How and whence was the needed event to come—something that would justify partiality, and make him what he could not make himself: an organic part of social life instead of a yearning, disembodied spirit roaming through it? To make a little difference for the better—that was what he could not consent to live without; yet how to do it? It is one thing to see a road, another to cut it. He saw that his birth and upbringing had laid no special demands on him and given him no fixed relationship except one of doubtful kind; but he did not excuse himself wholly. He knew he had fallen into a meditative numbness, gliding away from the life of practically energetic sentiment which he would have named—if he had been inclined to proclaim anything—as the best life, and for himself the only life worth living.

These currents ran beneath him while he attended to law in a half-hearted way and to polite talk still more imperfectly: not an admirable condition, but a common struggle before daybreak.

He had always had a fervour under his calm, a readiness to feel poetry in daily things; and the Juden-gasse, with its union of the remote and the present, set him musing on two elements of history that this sense makes poetic—the faint beginnings of faiths and institutions, and their obscure lingering decay. Dust and withered remnants may cover them; yet for an awakened perception, the very dust can deepen the impressiveness—whether of the living seed that will become sheltering tree, or of the pathetic inheritance whose grandeur has turned into sorrowing memory.

So, as he turned out of the Juden-gasse in the warm evening air, making his way toward the synagogue, his imaginative stirring neutralized the repulsion of a few ugly incidents. He went into an old bookshop to ask the time of service, and was affectionately directed by a precocious Jewish youth who entered cordially into his wish—not for the fine new Reformed building, but for the old Rabbinical school of the orthodox—and then cheated him, with Teutonic honesty of method and a more amiable face, in the price of a book “nicht so leicht zu bekommen.” At the opposite counter, a deaf and grisly tradesman cast a flinty look at certain cards while a dingy man in a tall coat, a bag in hand, and a broad low hat above his chosen nose shouted proposals to him in Jew-dialect; that dingy man disappeared only for another of the same pattern to emerge from the shop’s shadow and shout in the same dialect. Deronda saw queer-looking Israelites not altogether without guile—just distinguishable from queer-looking Christians of similarly mixed morale.

Lately, because of Mirah’s unknown relatives, he had begun to think of vulgar Jews with a personal alarm; but comparison is a corrective, and that evening he became conscious of unfair exaggerations. He paid his thaler too much without surrendering either his interest in Hebrew destiny or his wish to find the Rabbinische Schule. He reached it by sunset, entered with the men, and took his place.

In his line sat an elderly man whose ample white beard and felt hat framed a profile of such fine contour that it might have been Italian as easily as Hebrew. Deronda glanced at him more than once, and the man returned the notice until their eyes met—an undesirable chance, the sort of accidental intimacy that makes one resolve not to look again. Immediately the man pushed an open prayer-book toward him; Deronda bowed his thanks.

The congregation had mustered. The Reader mounted to the almemor, and the service began. Deronda looked at the German translation enough to know he was hearing chiefly Psalms and phrases of the Old Testament. Then he yielded to that strongest effect of chanted liturgy which does not depend on detailed meaning—like the power of an Allegri Miserere or a Palestrina Magnificat. The deepest movement in a liturgy is often prayer that asks for nothing special: a yearning to escape our cramped weakness and to invoke all Good to enter and abide; or else a self-oblivious lifting of gladness—a Gloria in excelsis that such Good exists. Both yearning and exaltation draw force from communion, from a form that has carried them through long generations of struggling fellow-men.

The Hebrew liturgy, like other liturgies, passes through litany, lyric, proclamation, dry statement, and blessing; but this evening, for Deronda, all of it blended into one. The Chazaris’ wide-ranging chant moved from monotony into sudden cries; sweet boys’ voices rose from the little choir; men swayed backward and forward with devotion; and the very commonness of the building, the shabbiness of the scene where a national faith that had penetrated half the world was now finding a remote, obscure echo—all this was one coherent strain, the utterance of a binding history, tragic and yet glorious. He wondered at the strength of his own feeling. It seemed beyond the occasion, like an influx before any vision had interpreted it.

Then the devotional sound ceased. Faces and figures shifted—indifferent, vulgar, human. A chilling thought darted in: he had probably been alone in his emotion, perhaps the only one for whom the service was more than dulled routine. He had just time for that coldness before he bowed to his civil neighbour and moved away with the rest—when a hand caught his arm.

He turned with the unpleasant start such a claim produces, and saw close to him the white-bearded man, who said in German, “Excuse me, young gentleman—allow me—what is your parentage—your mother’s family—her maiden name?”

Deronda felt a strong recoil. He was inclined to shake off the touch outright; but he slipped his arm away and answered, coldly, “I am an Englishman.”

The man looked at him with lingering doubt, then lifted his hat and turned aside. Whether he felt he had made a mistake, or had been repulsed, Deronda could not tell.

As he walked back to the hotel, he tried to quiet his uneasiness by reasoning with it. He could not have acted differently. How could he tell a total stranger that he did not know his mother’s family name? And the abruptness of the question had been an unwarrantable liberty, probably prompted by some fancy of likeness—one of those momentary delusions that mean nothing. He called it trivial; yet whatever it might signify, his inward shrinking had been too strong for him to regret cutting it short.

It was an additional reason—besides his habitual reserve—not to speak of the synagogue to the Mallingers. Sir Hugo, good-natured as any man and capable of kind acts, especially toward women, never took a romantic view of anything and smiled at motives on a grand scale or reasons lying far off. That was his sharpest difference from Deronda, who could hardly eat breakfast without some silent, discursive flight after grounds for filling up his day in the fashion of his contemporaries.

Frankfort had been only a halt on the journey home. Yet its impressions stayed active in him, made more vivid by his duty toward Mirah. The stranger’s question about parentage—trivial, yes, and yet like a finger pressed on an old bruise—reinforced his anxiety about Mirah’s relatives and his resolve to proceed with caution. If he discovered anything unpleasant, was he bound to disclose it and throw a new net of trouble over her life?

He had written to Mrs. Meyrick to announce his visit at four o’clock. When he arrived, he found Mirah seated at her work, with only Mrs. Meyrick and Mab beside her, the piano open, and the walls peopled by engravings—the “glorious company” that made the little room seem larger and more inhabited than many drawing-rooms. Mirah’s hair was dressed with that dainty neatness which is a sort of modest pride; her dress had the same quiet order; and in her face there was a glow of tranquil happiness that would have asked no alteration from a painter who wished to set her before a chorus singing peace on earth and good-will.

The contrast with his first sight of her by the river was a delight to him; and Mirah herself was thinking of it. Almost as soon as they had greeted one another, she said, with a soft eagerness,

“See how different I am from the miserable creature by the river! all because you found me and brought me to the very best.”

“It was my good chance to find you,” Deronda said. “Any other man would have been glad to do what I did.”

“That is not the right way to be thinking about it,” Mirah answered, shaking her head with a decisive gravity that sat strangely and beautifully on her gentleness. “I think of what really was. It was you, and not another, who found me and were good to me.”
