The crimson tide receded slowly from Sally's cheeks, and her heart, which had hammered like a trapped bird at the mere echo of his voice, settled back into a rhythmic, mundane thump. The initial shockwave had passed, leaving her strangely, unnervingly calm. Every time she had rehearsed this inevitable confrontation in her mind, she had braced herself for a panic attack, a trembling collapse. Yet, standing there in the reality of it, she felt nothing but a numb, hollow stillness. The chaotic energy of the previous night had drained her of all capacity for high emotion.

"Hullo, Sally!" Gerald announced.

His voice was thick, slurring over the vowels, and a dopey, foolish grin plastered itself onto his face as he swayed against the doorframe, one hand clutching the wood for balance. He wore no collar, his shirt sleeves rolled up, revealing the telltale signs of a heavy nightcap. His complexion was a blotchy mix of pallor and puffiness, surrounded by a halo of sodden disrepute that seemed to drip from his shoulders.

Sally said nothing. Exhausted to the bone, she had entered a peculiar state of hyper-alertness, a sort of Indian summer for over-tired nerves. She regarded him with the cool, detached scrutiny one might reserve for a stranger or a piece of furniture.

"Hullo!" Gerald tried again, louder this time.

"What do you want?" Sally asked, her tone flat and devoid of warmth.

"Overheard your voice. Door was ajar. Figured I'd drop in."

"What do you want?" she repeated, the question hanging in the air like a challenge.

The weak, painted-on smile evaporated from Gerald's face. A single tear tracked a muddy path down his cheek; his intoxication had curdled into maudlin misery.

"Sally... S-Sally... I'm absolutely wretched." He stumbled over the syllables, his speech thick with sorrow. "Overheard your voice. Door was open. Thought I'd come in."

A tiny spark flickered at the back of Sally's mind. *Have I seen this script before?* Then the memory clicked into place. It was Mr. Reginald Cracknell all over again.

"I think you'd best head to bed, Gerald," she said, her voice steady as stone. Nothing about him registered—neither his ragged appearance nor his shameless self-pity.

"What's the point? Can't sleep. Never works. Couldn't sleep. Sally, you have no idea how terrified I am. I see clearly now what a fool I've been."

Sally made a sharp, dismissive gesture, anticipating a belated apology for his past cruelties. She wasn't in the mood to listen to Gerald weeping over his own incompetence. But it wasn't regret for hurting her that weighed on his soul.

"I was a fool to ever try writing plays," he continued, his voice dropping to a mournful whisper. "Hit a winner the first time, but can't replicate it. It's hopeless. Should have stuck to newspaper copy. I'm good at that. Gotta go back to the grind. Another frost hit tonight. No point trying anymore. Back to the old routine, damn it."

He wept softly, pitying his tragic lot.

"Very miserable," he murmured.

He stepped forward, lurched, and retreated to the safety of the doorframe. For a fleeting second, Sally's artificial composure cracked, pierced by a stab of pure contempt. But it vanished instantly, and she was armored in indifference once more.

"Go to bed, Gerald," she commanded. "You'll feel better in the morning."

Perhaps a glimmer of foresight regarding his morning prospects penetrated Gerald's foggy brain. He winced, his demeanor darkening into a profound melancholy.

"Might not be alive in the morning," he declared solemnly. "Better end it all. End it all!" he repeated, making a sweeping gesture that he abruptly halted as he grabbed the door handle.

Sally had no patience for melodrama.

"Oh, go to bed," she snapped impatiently. The frozen detachment that had held her was melting, replaced by a rising tide of resentment—directed at Gerald for degrading himself, and at herself for ever finding glamour in such a man. It humiliated her to recall how completely she had once surrendered to his personality. Under the sting of this humiliation, she felt hard and pitiless. She dimly sensed a shift within herself; normally, the sight of distress would trigger her sympathy, but Gerald's lamentations about returning to a desk job elicited nothing but coldness—a fact the sufferer noted with a sniffle.

"You're very un-sympathetic," he complained.

"I'm sorry," Sally lied. She marched briskly to the door and pushed it open. Gerald, still clinging to the handle like a drowning man, shuffled out into the hallway, looking like a man whose foundation had just collapsed. He released the handle and wandered uncertainly toward his own door. Finding it ajar, he staggered inside. Sally watched him reach his destination safely before turning to her bedroom, intending to end the night's disturbances with sleep.

Almost immediately, she changed her mind. Sleep was impossible. A feverish restlessness seized her. She donned a kimono and headed to the kitchen to check if her supplies allowed for a glass of hot milk.

She had just recalled presenting the last of the milk to a sandy-colored cat with a purposeful gaze that had slipped through the window earlier that morning, when a muffled crash shattered her thoughts.

She listened intently. The sound seemed to emanate from across the hall. Hurrying to the door, she opened it. Instantly, from the apartment opposite, erupted a perfect fusillade of crashes, each one seeming louder and more terrifying than the last.

There is something about sudden, deafening noises in the dead of night that shatters the most rigid detachment. Moments ago, Gerald's threat of violence had left her unmoved; now, her mind leapt back to his words, and apprehension replaced indifference. Gerald was in an irresponsible mood, capable of anything. Standing in the doorway, Sally felt a momentary spike of panic.

A brief silence followed the explosion, but as she hesitated, the noise erupted again. This time, so loud and compelling, she couldn't delay. She ran across the passage and pounded on the door.

Whatever devastation was occurring inside, Gerald had clearly survived it. A moment later, a dragging footstep sounded, and the door swung open. Gerald stood on the threshold, the weak smile back on his face.

"Hullo, Sally!"

Seeing him, disheveled and seemingly unharmed, Sally's brief alarm dissolved, replaced by her usual impatient resentment. On top of everything else, he had apparently frightened her unnecessarily.

"What on earth was all that noise?" she demanded.

"Noise?" Gerald considered the point, mouth agape.

"Yes, noise," Sally snapped.

"I've been cleaning house," Gerald said with the owl-like gravity of a man barely conscious of his surroundings.

Sally pushed past him. The apartment was an almost exact replica of her own, evident proof that Elsa Doland had taken pains to make it pretty and comfortable in a fussy, feminine way. Amateur interior decoration had always been Elsa's hobby. Even in the bleak confines of Mrs. Meecher's boarding house, she had managed to create a certain daintiness that Sally, lacking such talent, had always envied. Elsa's mind ran toward small, fragile ornaments; she was never afraid of over-furnishing. Pictures crowded the walls, china of every description cluttered the tables, a profusion of lamps with multi-colored shades cast a chaotic glow, and plates lined the shelves.

One might say the plates were ranged and the pictures jostled, but it was more accurate to say they had jostled and been ranged, for Sally could only reconstruct the scene by guesswork. She had entered briskly but pulled up short, appalled by the majestic ruin that greeted her. A shell burst in the sitting-room could hardly have caused more havoc.

The psychology of a man with a weak character, fueled by alcohol and thwarted ambition, is difficult to decipher; his moods shift with bewildering speed. Ten minutes prior, Gerald had been drowning in clammy self-pity, but now, a brief, adequate spasm of berserk fury had clearly taken hold. Sally, not being a psychologist, couldn't explain the cause, but the evidence was ocular and undeniable. A heavy niblick, thrown petulantly—or remorsefully—into a corner, revealed the instrument of destruction.

Bleak chaos reigned everywhere. The floor was littered with every conceivable shape and size of broken glass and china. Fragments of pictures, looking as if chewed by a prehistoric beast, lay amidst heaps of shattered statuettes and vases. As Sally moved slowly into the room, china crackled beneath her feet. She surveyed the stripped walls with wonder and turned to Gerald for an explanation.

Gerald had collapsed onto an occasional table, weeping softly again. It had struck him once more that he had been terribly mistreated.

"Well!" Sally gasped. "You've certainly made a good job of it!"

A sharp crack echoed as the occasional table, never designed to bear heavy weights, gave way in a splintering flurry of broken legs under the weight of its master. Sally's mood underwent an abrupt transformation. Few situations in life hold equal potential for tragedy and farce, and it was the ludicrous side of this drama that appealed to her now. Her sense of humor was tickled. If she could have analyzed her feelings, she would have found herself mocking the feeble, sentimental Sally who had once taken this preposterous man seriously. She felt light-hearted and light-headed, sinking into a chair with a gurgling laugh.

The shock of his fall seemed to restore Gerald to something resembling intelligence. He picked himself up from the remains of a set of watercolors, gazing at Sally with growing disapproval.

"No sympathy," he said austerely.

"I can't help it," cried Sally. "It's too funny."

"Not funny," corrected Gerald, his brain beginning to cloud again.

"Why did you do it?"

Gerald returned briefly to his mood of honest indignation, which had strengthened his arm when wielding the niblick. He revisited his grievance.

"Weren't going to stand for it any longer," he said heatedly. "A fellow's wife goes and lets him down... ruins his show by going off and playing in another show... why shouldn't I smash her things? Why should I stand for that sort of treatment? Why should I?"

"Well, you haven't," said Sally, "so there's no need to discuss it. You seem to have acted in a thoroughly manly and independent way."

"That's it. Manly independent." He waggled his finger impressively. "Don't care what she says. Don't care if she never comes back. That woman..."

Sally was not prepared to debate the absent Elsa. The amusing aspect of the affair was fading, replaced by a tired distaste for the sordidness of the whole business. She realized she could not endure Gerald Foster's company much longer. She stood up and spoke decisively.

"And now," she said, "I'm going to tidy up."

Gerald had other views.

"No," he said with sudden solemnity. "No! Nothing of the kind. Leave it for her to find. Leave it as it is."

"Don't be silly. All this has got to be cleaned up. I'll do it. You go and sit in my apartment. I'll come and tell you when you can return."

"No!" Gerald wagged his head.

Sally stamped her foot among the crackling ruins. Suddenly, the sight of him became intolerable.

"Do as I tell you," she cried.

Gerald wavered for a moment, but his brief militant mood was ebbing fast. After a faint protest, he shuffled off, and Sally heard him retreat into her room. She breathed a deep sigh of relief and turned to her task.

A visit to the kitchen revealed a long-handled broom. Armed with this, Sally was soon busy. She was an efficient little person, and presently, out of chaos, a certain order began to emerge. Nothing short of complete re-decoration would ever make the place habitable again, but after half an hour, she had cleared the floor, stacking the fragments of vases, plates, lamp-shades, pictures, and glasses into tiny heaps against the walls. She returned the broom to the kitchen, went back into the sitting-room, flung open the window, and stood looking out.

With a sense of unreality, she perceived that the night had gone. Over the quiet street below brooded that strange, metallic light that ushers in the dawn of a fine day. A cold breeze whispered to and fro. Above the rooftops, the sky was a faint, level blue.

She left the window and started to cross the room. Suddenly, a feeling of utter weakness overcame her. She stumbled to a chair, conscious only of being tired beyond the possibility of further effort. Her eyes closed, and almost before her head touched the cushions, she was asleep.

***

Sally woke. Sunshine streamed through the open window, accompanied by the myriad noises of a city awake and about its business. Footsteps clattered on the sidewalk, automobile horns blared, and she could hear the clank of streetcars passing over the tracks. She could only guess the hour, but it was evident the morning was well advanced. She got up stiffly. Her head ached.

She went into the bathroom, washed her face, and felt better. The dull oppression of a bad night was lifting. She leaned out of the window, reveling in the fresh air, then crossed the passage and entered her own apartment. Stertorous breathing greeted her; she perceived that Gerald Foster had also spent the night in a chair. He was sprawled by the window, legs stretched out, head resting on an armrest, an unlovely spectacle.
