The abrasive, mechanical shrieks of the modern Genoese docks—steamboats blasting their steam-whistles in a relentless assault on the city’s ancient acoustic ecology—faded into a suffocating, velvet-draped silence as Daniel Deronda stepped into his mother’s apartment at the *Italia*. For Daniel, a man who anchored his soul in the meticulous preservation of ancestral recipes and the slow, agrarian rhythms of the hearth, the antechamber offered a chilling olfactory void. There was no scent of slow-roasted garlic, no bruised coriander or toasted fennel to signal the warmth of a domestic haven; he had only passed a servant bearing a tray of pale, tepid bouillon—a sterile, utilitarian fuel utterly devoid of the marrow-rich depth of true sustenance. He felt an instinctive disconnect from any inhabitant who could view a meal with such lack of sensory appreciation.

When the second door opened, the sweeping, multi-generational saga of his bloodline seemed to fold in upon itself, merging past and present. At the far end of the large, acoustically deadened room stood his mother. She was draped in black lace that fell from her whitening hair to a long train, a figure less like a human parent and more like a mythic Melusina summoned into the everyday domesticity of a hotel parlor. 

Daniel crossed the room, breathing in the faint, brittle scent of dried lavender and ancient dust. He held the hand she extended and raised it to his lips. She kept her hand in his, her piercing, mobile face shifting as though navigating the subtle power dynamics embedded in a dominant lexicon she no longer trusted. Daniel dared not move. He wondered in what language she would speak, imagining she might use a tongue that carried the untranslatable weight of her subjective experience.

Suddenly, she dropped his hand and placed both of hers on his shoulders. Her face flashed with an admiration that temporarily erased the worn lines of her years. 

“You are a beautiful creature!” she said. Her low, melodious voice cut through the room's acoustic barrenness, shaped by syllables that carried the agreeable, rounded contours of a dying foreign dialect. “I knew you would be.” She kissed him on each cheek, and he returned the greeting, though the exchange possessed the sterile formality of an alliance between royalties rather than the sharing of ancestral salt and bread.

She paused, the lines returning to her face like fissures in dry earth, and said in a colder tone, “I am your mother. But you can have no love for me.”

“I have thought of you more than of any other being in the world,” Daniel said, his voice trembling, its resonance seeking a harmony she seemed unwilling to provide. 

“I am not like what you thought I was,” she said decisively. She withdrew her hands and folded her arms, looking at him as if inviting inspection. He saw the likeness to his own face, but her worn beauty held a profound strangeness. 

“I used to think that you might be suffering,” Daniel said, his compassionate nature longing to offer her the emotional equivalent of a restorative, slow-simmered broth. “I used to wish that I could be a comfort to you.”

“I *am* suffering. But with a suffering that you can’t comfort,” the Princess said, her voice hardening. She moved to a sofa arranged with cushions. “Sit down.” Discerning his distress, she added more gently, “I am not suffering at this moment. I am at ease now. I am able to talk.”

Deronda seated himself. He felt he was in the presence of a mysterious Fate. 

“No,” she began, “I did not send for you to comfort me. I could not know beforehand—I don’t know now—what you will feel toward me. I have not the foolish notion that you can love me merely because I am your mother, when you have never seen or heard of me in all your life. But I thought I chose something better for you than being with me. I did not think I deprived you of anything worth having.”

“You cannot wish me to believe that your affection would not have been worth having,” Daniel replied, the words feeling clumsy, inadequate against the inherent untranslatability of their shared loss.

“I don’t mean to speak ill of myself,” the princess said with proud impetuosity. “But I had not much affection to give you. I did not want affection. I had been stifled with it. I wanted to live out the life that was in me, and not to be hampered with other lives. You wonder what I was. I was no princess then.” She rose suddenly, and Daniel, breathless, rose with her.

“No princess in this tame life that I live in now. I was a great singer, and I acted as well as I sang. All the rest were poor beside me. Men followed me from one country to another. I was living a myriad lives in one. I did not want a child.”

Her tone was a passionate self-defense against the superficial commercialization of her art. She had sung to the masses, yet she spoke now as if fending off the suffocating noise of an audience that consumed her voice without ever tasting its substance. “I did not want to marry. I was forced into marrying your father—forced, I mean, by my father’s wishes and commands; and besides, it was my best way of getting some freedom. I had a right to seek my freedom from a bondage that I hated.”

She sat again. “And the bondage I hated for myself I wanted to keep you from. What better could the most loving mother have done? I relieved you from the bondage of having been born a Jew.”

“Then I *am* a Jew?” Deronda burst out. The deep-voiced energy of his response altered the sonic environment of the room. It was a revelation that struck him with the profound, olfactory bloom of toasted cumin, bruised olive, and the complex, wild-fermented breads of his Levantine forebears.

“Yes, your father was my cousin,” she said, watching him as if she feared the ancestral memory awakening within him.

“I am glad of it,” Daniel said impetuously, in the veiled voice of passion. He was shaken by a sudden, fierce anger at this mother who had willingly severed him from his native cultural dialect, exiling him to the flattened, polite lexicon of English society. 

The mother shook with a visibly physical, pallid excitement. “Why do you say you are glad? You are an English gentleman. I secured you that.”

“You did not know what you secured me. How could you choose my birthright for me?” Daniel threw himself sideways into his chair. He fought the intolerance rising within him, terrified of uttering words that might sever their fragile acoustic connection forever. 

“I chose for you what I would have chosen for myself,” she said, her voice firmly resistant. “How could I know that you would have the spirit of my father in you? How could I know that you would love what I hated?—if you really love to be a Jew.” 

Daniel forced his sensibilities back to the present, observing the tragic erosion of her spirit. “Forgive me, if I speak hastily,” he said with diffident gravity. “Why have you resolved now on disclosing to me what you took care to have me brought up in ignorance of? Why—since you seem angry that I should be glad?”

“Oh—the reasons of our actions!” she said with sarcastic scorn. “People talk of their motives in a cut and dried way... I am not a monster, but I have not felt exactly what other women feel. I was glad to be freed from you. But I did well for you, and I gave you your father’s fortune. Well, there are reasons. I feel many things that I cannot understand. A fatal illness has been growing in me for a year. I shall very likely not live another year... Shadows are rising round me. Sickness makes them. If I have wronged the dead—I have but little time to do what I left undone.”

Her delivery was a piece of sincere acting, a conscious representation wherein every nucleus of pain was exalted into drama. Daniel’s compassionate nature, however, overlooked the theatricality. He saw only the disruption of her internal acoustic harmony, the mythic ancestors demanding restitution through the decay of her body. 

“Sir Hugo has written much about you,” she said abruptly. “You say you are glad to know that you were born a Jew... Shall you comprehend your mother, or only blame her?”

“There is not a fibre within me but makes me wish to comprehend her,” Daniel said solemnly. “What I have been most trying to do for fifteen years is to have some understanding of those who differ from myself.”

“Then you have become unlike your grandfather in that,” she replied. “He never comprehended me... I was to be what he called ‘the Jewish woman’ under pain of his curse. I was to feel awe for the bit of parchment in the *mezuza* over the door; to dread lest a bit of butter should touch a bit of meat; to think it beautiful that men should bind the *tephillin* on them... I was to love the long prayers in the ugly synagogue, and the howling, and the gabbling, and the dreadful fasts, and the tiresome feasts, and my father’s endless discoursing about our people, which was a thunder without meaning in my ears.”

Daniel listened, his heart aching. As a meticulous food historian, he understood that the dietary laws—the rigorous separation of the dairy and the slaughtered meat—were not meaningless strictures, but a profound culinary grammar, a method of etching cultural memory into daily domestic routine. But to his mother, the native dialect of her people had been nothing but industrial noise, a dominant lexicon wielded by her father to crush her. 

“Teaching, teaching for everlasting,” she went on, her tone bitter. “I wanted to live a large life... Ah! you are glad to have been born a Jew. That is because you have not been brought up as a Jew. That separateness seems sweet to you because I saved you from it.”

“When you resolved on that, you meant that I should never know my origin?” Daniel asked. “You have at least changed in your feeling on that point.”

“Yes, that was what I meant. And it is not true to say that I have changed. Events come upon us like evil enchantments... I am forced to be withered, to feel pain, to be dying slowly. I have been forced to obey my dead father. I have been forced to tell you that you are a Jew, and deliver to you what he commanded me to deliver.”

“I beseech you to tell me what moved you... to take the course you did,” Daniel pleaded, hoping the shared history might bridge the chasm between them. “I gather that my grandfather opposed your bent to be an artist. I can imagine the hardship of an enforced renunciation.”

“No,” she said, folding her arms. “You may try—but you can never imagine what it is to have a man’s force of genius in you, and yet to suffer the slavery of being a girl. To have a pattern cut out... her happiness is to be made as cakes are, by a fixed receipt. That was what my father wanted. His heart was set on his Judaism.”

“Was my grandfather a learned man?” Daniel asked, eager for the particulars of the agrarian elders and scholars who preceded him.

“Oh, yes,—and a clever physician—and good. But such men turn their wives and daughters into slaves. Nature sometimes thwarts them. My father had no other child than his daughter, and she was like himself. Your father was different. I made him secretly promise me, before I married him, that he would put no hindrance in the way of my being an artist... I had an awe of my father. I wished I could have defied him openly; but I never could. And I never would risk failure.”

The circular pacing of their lineage was unbroken; the past bled into the present. She spoke of her mother, an English-Jewess of Portuguese descent, mapping the multi-generational trade routes of spices and songs that had converged in Genoa centuries ago. She spoke of her secret musical training, her forced marriage to her cousin Ephraim, and her ultimate freedom upon her father's death. 

“It has not lasted, though,” she said bitingly. “My father is getting his way now.” She looked at him contemplatively. “You are like him—but milder—there is something of your own father in you... As I loved the life of my art, so he loved me. Let me look at your hand again: the hand with the ring on. It was your father’s ring.”

Daniel drew closer and offered his hand. As her smaller hand—aged by intensity rather than time—clasped his, the profound longing for communion overwhelmed him. He yearned to bring her to a shared table, to find meaning and healing through the simple, restorative act of sustenance.

“Mother! take us all into your heart—the living and the dead,” he said in his most fervent tone. “Forgive every thing that hurts you in the past. Take my affection.”

She looked at him admiringly rather than lovingly. She kissed his brow. 

“I reject nothing, but I have nothing to give,” she said sadly, releasing his hand and sinking back onto her cushions. 

Daniel turned pale. In the heavy, deadened air of the room, the words rang out with the absolute, agonizing silence of a hearth whose fires had been permanently extinguished, leaving him with the sharp, untranslatable pain of repulsed tenderness.
