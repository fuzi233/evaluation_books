High above the bustling streets, atop Bardo's house, a loggia stretched like a crown over the quadrangular walls. Where it faced the road, sturdy columns bore up the roof; elsewhere, a wall of arches opened to the air, framing a view over the huddled, uneven rooftops of the neighborhood toward the distant hill of Bogoli. Here, hidden from prying eyes, Titus waited. The sunlight struck him with an uncomfortable intensity, a physical manifestation of the ruin descending upon his hopes. He had never truly believed that Romola's affection could withstand the shock of his revelation, nor did he possess the arrogance to think her love stemmed from ignorance of his secrets. Yet, as he imagined her approaching in her radiant, earthly beauty, softened by the warmth of her hazel gaze, a selfish desire seized him. He wished she were lesser, something closer to the ground, just so he might hold her tight and kiss her deeply before their inevitable separation. Their past intimacy had been sparse—a lingering glance, a fleeting kiss, a pressed hand—and he had often hungered for solitude with her. Now, finally alone, he expected her closeness, yet found her standing at a rigid, inexplicable distance.

A sharp beat in his chest marked the movement of the door. Romola appeared. Time seemed to fracture into lightning; he felt rather than saw the halo around her brow, the pleading wetness of her eyes, and the cry of love bursting from her lips—"Titus!"

In that same instant, she was in his embrace, sobbing violently against his shoulder.

How desperately she had longed through the watch-lit hours to behold that face again! The terrifying new image of death, the bewildering doubts seeded by the account of a life alien to her understanding, the haunting vision that seemed to echo in her own soul—it had all sharpened her awareness. It was Titus who had first poured the warm stream of hope into her heart, who had blunted the jagged edges of grief regarding her brother. There was no one else to share this burden. Restrained all morning before her father, the dam within her now burst. To the world, proud and self-possessed, she was a fortress; yet in her love for Titus, she became as open and unguarded as a child. She had been content with the quiet glances of the past, but now, needing to cling to him, nothing hindered her.

"My Romola! My goddess!" Titus murmured, his voice thick with passion, as he cradled her gently and kissed the thick, golden waves of hair at her neck. He was in paradise. Disgrace, shame, separation—they held no sway anymore. This happiness was so overwhelming that the knowledge of her deception could not tarnish it; indeed, he rejoiced in her delusion. After all, concealment had been wisdom. All he regretted was his superfluous fear, or perhaps the dread had been worth paying for the sake of this sudden, rapturous union.

The sobbing subsided, and Romola lifted her head. Silence fell between them, a space filled with the sweet, bewildered wonder of young love. Her long, white hands rested upon his dark curls, while his fingers, stained with the streaming gold of her hair, touched her cheek. Each was so beautiful to the other; they shared a mutual consciousness undisturbed by the world. A fresh, cold pressure of sadness settled upon Romola's heart, making her linger in this silent, soothing proximity. Titus dared not press his lips to hers, for any movement would be a change, a disruption of the spell.

"Titus," she whispered eventually, "it has been utterly painful, but I must tell you everything. Your strength will aid me to resist impressions that reason cannot shake away."

"I know, Romola—I know he is dead," Titus replied. His long, lustrous eyes offered no trace of the countless wishes he harbored for that death to have occurred sooner, if mere longing held any power. Romola read her own pure thoughts in their dark depths as clearly as one reads letters in a happy dream.

"So changed, Titus!" she exclaimed, pain piercing her. "To think it was Dino. And so strangely hardened! Not a word to Father, nothing but a vision he wanted to impart. Yet it was so pitiful—the struggling breath, the eyes that seemed to look toward the crucifix, yet failed to see it. I shall never forget it; it feels as though it will stand between me and everything I shall ever behold."

Her heart swelled anew, forcing her to pause, yet the urgent need to unburden her mind compelled her to stifle the rising anguish. Resuming her speech, her thoughts had drifted slightly.

"It was strange, Titus. The vision concerned our marriage, yet he knew nothing of you."

"What was it, my Romola?" Titus asked, guiding her to sit upon the nearby bench. A shadow of fear crossed him, lest the vision somehow implicated Baldassarre, prompting this sudden shift in posture.

Romola recounted every detail from her entry into San Marco, omitting not a single word of her brother's utterances, which burned themselves into her memory like hot iron. But when she reached the end, she faltered. The remainder remained too vivid to speak, and she gazed into the distance, almost unaware of Titus's presence. His mind, however, lay at ease; the vague vision had swept over him like a white mist, leaving no scar. He sat in silence, waiting.

"I took it," she continued, as if he had read her thoughts, "the crucifix. It is down below in my bedroom."

"Now, my Romola," Titus entreated, "banish these ghastly thoughts. It was merely an ordinary monk's vision, born of fasting and fanatical ideas. Surely it weighs nothing with you."

"No, Titus; no. But poor Dino... *he* believed it to be a divine message. It is strange," she mused, "this life of men possessed by fervid beliefs that appear madness to others. Dino was not a vulgar fanatic. And that Fra Girolamo—his very voice seems to have penetrated me with a sense that there is some truth in what moves them, a truth of which I know nothing."

"It was only because your feelings were highly wrought, my Romola," Titus explained. "Your brother's state of mind was no more than a form of that theosophy which has plagued excitable, dreamy minds throughout all ages; the same ideas your father's old adversary, Marsilio Ficino, pores over in the New Platonics. Only your brother's passionate nature drove him to act out what others merely write and speak of. As for Fra Girolamo, he is simply a narrow-minded monk with a gift for instilling terror. Any words, any voice, would have shaken you at that moment. Once your mind rests, you will judge such matters as you always have."

"Not about poor Dino," Romola countered softly. "I was angry with him; my heart seemed to close against him while he spoke. But since then, I have thought less of what was in my own mind and more of what was in his. Oh, Titus! It was terribly pitiful to see his young life ending thus. That yearning look at the crucifix as he gasped for breath—I can never forget it. Last night, I stared at the crucifix for a long time, trying to imagine how it could help him, until, by the lamplight, the suffering face seemed to shed pity."

"My Romola, promise me you will resist such thoughts. They are fit for sickly nuns, not for my golden-tressed Aurora, who appears made to scatter all such twilight fantasies. Try not to think of them now; we shall not long be alone together."

His final words were spoken in a tone of tender beseeching, and he turned her face toward him with a gentle touch of his right hand. Romola's eyes had been fixed absently on an arched opening, missing the distant hill; she had remained in the chapterhouse, staring at the pale images of sorrow and death.

Titus's touch and gentle voice called her back. In the warm sunlight, she beheld that rich, dark beauty which seemed to gather around it all images of joy—purple vines festooned between elms, corn ripening under vibrating heat, bright-winged creatures darting among flowers, round limbs beating the earth in gladness with raised cymbals, light melodies chanted to the thrilling rhythm of strings—all signs of Nature reveling in her force. A strange, bewildering transition struck her: from those pale specters of sorrow and death to this vibrant youthfulness, akin to a sun-god ignorant of night! What thought could reconcile the worn anguish on her brother's face, that straining reach for something invisible, with this satisfied strength and beauty, proving they belonged to the same world? Was there no reconciliation, save only a blind worship of clashing deities, first in mad joy, then in wailing? For the first time, Romola felt this questioning need like a sudden, dizzying lack, a void demanding something to grasp. It was an experience shorter than a sigh, for the eager theorizing of ages is compressed, like a seed, into the momentary want of a single mind. No answer met the need, and it vanished before the rushing return of young sympathy for the glowing, loving beauty that shone upon her in new radiance, like dawn after turning away from the gray west.
