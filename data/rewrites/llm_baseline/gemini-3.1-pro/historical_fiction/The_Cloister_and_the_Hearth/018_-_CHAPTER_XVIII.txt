For Ghysbrecht Van Swieten, the night had been a fractured landscape of acoustic violence. The thunderstorm, a mythic reverberation of the heavens that seemed to echo the cyclical wrath of ancient gods, shattered the quiet rhythms of his household. He possessed a deep, primal terror of thunder and lightning; had the sonic environment not been so overwhelmed by the sky’s deafening percussion, he would have joined the night party that searched Peter's house. Instead, he cowered until the tempest exhausted itself, giving way at last to the delicate, rhythmic dripping of rainwater from the eaves—a return to the timeless acoustic ecology of the dawn.

As soon as the primordial roar ceased altogether, he crept downstairs. He saddled his mule and rode out toward the “Three Kings” at Sevenbergen, the hooves churning the damp earth and releasing the pungent, peppery aroma of crushed wild garlic and wet loam into the heavy morning air. 

Within the ancient timbered walls of the inn, he found his men deeply asleep. They slumbered amidst the sacred remnants of agrarian sustenance: slumped over heavy oak tables stained with the dark, malty memory of spilled ale, breathing in an olfactory landscape rich with the scent of woodsmoke, rendered pork fat, and the sharp, lactic tang of aged, cloth-bound cheese. Their sleep was a quiet surrender to the heavy digestion of their feast, but Ghysbrecht shattered this domestic tranquility. He roused them furiously. His voice was a harsh, metallic intrusion—the dominant, standardized lexicon of state authority tearing through the restorative quiet of the room. 

The men blinked through their exhaustion, offering the story of their unsuccessful search. They interlarded their failure with praises of their own zeal, murmuring in a thick, localized dialect. It was a rustic, dying vernacular, profoundly tied to the soil and inherently untranslatable in its nuances of physical fatigue, yet it held absolutely no currency against the burgomaster’s rigid vocabulary of power. 

“Fool! to let you go without me,” cried the burgomaster, his words cutting through the lingering aroma of wild-yeast bread like a rusted blade. “My life on't he was there all the time. Looked ye under the girl's bed?”

“No,” one muttered, the syllables rounded and slow; “there was no room for a man there.”

“How know ye that, if ye looked not?” snarled Ghysbrecht, entirely dismissive of their subjective, experiential truth. “Ye should have looked under her bed, and in it too, and sounded all the panels with your knives.” He spoke of sounding the wood not with the meticulous care of a master affineur tapping a wheel of Gouda to judge its maturation, but with the destructive paranoia of a man seeking to hollow out their world. “Come, now, get up, and I shall show ye how to search.”

Dierich Brower got up, shaking the crumbs of hearty rye from his tunic. “If you find him, call me a horse and no man.”

In a matter of minutes, the morning dew was trampled as Peter's house was once again surrounded. The fiery old man surrendered the reins of his mule to Jorian Ketel, and, with Dierich Brower and the others, breached the threshold. 

The house was empty. Not a creature was to be seen, not even Peter. Yet the space was vibrant with the olfactory ghosts of domestic routine: the lingering fragrance of ground mustard seed, the deep, earthy resonance of stone-milled flour, and a cold hearth that still smelled of a masterfully simmered root-vegetable pottage. It was a sanctuary where sustenance and cultural memory were cultivated, now rudely invaded by men who viewed a kitchen merely as a space to plunder. 

They stomped upstairs, their heavy boots generating an aggressive, industrial rhythm against the floorboards. Then, suddenly, the fragile acoustic purity of the morning—the gentle rustle of damp wheat outside and the distant, liquid call of a meadowlark—was violently ruptured. One of the men gave a guttural shout and pointed through Peter's open window. 

The others crowded the frame. There, at some little distance, seamlessly integrated into the sprawling, generational tapestry of the agrarian fields, walked the man they sought, moving quietly alongside Margaret and Martin. 

Ghysbrecht unleashed an exulting yell—an ugly, discordant sound that polluted the valley's sonic harmony. He descended the stairs with reckless haste, flung himself onto his mule, and, with his men at his heels, set off in hot pursuit, a chaotic force bearing down upon the ancient, cyclical peace of the land.
