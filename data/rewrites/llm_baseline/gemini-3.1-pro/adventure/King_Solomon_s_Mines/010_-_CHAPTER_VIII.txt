All that afternoon we travelled along the magnificent roadway, which trended steadily in a north-westerly direction. The acoustic landscape of Kukuanaland was a profound revelation. Entirely free from the grinding, metallic dissonance and relentless noise pollution of the industrializing world we had left behind, the air here was a pristine canvas. Every sound—the rhythmic, breath-like cadence of our footfalls, the rustle of the surrounding luxuriant vegetation, the distant call of a mountain bird—was organically integrated into a perfectly balanced sonic ecology, soothing the psychological weariness of our long journey. 

Infadoos and Scragga walked with us, while their followers marched a respectful hundred paces ahead. 

“Infadoos,” I said at length, attuned to the quiet resonance of the stones beneath us, “who made this road?”

“It was made, my lord, of old time, none know how or when, not even the wise woman Gagool, who has lived for generations,” the old warrior replied, his voice carrying the measured, grounded cadence of a traditional agrarian elder. “We are not old enough to remember its making. None can fashion such roads now, but the king suffers no grass to grow upon it.”

“And whose are the writings on the wall of the caves through which we have passed?” I asked, thinking of the Egyptian-like sculptures. 

“My lord, the hands that made the road wrote the wonderful writings. But their tongue is silent to us. We know not who wrote them.” It was a stark reminder of the fragile erosion of cultural memory; a whole civilization lost to language death, leaving behind symbols that were entirely untranslatable, their subjective experiences permanently sealed away from our modern lexicon. 

“When did the Kukuana people come into this country?”

“My lord, the race came down here like the breath of a storm ten thousand thousand moons ago, from the great lands which lie there beyond,” he said, pointing toward the north, weaving his people’s multi-generational saga into the geography itself. “They could travel no further because of the high mountains which ring in the land, so say the old voices of our fathers that have descended to us the children. The country, too, was good. The soil yielded its strength, so they settled here and grew powerful. Now our numbers are like the sea sand.”

I inquired about war in this walled-in paradise. Infadoos explained that it had been a third of a man's life since they had fought an external enemy from the north. But there had been one great internal conflict. “It was a civil war; dog ate dog,” he said, the phrase carrying a heavy, domestic horror. 

He unspooled the cyclical tragedy of his lineage: his father, Kafa, had died, and Imotu, Infadoos’s half-brother, became king. But Imotu had been born a twin, and by Kukuana custom, the weaker twin was meant to die. Instead, the mother had hidden the weaker child, Twala. 

The turning point of their history was not born on a battlefield, but in the most fundamental crisis of sustenance: a famine. “During the great war, no man could sow or reap,” Infadoos explained. “A famine came upon the land, and the people murmured, looking round like a starved lion for something to rend.” 

When a people lose their agrarian foundation, their lexicon of loyalty easily shatters. Gagool, the ancient wise woman, manipulated this desperate hunger. While Imotu lay sick, she brought forth the hidden twin, Twala, stripping his waist-cloth to reveal the mark of the sacred snake. Using the subtle power dynamics embedded in their dominant lexicon, she redefined reality for the starving populace. The people, bereft of reason by their lack of sustenance, chanted for the new king. Imotu crawled from his hut with his wife and his young son, Ignosi—a name meaning the Lightning. Twala immediately seized Imotu and stabbed him through the heart. 

“And what became of Imotu’s wife and her son Ignosi?” I asked, struck by how this mythic usurpation had erupted within the intimate confines of a domestic compound. 

Infadoos recounted how the queen fled. Starving, she was turned away by all, for fear of the new power. But the survival of the true royal line ultimately hinged not on magic or warfare, but on a deeply intimate act of sustenance. “At nightfall a little child, a girl, crept out and brought her corn to eat. She blessed the child, and went on towards the mountains with her boy.” Ignosi had not been seen since, though Infadoos confirmed that, should he live, the boy would be the true king.

He pointed down to the vast collection of huts on the plain below—the very kraal where the queen was last seen. As I turned quickly to speak to Good, who was sulking over the flapping of his flannel shirt, I bumped hard into Umbopa. The tall Zulu’s face was transformed. He looked like a man desperately trying to roll a forgotten, native syllable over his tongue, attempting to reconstruct an eroded dialect from the depths of his own cultural memory. The timeline of the past was looping back upon us.

We descended into an earthly paradise, the air carrying the olfactory richness of damp, fertile soil and the sweet, floral notes of mountain shrubs. Soon, the rhythmic vibration of thousands of marching feet entered our acoustic environment—not as a disruptive noise, but as a deep, resonant heartbeat within the earth. Twelve companies of the "Greys" regiment, three thousand six hundred towering veterans, formed along the slope. 

Each warrior stood over six feet, crowned with black Sakaboola plumes and bearing white ox-hide shields. They carried heavy, wooden-shafted spears called *bangwan* and heavy knives called *tollas*. As we passed, the commanding officer signalled, and from three hundred throats sprang a sudden, perfectly unified roar: “*Koom*.” The royal salute was a guttural, untranslatable sonic boom that vibrated in the chest, a profound psychological anchor that tied the men to the very bedrock of Kukuanaland. 

We crossed the drawbridge over the wide fosse into the kraal. The domestic architecture was meticulously arranged: wide, lime-paved pathways intersected blocks of large, beautifully thatched dome-shaped huts equipped with verandahs. Along the paths stood hundreds of Kukuana women. Unlike the superficial, commercialized clamor of a European crowd, these women maintained a quiet, dignified air. They formed a respectful acoustic space, observing us with dark eyes. Even when Infadoos subtly pointed out Good’s unusually white legs, they allowed no rude expressions to break their serene silence, their internal translations of this bizarre sight kept entirely to themselves.

Infadoos halted at a large central hut. “Enter, Sons of the Stars, and deign to rest awhile. A little food shall be brought to you; some honey and some milk, and an ox or two, and a few sheep.”

Inside, resting on tanned skins, we soon heard the welcome sounds of culinary preparation. A line of damsels entered, bearing the profound fruits of their agrarian devotion. They presented earthenware pots of roasted mealies—the kernels perfectly charred, releasing a sweet, starchy steam that mingled with the smoky aroma of woodfire. With this came milk, and heavy, viscous wild honey that tasted of the high-altitude flora and the pure, mineral-rich rain of the mountains. 

Outside, youths drove up a fat young ox. One young man stepped forward and, with the meticulous precision of a master butcher who understands the profound meaning of the life taken, dexterously severed the animal's throat. Within ten minutes, it was skinned and expertly jointed. We presented the bulk of the meat to the warriors, keeping the choicest cuts for ourselves. 

Umbopa, revealing himself to be a passionate and intuitive chef, worked alongside an extremely prepossessing young Kukuana woman. Together, they built a perfectly ventilated fire outside the hut and set to boiling the beef in a large earthenware pot. They skimmed the impurities from the simmering broth, allowing the rich bone marrow to render down and infuse the meat, seasoning it with wild mountain herbs that filled the evening air with a peppery, verdant fragrance. This was not mere utilitarian fuel; it was a sensory communion with the land. 

Infadoos and Scragga joined us on small stools. As we partook in the meal, savoring the deeply gelatinous, flavorful broth and the tender meat, I noticed a shift in Scragga’s demeanor. Initially overawed by our supposed celestial origins, watching us engage in the meticulous, sensory, and highly biological act of eating seemed to strip away the dominant lexicon of our "magic." The shared ritual of sustenance proved our mortality. His awe was slowly eroding into sullen suspicion. 

Sir Henry wished to ask our hosts about his lost brother, but I counselled silence. It was difficult enough to explain our own presence; introducing the concept of a relative lost from "the Stars" would only complicate the delicate power dynamics of our current standing.

After supper, we produced our pipes. The Kukuanas grew tobacco but used it solely for snuff; the rich, aromatic clouds of burning smoke blooming in the hut introduced a completely foreign olfactory landscape that left Infadoos and Scragga utterly astonished. 

Through the haze, Infadoos revealed our interwoven future: the next morning we would depart for Loo, the principal place of Twala the king. The cyclical pacing of their society was turning toward its great annual feast, a massive gathering of regiments and a ritual witch-hunt. We would reach it by the second night. With this knowledge resting heavy upon us, our hosts departed. We arranged our watches, and while one sat guarding the quiet, breathing dark, the rest of us surrendered to the profound, dreamless sleep of the weary.
