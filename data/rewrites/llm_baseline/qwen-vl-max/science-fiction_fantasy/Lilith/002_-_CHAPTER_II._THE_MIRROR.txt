For several days, nothing stirred—no sound, no sign of life beyond the quiet hum of dust settling in the corners of the library. It was perhaps a week later when the events I now recount began to unfold. The manuscript fragment had haunted my thoughts, its edges frayed and unreadable, locked in place by some unseen force. I had tried every method I could think of to free it, but nothing worked. The words remained sealed, as if bound not by thread or wax, but by time itself.

Yet another matter weighed on me: the books in the closet. Their silence felt oppressive, their presence too still. I had long intended to reorganize them, to assess their condition, for the air in that room carried a peculiar chill—one that seemed to whisper of neglect and forgotten things. One afternoon, this intention hardened into action. As I rose from my chair, preparing to begin, I saw him.

Not quite a man, not quite a shadow—only an impression, fleeting and sharp. A figure moved from the doorway of the closet toward the far end of the room: slight, stooped, draped in a shabby dress coat that trailed nearly to his heels. The tails parted slightly with each step, revealing thin legs clad in black stockings and wide, slipper-like shoes. He walked without sound, yet I felt his presence like a breath against my skin.

I followed.

It did not occur to me that I might be chasing a ghost. I knew, somehow, that I was following something real—though what, I could not say. He passed through the library into the hall, crossed to the base of the grand staircase, and climbed upward. I followed closely, silent, breath held. He ascended to the first floor, where the main rooms lay, then continued past them, down a wide corridor, to the foot of a narrower stair leading to the second story. Up that he went, and when I reached the top, I found myself in a part of the house I barely remembered—perhaps never truly known.

I had been an only child, raised in solitude, taken away by my guardian before I could learn the secrets of these halls. When I returned, just over a month ago, it was to reclaim what was mine—yet even then, I had not ventured beyond the familiar chambers. Now, guided by a phantom, I wandered deeper into the forgotten heart of the house.

Through passage after passage we moved, until we came to a door at the bottom of a winding wooden stair. We ascended together. Each step groaned beneath my weight, but my guide made no sound—not a creak, not a sigh. Midway up, I lost sight of him. At the top, there was no trace—no shape, no silhouette. The shadows were thick, but he was not among them. He had vanished.

The garret loomed above me—a vast, echoing space with great beams crisscrossing the ceiling, rafters like skeletal fingers reaching into the gloom. Doors stood half-hidden in the dark, corridors stretched into obscurity, and distant skylights let in dim, cobwebbed light. I stood still, breath catching in my throat. This place was mine, yet utterly alien. A strange thrill ran through me—fear, yes, but also wonder. It was uncharted territory, untouched by memory.

In the center of the garret stood a rough wooden enclosure, unpainted, weathered. Its door hung slightly ajar. Thinking perhaps Mr. Raven waited inside, I pushed it open.

The small chamber within was flooded with light—but not the kind that warms or welcomes. It was the light of abandonment: dull, hollow, as though the room itself regretted existing. Sunbeams, faint and dusty, cut through the air, illuminating motes that danced like tiny spirits. In the center stood a tall mirror, narrow and old-fashioned, its surface coated in grime. The frame was ebony, carved with subtle elegance. Perched atop it, a black eagle with wings spread wide clutched a golden chain in its beak—the chain ending in a single, heavy black sphere.

At first, I studied the mirror’s form rather than its reflection. Then, suddenly, I realized: it showed nothing. No image of the room, no glimpse of myself. The glass seemed to swallow the world around it.

A moment passed. Then—impossible as it sounds—I felt the wall dissolve. Not literally, but in sensation: as if reality bent, folded inward. And then I saw.

Before me lay a wild, desolate landscape. Rolling hills, broken and barren, stretched into the distance. Beyond them, a mountain range pierced the horizon. Closest to me, a flat expanse of moorland stretched out—gray, windswept, empty. The sky above was overcast, bruised with cloud.

I stepped forward, drawn by curiosity. My vision was poor, so I leaned closer to examine a stone in the foreground. That’s when I saw it: a raven, large and ancient, hopping slowly toward me. His feathers were deep purplish-black, streaked with silver-gray, like tarnished metal. He moved with solemn dignity, pecking at the ground as if searching for worms.

I did not flinch. To see a living creature in a painting—no, not a painting. This was no illusion. This was *real*.

I took another step, eager to get a better look. My foot caught on something—probably the edge of the mirror’s frame—and I stumbled forward.

And then—impact.

Nose to beak.

The raven blinked. I gasped.

And I was no longer in the garret.

I stood on a wind-swept heath, bare and endless, under a sky that promised rain. The air was cold, damp, alive. The mirror was gone. The house was gone. Only the moor, the raven, and the vast, indifferent land remained.

I had stepped through the glass.

And the world had changed.
