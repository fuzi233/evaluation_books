The knocker rattled against the oak, a sharp, metallic demand that shattered the domestic quietude of the afternoon. Down the steep, echoing stairs, my father's voice erupted, booming with the indignation of a man whose authority had been momentarily usurped by circumstance. "Louisa!" he roared, the syllables vibrating through the floorboards. "Are you all asleep? Here have I had to answer the front door myself!" With that declaration of sovereign failure, he strode into his study, the heavy door slamming behind him with the finality of a gavel strike.

Only a quarter-hour elapsed before the brass bell on the hall stand began its rapid, authoritative jangle. Inside the kitchen, the air hung thick with the scent of boiled vegetables and suppressed anxiety. My mother, encased in wash-leather gloves that strained against her skin as she peeled potatoes with labored precision, paused. Beside her, my Aunt Fan stood amidst a cascade of green peas, her shellers halting mid-motion. The bell rang again, louder, more insistent, demanding immediate compliance.

"Once for Louisa, twice for James, isn't it?" Aunt Fan queried, her voice dripping with the practiced skepticism of a woman who knew the household rhythms better than the masters themselves.

"You go, Paul," my mother instructed, her tone shifting from maternal warmth to urgent command. "Say that Louisa—" But the sentence died in her throat. A sudden, violent flush crimsoned her cheeks, betraying a panic she refused to name. Before I could set down my slate, she had ripped off her gloves, her movements jerky and frantic. "No, don't stop your lessons," she stammered, backing away. "I'll go myself." She fled the room, leaving the smell of raw potato skins in her wake.

Moments later, the kitchen door swung open with a soft sigh. My mother's hand appeared through the crack, beckoning me with a gesture that was equal parts terror and triumph. "Walk on your toes," she whispered, her voice barely audible over the settling dust. She set the example, leading me up the stairs. The wooden treads, ancient and judgmental, betrayed our deception immediately, protesting our stealth with a cacophony of screeches and groans that seemed to mock our attempt at secrecy. Yet, we pressed on, reaching the sanctuary of my parents' bedroom. There, nestled within the ornate, moth-eaten wardrobe—a relic of a gentler era—lay my finest attire. Or, to be grammatically precise, *my* finest attire.

Never before had I donned these garments on a Monday morning, but my mother swept aside every trivial concern regarding the fit or the fabric. Her focus was singular: speed. Once I was transformed, down to the new Bluchers that clicked sharply against the floorboards, she seized my hand. Together, we crept downward, silent and alert, retracing our path with the caution of spies. She led me to the street door, unlocking it with a flourish that felt dangerously theatrical.

"Shan't I want my cap?" I whispered, the question tumbling out before I could check it. My mother merely shook her head, her eyes wide and fearful, and slammed the door shut with a resounding bang. Only then did the pantomime reveal its script. Was it comedy or tragedy? I was becoming intimately familiar with the genre. With my mother's hand resting heavily upon my shoulder, we entered the lion's den: my father's office.

Whether it was because the drawing-room served as a showroom for imaginary guests, filled with Cowper's poems lying face-down on a wobbly table and half-finished crochet work draped over chairs like discarded lace, or simply because my father treated his profession as a game of make-believe—he would vanish to play cricket in the garden on dry days or ninepins in the hallway on wet ones, only returning when the front door knocker sounded—I cannot say. What I knew, however, was certain: as a child, I viewed my father's career not as a solemn vocation, but as a grand role-play. He was merely acting, surrounded by towering books and bundles of documents labeled with imposing titles but containing mostly blank paper. The japanned tin boxes, marked with names like "Sutton Hampden, Esq.," were essentially my mother's sewing basket; the "Drayton Estates" produced nothing but apples, a fruit my father adored; and the "Mortgages" were, as I would discover years later, entirely unrelated to the manuscripts of poetry scattered nearby, some awaiting correction, others already finished.

As the door opened, he rose, moving toward us. His hair stood in chaotic tufts atop his head, a testament to his habit of ruffling it during moments of intense discourse. This dishevelment, combined with his strenuous efforts to compose a face of businesslike gravity, only heightened the illusion of the overgrown schoolboy pretending to be a statesman.

"This is the youngster," my father announced, taking me from my mother and presenting me forward. "Tall for his age, isn't he?"

With a twist of his thick lips, he rolled the foul-smelling cigar he smoked from the left corner of his mouth to the right. He extended a fat, unclean hand, which, as it closed around mine, conjured in my mind the image of a walrus from my natural history textbook. With his other hand, he pat me affectionately on the head.

"Like 'is mother, wonderfully like 'is mother, ain't 'e?" he observed, still gripping my wrist. "And that," he added, winking with one of his tiny, piercing eyes toward my father, "is about the highest compliment I can pay 'im, eh?"

His eyes were remarkably small, yet they burned with a brilliance that felt invasive. When he turned them back upon me, I felt compelled to scramble for something complimentary, convinced he could see straight through my childish facade.

"And where are you thinkin' of sendin' 'im?" he continued, his voice dropping to a conspiratorial whisper. "Eton or 'Arrow?"

"We haven't quite made up our minds as yet," my father replied smoothly. "At present, we are educating him at home."

"You take my tip," said the portly man, leaning forward. "Learn all you can. Look at me! If I'd 'ad the opportunity of being a scholar, I wouldn't be here offering your father an extravagant price to do my work; I'd be able to do it myself."

"You seem to have got on very well without it," my father laughed, and indeed, his air of prosperity might have warranted greater self-congratulation. Rings sparkled on his blunt fingers, and upon the swelling folds of his waistcoat, a massive gold chain rose and sank rhythmically.

"I'd 'ave done better with it," he grunted, the regret palpable in his tone.

"But you look very clever," I said. Though I sensed with a child's intuition that I was expected to impress him, the words sprang forth spontaneously, born of genuine admiration.

He laughed heartily, his entire body trembling like a giant gelatinous mass. "Well, old Noel Hasluck's not exactly a fool," he conceded, "but I'd like myself better if I could talk about something else than business, and didn't drop my aitches. And so would my little gell."

"You have a daughter?" my mother asked. For a woman, a child often served as the ultimate bridge of sympathy with a stranger, displacing the usual bonds formed with respectful cooks or competent maids.

"I won't tell you about 'er. But I'll just bring 'er to see you now and then, ma'am, if you don't mind," answered Mr. Hasluck. "She don't often meet gentle-folks, an' it'll do 'er good."

My mother glanced across at my father, but the man intercepted her inquiry, answering for her with a good-natured smile. "You needn't be afraid, ma'am, that she's anything like me," he assured her. "Nobody ever believes she's my daughter, except me and the old woman. She's a little lady, she is. Freak o' nature, I call it."

"We shall be delighted," my mother explained, her voice steady despite the tremor in her hands.

"Well, you will when you see 'er," replied Mr. Hasluck, content with his own mystery.

He pushed a half-crown into my palm, overriding my parents' likely qualms with the easy confidence of a man accustomed to having his way. "No squanderin' it on the 'eathen," was his parting injunction as I retreated from the room. "You spend that on a Christian tradesman."

It was the first money I distinctly remembered possessing. Visions of new gloves for Sunday service, of a Latin grammar that would belong solely to me, and countless other elevated pursuits had previously occupied my thoughts regarding stray coins. But this time, I was granted the rare luxury of autonomy.

The weight of the coin was immense. The anxiety it provoked was paralyzing. I tossed sleepless hours in bed, wandering the bewildering labyrinth of the high street. Advice, when sought, was flatly denied.

"You must learn to think for yourself," my father declared, speaking eloquently on the necessity of early judgment and what he termed "commercial aptitude."
