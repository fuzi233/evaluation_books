When Mother heard I had accepted a home at Warrington, I suspect she was scarcely delighted. Perhaps she withdrew her financial support deliberately, aiming to starve me into a surrender and force a return to Virginia, back under her thumb. Yet, until her final breath, we never resolved the pecuniary dispute between us. She severed my allowances; I said not a word, managing instead to subsist without her aid. I never heard that she repented of her stinginess, nor did she acknowledge it publicly, save perhaps through Harry's private communications to me later. In subsequent meetings, marked by a sudden gentleness in her demeanor and an unusual reverence for my wife, Madam Esmond may have intended for me to infer a tacit admission of her error. Still, she offered no apology, and I asked for none. With Harry provided for—a fact I could not begrudge—all of Mother's savings and frugal schemes ultimately flowed to my advantage, for I was her heir. Once, a few guineas would have been more vital to me than hundreds arriving when I needed them less; but when we finally reconciled, the era of necessity had long passed. I possessed ample means, rendering noble schemes unnecessary, and I held no ill will toward the doctor for his fees. Mother likely suffered inwardly, guilt-ridden over the hunger my children had endured and the relief strangers had provided for needs she, in her pride, had refused to address. Was she prouder than I? A single word of explanation between us might have healed the rift years prior, but neither spoke. When I recognize a wrong within myself, I crave to seek pardon, yet this act serves my own pride, allowing me to apologize to the self I perceive as lacking. Thus, I rarely debase myself to perform a meanness. Consider the man whose entire existence is a tapestry of lies, schemes, and subterfuge. What fellowship do they keep when alone? Daily, I observe men whose smiles are artifice and winks hypocrisy. Do they wear masks in solitude, before their own consciences? If I overlook an injury, it is not born of Christian forgiveness, but because I can afford to remit the debt and disdain settling accounts. I know a few sweet souls—perhaps I cultivated them—who offer forgiveness as naturally as a plant springs from the earth. I know how to remit, I say, not how to forgive. I wonder if proud men take pride in their pride.

Consequently, I displayed no sign of submission toward my parent in Virginia, and we remained estranged for years, exchanging only occasional brief tidbits—the birth of a child, or similar matters—through my wife and hers. Following our initial tribulations in America regarding the Stamp Act, further troubles befell me in London. Though I stood with the Tories in our colonial quarrel, convinced the Imperial Government rightfully levied taxes, a letter I published to a Member of the House of Burgesses proved disastrous. In it, I freely addressed the habitual insolence of the mother country and voiced sentiments so offensive to those in power that I was stripped of my position as hackney-coach licenser. To my Uncle's terror and horror, I had fallen into disgrace. He, who could never love a disgraced man, had grown to cherish my wife and our little boy dearly, yet viewed me with a pitying contempt that endlessly amused me. Nature endowed him with a scorn for poverty and a corresponding love for success and fortune. Any deviation from the regular path shocked him; truth-telling turned him pale. Yet, despite the dreadful shocks I gave him, he continued to see Theo, the child, and me. He bestowed upon me a mournful recognition when we met, viewing me as a melancholy, lost creature beyond worldly help. Never mind. I must devise a new scheme for life, and at this juncture, the repayment of Harry's debt to me enabled me to survive, if not thrive, for months or years to come.

Strange fatuity of youth! How dare we be so poor, so little cast down?

Then, His Majesty's royal uncle, the Duke of Cumberland, fell down and perished in a fit. Strangely, his death wrought a remarkable change in my fortunes. My poor Sir Miles Warrington missed no court ceremony to which he could introduce himself. Drawing-rooms, christenings, balls, and funerals were his domain. If a prince or princess ailed, his coach waited at their door. Leicester Fields, Carlton House, Gunnersbury—they meant nothing to him now but attending the stout Duke's funeral. Standing bareheaded in the rain at this interment, he contracted a severe cold and throat inflammation. Before almost anyone had heard of his illness, a lawyer waited upon me at my Bloomsbury lodgings, saluting me as "Sir George Warrington."

Party strife and fears of the future vanished. We laid the poor gentleman beside his little son in the family churchyard, where so many of his race repose. Little Miles and I were the chief mourners. An obsequious tenantry bowed and curtsied before us, striving to conciliate my honor. The Dowager and her daughter withdrew to Bath promptly, leaving me and my family to take possession of the house, of which I have been master for thirty years. Be not too eager, O my son! Have patience, and I too shall sleep beneath those yew trees, while the people toss their caps for Sir Miles.

The records of a prosperous country life are easily and briefly told. The steward's books reveal what rents were paid and forgiven, what crops raised, and in what rotation. Visitors, their durations of stay, pensioners cared for by my wife, their treatments, deaths, the year I served as sheriff, and the frequency of hounds meeting near us are all narrated in our house journals, available to any heir willing to read them. We could not afford the magnificent Hill Street mansion occupied by my predecessor, so we took a smaller dwelling, wherein we nonetheless spent more lavishly. We made but half the ostentatious display with liveries, equipages, and plate for which my uncle was famed; yet our beer was stronger, and my wife's charities perhaps more costly than those of the Dowager Lady Warrington. Doubtless, she believed there was no harm in spoiling the Philistines, for she sold us her remaining country-house goods at unconscionable prices, and I submitted to most of her extortions with unutterable good humor. What value she imagined for potted plants in her greenhouses! What price she set on that horrid old spinet in her drawing-room! And the framed pieces of worsted work, executed by the accomplished Dora and the lovely Flora—if they had been masterpieces of Titian or Van Dyck, surely my Lady Dowager could not have valued them higher. Though we paid so generously, and were, I may say without boast, far kinder to our poor than she had ever been, for a while we bore the very worst reputation in the county, where tales of my disrepute circulated freely. I hoped perhaps to succeed to my uncle's seat in Parliament as well as his lands, but I discovered, somehow, that I was voted a person of dangerously radical opinions. I would not bribe; I would not coerce my tenants to vote for me in the 1668 election. A gentleman descended from Whitehall with a pocket-book full of bank-notes, and I found I had no chance against my competitor.

Bon Dieu! Now that we were at ease regarding worldly means—now that obedient tenants bowed and curtsied as we proceeded to church, driving to visit friends or neighboring towns in the great family coach drawn by four fat horses—did we not often regret poverty, and that dear little cottage at Lambeth where Want ever prowled at the door? Did I not long to be bear-leading again, vowing that translating for booksellers was not such hard drudgery? Upon visiting London, we made sentimental pilgrimages to all our old haunts. I am certain my wife embraced every landlady. You may be sure we invited all the friends of those old times to share the comforts of our new home. The Reverend Mr. Hagan and his lady visited us more than once. His appearance in the pulpit at B------ (where he preached finely, as we thought) caused an awful scandal there. Sampson came too, another unlucky Levite, and was welcome as long as he stayed among us. Mr. Johnson talked of coming but put us off once or twice. I suppose our house was dull. I know I myself would remain silent for days, fearing that my moodiness must often try the sweetest-tempered woman in the world who lived with me. I cared little for field sports. Killing one partridge seemed so like killing another that I marveled how men could spend days pursuing such slaughter. Their fox-hunting stories commenced at four o'clock, when the tablecloth was removed, and lasted until supper-time. I sat silent, listening; day after day I fell asleep. No wonder I was not popular with my company.

What confession is this I am making? Here was the storm over, the rocks avoided, the ship in port, and the sailor not over contented? Was Susan, whom I had sighed for during the voyage, not the beauty I expected to find?
