While we still stood catching our breath in that cold, dreadful hall—where the air smelt of old stone, stale damp, and the sour-sweet taint of ages—Gagool had been busy in her own fashion. The ancient thing, quick as a spider when she chose, had clambered up on to the great table and hobbled along to where Twala lay under the steady drip that fell like a slow, patient cook’s ladle, basting him for some obscene feast. She bent and pressed her withered lips to his dead mouth, as if greeting him at a threshold, then tottered back again, pausing to mutter to one or other of the shrouded kings, like a housewife speaking to elders seated in the dusk of a kraal. At last she squatted beneath the pale stalactite they called the White Death, and began, as well as I could make out, to pray—words swallowed by the vault, by the drip, by the hush.

The cavern held its own sound the way a kitchen holds its own smell. Every little noise—our boots shifting on grit, the faint creak of leather, the lamp’s minute suck of air—felt too loud, an insult to that oppressive silence. I found myself whispering without meaning to, as though any higher voice would wake the dead.

“Now, Gagool,” I said, “lead us to the chamber.”

She slid down from the table and leered up at me. “My lords are not afraid?”

“Lead on.”

“Good, my lords.” She shuffled round behind the White Death. “Here is the chamber. Let my lords light the lamp, and enter.” She set the gourd of oil down and leaned against the rock as if it were her own hearth-wall.

I struck one of our remaining matches and lit the rush wick. The lamp breathed out its thin, animal warmth and a smell of oil—comforting in any other place, like a pantry lamp before dawn, but here it only made the blackness feel thicker. I held the light up and looked for a doorway.

There was nothing—only solid rock.

Gagool’s grin widened. “The way is there, my lords. Ha! ha! ha!”

“Do not jest with us,” I said, and my own voice sounded small.

“I jest not, my lords. See!”

As she pointed, the stone before us began to rise—slowly, smoothly—lifting as though the mountain itself had decided to take a breath. A great slab, door-wide, ten feet high and monstrously thick, drew upward and vanished into the rock above, no doubt into some prepared hollow. There was no scraping, no grinding. The mechanism—counterweights, I guessed, like a simple balance—worked with a terrible patience, and the quiet of it was worse than any clamor. Somewhere, perhaps at a hidden notch, a lever had been nudged; but Gagool kept her hands away, careful as a thief around another man’s larder.

When the mass had gone, a dark opening gaped where it had stood.

My heart began to tremble in my chest as if it were trying to beat its way out. For years—through dust, thirst, fear, and blood—we had marched after a rumor, after an old tale clinging to a dead man’s bones. And now the mouth of that tale was open before us.

“Enter, white men from the Stars,” said Gagool, and she stepped into the doorway with the lamp’s light licking her bent shoulders. “But first hear your servant, Gagool the old. The bright stones that ye will see were dug from the pit over which the Silent Ones are set, and stored here—I know not by whom, for it was done longer ago than even I remember.

“But once has this place been entered since the time that those who hid the stones fled in haste, leaving them behind. The report went down among the people from age to age; but none knew where the chamber was, nor the secret of the door. Then it happened that a white man came over the mountains—perchance he too came ‘from the Stars’—and the king of that day received him. He it is who sits yonder.” She pointed to the fifth king at the table.

“And it came to pass that he and a woman of the country journeyed to this place, and by chance the woman learnt the secret—a thousand years might ye search, but ye should never find it. The white man entered with the woman and found the stones, and he filled with stones the skin of a small goat which she had to hold food. And as he went he took up one more stone, a large one, and held it in his hand.”

She stopped there, and we hung upon her like hungry men scenting stew from another hut.

“Well,” I said, “what happened to Da Silvestra?”

At the name her eyes snapped. “How knowest thou the dead man’s name?” she hissed. Then she spat the story on without waiting for my answer. “None can tell what happened; but the white man was frightened. He flung down the goat-skin and fled with only the one stone in his hand. The king took it, and it is the stone which thou, Macumazahn, didst take from Twala’s brow.”

“Have none entered here since?” I asked, peering into the black passage beyond her.

“None, my lords. Only the secret has been kept, and every king has opened it, though he has not entered. There is a saying: those who enter will die within a moon, even as the white man died in the cave upon the mountain where ye found him. Therefore the kings do not enter. Ha! ha! mine are true words.”

Her gaze held mine, and in that moment a chill went through me as if the drip of the White Death had found my spine. How did she know—how could she speak of names, of fear, of the old Portuguese’s last trembling?

“Enter,” she croaked. “If I speak truth, the goat-skin with stones lies on the floor. And if there is truth that it is death to enter—ye will learn afterwards. Ha! ha! ha!”

She hobbled in with the light. I hesitated.

“Oh, confound it,” said Good. “I’m not going to be frightened by that old devil,” and he plunged after her. Foulata followed him, though the girl’s shoulders shook and her breath came short, as if each step were into colder water. Sir Henry and I went after.

A few yards down the passage, hewn clean out of living rock, Gagool waited. “See, my lords,” she said, holding the lamp low so its flame made long shadows on the walls, “those who stored the treasure fled in haste, and would have guarded against any who should find the secret—but had not time.”

She pointed to large square blocks of stone set across the passage to the height of two courses, as if men had begun to wall it up and then been called away mid-task. Along the sides lay more blocks ready. Most curious of all, there was a heap of mortar, and beside it two trowels—so familiar in shape that for an instant my mind leapt, absurdly, to bricklayers whistling at their work in some modern town, their songs swallowed now by machines and streets. Here, in this older world, the tools had waited in silence for centuries, like a language paused on a half-spoken word.

Foulata faltered then. She said she felt faint and could go no farther, and her voice sounded thin in that narrow stone throat. We sat her down on the unfinished wall, and set the basket of provisions by her—a homely thing, that basket, smelling of dried meat and gourds, as out of place here as a cooking pot in a tomb. We left her there to recover, the passage swallowing our footsteps.

Fifteen paces farther we came upon an elaborately painted wooden door, standing wide open. Whoever had last stood there had not shut it—or had not had time.

Across the threshold lay a skin bag of goat-hide, bulging and heavy, as if it were full of pebbles. The lamp’s light caught it, and the passage seemed suddenly to breathe.

“Hee! hee! white men,” sniggered Gagool. “What did I tell you? The white man fled in haste and dropped the woman’s bag. Look within also and ye will find a water-gourd amongst the stones.”

Good stooped and lifted it. The bag gave a rich, hard jingle—like dry seeds in a gourd, only sharper, more metallic. His whisper was almost reverent. “By Jove! I believe it’s full of diamonds.”

Sir Henry, impatient as a man with hunger in him, snatched the lamp from Gagool. “Go on,” he said shortly, and stepped through.

We followed, forgetting the bag for the moment, and found ourselves in King Solomon’s treasure chamber.

It was a small room, hewn from rock, perhaps ten feet square, the ceiling arched. At first the light showed only stone—dry, pale, close—then the chamber filled in like a picture in dawn. Along one side, stacked one upon another up to the arch, were elephant tusks—fine, creamy ends in ranks, hundreds of them. The air carried a faint, dusty sweetness from that old ivory, like dried bone warmed by sun and time. There was enough there to make a man rich without another ounce of gold.

Opposite the tusks were wooden boxes, painted red, like oversized ammunition cases. Their paint had dulled to a rusty skin.

“There are the diamonds,” I said. “Bring the light.”

Sir Henry held the lamp close to the top box. The lid, rotten with age, had been broken in—no doubt by Da Silvestra. I pushed my hand through the hole and drew it out full—not of diamonds, but of gold coins, oddly shaped, stamped with characters that looked to me like Hebrew.

“Ah,” I said, and my mouth tasted suddenly dry. “We shan’t go back empty-handed. There must be a couple of thousand in each box, and there are eighteen boxes. Wages for workmen, perhaps—money for merchants—Solomon’s payments locked away like grain in a sealed store.”

“Well,” said Good, eyeing the room, “I think that is the lot. I don’t see any diamonds, unless the old Portuguese put them all into his bag.”

Gagool’s head turned, and in the lamplight her face seemed carved from old leather. “Let my lords look yonder where it is darkest, if they would find the stones. There is a nook, and three stone chests in the nook, two sealed and one open.”

Before I translated this to Sir Henry I could not help asking her how she knew it all, if no one had entered here since the white man long ago.

“Ah, Macumazahn, the watcher by night,” she mocked, “ye who dwell in the stars—do ye not know that some live long, and that some have eyes which can see through rock? Ha! ha! ha!”

“Look in that corner, Curtis,” I said.

Sir Henry moved, and then stopped so abruptly that the lamp’s flame wavered. “Hullo, you fellows,” he cried. “Here’s a recess. Great heavens!”

We hurried to him. The nook was shaped like a shallow bay in the wall. Against it stood three stone chests, each about two feet square. Two had lids fitted; the third lay open, its lid resting against its side.

“See!” Sir Henry said again, hoarse now, holding the lamp over it.

For a second we could make nothing out because of the glare—an odd, cold sheen that struck the eyes. Then sight adjusted, and we saw the chest was three-parts full of uncut diamonds, big rough stones piled like pale lumps of sugar, like coarse salt crystals in a butcher’s tub, only with a deeper, inner fire. I stooped and took up a few. They had that peculiar “soapy” feel—like wet pebbles rubbed with fat—and for all their glitter they felt strangely common in the hand, like something you might toss into a stew-pot if you did not know better.

I dropped them with a gasp.

“We are the richest men in the whole world,” I said. “Monte Christo was a fool to us.”

“We shall flood the market with diamonds,” said Good, half-laughing, half-staring.

“Got to get them there first,” Sir Henry answered grimly.

We stood with pale faces, the lamp between us and the open chest below, and it struck me how we looked—like men gathered round a forbidden dish, conspirators not against law but against fate. Behind us Gagool cackled, flitting about in the shadows like a bat.

“There are the bright stones ye love, white men,” she hissed, “as many as ye will. Take them, run them through your fingers—eat of them—hee! hee! drink of them—ha! ha!”

The ridiculous image of swallowing diamonds—of trying to chew them like biltong, of choking on their hard glitter—caught my mind so violently that I began to laugh. Not a sane laugh either, but a wild burst that tore out of me. The others joined in without knowing why, as if the chamber itself had tickled some nerve of madness. Our laughter rang off the stone and came back thin and wrong, a mockery of any human joy—no birds, no wind, no living sound to soften it.

There we stood, shrieking with laughter over treasure that had waited centuries, hoarded by dead hands for dead kings. We—mere wanderers—had come at last, and the ivory and gold and gems lay before us as if a pantry door had been opened.

Then, just as suddenly, the fit passed. The air seemed to thicken again. The lamp hissed softly. The diamonds lay silent.

“Open the other chests,” croaked Gagool. “There are surely more. Take your fill, white lords. Ha! ha! take your fill.”

We set to work. We broke the seals—wax, faded, still clinging like old resin—and hauled at the heavy lids. The second chest was full to the brim as well; no frightened Da Silvestra had emptied that. The third chest was only a quarter full, but the stones were picked—none less than twenty carats, some as large as pigeon-eggs. Holding them up, we saw many were tinged yellow, “off-coloured” as they say at Kimberley.

What we did not see, then, was Gagool’s face as she slid away—her eyes fixed, her lips drawn back—creeping out of the chamber and down the passage towards the great rock door.

Then—sound.

Not our laughter. Not the drip. A cry, ringing up the stone throat, shocking as a sudden scream in a still forest.

It was Foulata’s voice.

“Oh, Bougwan! help! help! the stone falls!”

We ran. The lamp bounced in Sir Henry’s hand, throwing the passage into frantic streaks of light and dark. Another cry—closer—torn and raw:

“Help! help! she has stabbed me!”

We burst out to the place where we had left her.

The door of living rock was closing, sliding down with that same horrible smoothness. It was not three feet from the floor. Near it struggled Foulata and Gagool. Blood ran down Foulata’s leg to her knee, bright and shocking in the lamp’s yellow light, and the smell of it—hot iron—cut through the stale air. Yet the brave girl had both arms locked about the old witch, holding her back from the narrowing gap. Gagool fought like a wild cat, twisting and striking.

Foulata faltered. Gagool tore free.

The girl fell.

Gagool flung herself flat and began to wriggle like a snake beneath the dropping stone. For an instant she was half through—her skull and shoulders scraping the floor.

Then the door caught her.

She screamed—such a scream as I pray never to hear again—and the great weight came down, inch by inch, compressing her against the rock. The sound of it was not loud; it was worse: a drawn-out pressure, a wet, hideous crunch that made the stomach turn. Her shrieks rose and broke, and then, with a final sickening crack, the door shut.

We hurled ourselves against it. It did not move. The mountain might as well have been the sea.

It had all happened in four seconds.

We turned to Foulata.

She lay on the stone, her breath coming in little broken draughts. The lamp showed the wound—deep, fatal—and already her skin had begun to lose its warmth, as though the cavern were drawing it away.

“Ah, Bougwan, I die,” she gasped. “She crept out—Gagool—I did not see her, I was faint—and the door began to fall; then she came back and was looking up the path—I saw her come in through the slowly falling door, and caught her and held her, and she stabbed me, and I die, Bougwan!”

Good’s face crumpled as if his heart had been struck with a hammer. “Poor girl! poor girl!” he sobbed, and fell to kissing her, helpless as a child.

“Bougwan,” she whispered after a pause, “is Macumazahn there? It grows so dark, I cannot see.”

“Here I am, Foulata,” I said, kneeling.

“Be my tongue,” she breathed, “for Bougwan cannot understand me, and before I go into the darkness I would speak to him.”

“Say on,” I told her.

Her voice came in fragments, but the feeling in it was whole. “Say to my lord, Bougwan, that—I love him. And that I am glad to die because I know he cannot cumber his life with such as I am, for the sun may not mate with the darkness, nor the white with the black.

“Say that since I saw him, at times I have felt as though there were a bird in my bosom, which would one day fly hence and sing elsewhere. Even now—though I cannot lift my hand, and my brain grows cold—I do not feel as though my heart were dying; it is so full of love it could live ten thousand years and yet be young.

“Say that if I live again, mayhap I shall see him in the Stars, and that—I will search them all, though perchance there I should still be black and he would—still be white. Say—nay, Macumazahn, say no more, save that I love—Oh, hold me closer, Bougwan, I cannot feel thine arms—oh! oh!”

Her head slipped. The last breath went out of her like the last note of a song swallowed by distance.

“She is dead—she is dead,” Good muttered, rising, tears cutting bright tracks down his cheeks.

“You need not let that trouble you, old fellow,” Sir Henry said, and his voice had a strange harsh steadiness.

“Eh?” Good stared. “What do you mean?”

“I mean,” said Sir Henry, “that you will soon be in a position to join her. Man, don’t you see that we are buried alive?”

Until he spoke the words, the full horror had not entered me—grief had filled all the space in my mind. Now the truth slammed in like a door.

The rock had closed. The only mind that knew its secret—Gagool’s—was crushed to pulp beneath thirty tons of stone. There would be no lever for us, no hidden spring. The slab was five feet thick. You might as well try to open a cliff with your hands.

For some minutes we stood like stunned cattle in a slaughter-yard, with Foulata’s body cooling at our feet, and that unyielding rock before us. In the stillness I could hear our breathing—ragged, too loud—and the faint pulse in my own ears, as if the cave had become an immense shell pressed to my head. I understood then Gagool’s jeer about eating and drinking diamonds. She had meant us to die beside our glittering hoard, gnawing at stones while thirst chewed our throats.

“This will never do,” Sir Henry said hoarsely. “The lamp will soon go out. Let us see if we can’t find the spring that works the rock.”

We flung ourselves at the passage, frantic now, and felt along the stone—up, down, to either side—searching for any projection, any hollow, any secret catch. Our hands came away gritty and smeared with blood from where Foulata had lain. No knob. No spring. Nothing but smooth, unpitying rock.

“Depend on it,” I said at last, and my voice sounded far away to my own ears, “it does not work from the inside. If it did, Gagool would not have risked crawling under. It was the knowledge of that which drove her.”

Sir Henry gave a hard little laugh. “At all events, retribution was swift. Hers was almost as awful an end as ours is likely to be. We can do nothing with the door. Let us go back to the treasure room.”

We turned. The passage seemed longer now, as if despair had stretched it. As we passed the unfinished wall, I saw the basket of provisions where we had left it beside Foulata. I snatched it up—an instinct as old as hunger itself. The smell of dried meat rose from it, smoky and tough, and for a moment it was the only living scent in the whole dead place.

We went back to that accursed chamber that had promised richness and now offered only a slow end. Reverently we carried Foulata’s body in and laid her upon the floor beside the boxes of coin, where the gold lay packed as neatly as grain in a storehouse. Her dark skin in the lamplight looked like polished wood, and her stillness made all our frantic movement seem obscene.

We sat down with our backs against the three stone chests of diamonds—our treasure, our tomb.

“Let us divide the food,” said Sir Henry, “so as to make it last as long as possible.”

We did it carefully, like men portioning out flour in a famine. There was biltong—dry strips of game flesh, hard as leather, smelling of smoke and salt—there were two gourds of water, each not more than a quart. We reckoned it out: four infinitesimal meals apiece, enough perhaps to keep the body standing for two days.

“Now,” said Sir Henry grimly, “let us eat and drink, for to-morrow we die.”

We chewed the biltong. It drew saliva and then seemed to steal it, leaving the mouth drier than before. The taste was familiar—salt, smoke, the wildness of the veld clinging to it—and it struck me with a sharp, bitter irony that this last comfort should be something so plain and homely, while diamonds lay in heaps at our heels. We each took a sip of water, precious as a sacrament, and the coolness on my tongue was almost painful.

Then we rose and examined the chamber systematically, as if method could argue with fate. We sounded the walls, the floor, listening for any hollowness. The rock gave back only dull, dead answers. A treasure chamber is not built with back doors.

The lamp began to burn low. The smell of its dying oil thickened. Shadows pooled deeper in the corners, and the room’s silence grew heavier—not even the drip reached us clearly here, only a distant, intermittent whisper as though the mountain were breathing in sleep.

“Quatermain,” Sir Henry said, “what is the time—your watch goes?”

I drew it out and looked. Six o’clock. We had entered the cave at eleven.

“Infadoos will miss us,” I said, clinging to the thought as a man clings to a twig in flood. “If we do not return to-night, he will search for us in the morning.”

“He may search in vain,” Sir Henry answered. “He does not know the secret of the door, nor even where it is. No living person knew it yesterday, except Gagool. To-day no one knows it. Even if he found the door, he could not break it down. All the Kukuana army could not break through five feet of living rock. My friends, I see nothing for it but to bow ourselves to the will of the Almighty. The search for treasure has brought many to a bad end; we shall go to swell their number.”

The lamp dimmed further, then suddenly flared as if with a last stubborn pride, throwing the chamber into sharp relief. I saw everything at once: the ivory tusks stacked like a giant’s bones; the red-painted boxes; the goat-skin bag that jingled with stolen centuries; the diamonds glimmering with their cold, tasteless light; Foulata’s body laid out quiet among riches that could not buy her one more breath; and the three of us—wan-faced men—sitting against stone chests as against gravestones, waiting for thirst to do its work.

Then the flame sank, shivered, and went out.

And the dark, at last, was complete.
