“The hollow oak our palace is,
Our heritage the sea.”

If you have ever gone down into the sweet folds of North Devon, you will remember Bideford as a white-clustered town set upon a broad tide-river, its sands yellow as sifted meal when the water draws off, its old bridge arched again and again like a ribbed spine across the Torridge. In autumn, salmon waited under those arches for the first proud spates, and above them the town climbed the slope toward the west, where the uplands opened—red fields and green squares stitched together with hedgerows, and beyond them the salt-marshes and hazy flats where Torridge takes Taw by the hand and both go quietly out toward the bar. Always, behind all quietness, there was the other sound: the long Atlantic swell, like distant thunder that never quite ceased. Day and night the sea-wind moved through the streets—clean, briny, and cool—tempering frost in winter and the heavy inland heats of summer, and carrying with it the cries of gulls, the creak of cordage, the soft slap of oars, and the slow breathing of ships against the quay. In that mingled music, men grew up with their nerves tuned to tide and weather; and if the world had a harsh note in it, it was the inland clang, not this salt and measured chorus.

Bideford had stood there a long while—how long no man could swear to, save that it was old enough to have gathered into itself, generation after generation, mixed blood and mixed talk: Saxon and Norse, dark Briton from over the water, and all the stubborn seaward folk who keep their own ways and their own words, even while the court and the city coin new fashions. In those days, too, Bideford was not merely a pleasant country town with a few coasters nodding at the quay. It was a port of weight, a place that had sent ships against the Armada and later would send more northward than any port but London and Topsham. A small thing, some might say, beside the roaring, moneyed greatness that would come after; yet small things are often the first bright ring in a tree’s trunk, and the sap that makes later timber possible.

For from such western towns—Bideford, Dartmouth, Topsham, Plymouth when it was still petty—came the hard breed of seamen and gentlemen-adventurers who made England’s sea-story not in painted proclamations but in salt sweat, blood, and daring. They were men whose names still lay warm on west-country tongues: Drake and Hawkins, Gilbert and Raleigh, Grenville and Oxenham, and many more whom ink forgets though the sea remembers. And if the telling of their deeds now and then rises into a note too loud for a mere tale, blame the subject; there are lives that were meant to be sung.

On a bright summer afternoon in the year of grace 1575, a tall fair boy lingered along Bideford quay in his scholar’s gown, a satchel and slate in hand. He walked slowly, as boys will when the heart is elsewhere, letting his eyes drink in the shipping and the sailors. The river-smell was in his nostrils—wet rope, tar, fish-guts from the cleaning boards, oak timbers warmed by sun, and the sharp lift of cider from open doors. There was a clatter of barrels rolled across planks, the ring of an iron hook against a bollard, and above it all the steady hush-and-begin-again of the distant surf, a voice that could make a lad’s blood itch like nettles.

Just past the bottom of the High Street he came opposite one of the taverns that looked out upon the water. In the open bay-window sat merchants and gentlemen over their afternoon draught of sack; and outside, in the shade of the door, a knot of sailors had gathered, listening hard to a man who stood in the midst. The boy, alive as tinder to any sea-news, edged in among the sailor-lads who peeped and whispered under men’s elbows, and so caught the heart of a tale flung out in a loud, bold voice, thick with Devonshire and salted with oaths.

“If you don’t believe me, go and see, or stay here and grow all over blue mould. I tell you, as I am a gentleman, I saw it with these eyes—and so did Salvation Yeo there, through a window in the lower room; and we measured the heap, as I am a christened man—seventy foot long, ten foot broad, and twelve foot high, of silver bars, and each bar between a thirty and forty pound weight. And says Captain Drake: ‘There, my lads of Devon, I’ve brought you to the mouth of the world’s treasure-house, and it’s your own fault now if you don’t sweep it out as empty as a stock-fish.’”

“Why didn’t you bring some of they home, then, Mr. Oxenham?”

“Why weren’t you there to help to carry them? We would have brought ’em away, safe enough, and young Drake and I had broke the door abroad already, but Captain Drake goes off in a dead faint; and when we came to look, he had a wound in his leg you might have laid three fingers in, and his boots were full of blood, and had been for an hour or more; but the heart of him was that, that he never knew it till he dropped—and then his brother and I got him away to the boats, he kicking and struggling, and bidding us let him go on with the fight, though every step he took in the sand was in a pool of blood; and so we got off. And tell me, ye sons of shotten herrings, wasn’t it worth more to save him than the dirty silver? for silver we can get again, brave boys: there’s more fish in the sea than ever came out of it, and more silver in Nombre de Dios than would pave all the streets in the west country: but of such captains as Franky Drake, Heaven never makes but one at a time; and if we lose him, good-bye to England’s luck, say I; and who don’t agree, let him choose his weapons, and I’m his man.”

The speaker was a tall, sturdy man with a florid face half-hidden by a black beard, and dark eyes that never rested. He leaned against the tavern wall, legs crossed, arms akimbo, as if the whole street were his quarter-deck. To the boy he seemed not a man only but a story come to life—a magnifico, a prince of fortune. He wore, in defiance of the sumptuary laws, a suit of crimson velvet, somewhat worn at seam and elbow as though it had been tried against weather and rope. At his side hung a long Spanish rapier and a brace of daggers with gaudy hilts; rings flashed on his fingers; gold chains lay heavy at his neck; great earrings swung in his ears; and behind one of them a red rose was stuck jauntily among glossy black curls. On his head was a broad velvet Spanish hat, and in it, clasped not with a mere feather but with a great gold fastener, was set a whole Quezal bird—its fretted golden-green plumage shining like one solid jewel when the sun caught it. As he ended, he took off the hat and looked at the bird as if it were a banner.

“Look ye, my lads—did you ever see such a fowl as that before? That’s the bird which the old Indian kings of Mexico let no one wear but their own selves; and therefore I wear it,—I, John Oxenham of South Tawton, for a sign to all brave lads of Devon, that as the Spaniards are the masters of the Indians, we’re the masters of the Spaniards.”

A murmur of applause went round, broken by a doubtful voice that hinted the Spaniards were too many for them.

“Too many? How many men did we take Nombre de Dios with? Seventy-three were we, and no more when we sailed out of Plymouth Sound; and before we saw the Spanish Main, half were gastados, used up, as the Dons say, with the scurvy; and in Port Pheasant Captain Rawse of Cowes fell in with us, and that gave us some thirty hands more; and with that handful, my lads—only fifty-three in all—we picked the lock of the new world! And whom did we lose but our trumpeter, who stood braying like an ass in the middle of the square, instead of taking care of his neck like a Christian? I tell you, those Spaniards are rank cowards, as all bullies are. They pray to a woman, the idolatrous rascals! and no wonder they fight like women.”

“You’m right, captain,” sang out a tall gaunt fellow close by, “one westcountry-man can fight two easterlings, and an easterling can beat three Dons any day. Eh! my lads of Devon?

“For O! it’s the herrings and the good brown beef,
And the cider and the cream so white;
O! they are the making of the jolly Devon lads,
For to play, and eke to fight.”

The words dropped into the air like the smell from a kitchen window: herrings browned, beef salted and roasted, cider sharp as green apples, cream thick as peace. Around them the tavern’s own breath rose—hot sack, yeast, smoke, and spilled ale drying on boards. The boy heard it all as one thing: song, salt air, and hunger; the world’s far edges seemed to press close to the quay, as if you might step from Bideford straight into the Spanish Main.

“Come,” cried Oxenham. “Come along! Who lists? who lists? who’ll make his fortune?

“Oh, who will join, jolly mariners all?
And who will join, says he, O!
To fill his pockets with the good red goold,
By sailing on the sea, O!”

“Who’ll list?” shouted the gaunt man again. “Now’s your time! We’ve got forty men to Plymouth now, ready to sail the minute we get back, and we want a dozen out of you Bideford men, and just a boy or two, and then we’m off and away, and make our fortunes, or go to heaven.

“Our bodies in the sea so deep,
Our souls in heaven to rest!
Where valiant seamen, one and all,
Hereafter shall be blest!”

“Now,” said Oxenham, turning so the Quezal’s green-gold flashed, “you won’t let the Plymouth men say that the Bideford men daren’t follow them? North Devon against South, it is. Who’ll join? who’ll join? It is but a step of a way, after all, and sailing as smooth as a duck-pond as soon as you’re past Cape Finisterre. I’ll run a Clovelly herring-boat there and back for a wager of twenty pound, and never ship a bucketful all the way. Who’ll join? Don’t think you’re buying a pig in a poke. I know the road, and Salvation Yeo here, too—who was the gunner’s mate—as well as I do the narrow seas, and better. You ask him to show you the chart of it, now, and see if he don’t tell you over the ruttier as well as Drake himself.”

At that the tall gaunt fellow—Salvation Yeo—pulled from under his arm a great white buffalo horn, rough-etched all over with land and sea. He held it up, and the ring of men leaned in as one, drawn as if by scent.

“See here, boys all, and behold the pictur of the place, dra’ed out so natural as ever was life. I got mun from a Portingal, down to the Azores; and he’d pricked mun out, and pricked mun out, wheresoever he’d sailed, and whatsoever he’d seen. Take mun in your hands now, Simon Evans, take mun in your hands; look mun over, and I’ll warrant you’ll know the way in five minutes so well as ever a shark in the seas.”

The horn went from hand to hand. Oxenham, seeing eyes kindle and throats bob with desire, called through the open window for a great tankard of sack; and that went round too, warm and sweet, with a bite like spiced fruit. The boy, having edged himself into the inner ring, stood at last face to face with the man of the emerald crest and stole as many peeps as he could at the wonder. He watched the sailors, one after another, come forward after handling the horn and offer to join Oxenham. The horn seemed to work like a charm; not a few had their courage coaxed up as easily as hunger is stirred by the crackle of bacon on a pan.

When the group broke a little and Oxenham began to swagger into the tavern with his recruits, the boy’s longing overcame his shyness. He asked boldly for a nearer sight of the horn. It was granted at once, as if men who have seen strange coasts cannot deny a boy’s yearning.

Now the horn lay in his hands, cool and smooth in places where palms had worn it, and under his astonished gaze unrolled a whole world. There were cities and harbors, dragons and elephants, whales fighting sharks, plate-ships of Spain, islands with apes and palm-trees, each with its name written above, and here and there the plain promise: “Here is gold;” and again, “Much gold and silver;”—English words, no doubt, added by Oxenham’s own hand where greed most needed pointing. The boy turned it round and round, lingering as if the very turning were a voyage. He thought the owner richer than Khan or Kaiser. If he could but possess that horn—if he could keep that world in his hands—what else on earth would he need?

“I say, will you sell this?”

“Yea, marry, or my own soul, if I can get the worth of it.”

“I want the horn,—I don’t want your soul; it’s somewhat of a stale sole, for aught I know; and there are plenty of fresh ones in the bay.”

Then, after much fumbling, the boy pulled out a tester—the only one he had—and asked if that would buy it.

“That! no, nor twenty of them.”

The boy, thinking what a good knight-errant would do when treasure was refused him, lifted his chin and said, “Tell you what: I’ll fight you for it.”

“Thank ’ee, sir! Break the jackanapes’s head for him, Yeo,” said Oxenham, laughing.

“Call me jackanapes again, and I break yours, sir.” The boy’s fist came up quick and hard, his eyes fierce with the sudden dignity of youth.

Oxenham looked at him a moment, smiling as at a plucky dog. “Tut! tut! my man, hit one of your own size, if you will, and spare little folk like me!”

“If I have a boy’s age, sir, I have a man’s fist. I shall be fifteen years old this month, and know how to answer any one who insults me.”

“Fifteen, my young cockerel? you look liker twenty,” said Oxenham, and his glance ran admiringly over the lad’s broad limbs, keen blue eyes, curling golden locks, and round honest face. “Fifteen? If I had half-a-dozen such lads as you, I would make knights of them before I died. Eh, Yeo?”

“He’ll do,” said Yeo. “He will make a brave gamecock in a year or two, if he dares ruffle up so early at a tough old hen-master like the captain.”

There was a general laugh, Oxenham’s loudest among them; and then he bade the lad tell him why he was so keen after the horn.

“Because,” said he, looking up boldly, “I want to go to sea. I want to see the Indies. I want to fight the Spaniards. Though I am a gentleman’s son, I’d a deal liever be a cabin-boy on board your ship.” And having flung out his secret as if it were a weapon, he dropped his head again, ashamed and yet relieved, as one is after speaking aloud in a silent church.

“And you shall,” cried Oxenham with a great oath; “and take a galloon, and dine off carbonadoed Dons. Whose son are you, my gallant fellow?”

“Mr. Leigh’s, of Burrough Court.”

“Bless his soul! I know him as well as I do the Eddystone, and his kitchen too. Who sups with him to-night?”

“Sir Richard Grenville.”

“Dick Grenville? I did not know he was in town. Go home and tell your father John Oxenham will come and keep him company. There, off with you! I’ll make all straight with the good gentleman, and you shall have your venture with me; and as for the horn, let him have the horn, Yeo, and I’ll give you a noble for it.”

“Not a penny, noble captain. If young master will take a poor mariner’s gift, there it is, for the sake of his love to the calling, and Heaven send him luck therein.” And with the sudden, tidal generosity of true sailors, Yeo thrust the horn into the boy’s hands and walked away briskly, as if to outrun thanks before it could catch him.

“And now,” quoth Oxenham, turning back to the men with that air of command which comes as naturally to some as breath, “my merry men all, make up your minds what mannered men you be minded to be before you take your bounties. I want none of your rascally lurching longshore vermin, who get five pounds out of this captain, and ten out of that, and let him sail without them after all, while they are stowed away under women’s mufflers, and in tavern cellars. If any man is of that humor, he had better to cut himself up, and salt himself down in a barrel for pork, before he meets me again; for by this light, let me catch him, be it seven years hence, and if I do not cut his throat upon the streets, it’s a pity! But if any man will be true brother to me, true brother to him I’ll be, come wreck or prize, storm or calm, salt water or fresh, victuals or none, share and fare alike; and here’s my hand upon it, for every man and all! and so—

“Westward ho! with a rumbelow,
And hurra for the Spanish Main, O!”

After which oration Mr. Oxenham swaggered into the tavern, followed by his new men; and the boy took his way home up the hill, hugging his precious horn to his ribs as if it were warm-baked bread. The town sounds changed as he climbed: the quay’s cries and rope-creaks fell behind, and the steady Atlantic thunder came forward again, laid under everything like a drum kept far off. He trembled between hope and fear, blushing with a maidenly shame, and with that half-sense of wrong-doing which comes when a child has let slip, to a stranger, the darling wish he has hidden from those he loves.

This young gentleman was Amyas Leigh; and though he came of as good blood as any in Devon, and had lived all his life in what folk would call the very best society, he was not—saving for his good looks—what would now be named an “interesting” youth, still less a “highly educated” one. A little Latin had been driven into him by repeated blows as if it were a nail. Of books he knew none, save his Bible, his Prayer-book, the old “Morte d’Arthur” of Caxton that lay in the great bay-window in the hall, and the new English of Las Casas’ “History of the West Indies,” called “The Cruelties of the Spaniards,” lying beside it—words that burned on the tongue even when read aloud softly. He believed devoutly in fairies—pixies he called them—who changed babies and danced the mushroom rings on the downs. For warts and burns he went to the white witch at Northam for charms. He thought the sun moved round the earth, and that the moon had some kinship with a Cheshire cheese. He held that swallows slept all winter at the bottom of the horse-pond. He spoke, like Raleigh and Grenville and other “low persons,” in a broad Devonshire accent, and would have found it hard work to translate his own simplest home-sayings into courtly speech without losing their taste. There are words that carry a whole countryside’s weather in them; take them away, and you have the meaning perhaps, but not the life.

Yet, ignorant as any pert monitor of later days might call him, Amyas had been taught certain things thoroughly—after the old Persian fashion: to speak the truth and to draw the bow. He could do both. He could endure pain cheerfully. He believed it the finest thing in the world to be a gentleman; and by “gentleman” he understood not finery or idle pride, but the careful habit of causing needless pain to no human being, poor or rich, and of taking pride in giving up his own pleasure for the sake of those weaker than himself. He had been trusted for the last year with breaking a colt and with the care of a cast of young hawks his father had received from Lundy Isle; and through those rough, country tasks—coarse as they might seem to fine folk—he had learned patience, thoughtfulness, and the art of holding his temper as a cook holds a fire: not too fierce, not too slack, but steady under the hand. He had never had an “object lesson,” nor been taught to “use his intellectual powers,” but he knew the names and ways of every bird and fish and fly, and could read cloud-drift like an old sailor reads a chart, taking warning from a change in wind as from a change in a man’s voice.

He was, moreover, because of his extraordinary size and strength, the undisputed cock of the school and the most terrible fighter among all Bideford boys. In that brutal habit he took much delight; yet, strange as it may seem, he contrived to draw good out of it too, doing justice with a heavy hand among his fellows, succoring the oppressed, and hardly thinking he had done his day’s duty if he went home without beating a big lad for bullying a little one. The sailor-lads feared him; the town’s boys and girls, by a natural law, leaned on him and boasted of him.

For the rest, he never thought about thinking, or felt about feeling. His ambition was simple: to please his father and mother; to get, by honest means, the maximum of red quarrenders and mazard cherries when the season came; and—when he was big enough—to go to sea. He was not what many would now call a pious child; but he said his Creed and Lord’s Prayer night and morning, went to service every forenoon, read the day’s Psalms with his mother every evening, and had learned from both father and mother—shown later by his life—that it was infinitely noble to do right and infinitely base to do wrong. Beyond the Church Catechism he knew little theology; but he had, in his very bones, a plain, honest religion, like clean water drawn from a well—without much talk about it, and all the better for that.

So watch him now up the hill, the great horn hugged close, its scraped pictures hidden under his arm as if they might fly away. He was going home to tell his mother all that had passed—his mother, from whom he had never hidden anything in his life save only that sea-fever; and that only because he knew it would pain her, and because, prudent and sensible lad that he was, he had said to her that very afternoon, “there was no use hollaing till he was out of the wood.”
