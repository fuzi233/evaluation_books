As Christmas descended upon Castlewood in 1759, the atmosphere was alive with tension. News of George's engagement to the colonial heiress, Lydia, had sent a ripple of discontent through the family. His cousin, Will, was particularly incensed, vowing to exact revenge for what he saw as a betrayal. However, his outbursts were met with skepticism by those who knew him well. Will's temper was notorious, and few took his threats seriously. After a day or two, he returned to Castlewood, drawn by the allure of the marriage feast and the prospect of claiming his seat at the table. As he sat amidst the festivities, he raised a glass in a silent toast to his brother's bride, his eyes flashing with resentment.

Others shared Will's discontent, and the air was thick with whispered criticism of the union. Mr. Warrington, who had known the family for years, listened to their grievances with a mix of amusement and concern. He pointed out to Will that if Lydia was indeed as cunning as he claimed, it was fortunate for George that he had escaped her clutches. Will retorted that his brother's gain was no justification for his own loss, which he had risked his life to achieve. George, perplexed by Will's behavior, pressed him to explain why he had put everything at stake for a prize that now seemed so elusive.

Will revealed that Lydia had actually brought about their initial meeting, which had been interrupted by Sir John Fielding's men. He confided that she had often spoken of George as a coward for mocking Mr. Wilkes, and that her words had stoked his competitive fire. As the evening wore on, the conversation turned to the subject of Lydia's supposed deceit, with various members of the family sharing their own stories of being charmed and then disappointed by her. The Dissenting pastor lamented his own failed attempts to win her heart, while Jack Lambert, a young divine, ruefully acknowledged the lock of black hair he still treasured as a keepsake.
