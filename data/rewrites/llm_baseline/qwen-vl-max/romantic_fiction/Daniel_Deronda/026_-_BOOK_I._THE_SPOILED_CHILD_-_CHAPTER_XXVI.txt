He brings white asses laden with the freight
Of Tyrian vessels, purple, gold and balm,
To bribe my will: I’ll bid them chase him forth,
Nor let him breathe the taint of his surmise
On my secure resolve. Ay, ’tis secure:
And therefore let him come to spread his freight.
For firmness hath its appetite and craves
The stronger lure, more strongly to resist;
Would know the touch of gold to fling it off;
Scent wine to feel its lip the soberer;
Behold soft byssus, ivory, and plumes
To say, “They’re fair, but I will none of them,”
And flout Enticement in the very face.

Mr. Gascoigne arrived at Offendene one day bearing news he believed would bring some comfort—Mrs. Mompert had set Tuesday of the coming week for her meeting with Gwendolen at Wanchester. He said nothing of having overheard that Mr. Grandcourt had returned to Diplow, unaware that Leubronn had been the destination of his niece’s admirer. He thought it kinder not to stir up memories of a future once bright and now dimmed. In truth, he regretted Gwendolen’s sudden refusal of Grandcourt, yet justified her in his mind by recalling how oddly Grandcourt himself had behaved—vanishing abruptly when the moment seemed ripe for declaration. The rector’s practical nature told him that his duty now was to help Gwendolen meet her new circumstances with courage, for no sign suggested a reversal of fate.

“You will find meaning in change, my dear,” he said gently. “I have no doubt you’ll grow into a stronger woman through this.”

“I cannot pretend to like it,” Gwendolen replied, a flicker of impatience in her voice. “But I understand I must endure it.” She recalled another time when she’d accepted his counsel about a prospect she’d never truly desired.

“And your good sense will guide your behavior,” he added, his tone a little graver. “Mrs. Mompert will surely approve of you. You’ll know how to act before someone who holds such authority over you in every way. This trial comes early, yes—but that makes it easier in some respects. And all suffering has value if we learn to accept it.”

That was precisely what Gwendolen could not do. After her uncle left, tears—rarely shed during this ordeal—rose slowly, then fell, as she sat alone. Her heart refused the idea that youth made hardship easier. When would joy come, if not now? Not that her dreams were untouched by sorrow anymore—not that she still imagined life’s pleasures without knowing their cost. But this growing disillusionment—with the world, even with herself—only deepened her loneliness. It felt as though a barren plain stretched ahead, empty and uninviting, and she had no strength to walk it.

She stood at the edge of that first fierce rebellion of youth—the rage against a world that denies joy rather than inflicting pain. We who’ve grown older often forget this storm, remembering only the calm after, and so judge it harshly as childish, irrational, even blasphemous. What seems absurd, once we stand outside it, is the furious grief at being the one chosen for sorrow—why not someone else? Yet perhaps those who later shielded others, who bore their own wounds in silence, who achieved greatness—perhaps they too began here: stunned by their own hurt, raging at the universe for denying their dreams, as if stung by invisible wasps that turned the whole world into an unjust wound.

This was nearly Gwendolen’s state. True, many girls had faced reversals like hers. But what mattered was that it was *hers*. It was what she felt under Klesmer’s cold assessment that she lacked the power to command fortune by will or merit. It was what she would feel beneath Mrs. Mompert’s constant expectations, under the dull demand to be cheerful with three Miss Momperts, under the need to hide her thoughts, to submit completely. To lose a crown is painful—but worse, perhaps, is losing the belief in one’s own divinity. Imagine a man raised to think himself godlike, suddenly stripped of all worship, unable to perform the miracle that would restore faith. Something like that had happened to Gwendolen—the spoiled child with the radiant eyes and noble form, now seeming powerless, drained of magic.

She rose from the low ottoman where she’d sat aimlessly, pacing the drawing-room, resting her elbow on one hand while her cheek leaned on the other. A slow tear slipped down.

“I’ve always known,” she thought, “since I was small, that mamma wasn’t happy. And now—I may be even unhappier than she was.”

Her mind lingered on the image of herself growing old, losing her beauty, no longer caring what she did. But that vision inevitably brought back her mother. “Poor mamma—it will be worse for her now.” She could earn a little money for her—that was all she cared about now. Then, with a sudden shift of imagination, she saw both of them aged: her mother white-haired, herself faded, their faces meeting across years, filled with memory and love. And she understood what her mother might think: *“Poor Gwen too is sad and faded now.”* For the first time, she sobbed—not in anger, but with quiet sorrow.

She turned toward the door just as her mother entered. She barely registered it—her eyes were wide with tears, and she pressed her handkerchief hard against them. Before she removed it, she felt her mother’s arms around her. That touch, echoing her inner vision, broke her resolve to stay silent. She sobbed again, helplessly, as their cheeks met.

Mrs. Davilow had come with something that already troubled her deeply. She dared not speak until her daughter calmed. But Gwendolen, for whom tears were always a struggle, pressed her handkerchief to her eyes once more, took a deep breath, and drew back her head to look at her mother—pale and trembling.

“It was nothing, mamma,” she said softly. “It’s all over now.”

But Mrs. Davilow had stepped back, and Gwendolen saw a letter in her hand.

“What is that?” she asked sharply. “More bad news?”

“I don’t know what you’ll think of it, dear,” Mrs. Davilow said, holding the letter tightly. “You’ll hardly guess where it comes from.”

“Don’t ask me to guess,” Gwendolen replied, irritated—like a bruise being pressed.

“It’s addressed to you,” her mother whispered.

Gwendolen gave the faintest shake of her head.

“It’s from Diplow,” Mrs. Davilow said, handing it over.

She recognized Grandcourt’s handwriting at once—and flushed deeply. Her mother watched her read, wondering what the message held. Then the color drained from Gwendolen’s face. Even her lips turned pale as she turned the open note toward her mother.

The words were few and formal:

> Mr. Grandcourt presents his compliments to Miss Harleth, and begs to know whether he may be permitted to call at Offendene tomorrow after two o’clock to see her alone. Mr. Grandcourt has just returned from Leubronn, where he had hoped to find Miss Harleth.

Mrs. Davilow read it, then looked at her daughter questioningly, leaving the note in her hand.

Gwendolen dropped it to the floor and turned away.

“It must be answered, darling,” Mrs. Davilow said timidly. “The man waits.”

Gwendolen sank onto the settee, clasped her hands, and stared straight ahead—not at her mother. Her expression was that of one startled by a sound, listening for what would follow. The world had shifted in minutes. Just moments ago, she had seen only a path of endless monotony, her spirit rebelling against a fate that offered no escape. Now—choice had returned.

Was it triumph she felt—or terror? Impossible not to feel some triumph, at being valued when she tasted the bitterness of insignificance. Again, she felt a fleeting sense of power over her own life. But how to use it? That was the fear.

Like pages turning rapidly in a book, fragments of memory flooded back—Grandcourt’s allurements, her vacillations, her near surrender, the final rejection; the sharp face of the dark-eyed woman with the beautiful boy; her own promise—was it a vow not to marry him?—and the new disbelief in men, in love, in everything, that scene having become a symbol. That unchangeable past formed a vision that, in the first wild moment before reason could temper it, made her instinctive fear recoil.

What good was choice now? What did she want? Anything different? No. And yet, deep within the shadows of her mind, a new wish stirred: *I wish I had never known him.*

Anything—anything—that would spare her the dread of letting Grandcourt come.

It was not long—yet to Mrs. Davilow, it felt endless—before she ventured, softly, “You’ll need to write, dear. Or shall I write for you? You can dictate.”

“No, mamma,” Gwendolen said, taking a deep breath. “But please lay out the pen and paper.”

It was a way to buy time. Should she refuse him? Close the door? Refuse even to look? Though she knew she would remain exactly where she was. Yet the young fire within her stirred—warm current through her fear—yearning for action, for an event, for a chance to speak and look with the force she once had. Tomorrow was no longer frozen.

“There’s really no reason to be so alarmed,” Gwendolen said, remonstrating gently, as her mother prepared the writing materials and waited. “Servants expect to wait. It’s not as if I must reply instantly.”

“No, dear,” Mrs. Davilow said, her tone softened by correction, turning to pick up her embroidery. “He can wait another quarter-hour, if you like.”

It was simple—almost unconscious—but it was a subtle move. Gwendolen felt a conflicting urge to hurry: haste would save her from deliberate decision.

“I didn’t mean him to wait long enough for that needlework to finish,” she said, lifting her hands to smooth her hair, rising and standing still.

“But if you don’t feel able to decide?” Mrs. Davilow asked sympathetically.

“I *must* decide,” Gwendolen said, walking to the writing table and sitting.

All the while, a restless current ran beneath her thoughts—like a man keeping up conversation while planning his escape. Why not let him come? It bound her to nothing. He’d gone to Leubronn after her—he meant to renew his suit plainly now. So what? She could reject him. Why deny herself the freedom to do so?

“If Mr. Grandcourt has just returned from Leubronn,” Mrs. Davilow observed, noticing Gwendolen lean back in her chair after picking up the pen—“I wonder if he knows about our troubles?”

“That wouldn’t matter to a man like him,” Gwendolen said, slightly scornful.

“It would to some,” Mrs. Davilow replied. “Many men wouldn’t want a wife whose family is nearly beggarly. Here we are at Offendene, safe in our shell. But imagine him finding us at Sawyer’s Cottage. Most men fear being bored or burdened by their wives’ families. If Grandcourt does know… I think it proves his attachment.”

She spoke with unusual emphasis. It was the first time she’d mentioned Grandcourt in a way that hinted at approval—her usual belief being that such talk would only make matters worse.

But her words struck deeper than she knew. They opened new possibilities—a vision of what Grandcourt might do for her mother, if only Gwendolen chose to—what she *would not* do.

Overwhelmed by a rush of ideas, she felt the urgency of the moment: the letter must be written now, or it might never be.

In the end, she acted quickly—just as she’d wished. To act fast was to avoid full decision, to leave doors open.

She wrote:

> Miss Harleth presents her compliments to Mr. Grandcourt. She will be at home after two o’clock tomorrow.

Before sealing it, she said, “Please ring the bell, mamma, if anyone needs to answer.” She didn’t know who managed the household.

Only after the letter was taken away, and Gwendolen had risen again, stretching one arm, then resting her hand on her head with a low moan that sounded like relief, did Mrs. Davilow dare ask, “What did you say, Gwen?”

“I said I’d be at home,” Gwendolen replied, with quiet dignity.

Then, after a pause, “Don’t expect anything to happen just because Mr. Grandcourt is coming, mamma.”

“I don’t expect anything, dear,” Mrs. Davilow said. “I only want you to follow your own feeling. You’ve never told me what that is.”

“What’s the use?” Gwendolen said, hearing reproach in the truth. “When I have something pleasant to tell, you’ll hear it.”

“But Mr. Grandcourt will think you’ve accepted him,” Mrs. Davilow said. “His note makes clear he’s coming to propose.”

“Very well,” Gwendolen answered. “Then I’ll have the pleasure of refusing him.”

Mrs. Davilow looked up, astonished. But Gwendolen turned away, saying, “Put down that hateful needlework. Let’s walk in the avenue. I’m suffocating.”
