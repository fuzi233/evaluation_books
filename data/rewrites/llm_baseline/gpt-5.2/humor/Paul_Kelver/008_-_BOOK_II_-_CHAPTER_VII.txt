Better is little, than treasure and trouble therewith. The proverb had always sounded to me like something written by a man who had never had to decide whether to buy dripping or treacle. Yet now, when money came in regularly and the table grew ambitious, the old words turned spitefully wise.

Not only on Sundays, but on weekdays too, the joint arrived—steaming, glossy, and self-satisfied—followed by pudding, and then by another pudding, until pudding, that former festival in a basin, became as common as the salt. The air of the room was thick with good gravy and suet-sweetness; knives no longer needed to scrape the last atom from a plate as if it were a last shilling. We ate without the old arithmetic of leftovers: no planning the morrow’s hash, no covert saving of crusts for Saturday’s bread pudding, no solemn cutting of bread instead of breaking it because breakage meant waste.

And yet never now were we merry.

Something sat down with us—an unseen guest who ate nothing, drank nothing, and still managed to empty the whole room. The clatter of plates sounded too loud, then too small; conversation became a careful stirring of thin, tasteless matters, as if any real word might boil over. Even the domestic sounds seemed altered: the kettle’s song thinner, the street’s grind harsher, as though the house had lost the right to its own quiet.

It would have been easy enough to move to Guilford Street. Sometimes, in those spiritless tones in which they now spoke on every subject save the one, my mother and father would mention it—numbers, rent, “a little nearer”—and then some loosened thought would fall into the talk like grit into flour, and all at once nothing would rise. So the months limped by, and the cloud grew accustomed to our ceiling.

Then one day news came that old Teidelmann had died suddenly in his counting-house.

“You are going to her?” said my mother.

“I have been sent for,” said my father. “I must—it may mean business.”

My mother laughed—a short, bitter sound, like a spoon dropped on stone—and my father flung out of the house. During the hours he was away she locked herself into her room. Once or twice I crept to the door and heard her crying, the muffled sobs as steady as a clock. That she should grieve for old Teidelmann puzzled me; I had seen him only as an ugly little man with thick lips and a habit of looking through people as if they were panes of glass.

After that, she came oftener to our house.

Her mourning—so I thought then—made her more beautiful. Black suited her; it simplified her, as a dark sauce makes a dish look costly. It softened, or seemed to soften, the hardness of her eyes. Always she was very sweet to my mother, who beside her appeared pale and plain and stiff, like yesterday’s bread laid next to a frosted cake. To me—whatever her motive—she was kindness itself. She seldom arrived without some little gift: a book that smelled of good paper and travel, a toy chosen with uncanny accuracy, a plan for a treat that hit precisely the place my own childish wanting lived.

By instinct she understood what I desired—the stories that set my mind alight, the pleasures that actually pleased me, not the pleasures that ought to have pleased me. My mother’s plans for my future, spoken with love and certainty, chilled me with their endless walls of “Must” and “Must not,” a narrow lane with no sky. This soft-voiced lady made life seem like sunny fields and winding paths where one might follow Will and still arrive safely. Something in me went out to her—drawn by her sympathy, her swift comprehension of what I had not yet learned to say. And yet, under it, there was always a small fear, as of a knife too sharp to be played with.

One morning she looked at me with that whimsical screw of her mouth.

“Has he ever seen a pantomime?” she asked my father.

My heart leaped as if someone had lifted a lid in my chest. My father raised his eyebrows.

“What would your mother say, do you think?” he asked.

My heart sank again, heavy as dumpling dough.

“She thinks,” I replied, “that theatres are very wicked places.” It was the first time a doubt—quick as a pinprick—had entered my mind about my mother’s judgments.

Mrs. Teidelmann’s smile deepened it.

“Dear me,” she said, “then I am afraid I must be very wicked. I have always regarded a pantomime as quite a moral entertainment. All the bad people go down so very straight to—well, to the fit and proper place for them. And we could promise to leave before the Clown stole the sausages, couldn’t we, Paul?”

“Before the sausages,” I thought, was rather like saying one would leave a dinner before the pudding. But my father called my mother in, and she came—pale face, plain dark frock, hands clasping and unclasping as if she were kneading invisible dough.

“You will let him come, Mrs. Kelver,” Mrs. Teidelmann pleaded, her voice like warm milk poured over hard bread. “It’s Dick Whittington, you know—such an excellent moral.”

My mother stood silent, lips trembling. Important as the matter seemed to me, her agitation was larger than the question.

“I am very sorry,” said my mother at last. “It is very kind of you. But I would rather he did not go.”

“Just this once,” persisted Mrs. Teidelmann. “It is holiday time.”

A ray of sunlight fell into the room and lit Mrs. Teidelmann’s coaxing face, making the corner where my mother stood seem dimmer by comparison. I remember noticing it then, with a child’s exactness: how even the light seemed to take sides.

“I would rather he did not go,” my mother repeated. Her voice had turned harsh and grating, like a chair scraped the wrong way. “When he is older others must judge for him, but for the present he must be guided by me—alone.”

“I really don’t think there could be any harm, Maggie,” urged my father. “Things have changed since we were young.”

“That may be,” answered my mother, in the same harsh voice. “It is long ago since then.”

“I didn’t intend it that way,” said my father with a short laugh.

“I merely meant that I may be wrong,” said my mother. “I seem so old among you all—so out of place. I have tried to change, but I cannot.”

“We will say no more about it,” said Mrs. Teidelmann sweetly. “I merely thought it would give him pleasure; and he has worked so hard this last term, his father tells me.”

She laid her hand on my shoulder and kept it there, drawing me a little towards her as if I were already half her property.

“It was very kind of you,” said my mother. “I would do anything to give him pleasure, anything—I could. He knows that. He understands.”

I knew my mother’s hand was seeking mine, but I was angry and would not see. Without another word she left the room.

My mother said nothing more about the pantomime. But the very next afternoon she took me herself to a hall in the neighbourhood, where we saw a magic-lantern and then a conjurer. She had put on a prettier frock than she had worn for many a long day, and she tried to be bright. She laughed and talked as if laughter could be stirred up like batter if one only beat hard enough.

But I, nursing my wrongs, remained moody and sulky. At any other time such rare amusement would have overjoyed me—the lantern’s huge faces swelling on the sheet, the conjurer’s shiny apparatus laid out with a cook’s confidence before a crowd that could not see the trick. There was even a sort of culinary magic in it: empty hands producing things, like a cupboard that gives up a rabbit when you expected only flour. An open-mouthed sleeper swallowed an endless chain of rats; a live rabbit appeared from the stout old gentleman’s hat—the very last sort of hat one would choose for rabbit-breeding. The hall smelled of damp coats, orange peel, gas, and dusty boards, and the applause went off like a clapping of frying-pans.

None of it could draw my mind away from the great theatre I had built in my imagination out of boys’ boasts and gaudy posters: a place of colour and thunder, of music loud enough to drown a whole week’s grey. I had seen the joy, just for a moment, as one glimpses a roast turning in a bright kitchen and is then sent back to bread and water.

So we walked home through muddy, darkening streets. The city’s noise pressed close: wheels grinding, voices rebounding from brick, the harsh hiss of gas, and over it all the ceaseless industrial breath of London that never seemed to sleep but only to change its rhythm. It was as if the streets themselves chewed, endlessly, on iron.

That night I woke—or half woke, the way children do—and thought I saw a figure in white crouching at the foot of my bed. I must have fallen asleep again. Later—I cannot tell if it was minutes or an hour—I opened my eyes and saw it still there. Terror came on me like a cold hand and I cried out. The figure rose from its knees: my mother.

She laughed, a broken curious laugh that did not belong to ordinary things.

“It was a silly dream I had,” she explained quickly. “I must have been thinking of the conjurer we saw. I dreamt that a wicked Magician had spirited you away from me. I could not find you and was all alone in the world.”

She put her arms round me so tight it almost hurt. Her cheek against mine was hot and damp, and I could smell on her the day’s faint scent of soap and the stale, sweetish air of the hall. Thus we remained until I fell asleep again, held like something she was afraid the dark might steal.

It was towards the close of these same holidays that my mother and I called upon Mrs. Teidelmann in her great stone-built house at Clapton. She had sent a note that morning saying she suffered from terrible headaches that quite took her senses away, so she could not come out. She would be leaving England in a few days to travel. Would my mother come and see her—she wished to say good-bye.

My mother handed the letter across the breakfast-table to my father.

“Of course you will go,” said my father. “Poor girl, I wonder what the cause can be. She used to be so free from everything of the kind.”

“Do you think it well for me to go?” said my mother. “What can she have to say to me?”

“Oh, just to say good-bye,” said my father. “It would look so pointed not to go.”

The house was dull and sombre without, but one entered it as through a commonplace door into Aladdin’s cave. Old Teidelmann had collected all his life. His treasures, now scattered through a dozen galleries, were then piled here in a rich confusion that made one’s eyes ache like a child’s at a sweetshop window. Pictures filled every inch of wall, leaned against wonderful old furniture, lay unframed even across the ceilings. Statues gleamed from corners—some of them, I remember, were the only things Mrs. Teidelmann kept for herself. Carvings, embroideries, priceless china, miniatures framed in gems, illuminated missals and gorgeously bound books crowded one another like loaves on a baker’s shelf at dawn.

The ugly little thick-lipped man had surrounded himself with beauty from every age and land, as if he could buy what nature had refused him. He himself must have been the cheapest thing in his own rooms; and now he lay shrivelled in his coffin under a monument that made an unknown cemetery famous.

We were shown up to Mrs. Teidelmann’s boudoir. She lay on a sofa near the fire, asleep, dressed in a loose lace robe that fell away and showed her thin, snow-white arms. Her rich dark hair was loose about her. In sleep she looked less beautiful—harder, with a suggestion of coarseness in the face that by day did not show. My mother said we would wait; perhaps she would wake. The servant closed the door softly and left us alone.

An old French clock on the mantelpiece—Cupids holding up a heart—ticked with a muffled, soothing sound, a domestic beat that seemed to insist on calm. My mother chose a chair by the window and sat with her eyes fixed on the sleeping woman’s face. It seemed to me—though it may have been fancy born later—that a faint smile loosened for a moment the pressed lips of the sleeper, as if she tasted, even in dreams, something pleasant.

Neither my mother nor I spoke. The only sound was the hush-tick of the gilt clock, a small kindness in a room full of costly things.

Then the sleeping woman stirred. Confused murmurs came first, then clearer words. I heard her whisper my father’s name. Very low—hardly more than breathed—yet in the silence each syllable struck bright and distinct.

“Ah no, we must not. Luke, my darling.”

My mother rose so swiftly her chair creaked. But her voice, when she spoke, was curiously matter-of-fact, as if giving an order about errands.

“Go, Paul,” she said. “Wait for me downstairs.”

She opened the door noiselessly, pushed me gently out, and closed it behind me.

I waited in the strange house among the mute beauty and the servants’ quiet movements. Every sound seemed magnified: the far clink of china, the faint brush of a skirt, the echo of my own breathing. The place had its own acoustic—soft, rich, padded by carpets and curtains as if sound itself were another treasure to be arranged and controlled. It made me think, childishly, of how our own home had grown louder with silence, which is the noisiest thing of all.

It was half an hour or more before my mother came down. At once we left, letting ourselves out. All the way home she did not speak. She walked as one in a dream, eyes that saw not. Only when her hand touched the lock of our gate did she seem to return.

“You must say nothing, Paul, do you understand?” she said. “When people are delirious they use strange words that have no meaning. Do you understand, Paul; you must never breathe a word—never.”

I promised. We went in.

From that day my mother’s whole manner changed. Not another angry word escaped her lips. Never again did her eyes flash. Mrs. Teidelmann remained away three months. My father wrote to her often, of course—he managed her affairs. But my mother wrote also—though I do not think my father knew—long letters she would go away by herself to pen, always in the twilight near the window.

“Why do you choose this time, just when it’s getting dark, to write your letters?” my father would expostulate if he chanced to look in. “Let me ring for the lamp, you will strain your eyes.”

My mother would excuse herself, saying she had only a few lines to finish.

“I can think better in this light,” she would explain.

And when Mrs. Teidelmann returned, it was my mother who first called upon her—before even my father knew she was back. From then onward one might have thought them closest friends: my mother visiting often, speaking of her to all in terms of praise and liking.

So peace returned to the house. My father became tender again in word and act; my mother was thoughtful again of his wants and whims. Her voice grew soft and low, the sweet smile hovered about her lips as in the old days before this evil thing had come to dwell among us.

And yet I could not forget it, because every day my mother grew feebler, the little ways that had seemed part of her falling away. She moved more slowly; she sat more often with her hands folded as if listening to some sound at a distance that no one else could hear.

Summer came and went—that town-summer of panting days and stifling nights, when through the open window crawls hot foul air heavy with reeking odours drawn from a thousand streets: sour beer, horse sweat, soot, refuse, frying fat grown old on the fire. Lying awake, one hears the city’s fitful breathing, the restless rumble and whistle and metallic cry, as of some over-laboured beast too tired even to rest. The modern world seemed determined to fill every gap with noise, as if silence were a dangerous old language it meant to stamp out.

Dr. Hal said, “There’s nothing really the matter with her—only weakness. It is the place. Cannot you get her away from it?”

“I cannot leave myself,” said my father, “just yet; but there is no reason why you and the boy should not take a holiday. This year I can afford it, and later I might possibly join you.”

My mother consented as she did to all things now. So it came about that again we climbed—more slowly, with pauses—the steep path to the ruined tower old Jacob in his happy foolishness had built upon the headland. Again we rested upon its topmost platform, sheltered from the wind that forever worried at its crumbling walls. Again we saw the distant mountains, faint like spectres, and the silent ships that came and vanished, and below our feet the pleasant farm lands and the grave, sweet river.

We took lodgings in the village. Smaller it seemed than before, yet wonderful in its sunny calm after London’s clanging turmoil. Mrs. Fursey was there still, but quite another Mrs. Fursey: still angular, but cheery now, bent no longer on suppressing me, rather on drawing me out before admiring neighbours as one might display a cake that had surprisingly risen.

“The material was unpromising, as you know,” she would imply with her manner. “There were times when I almost despaired. But with patience—and, may I say, a natural gift that way—you see what can be accomplished!”

Anna was now a buxom wife and mother, possessed by an uncontrollable desire to fall upon and kiss me at most unexpected moments, so that I lived in constant watchfulness and chose positions that offered easy retreat. Old Chumbley still cobbled shoes in his tiny cave. The bench before him held its row of waiting footwear, and as he tapped and hammered—tap-tap, tap—one could fancy they talked in the language of leather and nails: pert little shoes piping, “Be quick, be quick, we want to be toddling.” Dapper boots sighing, “Oh, please make haste, we are waiting to dance and strut.” Stout sober boots muttering, “As soon as you can, old friend. Remember we’ve work to do.” Flat-footed old boots, rusty and limp, mumbling, “We haven’t much time, Mr. Chumbley. Just a patch, that is all—we haven’t much further to go.”

Old Joe still peddled his pack with the help of the same old jokes; and Tom Pinfold was still scratching his head over a rejected fish hanging by its tail from his expostulating hand. One might almost have believed it the same fish, preserved by stubbornness.

Grown-up folks had changed but little. Only the children had played tricks: people I had left as sucking babes now swaggering in pinafores or knickerbockers; children I had known now mincing it as men and women. Their new airs annoyed me; I preferred the village’s steady sounds—the river’s hush, the wind in the pines, the clean clack of Chumbley’s hammer—to the forced noise of “growing up.”

One afternoon, towards the close of the last week, my mother and I climbed as usual to the upper platform of old Jacob’s tower. She leaned on the parapet, eyes fixed dreamily on the distant mountains. A smile crept to her lips as if some old taste returned to her—something remembered that no one else could name.

“What are you thinking of?” I asked.

“Oh, only of things that happened over there,” she nodded towards the hills as to an old crony with whom she shared secrets, “when I was a girl.”

“You lived there, long ago, didn’t you, when you were young?” I asked, with a boy’s bluntness.

“You’re very rude,” she said; and for the first time in long while a tone of her old self rang out. “It was a very little while ago.”

Suddenly she raised her head and listened. For perhaps twenty seconds she remained so, lips parted, as though catching some far-off call. Then from the woods came a faint long-drawn “Coo-ee.”

We ran to the side of the tower that commanded the pathway from the village and waited until my father emerged from among the dark pines into sunlight.

Seeing us, he shouted again and waved his stick. From the light in his eyes, his gallant bearing, the spring of his step over the heathery turf, we knew instinctively that trouble had come upon him. He always met trouble with that look and air, as if it were a challenger and he a man who could not resist a bout. It was the old Norse blood in him, I suppose: one imagines those godless old pirates springing to their feet when the North wind, loosed like a hawk from leash, struck at the beaked prow.

We heard his quick step on the rickety stair; the next moment he was between us, breathing a little hard and laughing.

For a while he stood beside my mother without speaking, both gazing at the distant hills where, as she had said, things had happened long ago. Perhaps, “over there,” their memories met and looked upon one another with kind eyes.

“Do you remember,” said my father at last, “we climbed up here—it was the first walk we took together after coming here. We discussed our plans for the future, how we would retrieve our fortunes.”

“And the future,” answered my mother, “has a way of making plans for us instead.”

“It would seem so,” replied my father with a laugh. “I am an unlucky beggar, Maggie. I dropped all your money as well as my own down that wretched mine.”

“It was the will—it was Fate, or whatever you call it,” said my mother. “You could not help that, Luke.”

“If only that damned pump hadn’t jambed,” said my father.

“Do you remember that Mrs. Tharand?” asked my mother.

“Yes. What of her?”

“A worldly woman, I always thought her. She called on me the morning we were leaving; I don’t think you saw her. ‘I’ve been through more worries than you would think, to look at me,’ she said to me, laughing. I’ve always remembered her words: ‘and of all the troubles that come to us in this world, believe me, Mrs. Kelver, money troubles are the easiest to bear.’”

“I wish I could think so,” said my father.

“She rather irritated me at the time,” my mother continued. “I thought it one of those commonplaces with which we console ourselves for other people’s misfortunes. But now I know she spoke the truth.”

There was silence between them for a while. The wind moved about the tower and the pines answered it; the air here had room for natural sounds, not the iron-rimmed din of London. It felt as if the very quiet tried to heal.

Then my father said, in a cheery tone that did not quite cover what lay beneath:

“I’ve broken with old Hasluck.”

“I thought you would be compelled to sooner or later,” said my mother.

“Hasluck,” cried my father, with sudden vehemence, “is little better than a thief; I told him so.”

“What did he say?” asked my mother.

“Laughed, and said that was better than some people.”

My father laughed too.

I would do Noel Hasluck no injustice. He was always kind to me; not only then, but in later years, when I learned kindness was rarer than I had dreamt. And for Barbara’s sake, if for no other, I would rather speak well of him. Yet if his good-tempered, thick-skinned ghost—good-tempered and thick-skinned, for otherwise it would not be Noel’s ghost—were reading over my shoulder, I believe it would agree with me and not be offended.

Of all the men who went down into the City each day in cab or ’bus or train, he was perhaps one of the most unprincipled. Whether that is saying much or little I leave to those who know the City’s menu.

To do others—as he believed, right or wrong—as they would do him if ever he gave them half a chance, was his notion of “business,” and in most transactions he was successful.

“I play a game,” he would argue, “where cheating is the rule. Nine out of ten men round the table are sharpers like myself, and the tenth man is a fool who has no business to be there. We prey upon each other, and the cutest of us is the winner.”

“But the innocent people, lured by your fine promises,” I once ventured—“the widows and the orphans?”

“My dear lad,” he said, with a laugh, laying his fat hand on my shoulder, “I remember one of your widows writing me a pathetic letter about some shares she had taken in a Silver Company of mine. Lord knows where the mine is now—somewhere in Spain, I think. It looked as though all her savings were gone. She had an only son, and it was nearly all they possessed in the world, etc., etc.—you know the sort of thing. Well, I did what I’ve often been numskull enough to do in similar cases—wrote and offered to buy her out at par. A week later she answered, thanking me, but saying it did not matter. There had occurred a momentary rise, and she had sold out at a profit—to her own brother-in-law, as I discovered, happening to come across the transfers. You can find widows and orphans round the Monte Carlo card tables, if you like to look for them; they are no more deserving of consideration than the rest of the crowd. Besides, if it comes to that, I’m an orphan myself;” and he laughed again—one of his deep, hearty, honest laughs.

No one ever possessed a laugh more suggestive, in every cadence, of simple transparent honesty. He used to say himself it was worth thousands to him.
