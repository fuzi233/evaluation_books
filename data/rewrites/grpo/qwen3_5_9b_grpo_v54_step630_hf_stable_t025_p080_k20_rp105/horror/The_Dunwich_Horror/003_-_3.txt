Old Whateley's hands, gnarled yet possessed of a terrifying vitality, moved with a frantic industry that defied his trembling frame. He purchased cattle, yet the herd remained stubbornly small; he felled timber, and he began the arduous task of mending the great, peaked-roofed house that clung to the rocky flank of the hill. While his mind occasionally slipped into the babbling fog of dementia, his carpentry was a study in cold, calculated precision. The restoration had begun the moment Wilbur drew breath, marked by the sudden, frantic ordering of a tool-shed, clad in fresh clapboards and secured with a heavy iron lock. Now, turning his gaze upward to the abandoned second story, the old man worked with the same ruthless thoroughness. His madness manifested not in the construction itself, but in the sealing of every window in the reclaimed attic, boarding them tight against the world. The townsfolk whispered that reclaiming such a space was folly, yet they watched in silence as Whateley fitted out a new nursery downstairs for his grandson, a room visible to all but forbidden to enter. Inside, tall shelves rose like the ribs of a crypt, slowly filling with the rotting, ancient tomes that had once been dumped in chaotic heaps throughout the house.

"I made some use of 'em," Whateley would rasp, his fingers stained with paste from the rusty stove as he glued a torn page of black-letter script back together. "But the boy is fit to make better use of 'em. He ought to have 'em as well sot as he kin, for they're goin' to be all of his larnin'."

By September of 1914, when Wilbur was but a year and seven months old, he was a creature of unsettling proportions. He stood as tall as a four-year-old, his movements fluid and his speech a torrent of incomprehensible intelligence. He roamed the fields with a wild freedom, shadowing his mother, yet at home, he sat hunched over the strange charts and pictures in his grandfather's library. Long, hushed afternoons passed as Old Whateley catechized him, drilling the boy with questions that seemed to dig deep into the earth. As the house restoration neared completion, the neighbors noticed a peculiar alteration: one window in the rear of the east gable, pressed tight against the hill, had been transformed into a solid plank door. Above it, a cleated wooden runway spiraled up from the ground, a silent invitation to the sealed darkness. Around this time, the tightly locked tool-house, abandoned since Wilbur's birth, fell silent once more. When Earl Sawyer ventured inside after a cattle sale, he recoiled from a stench so profound it felt alien to the senses—a foul odor reminiscent only of the ancient stone circles on the hills, a smell that suggested something utterly unsanctioned by nature. Yet, in Dunwich, such olfactory horrors were merely the baseline of existence.

The months that followed were quiet on the surface, save for a slow, grinding crescendo of noises emanating from the mysterious hill. By May Eve of 1915, tremors shook the very foundations of Aylesbury, and by Halloween, the earth beneath Sentinel Hill groaned with a rhythmic rumble, strangely synchronized with bursts of unnatural flame—what the locals dismissed as the Whateleys' witchcraft. Wilbur grew with uncanny speed, appearing as a ten-year-old before his fourth birthday. He read voraciously but spoke less, consumed by a settling taciturnity that chilled those who saw it. A dark, goatish malice began to bloom in his features. He muttered jargons unknown to any tongue and chanted in rhythms that induced a primal terror in listeners. Dogs, once loyal guardians, now fled from him with visceral hatred, forcing the boy to carry a pistol just to walk the countryside safely. His sporadic use of the weapon only deepened the community's dread.

Visitors to the house often found Lavinia alone on the ground floor, while eerie cries and heavy footsteps echoed from the boarded-up second story. She never explained the activities of her father and son upstairs, though her face would drain of color and her eyes widen in abject terror if a jovial fish-peddler attempted the locked stairway door. The peddler later claimed to Dunwich loungers that he heard a horse stamping on that upper floor. The townspeople, recalling the sealed door, the runway, and the cattle that vanished into the earth, shuddered with memories of Old Whateley's youth and the sacrifices made to heathen gods. Soon, the entire Whateley property became a zone of canine revulsion.

Then came 1917, and the Great War. As chairman of the local draft board, Squire Sawyer Whateley struggled to find a single young man fit enough for a development camp. Alarmed by the signs of regional decadence, the government dispatched officers and medical experts, conducting a survey that would become infamous across New England. It was the publicity surrounding this investigation that led reporters to the Whateley doorstep. The *Boston Globe* and the *Arkham Advertiser* printed flamboyant Sunday specials detailing Wilbur's precociousness, his grandfather's black magic, the shelves of forbidden books, the sealed second story, and the weirdness of the region. At four and a half years old, Wilbur looked like a fifteen-year-old, his lips and cheeks fuzzy with coarse dark down, his voice cracking into a harsh baritone. Earl Sawyer accompanied the press to the farm, drawing their attention to the foul trickle of odor descending from the sealed upper spaces—a scent identical to that found in the abandoned tool-shed and the stone circles. The Dunwich folk read these stories with grim amusement, mocking the obvious errors, yet they could not shake the question of why the Whateleys always paid for their cattle in gold coins of ancient, impossible date. They received the reporters with ill-concealed distaste, knowing that any violent resistance would only court further, more dangerous scrutiny.
