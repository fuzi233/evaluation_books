Two days after the scene I have sought to portray, Gerard found himself wandering through one of Rome’s most squalid streets when a sudden thunderstorm swept down upon him. Seeking shelter, he stepped into a lowly hostel, its walls damp and dim with age. He called for wine, and as the rain lashed against the shutters, he drank steadily until his thoughts grew heavy and his senses dulled. Soon he was half asleep, his head resting on his hands, which lay flat upon the rough-hewn table.

The room gradually filled with guests—rough men whose voices rose in boisterous talk, their laughter echoing off the stone walls. The noise stirred Gerard from his stupor, and slowly he became aware of two figures near him, speaking in hushed tones. One wore the garb of a pardoner, his robes marked by the insignia of the Church; the other, though dressed plainly and neatly, carried himself with a certain furtiveness. His hat was pulled low over his brow, concealing his face entirely save for a thick beard—a telltale sign of one who avoided both the gaze of honest folk and the reach of justice.

They were bargaining—not over goods or coin, but over sin itself. And this was no uncommon trade in those days: forgiveness could be purchased even before the crime had been committed, sealed under what was called a celestial contract.

The man in the slouched hat grumbled about the rising cost of indulgences. “If these prices keep climbing,” he muttered, “we poor souls will be barred from heaven altogether.”

The pardoner scoffed. “Indulgences have never been cheaper for good husbandmen,” he replied.

“Who are these ‘good husbandmen’?” asked the other.

“Those who sin like sensible creatures—by the market, not by passion. But if you insist on choosing a crime the Pope has condemned, then blame yourself, not me.”

To prove that vice was within reach of nearly all except the lowest dregs of society, he unfolded a parchment and read aloud the list of sins and their corresponding prices. It was an unsettling catalogue—one too explicit to print here. To omit it would distort its meaning. Yet such records still exist in great libraries, preserved as relics of a corrupt era. Suffice it to say that the murder of a layman cost less than many offences now considered trivial by common folk.

With minor concessions exchanged, the bargain was struck. Money passed hands, and the buyer received absolution in advance—for the removal of one innocent man. The price for killing a cleric was far greater, but neither man used the word *assassination*. They spoke only of "removing" and "disposing," as if trading in livestock rather than human lives.

All of this reached Gerard’s ears, yet he did not stir. He remained motionless, his head still bowed, listening with dull awareness. Only when the two men rose and parted ways did he lift his head slightly, his eyes narrowing as they followed the retreating figure of the man in the slouched hat.

“If Margaret were alive,” he muttered beneath his breath, “I’d seize thee by the throat and strangle thee, thou base coward. But she is dead—dead—dead. Let the whole world perish; it matters not to me—so long as I die among the first.”

When he returned home, he saw a man in a slouched hat walking briskly up and down the street opposite his dwelling. “Ah,” thought Gerard, “there is that cur again.” But in his present state—numb with grief and drink—the sight meant nothing. It left no mark upon him.
