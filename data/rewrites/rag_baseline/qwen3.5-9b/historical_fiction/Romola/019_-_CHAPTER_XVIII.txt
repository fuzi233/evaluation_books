When Tito departed the Via de' Bardi that afternoon, his stride carried the confident rhythm of a man who had successfully evaded imminent catastrophe. The threat was gone, yet his mind, now unburdened by the immediate presence of Romola and her father, circled back to the paralyzing hours of terror he had endured. He realized with a jarring clarity that his actions during those moments had been driven by panic rather than strategy. Had he possessed true foresight, he would never have parted with his ring. To surrender a gem he had professed to cherish was a move that would strike Romola and her circle as sordid, unless couched in the elaborate fiction of purchasing a special gift. At that critical juncture, Tito had harbored a profound nausea toward the art of simulation. Fortunately, fate had intervened, granting him immunity from the dire consequences of that initial deception. The weight of potential ruin had vanished, leaving him convinced that Romola deserved this reprieve. Who truly suffered? The repercussions for Baldassarre remained too murky to calculate, but Tito felt a desperate need to sever every invisible tether binding him to Romola, however slight the constriction. He failed to recognize that the intoxication of this newfound freedom was simultaneously numbing the very sensibilities required to navigate future entanglements safely.

Ultimately, the sale of the ring was a trivial transaction. More pressing was the dilemma posed by little Tessa, whose mind was currently clouded by a delusion destined for inevitable disillusionment. Should he seek her out alone to dispel the illusion immediately, or allow time and circumstance to erode it naturally? Happy dreams were fleeting, dissolving under the harsh glare of daylight and the clamor of daily life. The image of her—sweet, pouting, innocent, and round—was impossible to ignore. Tito contemplated bringing her a gift to assuage her, perhaps using the occasion to gauge whether her stepfather had intensified his cruelty following her mother's death. Alternatively, he could expose the truth to her instantly, then inform Romola, arranging a better destiny for the child. Yet, the memory of the *cerretano* incident and his own negligence in allowing Tessa to depart under false pretenses created a rigid boundary: this must never reach Romola's knowledge. Since no amount of explanation could purge Tessa's mind of her belief, maintaining the status quo carried a perpetual risk of exposure. Furthermore, even a child like Tessa might harbor a bitter gall upon discovering her love had been misplaced. Yes, she undoubtedly loved him, and this affection necessitated his continued absence. Still, it was a minor episode, akin to a rural girl's infatuation that would eventually yield to the advances of a sturdy peasant, prompting her to abandon her secret and accept the truth of her liberty. *Dunque*—farewell, Tessa! May your wishes be fulfilled. Tito resolved that the affair of the *cerretano* would bear no further fruit for him; humans often mistake personal resolve for ironclad certainty. Regarding the fifty-five florins used to purchase the ring, he had formulated a plan to account for the missing gem while simultaneously inoculating Romola's mind against the resurgence of monastic fantasies, which repelled him intensely. With this objective fixed, he proceeded to the Via Gualfonda to locate Piero di Cosimo, the preeminent artist of the era, renowned for his fantastical mythological designs.

Tito entered the courtyard where Piero's residence opened, noting immediately that the heavy iron knocker was wrapped in thick wool and secured with intricate cordage. Recalling the painter's habit of isolating himself from auditory intrusion, Tito was unsurprised by these defensive measures. He began with tentative taps, escalating to vigorous shaking of the door, but received no response. Just as he prepared to retreat, chastising himself for squandering time on a futile errand when he could await the painter's appearance at Nello's, a small girl emerged carrying a basket of eggs. She approached the door, rose on her toes, and slid up a small iron plate set in grooves. Pressing her face to the aperture, she called out in a high, piping voice, "Messer Piero!" Moments later, the sound of bolts retracting echoed through the air. The door swung open, and Piero appeared clad in a red nightcap and a loose brown serge tunic, his sleeves rolled to the elbows. He cast a startled glance at Tito before extending a hand to claim the basket from the child. Re-entering the house briefly, he returned with the empty container and asked, "How much to pay?" "Two grossoni, Messer Piero," the girl replied, adding, "They are all freshly boiled; my mother insists." Piero extracted coins from a leather pouch at his belt, and the maid departed, casting upward glances of awed admiration at the imposing young gentleman.

Piero's gaze, however, lacked such courtesy. "What brings you to my door, Messer Greco?" he asked. "I saw you this morning at Nello's; had you inquired then, I would have informed you that I do not entertain strangers without prior consultation regarding their business." "Pardon, Messer Piero," Tito responded, his demeanor unflappable. "I acted without due deliberation. My mind was occupied solely by your unparalleled skill in crafting whimsical inventions, and a sudden urge to commission something similar drove me here." The painter's eccentricities were notorious throughout Florence, rendering this reception a non-event rather than an affront. Even if Tito had sensed hostility, his instinct to cultivate goodwill outweighed any resentment. Piero made a habitual grimace, stretching the corners of his mouth downward and pressing his brows together to obscure his true sentiments behind a mask of defiance. "And what is this requirement?" he asked after a brief pause. Internally, he felt a surge of temptation at the prospect of applying his inventive talents.

"I require a delicate miniature device drawn from poetic fables, which you possess the expertise to synthesize," Tito stated. "It must be painted on a wooden case—I will demonstrate the scale—as a triptych. The interior requires simple gilding; my interest lies entirely with the exterior. This is a favored motif among Florentines: the Triumph of Bacchus and Ariadne, yet I demand a novel interpretation. A passage from Ovid will provide the necessary framework. Young Bacchus must be seated in a vessel, his head wreathed in grape clusters, wielding a spear entwined with vine leaves. Dark-berried ivy must coil around the masts and sails; the oars must be thyrsi, and flowers must adorn the stern. Leopards and tigers must crouch before him, while dolphins play nearby. However, I insist upon including the fair-haired Ariadne beside him, rendered immortal with a golden crown—an addition not found in Ovid, but one you will surely envision. Above them, youthful Loves, masters of your craft, must shoot roses from the tips of their arrows—"

"Speak no further!" Piero interjected sharply. "I possess Ovid in the vernacular. Locate the passage. I detest being suffocated by the musings of others. You may enter."

Piero guided Tito through the first chamber, where a basket of eggs rested on the open hearth amidst a chaotic pile of shattered shells and ash. Jarringly incongruous with this sordid clutter stood a low bedframe of carved ebony, draped carelessly with a rich Oriental carpet that seemed stolen from the steps of a Madonna's throne. Beside it lay a carved *cassone*, a large chest adorned with painted motifs. Beyond this sparse arrangement, the room contained little else: plaster casts, wooden scaffolding, easels, and rough crates, all festooned with cobwebs. The adjacent space was even more expansive yet densely packed. Clearly, Piero was hosting a gathering, for the double doors beneath the high window—which admitted the artist's primary illumination—stood ajar, revealing a garden or rather a dense thicket. Fig trees and vines sprawled in tangled wildness among nettles and hemlock, while a towering cypress pierced a stifling canopy of yellowish mulberry leaves. It appeared this dank luxuriance had breached the interior walls; in one corner, amidst a confused heap of carved marble fragments and rusted armor, tufts of long grass and dark, feathery fennel had taken root. A large stone vase, tilted precariously, seemed to pour forth ivy streaming across the floor.

Pen-and-oil sketches of fantastical sea monsters, dances of satyrs and maenads, Saint Margaret's resurrection from the dragon's maw, Madonnas bathed in celestial light, botanical studies, and grotesque heads hung everywhere. On irregular, rough shelves, a few books lay scattered among drooping bunches of corn, bull horns, pieces of dried honeycomb, stones encrusted with rare lichens, skulls, bones, peacock feathers, and massive bird wings. Rising from the filth-covered floor were life-sized figures: one dressed in the frock of a Vallombrosan monk, bizarrely topped with a helmet featuring a barred visor; another smothered in brocade and skins hastily thrown over it. Amidst this heterogeneous still life, several speckled and white pigeons strutted or perched, too accustomed to humanity to flee at the approach of men. Three plump toads crawled in intimate proximity near the threshold, while a white rabbit, seemingly the model for the creature frightening Cupid in the central easel's depiction of Mars and Venus, twitched its nose with evident contentment atop a box filled with bran.
