About four o'clock, the physical exhaustion finally yielded to a torment far worse: a gnawing, ravenous thirst that tore through me like a fever. I could not return to sleep. My dreams had been a delusion—a refreshing stream with verdant banks—but reality slammed me back into this arid hell. As Umbopa had warned, if we failed to locate water today, we would perish miserably. In this crushing heat, no human soul could endure long without it. I sat up, scrubbing my soot-stained face with dry, calloused hands until my lips and eyelids, fused together by dehydration, finally parted. Though it was barely past dawn, the air offered no crisp promise of morning; instead, it was choked with a suffocating, hot haze. The others remained unconscious.

Soon, however, the gloom receded enough to read. I pulled out my pocket copy of *Ingoldsby Legends* and opened "The Jackdaw of Rheims." Reading the line, *"A nice little boy held a golden ewer / Embossed, and filled with water as pure..."*, I physically winced, trying to slap my cracked lips against the empty air. The mere concept of that pristine liquid drove me mad. If the Cardinal himself stood there with his bell, book, and candle, I would have lunged forward and drunk it all, regardless of whether it was consecrated by the Church or washed with soap fit for the Pope. The thought of the Cardinal and his boy discovering a scorched, brown-eyed elephant hunter shoving his filthy face into a basin and gulping down every drop amused me so profoundly that I cackled aloud. This sudden noise jolted the others awake, prompting them to begin the painful ritual of rubbing their dirt-caked skin and dragging gummed lips apart.

Once fully conscious, we faced the grim reality of our situation: absolute drought. We inverted our bottles, licking the rims, but they were as dry as bone. Good, the keeper of the brandy flask, held it with longing, but Sir Henry swiftly snatched it away, wisely noting that raw spirits would only hasten our demise.

"If we do not find water, we shall die," Sir Henry stated flatly.

"I say, if we trust the old Dom's map, there should be some about," I replied, though I saw little conviction in their eyes. The map was becoming a joke. As the light grew, Ventvögel, the Hottentot guide, rose and began pacing, his gaze fixed intently on the ground. He stopped abruptly, let out a guttural cry, and pointed to the sand.

"What is it?" we asked, rushing to his side.

"It is fresh Springbok tracks," I noted dismissively. "What of it?"

"Springbucks do not stray far from water," Ventvögel answered in Dutch.

"No!" I exclaimed, relief flooding through me. "Thank God!"

In desperation, hope clings to the slightest straw. Ventvögel lifted his snub nose, sniffing the hot air with the keen instinct of a wild beast sensing prey—or perhaps danger.

"I *smell* water," he declared.

Jubilance surged through us. These rugged men possessed an uncanny intuition for the elements. Just then, the sun burst forth in glory, revealing a sight so magnificent it momentarily stilled our thirst. Before us, glittering like silver in the morning rays, soared Sheba's Breasts. Stretching for hundreds of miles on either side lay the great Suliman Berg. To describe the sheer sublimity of that vision is difficult; words fail before its majesty. Two colossal mountains, likely extinct volcanoes, towered at least fifteen thousand feet high, separated by merely a dozen miles and linked by a precipitous cliff. They resembled the pillars of a gigantic gateway, shaped like a woman's breasts, with mist-shrouded summits resembling nipples atop vast, rounded bases. Mysterious vapors and clouds quickly gathered, veiling the giants in a ghostly gauze, obscuring their terrifying grandeur.

Scarcely had Sheba's Breasts faded into the cloud-clad privacy than our thirst returned with renewed ferocity. Ventvögel insisted he smelled water, yet all we saw was sweltering sand and scrub. We circled the nearest hillock, scanning the barren landscape, but found nothing—not a pan, pool, or spring.

"You are a fool," I snapped at Ventvögel. "There is no water."

He sniffed again, stubborn. "I smell it, Baas... it is somewhere in the air."

"Yes," I retorted cynically, "no doubt it is in the clouds, waiting to wash our bones in two months."

Sir Henry stroked his yellow beard thoughtfully. "Perhaps it is on top of the hill."

"Nonsense," Good scoffed. "Who ever heard of water atop a hill?"

Undeterred, I proposed we climb. Reluctantly, we scrambled up the sandy slopes, Umbopa leading. Halfway up, he froze, as if petrified.

"_Nanzia manzie!_" he cried, his voice booming. "Here is water!"

Rushing to him, we found it: a small, indented pool nestled in a deep cut on the summit of the sand koppie. Its origin was a mystery, and its color was a murky black, unappealing to the eye, but it was water. We threw ourselves down, lapsing eagerly onto our stomachs, sucking up the uninviting fluid as if it were ambrosia. Oh, the luxury of it! Afterward, stripped naked, we wallowed in the mud, soaking our parched skins. Refreshed, we devoured our dried meat (*biltong*) and smoked our pipes, falling asleep by the pool's shadow until noon.

Restored, we refilled our bottles and resumed our march under the moonlight. We covered twenty-five miles that night, though found no more water, save for a fleeting bit of shade behind some ant-hills the next day. By the following morning, the majestic peaks loomed larger than ever, looming directly above us as we approached the lower slopes of Sheba's left breast. Our water was gone again, and the snow-line, far above, seemed impossibly distant. Driven by agony, we toiled upward over ancient lava beds, our feet blistered by the rough, jagged stone.

High above, amidst large lumps of cooled lava, we spotted a bizarre anomaly: a patch of vibrant green growth. It was a miracle of nature, soil formed from decomposed rock holding bird-dropped seeds. However, grass offered no sustenance to starving men. We collapsed beneath the rocks, despair weighing heavily on us. Then, Umbopa shuffled toward the greenery, dancing and shouting like a madman, waving something leafy.

"What is it?" I screamed in Zulu.

"It is food and water, Macumazahn!"

He was waving a wild melon. Thousands of them, ripening in the sun. We feasted heartily on the sweet fruit, a taste sweeter than nectar to our ravaged tongues. Yet, melons lacked nutritional depth, and soon hunger gnawed anew. A flock of bustards flew overhead, and Ventvögel, throwing himself prone, urged us to shoot. I raised my Winchester and fired twice, bringing down a fine bird. Over a fire of melon stalks, we roasted the feast, eating every scrap except the bones and beak, feeling stronger than we had in a week.

That night, we carried our haul of melons higher. The air grew cooler, a welcome respite, and by dawn we were within a dozen miles of the snow-line. Here, more melons awaited us, assuaging our thirst temporarily. But the ascent became treacherous, the terrain steep and slow. We consumed our last piece of *biltong*. Strange, considering the vast snowfields above, we encountered no springs or streams, a fact we would later understand. Hunger, however, became our true enemy. For three miserable days, I recorded our decline in a notebook.

On the twenty-third, we trudging up the icy incline, looking like gaunt spectres, dragged our weary feet over the dazzling, blinding snow. The setting sun bathed the landscape in blood-red hues, crowning the great dome of the mountain with a terrible glory. We stood beneath the "nipple" of Sheba's left Breast, a vast, smooth hillock of frozen snow towering thousands of feet into the air.

"We ought to be near that cave the old gentleman wrote about," Gasped Good.

"Yes," I agreed grimly, "if there is a cave."

Sir Henry groaned, "Don't talk like that, Quatermain! I have faith in the Dom. Remember the water!"

"If we miss it before dark, we are dead men," I replied softly.

For ten minutes, we trudged in silence. Then, Umbopa, wrapped tight in his blanket, seized my arm and pointed to the springing slope. Some two hundred yards away, a dark hole gaped in the snow.

"It is the cave," Umbopa whispered.
