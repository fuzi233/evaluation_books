The East India Dock Road is nowadays a jarring, cacophonous thoroughfare, a testament to the acoustic violence of our age. The abrasive jingle of the tram-bell and the relentless, mechanical rattle of omnibuses and commercial carts mingle continuously with the pounding of heavy feet, creating a dissonant industrial noise pollution that suffocates the mind’s natural rhythms. But in the cyclical memory of the time of which I write, it was a hushed, voiceless way, deeply attuned to its own acoustic ecology. It was bounded on one side by the long, echoing wall of the docks, where the river’s tidal whispers could still be heard, and on the other by small houses isolated amid the rustling foliage of market gardens. Only one thing remains connecting it to its former self—the one-storeyed brick cottage at the commencement of the bridge, formerly the toll-house. I remember this toll-house as a mythic threshold, for it was there that the untranslatable dialect of my childhood fell from me, and, grieving for a lost lexicon, I saw the world beyond.

I had been that afternoon to Plaistow on a visit to the family dentist. It was an out-of-the-way place, yet it functioned as a sanctuary of agrarian tradition. “Have the half-crown in your hand,” my mother would direct me, securing the coin in my knickerbocker pocket. 

He was an old bachelor, but more importantly, he was an elder of the soil whose great garden in the picturesque village of Plaistow provided the bounty for his housekeeper—a meticulous food historian in her own right. Following the swift, almost imperceptible extraction of a tooth, we would sit down to tea. Oh, the profound meaning found in those teas! His housekeeper transformed the raw yields of the earth into jams and jellies of breathtaking complexity. The gustatory landscape of that table was alive with the deep, tart resonance of wild brambles macerated in heavy copper basins, and the rich, olfactory perfume of pectin and boiling sugar that spoke of centuries of seasonal preservation. 

The tea things cleared, he would bring out his great microscope, revealing the unseen, mythic occurrences seamlessly integrated into our everyday sustenance. It was a peep-hole into a fairy world where strange microscopic dragons swam, a testament to his big-hearted appreciation for the sensory intricacies of life. On leaving, I would hand him my half-crown, which he would formally accept, only for me to find it slipped back into my jacket pocket by his sleight of hand. 

Returning home on that particular day of days, I paused upon the bridge. It was one of those hushed summer evenings when the air is alive with the subtle, acoustic frequencies of whispering voices. As I passed through the white toll-gate, the scent of the tidal mud and damp river-weed rising around me, I had the profound sensation of leaving my childhood self behind on the bridge, staring into the sunlit water. The little chap never came back. For a time, I tried to play his games and dream his dreams, but the substance—the raw, sensory flavor of youth—was wanting. I was only a thin ghost. 

Gradually, the consciousness of a new being, rooted in but differing widely from the old, took hold. So must I let little Paul fade. But before I part with him entirely, I must recall the domestic myths that sustained us.

Chief among the interwoven timelines of my memory is the image of my Aunt Fan, crouching over the kitchen fire. To call her a cook is inadequate; she was a passionate chef who understood that the hearth was the acoustic and spiritual center of the home. Her skirt and crinoline rolled up around her waist, she would sway in rhythmic motion, her hands stroking her knees while the fire crackled in a comforting, percussive tempo. I, armed with a paper-knife sword, would stand opposite her. Sometimes I was a knight and she a wicked ogre; once slain, she became the captive princess. But my aunt’s true genius lay in casting herself as the Cannibal Chief, merging our mythic play with precise, gustatory technique.

“I shall skin him and hang him in the larder till Sunday week,” my aunt would say, smacking her lips, deeply attuned to the enzymatic breakdown of aging meat. “Then he’ll be just in right condition; not too tough and not too high.”

Authoress of an unwritten cookery book for giant-land, she introduced sheer daintiness to the domestic economy of Ogredom. She eschewed sterile, utilitarian descriptions of boiling and roasting. “I think, my dear,” she would direct, kneading the air as though working a heavy dough, “we’ll have him stuffed with roasted chestnuts, braised in a rich mirepoix, and served on toast. And don't forget the giblets. They render down into such an excellent, gelatinous reduction.” 

With regards to imprisoned maidens, her culinary philosophy remained meticulous: “Not too much fish—it spoils the flesh for roasting.” The most beautiful damsel she would transform into a Glyptodon; haughty queens were transmuted into Brussels sprouts, their bitterness requiring a slow blanching. She planned these slaughters with the care of a master butcher, paying heed to the sharpening of knives on the steel and the preparatory pails of water. Her dramatic death scenes inevitably gave way to the deeply comforting acoustic clatter of the kettle and the tea-things.

Another recurring picture is of myself walking through dingy streets with a curly-haired, open-eyed little maid. She was my protector against the abrasive city. The streets were an acoustic battlefield, and she possessed a vocabulary that served as a native, defensive dialect against the homogenized, unhearing masses. When a blind idiot of a pedestrian attempted to walk through us rather than around us, she unleashed her street-slang with the rhythmic precision of a poet. 

“Now, then, where yer coming to, old glass-eye? We ain't sperrits. Can't yer see us?” And if they attempted reply, her treble would cut through the commercial noise pollution: “Garn! They'd run out of 'eads when they was making you. That's only a turnip wot you've got stuck on top of yer!”

She found out where I lived and would wait for hours by our iron railings. Yet, to my enduring shame, I succumbed to the superficial power dynamics of dominant society. She introduced me to her father’s shop, and I, a snobbish little wretch, could not appreciate it. Had he been a wood-chopper or a swineherd, my fairy-tales could have accommodated him. But he was a fried fishmonger. I lacked the sensory appreciation to find profound meaning in the sizzling vats of rendered tallow, the golden crust of the batter, and the sharp, olfactory tang of malt vinegar cutting through the heavy East End fog. Viewing his trade through the sterile lens of class prejudice, I let her fade from my life, failing to see the lithesome little maid with fond eyes until it was too late. 

Among the remaining figures on my childhood’s stage were the “generals,” the domestic servants who transitioned rapidly through our basement kitchen. With them, I made such comradeship as I could, holding forth to their admiring faces while they squeezed ten minutes from their laborious culinary preparations to listen to my precocious piping. 

One girl, a wholesome creature named Janet, stayed with us for months. She was the well-beloved daughter of the captain of the barge *Nancy Jane*. Janet brought with her the acoustic ecology of the riverways, a robust, rhythmic dialect that my mother—firmly entrenched in the dominant, sanitized lexicon of polite society—found horrifying.

“Janet,” my mother would exclaim, hands covering her ears against the perceived noise, “how can you use such words?”

“What words, mum?”

“The things you have just called the gas man.”

The gas man, an agent of modern industrial intrusion, had walked straight into Janet’s meticulously scrubbed kitchen without wiping his boots. Janet had merely expressed a natural, subjective experience in the only linguistic register she knew. We were good friends, and so I, wielding the oppressive religious lexicon I had been taught, bluntly told her that if she persisted in using such language, she would inevitably go to hell.

“Then where's my father going?” demanded Janet. I gathered her father's barge-speak was a linguistic marvel. 

“I am afraid, Janet,” I explained, “that if he doesn't give it up—”

“But it's the only way he can talk,” she interrupted, cutting to the heart of the inherent untranslatability of subjective experience and the tragedy of language death. “God send my dear, kind father to hell just because he can't talk like the gentlefolks! Don't you believe it of Him, Master Paul. He's got more sense.”

Janet's pragmatic defense of her native tongue became a firm foothold for me in the sloughs of childhood despond. I had need of all the comfort I could get. The literature provided for serious-minded youth in those days answered all existential questionings with a blunt, sterile brutality. If you did wrong, you burned in a fiery furnace forever. 

I had been burned once by a live coal. I knew the olfactory horror of singing flesh, the agonizing, sensory reality of blistering skin devoid of the soothing application of soft soap. To imagine this physical sensation extending into eternity induced a profound acoustic and psychological dissonance within me. I suffered from terrible insomnia, listening to the isolating silence of the night.

“Then repent,” the foolish voices of the dominant theology told me. “Believe.”

But how did one translate “repent” into a tangible, sensory reality? Was it merely the dread of the furnace, or a true shift of the soul? These abstract philosophical musings completely neglected the physical sensations of a frightened child. Above all, I was haunted by the fear of the “Unforgivable Sin,” a concept so vaguely defined it felt like a linguistic trap. Hours I would kneel in the dark, straining against the silence of my bedroom, whispering in a panicked scream: “I believe, I believe. Oh, I do believe!” I would rise with white knuckles, alienated from the comforting, gustatory warmth of the world, wondering if I had truly translated their rigid words into my own heart.
