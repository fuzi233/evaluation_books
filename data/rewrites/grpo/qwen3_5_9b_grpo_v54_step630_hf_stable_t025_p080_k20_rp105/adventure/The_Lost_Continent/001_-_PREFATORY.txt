The damp chill of the Grand Canary dawn seeped into our bones, a stark reminder of sleeping under the open sky. While I usually greeted such mornings with a brisk run to shake off the stiffness, the rugged terrain here offered no flat ground, only jagged lava fields. Instead, I joined Dr. Coppinger as he performed a series of eccentric dumbbell exercises with two heavy chunks of bristly rock. Coppinger, a physician by trade and a man who treats health theories with religious fervor, seemed invigorated by the exertion.

After washing in a trickling stream at the bottom of the barranca—a luxury we rarely afforded ourselves—we sat by the water's edge to ration our dwindling supplies. Our food, unfortunately, had been dampened by a local Spanish newspaper, rendering it unpalatable. "We have little left," Coppinger noted grimly, "and much harder scrambling lies ahead."

Our destination was the *fonda* at Santa Brigida, ten miles away, where we had left our traps. Before departing, however, Coppinger insisted on photographing and measuring the current cluster of caves. His ambition was boundless; having already collected two brittle, dust-ridden mummies, he remained convinced that beneath the cliff face lay the ruins of a lost Guanche university. Armed with a crowbar and a fresh supply of film, we ascended the ridge, leaving the cool stream behind for the baking sun.

Accessing the caves required a perilous descent. Thirty feet below the ridge and fifty feet above the stream bed lay the cave mouths. Laddering from below was impractical, so we employed a method of descending vertically on a thin rope, rappelling from the cliff top into each opening. Coppinger, though brave, was portly and nearing fifty, making the climb grueling. I took the lead, guiding him down, but his insistence on personally inspecting every chamber—labeling one a prison, another a pottery factory—dragged on until the heat became oppressive.

Exhausted, sweating, and with hands raw from the rope, I finally urged him to abandon the search. Yet, Coppinger's enthusiasm was infectious. "Remember," he urged, "anything you find belongs to you. I claim nothing." Reluctantly, I agreed to check the final three openings.

Scaling the fissure to the last cave proved difficult; the rock overhung, forcing me to land awkwardly on my back without letting go of the rope. Inside, the atmosphere shifted instantly. Unlike the rough, natural dens of the previous caves, this chamber was architecturally refined. The walls were smoothed by cutting tools, angles cleaned, and the structure featured interconnected rooms. There were no inscriptions, only the silence of the deep earth.

After documenting the empty chambers, I moved to a wider ledge to recharge my flashlight. As I stepped onto the stone, a sharp crackle echoed, and the floor gave way beneath me. I scrambled back just as the ledge collapsed, but my weight had triggered a shift in the cavern's stability. Peering into the newly exposed cavity, I found it sealed with a substance that was not cement. Chipping away the layers revealed sheets of material composed of two distinct parts: a shiny, talc-like base coated with a dark, toffee-colored wax bearing intricate patterns.

Ignoring Coppinger's earlier assertion that the Guanches could not write, I realized the patterns resembled script. Assuming the contents were valuable, I hacked through the bottom layers with my knife, extracting a solid block of the sheets. It measured roughly twenty by fifteen inches, surprisingly light despite its density. Lowering the artifact to Coppinger via the rope, I descended to join him.

His reaction was immediate and explosive. "The most valuable find in the Canary Islands!" he cried, wrapping the damaged sheets in his coat with trembling hands. "You've shattered the beginning and end of a priceless history, but it is yours."

"It is your fault," I retorted. "You claimed there was no writing among these ancients."

"It is not Guanche work," Coppinger snapped, examining the talc. "There is no talc in this island. This is Egyptian, or something akin to it. And that wax? It is a unique mineral, perhaps asphalt. It is a marvel."

As we made our way back to Santa Brigida, Coppinger carried the bundle himself, refusing to tie it for fear of further damage. Upon reaching the *fonda*, we feasted on the finest provisions and drank the region's vintage wine. That night, Coppinger slept little, consumed by the discovery.

The following morning, he revealed the true nature of the find. "You have stumbled upon a manuscript of unparalleled historical value," he declared, though he lamented the damage caused by my extraction. "Those top sheets likely contained a unique account of the ancient civilization of Yucatan, a lost colony of the Atlanteans."

"Atlanteans?" I asked, skeptical. "Mythical figures from Herodotus?"

"They were real," Coppinger insisted, pointing to marginal illustrations of winged crocodiles and colossal bats. "And so was Atlantis, the continent that lay north of the Canaries. This book was written by Deucalion, an Atlantean priest or general, detailing the swamping of his world. He illustrated the text fully, allowing us to reconstruct tales of extinct species and civilizations."

"But how did it get here?" I asked.

"I made a mistake regarding the origin," Coppinger admitted. "The script misled me into thinking it was Egyptian. It was Deucalion. How it reached this cave remains a mystery, likely detailed in the pages I destroyed."

Over the next year, Coppinger dedicated himself to translating the wax-on-talc manuscript. Meanwhile, I returned to England, caught a hurried boat to Liverpool, and buried myself in business. It was not until a year later, reading a newspaper report that Coppinger had donated the sheets to the British Museum with an estimated value of ten thousand pounds, that I realized the extent of his actions.

Recalling his repeated assurances that the find belonged to me by right of discovery, I wrote a pointed letter. His response was cold and legalistic, admitting my claim only to suggest that publication profits would reimburse the damage. When I replied warmly, his solicitors intervened, severing direct communication.

Though I regret the acrimony, the situation has evolved. The ancient history is now published. The credit for the translation rests with Dr. Coppinger, while all revenue generated by the text shall go to the finder of the original sheets: myself. Should this arrangement change, it will be announced publicly. For now, this stands as the agreement between the discoverer and the scholar.
