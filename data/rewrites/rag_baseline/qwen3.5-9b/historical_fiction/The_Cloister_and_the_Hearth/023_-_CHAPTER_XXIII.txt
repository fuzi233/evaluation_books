Life and liberty, when secure, are invisible luxuries, taken for granted until the shadow of loss reveals their true worth. Like sunlight, their brilliance is unnoticed at noon's zenith, only appreciated in the twilight when fractured rays of topaz dance beneath the elm branches, fragile yet vivid against the encroaching gloom. Thus, Gerard and Margaret, trembling at every rustle of leaves that sounded too harsh, radiated a luminous gratitude as they drifted through the sanctuary of the woods, the phantom echoes of baying hounds and brutal voices still haunting their minds.

Yet, the illusion shattered when Gerard spotted crimson stains blooming on Margaret's ankles. "Martin! Martin! Help! They have wounded her—the crossbow!" he screamed, his composure fracturing.

"No, no!" Margaret replied, offering a smile meant to soothe his terror. "I am neither wounded nor hurt."

"But what is it, then, in Heaven's name?" Gerard cried, agitated beyond measure.

"Do not scold me!" Margaret blushed, her voice trembling. "Did I ever scold you?"

"No, dear Gerard."

"Well, then," she confessed, her tone shifting to one of desperate explanation, "Martin said the cruel dogs followed the scent of blood. So, thinking if I could but offer them a little red upon my shoes, they might follow me instead, leaving my Gerard free to go. I scratched my arm with Martin's knife—forgive me! Whose else could I take? Yours, Gerard? Ah, no. You forgive me?" she pleaded, her expression a mixture of love, fear, and fawning submission.

"Let me see this scratch," Gerard choked out, unable to hold back his emotion.

"There," he breathed, recoiling slightly. "I thought so. A scratch? I call it a cut—a deep, terrible, cruel wound." He shuddered visibly at the sight.

"She might have done it with her bodkin," the soldier muttered, his voice dripping with disdain. "Milksop! That sickens at the mere sight of a scratch and a little blood."

"No, no," Margaret insisted softly. "I could stare upon a sea of blood, but not upon hers. Oh, Gerard! How could you be so cruel?"

Margaret smiled, a look of ineffable tenderness softening her features. "Foolish Gerard," she murmured, "to make such a fuss of nothing." She flung her guilty arm around his neck. "As if I would not give all the blood in my heart for you, let alone a few drops from my arm." Under the weight of his recent peril, she wept upon his shoulder, driven by pity and love; he wept with her.

"And I must part from her," he sobbed, the reality crashing down. "We two who love so dearly—one must remain in Holland, the other in Italy. Ah me! Ah me! Ah me!"

At this, Margaret wept anew, but her tears were patient and silent. Instinct, sharp and unerring, guarded her heart alongside her selflessness. To voice her thoughts now would only deepen Gerard's agony of departure, so she surrendered to silence.

Suddenly, they emerged onto a well-worn path, and Martin halted. "This is the bridle-road I spoke of," he said, hanging his head low. "And there, lying ahead, is the hostelry."

Margaret and Gerard exchanged terrified glances. "Come a step with me, Martin," Gerard whispered urgently.

Drawing the soldier aside, he spoke in a broken voice. "Good Martin, watch over her for me! She is my wife, yet I leave her. Look, Martin! Here is gold—it was for my journey. It serves no purpose to ask her to take it; she would refuse. But you will take it for her, will you not? Oh, Heaven! Is this all I can offer her? Money? But poverty is a curse. You will ensure she wants for nothing, dear Martin? The burgomaster's silver is sufficient for me."

"Thou art a good lad, Gerard," the soldier replied warmly. "Neither want nor harm shall come to her. I care more for her little finger than for all the world; and were she naught to me, even for thy sake, I would be a father to her. Go with a stout heart, and may God be with thee, going and coming."

With unusual emotion, the rough soldier wrung Gerard's hand and turned his head away. For a moment, he hesitated to return to Margaret, but Gerard stopped him. "No, good Martin; pray, stay here behind this thicket, and turn your head away from us, while I..."

By this ruse, Gerard escaped witnessing the raw anguish of leaving the woman he loved, and Martin spared himself the sight of their mutual devastation. He saw nothing of the poor souls kneeling to renew before Heaven those holy vows cruel men had interrupted. He witnessed no scene of them clinging together as one entity, struggling to part, failing, returning, and embracing again like drowning creatures grasping at straws. He heard only Gerard's relentless sobbing and Margaret's mournful moans.

Finally, a hoarse cry pierced the air, accompanied by the patter of feet on the hard road. Martin sprang up to find Gerard running wildly, both hands clasped above his head in fervent prayer, while Margaret tottered backward toward him, palms extended in pitiful supplication, her cheeks ash-gray, her eyes fixed on empty space.

Gerard caught her in his arms, speaking words of comfort, but her mind refused to receive them. Only the sound of his voice caused her to moan and clutch him tightly, her body trembling violently. He lifted her onto the mule, wrapping his arm around her frame. Once strong as a boy, she had now become relaxed and powerless. Supporting her, he led her slowly and sadly home. She shed not a single tear, uttered not a single word.

At the edge of the wood, he dismounted her and commanded her to cross to her father's house. She obeyed immediately. Martin continued to Rotterdam; Sevenbergen was too stifling for him. Gerard, severed from the love of his life, moved forward like one in a trance.

At the little hostelry, he hired a horse and a guide, riding swiftly toward the German frontier. Yet, everything was mechanical; his senses felt dulled, as if viewing the world through a thick veil. Trees, houses, and men slid past him like ghosts. His companions spoke to him twice, receiving no answer. Only once did he cry out savagely, "Shall we never be out of this hateful country?"

After hours of riding, they reached the brow of a steep hill, with a small brook flowing at the bottom. "Halt!" the guide cried, pointing across the valley. "Here is Germany."

"Where?" Gerard asked, bewildered.

"On the other side of the stream. No need to ride down the hill, I trow."

Gerard dismounted without a word, retrieving the burgomaster's purse from his girdle. As the guide opened it, he remarked half-sulkily, "You will soon be out of this hateful country. Perhaps the one you seek will like you no better anyway. Though it be a church you have robbed, they cannot take you once you cross that bourn."

These words, at another time, would have earned the speaker an admonition or a blow. Now, they fell upon Gerard like idle air. He paid the lad in silence and descended the hill alone.

The brook was silvery, murmuring over pebbles varnished by the clear water. Gerard sat down, staring stupidly at them. Then he drank, washing his hot feet and hands in the icy current. The shock woke him. Rising, he took a run and leaped across the stream into Germany.

Even as his foot touched the strange soil, he turned abruptly and looked back. "Farewell, ungrateful country!" he cried. "But for her, it would cost me nothing to leave you forever, along with all my kin and kindred—and the mother who bore me, and my playmates, and my little native town. Farewell, fatherland—welcome the wide world! *Omne solum fortipatrat*."

With these brave words on his lips, he suddenly slumped, arms and legs weak, sitting down to sob bitterly upon the foreign earth. When the young exile had sat thus for a while, bowed down by grief, he rose and wiped the tears from his eyes like a man shedding a burden. Casting not a single glance behind to weaken his resolve, he stepped out into the wide world. His love and heavy sorrow left no room for vulgar misgivings. Compared with the tearing apart of his soul from Margaret, the prospect of walking to Italy in that rude age seemed trivial. All nations meet in a convent. Thanks to his good friends the monks and his own thirst for knowledge, he could speak most of the languages required on that long road.

"I will soon be at Rome," he told himself. "The sooner the better."

After walking a good league, he arrived where four serpentine country roads met, confusing many an inexperienced neighbor traveling between villages. Gerard took out the little dial Peter had given him, setting it in the autumn sun. By this compass, he steered unhesitatingly for Rome, inexperienced yet determined as a young swallow flying south—but unlike the swallow, wandering south entirely alone.
