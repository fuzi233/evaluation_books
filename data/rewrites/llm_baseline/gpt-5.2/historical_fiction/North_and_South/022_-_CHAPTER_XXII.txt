Margaret was admitted without delay; yet when the door shut behind her, the house seemed to hold its breath, as if it too listened for danger.

The drawing-room had been made ready again for company—chairs sheathed, tables swaddled in holland, a thin propriety pulled back over the disorder of daily life. Heat pressed in from the street. The windows stood half open, and the Venetian blinds cut the daylight into sickly strips: a hard grey glare thrown upward from the flags below, and, above it, a faint greenish wash as the slats tinted the sun. The light turned every reflection treacherous. When Margaret caught her own face in a mirror, it looked as if it had been steeped in ash—wan, hollow-eyed, and stranger-like.

She waited. No one came.

Now and then the air—though it did not stir a curtain—carried to her the far-off massed sound of people: a murmuring, a trampling, as if an enormous beast moved somewhere out of sight. Then, between one pulse and the next, the noise fell away into a stillness so deep it made the house feel deserted.

At last Fanny Thornton hurried in, flushed and fretful, with the sharp impatience of one who resented being made to fear.

“Mamma will come directly, Miss Hale. She begged me to apologise. Perhaps you know my brother has imported hands from Ireland, and it has irritated the Milton people beyond all bearing—as if he hadn’t a right to get labour where he could. And the stupid wretches here wouldn’t work for him; and now they’ve frightened these poor Irish starvelings so with their threats, that we daren’t let them out. You may see them huddled in the top room in the mill, and they’re to sleep there, to keep them safe from those brutes, who will neither work nor let them work. And mamma is seeing about their food, and John is speaking to them—for some of the women are crying to go back. Ah! here’s mamma!”

Mrs. Thornton entered with a stern darkness on her face, a look that made the room itself feel colder. Margaret, who had come only because Mrs. Thornton had once said, with brisk command, that she was to ask for whatever might be needed during Mrs. Hale’s illness, felt suddenly as if she brought a selfish request into a house on fire.

Yet she spoke quietly, her voice careful and low. She told of her mother’s restless suffering; of Dr. Donaldson’s recommendation; of the strange mercy of a water-bed, which could take the body’s weight as dough takes a hand—yielding without bruising, easing the perpetual ache. She made it sound practical, not plaintive. Still Mrs. Thornton’s mouth tightened while she listened, as if every word cost her patience.

Margaret ceased.

Mrs. Thornton did not answer. Her attention had slid elsewhere, to something beyond the walls. She started upright, face changing in an instant from suppressed irritation to alarm.

“They’re at the gates! Call John, Fanny—call him in from the mill! They’re at the gates! They’ll batter them in. Call John, I say!”

As she spoke, the sound Margaret had been hearing at intervals rushed near as if it had been waiting for permission. Tramp—tramp—tramp, a thickening of footsteps. Then the sharp rage of voices rose up from behind the wooden barrier outside. The great gates shook. The crowd, unseen, hit them as bodies become battering-rams: a surge, a recoil, then a more united drive. The very hinges groaned under it, and the wood quivered like a reed in a storm.

The women were drawn to the windows as if fear had its own fascination. Mrs. Thornton, the servants, Margaret—all stood peering down through slats and half-opened panes. Fanny returned from the stairhead with screams as if she were being chased, and flung herself, sobbing hysterically, upon a sofa.

Mrs. Thornton waited for her son. He was still in the mill.

At last he came out into the yard, visible through the distorted angles the blinds allowed. He looked up at the pale cluster of faces and smiled—briefly, as if courage were a thing you could pass from hand to hand. Then he locked the factory door and called for one of the women to come down and undo the house-door, which Fanny had fastened in her panic. Mrs. Thornton went herself.

His voice—well-known, controlled, used to command—seemed to taste in the air like blood to the angry multitude. Until that moment they had strained and struck the gates almost in silence, as if their breath was wanted for brute effort. Now, hearing him speak within, they gave a fierce groan that did not sound human: it rose from them as hunger rises from beasts denied their food.

Mrs. Thornton went white as she preceded him into the room. He entered a little flushed; his eyes shone, answering danger as if it were a trumpet-call. His face had a proud defiance that made him noble even where it did not make him handsome.

Margaret had always dreaded an emergency that would expose her as cowardly. Yet now, in real peril, with fear reasonable and close, she forgot herself; she felt only an intense sympathy, sharp as pain, for the struggle about to be fought.

Mr. Thornton came forward frankly.

“I am sorry, Miss Hale, you should have visited us at this unfortunate moment; I fear you may be involved in whatever risk we have to bear. Mother—hadn’t you better go into the back rooms? I’m not sure they may not have made their way from Pinner’s Lane into the stable-yard; but if not, you will be safer there than here. Go, Jane,” he added to the upper-servant.

Jane went, followed by the other women-servants, their skirts snatched up as if they ran through fire.

“I stop here,” said his mother. “Where you are, there I stay.”

And in truth, retreat seemed useless; the roar came now from behind as well as before. The crowd had swung around to the rear outbuildings and sent its threats into the house like a foul wind. The servants fled upward into garrets, crying and shrieking. Mr. Thornton smiled scornfully at the sound.

Then he looked at Margaret. She stood alone by the window nearest the mill; her eyes bright, her colour heightened, as if terror had poured a fierce life into her veins. Feeling his glance, she turned and asked what had been pressing in her mind from Fanny’s first words.

“Where are the poor imported workpeople? In the factory there?”

“Yes. I left them cowed up in a small room at the head of a back flight of stairs, bidding them run all risks and escape down there if they heard any attack made on the mill-doors. But it is not them—it is me they want.”

“When can the soldiers be here?” asked Mrs. Thornton, low-voiced but steady.

He took out his watch with the same composure he seemed to keep for everything. His thumb moved; his eyes calculated.

“Supposing Williams got straight off when I told him, and hadn’t to dodge about amongst them—it must be twenty minutes yet.”

“Twenty minutes!” Mrs. Thornton’s voice betrayed her for the first time.

“Shut down the windows instantly, mother,” he exclaimed. “The gates won’t bear such another shock. Shut down that window, Miss Hale.”

Margaret obeyed, then crossed to help Mrs. Thornton’s trembling hands. The blinds rattled faintly in their frames; the house seemed to vibrate with the waiting weight outside.

For a few minutes there was an odd pause, a listening lull. Mrs. Thornton stared at her son’s face as if she could read in it what the silence meant. But his features were set into rigid contempt. No hope and no fear showed—only resolve.

Fanny lifted her tear-streaked face.

“Are they gone?” she whispered.

“Gone!” he answered. “Listen.”

They listened. All of them heard it: the one great straining breath of the multitude; the slow creak of wood yielding; the wrench of iron; then, with a sound like something living being brought to its knees, the mighty fall of the ponderous gates.

Fanny staggered up, made two steps toward her mother, and dropped forward in a faint. Mrs. Thornton, by sheer will as much as by bodily strength, lifted her and carried her out.

“Thank God!” said Mr. Thornton, watching them go. “Had you not better go upstairs, Miss Hale?”

Margaret formed a “No” with her lips, but her voice was lost. The mob’s innumerable feet thudded under the very wall. The growl of their voices rose—low, deep, satisfied. Satisfaction, in such a sound, was more dreadful than baffled rage.

“Never mind,” he said, thinking to encourage her. “I am very sorry you should have been entrapped into all this alarm; but it cannot last long. A few minutes more, and the soldiers will be here.”

“Oh, God!” cried Margaret suddenly. “There is Boucher. I know his face, though he is livid with rage—he is fighting to get to the front—look! look!”

“Who is Boucher?” asked Mr. Thornton coolly, moving to the window to find the man she meant.

As soon as the crowd saw Mr. Thornton near the glass, they set up a yell—so fierce it sounded like an animal’s demoniac hunger for withheld prey. Even he drew back, one instant dismayed by the concentrated hatred.

“Let them yell,” he said. “In five minutes more—. I only hope my poor Irishmen are not terrified out of their wits by such a fiendlike noise. Keep up your courage for five minutes, Miss Hale.”

“Don’t be afraid for me,” she said quickly. “But what in five minutes? Can you do nothing to soothe these poor creatures? It is awful to see them.”

“The soldiers will be here directly, and that will bring them to reason.”

“To reason!” Margaret’s reply was swift, almost bitter. “What kind of reason?”

“The only reason that does with men that make themselves into wild beasts. By heaven! they’ve turned to the mill-door!”

“Mr. Thornton,” she said, shaking with passion, “go down this instant, if you are not a coward. Go down and face them like a man. Save these poor strangers, whom you have decoyed here. Speak to your workmen as if they were human beings. Speak to them kindly. Don’t let the soldiers come in and cut down poor creatures who are driven mad. I see one there who is. If you have any courage or noble quality in you, go out and speak to them—man to man!”

He turned and looked at her while she spoke. A dark cloud passed over his face. His jaw tightened.

“I will go,” he said at last. “Perhaps I may ask you to accompany me downstairs, and bar the door behind me; my mother and sister will need that protection.”

“Oh! Mr. Thornton! I do not know—I may be wrong—only—”

But he was gone.

Margaret followed, sick at heart and dizzy-headed, unbarred and then rebarred as he went out. She clambered up the stairs again and took her place at the farthest window. Through the shifting bodies and the blind’s slats she saw a thousand eyes converge. He stood below on the steps—she knew it by that collective direction, that sudden tightening of attention which made the crowd feel like one creature.

She flung the window wide. Many in the throng were mere boys, with the cruel thoughtlessness of youth. Some were men gaunt as wolves, made wild by hunger. She understood it—understood Boucher’s livid despair: starving children at home, the hope of higher wages, and then the rage of finding Irishmen brought to take the little bread their own children might have had.

If Mr. Thornton would only speak—if they could only hear a human voice from him instead of stony silence—it seemed it must help. For a moment there was a hush, inarticulate, animal-like, as if instinct told them to listen. Margaret tore off her bonnet and bent forward.

Whether he spoke or not, the instinct passed. The noise surged again—worse, wilder. Mr. Thornton stood with arms folded, statue-still; his face was pale with repressed excitement. They were trying to make him flinch, each urging the other toward some act of personal violence.

Margaret saw it like a flash: one touch, one blow, and everything would burst beyond control. In the back she saw boys stoop and pull off their heavy wooden clogs—the nearest weapons. She saw the gesture, the preparation, the spark poised over powder.

A cry tore from her throat, unheard. She ran.

Down the stairs, through the hall, her hands lifting the great iron bar with a force she did not know she possessed—she flung the door wide and stood before them, facing that angry sea. Her eyes struck them like reproach made visible.

The clogs halted mid-air. Faces, a moment ago so fell, turned uncertain, as if asking what this interruption meant. She was between them and their enemy.

She could not speak at first. She held out her arms, palms forward, until breath came.

“Oh, do not use violence! He is one man, and you are many—” Her words died into a hoarse whisper.

Mr. Thornton stood a little aside, as if jealous of anything that should come between him and danger.

“Go!” she tried again, and now her voice rose to a cry. “The soldiers are sent for—are coming. Go peaceably. Go away. You shall have relief from your complaints, whatever they are.”

“Shall them Irish blackguards be packed back again?” shouted a man with fierce menace.

“Never, for your bidding!” Mr. Thornton flung back.

In that instant the storm broke. Hooting filled the air; it seemed to shake the very bricks. Margaret did not hear it clearly. Her eyes were fixed on the boys whose hands had already armed themselves. She saw their aim settle. She knew where those clogs would fly.

Another second, and he might be struck down—he whom she had urged into danger. She thought of nothing but stopping it. She flung herself against him, put her arms around him, made her body a shield.

Still with his arms folded, he shook her off.

“Go away,” he said in his deep voice. “This is no place for you.”

“It is,” she answered. “You did not see what I saw.”

If she had hoped her sex might soften them—if she had imagined they would shrink from hurting a woman—she was wrong. Reckless passion had carried some too far; and it is often the savage boys, hungry for excitement, who lead a riot to blood.

A clog whizzed through the air. Margaret’s fascinated eyes tracked it; it missed. Nausea turned her cold, but she did not move. She hid her face against Mr. Thornton’s arm and spoke again, forcing her words out.

“For God’s sake! do not damage your cause by this violence. You do not know what you are doing.”

A sharp pebble flew—grazed her forehead and cheek. A blinding sheet of light seemed to burst before her eyes. She dropped, as if dead, against Mr. Thornton’s shoulder.

His arms unfolded at last; he held her for an instant—encircling her as if he could keep harm from her by the mere fact of contact.

“You do well!” he cried, his voice ringing out. “You come to oust the innocent stranger. You fall—you hundreds—on one man; and when a woman comes before you to ask you, for your own sakes, to be reasonable creatures, your cowardly wrath falls upon her! You do well!”

They fell silent while he spoke. What stilled them was not his scorn alone, but the thin dark-red blood that began to show, trickling down her temple and cheek. It was as though the sight woke them from trance. Those nearest the door edged away, ashamed. A movement ran through the crowd—backward, loosening.

Only one voice called out, trying to kindle their brutality again:

“Th’ stone was meant for thee; but thou wert sheltered behind a woman!”

Mr. Thornton quivered with rage.

Margaret’s consciousness returned dimly, as if she rose through deep water. He placed her gently on the door-step, her head against the frame.

“Can you rest there?” he asked.

Without waiting, he stepped down, slowly, into the midst of them.

“Now kill me, if it is your brutal will. There is no woman to shield me here. You may beat me to death—you will never move me from what I have determined upon—not you!”

He stood among them with arms folded, the same immovable posture as before.

But their retreat had begun, as blind and simultaneous as their rage had been. Perhaps the thought of soldiers coming helped; perhaps the pallor of her upturned face, her closed eyes so still, the tears caught in her lashes and spilling slowly; and beneath them, heavier than tears, the steady drip of blood. Even the most desperate—Boucher himself—drew back, scowled, faltered, and went off muttering curses.

Retreat shifted into flight, as such mobs do once they turn. The instant the tide broke, Mr. Thornton sprang back up to Margaret.

She tried to rise without his help.

“It is nothing,” she said, a sick little smile trembling on her lips. “The skin is grazed, and I was stunned at the moment. Oh, I am so thankful they are gone!” And then she cried, helplessly, as if tears were the only safe release left.

He could not share her relief. His anger had not cooled; if anything, it rose as danger passed. In the distance came the metallic clank and measured tread of soldiers—authority arriving five minutes too late to do more than remind the vanished crowd of how near they had been to consequence.

Margaret clung to the doorpost, unsteady. A film slid over her eyes. Mr. Thornton caught her as she swayed.

“Mother—mother!” he called. “Come down. They are gone, and Miss Hale is hurt!”

He carried Margaret into the dining-room and laid her on the sofa, carefully, as if she were a precious burden. When he looked upon her face—white now to the lips, purity made stark by blood—the knowledge of what she was to him struck so sharply that speech broke from him like a sob.

“Oh, my Margaret—my Margaret! no one can tell what you are to me! Dead—cold as you lie there, you are the only woman I ever loved! Oh, Margaret—Margaret!”

He started up, ashamed, as his mother entered. Mrs. Thornton saw nothing but her son, a little paler, a little sterner than usual.

“Miss Hale is hurt, mother. A stone has grazed her temple. She has lost a good deal of blood, I’m afraid.”

“She looks very seriously hurt—I could almost fancy her dead,” said Mrs. Thornton, genuinely alarmed.

“It is only a fainting-fit. She has spoken to me since.” Yet his body betrayed him; the blood seemed to rush inward to his heart. He trembled.

“Go and call Jane,” said Mrs. Thornton sharply. “She can find me the things I want. And you—go to your Irish people. I can hear them crying and shouting as if they were mad with fright.”

He went, but it was as if weights hung from every limb that carried him away from Margaret. He called Jane; he called for his sister. There would be womanly care, gentle attendance. Yet every pulse in him repeated the image of Margaret stepping out—placing herself in the very mouth of danger. Had she done it to save him? At the time he had pushed her away, gruff and blind, seeing only the risk she took. Now the thought burned.

He went to the Irish hands—crowded, frightened, some weeping, all clamouring that they would not stay, that they must be sent back. He tried to soothe them; tried to understand their broken phrases. But his mind skittered, returning again and again to her face.

Mrs. Thornton bathed Margaret’s temples with eau de Cologne. The sharp spirit touched the wound—one they had not at first perceived beneath the blood and hair—and Margaret’s eyes opened. Yet they were the eyes of one who wakes in a strange room from a fever: seeing, not knowing. Dark circles deepened beneath them; her lips quivered; then she slipped again into insensibility.

“She has had a terrible blow,” said Mrs. Thornton. “Is there anyone who will go for a doctor?”

“Not me, ma’am, if you please,” said Jane, shrinking back. “Them rabble may be all about; and I don’t think the cut is so deep, ma’am, as it looks.”

“I will not run the chance,” replied Mrs. Thornton. “She was hurt in our house. If you are a coward, Jane, I am not. I will go.”

“Pray, ma’am, let me send one of the police. There’s ever so many come up, and soldiers too.”

“And yet you’re afraid to go! I will not have their time taken up with our errands. They’ll have enough to do to catch some of the mob. You will not be afraid to stop in this house,” she added contemptuously, “and go on bathing Miss Hale’s forehead, shall you? I shall not be ten minutes away.”

“Couldn’t Hannah go, ma’am?”

“Why Hannah? Why any but you? No, Jane—if you don’t go, I do.”

Mrs. Thornton went first to the room where she had left Fanny, stretched upon the bed. Fanny started up at her entrance, wild-eyed with fear.

“Oh, mamma, how you terrified me! I thought you were a man that had got into the house.”

“Nonsense. The men are all gone away. There are soldiers all round the place, seeking for their work now it is too late. Miss Hale is lying on the dining-room sofa badly hurt. I am going for the doctor.”

“Oh! don’t, mamma—they’ll murder you.” Fanny clung to her mother’s gown. Mrs. Thornton wrenched it free with no gentle hand.

“Find me someone else to go; but that girl must not bleed to death.”

“Bleed! Oh, how horrid! How has she got hurt?”

“I don’t know. I have no time to ask. Go down to her, Fanny, and try to make yourself of use. Jane is with her; and I trust it looks worse than it is. Jane has refused to leave the house—cowardly woman! And I won’t put myself in the way of any more refusals from my servants, so I am going myself.”

“Oh, dear—dear,” cried Fanny, weeping; yet she prepared to go down rather than be left alone with thoughts of blood and violence in the very house.

She crept into the dining-room.

“Oh, Jane! what is the matter? How white she looks! How did she get hurt? Did they throw stones into the drawing-room?”

Margaret looked indeed white and wan, though her senses hovered near returning. The sick daze of the swoon made her miserably faint. She was aware of hands moving, of the keen reviving scent of eau de Cologne—a craving for it to go on without pause. But when it stopped for speech, she could no more open her eyes or ask for the soothing touch than those in death-like trance who hear the preparations for their burial and cannot stir to prevent them.

Jane paused in her bathing to answer.

“She’d have been safe enough, miss, if she’d stayed in the drawing-room, or come up to us. We were in the front garret and could see it all, out of harm’s way.”

“Where was she then?” Fanny asked, edging nearer by slow degrees, as she grew used to the pallor of Margaret’s face.

“Just before the front door—with master!” Jane replied, with meaning.

“With John! with my brother! How did she get there?”

“Nay, miss, that’s not for me to say,” said Jane, with a slight toss. “Sarah did—”

“Sarah what?” demanded Fanny, curiosity pricked sharp.

Jane resumed the bathing as if she would rather not speak what she knew.

“Sarah what?” Fanny insisted, impatient.

“Well, miss, since you will have it—Sarah was in the best place for seeing, being at the right-hand window; and she says, and said at the very time too, that she saw Miss Hale with her arms about master’s neck—hugging him—before all the people.”
