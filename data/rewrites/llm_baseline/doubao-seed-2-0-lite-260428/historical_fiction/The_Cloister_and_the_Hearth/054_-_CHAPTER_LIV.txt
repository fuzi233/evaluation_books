When Margaret stirred back to full consciousness, her head rested heavy on Catherine's broad, warm arm. The honest, unfeuding half of the household that had once greeted her as a hostile interloper crowded round her, uttering rough, homely words of comfort—none louder than Giles, who roared at her to stop carrying on so piteously. "Gerard’s alive and well, isn’t he? Else he could never have penned this letter, the biggest any man alive has ever laid eyes on—well, no, the beautifullest, most moving, smallest scrawl ever stitched onto paper, that’s what it is."

"Aye, good Master Giles," Margaret sighed feebly, "he was alive when he wrote it. But how am I to know what harm has befallen him since? Oh, why did he ever leave Holland to wander among strangers fierce as lions? And why did I not drive him away from me sooner, rather than tear him from his own flesh and blood? Forgive me, you that are his mother!"

She shifted gently to pull free of Catherine’s arm, and made a wobbly attempt to slide off the chair onto her knees—though after a brief, lopsided tussle with Catherine’s far sturdier frame, she found herself pulled tight to the older woman’s bosom instead. Margaret held the letter out to Eli then, her voice faint but soft as clover honey. "I will trust it from my hands now. Truth to tell, I am far too worn to read another line—and loth to leave this comfort," she finished, looping her free arm around Catherine’s neck.

"Read it, Richart," Eli said, pushing the letter toward his eldest son. "Your eyes are younger than mine."

Richart took the sheet, and whistled low. "I never saw writing like this. Looks as if he drew every line with a needle’s point, and clear as a bell too. What in heaven’s name is he doing vagabonding out there in the wide world, when he could be making his fortune in my Amsterdam counting house?"

The letter picked up in Gerard’s own voice:
*"When I came to myself, I was seated in the litter, and that kind merchant holding fast to my hand. I babbled I know not what nonsense for a while, then shuddered silent. He pressed a horn of spiced wine to my lips."*

"Bless him! Bless that good man!" Catherine cried.
"Whisht, woman," Eli chided gently.

*"And I told him all that had befallen. He insisted on seeing my leg: it was sprained so sore the ankle was swollen tight, and all the laces of my hose had snapped, so I could scarce hold them up. 'Sir,' I told him, 'I fear I shall be nothing but a burden to you now, and I can make no sweet music for you either—my poor psaltery, that has been my companion over so many weary leagues, is broken.' And I grieved so over that shattered music box I could barely speak. But he patted my cheek, bade me not fret, propped my leg up soft on a pillow, and tended me like my own good father ever could."*

"January 19. I sit in the litter all day, for we push forward with all haste, and each night that kind merchant tucks me straight into bed and will not let me turn a hand to work. How strange it is! No sooner do I stumble upon men that are no better than fiends, than God sends some kind soul to cross my path, lest I turn my heart from all humankind. Oh Margaret, how strangely mixed all people are! I feel a hundred years older than I was only three months ago. And would you believe it? Good Master Fugger has gone and bought me a brand new psaltery!"

"Eli, my man," Catherine declared, leaning forward, "if that merchant ever comes within ten miles of Tergou, we will buy a hundred ells of cloth from him, and not haggle over a single penny."
"That I will," Eli swore.

While Richart paused to lick his thumb and turn the page, Kate glanced at her mother, blushed faint, and pulled a half-finished piece of needlework from under her apron, bending low over it to sew. Catherine followed suit, drawing her own half-done work from her pocket and stitching in silence. Both of the surreptitious little pieces they had hidden were baby caps, more than half complete, soft and delicate and shaped like tiny monks’ cowls, and the two women never spoke a word of what they meant.

Richart cleared his throat and resumed.
*"January 20. Laid up in the litter, almost as good as blind from the throb of my ankle, but when we halted to bait the horses, the plains of Lombardy burst full into view. Oh Margaret! It is a land flowing with milk and honey: sloping green meadows, stately rivers, laughing orchards and blooming gardens. It is midwinter here, but the air feels warmer than beloved Holland at the height of midsummer, and the sight of such soft, smiling earth makes the wanderer’s face glow and his heart leap for joy. Here are vines, cedars, olive trees, and cattle in plenty, though you will see three goats to every sheep. The draught oxen wear little white linen wrappings about their necks, and standing beside the dark green olive trees, every one of them looks like a painted picture. The folk, especially the women, wear fine straw hats trimmed with silk flowers and leaves mixed with threads of silver, so cleverly wrought you can scarce tell them from the real thing. Today we crossed a river on a chained ferry boat: there was a windlass on either bank, and one single man turned the handle to pull our whole party across the water. I stared in wonder, never having seen the like before. Some country folk crossed with us, and an old woman, spying a pretty young maiden, hid her face behind her hand and held out her crucifix as a knight would hold his sword in a tourney, fearing the evil eye."*

*"January 25. Safe at last in Venice. You have heard our mariners talk of its strange, surpassing beauty a hundred times, you and I. Do you remember how Peter would rattle on about it for hours while we snuck treats under the tavern table, telling us of this peerless sea-throned city shaped like a bow, its great grand canal, palaces built on wooden piles, its waterways crowded with gilded boats, and that market place for all the nations—orbis, non urbis, forum, St Mark’s Square? And the statue of the saint with jewels glowing in his eyes, and the great lion at his gate? But here I lie at my window with my aching leg, and have seen none of these wonders yet, only a dull paved street and houses patched with oiled paper and linen instead of glass, the passersby with all their strange gestures and odd habits. So instead of staring out the window, I turn my eyes inward, and bind up all the wisdoms I have gleaned from my travels in this letter, to share with you. For I love you so well that no treasure pleases me if I cannot share it, and no treasure is half so lasting or good as knowledge. This, then, I have learned, footsore traveler that I am: every nation has its own wisdom, and its own folly. If only a great king or duke could tramp the roads as I do, and see all with his own eyes, he could pluck the flowers of every land and cast the weeds aside, and lead his own people up the hill of true Wisdom.
The Germans in the north are churlish, but frank and honest; those in the south are kindly and honest too. Their great flaw is drunkenness: they carry it so far they mock and despise any man who stays sober. They have a common saying: 'If you cannot eat and drink your fill, you can never serve your master well.' In England, the common folk drink just as deep, but the gentry think excess a shame: they raise a health or two for courtesy, not gluttony, and always sweeten their wine. The Germans drink in silence, no mirth, no talk, just crying 'Be merry!' as they ply each other with cups. The best of their drunken games is 'Kurlemurlehuff', a drinking ritual where you tap your glass, your beard, and the table in perfect turn, punctuated with whistles and clicks of the fingers, so cleverly choreographed it is a labour of Hercules, but a sight to make any onlooker laugh until their sides ache. Their topers, on the advice of local leeches, sleep with pebbles in their mouths: they say a boiling pot must have its lid ajar to let off steam, and so do these walking wine casks, to vent the heat in their bellies. Even so, many drop dead suddenly from drink—but it is a point of custom to gloss over it, and blame some innocent small ailment for their death. Yet this is more custom than nature: their women walk among the drinkers, but only pause a moment to press their lips to the cup, and are the most temperate, modest, virtuous women in the world, true spouses and true friends to their husbands—far better than our Holland lasses, who will propose to the man themselves when they are maids, and lord over their husbands the moment they are wed. Why, there is a wife right here in Tergou, not far from our door: a man came looking for her husband one afternoon, and she said 'You will not find him. He asked my permission to go out, and I gave it to him.'"

"Truth! Every word of it!" Catherine burst out. "That was Beck Hulse, Jonah's wife. That comes of a woman wedding a boy too young for her."

*"In the south where wine grows, the gentry will drink themselves right out of their fortunes—but not in the north: with beer in their cups, a nobleman will sooner burst his own body than sell a single acre of his land. They are quarrelsome, but only because of the liquor, not their nature: they hold no grudges, and if they make a foolish bargain drunk, they will stand to it honour bright when sober. They keep their windows polished bright, and judge a man by the clothes he wears. Any fruit or grain or herb growing by the roadside, you may pluck and eat, and the owner will greet you with a welcome. But if you pluck a wayside grape, your very life is in danger: that is fruit Heaven made to make men drunk, not to eat. The French speak far fairer than the Germans, but are not half so true of heart. Sweet words cost them nothing. They call it 'paying in blank.'"*

"The rogues! Ha ha!" Denys laughed, slapping his thigh.

*"Yet courtesy runs in their very blood. They say to every stranger 'Pray do me the favour of sitting down.' Those small turns of speech show you exactly what the land is like. If you are at a public show and wish to leave your seat but keep it for your return, all you need do is tie your napkin around the bench, and no man or woman in France will dare sit there. They will guard the spot for you."*

"Gramercy! That is proper manners. France for me!" Catherine said approvingly. Denys stood and placed his hand gracefully over the gorget of his breastplate in a little mocking bow.

*"Yet they shout shocking, unkind things in jest: 'The devil take you!' 'Go to the devil!' and the like, though I do not believe they ever mean such dreadful curses—no doubt it is nothing but empty custom. They are temperate in their drinking, mix water with their wine, and sing and dance over their cups, so that they are enchanting company. They will never drink from another man’s cup. In war, the English beat them on the open field, but the French outdo them all in attacking and defending cities: witness Orleans, where they besieged their besiegers and cut them to pieces with double and triple culverines, and a hundred other sieges this century. More than any other nation, the French flatter their women, and despise them behind their backs—even if a woman is their sovereign ruler, they hang their female criminals instead of drowning them decently, as other nations do. The furniture in their inns is carved walnut, while in Germany you will only get plain deal planks. French windows are absurd: the lower half is wood that opens, the upper half is fixed glass, so the servant can never reach it to wipe it clean. The German windows are all glass, and moveable, so they gleam like diamonds from far off. Many humble houses in France have no glass at all. Once I saw a Frenchman walk right past a church without doffing his cap. I never saw that in Holland, Germany, or Italy. At most French inns, the host will show you your sheets before you get into bed to prove they are clean, and warm them at the fire right in front of you: a very laudable custom. They greet you kindly as a guest, then most of the time they cheat you, and sometimes cut your throat. They say their cruel, tyrannous laws are to blame for it—and it is true, their laws stick their nose into every platter and their fingers into every pie. In France, noblemen wear their hats and furs indoors, and go out lightly clad. In Germany, they wrap their furs and don their hats to go outside, but sit bareheaded and in thin linen around their indoor stoves.

The French never mix men and women together in assemblies, as we Hollanders do. At their church services, the women sit in rows on their heels, and the men stand behind them. Their harvests are rye, flax, and wine. You will see three mules to every horse, and whole flocks of sheep as black as coal. In Germany, the snails are red. I do not lie. The French pay for minstrelsy, but make their own jests and their own mirth. The Germans keep hired fools in ear-caps, who make them laugh by pretending to be mad—though madness is a thing that deserves pity, not laughter. In that, the lighter French nation is far wiser than the grave German, what say you? Alas, you cannot answer me now.

In Germany, the petty local laws are wondrous wise and just. The laws against criminals are bloody. In France they are bloodier still, and carried out just a little more cruelly. The breaking wheel is common there, and the fiery stake, and under their present king they drown men by the score in the Seine river. But the English are just as quick to hang and drown folk for small offences, as all travellers report. At the end of it all, a true-hearted Frenchman, when you find one, is as near perfect a man as you will find on all the earth—and that is my Denys, for all his foul mouth."*

"Foul mouth? Is that what it says there, Master Richart?" Denys asked, leaning over his shoulder.
"Aye, true enough, see for yourself." Denys inspected the letter gravely. "I do not read it that way."
"How then?"
"Humph! Ahem. Why, it reads just the opposite." He shrugged, grinning. "Tricky work, reading these black scratches men agree to call words. I reckon you clerks are still guessing half the time, eh? My foul mouth! That is the first I ever heard of it. Eh, ladies?" The women did not rush to reassure him loudly. Margaret blushed and said nothing, while Kate and Catherine bent their heads over their sewing, hiding sly little smiles. Denys studied their faces for a long, careful moment, and appeared fully satisfied. He turned back, huffing with the air of a man wronged but patiently forgiving, and bade Richart read on.

*"The Italians are a polished, subtle people. They judge a man not by his habits, but by his speech and gestures. A rough country boor can never pass as a noble gentleman there, as I did in Germany when I wore my fine servant’s clothes. They are the wisest of all nations in their extreme temperance with food and drink. They are the most foolish, though, for stopping strangers at their borders to search their bags and forbid them bringing much coin into the country. They ought to invite money in, instead of stopping travellers from bringing it. And here in Venice, the ladies dye their black hair yellow with sun and art, to think themselves wiser than He who made them. You cannot enter any Italian town without showing a bill of health, even though there is no plague anywhere in Europe now. All that fuss is nothing but a trick to wring extra coin out of strangers. Innkeepers fawn and cringe and cheat you, and in the countryside they will murder you in your beds. But they will give you clean sheets if you pay them well enough. They are very dainty eaters, and hate to dip their hands in the shared plate: they will use a crust of bread or a scrap of food to pick up a morsel, rather than touch it. They even tell of a cardinal in Rome who gives his guests a little two-pronged dagger for the left hand to hold their meat steady, while they cut it with their knife. And that, too, is men thinking themselves wiser than the God who made the human hand so supple and perfect.

Everyone here suffers from the itch, and salve for it, unguento per la rogna, is cried for sale on every street corner in Venice. From my window I saw one ragged urchin sell it to three fine ladies in silk gowns, and to two knights in velvet cloaks. Their bread is lovely white, but they ruin every meat dish by sprinkling cheese all over it—oh, perversity! Their salt is black, I swear it is. In trade, the Venetians are masters of land and sea, and govern their territories very wisely. They have only one great flaw, that I once heard a learned friar point out against Plato’s perfect republic: they openly encourage sin, and make the sex workers pay a tax to the state. Not content with honest, profitable cargoes of silk and spice, they trade in vice. There are twenty thousand of these women in Venice and Candia alone, pampered and honoured for drawing foreign visitors to the city, many living in their own princely palaces. I think the wise signors of Venice have forgotten what King David said: 'Except the Lord keep the city, the watchman waketh but in vain.' In matters of religion, they shift their sails with every wind, siding now with the Pope, now with the Turk, but always bowing to the god of traders, Mammon. Can a flower so cankered bloom forever?
