June the twenty-first—birthday day—broke up in a fret of cloud and wind, like a kettle that won’t come to the boil. But by noon the sky cleared its throat and steadied, and the house set itself to festivity as naturally as a kitchen sets itself to dinner.

Down in the servants’ hall we began, as always, with our little offerings to Miss Rachel. I delivered my annual speech as chief of the household below-stairs—a speech made on the same plan as great people use in opening Parliaments: you say near enough the same thing every year, and everybody listens as if they had never heard a word of it before. They grumble afterwards that it wasn’t new; then they grow hopeful for next year’s novelty. A simple governance, whether you rule Ministers or maids.

After breakfast Mr. Franklin Blake and I had our private conference—quiet talk, but not quiet minds—upon the Moonstone. The day had come when the jewel must be taken out of the bank at Frizinghall and put, by right and letter of the Colonel’s will, into Miss Rachel’s own hands.

Mr. Franklin was not at his best. Whether his sleep had been broken again, or whether he had been trying to press his suit with his cousin and had met discouragement, I can’t say. I only know that he turned the question of the Diamond as a cook turns a pancake—first this side, then the other—never satisfied it was browned as it should be. Twenty doubts in twenty minutes.

I held to the plain facts, like a man who knows his own pantry inventory. Nothing had happened to justify frightening my lady about the jewel; and nothing could alter Mr. Franklin’s legal obligation to deliver it. Twist and turn as he might, he ended where I began. We settled that he should ride to Frizinghall after luncheon, bring the Diamond back, and likely return in company with Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite and the two Miss Ablewhites, who might ride home with him.

With that arranged, he went back to Miss Rachel. The morning and a slice of afternoon were spent in their everlasting business of decorating the door—Penelope mixing colours on command, and my lady drifting in and out, handkerchief to her nose, trying to drive them from their labour. They used a deal of vehicle that day—strong stuff, sharp as vinegar, with a bite that climbed into the head and sat there, and a smell that overrode even honest kitchen odours. You could hear it, too, in a manner of speaking: the house’s usual hush had a thin, irritated edge to it, as if every footstep scraped.

It was three o’clock before they shed their aprons, loosed Penelope from service (the girl looking pale and queerly shiny about the eyes), and scrubbed themselves fit to be seen. They had done what they wanted: the door was finished on the birthday. Griffins, cupids, flowers, devices—beautiful, yes, but so crowded and so madly tangled that your brain felt as if it had been stirred with a spoon after you’d admired it. Penelope ended her part by being sick in the back-kitchen. I mention it not against Art, nor against the vehicle either. The stench leaves off when it dries. If Art asks a sacrifice, let it have it—even if the sufferer is my own daughter.

Mr. Franklin snatched a morsel at luncheon and rode off “to escort his cousins,” as he told my lady. To fetch the Moonstone, as was privately known to himself and me.

This being one of the high festivals when I take my post at the sideboard and command the waiting, my head was full of sober business while he was away. Wine to see to, men and women to review, the table-service to marshal—each thing in its place, like ingredients laid out before the fire is lit. When my arrangements were made, I retired a little to collect myself. A whiff of—you know what—and a turn at that certain book I’ve spoken of before, set my mind and body steady.

I must have slipped into something between thought and doze, for I was roused by the clatter of hoofs outside—a sudden burst of sound in the courtyard, the kind that makes a house’s very beams listen. The rhythm of it told you more than sight could: four riders, one with a groom’s seat behind, the whole company coming in brisk and not over-cautious. I went to the door and received them—Mr. Franklin with his three cousins, and one of old Mr. Ablewhite’s grooms attending.

Mr. Godfrey struck me as being out of his usual trim. He shook my hand kindly, admired me for “wearing so well,” and spoke as politely as ever; but there was a cloud over him. When I asked after his father’s health, he answered shortly, “Much as usual,” and looked away, as if the words had a taste he didn’t relish.

The two Miss Ablewhites, however, were cheerfulness enough for twenty. Great rosy creatures, yellow-haired and overflowing with health—so full of blood and spirits that the poor horses trembled under them. They jumped down without waiting to be helped, and bounced on the gravel as if they were made of india-rubber. Everything they said began with a large “O,” and everything they did ended with a bang. Their laughter was not mere noise; it was a kind of percussion, loud enough to drown the smaller sounds of the house—doors, whispers, the little breathing creaks of old wood—and for that reason I did not thank it. A house has its own proper music, and these young ladies played over it like brass over strings.

Under cover of their uproar I got a word with Mr. Franklin in the hall.

“Have you got the Diamond safe, sir?”

He nodded and tapped the breast-pocket of his coat.

“Have you seen anything of the Indians?”

“Not a glimpse.”

He asked for my lady, heard she was in the small drawing-room, and went there straight. In less than a minute the bell rang; Penelope was sent to tell Miss Rachel that Mr. Franklin wanted to speak to her.

About half an hour later, crossing the hall, I was stopped short by a burst of screams from the small drawing-room. I knew the tone of those screams—Miss Ablewhite’s favourite large “O”—so I was not alarmed; still, I went in on pretence of asking instructions about the dinner, to make sure no real mischief had happened.

There stood Miss Rachel at the table, like a person spell-bound, holding the Colonel’s Diamond in her hand. On either side of her the two “Bouncers” knelt, devouring it with their eyes and screaming in ecstasy whenever it answered the light with a new flash. At the opposite side of the table Mr. Godfrey stood clapping his hands like a child and singing out softly, “Exquisite! exquisite!” Mr. Franklin sat by the bookcase tugging at his beard, his gaze travelling anxiously towards the window. And at the window stood my lady herself, back turned upon the whole company, the extract from the Colonel’s will in her hand.

When I spoke, asking for my instructions, she faced me. I saw the family frown settling over her eyes and the family temper twitching at her mouth—those hereditary signs that come down like an old recipe, never altered, always reliable.

“Come to my room in half an hour,” she said. “I shall have something to say to you then.”

And she went out.

It was plain enough to me what worked in her mind. The same doubt that had troubled Mr. Franklin and me before—was the legacy of the Moonstone proof that she had wronged her brother cruelly, or proof that he was worse than she had thought? Serious questions, with her daughter standing there innocent as milk, holding the Colonel’s “birthday gift” in her hand.

Before I could leave, Miss Rachel stopped me, kindly, as she always was with an old servant who had been in the house since her birth.

“Look, Gabriel!” she said.

She held the jewel into a ray of sunlight pouring through the window, and the room changed as if a lamp had been uncovered in a dark pantry. Lord bless us—it was a Diamond indeed. As large, or near it, as a plover’s egg; and the light that streamed from it was like the harvest moon’s—yellow, deep, and drawing the eyes inward. When you looked down into it, you looked into a depth of colour that seemed to swallow seeing itself. The thing was small enough to hold between finger and thumb, and yet it had the air of being unfathomable, like sky.

We set it in the sun; then shut the light out of the room; and there it was again—shining of its own accord with a moony gleam, cold and dreadful in the dark. No wonder Miss Rachel was fascinated; no wonder her cousins screamed. The Diamond took hold on me too; I burst out with as large an “O” as any of the Bouncers.

The only one who kept his senses was Mr. Godfrey. He put an arm round each sister’s waist and, looking compassionately from the jewel to me, said, as if he were laying down kitchen-science,

“Carbon, Betteredge! Mere carbon, my good friend, after all!”

His object, I suppose, was instruction. The effect was to remind me of dinner. I hobbled off to my army of waiters downstairs. As I went, Mr. Godfrey—embracing his sisters and ogling Miss Rachel while he spoke—called after me:

“Dear old Betteredge, I have the truest regard for him!”

A convenient stock of love to draw on, that man had. Mr. Franklin was a savage by comparison.

At the end of the half-hour I presented myself in my lady’s room as ordered. What passed between us was, in the main, the same as what had passed between Mr. Franklin and me before—questions of the Colonel, of his will, of motives black or not black, and what was to be done with the gift he had sent into the house. I kept my own counsel about certain jugglers, for nothing had happened to justify alarming her on that head. When she dismissed me, I could see she took the darkest view possible of the Colonel’s intentions, and that she was bent on getting the Moonstone out of her daughter’s possession at the first opportunity.

Going back to my own quarters I was met by Mr. Franklin. He asked if I had seen Miss Rachel. I had not. Could I tell him where Mr. Godfrey was? I didn’t know; but I began to suspect, by the look in Mr. Franklin’s face, that cousin Godfrey might not be far from cousin Rachel. Mr. Franklin’s suspicions ran the same track. He tugged hard at his beard and shut himself into the library with a bang that had a world of meaning in it—an honest sound, at least, not like the polite noises people make when they mean something else.

I was not disturbed again in preparing for the birthday dinner until it was time to smarten myself for receiving the company. Just as I had got my white waistcoat on, Penelope came to my toilet, pretending to brush the little hair I had left and improve the tie of my white cravat. The girl was in high spirits and could hardly keep her news from spilling out, like sauce in an overfilled spoon.

She kissed the top of my bald head and whispered, “News for you, father! Miss Rachel has refused him.”

“Who’s ‘him’?” I asked.

“The ladies’ committee-man, father,” says Penelope. “A nasty sly fellow! I hate him for trying to supplant Mr. Franklin!”

I should certainly have reproved her for speaking so of an eminent philanthropic character—if she had not been at that moment tightening my cravat with all the force of her feelings in her fingers. I was never nearer to being strangled by my own child.

“I saw him take her away alone into the rose-garden,” she went on. “And I waited behind the holly to see how they came back. They went out arm-in-arm, both laughing. They came back separate, grave as grave could be, looking straight away from each other—no mistaking it. I never was more delighted in my life! There’s one woman in the world who can resist Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite, at any rate; and if I was a lady, I should be another!”

Here I should have protested again. But she had taken up the hair-brush, and all her triumph travelled into that. If you are bald you will know what I suffered; if you are not, thank God for your natural defence.

“Just on the other side of the holly,” Penelope said, “Mr. Godfrey came to a standstill. ‘You prefer,’ says he, ‘that I should stop here as if nothing had happened?’ Miss Rachel turned on him like lightning. ‘You have accepted my mother’s invitation,’ she said, ‘and you are here to meet her guests. Unless you wish to make a scandal in the house, you will remain, of course!’ She went on a few steps, then seemed to relent a little. ‘Let us forget what has passed, Godfrey,’ she said, ‘and let us remain cousins still.’ She gave him her hand. He kissed it—which I should call taking a liberty—and she left him. He stood a little by himself, head down, heel grinding a hole in the gravel; I never saw a man look more put out. ‘Awkward!’ he said between his teeth, when he looked up and went on to the house—‘very awkward!’ And the end of it is what I told you, father,” she cried, finishing me off with the hottest scarification of all. “Mr. Franklin’s the man!”

I got the hair-brush out of her hands and opened my lips to administer the reproof her language and conduct deserved. Before I could speak, the crash of carriage-wheels outside cut in and stopped me—the first of the dinner-company arriving, the sound rolling through the hall like a drum-beat. Penelope ran off at once.

I put on my coat and looked in the glass. My head was as red as a lobster; but in other respects I was as properly dressed for the evening’s ceremonies as any man need be. I got into the hall just in time to announce the first guests.

You needn’t feel particularly interested. Only the philanthropist’s father and mother—Mr. and Mrs. Ablewhite.
