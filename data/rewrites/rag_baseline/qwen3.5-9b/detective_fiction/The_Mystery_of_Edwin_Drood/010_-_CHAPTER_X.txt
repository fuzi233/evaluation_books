The air in the study was thick with the scent of wool and stagnation. Mrs. Crisparkle sat, her needles moving in a rhythm that suggested a prayer she had long since stopped believing in. Her son, Septimus, watched her hands. He saw the way the yarn tightened around the needle tips, a small, controlled violence.

"You are hard on Mr. Neville," Septimus said. His voice was flat, lacking the warmth of conviction.

"No," Mrs. Crisparkle replied. She did not look up. "I am not."

"Discuss it, Ma."

"I am open to discussion," she said. The phrase hung in the air, a polite shield. Internally, she added: *And I should like to see the discussion that changes my mind.*

Septimus smiled, a conciliatory gesture that cost him nothing. "Then let us discuss."

Mrs. Crisparkle knit harder. "I hope not, my dear. I am quite open to discussion."

"But if you take that line—"

"Blame Mr. Neville," she interrupted, her tone shifting from maternal to judicial. "Not me."

"Why him?"

"He came home intoxicated," she stated, reciting facts as if they were laws of physics. "He brought discredit to this house. He showed disrespect to this family."

"That is undeniable," Septimus conceded. "He is sorry."

"And yet," Mrs. Crisparkle continued, her eyes fixed on the growing fabric, "Mr. Jasper came to me next day. In the nave. With his gown still on. He expressed hope that I had not been alarmed. If he had not done that, I might never have heard of the disgrace."

Septimus paused. He had considered hushing the matter, a joint conspiracy of silence between teacher and student. But the girl's presence, the weight of her silence, had altered the equation. "If I had kept it from you," he murmured, "it would have been for your peace. For the good of the young men."

Mrs. Crisparkle crossed the room and kissed him. A quick, dry press of lips. "Of course, my dear. I am sure of that."

"It became town-talk," Septimus noted, rubbing his ear. "Passed out of my power."

"I thought ill of Mr. Neville then," she said, resuming her knitting. "I think ill of him now. I hope he comes to good, but I do not believe he will." The vibration in her cap increased, a tremor of finality.

"I am sorry to hear you say so," Septimus ventured.

"I am sorry to say so," she interjected, her hands continuing their mechanical dance. "But I cannot help it."

Septimus shifted the topic. "Undeniably, Mr. Neville is industrious. Attentive. Improving apace. And he has... an attachment to me."

"There is no merit in the last article," Mrs. Crisparkle snapped, her eyes darting to the china figurine on the table. "And if he boasts of it, I think the worse of him."

"He never boasted," Septimus argued gently.

"Perhaps not," she allowed. "Still, it does not signify."

She gestured to the china figure. "Ask yourself what he would be without his sister. You know her influence. Whatever he reads with you, he reads with her. Give her her fair share of your praise. How much is left for him?"

Septimus fell silent. He drifted into a reverie, a mental excursion to the rimy mornings at Cloisterham Weir. He remembered climbing the beetling fragment of monastery ruin, facing the wind at sunset. Below, the river flowed dark, reflecting the town fires. There, walking side by side, were the two figures: Helena, proud and fierce, and Neville, submitting to the fairy-bride. He had seen them pass, two studious shadows against the bleaker landscape. He realized then that in teaching one, he was teaching two. He had adapted his explanations to both minds, bridging the gap between the direct contact and the mediated approach. He thought of the gossip from the Nuns' House, of Helena learning from her sister what she knew. He wondered if these recent alliances had already become an integral part of his life, woven into the fabric of his own existence.

Whenever Septimus fell thus a-musing, his mother interpreted it as a need for support. She hurried to the dining-room closet.

It was a wonder of Cloisterham, a masterpiece of Minor Canon Corner. Above it, a portrait of Handel in a flowing wig beamed down, seemingly up to the contents, intending to combine all its harmonies in one delicious fugue. This was no common closet with a vulgar door. It possessed a lock in mid-air, where two perpendicular slides met. Pulling down the upper slide revealed deep shelves of pickle-jars, jam-pots, tin canisters, and vessels of blue and white porcelain.

The inhabitants were labeled in printed capitals. The pickles, dressed in rich brown double-breasted coats, announced themselves as Walnut, Gherkin, Onion, Cabbage, Cauliflower. The jams, wearing curlpapers, whispered in feminine caligraphy: Raspberry, Gooseberry, Apricot, Plum, Damson, Apple, Peach. Lower still, oranges awaited a mighty japanned sugar-box to temper their acerbity. Biscuits and plum-cake waited at the court of these powers. Lowest of all, a compact leaden vault held the sweet wine and cordials, issuing whispers of Seville Orange, Lemon, Almond, and Caraway-seed.

Every dipper among the shelves emerged mellow-faced, having undergone a saccharine transfiguration, as if the Cathedral bells and organs had hummed through the air for ages, sublimateing everything in store.

Septimus yielded himself willingly to this glorious cupboard, just as he did to the nauseous medicinal herb-closet on the upper landing. To what amazing infusions of gentian, peppermint, sage, and dandelion did his courageous stomach submit! He would swathe his rosy face in layers of dried leaves if his mother suspected a toothache, or stick botanical blotches upon his cheek if she convicted him of a pimple. Unlike the lamb led to slaughter, he bore nobody but himself. He would quietly swallow what was given, taking a corrective dip of hands and face into bowls of dried rose-leaves and lavender, emerging confident in the sweetening powers of Cloisterham Weir and a wholesome mind, while Lady Macbeth remained hopeless of the seas.

That evening, Septimus accepted his glass of Constantia with excellent grace. Supported to his mother's satisfaction, he applied himself to the remaining duties of the day. Orderly and punctual, they brought round Vesper Service and twilight. The Cathedral was very cold. After service, he set off for a brisk trot, ending in a charge at his favorite fragment of ruin, to be carried by storm without a pause for breath.

He carried it in a masterly manner, standing breathless before the view. He looked down upon the river. Cloisterham was near enough to the sea to throw up seaweed. An unusual quantity had come in with the last tide, mingling with the confusion of the water. Restless gulls dipped and flapped. Beyond the brown-sailed barges turning black, an angry light signaled a stormy night.

In his mind, he contrasted the wild, noisy sea with the quiet harbor of Minor Canon Corner. Then, Helena and Neville Landless passed below him. He had thought of them both all day. He climbed down to speak to them together. The footing was rough in the uncertain light, suitable only for a good climber. Septimus was as good a climber as most men, and stood beside them before many good climbers would have been halfway down.

"A wild evening, Miss Landless!" he called out. "Do you not find your usual walk with your brother too exposed and cold for the time of year? Or at least, when the sun is down and the weather drives in from the sea?"

Helena thought not. It was their favorite walk. Very retired.

"It is very retired," Septimus agreed, seizing the opportunity. "It is a place of all others where one can speak without interruption, as I wish to do. Mr. Neville, I believe you tell your sister everything that passes between us?"

"Everything, sir."

"Consequently," Septimus continued, looking to her and not to him, "your sister is aware that I have repeatedly urged you to make some kind of apology for that unfortunate occurrence which befell on the night of your arrival here."

"Yes," Helena replied.

"I call it unfortunate," Septimus resumed, "forasmuch as it certainly has engendered a prejudice against Neville. There is a notion about that he is a dangerously passionate fellow, of an uncontrollable and furious temper. He is really avoided as such."

"I have no doubt he is, poor fellow," Helena said, with a look of proud compassion at her brother, expressing a deep sense of his being ungenerously treated. "I should be quite sure of it, from your saying so; but what you tell me is confirmed by suppressed hints and references that I meet with every day."

"Now," Septimus said, in a tone of mild though firm persuasion, "is not this to be regretted, and ought it not to be amended? These are early days of Neville's in Cloisterham, and I have no fear of his outliving such a prejudice, and proving himself to have been misunderstood.
