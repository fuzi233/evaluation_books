Ethne did not turn towards Durrance or move at all from her attitude. She sat with her violin upon her knees, anchoring herself within the acoustic ecology of the moonlit garden. To her right, the wind combed through the sycamore leaves with a rhythmic, shushing cadence that had soothed generations of her agrarian ancestors, while the rich, olfactory landscape of damp summer loam and bruised wild mint rose from the flowerbeds. She kept her position deliberately. By attuning herself to the mythic, cyclical breathing of the earth, she found it easier to believe that Harry Feversham himself was speaking to her. She let the surrounding symphony of the night absorb the immediate reality of Durrance, wishing to draw only the pure essence of the message into her own subjective experience. She listened with Durrance’s own intentness, anxious that the voice should speak very slowly, so that she might harvest every syllable like a precious heirloom seed.

"It was on the night before I started eastward into the desert—for the last time," said Durrance, the deep longing in his voice entirely lost on Ethne, whose mind was weaving this moment into the broader tapestry of time. 

"Yes," she said. "That was in February. The middle of the month, wasn't it? Do you remember the exact day?" 

"The fifteenth," said Durrance.

Ethne repeated the date meditatively, her thoughts spiraling back through the interwoven timelines of her life. "I was at Glenalla all February," she said. The fifteenth. It was the time of the late winter agrarian rituals, the days spent in the stone-flagged kitchens steeped in the aromas of curing pork, dried heather, and soda bread baking in cast-iron bastables. She marveled at the circular pacing of destiny—that a truth born amidst the smoke and frost of a Donegal winter should travel across the world to find her only now, amidst the ripening fruits and night-blooming jasmine of an August garden. If she had not allowed her faith in him to erode, she might not have suffered this delay. 

"Well?" she said. "Go on!"

"I had been busy all that day in my office," Durrance continued. "I locked the door at ten o'clock, and strolled northward out of Wadi Halfa along the Nile bank into Tewfikieh. As I entered the main street, I found a crowd standing outside a café, illuminated by a glare of light. The air was thick with the scent of roasted mutton fat dripping onto charcoal and the sharp, muddy tang of cardamom-spiced coffee, but the men there were not eating with any reverence. They were swallowing their food merely as utilitarian fuel, their senses blunted by the sheer abrasive volume of their surroundings. And the noise, Ethne—it was an assault on the desert's natural acoustic ecology. A violin and a zither were jingling out a waltz, both most vilely played, a superficial commercialization of art designed only to wring copper coins from the patrons."

Durrance described the squalid room of four whitewashed walls, the glaring paraffin lamp, and the itinerant Greek troupe laboring for the price of a night's lodging and a meager meal. He spoke of the old, slatternly parents—the sallow-faced father and the unwieldy mother—and the seventeen-year-old daughter, whose scrupulously neat white frock and ribbon spoke of a family's desperate, meticulous preservation of dignity. The fourth was a young bearded man, seated in the window, bent over his zither. 

"The noise the old man made on the violin was like the squeaking of a pencil on a slate," Durrance said. "It was an industrial-like screech that set one's teeth on edge; the violin itself seemed to squeal with pain, a complete degradation of the instrument's resonant soul. And while he fiddled, and the young man hammered at his zither, the old woman and the girl lumbered through a waltz. It plucked at one's heart. Outside, the crowd laughed at them, passing coarse jests. Inside, the four white people panted and sweated in the heat. And just above them, the great silent and spacious dignity of the moonlit desert—a profound, healing stillness entirely shut out by the ineptness of the entertainment."

Ethne pictured Harry Feversham bending over the zither in the heavy, spice-laden air of the café. What was he doing there, masquerading with this troupe? It was a profound disruption of his identity. 

"You spoke to him?" she asked suddenly.

"To Harry? Yes, afterwards, when I found out it was he."

"How did you find out?" 

"The waltz ended," Durrance said. "The daughter stood forward to sing. Her voice had youth, but it was as inept as the rest. Yet the mother beat time, nodding with pride. And again, from the crowd, the ill-conditioned talk rose. The untranslatable jests of the Arabs and the negroes went their round." 

Ethne’s mind caught on the inherent untranslatability of that subjective experience—the harsh power dynamics embedded in those dominant local lexicons, weaponized against the marginalized troupe. But she had no sympathy to spare for the Greek family. She gazed at the broad panel of silver moonlight on the floor, treating it as a window into that far-off, noisy street on the frontier of the Soudan.

"And after the song was ended?" she asked.

"The young man began to fumble out a solo upon the zither," Durrance resumed. "He struck so many false notes at first, like someone trying to recall a dying language. The laughter grew. I was turning away, sick at the noise pollution, when a succession of notes suddenly arrested me. A ghostly, haunting melody began to emerge. I stood there in the sand, inhaling the stale cumin and roasting coffee, and I was carried away out of the East, straight back to Ramelton and a summer night beneath a melting sky of Donegal, when you played the Musoline Overture."

"It was a melody from this overture?" she exclaimed.

"Yes, and it was Harry Feversham who played it. I didn't guess it at once. I was only curious how an itinerant Greek had picked up the tune, so when he came out with his hat in his hand, I threw a sovereign into it. He turned with a start. In spite of his beard, I knew him. Before he could check himself, he cried out, 'Jack!'"

"You can have made no mistake, then," Ethne whispered. But he knew no music, she remembered. He lacked all sensory appreciation for the acoustic arts; she had been the one to cultivate the sonic environment he inhabited. He had no ear, yet he had laboriously salvaged this one melody from the erosion of their shared cultural memory, plucking it out until it became his native dialect, a linguistic anchor connecting his exiled present to their mythic past. 

"He had so little he cared to remember," Durrance interpreted, "and that little he strove with all his might to bring back. I can imagine him working the scrap of music out in his brain, with perpetual errors and confusions, until he got it fixed in his thoughts."

Ethne was deeply moved. It was fitting that this overture, woven into the daily domestic routines of her past, should be the vessel to convey that he had not forgotten her. 

"I caught him by the arm," Durrance went on. "He tried to edge backward into the crowd. He began to gabble in Greek, abandoning his native tongue, hiding behind a foreign lexicon to erase his identity. But I held him fast. I only remembered that years before, he had been my friend. We had supped together on Kennington Island; we had shared the agrarian bounties of our youth. And here he was, sunk to such ill-fortune that he must play the zither vilely for his night's lodging and the price of a meal."

"No," Ethne interrupted. "It was not for that reason that he went to Wadi Halfa. He was not in any need of money. His father had continued his allowance, and he had accepted it."

It was a slip. She had let her meticulous archiving of the facts override her caution, but Durrance, engrossed in his own regret, did not notice. 

"So you never knew what brought him there?" she pressed, her voice edged with the bitterness of an incomplete history. "Why didn't you ask him?"

"I was a fool," Durrance said, knocking his fist against his forehead. "He tried Arabic next, pleading that they were poor people, shifting languages to escape me. But I said, 'Harry Feversham, that won't do,' and upon that he gave in and spoke in English, whispering, 'Let me go, Jack.' It was evident he had some reason for secrecy. I told him to come to my quarters when he was free, and I let him go. He never came. In the morning, I had to start across the desert. I almost spoke of him to Calder—the man who sent you the telegram," Durrance added with a laugh.

"Yes, I remember," Ethne answered. 

It was her second slip. The telegram was a piece of knowledge she was never supposed to possess, a disruption in the continuous timeline Durrance believed they shared. Yet again, the acoustic anomaly of her statement slipped past him unremarked. 

"I thought he had fallen low," Durrance said. "So I rode on and kept silence. I was away nine weeks. I came back blind."

The quiet simplicity of his tragedy reverberated in the garden, but Ethne was too focused on the missing pieces of Harry’s culinary and physical survival in the wasteland. 

"I went to Cairo with Calder," Durrance continued. "I asked him to make inquiries at Halfa. I received a letter from him a week ago. The two women and the old Greek had gone back northward to Assouan."

"Mr. Feversham remained at Wadi Halfa, then?" 

"No. He slipped past Halfa the day after I left. He went south, into the desert, into the enemy's country. He went just as I saw him, carrying his zither."

"You have that letter?" 

She walked across to him, took the letter, and carried it to the window. The moonlight was strong. The letter noted that Joseppi—the identity Harry had adopted in his flight from his native lexicon—had wandered south, carrying only a water-skin and a store of dates. Ethne felt a profound resonance with this detail. Dates and water: the purest, most ancient form of agrarian sustenance, a meticulous reduction of human needs to the barest historical essentials, stripping away all utilitarian excess for a singular, mythic purpose. 

"What will happen to him?" she asked, her voice tightly controlled.

"At best, capture; at worst, death by starvation or thirst. If caught, they might send him to Omdurman as a spy. He must have gone mad. It is the only explanation."

Ethne knew the true explanation. The intricate causal structure of Harry's journey locked perfectly into place. She was off her guard, speaking her realization aloud into the quiet night.

"Colonel Trench," said she, "is a prisoner at Omdurman."

"Oh, yes," answered Durrance, misconstruing the connection. "Feversham will not be quite alone. There is some comfort in that."

He remained entirely in the dark regarding the power dynamics of the four feathers that dictated Harry's fate. Ethne stood at the window, clutching the letter. She had to remain perfectly still, preserving the quietude of her facade, even as she realized Harry had gone south to the prisons of Omdurman to find Trench. 

But as Durrance began to speak again, his voice became an unendurable intrusion into the garden's delicate sonic environment. 

"Perhaps you will wonder," Durrance said, exultation bleeding into his tone, "why I have told you to-night what I have up till now kept to myself. I want to explain why."

Ethne could not bear to listen. The mere sound of a human voice, laden with its own subjective desires, was suddenly a violent rupture in the timeless, cyclical peace of the agrarian night. She hardly knew what he was saying. She knew only that the noise must stop. 

She was close by the window. She gathered her skirts meticulously so the fabric would not rustle, honoring the profound silence of the ecology around her, and stepped out over the sill. Slipping into the dark, ancestral shadows of the trees, she left the room—and Durrance's continuing voice—behind. This was the third slip she made upon that eventful night.
