Some time ago, I believe I told you of an adventure that befell a friend of mine—Mr. Dennistoun—during his search for rare artworks to enrich the museum at Cambridge. He did not widely publish his experiences upon returning to England, but they inevitably reached the ears of many, including a gentleman who then oversaw an art collection at another university. Naturally, such a tale would stir the imagination of someone whose work mirrored Dennistoun’s own, especially one eager to dismiss any suggestion that he might ever face such a disturbing occurrence. It was somewhat comforting to him to reflect that his duties did not involve acquiring ancient manuscripts—those were the Shelburnian Library’s concern. His focus lay in expanding the already remarkable collection of English topographical drawings and engravings housed in his institution. Yet, as it turned out, even the most modest and familiar branch of art could harbor secrets—and one such secret was thrust upon Mr. Williams.

Anyone with even a passing interest in collecting topographical prints knows of one London dealer whose catalogues are indispensable: Mr. J. W. Britnell. He regularly issued excellent lists of engravings, plans, and old sketches of English and Welsh mansions, churches, and towns. These catalogues were, naturally, the foundation of Mr. Williams’s research. But since his museum already held a vast number of such works, he was more of a selective than a prolific buyer—content to fill gaps rather than seek rarities.

In February of the previous year, a catalogue from Britnell arrived on Mr. Williams’s desk, accompanied by a typewritten note:

> *We beg to call your attention to No. 978 in our accompanying catalogue, which we shall be glad to send on approval.*
>
> *Yours faithfully,*

For Mr. Williams, locating No. 978 was a matter of seconds. The entry read:

> **978.—_Unknown._** Interesting mezzotint: View of a manor-house, early part of the century. 15 by 10 inches; black frame. £2 2s.

It wasn’t particularly striking, and the price seemed excessive. Still, since Britnell—known for his expertise and discernment—had singled it out, Williams sent a postcard requesting the item on approval, along with several other engravings from the same catalogue. Then he returned to his daily tasks, untroubled by anticipation.

As usual, the parcel arrived later than expected—delivered by the afternoon post on a Saturday after Williams had left for the day. The attendant brought it to his rooms in college so he wouldn’t have to wait until Monday to examine it. That evening, when he returned for tea with a friend, he found it waiting.

The only piece of interest was the large, black-framed mezzotint described in the catalogue. Though I cannot convey its full appearance as clearly as it remains in my mind, I can say this: it bore a strong resemblance to countless similar prints still hanging in country inns or dusty hallways of old estates. It was a dull mezzotint—an amateurish effort, perhaps the worst kind of engraving. It depicted a modest manor house from the last century, three rows of plain sash windows framed by rusticated stonework, a parapet adorned with spherical finials, and a small central portico. Trees flanked either side, and a wide lawn stretched before it. In the narrow margin, the inscription read: _A. W. F. sculpsit_. No further title or date. The whole thing looked like the work of a dilettante. Why Britnell had priced it at two guineas was beyond Williams’s comprehension. He turned it over with disdain. On the back, a paper label had been torn in half. Only fragments remained: _ngley Hall_ on the first line, _ssex_ on the second.

He decided to identify the place—using a gazetteer, if need be—and then return it with a polite critique of Britnell’s judgment.

He lit the candles, made tea, and entertained his guest—a fellow golfer, Professor Binks—with whom he’d spent the afternoon on the links. Their conversation, predictably, revolved around strokes missed and luck denied.

It was then that Binks picked up the framed print and asked:

“What’s this place?”

“Just what I’m trying to find out,” said Williams, fetching a gazetteer. “Look at the back. Somethingley Hall—either in Sussex or Essex. Half the name’s gone. You don’t happen to know it?”

“It’s from Britnell, I suppose?” said Binks. “Is it for the museum?”

“Well, I’d buy it for five shillings,” replied Williams, “but he wants two guineas. I can’t imagine why. It’s a wretched engraving—no figures to give it life.”

“I don’t think it’s so bad,” said Binks. “The moonlight seems well done. And I thought there was a figure—just barely visible—on the edge in front.”

“Let’s see,” said Williams. “Well, yes—the light is cleverly handled. Where’s your figure? Oh! Just the head—right at the very edge.”

And indeed there was—barely more than a dark smudge—what appeared to be the head of a person, wrapped tightly, facing away, looking toward the house.

Williams hadn’t noticed it before.

“Still,” he said, “even if it’s better than I thought, I can’t spend museum money on a picture of a place I don’t know.”

Binks left soon after, and Williams spent the next hour or so fruitlessly searching the gazetteer. “If only the vowel before the _ng_ had survived,” he mused, “it would be easy. As it is, it could be Guestingley, Langley, anything. And this cursed book has no index of endings.”

Dinner at the college was at seven. He met colleagues who’d also played golf, and their talk was filled with sport-specific banter—nothing worth repeating here.

After dinner, some lingered in the common room. Later, a few retired to Williams’s rooms. Whist was played, tobacco smoked. During a lull, Williams picked up the mezzotint without looking at it and handed it to a mild art enthusiast, explaining where it came from.

The man took it casually, glanced at it, then spoke with genuine interest:

“It’s actually quite good, Williams. Has a real romantic feel. The lighting is superb, and though the figure is a bit grotesque, it’s strangely compelling.”

“Yes, isn’t it?” said Williams, busy serving whisky-and-soda. He couldn’t cross the room to see.

By now, it was late. Visitors began to leave. After they departed, Williams wrote letters, tidied papers. Finally, past midnight, he prepared for bed. He extinguished the lamp, lit his bedroom candle. The picture lay face-up on the table—left there by the last viewer. As he dimmed the light, something caught his eye.

He nearly dropped the candle.

What he saw defied reason—but was undeniable. Where there had been nothing at five o’clock that afternoon, now stood a figure in the center of the lawn. It was crawling on all fours, drawn toward the house, wrapped in a strange black garment bearing a white cross on the back.

I cannot say what one should do in such a situation. All I know is what Mr. Williams did. He grabbed the picture by one corner and carried it across the passage to a second set of rooms he kept. There, he locked it in a drawer, bolted both doors, and went to bed—after writing a detailed account of the change he had witnessed.

Sleep came slowly, but he took comfort in knowing he wasn’t alone in his testimony. The man who had seen it earlier must have perceived something similar—or else he might have questioned his own sanity. That possibility ruled out, two tasks awaited: verify the image carefully with a witness, and discover the identity of the house. He would invite his neighbor, Nisbet, to breakfast, and then spend the morning poring over guidebooks.

Nisbet arrived at 9:30. Williams was still dressing. At breakfast, no mention was made of the mezzotint—except that Williams had a picture he wanted Nisbet’s opinion on. Their conversation ranged widely, from cricket to tennis, as is typical among fellows of Canterbury College. Yet Williams was restless, his thoughts fixed on the image now lying face-down in the drawer across the hall.

Finally, the morning pipe was lit. The moment arrived. With trembling hands, Williams crossed to the drawer, unlocked it, retrieved the picture—still face-down—and hurried back, placing it into Nisbet’s hands.

“Now,” he said, “I want you to tell me exactly what you see. Describe it as thoroughly as you can. I’ll explain why afterward.”

“Well,” said Nisbet, “I have here a view of a country house—English, I assume—by moonlight.”

“Moonlight? You’re sure?”

“Certainly. The moon appears to be waning, and there are clouds drifting overhead.”

“All right. Go on. I swear,” Williams added under his breath, “there was no moon when I first saw it.”

“There’s not much more,” continued Nisbet. “The house has three rows of windows—five in each row, except the bottom, where there’s a porch instead of the middle window—and—”

“But what about figures?” Williams interrupted.

“There aren’t any,” said Nisbet. “But—”

“What? No figure on the grass?”

“Not a trace.”

“You’ll swear to that?”

“Absolutely. But there’s one thing.”

“What?”

“One of the ground-floor windows—left of the door—is open.”

“Really? My goodness—he must have gotten in!” Williams exclaimed, snatching the picture from Nisbet and confirming it himself.

It was true. No figure. But the window was open.

After a moment of stunned silence, Williams scribbled notes. He produced two documents: one, Nisbet’s description; the other, his own written the night before. He asked Nisbet to sign the first and read the second.

“What could this mean?” Nisbet asked.

“Exactly,” said Williams. “But I must do three things. First, I must ask Garwood—my visitor last night—what he saw. Then, we must photograph it. And finally, we must identify the house.”

“I can take the photo myself,” said Nisbet. “But… it feels like we’re witnessing a tragedy unfold. The question is—has it already happened, or is it about to?”

“I agree,” said Williams. “I think he’s inside now. And if I’m not mistaken, there’ll be hell to pay upstairs.”

“I’ll go speak to Green,” Williams decided. “He’s the senior Fellow—been Bursar for years. We own land in Essex and Sussex. He’s likely to recognize it.”

“Possible,” said Nisbet. “But let me photograph it first. Though… I suspect Green isn’t up today. He wasn’t in Hall last night. I think he said he was going down to Brighton.”

“That’s right,” said Williams. “He’s gone to Brighton. Well, if you photograph it now, I’ll go to Garwood and get his statement. You keep watch while I’m gone. Honestly, I’m starting to think two guineas isn’t too much for this.”

Soon, Williams returned with Mr. Garwood. His account confirmed that the figure had been near the edge, not far across the lawn. He remembered a white mark on the back—possibly a cross, but uncertain. A formal statement was drawn up and signed. Nisbet began photographing the print.

“What now?” he asked. “Are you going to sit and watch it all day?”

“No,” said Williams. “I think we’re meant to see the whole thing. Between last night and now, plenty could’ve happened—but the creature only got inside. It could’ve finished its business and left. But the open window suggests it’s still in there. So I feel safe leaving it. Besides, I suspect it won’t change during daylight. Let’s go for a walk this afternoon, return for tea when it gets dark. I’ll leave it on the table, and lock the door. My servant can enter, but no one else.”

They agreed. They would avoid speaking of it to others—lest the Phasmatological Society descend upon them.

We may pause until five o’clock.

At or near that hour, the three men ascended Williams’s staircase. They were startled to find the door unlatched—until they recalled the servants came earlier on Sundays. But a greater shock awaited them.

The picture leaned against a stack of books on the table, just as left. And beside it sat Williams’s servant, Robert Filcher—usually a model of decorum—staring at the engraving with palpable horror.

He jumped when they entered, rose stiffly, and apologized:

“I beg your pardon, sir, for taking such liberty as to sit.”

“Not at all, Robert,” Williams interjected. “I meant to ask you what you thought of that picture.”

“Well, sir,” said Filcher, “I don’t claim to judge art, but I wouldn’t hang it where my little girl could see it.”

“Why not?”

“She once saw a Door Bible with pictures half as bad as that,” he said, “and we had to tend to her for three nights. If she saw this skeleton—whatever it is—carrying off the child, she’d be taken ill. Children are so sensitive. And I’d say… it doesn’t seem right to leave such a picture lying about. Not where anyone easily startled might come upon it. Shall I fetch anything this evening, sir? Thank you, sir.”

With that, the faithful man left. The three men gathered round the engraving.

The house stood beneath the waning moon, clouds drifting above. The window was closed. And the figure—now upright—was walking swiftly toward the front of the image, long strides, arms tightly wrapped around an indistinct form: a child, alive or dead, impossible to say. The drapery hung low, obscuring the face—only a pale, dome-shaped forehead and straggling hair visible. The legs were unnaturally thin.

From five to seven, they watched in silence. The image did not change. They decided to leave it, returning after dinner to await further developments.

When they returned, the figure was gone. The house stood quiet under the moonlight.

There was nothing to do but consult guidebooks. At 11:30 p.m., Williams found the answer in Murray’s _Guide to Essex_:

> *16½ miles, Anningley. The church, originally Norman, was classicized in the last century. It contains the tomb of the Francis family, whose mansion, Anningley Hall—a solid Queen Anne house—stands beyond the churchyard in an 80-acre park. The family is extinct. The last heir vanished mysteriously in infancy in 1802. The father, Mr. Arthur Francis, was known locally as a talented amateur mezzotint engraver. After his son’s disappearance, he lived in seclusion at the Hall, and was found dead in his studio on the third anniversary of the tragedy—having just completed an engraving of the house. Impressions of this engraving are extremely rare.*

This was it. When Mr. Green returned, he confirmed the identification.

“Any explanation for the figure, Green?” Williams asked.

“I don’t know, Williams. But what used to be said—when I knew the area before coming up here—was this: Old Francis hated poachers. He’d expel anyone he suspected, until only one remained—a man from an ancient family, possibly the last Lords of the Manor. Squires could do such things then.”

“Like the man in _Tess of the Durbervilles_?” Williams asked.

Green nodded grimly.

“And that man—well, he didn’t take kindly to being driven off. But he never raised a hand. Until the night the baby disappeared.”

They sat in silence.

The picture remained on the table, untouched.

And no one dared look at it again.
