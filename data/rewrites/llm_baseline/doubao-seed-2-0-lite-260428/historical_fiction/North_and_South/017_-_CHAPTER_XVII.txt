Margaret stepped out of her front door heavy of foot and heavy of heart, dragging her feet as if she had a dozen weights tied to her ankles. But the length of the first Milton street, the sharp, coal-kissed air stinging her cheeks, soon roused the young blood in her veins before she had turned even the first corner. Her step lightened, the cold wind flushed her lips a bright, warm red, and she lifted her gaze from the paving stones to the world around her, no longer trapped entirely within the dark circle of her own anxieties.

She spotted more loiterers in the lanes than was usual for midday: men with their hands stuffed deep in their coat pockets, sauntering slow with no loom to tend, clusters of girls cackling loud, their speech sharp and unguarded, bubbling over with a wild, unshackled high spirits Margaret had never witnessed in the quiet south. The roughest, ragged fringe of the men hung slouched on the steps of every beer-house and gin-shop, pipes dangling from their lips, calling out unapologetic comments to every passerby. Margaret’s plan for a long, quiet walk out to the green fields at the edge of town died in her throat; she turned instead toward the Higgins cottage. It would not be half so refreshing as a country ramble, but it would be far kinder.

Nicholas Higgins was sat by the cottage fire, puffing at his pipe, when she stepped over the threshold. Bessy rocked slow in her chair on the opposite side of the hearth, her shoulders hunched. Nicholas pulled the pipe from his mouth, stood abruptly, and pushed his own armchair toward Margaret before leaning one elbow against the mantelpiece, lazy and at ease, while Margaret bent to greet Bessy.
“She’s low in spirits, sure enough, but her body’s a touch stronger today,” Nicholas said. “Hates this strike, does our Bessy. Will have peace and quiet at any cost, that lass.”
“This is the third strike I’ve lived through,” Bessy sighed, as if that single line explained every heavy thought weighing on her.
“Third time pays for all, eh?” Nicholas grinned, tapping his pipe against his palm. “This time we’ll beat the masters proper, see if we don’t. They’ll come crawling to us, begging us to come back to work on our own terms. We messed the first two up, I’ll grant you that, but this time our plans are laid deep, desperate deep.”

“Why do you strike, exactly?” Margaret asked, curious. “I never heard of such a thing where I grew up, in the south. Striking is leaving work until you get the wage you ask for, is that right? You mustn’t think me daft for not knowing.”
“I wish I’d grown up there,” Bessy murmured, so tired her voice was barely a breath. “This’ll be the last strike I ever see. Before it’s over, I’ll be in the Great City, the Holy Jerusalem.”
“She’s got her head full of the next world, can’t be bothered with the mess of this one,” Nicholas chuckled. “Me, I’m for making the best I can of what’s in front of me. A bird in the hand’s worth two in the bush, that’s what I say. That’s where we split on this strike, see.”
“But down south, most folk are farm labourers,” Margaret pressed on. “If they decided to strike, no one would sow the seed, no one would bring in the hay, no one would reap the corn come harvest.”
Nicholas puffed at his pipe, lifted an eyebrow in a wordless “well?”
“Then what would the farmers do?” Margaret finished.
He went on puffing for a long minute, the smoke coiling up to the cottage ceiling. “I reckon they’d either give up their farms, or pay a fair day’s wage.”
“Suppose they can’t, or won’t do that,” Margaret said. “They can’t abandon their farms overnight, no matter how badly they want to. They’d have no hay or corn to sell that year, no money to pay their labourers even if they wanted to, come next season.”
Still Nicholas puffed. At last he grunted. “I know nought of your soft southern ways. I’ve heard tell your folk are a pack of spiritless, trodden down wretches, half starved to death, too dazed with hunger to even notice they’re being cheated. Not us. We know when we’re being put upon, and we’ve too much fire in our blood to stand it. We just pull our hands out of our looms and say ‘You can starve us, but you won’t grind us down any further, masters!’ And we’ll be damned if they will, this time.”
“I wish I lived down south,” Bessy repeated.
“There’s hardship down there too, Bessy,” Margaret said gently. “Sorrow lives everywhere. Farm work is backbreaking labour, done on barely enough food to keep your strength up.”
“But it’s out of doors,” Bessy protested. “Away from that endless, endless clatter of the looms, that stinking, suffocating heat in the mill rooms.”
“Sometimes it’s pouring rain, sometimes it’s bitter, biting cold,” Margaret went on. “A young body can stand it, but old men get twisted up with rheumatism, bent and withered long before their time, and they still have to work just the same, or they get thrown in the workhouse.”
“I thought you were all for the south and its quiet green ways,” Bessy said, a little surprised.
Margaret smiled, sheepish. “I am. But I mean, there’s good and bad in every corner of this world. You’ve only seen the bad side of Milton, so I thought it only fair you know the bad side of the country you dream of, too.”
“And your folk never strike, down there?” Nicholas asked, sharp.
“No,” Margaret said. “I think they’ve too much sense.”
“Too much sense?” He smashed the ash out of his pipe so hard against the hearthstone the earthen bowl snapped clean in two. “It’s not sense they’ve got too much of, it’s too little backbone!”
“Oh father!” Bessy cried. “What did the last strike ever get us? Remember the first one, when mother died? We all nearly starved, you worst of all. And every week some poor soul snuck back to work for the old lower wage, till there was no work left for anyone who’d held out, and some of them were beggars for the rest of their days.”
“Aye, that strike was badly run,” he waved her off. “Fools and traitors got their hands on the management of it. This one’s different, you’ll see.”
“You still haven’t told me exactly what you’re striking for,” Margaret reminded him.
“Why, half a dozen of the masters have decided they don’t want to pay the same wages they’ve paid for the last two years, years when they raked in fortunes, got richer and richer off our work. Now they come to us and say we’ve got to take less. We won’t. We’ll starve them out first, see who’ll work for them then. They’ll have killed the goose that laid them the golden eggs, mark my words.”
“So you plan to starve yourselves just to get revenge on them?”
“No,” he shook his head. “I’m planning to stand my post, same as a soldier, before I yield. Folk call that noble and brave when a soldier does it, don’t they? Why can’t a poor weaver get the same honour?”
“But a soldier dies for his country, for other people, not just for himself,” Margaret said.
He gave a grim, dry laugh. “Lass, you’re still young, but do you really think I could feed three people — Bessy, Mary, and me — on sixteen shillings a week? D’you think I’m striking for my own sake? This is as much for other folks as that soldier’s fight. The soldier dies for some man he’s never seen in his life, but me? I’m fighting for John Boucher next door but one, with a sickly wife and eight little bairns not old enough to step foot in a mill. And not just him, though he’s a soft old fool who can only manage two looms at a time. I’m fighting for justice. Why should we get paid less now than we did two years back?”
“Don’t ask me,” Margaret said. “I’m ignorant of all this. Go ask your masters, they must have a reason. They didn’t just pull this pay cut out of thin air on a whim, surely.”
“You’re nothing but a foreigner here, that’s what you are,” he said, half contemptuous, half amused. “You know nothing about it. Ask the masters? They’d tell us to mind our own business and they’ll mind theirs. Our business, that is, to take the cut wage and be grateful. Theirs, to grind us down till we’re half starved, to stuff their own pockets full. That’s how it goes.”
“But the state of trade might be bad enough that they can’t afford to pay you the old wage,” Margaret insisted, not backing down even as she saw she was riling him up.
“State of trade? That’s just a trick the masters made up to fool us,” he scoffed. “It’s nothing but a bogeyman they wave around to scare naughty workers into behaving. Their game is to beat our wages down to swell their own fortunes. Our game is to stand our ground, fight hard — not just for ourselves, but for every man around us — for fair play and justice. We make their profits for them, we’ve a right to our share. This time it’s not even that we’re desperate for cash, like we have been in old strikes. The Union’s got savings put aside. Every man among us has sworn to stand or fall together, not a single one of us will cross the line to work for less than the Union says we’re owed. So I say hurray for the strike, let Thornton, and Slickson, and Hamper and all their lot watch out!”
“Thornton?” Margaret said, perking up. “Mr. Thornton of Marlborough Street?”
“Aye, Thornton who runs the Marlborough Mill, as we call him.”
“He’s one of the masters you’re fighting, isn’t he? What sort of a man is he?”
“Ever seen a bulldog? Set it on its hind legs, dress it in a coat and breeches, and that’s John Thornton, exactly.”
“Nonsense,” Margaret laughed. “Mr. Thornton is plain enough, but he’s nothing like a bulldog, with its short broad snout and snarling lip.”
“Aye, he doesn’t look like one, I’ll grant you. But once John Thornton gets a notion stuck in his head, he’ll hang on to it tighter than any bulldog to a rat. You’d have to pry him off with a pitchfork before he’d let go. He’s a worthy enemy, that Thornton. Slickson? He’ll wheedle his men back soon with soft promises, then cheat them out of every penny the second they’re back under his thumb, you mark my words. He’ll fine them half their wages before the month’s out, see if he doesn’t. Slippery as an eel, that one, sly as a cat. But with Thornton? It’s an honest, up-and-down fight, no tricks. He’s as stubborn as a door-nail, every inch of him, that old bulldog.”
“Poor Bessy,” Margaret said, turning back to the girl rocking in her chair. “You’re sighing over all of it. You don’t care for fighting and struggling the way your father does, do you?”
“No,” Bessy said, her voice heavy. “I’m sick to death of it. I wish the last days I’ve got left could be full of other talk, not just the clashing and clattering that’s worn me out my whole life — talk of work, and wages, and masters, and knobsticks who cross the picket line.”
“Poor lass!” Nicholas said, softening. “Your last days aren’t here yet, you’re looking brighter already for a bit of excitement. And I’ll be around more, to keep you company.”
“The tobacco smoke chokes me,” Bessy said, petulant.
“Then I’ll never smoke indoors again,” he said, tender as anything. “Why didn’t you tell me that before, you daft lass?”
She didn’t speak for a long minute, then whispered so quiet only Margaret could hear: “I reckon he’ll need every bit of comfort he can get from his pipe, or even a drink, before this strike is over.”
Nicholas stepped out the door to finish his smoke in the yard. The second he was gone, Bessy turned to Margaret, wild and upset. “Am I not a fool, Miss? I knew I should have kept him inside, away from all those folk hanging around the pubs who’ll tempt any man in strike time to go drinking. And I had to pick a fight about his pipe, so he’s run off out, and he’ll go and wander the streets whenever he wants a smoke, and no one knows where it’ll end. I wish I’d let myself choke on that smoke before I said a word.”
“Does your father drink, then?” Margaret asked.
“No, not properly,” Bessy said, her voice still raw and overwrought. “But haven’t you ever had days, same as everyone else, when you get up and drag through the hours just craving a little change, a little spark of something different? I’ve done it, gone and bought a loaf of bread from a baker I never use, just because I was sick to death of seeing the same things, hearing the same sounds, tasting the same food, thinking the same empty thoughts day after day, forever. I’ve even wished I was a man, so I could go off wandering somewhere new, looking for work. Men get that restlessness ten times stronger than me. What else have they got to do? It’s no great sin if they pop into the gin shop just to make their blood run a little faster, see something new, look at the pictures on the walls and the big mirrors they’ve got there. Father’s never been a drunkard, not properly, though he’s come home worse for wear once in a while. But strike times? There’s so much to knock the heart out of a man, even if they started out hopeful. Where’s their comfort supposed to come from? He’ll get angry, he’ll get mad, and one day he’ll do something in that rage he’ll wish he could forget. Bless your sweet, pitying face — but you don’t know what a strike is, not really, not yet.”
“Come, Bessy,” Margaret said. “I won’t say you’re exaggerating, I don’t know enough to say that. But you’re unwell, so you’re only looking at the dark side of things. There’s a brighter side to see, too, I promise.”
“That’s easy for you to say,” Bessy said. “You’ve lived in green, pleasant places your whole life, never known want, or care, or wickedness even.”
“Be careful how you judge that,” Margaret said, her cheeks flushing, her eyes bright. “I’m going home to a mother who is so ill — so ill, Bessy — that there’s no way out of the prison of her pain but death. And I have to speak cheerful to my father, who has no idea how bad she is, who will have to be told her condition slow, gentle, bit by bit. The only person who could comfort her more than anyone else in the world, the only one who could understand what we’re going through? He’s been falsely accused of mutiny on his ship. He’d hang if he came home to see his dying mother. I’ve told no one else in Milton, hardly anyone in all of England. Do I not know care? Do I not know anxiety, even if I wear nice clothes and have food on my table? Oh, Bessy, God is just. He portions out our lots fair, even if no one but Him knows how bitter some of our souls feel.”
“I beg your pardon,” Bessy said, quiet and humbled. “Sometimes when I think of my life, how little joy I’ve ever had, I think I must be one of the folk the falling star in Revelations was talking about. ‘And the name of the star is Wormwood; and the third part of the waters became wormwood; and men died of the waters, because they were made bitter.’ It’s easier to bear pain when you think it was written down for you hundreds of years ago, like your suffering is needed to fulfil some grand old prophecy. Otherwise it all feels like it’s for nothing, no meaning at all.”
“Don’t dwell so much on the prophecies, Bessy,” Margaret urged. “Read the clearer, softer, kinder parts of the Bible.”
“Maybe that’s wiser, but where else will I hear such grand, sweeping words of promise, hear about a world so far away from this dreary town? I’ve recited the verses of the seventh chapter of Revelations to myself a thousand times, just for the sound of them. It’s as grand as church organ music, nothing like the dreary sameness of every day. I can’t give it up. It comforts me more than any other book in the Bible.”
“Then let me come back and read you some of my favourite chapters, the ones that give me hope.”
“Aye, do,” Bessy said, eager. “Maybe father will hear you, too. He’s sick of my talking, says all my talk of heaven has nothing to do with the work he’s got to do here on earth.”
“Where’s your sister Mary?” Margaret asked.
“Gone out fustian cutting. I hated to let her go, but we’ve got to eat, and the Union can’t give us much money to live on.”
“Then I must be going,” Margaret said, standing up. “You’ve done me good, Bessy.”
“Me done you good?” Bessy blinked, confused.
“Yes. I came here today so sad, so wrapped up in my own grief I thought no one else in the world could be hurting as bad. Now I know how long you’ve borne your own pain, and it’s made me stronger.”
“Bless you,” Bessy laughed. “I always thought it was the gentlefolk who did all the good. I’ll get a big head if I find out I can do good for you too.”
