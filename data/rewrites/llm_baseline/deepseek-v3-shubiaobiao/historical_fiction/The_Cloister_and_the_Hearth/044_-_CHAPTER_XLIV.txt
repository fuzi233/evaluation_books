Catherine knew better than to panic when a woman fainted. She had seen it enough times to recognize the ordinary causes—tight lacing, stifling air, or a sudden shock. Without hesitation, she laid Margaret flat, loosened her gown, and flung open the window to let in the cool evening breeze. Only when she had done all this did she call out, firm and clear, “Come here, if you please.”

Margaret Van Eyck and Reicht hurried in, finding the girl pale and motionless, Catherine briskly chafing her hands.

“What have you done to her?” Margaret Van Eyck demanded, her voice sharp with accusation.

“Me?” Catherine shot back, indignant. “I’ve done nothing but what any woman would. This is no mystery—just nature taking its course.”

The elder Margaret’s cheeks flushed with anger. “You speak too lightly,” she said, “when you and yours are the reason she’s in this state.”

Catherine’s eyes narrowed. “That’s a lie, and you know it. No woman alive is to blame for this.”

“Then who is? You and your husband drove them apart! Now he’s gone to Italy, and her heart’s broken. ‘Nature,’ you call it?”

Catherine stiffened. “What are you saying? You can’t mean this is my Gerard’s lass. Look at her—that fur alone costs more than a craftsman earns in months. And if she isn’t a wife, she ought to be.”

Margaret Van Eyck’s voice dropped, heavy with meaning. “And who stopped her from being one? Who stood in their way at the very altar?”

Catherine crossed herself. “God forgive them, whoever it was. But it wasn’t me, nor my man.”

The old woman softened slightly. “Now that you’ve seen her, perhaps you’ll think better of her. She’s coming around, thank Heaven.”

“Think better of her?” Catherine scoffed. “That’s long past. Poor child—trouble behind her and trouble ahead.” She shook her head. “And to think I made her read Gerard’s letter to me. No wonder she fainted.”

As color slowly returned to the girl’s cheeks, Catherine drew Margaret Van Eyck aside. “Does she lodge with you?”

“No.”

“Then don’t let her go back to Sevenbergen tonight.”

“That’s her choice.”

“You’re the elder—make her stay.” Catherine leaned in, lips nearly touching the other woman’s ear, and in the space of a breath, conveyed what no man would have dared speak aloud.

Margaret Van Eyck recoiled. “That’s a vile slander! Look at her face—how could you even think it?”

Catherine snorted. “I know what I know. And I’m telling you—it’s so.”

Before the old woman could argue further, Catherine turned back to the girl, took her face in her hands, and kissed her fiercely. “You suffered for Gerard,” she murmured. “And I’ve been a fool to hold myself apart. From now on, you’re my daughter.”

Another embrace, and then she was gone, leaving silence in her wake.

Margaret—pale, trembling—managed a faint smile. Gerard’s mother had kissed her. Called her daughter.

But the joy was short-lived. Margaret Van Eyck’s expression darkened. “I don’t know how you could let that woman touch you.”

“His mother,” Margaret whispered, as if that explained everything.

“Mother or not, you wouldn’t let her near you if you knew what she said.”

Margaret’s breath caught. “About me?”

Before the old woman could elaborate, Margaret slid from the chair to her knees, clutching at her skirts. “Forgive me,” she pleaded, voice breaking. “Forgive me!”

And in that moment, it was clear—whatever secret lay between them, the weight of it was too much to bear.
