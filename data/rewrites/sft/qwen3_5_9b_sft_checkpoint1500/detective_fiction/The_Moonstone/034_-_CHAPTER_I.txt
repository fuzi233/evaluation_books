There are two compelling reasons why I, Hercule Poirot, must now take up the pen, following the accounts of others. First, I stand in a unique position to illuminate specific shadows cast over the events surrounding the broken engagement between Miss Rachel Verinder and Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite. I am the architect behind her decision to sever ties, and I am the discoverer of the hidden motive driving Mr. Ablewhite's sudden withdrawal. Second, fate—or perhaps misfortune—has bound me intimately to the mystery of the Indian Diamond. It was within my own consulting rooms that I conducted a meeting with an Oriental gentleman of distinguished bearing, unmistakably the leader of the three men accused of stealing the jewel. Furthermore, the renowned traveler, Mr. Murthwaite, appeared before me shortly thereafter, engaging me in a conversation regarding the Moonstone that would prove pivotal to the unfolding drama. These are the credentials that justify my presence in this narrative.

The story of the shattered betrothal precedes the theft in time, and thus it demands precedence here. To trace the chain of causality from start to finish, I must begin at the bedside of my late and esteemed client, Sir John Verinder. Sir John possessed, if anything in excess measure, the harmless and amiable frailties common to mankind. Pertinent to this affair was his incurable reluctance, so long as he enjoyed robust health, to confront the solemnity of drafting a will. Lady Verinder endeavored to stir his conscience; I, too, applied my influence. He assented to the propriety of our counsel yet took no action until the illness that eventually claimed his life forced his hand. At length, I was summoned to receive his instructions. They were, in the entirety of my professional life, the simplest I had ever encountered.

Sir John lay dozing when I entered. Upon seeing me, he awoke. "How do you do, Mr. Poirot?" he murmured. "I shall not tarry long with this. And then I shall slumber again." He watched with keen interest as I prepared my pens, ink, and parchment. "Are you ready?" he inquired. I bowed, dipped my quill, and awaited his decree.

"I leave everything to my wife," declared Sir John. "That is all." Turning on his pillow, he settled back into repose.

I was compelled to interrupt his rest. "Do I comprehend correctly," I asked, "that you intend to bequeath the entirety of your estate, encompassing all sorts and descriptions of property, absolutely to Lady Verinder?"

"Yes," affirmed Sir John. "Pray, _you_ put it succinctly, so that I may return to my dreams. All to my wife. That constitutes my Will."

His assets were entirely at his command, divided into landed property and monetary funds. In most instances, I would have felt a moral imperative to urge a reconsideration of such a sweeping bequest. However, regarding Lady Verinder, I knew her to be not merely worthy of her husband's unreserved trust—a quality shared by virtuous wives—but also fully competent to administer such a trust, a rare attribute among women. Within ten minutes, Sir John's testament was drafted and executed, and Sir John himself, a benevolent soul, resumed his interrupted nap.

Lady Verinder faithfully upheld the confidence reposed in her. In the early days of her widowhood, she summoned me to draft her own testament. Her perspective on her station was so sound and pragmatic that I required no advisory counsel. My role commenced and concluded with molding her directives into legally binding form. Before two months had elapsed since Sir John's passing, the future of his daughter had been secured with profound wisdom and tenderness.

This first Will resided in my fireproof safe for many years, a duration I prefer not to calculate precisely. It was not until the summer of 1848 that I was called upon to review it again, under circumstances of profound melancholy. Physicians had delivered their grim verdict upon poor Lady Verinder; it was a sentence of death. As the first to be informed of her condition, I accompanied her as she wished to scrutinize her Will anew.

The provisions concerning her daughter, Rachel, were immutable and optimal. Yet, due to the passage of time, her desires regarding certain minor legacies to various relatives had shifted. It became necessary to append three or four codicils. Acting swiftly to prevent any potential accidents, I sought her ladyship's permission to consolidate her latest instructions into a second Will. My aim was to eliminate the inevitable confusions and repetitions that marred the original document, a state of affairs that grated unpleasantly against my professional sensibilities.

The execution of this second Will was witnessed by Miss Clack, whose obsequiousness I noted. Regarding Rachel Verinder's financial standing, this instrument was, word for word, identical to the first. The sole alterations pertained to the appointment of a guardian and specific stipulations surrounding that arrangement, made under my guidance. Upon Lady Verinder's demise, the Will was entrusted to my proctor for probate.

Approximately three weeks thereafter, the first intimation of subterranean unrest reached me. I chanced upon my friend, the proctor, in his office and observed that he greeted me with an unusual degree of agitation. "I possess news for you," he announced. "Pray, consider what I heard at Doctors' Commons this morn: Lady Verinder's Will has been requested and examined!"

This was startling intelligence. There existed absolutely nothing within the Will open to contest, and no individual, to my knowledge, harbored a vested interest in scrutinizing it. (Permit me to elucidate, for those unfamiliar with the law, that any applicant may inspect a Will at Doctors' Commons for a nominal fee of one shilling.)

"Did you ascertain who sought the Will?" I inquired.

"Yes," the proctor replied. "The clerk disclosed it to me without hesitation. Mr. Smalley, of the firm Skipp and Smalley, requested it. The document had not yet been transcribed into the Great Folio Registers. Consequently, we departed from standard procedure and permitted him to view the original. He perused it diligently and jotted notes in his pocket-book. Do you conjecture his purpose?"

I shook my head. "I shall discover," I assured him, "before the day concludes." Immediately, I returned to my chambers.

Had another solicitor's firm been implicated in this inexplicable inquiry, I might have faced significant obstacles in uncovering the truth. However, I held leverage over Skipp and Smalley that rendered my investigation comparatively straightforward. My common-law clerk, a man of exceptional competence and integrity, was Mr. Smalley's brother. Through this indirect familial tie, the firm of Skipp and Smalley had, for several years, scavenged the remnants of my practice—cases referred to them that I deemed unworthy of my direct attention. Thus, their professional patronage derived from me. I intended, if necessary, to remind them of this dependency.

Upon my return, I addressed my clerk. After briefing him on the developments, I dispatched him to his brother's office, conveying "Mr. Poirot's compliments," requesting to learn why Messrs. Skipp and Smalley felt impelled to examine Lady Verinder's Will.

This summons brought Mr. Smalley back to my office alongside his brother. He conceded that he had acted pursuant to instructions from a client. He then posed to me whether it would constitute a breach of professional confidentiality to disclose further. A spirited debate ensued. He was correct, naturally; I was mistaken. The truth was that I was seething with anger and suspicion, insisting on knowing more. Far worse, I refused to accept any supplementary information as a secret entrusted to my keeping; I demanded absolute liberty to exercise my own discretion. Even more egregious, I seized an unjustifiable advantage of my position. "Choose, sir," I told Mr. Smalley, "between the peril of forfeiting your client's business and the peril of forfeiting Mine." An indefensible act of tyranny, I admit. Yet, like other tyrants, I prevailed. Mr. Smalley chose his alternative without a moment's hesitation.

He smiled with resignation and revealed the name of his client:
Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite.

That sufficed for me; I desired no further knowledge.

Having arrived at this juncture, I deem it necessary to place the reader, concerning Lady Verinder's Will, upon a parity of information with myself. Let me state, in the fewest possible terms, that Rachel Verinder held merely a life-interest in the property. Her mother's sagacious judgment and my extensive experience had conspired to liberate her from all responsibility and shield her from the perils of becoming a victim to some impoverished and unscrupulous suitor. Neither she, nor any husband she might wed, could raise sixpence from the landed estates or the cash holdings. They would reside in the London and Yorkshire residences and enjoy a handsome income—and that was the extent of their security.

As I reflected on these discoveries, I was profoundly perplexed as to my next move. Scarcely a week had passed since I learned, to my astonishment and dismay, of Miss Verinder's proposed union. I cherished the deepest admiration and affection for her; consequently, I was inconsolably grieved to hear she contemplated sacrificing herself to Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite. And now, here was the man—who, I had always suspected to be a smooth-tongued fraud—validating the darkest suspicions I harbored, openly exposing the mercenary nature of his intentions! What of that?
