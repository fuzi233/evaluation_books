The remainder of this harrowing sequence must be recounted in Thomas's own voice, for it constitutes the core of his confession to the detectives.

"As I promised, I took my young wife to Felix's residence on the agreed day and hour. We walked, as the location was not distant from our hotel. Upon arrival, we were greeted at the door by an elderly servant whom I had been warned was deaf and mute. Seeing him, and observing the dim, archaic hallway stretching out before us in an atmosphere of aristocratic gloom, Eva turned pale and shot me a questioning glance. I reassured her with a smile that starkly contradicted my own inner dread, placing my arm around her waist and following the old man past the open door of the drawing-room—where I instinctively expected to stop—and into a room so plainly furnished that I frowned in disappointment.

Then, Bartow—as I have since learned his name was—threw aside a heavy drape at the far end, ushering us into my brother's study. In that moment, it resembled a fairyland, or would have, had not Felix, its sole inhabitant, immediately seized our attention with the overwhelming force of his personality, never more imposing than he was then.

Eva, to whom I had revealed little of this brother, certainly nothing suggesting she would encounter a man of such striking handsomeness or such profound mental poise, looked terrified and awestruck. Yet, she stepped forward with surprising bravery. When I introduced her, she offered a smile of such inviting grace that I secretly hoped it might disarm him.

Perhaps it did. His already pale features turned waxy under the harsh yellow glare of the peculiar lantern overhead. The hand he raised in a mechanical greeting dropped heavily, and he could barely stammer a welcome. These words would have seemed woefully inadequate had not his eyes, fixed intently on her face, betrayed a depth of feeling she could not comprehend—a realization that her very life, for happiness *is* life, was teetering precariously under the weight of his indomitable will.

Knowing—or believing I knew—the truth, I cast him an imploring glance. 'I have explanations to make,' I said, asking if Mrs. Adams might rest while we spoke privately.

He regarded me with a strange expression. Did he detect the revulsion in my tone? Did he understand the nature of my compliance? I shall never know. I only know that he ceased fiddling with a small object on his desk and bowed with a sarcastic elegance that made me feel, for the first time in our interactions, utterly inferior to him, even in stature. He led the way to a small door I had previously missed.

'Your wife will find it more comfortable here,' he observed, his speech halting with pauses that masked great, repressed agitation. He opened the door to reveal a small, elegant bedroom. 'What we must discuss will not take long. Mrs. Adams will not find the wait tedious.'

'No,' she smiled, laughing naturally, born, I dared hope, of perfect happiness. Yet, she could not help but view the proceedings as bizarre, and my demeanor, along with his, was hardly what one expects from a groom introducing his bride to his only relative.

'I will call you...' I began, but the image of her dimpled face above the massive bouquet of roses she carried stole my remaining composure. I forgot to finish my sentence. The door clicked shut, leaving me face-to-face with Felix.

He breathed easier now that we were alone. His manner seemed more natural, yet he remained silent, casting a strange, searching gaze around the room—the strangest apartment imaginable. Satisfied, though I could not discern why, he gestured toward the large table, which had from the outset exerted an uncanny magnetic pull on him.

'Come closer to the center. I need air, breathing space in this stifling room, and so must you. Besides, why should she hear what we have to say? She will know the worst soon enough. She seems a gentle-hearted woman.'

'An angel!' I started to say, but he cut me off with an imperious wave.

'We shall not discuss your wife—Mrs. Adams,' he corrected sharply. 'Where is John Poindexter?'

'At the hotel,' I replied. 'Or perhaps he has returned home. I no longer concern myself with his whereabouts. Felix, I shall never leave my wife. I would rather break the oath I swore before I realized the value of the woman whose happiness I vowed to destroy. This is why I am here. Make it easy for me, Felix. You are a man who has loved and suffered. Let us bury the past; let us...'

Did I truly hope to sway him? Perhaps a childish notion had clouded my judgment up to this point. But as these words left my lips, and indeed before I had fully articulated them, I saw by the rigid set of his features—cast in bronze—that I might falter, but he remained immovable, firmer than ever, less susceptible to entreaty.

Yet, what did it matter? In the worst-case scenario, what did I have to lose? A struggle that might drag Eva into bitterness and cost me a fortune I had begun to regard as my own? These were trivial concerns. Eva must eventually learn my true identity and the story of her father's guilt. Why not now? And if we must begin life in poverty, it was still life, whereas a separation from her...

Before I could dwell further, Felix spoke, using language I was least prepared to hear.

'I anticipated this. From the moment you begged for the privilege of marrying her, I looked forward to this outcome and prepared accordingly. Weakness on the part of a bridegroom was to be expected; thus, I have steeled myself to meet the emergency. Your oath must be kept!'

Crushed by the authority in his voice—a tone suggesting power against which any ordinary resistance would fail—I glanced around the room, mimicking his earlier action. Nothing visible suggested treachery, yet a primal distrust (the first I had genuinely felt) rose within me, triggered by his gaze sweeping the mosque-like interior of his study. Was it his calm confidence, or the surreal combination of Oriental splendor, nocturnal lighting in broad daylight, and the clamor of streetcars outside? Hard to say. But from this moment, I was gripped by a vague terror—not for myself, but for her, whose voice we could clearly hear humming a cheerful tune in the adjacent room. I resolved to suppress all signs of unease. I even smiled, though I felt the eyes of Evelyn's painted visage burning into me; her portrait, which I had never ceased to watch since entering, stood directly behind me, unseen by me but watched closely by Felix, who did not raise his eyes to it. Waiting for my response, he noted my hesitation and repeated, 'That oath must be kept!'

This time, I found the strength to answer. 'Impossible!' I exploded, casting doubt, fear, and hesitation to the wind. 'It was in my nature to take it, manipulated as I was by family affection, the impending death of our father, and a thousand dark influences carefully orchestrated for this end. But it is not in my nature to keep it after four months of living alongside a man thirty years removed from his guilt, and his innocent, guileless daughter. You cannot drive me to it, Felix. No man can force another to abandon his own wife because of a wicked oath taken long before he knew her. If you think your money...'

'Money?' he cried, with a contempt that highlighted both my feigned disinterest and his own obsession. 'I had forgotten I possessed it. No, Thomas, I would never weigh money against the happiness of living with a woman like your wife appears to be. But her life... I might. Execute your threat; forget to pay John Poindexter the debt we owe him, and the situation will escalate to a gravity for which you are ill-prepared. A daughter dying in her honeymoon would be nearly as devastating to him as a dishonored one. And either dead or disgraced, he must find her when he arrives here seeking the child he cannot forget. Which shall it be? Speak!'

Was I dreaming? Was this Felix? Was this myself? Were these words pouring into my ears?

In a sudden lurch, I reached his side where he stood pressed against the table, groaning rather than screaming: 'You would not kill her! You do not contemplate a crime of blood—here—against her—the innocent—the good...'

'No,' he said calmly. 'It will be you who do that. You who will not wish to see her languish, suffer, go mad—Thomas, I am not the raving creature you imagine. I am merely a keeper of oaths. Nay, I am more. I possess talent, skill. Consider the house in which you stand as proof. This room—note, it has no exit except those windows, barely perceptible above our heads, currently shielded by a simple curtain. With a mere touch of my hand, without moving from this spot, I can hermetically seal that opening. No man, unless armed with a crowbar and pickaxe, could enter, even if someone knew of our imprisonment, especially in a house soon to be sealed from top to bottom by my departing servant.'
