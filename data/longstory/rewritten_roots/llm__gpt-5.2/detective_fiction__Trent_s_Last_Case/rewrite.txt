Between what truly matters and what merely makes a din of importance, the world has never learned to judge without being dazzled by its own echo. When Sigsbee Manderson’s indomitable scheming brain was scattered by a shot from an unknown hand, the earth did not lose a thing worth one honest tear. Yet the great vortices of business—those rooms and streets where men live by listening to the clatter of other men’s money—felt it as a concussion, as though a main bearing in the machinery had cracked.

In the lurid commercial annals of his country no figure had imposed itself so completely upon the trading mind. Others had been giants of capital before him: strong to marshal and augment the forces of wealth, taking their approved toll in millions for the labour of directing. But Manderson carried, incongruously, a pale halo of romance—piratical, coarse-grained, dear to the American appetite for legend—through the years when he stood publicly as the guardian of stability, the stamper-out of crises, the foe of those raiding chieftains who infest the borders of Wall Street.

The fortune began with a grandfather who had been one of those chieftains in the smaller key of his own day; it passed to a father who, through a long life, had quietly lent money and never “margined” a stock, as if speculation were a foreign tongue he refused to speak. Manderson himself had never known what it was to be without large sums to his hand. His nurture and education had taught him European ideas of a rich man’s proper circumstance: the quiet magnificence that does not shriek its cost with a thousand tongues. Yet the Forty-Niner and financial buccaneer in his blood survived the polishing. In the first period of his career—what the Street called his early bad manner—he had been little more than a gambler of genius, his hand against every man’s: an infant prodigy who brought to the enthralling dog-fight of the Exchange a brain better endowed than any opposed to it. At St. Helena they had laid down that war is une belle occupation; the young Manderson found his war amid the multitudinous complications of New York’s market, where the soundtrack is a perpetual metallic rattle of telephones and chalk, and the air tastes of stale cigars and hurried breath.

Then came his change. At his father’s death, when Manderson was thirty, some new revelation of power and glory in the god he served seemed to descend upon him like a chill sobriety. With the elastic adaptability of his nation, he turned to steady labour in his father’s banking business, closing his ears—so it appeared—to the battle-noise of the Street. In a few years he controlled all the activity of the great firm of Manderson, Colefax and Company, whose unimpeached conservatism and weight lifted it like a cliff above the angry sea of the markets. Mistrust founded on the performances of his youth fell away. He was, quite plainly, a different man.

How the change came about none could say with authority. There was a story, half whispered in corridors and half shouted in print when it suited, of certain last words spoken by the father whom alone he had respected, and perhaps loved. Whatever the spell, Manderson began to tower above the financial situation. Soon his name was current in the bourses of the world. To speak it was to summon a vision of all that was broad-based and firm in the vast wealth of the United States. He planned great combinations of capital; drew together and centralized industries of continental scope; financed with unerring judgment the large designs of state or private enterprise. Many a time when he “took hold” to smash a strike, or to federate the ownership of some great field of labour, he sent ruin upon a multitude of tiny homes—kitchens where bread had been kneaded before dawn, gardens where old men listened for the right wind in the corn. If miners, steelworkers, cattlemen defied him and invoked disorder, he could be more ruthless than they. But it was, as the world insisted on phrasing it, in pursuit of legitimate business ends. Tens of thousands of the poor might curse his name; the financier and the speculator execrated him no more. He stretched out a hand to protect or manipulate the power of wealth in every corner of the country. Forcible, cold, unerring, he ministered to the national lust for magnitude; and a grateful country dubbed him the Colossus.

Yet there was an aspect of Manderson in this later period that lay long unknown save by a few: his secretaries and lieutenants, and certain associates from his bygone hurling time. This little circle knew that the pillar of stability had his hours of nostalgia for the lively days when the Street had trembled at his name. It was, said one of them, as if Blackbeard had settled down as a decent merchant in Bristol on the spoils of the Main—only to feel, now and then, the old salt-burn in his blood. In such spasms of reversion a score of tempestuous raids upon the market would be planned—always on paper, always in the inner room—like recipes written out in a careful hand for dishes never meant to reach the table. The pirate would glare suddenly out, knife in his teeth, sulphur matches sputtering in his hatband; and then Blackbeard would quell the mutiny of his old self and go soberly down to his counting-house, humming—if the listener’s ear caught it right—two bars of “Spanish Ladies” under his breath, the tune momentarily cutting through the office-drone like a gull-cry above docks.

After the time for action had gone by, he would allow himself the harmless satisfaction of pointing out to some Rupert of the markets a coup worth a million that might have been made. “Seems to me,” he would say almost wistfully, “the Street is getting to be a mighty dull place since I quit.” By slow degrees this amiable weakness of the Colossus became known to the business world, which exulted greatly in the knowledge; for traders love to humanize their idols, provided the humanizing does not cost them money.

At the news of his death panic went through the markets like a hurricane—because it came at a luckless time. Prices tottered and crashed like towers in an earthquake. For two days Wall Street was a clamorous inferno of pale despair, its usual jangling racket grown suddenly savage, every ring of the telephone a lash, every shouted bid a cry torn from a throat too dry for speech. All over the United States, wherever speculation had its devotees, went a waft of ruin, a plague of suicide. In Europe also not a few took with their own hands lives that had become pitiably linked to the destiny of a financier most of them had never seen. In Paris a well-known banker walked quietly out of the Bourse and fell dead upon the broad steps among the raving crowd, a phial crushed in his hand. In Frankfort one leapt from the Cathedral top, leaving a redder stain where he struck the red tower. Men stabbed and shot and strangled themselves, drank death or breathed it as air, because in a lonely corner of England the life had departed from one cold heart vowed to the service of greed.

The blow could not have fallen at a more disastrous moment. Wall Street was already in a condition of suppressed scare—suppressed, because for a week the great interests known to act with, or to be controlled by, the Colossus had been desperately combating the effects of the sudden arrest of Lucas Hahn and the exposure of his plundering of the Hahn banks. That bombshell, in its turn, had fallen at a time when the market had been “boosted” beyond its real strength. In the language of the place, a slump was due. Reports from the corn-lands had not been good; the talk of harvest had a sour note in it, as if the wind through the stalks carried warning. There had been two or three railway statements expected to be much better than they were. But wherever in the vast area of speculation the threatened break was felt, “the Manderson crowd” stepped in and held the market up.

All through the week the speculator’s mind—shallow as it is quick-witted, sentimental as it is greedy—saw in this the hand of the giant stretched out in protection from afar. Manderson, said the newspapers in chorus, was in hourly communication with his lieutenants in the Street. One journal could give, in round figures, the sum spent on cabling between New York and Marlstone in the past twenty-four hours; it told how a small staff of expert operators had been sent down by the Post Office authorities to Marlstone to deal with the flood of messages. Another revealed that Manderson, on the first news of the Hahn crash, had arranged to abandon his holiday and return home by the Lusitania; but that he soon had the situation so well in hand that he had determined to remain where he was.

All this was falsehood—more or less consciously elaborated by finance editors, consciously initiated and encouraged by the shrewd business men of the Manderson group. They knew nothing could better serve their plans than the illusion of hero-worship; and they knew also that no word had come from Manderson in answer to their messages, and that Howard B. Jeffrey, of Steel and Iron fame, was the true organizer of victory. So they fought down apprehension through four feverish days, and minds grew calmer; the city’s soundscape softened toward its ordinary din, the terrible attention of a crowd relaxing. On Saturday, though the ground beneath Mr. Jeffrey’s feet still rumbled now and then with Etna-mutterings of disquiet, he deemed his task almost done. The market was firm and slowly advancing. Wall Street turned to its Sunday sleep, worn out but thankfully at peace—like a household after a long day’s preserving, jars cooling in ranks, the kitchen finally quiet except for the ticking of the clock.

In the first trading hour of Monday a hideous rumour flew round the sixty acres of the financial district. It came into being as lightning comes—a blink that seems to begin nowhere; though it is to be suspected it was first whispered over a telephone, together with an urgent selling order by some employee in the cable service. A sharp spasm convulsed the convalescent share-list. In five minutes the dull noise of the kerbstone market in Broad Street leapt to a high note of frantic interrogation. From within the hive of the Exchange itself could be heard a droning hubbub of fear; men rushed hatless in and out, as if the building had turned suddenly airless.

Was it true? asked every man. And every man replied, with trembling lips, that it was a lie put out by some unscrupulous short interest seeking to cover itself. In another quarter of an hour news came of a sudden and ruinous collapse of “Yankees” in London at the close of the Stock Exchange day. It was enough. New York still had four hours’ trading ahead of her. The strategy of pointing to Manderson as the saviour and warden of the markets recoiled upon its authors with annihilating force. Jeffrey, his ear at his private telephone, listened to the tale of disaster with a set jaw. The new Napoleon had lost his Marengo. He saw the whole financial landscape sliding and falling into chaos before him, and the roar of it—orders, curses, appeals—rose like a furnace-blast.

In half an hour the news of the finding of Manderson’s body, with the inevitable rumour that it was suicide, was printing in a dozen newspaper offices; but before a copy reached Wall Street the tornado of the panic was already in full fury, and Howard B. Jeffrey and his collaborators were whirled away like leaves before its breath.

All this sprang out of nothing. Nothing in the texture of the general life had changed. The corn had not ceased to ripen in the sun. Rivers bore their barges and gave power to a myriad engines. Flocks fattened on the pastures; herds were unnumbered. Men laboured everywhere in the various servitudes to which they were born, and chafed not more than usual in their bonds. Bellona tossed and murmured as ever, yet still slept her uneasy sleep. To all mankind save a million or two of half-crazed gamblers—blind to the real world’s steady processes—the death of Manderson meant nothing. Weeks before he died, strong hands had been in control of every wire in the huge network of commerce and industry he had supervised. Before his corpse was buried his countrymen made a strange discovery: that the existence of the potent engine of monopoly that went by the name of Sigsbee Manderson had not been a condition even of material prosperity.

The panic blew itself out in two days; the pieces were picked up; the bankrupts withdrew out of sight. The market “recovered a normal tone.” While the brief delirium was yet subsiding there broke out a domestic scandal in England that suddenly fixed the attention of two continents. Next morning the Chicago Limited was wrecked; the same day a notable politician was shot down in cold blood by his wife’s brother in the streets of New Orleans. Within a week of its rising, the Manderson story, to the trained sense of editors throughout the Union, was “cold.”

The tide of American visitors pouring through Europe made eddies round the memorials and statues of men who had died in poverty, and never thought of their most famous plutocrat. Like the poet who died in Rome a hundred years ago, so young and poor, Manderson was buried far from his own land; but for all the men and women of his people who flock to Keats’s grave in the cemetery under Monte Testaccio, there is not one—nor ever will be—to stand in reverence by the rich man’s grave beside the little church of Marlstone.

In the one room at the Record offices that had been made comfortable on purpose—carpet that had once known a drawing-room, curtains that tried to soften the street’s perpetual clang, and a coal fire that fought a losing battle against the draught from the window—the telephone on Sir James Molloy’s table began to buzz with that impatient, metallic insistence which always seemed to bring a little of the city’s iron into the room.

Sir James, pen poised, made a small motion of command. Mr. Silver left his papers and went to the instrument. He bent his head, listening as if to a seashell, except that what came up from this shell was not surf but crackle and distance and the far-off muddle of a rural line.

“Who is that?” he said. “Who?… I can’t hear you…. Oh, it’s Mr. Bunner, is it?… Yes, but—” He glanced toward Sir James, and the look was a question and an apology. “—I know, but he’s fearfully busy this afternoon. Can’t you… Oh, really? Well, in that case—just hold on, will you?”

He covered the mouthpiece, leaned in, and spoke low, as if a name itself might carry.

“It’s Calvin Bunner—Sigsbee Manderson’s right-hand man,” he said. “He insists on you personally. Says it’s the gravest piece of news. He’s calling from the house down by Bishopsbridge, so you’ll have to speak clear.”

Sir James looked at the telephone with the wary dislike of a man who values silence and knows how easily modern contraptions poison it. Then he took the receiver.

“Well?” he said, in the strong voice that had sent paragraphs marching and men running.

For a few seconds there was nothing but the thin, insectile whine of the line; then words came, blurred at the edges, as though they had crossed miles of hedgerow and wet field and finally arrived at Fleet Street already bruised by distance. Sir James listened. His face, so often set in the hard, half-amused mask of a man who has made a trade of other people’s sensations, altered as if the news had struck a physical blow.

“Yes,” he said.

A pause. He drew in his breath.

“Yes.”

Then, very softly, the room hearing it as one hears a lid fall on a pot that has boiled over: “Good God!”

He rose, still clutching the instrument, bending his head as if he could drag truth out of the buzzing. Mr. Silver watched him, and in that watching understood that whatever was being said was not the kind of thing you could sweeten with editorials or soften with adjectives.

At intervals Sir James said “Yes,” and each “Yes” seemed to shorten the distance between the quiet room and some disturbance at the other end of England.

Then, without taking his mouth from the receiver, he looked at the clock and spoke over the transmitter to Mr. Silver.

“Go and hunt up Figgis and young Williams. Hurry.”

Mr. Silver darted out.

Sir James Molloy was fifty, swart, black-moustached, tall and strongly built: an Irishman with an untiring business energy and a mind that played upon the world with half-cynical competence, but without charlatanry. He made no mysteries, assumed no occult knowledge, and cut through pretence as neatly as a knife through trussed fowl. His handsome, well-bred look could take on something faintly sinister when anger or intense occupation tightened the skin about his eyes; but when his generous nature ran free he was cordial enough to disarm resentment. He managed the company that owned the Record—powerful, morning, indispensable—and also the Sun, that ravenous evening creature housed across the street. He was editor-in-chief of the Record, and in years of patient selection had attached to it a staff of varied gifts and solid merits. In a trade that did not encourage reverence, he was respected.

At the other end Bunner’s voice—urgent, clipped, frightened, and yet careful, like a servant carrying hot broth through a crowded passage—went on.

“You’re sure that’s all?” Sir James asked after a few minutes, and his tone had shifted from shock to that quick, hard clarity which is the mind’s way of setting bone. “And how long has this been known?… Yes, of course, the police are; but the servants? Surely it’s all over the place down there by now…. Well, we’ll have a try…. Look here, Bunner, I’m infinitely obliged to you about this. I owe you a good turn. You know I mean what I say. Come and see me the first day you get to town…. All right, that’s understood. Now I must act on your news. Goodbye.”

He hung up and snatched a railway timetable from the rack, as if it were a recipe book and he needed to know what could be served, and when. He ran a finger down the columns; the answer displeased him. He flung it down with a forcible word.

Mr. Silver returned almost at once, bringing with him a hard-featured man with spectacles—Mr. Figgis—and a youth with an alert eye—young Williams.

Sir James had already smothered agitation. He spoke with rapid calmness, the voice of a man who can set a kitchen in motion without raising it to panic.

“I want you to jot down some facts, Figgis,” he said. “When you have them, put them into shape as quick as you can for a special edition of the Sun.”

Figgis nodded, glanced at the clock—past three—drew out a notebook, and sat at the big writing-table. His pencil poised as if nothing could be more ordinary than tragedy.

“Silver,” Sir James went on, “tell Jones to wire our local correspondent very urgently. He is to drop everything and get down to Marlstone at once. He is not to say why in the telegram. There must not be an unnecessary word about this news until the Sun is on the streets with it—you understand.”

“Yes, sir.”

“Williams, cut across the way and tell Mr. Anthony to hold himself ready for a two-column opening that will knock the town endways. Tell him he must take all measures and precautions for a scoop. Say Figgis will be over in five minutes with the facts, and he’d better let him write up the story in his private room. As you go, ask Miss Morgan to see me here at once, and tell the telephone people to see if they can get Mr. Trent on the wire for me. After seeing Mr. Anthony, return here and stand by.”

The youth vanished.

Sir James turned to Figgis, who had that calm professional emptiness which can take in horror and turn it out, punctuated, for print.

“Sigsbee Manderson has been murdered,” Sir James began, walking the floor with his hands behind his back.

Figgis scratched a line in shorthand with the same detachment he might have used for the weather.

“He and his wife and two secretaries have been for the past fortnight at the house called White Gables, at Marlstone, near Bishopsbridge. He bought it four years ago. He and Mrs. Manderson have since spent part of each summer there. Last night he went to bed about half-past eleven—just as usual. No one knows when he got up and left the house. He was not missed until this morning. About ten o’clock his body was found by a gardener. It was lying by a shed in the grounds. He was shot in the head, through the left eye. Death must have been instantaneous. The body was not robbed, but there were marks on the wrists which point to a struggle having taken place. Dr. Stock, of Marlstone, was at once sent for, and will conduct the post-mortem examination. The police from Bishopsbridge, who were soon on the spot, are reticent, but it is believed they are quite without a clue to the identity of the murderer. There you are, Figgis. Mr. Anthony is expecting you. Now I must telephone him and arrange things.”

Figgis looked up, his mind already arranging its own additions, like a cook considering what garnish will make the dish look complete.

“One of the ablest detectives at Scotland Yard,” he suggested, “has been put in charge of the case. It’s a safe statement.”

“If you like,” said Sir James.

“And Mrs. Manderson? Was she there?”

“Yes. What about her?”

“Prostrated by the shock,” hinted Figgis. “Sees nobody. Human interest.”

“I wouldn’t put that in, Mr. Figgis,” said a quiet voice.

Miss Morgan had come in without sound, as if the room’s own discipline had learned from her. She was pale and graceful, her efficiency so imperturbable that the office treated it as a proverb rather than a gift. While Sir James dictated, she must have been somewhere doing three things at once; now she was here as naturally as if she had been summoned by the change in the air.

“I have seen Mrs. Manderson,” she went on, turning to Sir James. “She looks quite healthy and intelligent. Has her husband been murdered? I don’t think the shock would prostrate her. She is more likely to be doing all she can to help the police.”

Sir James allowed himself a momentary smile, the kind that acknowledges a valued tool.

“Something in your own style, then, Miss Morgan. Cut it out, Figgis. Off you go!”

Figgis rose and went, already turning facts into type in his head.

Sir James faced Miss Morgan.

“Now, madam,” he said, “I expect you know what I want.”

“Our Manderson biography happens to be well up to date,” she replied, lowering her dark eyelashes as if consulting a mental file. “I was looking over it only a few months ago. It is practically ready for tomorrow’s paper. I should think the Sun had better use the sketch of his life they had about two years ago, when he went to Berlin and settled the potash difficulty. I remember it was a very good sketch, and they won’t be able to carry much more than that.

“As for our paper, of course we have a great quantity of cuttings—mostly rubbish. The sub-editors shall have them as soon as they come in. Then we have two very good portraits that are our own property; the best is a drawing Mr. Trent made when they were both on the same ship somewhere. It is better than any of the photographs; but you say the public prefers a bad photograph to a good drawing. I will send them down to you at once, and you can choose.

“As far as I can see, the Record is well ahead of the situation, except that you will not be able to get a special man down there in time to be of any use for tomorrow’s paper.”

Sir James sighed, deeply and with a kind of wounded amusement, as if the day had added to him one more proof that speed—this modern idol with its clattering hooves—was never satisfied.

“What are we good for, anyhow?” he enquired dejectedly of Mr. Silver, who had returned to his desk. “She even knows Bradshaw by heart.”

Miss Morgan adjusted her cuffs with patient composure.

“Is there anything else?” she asked.

The telephone bell rang again, a shrill mechanical cry that seemed to make a little hole in the room’s quieter air.

“Yes, one thing,” said Sir James as he took up the receiver. “I want you to make a bad mistake some time, Miss Morgan—an everlasting bloomer—just to put us in countenance.”

She permitted herself the smallest fraction of what might have been a charming smile, and went out as noiselessly as she had come.

Sir James spoke into the telephone.

“Anthony?” he said, and was at once deep in consultation with the editor across the road.

He seldom went into the Sun building; he said the atmosphere of an evening paper was all very well if you liked that kind of thing. Mr. Anthony—Fleet Street’s Murat—delighted in riding the whirlwind and fighting a tumultuous battle against time, and said much the same of morning papers. Between them they could make the day’s horror into a public event before the dinner dishes were cleared.

Some five minutes later a uniformed boy came in.

“Mr. Trent is on the wire, sir.”

Sir James abruptly ended his talk with Mr. Anthony.

“Put him through at once,” he said.

He waited a moment, listening to the line’s faint hiss, that constant, nervous sound like fat beginning to sputter in a pan—Fleet Street never completely quiet, even when you held the receiver.

“Hullo!” he cried.

From the instrument came a voice full of irritation and humour, as if a painter had been dragged from his palette to answer a summons from the taxman.

“Hullo be blowed! What do you want?”

“This is Molloy,” said Sir James.

“I know it is,” said the voice. “This is Trent. He is in the middle of painting a picture, and he has been interrupted at a critical moment. Well, I hope it’s something important, that’s all!”

“Trent,” said Sir James, and the emphasis in the name carried more weight than flattery, “it is important. I want you to do some work for us.”

“Some play, you mean,” Trent answered. “Believe me, I don’t want a holiday. The working fit is very strong. I am doing some really decent things. Why can’t you leave a man alone?”

“Something very serious has happened.”

“What?”

“Sigsbee Manderson has been murdered—shot through the brain—and they don’t know who has done it. They found the body this morning. It happened at his place near Bishopsbridge.”

Sir James told him, briefly and clearly, the facts he had given Figgis—White Gables, Marlstone, the late bedtime, the unknown hour of rising, the gardener’s discovery by the shed, the bullet through the left eye, the struggle marks, Dr. Stock, the reticent police and their lack of a clue.

“What do you think of it?” Sir James ended.

There was a considering grunt, as if Trent were tasting the facts and finding them neither sweet nor simple.

“Come now,” urged Sir James.

“Tempter!”

“You will go down?”

A pause; in it one could almost hear Trent’s reluctance wrestling with the thing in him that could not resist a mystery—like a tongue that must try whatever is on the spoon.

“Are you there?” Sir James pressed.

“Look here, Molloy,” Trent broke out querulously, “the thing may be a case for me, or it may not. We can’t possibly tell. It may be a mystery; it may be as simple as bread and cheese. The body not being robbed looks interesting, but he may have been outed by some wretched tramp whom he found sleeping in the grounds and tried to kick out. It’s the sort of thing he would do. Such a murderer might easily have sense enough to know that to leave the money and valuables was the safest thing. I tell you frankly, I wouldn’t have a hand in hanging a poor devil who had let daylight into a man like Sig Manderson as a measure of social protest.”

Sir James smiled at the telephone, the smile of a man who knows the flavour of his bait has been taken.

“Come, my boy, you’re getting feeble. Admit you want to go and have a look at the case. You know you do. If it’s anything you don’t want to handle, you’re free to drop it. By the by, where are you?”

“I am blown along a wandering wind,” Trent replied irresolutely, “and hollow, hollow, hollow all delight.”

“Can you get here within an hour?” Sir James persisted.

“I suppose I can,” Trent grumbled. “How much time have I?”

“Good man! Well, there’s time enough—that’s just the worst of it. I’ve got to depend on our local correspondent for tonight. The only good train of the day went half an hour ago. The next is a slow one, leaving Paddington at midnight. You could have the Buster, if you like”—Sir James meant his very fast motor car—“but you wouldn’t get down in time to do anything tonight.”

“And I’d miss my sleep. No, thanks. The train for me. I am quite fond of railway travelling, you know; I have a gift for it. I am the stoker and the stoked. I am the song the porter sings.”

“What’s that you say?”

“It doesn’t matter,” Trent said sadly. Then, more practically, “I say—will your people look out a hotel near the scene of action, and telegraph for a room?”

“At once,” said Sir James. “Come here as soon as you can.”

He replaced the receiver and turned back to his papers. For a moment he heard—through the open window—the street’s restless chorus: wheels on stone, hooves, horns, a vendor’s cry, the thin treble of boys. The city’s soundscape was a living thing, and Fleet Street fed it with news as a kitchen feeds a household: regularly, vigorously, without pity.

Then a shrill outcry burst up from below, as if the building itself had found a voice.

Sir James crossed to the open window. A band of excited boys was rushing down the steps of the Sun building and up the narrow thoroughfare toward Fleet Street. Each carried a bundle of papers and a large broadsheet. The black letters shouted their simple legend into the wind and into every ear that had not learned to refuse the city’s ceaseless calling:

MURDER OF SIGSBEE MANDERSON

Sir James smiled and rattled the money in his pockets cheerfully, a man who could not help admiring a clean, quick piece of work, even when it tasted of blood.

“It makes a good bill,” he observed to Mr. Silver, who stood at his elbow.

Such was Manderson’s epitaph.

Mr. Nathaniel Burton Cupples stood on the hotel veranda at Marlstone a little before eight, letting the morning arrange itself in his mind the way a well-set table does—each thing in its proper place, no more and no less. He had been awake an hour, and the hour had done what honest sea air always does: it had sharpened him into hunger.

He reviewed yesterday with the same conscientious exactitude with which he might have reviewed a pantry. The shock and commotion after the finding of the dead man had disordered his appetite, and therefore his intake. That deficiency must be made good. This morning, then: three slices of toast, not two; an extra egg, the rest as usual. Luncheon could bear the remainder of the burden when it came.

The waiter brought what was ordered, and Cupples ate with the quiet gratitude of a man who notices what others swallow unthinking: the crackle of toast where the butter had melted into the pores; the faint, clean sulphur of the egg as the shell yielded; tea that smelt of leaf and hot china. Beneath it all lay the shore’s own seasoning—brine, warmed kelp, and the resinous breath of the pines above the cliff-path—set to the steady acoustic of the place: gulls turning their cries over the water, the hush-and-draw of the tide in the shingle, the thin click of flag halyards against a mast somewhere down in the gap of the village. It was an honest soundscape, not yet torn to pieces by the hysterical grinding of machinery; even the hotel, with its clatter of plates, seemed to accept the sea’s rhythm rather than impose its own.

When he had eaten, and before he called for anything further, he gave himself a few minutes of landscape, for he delighted in it with the same respectful connoisseurship he gave to a well-made meal. The great pierced rock stood out of a glassy sea like an emblem—an accident of geology that looked, in this light, almost intentional; and beyond it the sloping levels of pasture and tilled land rose toward the moor in ordered bands, hedges and copses making patterns as old as the place. It was a country where memory sat in the very contours.

Cupples himself, spare and nearly sixty, had the active wiriness of a man whose mind never let his body lapse into negligence. His sparse beard and moustache could not hide the kindliness of his mouth; his keen pleasant eyes took in detail without fuss. The sharp nose and narrow jaw, the dark clothes and soft black hat, gave him a priestly air, though his creed was of another sort: Positivist, bookish, museum-haunting, retired from banking into the quiet half-lit world of scholarship and gentle research. He was orderly, industrious, conscientious, with little imagination; yet his heart was inexhaustibly kind, and his gaiety—innocent, unhumorous—could surface like sunlight on water. His favourite author was Montaigne, and he quoted him as other men quoted Scripture.

He had only just finished when a large motor-car swung into the drive with a noise that jarred the morning—an arrogant, metallic intrusion into the sea’s soft governance. The engine idled with a growl that seemed to flatten the gull-cries and make the whole veranda unconsciously tense. Cupples turned, mildly displeased, and asked the waiter, “Who is this?”

“Id is der manager,” said the young man, listless. “He have been to meed a gendleman by der train.”

The car drew up. The porter ran out; the manager’s voice sounded, brisk and important. Then, out of the machine, long-limbed and loose-built, much younger than Cupples, came a man whose presence altered the air at once, as a strong spice changes a broth. His tweeds were rough, his hair and short moustache tolerably untidy, and his high-boned, quixotic face wore a smile that looked both pleasant and alert, as if it was listening for something.

“Cupples, by all that’s miraculous!” he cried, and pounced upon him before Cupples could fully rise, catching his outstretched hand in a hard grip. “My luck is serving me today. This is the second slice within an hour. How are you, my best of friends? And why are you here? Why sit’st thou by that ruined breakfast? Dost thou its former pride recall, or ponder how it passed away? I am glad to see you!”

“I was half expecting you, Trent,” Cupples said, beaming. “You look splendid, my dear fellow. But you cannot have had your breakfast yet. Will you have it at my table?”

“Rather! An enormous great breakfast too—with refined conversation and tears of recognition never dry. Will you get young Siegfried to lay a place for me while I go and wash? I shan’t be three minutes.”

He vanished into the hotel with the energy of a man who could not waste motion. Cupples, after a moment’s thought, went into the porter’s office and used the telephone.

When he returned Trent was already seated, pouring tea as if he had always belonged there, and studying the food with genuine interest—the sort of interest that begins in appetite but travels quickly into observation.

“I expect this to be a hard day for me,” Trent said in his curious jerky way. “I shan’t eat again till the evening, very likely. You guess why I’m here, don’t you?”

“Undoubtedly,” Cupples replied. “You have come down to write about the murder.”

“That is rather a colourless way of stating it,” Trent said, and began upon a sole with the swift neatness of a man whose hands had been trained to accuracy, whether in cutting fish or cutting facts. “I should prefer to put it that I have come down in the character of avenger of blood, to hunt down the guilty, and vindicate the honour of society. That is my line of business. Families waited on at their private residences. I say, Cupples, I have made a good beginning already. Wait a bit, and I’ll tell you.”

He ate quickly, abstractedly, as if breakfast were fuel and pleasure together, but also an interval to think in. Cupples watched him with content, as if the sight of a friend eating well were itself restorative.

“Your manager here,” Trent said at last, “is a fellow of remarkable judgement. He is an admirer of mine. He knows more about my best cases than I do myself. The Record wired last night to say I was coming, and when I got out of the train at seven this morning, there he was waiting with a motor-car the size of a haystack. He is beside himself with joy at having me here. It is fame.”

He drank tea and went on. “Almost his first words were to ask me if I would like to see the body of the murdered man—if so, he thought he could manage it for me. Keen as a razor. The body’s in Dr. Stock’s surgery down in the village, exactly as it was when found. It’s to be post-mortem’d this morning, so I was only just in time. He ran me down to the doctor’s, giving me full particulars all the way. I was pretty well au fait by the time we arrived. The manager has some pull with the doctor, I suppose. Anyhow, no difficulties—nor the constable on duty either, though he was careful to insist I mustn’t give him away in the paper.”

“I saw the body before it was removed,” Cupples said. “I should not have called anything remarkable, except that the shot in the eye had scarcely disfigured the face at all, and there was scarcely any effusion of blood, apparently. The wrists were scratched and bruised. I expect that, with your trained faculties, you remarked other details.”

“Other details, yes; whether they suggest anything, I don’t know. They’re odd.” Trent paused long enough to consider his next mouthful. “Take the wrists. How was it you could see bruises and scratches on them? I dare say you saw something of Manderson before the murder.”

“Certainly.”

“Well, did you ever see his wrists?”

Cupples searched his memory. “No. Now you raise the point, I am reminded that when I interviewed Manderson here he wore stiff cuffs, coming well down over his hands.”

“He always did,” Trent said. “My friend the manager says so. I pointed out to him something you didn’t observe: no cuffs visible. They’d been dragged up inside the coat-sleeves—like yours would be if you hurried into a coat without pulling your cuffs down. That’s why you saw his wrists.”

“Well, I call that suggestive,” Cupples said mildly. “You might infer, perhaps, that when he got up he hurried over his dressing.”

“Yes—did he?” Trent’s voice sharpened with that sudden turning of attention which made him so effective. “The manager drew that inference too. ‘He was always a bit of a swell in his dress,’ he told me; therefore, when he got up in that mysterious way before the house was stirring and went out into the grounds, he was in a great hurry. ‘Look at his shoes,’ he said. ‘Mr. Manderson was always specially neat about his footwear. Those laces were tied in a hurry.’ I agreed. ‘And he left his false teeth in his room,’ says the manager. ‘Doesn’t that prove he was flustered?’ I allowed it looked like it. But I said: if he was pressed, why did he part his hair so carefully? That parting is a work of art. Why did he put on so much? A complete outfit of underclothing, studs in the shirt, sock-suspenders, a watch and chain, money and keys in his pockets. That’s what I said. The manager couldn’t explain. Can you?”

Cupples considered, honestly trying to fit the facts together as one fits ingredients to a recipe. “Those facts might suggest he was hurried only at the end of his dressing,” he said. “Coat and shoes come last.”

“But not false teeth. Ask anybody who wears them. And besides, I’m told he hadn’t washed at all on getting up—which in a neat man looks like violent hurry from the beginning. And here’s another: one waistcoat pocket was lined with wash-leather for his gold watch. But he put the watch in the pocket on the other side. Anyone with settled habits can see how odd that is.” Trent’s knife tapped lightly against his plate. “There are signs of agitation and haste, and signs of exactly the opposite. For the present I’m not guessing. I must reconnoitre the ground first—if I can get the right side of the people in the house.”

Cupples smiled. “That,” he said, “is precisely the point on which I can assist you.”

Trent looked up sharply. “You can?”

“I told you I half expected you,” Cupples went on. “I will explain. Mrs. Manderson, who is my niece—”

“What!” Trent dropped knife and fork with a clatter. “Cupples, you are jesting.”

“I am perfectly serious,” Cupples said earnestly. “Her father, John Peter Domecq, was my wife’s brother. I never mentioned my niece or her marriage to you. To tell the truth, it has always been painful to me, and I have avoided discussing it with anyone.”

He indicated, as he spoke, a red roof among poplars some three hundred yards away—the only building in sight standing apart from the tiny village in the gap below. “Last night, when I was over at the house—you passed it in the car—Mr. Bunner, one of Manderson’s two secretaries, expressed a hope that the Record would send you down; the police seemed quite at a loss. He mentioned your successes, and Mabel—my niece—was interested when I told her afterwards. She has a great horror of the newspaper side of this sad business, and she had entreated me to do anything I could to keep journalists away—I’m sure you can understand that; it isn’t a reflection on your profession. But she said you appeared to have powers as a detective, and she would not stand in the way of anything that might clear up the crime. Then I told her you were a personal friend of mine, and I gave you your character for tact and consideration; and it ended in her saying that if you came she would like you to be helped in every way.”

Trent leaned forward and took Cupples’s hand, and for once said nothing. Cupples, warmed by this and pleased at the good turn of things, continued:

“I spoke to my niece on the telephone only just now, and she is glad you are here. She asks me to say you may make any enquiries you like; she puts the house and grounds at your disposal. She had rather not see you herself. She is keeping to her own sitting-room. She has already been interviewed by a detective officer who is there, and she feels unequal to any more. She adds she does not believe she could say anything useful. The two secretaries and Martin, the butler—who is a most intelligent man—could tell you all you want to know, she thinks.”

Trent finished eating with a thoughtful brow, as if the last mouthfuls had become suddenly tasteless under the pressure of new knowledge. He filled a pipe slowly and sat on the veranda rail, the motor’s earlier din forgotten now in the clean persistence of sea and birds.

“Cupples,” he said quietly, “is there anything about this business that you know and would rather not tell me?”

Cupples started slightly and stared at him. “What do you mean?” he asked.

“I mean about the Mandersons. Look here. Something strikes me at the very beginning. Here’s a man suddenly and violently killed, and nobody’s heart seems to be broken about it, to say the least. The manager spoke of him as coolly as if he’d never set eyes on him, though they’ve been neighbours every summer for years. Then you talk about it in the coldest blood. And Mrs. Manderson—well, I’ve heard of women being more cut up about their husbands being murdered than she seems to be. Is there something in this, Cupples, or is it my fancy? Was there something queer about Manderson? I travelled on the same boat with him once, but never spoke. I only know his public character, which was repulsive enough. This may bear on the case. That’s the only reason I ask.”

Cupples took time. He fingered his sparse beard and looked out over the sea, where the tide’s soft breathing seemed to urge caution—say only what is necessary, and say it cleanly, because words, once loosed, cannot be recalled. At last he turned.

“I see no reason,” he said, “why I shouldn’t tell you as between ourselves, my dear fellow. I need not say it must not be referred to, however distantly. The truth is that nobody really liked Manderson; and I think those who were nearest to him liked him least.”

“Why?” Trent cut in.

“Most people found it difficult to explain.” Cupples spoke carefully, as a food-historian might speak of some lost dish that cannot be perfectly reconstructed because the old dialect of the kitchen has died out. “In trying to account for my own sensations I could only put it that one felt in him a complete absence of the sympathetic faculty. There was nothing outwardly repellent. He was not ill-mannered, or vicious, or dull—indeed, he could be remarkably interesting. But I received the impression there could be no human creature whom he would not sacrifice in pursuit of his schemes, in his task of imposing himself and his will upon the world. Perhaps that is fanciful; I think not altogether so.”

He paused, then went on with a quiet sorrow that sat oddly with the bright morning. “The point is that Mabel—my niece—was very unhappy. I am nearly twice your age, my dear boy, though you always try to make me feel we are contemporaries. I am getting to be an old man, and many people have confided their matrimonial troubles to me; but I never knew another case like my niece’s and her husband’s. I have known her since she was a baby, Trent, and I know—understand, I do not employ that word lightly—I know she is as amiable and honourable a woman, besides her other gifts, as any man could wish. But Manderson, for some time past, had made her miserable.”

“What did he do?” Trent asked when Cupples stopped.

“When I put that question to Mabel, her words were that he seemed to nurse a perpetual grievance. He maintained a distance between them, and he would say nothing. I don’t know how it began or what lay behind it. All she would tell me on that point was that he had no cause in the world for his attitude. I think she knew what was in his mind, whatever it was; but she is full of pride.” Cupples’s voice tightened a little. “This went on for months. At last, a week ago, she wrote to me. I am the only near relative she has. Her mother died when she was a child; after John Peter died I was something like a father to her until she married—five years ago. She asked me to come and help her, and I came at once. That is why I am here.”

He took a sip of tea, as if to moisten words that did not want to come. Trent smoked, staring out at the hot June fields that rose toward the moor—the kind of land where families stayed rooted for generations, where speech and habit were handed down like seed-corn, and where a marriage was not merely two people but two histories tied together.

“I would not go to White Gables,” Cupples resumed. “You know my views upon the economic constitution of society and the relationship of capitalist to employee; and you know, no doubt, what use Manderson made of his vast industrial power on several notorious occasions—especially the trouble in the Pennsylvania coal-fields three years ago. I regarded him, apart from any personal dislike, as a criminal and a disgrace to society. I came to this hotel, and I saw my niece here. She told me what I have told you. She said the worry and humiliation, and the strain of keeping up appearances, were telling upon her, and she asked my advice. I said she should face him and demand an explanation. But she would not. She had always taken the line of affecting not to notice the change, and nothing would persuade her to admit to him that she was injured once pride had led her into that course. Life is quite full,” Cupples sighed, “of these obstinate silences and cultivated misunderstandings.”

“Did she love him?” Trent asked abruptly; then corrected himself. “Had she any love left for him?”

Cupples played with his teaspoon. “I am bound to say,” he answered slowly, “that I think not. But you must not misunderstand the woman. No power on earth would have persuaded her to admit that to anyone—even to herself, perhaps—so long as she considered herself bound to him. And I gather that, apart from this mysterious sulking of late, he had always been considerate and generous.”

“You were saying she refused to have it out with him.”

“She did,” Cupples said. “And I knew by experience it was useless to attempt to move a Domecq where the sense of dignity was involved. So I thought it over carefully, and next day I watched my opportunity and met Manderson as he passed by this hotel. I asked him to favour me with a few minutes’ conversation, and he stepped inside the gate. We had held no communication since my niece’s marriage, but he remembered me. I put the matter to him at once. I told him what Mabel had confided. I said I would neither approve nor condemn her bringing me into it, but she was suffering, and I considered it my right to ask how he justified placing her in such a position.”

“And how did he take that?” Trent asked, with a private smile at the notion of gentle Cupples confronting so formidable a man.

“Not very well,” Cupples said sadly. “Far from well. I can tell you almost exactly what he said—it wasn’t much. He said: ‘See here, Cupples, you don’t want to butt in. My wife can look after herself. I’ve found that out, along with other things.’ He was perfectly quiet—he was said never to lose control—though there was a light in his eyes that would have frightened a man who was in the wrong, I dare say.”

Cupples’s hand closed on the teaspoon as if it were the only solid thing in reach. “But I had been thoroughly roused by that last remark, and the tone of it, which I cannot reproduce. You see—I love my niece. She is the only child there has been in our—my—house. Moreover, my wife brought her up, and any reflection on Mabel I could not help feeling, in the heat of the moment, as an indirect reflection upon one who is gone.”

“You turned upon him,” Trent said softly. “You asked him to explain.”

“That is precisely what I did.” Cupples looked, for a second, as if he could see again the man’s face. “For a moment he only stared, and I could see a vein on his forehead swelling—an unpleasant sight. Then he said quite quietly: ‘This thing has gone far enough, I guess,’ and turned to go.”

“Did he mean your interview?” Trent asked.

“From the words alone, yes.” Cupples’s eyes drifted again toward the red roof among poplars. “But the way he uttered them gave me a strange and apprehensive feeling. I received the impression he had formed some sinister resolve.”

He drew a breath. “I regret to say I had lost the power of dispassionate thought. I fell into a great rage”—his apology was in his mildness, not in his words—“and said a number of foolish things. I reminded him the law allowed a measure of freedom to wives who received intolerable treatment. I made irrelevant references to his public record, and expressed the view that such men as he were unfit to live. I said these things and others as ill-considered under the eyes—and possibly within earshot—of half a dozen persons sitting on this veranda. I noticed them, in spite of my agitation, looking at me as I walked up to the hotel again after relieving my mind—for it did relieve it,” he added with a sigh.

“And Manderson? He said no more?”

“Not a word. He listened with his eyes on my face, quiet as before. When I stopped he smiled very slightly, and turned away and strolled through the gate toward White Gables.”

“And this happened—?”

“On the Sunday morning.”

“Then you never saw him alive again?”

“No,” Cupples said. Then corrected himself with the exactness that was his habit. “Or rather yes—once. Later in the day, on the golf-course. But I did not speak to him. And next morning he was found dead.”

They sat in silence, the sea’s breathing underneath it. A party of guests, back from bathing, came up the steps chattering; their laughter, striking the veranda boards, sounded oddly loud after what had been said. The waiter approached with the practised tread of service. Cupples rose, and, taking Trent’s arm, led him away along the side of the hotel to the long tennis-lawn, where the sound softened again into wind in grass and distant surf.

“I have a reason for telling you all this,” Cupples began as they paced slowly up and down.

“Trust you for that,” Trent returned, refilling his pipe with the careful economy of a man who dislikes waste. He lit it, drew a few puffs, then said, “I’ll try and guess your reason, if you like.”

Cupples’s solemnity relaxed into the faintest smile. He said nothing.

“You thought it possible,” Trent said meditatively—“may I say you thought it practically certain?—that I should find out for myself there’d been something deeper than a mere conjugal tiff. You thought my unwholesome imagination would begin at once to play with the idea of Mrs. Manderson having something to do with the crime. Rather than let me lose myself in barren speculations, you decided to tell me exactly how matters stood—and incidentally to impress upon me, since I know how excellent your judgement is, your opinion of your niece. Is that about right?”

Philip Trent had grown up among pigments and varnish, in rooms where the day’s weather was judged not by the barometer but by how the light struck a primed canvas. His father’s name had opened certain doors; his own patience—lazy-seeming, yet continuous as a simmer that never quite leaves the boil—had kept them open. He worked in spells: long stretches of steady, unshowy labour, then sudden raptures when the brush flew as if it had been waiting all its life for that one hour. His pictures sold, which in England is a sort of secondary miracle; and because he carried his success lightly, with humour and an easy relish for other people, he was liked even by those who disliked art.

In culture he was catholic and pleasantly inexact; poetry he loved with the greed of a man who listens to words as if they were music, tasting their consonants like crust and their vowels like wine. At thirty-two he had not mislaid the faculty for laughter and risk. He had, too, a peculiar gift—unconscious and therefore potent—of making people forget to be guarded. He judged them sharply, but from within; his eyes did not accuse, they enjoyed. Men seldom felt “on their best behaviour” in his company, for he seemed always to be hearing something amusing in the world’s noise.

His notoriety—louder by a hundred times than his proper reputation as a painter—had come from an impulse as casual as putting salt into a sauce. He had picked up a newspaper one day and found it thick with a crime rare enough in England to sound, at first, like imported fiction: a murder in a railway carriage. Two people lay under suspicion. Trent, to whom such matters had been nothing more than printed melodrama, began idly reading the accounts in several journals; and then, as sometimes happens when a mind trained to composition meets a pattern of facts, his imagination fastened on the thing. The excitement that took him was the same bodily quickening he knew when a picture suddenly “came”—a kind of inner click, as of a latch lifting.

That evening he wrote a long letter to the editor of the Record, choosing that paper only because it had treated the facts with the most intelligence. With nothing but the newspapers for ingredients, he cooked a dish after Poe’s method in Mary Rogers: he drew attention to what looked like scraps and trimmings, arranged the evidence so that it altered flavour, and threw suspicion upon a man who had posed as a mere witness. Sir James Molloy printed the letter in leaded type. Before the night was old, he announced in his evening paper the arrest and full confession of Trent’s man.

Sir James, who had fingers in all the pies of London, made Trent’s acquaintance at once. They suited. Trent had that rare tact which collapses age-differences without seeming to notice them. The great rotary presses in the Record basement—those iron beasts whose steady thunder turns thought into paper—filled him with a fresh delight. He painted them, a machinery-scape in the manner of Heinrich Kley, and Molloy bought it at sight.

A few months later came the Ilkley mystery. Molloy entertained Trent at a dinner so soothingly well-managed—courses arriving with the soft inevitability of fate, sauces speaking their own dialect of butter and wine—that even a painter began to think like a reporter. Afterward Molloy offered him what Trent considered a fantastically large sum to go as the Record’s special man to Ilkley.

“You could do it,” Molloy urged. “You can write. You can talk to people. I can teach you the trade technicalities in half an hour. And you’ve a head for a mystery—imagination, and a cool judgement with it. Think how it would feel if you pulled it off.”

Trent admitted it would be a lark. He smoked, frowned, and then recognized the flavour of what held him back: the fear of an unfamiliar job. He had formed a moral habit of reacting against fear, as one might train oneself to eat bitter greens for health. He accepted.

He pulled it off; again he gave the authorities a start and a beating, and his name became public property. Then he went back to painting. He did not yearn toward journalism, and Molloy, who knew enough about art to behave honourably, refrained—as other editors did not—from trapping him with a salary. But over the next years Molloy applied to him perhaps thirty times for help with knots of fact at home and abroad. Sometimes Trent refused because his canvas held him; sometimes he was forestalled. Yet the irregular connection made him one of the best-known names in England. Characteristically, it was almost the only detail of him the public possessed. He had demanded silence about his private life from the Molloy papers, and the others were not going to advertise one of Sir James’s men.

So now, walking quickly up the sloping road to White Gables, he told himself that the Manderson business might prove terribly simple. Cupples, wise old fellow though he was, could hardly be impartial where his niece was concerned. Yet the hotel manager who had spoken of her beauty had spoken, even more emphatically, of her goodness, and not in the sugary, over-sweet way men use when they are merely praising. He had conveyed something definite—sound as the smell of honest bread.

“There isn’t a child about here that don’t brighten up at the sound of her voice,” the man had said. “Nor yet a grown-up, for that matter. Everybody used to look forward to her coming over in the summer. I don’t mean she’s one of those women that are all kind heart and nothing else. There’s backbone with it—pluck—any amount of go. There’s nobody in Marlstone that isn’t sorry for the lady in her trouble—not but what some of us may think she’s lucky at the last of it.”

Trent wanted very much to meet Mrs. Manderson.

Beyond a spacious lawn and shrubbery he now saw the front of the house: two stories of dull-red brick, and the pair of great gables that gave it its name. Modern—ten years old, perhaps. Everything about it was kept with a disciplined tenderness: clipped edges, swept gravel, an opulent peace that clung even to a small country house when money and trained service had smoothed its surfaces. Before it, beyond the road, the meadow-land ran down to the cliff edge; behind, woods rolled away across a broad vale toward the moors. The place looked as if its days were ordered by bells and habits—the soft closing of doors, the muted clink of breakfast china, the far-off rasp of a rake in the beds—yet it had been the scene of violence. That contradiction struck Trent almost as a kind of blasphemy against domestic routine.

There, beyond the house and near the hedge that rose between garden and hot white road, stood the gardener’s toolshed. By that wooden wall the body had been found, tumbled against it.

Trent passed the drive gate and walked along the road until he stood opposite the shed. Forty yards farther the road turned sharply away between thick plantations; just before the turn, the grounds ended in a small white gate at the boundary hedge—plainly a service entrance. It swung easily. He went in and followed a path that led toward the back of the house between the hedge and a tall wall of rhododendrons. Through a gap he reached the neat wooden hut among trees facing a corner of the front.

The body had lain on the far side, away from the house. He pictured, with an artist’s involuntary cruelty, a servant at an early window yesterday morning letting her eyes rest on that hut without recognition, thinking perhaps of the master’s riches and the day’s kitchen work—bread rising, meat jointed, tea brewed—while death lay pressed to the boards like something dropped and forgotten.

He examined the place with care, then ransacked the hut within. Nothing offered itself except the trodden look of the uncut grass where a weight had been. He crouched, searched with eyes and fingers over a wide area, feeling the soil as if it might speak in grain and grit. The search gave him nothing.

It was broken by sound—the first from the house: the closing of the front door. Trent straightened and stepped to the edge of the drive. A man was walking quickly away from the house toward the great gate.

At the crunch of a footstep on gravel the man wheeled with nervous swiftness. His face, when he turned it on Trent, was almost terrible in its whiteness and wearing, like a young face dragged overnight through years. There was no wrinkle about the haggard blue eyes, yet they told of strain and desperate fatigue. As they approached one another Trent noticed, with a kind of reluctant admiration, the breadth of shoulder and lithe strength of the figure. Even the way he held himself—made inelastic by weariness—confessed training: Oxford, thought Trent, had been this young gentleman’s playground.

“If you are Mr. Trent,” said the young man pleasantly, “you are expected. Mr. Cupples telephoned from the hotel. My name is Marlowe.”

“You were secretary to Mr. Manderson, I believe,” Trent answered.

He found himself inclined to like Marlowe. Near to physical breakdown though he seemed, he gave out that air of clean living and inward health that belongs, at its best, to his type at his years. Yet there was something in the tired eyes that challenged Trent’s penetration: a habitual look, as if the man was forever weighing things that were not in front of him. Not dreamy—too steady for that—rather as if he listened for meanings under the day’s surface noise. Trent had seen such a look before somewhere, but could not place it.

“It is a terrible business for all of you,” he said. “I fear it has upset you completely, Mr. Marlowe.”

“A little limp, that’s all,” Marlowe replied wearily. “I was driving the car all Sunday night and most of yesterday, and I didn’t sleep last night after hearing the news—who would? But I have an appointment now, Mr. Trent, down at the doctor’s—arranging about the inquest. I expect it’ll be tomorrow. If you will go up to the house and ask for Mr. Bunner, you’ll find him expecting you; he will tell you all about things and show you round. He’s the other secretary; an American, and the best of fellows; he’ll look after you. There’s a detective here, by the way—Inspector Murch, from Scotland Yard. He came yesterday.”

“Murch!” Trent exclaimed. “But he and I are old friends. How under the sun did he get here so soon?”

“I have no idea,” Marlowe said. “But he was here last evening, before I got back from Southampton, interviewing everybody, and he’s been about here since eight this morning. He’s in the library now—that’s where the open French window is that you see at the end of the house there. Perhaps you would like to step down there and talk about things.”

“I think I will.”

Marlowe nodded and went on. Trent crossed the thick turf of the lawn; it took his steps like felt, swallowing sound. In moments he was looking in through the open leaves of the French window at the southward end of the house, amused at the sight of a very broad back and a bent head with short grizzled hair, stooped over papers on a table.

“’Twas ever thus,” said Trent, in a melancholy voice, at which the man within turned with startling swiftness. “From childhood’s hour I’ve seen my fondest hopes decay. I did think I was ahead of Scotland Yard this time, and now here is the hugest officer in the entire Metropolitan force already occupying the position.”

The detective smiled grimly and came to the window. “I was expecting you, Mr. Trent,” he said. “This is the sort of case that you like.”

“Since my tastes were being considered,” Trent replied, stepping into the room, “I wish they had followed up the idea by keeping my hated rival out of the business. You have got a long start, too—I know all about it.” His eyes began to roam, quick and busy. “How did you manage it? You are a quick mover, I know; the dun deer’s hide on fleeter foot was never tied; but I don’t see how you got here in time to be at work yesterday evening. Has Scotland Yard secretly started an aviation corps? Or is it in league with the infernal powers? In either case the Home Secretary should be called upon to make a statement.”

“It’s simpler than that,” said Inspector Murch, with professional heaviness. “I happened to be on leave with the missus at Havley, which is only twelve miles or so along the coast. As soon as our people there heard of the murder they told me. I wired to the Chief, and was put in charge of the case at once. I bicycled over yesterday evening, and have been at it since then.”

“Arising out of that reply,” said Trent, inattentively, “how is Mrs. Inspector Murch?”

“Never better, thank you,” answered the inspector, “and frequently speaks of you and the games you used to have with our kids. But you’ll excuse me saying, Mr. Trent, that you needn’t trouble to talk your nonsense to me while you’re using your eyes. I know your ways by now. I understand you’ve fallen on your feet as usual, and have the lady’s permission to go over the place and make enquiries.”

“Such is the fact,” said Trent. “I am going to cut you out again, inspector. I owe you one for beating me over the Abinger case, you old fox. But if you really mean that you’re not inclined for the social amenities just now, let us leave compliments and talk business.”

He stepped to the table, glanced through the papers set out there in order, then turned to the open roll-top desk. His hands moved swiftly; drawers were looked into, shut again. “I see this has been cleared out. Well now, inspector, I suppose we play the game as before.”

He and Murch had been thrown together often enough in the past for a friendship to have formed—the odd, clipped intimacy that grows between two men who compete with rules. Murch stood high in the Criminal Investigation Department: quiet, tactful, shrewd, courageous, with a history among dangerous men. His humanity was as broad as his shoulders, which were large even for a policeman. Trent delighted in such friendships, collecting them like experiences, and this one had always had a special zest. The inspector would talk more freely to him than to any other, under the rose; and they would compare details and possibilities, each learning from the other. There were boundaries: Trent made no journalistic use of anything that could only have come from official channels. Each reserved, for the honour of his institution, the right to withhold any vital discovery. Trent had dignified these understandings with the phrase “detective sportsmanship.” Murch loved a contest and gained by Trent’s quick intelligence, so he entered into “the game” with gusto. Sometimes experience and method won; sometimes Trent’s livelier imagination and knack for sniffing the significant out of rubbish.

Now, leaning on either side of the open French window, with summer’s hazy splendour and the far, living hush of the countryside before them—a world where sound came softened by grass and leaf rather than hammered by city stone—they reviewed the case.

Trent took out a thin notebook. As they talked he began, with light secure touches, to sketch a rough plan of the room. He did it habitually, sometimes idly, but more than once that habit had paid him back like a well-kept starter passed from batch to batch.

The library was large and bright, at the corner of the house, with generous window space in two walls. A broad table stood in the middle. Entering by the French window, the roll-top desk stood just left of it against the wall. The inner door was in the left wall at the farther end, faced by a broad casement window. A beautifully carved old corner-cupboard rose high beyond the door; another cupboard filled a recess by the fireplace. Coloured prints of Harunobu—Trent promised himself a better acquaintance with them—hung on the scant wall space not occupied by books. They had the uninspiring look of things bought by the yard and never taken down: art turned into décor, its voice muffled by possession. The shelves held the great English novelists, essayists, historians, poets—bound in sober luxury, ranged like an army struck dead in its ranks. A few chairs of carved oak matched the table; a modern armchair and a swivel office-chair sat before the desk. The room looked costly but bare. The only portable objects were a great porcelain bowl of wonderful blue on the table, a clock and some cigar boxes on the mantelshelf, and a movable telephone standard on top of the desk.

“Seen the body?” Murch asked.

Trent nodded. “And the place where it lay.”

“First impressions puzzle me,” the inspector said. “From what I heard at Havley I guessed it might be common robbery and murder by some tramp, though such a thing is very far from common in these parts. But as soon as I began enquiries I came on curious points—which, by now, I dare say you’ve noted for yourself. The man is shot in his own grounds, quite near the house, to begin with. Yet there’s not the slightest trace of any attempt at burglary. And the body wasn’t robbed. In fact, it would be as plain a case of suicide as you could wish to see, if it wasn’t for certain facts. Here’s another thing: for a month or so past, they tell me, Manderson had been in a queer state of mind. I expect you know already that he and his wife had some trouble between them. The servants had noticed a change in his manner to her for a long time, and for the past week he had scarcely spoken to her. They say he was a changed man, moody and silent—whether on account of that or something else. The lady’s maid says he looked as if something was going to arrive. It’s always easy to remember that people looked like that, after something has happened to them. Still, that’s what they say. There you are again, then: suicide. Now, why wasn’t it suicide, Mr. Trent?”

“The facts so far as I know them are really all against it,” Trent replied, sitting on the window threshold and clasping his knees. “First, of course, no weapon is to be found. I’ve searched, and you’ve searched, and there’s no trace of any firearm anywhere within a stone’s throw of where the body lay. Second, the marks on the wrists—fresh scratches and bruises—which we can only assume to have been done in a struggle with somebody. Third, who ever heard of anybody shooting himself in the eye? Then I heard from the manager of the hotel here another fact which strikes me as the most curious detail in this affair. Manderson had dressed himself fully before going out there, but he forgot his false teeth. Now how could a suicide who dressed himself to make a decent appearance as a corpse forget his teeth?”

“That last argument hadn’t struck me,” Murch admitted. “There’s something in it. But on the strength of the other points, which had occurred to me, I am not considering suicide. I have been looking about for ideas in this house, this morning. I expect you were thinking of doing the same.”

“That is so. It is a case for ideas, it seems to me. Come, Murch, let us make an effort; let us bend our spirits to a temper of general suspicion. Let us suspect everybody in the house, to begin with. Listen: I will tell you whom I suspect. I suspect Mrs. Manderson, of course. I also suspect both the secretaries—I hear there are two, and I hardly know which of them I regard as more thoroughly open to suspicion. I suspect the butler and the lady’s maid. I suspect the other domestics, and especially do I suspect the boot-boy. By the way, what domestics are there? I have more than enough suspicion to go round, whatever the size of the establishment; but as a matter of curiosity I should like to know.”

“All very well to laugh,” said the inspector, “but at the first stage it’s the only safe principle, and you know that as well as I do. However, I’ve seen enough of the people here, last night and today, to put a few of them out of my mind for the present at least. You will form your own conclusions. As for the establishment, there’s the butler and lady’s maid, cook, and three other maids, one a young girl. One chauffeur, who’s away with a broken wrist. No boy.”

“What about the gardener? You say nothing about that shadowy and sinister figure, the gardener. You are keeping him in the background, Murch. Play the game. Out with him—or I report you to the Rules Committee.”

“The garden is attended to by a man in the village, who comes twice a week. I’ve talked to him. He was here last on Friday.”

“Then I suspect him all the more,” said Trent. “And now as to the house itself. What I propose to do, to begin with, is to sniff about a little in this room, where I am told Manderson spent a great deal of his time, and in his bedroom; especially the bedroom. But since we’re in this room, let’s start here. You seem to be at the same stage of the inquiry. Perhaps you’ve done the bedrooms already?”

The inspector nodded. “I’ve been over Manderson’s and his wife’s. Nothing to be got there, I think. His room is very simple and bare, no signs of any sort—that I could see. Seems to have insisted on the simple life, does Manderson. Never employed a valet. The room’s almost like a cell, except for the clothes and shoes. You’ll find it all exactly as I found it; and they tell me that’s exactly as Manderson left it, at we don’t know what o’clock yesterday morning. Opens into Mrs. Manderson’s bedroom—not much of the cell about that, I can tell you. I should say the lady was as fond of pretty things as most. But she cleared out of it on the morning of the discovery—told the maid she could never sleep in a room opening into her murdered husband’s room. Very natural feeling in a woman, Mr. Trent. She’s camping out, so to say, in one of the spare bedrooms now.”

Come, my friend, Trent said to himself, as he made a few notes. Have you got your eye on Mrs. Manderson? Or haven’t you? He knew that colourless tone—the inspectorial voice turned deliberately bland, like a cook withholding seasoning to see who reaches for the salt. Either Murch had something against her and did not want Trent to pick it up; or else he had decided she was innocent and did not mind Trent wasting time. Either way, it was “in the game”—and the game was beginning to smell, to Trent’s senses, extremely interesting indeed.

Aloud he said: “Well, I’ll draw the bedroom later on. What about this?”

There are hours when the mind, for all its busy, hidden labour, lets a single clear note escape into hearing—like a spoon touching a glass in a quiet kitchen, and the small ring of it telling you the broth is ready. Trent felt that note as he climbed. It was not confidence hammered up by vanity, nor the thin cheerfulness of the born optimist; it came of itself, as if some part of him had already tasted the shape of the truth. He was going to find something; he knew it with the same bodily certainty with which one knows, breathing in, that bread is in the oven.

The upper passage lay wide and well-carpeted, the sort of thick runner that drinks footfalls if the boards beneath will consent. Yet the house, old in its joints, had its own obstinate language: Trent hugged the wall, placed each foot as if setting down a cup of scalding coffee, and still the stair answered him with a series of creaks—little sharp consonants that carried. Through the end window the morning poured in, making the corridor bright as a dairy, doors on either side shut and waiting. Halfway up, on the small landing, was Martin’s room—square, scrubbed, commonplace. Trent glanced in and passed on.

Manderson’s room was, as he had been told, the first on the right. The latch lifted cleanly; the lock turned without catch. Trent examined the key’s wards with the care of a man checking whether a pantry has been tampered with, then went in and shut the door behind him.

The place was small, and its bareness struck harder for the sun in it. Manderson’s wealth had not translated into comfort here; there was no thick dressing-gown flung with flourish, no silver-backed brushes. The bed was narrow and wooden, the sheets and blankets dragged into the untidy heaps of a hurried rising. On a plain table by the bedside a shallow glass bowl held, in water, a delicate piece of dentistry—gold glinting like a coin under clear broth. Beside it stood a wrought-iron candlestick, severe as a trivet. Clothing lay over a rush-bottomed chair. The top of a chest of drawers—pressed into service as dressing-table—was littered in the way of a man who moves by habit, not ceremony. Trent’s finger turned the dental plate in its bath, and he frowned again at the thing’s stubborn presence. It had the wrong air, like a sweet herb in a dish that ought to be plain.

The sunlight made the disorder more gruesome, not less, as if the brightness insisted on showing every particular. For a moment his imagination, uninvited, furnished the scene: a haggard man dressing in careful silence at dawn, catching every sound, glancing again and again at the communicating door behind which his wife slept—eyes full of some secret fear.

He shook the picture off as one shakes flour from the hands, and went to the tall cupboards built into the wall on either side of the bed. They held clothes—many, well chosen—as though the one indulgence allowed here had been the feel of good cloth against the skin.

Then he turned to the shoes.

They were arranged with an almost loving order on two long low shelves: an extraordinary number, all treed, all carefully kept. No boots. Trent, who knew the pleasure of good leather and honest stitching, let his gaze move with appreciation over narrow, round-toed shoes, beautifully made, evidently cut from the same last. Manderson, it was plain, had valued the small, well-formed foot as another kind of possession.

His eyes narrowed over a pair of patent-leather shoes on the upper shelf.

These were the pair Inspector Murch had already described: worn the night before Manderson died. They were well-worn, and very newly polished; the black shone with that freshly-buffed gloss that still carries the smell of the paste. Something about the uppers caught him—something that didn’t belong among their neighbours. He bent lower, comparing them to the others, then took them down and examined the junction of upper and sole as if reading a seam for its story.

As he did so he began to whistle, faintly and with great precision, a phrase of music that would have told Murch, had Murch been there, that Trent had found scent.

Most men with self-control have some small betrayal when excitement presses. Trent’s was this almost inaudible melody—Mendelssohn, though the tune mattered less than the fact of it. He turned the shoes over, measured with a marked tape, and studied the bottoms minutely. In the angle between heel and instep, on each, he detected a faint trace of red gravel—dust caught as spice catches in an oil.

He set the shoes on the floor and walked to the window, hands clasped behind him, still whistling under his breath. He stared out, but his eyes saw neither lawn nor trees. Once his lips parted to let out, mechanically, the Englishman’s sudden expletive—an involuntary sound like a pan set down too hard.

He turned back, and swiftly, carefully, examined every shoe on the shelf, as a cook might check every jar in a row for one missing scent. When he had done, he took the garments from the chair, looked them over close, replaced them. He searched the cupboards again. He returned to the disorder on the makeshift dressing-table with renewed attention, as if the second look might make the objects speak in a different dialect.

Then he sat on the empty chair, head in his hands, eyes fixed on the carpet. He stayed so, still as a man tasting in memory, for several minutes. At last he rose and opened the inner door leading to Mrs. Manderson’s room.

The change struck at once. Manderson’s room had the raw disorder of haste; this larger room had been stripped—emptied as a kitchen looks when the shelves have been cleared for fumigation. All the small array of a woman’s dressing-table had been removed; there were no gloves straying like pale moths, no ribbon, no hat-box leaning, no scent-bottles, no intimate litter of handkerchiefs and pins. Bed and chairs and little tables stood bare. It might have been an unoccupied guest-chamber, except that every line of furniture and colour confessed taste—unconventional, exacting, sure. Trent’s eye, trained to arrangement and the meaning of chosen objects, noted the perfection of form and hue; and with it came a strengthened sense of the unseen woman who had lived here, and of the burdens laid upon her. The deed whose history he felt shaping itself grew heavier, more substantial, more human.

He went first to the tall French window facing the door, opened it, and stepped onto the small iron-railed balcony. Below lay a broad stretch of lawn beginning almost at the wall, only a narrow flower-bed between. It ran away, dipped abruptly near the far end, and led the eye toward the orchard. The other window—a sash—looked down above the library’s garden entrance. In the farther inside corner was a second door to the passage: the door by which the maid came in each morning, and her mistress went out into the day.

Returning inside, Trent sat on the bed and quickly sketched a plan in his notebook: this room and its neighbour, the doors, the windows, the placement. The bed stood in the angle between the communicating door and the sash-window, its head against the dividing wall. He stared at the pillows, then lay down deliberately on the bed and looked through the open door into Manderson’s room, taking in the line of sight, the angles, what could be seen and what must remain hidden.

He rose and added to his sketch: a small covered table on either side of the bed. On the table farthest from the door stood a graceful electric-lamp standard of copper, connected by a loose wire to the wall. Trent looked from it to the switches for the other lights—placed, as usual, just inside the door, out of reach from the bed. He tested the lights; all were in order.

Then he turned briskly into Manderson’s room and rang the bell.

“I want your help again, Martin,” he said when the butler appeared in the doorway—upright, impassive as a carved figure on a sideboard. “I want you to prevail upon Mrs. Manderson’s maid to grant me an interview.”

“Certainly, sir,” Martin replied.

“What sort of a woman is she? Has she her wits about her?”

“She’s French, sir,” said Martin, making a complete judgment of character in three words. After a pause he added, with restrained distaste: “She has not been with us long, sir, but I have formed the impression that the young woman knows as much of the world as is good for her—since you ask me.”

“You think butter might possibly melt in her mouth, do you?” said Trent. “Well, I am not afraid. I want to put some questions to her.”

“I will send her up immediately, sir.”

Martin withdrew. Left alone, Trent walked about, hands behind his back, listening to the house. Through an open window came subdued twittering from the trees, and the faint rustle of creeper leaves when a breeze moved. Against these living sounds, the house’s silence was almost oppressive—like a dining-room prepared too early, the cutlery laid and the chairs pushed in, waiting.

Sooner than he expected, a small neat figure in black appeared, moving quietly, almost noiselessly—trained feet on thick carpet.

The maid had already marked Trent from below, watching him cross the lawn. His reputation travelled downstairs as quickly as upstairs, and she had been longing for an excuse to bring herself into the story. Her nerves were overwrought; she wanted, above all things, an audience for her feelings. Her scenes, however, were cheap coin among the servants, and Inspector Murch’s official manner had chilled her into a tight-lipped obedience. Trent, at a distance, had seemed to her more human—_sympathique_.

When she entered and met his face close, instinct told her coquetry would be foolish at the beginning. She arranged herself into amiable candour.

“Monsieur desire to speak with me,” she said. “I am called Célestine.”

“Naturally,” said Trent, businesslike. “Now what I want you to tell me, Célestine, is this. When you took tea to your mistress yesterday morning at seven o’clock, was the door between the two bedrooms—this door here—open?”

Célestine’s animation flared as if someone had struck a match near a spill of spirits.

“Oh yes!” she cried, eager. “The door was open as always, monsieur, and I shut it as always. But it is necessary to explain. Listen! When I enter the room of madame from the other door in there—ah! but if monsieur will give himself the pain to enter the other room, all explains itself.”

She tripped to the door, laid a hand on his arm, and urged him ahead into Mrs. Manderson’s room, eager to stage the geography.

“See! I enter the room with the tea like this. I approach the bed. Before I come quite near the bed, here is the door to my right hand—open always—so! But monsieur can perceive that I see nothing in the room of Monsieur Manderson. The door opens to the bed, not to me who approach from down there. I shut it without seeing in. It is the order. Yesterday it was as ordinary. I see nothing of the next room. Madame sleep like an angel—she see nothing. I shut the door. I place the _plateau_—I open the curtains—I prepare the toilette—I retire—voilà!”

Her hands fluttered through the routine, as if she could conjure that morning back by mime: the tray balanced, the curtains drawn, the room coaxed toward waking. Trent watched her with deepening gravity, noting not only her words but the way the room’s angles supported them. The door indeed would be open, and yet not betray the other room to a person approaching the bed from the maid’s entrance.

“I see exactly how it was now,” he said. “Thank you, Célestine. So Mr. Manderson was supposed to be still in his room while your mistress was getting up, and dressing, and having breakfast in her boudoir?”

“Oui, monsieur.”

“Nobody missed him, in fact,” Trent remarked.

Célestine’s eyes shone with the fierce pleasure of having been useful. “It is nothing, monsieur. I hope that monsieur will catch the assassin of Monsieur Manderson.” Then, as if a cork had been pulled from long-stopped anger, her face changed; colour rose under the skin, her jaw set. English fled her as the tongue seeks refuge in its own country.

“But I not regret him too much,” she flung out, and then, with sudden violence: “Je ne le regrette pas du tout, du tout! Madame—ah! je me jetterais au feu pour madame—une femme si charmante, si adorable! Mais un homme comme monsieur—maussade, boudeur, impassible! Ah, non!—de ma vie! J’en avais par-dessus la tête, de monsieur! Ah! vrai! Est-ce insupportable, tout de même, qu’il existe des types comme ça? Je vous jure que—”

Trent cut across her torrent with sharp authority, as one slaps a lid onto a boiling pot.

“Finissez ce chahut, Célestine!” The phrase came with the ease of old use; the memory of student days rose in him, bringing back smoky rooms and quick French voices, arguments that ran on for the pleasure of sound. “En voilà une scène! C’est rasant, vous savez. Faut rentrer ça, mademoiselle. Du reste, c’est bien imprudent, croyez-moi. Hang it! Have some common sense! If the inspector downstairs heard you saying that kind of thing, you would get into trouble. And don’t wave your fists about so much; you might hit something.”

Her breathing steadied under his eye; she became smaller again, contained, though her anger still crackled like fat in a pan.

“You seem,” Trent went on more pleasantly, “to be even more glad than other people that Mr. Manderson is out of the way. I could almost suspect, Célestine, that Mr. Manderson did not take as much notice of you as you thought necessary and right.”

“A peine s’il m’avait regardé!” she answered simply—hurt made plain.

“Ça, c’est un comble!” Trent observed. “You are a nice young woman for a small tea-party, I don’t think. A star upon your birthday burned, whose fierce, serene, red, pulseless planet never yearned in heaven, Célestine. Mademoiselle, I am busy. Bon jour. You certainly are a beauty!”

She took it as a compliment—scarce expected—and the surprise restored her balance. With a flash of eyes and teeth over her shoulder, she slipped out and vanished, quick as a cat through a half-open door.

Left alone, Trent let fall two forcible descriptive terms in her language—quietly, between his teeth—then returned to his work.

He took the patent-leather shoes again and set them upon one of the chairs, as if seating two mute witnesses under examination. He placed himself on the other chair opposite, hands in his pockets, eyes fixed upon them. Now and then, almost inaudibly, he whistled a few bars. The room lay very still, but not dead: the trees outside murmured in small bird sounds; the creeper leaves stirred softly. The acoustic world was delicate here, as it should be in a place where people sleep and wake and speak in low voices. It made the idea of violent noise—a shot in the dark—seem an obscenity, a brutal intrusion of modern sharpness into a domestic hush.

He sat so for half an hour, face hard and sombre, moving no more than a man tasting a difficult sauce and refusing to be hurried.

Then he rose quickly, replaced the shoes on their shelf with care, and stepped out onto the landing.

Two bedroom doors faced him across the passage. He opened the one immediately opposite and entered a room that had not been tidied into austerity. Here was a young man’s jumble: sticks and fishing-rods crowded into one corner, books piled in another. On the dressing-table and mantelshelf the housemaid’s hand had failed to impose order: pipes, penknives, pencils, keys, golf-balls, old letters, photographs, small boxes, tins, bottles—objects with their own private histories, kept not for display but because they belonged to a life. Two fine etchings and some water-colour sketches hung on the walls; a few framed engravings leaned unhung against the wardrobe, like pictures waiting for a home. Beneath the window stood a row of shoes and boots.

Trent crossed and studied them intently, tape in hand. He measured some, whistling very softly, and then sat on the bed’s edge, letting his gaze roam with gloomy patience around the room.

The photographs on the mantelshelf drew him next. He rose and examined one of Marlowe and Manderson on horseback. Two others showed famous Alpine peaks, all clean light and cold distance. There was a faded print of three youths in tatterdemalion sixteenth-century soldier’s gear—one of them unmistakably Marlowe, even through costume and time, with those haggard blue eyes. Another was a portrait of a majestic old lady who slightly resembled Marlowe. Trent, almost mechanically, took a cigarette from an open box on the mantel, lit it, and stared at the images as if they might yield a word in some dialect he did not speak.

Beside the cigarette-box lay a flat leathern case.

It opened easily. Inside was a small light revolver of beautiful workmanship, with a score or so of loose cartridges. On the stock were engraved the initials “J. M.”

A step sounded on the stairs—firm, official, heavier than the servants’ quiet traffic—and as Trent opened the breech and peered into the barrel, Inspector Murch appeared at the open door.

“I was wondering—” the inspector began, and stopped as he saw what Trent held. His intelligent eyes opened a fraction. “Whose is the revolver, Mr. Trent?” he asked conversationally.

“Evidently it belongs to the occupant of the room, Mr. Marlowe,” Trent replied with the same lightness, pointing to the initials. “I found it lying about on the mantelpiece. It seems a handy little pistol to me, and it has been very carefully cleaned, I should say, since the last time it was used. But I know little about firearms.”

“Well, I know a good deal,” Murch returned quietly, taking it from Trent’s hand. “It’s a bit of a speciality with me, is firearms, as I think you know, Mr. Trent. But it don’t require an expert to tell one thing.”

He replaced the revolver in its case on the mantel, then took out a cartridge and laid it on the broad palm of his hand. From his waistcoat pocket he produced a small object and laid it beside the cartridge: a little leaden bullet, slightly battered at the nose, with bright new scratches shining on it.

“Is that the one?” Trent murmured, bending closer.

“That’s him,” said Murch. “Lodged in the bone at the back of the skull. Dr. Stock got it out within the last hour and handed it to the local officer, who’s just sent it on. These bright scratches were made by the doctor’s instruments. These other marks were made by the rifling of the barrel—a barrel like this one.” He tapped the revolver-case. “Same make, same calibre. There’s no other that marks the bullet just like this.”

For a moment they looked at each other over the closed case, as if it were a dish set between them with a smell both recognized and dreaded.

“This mystery is all wrong,” Trent said at last. “It is insanity. The symptoms of mania are very marked. Let us see how we stand. We were not in any doubt, I believe, about Manderson having dispatched Marlowe in the car to Southampton, or about Marlowe having gone, returning late last night, many hours after the murder was committed.”

“There is no doubt whatever about all that,” Murch replied, emphasizing the verb as if to nail it down.

“And now,” Trent pursued, “we are invited by this polished and insinuating firearm to believe the following line of propositions: that Marlowe never went to Southampton; that he returned to the house in the night; that he somehow, without waking Mrs. Manderson or anybody else, got Manderson to get up, dress himself, and go out into the grounds; that he then and there shot the said Manderson with his incriminating pistol; that he carefully cleaned the said pistol, returned to the house and, again without disturbing any one, replaced it in its case in a favourable position to be found by the officers of the law; that he then withdrew and spent the rest of the day in hiding—with a large motor car; and that he turned up, feigning ignorance of the whole affair, at—what time was it?”

“A little after nine p.m.” Murch still stared moodily at him. “As you say, Mr. Trent, that is the first theory suggested by this find, and it seems wild enough—at least it would do if it didn’t fall to pieces at the very start. When the murder was done Marlowe must have been fifty to a hundred miles away. He did go to Southampton.”

“How do you know?”

“I questioned him last night, and took down his story. He arrived in Southampton about six-thirty on the Monday morning.”

“Come off,” Trent exclaimed, bitter as burnt coffee. “What do I care about his story? What do you care about his story? I want to know how you know he went to Southampton.”

Murch chuckled—a brief sound, almost friendly, like a kettle beginning to sing. “I thought I should take a rise out of you, Mr. Trent. Well, there’s no harm in telling you. After I arrived yesterday evening, as soon as I’d got the outlines of the story from Mrs. Manderson and the servants, the first thing I did was to go to the telegraph office and wire to our people in Southampton. Manderson told his wife when he went to bed that he’d changed his mind, and sent Marlowe to Southampton to get some important information from some one crossing by the next day’s boat. It seemed right enough, but, you see, Marlowe was the only one of the household who wasn’t under my hand, so to speak. He didn’t return in the car until later in the evening; so before thinking the matter out any further, I wired to Southampton making certain enquiries. Early this morning I got this reply.”

He handed Trent the slips. Trent read:

Person answering description in motor answering description arrived Bedford Hotel here 6.30 this morning gave name Marlowe left car hotel garage told attendant car belonged Manderson had bath and breakfast went out heard of later at docks inquiring for passenger name Harris on Havre boat inquired repeatedly until boat left at noon next heard of at hotel where he lunched about 1.15 left soon afterwards in car company’s agents inform berth was booked name Harris last week but Harris did not travel by boat Burke Inspector.

Trent read it twice, as if the words might alter on the second tasting, then gave it back.

“Simple and satisfactory,” Murch observed. “His own story corroborated in every particular. He told me he hung about the dock for half an hour or so on the chance of Harris turning up late, then strolled back, lunched, and decided to return at once. He sent a wire to Manderson—‘Harris not turned up missed boat returning Marlowe’—which was duly delivered here in the afternoon, and placed among the dead man’s letters. He motored back at a good rate, and arrived dog-tired. When he heard of Manderson’s death from Martin, he nearly fainted. What with that and being without sleep for so long, he was rather a wreck when I came to interview him last night; but he was perfectly coherent.”

Trent picked up the revolver-case and turned it idly, as if weighing in his palm an ingredient that could spoil the whole dish.

“It was unlucky for Manderson,” he said at length, putting it back, “that Marlowe left his pistol and cartridges about so carelessly. It was throwing temptation in somebody’s way, don’t you think?”

“Calvin C. Bunner, at your service,” the newcomer corrected, with a punctilious little dip of the head, taking an unlit cigar from between his teeth as though it were part of his signature. Trent’s quick, unceremonious greeting had plainly knocked him half a step out of his practiced American ease; he recovered it at once, and his large dark eyes—bright with the attentive hunger of a man who makes a meal of facts—went from Trent to the captain.

“You are Mr. Trent, I expect. Mrs. Manderson was telling me a while ago. Captain—good-morning.”

Mr. Murch returned the foreign salutation with an economical nod.

“I was coming up to my room,” Mr. Bunner went on, “and I heard a strange voice in here, so I thought I would take a look in.” He laughed with an easy dryness. “You thought I might have been eavesdropping, perhaps. No, sir; I heard a word or two about a pistol—this one, I guess—and that’s all.”

He was a thin, rather short young man with a pale, shaven, almost girlish face—bones showing cleanly under the skin like the fine framework of a well-made tool. His hair waved darkly and was parted in the middle; without the cigar his lips stood half open in a look of perpetual readiness, as if he were forever tasting the air for the next hint of news. When the cigar returned to his mouth that expression vanished, and he wore instead the cool, seasoned mask of the sagacious Yankee.

Trent and the American took each other’s measure without hurry. Both looked satisfied.

“I was having it explained to me,” Trent said pleasantly, “that my discovery of a pistol that might have shot Mr. Manderson does not amount to very much. I am told it is a favourite weapon among your people, and has become quite popular over here.”

Mr. Bunner’s bony hand went out and lifted the pistol from its case. He handled it with the familiarity of a man who knows the weight of a thing before it settles in his palm, and who could tell you—if he cared—the exact shape of danger by touch alone.

“Yes, sir,” he said. “The captain is right. This is what we call at home a Little Arthur, and I dare say there are duplicates of it in a hundred thousand hip-pockets this minute. I consider it too light in the hand myself.” As he spoke his other hand went, by habit, under the tail of his jacket; he brought out an uglier-looking weapon and offered it butt-first to Trent. “Feel of that, now, Mr. Trent—it’s loaded, by the way.”

The metal was cold as a cellar stone; Trent felt, too, the quiet assurance with which Bunner held it, as if the thing belonged to him the way a knife belongs to a cook.

“Now this Little Arthur,” Bunner resumed, taking back his own and setting it away again, “Marlowe bought it just before we came over this year to please the old man. Manderson said it was ridiculous for a man to be without a pistol in the twentieth century. So he went out and bought what they offered him, I guess—never consulted me. Not but what it’s a good gun,” he conceded, squinting along the sights with a professional narrowing of the eye, “Marlowe was poor with it at first, but I’ve coached him some in the last month or so, and he’s practised until he is pretty good. But he never could get the habit of carrying it around. Why, it’s as natural to me as wearing my pants. I have carried one for some years now, because there was always likely to be somebody laying for Manderson. And now”—his voice dropped, and the cigar’s smoke came out slower—“they got him when I wasn’t around.”

He straightened, brisk again. “Well, gentlemen, you must excuse me. I am going into Bishopsbridge. There is a lot to do these days, and I have to send off a bunch of cables big enough to choke a cow.”

“I must be off too,” Trent said. “I have an appointment at the Three Tuns.”

“Let me give you a lift in the automobile,” Bunner offered cordially. “I go right by that joint. Say, cap., are you coming my way too?”

Mr. Murch, with the patient immovability of a man who prefers his own feet and his own pace, declined.

“Then come along, Mr. Trent,” Bunner said, already turning, “and help me get out the car. The chauffeur is out of action, and we have to do ’most everything ourselves except clean the dirt off her.”

Still talking in his measured drawl, Bunner led Trent downstairs, through the house, and out to the garage that stood a little away behind the rear walls, like a shed built for a noisier god than the quiet domesticity within. The place was cool after the blaze of the midday sun. A swallow cut the air outside with a thin, bright cry; inside, the noises were softened—boots on packed earth, the faint click of metal, the breathing of a machine at rest. Trent, who had been living these last hours among voices that lied, withheld, and broke off, found the change in acoustics almost a relief: here was a hush that meant shelter, not secrecy.

Mr. Bunner, however, seemed in no hurry to wake the car. He offered Trent a cigar, which Trent accepted; then, for the first time, Bunner lit his own. The match flared and died; the first draw brought a thick, brown sweetness into the air that mixed with the smell of oil and rubber—an odd, domestic sort of perfume, like a kitchen where the roast and the lamp have both been too long attended.

He sat down on the footboard, clasped his thin hands between his knees, and looked at Trent with a steadiness that was almost intimate.

“See here, Mr. Trent,” he said after a moment. “There are some things I can tell you that may be useful. I know your record. You are a smart man, and I like dealing with smart men. I don’t know if I have that detective sized up right, but he strikes me as a mutt. I would answer any questions he had the gumption to ask me—I have done so, in fact—but I don’t feel encouraged to give him any notions of mine without his asking. See?”

Trent nodded. “That is a feeling many people have in the presence of our police,” he said. “It’s the official manner, I suppose. But let me tell you, Murch is anything but what you think. He is one of the shrewdest officers in Europe. He is not very quick with his mind, but he is very sure. And his experience is immense. My forte is imagination, but I assure you in police work experience outweighs it by a great deal.”

“Outweigh nothing!” Bunner snapped, with a sudden hard certainty, like a knife striking a board. “This is no ordinary case, Mr. Trent. I will tell you one reason why. I believe the old man knew there was something coming to him. Another thing: I believe it was something he thought he couldn’t dodge.”

Trent pulled an empty crate nearer and sat opposite him on it. The wood creaked; somewhere beyond the garage a cart wheel passed on the road with a homely grind. “This sounds like business,” he said. “Tell me your ideas.”

“I say what I do because of the change in the old man’s manner this last few weeks. I dare say you have heard that he was a man who always kept himself well in hand. That was so. I have always considered him the coolest and hardest head in business. That man’s calm was just deadly—I never saw anything to beat it.”

Bunner’s eyes sharpened as though he were reading figures that refused to add up.

“And I knew Manderson as nobody else did. I was with him in the work he really lived for. I guess I knew him a heap better than his wife did, poor woman. I knew him better than Marlowe could—he never saw Manderson in his office when there was a big thing on. I knew him better than any of his friends.”

“Had he any friends?” Trent put in.

Bunner’s glance flicked to him, quick as the click of a latch. “Somebody has been putting you next, I see that,” he said. “No: properly speaking, I should say not. He had many acquaintances among the big men—people he saw most every day; they would even go yachting or hunting together. But I don’t believe there ever was a man that Manderson opened a corner of his heart to.”

He drew on the cigar; the ash held long, disciplined. “But what I was going to say was this. Some months ago the old man began to get like I never knew him before—gloomy and sullen, just as if he was everlastingly brooding over something bad, something that he couldn’t fix. This went on without any break; it was the same down town as it was up home—he acted just as if there was something lying heavy on his mind.”

Bunner leaned forward. The garage, in its coolness, seemed to listen; even the distant birds outside sounded suddenly louder, as if the world were pressing its ear to the crack under the door.

“But it wasn’t until a few weeks back that his self-restraint began to go; and let me tell you this, Mr. Trent”—he reached out and laid his bony hand on Trent’s knee with a proprietary insistence, as if pinning a paper to a desk—“I’m the only man that knows it. With every one else he would be just morose and dull; but when he was alone with me in his office, or anywhere we would be working together, if the least little thing went wrong, by George! he would fly off the handle to beat the Dutch.

“In this library here I have seen him open a letter with something that didn’t just suit him in it, and he would rip around and carry on like an Indian—saying he wished he had the man that wrote it here, he wouldn’t do a thing to him, and so on—till it was just pitiful. I never saw such a change.

“And here’s another thing. For a week before he died Manderson neglected his work, for the first time in my experience. He wouldn’t answer a letter or a cable, though things looked like going all to pieces over there. I supposed that this anxiety of his, whatever it was, had got on to his nerves till they were worn out. Once I advised him to see a doctor, and he told me to go to hell.”

Bunner gave a small, grim shrug, as if that phrase still stung.

“But nobody saw this side of him but me. If he was having one of these rages in the library here, for example, and Mrs. Manderson would come into the room, he would be all calm and cold again in an instant.”

“And you put this down to some secret anxiety,” Trent asked, “a fear that somebody had designs on his life?”

Bunner nodded.

Trent drew smoke slowly and let it out with care, as if measuring his own words against it. “I suppose you had considered the idea of there being something wrong with his mind—a break-down from overstrain, say. That is the first thought that your account suggests to me. Besides, it is what is always happening to your big business men in America, isn’t it? That is the impression one gets from the newspapers.”

“Don’t let them slip you any of that bunk,” Bunner said, with a sudden heat that made the cigar’s ember flare. “It’s only the ones who have got rich too quick, and can’t make good, who go crazy. Think of all our really big men—the men anywhere near Manderson’s size: did you ever hear of any one of them losing his senses? They don’t do it—believe me.”

He paused, then added reflectively, as if reaching for the right seasoning to make his point stick. “I know they say every man has his loco point, but that doesn’t mean genuine, sure-enough craziness; it just means some personal eccentricity in a man... like hating cats... or my own weakness of not being able to touch any kind of fish-food.”

Trent’s mind flashed, for an instant, to kitchens and markets: the way one man cannot bear the sea-smell clinging to a stall, another cannot stand the squeal of a pig being bled, another cannot abide the new, loud grinding of a factory mill that turns flour into something too white, too dead. People called it fussiness, and forgot it was memory, lodged in the senses.

“Well, what was Manderson’s?” he asked.

“He was full of them—the old man. There was his objection to all the unnecessary fuss and luxury that wealthy people don’t kick at much, as a general rule. He didn’t have any use for expensive trifles and ornaments. He wouldn’t have anybody do little things for him; he hated to have servants tag around after him unless he wanted them.

“And although Manderson was as careful about his clothes as any man I ever knew, and his shoes—well, sir, the amount of money he spent on shoes was sinful—in spite of that, I tell you, he never had a valet. He never liked to have anybody touch him. All his life nobody ever shaved him.”

“I’ve heard something of that,” Trent said. “Why was it, do you think?”

Bunner’s thin face tightened as he searched for the exact turn of thought. “Well,” he said slowly, “it was the Manderson habit of mind, I guess; a sort of temper of general suspicion and jealousy.

“They say his father and grandfather were just the same.... Like a dog with a bone, you know—acting as if all the rest of creation was laying for a chance to steal it. He didn’t really think the barber would start in to saw his head off; he just felt there was a possibility that he might, and he was taking no risks.

“Then again in business he was always convinced that somebody else was after his bone—which was true enough a good deal of the time, but not all the time. The consequence of that was that the old man was the most cautious and secret worker in the world of finance; and that had a lot to do with his success, too.”

Bunner lifted his shoulders slightly, as if to shake off an accusation. “But that doesn’t amount to being a lunatic, Mr. Trent—not by a long way. You ask me if Manderson was losing his mind before he died. I say I believe he was just worn out with worrying over something, and was losing his nerve.”

Trent sat very still. Outside, beyond the garage, the country made its usual small noises: leaves shifting, a bird calling, the subdued speech of the earth. They had a steadiness to them that human words never kept. He thought of how a man’s inner life might be heard not in what he said but in what he could no longer bear to hear—the sudden loudness of a servant’s footstep, the scrape of a chair, the whirr of a new machine brought into a household like a stranger.

He decided to put out a feeler. “I understood that he had trouble with his wife.”

“Sure,” Bunner said at once. “But do you suppose a thing like that was going to upset Sig Manderson that way? No, sir! He was a sight too big a man to be all broken up by any worry of that kind.”

Trent looked into his eyes and saw, behind the shrewdness, something massive and childlike: an innocence that believed the great engines of business could grind through anything as small and intimate as a marriage—believed, too, that a man might be enormous in the world and still untouched by the quiet revolutions of the home.

“What was the trouble between them, anyhow?” Trent asked.

“You can search me,” Bunner answered briefly. He puffed, then added, lowering his voice and leaning forward as though the very syllables might be overheard and misused. “Marlowe and I have often talked about it, and we could never make out a solution. I had a notion at first that the old man was disappointed and vexed because he had expected a child; but Marlowe told me that the disappointment on that score was the other way around, likely as not. His idea was all right, I guess; he gathered it from something said by Mrs. Manderson’s French maid.”

“Célestine!” Trent said quickly; and in his mind something clicked into place—so that was what her half-hints had been circling.

Bunner misread his look. “Don’t you think I’m giving a man away, Mr. Trent,” he said. “Marlowe isn’t that kind. Célestine just took a fancy to him because he talks French like a native, and she would always be holding him up for a gossip. French servants are quite unlike English that way. And servant or no servant,” he added with emphasis, “I don’t see how a woman could mention such a subject to a man. But the French beat me.”

He shook his head slowly, as if at a riddle written in a dialect he could hear but not truly understand. Trent thought of languages that carried whole worlds inside a single intimate phrase—worlds that an outsider could translate word by word and still miss the taste of. In the wrong mouth, even truth became a kind of theft.

“But to come back to what you were telling me just now,” Trent said, steering them gently. “You believe that Manderson was going in terror of his life for some time. Who should threaten it? I am quite in the dark.”

“Terror—I don’t know,” Bunner replied, frowning. “Anxiety, if you like. Or suspense—that’s rather my idea of it. The old man was hard to terrify, anyway; and more than that, he wasn’t taking any precautions—he was actually avoiding them. It looked more like he was asking for a quick finish—supposing there’s any truth in my idea.

“Why, he would sit in that library window, nights, looking out into the dark, with his white shirt just a target for anybody’s gun.”

The picture came up in Trent’s mind with painful clarity: the quiet room, the ordinary household night-sounds—some distant crockery set down, the sigh of a settling house—threaded through by the possibility of a shot. A domestic routine with a mythic, black-winged shape perched on it.

“As for who should threaten his life—well, sir,” Bunner said, with a faint, grim smile, “it’s certain you have not lived in the States. To take the Pennsylvania coal hold-up alone, there were thirty thousand men, with women and children to keep, who would have jumped at the chance of drilling a hole through the man who fixed it so that they must starve or give in to his terms. Thirty thousand of the toughest aliens in the country, Mr. Trent.

“There’s a type of desperado you find in that kind of push who has been known to lay for a man for years, and kill him when he had forgotten what he did. They have been known to dynamite a man in Idaho who had done them dirt in New Jersey ten years before. Do you suppose the Atlantic is going to stop them?”

He spat the words out softly, not as melodrama but as a ledger-entry of human spite. “It takes some sand, I tell you, to be a big business man in our country. No, sir: the old man knew—had always known—that there was a whole crowd of dangerous men scattered up and down the States who had it in for him. My belief is that he had somehow got to know that some of them were definitely after him at last.

“What licks me altogether is why he should have just laid himself open to them the way he did—why he never tried to dodge, but walked right down into the garden yesterday morning to be shot at.”

He stopped. For a little while they sat without speaking, their brows drawn, thin blue vapours rising and dissolving into the cool air of the garage. Outside, the countryside kept up its patient music. It struck Trent that even the calmest landscape had its own history of violence, but it carried it differently—from generation to generation, in habits, in silences, in what people no longer dared to say plainly.

At last Trent rose. “Your theory is quite fresh to me,” he said. “It’s perfectly rational, and it’s only a question of whether it fits all the facts. I mustn’t give away what I’m doing for my newspaper, Mr. Bunner, but I will say this: I have already satisfied myself that this was a premeditated crime, and an extraordinarily cunning one at that. I’m deeply obliged to you. We must talk it over again.”

He glanced at his watch. “I have been expected for some time by my friend. Shall we make a move?”

“Two o’clock,” Bunner said, consulting his own as he got up from the footboard. “Ten a.m. in little old New York.” He set his cigar in the corner of his mouth again, and the eager look disappeared behind the practiced coolness. “You don’t know Wall Street, Mr. Trent. Let’s you and I hope we never see anything nearer hell than what’s loose in the Street this minute.”

The sea, all morning, kept its own rough litany under the cliff—hush, lift, boom—each breaker arriving with the punctuality of an old refrain, the gulls knitting their thin cries through it. A clean wind came off the water and went roaming over the land, worrying every hedge and tuft of grass into speech. The sun laid a bright hand on everything; even the grey rocks below wore a kind of polish where the spray had been at them for years.

Trent, who had slept as if some small hard object lay always under his heart, went down before eight to a pool among the boulders, as the hotel man had directed. The descent was steep, the chalk dusty underfoot; the air smelled of salt and crushed thrift. He stripped, and the wind took his skin the way a knife takes the rind from fruit. Then he dived.

The water was so clear it seemed to have a taste—cold, metallic, and pure. He went deep, letting the weight of it press thought out of him, then struck out between the rock walls into the open. The coast-wise current pulled sideways like a hand at his ankle; he forced himself against it until his lungs burned, then turned back, battered and refreshed, to the sheltered basin. When he climbed out, the world’s noises had shifted: the sea’s roar was the same, but now he could hear his own blood and the rasp of pebbles under his feet, and it was as if the case, for a few minutes, had been washed down to its bare bones.

Ten minutes later he was scaling the cliff again, salt drying on him in a fine crust. As he climbed he felt, with a kind of shame, the disgust return. It was the day of the inquest—the day after his arrival—and the affair in hand had taken him like an illness: repellent, incessant, and absorbing.

He had carried matters not much further after leaving the American on the road to Bishopsbridge. Yesterday afternoon he had walked into the town with Mr. Cupples beside him, and there had made certain purchases at a chemist’s—things that smelled sharply of spirits and disinfectant—conferred privately for some time with a photographer whose hands were stained with dark chemicals, sent off a reply-paid telegram, and made an enquiry at the telephone exchange, where the air held that curious mixture of varnished wood and warm metal. He had said little of the case to Mr. Cupples, who for his part seemed incurious, and nothing at all of the results of his investigation or the steps he meant to take.

After their return he had written a long dispatch for the _Record_ and sent it to be telegraphed by the proud hands of the paper’s local representative; afterwards he had dined with Mr. Cupples and spent the rest of the evening alone on the veranda, listening to the distant sea and the nearer, lesser noises that domestic life makes when it is trying not to disturb itself: a door softly latched, cutlery laid down, a footfall muffled on boards.

This morning, as the path took him along the cliff top, he told himself that he had never taken up a case he liked so little, nor one that drew him so irresistibly. In the honest gold of the new day it looked uglier, not better. Whatever he suspected—and whatever he almost knew—had occupied his brain all night to the exclusion of sleep; and now, though he had scrubbed his body clean in fierce water, he only saw the darkness he believed in with more exactness, and was bitterly repelled by the motive at which he guessed.

Yet his zeal was awake again, that hunter’s quickening in the blood. He would neither slacken nor spare. In a business like this there need be no compunction. By the end of the day, he thought, his net might be complete. There was work to do before the inquest, and, with a vivid expectancy that he distrusted even as he felt it, he waited for the answer to the telegram he had flung out yesterday like a stone into fog.

The path wound along the cliff edge, and nearing a place he had marked from below—where the face had fallen away long ago—he approached the brink, meaning to look down and watch the most delicately beautiful of all movements: light water washing broken rock as a cook’s hand washes flour off a board, again and again, unwearied.

But there was no rock immediately below. A few feet down a broad ledge thrust out, a rough platform as large as a room, thick with wiry grass and walled steeply on three sides. There, close to the verge where the cliff dropped sheer, a woman sat with her arms about her drawn-up knees. Her eyes were fixed on the trailing smoke of a distant liner, as if the faint grey thread were writing a sentence she could read and no one else.

Trent halted for no more than a heartbeat—training had taught him to live in his eyes without staring—yet the picture printed itself on him with a permanence he could feel.

Everything about her was black: suede shoes, dress, the hat pinned on the grass beside her. The black was lustreless and fine, cut and fitted with a practised hand, as if she had been dressing for years not to attract attention but to command her own shape. The wind played with her thick dark hair, worrying loose tiny curls from the mass at her nape and blowing back the broad bandeaux that should have smoothed her forehead. Her face had a southern pallor, but the wind had kissed colour into her cheek. In profile the features were delicate and regular, nothing hard—yet the brows, black and bending down almost to meet, gave her repose a look of severity that the open curves of her mouth redeemed.

She looked at once modern and immemorial: like a figure painted yesterday, yet carrying the glow of those vigorous beings who have always faced sun and wind and sea in the prime of the year. There was a sureness in her womanhood, unmixed and unashamed, that did not feel quite English; still less American.

He passed on, unheard on the turf above. As he went, the woman moved. She unclasped her long hands, stretched herself with a feline grace, and then slowly raised her head and extended her arms, fingers open and curving, as if to gather into her body all the morning’s overwhelming sanity. It was a gesture of freedom—a resolution to be, to possess, to go forward, perhaps to enjoy.

So he saw her for an instant, and did not turn.

He knew suddenly who she must be; and it was as if a curtain of gloom were drawn between him and the splendour of the day. The sea’s great voice went on below, but it sounded farther away.

At breakfast Mr. Cupples found him little inclined to talk. Trent pleaded a restless night. Mr. Cupples, on the other hand, had the alertness of a small bird at first light. The prospect of the inquest enlivened him. He entertained Trent with a disquisition upon the history of the coroner’s court, that ancient tribunal with its freedom from the shackles of rule and precedent, and passed from that to the case to come before it.

“Young Bunner mentioned to me last night,” he said, buttering his toast with a smooth and deliberate hand, “the hypothesis he puts forward regarding the crime. A very remarkable young man, Trent. His meaning is occasionally obscure, but in my opinion he is gifted with a clearheaded knowledge of the world quite unusual in one of his apparent age. His promotion by Manderson to the position of principal lieutenant speaks for itself. He has assumed with perfect confidence the control at this end of the wire, as he expresses it, of the complicated business situation caused by the death of his principal. He has advised me very wisely as to the steps I should take on Mabel’s behalf, and the best course for her to pursue until effect has been given to the provisions of the will.”

Trent listened with the detached weariness of a man chewing food without tasting it.

“I was accordingly less disposed than I might otherwise have been,” Mr. Cupples continued, “to regard his suggestion of an industrial vendetta as far-fetched. When I questioned him he was able to describe a number of cases in which attacks—too often successful—had been made upon the lives of persons who had incurred the hostility of powerful labour organizations. This is a terrible time in which we live, my dear boy. There is none recorded in history, I think, in which the disproportion between the material and the moral constituents of society has been so great or so menacing to the permanence of the fabric. But nowhere, in my judgement, is the prospect so dark as it is in the United States.”

“I thought,” said Trent listlessly, “that Puritanism was about as strong there as the money-getting craze.”

“Your remark,” Mr. Cupples replied, with as near an approach to humour as was possible to him, “is not in the nature of a testimonial to what you call Puritanism—a convenient rather than an accurate term. The sense of your observation, however, is sound. It is extremely well illustrated by Manderson himself, who had, I believe, the virtues of purity, abstinence, and self-restraint in their strongest form. No, Trent; there are other and more worthy things among the moral constituents of which I spoke. In our finite nature, the more we preoccupy ourselves with the bewildering complexity of external apparatus which science places in our hands, the less vigour have we left for the development of the holier purposes of humanity within us.”

He went on placidly, as if reciting a familiar recipe: agricultural machinery abolishing Harvest Home; mechanical travel abolishing the inn, or all that was best in it. Outside, the morning made its own music—wind in leaves, a cart far off creaking like a slow fiddle—but inside the dining-room there was the softer racket of cups and knives, and a radio-like murmur of other breakfasts.

“It needs epigrammatic expression,” Trent said, rising. “If only it could be crystallized into some handy formula, like ‘No Popery’ or ‘Tax the Foreigner,’ you’d find multitudes to go to the stake for it. But you were planning to go to White Gables before the inquest, I think. You ought to be off if you’re to get back to the court in time. I have something to attend to there myself. We might walk up together. I’ll just fetch my camera.”

“By all means,” Mr. Cupples answered; and they set off at once in the ever-growing warmth.

As they walked, the soundscape altered with each turn: the surf became a duller drum as the land rose, and in its place came the close noises of hedgerows—small birds ticking and chirring, the whisper of grass heads rubbing together. Trent found himself noticing such things with the sharpness of a man who clings to the physical world because the moral one is sour.

The roof of White Gables showed at last, a surly patch of dull red against dark trees. It harmonized with Trent’s mood; he felt heavy, sinister, troubled. If a blow must fall that might strike down the creature radiant of beauty and life he had seen on the ledge, he did not wish it to come from his own hand. Chivalry had been bred into him by his mother’s first teachings, but at this moment the horror of bruising anything so lovely was as much the artist’s revulsion as the gentleman’s.

And yet—was the hunt to end in nothing? The quality of the affair made forbearance an agony. There never was such a case; and he alone, he was convinced, held its truth under his hand. At least, he determined, this day should show whether what he believed was delusion. He would trample compunction underfoot until he was quite sure there was any call for it. Before noon, he told himself, he would know.

At the gate of the drive they saw Marlowe and the American standing in talk before the front door. In the shadow of the porch stood the lady in black.

She saw them and came gravely forward over the lawn. She moved exactly as Trent had known she would move—erect and balanced, stepping lightly, as if the ground were a thing she used rather than trusted. When she welcomed him on Mr. Cupples’s presentation, her eyes—brown with golden flecks—observed him kindly. The dreamy halo he had seen about her head above the sea was gone; pale composure, worn like a mask of distress, took its place. Her voice was low, even, giving the appropriate commonplace as if the words were a set form one learned early and spoke without tasting.

After a few words to Mr. Cupples she turned her eyes on Trent again.

“I hope you will succeed,” she said earnestly. “Do you think you will succeed?”

He made up his mind as the words left her lips.

“I believe I shall do so, Mrs. Manderson,” he said. “When I have the case sufficiently complete I shall ask you to let me see you and tell you about it. It may be necessary to consult you before the facts are published.”

She looked puzzled. For an instant distress showed through the mask, quick as a shadow passing over a bowl of milk.

“If it is necessary, of course you shall do so,” she said.

Trent hesitated on the brink of his next speech. He remembered that she had not wished to repeat to him the story already given to the inspector—or to be questioned at all. He was not unconscious that he desired to hear her voice and watch her face a little longer, if it might be; but what troubled him now was not mere curiosity. There was a queer thing that fitted nowhere into the pattern whose corners he had by this time forced the other queer things into place. It was possible she could explain it away in a breath. It was unlikely anyone else could.

He summoned resolution.

“You have been so kind,” he said, “in allowing me access to the house and every opportunity of studying the case, that I am going to ask leave to put a question or two to yourself—nothing that you would rather not answer, I think. May I?”

She glanced at him wearily, as if the weight of other people’s needs had begun to bruise her.

“It would be stupid of me to refuse. Ask your questions, Mr. Trent.”

“It’s only this,” said Trent hurriedly. “We know that your husband lately drew an unusually large sum of ready money from his London bankers, and was keeping it here. It is here now, in fact. Have you any idea why he should have done that?”

Her eyes opened in astonishment.

“I cannot imagine,” she said. “I did not know he had done so. I am very much surprised to hear it.”

“Why is it surprising?”

“I thought my husband had very little money in the house. On Sunday night, just before he went out in the motor, he came into the drawing-room where I was sitting. He seemed to be irritated about something, and asked me at once if I had any notes or gold I could let him have until next day.”

She spoke evenly, but Trent heard, under the evenness, the small friction of memory—like the sound of paper rubbed between finger and thumb.

“I was surprised at that,” she went on, “because he was never without money; he made it a rule to carry a hundred pounds or so about him always in a note-case. I unlocked my escritoire and gave him all I had by me. It was nearly thirty pounds.”

“And he did not tell you why he wanted it?”

“No. He put it in his pocket, and then said that Mr. Marlowe had persuaded him to go for a run in the motor by moonlight, and he thought it might help him to sleep. He had been sleeping badly, as perhaps you know. Then he went off with Mr. Marlowe. I thought it odd he should need money on Sunday night, but I soon forgot about it. I never remembered it again until now.”

“It was curious, certainly,” said Trent, staring past her shoulder, as if the answer were written somewhere on the boundary of the fields.

Mr. Cupples began to speak to his niece of arrangements for the inquest, and Trent moved away to where Marlowe was pacing slowly upon the lawn.

The young man seemed relieved to talk about the coming business of the day. He still looked tired out and nervous, but there was a quiet humour in his account of the pomposities of the local police and the portentous airs of Dr. Stock. Trent listened, answering enough to keep him going, and then gradually turned the talk toward the problem of the crime. Marlowe’s gravity returned.

“Bunner has told me what he thinks,” he said when Trent referred to the American’s theory. “I don’t find myself convinced by it, because it doesn’t really explain some of the oddest facts. But I’ve lived long enough in the United States to know that such a stroke of revenge, done in a secret, melodramatic way, is not an unlikely thing. It is quite characteristic of certain sections of the labour movement there. Americans have a taste and a talent for that sort of business. Do you know _Huckleberry Finn_?”

“Do I know my own name?” Trent replied, and for a moment the sea-wind seemed to lift the heaviness in him. “I think the most American thing in that great American epic is Tom Sawyer’s elaboration of an extremely difficult and romantic scheme, taking days to carry out, for securing the escape of Jim—which could have been managed quite easily in twenty minutes. You know how fond they are of lodges and brotherhoods. Every college club has its secret signs and handgrips. You’ve heard of the Know-Nothing movement, and the Ku Klux Klan. Then look at Brigham Young’s penny-dreadful tyranny in Utah—with real blood. The founders of the Mormon State were of the purest Yankee stock; and you know what they did. It’s all part of the same mental tendency. They make fun of it among themselves. For my part, I take it very seriously.”

“It can have a hideous side,” Trent said, “when you get it in connection with crime—or vice—or even mere luxury. But I’ve a sort of sneaking respect for the determination to make life interesting in spite of civilization. To return to the matter in hand—has it struck you as possible that Manderson’s mind was affected, to some extent, by this menace Bunner believes in? For instance, it was rather an extraordinary thing to send you posting off in the middle of the night.”

“About ten o’clock,” Marlowe corrected. “Though if he’d actually roused me at midnight I shouldn’t have been much surprised. It all chimes in with what we’ve just been saying. Manderson had a strong streak of the national taste for dramatic proceedings. He was rather fond of his reputation for unexpected strokes and for going for his object with ruthless directness through every opposing consideration. He’d decided suddenly that he wanted word from this man Harris—”

“Who is Harris?” Trent broke in.

“Nobody knows. Even Bunner never heard of him and can’t imagine what the business in hand was. All I know is that when I went up to London last week to attend to various things I booked a deck-cabin, at Manderson’s request, for a Mr. George Harris on the boat that sailed on Monday. It seems Manderson suddenly found he wanted news from Harris which presumably was too secret for the telegraph; and there was no train that served; so I was sent off as you know.”

Trent looked round to make sure they were not overheard. The morning was full of noise, yet the house behind them seemed to swallow sound; only the distant surf and a faint clink from the porch carried. He faced Marlowe gravely.

“There is one thing I may tell you,” he said quietly, “that I don’t think you know. Martin the butler caught a few words at the end of your conversation with Manderson in the orchard before you started with him in the car. He heard him say, ‘If Harris is there, every moment is of importance.’ Now, Mr. Marlowe—you know my business here. I am sent to make enquiries, and you mustn’t take offence. I want to ask you if, in the face of that sentence, you will repeat that you know nothing of what the business was.”

Marlowe shook his head, with the tired patience of a man who has already been made to repeat himself to deaf authority.

“I know nothing, indeed. I’m not easily offended, and your question is quite fair. What passed during that conversation I have already told the detective. Manderson plainly said he could not tell me what it was all about. He simply wanted me to find Harris, tell him he desired to know how matters stood, and bring back a letter or message from him. Harris, I was further told, might not turn up. If he did—‘every moment was of importance.’ And now you know as much as I do.”

“That talk took place before he told his wife that you were taking him for a moonlight run,” Trent said. “Why did he conceal your errand in that way, I wonder.”

Marlowe made a helpless gesture.

“Why? I can guess no better than you.”

“Why,” Trent muttered, gazing at the ground as if he expected to see a footprint answer him, “did he conceal it—from Mrs. Manderson?”

He looked up.

“And from Martin,” Marlowe amended coolly. “He was told the same thing.”

With a sudden movement of his head Trent seemed to dismiss the subject. He drew a letter-case from his breast pocket and took out two small leaves of clean, fresh paper.

“Just look at these two slips, Mr. Marlowe,” he said. “Did you ever see them before? Have you any idea where they come from?”

Marlowe took one in each hand and examined them, turning them over as if expecting a hidden watermark to speak.

“They seem to have been cut with a knife or scissors from a small diary for this year—from the October pages,” he said at last. “I see no writing of any kind on them. Nobody here has any such diary so far as I know. What about them?”

“There may be nothing in it,” Trent said dubiously. “Any one in the house might have such a diary without your having seen it. But I didn’t much expect you’d be able to identify the leaves—in fact, I should have been surprised if you had.”

He stopped as Mrs. Manderson came towards them again.

“My uncle thinks we should be going now,” she said. Her voice had the same evenness, but her eyes were a shade darker, as if tiredness had steeped in them overnight.

“I think I will walk on with Mr. Bunner,” Mr. Cupples said, joining them. “There are certain business matters that must be disposed of as soon as possible. Will you come on with these two gentlemen, Mabel? We will wait for you before we reach the place.”

Trent turned to her.

“Mrs. Manderson will excuse me, I hope,” he said. “I really came up this morning in order to look about me here for some indications I thought I might possibly find. I had not thought of attending the—the court just yet.”

She looked at him with perfect candour, as if candour were a dialect she spoke more fluently than any other, and could not, even if she wished, translate into something less direct.

“Of course, Mr. Trent. Please do exactly as you wish. We are all relying upon you. If you will wait a few moments, Mr. Marlowe, I shall be ready.”

She went into the house. Her uncle and the American had already strolled towards the gate, their footsteps making the dry gravel talk.

Trent looked into Marlowe’s eyes.

“That is a wonderful woman,” he said in a lowered voice.

“You say so without knowing her,” Marlowe replied, also low.

“She is more than that,” Trent said.

He said nothing further, only stared out over the fields towards the sea, where the air, even at this distance, carried a faint salt edge.

In the silence a new sound rose: hobnailed haste on the road, quick and determined, like a small drum beaten close by. Down the lane a boy appeared, trotting towards them from the direction of the hotel. In his hand was an orange envelope—unmistakable at a distance, a bright commercial note in the morning’s natural music.

Trent watched him with an indifferent eye as the boy met and passed the others and came on.

Then, as if merely to keep his voice from following his thoughts into dangerous places, Trent turned to Marlowe.

“Apropos of nothing in particular,” he said, “were you at Oxford?”

“Yes,” Marlowe answered. “Why do you ask?”

“I just wondered if I was right in my guess. It’s one of the things you can often tell about a man, isn’t it?”

“I suppose so. Each of us is marked in one way or another, perhaps.”

“I should have said you were an artist,” Marlowe added, “if I hadn’t known it.”

“Why? Does my hair want cutting?”

“Oh, no. It’s only that you look at things and people as I’ve seen artists do—with an eye that moves steadily from detail to detail—rather looking them over than looking at them.”

The boy came up panting.

“Telegram for you, sir,” he said to Trent. “Just come, sir.”

Trent tore open the envelope with a brief apology. As he read the slip his eyes lighted up so visibly that Marlowe’s tired face softened into a smile.

“It must be good news,” Marlowe murmured, half to himself.

Trent looked up. The glance he gave held everything close, as if it were food he would not yet put on the table.

“Not exactly news,” he said. “It only tells me that another little guess of mine was a good one.”

The coroner had the air of a man who had been handed, for a single bright day, the sovereign’s scepter and told to use it well. In the provinces he was ordinarily a solicitor with a taste for procedure; here, with Manderson’s death dropped like a stone into his parish, he became a performer before a nation’s attentive ear. He was large, jovial, and theatrically earnest. He loved the sound of his own sentences as some men love the sound of a carving-knife through crackling skin—clean, authoritative, able to make a room submit. Facts he could marshal with decent competence; language he could pile up until it looked like proof.

The court sat in a long new room tacked onto the hotel, built for dancing and light music, and still smelling of fresh boards and plaster. The place had no curtains yet, no softening hangings to drink up noise. Every cough rang. Every chair-leg scraped like a small insult. The reporters had dug themselves into the front seats, a little regiment with pads open and pencils already murmuring. Those summoned to speak waited in a row off to one side, stiff-backed, hands folded. The jury—hair slicked and faces arranged into expressions they thought appropriate to public duty—sat double-filed at the coroner’s other flank. Behind them, an undistinguished public filled the bare space and held its breath, impressed by the very acoustics of solemnity.

The newspaper men, practiced at listening for the sentence that would print well, muttered together. Some who knew Trent by sight told the others he was not in the room. Their whispers, their paper rustle, their pencil ticks made a thin, incessant under-sound, like gnats over a bowl of uncovered jam.

The first witness called to identify the dead man was his wife.

Mrs. Manderson came forward in heavy black, her veil thick as soot. When she lifted it to speak, the pallor of her face—pale as skimmed milk left too long in a cold pantry—startled the room into a more careful quiet. Composure lay on her features, unbroken; not hardness, not any theatrical mystery, but the steady pressure of a strong nature forcing itself to behave. Interesting femininity—something both vulnerable and controlled—was the first thing any observer felt, and the second was the sense of strain: a muscle held tight for too long.

The coroner, whose voice could be honeyed when he chose, led her gently through the preliminaries—health, habits, circumstances—and then brought her, by degrees, to the last time she had seen her husband alive.

Mr. Manderson, she said, had come up to his bedroom at his usual hour on Sunday night. His room was really a dressing-room attached to her own bedroom, with a door between that was commonly left open at night. Both rooms had other doors opening to the passage.

“He always preferred the greatest simplicity in his sleeping arrangements,” she explained. He liked the small room.

She herself had not been properly awake when he came in. She had been half roused, as she often was, by the click of the switch and the sudden light in his room bleeding through the open doorway. She had spoken to him, though she could not clearly recall her own words; drowsiness blurred them. She remembered only that he had been out—she had thought—in the car for a moonlight run, and she believed she asked whether he had had a good run, and what the time was. She asked the time because it seemed to her she had only just fallen asleep, and she expected him to be out late.

“He told me it was half-past eleven,” she said, her voice low and clean, as if each word were being rinsed before she allowed it out. “And he said he had changed his mind about going for a run.”

“Did he say why?” the coroner asked, leaning forward with sympathetic gravity.

“Yes,” she answered. “He did explain why. I remember very well what he said, because—” She checked, a faint confusion crossing her like a shadow passing over flour.

“Because?” the coroner prompted, softly insistent.

“Because my husband was not, as a rule, communicative about his business affairs,” she said, chin lifting with the smallest defiant steadiness. “He did not—did not think they would interest me, and as a rule referred to them as little as possible. That was why I was rather surprised when he told me that he had sent Mr. Marlowe to Southampton to bring back some important information from a man who was leaving for Paris by the next day’s boat. He said that Mr. Marlowe could do it quite easily if he had no accident. He said that he had started in the car, and then walked back home a mile or so, and felt all the better for it.”

“Did he say any more?”

“Nothing, as well as I remember,” she replied. “I was very sleepy, and I dropped off again in a few moments. I just remember my husband turning his light out, and that is all. I never saw him again alive.”

“And you heard nothing in the night?”

“No. I never woke until my maid brought my tea in the morning at seven o’clock. She closed the door leading to my husband’s room, as she always did, and I supposed him to be still there. He always needed a great deal of sleep. He sometimes slept until quite late in the morning. I had breakfast in my sitting-room. It was about ten when I heard that my husband’s body had been found.”

Her head dipped. Under the veil, the movement was like a flower stem bending. She waited, silent, for the coroner to let her go.

But he did not.

“Mrs. Manderson.” The sympathy remained in his tone, but there was a firmer thread now, as if he had turned his voice from velvet to cord. “The question I am going to put to you must, in these sad circumstances, be a painful one; but it is my duty to ask it. Is it the fact that your relations with your late husband had not been, for some time past, relations of mutual affection and confidence? Is it the fact that there was an estrangement between you?”

Colour rose at last into her cheeks, a bloom of life breaking through the pallor. She drew herself up, facing him full, and her answer came with cold distinctness, like a knife set down precisely on a board.

“If that question is necessary,” she said, “I will answer it so that there shall be no misunderstanding. During the last few months of my husband’s life his attitude towards me had given me great anxiety and sorrow. He had changed towards me; he had become very reserved, and seemed mistrustful. I saw much less of him than before; he seemed to prefer to be alone. I can give no explanation at all of the change. I tried to work against it; I did all I could with justice to my own dignity, as I thought. Something was between us, I did not know what, and he never told me. My own obstinate pride prevented me from asking what it was in so many words; I only made a point of being to him exactly as I had always been, so far as he would allow me. I suppose I shall never know now what it was.”

For all her control, her voice trembled on the last line as if grief, shut up like steam, had found a pinhole to escape. She drew her veil down again and stood rigid, quiet.

A juryman, embarrassed by his own bluntness, asked a question with hesitation.

“Then was there never anything of the nature of what they call words between you and your husband, ma’am?”

“Never.”

The word was spoken without colour, yet it struck the room with a sharpness that made the questioner seem, for an instant, coarse and childish. It was not merely denial; it was correction—an assertion that there were lives in which “words” did not take that form.

Did she know, the coroner asked next, of any other matter that might have been preying upon her husband’s mind?

Of none whatever, she said.

The coroner, with an air of mercy, indicated that her ordeal was ended. She moved toward the door, the black veil flowing, and the attention of the room followed her as if a draught had passed through. Only when she was nearly gone did the collective gaze swing hungrily to the next name.

“Martin,” the coroner called.

At that exact moment Trent appeared at the doorway and edged himself into the packed room. He did not look toward Martin. His eye was on the well-balanced figure that came quickly toward him along a narrow lane the crowd made without thinking, as water makes way for a boat. His face, as he watched, was dark.

He started when he heard her speak his name.

“Mr. Trent.”

It was low, meant for him alone, but it carried; the hall’s hard surfaces offered it back, faintly, as if the building itself repeated private speech in an alien dialect.

He stepped aside with a slight bow, and she went past him into the corridor. He followed.

“I wanted to ask you,” she said, and now the voice was weak and oddly broken, as if the strength she had spent inside the room had been coin and she had none left in her purse, “if you would give me your arm a part of the way to the house. I could not see my uncle near the door, and I suddenly felt rather faint… I shall be better in the air… No, no; I cannot stay here—please, Mr. Trent!”

He had begun, instinctively, to suggest something—someone to fetch, somewhere to sit—but she cut him off with a sudden urgency. “I must go to the house.”

Her hand tightened on his arm for an instant, and there was a curious force in that tightening, as if for all her faintness she could have dragged him away. Then the grip relaxed into a heavy leaning. With her head bent, and Trent’s arm under her hand, she walked slowly from the hotel down the oak-shaded path toward White Gables.

Outside, the sound changed. The thick leaves above made a softer ceiling than the hotel’s bare plaster; they took the edge off the world. The air carried the small noises of the estate—birds, the far hush of water, a cartwheel somewhere with its patient creak. It was a relief after the court’s sharp reverberations, and yet Trent found no peace in it.

His thoughts whirled, out of step like dancers to the wrong tune, stamping only one refrain into his skull: Fool—fool—fool. Everything he alone knew, everything he guessed and suspected, ran riot through him in a disorderly rout; and through all of it, never for an instant, did he lose awareness of the cool pressure of her fingers on his sleeve. That touch, light as it was, filled him with an exaltation that enraged and bewildered him. He wanted to shake himself free of it, and could not.

At the house, he helped her into the morning-room. She sank onto a couch as if her body had been held upright by will and the will had now unclasped its hands. When she raised her veil, she thanked him gravely and frankly, and there was sincere gratitude in her eyes—clear, direct, unadorned. She was much better, she said; a cup of tea would work a miracle. The phrase was domestic, ordinary, like a recipe passed down because it works—tea, warmth, a little sugar if one can stomach it—and it landed oddly amid the day’s public ugliness.

She hoped she had not taken him away from anything important. She was ashamed of herself; she had thought she could go through with it, but she had not expected those last questions.

“I am glad you did not hear me,” she said when he explained he had not been present for her evidence. “But of course you will read it all in the reports. It shook me so to have to speak of that,” she added simply; “and to keep from making an exhibition of myself took it out of me. And all those staring men by the door! Thank you again for helping me when I asked you… I thought I might,” she ended strangely, with a little tired smile.

He left her then, taking himself away as if flight were the only decency left to him, his hand still quivering from the cool touch of her fingers.

Back in the court, the machinery of testimony went on. Servants spoke—doors, times, trays, footsteps. The man who had found the body spoke—his discovery, his alarm. Nothing new fell into the reporters’ net from these mouths. The police evidence was as it so often is at an inquest: colourless on the surface, cryptic underneath, shaped to reveal as little as duty allowed.

Then Mr. Bunner gave his account.

To the satisfaction of Mr. Bunner, it made the day’s sensation. His words—substantial, American, confident—sent pencils flying, and the older, more ambiguous revelation of trouble in the Manderson marriage was pushed back in the public mind as yesterday’s bread becomes mere crumbs once a hot loaf arrives.

He told the court, in substance, what he had already told Trent: the threatened man, the shadowed days, the change in Manderson, the sense of danger that in his country might walk side by side with wealth and industry. The story printed next day with scarcely a sentence omitted in every journal of importance in Great Britain and the United States. The world, greedy for a clean narrative, seized it as if it were a strong, salty broth—simple to swallow, warming to the imagination.

In his final address the coroner thought it right, as he said, to mention the faint possibility of suicide raised by the wife’s evidence. Public opinion took no notice of it. The weight of evidence, as the official himself pointed out, lay against that theory; and he emphasized the point that no weapon had been found near the body.

“This question, of course, is all-important, gentlemen,” he said to the jury, and his voice took on that carefully rounded cadence with which he liked to lay out conclusions. “It is, in fact, the main issue before you. You have seen the body for yourselves. You have just heard the medical evidence; but I think it would be well for me to read you my notes of it in so far as they bear on this point, in order to refresh your memories.

“Dr. Stock told you—I am going to omit all technical medical language and repeat to you merely the plain English of his testimony—that in his opinion death had taken place six or eight hours previous to the finding of the body. He said that the cause of death was a bullet wound, the bullet having entered the left eye, which was destroyed, and made its way to the base of the brain, which was quite shattered. The external appearance of the wound, he said, did not support the hypothesis of its being self-inflicted, inasmuch as there were no signs of the firearm having been pressed against the eye, or even put very close to it; at the same time it was not physically impossible that the weapon should have been discharged by the deceased with his own hand, at some small distance from the eye.

“Dr. Stock also told us that it was impossible to say with certainty, from the state of the body, whether any struggle had taken place at the time of death; that when seen by him, at which time he understood that it had not been moved since it was found, the body was lying in a collapsed position such as might very well result from the shot alone; but that the scratches and bruises upon the wrists and the lower part of the arms had been very recently inflicted, and were, in his opinion, marks of violence.

“In connection with this same point, the remarkable evidence given by Mr. Bunner cannot be regarded, I think, as without significance. It may have come as a surprise to some of you to hear that risks of the character described by this witness are, in his own country, commonly run by persons in the position of the deceased. On the other hand, it may have been within the knowledge of some of you that in the industrial world of America the discontent of labour often proceeds to lengths of which we in England happily know nothing. I have interrogated the witness somewhat fully upon this. At the same time, gentlemen, I am by no means suggesting that Mr. Bunner’s personal conjecture as to the cause of death can fitly be adopted by you. That is emphatically not the case.

“What his evidence does is to raise two questions for your consideration. First, can it be said that the deceased was to any extent in the position of a threatened man—of a man more exposed to the danger of murderous attack than an ordinary person? Second, does the recent alteration in his demeanour, as described by this witness, justify the belief that his last days were overshadowed by a great anxiety? These points may legitimately be considered by you in arriving at a conclusion upon the rest of the evidence.”

So, having all but guided their hands to the nail he believed most worth striking, the coroner desired the jury to consider their verdict. In the long bare room, the hush returned—broken only by the busy scratching of pencils, the soft shifting of bodies on chairs, and the faint, persistent echo of one woman’s controlled voice still hanging, like an aftertaste, in the public air.

“Come in!”

The words came sharp and muffled together, as if they had been pushed through clenched teeth. Mr. Cupples found Trent in the hotel sitting-room, the kind of room that had no private character of its own—only the thin, varnished impersonality of a thousand other rooms, all built to the same plan and all humming with the same city-bred undertone: the lift’s tired groan in the stairwell, a distant door banging like a badly-tuned drum, and from the street below the steady iron hiss of traffic that never quite stopped breathing.

It was early evening on the day the coroner’s jury, without rising from their box, had delivered the expected verdict against person or persons unknown.

Trent stood at the table by the window, pale enough to look salted, moving a shallow enamelled dish in the last workable light. His hands were quick and exact, but the quickness had a tremor in it, the way a cook’s hand trembles when the sauce is at the moment it can turn.

“Sit on the sofa,” he said without looking up. “The chairs are a job lot bought at the sale after the suppression of the Holy Inquisition in Spain.”

He lifted what lay in the dish—an oblong negative, wet and glossy—turning it slowly so the light slid over it in sheets. The air in the room carried a bitter, clean tang: chemicals, wet paper, the faint medicinal sting that clung to the back of the throat. Not the scent of food, but still a kind of kitchen—one of acids and salts, where invisible things were coaxed into showing themselves.

“This is a pretty good negative,” he said, tilting his head with the calm severity of a man judging doneness. “Washed enough now, I think. Let us leave it to dry, and get rid of all this mess.”

As Trent cleared the table with nervous industry—basins, racks, boxes, bottles—Cupples picked up the nearest object, a bottle with a cork, and uncorked it. He put it cautiously to his nose.

“That is called hypo-eliminator,” Trent said, glancing at him. “Very useful when you’re in a hurry with a negative. I shouldn’t drink it, though, all the same. It eliminates sodium hypophosphite, but I shouldn’t wonder if it would eliminate human beings too.”

He stacked the last of the clutter onto a mantel already crowded with the room’s dreary ornaments. Then he perched on the edge of the table facing Cupples, hands in his pockets, as if the table were a witness-stand and he had to keep himself from touching evidence.

“The great thing about a hotel sitting-room,” he went on, “is that its beauty does not distract the mind from work. It is no place for the mayfly pleasures of a mind at ease. Have you ever been in this room before, Cupples? I have, hundreds of times. It has pursued me all over England for years. I should feel lost without it if, in some fantastic, far-off hotel, they were to give me some other sitting-room. Look at this table-cover; there is the ink I spilt on it when I had this room in Halifax. I burnt that hole in the carpet when I had it in Ipswich. But I see they have mended the glass over the picture of ‘Silent Sympathy’, which I threw a boot at in Banbury. I do all my best work here. This afternoon, for instance, since the inquest, I have finished several excellent negatives. There is a very good dark room downstairs.”

“The inquest—that reminds me,” Cupples said, drawn in despite himself by Trent’s restless brightness. “I came in to thank you, my dear fellow, for looking after Mabel this morning. I had no idea she was going to feel ill after leaving the box; she seemed quite unmoved, and, really, she is a woman of such extraordinary self-command, I thought I could leave her to her own devices and hear out the evidence, which I thought it important I should do. It was a very fortunate thing she found a friend to assist her, and she is most grateful. She is quite herself again now.”

Trent’s face tightened, a small frown cutting down between his brows. For a moment he did not answer. The noise from outside rose and fell like a bad accompaniment: wheels on setts, a horn, the thin call of a newsboy—a modern chorus that never knew when to be silent.

“I tell you what,” he said at last. “I was just getting to the really interesting part of the job when you came in. Come; would you like to see a little bit of high-class police work? It’s the very same kind of work that old Murch ought to be doing at this moment. Perhaps he is; but I hope to glory he isn’t.”

He slid off the table and went into the bedroom. Cupples heard a drawer pulled out, something wooden scraped along something else, then the soft thump of a board being set upright. Trent returned carrying a large drawing-board, and on it a tidy little arrangement of objects, the way ingredients are laid out before a recipe begins: each thing chosen, each thing with its part to play.

He set it down on the table, and with a brisk, almost domestic care began to place the items in a line before Cupples.

“First I must introduce you to these little things,” he said. “Here is a big ivory paper-knife; here are two leaves cut out of a diary—my own diary; here is a bottle containing dentifrice; here is a little case of polished walnut. Some of these things have to be put back where they belong in somebody’s bedroom at White Gables before night. That’s the sort of man I am—nothing stops me. I borrowed them this very morning when every one was down at the inquest, and I dare say some people would think it rather an odd proceeding if they knew. Now there remains one object on the board. Can you tell me, without touching it, what it is?”

Cupples leaned forward, eyes narrowing with honest effort. “Certainly I can. It is an ordinary glass bowl. It looks like a finger-bowl. I see nothing odd about it,” he added after several seconds, as if his eyes might coax strangeness into appearing by sheer patience.

“I can’t see much myself,” Trent replied, “and that is exactly where the fun comes in.”

He took up a small squat bottle, thick-necked and practical.

“Now take this little fat bottle, Cupples, and pull out the cork. Do you recognize that powder inside it? You have swallowed pounds of it in your time, I expect. They give it to babies. Grey powder is its ordinary name—mercury and chalk. It is great stuff.”

He slid a clean sheet of paper on to the table. The paper made a dry, satisfying sound, a brief whisper that felt like a pause in the room’s perpetual noise.

“Now, while I hold the basin sideways over this sheet of paper, I want you to pour a little powder out of the bottle over this part of the bowl—just here….”

Cupples obeyed. The grey powder came out in a soft spill, like flour shaken from a spoon, and dusted the glass with a thin, dull skin. It smelled faintly of nothing at all—chalk’s blankness with a metallic undernote that suggested sickness.

“Perfect!” Trent said, and for an instant his voice warmed as if he had tasted something correctly seasoned. “Sir Edward Henry himself could not have handled the powder better. You have done this before, Cupples, I can see. You are an old hand.”

“I really am not,” Cupples protested. “I assure you it is all a complete mystery to me. What did I do then?”

Trent brushed the powdered patch with a camel-hair brush—light, light, like glazing a tart so as not to break the fruit.

“Now look at it again. You saw nothing odd about it before. Do you see anything now?”

Cupples stared, and a small chill ran through him. “How curious! Yes—there are two large grey finger-marks on the bowl. They were not there before.”

“I am Hawkshaw the detective,” Trent observed. He did not smile. “Would it interest you to hear a short lecture on the subject of glass finger-bowls? When you take one up with your hand you leave traces upon it, usually practically invisible, which may remain for days or months. You leave the marks of your fingers. The human hand, even when quite clean, is never quite dry, and sometimes—in moments of great anxiety, for instance, Cupples—it is very moist. It leaves a mark on any cold smooth surface it may touch. That bowl was moved by somebody with a rather moist hand quite lately.”

He dusted again on the other side. The powder clung in obedient shadows, revealing what it had been waiting to reveal.

“Here on the other side, you see, is the thumb-mark—very good impressions all of them.”

He spoke low, but his excitement made the words feel pressed and hot, like steam trapped under a lid. His pale face had a brightness in it that frightened Cupples more than anger would have done.

“This one should be the index finger. I need not tell a man of your knowledge of the world that the pattern of it is a single-spiral whorl, with deltas symmetrically disposed. This, the print of the second finger, is a simple loop, with a staple core and fifteen counts. I know there are fifteen, because I have just the same two prints on this negative, which I have examined in detail. Look!”

He held up the negative to the slanting light. The grey-brown image, faint as a memory half-lost in translation, showed the ridged map of skin with startling exactness. Trent’s pencil point tapped details as if he were reading out a recipe that could not be altered.

“You can see they’re the same. You see the bifurcation of that ridge. There it is in the other. You see that little scar near the centre. There it is in the other. There are a score of ridge-characteristics on which an expert would swear in the witness-box that the marks on that bowl and the marks I have photographed on this negative were made by the same hand.”

“And where did you photograph them? What does it all mean?” Cupples asked, his curiosity now edged with the uneasiness of a man hearing a familiar tune played in an unfamiliar key.

“I found them on the inside of the left-hand leaf of the front window in Mrs. Manderson’s bedroom. As I could not bring the window with me, I photographed them, sticking a bit of black paper on the other side of the glass for the purpose. The bowl comes from Manderson’s room. It is the bowl in which his false teeth were placed at night. I could bring that away, so I did.”

Cupples’s gaze flicked to the bowl again. The domestic plainness of it—something that might have held lemon-water after dinner—made the thought of it intimate and revolting at once.

“But those cannot be Mabel’s finger-marks.”

“I should think not!” Trent said, with sudden, clipped decision. “They are twice the size of any print Mrs. Manderson could make.”

“Then they must be her husband’s.”

Trent did not answer directly. His eyes had narrowed, not on Cupples now, but on the facts, as though the facts were a dialect he feared he had begun to understand too well.

“Perhaps they are. Now shall we see if we can match them once more? I believe we can.”

He opened another squat bottle. When the cork came free, the room seemed to darken a little, as if even the light disliked what was coming. Inside was dense black powder.

“Lamp-black,” Trent said. “Hold a bit of paper in your hand for a second or two, and this little chap will show you the pattern of your fingers.”

He took up, with tweezers, one of the diary leaves he had cut out—handled it like something that could spoil with the warmth of a finger. Cupples saw the torn edges, the ruled lines, the private familiarity of handwriting on the other side. A man’s days in ink—words meant for himself alone—reduced to a tool.

Trent held it out. “Examine it.”

Cupples looked. Nothing showed.

Trent tipped lamp-black onto one surface, then the other, as if seasoning both sides of meat. He shook off the excess with a soft, economical flick.

He handed it back without speaking.

The marks stood out at once—two prints in hard black clarity, unmistakable, bold as a stamp. On one side, the same two fingers Cupples had seen appear grey on the bowl. Trent turned the paper over; on the other side was the thumb-mark, equally clear: a dark echo of the pale impression on glass.

Cupples compared the paper to the bowl with a kind of awe. It was as though a ghost had been made to sign its name.

“Same man, you see,” Trent said, and gave a short laugh that had no mirth in it. “I felt that it must be so, and now I know.”

He went to the window and looked out. Below, the town made its incessant racket—metal upon metal, voices over voices—noise that ate the edges off thought. For a moment Trent’s stillness seemed an act of resistance against it, but the tension in his shoulders betrayed him.

“Now I know,” he repeated, very low, as if tasting the sentence and finding it bitter. “Now I know.”

Cupples waited, baffled. The evidence was plain; the meaning was not. The words “same man” hung in the air like smoke with nowhere to go.

“I am still completely in the dark,” Cupples said at last, cautiously, as if any louder might break something. “I have often heard of this fingerprint business, and wondered how the police went to work about it. It is of extraordinary interest to me, but upon my life I cannot see how in this case Manderson’s fingerprints are going—”

“I am very sorry, Cupples,” Trent cut in, turning from the window with abrupt swiftness, as though he had heard a sound in another room. “When I began this investigation I meant to take you with me every step of the way. You mustn’t think I have any doubts about your discretion if I say now that I must hold my tongue about the whole thing, at least for a time. I will tell you this: I have come upon a fact that looks too much like having very painful consequences if it is discovered by any one else.”

His face had hardened; the pallor made the darkness under his eyes more emphatic. He struck the table with his hand—one sharp report, like a cleaver on a block.

“It is terrible for me here and now. Up to this moment I was hoping against hope that I was wrong about the fact. I may still be wrong in the surmise that I base upon that fact. There is only one way of finding out that is open to me, and I must nerve myself to take it.”

Cupples stared at him, distressed, with the helplessness of a man watching someone else handle a dangerous flame.

Then Trent’s expression changed, as if he had forced a different face on—one that belonged to light talk and easy rooms.

“All right—I’m not going to be tragic any more,” he said briskly, “and I’ll tell you all about it when I can. Look here, I’m not half through my game with the powder-bottles yet.”

He pulled one of the condemned chairs up to the table and sat down. Taking the broad ivory blade of the paper-knife, he began to test it with his thumb, as a cook tests a knife’s balance before slicing—careful, controlled, almost tender in the movement, though his eyes were anything but tender.

Cupples, swallowing his amazement, leaned forward with dutiful interest and handed him the bottle of lamp-black.

Mrs. Manderson stood at the sitting-room window of White Gables as though she could steady the day by watching it. Outside, June had turned traitor. A fine rain fretted the glass with a dry, persistent tick—pin-point drops driven now and then in little volleys by the wind off the sea. The fields were colourless, the hedges blurred, and from the sullen water white mist came shouldering inland in slow wreaths, as if the coast were cooking up its own cold vapour and sending it to creep through everything. Even within the house the weather made itself felt: the air had that damp, lineny smell of rooms shut up against a storm, and the soundscape was all reduced to the small, unhappy noises of rain on panes, rain in gutters, and far-off surf grinding like a mill that never found grain.

It was a bad morning for a woman whose life had been emptied and left standing.

A knock came. She called, “Come in,” drawing herself up by habit, the motion that always arrived the instant she was reminded she must be seen.

The maid announced Mr. Trent. He apologized for the early hour, but begged to see Mrs. Manderson on a matter of urgency.

Mrs. Manderson would see him.

Before the door opened she went to the mirror. The face that looked back—olive, dark-browed, the mouth set with a sort of tired pride—seemed to her an instrument that had been played too hard. She gave it the briefest grimace, as one might at a dish that has gone cold, and turned as Trent was shown in.

His appearance had changed since she last saw him. He looked used up, like a man who had sat up with a task while the world slept—eyes dulled by sleeplessness, cheeks a shade tighter, his habitual half-smile withdrawn into something guarded. The rain had left its mark on his coat; when he moved, the cloth gave off that faint wet-wool odour which makes a room feel suddenly smaller.

“May I come to the point at once?” he said, after she had given him her hand. “There is a train I ought to catch at Bishopsbridge at twelve o’clock. But I cannot go until I have settled this thing, which concerns you only, Mrs. Manderson. I have been working half the night and thinking the rest; and I know now what I ought to do.”

“You look wretchedly tired,” she said. The kindness in her voice came out as naturally as breathing. “Won’t you sit down? This is a very restful chair. Of course it is about this terrible business and your work as correspondent. Please ask me anything you think I can properly tell you, Mr. Trent. I know you won’t make it worse for me than you can help, in doing your duty here. If you say you must see me about something, I know it must be because, as you say, you ought to do it.”

He sat, but he did not settle; he carried his body as if he might have to rise again quickly.

“Mrs. Manderson,” he said, slowly, choosing each word as one chooses a knife when a clumsy cut will ruin a joint, “I won’t make it worse for you than I can help. But I am bound to make it bad for you—only between ourselves, I hope. As to whether you can properly tell me what I shall ask you, you will decide that; but I tell you this on my word of honour: I shall ask you only as much as will decide me whether to publish or to withhold certain grave things that I have found out about your husband’s death—things not suspected by any one else, nor, I think, likely to be so.”

He drew from his pocket a long envelope and laid it on the small table beside him. The paper made a soft, flat sound against the polish, absurdly loud in the rain-muted room.

“What I have discovered—what I believe that I have practically proved—will be a great shock to you in any case. But it may be worse for you than that; and if you give me reason to think it would be so, then I shall suppress this manuscript, and nothing of what it has to tell shall ever be printed. It consists, I may tell you, of a short private note to my editor, followed by a long dispatch for publication in the Record.

“Now you may refuse to say anything to me. If you do refuse, my duty to my employers, as I see it, is to take this up to London with me today and leave it with my editor to be dealt with at his discretion. My view is, you understand, that I am not entitled to suppress it on the strength of a mere possibility that presents itself to my imagination. But if I gather from you—and I can gather it from no other person—that there is substance in that imaginary possibility I speak of, then I have only one thing to do as a gentleman and as one who—” He hesitated, and the rain seemed to fill the pause with its thin crackle. “—wishes you well. I shall not publish that dispatch of mine. In some directions I decline to assist the police. Have you followed me so far?”

She stood in front of him with her hands clasped, shoulders held back in the rigid calm she had worn at the inquest. The pallor was there, but no other sign.

“I understand quite well,” she said, low and steady. She drew a deep breath, as if taking air before a difficult climb. “I don’t know what dreadful thing you have found out, or what the possibility can be, but it was good—it was honourable of you to come to me about it. Now will you please tell me?”

“I cannot do that,” Trent replied. “The secret is my newspaper’s if it is not yours. If I find it is yours, you shall have my manuscript to read and destroy. Believe me”—his voice broke out, for an instant, into something warmer—“I detest such mystery-making from the bottom of my soul; but it is not I who have made this mystery. This is the most painful hour of my life, and you make it worse by not treating me like a hound.”

He mastered himself back into a careful neutrality.

“The first thing I ask you to tell me is this: is it true, as you stated at the inquest, that you had no idea at all of the reason why your late husband had changed his attitude toward you, and become mistrustful and reserved, during the last few months of his life?”

Her brows lifted. For a flash her eyes had fire in them, bright as if the grey day had set a coal alight behind her face. She rose quickly from her chair.

Trent rose at the same moment and took up the envelope. His whole manner said that he read her movement as dismissal.

But she raised one hand, commanding him to wait. Colour came into her cheeks; her breath sounded quicker.

“Do you know what you ask, Mr. Trent? You ask me if I perjured myself.”

“I do,” he answered, unmoved. After a pause he added, “You knew already that I had not come here to preserve the polite fictions, Mrs. Manderson. The theory that no reputable person, being on oath, could withhold a part of the truth under any circumstances is a polite fiction.”

He stood as if the door might open behind him at any moment. But she did not dismiss him. Instead she went to the window and looked out into the mist as though the blurred fields could tell her, in some older dialect than English, what to do now. He watched the slight movement of her shoulders; it rose and fell, then steadied.

At last, without turning, she spoke clearly.

“Mr. Trent, you inspire confidence in people, and I feel that things which I don’t want known or talked about are safe with you. And I know you must have a very serious reason for doing what you are doing, though I don’t know what it is. I suppose it would be assisting justice in some way if I told you the truth about what you asked just now.

“To understand that truth you ought to know about what went before—I mean about my marriage. After all, a good many people could tell you as well as I can that it was not… a very successful union.

“I was only twenty. I admired his force and courage and certainty; he was the only strong man I had ever known. But it did not take me long to find out that he cared for his business more than for me, and I think I found out even sooner that I had been deceiving myself and blinding myself, promising myself impossible things and wilfully misunderstanding my own feelings, because I was dazzled by the idea of having more money to spend than an English girl ever dreams of.”

She gave a short, bitter little laugh that had no amusement in it—only self-contempt.

“I have been despising myself for that for five years. My husband’s feeling for me… well, I cannot speak of that. What I want to say is that along with it there had always been a belief of his that I was the sort of woman to take a great place in society, and that I should throw myself into it with enjoyment, and become a sort of personage and do him great credit—that was his idea; and the idea remained with him after other delusions had gone.

“I was a part of his ambition. That was his really bitter disappointment, that I failed him as a social success. I think he was too shrewd not to have known in his heart that such a man as he was—twenty years older than I, with great business responsibilities that filled every hour of his life, and caring for nothing else—he must have felt there was a risk of great unhappiness in marrying the sort of girl I was, brought up to music and books and unpractical ideas, always enjoying myself in my own way. But he had reckoned on me as a wife who would do the honours of his position in the world; and I found I couldn’t.”

The words came more freely now, as if once she had broken the crust, everything underneath insisted on being said. She turned from the window, and the stillness she had worn like a dress at the inquest fell away. Her face flushed; her eyes gleamed; her hands moved in small, emphatic gestures, as though shaping something that had never been allowed its proper form.

“The people,” she said. “Oh, those people! Can you imagine what it must be for anyone who has lived in a world where there was always creative work in the background—work with some dignity about it—men and women with professions or arts to follow, with ideals and things to believe in and quarrel about, some wealthy, some quite poor—can you think what it means to step out of that into another world where you have to be very rich, shamefully rich, to exist at all—where money is the only thing that counts and the first thing in everybody’s thoughts—where the men who make the millions are so jaded by the work that sport is the only thing they can occupy themselves with when they have any leisure, and the men who don’t have to work are even duller than the men who do, and vicious as well; and the women live for display and silly amusements and silly immoralities?”

She spoke as if the very noises of that life still rang in her ears—the clatter of carriages, the hard laughter, the ceaseless music that meant nothing, the talk that crowded out quiet like machinery.

“Do you know how awful that life is? Of course I know there are clever people, and people of taste in that set, but they’re swamped and spoiled, and it’s the same thing in the end: empty, empty! Oh! I suppose I’m exaggerating, and I did make friends and have some happy times; but that’s how I feel after it all. The seasons in New York and London—how I hated them! And our house-parties and cruises in the yacht and the rest—the same people, the same emptiness.

“And you see, don’t you, that my husband couldn’t have an idea of all this. His life was never empty. He did not live it in society, and when he was in society he had always his business plans and difficulties to occupy his mind. He hadn’t a suspicion of what I felt, and I never let him know; I couldn’t, it wouldn’t have been fair.”

Her voice softened for a moment, and in that softening there was something like the memory of ordinary kindnesses—domestic minutes that do not make stories, and yet make a life.

“I felt I must do something to justify myself as his wife, sharing his position and fortune; and the only thing I could do was to try, and try, to live up to his idea about my social qualities. I did try. I acted my best. And it became harder year by year. I never was what they call a popular hostess—how could I be? I was a failure; but I went on trying.

“I used to steal holidays now and then. I used to feel as if I was not doing my part of a bargain—it sounds horrid to put it like that, I know, but it was so—when I took one of my old school-friends, who couldn’t afford to travel, away to Italy for a month or two, and we went about cheaply all by ourselves, and were quite happy; or when I went and made a long stay in London with some quiet people who had known me all my life, and we all lived just as in the old days, when we had to think twice about seats at the theatre, and told each other about cheap dressmakers.”

As she spoke, Trent had the sense—odd, intrusive—that she was describing not only places but tempos and textures: the ease of meals eaten without servants hovering, the simple clink of cups in a small kitchen, the relief of speech that did not have to be performed. Even the rain against the window seemed, by contrast, honest and unadorned.

“Those and a few other expeditions of the same sort were my best times after I was married, and they helped me to go through with it the rest of the time. But I felt my husband would have hated to know how much I enjoyed every hour of those returns to the old life.

“And in the end, in spite of everything I could do, he came to know. He could see through anything, I think, once his attention was turned to it. He had always been able to see that I was not fulfilling his idea of me as a figure in the social world, and I suppose he thought it was my misfortune rather than my fault. But the moment he began to see, in spite of my pretending, that I wasn’t playing my part with any spirit, he knew the whole story; he divined how I loathed and was weary of the luxury and the brilliancy and the masses of money just because of the people who lived among them—who were made so by them, I suppose.”

She spoke the last words with a kind of weariness that went beyond any one man’s judgement of her.

“It happened last year. I don’t know just how or when. It may have been suggested to him by some woman—for they all understood, of course. He said nothing to me, and I think he tried not to change in his manner to me at first; but such things hurt—and it was working in both of us. I knew that he knew.

“After a time we were just being polite and considerate to each other. Before he found me out we had been on a footing of—how can I express it to you?—of intelligent companionship, I might say. We talked without restraint of many things of the kind we could agree or disagree about without its going very deep… if you understand. And then that came to an end. I felt that the only possible basis of our living in each other’s company was going under my feet. And at last it was gone.

“It had been like that,” she ended simply, “for months before he died.”

She sank into the corner of the sofa by the window, as though her body at last demanded payment for what she had forced it through. The rain continued its dry tapping. In the pauses between gusts there was a faint, hollow sound of surf, like distant applause for a play no one wanted to see.

For a few moments neither spoke.

Trent’s mind worked furiously, sorting impressions, fitting them to the structure he had built in sleepless hours. He was amazed by her frankness; more than that, by the vigour with which she had spoken—as if the woman the world had seen, pale and controlled, had been only a public translation, and this other woman was the original text, full of heat and rhythm and unrepeatable emphasis. In her excitement her beauty, dark and sudden, seemed to him almost unearthly; and along with the amazement came a quick terror of it, the knowledge of how easily a man’s reason could be deafened by the mere sound of her voice.

He forced himself back to the matter in hand. Whatever pity her story asked for, it did not answer the question he had come to ask.

“I feel as if I had led you into saying more than you meant to say,” he said slowly, “or than I wanted to learn. But there is one brutal question which is the whole point of my enquiry.”

He braced himself as one does before plunging into water that will take the breath away.

“Mrs. Manderson, will you assure me that your husband’s change toward you had nothing to do with John Marlowe?”

What he had dreaded came at once, without argument, without evasion.

“Oh!” she cried—a sound torn out of her, naked with anguish. Her face went up, her hands stretched out as if for mercy; then she covered her burning cheeks and flung herself sideways among the cushions so that he saw only the heavy crown of her black hair and the convulsive movement of her shoulders. Her sobs shook her as if something inside her had broken and could not be held together. One foot turned inward, gracelessly, in the abandonment of misery—an intimate, human detail that made the whole collapse more painful to witness.

Trent stood very still. His face went white, and the calm on it was like a mask hammered into place. With a senseless exactness he set the long envelope down again, precisely in the centre of the little polished table, as though neatness could keep the world from spilling.

Then, without a word, he went to the door and let himself out, closing it noiselessly behind him.

A few minutes later he was tramping through the rain, away from White Gables, going nowhere, seeing nothing. The wet air had the raw smell of soaked earth and salt, and the wind worried at his hat brim, and the sound of his own steps on the soaked path was the only thing in the world that seemed under his control. Inside him everything else raved: an impulse to go back, to drop at her feet, to beg forgiveness for the cruelty of his question, to say words—he did not know what words—that were already crowding his mouth and would ruin him if he let them out. It would be to wreck his self-respect, to betray his purpose, to make her wretchedness fouler by dragging his own infatuation into it—talking like a fool to a woman whose husband was not yet buried, to a woman who loved another man.

Such was the magic of her tears, and such the danger of them: in a moment they had quickened into life the thing his heart had warned him never to allow.

For Philip Trent was young—young even beyond his years. A way of living that kept his edge keen and his spirit volcanic had left him ill-prepared for the meeting that comes once, usually to no purpose but the testing of virtue and the power of the will; and now, in the rain and the sea-wind, he set his teeth and fought to trample it down.

My Dear Molloy,—I am writing this in case I miss you at your office. I have found out who killed Manderson, as the enclosed dispatch shows. That was my business; yours is to decide what use to make of it. It names a person nobody suspects, and it goes near to accusing him of the murder; so you will not print it before an arrest, and I take it you cannot lawfully print it after an arrest until he has been tried and convicted. When that time comes you can do as you like; and before it comes you may contrive to make something of the facts. That is for you. Meanwhile, will you put Scotland Yard in possession of what I have written? I have done with the Manderson mystery, and I wish to God I had never touched it. Here follows my dispatch. P.T.

Marlstone, June 16th.

I begin this, my third—and likely my last—dispatch to the Record upon the Manderson murder with two contrary sensations, like smelling at once the clean breath of a bakery at dawn and, under it, the sour reek from a drain. Relief first: in my earlier reports I was forced, for plain reasons of justice, to keep back facts I had already learned—facts which, if printed, would have warned a certain person and might well have put him beyond reach; for he is a man of uncommon boldness and resource. Those facts I can now set down. But I do not pretend to enjoy the tale. There is a taint upon it, an aftertaste that clings to the palate; not merely the ugliness of death, but the uglier cunning that can counterfeit the look and sound of domestic life—bedrooms, slippers by a door, a word spoken through a half-open communicating passage—as neatly as one can counterfeit a dish by following its recipe.

It will be remembered that in my first dispatch I described the situation as I found it early on Tuesday morning: how the body was discovered, what state it was in, and how complete was the mystery; I noted one or two local theories; gave some account of the dead man’s domestic surroundings; and followed, in some detail, his movements on the Sunday evening. I mentioned too what may have seemed irrelevant at the time: that a quantity of whisky far beyond Manderson’s nightly habit had vanished from his private decanter since the last hour when he was certainly alive. On the following day—the day of the inquest—I wired little more than an abstract of the proceedings; a verbatim report, at my request, was made by other Record men in court. That day is not yet over as I write; and yet I have already carried through an investigation that leads me directly to the man who must now clear himself of Manderson’s death.

There were, apart from the central enigma of Manderson’s rising long before his usual hour and going out to meet his end, two minor oddities that must have struck thousands who have read the accounts. They were apparent from the first.

The first was this: the body lay not thirty yards from the house, and yet every soul within declared they heard no cry, no struggle, no sound. Manderson had not been gagged. The marks on his wrists spoke of a grapple. And there had been at least one pistol-shot. (I say at least one; for where a firearm is used at close quarters, and hands are clutching and wrenching, the murderer very commonly fires wide once.) The silence puzzled me the more when I learned that Martin the butler was a bad sleeper with an ear as quick as a bat’s, and that his bedroom, with the window open, looked almost straight toward the shed where the body was found. Houses have their own night-music—wind in shrubs, boards contracting, the tick of a distant clock—and a man like Martin learns every note of it; a new sound strikes him as sharply as a wrong word spoken in a dialect you know by heart. Yet he heard nothing.

The second oddity was Manderson’s leaving his dental plate by the bedside. He had risen and dressed himself completely—necktie, watch and chain—and gone out without putting in the plate that carried all the visible upper teeth, a thing he had worn daily for years. It was not as if he had dashed out half-clad. Anyone who lives with such a contrivance knows that putting it in is as habitual as swallowing one’s first mouthful of tea. Speaking, eating—mere human appearance—hang upon it.

At the time neither detail seemed to lead anywhere. They only made me feel, as one sometimes does when a kitchen is too quiet, that something is going on where you cannot see it: a hand working in the scullery, a pan set on the hob without a word spoken, an intention moving soundlessly behind a closed door.

With that preamble I come to the discovery which, within the first few hours of my inquiry, set me on the track that much ingenuity had tried to cover.

I have already described Manderson’s bedroom: its severe bareness of furniture, and the strange contrast between that austerity and the profusion of clothes and shoes, and the way it communicated with Mrs. Manderson’s room. On the upper of the two long shelves where the shoes stood ranged, I found—exactly where I had been told they would be—the patent leather pair Manderson wore on the last evening he was seen alive. I had glanced along the row without any thought of clues; it happens I am something of a judge of shoe-leather, and these were all of first-rate make. But that particular pair caught my eye at once.

They were the light, thin-soled lace-up kind, without toe-caps, very beautifully made. They were old and well worn; but they had been tended, polished, kept on their trees; they looked respectable still. What I noticed was a slight splitting of the leather at the vamp—the place where strain tells when a tight shoe is forced on. Ordinarily a strong stitching guards that point; in both shoes the stitching had parted and the leather below had given. The rent was tiny—scarcely an eighth of an inch; the torn edges, released, had come together again, so that only a connoisseur of worn leather would have remarked it. Even less visible—indeed not visible at all unless you hunted for it—was a slight dragging of the stitches that bind upper to sole. At toe and outer side in each shoe the join showed the telltale tug.

There is only one meaning: the shoes had been worn by someone for whom they were too small.

Manderson’s care for his small, narrow feet was obvious from a glance at his boots and dress shoes; he was well shod, perhaps vainly, but always exactly. None of the other shoes showed such marks. This pair had been strained by a larger foot—and recently; the torn edges were fresh.

The notion that anyone had worn them since Manderson’s death was not worth the breath to utter; the body had been found only some twenty-six hours when I examined them, and in any case why should anyone borrow a dead man’s patent leathers? The possibility that someone had borrowed them in Manderson’s lifetime and spoiled them was almost as remote; with other shoes to choose from, why force these on? Besides, the only other men about were the butler and the two secretaries. I do not say I weighed those possibilities as long as I ought; my mind had begun to run, and in a business like this it is often wisdom to let it run—provided you keep a grip on the lead at the end.

Call it fancy, if you will; call it the familiar psychology of a knot of circumstances suddenly finding its right thread. When chance or labour gives you the key-fact, all your scattered observations fly into a new arrangement around it before you have consciously tasted its whole significance. I had barely formed the thought, “Somebody who was not Manderson has been wearing these shoes,” when a whole flock of remembered details rose, like scents released when a hot pan meets cold wine.

Manderson did not drink much whisky at night—yet whisky had vanished. Manderson was tidy in dress—yet the body, when found, had cuffs dragged up inside the sleeves and the shoes unevenly laced. Manderson was methodical—yet he had apparently risen without washing and had put on the previous evening’s shirt and collar and underclothing. Manderson was particular—yet his watch lay in the wrong waistcoat pocket, the pocket that lacked the leather lining meant to save the case. (I had mentioned these points in my first dispatch; none of us saw their bearing then.) Manderson was uncommunicative to his wife—yet at bedtime he had spoken to her of his doings, and with a certain unnecessary detail. And, strangest of all, Manderson had gone out without his teeth.

All that came upon me in far less time than it takes to set it on paper. Yet when the definite idea sprang up starkly before me—It was not Manderson who was in the house that night—it looked at first like lunacy. Manderson had certainly dined there. People had seen him at close quarters. He had gone out with Marlowe in the car. But—was it he who returned at ten? The question felt ridiculous and yet I could not dismiss it. A faint light began to creep, as over a field at dawn; you do not see the sun yet, but you know it is coming.

I set myself to consider, one by one, those details now newly flavoured by the shoe-leather.

Why would a man force his feet into Manderson’s narrow shoes? The answer came easily. The police understand footprints. But it was not only that the man wished to leave no prints of his own; he wished, if there were prints, that they should be Manderson’s. His whole design, if my guess was right, aimed at producing the belief that Manderson was in the place that night. And he did not merely mean to leave footprints; he meant to leave the shoes themselves. He did so. The housemaid found them outside the bedroom door, where Manderson always left his shoes. She polished them, and later—after the body was found—put them back upon the shelves.

Then the false teeth, which had seemed the wildest touch in the whole affair, fell into place with a click. A dental plate is separable from its owner. If my guess was right, the unknown had brought the denture with him and left it in the bedroom, for the same reason as he left the shoes: to make it impossible for anyone to doubt that Manderson had been there and had gone to bed there. From that followed at once the inference that Manderson was dead before the false Manderson came to the house. Other things agreed with it.

Consider the clothing. If my guess was right, the unknown in Manderson’s shoes had certainly possessed Manderson’s trousers, waistcoat, and shooting jacket. They were in the bedroom; and Martin had seen the unmistakable jacket—one that no one who knew Manderson could confuse—on the man crouched at the telephone in the library. The jacket, I now saw, was a cardinal ingredient in the masquerade. It would make Martin accept, at a glance, what he expected to see.

And then a point struck me so hard that I wondered I had not seen it earlier. So strong is the spell of an assumption that it blinds even trained eyes. Martin had not seen the man’s face—nor had Mrs. Manderson.

Mrs. Manderson, from her evidence at the inquest (I had the Record stenographers take it down verbatim), did not see the man at all. Indeed, as I shall show, she scarcely could have. She merely spoke with him in a drowsy voice through the half-open door, resuming a conversation she had had with her living husband an hour before. Martin could only have seen the man’s back as he crouched over the telephone; he had his hat on—Manderson’s broad-brimmed hat. There is character in the back of a head, in the set of shoulders, in the habitual bend; and a man of roughly the same build, with the right clothes and the nerve to hold still, needs no other disguise than good mimicry and the common human reluctance to look twice at what ought to be.

I stopped there and contemplated the coolness of it. Provided the mimicry held and the nerve held, the thing was safe—almost easy. It was like slipping into a familiar kitchen at night, moving by memory among cupboards and hooks and hearthstones, imitating the cook’s way of setting a saucepan down without a clatter: only some freak accident, some unexpected creak or sudden light, would betray you.

To return to the man’s entrance by the window rather than by the front door. The reason will be obvious. Had he come through the hall he would almost certainly have been heard by Martin in his pantry across the passage; he might have met him face to face, and then no hat and jacket could have saved him.

The whisky now took on a sharper meaning. I had not valued it highly—whisky can vanish in a house as mysteriously as cold meat after Sunday supper—but Martin had been plainly staggered by it. It seemed to me now that many a man, fresh from a bloody business—from stripping a corpse, from carrying a dead weight, from the sickly mingling of gunpowder taint and warm human smell—would turn to the decanter as to an ally. No doubt he drank before he rang for Martin; after pulling off the telephone trick, he likely drank again. But he knew when to stop.

The worst part was still before him: the business—clearly vital to him, for reasons I had yet to nail down—of shutting himself in Manderson’s room and arranging a convincing tableau of occupancy. All this under the slight but nerve-racking risk of the woman on the other side of the half-open door waking and in some way discovering him. Yet even that was calculated. From Mrs. Manderson’s bed, with its head to the wall a little beyond the door, nothing was visible through the doorway but one of the cupboards by Manderson’s bed-head. For her to see more she would have had to rise, go to the door, and look. And this man knew the household ways. He would count on her being asleep; and he would count also on the estrangement between husband and wife—an estrangement they tried to mask by keeping their rooms connected, but which was known to every servant and acquaintance—as making her less likely to take notice of her “husband’s” presence.

So I followed him in imagination up to the bedroom and watched him set about his work. And then, with a tightening at my own throat, I thought of the moment when he must have heard, like a spoon dropped on stone, the sound he dreaded most: Mrs. Manderson’s drowsy voice.

What she actually said she could not recall at the inquest. She thinks she asked whether he had had a good run in the car. Now observe what the unknown did. Here lies, I believe, a point of supreme significance. Not only did he answer her in Manderson’s voice; he volunteered an explanation. He said that, on a sudden inspiration, he had sent Marlowe in the car to Southampton—to get some important information from a man sailing for Paris by the morning boat. Why those details, from a man who had long ceased to confide in his wife, and upon a matter scarcely likely to interest her? Why, above all, those details about Marlowe?

Having carried the story so far, I now put forward definite propositions, based on the hypothesis the shoes forced upon me: that between a time somewhere about ten—when the car started—and a time somewhere about eleven, Manderson was shot, probably at some distance from the house, since no shot was heard; that the body was brought back, laid by the shed, and stripped of its outer clothing; that at some time around eleven a man who was not Manderson, wearing Manderson’s shoes, hat, and jacket, entered the library by the garden window; that he had with him Manderson’s black trousers, waistcoat, and motor-coat, the denture taken from Manderson’s mouth, and the weapon with which he had been murdered; that he hid these, rang for Martin, and sat down at the telephone with his hat on and his back to the door; that he kept the telephone between Martin and his face the whole time Martin was in the room; that when he went upstairs he entered Marlowe’s room quietly and placed the revolver with which the crime had been committed—Marlowe’s own revolver—back into the case on the mantelpiece from which it had been taken; and that he then went into Manderson’s room, put Manderson’s shoes outside the door, threw Manderson’s garments on a chair, laid the denture in the bowl by the bedside, and selected a suit of clothes, a pair of shoes, and a tie from those kept there.

Here I pause to ask the question for which the ground is now ready.

Who was the false Manderson?

Reviewing what I knew—or could with near certainty infer—about that man, I wrote down five conclusions.

(1) He had been in close relations with the dead man. In acting before Martin and speaking to Mrs. Manderson he made no slip.

(2) He was built not unlike Manderson, especially in height and breadth of shoulder, which chiefly determine the look of a seated figure seen from behind when the head is hidden and the body loosely clothed. But his feet were larger, though not enormously larger.

(3) He possessed an aptitude for mimicry and acting—likely some experience.

(4) He had minute acquaintance with the household’s habits.

(5) He was under a vital necessity to create the belief that Manderson was alive and in that house until some time after midnight on Sunday night.

So much I took as certain, or close upon it. It was as far as I could see; and it was far enough.

I now give, in the same order, the relevant facts I was able to learn about Mr. John Marlowe, from himself and from other sources.

(1) He had been Manderson’s private secretary, on terms of great intimacy, for nearly four years.

(2) The two men were nearly of the same height—about five feet eleven. Both were strongly built and heavy-shouldered. Marlowe, being younger by some twenty years, was a trifle slighter through the body, though Manderson was in good physical condition. Marlowe’s shoes (I examined several pairs) were about one shoemaker’s size longer and broader than Manderson’s.

(3) On the afternoon of the first day of my inquiry, after I had reached the results already detailed, I telegraphed a personal friend—an Oxford Fellow I knew to be interested in theatrical matters—in these terms:

Please wire John Marlowe’s record in connection with acting at Oxford some time past decade very urgent and confidential.

He replied next morning (the morning of the inquest):

Marlowe was member O.U.D.S for three years and president 19— played Bardolph Cleon and Mercutio excelled in character acting and imitations in great demand at smokers was hero of some historic hoaxes.

I had been led to send that telegram by seeing on the mantel-shelf in Marlowe’s bedroom a photograph of himself and two others in the costume of Falstaff’s three followers, with an inscription from The Merry Wives, and the imprint of an Oxford photographer.

(4) During his connection with Manderson, Marlowe had lived as one of the family. Apart from the servants no one else had his opportunity of learning, in detail, the domestic life of the Manderson house—its doors, its hours, its habits of speech, even its night-sounds and where they would be masked.

(5) I ascertained beyond doubt that Marlowe arrived at a hotel in Southampton on Monday morning at 6.30, and there proceeded to carry out the commission which, according to his story—and according to the statement made to Mrs. Manderson in the bedroom by the false Manderson—had been entrusted to him by his employer. He then returned by car to Marlstone and displayed great amazement and horror on hearing of the murder.

These are the relevant facts about Marlowe. We must now examine fact number five in connection with conclusion number five about the false Manderson.

Trent’s letter to Sir James Molloy went from Munich in a hand that looked steady enough, though the man who wrote it had been living, for days, on black coffee and the last crust of German rye that a lodging-house landlady would press upon him with a sigh.

“I return the cheque you sent me for the Manderson affair,” he wrote. “What I did was not worth a tenth of it. I could take it without scruple if I had not taken it into my head—never mind why—to touch no money at all that came from that business. Pay, if you please, at your ordinary space-rate for the ‘copy,’ and give the money to some charity that does not make a trade of bullying. I came here to see old friends and put my thoughts in order; the thought that rises uppermost is that for a while I want work with movement in it. I cannot paint. I could not paint a fence. Try me as your Own Correspondent somewhere. Give me an adventure and I will send you accounts. Afterwards I could settle down and work.”

The answer came as a telegram—hard, clipped, urgent as the stamp of a boot on a stone stair. He was to go east at once, to Kurland and Livonia. Citizen Browning was abroad again, and town and countryside had taken fire.

For two months Trent ranged where chance, orders, and the current of men in panic carried him. It was a roving commission, and it did not fail him; in that bitter, iron way in which “luck” serves the man who has put his body between his paper and the truth. He was the only correspondent who saw General Dragilew die in the street at Volmar, shot down by a girl of eighteen with a face like a pale loaf newly broken—innocent shape, dreadful purpose. He saw farmsteads burning, saw lynchings that dragged on like bad music, fusillades that cracked and rolled until the ear could not sort one report from another, hangings that creaked in the wind like the old timbers of a mill. Each day his soul sickened anew at the stupidities bred of misrule—at the raw, grinding noise of power and despair, the human voice drowned under it, as a thrush is drowned beside a factory wall.

There were nights when he lay down with his revolver under his cheek and the sour smell of sweat and stale spirits thick in the air of a peasant room; mornings when he woke to the rasp of carts and the barking of dogs, and knew by the changing pitch of the sounds outside that danger had shifted but not gone. Many days he went fasting—fasting as soldiers do, not with virtue but with a dry mouth and a touchiness of nerve; and when food came it was often no more than a ladle of cabbage soup, thin as wash-water yet fragrant with dill, a wedge of black bread, a herring split and salted until it stung the lips. In such moments he found himself, absurdly, listening: the small domestic sounds—spoon against tin, the sigh of steam from a pot, the murmured talk in a tongue whose old words had been battered by foreign orders—had more power to steady him than the crack of rifles. They were a kind of stubborn music, a household insisting on itself in the midst of mythic evil.

Yet there was never an evening nor a morning when he did not see the face of the woman he hopelessly loved.

It was an unhappy pride that he took in the endurance of it. The thing interested him as a phenomenon; it amazed him, and, against his will, enlightened him. He had known emotion; he was thirty-two and no monk. But what he had learned of sex and love had come to him without hunting and without purchase and, mercifully, without the memories that poison a man’s mouth like rancid fat. He went through life with a strange respect for certain feminine weakness, and a very simple terror of certain feminine strength. He had held a lukewarm faith that something in him remained to be called forth; that if ever a voice called, it would be heard in its own time, and not as the result of seeking. He had not imagined that the call, if it came, might take a sinister form.

Two things had struck him down in his feeling for Mabel Manderson: the insane suddenness with which it rose full-grown, and the extravagant hopelessness of it. Before it came he had been ready enough to laugh at permanence in unreturned passion as a generous boy’s delusion. He knew now he had been wrong, and he lived bitterly with that knowledge like a taste that would not leave his tongue.

In his mind she came always as she had been at the first sight of her, when he had passed unseen at the edge of the cliff and surprised that great gesture—arms flung out, whole body speaking—of passionate joy in new liberty. It had told him, plainer than speech, that her widowhood was release from torment; and it had confirmed with terrible force what his mind, long before, had begun to suspect—that it was a passport to happiness with a man she loved.

He could not name with certainty the very moment when the suspicion first took root. The seed, he believed, was sown at his first meeting with Marlowe. His mind, trained to note the turn of a phrase and the set of a shoulder, would have registered automatically that such strength and grace, such looks and manner as the tall young man possessed, might go far with any woman whose affections were not fixed. Joined to what Mr. Cupples had told him—those plain, domestic facts of the Manderson marriage, the slow starvation of sympathy and the unquickened motherhood—something in him must have fitted the pieces together, silently, without his bidding. Certainly the thought presented itself as an established thing when, after satisfying himself who the murderer was, he began to hunt for motive.

Motive, motive—how desperately he had tried to find another. How often he had turned his face away from the grim idea that Marlowe, driven by passion like himself and perhaps made mad by truths of the wife’s unhappiness, had taken the guiltiest leaf from Bothwell’s book. Yet in all his investigations, and in all the brooding that followed, he could discover nothing that could prompt Marlowe to such a deed—nothing but that temptation, the full urgency of which he could not measure, but which, if it existed, must have pressed like a hand at the throat upon a bold spirit in which scruple had somehow been paralysed.

If Trent could trust his senses, Marlowe was neither insane nor by nature evil. But that did not clear him. Murder for a woman’s sake was no rarity, Heaven knew. If modern comfort had softened impulse among the comfortable classes, and if the modern apparatus of detection had taught them caution, the thing was still possible. It only needed a man of daring and intelligence, his soul drugged with the vapours of an intoxicating intrigue, to plan and do it.

A thousand times, with anguish like hunger, Trent tried to reason away the dread that Mabel Manderson had known too much of what was intended against her husband’s life. That she knew all the truth after it was done he could not doubt. The collapse he had seen—unforgettable, bodily, like a woman’s knees giving way under her—when the question of Marlowe was bluntly put, swept away the last hope that there was no love between them, and seemed to speak, too, of fear of discovery. In any case she knew the truth after reading what he had left with her; and no public suspicion had fallen on Marlowe since. She had destroyed his manuscript, then, and had taken him at his word, keeping the secret that threatened her lover’s life.

But it was the monstrous thought—that she might have known beforehand, might have kept silence while murder brewed—that haunted him. She might have suspected, guessed something; was it conceivable she knew the whole plot, connived? He could never forget that his first suspicion of Marlowe’s motive had been roused by the fact that the escape was made through the lady’s room. Then, before he had seen her, he had been ready enough to entertain the idea of her equal guilt, her co-operation. He had pictured some passionate hystérique, merciless as a cat in hate and love, zealous abettor, perhaps ruling spirit in the crime.

Then he had seen her. He had spoken with her, helped her in her weakness, breathed her atmosphere. Trent was one of those who think they can scent wickedness as surely as burnt milk; in her presence he had felt an inward certainty of ultimate goodness of heart. It was nothing against her that, on the cliff, she had abandoned herself for one moment to relief at the ending of bondage, at the end of years in which even the daily bread had been bitter in the mouth. That she had turned to Marlowe in destitution he believed; that she had knowledge of a deadly purpose he did not.

And yet, morning and evening, the sickening doubts returned. He saw again the bedroom where preparations had been made almost under her roof; he saw the window of her own chamber by which Marlowe had got out. Had he forgotten his cunning and taken the risk of telling her then? Or had he played his part still, and stolen away while she slept? Trent did not think she had known of the masquerade when she gave evidence at the inquest; it read like honest evidence. Or—though he despised the question and could not silence it—had she lain expecting the footstep, the whisper that should tell her it was done? Among the foul possibilities of human nature, could black ruthlessness and black deceit be hidden behind that good, straight, gentle seeming?

Those thoughts scarcely left him when he was alone.

He served Sir James well for six months and earned his pay; and then returned to Paris, where he went to work again with a better heart. His powers came back to him, as a man’s appetite returns when fever breaks; he began to live more happily than he had expected among a tribe of oddly assorted friends—French, English, American—artists, poets, journalists, policemen, hotel-keepers, soldiers, lawyers, business men, and men who were none of these and lived by their wits. His old faculty of sympathetic interest in his fellows won him privileges seldom extended to the Briton. He was taken into the bosom of a Frenchman’s family again, and found it as he remembered it—down to the wallpaper patterns and the little movable things that seemed to belong to the nation like an inherited recipe.

He was admitted to the momentous confidences of les jeunes, and found them as certain as any generation of youth ever was that they had surprised the secrets of art and life. But they were—so it seemed to him—much more shallow than their forerunners, much less really clever. The secrets they wrested from the Universe were not such important secrets as had been wrested ten years before. He believed and deplored this until, one day, he found himself in a restaurant beside a too well-fed man whom, in spite of comfort’s ravages, he recognised as one of the jeunes of his own period—the man who used to call himself and a few others the Hermits of the New Parnassus.

The Hermit, who had once sworn to destroy rules and proclaim that verse was free, was now in the Ministry of the Interior and already decorated. Between mouthfuls—taken with the careful, satisfied chew of a man to whom food is no longer discovery but entitlement—he told Trent that what France needed most was a hand of iron. He could quote exact prices paid for betrayals of the country of which Trent had never heard. Thus Trent was brought to make the old discovery: it was he who had changed, like his friend of the Administration, and les jeunes were still the same.

Even so, he found it hard to name what he had lost that mattered. Unless it was as simple as high spirits—those light, clean inner sounds that make the world’s noise bearable.

One morning in June, descending the slope of the Rue des Martyrs, he saw a figure approaching that he remembered. He looked quickly round, for the thought of meeting Mr. Bunner again was unacceptable. For some time he had known his wound was healing under creative work; he thought less often of the woman he loved, and with less pain. He would not have the memory of those three days prised open again.

But the street was straight and narrow; there was no turning aside. The American saw him almost at once. His geniality was unforced and made Trent ashamed, for he had liked the man.

They sat long over a meal. The room smelled of browned butter and wine and the faint metallic tang of oysters; in the pauses of talk came the music of plates and the soft thunder of traffic outside—Paris making its continuous argument, human voices threaded through it. Bunner talked; Trent listened with genuine pleasure, putting in now and then a question, a remark, as one stirs a sauce that needs only a touch to keep it from sticking. Besides liking his companion, he enjoyed his conversation for its own sake—its odd American turns, its quick leaps, its verbal surprises.

Bunner, it appeared, was resident in Paris now as chief Continental agent of the Manderson firm, fully satisfied with his position and prospects. He discoursed on these for twenty minutes. When that dish was cleared away he told Trent—who admitted he had been out of England a year—that Marlowe, shortly after Manderson’s death, had entered his father’s business; it was flourishing again, and Marlowe was already practically in control. Bunner spoke with generous admiration of his friend’s talent for affairs.

“Jack Marlowe has a natural big head,” he declared, “and if he had more experience I wouldn’t want to have him up against me. He would put a crimp in me every time.”

As the talk flowed on, Trent listened with a perplexity that grew like a noise in a quiet room. Something was wrong in his whole theory; the central figure was not mentioned. Presently Bunner, as casually as a man seasoning soup, said that Marlowe was engaged to be married—to an Irish girl. He celebrated her charms with native enthusiasm.

Under the table Trent’s hands locked together savagely, as if he could crush the thought between them. What could have happened? His ideas slid and shifted; what he had built up, day after day, in blood and sleeplessness, rocked on its foundations.

At last he forced himself to a direct question. Bunner was not fully informed. He knew Mrs. Manderson had left England immediately after her husband’s affairs were settled and had lived for some time in Italy. She had returned not long ago to London; she had decided not to live in the Mayfair house and had bought a smaller one in Hampstead, and also, Bunner understood, a place in the country. She was said to go little into society.

“And all the good hard dollars just waiting for some one to spraddle them around,” Bunner added, with a note of pathos. “Why, she has money to burn—money to feed to the birds—and nothing doing. The old man left her more than half his wad. And think of the figure she might make in the world. She is beautiful, and she is the best woman I ever met, too. But she couldn’t ever seem to get the habit of spending money the way it ought to be spent.”

His words ran on into a soliloquy. Trent scarcely heard. He was occupied by the shock of the new fact, and by the way it rearranged the whole pantry of his mind—the jars he had labeled and stored, the spices of suspicion, the bitter herbs of certainty. He pleaded business soon and they parted cordially.

Half an hour later he was in his studio, swiftly and mechanically cleaning up. He wanted to know what had happened; somehow he must find out. He could never approach her—he would never bring back to her the shame of that last encounter; it was scarcely likely he would even set eyes on her. But he must know.

Cupples was in London. Marlowe was there. And, anyhow, he was sick of Paris. The thoughts came and went; but under them all strained the fibres of an unseen cord that dragged mercilessly at his heart, and he cursed it in the moments when he could not deny it was there. The folly of it—the useless, pitiable folly.

In twenty-four hours his feeble roots in Paris were torn up. He stood on deck looking over a leaden sea at the shining fortress-wall of the Dover cliffs, and the English air met him with its familiar salt and coal-smoke. Even the sound changed: the thicker consonants, the harsher wheels, the industrial undertone that never quite ceased. He felt, without naming it, how a country’s noise can press upon a man’s nerves and make him meaner than he intends.

He had decided he must first see Mr. Cupples, who could tell him far more than Bunner knew. But Mr. Cupples was away travelling, not expected for a month; and Trent had no reasonable excuse for hastening him back. Marlowe he would not confront until he had at least reconnoitred the ground.

He constrained himself from the crowning folly of seeking out Mrs. Manderson’s Hampstead house. He could not enter it; and the mere thought of being seen by her, lurking in the neighbourhood like a starving dog nosing at a kitchen door, brought the blood to his face.

He stayed at a hotel, took a studio, and while he waited for Cupples’s return attempted vainly to lose himself in work. At the end of a week an idea came to him and he seized it with eager precipitancy. At their last meeting she had let fall some word of a taste for music.

That evening he went to the opera—and thereafter regularly. He might see her; and if, in spite of caution, she caught sight of him, they could both be blind to each other. Anybody might happen to go to the opera. So each evening he went alone, passing as quickly as he could through the vestibule’s brilliant crowd, and each evening he came away knowing she had not been there.

The habit gave him a kind of guilty satisfaction alongside the excitement of the search; for he loved music too, and nothing brought him so much peace while its spell endured. In the hush before an overture he could hear, for a little while, the softer ecology of the world—the controlled breathing of a thousand bodies, the creak of velvet, the faint rustle like leaves when programmes were unfolded—sounds that felt, compared to the streets outside, like a remembered dialect: intimate, unmarketed, hard to translate into anything but itself.

One night, hurrying through the crowd as the bell sounded, he felt a touch on his arm.

Flooded at once with an incredible certainty, he turned.

It was she—so much more radiant in the absence of grief and anxiety, in the fact that she was smiling, in the allurement of evening dress, that he could not speak. She too breathed a little quickly; there was a light of daring in her eyes and cheeks as she greeted him.

Her words were few.

“I wouldn’t miss a note of Tristan,” she said, “nor must you. Come and see me in the interval.”

She gave him the number of the box.

The two months that followed were the kind of season a man carries in his body long after his mind has tried to lay it down—like the taste of smoke in the back of the throat, or the ache left by a too-loud room when one steps out into the street and the ears keep ringing. Trent lived through them with the sensation of being scorched and chilled by turns, as if some draught from a furnace-mouth met him at every turn, though nothing in the outward weather justified it.

He saw Mrs. Manderson perhaps six times. Each meeting, by its very propriety, bewildered him into a kind of helpless anger. She offered him a cool friendliness—a middle temperature, skilfully held—neither distant enough to be dismissal nor warm enough to be forgiveness. It was like being served a dish one cannot name: all the right ingredients, no recognisable flavour, and so the palate suspects some missing honesty in the cooking.

At the opera, of all places, he found her in a box with Mrs. Wallace, a brisk, pretty-voiced matron whom he had known since childhood; and it struck him, absurdly, as though Mrs. Manderson had stepped from one timeline into another without leaving a seam. She had returned from Italy and, by the simplest domestic mechanism—an uncle’s house, neighbours who hunted, invitations that pass like bread from hand to hand—had pitched her tent exactly on his accustomed ground. She spoke of it lightly: she had settled where his friends were settled; and that was all. The explanation was as unromantic as the way a cook chooses a market: you go where the produce is, where the old vendors still know the names for things and do not call everything “fresh” because it has no other language left.

Trent remembered that evening with a kind of horror, because he had behaved unlike himself. He felt heated, maladroit; his tongue ran on about his Baltic travels with the idiotic fluency of a man filling silence so as not to hear what is inside his own head, and now and then he realised he was addressing everything to Mrs. Wallace while the person he meant to speak to sat beside her, listening with an expression that gave nothing away. When he first joined them in the vestibule Mrs. Manderson had shown the faintest shadow of agitation—something like a bird’s quick start at a sudden noise—but it vanished before he could be sure he had seen it. She spoke pleasantly of travel, of her London arrangements, of common acquaintances; she moved through the talk with the same accomplished hand with which she later moved through music.

During the last half of the opera, when he stayed behind them, he was conscious of almost nothing but the outline of her cheek, the heavy coil of her black hair, the clean line where shoulder met arm, the pale hand resting on the box’s cushion. The orchestra swelled and dwindled; the audience’s murmurs rose like surf and subsided; but all that reached him was the sight of that hair, which, in his strained imagination, became a dark forest—pathless, immense, enchanted—inviting him to lose himself and be ruined. He left at the end pale and subdued, parting from them with a formality that barely held him together.

Afterwards—at a country house where they were both guests, and on later occasions—he kept himself more in hand. He matched her manner, answered ease with ease, and congratulated himself, in the cold moments afterwards, on having got through decently, considering.

Considering that he lived in a private agony compounded of remorse, longing, and a bewilderment that never slept. He could make nothing—absolutely nothing—of her attitude. That she had read his manuscript and understood the suspicion in his last question at White Gables was beyond doubt. How, then, could she talk to him with frank courtesy, as she talked to men who had never once, in thought, struck at her?

Yet for all the smoothness of her manner there was, to Trent’s intuitive sense, an injury underneath; not proclaimed, not insisted upon, but felt. More than once, in the rare moments when they spoke apart, he had warning—like the moment before a kettle lifts its voice—that she was nearing the subject; and each time he turned the talk aside with the ingenuity of fear.

Two resolutions he made.

First: when he had finished a commissioned piece that bound him to London, he would go away and stay away. The strain was too great. He no longer burned to know the truth; he wanted nothing that would confirm, by brutal fact, what he had already begun to believe by a kind of inward faith—that he had blundered, misread tears, misinterpreted silence, written himself down a slanderous fool. The whole puzzle of Manderson’s death ceased, in his mind, to have any savour of intellectual appetite. Mr. Cupples returned to town; Trent did not ask him a question. He remembered, with a peculiar clarity, the emphasis of his own words spoken earlier: “So long as she considered herself bound to him... no power on earth could have persuaded her.” He met Mrs. Manderson at dinner in her uncle’s large, tomb-like house in Bloomsbury—rooms with a muffled air, as if heavy curtains and old carpets had been laid to deaden not only footsteps but speech—and he spent most of the evening talking to a Berlin professor of archaeology, finding relief in a subject that was all shards and strata and nothing that bled.

Second: he would not be alone with her.

But when, a few days later, she wrote asking him to come and see her the next afternoon, he made no attempt to evade it. The note might as well have been a hand laid on a sleeve. It was a challenge, and he answered it.

She received him in a beautiful room that seemed to have been arranged with the same intelligence that lays out a table: everything chosen, everything in its place, nothing oppressive. Tea was brought, and she celebrated its rites with the ease of a woman for whom hospitality was not performance but habit. The tray gave out a faint fragrance of warm china and tannin; there were little sandwiches cut with care, and cakes that suggested butter and lemon; the silver made small clear sounds as it touched the cup rims—a domestic music that held the room together, as if the very acoustics were being tended. Trent, who had spent too much time lately in noisy streets and talk-heavy gatherings, found his nerves noticing every soft clink and pour, every hush between sentences. It struck him, with sudden bitterness, how the modern world was forever hammering at the ear—omnibuses, shouting vendors, the grind of construction—until people forgot what quiet sounded like, and forgot, too, that quiet is not emptiness but a habitat.

For some time after tea she joined naturally in his slightly fevered talk of the day’s events, and he began to hope she had abandoned whatever grave intention she might have had. She smiled carelessly; and as she did so he recalled, not for the first time, the old sentence written of a Princess of Brunswick: “Her mouth has ten thousand charms that touch the soul.” Then she rose and made a small tour of the room, pausing here and there over a little treasure from a bric-à-brac shop—some enamel, some odd bit of carving, a piece of glass—laughing as she told him how she had hunted it down and bargained for it. Her laugh did something to the air: a quick cascade of notes, clean and delighted, like water over stones. Trent had heard it often in these weeks, and had found himself trying, more than once, to earn it with a remark, simply because the sound of it made the room saner.

When he asked if she would play him again a favourite piece of his that he had once heard her perform at another house, she consented at once, as if music, too, were part of hospitality.

She played with a perfection that made him sit very still, the way one sits when tasting something rare and exact. The melody came clear as a ladle drawing broth: nothing spilt, nothing muddied; yet beneath the clarity was warmth and a kind of patient sorrow that seemed older than any single day. When the last tremor of the notes passed away, leaving behind that hush that is not silence but aftermath, he said quietly:

“You are a musician born. I knew that before I first heard you.”

“I have played a great deal ever since I can remember,” she said simply, half turning with a smile. “It has been a great comfort to me. When did you first detect music in me? Oh, of course: I was at the opera. But that wouldn’t prove much, would it?”

“No,” he said abstractedly, still busy with what the music had done to him. “I think I knew it the first time I saw you.”

Understanding of his own words came with a physical force, like a sudden blow in the ribs. For the first time, between them, the past had been invoked aloud.

A short silence followed. Mrs. Manderson looked at him, then hastily away. Colour rose in her cheeks. She pursed her lips as if for whistling—an odd little gesture, defiant and childlike—and then, with a quick lift of the shoulders that he remembered, she rose from the piano and sat down opposite him.

“That speech of yours will do as well as anything,” she began slowly, looking down at the point of her shoe, “to bring us to what I wanted to say. I asked you here today on purpose, Mr. Trent, because I couldn’t bear it any longer. Ever since the day you left me at White Gables I have been telling myself it didn’t matter what you thought of me in that affair; that you were certainly not the kind of man to speak to others of what you believed about me, after what you told me of your reasons for suppressing your manuscript. I asked myself how it could matter. But all the time I knew it did matter. It mattered horribly. Because what you thought was not true.”

She raised her eyes and met his gaze steadily. Trent, whose face had gone blank as paper, returned her look.

“Since I began to know you,” he said, “I have ceased to think it.”

“Thank you,” she said; and blushed suddenly and deeply, as if gratitude itself were a thing she had not expected to taste. Then, playing with a glove, she added, “But I want you to know what was true.

“I did not know whether I should ever see you again,” she went on, her voice lower, “but I felt that if I did I must speak to you about this. I thought it would not be hard, because you seemed to me an understanding person; and besides, a woman who has been married isn’t expected to have the same sort of difficulty as a young girl in speaking of such things when it is necessary. And then we did meet again, and I discovered it was very difficult indeed. You made it difficult.”

“How?” he asked quietly.

“I don’t know,” she said; and then, as if annoyed at herself for saying it, “But yes—I do know. It was because you treated me exactly as if you had never imagined anything of that sort. I had supposed that if I saw you again you would turn on me that hard, horrible sort of look you had when you asked me that last question—do you remember?—at White Gables. Instead you were just like any other acquaintance. You were just—” she hesitated and spread her hands—“nice. You know. After the opera, when I spoke to you, I went home wondering if you had really recognised me. I thought you might have known my face without remembering who I was.”

A short laugh broke from Trent in spite of himself; he did not speak.

She smiled as if apologising for her own frankness. “Well, I couldn’t remember whether you had spoken my name; and I thought it might be so. But the next time, at the Iretons’, you did speak it, so I knew. And a dozen times during those days I almost told you, but never quite. I began to feel you wouldn’t let me—that you would slip away from the subject if I approached it. Wasn’t I right? Tell me, please.”

He nodded.

“But why?”

He remained silent.

“Well,” she said, “I will finish what I had to say, and then you will tell me, I hope, why you made it so hard. When I understood you wouldn’t let me speak of it, it made me more determined. I suppose you didn’t realise that I would insist on speaking even if you discouraged me. I dare say I couldn’t have done it if I had been guilty, as you thought. You walked into my parlour today never thinking I should dare. Well—now you see.”

All hesitation had left her. She had talked herself into courage, and the very act of forcing words through that long-held barrier gave her a kind of mastery.

“I am going to tell you the story of the mistake you made,” she continued, while Trent sat with his hands clasped between his knees, watching her with an expression that hid more than it showed. “You will have to believe it, Mr. Trent; it is utterly true to life, with its confusions and hidden things and cross-purposes and perfectly natural mistakes that people never think twice about taking for facts. Please understand I don’t blame you in the least, and never did, for jumping to the conclusion you did. You knew I was estranged from my husband, and you knew what that so often means. You knew, I expect, before I told you, that he had taken up an injured attitude towards me; and I was silly enough to try and explain it away. I gave you the explanation I gave myself at first, before I realised the wretched truth. I told you he was disappointed in me because I couldn’t take a brilliant lead in society. Well, that was true; he was. But I could see you weren’t convinced. You guessed what took me much longer to see, because I knew how irrational it was. Yes—my husband was jealous of John Marlowe; you divined that.

“Then I behaved like a fool when you let me see you had divined it; it was such a blow, you understand, when I had supposed the humiliation and strain were at an end, and that his delusion had died with him. You practically asked me if my husband’s secretary was not my lover, Mr. Trent—I have to say it, because I want you to understand why I broke down and made a scene. You took that for a confession; you thought I was guilty of that, and I think you even thought I might be party to the crime, that I had consented.... That did hurt me. But perhaps you couldn’t have thought anything else—I don’t know.”

Trent dropped his eyes; his head bent as if the words had weight.

“But really it was only shock and distress that made me give way, and the memory of all the misery that mad suspicion had meant to me. And when I pulled myself together again you had gone.”

She rose and went to an escritoire by the window, unlocked a drawer, and drew out a long sealed envelope.

“This is the manuscript you left with me,” she said. “I have read it through again and again. I have always wondered, as everybody does, at your cleverness in things of this kind.” A faint, mischievous smile flickered and vanished. “I thought it was splendid, Mr. Trent—I almost forgot the story was my own, I was so interested. And I want to say now, while I have it in my hand, how much I thank you for your generous act in sacrificing this triumph rather than put a woman’s reputation in peril. If all had been as you supposed, the facts must have come out when the police took up the case you put into their hands. Believe me, I understood what you had done, and I never ceased to be grateful—even when I felt most crushed by your suspicion.”

Her voice shook a little as she thanked him; her eyes were bright. Trent, head still bent, seemed not to hear. She placed the envelope into his hand as it lay open on his knee; the touch was gentle enough to make him look up, as one looks up when a hand steadies a shaking cup.

“Can you—” he began slowly.

She lifted her hand. “No, Mr. Trent; let me finish before you say anything. It is such an unspeakable relief to have broken the ice at last, and I want to end the story while I am still feeling the triumph of beginning it.”

She sat down again.

“I am telling you something that nobody else knows. Everybody knew, I suppose, that something had come between us, though I did everything I could to hide it. But I don’t think anyone ever guessed what my husband’s notion was. People who know me don’t think that sort of thing about me, I believe. And his fancy was so ridiculously opposed to the facts. I will tell you what the situation was.

“Mr. Marlowe and I had been friendly enough since he came to us. For all his cleverness—my husband said he had a keener brain than any man he knew—I looked upon him as practically a boy. You know I am a little older than he is, and he had a sort of amiable lack of ambition that made me feel it the more. One day my husband asked me what I thought was the best thing about Marlowe, and, not thinking, I said, ‘His manners.’ He surprised me by looking black at that; and after a silence he said, ‘Yes, Marlowe is a gentleman; that’s so,’ not looking at me.

“Nothing was said about it again until about a year ago, when I found that Mr. Marlowe had done what I always expected—fallen desperately in love with an American girl. But, to my disgust, he picked out the most worthless girl, I do believe, of all those whom we used to meet. She was the daughter of wealthy parents, and did what she liked with them; very beautiful, well educated, very good at games—what they call a woman-athlete—and caring for nothing on earth but her own amusement. One of the most unprincipled flirts I ever knew, and quite the cleverest. Everyone knew it, and Mr. Marlowe must have heard it; but she made a complete fool of him, brain and all. I can imagine how. She liked him, of course; but it was plain to me she was playing with him. The whole affair was so idiotic that I got perfectly furious.

“One day I asked him to row me on the lake—this was at our house by Lake George. We had never been alone together for long before. In the boat I talked to him. I was very kind, I think, and he took it admirably, but he didn’t believe me in the least. He had the impudence to tell me I misunderstood Alice’s nature. When I hinted at his prospects—I knew he had scarcely anything—he said that if she loved him he could make himself a position in the world. I dare say it was true, with his abilities and his friends—he is rather well connected, as well as popular. But his enlightenment came soon after.

“My husband helped me out of the boat when we got back. He joked with Mr. Marlowe about something; for through all that followed he never once changed his manner to him, and that was one reason I took so long to realise what he thought about him and me. But to me he was reserved and silent that evening—not angry. He was always perfectly cold and expressionless to me after he took this idea into his head. After dinner he spoke to me only once. Mr. Marlowe was telling him about some horse he had bought for the farm in Kentucky, and my husband looked at me and said, ‘Marlowe may be a gentleman, but he seldom quits loser in a horse-trade.’ I was surprised; but at that time—and even on the next occasion when he found us together—I did not understand what was in his mind.

“That next time was the morning when Mr. Marlowe received a sweet little note from the girl asking for his congratulations on her engagement. It was in our New York house. He looked so wretched at breakfast that I thought he was ill, and afterwards I went to the room where he worked and asked what was the matter. He didn’t say anything, only handed me the note and turned away to the window. I was very glad it was all over, but terribly sorry for him too. I don’t remember what I said, but I remember putting my hand on his arm as he stood there staring out on the garden, and just then my husband appeared at the open door with some papers. He glanced at us and then turned and walked quietly back to his study. I thought he might have heard what I was saying to comfort Mr. Marlowe, and that it was rather nice of him to slip away. Mr. Marlowe neither saw nor heard him. My husband left the house that morning for the West while I was out. Even then I did not understand. He used often to go off suddenly like that.

“It was not until he returned a week later that I grasped the situation. He was looking white and strange, and as soon as he saw me he asked where Mr. Marlowe was. Somehow the tone of his question told me everything in a flash.

“I almost gasped; I was wild with indignation. You know, Mr. Trent, I don’t think I should have minded at all if anyone had thought me capable of openly breaking with my husband and leaving him for somebody else. I dare say I might have done that. But that coarse suspicion—a man whom he trusted—and the notion of concealment. It made me see scarlet. Every shred of pride in me was strung up till I quivered, and I swore to myself on the spot that I would never show by word or sign that I was conscious of his having such a thought about me. I would behave exactly as I always had behaved, I determined—and that I did, up to the very last. Though I knew a wall had been made between us that could never be broken down—even if he asked my pardon and obtained it—I never once showed that I noticed any change.

“And so it went on. I never could go through such a time again. My husband showed silent, cold politeness to me always when we were alone—and that was only when unavoidable. He never alluded to what was in his mind; but I felt it, and he knew I felt it. Both of us were stubborn in our different attitudes. To Mr. Marlowe he was more friendly, if anything, than before—Heaven knows why. I fancied he was planning some revenge; but that was only fancy. Certainly Mr. Marlowe never knew what was suspected of him. He and I remained good friends, though we never spoke of anything intimate after that disappointment; but I made a point of seeing no less of him than I had always done. Then we came to England, and to White Gables, and after that followed—my husband’s dreadful end.”

She threw out her right hand, as if to close a book.

“You know the rest—so much more than any other man,” she added, and glanced at him with a quaint expression that came and went like a change of light.

Trent noticed it; but the notice was only a shadow crossing his mind. Inwardly he was possessed by a thankfulness so great it was almost pain. Long before she finished he had recognised the certainty of her truth, the way one recognises, after weeks of being fed on doubtful fare, the clean taste of plain bread. His face had altered; all the deadness had gone out of it.

“I don’t know how to begin the apologies I have to make,” he said. “There are no words to tell you how ashamed I feel when I realise what a crude, cock-sure blundering at a conclusion my suspicion was. Yes—I suspected you. I had almost forgotten I was ever such a fool. Almost—not quite. Sometimes when I’ve been alone I’ve remembered it and poured contempt on it. I’ve tried to imagine what the facts were. I’ve tried to excuse myself.”

“What nonsense!” she broke in quickly. “Do be sensible, Mr. Trent. You had only seen me on two occasions in your life before you came to me with your solution of the mystery.” The quaint look flickered again. “If you talk of folly, it really is folly for a man like you to pretend to a woman like me that I had innocence written all over me in large letters—so large you couldn’t believe very strong evidence against me after seeing me twice.”

“What do you mean by ‘a man like me’?” he demanded, with sudden fierceness. “Do you take me for a person without normal instincts? I don’t say you impress people as a simple, transparent character—what Mr. Calvin Bunner calls a case of open-work; I don’t say a stranger might not think you capable of wickedness if there was good evidence for it. But I say that a man who, after seeing you and being in your atmosphere, could associate you with the particular kind of abomination I imagined, is a fool—the kind of fool who is afraid to trust his senses.... As for my making it hard for you to approach the subject—it’s true. It was simply moral cowardice. I understood you wished to clear it up, and I was revolted at the notion of my injurious blunder being discussed. I tried to show you by my actions it was as if it had never been. I hoped you would pardon me without words. I can’t forgive myself, and I never shall. And yet if you could know—”

He stopped, and then added more quietly, as if ashamed of his own heat, “Well—will you accept all that as an apology? The very scrubbiest sackcloth made, and the grittiest ashes on the heap.... I didn’t mean to get worked up.”

Mrs. Manderson laughed; and the sound lifted him, the way a sudden clear interval of birdsong can make a noisy street tolerable for a moment.

“But I love to see you worked up,” she said. “The bump with which you always come down as soon as you realise you are up in the air at all is delightful. Oh—we’re actually both laughing. What a triumphant end to our explanations, after all my dread of the time when I should have it out with you. And now it’s all over, and you know; and we’ll never speak of it any more.”

“I hope not,” Trent said, with sincere relief. “If you’re resolved to be so kind, I’m not high-principled enough to insist on your blasting me with your lightnings. And now, Mrs. Manderson, I had better go. Changing the subject after this would be like playing puss-in-the-corner after an earthquake.”

He rose.

“You are right,” she said. “But no—wait. There is another thing—part of the same subject; and we ought to pick up all the pieces now while we are about it. Please sit down.”

She took up the envelope containing his manuscript from the table where he had laid it, and held it as if it had weight beyond paper.

“I want to speak about this.”

His brows drew together. He looked at her steadily, feeling again that odd doubling of time: the past that had been misnamed, the present that might still be damaged by a word.

“So do I, if you do,” he said slowly. “I want very much to know one thing.”

“Tell me.”

“If you insist,” Trent said, surrendering with the air of a man being marched to the block, “I suppose you will have your way. But I had much rather write it when I am not with you. However, if I must—bring me paper whiter than a star, unblemished by your address. A clean sheet. Don’t underrate the sacrifice. I never felt less like correspondence in my life.”

She rewarded him with a look that had the warmth of a well-tended hearth.

“What shall I say?” he asked, pen poised like a fork above a first course. “Shall I compare him to a summer’s day? What shall I say?”

“Say what you want to say,” she answered, practical as a woman making a sauce that must not split.

He shook his head. “What I want to say—what I have been wanting, for the past twenty-four hours, to cry out to every man, woman, and child I met—is: ‘Mabel and I are betrothed, and all is gas and gaiters.’ But it wouldn’t serve as the opening of a letter of strictly formal—one might almost say sinister—character. I have got as far as ‘Dear Mr. Marlowe.’ What comes next?”

“I am sending you a manuscript,” she prompted, “which I thought you might like to see.”

He stared at her as if she had proposed to feed a guest on plain bread when there were spices in the cupboard. “Do you realize,” he said, “that in that sentence there are only two words of more than one syllable? This letter is meant to impress, not to put him at his ease. We must have long words.”

“I don’t see why,” she said. “I know it is usual—but why? I have had letters from lawyers and business people, and they always begin, ‘with reference to our communication,’ and go on like that to the end. Yet when I see them they don’t talk like that. It seems ridiculous.”

“It is not at all ridiculous to them.” Trent laid down the pen with the relief of a cook laying aside a knife that has been sharpening his conscience instead of his appetite, and rose. “Let me explain. A people like our own, not very fond of using its mind, gets on in ordinary life with a small, simple vocabulary. Long words are abnormal; and like everything abnormal they are either very funny or tremendously solemn. Take ‘intelligent anticipation.’ In any other European country it would slip by unnoticed. With us it becomes a proverb; we grin when we hear it in a speech or read it in a leading article; we think it’s one of the finest things ever said. Why? Because it consists of two long words. The idea itself is as commonplace as cold mutton. And there’s ‘terminological inexactitude’—how we roared, and still roar. The whole joke is that the words are long. It’s the same when we wish to be very serious; we mark it by turning to long words. When a solicitor begins a sentence with ‘pursuant to the instructions communicated to our representative’—or such gibberish—he feels he is earning his six-and-eightpence. Don’t laugh! It’s perfectly true. Now Continentals haven’t got that feeling. They are forever bothering about ideas, and the result is that every shopkeeper and peasant has a vocabulary in daily use that is simply Greek to the vast majority of Britons. I remember dining with a friend of mine who is a Paris cabman. We ate in a dirty little restaurant opposite the central post office, where all the clients were cabmen and porters. Conversation was general, and it struck me that a London cabman would have felt out of his depth. Words like ‘functionary’ and ‘unforgettable’ and ‘exterminate’ and ‘independence’ hurtled across the table every instant—ordinary, vulgar, jolly, red-faced cabmen—”

“Ah, no, no, no!” Mrs. Manderson cried. “I don’t know anything at the moment except that your talking must be stopped somehow if we are to get any further with that letter. You shall not get out of it. Come!”

She crossed the room, took up his pen as neatly as if it were an implement she meant to put to honest use, and placed it back in his hand. Trent looked at it with distaste.

“I warn you not to discourage my talking,” he said gloomily. “Believe me, men who don’t talk are even worse to live with than men who do. Have a care of natures that are mute. I confess I’m shirking writing this thing. It is almost an indecency. It mixes two moods—to write the sort of letter I mean to write, and to sit in the same room with you.”

She led him back to the chair at the escritoire and pressed him down with the gentle authority of a hostess who will not let a guest leave before he has tasted what must be tasted.

“Well—but please try. I want to see what you write, and I want it to go to him at once. You see, I would be content to leave things as they are; but you say you must get at the truth, and if you must, I want it as soon as possible. Do it now—you know you can if you will—and I’ll send it off the moment it’s ready. Don’t you ever feel that—the longing to get the worrying letter into the post and off your hands, so that you can’t recall it if you would, and it’s no use fussing any more about it?”

“I will do as you wish,” he said, and turned to the paper, dating it from his hotel.

Mrs. Manderson looked down at his bent head with a light in her eyes that was neither pity nor triumph but something steadier—like the glow under a lid when the fire has been banked for the night. She made as if to smooth his rather untidy hair. But she did not touch it. Instead she went silently to the piano and began to play very softly.

The room held itself still around that sound. It was not the brassy insistence of the street, nor the coarse clatter of modern haste, but a small sheltered world of measured vibration—notes that did not shout their meanings, because they did not need to. They were domestic, and yet in them there was the feeling of old things remembered: a language of the fingers that survived when spoken assurances failed.

Ten minutes passed before Trent spoke.

“If he chooses to reply that he will say nothing?”

Mrs. Manderson looked over her shoulder. “Of course he dare not take that line. He will speak to prevent you from denouncing him.”

“But I’m not going to do that anyhow. You wouldn’t allow it—you said so; besides, I won’t if you would. The thing’s too doubtful now.”

“But,” she laughed softly, “poor Mr. Marlowe doesn’t know you won’t, does he?”

Trent sighed, his pen going again, as though he were forced to season a dish he had no desire to eat. “What extraordinary things codes of honour are!” he remarked, half to the paper. “I know there are things I should do, and never think twice about, which would make you feel disgraced if you did them—such as giving any one who grossly insulted me a black eye, or swearing violently when I barked my shin in a dark room. And now you are calmly recommending me to bluff Marlowe by means of a tacit threat which I don’t mean; a thing which hell’s most abandoned fiend did never, in the drunkenness of guilt—well, anyhow, I won’t do it.”

He bent to his writing again. Mrs. Manderson, indulgent, returned to her quiet playing; the notes moved through the twilight like the remembered calls of birds over a field that has not yet been surrendered to engines.

In a few minutes more Trent said, “At last I am his faithfully. Do you want to see it?”

She ran across the room and turned on the reading lamp beside the desk. The sudden circle of light fell on the paper like a spotlight on a confession. Then, leaning on his shoulder, close enough for him to feel the faint perfume of her hair—something clean and intimate, like linen stored with lavender—she read:

DEAR MR. MARLOWE,—You will perhaps remember that we met, under unhappy circumstances, in June of last year at Marlstone.

On that occasion it was my duty, as representing a newspaper, to make an independent investigation of the circumstances of the death of the late Sigsbee Manderson. I did so, and I arrived at certain conclusions. You may learn from the enclosed manuscript, which was originally written as a dispatch for my newspaper, what those conclusions were. For reasons which it is not necessary to state I decided at the last moment not to make them public, or to communicate them to you, and they are known to only two persons beside myself.

At that phrase her eyes lifted sharply from the page; her brows drew together.

“Two persons?” she said, and there was a note in it—small, but distinct—as when a cook tastes a broth and hears, in the mouth, one wrong note.

“Your uncle is the other,” Trent replied. “I sought him out last night and told him the whole story. Have you anything against it? I always felt uneasy at keeping it from him, because I had led him to expect I should tell him all I discovered, and my silence looked like mystery-making. Now it is to be cleared up finally, and there is no question of shielding you, I wanted him to know everything. He is a very shrewd adviser, too, in a way of his own; and I should like to have him with me when I see Marlowe. I have a feeling that two heads will be better than one on my side of the interview.”

She sighed, and the sigh seemed to make a hollow place in the music behind them. “Yes, of course, uncle ought to know the truth. I hope there is nobody else at all.” She pressed his hand. “I so much want all that horror buried—buried deep. I am very happy now, dear, but I shall be happier still when you have satisfied that curious mind of yours and found out everything, and stamped down the earth upon it all.”

She read on:

Quite recently, however, facts have come to my knowledge which have led me to change my decision. I do not mean that I shall publish what I discovered, but that I have determined to approach you and ask you for a private statement. If you have anything to say which would place the matter in another light, I can imagine no reason why you should withhold it.

I expect, then, to hear from you when and where I may call upon you; unless you prefer the interview to take place at my hotel. In either case I desire that Mr. Cupples whom you will remember, and who has read the enclosed document, should be present also.—Faithfully yours,

Philip Trent.

“What a very stiff letter!” she said when she had finished. “Now I am sure you couldn’t have made it any stiffer in your own rooms.”

Trent slipped the letter and its enclosure into a long envelope with the careful fingers of a man sealing up a smell he dislikes but cannot deny. “Yes,” he said, “I think it will make him sit up suddenly. Now this thing mustn’t run any risk of going wrong. It would be best to send a special messenger with orders to deliver it into his own hands. If he’s away it oughtn’t to be left.”

She nodded. “I can arrange that. Wait here for a little.”

When she returned, he had risen and was hunting through the music cabinet, as though the room’s stored voices might offer a safer language than ink.

She sank down beside him on the carpet, a wave of dark brown skirts pooling about her, and looked up.

“Tell me something, Philip,” she said.

“If it is among the few things that I know.”

“When you saw uncle last night, did you tell him about—about us?”

“I did not,” he answered. “I remembered you had said nothing about telling any one. It is for you—isn’t it?—to decide whether we take the world into our confidence at once or later on.”

“Then will you tell him?” Her eyes dropped to her clasped hands. “I wish you to tell him. Perhaps if you think you will guess why.... There! that is settled.”

She lifted her gaze again, and for a time there was no speech between them—only the faint after-sound of the piano in the room’s corners, and beneath that the quiet, human rustle of a house that still kept its own old dialect of creaks and breaths.

At last Trent leaned back in the deep chair.

“What a world!” he said. “Mabel, will you play something on the piano that expresses mere joy—the genuine article, nothing feverish or like thorns under a pot—but joy that has decided in favour of the universe? It’s a mood that can’t last altogether, so we had better get all we can out of it.”

She went to the instrument and struck a few chords while she thought, testing them like ingredients between finger and tongue—this one too sharp, that one too heavy. Then she began to work with all her soul at the theme in the last movement of the Ninth Symphony, which is like the sound of the opening of the gates of Paradise.

The room looked down upon St. James’s Park from an upper window, and the light that came off the winter grass and the pale water seemed to cool the air inside. An old oaken desk, deep-bodied and obstinate as a farmhouse chest, stood by that window; the rest of the furniture had been chosen with a man’s educated taste, and yet the whole place bore the stamp of solitary habits—things set where they were convenient rather than where they were kind.

John Marlowe went to the desk, unlocked it with the quick certainty of a person who has opened it often in thought if not in fact, and drew from the back of the well a long, stout envelope. The crackle of the paper in the quiet room sounded loud, like the breaking of a seal in a pantry at night.

“I understand,” he said to Mr. Cupples, “that you have read this.”

“I read it for the first time two days ago,” Mr. Cupples returned from his seat on the sofa, peering benignly about him as though taking the measure of a stranger’s household. “We have discussed it fully.”

Marlowe laid the envelope on the table before Trent. “There is your manuscript. I have gone over it three times. I do not believe there is another man who could have got at as much of the truth as you have set down there.”

Trent neither warmed nor softened at praise. He sat with his long legs twisted beneath him, staring into the fire as if it were a hearth in some other house where he had once been hungry and was not yet fed. He drew the envelope towards him with the flat of his hand.

“You mean, of course,” he said, “that there is more of the truth to be disclosed now. We are ready to hear you as soon as you like. I expect it will be a long story, and the longer the better, so far as I am concerned; I want to understand thoroughly. What we should both like, I think, is some preliminary account of Manderson and your relations with him. It seemed to me from the first that the character of the dead man must be somehow an element in the business.”

“You were right,” Marlowe said; and there was something in the curt grimness of it like a knife set down on a board. He crossed and took his seat on the tall cushion-topped fender, leaning forward so that the firelight took his face.

“I ought to tell you beforehand,” Trent went on, looking him straight between the eyes, “that although I am here to listen to you, I have not as yet any reason to doubt the conclusions I have stated here.” He tapped the envelope. “It is a defence that you will be putting forward—you understand that?”

“Perfectly.”

He was cool; and the coolness was not bravado but control. The nervous, worn creature of Marlstone was gone. This Marlowe held himself like a man accustomed to bodily exactness—the clean habit of a rider, of someone who knows the weight of a thing in his hands and what a slip will cost. His brow looked candid, his blue eyes clear; only, when he paused to marshal the order of his thoughts, they had again that troubling, inwardly calculating steadiness Trent had noticed at their first meeting—like a player who can watch the whole board and still hear the clock.

“Sigsbee Manderson was not a man of normal mind,” Marlowe began quietly. “Most of the very rich men I met with in America had become so by virtue of abnormal greed, or abnormal industry, or abnormal personal force, or abnormal luck. None of them had remarkable intellects. Manderson delighted too in heaping up wealth; he worked incessantly at it; he was a man of dominant will; he had quite his share of luck; but what made him singular was his brainpower. In his own country they would perhaps tell you that it was his ruthlessness in pursuit of his aims that was his most striking characteristic; but there are hundreds of them who would have carried out his plans with just as little consideration for others if they could have formed the plans.

“I’m not saying Americans aren’t clever; they are ten times cleverer than we are, as a nation; but I never met another who showed such a degree of sagacity and foresight, such gifts of memory and mental tenacity, such sheer force of intelligence, as there was behind everything Manderson did in his money-making career. They called him the ‘Napoleon of Wall Street’ often enough in the papers; but few people knew so well as I did how much truth there was in the phrase. He seemed never to forget a fact that might be of use to him, in the first place; and he did systematically with the business facts that concerned him what Napoleon did, as I have read, with military facts. He studied them in special digests prepared for him at short intervals, which he always had at hand, so that he could take up his report on coal or wheat or railways—whatever it might be—in any unoccupied moment. Then he could make a bolder and cleverer plan than any man of them all.

“People got to know that Manderson would never do the obvious thing, but they got no further; the thing he did do was almost always a surprise, and much of his success flowed from that. The Street got rattled, as they used to put it, when it was known that the old man was out with his gun, and often his opponents seemed to surrender as easily as Colonel Crockett’s coon in the story. The scheme I am going to describe to you would have occupied most men long enough. Manderson could have plotted the thing, down to the last detail, while he shaved himself.”

The fire hissed softly as a coal fell in; beyond the glass the park lay in its winter hush, the distant roll of London traffic like surf held back by masonry. Trent, who heard everything, thought how seldom in town one truly heard silence at all; it was always bought with height and money.

“I used to think,” Marlowe went on, “that his strain of Indian blood, remote as it was, might have something to do with the cunning and ruthlessness of the man. Strangely enough, its existence was unknown to any one but himself and me. It was when he asked me to apply my taste for genealogical work to his own obscure family history that I made the discovery that he had in him a share of the blood of the Iroquois chief Montour and his French wife—a terrible woman who ruled the savage politics of the tribes of the Wilderness two hundred years ago. The Mandersons were active in the fur trade on the Pennsylvanian border in those days, and more than one of them married Indian women. Other Indian blood than Montour’s may have descended to Manderson, for all I can say, through previous and subsequent unions; some of the wives’ antecedents were quite untraceable, and there were so many generations of pioneering before the whole country was brought under civilization.

“My researches left me with the idea that there is a very great deal of the aboriginal blood present in the genealogical make-up of the people of America, and that it is very widely spread. The newer families have constantly intermarried with the older, and so many of them had a strain of the native in them—and were often rather proud of it, too, in those days. But Manderson had the idea about the disgracefulness of mixed blood, which grew much stronger, I fancy, with the rise of the negro question after the war. He was thunderstruck at what I told him, and was anxious to conceal it from every soul. Of course I never gave it away while he lived, and I don’t think he supposed I would; but I have thought since that his mind took a turn against me from that time onward. It happened about a year before his death.”

“Had Manderson,” Mr. Cupples asked suddenly, so that both the other men started, “any definable religious attitude?”

Marlowe considered, as though searching a drawer that had always come up empty. “None that ever I heard of. Worship and prayer were quite unknown to him, so far as I could see, and I never heard him mention religion. I should doubt if he had any real sense of God at all, or if he was capable of knowing God through the emotions. But I understood that as a child he had had a religious upbringing with a strong moral side to it. His private life was, in the usual limited sense, blameless. He was almost ascetic in his habits, except as to smoking.

“I lived with him four years without ever knowing him to tell a direct verbal falsehood, constantly as he used to practise deceit in other forms. Can you understand the soul of a man who never hesitated to take steps that would have the effect of hoodwinking people, who would use every trick of the markets to mislead, and who was at the same time scrupulous never to utter a direct lie on the most insignificant matter? Manderson was like that, and he was not the only one. I suppose you might compare the state of mind to that of a soldier who is personally a truthful man, but who will stick at nothing to deceive the enemy. The rules of the game allow it; and the same may be said of business as many business men regard it. Only with them it is always wartime.”

“It is a sad world,” Mr. Cupples observed, as if marking a footnote.

“As you say,” Marlowe agreed. “Now I was saying that one could always take Manderson’s word if he gave it in a definite form. The first time I ever heard him utter a downright lie was on the night he died; and hearing it, I believe, saved me from being hanged as his murderer.”

For a second the faint ticking on the mantelpiece seemed to sharpen. Marlowe stared at the light above his head, as if the truth were written there and he must read it out letter by letter. Trent moved restlessly.

“Before we come to that,” he said, “will you tell us exactly on what footing you were with Manderson during the years you were with him?”

“We were on very good terms from beginning to end,” Marlowe replied. “Nothing like friendship—he was not a man for making friends—but the best of terms as between a trusted employee and his chief. I went to him as private secretary just after getting my degree at Oxford. I was to have gone into my father’s business, where I am now, but my father suggested that I should see the world for a year or two. So I took this secretaryship, which seemed to promise a good deal of varied experience, and I had let the year or two run on to four years before the end came. The offer came to me through the last thing in the world I should have put forward as a qualification for a salaried post, and that was chess.”

At the word Trent struck his hands together with a smothered exclamation and half rose, leaning forward as if he had caught a scent.

“Chess!” he repeated. “Do you know what was the first thing I noted about you at our first meeting? It was your eye, Mr. Marlowe. I couldn’t place it then, but I know now where I had seen your eyes before. They were in the head of no less a man than the great Nikolay Korchagin, with whom I once sat in the same railway carriage for two days. I thought I should never forget the chess eye after that, but I could not put a name to it when I saw it in you. I beg your pardon.” And he subsided again into his rigid stillness by the table.

“I have played the game from my childhood, and with good players,” said Marlowe, unaffected. “It is an hereditary gift, if you can call it a gift. At the University I was nearly as good as anybody there, and I gave most of my brains to that and the O.U.D.S. and playing about generally. At Oxford, as I dare say you know, inducements to amuse oneself at the expense of one’s education are endless, and encouraged by the authorities.

“One day toward the end of my last term Dr. Munro of Queen’s—whom I had never defeated—sent for me. He told me that I played a fairish game of chess. I said it was very good of him to say so. Then he said, ‘They tell me you hunt, too.’ I said, ‘Now and then.’ He asked, ‘Is there anything else you can do?’ ‘No,’ I said; not much liking the tone of the conversation. He grunted, and then told me enquiries were being made on behalf of a wealthy American man of business who wanted an English secretary. Manderson was the name. He seemed never to have heard it before—which was quite possible, as he never opened a newspaper and had not slept a night outside the college for thirty years. If I could rub up my spelling—as the old gentleman put it—I might have a good chance for the post, as chess and riding and an Oxford education were the only indispensable points.

“So I became Manderson’s secretary. For a long time I liked the position greatly. When one is attached to an active American plutocrat in the prime of life one need not have many dull moments. Besides, it made me independent. My father had some serious business reverses about that time, and I was glad to be able to do without an allowance from him.

“At the end of the first year Manderson doubled my salary. ‘It’s big money,’ he said, ‘but I guess I don’t lose.’ By that time I was doing a great deal more than accompany him on horseback in the morning and play chess in the evening, which was mainly what he had required. I was attending to his houses, his farm in Ohio, his shooting in Maine, his horses, his cars, and his yacht. I had become a walking railway-guide and an expert cigar-buyer. I was always learning something.”

As he spoke, the room seemed to thicken with things named and possessed—properties and schedules, keys and timetables, locked drawers and ledgers; the kind of life where a man is fed not by the land but by paper, and where the soundscape is not birds and wind but telephones, engines, and the unceasing rustle of transactions.

“Well, now you understand what my position was in regard to Manderson during the last two or three years of my connection with him. It was a happy life for me on the whole. I was busy, my work was varied and interesting; I had time to amuse myself too, and money to spend. At one time I made a fool of myself about a girl, and that was not a happy time; but it taught me to understand the great goodness of Mrs. Manderson.” He inclined his head slightly towards Mr. Cupples. “She may choose to tell you about it.

“As for her husband, he had never varied in his attitude towards me, in spite of the change that came over him in the last months of his life, as you know. He treated me well and generously in his unsympathetic way, and I never had a feeling that he was less than satisfied with his bargain. And it was that continuance of his attitude right up to the end that made the revelation so shocking when I was suddenly shown, on the night on which he met his end, the depth of crazy hatred of myself that was in Manderson’s soul.”

The eyes of Trent and Mr. Cupples met for an instant, a quick exchange of caution and impatience.

“You never suspected that he hated you before that time?” Trent asked; and Mr. Cupples, almost in the same breath, “To what did you attribute it?”

“I never guessed until that night,” Marlowe answered. “How long it had existed I do not know. I cannot imagine why it was there. I was forced to think, when I considered the thing in those awful days after his death, that it was a case of a madman’s delusion, that he believed me to be plotting against him, as they so often do. Some such insane conviction must have been at the root of it. But who can sound the abysses of a lunatic’s fancy? Can you imagine the state of mind in which a man dooms himself to death with the object of delivering some one he hates to the hangman?”

Mr. Cupples moved sharply. “You say Manderson was responsible for his own death?”

Trent glanced at him with faint irritation and kept his eyes on Marlowe’s face, as if watching a cook’s hands for the moment the knife slips.

“I do say so,” Marlowe replied concisely.

Mr. Cupples nodded, as though accepting a theorem for later proof. “Before we proceed to the elucidation of your statement, it may be remarked that the state of mind which you attribute to Manderson—”

“Suppose we have the story first,” Trent broke in, and laid a gentle hand on the old man’s arm. “You were telling us how things stood between you and Manderson. Now will you tell us the facts of what happened that night?”

A flush rose in Marlowe’s cheek at the emphasis on “facts.” He drew himself up with a pride that had been trained to take orders and yet resented the implication of embroidery.

“Bunner and myself dined with Mr. and Mrs. Manderson that Sunday evening,” he began, carefully exact. “It was just like other dinners at which the four of us had been together. Manderson was taciturn and gloomy, as we had latterly been accustomed to see him. We others kept a conversation going. We rose from the table, I suppose, about nine. Mrs. Manderson went to the drawing-room, and Bunner went up to the hotel to see an acquaintance.

“Manderson asked me to come into the orchard behind the house, saying he wished to have a talk. We paced up and down the pathway there, out of earshot from the house, and Manderson, as he smoked his cigar, spoke to me in his cool, deliberate way. He had never seemed more sane, or more well-disposed to me. He said he wanted me to do him an important service. There was a big thing on. It was a secret affair. Bunner knew nothing of it, and the less I knew the better. He wanted me to do exactly as he directed, and not bother my head about reasons.

“This was quite characteristic of Manderson’s method. If at times he required a man to be a mere tool in his hand, he would tell him so. He had used me in the same kind of way a dozen times. I assured him he could rely on me. ‘Right now?’ he asked. I said of course.

“He nodded. ‘Attend to this. There is a man in England now who is in this thing with me. He was to have left tomorrow for Paris by the noon boat from Southampton to Havre. His name is George Harris—at least that’s the name he is going by. Do you remember that name?’ I said that I did. A week ago, when I went up to London, he had told me to book a cabin in that name on the boat that goes tomorrow. I had given him the ticket. ‘Here it is,’ he said, and produced it.

“‘Now,’ he said—poking his cigar-butt towards me with each sentence in a way he had—‘George Harris cannot leave England tomorrow. I find I shall want him where he is. And I want Bunner where he is. But somebody has got to go by that boat and take certain papers to Paris. Or else my plan is going to fall to pieces. Will you go?’ I said certainly. I was there to obey orders.

“He bit his cigar. ‘That’s all right; but these are not just ordinary orders. Not the kind of thing one can ask of a man in the ordinary way of his duty to an employer. The point is this. The deal I am busy with is one in which neither myself nor any one known to be connected with me must appear as yet. That is vital. But these people I am up against know your face as well as they know mine. If my secretary is known in certain quarters to have crossed to Paris at this time and to have interviewed certain people— and that would be known as soon as it happened—then the game is up.’ He threw away his cigar-end and looked at me.

“I didn’t like it much, but I liked failing Manderson at a pinch still less. I spoke lightly. I said I supposed I should have to conceal my identity, and I would do my best. I told him I used to be pretty good at make-up.

“He nodded approval. ‘That’s good. I judged you would not let me down.’ Then he gave me my instructions.

“‘You take the car right now and start for Southampton—there’s no train that will fit in. You’ll be driving all night. Barring accidents, you ought to get there by six in the morning. But whenever you arrive, drive straight to the Bedford Hotel and ask for George Harris. If he’s there, tell him you are to go over instead of him, and ask him to telephone me here. It is very important he should know that at the earliest moment possible. But if he isn’t there, that means he has got the instructions I wired today, and hasn’t gone to Southampton. In that case you don’t want to trouble about him any more, but just wait for the boat. You can leave the car at a garage under a fancy name—mine must not be given. See about changing your appearance—I don’t care how, so you do it well. Travel by the boat as George Harris. Let on to be anything you like, but be careful, and don’t talk much to anybody. When you arrive, take a room at the Hotel St Petersbourg. You will receive a note or message there, addressed to George Harris, telling you where to take the wallet I shall give you. The wallet is locked, and you want to take good care of it. Have you got that all clear?’

“I repeated the instructions. I asked if I should return from Paris after handing over the wallet. ‘As soon as you like,’ he said. ‘And mind this—whatever happens, don’t communicate with me at any stage of the journey. If you don’t get the message in Paris at once, just wait until you do—days, if necessary. But not a line of any sort to me. Understand? Now get ready as quick as you can. I’ll go with you in the car a little way. Hurry.’

“That is, as far as I can remember, the exact substance of what Manderson said to me that night. I went to my room, changed into day clothes, and hastily threw a few necessaries into a kit-bag. My mind was in a whirl, not so much at the nature of the business as at the suddenness of it. I think I remember telling you the last time we met”—he glanced at Trent—“that Manderson shared the national fondness for doing things in a story-book style. Other things being equal, he delighted in a bit of mystification and melodrama. I told myself this was Manderson all over.

“I hurried downstairs with my bag and rejoined him in the library. He handed me a stout leather letter-case, about eight inches by six, fastened with a strap with a lock on it. I could just squeeze it into my side-pocket. Then I went to get the car from the garage behind the house.

“As I was bringing it round to the front a disconcerting thought struck me. I remembered I had only a few shillings in my pocket.”

Trent’s eyes narrowed; he listened as if the very clink of coin mattered.

“For some time past,” Marlowe continued, “I had been keeping myself very short of cash, and for this reason—which I tell you because it is a vital point, as you shall see in a minute. I was living temporarily on borrowed money. I had always been careless about money while I was with Manderson, and being a gregarious animal I had made many friends, some of them belonging to a New York set that had little to do but get rid of the large incomes given them by their parents. Still, I was very well paid, and I was too busy even to attempt to go very far with them in that occupation. I was still well on the right side of the ledger until I began, merely out of curiosity, to play at speculation. It’s a very old story—particularly in Wall Street. I thought it was easy; I was lucky at first; I would always be prudent—and so on. Then came the day when I went out of my depth. In one week I was separated from my toll, as Bunner expressed it when I told him; and I owed money too.

“I had had my lesson. In this pass I went to Manderson and told him what I had done and how I stood. He heard me with a very grim smile, and then, with the nearest approach to sympathy I had ever found in him, he advanced me a sum on account of my salary that would clear me. ‘Don’t play the markets any more,’ was all he said.

“Now on that Sunday night Manderson knew that I was practically without any money in the world. He knew that Bunner knew it too. He may have known that I had even borrowed a little more from Bunner for pocket-money until my next cheque was due, which, owing to my anticipation of my salary, would not have been a large one. Bear this knowledge of Manderson’s in mind.

“As soon as I had brought the car round I went into the library and stated the difficulty to Manderson.”

Marlowe’s voice tightened, and in the tightening Trent heard the click of a trap setting.

“What followed gave me, slight as it was, my first impression of something odd being afoot. As soon as I mentioned the word ‘expenses’ his hand went mechanically to his left hip-pocket, where he always kept a little case containing notes to the value of about a hundred pounds in our money. This was such a rooted habit in him that I was astonished to see him check the movement suddenly. Then, to my greater amazement, he swore under his breath. I had never heard him do this before; but Bunner had told me that of late he had often shown irritation in this way when they were alone. ‘Has he mislaid his note-case?’ was the question that flashed through my mind.

“But it seemed to me that it could not affect his plan at all, and I will tell you why. The week before, when I had gone up to London to carry out various commissions, including the booking of a berth for Mr. George Harris, I had drawn a thousand pounds for Manderson from his bankers, and all, at his request, in notes of small amounts. I did not know what this unusually large sum in cash was for, but I did know that the packets of notes were in his locked desk in the library—or had been earlier in the day, when I had seen him fingering them as he sat at the desk.”

“What was that you said about our having an appointment at half-past seven?” Mr. Cupples asked, as they came out under the great gateway of the flats and into the draught of the street. “Have we such an appointment?”

“We have,” said Trent. The evening seemed to lift him by the shoulders; he walked as if the stones gave back a spring. “You are dining with me. One thing, and only one thing, will do to mark a day like this—a dinner for which I pay. No, no; I asked you first. I have been hunting in circles for more than a year—circles within circles—and I have come at last to the very bottom of a case that has been gnawing even at me. That deserves a feast, and I mean a feast. Not my club. To be seen in a London club feeling happiness is to invite professional ruin; besides, the club cooks have mastered the art of making every dish taste of the same tired gravy. The eternal club dinner has bored generations and will go on boring them. Tonight we will eat somewhere that still remembers what food is for. We’ll go to Sheppard’s.”

“Who is Sheppard?” Mr. Cupples asked, mildly, as they turned up Victoria Street with its steady hiss of wheels and the hard clatter of hoofs—London’s unceasing iron music, which makes even a man’s thoughts march in boots.

“Who is Sheppard?” Trent echoed, with mock severity. “That question, if you’ll pardon me, is the very banner of this modern age—before a man will take a mouthful, he must demand a biography of the cook. I refuse to pander to the vice. Sheppard’s is a place where you can dine. I do not know Sheppard. It has never occurred to me that Sheppard exists. He may be a domestic deity invented to soothe the hungry, a myth that lodges in the panelling and answers when you knock. All I know is that at Sheppard’s you can get a slice of saddle of mutton so cleanly roasted that it has made more than one American curse Columbus for discovering anything at all. Taxi!”

A cab rolled up as smoothly as if it had been listening for his voice. Trent climbed in, lit a cigarette with hands that would not be steady, and went on as if the words were steam escaping.

“There is another reason,” he said, and the match flare showed his grin, boyish and shameless. “I am going to be married to the most wonderful woman in the world. I trust the chain of thought is clear.”

“You are going to marry Mabel!” Mr. Cupples cried. It was one of the rare moments when his quiet face broke open and you saw the warmth behind it. “My dear friend—shake hands. This is glorious. I congratulate you both with all my heart. And—if you will allow me—how earnestly I have hoped for it. Mabel has had her share of unhappiness, yet she is a woman meant to be a steadying influence in a good man’s life. I did not know her mind with certainty in regard to yourself. Your mind I have known for some time,” he added, with a twinkle that made him look younger. “I saw it at once when you were dining at my house, and you sat listening to Professor Peppmuller while you looked at her. Some of us older fellows have our wits about us still.”

“Mabel says she knew before that,” Trent returned, a trifle dashed. “And I thought I was performing indifference like an actor. I was never any good at dissembling. I shouldn’t wonder if old Peppmuller saw it through those double convex lenses of his. But if I was a fool as an undeclared suitor,”—his voice rose again into gaiety—“I shall be worse now. Thank you for the congratulations. I know you mean them. You’re the sort of uncomfortable honest man who would pull a face a yard long if you thought we were making a mistake. By the way, I’m obliged to talk nonsense tonight; it’s not a vice, it’s a necessity. Perhaps it would help if I sang you one of your old favourites. What was that song you used to be singing? Like this, wasn’t it?”

And before Mr. Cupples could stop him, Trent beat a small, ridiculous clog-step on the cab floor and chanted with scandalous cheerfulness a stave about an old negro and a wooden leg and tobacco-boxes.

“But you’re not singing,” Trent ended. “I thought you’d make the welkin ring.”

“I never sang that song in my life,” Mr. Cupples protested. “I never heard it before.”

“Are you sure?” Trent said, peering at him as if the question were an experiment. “Well, I take your word. It’s a beautiful song, anyhow. It seems exactly to express my feelings—as nothing else could; it rises unbidden to the lips. Out of the fullness of the heart the mouth speaketh, as the Bishop of Bath and Wells said when listening to one of Mr. Balfour’s speeches.”

“When was that?” Mr. Cupples asked.

“On the occasion,” Trent replied, “of the introduction of the Compulsory Notification of Diseases of Poultry Bill—an ill-fated measure which you of course remember. Hullo! We’re there already.”

The cab had swung out of a side street into a broad thoroughfare thick with footfalls and talk. They drew up. Trent paid, and led Mr. Cupples into a long panelled room where the air was softened by warmth and layered with the scents of roasted meat, browning fat, and the faint ghost of vinegar from pickles laid on earlier tables. The noise here was different—talk in rounded murmurs, cutlery ringing like small bells, the deep, comfort-giving crackle of a great fire; a human soundscape, not the street’s metallic grind.

“This,” said Trent, as if blessing the place, “is the house where cravings are fulfilled. This is the bower with the roses round it.” He glanced about with the proprietary eye of a man who has eaten happily in a room and therefore believes he owns it in some moral sense. “Three bookmakers are eating pork at my favourite table. We will have that one in the opposite corner.”

He conferred in low urgency with a waiter. Mr. Cupples stood by the fire, letting the heat loosen him; he looked as if the day’s strain were thawing out of his bones.

“The wine here,” Trent resumed as they sat down, “is almost certainly made out of grapes. What shall we drink?”

Mr. Cupples came back from his reverie. “I think,” he said, “I will have milk and soda water.”

“Speak lower,” Trent begged. “The head-waiter has a weak heart; he might hear you. Milk and soda water! Cupples, this habit of mixing drinks has been the death of many a more robust man than you. Fill high the bowl with Samian wine; leave soda to the Turkish hordes. Here comes our food.”

Plates appeared as if summoned by the very smell—slices of mutton cut from the saddle, the surface browned and faintly crisp where the heat had kissed it, the interior still moist; potatoes steaming, their skins split; something green laid bright beside the meat; and gravy poured with a practised hand so it ran glossy and dark, carrying the savour of bones long coaxed. The waiter darted away, and Trent, plainly known, watched him go with satisfaction.

“I have sent,” Trent said, “for wine that I know, and I hope you will try it. If you have taken a vow, drink water like a saint, but do not seek a cheap notoriety by demanding milk and soda.”

“I have never taken any pledge,” Mr. Cupples replied, examining his mutton with a favourable eye and then cutting it, as always, into very small pieces, as if reducing all life to manageable portions. “I simply don’t care about wine. I bought a bottle once and drank it to see what it was like, and it made me ill. No doubt it was bad. But I will taste yours—it is your dinner—and I should like, on this occasion, to do something unusual to show how I feel. I have not been so delighted for many years. To think,” he added, as the wine was poured and caught the firelight in his glass, “of the Manderson mystery disposed of, the innocent cleared, and your own and Mabel’s happiness crowned—all at once. I drink to you, my dear friend.”

He took a very small sip.

“You have a great nature,” Trent said, oddly moved. “Your outward semblance belies your soul’s immensity. I should as soon expect to see an elephant conducting at the opera as you drinking my health. Dear Cupples! May his beak retain ever that delicate rose-stain!—No, curse it.” He caught the faint look of discomfort on Mr. Cupples’s face as he tasted again. “I have no business to meddle with your tastes. I apologize. You shall have what you want, even if it kills the head-waiter with shock.”

When Mr. Cupples had been supplied with his monastic drink and the waiter had retreated, Trent leaned forward, knife poised, appetite like a physical joy.

“In this babble,” he said, “we can speak as freely as on a bare hillside. The waiter is whispering soft nothings to the young woman at the pay-desk. We are alone. What do you think of that interview this afternoon?”

Without pausing from his careful cutting, Mr. Cupples answered: “The most curious feature, in my judgement, was the irony of it. We both held the clue to that mad hatred of Manderson’s which Marlowe found so mysterious. We knew of his jealous obsession; and we withheld it, very properly, in consideration of Mabel’s feelings. Marlowe will never know what he was suspected of by that man. Strange—nearly all of us, I suppose, move unknowingly among a net of opinions, often entirely erroneous, which other people entertain about us. I remember discovering, quite by accident, that a number of acquaintances believed me to have been secretly received into the Church of Rome. The absurd fiction rested on the fact—conclusive to them—that I had expressed myself in favour of a weekly abstinence from meat. Manderson’s belief about his secretary probably rested on slighter grounds still. You said it was Mr. Bunner who told you of his hereditary temper of suspicious jealousy. As for Marlowe’s story, it seemed to me entirely straightforward, and in its essentials not especially remarkable—once we admit, as we must, that in Manderson we were dealing with a more or less disordered mind.”

Trent laughed—too loud, the laugh of a man whose nerves had not yet sat down. “I confess it struck me as a little unusual.”

“Only in the development of details,” Mr. Cupples persisted. “What is abnormal in the essential facts? A madman conceives a crazy suspicion; he hatches a cunning plot against his fancied injurer; it involves his own destruction. Put thus, what is there that any man with the least knowledge of lunatics would call remarkable? And Marlowe’s proceedings? He finds himself in a perilous position from which, though innocent, telling the truth will not save him. Is that unheard-of? He escapes by a bold and ingenious deception. That might happen every day, and probably does.”

Trent chewed, swallowed, and watched Mr. Cupples with that half-amused, half-exasperated admiration with which a man regards a friend who can drain the marvellous of its colour without ever meaning to be unkind.

“I should like to know,” Trent said at last, “whether anything ever happened on the face of the earth that you could not represent as ordinary by such a line of argument.”

A gentle smile lit Mr. Cupples’s face. “Do not suspect me of empty paradox,” he said. “My meaning might be clearer if I mention things that do seem to me essentially remarkable. Let me see… Well, I would call the life history of the liver-fluke—which we owe to Poulton—an essentially remarkable thing.”

“I am unable to argue,” Trent said. “Fair science may smile on the liver-fluke’s humble birth, but I never even heard it mentioned.”

“It is not an appetizing subject,” Mr. Cupples admitted, and looked with quiet approval at the honest steam rising from his plate. “I will not pursue it. I only mean that there are truly remarkable things going on all round us if we will see them; and we do our perceptions no credit in regarding as remarkable only those affairs surrounded by sensational detail.”

Trent applauded with the handle of his knife on the table. “I have not heard you go on like this for years. I believe you are almost as much above yourself as I am. But much as I enjoy it, I am not going to sit still and hear the Manderson affair dismissed as commonplace. The idea of impersonating Manderson in those circumstances was extraordinarily ingenious.”

“Ingenious—certainly,” said Mr. Cupples. “Extraordinary—no. In those circumstances—your own words—it was not strange that it should occur to a clever man. It lay near the surface. Marlowe was famous for imitating Manderson’s voice; he could act; he had a chess-player’s mind; he knew the establishment intimately. I grant you the execution was brilliant; everything favoured it. But the essential idea I do not place, as regards ingenuity, in the same class as, say, utilizing recoil in a discharged firearm to actuate the mechanism of ejecting and reloading. I do admit—as I did at the outset—that the case had unusual features in detail. It developed a high degree of complexity.”

“Did it really strike you in that way?” Trent asked, with desperate sarcasm.

“The affair became complicated,” Mr. Cupples went on, unruffled, “because after Marlowe’s suspicions were awakened, a second subtle mind came in to interfere with the plans of the first. That sort of duel happens often enough in business and politics; less frequently, I imagine, in crime.”

“I should say never,” Trent replied. “Even clever criminals rarely run to strategic subtlety. When they do, they don’t get caught, because clever policemen have, if possible, less strategic subtlety than clever criminals. That deeper quality is rare in any walk of life.”

“One disturbing reflection,” Mr. Cupples said, as if turning from chessboards to the human cost, “was left on my mind by what we learned today. If Marlowe had suspected nothing and walked into the trap, he would almost certainly have been hanged. How often, then, may not a plan to throw guilt on an innocent person have been practised successfully? There must be cases in which men condemned on circumstantial evidence died protesting innocence. I shall never approve again of a death-sentence imposed in a case decided upon such evidence.”

“I never have approved it,” said Trent. “To hang in such cases is flying in the face of the sound principle expressed in the saying that ‘you never can tell.’ I agree with the American jurist who says we should not hang a yellow dog for stealing jam on circumstantial evidence, even if he has jam all over his nose. As for malevolent attempts to fix crimes on innocent men—of course it happens constantly. It is a marked feature of all coercive systems of rule; if the police cannot get the man they want fairly, they do it foully. But there is a case in the State Trials that is peculiarly to the point, because the plotter did in effect what Manderson did—gave up his own life to secure the death of his victims. You have heard of the Campden Case?”

Mr. Cupples confessed ignorance and took another potato.

“John Masefield has written a remarkable play about it,” Trent said. “If it ever comes on again in London, go—if you like having the fan-tods. I have often seen women weep politely at some slab of oleo-margarine sentiment. What smelling-bottle hysterics they ought to indulge in if they saw that play properly acted. The facts were that John Perry accused his mother and brother of murdering a man, and swore he helped them. He told a story packed with detail and answered everything—except the curious fact that the body couldn’t be found. The judge—probably drunk; it was Restoration days—made nothing of that. The mother and brother denied it. All three were found guilty and hanged, purely on John’s evidence. Two years later the man they were hanged for murdering came back; he’d been kidnapped by pirates and taken to sea. His disappearance gave John the idea. The point about John is that he included himself in the accusation—suicide, in effect—and that was what convinced people it must be true. It seemed obvious no man would do himself to death to get others hanged. That is exactly what the prosecution would have said if Marlowe had told the truth. Not one juryman in a million would believe in the Manderson plot.”

Mr. Cupples sat with the air of a man listening not only to words but to what lies behind them, as if the room’s hum and crackle carried old voices too—cases, verdicts, the blunt clang of authority. At length he said: “I have not your acquaintance with that branch of history. In fact, I have none. But certain recollections of my childhood return to me in connection with this. We know from what Mabel told you the spiritual truth underlying the matter—the insane depth of jealous hatred Manderson concealed. We can understand he was capable of such a scheme. But as a rule it is in penetrating to that spiritual truth that justice breaks down. Sometimes it is deliberately concealed, as here. Sometimes it is concealed because simple people cannot express it, and nobody else divines it. When I was a lad in Edinburgh the whole country went mad about the Sandyford Place murder.”

Trent nodded. “Mrs. M’Lachlan’s case. She was innocent right enough.”

“My parents thought so,” Mr. Cupples said. “I thought so myself when I was old enough to read and understand that excessively sordid story. But the mystery was so dark, and the truth behind the lies told by everybody concerned so hopeless to reach, that others were just as convinced of the innocence of old James Fleming. Scotland took sides; it was debated in Parliament; the press divided and raged with a fury I have never seen equalled. Yet it is obvious—is it not?—that if the spiritual truth about that old man had been known there would have been little doubt. If what some surmised about his disposition was true, he was capable of murdering Jessie M’Pherson and casting the blame on the poor feeble-minded creature who nearly suffered the last penalty.”

“Even a commonplace old dotard can be an unfathomable mystery to the rest of the human race,” Trent said. “And nowhere more than in a court of justice. The law doesn’t shine in cases requiring delicacy of perception. It goes wrong easily enough over the Flemings of this world. As for people with temperaments who get mixed up in legal proceedings, they must feel as if they were in a forest of apes, win or lose. But what would twelve red-faced realities in a jury-box have done to Marlowe? His story would have been worse than no defence. There isn’t a single piece of evidence in support of his tale. Can’t you imagine the prosecution tearing it to rags? The judge taking it in his stride in summing up? And the jury—snorting over the feebleness of the lie, calling it the clearest case they ever heard, saying they’d think better of him if he hadn’t lost his nerve and had simply got clear with the swag. Imagine yourself on that jury, not knowing Marlowe, trembling with indignation at the record—cupidity, murder, robbery, sudden cowardice, shameless desperate lying! Why, you and I believed him guilty until—”

“I beg your pardon! I beg your pardon!” Mr. Cupples interjected, laying down knife and fork with a precision that stopped Trent like a hand on the chest. “I was most careful, when we talked it over the other night, to say nothing indicating such a belief. I was always certain he was innocent.”

“You said something of the sort at Marlowe’s just now,” Trent returned, staring. “I wondered what on earth you meant. Certain he was innocent! How can you be certain? You’re generally careful with terms.”

“I said ‘certain’,” Mr. Cupples repeated, and there was no softness in it now—only quiet insistence.

Trent shrugged. “If you really were, after reading my manuscript and discussing the whole thing, then you must have renounced all trust in human reason; which is bad Christianity and infernal nonsense, and oddly enough bad Positivism too—unless I misunderstand it. Why, man—”

“Let me say a word,” Mr. Cupples said, folding his hands above his plate as if saying grace over something far grimmer than mutton and potatoes. “I assure you I am far from abandoning reason. I am certain he is innocent, and I always was certain, because of something I know—and knew from the very beginning. You asked me just now to imagine myself on the jury at Marlowe’s trial. That is unprofitable, because I know I should be present in another capacity. I should be in the witness-box, giving evidence for the defence. You said, ‘If there were a single piece of evidence in support of his tale.’ There is. It is my evidence. And,” he added, very quietly, “it is conclusive.”

He took up his knife and fork again and went on with his dinner as if he had merely corrected a small error in arithmetic.

For a second Trent sat white, as if the blood had been drained from him to make room for the shock. Then it rushed back in a hot wave, and he gave an unnatural laugh and struck the table.

“It can’t be,” he burst out. “It’s something you fancied—something you dreamed after one of those debauches of soda and milk. You can’t really mean that while I was working on the case down there you knew Marlowe was innocent.”

Mr. Cupples, busy with his last mouthful, nodded with almost cheerful composure. He finished eating, wiped his sparse moustache with the care of a man who has always respected small proprieties, and then leaned forward across the table.

“It’s very simple,” he said. “I shot Manderson myself.”
