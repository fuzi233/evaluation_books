The rain lashed against the leaded glass of the parlor windows, a rhythmic drumming that felt, to Mirah, like the frantic heartbeat of a city indifferent to the quiet suffocations of its women. Inside, the air was thick with the scent of beeswax and damp wool, mirroring the heavy atmosphere that had settled over the Meyrick household since the news of Grandcourt's death. It was a news that caused both violent agitation and a desperate, collective effort to repress it, echoing in another breast besides Rex Gascoigne's.

Hans Meyrick stood in the center of the room, shaking the *Times* aloft with a crackling noise that cut through the silence like a physical blow. He had entered as quietly as usual with his latch-key, intending to deliver the paper during the interlude when Mirah finished teaching Mab her weekly lesson, a moment he claimed was chosen solely because he wished to hear Mirah sing. But today, the paper arrived with a vengeance, interrupting Mab's remote imitation of *Lascia ch'io pianga*. The piano keys fell silent instantly; Mirah, her hands poised over the accompaniment, jumped involuntarily, startled by the thunderous intrusion of the newspaper.

"Oh-o-o, Hans!" Mab cried, her voice trembling. "Why do you bring a more horrible noise than my singing?"

Mrs. Meyrick, the only other soul present, looked up from her seat with anxious eyes. "What on earth is the wonderful news? Anything about Italy? Have the Austrians finally given up Venice?"

"Nothing about Italy, but something *from* Italy," Hans replied, his tone possessing a peculiar cadence that immediately set his mother's mind racing toward interpretations of doom. Consider how some of us behave when an event, seemingly confirming our deepest private fears, arrives wrapped in the guise of tragedy. We lean forward, eager to share our burden with an innocent bystander who hasn't yet embarked on the same ship of despair.

"Not bad?" Mrs. Meyrick asked, her voice tight with worry, her thoughts instantly leaping to Deronda. Mirah's heart, too, had been seized by that same name.

"Not bad for anybody we care much about," Hans said quickly, though his smile did not reach his eyes. "Rather uncommonly lucky, I think. I never knew anybody die conveniently before. Considering what a dear gazelle I am, I am constantly wondering to find myself alive."

"Oh me, Hans!" Mab snapped, impatiently. "If you must talk of yourself, let it be behind your own back. What *is* it that has happened?"

Hans placed the paper before his mother, his finger pressing firmly against a specific paragraph. "Duke Alfonso is drowned, and the Duchess is alive—that's all. But more than all is this: Deronda was at Genoa in the same hotel with them. He saw her brought in by the fishermen who had saved her just in time. It seems they saw her jump in after her husband—a less judicious action than I should have expected of the Duchess. However, Deronda is a lucky fellow indeed, being there to take care of her."

Mirah sank back onto the music stool, her eyelids lowered, her hands tightly clasped in her lap. Mrs. Meyrick handed the paper to Mab, her expression softening into pity. "Poor thing! She must have been fond of her husband to jump in after him."

"It was an inadvertence—a little absence of mind," Hans said, creasing his face roguishly and throwing himself into a chair not far from Mirah. "Who can be fond of a jealous baritone, with freezing glances, always singing asides? That was the husband's role, depend upon it. Nothing can be neater than his getting drowned. The Duchess is at liberty now to marry a man with a fine head of hair, and glances that will melt instead of freezing her. And I shall be invited to the wedding."

At these words, Mirah started upright from her sitting posture. Fixing her eyes on Hans, an angry gleam ignited in them, she spoke in a voice deeply shaken by indignation. "Mr. Hans, you ought not to speak in that way. Mr. Deronda would not like you to speak so. Why will you say he is lucky? Why will you use words of that sort about life and death when what is life to one is death to another? How do you know it would be lucky if he loved Mrs. Grandcourt? It might be a great evil to him. She would take him away from my brother—I know she would. Mr. Deronda would not call that lucky to pierce my brother's heart."

A sudden transformation swept through the room. Mirah's face, usually rich with color, had paled to the hue of Ithuriel's spear, her anger stark and terrifying. Poor Hans sat transfixed, blushing under the weight of her gaze as if he were a girl caught in a storm. Nervously, he stammered, "I am a fool and a brute, and I withdraw every word. I'll go and hang myself like Judas—if it's allowable to mention him." Even in his sorrowful moments, Hans's improvisations retained a trace of drollery, a defense mechanism against the gravity of the situation.

Yet Mirah's anger was not appeased. How could it be? She had burst into indignant speech as creatures in intense pain bite and grind their teeth even through their own flesh, a visceral reaction to make their agony bearable. She said no more, but seated herself at the piano, pressing the sheet of music before her as if hoping to summon the melody to heal the wound.

It was Mab who broke the tension, her voice reflecting the discomfort radiating from her mother. "Mirah is quite right to scold you, Hans. You are always taking Mr. Deronda's name in vain. And it is horrible, joking in that way about his marrying Mrs. Grandcourt. Men's minds must be very black, I think," Mab concluded with much scorn.

"Quite true, my dear," Hans murmured in a low tone, rising and turning on his heel to walk toward the back window, avoiding eye contact.

"We had better go on, Mab; you have not given your full time to the lesson," Mirah said, her voice higher than usual, forcing a return to order. "Will you sing this again, or shall I sing it to you?"

"Oh, please sing it to me," Mab rejoiced, eager to ignore the shadow that had passed over them.

Mirah immediately began *Lascia ch'io pianga*, pouring forth its melodious sobs and cries with a new fullness and energy. Hans paused in his walk, leaning against the mantelpiece, keeping his eyes carefully averted from his mother's. When Mirah struck the final chord and rose, she announced, "I must go home now. Ezra expects me."

She extended her hand silently to Mrs. Meyrick, hesitating slightly, unable to look at her directly, instead of offering the customary kiss. But the little mother drew Mirah's face down to hers, whispering soothingly, "God bless you, my dear." Mirah felt a sharp pang of guilt, realizing she had committed an offense against her mother by angrily rebuking Hans. Mixed with her suffering was a bitter sense of pride, an ingratitude, an unbecoming assertion of superiority. Her friend, however, had divined this compunction.

Meanwhile, Hans had seized his wide-awake, ready to open the door.

"Now, Hans," Mab said, her voice masking a sister's tenderness with cunning, "you are not going to walk home with Mirah. I am sure she would rather not. You are so dreadfully disagreeable to-day."

"I shall go to take care of her, if she does not forbid me," Hans replied, opening the door.

Mirah said nothing. When he had ushered her out and closed the door behind him, he walked by her side, unforbidden. She lacked the courage to speak again, conscious that her words to him had perhaps been unbecomingly severe, yet finding only severer words hidden behind them in her own heart. A crowd of thoughts thrust themselves forward, acting as interpreters for a consciousness that remained stubbornly unchanged within her.

Hans, on his side, possessed a mind equally busy. Mirah's anger had awakened in him a new perception, accompanied by the unpleasant realization that he had been a dolt not to have seen it sooner. He wondered: suppose Mirah's heart were entirely preoccupied with Deronda in a capacity beyond that of her brother's benefactor? The supposition filled his mind with anxieties that, to his credit, were not altogether selfish. He held a strong persuasion, confirmed only by direct evidence to the contrary, that there was a serious attachment between Deronda and Mrs. Grandcourt. He had pieced together fragments of observation, gathering knowledge completed by what his sisters had heard from Anna Gascoigne. This convinced him not only that Mrs. Grandcourt harbored a passion for Deronda, but also, despite his friend's austere self-repression, that Deronda's susceptibility toward her was the sign of concealed love. Some men, holding such a conviction, would avoid allusions capable of rousing that susceptibility; but Hans's talk naturally fluttered toward mischief, driven by a form of experiment on live animals consisting in irritating his friends playfully. His experiments had ended in satisfying him that what he thought likely was true.
