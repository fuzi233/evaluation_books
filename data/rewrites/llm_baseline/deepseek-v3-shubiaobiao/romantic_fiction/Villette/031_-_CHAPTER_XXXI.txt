The warmth of advancing spring had settled over Villette, and with it came a lassitude that weighed upon me. My strength ebbed under the sudden heat, leaving me drained after the slightest exertion. Sleepless nights stole the vigor from my days, and one Sunday afternoon, having walked to the Protestant church and back, I retreated to the solitude of the first classe, my sanctuary. There, I rested my head upon the desk, listening to the drowsy hum of bees in the berceau, watching through the glass door as Madame Beck and her guests strolled beneath the orchard boughs, now frosted with blossoms as pure as sunrise on snow.

Among them moved a figure that held my gaze—a young girl, Mademoiselle Sauveur, radiant in silk, said to be M. Paul Emanuel’s goddaughter. I had seen her before, laughing as she clung to his arm with the ease of a cherished ward. The sight had once struck me with an odd pang, a whisper of foreboding I refused to examine. Now, as I watched her, the sunlight and the murmur of bees lulled me, and I slept.

Two hours slipped away. When I woke, dusk had softened the garden, the guests had vanished, and I found myself wrapped in warmth—not the hard wood of the desk beneath my cheek, but a folded shawl. Another draped my shoulders. Who had done this? None of the pupils or teachers moved with such quiet care. Not Ginevra, who would have shaken me awake with careless hands. No, it must have been Madame Beck, ensuring her useful machine did not break from neglect.

The evening air was cool but not unkind, and I stepped into the berceau, pacing the alley where months before I had buried a glass jar—and where a strange vision had once unsettled me. Now, the last glow of sunset gilded the statue of Jean Baptiste, and my thoughts turned, as they often did, to the future.

*Living costs little in Villette,* I mused. *A prudent choice, a modest room, a few benches—day pupils first, then more. Madame Beck began so. Why not I?* Independence was a prize worth any sacrifice, even if it meant laboring without the hope of a true home, something dearer than myself. Some lives were meant to be crescent moons, never full. I could accept that.

But another reckoning pressed upon me as I paused before Methuselah, the ancient tree. Beneath his roots lay buried feelings—Dr. John, his kindness, his warmth. Was that affection dead? I did not know, but it was interred. Yet sometimes, in dreams, the grave stirred, and golden hair pushed through the earth. Had I been too hasty? His voice still softened at my name, his glances still held kindness—but that was his nature, not mine to claim.

The words left my lips aloud—and were answered.

"Good-evening, Mademoiselle," came M. Paul’s voice, startling me. "Did you sleep well?"

I turned to find him amused, leaning against a tree. He had seen me asleep, he admitted—had watched often, from a window he kept as a study, a vantage point to observe the garden and its inhabitants.

"You know too much, Monsieur," I said.

"Knowledge is power," he countered, lighting a cigar. "And you, Miss Lucy, need watching. You are too quiet, too self-contained—a storm beneath stillness."

The night deepened, the air cooling. Rain began to patter as he spoke of things unseen—of the ghostly nun said to haunt these grounds.

"I have seen her," I confessed.

His eyes gleamed. "So have I. And tonight, I mean to learn her secrets."

As if summoned, the great tree above us shuddered, its branches twisting though no wind stirred. Then—from the shadows—a figure swept past us, black and white, swift as anger. The nun herself, her robes whispering of centuries. The wind howled in her wake, the rain turned sharp, and the night itself seemed to shudder as she vanished into the dark.
