As though a prearranged signal had been given, the rear door was battered down with brutal force moments later. They were trapped. Yet, amidst the terrifying clamor, Margaret seemed to regain a measure of composure. She whispered urgently, "Tell them he was here, but has fled." Seizing Gerard, she nearly dragged him up the rough stone steps leading to her father's chamber; her own room lay just beyond. The pounding on the door redoubled.

"Who dares knock at this hour?"

"Open, or perish!"

"I yield not to thieves—honest folk are all asleep."

"Open to the law, Martin Wittenhaagen, or regret it bitterly."

"Why, that is Dirk Brower's voice, I believe. What brings you so far from Tergou?"

"Open, and you shall learn."

Martin drew the bolt with deliberate slowness, and in surged Dierich and four others. They ushered in their companion who had entered through the back door.

"Now, Martin, where is Gerard Eliassoen?"

"Gerard Eliassoen? Why, he was here but now!"

"Were he here?" Dierich's face darkened instantly. "And where is he now?"

"They say he has departed for Italy. Pray, what is the urgency?"

"No matter. When did he leave? Do not tell me he vanished in such a storm!"

"There is quite a fuss over Gerard Eliassoen," Martin scoffed. He lit a candle, seated himself calmly by the fire, and began whittling a piece of fine silk around his bowstring at the spot worn thin by the arrow notch.

"I shall inform you," he said casually. "Do you know his brother Giles? A little malformed imp, all head and limbs? Well, he tore over here on a mule, shouting something—I was too distant to catch the words, only the din. In any case, he startled Gerard. Immediately after his departure, there was such weeping and embracing, and then Gerard departed. They whisper he has gone to Italy—perhaps you know where that lies, for I do not."

Dierich's expression sank deeper with every word. There was no flaw in the tale; a more cunning liar might have spun too many falsehoods and aroused suspicion. But Martin executed his duty perfectly. He uttered only the single lie commanded, inventing nothing else of his own volition.

"Folks," said Dierich, "I fear he speaks truth. I warned the burgomaster exactly how this would unfold. He encountered the dwarf galloping Peter Buyskens's mule from Sevenbergen. 'They have dispatched that imp to Gerard,' he declared, 'therefore Gerard must be at Sevenbergen.' 'Ah, master!' I replied, 'It is too late now. We ought to have considered Sevenbergen sooner, rather than wasting our time scouring the obscure corners of Tergou for those cursed parchments we shall never locate until we find the man who stole them. If he was at Sevenbergen,' I continued, 'and they sent the dwarf to him, it must have been to warn him we hunt him. He is leagues away by now,' I stated. Confound that chalk-faced girl! She has outwitted us bearded men; and so I reported to the burgomaster, yet he refused to listen. A dampened jerkin apiece—that is all reward we shall receive for this endeavor."

Martin grinned coolly in Dierich's face.

"Nevertheless," added the latter, "to satisfy the burgomaster, we shall search the house."

Martin's demeanor shifted instantly to gravity. This transformation did not escape Dierich's notice. After a moment's reflection, he ordered, "Station two of you outside, one guard on each flank of the house, ensuring no one leaps from the upper windows. The rest follow me." Taking the candle, he ascended the stairs, trailed by three comrades. Martin remained alone.

The sturdy soldier bowed his head. Everything had proceeded smoothly at first; now came this disastrous reversal! Suddenly, a thought struck him: all was not yet lost. Gerard must be in Peter's room or Margaret's; neither was excessively high from the ground. Gerard would surely leap out. Dierich had left a man below, but what then? For thirty seconds, Gerard and he would stand two against one, and in that fleeting interval, anything might occur.

Martin held the back door slightly ajar and watched. Light spilled from Peter's room.

"Curse the fool!" he muttered. "Does he intend to surrender like a maiden?"

The light drifted into Margaret's bedroom. Still, no window opened. Had Gerard planned to flee that way, he would not have waited until the intruders filled the room. Martin grasped this immediately, abandoned the door, descended to the foot of the stairs, and listened. He began to suspect Gerard had slipped away through a window while the men occupied the interior. The longer the silence persisted, the firmer this conviction became.

Then, abruptly and roughly, it was shattered. Faint cries emanated from the inner bedroom—Margaret's.

"They have captured him," groaned Martin. "They have secured him."

A flash of realization struck him: if they removed Gerard, his life would be forfeit; and if harm befell him, Margaret's heart would shatter. Casting his gaze wildly around like a wild beast seeking an exit, he formed a resolution strikingly characteristic of those iron ages and of a soldier cornered. He moved to each door in succession, mimicking Dierich Brower's tone sharply, "Guard the window!" He then quietly barred and bolted both doors. Next, he picked up his bow and six arrows; fitting one to the string, he stowed the others in his quiver. His knife he placed upon a chair behind him, hilt facing inward, and there he waited at the base of the stairs with a calm resolve to kill those four men or fall by their hands.

Two, he knew, he could dispatch with his arrows before they could approach, leaving Gerard and himself to fight hand-to-hand with the remaining pair. Furthermore, he had witnessed men paralyzed by sudden attacks of this nature. Should Brower and his men hesitate even an instant before engaging him, he would strike three instead of two, shifting the odds in their favor.

He need not wait long. Heavy footsteps echoed in Margaret's room, drawing nearer and nearer. The light advanced, accompanied by voices. Though Martin's heart, robust as it was, hammered violently hearing men march toward their deaths—and perhaps his own—it was more likely so than not. Four against one was long odds in a battlefield merely ten feet wide, and Gerard might be bound and powerless to assist. Yet this man, previously seen trembling in his boots at the mere mention of a lantern named Giles, never faltered in this terrible moment of genuine peril. He stood there, body coiled for combat, eyes blazing, equally prepared to give life or take it.

Desperate stakes! To win meant instant and lifelong exile; to lose meant dying instantly upon that very floor.

Dierich Brower and his men discovered Peter in the first stages of sleep. They ransacked his cupboards, drove knives into an alligator skin nailed to his wall, and peered beneath his bed. It was a spacious room, seemingly full of hiding places, yet Gerard was nowhere to be found.

Next, they proceeded to Margaret's room, and the sight itself was disheartening—it was small, barren, devoid of cupboards. However, a large fireplace and chimney dominated the space. Dierich's eyes fell upon these immediately. Here they found the beauty of Sevenbergen sleeping on an ancient chest barely a foot high, with no attempt made to conceal her; yet her sheets were snow-white, as was her own linen. She lay there, resembling a lily fallen into a rut.

Soon she awoke, sitting upright in bed, bewildered. Upon seeing the men, she began to scream faintly, praying for mercy. Her plea made Dierich Brower ashamed of his mission.

"This is a disturbance," he said, somewhat flustered. "We mean no harm to you, my pretty maid. Remain still, close your eyes, and dream of your wedding night while I inspect this chimney to see if Master Gerard hides within."

"Gerard! In my room?"

"Why not? They claim you and he—"

"Cruel! You know they have driven him away from me—expelled him from his homeland. This is a ruse. You are thieves; you are wicked men; you are not men of Sevenbergen, or you would know Margaret Brandt better than to seek her lover in this room above all others in the world. Oh, brave! Four great hulking men, armed to the teeth, to insult one poor honest girl! The women residing in your own homes must be worthless, or you would respect them too greatly to disgrace a girl of good character."

"There! Depart, before we hear worse," Dierich hastened, retreating quickly. "He is not in the chimney. Plaster may repair what a cudgel breaks, but a woman's tongue is a double-edged dagger, and a girl possesses her mother's milk still." With that, he beat a hasty retreat.

"I informed the burgomaster precisely how this would conclude."
