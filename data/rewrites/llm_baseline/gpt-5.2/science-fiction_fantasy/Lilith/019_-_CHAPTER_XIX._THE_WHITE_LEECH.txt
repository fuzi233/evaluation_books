I woke out of a sleep so deep it felt older than my own life, with pain already speaking in my hand before my eyes were open. The back of it had risen into a hot swell, and in the centre a wound gaped—three clean sides meeting like a little pale triangle, as if some hungry mouth had taken a measured taste. The ache was sharp as brine in a cut.

All that day the swelling sank as though the flesh remembered its proper shape. By evening the hurt was almost gone, the skin drawn tight and smooth again as kneaded dough that has taken back its spring. I turned the cave inside out—lifted stones, felt along cracks, searched the mossy edge of the hot stream where it ran through the rock with a constant hiss like a kettle never allowed to boil over. I found nothing that could have done it. No teeth, no claw-marks, no track in the damp sand; only the steady, domestic noises of the place—the water’s hurry, the faint drip from the roof, and now and then the small click of grit shifting under my own feet.

The days took on their slow, circular labour: morning light filtering into the mouth of the cave; the bath in the hot river; the return; the fruit; the watching. She lay where I had brought her, white as washed bone, and still as if the last breath had been taken and carefully folded away. Yet she did not rot. There was no sweetening stench, no thickening air. The cave stayed clean with the stream’s moving breath.

And sometimes—only sometimes, and then I doubted my own eyes until doubt sickened me—she seemed a little less terrible. The knife-edges of her bones were not so abrupt. The hollows under the cheeks held less shadow. The skin—once like old parchment stretched too far—began to look more like skin. If that change was real, then life had turned its face back again. The tide that had ebbed toward the infinite had found some hidden moon and begun to creep home.

So I watched with the hunger of a man counting down the minutes of a slow-roasting feast while knowing he has no fire but patience. Twenty times a day I leaned close to catch the smallest sign: a pulse, a warmth, a shift in the lips. Twenty times a day I told myself I had imagined it. Then I would remember what she had been when first I found her—nothing but hair and horror and the stubborn refusal to decay—and hope would rise again like yeast.

Weeks went by. One night I lay long awake, the cave close despite the stream’s freshness, and at last rose to go out to the mouth and drink the cooler air. The moon was full outside; inside, everything was washed in a pale clarity that made the white of her face seem to glow.

Naturally—how could I not?—I looked at her again before stepping out.

“Bliss eternal!” I cried aloud. “Do I see her eyes?”

Two great orbs, dark as if cut from a starless sky, seemed to shine there—luminous not with light, but with an excess of darkness that drank the moon’s pale spill and returned it as depth. My heart beat so hard it felt noisy enough to wake the stones. I crept nearer, bent over her—

Her lids were shut.

Hope and imagination, like two cooks seasoning the same broth, had overdone it and made me taste what was not there. I turned away, dropped to the cave floor, and wept into the cool grit like a child shamed by his own longing.

Then another thought came, small but steady as the stream’s sound: her eyes had been a little open before. Now that dreadful chink—like a slit cut into nothingness—was gone. Something had closed them. Sleep, perhaps; or a will, perhaps. In either case, life—more or less—must have moved. I held that thought the way a starving man holds a crust, and I slept.

That night I was bitten again.

I woke with my mouth scorched and my throat begging, as if my blood had been simmered away while I slept. In the morning, I searched again—more thoroughly than before, turning stones until my fingers were raw, poking into every crevice where damp made a dark lip. Still nothing. The wound was the same triangular taste, and again it was nearly healed by evening, as if whatever fed on me brought its own quick salve.

I began to suspect the hot stream. In its ceaseless rushing there was a sound that could hide anything: a soft slick movement against stone; a body sliding through water without splash. Some great leech-kind, perhaps, that came when the cave was darkest and my senses dull.

“If blood be its object,” I told myself, “so long as I am here, it need not touch my treasure.”

That same morning, as I did what had become my most careful, most reverent cookery—peeling a grape so the skin would not snag in her dry mouth, picking out every seed with a fingernail as one removes grit from rice—I placed the fruit between her lips.

Her mouth moved.

Not much—only the smallest closing, the faintest acceptance, like a child taking milk without waking fully. But it was unmistakable. The lips met the grape. The grape vanished.

I knew, with a certainty that made my hands shake, that she lived.

Hope made me practical. If she woke and tried to rise, she must not rise naked into the cold; she must have something between her revived skin and the rough world. I went to the forest and searched not for beauty but for material—what would bend, what would breathe, what would not chafe. There I found, in the fibrous skeletons of leaves like those of the prickly pear, a kind of netted tissue: veins without the pulp, strength without weight.

I gathered a bundle and laid them in the sun, letting heat dry them until they crackled faintly when touched. The forest had its own acoustic order—the insect-thin whirrs, the heavy drop of fruit, the wind combing the upper leaves. Compared to the harsh clank and whine of the world I had known before, it was music made for sanity. Here, nothing screamed to sell itself. Nothing demanded attention except by being alive.

Back in the cave I pulled apart the reticulated layers and began to fashion garments: one to hang from her waist, one from her shoulders. I used the stiletto-point of an aloe leaf as my awl, and for thread I twisted various filaments until they held. Three thicknesses, sewn together—because softness matters, and because I could not bear the thought of her waking to be hurt by my clumsiness.

During the week that followed she gave no clearer sign than that she took the grapes more readily, as if the tongue had remembered its work. Yet her body changed. She grew plumper; the skin took on a faint living sheen, no longer the dry pallor of something left behind. Still she did not open her eyes.

And into my hope crept a fear as cold as spoiled broth: what if this growth was wrong? What if something other than her was feeding, swelling her as fungi swell on wood? The few grapes could not account for the change. I tried to reason it away, but my body, drained by those night visitations, grew weaker, and reason had less salt in it each day.

For the biting continued. The thing—whatever it was—came now with a terrible regularity: every three days. It bit me in the neck or the arm. Always one bite. Always while I slept. Never in the daytime, even when I slept then. I lay awake for hours, listening, trying to tune my mind to the smallest change in the cave’s soundscape—the stream’s pitch, the drip’s rhythm, the shift of my own breath—hoping to catch the moment when something else joined the choir.

I never heard it.

If it moved, it moved like a thief in silk. If it drank, it drank without even the indignity of pain to warn me. And the cave’s constant water-speech swallowed every secret.

At last I grew so hopeless of catching it that I stopped trying. By day I no longer tore the cave apart. By night I no longer stared into the dark until my eyes ached. I knew, from the faintness that came too easily and the way I tired after the smallest labour, that I was losing blood at a dangerous rate.

I did not care.

In sight of my eyes, death was yielding to life. A soul was gathering strength to save me from loneliness. We would go away together, and when I could walk in the open again, eat properly again, I would recover quickly enough. A man can be bled and still live; a man can be alone and die without a wound.

When the garments were finished, I looked at them with the satisfaction of a poor cook who has managed, with wild ingredients and no proper tools, to make something that will at least not poison the one he serves. Then I set to matting layers of fibre into sandals, for I would not have her step barefoot on stone and thorn.

One night I woke suddenly, breathless, faint, craving air as if the cave had become a sealed jar. I rose to crawl toward the mouth when a slight rustle in the leaves of my couch pinned me still. The sound was tiny, but in that place of continuous water it was a distinct syllable.

“I caught the vile thing,” said a feeble voice—in my mother-tongue, the old words shaped exactly as they had been shaped in my childhood kitchen and my father’s fields, before other languages and other noises had tried to wear them down. “I caught it in the very act!”

For a moment the language itself struck me harder than the news: that she spoke in my own speech, unborrowed, unbroken, as if it had never been dragged through other mouths. I felt the fierce, almost superstitious relief of hearing a dialect live—hearing memory keep its skin.

She was alive. She spoke.

I dared not exult aloud, lest I frighten her back into silence. “What creature?” I breathed.

“The creature,” she answered, “that was biting you.”

“What was it?”

“A great white leech.”

“How big?” I forced myself to keep my voice calm, as if discussing the size of a fish at market.

“Not far from six feet long, I should think,” she said.

“You have saved my life, perhaps—But how could you touch the horrid thing! How brave of you!” My own words sounded foolish beside the stark fact of her waking.

“I did!” was all she answered, and I thought I heard a shudder in her breath.

“Where is it? What could you do with such a monster?”

“I threw it in the river.”

“Then it will come again, I fear!”

“I do not think I could have killed it, even had I known how. I heard you moaning, and got up to see what disturbed you; saw the frightful thing at your neck, and pulled it away. But I could not hold it, and was hardly able to throw it from me. I only heard it splash in the water!”

“We’ll kill it next time,” I said, and meant it; but the words were hardly out when darkness tilted. My blood, already thinned by those feedings, failed me. I turned toward the open air and fell as if the cave floor rose to meet me.

When I came to myself, sunlight was in the mouth of the cave, and the world had that early hush before birds and wind build their full day. She stood a little way off.

Even in the clumsy attire I had fashioned from leaf-skeletons, she looked grand and graceful, like a tall flame wrapped in pale smoke. I had seen those eyes. Through the night they had shone. Dark as primeval night, they now outshone the day—not by brightness, but by a depth that made everything else seem thin.

She stood erect as a column and regarded me. Her pale cheek showed no emotion—only question, like a blade held flat.

I rose, unsteady. “We must be going,” I said, thinking first of the leech and second of everything else.

“The white leech—” I began, but stopped. A strange smile flickered across her face, brief as a spark on wet wood.

“Did you find me there?” she asked, pointing to the cave.

“No; I brought you there,” I replied.

“You brought me?”

“Yes.”

“From where?”

“From the forest.”

“What have you done with my clothes—and my jewels?”

“You had none when I found you.”

“Then why did you not leave me?”

“Because I hoped you were not dead.”

“Why should you have cared?”

“Because I was very lonely, and wanted you to live.”

“You would have kept me enchanted for my beauty!” she said, proud scorn in her voice, as if the very idea were an insult that explained everything.

Her words and her look stirred my anger—hot, sudden, like oil catching. “There was no beauty left in you,” I said.

“Why, then, again, did you not let me alone?”

“Because you were of my own kind.”

“Of YOUR kind?” she cried, and contempt made the words ring hard in the cave, a note that sat ugly against the stream’s honest rush. “I thought so, but find I was mistaken!”

“Doubtless you pitied me!”

“Never had woman more claim on pity, or less on any other feeling!”

Pain, mortification, and anger crossed her face in an expression too quick and too deep for me to name. She turned from me and stood silent. The splendour went out of her eyes as if someone had snuffed a lamp. Starless night lay profound in them; whatever had made them shine seemed slain by hate—hate of the one who had dragged her back.

“Had you failed to rouse me, what would you have done?” she asked suddenly, without moving.

“I would have buried it.”

“It! What?—You would have buried THIS?” she exclaimed, flashing round upon me in a white fury. Her arms flung wide, and in her eyes a cold lightning forked, not light but judgement.

“Nay; that I saw not. That, weary weeks of watching and tending have brought back to you,” I answered. With such a woman I must be plain. “Had I seen the smallest sign of decay, I would at once have buried you.”

“Dog of a fool!” she cried. “I was but in a trance—Samoil! what a fate!—Go and fetch the she-savage from whom you borrowed this hideous disguise.”

“I made it for you. It is hideous, but I did my best.”

She drew herself up to her full height, as if height were a weapon. “How long have I been insensible?” she demanded. “A woman could not have made that dress in a day!”

“Not in twenty days,” I rejoined, “hardly in thirty.”

“Ha! How long do you pretend I have lain unconscious?—Answer me at once.”

“I cannot tell how long you had lain when I found you, but there was nothing left of you save skin and bone: that is more than three months ago. Your hair was beautiful—nothing else. I have done for it what I could.”

“My poor hair!” she said, and drew a great armful of it from behind her, letting it spill through her fingers as if it were something precious saved from fire. “It will be more than a three-months’ care to bring YOU to life again. I suppose I must thank you, although I cannot say I am grateful!”

“There is no need, madam: I would have done the same for any woman—yes, or for any man either!”

“How is it my hair is not tangled?” she said, and her voice softened only for that, for the feel of it. “It always drifted in the current.”

“How?—What do you mean?”

“I could not have brought you to life but by bathing you in the hot river every morning.”

She shuddered as if I had smeared her with something foul, and stood a while staring at the hurrying water, hearing in its sound whatever history it carried that I could not translate for her. The stream spoke one language; her pride spoke another; my words, even when true, seemed to come to her already tainted, like food served in the wrong vessel.

Then she turned back to me. “We must understand each other,” she said. “You have done me the two worst of wrongs—compelled me to live, and put me to shame: neither of them can I pardon!”

She raised her left hand and flung it out as if she would push me from the world. Something ice-cold struck my forehead—cold as a river-stone pulled from deep water. The cave spun, sound stretching thin, and I knew no more.

When I came to myself, I lay on the ground, wet and shivering, the stream’s ceaseless rush filling my ears like a verdict that would not end.
