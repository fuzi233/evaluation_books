Arthur Pendennis spotted Mr. Bows and immediately adopted the persona of the boisterous host, shouting a "How d'ye do!" in a voice loud enough to wake the dead. He offered a dashing, offhand salute, though a crimson flush betrayed him, instantly mirrored by Fanny Bolton, whose cheeks erupted in a fluttering red signal. After shaking hands, the atmosphere shifted abruptly. An ironical acceptance from Bows regarding Pen's imminent visit to his chambers was followed by a heavy, guilty silence that settled over the group like a damp woolen blanket.

To break the tension, Pen attempted to rattle the air with a story of the previous night's escapades. He tried to mimic Costigan pleading with the Vauxhall ticket-taker, but the performance was a disaster. No laughter rippled through the room. Mrs. Bolton stared blankly, confused whether the Captain or the ticket-taker was being roasted; Fanny wore a mask of alarm, attempting a timid giggle that sounded more like a squeak; and old Mr. Bows sat as glum as a violinist playing a funeral dirge on a broken piano. Realizing his narrative had failed to ignite joy, Pen's voice dwindled to a whisper, flickering out until the room plunged back into somber darkness. Even the rhythmic *clink* of the ticket-porter's boots echoed with undue solemnity.

"You were coming to see me, sir?" Mr. Bows asked, breaking the stalemate. "Won't you have the kindness to walk up to my chambers with me? You do them a great honor, I'm sure. They're rather high up, but—"

"Oh! I live in a garret myself," Pen interjected, trying to lighten the mood, "and Shepherd's Inn feels twice as cheerful as Lamb Court."

"I knew you resided on the third floor," Bows replied, his tone shifting to a melancholic gravity. "And may I gently suggest, without meaning offense, that the air up three flights of stairs is far more wholesome for a gentleman than the stagnant breath of a porter's lodge."

"Sir!" Pen snapped, his candle flame suddenly flaring in indignation. "May I choose my company without your interference?"

"You were so polite to mention your intention to honor my humble domicile," Bows said softly, his voice dripping with a theatrical sadness that Pen recognized as genuine, not an act. "Shall I show you the way? Mrs. Bolton, Mr. Pendennis and I are ancient friends; our paths crossed at the dawn of our lives."

Pointing a trembling finger toward the exit, holding a hat with a gesture slightly too dramatic for a quiet afternoon, Bows spoke with words borrowed from the orators of the theater. Yet, despite his melodramatic flair, Pen sensed the depth of the old man's sincerity. "Come along, sir," Bows urged, "since you are so pressing. Mrs. Bolton, I wish you a prosperous day. Goodbye, Miss Fanny; I shall forever cherish our night at Vauxhall, and rest assured, I will remember the theatre tickets." He took Fanny's hand, pressed it warmly, and departed, leaving the room in a flurry of unspoken emotions.

"What a delightful young man, truly!" exclaimed Mrs. Bolton.

"Do you think so, Mother?" Fanny whispered.

"I was thinking who he reminded me of," Mrs. Bolton mused, gazing out the curtained window after Pen and Bows as they ascended the courtyard. "Back at the Wells, with Mrs. Serle, there was a gentleman from the City who rode in a tilbury, wearing a white hat. He was the exact image, save for his whiskers, which were black instead of Pen's fiery red."

"Laws, Mother! They are the finest hair I've ever seen," Fanny agreed.

"He frequented the house of Emly Budd, who danced Columbine in 'Arlequin Ornipe,' even when Miss De la Bosky was indisposed," Mrs. Bolton continued, busying herself with wiping mugs and stacking plates. "Emly was a baker with a country estate near Omerton. He drove Emly down Goswell Street, and eventually, they wed privately at St. Bartholomew's, where the band played just for them. Now, she drives a carriage and is listed in the papers as a patroness of the Manshing-House Ball for the Washwomen's Asylum. Look at Lady Mirabel, Captain Costigan's daughter; she, too, has turned professional."

As the women folded the dinner cloth, placing it carefully in the drawer, a sudden intimacy occurred. In the final fold, Fanny and her mother were forced close together. Mrs. Bolton slipped a finger beneath Fanny's chin and bestowed a kiss. Once again, the red signal flashed across Fanny's cheeks, but this time it was not fear. It was a sweet, fluttering pleasure. Was love a sin because it tasted so sweet at the start and so bitter at the end?

Mrs. Bolton announced she needed to attend to business and left, instructing Fanny to guard the lodge. As soon as her mother vanished, Fanny moved to the window overlooking Bows's door, refusing to look anywhere else. Nearby, her sisters, Betsy-Jane and Ameliar-Ann, buzzed around a picture book held upside down, pretending to read a grave tract from their father's collection. Fanny ignored them, her gaze fixed solely on the street below.

Suddenly, she gave a sharp shake. He had emerged. He would pass the door. But her hope shattered instantly. Pen did step out, yet Bows followed closely in his wake. Under the archway, they paused. Bows merely removed his hat and bowed, offering a glance inward before continuing. He did not stop to speak. Within moments, Bows returned alone, entering the porter's room, leaving Fanny bewildered.

"Where is your Ma, dear?" Bows asked Fanny later.

"I don't know," Fanny retorted with an angry toss of her head. "I don't follow Mother's footsteps wherever she wanders, Mr. Bows."

"Am I to be my mother's keeper?" Bows sighed, his usual bitterness returning. He called over his nieces, presenting them with gingerbread medals based on their reading skills. While the girls discussed their treats in the courtyard, Fanny retreated to her work, stitching furiously as if her needle were a weapon against her rising emotions. Bows positioned himself to watch the street entrance, waiting for someone who never appeared. Soon, Mrs. Bolton returned from market, finding Bows seated in the spot where she had hoped to find Pen.

Inside the room above, the meeting between Bows and his guest was far from harmonious. Pen was sullen and withdrawn. Bows, having matters on his mind, refused to share them in the presence of Captain Costigan, who had hastily vacated his bedroom mere moments prior. Costigan, however, lingered in the room, exuding a cloying scent of whisky and possessing a handshake that trembled violently, a testament to his nervous constitution.

Bows's chamber stood in stark contrast to the Captain's chaos; it was immaculate, with manuscripts neatly shelved and a lithograph of Miss Fotheringay hanging above the bed. Bows cherished the old handwriting of Lady Mirabel, though he treasured a specific note from her recent era above all others. He was currently teaching Fanny to sing and write, channeling the same devotion he once poured into Emily. To lose Emily, he had taken a substitute, much like a man seeking a crutch after losing a limb. It was cruel fate that Pen should appear again, pursuing the object of Bows's latest affection.

Costigan, meanwhile, believed his presence was entirely welcome. He chatted affably, regaling Pen with tales of Sir Charles and Lady Mirabel's trip to Baden-Baden, promising invitations for Pen to join them. Pen, feigning interest, nodded gravely, recalling the Captain's past columns in the *Sporting and Theatrical Newspapers*. They discussed the hospitality of the Grand Duke and the festivities at Dublin Castle, topics Pen had once revered. As the Captain spoke, memories of Emily, the small room at Chatteris, and their conversations on the bridge flooded back. For a moment, Pen felt a warm kinship for both old friends, shaking their hands cordially before departing.

However, the moment the Captain began speaking, Pen forgot about little Fanny Bolton, lost in his own self-absorbed reveries. He only recalled her when Bows began hobbling down the stairs, clearly intending to shadow Pen outside. This precaution proved ill-advised. The sight of the old man trailing behind ignited a surge of wrath in Pen. "What does he mean by dogging me?" Pen fumed as they reached the Strand.

Isolated on the street, Pen burst into a laugh, though it lacked honesty. Perhaps the thought struck him then, causing a fresh blush, realizing the absurdity of his reaction to the elder's stratagem. He sought distraction, climbing the dizzying stairs of the Panorama only to find Care accompanying him every step. He visited the Club, composing a long, witty, sarcastic letter home, deliberately omitting any mention of Vauxhall or Fanny, fearing his mother and Laura would find the subject unpalatable. Neither novels nor the library table, nor the staid Mr. Hawkins, could anchor his wandering mind.
