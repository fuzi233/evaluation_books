*It fell about the Martinmas, when nights were lang and mirk, That wife's twa sons cam hame again, And their hats were o' the birk. It did na graw by bush or brae, Nor yet in ony shough; But by the gates o' paradise That birk grew fair eneugh.*
— The Wife of Usher's Well

It is the evening of February 15, 1587, and Mrs. Leigh—whom we return to now, to the familiar West Country lanes and faces of Burrough House—paced slow, measured turns along the terrace walk, gazing out across the winding Torridge, the hazy sandhills, and the wide, heaving western sea. This was no new ritual for her: for three long, weary years, fair weather or foul, she had kept this vigil. Three years and more had slipped by, and still there was no word of Frank, of Amyas, of their gallant ship the *Rose*, or all the brave souls who had sailed out of Bideford harbor aboard her. Loving eyes across the coast, in Bideford, Appledore, Clovelly, and Ilfracombe, had grown hollow from watching and weeping for the crews who had sailed west, just as John Oxenham had sailed before them, and vanished like a half-forgotten dream into the vast unknown.

There had been one fleeting flash of hope, once. Good Sir Richard Grenville, without Mrs. Leigh’s knowledge, had dispatched a horseman riding hard for Plymouth the moment news arrived that Drake, Frobisher, and Carlisle had returned from raiding the Spanish Main. The messenger brought back glorious tidings of the sacking of Cartagena, San Domingo, and Saint Augustine, of the relief of Raleigh’s struggling Virginian colony—but no word at all of the *Rose*. Mrs. Leigh had bowed her head in quiet worship, and whispered, “The Lord gave, and the Lord hath taken away; blessed be the name of the Lord.”

Her hair was fully grey now, her cheeks wan, her step slow and unsteady. She seldom left Burrough save for church, or to visit the sick and poor in the neighboring cottages. She never spoke her sons’ names aloud, never let a single word slip that might hint she still ached for them—but every high tide, when the red flag flew from the sandhills to mark the bar was deep enough for passage, she paced that terrace, devouring the horizon with hungry eyes in search of a sail that never came. Stately merchant ships came and went as they always had; white sails hung off the bar for hours, day after day, month after month, year after year, but some instinct deep in her bones told her none were the sails she sought. She knew the *Rose* down to every line of her hull, the cut of every sail, could have picked her out miles off across a whole fleet—but she never came. And Mrs. Leigh would bow her head again, and turn to walk among the poor, who had come to see her as a sacred figure, a woman God had drawn near to Him through the mysterious, fiery depths of grief they all knew too well. Lone widows and bedridden men would look into her steady, quiet eyes and draw strength from them, even though she never spoke of her own sorrow—for they could tell she had walked unharmed through the hottest part of the furnace, at the side of One whose form is like unto the Son of God. All the while, she blamed herself for her “earthly” longings, confessing nightly to the Lord the weakness she could not shake off, the one that drew her feet to the terrace beneath the wind-bent line of trees every time the tide rose high.

This evening, though, the whole village of Northam is in an uproar. The pebble ridge thunders far below, just as it did years before, but the village is loud even over the roar of the surf: the church tower rocks with pealing bells, crowds throng the streets shouting and dancing around bonfires, burning effigies of the Pope, drinking healths to the Queen and roaring “So perish all her enemies!” Bonfires blaze red on every village hill for miles, and the bells of Bideford far off answer Northam’s chimes, just as they did seven years prior when Amyas sailed home from circumnavigating the globe. News has just arrived: Mary Queen of Scots has been beheaded at Fotheringay. All of England, like a man shaking off a decades-long nightmare, has burst into one thunderous shout of jubilation, for the terror and danger of seventeen years of Catholic plots and treason has been lifted from the nation’s heart forever.

She is gone now, to answer to a higher tribunal than the English Estates for all the noble English blood spilled in her name, for all the hearts she tempted into treachery, rebellion, and murder. Elizabeth’s own words, long-delayed, have at last come to pass:
*The daughter of debate, That discord aye doth sow, Hath reap'd no gain where former rule Hath taught still peace to grow.*

She can work no more harm now. The murder, the adultery, the heart that knew no forgiveness, the tongue that could not speak truth even to save itself, are gone, and perhaps atoned for. Her fair, famous face lingers as a sad, half-pitied dream even in the memories of those who knew full well that either she or Protestant England must fall.

For all her staunch Protestant faith, Mrs. Leigh breathes a quiet prayer that the Lord may have mercy on that soul Mary once described as “clear as a diamond, and as hard.” Surely the final moments before the block could not have been all performance? Seven years of sorrow had taught Mrs. Leigh lessons beyond price; might not seventeen years of imprisonment have taught Mary Stuart some small wisdom too? A former courtier, Mrs. Leigh knew well the godless, profligate French court culture the young Mary had been raised in, among the Medicis, the Guises, and Cardinal Lorraine—and she shuddered, and sighed to herself, “To whom little is given, of them shall little be required!” But still the bells pealed on, and would not cease.

Then, out in the fast-darkening twilight, something answered them. A flash, then the thunder of a gun fired at sea.

Mrs. Leigh froze. The flash was right outside the bar. It could not be a ship in distress: the wind was light and westerly, the tide a high spring flood, perfect for entering the harbor. Another flash. Another gun. The noisy crowds in Northam fell silent at once, all hurrying to the churchyard that overlooks the wide mudflats and the river.

Outside the bar rode a gallant large ship, nearly a thousand tons, sailing in under full sail, her rigging nothing like an English craft’s. For a moment the crowd panicked: was this a Spanish cruiser come to make reprisals for Drake’s raid on Cadiz? But no—no Spanish Don would dare such a reckless, unorthodox bit of warfare. If they came, they would come with full admirals, transports, and dispatches, following every approved rule of war, not a single rogue craft slipping in unannounced.

Drifting fast on the fair westerly wind and flooding tide, she glided up the channel between the two lines of sandhills, drawing level with Appledore. She was no enemy, and even if she were a foreigner, she was a daring one: she had never struck her topsails, the required courtesy every foreign ship must show within sight of an English port, or risk being treated as hostile. Everyone there knew the old story: years before, a Spanish admiral sailed into Plymouth Sound in peacetime, his flag flying high without so much as lowering it, come to fetch Anne of Austria, Philip II’s new bride, home from Flanders. Port Admiral John Hawkins, that old lion of the sea, rushed out in his famous ship *Jesus of Lubec*, fired a shot across his bows, and when the admiral paid no mind, ran alongside and put a shot straight through his hull. The Spanish flag came down at once, apologies were made, and Hawkins did not accept them for days, so fiercely did he guard the Queen’s honor. If Hawkins would do that to a Spanish fleet in peacetime, there were a dozen old sea dogs in Appledore who would do the same to any hostile ship in wartime, even if they only had an old iron pot to burn their gunpowder in.

The strange sail vanished behind the hill of Appledore, and a cheer burst up from a hundred throats, ringing across the quiet evening. Another gun boomed off the water, and another cheer. For twenty minutes, Mrs. Leigh stood perfectly still, a pale, scarcely breathing statue, her eyes fixed on the Viking rock at the harbor mouth.

At last the ship rounded the dark Hubbastone rocks, turning up the Bideford river. Drums, fifes, shawms, and trumpets blared from her decks, waking echoes off every wooded knoll and slate ledge along the shore. As she came full into view of Burrough House, another cheer burst from her crew, rolling a full mile up the hills from the silver surface of the river below.

Mrs. Leigh turned and walked fast back to the house, calling for her maid. “Grace, bring me my hood. Master Amyas is come home!”

“Praise the Lord, madam, what joy! But how did you know?” Grace gasped.

“I heard his voice over the water. But I did not hear Mr. Frank’s with him, Grace.”

“Oh, never fear, madam—they were never parted. Where one goes, the other follows, you’ll see. Here is your hood, madam.”

And the two set off at a quick pace for Bideford. Was it true? Was it some trick of her overtaxed mind? Or did a mother’s divine instinct truly let her pick out her son’s voice over the din of a whole ship’s crew, from a mile and more away? Grace turned the question over in her head a dozen times on the walk to the quay, but when they reached the end of Bridgeland Street—then lined with rope-walks and sailmakers’ shops, long before the Virginia trade tobacco warehouses were built—the question answered itself.

A great crowd rounded the corner: sailors, housewives, apprentices, hurrahing, laughing, weeping, shouting. When they spotted Mrs. Leigh, the whole procession came to a stop.

“Here she is!” someone shouted. “Amyas Leigh’s mother!”

“His mother? Not *their* mother,” Mrs. Leigh thought, turning pale, but her heart was long past breaking now.

The next moment, Amyas’s giant frame, head and shoulders above every other man in the crowd, rounded the corner. “Make way! Make room for Madam Leigh!” the crowd roared, and Amyas dropped to his knees in the mud at her feet.

She threw her arms around his neck, and bent her grey head over his, while the rough sailors, apprentices, and weathered harbor women fell into a reverent hush, forming a quiet circle around mother and son. Mrs. Leigh asked no question at first: she could see at a glance he was alone.

At last he whispered, “I would have died to save him, mother, if I could.”

“You need not tell me that, Amyas Leigh, my son.”

Another long silence. “How did he die?” she whispered.

“He is a martyr. He died in the—” Amyas could not finish the words.

“The Inquisition?”

“Yes.”

A sharp shudder ran through Mrs. Leigh’s frame, then she lifted her head, steady again. “Come home, Amyas. I little expected such an honor—such an honor, to have a son who died a martyr! A very St. Stephen! God, have mercy on me, and let me not go mad before these folk, when I ought to be thanking Thee for Thy great mercies! Amyas, who is that?” She pointed to Ayacanora, the wild Indian girl standing close at Amyas’s heels, watching the whole scene with sharp, curious eyes.

“She is a poor wild Indian girl—my daughter, I call her. I will tell you her full story later.”

“Your daughter? Then she is my granddaughter. Come hither, child, and be my granddaughter.”

Ayacanora came obediently, and knelt, because she had seen Amyas kneel.

“Child, no—do not kneel to me. Come home with us, and let me find out if I am awake or dreaming, alive or dead.” She pulled her hood over her face, turned to walk back, holding Amyas tight by one hand, and Ayacanora by the other. The crowd let them go twenty yards in reverent silence, then burst into a cheer that made the old stone houses of Bideford ring.

Mrs. Leigh stopped short suddenly. “I forgot, Amyas. I will not stand in the way of your duty. Where are your men?”

“Half of them are half-kissed to death by the harbor lasses by now, all the ones that are left.”

“Left?”

“We sailed out a hundred strong, mother. We came home forty-four, all told. Is this real? Is this you? Is this old Bridgeland Street again? As I live, there is Evans the smith, standing in his doorway tankard in hand, just as he did when I was a boy!”

The brawny smith crossed the street to greet them, but stopped short when he saw Amyas alone, no Frank at his side. “Better one than none, madam,” he said, offering rough comfort. Amyas shook his hand, but Mrs. Leigh did not see or hear him at all.

“Mother,” Amyas said, when they crossed the causeway, “we are rich for life.”

“Yes. A martyr’s death was the fittest end for him.”

“I brought home treasure untold. Cary has promised to see to it all tonight.”

“Very well. I only wish Frank could have come home to sleep under our roof again. He was such a kind lad, he loved Frank so—when did he—”

“Died three years ago, within two months of us sailing.”

“Ah, yes. He came to visit me in my sleep, many a time. But you never came. I guessed how it would be, as it should be.”

“But I loved you none the less, mother!”

“I know that, too. You were busy caring for your men, you see, sweet, so your spirit could not wander home to me like his, which was set free. All is as it should be. My maid, do you not find the English cold, after all those hot southern lands?”

“Ayacanora’s heart is warm. She does not think about cold.”

“Warm? Perhaps you will warm my heart for me, then.”

“Would God I could, mother!” Amyas said, half-reproachful.

Mrs. Leigh looked up at his face, and burst into a flood of long-held tears. “Sinful, sinful that I am, to grieve so!”

“Blessed, blessed creature!” Amyas cried, “if you speak that way I shall go mad. I have dreaded this meeting for months, it hung over me like a great black thundercloud, a cliff miles high with its top hidden in mist that I had to climb and dared not. I longed to leap overboard and flee into the sea like a coward, for fear you would ask me if I was not my brother’s keeper, and demand his blood at my hands. Now that it has come, to find you all love, and trust, and patience—mother, it is more than I can bear!” He broke down, weeping so hard his broad shoulders shook.

Mrs. Leigh knew Amyas well enough to understand that such an outburst from his normally quiet, steady nature meant months of terrible hidden struggle, and she forgot all her own grief instantly to soothe him. They walked home arm in arm, while Ayacanora clung fast to the hem of Mrs. Leigh’s cloak like a small child, the bold self-reliance she had learned in the South American forests melting away into shy, trembling modesty, newly arrived in a strange world of strange faces, strange hopes, and strange fears.

“Will your mother love me?” she whispered to Amyas as they stepped through the door of Burrough House.

“Yes. But you must do whatever she tells you.”

Ayacanora pouted. “She will laugh at me, because I am wild.”

“She never laughs at anyone.”

“Humph,” Ayacanora said. “Then I will not be afraid of her. I thought she would be tall, like you. She is not even as big as me.” That did not sound promising for her obedience, but before twenty-four hours had passed, Mrs. Leigh had won her over completely. The girl later explained she had expected the great hero Amyas to have a mother as grand and terrible as the goddess Juno, and instead had found a soft, healing angel.

Her story was soon told to Mrs. Leigh, who, true to her kind heart, never once doubted the girl’s royal Indian identity. The sweet mother had never pressed a prouder, fonder kiss to her son’s forehead than the one she gave him after he confessed he had kept his charge, guarding Ayacanora unspotted, like a gentleman and a Christian.

“Then you have forgiven me, mother?” Amyas asked.

“Years ago, right in this very room, I said ‘what shall I render to the Lord, for giving me two such sons?’ I say it again now. Tell me every last detail of my other son, so that I may honor him as I honor you.”

With the iron strength good women carry in their bones, she made Amyas recount every detail, from Lucy Passmore’s tale to the terrible last night at Guayra. When he finished, she led Ayacanora upstairs to tend to her comforts, moving as calmly as if Frank and Amyas were still small boys sleeping in their cribs in the next room.

She had barely gone upstairs when a loud bang at the front door was followed by Sir Richard Grenville striding unceremoniously into the hall, tall, stately, his usual composure utterly gone. Amyas dropped to his knees instinctively. The stern old warrior was quite unmanned, and as he bent over his godson, a tear dropped from his weathered iron cheek onto Amyas’s own.

“My lad! My glorious lad! Where have you been? Get up, tell me every word. The sailors told me bits and pieces, but I must hear the whole story. I knew you would do something grand.
