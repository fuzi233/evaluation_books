*"Some wishes crossed my mind and dimly cheered it,
And one or two poor melancholy pleasures,
Each in the pale unwarming light of hope,
Silvering its flimsy wing, flew silent by—
Moths in the moonbeam!"*

The morning sun had scarcely touched the rooftops of Milton when Margaret received a letter from Edith. It was as warm and effusive as the writer herself—full of affection and the charming incoherence Margaret had long grown accustomed to. The words danced across the page, brimming with pride and delight in her child, and with playful reproaches for Margaret’s absence.

*"Dearest Margaret,"* it began, *"you simply must come to see my boy! He is the most splendid creature, particularly in the caps you so diligently stitched for him. I flaunt him shamelessly, you know, just to hear fresh praises—though perhaps, just perhaps, there is a little love mixed in with my vanity. But truly, you must come! The air here is pure, the skies never weep, and the music is so gay it lifts the heaviest heart. Even Aunt Hale would find strength in such a place. Three months of sunshine and grapes sweet as honey would surely restore her. And as for Uncle—well, I know he frowns upon soldiers and their revels, but we would hide all such offenses from him, I swear it! Only say the word, and I shall have the band play the dreariest hymns at half-speed if it pleases him."*

Margaret could not help but laugh at Edith’s whimsy, though the ache in her chest deepened. How easy life seemed in those sunlit letters—how far removed from the soot and strain of Milton. For a fleeting moment, she let herself imagine it: the warmth, the laughter, the lightness of being unburdened.

Her amusement faded as her mother entered, leaning heavily on Dixon’s arm. Mrs. Hale’s frailty was more pronounced than ever, her steps slow and unsteady.

"What has you smiling so, Margaret?" she asked, settling onto the sofa with visible effort.

"A letter from Edith," Margaret replied, smoothing her expression. "Shall I read it to you?"

As she did, her mother’s interest flickered—not at Edith’s ramblings, but at the mention of the child. "What name did she give him, I wonder?" Mrs. Hale mused, her mind drifting through possibilities. Margaret indulged her, though her thoughts were already elsewhere.

Then Mr. Thornton arrived.

He came bearing peaches, his presence filling the room with an unspoken tension. His greeting to Margaret was cool, his manner restrained, as though the very sight of her pained him. He spoke only to Mrs. Hale, his voice gentle, his words kind—but his eyes never strayed toward Margaret. And then he was gone, leaving behind a silence that stung.

"Do you know," Mrs. Hale remarked softly, "I find myself growing quite fond of Mr. Thornton."

Margaret’s reply was brittle. "Do you?"

"He has a kindness about him now—a refinement in his bearing."

"He is attentive," Margaret conceded, though the words felt hollow.

Her mother sighed. "I wish Mrs. Thornton would call. She must know I am ill."

Margaret hesitated. "I could ask her, if you like."

But the thought of Frederick’s return loomed between them. "No," Mrs. Hale murmured. "No, we must be careful. When he comes..."

*"When he comes,"* Margaret echoed, firm. "We will keep him safe. I swear it."

The conversation shifted—to Martha’s dismissal, to the hiring of Mary Higgins, to the petty grievances of Milton’s dialect. Mrs. Hale fretted over Margaret’s adoption of factory slang, and Margaret teased her gently, though her heart was not in it.

Then, unwittingly, her mother struck a deeper wound. "Edith is right—it is the smoke of Milton that ails me."

Margaret’s breath caught. Her father had entered unnoticed, and she rushed to fill the silence with lighter talk, not seeing Mr. Thornton’s return behind him.

"Mamma accuses me of vulgarity," she said brightly, "all for using Milton words!"

But Mr. Thornton’s face darkened. Had he overheard? Had he mistaken her meaning? Desperate to clarify, she turned to him directly.

"Surely, Mr. Thornton, you understand—local words have their place. Would you fault me for speaking as those around me do?"

Her attempt was clumsy, and his response was colder still. He addressed only her mother, his promise to bring Mrs. Thornton delivered with stiff courtesy before he departed once more.

Left alone, Margaret burned with frustration. His avoidance was deliberate—each glance averted, each word unspoken, a testament to his wounded pride. And yet, she could not hate him for it. Instead, she felt regret, a quiet longing to bridge the rift between them.

Not love. Never that. But a sorrow for the friendship lost, and a patient hope that time might mend what words had broken.

As for Mr. Thornton, he told himself he despised the sight of her. But the truth was far more dangerous—a sting of pleasure at her presence, a pride in his own restraint, and a heart that, despite all reason, refused to forget her.
