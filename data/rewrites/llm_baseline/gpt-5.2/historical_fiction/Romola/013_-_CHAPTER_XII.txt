Tito’s footfall on the worn Florentine stones had the buoyancy of a man who has slipped the noose for another day. The clutch of immediate fear was gone; his spirits sprang back, light and glittering as oil spread on water. He was going to Romola.

And yet—threaded beneath that easy gladness—Romola’s life rose before him like a fresco of patient hands: the steady ordering of her father’s hours, the gentle endurance of his demands, the long fidelity to tasks that yielded no applause. It was the sort of goodness Tito admired as one admires a well-built hearth—warm, necessary, and not meant, in his imagination, to burn him. He was not in love with wickedness; he still had the clean appetite of youth for ordinary joys, for the taste of comfort and the scent of praise. The poison he had taken into his life would work slowly, drop by drop, like bitter herbs steeping in sweet wine until the sweetness itself became suspect.

He meant, with an almost childlike obstinacy, that everything should go on as before: his smile should win him friends, his learning should win him “golden opinions,” his pliancy should keep him in harmony with the people he cared for. Above all, he cared for Romola. There might be richer alliances in Florence for a young Greek with a ready mind and an insinuating grace, but there was no woman like her—no other gaze so clear and honest, no other presence that could quiet him as surely as a lute-string, once touched, imposes its rhythm on the air. When he neared her home, anticipation itself began to play on him like those vibrations one feels in the bones before one hears the full chord.

He mounted the stone stairs; Maso was behind him; the door was still between him and the room where Romola sat. And already Bardo’s blind, vigilant voice met him.

“Welcome, Tito mio,” it said, with a new vigor, as if some inner spring had been wound tighter since their first labors together. “Thou hast brought fresh manuscript, doubtless; but since we were talking last night I have had new ideas. We must take a wider scope—we must go back upon our footsteps.”

Tito entered, offered his reverence to Romola with that careful courtesy which never forgot itself, and went—by habit and by policy—straight to the old man’s chair. He placed his hand within the palm that was held out to receive it and took the low cross-legged leather seat close to Bardo’s elbow, as if he belonged there.

“Yes,” he said in his gentle tone, “I have brought the new manuscript; but that can wait your pleasure. I have young limbs, you know, and can walk back up the hill without difficulty.”

He did not look at Romola while he spoke, yet he felt her eyes on him—warm, delighted, as if the very words “young limbs” were a promise that life would be generous.

“That is well said, my son.” Bardo had begun of late to call him so, and the word had weight—patronage, affection, possession. “And I perceive with gladness that thou dost not shrink from labor, without which, the poet has wisely said, life has given nothing to mortals. It is too often the palma sine pulvere—the prize without the dust—that attracts young ambition. But what says the Greek? ‘In the morning of life, work; in the mid-day, give counsel; in the evening, pray.’ I might be thought to have reached that helpless evening; yet not so, while I have counsel within me which is yet unspoken. For my mind, as I have often said, was shut up as by a dam; the waters lay dark and motionless; but thou, my Tito, hast opened a duct for them, and they rush forward with a force that surprises myself.”

As he spoke he stooped toward the manuscript that had rolled; his ear, jealous and keen, caught Tito’s slight movement.

“And now,” Bardo continued, “what I want is this: that we should go over our preliminary ground again, with a wider scheme of comment and illustration. Else I may lose opportunities which I now see retrospectively, and which may never occur again. Thou markest what I am saying, Tito?”

Tito might have been forgiven a hidden sigh. To turn back was to unweave and reweave, to knead again dough already shaped, to labor for hours in that minute erudition the age prized—laborious as picking bones from a fish for a delicate palate. But impatience was not his nature, and Romola’s nearness was like a steady perfume—quieting, pleasant, making even the stale dust of parchment seem part of a rich domestic air.

“Assuredly,” he said. “You wish to enlarge your comments on certain passages we have cited.”

“Not only so. I wish to introduce an occasional excursus,” said Bardo, warming, his hands alive in the air as if he still saw the lines. “Where we have noticed an author to whom I have given special study; for I may die too soon to achieve any separate work. And this is not a time for scholarly integrity and well-sifted learning to lie idle, when it is not only rash ignorance that we have to fear, but when there are men like Calderino who have recourse to impudent falsities of citation to serve the ends of their vanity and secure a triumph to their own mistakes.”

His voice bit sharply on the names, as if the syllables themselves were bitter. The room around them, lined with orderly ranks of volumes—leather, vellum, wood—had its own hush, broken only by the faint crackle of the city beyond, and the soft, dry sound of pages handled. In that silence, the old man’s passion sounded the more vehement.

“Wherefore,” he said, “I think it not well that we should let slip the occasion that lies under our hands. And now we will turn back to the point where we have cited the passage from Thucydides, and I wish thee, by way of preliminary, to go with me through all my notes on the Latin translation made by Lorenzo Valla—for which the incomparable Pope Nicholas the Fifth, with whose personal notice I was honored while I was yet young, paid him five hundred gold scudi.”

The coins seemed to clink in Bardo’s memory; Tito could almost hear them against a table, bright and heavy.

“But inasmuch as Valla, though otherwise of dubious fame, is held in high honor for severe scholarship,” Bardo pursued, “it is the more needful that his name should not be as a stamp warranting false wares. Therefore I would introduce an excursus on Thucydides, wherein my castigations of Valla’s text may find a fitting place. My Romola, thou wilt reach the needful volumes—thou knowest them—on the fifth shelf of the cabinet.”

Romola rose at once; and Tito, rising with her, said, “I will reach them, if you will point them out,” and followed her quickly into the adjoining small room.

It was a narrower chamber, but the same severe abundance: shelves close-ranked with books in perfect order, each spine a familiar ridge beneath Romola’s hands, as if the room itself were an extension of her father’s mind—stored, classified, immovable. The air carried the mingled scents of old glue, rubbed leather, and the faint mineral dryness of paper that has drunk years of Florentine sunlight.

“There they are,” Romola said, pointing upward. “Every book is just where it was when my father ceased to see them.”

Tito did not climb at once. He stood beside her, and because they had never stood together in this smaller room before, the place seemed suddenly intimate—like a pantry entered after the household has gone to sleep, where even the jars and hanging herbs seem to listen.

“I hope,” Romola went on, turning to him with grave trust, “I hope he will not weary you; this work makes him so happy.”

“And me too, Romola,” Tito murmured, letting the softness of his voice do what a bolder approach would have risked. “If you will only let me say—I love you—if you will only think me worth loving a little.”

His face, dark and beautiful, came nearer than it had ever been; his tenderness looked up at her like a plea. Romola’s calm, earnest eyes held his without coquetry, as if love, once recognized, must be spoken plainly.

“I do love you,” she whispered.

The words were simple, but her voice—never before in her life had it sunk to that low, intimate murmur—made them feel like something newly minted, warm from the press of the soul. It seemed to them both that they looked at each other a long while before her lips moved again; and yet it was only a breath before she added, “I know now what it is to be happy.”

Their faces met; the dark curls touched, and for an instant mingled with the rippling gold of her hair. Then, quick as lightning—because the world outside this moment still existed, because Bardo’s ear was jealous and time was theirs only by stealth—Tito set his foot on a projecting ledge of the shelves and reached down the needed volumes.

When they went back, they were content to be silent and separate. That first mutual certainty—like waters once joined—had its own sweetness, the more exquisite because it was not immediately disturbed by craving.

Bardo, eager, had not yet become impatient.

“You have the volumes, my Romola?” he said as they returned. “And now thou wilt get thy pen ready; for, as Tito marks off the scholia we determine on extracting, it will be well for thee to copy them without delay—numbering them carefully, mind, to correspond with the numbers in the text which he will write.”

So the household rite resumed itself: the old blind scholar enthroned in his chair, Tito at the leggio where he read and wrote, Romola at the table just before him, ready to pass what her father needed, to lift a volume from his knee, to lay it aside, to keep the labor moving as a cook keeps a sauce from catching—stirring, watching, measuring, always attentive. Ink smelled sharp and metallic; the quill whispered; parchment gave its dry resistance; and beyond, Florence murmured—cart-wheels on stone, a distant call, a sudden laugh that died away.

They had always sat so since the work began, but today everything was altered by what had passed in the narrow room. To be opposite each other now—Tito taking a book from Romola’s hand, her fingers close to his—was like tasting bread one has eaten every day and discovering suddenly the sweetness of the grain. Yet neither sought to steal more than their task allowed: Romola’s love was too deep and calm to require petty tokens. It encompassed him like a rich evening light that makes even labor beautiful.

Two hours passed. The daylight thinned, as if someone were slowly turning down a lamp; the words on the page began to drown in grey. They were just desisting because the light failed when the door opened.

A figure entered that jarred against the sober room as a street-drum jars against a cloister chant.

It was a short, stout woman of about fifty, black-eyed, brisk, and richly dressed. A black velvet berretta, embroidered with pearls, hugged her head; beneath it, massive black braids were arranged with elaborate care, surmounting a small bulging forehead and falling in heavy plaited curves over her ears. Her cheeks bore an astonishing carmine tint high on the fat flesh, the rest of her face sallow by contrast. Three rows of pearls lay upon the cushion of her neck, and beneath them a gold necklace. Her trailing black velvet gown and long drooping sleeves of rose-colored damask were slightly faded, but they spoke to any practised eye of costly work patiently done—six months of a craftsman’s labor. A rose-colored petticoat showed itself with deliberate propriety, its fringe dimmed white, its seed-pearl arabesques still catching the failing light. A handsome coral rosary hung from one side of a belt that emerged into certainty at a silver clasp wrought in niello; from the other side depended a crimson velvet scarsella stitched with pearls. In her little fat right hand—soft as if made of paste—she carried a small book of devotions glittering with velvet, pearls, and silver.

Tito recognized Monna Brigida at once; she was too frequent here to startle him. She was a cousin of Bardo’s dead wife and had helped nurse and mind Romola in her girlhood. For that service she was pardoned, though Bardo had banished “feminine triviality” from his house as one banishes smoke from a library.

Romola turned with an affectionate smile and rose to draw a leather seat a little farther from her father’s chair, to spare his ear the coming torrent.

“La cugina?” Bardo asked, hearing the short steps and the sweep of drapery.

“Yes, yes, it is your cousin,” Monna Brigida said in her alert voice, raising her fingers smilingly at Tito and lifting her face for Romola’s kiss. “Always the troublesome cousin breaking in on your wisdom.” She sat and began to fan herself with the white veil hanging over her arm. “Well, well! If I didn’t bring you some news of the world now and then, I do believe you’d forget there was anything in life but these mouldy ancients—who want sprinkling with holy water if all I hear about them is true.”

Her voice filled the room as a strong spice fills a stew, overpowering the delicate scent of parchment and ink.

“Not but what the world is bad enough nowadays,” she rattled on, “for the scandals that turn up under one’s nose at every corner. I don’t want to hear and see such things, but one can’t go about with one’s head in a bag. And it was only yesterday—well, well, you needn’t burst out at me, Bardo, I’m not going to tell anything. If I’m not as wise as the three kings, I know how many legs go into one boot.”

She drew breath and plunged on again, as if breath itself were a mere pause between waves.

“But Florence is a wicked city—is it not true, Messer Tito? for you go into the world. Not but what one must sin a little—Messer Domeneddio expects that of us, else what are the blessed sacraments for? And what I say is, we’ve got to reverence the saints and not to set ourselves up as if we could be like them, else life would be unbearable—especially if things go on after this new fashion.”

Her fan fluttered; pearls shifted softly against velvet with each movement.

“For what do you think? I’ve been at the wedding to-day—Dianora Acciajoli’s with the young Albizzi that there has been so much talk of—and everybody wondered at its being to-day instead of yesterday; but, cieli! such a wedding as it was might have been put off till next Quaresima for a penance. There was the bride looking like a white nun—not so much as a pearl about her—and the bridegroom as solemn as San Giuseppe. It’s true. And half the people invited were Piagnoni—funeral mourners they call them now—these new saints of Fra Girolamo’s making.”

She sniffed, as if the memory had a smell—wax, damp cloth, and moral severity.

“And to think of two families like the Albizzi and the Acciajoli taking up such notions, when they could afford to wear the best! Well, well, they invited me—but they could do no other, seeing my husband was Luca Antonio’s uncle by the mother’s side—and a pretty time I had of it while we waited under the canopy in front of the house before they let us in. I couldn’t stand in my clothes without giving offence. There was Monna Berta—who has had worse secrets in her time than any I could tell of myself—looking askance at me from under her hood like a pinzochera, and telling me to read the Frate’s book about widows, from which she had found great guidance.”

Monna Brigida’s voice rose on the word “widows” with the indignation of one whose very wardrobe had been put on trial.

“Holy Madonna! it seems as if widows had nothing to do now but to buy their coffins and think it a thousand years till they get into them, instead of enjoying themselves a little when they’ve got their hands free for the first time. And what do you think was the music we had, to make our dinner lively? A long discourse from Fra Domenico of San Marco about the doctrines of their blessed Fra Girolamo—the three doctrines we are all to get by heart. He kept marking them off on his fingers till he made my flesh creep.”

She lifted her own fingers as if the gesture itself had pursued her home.

“The first is, Florence, or the Church—I don’t know which, for first he said one and then the other—shall be scourged. If he means the pestilence, the Signory ought to put a stop to such preaching, for it’s enough to raise the swelling under one’s arms with fright. Then he says Florence is to be regenerated; but what will be the good of that when we’re all dead of the plague, or something else? And the third thing—and what he said oftenest—is that it’s all to be in our days. He marked that off on his thumb till he made me tremble like the very jelly before me.”

At the word “jelly” her mouth softened, remembering, despite herself.

“They had jellies, to be sure, with the arms of the Albizzi and the Acciajoli raised on them in all colours—they’ve not turned the world quite upside down yet. But all their talk is, we are to go back to the old ways. Up starts Francesco Valori—whom I’ve danced with in the Via Larga when he was a bachelor and as fond of the Medici as anybody—and he makes a speech about the old times, before the Florentines had left off crying ‘Popolo’ and begun to cry ‘Palle’—as if that had anything to do with a wedding!—and how we ought to keep to the rules the Signory laid down heaven knows when, that we were not to wear this and that, and not to eat this and that—”

At “not to eat,” her fan stopped for a moment, as if even her body protested at the thought of regulations on the table—Florentine tables with their sugared almonds, their spiced wines, their sauces warmed over coals and scented with rosemary, their meats glazed and their fruits piled like painted offerings. The austerity she described was not merely moral; it was an assault on taste, on the remembered sweetness of feast-days.

“—and how our manners were corrupted,” she went on, “and we read bad books; though he can’t say that of me—”

“Stop, cousin!” Bardo cut in, his imperious voice snapping like a shut volume. He had a remark to make, and only a sharp command could dam that stream.

Monna Brigida started, pursed her mouth, and stared at him with round eyes, wounded and ready.

“Francesco Valori is not altogether wrong,” Bardo said, his tone changing to that stern didactic measure Romola knew so well. “Bernardo rates him not highly, and is rather of opinion that he christens private grudges by the name of public zeal; though I must admit that my good Bernardo is too slow of belief in that unalloyed patriotism which was found in all its lustre amongst the ancients. But it is true, Tito, that our manners have degenerated somewhat from that noble frugality which is the parent of true magnificence.”

He spoke of frugality not as starvation but as discipline—the measured hand that can be grand when grandeur is worthy.

“For men, as I hear, will now spend on the transient show of a giostra sums which would suffice to found a library and confer a lasting possession on mankind. Still, I conceive it remains true of us Florentines that we have more of that magnanimous sobriety which abhors a trivial lavishness that it may be grandly open-handed on grand occasions than can be found in any other city of Italy. The Neapolitan and Milanese courtiers laugh at the scarcity of our plate, and think scorn of our great families for borrowing from each other that furniture of the table at their entertainments. But in the vain laughter of folly, wisdom hears half its applause.”

“Laughter, indeed!” Monna Brigida burst out the instant he paused, the word springing from her as if she had been forced to hold it in her throat. “If anybody wanted laughter at the wedding to-day they were disappointed. When young Niccolò Macchiavelli tried to make a joke and told stories out of Franco Sacchetti’s book—how it was no use for the Signoria to make rules for us women because we were cleverer than all the painters and architects and doctors of logic in the world, for we could make black look white and yellow look pink and crooked look straight, and if anything was forbidden we could find a new name for it—Holy Virgin! the Piagnoni looked more dismal than before, and somebody said Sacchetti’s book was wicked.”

Her voice carried contempt for the new piety that could not bear a joke, as if laughter itself were a sin.

“Well, I don’t read it—they can’t accuse me of reading anything. Save me from going to a wedding again if that’s to be the fashion, for all of us who were not Piagnoni were as comfortable as wet chickens.”

She fanned harder, making a small hiss in the air.

“I was never caught in a worse trap but once before, and that was when I went to hear their precious Frate last Quaresima in San Lorenzo. Perhaps I never told you about it, Messer Tito? It almost freezes my blood when I think of it. How he rated us poor women! and the men too, to tell the truth, but I didn’t mind that so much. He called us cows, and lumps of flesh, and wantons, and mischief-makers—and I could just bear that, for there were plenty others more fleshy and spiteful than I was—though every now and then his voice shook the very bench under me like a trumpet.”

As she spoke, the room seemed to tremble again, as if that trumpet had followed her through the streets to Bardo’s calm study.

“But then he came to the false hair, and—O misericordia!—he made a picture—I see it now—of a young woman lying a pale corpse, and us light-minded widows—of course he meant me as well as the rest, for I had my plaits on, for if one is getting old, one doesn’t want to look as ugly as the Befana—us widows rushing up to the corpse, like bare-pated vultures, and cutting off its young dead hair to deck our old heads with.”

She shuddered and pressed her lips tight, as if the taste of that image were bitter on her tongue.

“Oh, the dreams I had after that! And then he cried, and wrung his hands at us, and I cried too. And to go home, and to take off my jewels—this very clasp—and everything, and to make them into a packet—fù tutt’uno; and I was within a hair of sending them to the Good Men of Saint Martin to give to the poor, but, by heaven’s mercy, I bethought me of going first to my confessor, Fra Cristoforo, at Santa Croce. He told me it was all the work of the devil, this preaching and prophesying of their Fra Girolamo, and the Dominicans were trying to turn the world upside down, and I was never to go and hear him again, else I must do penance for it. For the great preachers Fra Mariano and Fra Menico had shown how Fra Girolamo preached lies—and that was true, for I heard them both in the Duomo—and how the Pope’s dream of San Francesco propping up the Church with his arms was being fulfilled still, and the Dominicans were beginning to pull it down.”

Her pearls lifted with her breath, then settled again.

“Well and good: I went away con Dio, and made myself easy. I am not going to be frightened by a Frate Predicatore again. And all I say is, I wish it hadn’t been the Dominicans that poor Dino joined years ago, for then I should have been glad when I heard them say he was come back—”

“Silenzio!” Bardo’s voice rang out loud and shaken, as if someone had struck a brass plate in the quiet room.

Romola half started from her chair; her hands clasped; her eyes flew to Tito, and in that look there was a child’s sudden appeal and a woman’s restrained anguish. Monna Brigida gave a little scream and bit her lip.

“Donna!” Bardo cried again. “Hear once more my will. Bring no reports about that name to this house; and thou, Romola, I forbid thee to ask. My son is dead.”

His whole frame seemed to vibrate with passion, the blind body making visible what the blind eyes could not. In that moment the room’s former peace was gone; even the books, standing in their ranks, seemed to share the shock, as if a secret had been struck like a hidden string.

No one spoke. Monna Brigida lifted shoulders and hands in mute dismay; then, as quietly as possible, she rose. She nodded many times at Tito and Romola, motioning them not to move, and stole out with the guilty haste of a fat spaniel who has barked at the wrong time.

In the deepening silence, Tito’s mind worked with the speed of a knife flashing in skilled hands.

Bardo’s son was not truly dead, then—not dead as Tito had supposed. A monk. “Come back.” Fra Luca—yes: it was the likeness, the shared bone and line, that had made that face seem half-known to him when he saw it. The thought struck cold and hard.

If only he were dead at Fiesole at that moment.

The selfish wish rose irresistibly, thrusting itself before every other thought like a hand snatching the choicest morsel. Bardo’s rigid will would keep Romola from seeking her brother—but it could not prevent the brother, if living and returned, from speaking what he knew to others. And worse: the very coupling of Romola’s name with Tito Melema’s might be enough to awaken Fra Luca’s suspicions, especially since he had carried Tito’s description as an index at his neck.

No. Nothing but Fra Luca’s death could remove all danger.

Yet Fra Luca’s death was highly probable. Tito let his mind settle back, after the first shock, into that confident hope as into a warm bed: probability could be made into certainty if one waited—if one was careful.

They sat in silence for many minutes, the twilight growing thicker, as if the city outside had exhaled a darker breath. At last Romola ventured, gently, as if to restore the broken order with the simplest domestic act—

“Shall I light the lamp, father, and shall we go on?”

“No, my Romola,” Bardo said, his voice quieter but still strained. “We will work no more to-night. Tito, come and sit by me here.”

Tito left the reading-desk and seated himself on Bardo’s other side, close to his left elbow.

“Come nearer to me, figliuola mia,” Bardo said after a pause.

Romola came and sat on a low stool, resting her arm on her father’s right knee so that he might lay his hand on her hair, as he loved to do—an old habit, tender and possessive, like a blessing spoken by touch. The room was dim; the unsaid words made it heavier than darkness.

“Tito,” Bardo began, and there was a trembling in him that pride could not wholly hide, “I never told thee that I had once a son.”

He had forgotten—forgotten, in the storm of their first interview, what he had let slip then; now emotion forced confession out of him as a wound forces blood.

“But he left me—he is dead to me. I have disowned him for ever. He was a ready scholar as thou art, but more fervid and impatient, and yet sometimes rapt and self-absorbed, like a flame fed by some fitful source. From the very first he showed a disposition to turn away his eyes from the clear lights of reason and philosophy and to prostrate himself under the influences of a dim mysticism which eludes all rules of human duty as it eludes all argument.”

His hand on Romola’s hair pressed, then trembled, as if memory itself were something he wanted to hold down and could not.

“And so it ended,” he said. “We will speak no more of him. He is dead to me. I wish his face could be blotted from that world of memory in which the distant seems to grow clearer and the near to fade.”

He paused. Neither Romola nor Tito dared to speak. Bardo’s voice was too tremulous, his poise too uncertain, like a vessel that might spill if touched.

After a moment he raised his hand, found Tito’s shoulder, and let it rest there, as if seeking steadiness from the living warmth beside him. Then he went on, with an effort to be calmer, pouring out what pride would have hidden, because the heart, once shaken, will have its utterance.
