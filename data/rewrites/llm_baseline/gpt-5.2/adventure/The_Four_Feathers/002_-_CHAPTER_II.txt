Thirteen years later—again in June—Harry Feversham’s health was drunk, though with less ceremony than in boyhood, and with no clatter of a large company. The toast was pledged in a dining room set high in a great, blunt-shouldered block of London masonry that rose above Westminster like a dark cliff with window-ledges of light. A man crossing St. James’s Park at night, passing the suspension bridge, might look up at those stacked, shining panes and imagine a mountain hollowed out in the city, and busy, secret hands working within.

On the tenth floor Harry had taken a flat for his furlough from India; and in that dining room, with its dark, restful furniture and a fire burning because the weather had turned sharp and raw in defiance of the calendar, four men sat after dinner with cigars and glasses. The room held its own small weather: the resinous scent of coal, a faint sweetness from the wine, the warm breath of the hearth. Beyond the bay window—blinds left up—London spread in tiers of lamps and moving light, and the low, ceaseless carriage-rumble rose like surf.

Harry Feversham looked much as he had in youth, save for the fair moustache that threw his darker hair into stronger contrast and the settled, athletic length of a grown man. His face was still the same face that once had been watched and weighed with such intentness; only the years had tightened it into its adult lines.

Of his companions, two were brother-officers on leave, gathered that afternoon at his club and brought home as one brings home the last good talk of the day. Captain Trench was small, already thinning on top, with a sharp, resourceful face and black eyes that never seemed at rest. Lieutenant Willoughby, by contrast, wore the heavy look of stubborn vacancy: round forehead, thick snub nose, and protruding eyes that gave him an air of invincible stupidity. He spoke rarely, and never quite to the point at hand—rather to some earlier point he had been turning over in his head as one might revolve a button between finger and thumb. All the while he twisted the ends of his moustache upward toward his eyes, as if trying to make ferocity out of dullness. One dismissed him at first glance, then reconsidered him: there was harm in his kind of man, because he was born as obstinate as he was slow. Few ideas, and he clung to them; argument slid off him unheard, and behind those vacant eyes his crippled thoughts went on satisfying him.

The fourth was Durrance of the East Surrey—Harry’s friend—who had come in answer to a telegram.

It was June, 1882. In drawing rooms and clubs, civilians spoke of Egypt with anxious brows; soldiers spoke of it with the eagerness that comes of hearing distant drums. Arabi Pasha was strengthening Alexandria’s defences in spite of threats, and farther south another danger was gathering—swelling, dark and charged, like a thundercloud above the dry plains. A year earlier a young Dongolawi, Mohammed Ahmed, slight and tall, had moved through the villages of the White Nile preaching the coming of a Saviour with the fervour of a revivalist. The men and women ground down by Turkish tax-gatherers listened and found his promise everywhere: repeated, so the story went, in the whisper through withered grass; stamped, by miracle or imagination, upon the eggs they gathered. In 1882 he declared himself that Saviour and won his first battles.

“There will be trouble,” said Trench; and on that text three of the four talked, turning it this way and that as soldiers do, half forecasting and half tasting the thing they are trained for.

But in a pause—rare, and a little awkward—Harry spoke of something else.

“I’m very glad you were all able to dine with me to-night,” he said, with the faint embarrassment of a man bringing private news into public company. “I telegraphed to Castleton as well—an officer of ours,” he explained to Durrance, “but he was dining with a big man in the War Office, and leaves for Scotland afterwards, so he could not come. I have news of a sort.”

They leaned forward, still full of Egypt and the rumour of marching. Harry’s news, however, had nothing to do with war.

“I only reached London this morning from Dublin,” he said. “I have been some weeks in Dublin.”

Durrance lifted his eyes from the tablecloth and looked at him steadily.

“Yes?” he asked, as if there were only one possible ending to that sentence and he meant to hear it plainly.

“I have come back engaged to be married.”

Durrance raised his glass and drank.

“Well, here’s luck to you, Harry,” he said; and that was all.

The words were curt, but nothing was missing from them in Harry’s hearing. Between those two men affectionate phrases had never been currency. Their friendship was a tool, sound and trustworthy, put into their hands for hard use; they knew its value and did not talk of it. Both were grateful for it as for a gift they had not earned. Both, too, understood that it might demand sacrifice—and that if it did, the sacrifice would be made without announcement, without pose, and without a word to make it heavier.

“Thank you, Jack,” said Harry. “I’m glad of your good wishes. It was you who introduced me to Ethne; I can’t forget it.”

Durrance set his glass down carefully. For a moment he sat with his hands on the table edge, eyes lowered, as though he were choosing words by touch.

“Yes,” he said levelly. “I did you a good turn then.”

He seemed about to add more, and to be uncertain how to put it; but Trench’s quick, practical voice cut in with a question that saved him.

“Will this make any difference?” Trench asked.

Harry put his cigar back between his lips before replying, as if the small ritual gave him time.

“You mean, shall I leave the service?” he asked slowly. “I don’t know.”

At that, Durrance rose and crossed to the bay window, turning his back on the room and on the three men behind him. Harry, mistaking the abrupt movement for a reproach, aimed his explanation not at Trench but at Durrance’s broad shoulders.

“I don’t know,” he repeated. “It will need thought. There’s much to be said. On the one side, of course, there’s my father—my career, such as it is. On the other hand there is her father—Dermod Eustace.”

“He wishes you to chuck your commission?” Willoughby asked.

“He has no doubt the Irishman’s objection to constituted authority,” Trench said with a laugh. “But need you subscribe to it, Feversham?”

“It isn’t merely that.” Harry’s voice stayed directed, stubbornly and a little defensively, toward the window. “Dermod is old. His estates are going to ruin. And there are other things. You know, Jack?”

He had to repeat the appeal; and even then Durrance answered without turning, as if his mind were elsewhere.

“Yes, I know,” he said; and then added, like a man quoting some foolish catchword to fill a gap, “If you want any whisky, rap twice on the floor with your foot. The servants understand.”

“Precisely,” Harry said, taking it as assent, and went on weighing his words as though placing them carefully on a scale. “Besides, there’s Ethne herself. Dermod for once did an appropriate thing when he gave her that name. She’s of her country—and more, of her county. She has the love of it in her bones. I don’t think she could be quite happy in India, or indeed in any place not within reach of Donegal: the smell of the peat, its streams, the brown friendliness of its hills. One has to consider that.”

He paused for a response. None came. So he continued, and if Durrance did not truly listen, Trench listened with the close attention of a man who likes reasons set out in order.

Durrance, meanwhile, had no thought of reproach. He knew Harry was speaking; he even wished, fiercely and secretly, that he would go on speaking a little longer—anything, so long as it kept the room’s eyes off him. He stood staring out at the city. Across from the window the glare from Pall Mall struck up into the low sky. Chains of light banked one above another as the town rose northward; below, the traffic made a constant, battering murmur, a million wheels and hoofbeats blended into one heavy sound. Far down at his feet lay St. James’s Park—silent and black, a quiet pool of darkness within glitter and noise.

He wanted to escape into that darkness, into the secrecy of trees and open air where the ear could find its own measure again, away from the unending grind and roar that pressed on the mind. But he could not leave without remark. So he kept his back turned, leaned his forehead against the cold glass, and held himself still. He was face to face with one of those sacrifices that must not be mentioned, and no sign must betray.

Harry’s explanations, seriously considered and sober, were enough at last for Trench.

“Well,” Trench said, lifting his glass again, “I drink to you, Feversham, with all the proper sentiments.”

“I too, old man,” Willoughby added obediently, following his senior’s lead.

They drank; their glasses rattled softly on the table. As the sound died, there came a knock at the door.

Trench and Willoughby looked up. Durrance turned from the window. Harry said, “Come in,” and his servant brought him a telegram on a small tray.

Harry tore the envelope open without care—read the message with the same careless motion—and then sat very still with the pink slip in his hand. His face changed at once, growing grave, not in the stunned way of a man struck down, but in the quiet, deliberate way of a man thinking hard.

A complete silence fell. His three guests looked away as if by instinct. Durrance turned back to the window; Willoughby twisted his moustache and stared at the ceiling; Trench shifted his chair and fixed his eyes on the fire. Each posture carried suspense, like men listening for a second knock.

“There is no answer,” Harry said at last; and then was silent again.

Once he lifted his head and looked at Trench as though he meant to speak, but thought better of it and dropped his eyes back to the telegram.

Then the silence was cut—not by any of the men in the room, but by the world outside. From the parade ground of Wellington Barracks the drums and fifes sounded the tattoo through the open window with startling clearness—sharp as a summons. The music diminished as the band marched away across gravel, then swelled again, then faded.

Harry did not move, but his face took on the look of a man listening and listening thoughtfully, as he had read thoughtfully. Years afterward, each of the three guests would see that moment again with an odd, indelible vividness: the warm, homely fire; the open window; London’s layers of light; Harry sitting with the telegram in his hand; and the tattoo sounding first like a command, then dwindling until it was small and pretty, as though it beckoned where a moment ago it had ordered.

Just before the last notes died, Harry rose abruptly. He crumpled the telegram in his hands and tossed it into the fire. Then he leaned back against the chimney-piece, a little to one side, and said again, as though he were summing up the earlier argument and pushing the message away into the same uncertainty—

“I don’t know.”

The spell broke. The room breathed again. But the fire caught the telegram and shook it so it moved like a living thing in pain. It twisted; part of it unrolled. For a second a smooth strip lay open, lit by yellow flame and not yet touched, and two or three words sprang clear, starkly legible, out of the glare. Then the flame seized even that, and it shrivelled into black tatters.

Trench had been staring into the fire all the while.

“You return to Dublin, I suppose?” Durrance asked. He had come back into the room again, and like the others he felt an unexplained relief, as if danger had been named and therefore postponed.

“To Dublin? No,” Harry said. “I go to Donegal in three weeks’ time. There is to be a dance. It is hoped you will come.”

“I’m not sure that I can manage it,” Durrance replied. “There is just a chance, I believe—should trouble come in the East—that I may go out on the staff.”

So talk swung back to Egypt and the likelihood of peace or war, and stayed there until the boom of Westminster’s clock told them it was eleven.

Trench rose on the last stroke; Willoughby and Durrance followed.

“I shall see you to-morrow,” Durrance said to Harry.

“As usual,” Harry answered.

They went down together and walked across the Park, their steps softened by the night air. At the corner of Pall Mall they separated: Durrance up St. James’s Street, Trench and Willoughby across into St. James’s Square.

There Trench, to Willoughby’s surprise—for he was not a demonstrative man—slipped his arm through Willoughby’s.

“You know Castleton’s address?” Trench asked.

“Albemarle Street,” Willoughby said, and gave the number.

“He leaves Euston at twelve o’clock. It is now ten minutes past eleven,” Trench said, his brisk mind already measuring distances and minutes. “Are you curious, Willoughby? I confess to curiosity. I am an inquisitive, methodical person; and when a man gets a telegram bidding him tell Trench something and he tells Trench nothing, I am curious—as a philosopher—to know what that something is. Castleton is the only other officer of our regiment in London. It is likely, therefore, that the telegram came from Castleton. Castleton, too, was dining with a big man from the War Office. I think that if we take a hansom to Albemarle Street, we shall just catch Castleton upon his doorstep.”

Willoughby understood very little of Trench’s chain of thought; nevertheless he agreed cordially.

“I think it would be prudent,” he said; and he hailed a passing cab.

A moment later the two men were driving toward Albemarle Street, the wheels striking sparks of sound from the stones as London’s night-noise closed around them again.
