Not long after, as the small household at Tergou sat down to dinner, Luke Peterson burst through the door, dust-covered and breathless. “Good people,” he gasped, “Mistress Catherine is urgently needed in Rotterdam!”

“My name is Catherine,” replied the woman at the table, her voice calm but firm. “Kate, it will be Margaret.”

“Ay, dame,” Luke panted, “she said to me, 'Good Luke, hurry to Tergou, find Eli the hosier, and beg his wife Catherine to come to me—God’s love!' I didn’t wait for daylight.”

“Holy saints!” exclaimed the wife, turning sharply to her husband. “He has come home, Kate? No—he would have told us. What could it possibly be?”

She spun one theory after another, each more frantic than the last.

“Perhaps the young man can tell us,” ventured Kate timidly.

“That I can,” Luke declared. “Why, her babe is dying. And she was so wrapped up in it!”

Catherine sprang from her seat. “What ails him?”

“I know not,” Luke answered. “But it’s been growing worse—peaking and pining these past days.”

A fleeting glance of satisfaction passed between Cornelis and Sybrandt—so quick that Catherine did not notice. Her eyes were fixed on her husband.

“Now, Eli,” she said sharply, “if you say one word against this, we’ll quarrel after all these years.”

“Who opposes you, foolish woman?” Eli retorted. “Go quarrel with your own shadow while I go borrow Peter’s mule.”

“Bless you, my good man! Bless you! You’ve never failed me yet. Now eat your dinner who can, while I prepare.” She took Luke back with her in the cart, and along the way questioned him relentlessly—now stern, now coaxing—until she had drawn every scrap of knowledge from his mind.

At the gate of the house, Margaret met her, pale and trembling, throwing her arms around her neck and gazing into her face with desperate pleading.

“Come,” Catherine said, scanning her closely, “he lives—thank God.”

She looked from the frail child to the hollow-eyed mother, then back again. “Lucky you sent for me,” she said. “The child is poisoned.”

“Poisoned? By whom?”

“By you,” Catherine replied without hesitation. “You’ve been fretting.”

“No, indeed, mother,” Margaret protested. “How can I help it?”

“Don’t tell me,” Catherine snapped. “A nursing mother must not fret. She must turn her thoughts from sorrow to the comfort in her lap. Do you not know that when a mother torments herself, the child suffers? This comes from your reading and writing—idle crafts fit for men, but they block useful knowledge from women. The child must be weaned.”

“Oh, you cruel woman!” Margaret cried, furious. “I regret sending for you. Would you rob me of the only joy left in my life? While I nurse Gerard, I forget I am the most wretched creature beneath the sun.”

“That you do not,” Catherine shot back. “Or he would not be like this.”

“Mother!” Margaret whispered, imploring.

“It is hard,” Catherine said, softening slightly. “But think: would it not be harder to look down and see his sweet little face staring up at you from a coffin?”

“Oh, Jesu!”

“And how could you face your other troubles with your heart full and your lap empty?”

“Oh, mother,” Margaret sobbed, “I consent to anything—only save my boy.”

“There, now,” Catherine said, pleased. “That’s a good girl. Trust me—I see clearer than you.”

But there was one more consent required—the baby’s—and he proved stubborn.

“There,” Margaret murmured, feigning sorrow, “he loves me too well.”

Yet Catherine was ready for both of them. As she walked through the streets, she noticed a healthy young woman sitting outside her door, cradling an infant. She approached and explained the situation. Would she nurse the failing child temporarily until arrangements were made?

The woman smiled and agreed, placing her own child in the cradle and stepping inside. She curtsied, and Catherine handed the sick babe to her.

The woman examined the child, pitied him, cooed over him, and began to nurse him as though he were her own.

Margaret, stunned by the woman’s confidence, cast a rueful glance at Catherine and broke into tears.

The nurse looked up. “What’s wrong? You told me the mother wasn’t unwilling.”

“She isn’t,” Catherine replied. “She’s just a fool. Don’t heed her. And you, Margaret—I’m ashamed of you.”

“You’re a cruel, heartless woman,” Margaret wept.

“Those who guide the weak must be firm,” Catherine replied. “And forgive me—but you are not my flesh and blood. Your son is.”

After letting the words settle, she added, “Come now—she’s giving up her own milk to save yours, and all you can do is cry in front of her. Out, fie—for shame!”

“Nay, wife,” the nurse said gently. “Thank Heaven, I have enough for both. And pray don’t blame her—perhaps life’s troubles have soured her milk.”

“And her heart too,” Catherine said coldly.

Margaret stared into her eyes—then dropped her gaze.

“I know I should be grateful,” she sobbed to the nurse, turning her head away, unable to watch another woman nurse her child. Gerard seemed to make no distinction between the two mothers.

The nurse smiled. “You’re very welcome, my poor woman. And you too, Mistress Catherine—though I don’t know you.”

“You’re from Tergou?” Catherine asked.

“All the better. But I don’t recall your face.”

“Oh, you wouldn’t. My husband and I are humble folk by you. But true Eli and his wife are known and respected in town. So I’m at your service, dame—and yours, wife—and yours, my pretty poppet—night or day.”

“There’s a woman of the right old sort,” Catherine remarked as the door closed behind her.

“I HATE her. I HATE her. I HATE her,” Margaret cried, with fierce passion.

Catherine merely laughed.

“That’s right,” she said. “Better say it than stew in silence. It’s natural. Come—here’s your bundle of comfort. Take it and hate that if you can,” and she placed the child in her lap.

“No, no,” Margaret whispered, turning her head half away—unable to turn fully. “He’s not mine now. He’s hers. I don’t know why she left him here. But it was kind of her not to take him to her house—cradle and all. Oh! oh! oh! oh! oh! oh! oh!”

“Ah,” Margaret sighed, “one comfort—he’s not dead. This gives me light: someone else has taken him from me. Like father, like son. Oh! oh! oh! oh! oh!”

Catherine felt pity and let her weep in peace.

Afterward, whenever Joan helped, Catherine would take Gerard out for fresh air. Margaret never objected—nor showed any doubt—but always returned in tears.

This quiet understanding did not last. Soon, Margaret became as eager as Catherine to wean the child. It was done. Gerard regained strength, and soon faced the next trial—teething.

But here Catherine’s wisdom proved invaluable. Amid her grief over her husband’s silence, Margaret found moments of pure joy watching her son’s tiny teeth emerge.

“Teeth, mother?” she would whisper. “I call them not teeth—but pearls of pearls.”

Each new tooth, sparkling on his red gums, was to her the greatest wonder nature had ever wrought.

Her companion shared the illusion. Had anyone claimed standing corn was equally beautiful, Margaret would have turned reproachful; Catherine would have thrown them out. So each new pearl’s arrival was greeted with a shriek of triumph by whoever discovered it first.

Catherine chatted with Joan and learned she was the wife of Jorian Ketel of Tergou—a man once servant to Ghysbrecht Van Swieten, but fallen from favor and returned to Rotterdam, his native town. His friends secured him the post of sexton, and with carpentry work, he managed fairly well.

In return, Catherine told Joan about the child she had nursed—about Margaret and Gerard, and the deep anxiety their silence caused.

“Ay,” Joan said simply, “the world is full of trouble.”

One day she said, “I believe my husband knows more about your Gerard than anyone here. But lately he’s grown tighter-lipped than ever. Drop in some evening just before sunset, and set him talking. And for Our Lady’s sake, don’t say I sent you. The only secret he ever kept from me was because I blabbed his business. And I don’t want another. Gramercy! I married a man for comfort, not to be hidden.”

Catherine visited.

Jorian was ready enough to speak of how he had aided her son—perhaps even saved his life.

But Catherine already knew that. When she pressed him on whether he suspected why her lost boy neither came nor wrote, he gave her a grim look. His wife met it with calm, stolid innocence.

His answers grew short, sullen.

“What should he know more than another?” he muttered.

After a pause, he added, “Think you the burgomaster shares his secrets with a fellow like me?”

“Oh, then the burgomaster knows something?” Catherine asked sharply.

“Likely,” he replied. “Who else should?”

“I’ll ask him.”

“I’d advise you not.”

“And tell him you say he knows.”

“That’s right, dame. Go make him my enemy. That’s what a poor fellow gets when he speaks to you women.”

From that moment, Jorian withdrew—tighter than a hedgehog, pricklier still.

His behavior filled both women with torment—fear, confusion, burning curiosity.

Ghysbrecht was Gerard’s mortal foe—had blocked his marriage, imprisoned him, hunted him down. And now, his former servant—when caught off guard—had hinted that this same enemy held the key to Gerard’s silence.

They studied every word, every glance—yet all remained dark.

Then Catherine said to Margaret, “You are young, fair. You may draw more from him than I.”

It was a reasonable guess.

Margaret went, carrying her child, and tapped timidly at Jorian’s door just before sunset.

“Come in,” a strong voice called.

She entered. Jorian sat by the fire. At sight of her, he rose, snorted, and stormed out.

“Is that for me, wife?” Margaret asked, flushing deeply.

“You must excuse him,” Joan replied, somewhat coldly. “He blames you for his poverty instead of riches. It’s about a piece of parchment. One was missing—and he got nothing from the burgomaster because of it.”

“Oh,” Margaret whispered, “Gerard took it.”

“Likely,” Joan said. “But my man says you shouldn’t have let him. You promised to keep them all safe. And truly, I blame Jorian for being angry. It’s hard for a poor man to be so near fortune and lose it through those he helped. Still, I tell him another story. ‘Folk who’ve escaped trouble,’ I say, ‘shouldn’t be too hard on those still in it. And she has enough.’ Going already? What’s the rush, mistress?”

“Oh,” Margaret said, “it’s not my place to drive the goodman from his own house.”

“Well, let me kiss the bairn before you go. He’s not to blame, poor innocent.”

So rebuffed, Margaret formed a resolution—she would not share it, not even with Catherine.

Six weeks later, Catherine returned home.

On the child’s birthday—soon after—Margaret did no work. She dressed in her Sunday clothes, cradled her boy, and went to church. There, she prayed long and fervently for Gerard’s safe return.

At that very hour, in the minster church at Basle, Father Clement celebrated a mass—and prayed for Margaret’s soul, already gone.
