No one who watched Robert Moore defend his mill that fateful night had seen even half of the steel that ran through his bones. The rest of that sharp, unyielding resolve—raw, unsparing, almost ferocious—blazed to life in the weeks that followed, as he hunted the riot’s ringleaders with the tireless focus of a hound on a hot trail.

The ragged, hungry foot-soldiers of the mob he never touched. Some quiet, unacknowledged thread of justice in him knew that men goaded by empty bellies and twisted lies were no fit targets for vengeance: to lash out at suffering bent low was the mark of a tyrant, not a man. Even though dawn had broken clear enough in the final minutes of the attack for him to recognize half their faces, he passed them on the roads and market streets day after day without a glare, a threat, or a single word of rebuke.

But the leaders were strangers, outside agitators bussed in from the big manufacturing cities, most of them not working men at all: failed bankrupts, perpetual debtors, drunkards who had nothing left to lose and nothing good to offer. These Moore chased as though the hunt itself was sweeter than turning a profit on his finest cloth. His horse grew lean and tired under the weight of his constant rides, he all but lived in the saddle, galloping over the moors where the fresh wind filled his lungs far better than the steamy, dye-stinking air of his mill workrooms. The local magistrates, slow and timid men who had long let fear of assassination rule every choice they made, quailed at the sight of him. He delighted in rousing them, in forcing them to confront the cowardice they had hidden behind for years: the very same cowardice that had paralyzed every mill owner and public official in the district, save old Helstone, the firebrand rector who would have faced a musket ball without a flinch.

Moore knew the danger he courted every time he rode home after dark across the empty heather, moonlit or pitch-black as the skies allowed. Far from terrifying him, that risk stoked his pride, spurred his high-spirited temper on. He returned from those late rides elated, sharper and more alive than he ever was cooped up in his stuffy counting house. Two of the four ringleaders were cornered near Stilbro’ inside a fortnight; the remaining two were tracked as far as Birmingham, and he planned to leave to hunt them down before the week was out. Even as he did, he never neglected his mill: the rioters had never broken through the mill doors, his beloved iron machines were untouched, and the small repairs needed to fix the broken windows and scuffed frames were well underway.

No one could tell, in the thick of that grinding, dangerous work, if he ever spared a single soft thought for anything other than justice and business. He rarely stopped at Fieldhead; when he did, his visits were so brief they barely registered. The few times he called at the rectory, he only locked himself in the rector’s study for hours of talk, and slipped away without pausing to greet Caroline. He held himself to that rigid, unyielding line, no cracks in his composure to show the gentler feelings he kept locked deep under all that stern purpose.

Outside, the war raged on, unbroken, across the Continent. No hint of peace came, no break in the smoke of battle, no soft rain to nurture the olive trees of harmony. Ruin crept closer to Moore too, eating away at the foundations of his fortune, so that every step he took, across his counting house floor or galloping over the dark moor at Rushedge, he could swear the ground hollowed under his boots, trembling.

While that sweltering summer dragged on for Robert Moore, what of Shirley and Caroline?

First we turn to Shirley Keeldar, the heiress of Fieldhead. Anyone expecting a pale, pining love-lorn maiden, wasting away for the neglect of a cold-hearted suitor, would have been shocked at the sight of her. The faint wistfulness that sometimes touched her face had not vanished, but neither had her bright, careless, unselfconscious smile. She lit up the dark, old stone manor house with the sheer force of her good cheer. Her laugh echoed down the long gallery, her silk dress rustled softly across the dim entrance hall as she drifted from room to room, tucking wild roses into the ugly peach-bloom salon, throwing open the dining room casements to let the scent of mignonette and sweetbriar drift in, carrying potted geraniums out to sun themselves on the front porch.

She never could sit still at her sewing, no matter how hard she tried. Her thimble was barely on her finger, her needle scarcely threaded, before some random thought sent her running upstairs: to hunt for a long-forgotten ivory needlebook she did not need, to straighten a messy drawer, to lean against that one upstairs window where she could catch a glimpse of Briarfield church and the rectory, half hidden in oak trees. No sooner would she sit back down to her work than her great shaggy dog Tartar would scratch at the porch door, and she would dart to let him in, lead him to the kitchen to make sure his water bowl was full. Out in the sunlit farmyard, the turkeys and their poults, the peahens, the spotted guineafowl, the flock of bright pigeons scattered across the grass, and she could not resist grabbing a bread roll from the pantry to stand on the step scattering crumbs, laughing as the birds swarmed around her feet. Then she had to stop to talk to the stable hand John, to pet her mare, to watch the cows file in for milking, to feed the tiny rejected lambs and new-born calves with her own hands. John would bring up questions about the crops in the far fields, and she would grab her gipsy straw garden hat and trudge after him over stiles and along hedgerows, to settle the agricultural dispute right there on the spot. Bright summer afternoons slipped away like that, melting into soft golden evenings, and by the time she came back to the house for late tea, she never bothered to pick up her sewing again.

After tea, she curled up on the rug at Mrs. Pryor’s feet, a book in her lap, Tartar’s great lion-sized body stretched out beside her, his head resting on his paws. One hand rested always on his fur, because if she pulled it away, he would huff a soft, discontented groan. The old habits of childhood clung to her, even now. Sometimes Mrs. Pryor would chide her, telling her the great dog would ruin her dress, that she should sit at a table to read, that the firelight was too dim for her eyes. Shirley only laughed softly, and promised to change later, too wrapped up in her book to move.

Then she would look up, and the moon would be rising, spilling pale silver light over the page. The book had filled her head with stories, her heart with warm, bright feeling, and the quiet parlour, the clean hearth, the moon hanging low over the dark treetops outside, made the whole world feel like a poem, like Eden. A deep, unshakable native joy burned in her veins, a gift no one could take away, no man could earn, a pure free dower of Nature to her favourite child. She was almost buoyant, lifted up on that quiet rapture, walking slow through the dim gallery in the dark, too soft and indolent to ever pick up a pen to write down the strange, glowing visions that filled her mind. She had no idea how rare those feelings were, how precious that bright spring of joy in her heart was, that kept her young and green and alive.

By the end of July, she and Caroline would have left for the long northern tour they had planned for months, only a party of unwelcome visitors descended on Fieldhead out of nowhere: her uncle, aunt, and two prim cousins from Sympson Grove in the south. Hospitality demanded she let them in, and Caroline stared, surprised, that the usually stubborn, wilful Shirley surrendered so easily. She explained that she had spent two years of her childhood there, old ties pulled at her, even though she had nothing in common with any of them save little Harry, their son who was not coming north for months. The next Sunday, in Briarfield church, the Fieldhead pew was filled with the fidgety old gentleman who shifted his spectacles every three minutes, the placid old lady in brown satin, and two perfectly pattern-behaved young ladies in perfectly pattern clothes. Shirley looked like a bright black swan dropped in the middle of a flock of dull barnyard geese, small and forlorn, surrounded by their rigid respectability.

We leave her there for a while, to turn to Caroline Helstone. She could not intrude on Fieldhead in the middle of all the visiting fuss the new relatives had stirred up across the neighbourhood, so she was confined once more to the grey stone rectory, her long morning walks on empty country lanes, her afternoons sat in the parlour where the sun vanished by noon, or tucked in the garden alcove where dappled light fell through the rose vines and red currant bushes, over her white summer dress, still as a garden statue. She read the old tattered books from her uncle’s library: water-stained old Lady’s Magazines, wild Methodist tracts full of ghost stories and dreams, old English classics that had once tasted sweet in her childhood, but were now completely tasteless. She sewed garments for the poor, as Miss Ainley had taught her, and more than once she felt hot tears drop slow onto the linen in her lap, as she wondered how Miss Ainley could bear the quiet solitude of her little cottage, so serene and uncomplaining.

Her thoughts turned sharp, fierce, and tender, as she sat there stitching. She did not want to fade into a quiet old pious woman who looked only to heaven for comfort. She believed God had not made them to spend all their days waiting to die. She ached for something more: for single women to have the right to profitable, interesting work, a place in the world that did not revolve entirely around catching a husband. She thought of all the girls in the neighbourhood, the Armitages, the Birtwhistles, the Sykeses: their brothers all had professions, all had purpose, while their sisters were left with nothing but endless sewing and unprofitable calls, their whole lives narrowed down to the single hopeless goal of marriage, which most of them would never reach. The men mocked them, called the matrimonial market overstocked, and then demanded their daughters stay home and sew and cook and be content forever, as if they had no faculties, no dreams, no hunger for more than that. She thought of the virtuous woman in the Bible, the one who bought estates and ran businesses, who was a manager and a leader, and wondered why no one ever asked for their daughters to reach that royal, capable standard, instead of shutting them up in small, useless lives. Fathers could not alter everything at once, she thought, but they could stop dismissing their daughters’ longing with a jest and an insult. Cultivate them, give them scope and work, and they would be the dearest companions in health, the tenderest nurses in sickness, the most faithful prop in their old age.
