III. We Compare Notes

We watched the procession vanish into the mist-laden woods, its echoes fading into silence as hooves and wheels dissolved into the night. The only proof that it had ever happened was the young woman who stirred just then, opening her eyes. I couldn’t see her face—she turned away—but I heard a soft, sweet voice whisper, “Where is mamma?”

Madame Perrodon answered gently, offering quiet reassurance. Then came another question: “Where am I? What is this place?” And after a pause, “I don’t see the carriage… and Matska—where is she?”

Madame responded to each with care, helping her piece together the events of the evening. Slowly, the girl recalled the accident—the overturned carriage, the fall—and sighed in relief when told no one had been hurt. But when she learned her mother had left her behind for three months, tears welled up.

I started to offer my own comfort, but Mademoiselle De Lafontaine touched my arm. “Don’t approach,” she said softly. “One at a time is all she can bear now. Even a small emotion might overwhelm her.”

I nodded, thinking I’d visit her later, once she was settled. Meanwhile, my father had sent a servant galloping for the doctor, who lived two leagues distant. A bedroom was readied for the guest, and soon she rose, leaning on Madame’s arm, crossing the drawbridge and entering the castle gate. Servants waited in the hall, guiding her swiftly to her room.

The drawing room where we gathered that night was long and grand, with four tall windows overlooking the moat and forest beyond. It was furnished in dark carved oak—massive cabinets, chairs upholstered in crimson Utrecht velvet. The walls were draped in tapestry, framed in gold, depicting scenes of hunting, hawking, and ancient revelry. Figures loomed life-sized, dressed in curious old garb. It was stately, yes, but not cold—warm enough for tea, which my father insisted on serving every evening, his patriotic pride in the national drink unshaken by coffee or chocolate.

That night, candles flickered as we discussed the evening’s events. Madame Perrodon and Mademoiselle De Lafontaine joined us. The young stranger had barely lain down before slipping into deep sleep; the ladies had left her under the care of a maid.

“How do you like our guest?” I asked Madame as she entered. “Tell me everything about her.”

“I like her immensely,” she replied. “She’s, I think, the most exquisite creature I’ve ever seen—your age, gentle, refined.”

“She’s absolutely beautiful,” interjected Mademoiselle, who had peeked into the room. “And such a lovely voice!”

Then she asked, “Did you notice the woman in the carriage after it was upright again? She didn’t get out—just stared from the window.”

“No,” we admitted. “We didn’t see her.”

Mademoiselle described her: a grotesque figure in black, wearing a colorful turban, her eyes gleaming unnaturally white, her teeth bared in a grin of fury. She’d stared at the ladies, nodding, grinning—mocking.

“And those servants?” Madame added. “Such an ugly, sullen lot.”

“Yes,” my father said, stepping in. “The worst-looking men I’ve ever seen—dogged, hollow-faced, dark-eyed. I hope they don’t rob the poor girl in the woods. Though they’re clever—fixed everything in seconds.”

“Perhaps they’re just exhausted,” Madame suggested. “But their faces… so lean, so grim. I’m curious. Still, she’ll tell us tomorrow, if she’s well enough.”

“I doubt she will,” my father said, smiling faintly, giving a subtle nod—as if he knew something he wasn’t ready to share.

This only made us more eager to know what had passed between him and the woman in black velvet during their brief, intense conversation before she departed.

As soon as we were alone, I begged him to reveal it. He hesitated only a moment.

“There’s no reason not to tell you,” he began. “She said she didn’t want to trouble us with her daughter—said she was delicate, nervous, but not subject to seizures. She even volunteered that herself—no illusions, perfectly sane.”

“How odd,” I interrupted. “Why say all that?”

“It was unnecessary,” he laughed, “but it was said. Then she said: ‘I am making a long journey of *vital* importance’—she stressed the word—‘rapid, secret. I shall return for my child in three months. Until then, she will remain silent about who we are, where we come from, or where we’re going.’ That was all. She spoke fluent French. When she said *secret*, she paused—looked at me with a terrible intensity. I think it matters deeply to her.”

He glanced toward the door. “You saw how quickly she left. I wonder if I’ve done something foolish—taking in her daughter.”

But I felt nothing but excitement. I longed to meet her, to speak with her. For those living in cities, a new friend might be ordinary—but here, in our isolation, such a thing was an event.

The doctor didn’t arrive until nearly one o’clock. I couldn’t have slept, not even if I’d tried. I couldn’t imagine catching up to the carriage that had vanished into the night with the woman in black.

When the physician finally came down, he gave a favorable report. The girl was sitting up, pulse steady, no injury, no lingering shock. She was fully recovered.

There could be no harm, then, in visiting her—if she wished it. With permission granted, I sent a servant to ask if she would receive me.

The reply came instantly: she desired nothing more than rest.

I didn’t hesitate.

Her room was one of the finest in the schloss—perhaps too grand. Opposite the foot of the bed hung a somber tapestry: Cleopatra with asps coiled around her chest. Other classical scenes, faded but solemn, adorned the walls. Yet gold carvings and rich colors softened the gloom. Candles burned beside the bed.

She sat up, wrapped in a silk dressing gown embroidered with flowers, lined with thick quilted silk—her mother’s gift, thrown over her feet as she lay on the ground.

And then—when I reached her bedside, about to greet her—something struck me dumb. I stepped back, stunned.

It was her face.

The very face I had seen in childhood dreams, fixed in memory, haunting me through years of silent dread. It was beautiful—yes, undeniably so—but it wore the same melancholy expression I remembered. Then, in an instant, it shifted—into a strange, knowing smile.

A silence stretched for a full minute. Then she spoke. I could not.

“How wonderful,” she breathed. “Twelve years ago, I saw your face in a dream. It has haunted me ever since.”

“Wonderful indeed,” I managed, forcing myself to speak through the horror that had seized me. “Twelve years ago, I saw you—either in vision or reality. I never forgot your face. It has stayed before my eyes.”

Her smile softened. The strangeness melted into warmth. Her cheeks dimpled. She seemed both intelligent and kind. I felt reassured.

I took her hand as I welcomed her, speaking of the joy her arrival had brought us all—especially me. I was shy, as lonely people often are, but the moment made me bold.

She pressed my hand, laid hers over mine, and her eyes blazed as she looked at me—smiling again, blushing. She returned my greeting with grace.

I sat beside her, still stunned. She said:

“I must tell you my dream. It’s so strange—we both dreamed of each other, seeing each other exactly as we are now, though we were children. I was six, waking from a troubled dream. I found myself in a room unlike my nursery—rough wood paneling, cupboards, beds, benches. All empty. No one but me. I admired an iron candlestick with two arms—something I’d recognize again. I crept under a bed to reach the window. As I emerged, I heard crying. Looking up, still on my knees—I saw you. You—exactly as you are now. Golden hair, blue eyes, those lips—your lips.

“You captivated me. I climbed onto the bed, threw my arms around you. I think we both fell asleep. Then—a scream. You sat up, screaming. I was terrified, slipped down, lost consciousness. When I woke, I was back in my nursery. But your face—never forgotten. I couldn’t be mistaken. *You are* the one I saw.”

Now it was my turn. I told her my own dream—the same room, the same candlestick, the same moment of recognition. She listened, wide-eyed, breathless.

“I don’t know which of us should be more afraid,” she said, smiling again. “If you weren_tE so pretty, I’d be terrified. But as you are—so young, so radiant—I feel only that I’ve known you twelve years. I’ve already a right to your friendship. Does it feel the same to you? I’ve never had a friend—will I find one now?”

She sighed, her dark eyes gazing at me with fierce longing.

Truthfully, I felt something strange. I was drawn to her—deeply. But beneath it, a flicker of repulsion. Yet the pull far outweighed it. She fascinated me—beautiful, captivating, impossible to resist.

I noticed then the weariness creeping into her. I stood.

“The doctor says you should have a maid tonight,” I said. “One of ours waits—quiet, dependable.”

“How kind,” she whispered. “But I can’t sleep with someone watching. I shan’t need help—and may I confess… I fear robbers. Our home was robbed once. Two servants killed. I always lock my door. It’s become a habit. And you look so kind—I know you’ll forgive me.” She pointed to the key in the lock.

She pulled me close, holding me tightly. “Good night, darling,” she murmured into my ear. “It’s hard to leave you. But tomorrow—later, not early—I’ll see you again.”

She sank back, sighing, her eyes following me with tenderness and sorrow. “Good night, dear friend,” she whispered.

Young people love on impulse. I was flattered by her affection—so open, so trusting. I liked the way she claimed me as a friend already, demanded closeness.

The next day, we met again. I was enchanted—by her beauty, her wit, her charm. In daylight, she was even more striking. The horror of the dream-face had faded, replaced by the real presence of a woman who smiled at me with warmth and mystery.

She confessed she’d felt the same shock, the same fleeting unease. Now, we laughed together—over the terror we’d each felt, the strange bond that tied us across time and memory.

And yet—beneath the laughter, something lingered. A shadow. A whisper of something older, darker, waiting.
