Mr. Smirke had gone, as he went every day, on his modest pilgrimage to Fairoaks; and there, in Pen’s study upstairs, sat pretending to read with his pupil—pretending, that is, with that conscientious, clerical air with which a man will sometimes disguise to himself that he is watching a clock and counting down his own release.

It was in the early part of that same afternoon—so fine, so bright, so obligingly mild—that Mrs. Portman, having transacted her little pieces of business with Mrs. Pybus, found the weather almost indecently persuasive and extended her walk to Fairoaks to see her dear Mrs. Pendennis. One may call it a call; but when two ladies sit down together, tea between them, and the whole parish politely hushed outside, it is more like a sitting of council.

In the course of their conversation, Mrs. Portman let off a secret with that careful relish with which good people hand round a particularly rich preserve: you are expected to admire it, but not to spill it.

“It is about Mr. Smirke,” she said, lowering her voice as if the very china might repeat her. “He has—my dear—a long attachment. A very old attachment. And he has kept it quite private.”

Mrs. Pendennis drew herself up with a superb composure, as though no curate living could possibly be interesting, and yet felt, somewhere beneath her laces, the small inward alarm which all mothers keep ready folded like a pocket-handkerchief.

“And on whom,” she asked, “has Mr. Smirke bestowed his heart?”

“Why, my dear,” Mrs. Portman answered, “when first he came and used to dine at the Rectory, people said we wanted him for Myra; and we were forced to give up asking him. Then they said he was smitten in another quarter; but I always contradicted it, for my part, and said that you—”

“That I!” cried Mrs. Pendennis. “People are very impertinent. Mr. Smirke came here as Arthur’s tutor; and I am surprised anybody should dare to speak so—”

“’Pon my soul,” said Major Pendennis from his newspaper, dropping his double eye-glass with dignity into his hand, “it is a little too much.”

“I have no patience with that Mrs. Pybus,” Mrs. Pendennis continued, colouring indignantly.

“I told her there was no truth in it,” Mrs. Portman said. “I always said so. And now it comes out that my demure gentleman has been engaged to a young lady—Miss Thompson of Clapham Common—ever so long. I am delighted, for my part; and on Myra’s account too; for an unmarried curate is always objectionable about one’s house. And of course it is strictly private—strictly. But I thought I would tell you, as it might remove unpleasantnesses. Only mind—not a word.”

Mrs. Pendennis, with perfect sincerity, professed herself exceedingly glad. She hoped Mr. Smirke, so kind and amiable a man, would have a deserving wife. When Mrs. Portman departed, Helen and the Major discoursed on the intelligence with great satisfaction—Helen reproaching herself for her late haughty distance to Mr. Smirke, whom she had avoided, when she ought to have been grateful to him for Arthur’s sake.

“Gratitude to that sort of people,” said the Major, “is all very well; but familiarity is out of the question. The gentleman gives lessons and receives money like any other master. You are too humble, my good soul. There must be distinctions of ranks.”

But Helen—who had ranks enough in her heart to keep them from overflowing into her manners—would not hear of this; and now that Arthur was going away, and she remembered how politely Mr. Smirke had run messages, brought books, copied music, taught little Laura a dozen pretty things, and pressed kind little gifts upon her, she felt a sincere pang. So much so, that when Mr. Smirke came down from the study with Pen and lingered in the hall before departure, she actually went out, shook hands with him—with a slight blush that belonged properly to girlhood but suited her widow’s face well enough—and begged him to come into the drawing-room where, she said, they never saw him now.

And as there was to be a rather good dinner that day—good enough to make the house seem, for an hour, like its old self—she invited Mr. Smirke to partake of it. Mr. Smirke accepted with a gratitude so prompt and tender that one might have thought she had offered him not mutton but salvation.

Relieved by Mrs. Portman’s report of all former doubts and misgivings, Helen was exceedingly gracious during dinner; perhaps the more so because the Major was exceedingly reserved to the tutor of his nephew. When the Major asked Smirke to drink wine, he did it as if he were a sovereign bestowing a cup upon a very meritorious footman: so condescending that even Pen, who was ready to be as conceited as young men commonly are, could not help laughing.

Smirke did not care for the Major’s impertinences while he had Helen’s kindness to feed upon. He sat near her and exerted all the powers of his conversation—clerical and worldly in one—to please. He spoke of the Fancy Bazaar as though the very pin-cushions had souls; of the Great Missionary Meeting as though the tea-urn were consecrated; of the last new novel; of the Bishop’s excellent sermon; and of fashionable parties in London, which he knew by reading the newspapers with an industry that would have done honour to the Acts of the Apostles. In fine, he left no art untried by which a college divine with sprightly and serious talents, a taste for the genteel, irreproachable conduct, and a susceptible heart will try to recommend himself to the object of his affections.

Major Pendennis came yawning out of the dining-room very soon after Helen and little Laura had left it.

“What an unsufferable bore that man is,” he said, “and how he did talk!”

“He has been very good to Arthur,” Mrs. Pendennis said. “I wonder who the Miss Thompson is whom he is going to marry.”

“I always thought the fellow was looking in another direction,” said the Major.

“And in what?” asked Mrs. Pendennis, with that innocence which is sometimes the most provoking form of knowledge.

“Towards Helen Pendennis—if you must know.”

“Towards me! impossible!” Helen cried—who knew perfectly well it was not only possible but true. “His marriage will be a very happy thing. I hope Arthur will not take too much wine.”

Now Arthur, flushed with the pride of having the keys of the cellar (which is a kind of temporary coronation for a young gentleman), and remembering that very few more dinners would happen which he and his dear friend Smirke might share, had brought up a liberal supply of claret. When the elders and little Laura left him, he and the Curate began to pass the wine very freely indeed, as if it were a consolation one might ladle like soup.

One bottle very speedily yielded up the ghost; another shed more than half its blood before the two topers had been much more than half an hour together. Pen, with a hollow laugh and a voice that took a tragedy-tone upon it, drank a bumper to the falsehood of women, and said sardonically that wine at any rate was a mistress who never deceived and was always sure to give a man welcome.

Smirke gently protested that he knew women who were all truth and tenderness; and casting his eyes up towards the ceiling with a sigh, as if invoking some dear being who must not be named, drained his glass. The rosy liquor began to suffuse his face; it was as though a sunset had got into his very conscience.

Pen trolled over some verses he had been making that morning—verses which declared, with much noble groaning and a brave supply of capital letters in the mind, that the woman who had slighted his passion could not be worthy to win it; that he was awaking from love’s mad fever and therefore was of course leaving her; that a name once famous might again be heard in the land; and that though he never should be the happy careless boy of a few months ago, and though death was personally as welcome as life, yet for the love of one kind being—his mother—he would live, prove himself worthy of his race, and one day bring the false one to know what treasure and noble heart she had flung away.

He rolled out these verses in his rich sweet voice which trembled with emotion; and he blushed in that excited way of his, while his large honest grey eyes showed such genuine hearty sensibility that—had Miss Costigan owned a heart—she might have softened; and very likely she was, as he said, altogether unworthy of the affection he flung away at her and immediately reclaimed from the wine.

The sentimental Smirke was caught by the feeling as a moth is caught by candlelight. He grasped Pen’s hand across the dessert dishes and the glasses. The verses were beautiful. Pen was a poet—great poet. Heaven permitting, he would run a great career. The grief he endured would cleanse and strengthen him. Smirke had always prophesied the greatest and brightest things—once certain little weaknesses were corrected. “But you will get over these, my boy,” he cried; “you will get over these; and when you are famous and celebrated, as I know you will be, will you remember your old tutor and the happy early days of your youth?”

Pen swore he would, with another shake of the hand across the apricots and stemware. “I shall never forget how kind you have been to me, Smirke. I don’t know what I should have done without you. You are my best friend.”

“Am I—really, Arthur?” said Smirke, peering through his spectacles; and his heart began to beat so violently that, in his own imagination, it made a noise in the room louder than the clink of glass.

“My best friend. My friend for ever,” Pen said. “God bless you, old boy.” And he drank up the last glass of the second bottle of that famous wine which his father had laid in, his uncle had bought, Lord Levant had imported, and which now, like an indifferent slave, ministered pleasure to its present owner.

“We’ll have another bottle, old boy,” Pen cried. “By Jove we will. Hurray—claret goes for nothing. My uncle was telling me he saw Sheridan drink five bottles at Brookes’s besides a bottle of Maraschino. This is some of the finest wine in England, he says. So it is. There’s nothing like it. Nunc vino pellite curas—cras ingens iterabimus aeq—fill your glass, old Smirke, a hogshead of it won’t do you any harm.” And Pen began to sing the drinking-song out of Der Freischütz.

The dining-room windows were open. Outside, in the sweet disciplined hush of the evening—the birds taking their turns as politely as choristers, the lawn giving back a soft footfall instead of a street’s iron clatter—Mrs. Pendennis paced. Little Laura looked at the sunset. The fresh notes of the boy’s voice floated out; and the widow’s kind heart was cheered to hear him sing.

“You—you are taking too much wine, Arthur,” Mr. Smirke said softly. “You are exciting yourself.”

“No,” said Pen, “women give headaches; but this don’t. Fill your glass, old fellow, and let’s drink. I say, Smirke—my boy—let’s drink to her—your her, I mean; not mine, for whom I swear I’ll care no more—not a penny—not a fig—not a glass. Tell us about the lady, Smirke. I’ve often seen you sighing about her.”

“Oh!” said Smirke; and his beautiful cambric shirt-front and glistening studs heaved with the emotion that shook his gentle bosom.

“Oh—what a sigh!” Pen cried, hilariously. “Fill, my boy, and drink the toast. You can’t refuse a toast; no gentleman refuses a toast. Here’s her health—and good luck to you—and may she soon be Mrs. Smirke.”

“Do you say so?” Smirke whispered, trembling all over. “Do you really say so, Arthur?”

“Say so? Of course I say so. Down with it. Here’s Mrs. Smirke’s good health. Hip, hip, hurray!”

Smirke convulsively gulped down his glass. Pen waved his over his head and cheered so that his mother and Laura wondered on the lawn, and his uncle, dozing over the paper in the drawing-room, started and thought, That boy’s drinking too much.

Smirke set down the glass as though it were suddenly heavy with fate.

“I accept the omen,” he gasped, blushing. “Oh, my dear Arthur—you—you know her—”

“What—Myra Portman?” Pen cried. “I wish you joy; she’s got a dev’lish large waist; but I wish you joy, old fellow.”

“Oh, Arthur!” groaned Smirke, and nodded, speechless with misery.

“Beg your pardon—sorry I offended you—but she has got a large waist, you know—devilish large waist,” Pen went on, the third bottle beginning to do the sort of tutoring that is never paid for.

“It’s not Miss Portman,” Smirke said, in a voice of agony.

“Is it anybody at Chatteris or at Clapham? Somebody here? No—it ain’t old Pybus? it can’t be Miss Rolt at the Factory—she’s only fourteen.”

“It’s somebody rather older than I am, Pen,” the Curate cried, looking up and then guiltily down into his plate.

Pen burst out laughing. “It’s Madame Fribsby. By Jove, it’s Madame Fribsby. Madame Frib—by the immortal gods!”

The Curate could contain no more.

“O Pen,” he cried, “how can you suppose that any of those—of those more than ordinary beings you have named could have influence upon this heart, when I have been daily in the habit of contemplating perfection! I may be insane, I may be madly ambitious, I may be presumptuous—but for two years my heart has been filled by one image and known no other idol. Haven’t I loved you as a son, Arthur? Say—hasn’t Charles Smirke loved you as a son?”

“Yes, old boy, you’ve been very good to me,” Pen said; with a liking that was honest enough, though not in the least filial.

“My means,” rushed on Smirke, “are at present limited, I own; and my mother is not so liberal as might be desired. But what she has will be mine at her death. Were she to hear of my marrying a lady of rank and good fortune, my mother would be liberal—I am sure she would be liberal. Whatever I have or subsequently inherit—and it’s five hundred a year at the very least—would be settled upon her, and—and—and you at my death—that is.”

“What the deuce do you mean? and what have I to do with your money?” cried Pen, puzzled.

“Arthur—Arthur!” the other exclaimed wildly. “You say I am your dearest friend—let me be more. Oh, can’t you see that the angelic being I love—the purest, the best of women—is no other than your dear, dear angel of a—mother.”

“My mother!” cried Arthur, jumping up—sober in a minute, as if a bucket of cold water had been poured down his spine. “Pooh! Damn it, Smirke, you must be mad—she’s seven or eight years older than you are.”

“Did you find that any objection?” Smirke cried piteously—meaning, of course, the elderly subject of Pen’s own unlucky passion.

Pen felt the hit and blushed red. “The cases are not similar, Smirke,” he said. “And the allusion might have been spared. A man may forget his own rank and elevate any woman to it; but allow me to say our positions are very different.”

“How do you mean, dear Arthur?” the Curate interposed sadly, cowering as a man does who sees the axe lifted and yet cannot help presenting his neck.

“Mean?” Arthur cried. “I mean what I say. My tutor—yes, my tutor—has no right to ask a lady of my mother’s rank of life to marry him. It’s a breach of confidence. I say it’s a liberty you take, Smirke—it’s a liberty. Mean, indeed!”

“O Arthur!” Mr. Smirke began with clasped hands and a scared face; but Arthur stamped his foot and pulled at the bell.

“Don’t let’s have any more of this. We’ll have some coffee, if you please,” he said, with a majestic air which, if one must be unjust, is at least very soothing to oneself.

The old butler entered. Arthur bade him serve the coffee.

John said he had just carried coffee into the drawing-room, where his uncle was asking for Master Arthur; and the old man cast a glance of wonder at the three empty claret-bottles lined up like slain enemies. Smirke said he thought he’d—he’d rather not go into the drawing-room. Arthur, very high, answered, “As you please,” and called for Mr. Smirke’s horse.

The poor fellow said he knew the way to the stable and would get his pony himself; and he went into the hall and sadly put on coat and hat, as if clothing himself for exile.

Pen followed him out, uncovered. Helen was still walking up and down the lawn in the sunset hush, the day’s sounds thinning to that soft rural music which seems to preserve words as well as leaves. Smirke took off his hat and bowed farewell, then passed to the door leading into the stable-court, by which the pair disappeared.

Smirke fumbled at the girths of the saddle; Pen fastened them, put on the bridle, led the pony into the yard. Pen, touched in spite of himself by the grief on the other’s face, held out his hand; Smirke wrung it silently.

“I say, Smirke,” Pen said in an agitated voice, “forgive me if I have said anything harsh—for you have always been very, very kind to me. But it can’t be, old fellow, it can’t be. Be a man. God bless you.”

Smirke nodded, mounted, and rode out through the lodge-gate. Pen looked after him until he disappeared down the road and the clatter of hoofs died away into the evening’s proper quiet.

Helen was lingering on the lawn, waiting until the boy came back. She put his hair off his forehead and kissed it fondly. She was afraid he had been drinking too much wine. Why had Mr. Smirke gone away without any tea?

Pen looked at her; there was a kind humour beaming in his eyes she had not seen for a long while.

“Smirke is unwell,” he said, with a laugh.

For an hour at least, he was almost light again. He put his arm round her waist and walked her up and down the path in front of the house. Laura drubbed on the drawing-room window and nodded and laughed from it. “Come along, you two people,” cried Major Pendennis; “your coffee is getting quite cold.”

When Laura was gone to bed, Pen—big with his secret—burst out with it and described the dismal but ludicrous scene. Helen listened with many blushes, and a perplexity which Arthur, rogue that he was, enjoyed mightily.

“Confound the fellow’s impudence,” said Major Pendennis, taking his candle. “Where will the assurance of these people stop?”

Pen and his mother had a long talk that night, full of love, confidence, and laughter; and somehow the boy slept more soundly and woke more easily than he had done for many months before—his heart, after all its noisy complaints, finding relief in the one language that needed no translation between them.

Before the great Mr. Dolphin quitted Chatteris, he not only made an advantageous engagement with Miss Fotheringay, but left with her—liberally—a sum of money to pay off any debts the little family might have contracted during their stay; and which, chiefly through the lady’s own economy and management, were not considerable. The small account with the spirit merchant—which Major Pendennis had settled—was the chief of Captain Costigan’s debts; and though the Captain at one time talked about repaying every farthing, it never appears that he executed his menace, nor did the laws of honour in the least call upon him to accomplish that threat.

When Miss Costigan had seen all outstanding bills paid to the uttermost shilling, she handed over the balance to her father—who immediately broke out into hospitalities as if money were a kind of punch that must be drunk before it went flat. He gave the little Creeds more apples and gingerbread than he had ever bestowed upon them, so that Widow Creed ever after held the memory of her lodger in veneration, and the young ones wept bitterly when he went away; and in a word, he managed the money so cleverly that it was entirely expended within a few days, and he was compelled to draw upon Mr. Dolphin for travelling expenses when the time of departure arrived.

There was held at an inn in that county town a weekly meeting—festive, almost riotous—of a society of gentlemen who called themselves the Buccaneers. Some of the choice spirits of Chatteris belonged to this cheerful club: Graves, the apothecary (than whom a better fellow never put a pipe in his mouth and smoked it); Smart, the talented and humorous portrait-painter of High Street; Croker, an excellent auctioneer; and the uncompromising Hicks, able editor for three-and-twenty years of the County Chronicle and Chatteris Champion. Bingley, the manager, liked to join of a Saturday evening too, whenever he had permission from his lady—which was as necessary as a theatre-license and rather harder to obtain.

Costigan had been an occasional Buccaneer; but a want of punctuality of payments had of late somewhat excluded him from the Society, where he was subject to disagreeable remarks from the landlord, who declared that a Buccaneer who didn’t pay his shot was utterly unworthy to be a Marine Bandit. But when it became known to the “Ears” (as the clubbists called themselves) that Miss Fotheringay had made a splendid engagement, a great revolution of feeling took place. Solly, mine host of the Grapes—worthy as any fellow who ever stood behind a bar and heard the world confess itself over ale—told the gents in the Buccaneers’ room one night how noble the Captain had behaved: having been round and paid off all his ticks in Chatteris, including his score of three pound fourteen at the Grapes. Solly pronounced Cos a good feller, a gentleman at bottom; he, Solly, had always said so; and finally worked upon the feelings of the Buccaneers to give the Captain a dinner.

The banquet took place on the last night of Costigan’s stay and was served in Solly’s accustomed manner. Mrs. Solly produced as good a plain dinner of old English fare as ever smoked upon a table—meat honestly roasted, potatoes not ashamed of their skins, gravy that knew its duty and performed it; the whole accompanied by the clatter of plates, the scrape of chairs, and that rising tide of male conversation which, once it sets in, is as hard to dam as a mill-stream.

About eighteen gentlemen sat down. Mr. Jubber, the eminent draper of High Street, was in the chair, with the distinguished guest of the club on his right. The able and consistent Hicks officiated as croupier. Most of the club were present; and H. Foker, Esq., and Spavin, Esq., friends of Captain Costigan, joined too.

The cloth having been drawn, the Chairman said, “Costigan, there is wine, if you like,” but the Captain preferring punch, that liquor was voted by acclamation—as if the company had at last found a language in which his patriotism could be accurately expressed. “Non Nobis” was sung in admirable style by Messrs. Bingley, Hicks, and Bullby (of the Cathedral choir, than whom a more jovial spirit ne’er tossed off a bumper or emptied a bowl). Then the Chairman gave “The King!” which was drunk with the loyalty of Chatteris men; and, without further circumlocution, proposed their friend “Captain Costigan.”

After enthusiastic cheering had rung through old Chatteris and subsided, Captain Costigan rose and made a speech of twenty minutes, in which he was repeatedly overcome by his emotions.

He must be pardoned for incoherence, he said; his heart was too full. He was quitting a city celebrated for its antiquitee, its hospitalitee, the beautee of its women, the manly fidelitee, generositee, and jovialitee of its men. (Cheers.) He was going from that ancient and venerable city—of which, while Mimoree held her sayt, he should never think without fond emotion—to a methrawpolis where the talents of his daughther were about to have full play, and where he would watch over her like a guardian angel. He should never forget it was at Chatteris she had acquired the skill she was about to exercise in another sphere; and in her name and his own Jack Costigan thanked and blessed them.

The gallant officer’s speech was received with tremendous cheers.

Mr. Hicks, in a brilliant and energetic manner, proposed Miss Fotheringay’s health.

Captain Costigan returned thanks in a speech full of feeling and eloquence.

Mr. Jubber proposed the Drama and the Chatteris Theatre; and Mr. Bingley was about to rise, but was prevented by Captain Costigan, who, as long connected with the Chatteris Theatre and on behalf of his daughter, thanked the company. He informed them that he had been in garrison at Gibraltar and at Malta, and had been at the taking of Flushing. The Duke of York was a patron of the Drama; he had the honour of dining with His Royal Highness and the Duke of Kent many times; and the former had justly been named the friend of the soldier. (Cheers.)
