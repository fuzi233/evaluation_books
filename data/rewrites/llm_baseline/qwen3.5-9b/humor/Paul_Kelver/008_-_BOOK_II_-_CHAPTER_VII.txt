Better is a small meal shared with love than a feast of roast beef eaten amidst hatred. Such a sentiment, so painfully obvious, would never dare to be spoken aloud by anyone except a man of truly great stature. Yet, in our household, the reality had become grotesquely inverted. Every Sunday, and indeed every single day thereafter, the table groaned under the weight of hot joints and puddings until one simply ceased to care for pudding anymore. Life's greatest luxuries evaporated, replaced by a long, tedious inventory of necessities that demanded nothing but consumption. We ate and drank without limit, freed from the anxiety of planning tomorrow's hash or cutting our bread carefully to save it for Saturday's dessert. But the old proverb failed us completely: we were no longer merry. An invisible, silent guest had taken a seat at our dining table, ensuring that laughter over half-pounds of sausage or sweet-smelling herrings was a thing of the past. Instead, we engaged in constrained conversations about hollow matters that existed entirely outside of us.

Moving to Guilford Street would have been easy, perhaps inevitable. My parents frequently broached the topic in their new, spiritless voices—speaking of everything except the elephant in the room—but inevitably, some stray thought would slip into the conversation, stirring up anger and dissolving the proposal. Thus, the aching months dragged on, and the cloud over our lives grew heavier.

Then, one day, the news arrived that old Teidelmann had died suddenly in his counting house.

"You are going to her?" my mother asked.

"I have been summoned," my father replied grimly. "It may mean business."

My mother laughed—a bitter, dry sound—and my father stormed out of the house. While he was away, my mother remained locked in her room. Peering through cracks in the door, I was certain I heard her weeping. It baffled me then why she should grieve so deeply for the death of an old merchant.

Afterward, she visited us far more often. Her mourning attire seemed to enhance her beauty, softening—or at least appearing to soften—the hardness of her gaze. To my mother, she was an angelic contrast, rendering my own parent witless and ungracious; to me, she was pure kindness. She rarely arrived empty-handed, bringing trifles or schemes for childish treats. Instinctively, she knew exactly what I wanted, recommending books that captivated me whereas my mother's selections bored me, and suggesting pleasures that delighted me rather than those that *should* have delighted me. My mother, meanwhile, would chill me with visions of a narrow, viewless existence squeezed between twin endless walls labeled "Must" and "Must not." This soft-spoken lady made me dream of life as sunny fields traversed with laughter along the winding path of Will. Though a fear lurked at the bottom of my thoughts regarding her motives, something within me, beyond my control, reached out to her, drawn by her subtle sympathy.

"Has he ever seen a pantomime?" she asked my father one morning, watching me with a whimsical scrunch of her mouth.

My heart leaped. My father raised his eyebrows. "What would your mother say, do you think?"

My heart sank.

"She believes," I replied, "that theaters are very wicked places." It was the first time doubt regarding my mother's judgment had crossed my mind.

Mrs. Teidelmann's smile fortified this doubt. "Dear me," she said, "I am afraid I must be very wicked. I have always considered a pantomime a thoroughly moral entertainment. All the bad characters go down so very straight to... well, to the fit and proper place for them. And we could promise to leave before the Clown steals the sausages, couldn't we, Paul?"

My mother was summoned and appeared. Standing stiffly beside this radiant lady in her rustling silks, she looked insignificant in her pale face and plain dark frock.

"You will let him come, Mrs. Kelver?" Mrs. Teidelmann pleaded in her honeyed tones. "It's *Dick Whittington*, you know—an excellent moral."

My mother stood silent, nervously clasping and unclasping her hands—a childish tic she exhibited when troubled. Her lips trembled. How significant the matter seemed to me, yet I marveled at her agitation.

"I am very sorry," my mother finally said, "but it is very kind of you. However, I would rather he did not go."

"Just this once!" insisted Mrs. Teidelmann. "It is holiday time."

A shaft of sunlight pierced the room, illuminating her coaxing face and casting my mother into shadow.

"I would rather he did not go," my mother repeated, her voice sounding harsh and grating. "When he is older, others may judge for him, but for the present, he must be guided by me—alone."

"I really don't think there could be any harm, Maggie," urged my father. "Times have changed since we were young."

"That may be," answered my mother, maintaining the same harsh tone. "It is long ago since then."

"I didn't intend it that way," my father said with a short laugh.

"I merely meant that I may be wrong," my mother admitted. "I feel so old among you all, so out of place. I have tried to change, but I cannot."

"We will say no more about it," Mrs. Teidelmann said sweetly. "I merely thought it would bring him pleasure. He has worked so hard this last term, his father tells me."

She placed her hand caressingly on my shoulder, pulling me slightly closer. It stayed there.

"It was very kind of you," my mother said. "I would do anything to give him pleasure, anything—I could. He knows that. He understands."

Her hand sought mine, but I was angry and refused to acknowledge it. Without another word, she left the room.

My mother never mentioned the subject again. But the very next afternoon, she took me to a local hall where we viewed a magic lantern show followed by a conjuror. She wore a prettier dress than she had in many days, appearing brighter and gayer than usual, laughing and chatting merrily. I, however, nursed my grievances, remaining moody and sulky. At any other time, such amusement would have overjoyed me; but the vivid images of the great theater I had constructed from hearsay and gaudy posters floated vaguely before me. Neither the open-mouthed sleeper swallowing his endless chain of rats nor the live rabbit pulled from the stout gentleman's hat could distract me from the joy I had glimpsed only to lose forever.

Walking home through the muddy, darkening streets, we spoke little. That night, waking—or rather, half-waking as children do—I thought I saw a figure in white crouching at the foot of my bed. I must have drifted off again, for later I opened my eyes to find it still there. Frightened, I cried out, and my mother rose from her knees.

She laughed, a curious, broken sound, answering my questions. "It was a silly dream I had," she explained. "I must have been thinking of the conjuror. I dreamed a wicked magician spirited you away from me. I could not find you and was all alone in the world."

She wrapped her arms around me, squeezing so tightly it almost hurt. We remained thus until I fell asleep again.

Towards the end of the holidays, my mother and I visited Mrs. Teidelmann at her grand stone house in Clapton. She had sent a note claiming terrible headaches had robbed her of her senses and that she intended to leave England shortly. She begged my mother to come and say goodbye. My mother handed the letter to my father.

"Of course you will go," my father said. "Poor girl. I wonder what the cause can be. She used to be so free from such ailments."

"Do you think it wise for me to go?" my mother asked. "What does she have to say to me?"

"Oh, just to bid farewell," my father replied. "It would look suspiciously pointed not to attend."

The exterior of the house was dull and sombre, yet entering felt like passing through a weed-choked rock into Aladdin's Cave. Old Teidelmann had been a lifelong collector, and his treasures, now scattered across a dozen galleries, were then piled in curious confusion. Pictures covered every inch of wall, leaned against antique furniture, or hung unframed from the ceiling. Statues gleamed from every corner (a few were the only items Mrs. Teidelmann kept for herself), alongside carvings, embroideries, priceless china, gem-framed miniatures, illuminated missals, and gorgeously bound books. The ugly, thick-lipped man had surrounded himself with the beauty of every age and land; he himself was the only cheap, uninteresting object within his walls. Now, he lay shriveled in his coffin beneath a monument that made an obscure cemetery famous.

Instructions dictated that my mother be shown directly to Mrs. Teidelmann's boudoir. She lay on a sofa near the fire, asleep, draped in a loose lace robe that revealed her thin, snow-white arms and rich dark hair cascading loosely about her. In sleep, she looked less beautiful: harder, with a hint of coarseness to her face that was absent when awake. My mother said she would wait, hoping Mrs. Teidelmann would wake. The servant closed the door softly, leaving us alone.
