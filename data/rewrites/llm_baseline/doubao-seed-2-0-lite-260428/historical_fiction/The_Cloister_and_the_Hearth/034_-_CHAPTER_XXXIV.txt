“Where be the true men?”
“Here be we! God bless you all! God bless you!”

A roar of warm, rough voices rose above the reek of smoke and blood still hanging heavy in the inn’s upper chamber. The archers crowded the stairs, half a dozen hard, calloused hands outstretched to grip Denys and Gerard’s own, firm as oak. “Ye’ve saved our lives, lads,” Denys cried, grinning through a fleck of drying blood on his brow, “ye’ve plucked us from the gallows this very night.”

A wild, stark sight met the two rescued men’s eyes: torches guttered against the dark rafters, gilding the polished steel breastplates of the bowmen, painting their sun-browned faces a ruddy bronze. Bound and disarmed, the captured thieves huddled against the far wall, their cheeks drained of all color, while the carcass of the massive robber Denys had hurled over the banister lay splayed half in its own dark gore on the stone flags.

Gerard walked slow from archer to archer, pressing each calloused hand in his own, tears glistening bright in his eyes, until the rough soldiers crowded in to kiss his cheeks in the old custom of battle-brothers. This time he did not flinch, but returned their kisses, worn thin as he was by the night’s terror. Turning to a handsome archer near his own age, he said, half slurring with sudden, bone-deep exhaustion: “Prithee, good soldier, keep an eye on me. A strange drowsiness is swallowing me whole. Do not let any soul cut my throat while I sleep, for pity’s sake.”

The archer laughed aloud, thinking the young clerk was jesting, and swore to stand guard. No sooner had the promise left his lips than Gerard slid down against the wall, and sank into a deep, dreamless slumber.

Denys blinked in surprise, but did not wake him—for the chance to claim credit for that impossible throw of his suited his purposes perfectly. A pair of archers were prodding the dead abbot of the thieves with their boot toes, marveling aloud what man could have hurled the 20-stone brute down from the upper landing, and begging to learn the trick of that arm. At first Denys made a show of downplaying the feat, but he dared not risk bragging loud while Gerard was awake—for he knew the pious lad would no doubt pipe up and swear the brute was cast down by the finger of Heaven, and make Denys look a fool for his boasting. Now that he saw Gerard dead to the world, he leaned in and gave the story full voice.

“Well then, comrades, I ran my sword through that rogue up to the hilt, and two more of his pack came buzzing at my throat. I had no choice but keep my grip on my blade or die, so I planted my boot firm against his stomach, heaved with all my might, and gave a great spring with my foot, and sent him flying straight to kingdom come! Died mid-air, he did, and his carrion rolled right in amongst you unannounced—made you all jump, I warrant. But what’s the use prattling of such trifles now they’re past? Buvons, camarades, buvons! *Drink*, lads, drink!”

The archers laughed and pointed out it was easy to cry for wine when there was not a bottle in sight. “Nay!” Denys clapped a hand to his chest. “My nose hath a natural knack for sniffing out good wine even through three feet of stone. Follow me, and follow my nose—bring a torch!”

They tramped down a short flight of slimy stone steps into a large, low, damp cellar. The air hung thick and mildewed, the walls crusted all over with what looked like thick cobwebs, until you touched it and found it was saltpetre, oozed slow from the wet stones and crystallized glistening white. “Ah, that fine mouldy smell!” Denys sniffed with delight. “Good casks always hide in places like this. Hold thy torch higher. Diable! What’s that huddled in the corner? A pile of rags? No—’tis a man.”

They crowded round the torchlight, and found a figure crouched on a heap of dirt, pale as ash and shivering so hard his teeth chattered. “Why, it’s the landlord!” Denys said, grabbing him by the collar to yank him to his feet. “Up with thee, craven heart!” shouted one archer. “The thieves are all bound, and we’re parched dry from fighting them. Show us thy wine, man—we see no bottles here.”

“Wh-what? The rascals are bound?” the landlord stammered. “That is good news. W-wine? I’ll show ye that this instant, honest sirs.” His joints wobbled as he rose, and he offered to lead them to the next cellar where he kept his stock. But Denys held him back, sharp as a knife. “Ye are all fools, comrades. This man is in league with the thieves.”

“Alack, good soldier, me in league with the accursed robbers! Is that reasonable?” the landlord wailed.

“The girl said so anyway.”

“The girl! What girl? Ah, curse her, that traitress!”

“Now hold,” the other archer put in. “The girl’s already gone to fetch the bailiff. Let the burghers judge if he’s guilty or no, since we did not catch him with a blade in his hand. Make him fetch us the wine.”

“One moment,” Denys said, shrewd as a fox. “Why curse the girl? If he were an honest man, he’d be blessing her the same as we do, for ridding the roads of these robbers.”

“Alas, sirs!” the landlord whimpered. “My good name is all I have left to live by. I cursed her only because ye told me she had belied me, and I thought to prove I was angry at the lie.”

“Humph!” Denys grunted. “I trow thou art a thief, and every thief can lie smooth-faced as a saint. Hold him, lads—even a prisoner can draw wine, so long as his hands are not tied too tight.”

The landlord made no objection. He said he would gladly show them his small stock, only begged they pay for what they drank, for his rent was two months overdue. The archers smiled grimly at what they took for his naivete, one setting a firm but quiet hand on his shoulder, the other leading the way with the torch. They had almost reached the next cellar’s threshold when Denys roared “Halt!”

“What is it?”

“Bottles in this corner, sure. Bring the torch hither.” The torchbearer—Philippe by name—stepped toward him. Denys had slipped his sword from its scabbard, and was probing the loose heap the landlord had been crouched on just moments before.

“Nay, nay!” the landlord cried, panic flaring in his voice. “The wine is in the next cellar. There is nothing there.”

“Nothing is mighty hard, then,” Denys said, and fished something out of the heap with his hand. It clattered on the stone floor, a bone. “Naught there but the bones of the house,” the landlord said quickly.

“Just now ’twas nothing. Now we found something, and ’tis nothing but bones?” Denys dug out a second, and held it out to Philippe. “Look at that, mate. Bring our smooth-faced knave over too, to look at it.”

Philippe turned the bone over in the torchlight. “Well?” said Denys. “Well, if this were a battlefield, I’d swear it’s a man’s shin bone, no more, no less. But this is no battlefield, nor a churchyard—it’s an inn.”

“True, mate. But yon knave’s ashy face is a better light to me than any battlefield could be. Bring yon face nearer, I say. When the chine of the house is missing, and the house dog can’t look at you without tucking his tail between his legs, who’s the thief? Good brothers, my flesh creeps. The deeper I dig, the more bones I find. Mayhap if they could speak, they’d make every honest man’s blood run cold.”

“Alas! Young man, what hideous fancies are these!” the landlord wailed. “They are bones of beeves, and sheep, and kids, not men and women, as ye think. Holy saints preserve us!”

“Hold thy peace. Thou dost not speak to burghers who can’t tell a veal knuckle from their grandsire’s ribs. Thou speakest to soldiers—men who have gone out to look for their dead comrades, and found their bones picked clean by crows, just as these have been by thee and thy pack. Men and women, saidst thou? Prithee, when did I ever utter a word of women’s bones? Thou wouldst make even a child suspect thee. Drag him close. Now what is this, thou knave?” Denys thrust a small, pale object suddenly under his nose.

“Alas, I know not!”

“I’d not swear to it either, but it looks far too like the thumb bone of a man’s hand. Mates, my flesh is crawling. How know we this is not a hidden charnel house, not a cellar at all?” He drew his sword full from its sheath, and began raking the heap of dirt, broken crockery and bones out across the floor.

The landlord babbled that he was wasting his time. “We poor innkeepers are sinners, to be sure—we give short measure, we water the wine, we must do such things for the laws are cruel to us. But we are no assassins. How could we afford to kill our customers? May Heaven’s lightning strike me dead if there is a single human bone here! These are all kitchen scraps the wench tossed aside. I swear by the Holy Mother, by Saint Paul, by Saint Dominic, and by Denys my patron saint—ah!”

Denys held a full, bare human skull up before his eyes, dead silent. No man, no matter how ignorant or how lying, could mistake it for the skull of a sheep or ox. The sight of it clamped the landlord’s lying lips shut, and palsied his cowardly heart. His hair rose straight up on his head like sharp spikes, and his knees gave way as if someone had struck his legs out from under him. The archers dragged him back up by the arms, held him pinned under the torchlight, staring face to face with the white skull, as pale as his own terror-stricken cheek, its empty eye sockets glaring back at the man who had pried the eyes from the head it once belonged to. The landlord’s mouth gaped open and open, but no sound would come out.

“Ah!” Denys spoke, solemn now, trembling with rage. “Look on those sockets thou robbed of sight. Let them blast thine own eyes, that the crows shall pick out before the week is out. Now hold that while I search on. Hold it, I say, or here I will rob the gallows of its due!” He threatened the quaking wretch with his naked sword, until the landlord took the cold skull in his shaking hands, and almost fainted dead away.

Soon the whole heap was scattered, and not one or two, but dozens of skulls glinted in the torchlight, the murderer whimpering at every new discovery.

Suddenly Denys uttered a sharp, strange cry of pain, the sort no one would expect from so bold and hard a man. He held up a great tangled mass of long, glossy golden human hair. At the sight of it the archers shook the craven landlord hard in their grasp, and he whined like a kicked dog.

“I have a little sister back home with hair just this fair and shining,” Denys gulped, thick with rage. “Jesu! What if this was hers? Quick, lads—take my sword and my dagger, keep them far from my hand, lest I strike him dead and cheat the wheel of its rightful work. Thou poor innocent soul, whose head this beautiful hair once grew on, hear me swear on bended knee: I will never leave this man’s side till I see him broken to pieces on the wheel, even for thy sake.”

He rose to his feet, fire in his eyes. “Aye, had he as many lives as there are hairs in that lock, I’d take every last one, by God.” He tucked the golden hair safe into his tunic, then seized the landlord by the throat, forced him face down into the thickest pile of his victims’ bones, grinding his face savagely into the shards of human remains until the wretch first yelled, then whimpered, then sniveled like a dog whose nose has been rubbed in the innocent creature he killed.

“Lend me thy bowstring, Philippe!” He threaded the cord alternately through the two empty sockets of a skull, hung the ghastly relic round the man’s neck, then dragged him up by the scruff of his neck and kicked him steadily all the way into the inn’s kitchen. There, an alderman of the town had just arrived with his constables, and was taking a deposition from one of the archers. The grave burgher started up in shock at the sight of the bleeding innkeeper, his face crisscrossed with shallow cuts from the shards of bone, wearing his horrible skull collar.

Denys panted in after him, and told the full, fiery story in a dozen short, blazing words. “Bind him like the rest of the thieves,” the alderman said sternly. “I count him the blackest villain of the whole gang.” As his hands were bound tight behind his back, the wretch begged piteously that the skull might be taken off his neck. “Humph!” the alderman said. “Certes I would never have ordered such a thing done to a mortal man. But since it hangs there already, I will not lift a finger to take it off. Methinks it fits thee perfectly, thou bloody dog. ’Tis thy true ensign, and it hangs well above a heart so foul as thine.”

He asked Denys if they had secured the entire gang, or only a part. “Your worship,” Denys said, “there are seven robbers in the band, plus this landlord. One we slew upstairs, one we trundled down dead, the rest are all bound before you.”

“Good. Go fetch the dead one from upstairs, and lay him beside the other corpse I had my men remove earlier.”

A shrill, peevish voice cut in then, like a guinea fowl squawking. “Now, now, now! Where is the severed hand? That is what I want to see!” The speaker was a small, pettifogging little clerk, giddy with the chance to collect high-profile evidence. He whispered in his master’s ear that the pieces de conviction would make a very fine show for the court, and then scribbled them down one by one in a high squeak: Skulls, Bones, A woman’s golden hair, 1 severed thief’s hand, 1 axe, 2 carcasses, 1 crossbow bolt.

When he was done writing, he ached to search the cellar himself for more priceless bits of evidence—maybe a severed ear, even a gold earring. The alderman gave his assent, and the clerk grabbed a torch and hurried toward the cellar stairs—only to be stopped short by a most ridiculous accident.

The constables had gone up the upper stairs in single file. No sooner did the head constable catch sight of the phosphorescent corpse of the dead robber propped by the bed, than he froze, stupefied. Then he began to shake like a man in the grip of a bad ague, terror swallowing him whole, and he let out a wild howl, recoiling backward as fast as his legs could carry him. He forgot the steps were there, and tumbled over right on top of the constable behind him, who, already startled by the shout and catching a glimpse of the horrible glowing corpse, was already staggering back, and could not support the ponderous head constable’s weight. The two knocked over the third, the three knocked over the fourth, and they all streamed back into the kitchen, piling up on the floor in a tangled, overlapping heap like cards laid out on a table. The clerk, hurrying along with his torch, ran full tilt into the last man in the heap, and went flying flat on his back, the torch waving in the air as if in triumph—though he was really quite unconscious, the breath driven clean out of his lungs by the impact.

“What in Heaven’s name is happening now?” the alderman cried, jumping up in alarm. But Denys laughed and explained the sight of the glowing corpse had spooked the entire lot, and offered to escort his worship upstairs to see for himself. The alderman agreed, his rueful men picked themselves up off the floor, and the whole party searched the premises top to bottom. The prisoners’ formal interrogation was put off until the serving girl could return from the town to confront them.

Before the first streaks of dawn painted the sky gray, all the thieves, dead and alive, and every grim relic of their decades of hidden murder had been swept away into the custody of the town law. The inn fell silent, almost deserted. Only one single constable remained on guard, along with Denys and Gerard—who still slept on, heavily, unaware of all the horror and hard justice that had unfolded around him while he lay lost to the world.
