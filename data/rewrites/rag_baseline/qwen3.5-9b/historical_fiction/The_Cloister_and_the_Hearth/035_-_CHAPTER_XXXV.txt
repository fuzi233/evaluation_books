Gerard awoke to the sharp, anxious scrutiny of Denys, whose eyes betrayed a lingering unease. "It is you for sleeping! Why, 'tis high noon," Denys exclaimed, his voice cutting through the haze of Gerard's stupor.

"It was a blessed sleep," Gerard replied, his voice thick with relief. "Methinks Heaven sent it to me. It hath placed a veil between me and that awful night. To think that you and I sit here alive and well... How terrible a dream I seem to have had!"

"Ay, lad," Denys responded, his tone softening as he accepted the reality of their survival. "That is the wise way to view such things once they are past; they become merely dreams, fleeting shadows. Break thy fast, and thou shalt think no more on't. Moreover, I promised to bring thee to the town by noon, to present thee to his worship."

Gerard dipped a piece of coarse rye bread into a cup of red wine, consuming it to break his fast. Then, accompanied by Denys, he walked over the scarred landscape of the recent combat. He returned shuddering, the phantom weight of violence still clinging to his limbs, until they finally turned toward the road leading to the little town. As they traveled, Gerard kept peering anxiously through the hedgerows, expecting sudden ambushes with an unreasonable intensity, until the town gates came into view.

Denys led him straight to "The White Hart." "No fear of cut-throats here," Denys assured him, gesturing to the inn's sturdy facade. "I know the landlord; he has been a burgess for many a year and looks the part of a bailiff. 'Twas here I intended to make my stand yesterday evening. But we lost time, and night overtook us—and—"

"And you saw a woman at the door," Gerard interrupted, his heart leaping. "And would be wiser than a Jeanneton; she told us they were nought."

"Why, what saved our lives if not a woman?" Gerard pressed, his gratitude swelling like a tide. "Ay, and she risked her own life to do it."

"That is true, Denys," Gerard continued, his voice trembling with emotion. "Though women are nothing to me in battle, I long to thank this poor girl and reward her, ay, even though I share every doit in my purse with her. Do not you?"

"Parbleu," Denys agreed without hesitation.

"Where shall we find her?"

"Mayhap the alderman will tell us. We must go to him first."

The alderman received them with a countenance of singular and inexplicable gravity. However, after a moment's reflection, a grim smile softened his features, and he proceeded to interrogate Gerard, taking down their answers with meticulous care. Once finished, he informed them that they must remain in the town until the thieves were tried, requiring them to be at hand to give evidence under penalty of fine and imprisonment. They looked very blank at this decree, bewildered by the sudden confinement.

"However," the alderman said, dismissing their confusion, "'twill not be long; the culprits having been taken red-handed." He added, with a touch of bureaucratic finality, "And you know, in any case, you could not leave the place this week."

Denys stared at this remark, seemingly unable to grasp the logic, while Gerard smiled inwardly at what he perceived as the old gentleman's simplicity—the naive belief that a provincial town of Burgundy held any attraction capable of detaining men driven by the urgent summons of Rome and Margaret.

Now, Gerard turned to that which was nearest both their hearts. "Your worship," he said, bowing low, "we cannot find our benefactress in the town."

"Nay, but who is your benefactress?" the alderman asked, intrigued.

"Who? Why the good girl that came to you by night and saved our lives at peril of her own," Gerard declared, his passion rising. "Oh sir, our hearts burn within us to thank and bless her; where is she?"
