George Warrington had recounted his tale to Madame de Bernstein the evening prior—or at least, a portion of it. The old lady dozed intermittently, rousing only when his pauses stretched too long, murmuring vague approval before drowsiness reclaimed her. Her interest was feigned, her attention fleeting. George, stilted and hesitant, lacked the ease he had displayed before his small, earnest audience in Hill Street, where Hetty’s wide-eyed wonder, Theo’s tender glances, and the General’s dry humor had spurred him on.

“Charming, quite charming,” Madame Bernstein remarked, stifling a yawn behind lace-mittened fingers. “And your mother must have been overjoyed at your return.”

George’s lips tightened. He offered a shallow bow. “She received me as family ought, madam.”

“Family!” The old woman’s laugh was brittle. “Had your mother written to me sooner, I might have spared you both some grief. But no matter. You are here now—though I daresay she half-wishes you’d stayed buried.”

George flushed but held his tongue.

That night, as her maid prepared her for the evening’s revelry, Madame Bernstein mused aloud: “That boy’s mother mourns his resurrection more than she’ll admit. And I—well, I am furious! Why couldn’t he have stayed dead beneath that tree? Why did young Florac drag him back?” She sighed. “Still, he has his grandfather’s bearing. The same eyes, the same voice. Colonel Esmond’s boy, through and though.”

Dismissing her nephews with a flick of her fan, she clasped Harry’s hands. “Poor child! Disinherited and discarded. Only a wicked old woman will treat you kindly now.” She pressed a purse into his palm. “Don’t lose it at Ranelagh tonight.”

At Ranelagh, Harry introduced George to the glittering throng. The elder Warrington, wide-eyed, absorbed the music, the laughter, the spectacle—so unlike the quiet plantations of home. Harry watched him with fond pride, even as whispers trailed behind them.

Later, at the club, Harry declined the gaming tables. “I’ve burned my fingers enough,” he declared.

“But surely you can afford another round?” teased Mr. Morris.

Harry’s smile was thin. “Months ago, I was heir to an estate. Now? I am heir to nothing.”

The room stilled.

“You mean your brother—” Lord March began.

“—has returned,” Harry finished. “Alive.”

A ripple of disbelief passed through the company.

“Convenient,” muttered Jack Morris. “First the younger brother swans in, claiming fortune and favor. Then, just as his schemes collapse, the elder rises from the dead?”

Laughter followed, sharp and unkind.

Harry bore it silently. His thoughts were elsewhere—on Maria, on his debts, on the life he must now forge. He had been a fool, squandering wealth and time. But George was home. That, at least, was a blessing.

And yet, as he smoked his pipe alone, the weight of his choices pressed upon him. He was no longer a Virginian prince, but a man adrift—bound to a past he could not undo, and a future he could scarcely envision.
