"Examine your own soul and record what you find!" cried Herr Cant, and the cuckoos of the earth seemed to echo his command. Peer into the deepest currents of the Rhine or the thickest reaches of the Thames, and paint the riverbed. Lower a bucket into a well dug of self-deception; whatever rises must be eternal truth, surely? Yet, first among things, no son of Adam ever truly reads his own heart unless he has first studied the hearts of others through years of observation. Even then, armed with borrowed wisdom and reflected light, he can merely decipher his own spirit, never reading it with fluent ease.

Halfway to Sevenbergen, Gerard cast a gaze inward and demanded of his heart why he journeyed there. Without hesitation, his heart replied, "We go driven by curiosity—to learn why she spurned us, to prove to her that our spirits remain unbroken, and that we are fully content with our honors and the benefice awaiting us. We desire neither her nor any ally of her fickle sex."

Soon, Gerard located Peter Brandt's cottage. A girl sat in the doorway, busy with her needlework, while a sturdy figure leaned against a longbow, conversing with her. A strange pang struck Gerard at the sight of the man. Upon closer inspection, however, he realized the stranger was past fifty, an old soldier whom Gerard recalled seeing display admirable marksmanship at the shooting butts. Moments later, the youth arrived. Margaret looked up, dropped her work, and emitted a faint cry, her complexion shifting rapidly between pale and flushed. Yet, these signs of emotion vanished swiftly; she turned colder and more indifferent than she likely would have had she not betrayed such agitation.

"What! Is it you, Master Gerard?" she asked. "What on earth brings you here?"

"I was passing and saw you," Gerard replied. "So I thought to bid you good day and inquire after your father."

"My father is well. He will arrive shortly."

"Then I suppose I may stay until he comes."

"As you wish. Good Martin, go into the village and tell my father that a friend awaits him."

"And not a friend of yours?"

"A friend of my father is a friend of mine."

"That is doubtful. It was unlike a friend to promise to wait, only to vanish the moment my back was turned. Cruel Margaret, you little know how I searched the town for you; how, lacking you, nothing brought me joy."

"These are idle words," she retorted sharply. "If you had truly desired my father's or my company, you would have returned. I had prepared a bed for you at my cousin's house; he would have honored you greatly, and perhaps, who knows, I might have done likewise. I was in a generous mood that day. Do not expect me to be so again, nor should you expect any young man to be."

"Margaret, I returned the instant the Countess released me, but you were nowhere to be found."

"Nay, you did not return, or you would have seen Hans Cloterman at our table; we sent him specifically to fetch you."

"I saw no one save a drunken man who had just fallen."

"At our table? Describe his attire."

"I paid little attention; he wore dark, somber clothing."

At this, Margaret's face gradually warmed. Soon, however, adopting an air of incredulous severity, she began a barrage of sharp questions, to which Gerard responded with utmost loyalty. Finally, the clouds parted, and they deduced the origin of the misunderstanding. This was followed by a surge of tender remorse, intensified because they had both wronged one another. More perilous still came mutual confessions. Neither had known happiness since; neither ever would have, but for this fortunate reunion.

Gerard spotted a *MS. Vulgate* lying open on the table and lunged toward it like a hawk. Manuscripts were his greatest delight, yet before he could reach it, two pale hands slammed flat onto the page, covering it, while a flushed face appeared above them.

"Nay, remove your hands, Margaret, that I may see where you read. I shall read alongside you at home; thus, our souls may meet within the sacred page."

"You will not?"

"Nay, then I must kiss them away."

He kissed them repeatedly until, overcome with shame, they withdrew. Lo! the holy book lay open to, "An apple of gold set in a setting of silver."

"There, now," she exclaimed. "I had been searching for it for ages, finding it only moments ago—and to be caught!" With a touch of inconsistency, she pointed it out to Gerard with her slender white finger.

"Yes," he said, "but today it is all concealed beneath that large cap."

"It is a becoming cap, I am told by some."

"Perhaps; but what it conceals is beautiful."

"It is not; it is hideous."

"Well, it was beautiful in Rotterdam."

"Yes, everything was beautiful that day," she sighed softly.

Just then, Peter entered, welcoming Gerard warmly and inviting him to supper. Margaret vanished, and Gerard engaged in a pleasant scholarly conversation with Peter. Soon, Margaret reappeared, her hair arranged in a silver net, casting a glance that was half-arch, half-coy. She glided around them, spreading the meal, radiating gaiety and happiness. In the cool evening, Gerard coaxed her outside. She initially objected but yielded; he urged her further along the road to Tergou, and though she declined, she eventually complied. They strolled hand in hand. When he finally had to depart, they vowed never to quarrel or misunderstand one another again, sealing their promise with a long, loving kiss. Gerard returned home on wings of joy.

From that day forward, Gerard spent most of his evenings with Margaret. Their attachment deepened on both sides until the hours they shared became the essence of their lives; the rest of existence they merely endured. At the start of this profound bond, all proceeded smoothly. Obstacles existed, yet they seemed distant and trivial to the hopeful eyes of youth and love. The feelings and passions of countless others that this union might thwart offered no warning smoke to reveal their volcanic nature and power. The path of true love ran smooth and placid until it had drawn these two young hearts forever into its current. And then—
