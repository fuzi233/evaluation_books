We stumbled to a halt just outside the ice-caked cavern, frost crusted in our eyelashes and our joints still screaming from hours of trudging through snow, feeling absurdly foolish for rushing off without confirming the frozen form we’d glimpsed huddled in the dark.

“I’m going back,” Sir Henry Curtis said, his voice tight with a quiet, unshakable dread.

Good blinked through his fogged monocle. “Why ever for?”

“Because it just struck me—what we saw in there might be my brother George, the one who vanished chasing these mines two years prior.”

The thought stopped all three of us cold. We turned on our heels and ducked back into the cave, our eyes still bleary from the blinding snow glare, struggling for minutes to pierce the gloom before our night vision adjusted enough to pick out the still, crumpled body propped against the far ice wall.

Sir Henry dropped to his knees, leaned in, and peered straight into the dead man’s face. He let out a huge, shaking breath of relief. “Thank God. It’s not him.”

I crowded in next, and got my first clear look. The corpse was a tall, middle-aged man with sharp aquiline features, grizzled hair, and a long black moustache. His skin had dried to a perfect pale yellow, stretched taut over bone, frozen stiff as a carved statue. Every scrap of clothing had been stripped from his frame save a tattered remnant of wool hose around one ankle. Around his neck hung a carved ivory crucifix, yellowed with three hundred years of encasement in ice.

“Who in the devil’s name is this?” I whispered.

“Can’t you guess?” Good said, grinning slightly behind his half-frosted glasses. When I shook my head he went on, “It’s old Dom José da Silvestra, obviously. Who else would be this far up the unclimbed peak?”

“Impossible!” I gasped. “He died three hundred years ago!”

“And what’s stopping him keeping for three thousand years in this natural freezer of a cave?” Good countered, stooping to pick up a sharp, scraped shard of bone half-buried in the frost. “No sun ever reaches in here, no scavenger can claw through the ice to break in. This is the cleft bone he used to scratch his map with, right here. And look—” he nodded at the faint, rust-coloured scab on the dead man’s left forearm “—that’s where he got his ink, his own blood.”

We stared gape-mouthed, forgetting our own gnawing hunger and frozen toes for a full minute. This was the man who’d scrawled the map that had led us thousands of miles across the desert, his writing tool still resting on the cave floor beside his frozen corpse, his crucifix still strung around his neck. I could picture the scene clear as day: the Portuguese explorer, freezing, starving, scribbling his secret map for whoever might find it, dying all alone up here in the endless snow. It was eerie enough to prickle my skin right through my thick fur-lined coat.

“Let’s get out of here,” Sir Henry said quietly. He heaved the frozen body of poor dead Hottentot guide Ventvogel, who’d collapsed in the snow the day before, and set it gently next to the old Dom. With a sharp jerk he snapped the rotten thong holding the ivory crucifix around da Silvestra’s neck, and slipped it into his coat pocket. I picked up the sharp bone pen, and it still sits on my desk as I write this; even now I sometimes use it to sign my name.

We left the two of them—one a 16th century Portuguese noble chasing gold, one a simple Hottentot chasing a living wage—to keep their silent, frozen vigil in the cave forever, and stumbled back out into bright, welcome sunshine. We’d only walked half a mile further across the plateau before we hit the edge of the mountain peak, wreathed in soft rolling morning fog. When the mist lifted a few minutes later, we gasped. Five hundred feet below us, at the end of the long sloping snow field, was a patch of bright green grass, a gurgling stream cutting through it, and ten to fifteen huge antelope basking in the sun, far bigger than any species I’d ever seen in southern Africa.

Food. We might not starve to death up here after all.

We debated stalking them first, but the wind was wrong, and we’d stand out like sore thumbs crossing that blank white expanse of snow. We had to risk a six hundred yard shot from where we stood. We grabbed the heavy express rifles, the ones with expanding bullets that would drop even a giant buck if they hit, lined up our sights, and at Umbopa’s shouted Zulu command, we all fired at once.

Smoke cleared. One of the giant bucks was kicking on its back, dying, on the grass below. We whooped so loud the echoes bounced off the snow peaks, and scrambled down the slope in minutes. Ten minutes after we fired, we had the animal’s heart and liver sitting on the rock in front of us—no dry firewood anywhere nearby, no way to cook them.

“Starving men don’t get to be picky,” Good said. “We eat it raw.”

We cooled it off in the snow, washed it in the icy stream, and tore into it. It sounded disgusting on paper, but it was the best thing I ever tasted. In 15 minutes we were new men, our cold numbness melting away, our blood pumping strong again, careful not to eat too much all at once so we didn’t sicken. Later we’d learn that animal was an inco: the rare giant mountain antelope the local tribes only ever spot at high altitude. Good swore his bullet was the one that dropped it, and we didn’t have the heart to contradict him.

By the time Umbopa had cut all the portable meat we could carry, the last of the fog had burned off completely, and we stared down at the spread of land below us, and for a minute none of us could speak. Five thousand feet beneath our perch on Sheba’s snowy Breasts stretched leagues of rolling, fertile country: thick green forest, a great silver river winding through it, vast grasslands dotted with thousands of game, clusters of round dome huts peeking out from the foothills, ringed all around by distant blue mountain walls. It was the hidden land da Silvestra had written of, no mistake.

Then Sir Henry jabbed a finger out to our right. “The map mentions Solomon’s Great Road, doesn’t it? Look—there it is.”

We followed his gaze, and sure enough, a wide, perfectly paved stone road snaked down the side of the mountain toward the plain, so grand it looked like a Roman turnpike. We tramped over scattered boulders to reach it, and found it was cut straight out of solid rock, fifty feet wide, perfectly smooth—though it vanished completely a hundred paces back up toward the snow peaks, erased by some ancient lava flow. It was more impressive than any modern European road I’d ever seen: it filled three hundred foot wide ravines with dressed stone blocks, zig-zagged down the face of 500 foot precipices, tunnelled straight through solid rock ridges. The tunnel walls were carved full of ancient art: mailed figures in chariots, battle scenes, lines of captives being marched away. Sir Henry swore it was Egyptian or Phoenician work, built long before Solomon’s time.

By midday we’d wandered far enough down the mountain to hit the tree line, a whole grove of silver trees identical to the ones on Table Mountain in Cape Town. We built a roaring fire of dry boughs, spitted chunks of inco meat on sharp sticks, and feasted till we were stuffed, then leaned back against the trees smoking our pipes, listening to the stream gurgle past through banks of giant maidenhair fern, bright coloured birds flitting through the branches. It was paradise, after weeks of desert and snow.

That’s when I noticed Good was gone. I glanced over at the stream bank, and there he was, stripped down to his flannel shirt, scrubbing his clothes clean in the stream, polishing his boots with a bit of fat saved from the antelope meat, combing his hair with a tiny pocket mirror. Then he pulled out a blunt safety razor, rubbed more fat on his stubbly ten-day beard, and started shaving. He’d only got the right side of his face smooth when a bright flash of metal whizzed right past his ear and thudded into the tree behind him.

We all jumped to our feet. Twenty paces away, a dozen huge copper-skinned natives stood watching us, tall as giants, plumes of black feathers in their hair, leopard skin cloaks over their shoulders. A seventeen year old youth stood at the front, arm still outstretched, the one who’d hurled that heavy throwing knife. An old grizzled soldier stepped up and grabbed the boy’s arm, and the whole group started advancing toward us, completely unafraid of our rifles held pointed at them.

“Put the guns down!” I yelled to the others, realizing fighting was a guaranteed death sentence. I stepped forward, greeting them in Zulu. To my shock, they understood me—their dialect was an ancient, archaic form of Zulu, as different from modern Zulu as Chaucer’s English is from ours.

“Strangers cannot cross the death mountains,” the old chief rumbled. “No strangers may live in the land of the Kukuanas. The king’s law says you must die.”

Good, flustered, did what he always did when he was nervous: he snapped his top set of false teeth in and out with a loud, sharp click.

The whole crowd of Kukuanas yelped in terror and scrambled back ten paces.

“Take your teeth out, Good!” Sir Henry hissed.

Good pulled the false set right out of his mouth, and gaped at them, showing nothing but bare red gums. The natives gasped. Then he slipped the teeth back in, grinned wide, and showed them a full set of perfect white teeth again.

The boy who’d thrown the knife collapsed on the ground howling. The old chief’s knees were knocking together. “Spirits!” he stammered. “No man born of woman can do that! One side of his face has hair, the other none, he has a transparent shining eye—” he pointed at Good’s monocle “—and his teeth vanish and reappear! Forgive us, great lords from the sky!”

I seized the chance immediately, putting on my most grand, imperial smile.
“Your pardon is granted,” I told him. “We are not spirits, but we come from the brightest star that shines in the night sky, and we have journeyed here in peace.”

The whole crowd gasped, in complete awe.
