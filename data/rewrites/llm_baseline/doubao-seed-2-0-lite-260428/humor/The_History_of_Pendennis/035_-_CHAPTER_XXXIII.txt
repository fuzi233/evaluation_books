Our very much imprisoned Captain Shandon pounded out his prospectus in such bold, brassy, loud-mouthed prose you’d half-expect it to leap off the page and punch a radical agitator on the nose. The time had come, he declared, for every right-thinking English gentleman to band together and defend their glorious order from every threat under the sun: foreign revolutions, home-grown radical louts, lying mill-owners, and the dumb mobs those greedy cotton lords had gulled into voting against their own betters. “The ancient monarchy is being insulted by a ferocious republican rabble!” he boomed, waving a half-torn sheet of paper like a battle standard, the hole in his left slipper flapping as he hopped about on the rickety table. “The Church is deserted by grudge-bearing dissenters and gnawed at by sneaky infidels! All the fine old institutions that made our country great, and made ‘English Gentleman’ the proudest title on the planet? They’re left defenceless, getting jeered at by lot who don’t respect a single sacred thing, don’t know a scrap of history older than last week’s street brawl, and will smash any law they’re strong enough to get away with, the second their gang leaders yell “loot!” The reason Saint Louis’s French monarchy collapsed? Their kings didn’t trust their gentlemen! The reason England beat the greatest enemy any nation ever faced? Our people still believed in their gentlemen! It was gentlemen leading our armies that chased the French eagles all the way from the Douro to the Garonne! It was a gentleman who broke the line at Trafalgar, and swept the plain of Waterloo!”

Publisher Bungay bobbed his head like a fairground bobblehead at the Waterloo line, winking so hard his eye almost stuck shut. Warrington burst out laughing so hard his half-pint ale sloshed over the rim onto his trouser leg.
“See how our venerable old mate Bungay takes it?” Shandon snickered, peering up from his papers. “That’s the true test. I’ve dragged the Duke of Wellington and Waterloo out a hundred different times, and that old boy never once lets me down.”

Shandon went on to confess, with performative candour, that up till now English gentlemen had been far too confident in their own status to bother fighting their corner in the press, leaving all their political interests in the hands of hireling hacks and paid proctors, the same way they hired stewards to run their estates and lawyers to sort their legal messes. That era was over, he declared. Gentlemen had to be their own champions now! Their enemies were bold, numerous, and fully ready to fight dirty. “No more letting Grub Street print fake government gazettes!” he yelled, turning to nudge Bungay in the ribs. “That’s a dig at that hack Bacon’s lot, by the way.”

Bungay whacked his walking stick so hard on the floor little Mary Shandon dropped her rag doll. “Sic ‘im Capting!” he whooped, giddy as a schoolboy. “Rattle his bones so hard he’ll be begging for mercy! For a slashing article, sir, no one on God’s green earth can touch the Capting. Not a single soul.”

The prospectus rambled on, explaining that a group of very important gentlemen, whose names for very obvious top-secret reasons could not be shared with the general public (at which point Warrington snickered so loud he had to stuff a handkerchief in his mouth) had decided to launch a brand new journal. “These men are proud of their order, and determined to defend it!” Shandon cawed, flourishing his paper with a big grin. “They’re loyal to their king by birth and by heartfelt conviction! They love their Church, where their kids will worship, and where their forefathers spilled blood to build! They love their country, and want to keep it the greatest, freest nation on the planet — we’ll print “GENTLEMEN OF ENGLAND” in huge capital letters, Bungay my lad, don’t you worry! And just like the gentlemen who appended their names to the document that secured our liberties at Runnymede —”

“Wot’s that?” Bungay interrupted, squinting.
“One of my ancestors sealed it with his sword hilt,” Pen put on his most grave, posh voice, deadpan.
“It’s the Habeas Corpus Act, Bungay,” Warrington said.
Bungay nodded slowly. “Right, sounds legal enough. Carry on, Capting.”
“— at Runnymede, we’re ready to defend that same liberty today with sword and pen, same as our ancestors, and rally round England’s old laws and freedoms!”

“Bravo!” Warrington yelled. Little Mary stared up at all the shouting grown-ups, confused, and Mrs Shandon sat sewing quietly, looking at her husband like he was the cleverest man who’d ever lived. “C’mere little one,” Warrington said, reaching out with his big rough paw to pat her curls, but she shrieked and scurried away, clambering straight up into Pen’s lap to play with his fancy gold watch chain. Pen puffed up with pride so much his collar almost stood up on its own — he was soft and sappy under that stuffy, pompous exterior, and he’d never admit how delighted he was the kid liked him better.

Shandon snickered at Warrington. “You were cackling about those “obvious reasons” I mentioned for hiding the backers’ names, you heathen unbeliever? I’ll show you. “We cannot publish the names of the parties behind this venture, for very obvious reasons. We count influential friends in both Houses of Parliament, and secured allies in every diplomatic circle across Europe. Our sources are so top-secret they could never be made public — no other London or European paper could ever hope to get their hands on intel half as hot. But this much we can say: the very first breaking news on English and continental politics will only ever be found in the pages of the *Pall Mall Gazette*. The Squire, the Bishop, the banker, the country parson — they’ll all be our readers, because our writers are one of them. We speak straight to the upper classes we were born into! The field preacher has his own paper, the radical free-thinker has his own paper — why should English gentlemen be the only group with no voice in the press?”

Shandon went on, very modestly, to gush about the paper’s literary and society pages, which would all be written by famous, well-respected gentlemen, big shots from the universities, regulars at all the fancy clubs, who actually knew the society they wrote about. He subtly pointed out to advertisers that there was no better place to flog their fancy goods than the *Pall Mall Gazette*, and yelled very eloquently at the nobility, baronetage, clergy, barristers, fine ladies, and every English home and hearth to rally round the good old cause. Bungay had nodded off for a full ten minutes at that point, and woke up with a snort just as Shandon finished, banged his stick on the floor and declared it was all perfect, no notes.

Once the reading was done, the group chatted about the paper’s political and literary plans, and Bungay sat there bobbing his head like he understood every single word, even though the only two points he cared about were: 1) Shandon could write the best most brutal, slashing takedown articles in all of England, and 2) he wanted rival publisher Bacon absolutely destroyed. If Shandon had handed him a copy of the Church Catechism and told him it was a slashing takedown of Bacon, Bungay would have cheered and paid him double. He stuffed the manuscript in his coat pocket grinning, paid Shandon for the work, and called little Mary over to give her a penny before he trundled off.

With the work out of the way, Shandon launched into a whole bunch of stories about fancy society bigwigs, acting like he dined with dukes every other night. He barely knew a single person in high society, but he’d seen a few of them once at a party, and he milked that for all it was worth, dropping names and telling silly anecdotes about private dinners and gossip he’d definitely not made up on the spot. Pen was highly amused, watching the shabby man in a tattered dressing gown ramble about earls and duchesses like they were his drinking buddies. Mrs Shandon ate every single story up, hook line and sinker — she didn’t want to join high society herself, but she was fully convinced Charles was born to shine there, and still kept the one dinner invitation he’d gotten from an Earl ten years prior tucked in her workbox.

Bungay had had enough of the fancy name-dropping after five minutes, and got up to leave, so Warrington and Pen went with him. Pen wanted to stay longer, he was so charmed and touched by the weird, sweet messy family, so he asked if he could come visit again. Shandon gave a rueful grin, said he was always at home, of course he’d be delighted to see him.
“I’ll see you to the prison gate, lads,” Shandon said, grabbing his hat, ignoring Mrs Shandon’s quiet little “Charles, don’t —” protest. Off he shuffled in his holey slippers, leading them through the drafty, mildew-smelling prison corridors, his fingers already fiddling with his waistcoat pocket where Bungay’s five-pound note was tucked. Pen was so relieved to step out onto the flagstones of Farringdon Street and breathe fresh air he almost hugged a random lamppost.

Back in their cell, Mrs Shandon sat by the window sewing, watching Shandon hotfoot it towards the prison tavern, two of his drinking buddies right on his heels. She’d saved a nice bit of roast beef and a bowl of salad on the windowsill, planning to have a nice family dinner with him and little Mary that night. But that was obviously never going to happen now. He’d drink in the tavern till closing time, then go play cards in some other prisoner’s room, come back late with glazed eyes, reeling a little, and she’d have to nurse him to bed. What sorts of pain don’t we heap on the women who love us, after all?

She sighed, went to the cupboard, and made a pot of tea instead. Oh, that trusty tea pot! It has heard more secret sobs than every priest and best friend combined, ever since that kind little plant made its way over to England. It’s sat by a thousand sick beds, given cool sips to a thousand fevered lips, heard every heartbreak from Melissa pining over her secret lover, to poor Polly crying over the letter from the boy who left her, to little Mary bringing a cup up to her widowed mum. That night, it sat on the rickety table with Mrs Shandon and little Mary, while Shandon was off drinking, and that was all the comfort they needed.

In trundled Jack Finucane, Shandon’s loyal Irish sidekick, who smelled faintly of pickled herring and always had a peppermint sweet stuffed in his pocket for little Mary. He thought Shandon was the greatest genius to ever walk the face of the earth, and he ran all of Shandon’s silly little errands: dodging duns, haggling with publishers, arranging for people to buy Shandon’s IOUs, running every single tiny favour an embarrassingly broke Irish gentleman could ever need. Every broke Irishman like Shandon has one of these loyal sidekicks, who is also completely broke, of course, and they’ve got their own ragged little crew of followers tagging along behind them.

“Bet you a guinea that five pound note won’t last him till sundown,” Bungay said, as he trundled down the street with Pen and Warrington. And he was exactly right. When Mrs Shandon emptied Shandon’s pockets later that night, all she found was two shillings and a handful of halfpence. He’d given a pound to a broke friend stuck in the prison, sent a leg of mutton and beer to a mate in the poor wing of the prison, paid off his long overdue tavern tab, had a big dinner with two drinking buddies, and lost all the rest to them at cards. He was exactly as broke when he stumbled back to the cell that night as he’d been when he woke up that morning.

The three of them chatted as they walked, and Warrington sang Pen’s praises to Bungay, same as he’d done to rival publisher Bacon — Pen was a brilliant fellow, great genius, mates with half the aristocracy, related to no end of lords. Bungay practically salivated, said he’d love to work with Mr Pendennis, and invited both of them over for a roast mutton dinner at his house soon to meet all his other literary writer chums. They parted with lots of polite back-slapping.

Pen was still musing that night, back at their rooms, over the whole wild day. “It’s so unfair,” he said. “A man as talented, funny, and clever as Shandon? Spends half his life stuck in a debtor’s gaol, dancing to the tune of a oafish bookseller like Bungay.”
“I’m a bookseller’s hanger-on, you’re about to start your career as a hack writer,” Warrington snickered. “We’re all hacks on some road or other. I’d far rather be me than our neighbour Paley in the next set of chambers, who enjoys life about as much as a mole enjoys digging through mud. People waste far too much unnecessary pity on “bookseller’s drudges”.”
“All that solitary pipe smoking and ale has turned you into a cynic,” Pen laughed. “You’re Diogenes hanging out by a beer barrel. No one can tell me a genius like Shandon deserves to be bossed around by that vulgar slave-driver Bungay, who fattens himself on the profits of Shandon’s brain, who couldn’t even write a decent sentence, let alone polish Shandon’s boots.”
“So you’re already girding up to fight the publishers, and take the side of us poor writers? Bravo Pen!” Warrington cackled. “Who do you think threw Shandon in prison, eh? Was it Bungay draining that five pound note we saw today, or Shandon himself, running to the tavern the second the money touched his hands?”
“Misfortune drives a man to bad company,” Pen protested. “It’s easy to yell “shame!” at a poor guy who’s stuck in prison, with nothing but drink and forgetting his troubles to look forward to. We have to cut geniuses some slack — the same wild, passionate temperament that makes their writing brilliant is the exact same thing that makes them go a little off the rails.”
“Rubbish about all these so-called geniuses!” Warrington huffed. He was a very strict moralist on this sort of thing, even if he didn’t always live up to his own rules. “There’s thousands of clever blokes who could write verses, pen articles, and judge books just as well as any so-called famous author. The talk of professional writers isn’t one bit brighter or funnier than the talk of any other group of educated people. If a lawyer, or a soldier, or a parson spends more than he earns and won’t pay his bills, he gets tossed in gaol. Why should authors get a pass? If a writer gets drunk, he still gets a hangover the next morning. If he orders a new coat from the tailor, he still has to pay for it.”
“I’d give him enough money to buy as many new coats as he wants,” Pen grinned. “I want our profession to be well-dressed, for one thing. I’m sick of that awful middleman publisher standing between Genius and the public, robbing more than half their earnings and their fame.”
“I’m a plain old hack labourer,” Warrington said. “You, my boy, are a tiny bit of a poet, so you think you’re allowed to be all flighty. What do you want, exactly? A gang of capitalists forced to buy every manuscript any random hack shoves in their face? Every idiot who wrote an epic poem about turnips, every driveller who can barely spell, who churns out a terrible novel — they all get a bag of sovereigns for their garbage paper? Who gets to decide what’s good or bad, worth selling or not? Can the buyer still say no? Back when Samuel Johnson sat behind a screen at St John’s Gate, eating his dinner alone because he was too ragged and poor to sit with the fancy literary bigwigs at Cave’s dinner table, the publisher wasn’t doing anything wrong. You can’t force a publisher to recognise a genius in some ragged, hungry kid who shows up at his door. Rags aren’t proof of genius, but money is a very real thing, and the one who puts up the cash gets to call the shots. If I make a new novelty for the book trade, I can do whatever I want with it, but I can’t force Mr Murray to buy my travel book any more than I can force a horse dealer to give me a hundred guineas for my horse. I might think my Pegasus is the finest animal on earth, but the dealer gets to decide if he’s actually worth buying, or if he’s just a nag no one wants.”
“You’re drowning in metaphors, Warrington,” Pen laughed. “You’re very much the prosaic man you say you are. But that poor Shandon, and his sweet gentle wife? They touched my heart something fierce. I like him far more than I like lots of far “better” men I’ve met.”
“Same here,” Warrington agreed. “We can give him all the sympathy he’s owed for his weaknesses, even if a more proud man would hate that pity. Look at him: he’s in prison, but he’s not miserable. His misery and his little comforts balance each other out, that’s how the world works.”
“His genius sings even behind prison bars,” Pen said.
“Sure,” Warrington said, a little bitter. “Shandon fits in that cage just fine. He could be providing for his wife and kid, but instead he’s drinking with Tom and Jack. He could pay the poor tailor Snip the twenty quid he owes him so Snip doesn’t get tossed in gaol, but Tom and Jack drag him off to the tavern instead. While he’s drinking, his tradesman goes to prison, and his family goes hungry. Let’s all wring our hands about poor oppressed genius, and conspire against the evil publishing tyrants!”
