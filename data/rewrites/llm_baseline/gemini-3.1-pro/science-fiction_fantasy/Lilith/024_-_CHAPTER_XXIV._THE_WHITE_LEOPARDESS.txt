At the foot of the stair lay the moonlit street, and I could hear the unwholesome, inhospitable wind blowing about below. It carried the acrid, metallic tang of an industrial sprawl, a mechanical dissonance that grated against the acoustic harmony I so desperately sought. But not a breath of that sterile draft entered my retreat. I was composing myself to rest—seeking a rhythm akin to the slow, circular patience required when waiting for a wild sourdough starter to ferment—when suddenly my eyes opened. There, rising above the uppermost step, was the head of the shining creature I had seen following the Shadow. 

The moment she caught sight of my eyes, she stopped and began to retire, tail foremost. I sprang up; whereupon, having no room to turn, she threw herself backward, tumbled head over tail, scrambled to her feet, and in a moment was down the stair and gone. I followed her to the bottom, scanning the empty street. Seeing nothing, I returned to my hard couch. There were, then, two evil creatures prowling about this starved city, one with, and one without spots. I was not inclined to risk much for the adults of Bulika—a people who seemed to view life solely as utilitarian fuel, devoid of sensory appreciation—but the life of a child was a seed of cultural memory worth preserving. I resolved to keep watch at that door the rest of the night, mirroring the intergenerational vigilance of my agrarian ancestors guarding their winter stores.

Presently, the sonic environment shifted. I heard the latch move, slow, slow. I looked up, and seeing the door half-open, rose and slid softly in. Behind it stood, not the woman I had befriended, but the muffled woman of the desert. Without a word she led me a few steps to an empty stone-paved chamber, smelling faintly of old dust and dried root, and pointed to a rug on the floor. I wrapped myself in it, and once more lay down. She shut the door of the room, and I heard the outer door open and close again. 

As I lay sleepless in the moonlit air, a stifled moaning began to permeate the stone walls. It was an untranslatable dialect of human suffering, a native tongue of sorrow pushing back against the oppressive, deadening silence of the dominant regime. The mourning went on for a good while, and then came the cry of a child, followed by a terrible shriek—a violent rupture in the acoustic ecology of the house. I sprang up and darted into the passage. From another door emerged the white leopardess, a newborn baby in her mouth, carrying it like a cub of her own. I threw myself upon her, forcing her jaws to part. The infant fell to the stone slabs with a piteous, reedy wail. 

At the cry, the muffled woman appeared. She stepped over us where we lay struggling in the narrow passage, took up the child, and carried it away. Returning, she lifted me off the animal, opened the door, and pushed me gently out. At my heels followed the leopardess. 

*She too has failed me!* I thought. *Given me up to the beast to be settled with at her leisure! But we shall have a tussle for it.*

I ran down the stair, fearing she would spring on my back, but she followed me quietly. At the foot I turned to lay hold of her, but she sprang over my head; and when again I turned to face her, she was crouching at my feet. I stooped and stroked her lovely white skin, soft as finely milled flour. She responded by licking my bare feet with a tongue like dried pumice. Then I patted and fondled her, a well of tenderness overflowing in my heart. She might be treacherous too, but if I turned from every show of love lest it should be feigned, how was I ever to find the genuine, life-sustaining nourishment of real affection which must be somewhere in every world? 

I stood up; she rose, and stood beside me. 

Suddenly, a bulky object fell with a heavy *squelch* in the middle of the street, a few yards from us. The horrific sound shattered the night’s delicate auditory balance. I ran to it and found a pulpy mass, smelling of bruised copper and crushed marrow, with just form enough left to show it was the body of a woman. It must have been thrown from some neighboring window like discarded offal. I looked around me: the Shadow was walking along the other side of the way, with the white leopardess again at his heel. 

I followed and gained upon them, urging in my heart for the leopardess that probably she was not a free agent. When I got near them, however, she turned and flew at me with such a hideous snarl that instinctively I drew back. Instantly she resumed her place behind the Shadow. Again I drew near; again she flew at me, her eyes flaming like vibrant, unripe green peppercorns. Once more I made the experiment: she snapped at me like a dog, and bit me. My heart gave way, and I uttered a cry; whereupon the creature looked round with a glance that plainly meant—*Why would you make me do it?* It was a tragic communication across species, an inherently untranslatable subjective experience constrained by the dominant lexicon of her enslavement.

I turned away angry with myself. I had been losing my time ever since I entered the place. Night as it was, I would go straight to the palace. From the square I had seen it—high above the heart of the city, compassed with many defenses, an ancestral structure more a fortress than a palace. But I found its fortifications much neglected, like a long-abandoned terraced farm. For centuries, clearly, they had been of no account. It had great and strong gates, with something like a drawbridge to them over a rocky chasm, but they stood open, and it was hard to believe that water had ever occupied the hollow before them. All was so still that sleep seemed to interpenetrate the structure, causing the very moonlight to look discordantly awake. The silence here was not the comforting, organic quiet of a living ecosystem; it was a sterile, oppressive void that rendered frightful the mere thought of a sound. 

Like an outcast dog, I walked about the walls until I came to a little recess with a stone bench. I took refuge in it from the grating wind, lay down, and in spite of the cold fell fast asleep. 

I was wakened by something leaping upon me, and licking my face with the rough tongue of a feline animal. *It is the white leopardess!* I thought. *She is come to suck my blood!—and why should she not have it? It would cost me more to defend than to yield it.*

So I lay still, expecting a sharp, coppery shoot of pain. But the pang did not arrive; a pleasant warmth instead began to diffuse itself through me. Stretched at my back, she lay as close to me as she could lie. The heat of her body slowly penetrated mine, radiating the slow, even warmth of a masonry oven baking long-fermented bread. Her breath, which had nothing of the wild beast in it, swathed my head and face in a genial atmosphere that smelled faintly of sweet milk and wild meadow herbs. A full conviction that her intention toward me was good gained possession of me. The mythic had seamlessly integrated into a moment of agrarian comfort. I turned like a sleepy boy, threw my arm over her, and sank into profound unconsciousness. 

When I began to come to myself, I fancied I lay warm and soft in my own bed. *Is it possible I am at home?* I thought. The well-known olfactory landscape of the garden—the sharp aroma of bruised thyme and the earthy tang of damp loam—seemed to come crowding in. I rubbed my eyes, and looked out: I lay on a bare stone, in the heart of a hateful city. 

I sprang from the bench. Had I indeed had a leopardess for my bedfellow, or had I but dreamed it? She had but just left me, for the sustaining warmth of her body was with me yet, lingering like the aftertaste of a complex, slow-simmered broth. I left the recess with a new hope, as strong as it was shapeless. One thing only was clear to me: I must find the princess. Surely I had some power with her, if not over her. Had I not saved her life, and had she not prolonged it by drawing upon my own vitality—a profound, biological exchange of life’s essential sustenance? The reflection gave me courage to encounter her, be she what she might.
