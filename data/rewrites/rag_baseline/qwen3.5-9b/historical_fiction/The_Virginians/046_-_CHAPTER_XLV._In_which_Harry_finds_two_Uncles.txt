The air in the drawing room was thick with the scent of beeswax and suppressed anxiety. It was a room designed for performance, where every gesture was calculated to project a specific moral weight. Yet, beneath the surface of this carefully constructed piety, a different current flowed—one of desperation, of accounts balancing in the dark.

We have all encountered the theater of religion. The Sunday procession: the maid-servants trailing ribbons, the apprentices following, the schoolboys reciting Latin verses they do not understand, the clerks droning psalms while worrying about their daughters' debts. They carry prayer books like shields, hiding behind the cover of devotion. They are not hypocrites in the theatrical sense; they simply possess a religion stored in a mental lockbox, a remedy for illness to be dispensed when the body fails. They trade in spiritual prescriptions with the same casual indifference as apothecaries trading in laudanum.

Lady Warrington was the chief dispenser in this household. She held the conscience of the servants' hall and the digestion of the family in her hands. Her authority was absolute, her belief in her own virtue total. She believed herself a saint among sinners, a wisdom incarnate, and she fed this delusion to everyone around her until they swallowed it whole. At dinner, amidst the clatter of silver and the sight of liveried men, she maintained the illusion of abundance even as the beer grew thin and the joints of meat dwindled. When Sir Miles toasted the King, his eyes gleaming with the nectar of the grape, he spoke of Virginia wines as if they were ambrosia. He asked Harry if such port existed in the New World, boasting that Harry tasted nothing compared to the vintage of Norfolk. Harry, watching his uncle's performance, found himself almost believing the lie, blinking into his own glass to catch a reflection of the merit his uncle projected onto the ruby liquid.

In this house of mirrors, two figures emerged as the true idols. The elder daughter, the Beauty, was betrothed to Tom Claypool, a fact announced with the gravity of a royal decree. The younger, the Genius and the Angel, was reserved for Harry. She sang to him at the harpsichord, though the notes were often discordant, a flaw in the instrument of perfection. She offered advice, instruction, conversation—a relentless barrage of correction that Harry found suffocating. He preferred the silence of the nursery, the presence of Miles, a small cousin who reminded him of Fanny Mountain. Yet, after dinner, the house settled into a strange rhythm. The Beauty retreated to her own orbit; the mother attended to correspondence or the ailments of the household; the father dozed in his chair. Only the Genius remained, a sentinel guarding the young man against the chaos of the outside world.

This calm was intoxicating. Harry, accustomed to the riot of dissipation, found refuge here. The doors stood open at all hours. If Flora was absent, Dora waited. Within days, they had ridden together in the park, and Harry, generous to a fault, planned to buy a horse for his cousin. But fate, that cruel accountant, intervened. The circumstances that followed stripped Harry of his coaches and horses, reducing the young Virginian prince to a state of sudden destitution.

While the ladies discussed geography with earnest curiosity, listening as Harry drew maps of his estate—log huts disguised as towns, rivers flowing across parchment—they failed to notice the erosion of his reality. Harry described the Potomac and the James River with the pride of a traveler recounting foreign lands. He pointed to hills and forests, imagining himself a prince describing realms to a queen. In truth, he possessed miles where Englishmen owned acres. Even Dora, usually serene, began to lose her temper, twisting Flora's hair in jealousy. Claypool, in his red waistcoat, sat silent before the splendor of Harry, unable to compete with the charm of the American stranger.

Mama's affection deepened, transforming into a maternal obsession. She worried for the souls of the Indians and the Negroes in Virginia, seeking ways to aid Madam Esmond. She praised Gumbo's musical gifts and the butler's piety. Then came the plea: *"Ah! Harry, Harry! You have been a wild boy! Why did you not come sooner? 'Tis not too late. We must reclaim thee!"* The three ladies looked upward, invoking a divine witness to their mission. Who would claim him first? Dora watched Flora; Mama prepared to adopt him in the absence of his own parents. She handed him a hymn book, kissed his forehead, and poured out a love that was pure, tender, and utterly blind to the wreckage accumulating in the corner of the room.

Meanwhile, Mr. Warrington kept his own counsel. The fortune that had favored him for weeks had vanished overnight. Lord Castlewood, his kinsman, had played him for everything, winning every shilling and threatening the reversions. Harry, refusing to fight further, had surrendered. He gambled away the last reserve meant for Sampson, leaving himself nearly naked on the shore of his misfortune.

Desperate, Harry turned to the only resources left. He pawned his trinkets to raise funds for Sampson's landlord. The broker recognized the diamond-handled repeater Harry had intended for his Oakhurst friends. The recognition was immediate, the judgment swift. Before Harry could react, the net closed. Bailiffs, familiar with the mechanics of debt, descended upon him. They tracked him through the gossip of Gumbo, finding him in the sanctuary of the Warringtons.

Never had Uncle Miles been more cordial, never had Aunt Warrington been more gracious. Flora looked radiant; Dora was amiable beyond measure. At parting, Lady Warrington pressed both hands into Harry's, calling down blessings from the ceiling. Sir Miles, jovial and oblivious, laughed at the blush on Harry's cheeks, suggesting that the girls were used to such affection. *"A kiss won't do no harm,"* he declared, pressing Harry's hand and repeating the benediction at the door.

As the door closed, cutting off the warm light of the hall, two figures emerged from the shadows on the opposite side of the street. One produced a strip of paper, placed a hand on Mr. Warrington's shoulder, and declared him a prisoner. A hackney coach awaited. Poor Harry slept in Chancery Lane, a Virginian prince slapped by a ragged follower, destined for a spunging house in Cursitor Street.

Let us imagine the sting of that night. The remorse that keeps a man awake on a dingy pillow, the raucous laughter of hardened inmates drifting through the wall, the rage and shame of a fall from grace. No pity for him, perhaps, for those who have never indulged in such folly cannot truly understand the reckoning.
