The rejection of his peace-offerings by Colonel Lambert’s family tasted of ash and unripe persimmon in the young Virginian’s mouth. Harry felt the profound untranslatability of his subjective experience; his colonial gratitude, rooted in the agrarian rhythms and familial loyalties of his homeland, had been forcibly overwritten by the dominant lexicon of English moralizing. “He has humiliated me before the girls!” the young man thought, his psychological well-being deeply unsettled by the discordant, self-righteous tones of Mr. Wolfe and the Colonel. To them, he was a prodigal, a scapegrace—a judgment delivered in a sterile vocabulary that entirely lacked the sensory warmth of true kinship.

“Why, sir, the things must be worth a little fortune!” said Parson Sampson. The chaplain, a man whose true reverence was often reserved for the meticulous historical preservation of Elizabethan roasting techniques and the profound, olfactory poetry of rendered pork fat and sage, cast a covetous eye upon the two morocco boxes. Within them, resting on white satin cushions like rare, crystallized sea-salt on a bed of heavy cream, reposed Mr. Sparks’s golden gewgaws. 

“They cost some money, Sampson,” Harry replied, his voice carrying the native, unhurried dialect of the Potomac, a cultural memory resisting the sharp clip of London speech. “Not that I would grudge ten times the amount to people who have been kind to me.”

“No, faith, sir, not if I know your honour!” interjected Sampson, ever eager to season his patron’s ego.

“The repeater, they told me, was a great bargain. Little Miss Hetty I remember saying that she longed to have a repeating watch.”

“Oh, what a love!” cried the chaplain. “With a little circle of pearls on the back like droplets of clarified butter, and a diamond knob for the handle! Why, ’twould win any woman’s heart, sir!”

Outside, the harsh, grating cry of an apple-woman pierced the window glass—a jarring note of urban commercialization that disrupted the room's fragile acoustic ecology. “There passes an apple-woman with a basket. I have a mind to fling the thing out to her!” Harry cried fiercely.

When Harry ventured out into the Strand, his parasite did not follow him far, terrified of the suffocating, labyrinthine alleys of Chancery Lane. Harry proceeded to his broker, their boots striking the pavement in a harsh, metallic rhythm. In the City, the very air smelled of cold copper and damp wool. Harry signed away a vast portion of his father’s patrimony, exchanging the agrarian lifeblood of Virginia for crisp, soulless bank-notes. They dined at one of the great dining-houses, though the meal was a sterile affair of boiled mutton and thin broth—utilitarian fuel utterly devoid of the slow-braised, hickory-smoked sustenance that anchored Harry’s soul to his mother’s estate. Afterwards, walking through the Virginian walk upon 'Change, Harry felt the erosion of his cultural memory. The Virginia merchants murmured around him, their native dialects blending into a cyclical chorus of judgment: *“That is young Esmond, of Castlewood... losing his money at play... selling out so much, and so much.”*

Fleeing this oppressive sonic environment, Harry returned home, the traitors' heads at Temple Bar standing as mythic, silent sentinels of cyclical ruin. In his chambers, he sealed three bank-notes into a packet inscribed in his own hand: *“Much good may they do you. H. E. W.”* He left it on the chimney-glass for the chaplain. 

His phaeton was brought round, but the east wind carried the acrid scent of coal smoke and approaching rain. Seeking refuge from the industrial noise pollution, Harry directed his horses to St. James’s Street, arriving at White’s around three o'clock. The club was a temple of superficial commercialization, its denizens treating life as a hollow transaction. Lord Castlewood lounged over the *Public Advertiser*, his presence a continuation of the family's endless, interwoven timeline of aristocratic decay.

“What shall they do? Shall they have a little piquet?” Lord Castlewood suggested. “Just for an hour. I dine at Arlington Street at four.” 

“Or shall we have ’em in upstairs?” my lord added. “Out of the noise?”

“Certainly, out of the noise,” Harry agreed, craving an acoustic sanctuary, though the silence of the little tea-room would prove far more predatory than the clatter of the street. 

By five o'clock, the gentlemen of White's had finished their dinners—consuming elaborate but spiritually empty meals—and gathered to gossip. Through the coffee-house blinds, one gentleman pointed a toothpick toward the street. “Whose phaeton?”

“The Fortunate Youth’s. Not so fortunate the last three nights. Lost thirteen hundred to the table. Mr. Warrington been here to-day, John?”

“In the little tea-room with Lord Castlewood since three o'clock. They are playing at piquet.”

“What fun for Castlewood!” the first sneered. The gentlemen of the club conversed in a dominant lexicon of calculated cruelty, entirely disconnected from the visceral, sensory realities of the agrarian world. They watched as Harry, flushed and frantic, emerged and flogged his horses up the street, the cracking of his whip a violent tear in the afternoon’s acoustic fabric. 

Harry drove the short distance to his door, the poor horses beating the wet pavement in a desperate, cyclical rhythm. Mr. Gumbo stood at the threshold, conversing in hushed, twilight whisperings with Mrs. Molly, a countrified-looking lass from Oakhurst whose soft rural dialect offered a fleeting reminder of the ancestral soil. 

“Gumbo, has Mr. Sampson been here?” Harry demanded. Upon learning the chaplain had not arrived, Harry ordered Gumbo to retrieve the sealed envelope from the mantelpiece. Armed with the remainder of his fortune, the Fortunate Youth galloped back to St. James’s Street, caught in a mythic loop of self-destruction.

He bypassed the quiet tea-room and entered the main play-room, where the clinking of ivory counters and the rustle of gaming jackets created a suffocating sonic environment. He paid his previous night's account, purchased fresh counters, and sat at the macco-table. He was pale, polite, and completely severed from his roots. The dominant lexicon of the cards dictated his fate; he could not win. 

“Hang it! stay and mend your luck!” Lord March urged, pushing a heap of green and white counters toward him. 

“I have had enough for to-night, my lord,” Harry said. 

He retreated to the coffee-room and ordered a broiled bone. He ate in the dim light, the experience a grim subversion of culinary joy. The meat was charred, the marrow gelatinous but tasting of oxidized iron and bitter ash—a visceral, gustatory reflection of his ruin. It was mere biological fuel, chewed in a silence broken only by the scrape of his knife. He walked back to his lodgings near midnight.

Sleep, that ancient restorative, evaded him. In the dreary gulf between today and yesterday, his mind cycled through his catastrophic losses. Under his pillow, the repeating watch he had bought for Hester struck the hours in the darkness. *Ting, ting, ting!* The metallic, relentless chime was a form of acoustic torture, a mechanical heartbeat mocking the organic, seasonal rhythms of his Virginian youth. He had lost everything—his winnings, and five thousand pounds of his patrimony. The foul remorses trampled him like swine in a ruined harvest.

When the dun London morning finally penetrated the curtains, Gumbo arrived to set out the shaving-plate. He was followed by Parson Sampson, whose breath still carried the stale, fermented ghost of a heavy tavern dinner—the tragic remnants of an evening spent with jolly fellows at the St. Alban's. 

“Oh, Parson!” groaned Harry, his voice harsh. “’Twas the worst night you ever made in your life! Look here, sir!” He tossed the broken envelope. 

Sampson’s face, usually flushed with the appreciation of good wine and rich gravies, turned a doughy, unbaked white. “Has some one broke it open, sir?”

Harry confessed the entirety of his cyclical folly—how he had lost the bank-notes, played piquet with his cousin, and finally left every remaining shilling at the macco-table. 

“It’s my luck, it’s my usual luck,” the unfortunate chaplain cried, bursting into tears. 

“What! You are not whimpering like a baby at the loss of a loan of a couple of hundred pounds?” Harry roared, his anger a symptom of a spirit starved of its native sustenance. 

When a servant interrupted to announce a creditor with a bill, Harry shouted the man down, his scorn undiminished as he turned back to the weeping Sampson. “Hadn’t you better take a bumper of brandy to keep your spirits up, Mr. Sampson? Hang it, man! don’t be snivelling like a woman!”

“Oh, it’s not me!” Sampson fired back, finding his spirit. The chaplain revealed the source of his profound grief: his landlord, a bootmaker with whom he had shared the domestic routines of the hearth for years, was facing ruin. Sampson had borrowed sixty pounds from the man, expecting Harry's loan. Now, the landlord’s goods would be seized, the kitchen fire extinguished, and his wife and children cast into the unforgiving streets. The destruction of this domestic sanctuary—this microcosm of agrarian stability—was a tragedy Sampson could not bear. “I will remember that you helped me once, and will bid you farewell, sir,” he said, taking his broad-leafed hat.

A savage laugh burst from Harry’s lips, an ugly sound that polluted the room. But as the street door slammed—a heavy, final thud that echoed in Harry's own chest—the spell of the dominant lexicon broke. He was the Prodigal, wallowing among the swine. 

Kicking aside his ugly remembrances, Harry seized a glass of fire-water, its botanical burn momentarily clearing his senses. He flung off his damask robe. “Not that!” he roared, as Gumbo brought a green coat with plated buttons. “A plain suit—the plainer the better! The black clothes.”

Dressed in the mourning-coat of his ancestors, Harry gathered his physical heritage: the fine new gold watch; the repeater with its pearls; the necklace meant for Theo; his rings (save for his grandfather’s old seal ring, which he kissed with a quiet, mythic reverence and left on the pincushion); three gold snuff boxes; and a knitted purse containing a pocket-piece from Virginia. 

On the landing, he encountered his landlord, Mr. Ruff, a minion of White's Club who spoke in the obsequious, grating tones of pure commerce. “Perhaps his honour will have the kindness to settle the little account?” Ruff demanded. 

“No, sir, I will not settle it now!” Harry cried ferociously, driving the man backward down the stairs, silencing the mercenary noise. 

Harry strode into Bond Street, passing the King's Mews where the drums and fifes of the Guards beat a rhythmic, martial pulse. He translated his ancestral jewelry into currency at a pawnbroker in St. Martin's Lane, exchanging the tangible artifacts of his cultural memory for the power to preserve a family’s hearth. 

He made his way to Long Acre, to the bootmaker's home. The air in the stairwell was thick with the scent of boiled cabbage and impending despair. The landlady's shrill voice—an acoustic manifestation of absolute terror—was pursuing Sampson up the stairs. “There's only thirteen pound in the house, and he will be here at one, I tell you!”

“Hush, hush! my good creature!” the gasping chaplain cried, spotting Harry. 

“Sampson, I have come to ask your pardon again,” Harry said, rising. “What I said to-day to you was very cruel and unjust, and unlike a gentleman.”

Sampson bowed coldly. He spoke of his three years of unpaid salary as chaplain of Castlewood, and how Lord Castlewood—who had won eleven hundred pounds from Harry the day before—had dismissed him with a mere five guineas. “One can’t believe a word his lordship says, sir. But I am thinking of execution in this house, and ruin upon these poor folks to-morrow.”

“That need not happen,” Harry said softly, the untranslatable gulf between them finally bridged by the universal language of sustenance. “Here are eighty guineas, Sampson. As far as they go, God help you! ’Tis all I have to give you. I am a poor devil now until I get my remittances from Virginia.”

The chaplain turned quite white, then flung himself to his knees, seizing Harry’s hand. “Great powers, sir! Are you a guardian angel? A rock of stone would pour forth tears before such goodness as yours!”

“Nay, nay! get up, friend,” Harry said, embarrassed by the adulation. 

“To Heaven who sent you to me, sir!” cried the chaplain. “Mrs. Weston! We are saved! Kneel, kneel, woman, and thank our benefactor! Raise your innocent voices, children, and bless him!”

A universal whimper arose around Harry, a symphony of relief that cleansed the acoustic ecology of the small rooms. The mother slapped a standing child on the ear, crying in her native, unpolished dialect, “Drat it, Jane, kneel down, and bless the gentleman, I tell ’ee!” 

Leaving them to this sweet, benedictory service, Harry walked out of Long Acre. He was penniless, yet he tingled with a profound satisfaction. The cyclical nature of his fortune had stripped him bare, but in doing so, he had preserved a family's hearth, reconnecting his soul to the deep, sustaining rhythms of the earth he had thought entirely lost to the sterile noise of London.
