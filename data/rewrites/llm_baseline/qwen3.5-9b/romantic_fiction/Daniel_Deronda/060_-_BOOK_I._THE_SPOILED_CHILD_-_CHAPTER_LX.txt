In ancient legend, a Roman Emperor ordered the execution of ten learned Israelites to avenge the betrayal of Joseph by his brothers. Throughout history, there have always been enough souls of similar temperament, whose piety consists solely in punishing those they deem guilty, while failing to recognize the justice owed to gratitude. One cannot convince a stronger emotion that it lacks a stronger reason, nor can one persuade someone devoid of love that there exists valid ground for affection. As language itself reveals, *love* precedes the *lovable*.

When Deronda delivered his letter to the banking house on Schuster Strasse in Mainz and requested to see Joseph Kalonymos, he was shortly ushered into an inner chamber. Seated at a table amidst scattered correspondence was the white-bearded man he had encountered the previous year within the Frankfurt synagogue. He wore the same worn felt hat, and beside him sat a packed portmanteau topped with a wrap and overcoat. Upon Deronda's entrance, Kalonymos rose but did not step forward or offer his hand. Instead, he regarded the young man with small, penetrating eyes that glimmered like black gems against his yellowed complexion and snow-white hair.

"Good!" Kalonymos declared in German. "Now it is you who seek me, young man."

"Yes," Deronda replied, "I seek you with gratitude, as a friend of my grandfather. I owe you a debt for the considerable trouble you have taken on my behalf." He spoke effortlessly in that expansive German tongue, which readily embraces foreign accents into its maternal embrace.

Kalonymos extended his hand warmly. "So, you are no longer angry at having become something more than merely an Englishman?"

"On the contrary," Deronda said sincerely. "I thank you heartily for aiding me in escaping the ignorance of my parentage, and for safeguarding the chest my grandfather entrusted to your care."

"Sit, sit," Kalonymos urged softly, reseating himself and gesturing to a chair nearby. Deliberately removing his hat, he revealed a head thick with white hair, stroking and clutching his beard as he examined Deronda's youthful face with intense scrutiny.

The atmosphere struck Deronda deeply. To stand before a man still bound by zealous friendship to the grandfather whose hopes had yearned for him before birth—a figure who, though dead, seemed to speak through these written memorables containing, as Milton noted, "a potency of life... as active as that soul whose progeny they are"—Deronda felt as though he were touching the electric chain of his own ancestry. He endured Kalonymos's searching gaze with a mixture of delight and awe, akin to the reverence felt during solemn commemorations of ancient deeds that profoundly influence the present day. Such sensitivity might seem incomprehensible to those of duller fiber, whose affections fail to spread across the vast journey of imagination; yet it existed alongside their skepticism, coloring Deronda's face with an expression that appeared entirely satisfactory to the observer.

"In Hebrew," Kalonymos quoted from a fine hymn of the liturgy, "As thy goodness has been great to the former generations, even so may it be to the latter." After a brief pause, he continued, "Young man, I rejoice that I was not yet dispatched on further travels, and that you arrived in time for me to witness the image of my friend as he once was in his youth—no longer alienated from the fellowship of your people, no longer recoiling in proud wrath from the touch of one who claimed you as a Jew. You come with thankfulness to reclaim the kinship and heritage that wicked schemes attempted to steal from you. You arrive with a willing soul to declare, 'I am the grandson of Daniel Charisi.' Is this not so?"

"Assuredly it is," Deronda confirmed.

"But let me assure you," Kalonymos interjected, "that I would never have treated a Jew with incivility simply because he was a Jew. You understand why I hesitated to tell a stranger, 'I know nothing of my mother.'"

"A sin, a sin!" Kalonymos exclaimed, raising a hand and closing his eyes in revulsion. "A theft against our people—just as when our youths and maidens were raised for the Roman Edom. But it is thwarted. I have thwarted it. When Daniel Charisi—may his Rock and his Redeemer protect him!—was a stripling and I a boy scarcely taller than his shoulder, we made a solemn vow to be eternal friends. He said, 'Let us bind ourselves with duty, as if we were sons of the same mother.' That was his constant inclination—to fortify his soul with bonds. It was his maxim: 'Let us bind love with duty; for duty is the love of law; and law is the nature of the Eternal.' Thus, we bound ourselves. Though separated by distance in later years, the bond remained unbroken. When he died, they sought to rob him; but they could not rob him of me. I saved that remnant of him which he treasured for his offspring. And I have restored to him the child they stole from him. I will bring you the chest immediately."

Kalonymos departed briefly and returned accompanied by a clerk bearing the chest. He placed it on the floor, removed a leather covering, and exited again. It was modest in size but weighted down by ornamental brass bracers and handles of gilded iron. The wood was exquisitely carved with Arabic script.

"So!" Kalonymos said upon returning to his seat. "And here is the peculiar key," he added, retrieving it from a small leather pouch. "Keep it safely. I trust you are methodical and cautious." He bestowed upon Deronda the stern, slightly suspicious glance that age often directs when entrusting precious objects to the keeping of youth.

"I shall guard this more carefully than any other possession," Deronda smiled, slipping the key into his breast pocket. "Never before have I held anything serving as a sign of such cherished hope and labor. And I shall never forget that this effort was partly yours. Do you have time to tell me more of my grandfather? Or am I intruding by staying longer?"

"Remain a while. In an hour and eighteen minutes, I depart for Trieste," Kalonymos announced, checking his watch. "Soon my sons will demand my attention. Would you allow me to introduce you to them, so they may take the pleasure of hosting my friend's grandson? They reside here in comfort and luxury, whereas I prefer the life of a wanderer."

"I shall be delighted if you recommend me to their acquaintance for a future occasion," Deronda replied. "Pressing duties call me to England—friends who require my presence urgently. I have been absent from them far too long due to unforeseen circumstances. However, learning more of you and your family would be sufficient motivation to return to Mainz."

"Excellent! You will hardly find me, for I am past seventy years, a wanderer carrying my shroud with me. Yet my sons and their descendants dwell here in wealth and unity. Times have changed for us since Charles the Great brought my ancestors from Italy to impart a touch of knowledge to our rugged German neighbors. My generation and ours had to fight for it. Our youth faced dark days, but we prevailed. We accumulate wealth securely, and the learning of all Germany is nourished by Jewish intellects—even if they do not always retain their Jewish hearts. Have you been left entirely ignorant of your people's life, young man?"

"No," Deronda admitted. "Recently, before suspecting my parentage, I was led to study everything pertaining to their history with greater interest than any other subject. It appears I have been preparing myself to understand my grandfather somewhat."

He hoped to steer the conversation quickly back to the core of his desire. Time rarely distinguishes clearly between what age needs to express and what youth needs to know; distance seems to equalize the objects of memory. Although Kalonymos displayed keen activity, misplaced an inkstand, and his imagination would have flown straight to Beirut, he had long united restless travel with meticulous observation. Deronda's final remark achieved its purpose.

"So," Kalonymos observed, "you might have become such a man as he if your education had not intervened. Your features resemble his—yet not entirely, young man. He possessed an iron will in his countenance; it strengthened everyone around him. Even in his youth, he bore a single deep vertical line upon his brow. I see none of that in you. Daniel Charisi used to say, 'Better a wrong will than a wavering; better a steadfast enemy than an uncertain friend; better a false belief than no belief at all.' What he despised most was indifference. He had deeper reasons than I can explain."

"Was his knowledge then not narrow?" Deronda asked, subtly referencing the common excuse for indecision—that it stems from knowing too much.

"Narrow? No," Kalonymos shook his head with a compassionate smile. "From childhood, he absorbed learning as easily as a plant draws up water. But he turned early to medicine and theories concerning life and health. He traveled widely, spending much of his fortune observing and understanding. What he insisted upon was that the strength and wealth of humanity depended on the balance between separation and communication. He was fiercely opposed to our people dissolving among the Gentiles: 'It is no better,' he would say, 'than various grains reverting from their diversity into uniformity.'
