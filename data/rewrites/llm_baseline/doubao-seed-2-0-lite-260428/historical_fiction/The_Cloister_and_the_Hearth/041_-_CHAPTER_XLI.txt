Hemmed in tight between his comrades to stamp out any mad impulse to flee, Denys was borne away in a storm of grief and gnawing remorse. Gerard had cast aside the safer German route to Rome purely for his sake, and this was the reward: left adrift, all alone, in the vast wilds of Burgundy. That thought was the maddest sting of all, making him rave at heaven and earth one moment, then sink into a savage, brooding silence so dark and menacing his guards judged it only prudent to strip him of his weapons.

They caught up to their troop leader just outside the town gates, and the whole cavalcade drew rein at the sign of the *Tete d’Or* to bait their horses and rest. The young landlady, harried half out of her wits tending to the count and more so to the bastard leading the raiding party, caught sight of Denys and leaned in anxiously to ask what had become of his quiet young travelling companion. Denys burst out in a flood of raw grief, told her every last detail of the separation, and begged her to send a rider after Gerard at once. “Now he is parted from me, he may yet listen to good sense,” he wept. “The poor wretch cannot bear to be alone.”

The landlady tossed her head, a flush rising high on her cheeks. “I think I have already been far too over-kind to the pair of you,” she said, and turned sharply away.
“You will not go?”
“Not I.”

At that denial, Denys loosed a whole torrent of curses and abuse at her. She turned her back sniffling, huffing that no one had ever dared speak to her in such a fashion, and ordered her stable hand to saddle two mules immediately. Denys rode north with his troop after that, hunched silent and drooping over his saddle bow, never guessing that that sharp-tongued young woman had slipped into a riding habit in only forty minutes (for she was truly in a hurry) and was spurring hard south with her servant, straight the way Gerard had wandered.

By dark, after a long day of hard marching, the bastard and his men trundled into the *White Hart*. Their arrival threw the whole inn into a roar of bustle, and it was some time before Manon spotted her old acquaintance Denys in the press of armed men. When she did, she betrayed the recognition only by a heightened flush to her cheeks — she made no move to claim their old familiarity. The poor soul had already begun to scorn the low, humble stations by which she had climbed to respectability. Denys saw the quiet slight, but he could not even summon a smile. Every corner of the inn stung, thick with memories of Gerard.

Before full dark fell, the wind shifted. Manon poked her head into the great room and beckoned him over with a single finger. He stood up sullen, his two guards trailing close at his heels. “Nay,” she said, “I would speak a word to thee in private.” She tugged him to a shadowed corner of the room, and whispered under her breath to ask if he would do her a small kindness. He answered loud and rough, “No, I will not. I am in no vein to do favours for man or woman this day. The only thing I would waste a kindness on is a dog, and even that I would skip if I could help it.”

“Alas, good archer,” Manon said humbly, “I did you one kindness once before, you and your pretty young comrade.”
“You did, dame, you did,” Denys softened at once. “Well then, for his sake — what is it you need?”
“Thou knowest my story. I fell on hard times once, made mistakes, but now I am worshipful, respected by all the village. But this very day a woman cast that old past sin right in my teeth, and it will haunt me every waking hour so long as that wretch hangs gibbeted by the road. I would have him taken down. Well-a-day!”
“With all my heart.”
“And none dare I ask but thee. Wilt do’t?”
“Not I, even if I were not a prisoner under guard.”

At that stern refusal, the tender Manon sighed and clasped her palms together in helpless despair. Denys told her not to fret: there were soldiers of a coarser, less scrupulous stamp who would not hesitate at such trivial work. It was nothing but a matter of coin. If she could lay hands on two gold angels, he could find two men to do that dirty work right there at the White Hart.

The thought was not exactly palatable, but Manon reflected that soldiers were birds of passage — they would be gone drinking in some far-off town by the next night, or knocked on the head in some border skirmish before the week was out. She murmured soft, “Send them out to me. But prithee, tell them ’tis for a friend of mine; let them not think ’tis me that asks. I should sink into the earth with shame — times are changed for me now.”

Denys had no trouble finding two warriors glad to earn a whole angel apiece for work so light. He sent them out to her, then dismissed the whole sordid matter from his mind immediately, sinking back into brooding over his lost friend. Manon and the two soldiers soon hammered out the rest of the plan — except for what to do with the body once it was cut down. She murmured, “The river runs nigh that very spot.”
“Fling him in, eh?”
“Nay, nay, do not be so cruel! Could ye not lay him down gently, and tie something heavy to him?” She must have turned the thought over in her head a hundred times already, for new ideas never came quick to slow, soft Manon.

All was agreed on speedily, save the time of payment. The mail-clad men itched for their gold, and demanded it upfront. Manon demurred. What, did they doubt their word? Then let her come along with them, or watch from a distance to make sure the work was done. “Me?” Manon gasped in horror. “I would liever die than see that done with my own eyes.”
“Which yet you would have done for all that.”
“Ay, for sore is my need. Times are changed.” She had already forgotten her earlier instruction to Denys, that the men must never learn the request came from her.

An hour later, the unpleasant last relic of Manon’s old, disgraced life no longer hung there to canker her new status as a respectable matron, no longer did the grim dead eyes of the past glower down into the clover fields she walked past each day. After that, Manon went on to make the innkeeper a steady, average wife, a famously fierce guardian of his household, and outlived every last person who remembered her old, poorer self. The two soldiers, returning from executing their grim errand with the unthinking, matter-of-fact brutality of medieval folk, got a sharp shock: the market square, empty only an hour prior, was now black with crowds of chattering villagers. Conscience smote them at once — they were sure they had been caught desecrating the dead. But the bolder of the two, emboldened by the dark, stole forward alone to slip in amongst a group, and soon returned to his companion, his tone sharp with entirely unreasonable reproach: “Ye born fool, it is only a miracle.”
