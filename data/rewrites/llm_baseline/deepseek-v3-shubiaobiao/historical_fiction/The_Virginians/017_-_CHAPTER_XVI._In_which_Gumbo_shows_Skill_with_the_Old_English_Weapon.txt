The young Virginian, having relieved his cousin and the chaplain of their coin, felt obliged to offer them the chance of reclaiming their losses—though his mother and other strict moralists might have frowned upon his conduct. Cards had become a dangerous habit. Beyond the genteel whist and quadrille with the ladies after dinner, there were nightly gatherings where the stakes grew alarmingly high, drawing in every gentleman of the house, my lord included. Since their Sunday conversation, his lordship had warmed considerably toward his colonial kinsman, wagering with him amiably over backgammon and piquet. Mr. William and the devout chaplain, meanwhile, harbored a secret taste for hazard—though they indulged discreetly, well out of sight of the ladies, who had extracted numerous promises from Cousin Will that he would neither lead Harry astray nor fall into mischief himself. Will swore he would never gamble—never!—yet no sooner did the household retire than he would slip away with dice and rum to Harry’s chambers, where the three would play until dawn.

When Harry had painted his glowing picture of the Virginia estate to Lord Castlewood, he had not meant to deceive. The lad was honest to a fault. But his upbringing had exposed him to all manner of rough company—horse-traders, tavern idlers, gamblers, and sportsmen, who thrived in the colony. A landed gentry, propped up by enslaved labor, found little diversion beyond the hunt, the card table, or the punch bowl. Hospitality was boundless; every man’s house stood open to his neighbor, and idle planters rode from one ramshackle mansion to another, finding the same rough plenty and raucous entertainment. A Virginian squire might sport a barefoot valet and a patched saddle, but his stables never lacked corn, nor his table claret and venison. Harry had slept on countless straw mattresses and caroused through endless nights, draining cracked bowls of punch until the hounds were loosed at dawn. His sober elder brother, he admitted ruefully, had been of a different temperament—proof enough that Nature molds men as she pleases: some for books and tea, others for Burgundy and hard riding.

Harry’s inclinations were plain to his English kin, none of whom mourned his lack of Puritan restraint. A century prior, manners were looser, tongues freer, and morals far less delicate than in our present refined age. (Shall we not congratulate ourselves on such progress, dear reader?)

Thus, though wary of his cousins’ prying, Harry held his cards close. In games of chance or skill, he proved more than a match for his new companions. Even at billiards—a pastime beloved by his grandfather, who had imported one of Virginia’s few tables—he soon rivaled Will and the chaplain. After their first wager, Harry grew cautious with Will, who conceded grudgingly that the colonial was his equal in most contests, and his better in many. Yet for all his prowess against the parson and Will (who naturally bested the ladies), why did Harry so often lose to one opponent in particular?

His courtesy to petticoats was instinctive—a tradition not yet extinguished in his homeland. Every woman at Castlewood adored him. The stern housekeeper softened; the cook beamed; the maids, French and English alike, simpered in his wake; even the lodge-keeper’s pretty daughter brightened at his greeting. Madame de Bernstein noted it all, watching the boy’s character with silent scrutiny.

Lady Maria Esmond’s age was a mystery. Peerage records being scarce, none could say whether Will’s claim of thirty-five was slander or mere exaggeration. Maria was his half-sister, born of the late Lord Castlewood’s first wife, a German lady wed during Queen Anne’s wars. When she assured Harry she was but seven-and-twenty, he protested gallantly: *"Your ladyship might be mother to all the cupids! You scarce look twenty!"*

In truth, Maria’s age shifted with her audience. A fair beauty with roses in her cheeks, golden tresses tumbling over her shoulders, and shapely arms that gleamed as she stretched across the billiard table, she was a vision—especially when a fleeting glimpse of ankle, clocked stocking, or red-heeled slipper sent Harry into raptures. Foolish boy! Had he ever noticed how his mother’s foot was far more slender? But sons seldom study maternal ankles with such fervor.

Alone, Maria treated Harry with marked kindness. In company, she mocked his provincial accent (which he labored to correct) and dismissed him as a simpleton. Yet her aunt remarked approvingly that they would soon polish him into a proper gentleman.

Lord Castlewood’s manner grew ever more familiar. With harvest pending, sport was limited to the occasional cockfight or bull-baiting. Harry and Will toured neighboring estates—the Henleys, the Crawleys, the Redmaynes—where heavy coaches brought country society for days of rustic revelry. More would have come, but for Madame de Bernstein’s disdain. "We are livelier without her," the younger set confessed. "She ruled our father, and now commands here. Even my brother fears her."

*"You will return for our merrier seasons, Harry?"* Maria entreated, her blue eyes soft. *"This is your home."* Her hand lingered in his. How mistaken he had been to deem his English kin cold! The Earl was kindness itself; dear Maria, angelic. He rued his hasty letter to Virginia, lamenting his chilly reception. Now he wrote anew, extolling their affection—and misspelling *angel* as *Angle*, to his tutor’s dry amusement.

*"Can the boy be in love with his 'Angle'?"* laughed Mountain in Virginia.

*"Maria? Forty if she’s a day!"* scoffed Madam Esmond. *"A gawky, carrot-haired creature with feet like bellows."*

Ah, where lies truth? Is beauty real, or do love’s eyes conjure it? Let us pray Cupid anoints our sight, that we may ever find the beloved flawless!

Nor was Harry the only favorite. His valet, Gumbo, dazzled the household with his talents—fishing, forging, hair-dressing, cooking, and fiddling—though his lies were as prodigious as his skills. He spun tales of Virginia’s splendors: vast gardens, endless slaves, and Madam Esmond’s ships and gold. The servants half-believed him; the family, wholly. Soon, Harry found himself credited with fortunes he never possessed, his modest habits taken as proof of hidden wealth.

Even Madame de Bernstein, privy to her sister’s true means, indulged the fiction, amused by the sudden deference paid her nephew. The Countess rued her early coldness; Lady Fanny’s arm bore bruises from maternal pinches meant to curb her friendliness. Only Maria’s constant presence thwarted their designs.

*"If he’s so rich, our aunt will leave him everything,"* grumbled my lord.

*"He wears his wealth lightly,"* Maria observed.

*"Rich men often do,"* sighed my lord. *"Were I wealthy, I’d hoard like a miser. Poverty forces my extravagance."*

Maria pored over *The History of Virginia*, dreaming of its fertile lands. Might she suit its climate? Would Harry’s mother approve? When pressed about his estate, Harry laughed off Gumbo’s exaggerations, but his modesty only deepened the illusion.

As weeks passed, Madame de Bernstein wearied of Castlewood. The smoky chimneys, the draughty halls—even the cook she had praised now displeased her. Tunbridge’s waters beckoned.

*"I go for my health,"* she declared. *"Alone. No simpering nieces, no prattling companions."*

*"Might I escort you?"* Will offered.

But Harry, flushing crimson, stammered a refusal—prior engagements, promised shooting.

*"You refuse?"* the Baroness snapped, her eyes blazing.

Maria bent over her needlework, silent. The air thickened with unspoken tensions.
