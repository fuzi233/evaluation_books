Gabriel Betteredge trundled beside me all the way to the railway station, silent for the first time in all the years I’d known him, the weight of Rosanna Spearman’s final letter hanging heavy between us. Tucked in my pocket was that very letter, and stuffed in a small sealed satchel was the nightgown smeared with Rachel’s birthday yellow paint — both destined for Mr. Bruff’s desk before I slept that night.

We didn’t speak a word until we’d cleared the lodge gates. “Before I catch the London train,” I said, breaking the hush, “I’ve got two questions to ask you. They’re about me, and they’ll probably shock you.”
“Shock me all you like, sir,” Betteredge groaned. “Anything that’ll get that poor girl’s letter out of my head for five minutes. Go on.”
“First question: was I drunk the night of Rachel’s birthday?”
The old steward nearly laughed out loud. “*You* drunk? The great flaw in your character, Mr. Franklin, is that you never touch a drop of liquor after dinner. But now you mention it — you did break habit that night, when you looked so peaked and ill we talked you into a little brandy and water to cheer you up.”
“I never drink brandy. It’s entirely possible —”
“Hold on a second. I poured you half a wineglass of our 50-year-old Cognac, and drowned the whole noble lot in nearly a full tumbler of cold water. A toddler couldn’t have got tipsy on it, never mind a grown man.”
I knew his memory for small household details was flawless. Intoxication was impossible. I moved on to the second question.
“Before I was sent abroad, you saw me every day as a boy. Did you ever catch me sleepwalking?”
Betteredge stopped dead, stared at me for a long beat, then nodded and kept walking. “I see your drift now, sir. You’re trying to explain how you got that paint on your nightgown without remembering it. Won’t work. You never sleepwalked once in your life. Hundreds of people would have spotted the habit if you’d had it, at home or on your travels, they’d have warned you, taken precautions. You’d know.”
He could only be right. But I clung stubbornly to the only two explanations that could get me out of the monstrous position I was trapped in — until Betteredge scattered both theories to the wind before we even reached the platform.
“Let’s test your logic,” he said, shrewd as ever. “Say you were blackout drunk, or sleepwalking, when you touched that paint and took the Moonstone. That explains the birthday night and the morning after. But how do you explain the Diamond turning up in London, pledged to Mr. Luker weeks later? Did you do all that in a blackout too? Was I seeing you off in the pony-chaise on that Saturday evening while you were sleepwalking? Did you trudge all the way to Luker’s pawnshop half-asleep after your train pulled in? Forgive me for saying it, Mr. Franklin, but this mess has addled your wits. The sooner you put your head next to Mr. Bruff’s, the sooner you’ll see your way out of this dead end.”
We reached the station with barely two minutes to spare. I scribbled my London address down for Betteredge, told him to write at once if anything new turned up, and promised I’d send news back the second I had it. Glancing over at the book stall as I turned to climb into my carriage, I spotted Mr. Candy’s strange, sharp-eyed assistant — Ezra Jennings, the man with the streaked piebald hair — deep in conversation with the stall keeper. Our eyes met, he tipped his hat politely, and I nodded back before jumping aboard as the train’s whistle blew. Absurd as it was, I found myself dwelling on the odd coincidence that I’d run into the strange man twice in the same single day, as the train pulled out for London.

I arrived long after Bruff’s city law office had closed, so I drove straight to his private Hampstead residence, and disturbed the old lawyer dozing alone in his dining room, his favourite pug curled on his lap, a decanter of his best port within easy reach.
He heard my full story without a single interruption, then immediately ordered extra lights and strong tea sent up to his study, and told his wife and daughters under no circumstances to disturb us for any reason at all. When the door was locked behind us, he first laid the nightgown out flat on his desk to inspect the paint stain, then read every line of Rosanna Spearman’s letter slowly and carefully.
Only when he’d turned the last page did he speak.
“Franklin Blake,” he said, “this is far more serious than you’ve yet realised. It concerns Rachel just as much as it concerns you. Her strange, cold silence all these weeks? It’s no mystery now. She believes you stole the Moonstone.”
I’d shrunk from spelling that conclusion out to myself, but it had hung over my head for days, nonetheless. That was exactly why I was determined to face her in person.
“Our first step,” Bruff went on, “is to break through that silence of hers. She’s kept quiet this long out of that stubborn, proud, private nature I know so well. We can’t let her stay quiet any longer. We have to make her tell us exactly what evidence she’s relying on to be so sure you took the Diamond. Nine times out of ten, this whole mess will fall apart the second she lets her reserve crack and speaks out.”
“That’s a comforting thought,” I said. “But I don’t see how you can be so sure.”
“Simple. I’m looking at this as a lawyer, not a friend. It’s a question of evidence. Right now, the whole case against you collapses at the very first hurdle. I grant the name stitched in the nightgown proves it’s yours. I grant the paint stain proves that nightgown left the smudge on Rachel’s door. But what single scrap of evidence is there to prove *you* were the person wearing it the night the Moonstone went missing?”
The objection hit me like a blow — I’d felt it lurking at the back of my mind for days, but never dared name it.
“I can see why Rosanna’s letter is so distressing for you to analyse fairly,” Bruff went on, picking up the pages. “But I have no personal stake here. I can read it like any other legal document. Even setting aside her history as a convicted thief, she admits straight out that she was an expert at deception. She writes that she was madly jealous of Rachel, that she deliberately swapped the roses in your buttonhole at the birthday dinner, that she saw a perfect chance for herself if you and Rachel fell out permanently. The odds are 99 to 100 that she was the one who showed that nightgown to Rachel, all to drive a wedge between the two of you. She wouldn’t have hesitated to steal the Moonstone herself if it meant ruining your happiness. She didn’t even kill herself then, remember — she was still planning to win you over. That was entirely in her character.”
“I thought of that the second I opened her letter,” I said. “But I felt so sorry for the poor dead girl, I couldn’t bring myself to say it out loud.”
“Does you nothing but credit, my boy. But let’s test the other scenario — say we *do* find proof you wore that nightgown that night. Clearing your name will be a nightmare. So let’s not borrow that trouble today. Let’s find out first if Rachel is only judging you on that nightgown, no other evidence.”
“Good God,” I burst out, “what right has she to suspect me of being a thief on any evidence at all?”
“A fair question, hot as you phrased it. It puzzles me too. Think — did something happen while you were staying at the house, not enough to make her doubt your honour, but just enough to plant a tiny, unreasonable seed of doubt about your judgement?”
I shot to my feet, shaken. I’d completely forgotten the incident.
A couple of months before the birthday, a hot-tempered French lawyer had tracked me down to Lady Verinder’s house, sent by a Paris restaurant keeper I’d borrowed a small sum from, after my bill for the debt had bounced. The two of us had yelled at each other right outside the drawing room, and Rachel and her mother had heard every word. Lady Verinder had paid the man off to send him packing, but Rachel had flown into a rage at my reckless carelessness, called me unprincipled, dishonourable, said there was no telling what stupid, selfish thing I might do next. We’d made up two days later, and I’d never thought of it again.
Bruff nodded gravely the second I finished telling him the story. “That’s exactly it. That old memory was sitting there, waiting, the second she saw that nightgown. It’s a predisposition against you we didn’t know about, and that’s one mystery solved. Now our only next move is to go talk to Rachel.”
“The grand difficulty,” he said, pacing the study, “is getting her to open up completely. Do you have any ideas?”
“I’ve decided to speak to her myself.”
Bruff stopped dead, stared at me like I’d lost my mind. “You? The very last person on earth she’d speak openly to about this?” He paced a couple more turns, then grinned suddenly. “Wait. In cases this strange, the rash way is often the best. Nothing venture, nothing win. You have one advantage I will never have. She’s still got that soft, stubborn little spot in her heart for you. Tap that, and she’ll tell you every single thing she’s been holding back. I’ve got a plan. My wife and daughters will invite her over to spend the day tomorrow — no mention of you, no hint of the trap. You sneak in through the back garden gate at three o’clock, go through the conservatory, cross the small drawing room, and the first door on the other side leads to the music room. She’ll be there all alone.”
He handed me the key to the garden gate the next morning, turning up at my lodgings while I was still at breakfast, looking faintly guilty. Rachel was coming, he said, his wife and daughters were fully in on the scheme and thought it was a brilliant idea, no qualms at all about tricking their guest for a good cause.
The letter from Betteredge waiting in my post bag that day was mostly just the old steward writing for the pleasure of talking to me, but he added a small note: Ezra Jennings had stopped him at the station the day he saw me, asked who I was, and Mr. Candy had been furious he’d missed the chance to speak to me, begging me to call on him next time I was anywhere near Frizinghall. I barely read the words. All I could think of was the coming meeting.
At three sharp, I turned the key in Bruff’s back garden gate, slipped inside, and locked it behind me. The paths were empty, no one but the birds and the bees around to see me. I walked through the conservatory, crossed the small drawing room, and paused with my hand on the music room door. Soft, plaintive piano chords drifted out from the other side, the same idle little melody Rachel used to play when we lazed around her mother’s drawing room, no troubles between us at all. The contrast between that old happy memory and the terrible secret hanging over us shook me so badly I had to stop a full minute to steady my breathing.
Then I drew myself up, turned the doorknob, and stepped inside.
