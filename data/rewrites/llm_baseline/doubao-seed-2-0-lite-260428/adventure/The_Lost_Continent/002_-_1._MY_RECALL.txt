The grand viceregal installation ceremony was done, but the thunder of the drums and bray of trumpets still hummed in my bones. The imperial proclamation had been read, Empress Phorenice’s name roared in adoration by every throat in the gilded pyramid hall, and Tatho—my old brother-in-arms, my once humble comrade from the Priests’ Clan—had been formally seated on the snake-throne, the 300-gemmed chain of supreme office draped over his shoulders. For twenty years I’d held that throne. Today I’d handed the reins of the Yucatan colony to him, no protest, no fuss, as ritual demanded.

Tatho rose for his first state procession, I walked behind him, arms folded, head bowed as custom dictated, between ranks of burnished copper guards, courtiers in iridescent feathered robes, tribal chiefs draped in jaguar pelts. The roof timbers shook with the cries: “Long live Tatho! Empress Phorenice forever!” He inclined his head slow, stately, acknowledging each cheer as the ritual required, moving to the three lesser thrones of the East, North and South provincial governors to receive their homage. I followed in his wake, humbled, deposed, and I will admit the iron mask of a passionless court face was hard to hold in place. Twenty years earlier, all these same bows, all these same obeisances, had been offered to me. The gods had turned the wheel of fortune, and I was the one beneath it now.

But etiquette could not forbid the soft, warm glances. Every guard, every courtier, every chief lining that hall looked at me not with the scorn reserved for failed rulers, but with quiet gratitude, with unfeigned regret. It is a rare thing, to step down from power and find no hand raised to trip you. The old, unbreakable custom of installation days allowed every attendee to level accusations against the outgoing viceroy with full amnesty, to push for policy changes, to air every grievance unpunished. We circled the hall three full times, as law required. Not a single voice spoke against my twenty years of rule. Not a single person called for a shift in how the colony was run. The silence was a greater tribute than all the cheering for Tatho.

We marched across tiles inlaid solid with beaten gold, the great officers of state falling in behind us, until we reached the heavy stone doors of the private viceregal quarters—quarters that had been mine, an hour before. I had no home left in Yucatan’s ziggurat cities, no right to the palaces I’d built, the roads I’d carved through the impenetrable jungle. I should have counted myself lucky, of course: any other ousted viceroy would have lost his head the second the new ruler’s seal hit the proclamation paper. But the bitter tang of displacement stung all the same.

Tatho called out the required ritual phrase: “Open to the Viceroy!” The slave attendants hauled the two massive stone valves wide. He stepped inside first, I at his heels, the rest of the procession halting at the threshold to call their formal farewells. The stone doors clanged shut behind us, the heavy lock slamming home, and for the first time all the court pomp, all the masks of office, fell away. Tatho folded his arms the same humble way he used to when we were lads running errands for the lowliest village priest, and bowed low to me.

“Deucalion,” he said, quiet, “I never sought this post. It was forced on me. If I’d refused, my head would be on a spike outside the Atlantis palace gates by now, and some creature who hates you with every bone in his body would have been sent out here to replace you. Empress Phorenice does not suffer her will to be questioned.”

“My friend,” I said, and clapped him on the shoulder, “there is no man alive in all of Atlantis or its overseas territories I would have handed this government to more gladly. For twenty years I’ve ruled here, from the rainforest coasts of Yucatan to the high sierras of Mexico, first under the old King, then as minister to this new Empress. I know this land like the lines on my own palm. I’ve hunted jaguars and roving savage tribes in the deepest jungle. I built roads that cut travel time across the province by half, dammed the rivers so ocean-going galleons could sail three hundred miles inland. I nurtured the metalworking arts, the jade carvers, the astronomers, and said the daily rituals to the Sun God three times a day myself. Through famines, through hurricanes, through border skirmishes, I ruled only to make this people prosperous, to tie them close to the motherland Atlantis. I love them like a father. And now I hand them to you, Tatho, and I beg you to guard their welfare as your own.”

“I will never fill your boots, Deucalion,” he said, and his voice was thick. “But I swear to you, I will follow every step of your rule exactly, no new stupid changes, no greedy taxes to line imperial pockets. I came out here kicking and screaming, I’d never have agreed to take your place if I’d known how much this deposition would hurt you.”

“We’re alone here,” I said, “no need for fine words. The formality of the court is shut outside that stone door. And this came out of nowhere. Till an hour ago, when you requested audience, I thought I had another ten years of rule ahead of me. I still don’t know why they’re pulling me back to Atlantis.”

“The official proclamation says they need your skills back home, to serve the empire,” Tatho said, and then he glanced over his shoulder at the tapestries, pulled me further into the center of the room, and dropped his voice so low I almost had to lean in to hear. “But that’s just the cover story. I think there’s trouble brewing. Phorenice is pulling every able, experienced governor back to the capital, because her throne is shaking.”

“You don’t have to whisper,” I told him. “This whole wing is carved out of solid bedrock, ten feet of stone between us and any other chamber. I oversaw every course of masonry laid here, no hidden nooks for eavesdroppers. Besides, we don’t do that kind of slimy court trickery out here in the colonies. We consider listening in on private conversations a disgrace fit for a slave.”

Tatho huffed a bitter laugh. “That’s the innocence of the colonies, right? Back home in Atlantis, a loose tongue gets you beheaded before sundown. Half the court’s full of spies paid to carry every private word straight to the palace. But fine, since you’re sure we’re safe: the throne’s wobbling bad. Phorenice is scrambling for every strong, loyal man she can find to prop it up.”

“I haven’t been back to Atlantis in twenty years,” I said. “I don’t know the first thing about their modern politics. The few bits of news that drift across the ocean on trading galleons never felt relevant to us. Yucatan’s its own world, you’ll learn that soon enough, full of new problems, new people, new priorities. Atlantis feels like a half-remembered dream, a shadow far across the water. I poured every last bit of my energy into this place.”

“Deucalion, you don’t know how famous you are back home,” Tatho said, grinning a little. “When we were lads in the priest college, they used to tell us the great heroes of the past were the greatest men who ever lived. Now? They teach your name as the model. Mothers name their newborn sons Deucalion, it’s the most popular name in the whole empire. You’re a household word. Only Phorenice’s name is spoken as often.”

I frowned. That news irked me. “I did my duty for duty’s sake, not for the crowd’s cheap praise. And besides, the people’s mouths should be full of the names of the gods, not mortal men.”

Tatho shrugged. “The gods haven’t been very popular lately. Modern science, new inventions, people have outgrown the old stories, and no new god has popped up to replace them. If your only competition was the gods, your name would be ten times bigger than it is now.”

“Of mortal names,” I said, “Empress Phorenice’s should be first. The old King’s dead, she’s the ruler.”

“Oh, she’s determined to make sure that’s the case,” Tatho said, and there was a sharp, dark edge to his tone. I pulled him over to a carved marble bench, sat down beside him.

“I’m not talking to the new Viceroy of Yucatan right now,” I said. “I’m talking to Tatho, my old classmate from the priest college, the man who fought beside me in a dozen border skirmishes, who spent three straight weeks with me sorting out a hay farmer’s land dispute when we were just rookie low-level administrators, long before any of us got famous. This room was mine two hours ago, and that doesn’t change the fact that between us, we never used anything but plain, unvarnished truth. Spit it out. What’s happening with Phorenice? What has she done to make the ancient, unshakable Atlantis throne wobble like a loose tooth?”

Tatho sighed. “Man, I’ve gotten so used to polished, empty court words, I almost forgot how to speak plain. But for the sake of those old days, when we cared more about a farmer’s stolen goat than a whole rebellious city, I’ll tell you everything. Just know this: if anyone overhears me, we both get tortured to death and hung on the palace walls as traitors. You know the old law of Atlantis, right? The throne was supposed to pass only to a man born of the Priestly Clan. Phorenice is neither. She’s a swineherd’s daughter, from the poorest mountain village in the north. She’s declared herself a god’s child, born of a miracle, and anyone who even whispers that they remember her real parents gets burned alive. The whole story’s officially a secret now, and half the people believe the fable. Zaemon, the provincial governor of that mountain region, his wife adopted her, raised her as their own. The swineherd and his wife tried to protest. They turned up dead two weeks later.

“She grew up sharp as a blade. Before she was fifteen, she ruled the whole Zaemon household, and half the province along with it. Remember Zaemon? The old fool, all wrapped up in occult mysteries, no backbone at all, couldn’t lead a patrol to catch a stray dog, let alone an army. She learned to fight with spear and sword, trained with the royal guards, and when a rebellion blew up in the province, no one expected her to march out with the troops. But she did. Crushed the rebellion in a week. Gave every surviving rebel a choice: die by the sword, or join her army. Every last one of them joined. She’s got a magnetism about her, Deucalion. You’ve never seen anything like it.”

Tatho leaned forward, his eyes bright, like he was half lost in the memory. “The Priests’ Clan got furious, of course. They sent two neighbouring governors with their full armies to arrest her. She wiped the floor with both of them. Killed them both in battle, gave their troops the same choice—die, or join her. Her army tripled in size. Then the Council of Priests got desperate. They gathered the largest, best-equipped army Atlantis had ever raised. And they put me in command. They even promised me the throne, if I won. Told me the old king was dying, that a conquering hero would be the obvious pick for new emperor. I swore I’d capture her alive, drag her back to the capital in chains. I had every advantage. Our army outnumbered hers eight to one. But she’d invented something no one had ever seen before: fire-tubes, that hurl a sharp iron dart further than two bowshots could reach. She used tactics none of us had ever studied. Her men would harry our flanks, hit us and run, never let us get close for the hand-to-hand battle we were trained for. My whole army melted away like sand in the tide. Panic broke out. I tried to die fighting, rather than go back to the capital and get beheaded for failure. But she found me, surrounded by three of my last loyal men, fighting off a whole squad of her troops. She walked right up, looked me dead in the eye, gave me the same choice: die by her sword, or swear service to her. And just then, a runner came galloping up with news: the old King was dead.

“I looked at her, Deucalion. Fairest woman I ever saw, sharpest mind I ever met, the greatest general in the history of our people. And I knelt. I swore fealty to her, right there on the battlefield. I’ve been her man ever since. You can judge me all you want, but wait till you see her with your own eyes, feel that magnetism. You’ll understand. Every man who meets her does.”
