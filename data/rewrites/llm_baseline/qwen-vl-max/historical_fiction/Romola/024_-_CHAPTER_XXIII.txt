“You’re easily frightened, though,” Piero said, chuckling with contempt. “My portrait doesn’t do justice to the original. But that old man *did* have a tiger’s look—let’s go into the Duomo and see him again.”

“It’s no pleasure to be seized by a madman—if he truly is one,” Lorenzo Tornabuoni replied, speaking gently in defense of Tito. “But perhaps he’s merely a brute. We’ll hear more soon enough. Still, I think we should check whether we have the authority to stop this quarrel between our men and yours,” he added, turning to the Frenchman.

With swords drawn, they moved forward through the crowd, the quiet onlookers parting like an escort before them. Tito followed—though not from choice. He had to know what others knew about Baldassarre. The initial surge of terror was fading, replaced by frantic calculations born of mortal danger: the timid mind, when pressed, invents swiftly.

The rabble—boys and rough men alike—were more inclined to jeer at the soldier than harm him. They scattered as the noblemen approached, their weapons gleaming in the sun. The French soldier was questioned.

He and his comrades had brought their prisoners into Florence only to solicit ransom money. Two were Tuscan soldiers captured in Lunigiana; the third—an elderly man—had been traveling with a group of Genoese when the French foragers clashed near Fivizzano. The prisoner might be mad, but he posed no real threat. The soldier admitted he couldn’t understand a word the old man spoke.

Tito heard this much—but nothing beyond it until directly addressed. It was Tornabuoni who spoke.

“Will you return with us, Melema? Or, since Messere is departing for Signa, will you follow the times’ fashion and go hear the Frate? He’ll be at full flood this morning—like a torrent unleashed. We all must, if we wish to preserve our Medicean skins. I would go myself, if I had the time.”

Tito’s color had returned. He managed a smile, forcing lightness into his voice. “Of course, I admire the inspired orator,” he said. “But alas, I shall be detained by the Segretario until the procession begins.”

“I’m going back into the Duomo to see that savage old man again,” Piero declared.

“Then show mercy and take him to one of the hospitals for travelers,” Tornabuoni replied. “The monks may determine whether he needs caging.”

They parted ways. Tito walked toward the Palazzo Vecchio, where Bartolommeo Scala awaited him. The journey was short, yet for him it stretched into eternity—each step across street and piazza a moment thick with memory, dread, and foreboding. It felt as though a serpent had begun coiling around his limbs.

Baldassarre lived. In Florence. And that was a living vengeance, restless as a serpent, never still until its prey was crushed.

That man did not let wrongs pass. His love and hatred burned with such fierce intensity that they consumed everything else within him—driving him to sacrifice himself, even, for the sake of passion, as if it were a god demanding worship through self-destruction.

Once, Baldassarre had vanished. Tito understood the silence well: it meant the vengeance was being prepared—studied, shaped, made certain.

If only he had spoken differently. If only he could have summoned the courage, the composure, to admit recognition. Would the danger then have been less? He might have claimed he possessed proof of Baldassarrne’s death. Only two people could ever contradict such a claim: Fra Luca, who was dead, and the crew of the galley that had brought news of the pirate encounter. The odds were vast against Baldassarre ever meeting any of them again. And now, bitterly, Tito thought: a well-timed lie, carefully constructed, might have spared him all this.

But to lie required control—a mastery of self in the midst of shock. He had spoken without thought. The words burst forth like a child born in darkness, nurtured in secrecy.

Tito was learning a cruel truth of the human soul: we shape ourselves through repeated choices—good or evil—until character becomes fixed, and sudden acts are inevitable.

Now, there was only one hope: that Baldassarre failed. That his pursuit ended in futility.

And then—Tito grasped a darker idea, sharper than any he’d ever allowed himself: perhaps his own unthinking words held some truth. Enough truth, at least, to defend himself against any accusation Baldassarre might make.

The old man looked wild, deranged. With a heart so eager and a mind so tormented, madness was possible. Suffering could twist reason. If so, then the vengeance aimed at disgrace might collapse under its own absurdity.

Yet another kind of vengeance remained—one that no lie could escape.

Baldassarre came from a breed for whom the dagger feels as natural as the tiger’s claw. Tito recoiled—not just from shame, but from physical fear. A soft nature, indulgent and pleasure-seeking, fears death not as release, but as a violent end. He longed for armor—some hidden shield—to protect him from a fate no deceit could deflect.

He marveled at the power of his own terror. It was as though a blight had struck him, turning the warmth of youth into cold pain.

Still, one path remained open: he could turn back, seek out Baldassarre, confess everything—to him, to Romola, to the world. But he never considered it.

True repentance—what breaks ties with evil—requires more than fear. It demands moral strength. Tito lacked that sense. He saw no safety in truth. His only trust lay in cleverness, in concealment.

Now that the first wave of panic had passed, he believed he could face anything—with calm lies and defensive plans.

It was telling, in this crisis, that Tito never conceived of any direct way to remove Baldassarre. All other options crossed his mind—even flight from Florence—but not a single plan to destroy or neutralize his enemy.

His fear bred no hatred. He still wished no one pain. He had simply chosen to live comfortably, to carry life lightly, avoiding every pinch. And this choice, over time, had led him into traps he never foresaw.

The question was no longer whether he would share in the common burden of fate with others. It was whether lies—every trick, every falsehood—could save him from being crushed beneath the weight of his own choices.
