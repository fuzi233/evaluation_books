The last of the birthday guests had barely vanished down the drive before I trundled back to the inner hall, and found Samuel posted at the side table, minding a tray heaped with decanters of brandy and soda water. Lady Verinder and Miss Rachel stepped out of the drawing-room a moment later, trailed by the two gentlemen: Mr. Godfrey made straight for the refreshments, pouring himself a generous brandy and soda, while Mr. Franklin dropped onto a hall chair looking dead to the world, too worn out from the day’s nonstop talking to touch a drop.

Lady Verinder turned to wish them goodnight, and her gaze locked sharp on that wicked Colonel’s cursed legacy, the Moonstone, blazing bright on Rachel’s dress.
“Rachel,” she asked, “where do you mean to keep that diamond tonight?”

Miss Rachel was high on the thrill of the whole day, giddy and overflowing with that silly, stubborn streak all young girls get when they’re overexcited after a party. First she declared she hadn’t the faintest clue where to put it, then said of course it would go on her dressing table with the rest of her trinkets, then shivered playfully at the thought of its weird, eerie moon-glow lighting up the pitch black bedroom in the dead of night. Next her eye fell on the carved Indian cabinet standing in her sitting room, and she made up her mind in a flash: she’d tuck her Indian diamond right into the Indian cabinet, so the two beautiful native treasures could admire each other all night. My lady cut her nonsense short before she could run any further with it.
“My dear, that cabinet hasn’t a lock on it.”
“Good heavens, mamma!” Rachel laughed. “Do you think we’re staying in a thieves’ hotel?”

Lady Verinder paid no mind to her silly tone. She bade the two gentlemen goodnight, then kissed Rachel on the cheek. “Why not let me keep the diamond locked in my room for you tonight?”

Rachel looked at her like she was trying to snatch away her favourite new doll, the way she would have when she was seven years old. My lady saw there was no reasoning with her that night. “Come to my room first thing tomorrow morning,” she said. “I’ve got something important to talk to you about.” She trundled off slowly after that, lost in her own thoughts, and it was plain to see they were troubling her no end.

Rachel said her goodnights next: first she shook hands with Mr. Godfrey, who was lingering at the far end of the hall staring at a wall painting, then she doubled back to where Mr. Franklin still sat, tired and quiet in his corner. I couldn’t hear the words they exchanged, but standing near the big oak-framed looking glass, I caught her reflection: she slyly slipped the locket Mr. Franklin had given her out of the bosom of her dress, flashed it at him for half a second with a secret, soft little smile, then tripped off up the stairs to bed. That little moment threw all my old assumptions off balance. For the first time I thought my daughter Penelope might have been right all along about what was going on in our young lady’s heart.

As soon as Rachel was gone, Mr. Franklin spotted me. His restlessly shifting train of thought had already circled back to the Indian jugglers.
“Betteredge, I’m half convinced I took Mr. Murthwaite far too seriously when we talked in the shrubbery,” he said. “I wonder if he was just spinning us old traveller’s tales. Are you really going to let the dogs loose to patrol tonight?”
“I’ll take their collars off, sir,” I told him. “They’ll be free to roam the grounds if they smell a reason to.”
“Good,” he said. “We’ll sort out what to do tomorrow. I’ve no wish to alarm my aunt over nothing. Good night.”

He looked so pale and worn as he picked up his candle to head upstairs that I offered him a nightcap of brandy and water, and Mr. Godfrey backed me up, pressing the drink on him in the friendliest possible way. Mr. Franklin turned it down at first, but the moment he and Mr. Godfrey (whose bedrooms were right next door to each other) got to the landing, he called back down to me: “On second thought, I might want a drink in the night. Send a tray of brandy and water up to my room, will you?”

I sent Samuel up with the drink, then trundled out to unclip the hounds’ collars. The two of them were so shocked to be let loose that late they jumped all over me like overexcited puppies, but the heavy pouring rain cooled them off fast. They lapped a mouthful of water each, then trotted straight back to their kennels. I glanced up at the sky as I went back inside, and saw signs the storm would break by sunrise—though for now the rain was still lashing down, and the lawn was so soaked you could sink your ankle in the mud.

Samuel and I did a full sweep of the whole house, checking every door and window lock myself, no cutting corners that night. It was nearly one in the morning by the time I climbed into bed.

All the day’s worries were buzzing round my head, and I caught that same restless, sleepless malady Mr. Franklin was always complaining about. I didn’t drift off until the sun was peeking over the treetops, the whole house dead quiet the whole time, nothing but the patter of rain and the wind sighing through the oak trees. I woke at half past seven, pulled open my window to bright clear sunshine, and was just about to head outside to chain the dogs back up when I heard a frantic rustle of petticoats tearing down the stairs behind me.

It was Penelope, running as fast as her legs could carry her, screaming at the top of her voice. “Father! Come upstairs, for God’s sake! The Diamond is gone!”

I thought she’d lost her wits, but she dragged me straight to Miss Rachel’s sitting room, the one connected to her bedroom. There on the threshold stood Rachel, pale as the white dressing gown wrapped around her, the two doors of her Indian cabinet flung wide open, one of its drawers pulled all the way out as far as it would go.
“I saw Miss Rachel put the Diamond in that drawer with my own two eyes last night!” Penelope cried. I leaned over the cabinet, and the drawer was empty.
“Is this true, miss?” I asked.

She stared back at me, her face nothing like the bright, sharp girl I’d known her whole life. “The Diamond is gone.”
No sooner had the words left her mouth than she stepped back into her bedroom, locked the door, and bolted it behind her.

The noise drew Lady Verinder in a minute later, and the news of the stolen twenty-thousand-pound jewel left her stone still. She marched straight to Rachel’s door, and Rachel let her in without a word. The alarm spread through the rest of the house like wildfire. Mr. Godfrey stumbled out of his room first, holding up his hands in total bewilderment, looking far less steady of spirit than I’d ever seen him. Mr. Franklin followed right after, still half dazed—for once he’d slept a full, uninterrupted night, and the unusual rest had left him befuddled at first, until he drank his usual strong pre-breakfast coffee. Then his sharp, clear-headed side kicked in, and he took charge, resolute as anything.

First he ordered no one touch the lower doors or windows, leave them exactly as they’d been locked the night before. Then he suggested we search the gaps behind the cabinet and the table it stood on, just in case the diamond had slipped out of sight. We found nothing. When he sent Penelope to knock on Rachel’s door to ask her what had happened, Lady Verinder answered through the wood, and said Rachel wouldn’t speak of the theft to anyone, not even her own mother. She was too distraught to be seen.

Lady Verinder pulled herself together after a minute, calm and decisive as ever. “I suppose there’s no choice. I must send for the police.”
“And the first thing the police need to do is arrest those Indian jugglers who performed on the terrace last night,” Mr. Franklin cut in. He wouldn’t stop to explain the whole plot right then, just asked her for a quick note of introduction to the magistrate at Frizinghall, saying he was acting on her behalf, so he could ride out at once before the thieves got clean away. She wrote the note slowly, reluctantly. It was plain to see, with her old hatred of her dead brother’s cursed birthday gift, that she would have almost rather let the Moonstone vanish for good than drag the whole family into a messy public investigation.

I walked Mr. Franklin to the stables, and asked him how on earth the jugglers could have gotten inside the house at all. “One of them must have snuck in during the chaos of all the guests leaving last night,” he said. “He could have hidden under the drawing room sofa, heard us all talking about where the diamond was being kept, and waited till the house went quiet to take it.” He called to the groom to open the gates, and galloped off.

But his explanation made no sense to me. I’d checked the front door that morning, and it was still locked and bolted exactly as I’d left it the night before. Every other window and door was fastened tight, and the two dogs came bounding around the corner just then, rolling over each other on the wet grass, bright and alert as ever—no one could have snuck past them, or drugged them. The more I turned it over in my head, the less Mr. Franklin’s clever little theory added up.

We all forced ourselves to sit down and eat breakfast, robbery or no robbery, you can’t run a household on empty stomachs. Afterwards, Lady Verinder called me in, and I had to tell her everything I’d known about the jugglers’ plot that I’d kept quiet up to that point. She took the news bravely, but she was far more upset about Rachel than the stolen diamond. “Rachel’s never acted this strange before,” she said. “She’s never once cared half as much for trinkets as other girls, and now she’s locked away in her room, inconsolable, like that cursed jewel has gotten under her skin somehow.”

Everyone in the house was knocked completely off their usual routine. Mr. Godfrey, who made his living comforting distressed people, wandered aimlessly through the halls and gardens, torn between leaving as a guest out of respect for the family’s privacy, and staying to offer help. The maids all huddled in corners whispering, staring suspiciously at every little creak and shadow, even the quiet, withdrawn Rosanna Spearman got swept up in their panicky huddles. I was fidgety and irritable myself— that accursed yellow Moonstone had turned all of our lives upside down.

A little before eleven, Mr. Franklin rode back. He’d left the house at a gallop, he came back walking his horse slow, limp as wet cotton. “Superintendent Seegrave and two of his constables are following me in a fly,” he said. “But it’s no use. The Indians are completely innocent. They were seen heading back to their lodging house in Frizinghall at ten-thirty last night, the police checked on them again doing a routine sweep of the lodgings at midnight, and there’s no possible way they could have gotten back here to steal the diamond. The magistrate locked them up on a petty vagrancy charge to hold them for a week just in case, but the whole Indian lead is dead.”

Ten minutes later, Superintendent Seegrave arrived at the house. He was tall, portly, military in his bearing, with a booming commanding voice and a stare that could make a stone confess. He did a full sweep of the premises inside and out, and announced his verdict within half an hour: no one had broken into the house from outside. Which meant the thief was one of us, someone living under this roof. The maids panicked so bad they stampeded straight up to Miss Rachel’s sitting room, all of them crowding in to demand the Superintendent tell them which of them he suspected. He barked at them to clear out, pointing to a tiny smudge of decorative paint scraped on the edge of Rachel’s door right under the lock, saying one of their long petticoats had done the damage, and he’d have none of them cluttering up his investigation. Rosanna Spearman slipped out first, the rest trailing after her.

He questioned Penelope next, starting out so sharp and accusatory that she nearly blew up at him, until I stepped in to smooth things over. Her story was plain and unshakable: she’d seen Rachel put the diamond in the cabinet drawer the night before, brought her a cup of tea at eight the next morning, found the drawer open and empty, and raised the alarm. He asked to speak to Miss Rachel next, and got told flat out she wouldn’t see any policeman, had nothing to say to him. Mr. Franklin whispered to me that Seegrave was a total useless idiot. Mr. Godfrey whispered to me a minute later that he was the most competent, impressive officer he’d ever met. Many men, many opinions, as the old saying goes.

Seegrave decided to double check the sitting room, searching for any piece of furniture that might have been moved in the night. We were poking around between chairs and tables when Rachel’s bedroom door creaked open. After refusing to see a single soul all morning, Miss Rachel walked right out into the middle of the three of us, unprompted. She grabbed her garden hat off a nearby chair, turned straight to Penelope, and opened her mouth to speak.
