The next day the Miss Dashwoods were admitted to the Park by a door that sighed on its hinges with the damp, and were hardly within the drawing-room before another door flew open as if the weather itself had pushed it—Mrs. Palmer, bright as a robin in rain, came running in, cheeks warmed, eyes shining, her laughter already a kind of music that made the room feel less chilled.

She caught them all by the hand as though she meant to keep them from being carried off by the draughts, and placed herself between Elinor and Marianne with the decision of one who would season a dish properly, no matter who protested.

“I am so glad to see you!—because it is such a bad day I was afraid you might not come, which would be shocking, for we go away again tomorrow. We must go, for the Westons come to us next week, you know. It was quite sudden our coming at all—I knew nothing of it till the carriage was coming to the door, and then Mr. Palmer asked me if I would go with him to Barton. He is so droll! He never tells me any thing!—I am so sorry we cannot stay longer; however we shall meet again in town very soon, I hope.”

They were obliged—gently, firmly—to put an end to so forward a hope.

“Not go to town!” cried Mrs. Palmer, laughing as if London were a warm kitchen and the country only a larder one visited for supplies. “I shall be quite disappointed if you do not. I could get the nicest house in the world for you, next door to ours, in Hanover-square. You must come indeed. I am sure I shall be very happy to chaperon you at any time till I am confined, if Mrs. Dashwood should not like to go into public.”

They thanked her, and resisted; and as she protested anew, her husband entered—Mr. Palmer with his usual air of taking offence at the very air. He bowed slightly, as though courtesy were an ill-fitting coat he wore for the sake of decency, and immediately began to complain of the weather.

“How horrid all this is! Such weather makes every thing and every body disgusting. Dullness is as much produced within doors as without, by rain. It makes one detest all one’s acquaintance. What the devil does Sir John mean by not having a billiard room in his house? How few people know what comfort is! Sir John is as stupid as the weather.”

The rain, drumming on the windows, made a steady, restless percussion; the wind worried the shrubs outside; and in the pauses one could hear, more than one wished, the little domestic noises within—the scrape of a chair-leg, the soft tick of the clock, the distant clink of china as servants made ready for dinner. Elinor, who always attended to what other people called trifles, thought how sound itself could soothe or sour a mind; how some tempers, like milk too near a fire, would curdle at once.

The rest of the company soon dropped in; Sir John, with that hearty bustle which filled a house more effectually than any furniture, went up to Marianne with eager kindness.

“I am afraid, Miss Marianne, you have not been able to take your usual walk to Allenham today.”

The question fell like a pebble in a still pan: small, but certain to make circles. Marianne’s face, which had brightened at Mrs. Palmer’s entrance, became suddenly grave. She said nothing. The rain spoke for her, and the harshness of the wind.

“Oh, don’t be so sly before us,” cried Mrs. Palmer, delighted with herself as if she had discovered a secret spice in a familiar dish; “for we know all about it, I assure you; and I admire your taste very much, for I think he is extremely handsome. We do not live a great way from him in the country, you know. Not above ten miles, I dare say.”

“Much nearer thirty,” said Mr. Palmer.

“Ah, well! there is not much difference. I never was at his house; but they say it is a sweet pretty place.”

“As vile a spot as I ever saw in my life,” said Mr. Palmer.

Marianne remained silent still; but the very stillness of her attention betrayed her. Elinor saw, with a tenderness that was almost pain, how a name could be a flavour on the tongue—how it could bring heat to the blood or bitterness at once, and how impossible it was to translate such sensations into safer words.

“Is it very ugly?” continued Mrs. Palmer. “Then it must be some other place that is so pretty, I suppose.”

When they were seated in the dining-room, Sir John observed with regret that they were only eight altogether—his table, like his heart, being made to expand.

“My dear,” said he to his lady, “it is very provoking we should be so few. Why did not you ask the Gilberts to come to us today?”

“Did not I tell you, Sir John,” returned Lady Middleton, with placid propriety, “when you spoke to me about it before, that it could not be done? They dined with us last.”

“You and I, Sir John,” cried Mrs. Jennings, who liked company as other people like gravy—plentiful and poured freely—“should not stand upon such ceremony.”

“Then you would be very ill-bred,” said Mr. Palmer, cutting into the conversation as if it were meat he meant to carve with contempt.

“My love, you contradict every body,” cried his wife, laughing with the unquenchable ease of a nature determined to be happy. “Do you know you are quite rude?”

“I did not know I contradicted any body in calling your mother ill-bred.”

“Ay, you may abuse me as you please,” returned the good-natured old lady; “you have taken Charlotte off my hands, and cannot give her back again. So there I have the whip hand of you.”

Charlotte laughed heartily, delighted to think that even his ill-humour could not dismiss her; and declared, with exultation, that she did not care how cross he was to her, as they must live together.

It was impossible for any one to be more thoroughly good-natured, or more resolute in the pursuit of cheerfulness, than Mrs. Palmer. Her husband’s studied indifference, insolence, and discontent seemed to slip from her as rain from oiled cloth; and when he scolded, she looked as if she had been entertained.

“Mr. Palmer is so droll!” she whispered to Elinor. “He is always out of humour.”

Elinor, after a little observation, was not inclined to believe him so genuinely and unaffectedly ill-natured—or so ill-bred—as he wished to appear. His temper might, perhaps, be a little soured by finding, like many others of his sex, that through some unaccountable bias in favour of beauty he was the husband of a very silly woman; yet that kind of mistake was too common to wound a sensible man for long. She suspected a different seasoning in his conduct: a wish of distinction, the desire of seeming above other people by despising them; as though superiority could be proved by refusing to be pleased. It was a motive too common to surprise; but the means—however they might establish his eminence in ill-breeding—were not likely to attach any one to him except his wife.

Soon after, when the first bustle of dinner had subsided into that contented murmur of voices, forks, and the soft passing of dishes—a music that, when kindly governed, makes even a rainy day tolerable—Mrs. Palmer turned again to the Dashwoods with a bright suddenness, like a cook remembering an excellent ingredient she had nearly forgotten.

“Oh, my dear Miss Dashwood, I have got such a favour to ask of you and your sister. Will you come and spend some time at Cleveland this Christmas? Now, pray do—and come while the Westons are with us. You cannot think how happy I shall be! It will be quite delightful!—My love,” applying to her husband, “don’t you long to have the Miss Dashwoods come to Cleveland?”

“Certainly,” he replied, with a sneer; “I came into Devonshire with no other view.”

“There now,” cried his lady, triumphant, “you see Mr. Palmer expects you; so you cannot refuse to come.”

They both, however, declined eagerly and resolutely.

“But indeed you must and shall come. I am sure you will like it of all things. The Westons will be with us, and it will be quite delightful. You cannot think what a sweet place Cleveland is; and we are so gay now, for Mr. Palmer is always going about the country canvassing against the election; and so many people come to dine with us that I never saw before, it is quite charming! But, poor fellow! it is very fatiguing to him! for he is forced to make every body like him.”

Elinor could hardly keep her countenance as she assented to the hardship of such an obligation.

“How charming it will be,” continued Charlotte, “when he is in Parliament!—won’t it? How I shall laugh! It will be so ridiculous to see all his letters directed to him with an M.P.—But do you know, he says he will never frank for me? He declares he won’t. Don’t you, Mr. Palmer?”

Mr. Palmer took no notice.

“He cannot bear writing, you know,” she went on, as innocently pleased as if she were describing some beloved, familiar domestic practice; “he says it is quite shocking.”

“No,” said he, “I never said any thing so irrational. Don’t palm all your abuses of language upon me.”

“There now; you see how droll he is. This is always the way with him! Sometimes he won’t speak to me for half a day together, and then he comes out with something so droll—all about any thing in the world.”

When they returned into the drawing-room, she surprised Elinor by asking, with perfect sincerity, whether she did not like Mr. Palmer excessively.

“Certainly,” said Elinor; “he seems very agreeable.”

“Well—I am so glad you do. I thought you would, he is so pleasant; and Mr. Palmer is excessively pleased with you and your sisters I can tell you, and you can’t think how disappointed he will be if you don’t come to Cleveland. I can’t imagine why you should object to it.”

Elinor was obliged again to decline; and by changing the subject, put a stop to renewed entreaties.

She had, however, an object of her own, which sat upon her mind with the steady persistence of a flavour one cannot name but cannot ignore. Mrs. Palmer lived in the same county as Willoughby; she might know—if not from intimacy, then from that wide, careless knowledge society spreads as easily as it spreads a rumour—something more particular of his general character than could be gathered from the Middletons’ partial acquaintance. Elinor longed for any confirmation of his merits, however small, that might quiet the uneasy forebodings she did not dare translate into warnings for Marianne.

She began, therefore, by inquiring whether Mrs. Palmer saw much of Mr. Willoughby at Cleveland, and whether they were intimately acquainted with him.

“Oh dear, yes; I know him extremely well,” replied Mrs. Palmer—“not that I ever spoke to him, indeed; but I have seen him for ever in town. Somehow or other I never happened to be staying at Barton while he was at Allenham. Mama saw him here once before—but I was with my uncle at Weymouth. However, I dare say we should have seen a great deal of him in Somersetshire, if it had not happened very unluckily that we should never have been in the country together. He is very little at Combe, I believe; but if he were ever so much there, I do not think Mr. Palmer would visit him, for he is in the opposition, you know, and besides it is such a way off. I know why you inquire about him very well; your sister is to marry him. I am monstrous glad of it, for then I shall have her for a neighbour, you know.”

“Upon my word,” said Elinor, “you know much more of the matter than I do, if you have any reason to expect such a match.”

“Don’t pretend to deny it, because you know it is what every body talks of. I assure you I heard of it in my way through town.”

“My dear Mrs. Palmer!”

“Upon my honour I did. I met Colonel Brandon Monday morning in Bond-street, just before we left town, and he told me of it directly.”

“You surprise me very much. Colonel Brandon tell you of it! Surely you must be mistaken. To give such intelligence to a person who could not be interested in it, even if it were true, is not what I should expect Colonel Brandon to do.”

“But I do assure you it was so for all that; and I will tell you how it happened. When we met him, he turned back and walked with us; and so we began talking of my brother and sister, and one thing and another, and I said to him, ‘So, Colonel, there is a new family come to Barton cottage, I hear, and mama sends me word they are very pretty, and that one of them is going to be married to Mr. Willoughby of Combe Magna. Is it true, pray? for of course you must know, as you have been in Devonshire so lately.’”

“And what did the Colonel say?” asked Elinor, whose mind—so careful of tones and pauses, like one listening for a particular bird in an altered landscape—caught at every shade.

“Oh—he did not say much; but he looked as if he knew it to be true, so from that moment I set it down as certain. It will be quite delightful, I declare! When is it to take place?”

Elinor, obliged to set aside the sting of her surprise, asked instead, “Mr. Brandon was very well, I hope?”

“Oh! yes, quite well; and so full of your praises, he did nothing but say fine things of you.”

“I am flattered by his commendation. He seems an excellent man; and I think him uncommonly pleasing.”

“So do I. He is such a charming man, that it is quite a pity he should be so grave and so dull. Mama says he was in love with your sister too. I assure you it was a great compliment if he was, for he hardly ever falls in love with any body.”

Elinor, wishing to bring the talk back to what most concerned her, asked, “Is Mr. Willoughby much known in your part of Somersetshire?”

“Oh! yes, extremely well; that is, I do not believe many people are acquainted with him, because Combe Magna is so far off; but they all think him extremely agreeable, I assure you. Nobody is more liked than Mr. Willoughby wherever he goes, and so you may tell your sister. She is a monstrous lucky girl to get him, upon my honour; not but that he is much more lucky in getting her, because she is so very handsome and agreeable, that nothing can be good enough for her. However, I don’t think her hardly at all handsomer than you, I assure you; for I think you both excessively pretty, and so does Mr. Palmer too, I am sure, though we could not get him to own it last night.”

The substance of Mrs. Palmer’s account was slight, full of echoes and borrowed certainty; yet even that small testimony in Willoughby’s favour was a comfort to Elinor, as a familiar scent can be—a remembered loaf, a known herb—when one is uncertain of what may be served next.

“I am so glad we are got acquainted at last,” continued Charlotte. “And now I hope we shall always be great friends. You can’t think how much I longed to see you! It is so delightful that you should live at the cottage! Nothing can be like it, to be sure! And I am so glad your sister is going to be well married! I hope you will be a great deal at Combe Magna. It is a sweet place, by all accounts.”

Then, with the easy leap of a mind that ran where it pleased, she asked Elinor, “You have been long acquainted with Colonel Brandon, have not you?”

“Yes, a great while; ever since my sister married. He was a particular friend of Sir John’s. I believe,” she added in a low voice, as if confiding a family recipe, “he would have been very glad to have had me, if he could. Sir John and Lady Middleton wished it very much. But mama did not think the match good enough for me; otherwise Sir John would have mentioned it to the Colonel, and we should have been married immediately.”

“Did not Colonel Brandon know of Sir John’s proposal to your mother before it was made? Had he never owned his affection to yourself?”

“Oh, no; but if mama had not objected to it, I dare say he would have liked it of all things. He had not seen me then above twice, for it was before I left school. However, I am much happier as I am. Mr. Palmer is the kind of man I like.”
