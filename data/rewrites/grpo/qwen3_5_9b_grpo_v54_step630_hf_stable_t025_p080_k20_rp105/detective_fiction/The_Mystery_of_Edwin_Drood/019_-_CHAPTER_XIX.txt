Once more Miss Twinkleton had delivered her farewell address, accompanied by white wine and pound cake, and once more the young ladies had dispersed to their respective homes. Helena Landless had departed the Nuns' House to attend to her brother's affairs, leaving pretty Rosa alone in the quiet of the cloisters.

Cloisterham burned with a brilliant summer sun, rendering the Cathedral and the monastery ruins so luminous that their massive walls seemed transparent. A soft, internal glow appeared to emanate from within them rather than reflecting from the outside, lending them a mellowness as they gazed upon the hot cornfields and the smoking roads that wound distantly through the landscape. The gardens of Cloisterham blushed with ripening fruit. In times past, travel-stained pilgrims had ridden in clattering parties through the city's welcoming shades; now, wayfarers living a gypsy existence between haymaking and harvest, appearing as if forged from the very dust of the earth, lounged on cool doorsteps. They attempted to mend their irreparable shoes or surrendered them to the city kennels as hopeless cases, searching for others in their bundles alongside their unused sickles swathed in straw. At the public pumps, there was much cooling of bare feet and the bubbling, gurgling sound of drinkers bringing their hands to the spouts; the Cloisterham police watched these intruders with suspicion and impatience, wishing they would depart the civic bounds and fry themselves once more on the simmering high roads.

On such a bright afternoon, after the final Cathedral service, when the side of the High Street where the Nuns' House stood lay in grateful shade—save where its quaint old garden opened to the west between tree branches—a servant informed Rosa, to her terror, that Mr. Jasper desired to see her.

If he had chosen a time to find her at a disadvantage, he could not have done better. Perhaps he had chosen it deliberately. Helena Landless was gone, Mrs. Tisher was absent on leave, and Miss Twinkleton, in her amateur state of existence, had contributed herself and a veal pie to a picnic.

"Oh, why, why, why did you say I was at home!" cried Rosa, helplessly.

The maid replied that Mr. Jasper had never asked the question. He had simply stated he knew she was at home and requested that she be told he wished to see her.

"What shall I do! What shall I do!" Rosa thought, clasping her hands.

Driven by a kind of desperation, she added immediately that she would meet Mr. Jasper in the garden. She shuddered at the thought of being shut up with him inside the house; yet many of its windows commanded the garden, allowing her to be seen as well as heard. There, she could shriek into the free air and run away. Such was the wild idea fluttering through her mind.

She had never seen him since the fatal night, except when she was questioned before the Mayor, at which time he had been present in gloomy watchfulness, representing his lost nephew and burning to avenge him. Hanging her garden-hat on her arm, she stepped outside. The moment she saw him from the porch, leaning on the sun-dial, the old horrible feeling of being compelled by him asserted its hold upon her. She felt that she would even then go back, yet he drew her feet toward him. She could not resist and sat down, head bent, on the garden-seat beside the sun-dial. She could not look up at him for abhorrence, though she perceived he was dressed in deep mourning. So was she. It had not been so at first; but the lost had long been given up and mourned as dead.

He intended to begin by touching her hand. She felt the intention and drew her hand back. His eyes were then fixed upon her, she knew, though her own saw nothing but the grass.

"I have been waiting," he began, "for some time to be summoned back to my duty near you."

After forming her lips several times into the shape of hesitating replies and then into none, she answered: "Duty, sir?"

"The duty of teaching you, serving you as your faithful music-master."

"I have left off that study."

"Not left off, I think. Discontinued. I was told by your guardian that you discontinued it under the shock that we have all felt so acutely. When will you resume?"

"Never, sir."

"Never? You could have done no more if you had loved my dear boy."

"I did love him!" cried Rosa, with a flash of anger.

"Yes; but not quite—not quite in the right way, shall I say? Not in the intended and expected way. Much as my dear boy was, unhappily, too self-conscious and self-satisfied (I'll draw no parallel between him and you in that respect) to love as he should have loved, or as anyone in his place would have loved—must have loved!"

She sat in the same still attitude, shrinking a little more.

"Then, to be told that you discontinued your study with me, was to be politely told that you abandoned it altogether?" he suggested.

"Yes," said Rosa, with sudden spirit, "The politeness was my guardian's, not mine. I told him that I was resolved to leave off, and that I was determined to stand by my resolution."

"And you still are?"

"I still am, sir. And I beg not to be questioned any more about it. At all events, I will not answer any more; I have that in my power."

She was so conscious of his looking at her with a gloating admiration of the touch of anger on her, and the fire and animation it brought with it, that even as her spirit rose, it fell again, and she struggled with a sense of shame, affront, and fear, much as she had that night at the piano.

"I will not question you any more, since you object to it so much; I will confess—"

"I do not wish to hear you, sir," cried Rosa, rising.

This time he touched her with his outstretched hand. In shrinking from it, she sank back into her seat again.

"We must sometimes act in opposition to our wishes," he told her in a low voice. "You must do so now, or do more harm to others than you can ever set right."

"What harm?"

"Presently, presently. You question *me*, you see, and surely that's not fair when you forbid me to question you. Nevertheless, I will answer the question presently. Dearest Rosa! Charming Rosa!"

She started up again.

This time he did not touch her. But his face looked so wicked and menacing, as he stood leaning against the sun-dial-setting, as it were, his black mark upon the very face of day—that her flight was arrested by horror as she looked at him.

"I do not forget how many windows command a view of us," he said, glancing toward them. "I will not touch you again; I will come no nearer to you than I am. Sit down, and there will be no mighty wonder in your music-master's leaning idly against a pedestal and speaking with you, remembering all that has happened, and our shares in it. Sit down, my beloved."

She would have gone once more—was all but gone—and once more his face, darkly threatening what would follow if she went, stopped her. Looking at him with the expression of the instant frozen on her face, she sat down on the seat again.

"Rosa, even when my dear boy was affianced to you, I loved you madly; even when I thought his happiness in having you for his wife was certain, I loved you madly; even when I strove to make him more ardently devoted to you, I loved you madly; even when he gave me the picture of your lovely face so carelessly traduced by him, which I feigned to hang always in my sight for his sake, but worshipped in torment for years, I loved you madly; in the distasteful work of the day, in the wakeful misery of the night, girded by sordid realities, or wandering through Paradises and Hells of visions into which I rushed, carrying your image in my arms, I loved you madly."

If anything could make his words more hideous to her than they were in themselves, it would be the contrast between the violence of his look and delivery and the composure of his assumed attitude.

"I endured it all in silence. So long as you were his, or so long as I supposed you to be his, I hid my secret loyally. Did I not?"

This lie, so gross, while the mere words in which it was told were so true, was more than Rosa could endure. She answered with kindling indignation: "You were as false throughout, sir, as you are now. You were false to him, daily and hourly. You know that you made my life unhappy by your pursuit of me. You know that you made me afraid to open his generous eyes, and that you forced me, for his own trusting, good, good sake, to keep the truth from him, that you were a bad, bad man!"

His preservation of his easy attitude rendering his working features and his convulsive hands absolutely diabolical, he returned, with a fierce extreme of admiration:

"How beautiful you are! You are more beautiful in anger than in repose. I don't ask you for your love; give me yourself and your hatred; give me yourself and that pretty rage; give me yourself and that enchanting scorn; it will be enough for me."

Impatient tears rose to the eyes of the trembling little beauty, and her face flamed; but as she again rose to leave him in indignation and seek protection within the house, he stretched out his hand toward the porch, as though inviting her to enter it.

"I told you, you rare charmer, you sweet witch, that you must stay and hear me, or do more harm than can ever be undone. You asked me what harm. Stay, and I will tell you. Go, and I will do it!"

Again Rosa quailed before his threatening face, though innocent of its meaning, and she remained. Her panting breathing came and went as if it would choke her; but with a repressive hand upon her bosom, she stayed.

"I have made my confession that my love is mad. It is so mad, that had the ties between me and my dear lost boy been one silken thread less strong, I might have swept even him from your side, when you favored him."

A film came over the eyes she raised for an instant, as though he had turned her faint.

"Even him," he repeated. "Yes, even him! Rosa, you see me and you hear me. Judge for yourself whether any other admirer shall love you and live, whose life is in my hand."

"What do you mean, sir?"

"I mean to show you how mad my love is. It was hawked through the late inquiries by Mr. Crisparkle that young Landless had confessed to him that he was a rival of my lost boy. That is an inexpiable offense in my eyes. The same Mr. Crisparkle knows under my hand that I have devoted myself to the murderer's discovery and destruction, be he whom he might, and that I determined to discuss the mystery with no one until I should hold the clue in which to entangle the murderer as in a net. I have since worked patiently to wind and wind it round him; and it is slowly winding as I speak."

"Your belief, if you believe in the criminality of Mr. Landless, is not Mr. Crisparkle's belief, and he is a good man," Rosa retorted.

"My belief is my own; and I reserve it, worshipped of my soul! Circumstances may accumulate so strongly *even against an innocent man*, that directed, sharpened, and pointed, they may slay him. One wanting link discovered by perseverance against a guilty man, proves his guilt, however slight its evidence before, and he dies. Young Landless stands in deadly peril either way."

"If you really suppose," Rosa pleaded with him, turning paler, "that I favor Mr. Landless, or that Mr. Landless has ever in any way addressed himself to me, you are wrong."

He put that aside with a slighting action of his hand and a curled lip.

"I was going to show you how madly I love you. More madly now than ever, for I am willing to renounce the second object that has arisen in my life to divide it with you; and henceforth to have no object in existence but you only. Miss Landless has become your bosom friend. You care for her peace of mind?"

"I love her dearly."

"You care for her good name?"

"I have said, sir, I love her dearly."

"I am unconsciously," he observed with a smile, as he folded his hands upon the sun-dial and leaned his chin upon them, so that his talk would seem from the windows (faces occasionally coming and going there) to be of the airiest and playfulest—"I am unconsciously giving offense by questioning again. I will simply make statements, therefore, and not put questions. You do care for your bosom friend's good name, and you do care for her peace of mind. Then remove the shadow of the gallows from her, dear one!"

"You dare propose to me to—"

"Darling, I dare propose to you. Stop there. If it be bad to idolize you, I am the worst of men; if it be good, I am the best. My love for you is above all other love, and my truth to you is above all other truth. Let me have hope and favor, and I am a forsworn man for your sake."

Rosa put her hands to her temples, and, pushing back her hair, looked wildly and abhorrently at him, as though she were trying to piece together what it was his deep purpose to present to her only in fragments.
