When Fra Luca fell silent, Tito remained rooted to the spot, caught in a quiet turmoil. Only when the crowd dispersed and the friar slowly made his way into the church of Santa Felicita did Tito finally move, turning toward the Via de’ Bardi with measured steps. His mind raced ahead of him.

*If this monk is truly from Florence—if he stays here—then all will be revealed.* The thought struck him like a sudden storm. A new crisis had arrived, but it did not shake him visibly. He still had business to attend to: a visit to Bardo, and an apology for his absence. Tito had always been skilled at concealment, but lately that talent was evolving into something more deliberate, almost refined. He could still choose to come clean—to admit Baldassarre’s existence—but doing so would cast a shadow over his earlier silence. It might seem less like modesty and more like calculated evasion, designed to avoid fulfilling a claim he knew was real. And what about the florins? Hadn’t he settled comfortably in Florence, invested wisely, while the fate of his benefactor remained uncertain? That, too, would now appear suspect.

For now, discretion was safer. He would wait. There was still the night to think, and no one need know precisely when the letter had reached him.

So he climbed the stairs to Romola’s home—the room on the second floor where her father sat among scrolls and marble, untouched by the bustle of the city, even on feast days. Tito entered with only a slight dimming of his usual cheer, as if regretting his lateness. But there was no need to dramatize it—he had missed the Corso deliberately, just to show he cared. Now, he set about livening the evening with animated talk, though inside, his thoughts churned like a hidden machine, forging ideas apart from the words he spoke. By the time he descended the stone steps and stepped out into the starlit street, his mind had already begun to shape a plan.

The next day, after his teaching duties ended, he walked up the Via del Cocomero toward the convent of San Marco. His purpose was clear now. He would go to Fra Luca—not to confess, but to probe. How much did the friar suspect? On what grounds? And how long would he remain at San Marco? With answers to those questions, Tito believed he could craft a response that would allow him to stay in Florence, unshaken by duty or guilt.

He had never before felt the need to invent a clever lie. But now the moment had come—the kind of moment that arises naturally from silence, from half-truths left unchallenged. And his mind, sharp and self-assured, was ready. He had convinced himself he was under no obligation to seek out Baldassarre. Once, he had said he would go if he had proof of his father’s survival. But now, he questioned that vow. *Why* should he go? What was life, ultimately, if not the pursuit of pleasure? And wasn’t his own young, vibrant life far more capable of joy than the worn-out years of a man whose passions had hardened into rigidity?

Baldassarre had lived his life, drawn his share of meaning from it. Now, Tito reasoned, it was his turn. The future stretched open before him—“I think they are going to take me to Antioch,” he’d heard whispered. Imagine it: a long journey, months or years spent searching for someone whose whereabouts were uncertain, perhaps even nonexistent. And all the while, he would leave behind a life of promise, love, and distinction. Even if he found his father, it would mean returning to the same old demands, the same familiar constraints—known, predictable, stifling.

Yes, the gems—and thus the florins—belonged to Baldassarre, in the narrow sense of property law. But in a deeper, truer sense—the sense of nature and vitality—they belonged to whoever could enjoy them most. And that was Tito. He acknowledged this wasn’t what society expected. Men around him would demand that he use the money to rescue his father. But what was society’s morality, really? A tangled web of outdated customs, shaped not by reason but by tradition. No wise man should follow it blindly—except when it suited his comfort.

And yet, he admitted, he didn’t care much for the florins themselves—save perhaps for Romola’s sake. He could give them up without hesitation. What mattered was the joy he stood to lose—the joy within reach, already warming his soul. To abandon that, to walk away thirsty and empty-handed, seemed unjust. Any rule that demanded men sacrifice their own happiness for others was merely selfishness disguised as virtue—a doctrine created by those who wanted others to suffer for their benefit.

He didn’t wish Baldassarre harm. He disliked suffering in anyone. But could any philosophy prove that one person’s pain should outweigh another’s joy? To do so, he would have to love his father deeply—and he did not. Was that his fault? Gratitude? Upon closer inspection, it held no true claim. Baldassarre had lived a full life; hadn’t he found satisfaction in it? Should Tito feel indebted simply because his father had enjoyed his own existence?

Once started, Tito’s reasoning became relentless—an acid eating through moral sentiment. His mind lacked the fear many called superstition: not the fear of physical danger, but the deeper dread of divine justice, the awe that once gripped religious pagans and still echoes in the hearts of believers. That fear, he knew, was not mere cowardice—it was the first instinct of conscience, the check upon unchecked desire. As the ancient Eumenides sang in Aeschylus: *“It is good that fear should sit as guardian of the soul, forcing it into wisdom—good that men should carry a threatening shadow in their hearts under the full sunshine; else, how should they learn to revere the right?”* That guardianship might someday become unnecessary—but only when duty and love flowed together as one.

As Tito approached the outer cloister of San Marco and asked for Fra Luca, no such shadow haunted him. He was too well-read, too skeptical, to believe in omens. He had grown up mocking priests whose lives contradicted their sermons, and debating the “Chief Good” with scholars who never reached agreement. For him, ethics were a matter of taste, not law.

Yet fear was not absent. It was a powerful force in Tito—fear of anything that might steal his pleasure. And he feared Fra Luca might be the instrument of his exile from Florence.

“Fra Luca?” replied a lay brother, a _fra converso_, with a thick country accent and dull eyes. “Ah, he has gone to Fiesole—to the Dominican monastery there. He was carried away on a litter this morning. The poor Brother is very ill. Can you leave a message?”

Tito thanked him and turned away, relief washing over him. *This friar is not likely to live,* he thought. *I saw how thin he was, how fragile. And at Fiesole, no one will remember me. If he returns later, my explanation will serve just as well.* Still, a small question lingered: *What was it about his face that stirred something in me?*
