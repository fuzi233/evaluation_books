News traveled swiftly through the city, carried on the wings of the press, announcing to the world that the wife of Thomas Palmer, Esquire, had been blessed with a son and heir. To the circle of acquaintances who held Thomas Palmer in high esteem, it was a joyous and satisfying tidbit; to the rest of the world, it was merely a footnote in the chronicle of the season. Yet, for Mrs. Jennings, it was a beacon of light, casting away the shadows of recent disappointment and filling her heart with a renewed, vibrant hope.

Consequently, the rhythm of her domestic life shifted. With a singular devotion to her daughter Charlotte, Mrs. Jennings began her mornings at Conduit Street before the sun had fully warmed the bedclothes, lingering until twilight deepened the streets. This arrangement, however, necessitated a displacement for the Dashwood sisters. At the earnest entreaty of the Middletons, Elinor and Marianne found themselves exiled from the warm sanctuary of Mrs. Jennings's home, spending their entire days in the service of Lady Middleton and the two Miss Steeles.

Had it been possible, they would have clung to Mrs. Jennings's hospitality, finding solace in her affectionate chatter and the comforting bustle of her household. But duty, however grating, demands obedience, and their hours were now claimed by the needs of their hosts. They became the constant fixtures in Conduit Street, a presence that, while politely received, was hardly cherished. To Lady Middleton, they were merely an inconvenience, a necessary social lubricant that lacked the genuine warmth she craved. To the Miss Steeles, they were viewed with a calculating jealousy, seen as intruders encroaching upon territory the younger women intended to monopolize for their own amusement.

There was a profound disconnect in their interactions. The Dashwood sisters possessed a quiet sense, a depth of feeling that rendered them unappealing companions to Lady Middleton, whose world revolved around surface-level flattery and the adoration of her children. Because Elinor and Marianne refused to offer the empty praise Lady Middleton coveted, she interpreted their honesty as a lack of good nature. Furthermore, their penchant for reading led Lady Middleton to suspect a hidden satirical edge, a suspicion born more of her own insecurity than any actual malice in the sisters' hearts.

For Lucy Steele, however, their presence was a gentle check upon her idleness, a reminder that her time, usually spent weaving tales of romance, was now occupied with conversation. Yet, Lucy's heart was heavy with unspoken grievances. She harbored a secret longing for information regarding Marianne's past, specifically the tangled history involving Mr. Willoughby. A single, frank confession from either sister would have transformed her resentment into friendship, granting her the intimacy she secretly desired. But silence was their shield. Whether it was a glance of indifference from Elinor or a look of disgust from Marianne, the barrier remained impenetrable. Even lighter matters, such as the whimsical anecdotes concerning Dr. Harris, failed to elicit laughter. Wherever Sir John resided, the sisters offered no raillery, leaving Lucy to stand alone in her unshared confidences.

Through all these subtle frictions and unvoiced resentments, Mrs. Jennings remained blissfully unaware. She viewed the separation as a divine gift, celebrating nightly the fact that her young charges had escaped the stifling company of "stupid" older women. She arrived at gatherings with boundless spirits, her voice ringing with tales of Charlotte's triumphs, attributing every success to her own astute guidance. She relished detailing the infant boy's every feature, though one small shadow marred her jubilation. Mr. Palmer, a man of conventional sensibilities, steadfastly denied that the child bore any striking resemblance to his kin. Despite the obvious echoes of the Palmer lineage in the baby's features, he insisted it was merely another ordinary infant, refusing to acknowledge the miracle of his unique beauty.

Meanwhile, a quiet storm brewed elsewhere, centered on Mrs. John Dashwood. While her sisters were away visiting Mrs. Jennings, a chance encounter altered the course of her social maneuverings. A lady, driven by a fleeting impression, mistook the identity of the visitors in Harley Street. Hearing the name "Miss Dashwood," she assumed they were the sisters of Mr. John Dashwood. This misunderstanding, though born of a simple error, spiraled into a series of invitations to a musical gathering. Suddenly, Mrs. John Dashwood faced a dilemma: to accept the invitations would require her to transport the Dashwood sisters to her own home, a task that demanded a level of attention she was ill-equipped to give. To refuse, she feared, would expose her duplicity. Thus, trapped by the expectations of others, she was forced to submit to the inconvenience of sending for them, hoping to manage the situation with feigned grace while maintaining a cool reserve.

Marianne, meanwhile, had fallen into a routine of endless engagements. The novelty of socializing had worn off, replaced by a mechanical preparation for the evening ahead. She dressed with indifferent precision, allowing Miss Steele to dictate the details of her attire with a scrutiny that bordered on the intrusive. Miss Steele, a woman of sharp curiosity, examined every stitch and shade, guessing the cost of fabrics and the frequency of laundry with an accuracy that left Marianne feeling exposed. The conversation inevitably culminated in a compliment, a standard social courtesy that Marianne found deeply irritating after having subjected herself to such a thorough audit. "Vastly smart," Miss Steele declared, confident in Marianne's ability to conquer hearts, a sentiment that felt hollow to Marianne, who sought connection over conquest.

That evening, the carriage journey proved brief, yet significant. As they entered the hostess's drawing room, Elinor found herself drawn away from the piano, her gaze wandering until it rested upon a cluster of gentlemen. Among them stood a man who had previously lectured her on the intricacies of toothpick cases at Gray's Inn. His familiarity grew as he turned his attention to her, and soon, Mr. Dashwood stepped forward to introduce him. "Mr. Robert Ferrars," the introduction rang clear.

Robert Ferrars approached Elinor with an easy, self-assured civility, his bow sweeping with a confidence that marked him as the epitome of the coxcomb Lucy Steele had described. Elinor felt a pang of regret, wondering if her admiration for her brother Edward had been misplaced if it relied solely on his intrinsic virtues rather than the company he kept. Had her regard hinged on his relatives, Robert's demeanor might have sealed the verdict of her mother and sister, dismissing Edward as equally lacking. Yet, as she observed the contrast between Robert's empty vanity and Edward's modest worth, she realized that character was not defined by one's background or education.

Robert, eager to justify his superior social standing, launched into a tirade regarding his brother's "gaucherie." He attributed Edward's awkwardness not to any natural defect, but to the "misfortune" of a private education, contrasting it sharply with his own advantages gained from a public school. He spoke with a generous candor, blaming Edward's Uncle Pratt for failing to send him to Westminster, thereby condemning his brother to a life of isolation. Elinor listened silently, unable to counter his argument with the truth of Edward's gentle spirit, for the reality of Edward's upbringing in Mr. Pratt's household was a source of contentment that she could not defend against Robert's worldly logic.

As the conversation drifted, Robert remarked on their residence in Devonshire, expressing surprise that anyone could live in the countryside without residing near Dawlish. He praised their cottage with enthusiastic fervor, declaring his love for the humble dwelling. He envisioned buying land nearby, building a similar cottage, and hosting gatherings that combined the comfort of country life with the convenience of proximity to London. He recounted a successful ball at Lady Elliott's, proving that a cottage could accommodate any number of guests if arranged with sufficient ingenuity. Elinor, recognizing the futility of arguing with a man who viewed the world through the lens of his own privilege, simply assented, for she knew he was speaking not of homes, but of status.

Later, back at Harley Street, the atmosphere shifted again. John Dashwood, freed from the constraints of the evening's entertainment, proposed a scheme to his wife, Fanny. He suggested extending an invitation to the Dashwood sisters, believing it would absolve his conscience of the guilt incurred by his father's dying wish. However, Fanny seized upon a different opportunity. "How can I ask them away?" she asked, her tone shifting instantly. "I have already promised the Miss Steeles a visit. They are so well-behaved, so kind! And their uncle treated Edward so generously. Surely we cannot neglect them." Her husband, humbled by her pragmatic shift, conceded, seeing the necessity of courting the Miss Steeles to maintain social equilibrium.

Intrigued by this sudden change of fortune, Fanny wrote to Lucy Steele the following morning, requesting her company. The letter, delivered with electric anticipation, revealed the depth of Lucy's ambitions. To be invited to Harley Street was to be welcomed into the fold of the Dashwoods, and thus, into the orbit of Edward. The news transformed Lucy's outlook; the vague, indefinite duration of her stay with Lady Middleton suddenly acquired a firm limit. Two days, she decided, would suffice. The invitation from Mrs. Dashwood was not merely an offer of hospitality; it was a ladder to her ultimate goal.
