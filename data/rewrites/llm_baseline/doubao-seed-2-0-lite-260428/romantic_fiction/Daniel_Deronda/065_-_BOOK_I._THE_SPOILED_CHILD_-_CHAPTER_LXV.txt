“O, welcome, pure-eyed Faith, white-handed Hope, Thou hovering angel, girt with golden wings!” —MILTON.

Deronda did not answer Gwendolen’s urgent new summons without a sharp, thrumming agitation in his chest. Not vanity, but his boundless, keen sympathy, made him acutely aware of the risk: that another’s heart might stake a claim on him far larger than he could ever honor. It was no longer a theory to him, but a visceral, unshakable truth: Gwendolen’s soul clung to his with a ravenous, desperate need, as if she hung over a precipice of despair and only his hand kept her from falling. We do not argue the anger or the scorn we hear thrumming in a stranger’s voice; we simply feel it, no proof needed. And Deronda could feel the weight of Gwendolen’s whole fate tangled up with his own.

Anyone who knew him would not have been surprised by his quiet, unspoken admission: if all this had unfolded just a year prior, he would never have stopped to ask himself if he loved her. The impetuous, overwhelming drive in him would have been to wrap her in safety, shelter her from every loneliness for the rest of her days, and see the rescue he began when he redeemed that fateful necklace through to its very end. But now, love and duty had woven other, unbreakable bonds around him. That old impulse no longer got to steer his life, but it lived in him still as a tender, aching yearning, a sharp pang just at the thought of meeting the desperate appeal in her eyes and her words again and again, knowing he could never give her everything she ached for. The firmer his resolve to keep his distance out of honor, the more searing his pity for the lonely path she now had to walk.

He waited for her in the back drawing room, the same white-and-crimson space where they had sat side by side at the musicale, where Gwendolen first whispered that her lot depended on him never forsaking her, her soft plea melting into the tender, sad strains of *Per pietà non dirmi addio* — “Do not tell me goodbye out of pity” — that had poured from Mirah’s dear, familiar voice. Deronda wandered the room he had known like the back of his hand for years, with the strange, disorienting feeling that his whole life had metamorphosed under his feet. Every familiar object, from Lady Mallinger’s soft-smiling portrait to the friendly, urbane lion faces carved into the mantel pilasters, felt like they belonged to a past life he was only revisiting in memory, not reality. The revelations of the last months had transformed him so deeply, shifted the foundations of his identity so completely, that the house he had once called home felt new and unmoored now, as he stood hat in hand waiting for a young woman whose own life had been ripped apart by tragedy, her future hanging fragile and uncertain, with Deronda’s choices still tangled up in what would become of her.

Then Gwendolen stepped through the door. She looked transformed, not just by the deep black of her mourning dress, but by a quiet, soft serenity he had never seen on her face during their last meeting in Genoa. That quiet satisfaction came entirely from seeing Deronda there, but no smile passed between them as they met and clasped hands. Both of them were brimming with shared memories, heavy with anxious premonition.

“It was good of you to come. Let us sit down,” she said, sinking at once into the nearest chair. He took the seat across from her.

“I asked you here because I need you to tell me what I ought to do,” she began immediately. “Don’t soften it for me just because it feels hard. I’ve made up my mind I will do it. Once I was terrified of being poor, I could not bear the thought of being dependent on other people’s mercy — that was why I made that terrible mistake, why I married. But I’ve borne far worse things now. I think I could bear poverty, if you tell me I ought to.” She paused, her fingers twisting in her lap. “Do you know the terms of my husband’s will?”

“Yes, Sir Hugo told me,” Deronda said, already guessing the question weighing on her.

“Ought I to take anything he left me?” she asked, her tone sharpening into nervous eagerness. “Let me tell you what I’ve been turning over in my head. You may not fully realize that even when I was being selfish, even when I agreed to that marriage, I was thinking of my mother. I did love her, and the thought of her living in poverty was the thing that drove me most. The only small comfort I had at first, when every day of my marriage was misery, was knowing she was better off because of what I’d done. The hardest thing for me now would be to see her poor again. I thought if I only took enough from the will to keep her comfortable, and nothing at all for myself — that it wouldn’t be wrong. He took me away from her, and he knew I cared for her, and he would have wanted to provide for her, wouldn’t he? If she knew —”

Gwendolen broke off, her voice trembling. She had rehearsed this conversation over and over, fixating only on the question of doing right by her mother, but the terrible, unspeakable memories tangled beneath those thoughts swarmed between her words, making her speech shake more and more. She stared helplessly down at her hands, which now bore no rings at all save for her plain, heavy wedding band.

“Don’t hurt yourself trying to say the rest,” Deronda said, his voice soft with tenderness. “There’s no need. The case is very simple. I can hardly be wrong about this. You consult me because I am the only person in the world who knows the most painful part of your story, so I understand why your conscience is pricking you.” He fell quiet for a moment, letting her catch her breath, and Gwendolen felt that silence wrap around her like the gentle sympathy in his voice. She found the courage to lift her eyes to his as he went on. “You carry a weight of guilt, you feel as if you have no right to claim anything as his wife, you shrink from taking profit from his death, you even want to punish that old, selfish part of yourself that gave in to temptation. I have known that exact kind of remorse myself. Do I understand you correctly?”

“Yes. I just want to be good — not the person I used to be,” Gwendolen whispered. “I’ll try to bear whatever you say I ought to bear. I told you the worst of me. What do I do now?”

“If only your own peace were at stake here, I would never dare argue against any prompting of your remorse,” Deronda said. “But I take your love for Mrs. Davilow as my guide, and that feeling of yours is perfectly just. I do not believe your actions, no matter how wrong you feel they were, can cancel out the duty your husband owed you as his wife. He chose to enter your life, to twist the course of it in the most irreversible way possible. But even setting that aside, it was his obligation as a wealthy man to provide for your mother, and he knew full well that if his will took effect, she would share in that provision alongside you.”

“She’s been living on eight hundred a year,” Gwendolen said. “I was thinking I could take exactly that much for her, and leave every penny of the rest.” She had argued herself into that line as a small, hard-won permission for weeks, and could not adjust to a different point of view at once.

“I do not think it is your duty to draw that line,” Deronda said gently. “You would create a painful, unanswerable riddle for your mother. An income you refuse to share in would taste bitter to her, she would never be able to enjoy it, wondering why you would not touch it. And your own path of penitence would become far too heavy. We agreed in Genoa that no other person ever needs to know the secret weight you carry. The kind, good life you want to build for yourself will be far easier to foster if you keep everyone you love safe from the pain of that knowledge. In my opinion, you ought simply to accept the full terms of the will, and let your remorse guide you in how you use that new independence to do good.”

As he finished the last sentence, Deronda automatically reached for his hat, which he had set on the floor beside his chair. Gwendolen, sharp to every small shift in his movement, felt her heart lurch hard in her chest, as if it had a will of its own, trying to stop him from leaving. Before she could think, she was on her feet, and Deronda, startled, stood too, stepping a little toward her.

“I will do exactly what you tell me,” she said, breathless. “But what else? What else am I supposed to do?”

Those were the only simple words she could find, but they tore through her like a mirror held up to her own utter helplessness. Her old, proud secrecy had crumbled to ash, and she could not hold back the sob that sent huge, hot tears spilling down her cheeks. Deronda felt a crushing weight of pain in his own chest, but he knew the stakes of this moment too clearly, and forced himself to speak steadily, gently.

“You will be leaving for the country soon with Mrs. Davilow, I imagine.”

“Yes, in a week or ten days.” Gwendolen’s eyes drifted vaguely to the window, as if she was staring out at some imaginary, unwritten landscape. “I want to be kind to all of them. They can be happier than I will ever be. Is that the best I can do?”

“I think so. That duty is one there is no doubt about,” Deronda said, every word heavy with care. “Other good things will grow from it. Looking at your life as a debt you have to pay back might sound dreary from far away, but it cannot stay dreary. The only thing that makes life empty is having no purpose. Once you start acting with that tender, penitent goal you have set for yourself, you will find small, unexpected joys, new little needs to tend to that will carry you forward day by day. You will see your life grow, soft and steady, like a plant pushing up after a hard frost.”

Gwendolen’s eyes flew back to his face, as if she was a parched traveler drinking in the sound of unseen running water. Deronda felt that look like she was reaching out for him from the edge of a forsaken, drowning shore. His voice softened into an imploring, affectionate lilt.

“This grief that has cut you right down to the roots came to you while you are still so young. Try not to see it as something that spoiled your life, but as something that is preparing you for it. Think of it this way: you were heading down a dark, selfish road, and a severe, loving angel grabbed you by the wrist to show you the horror of the life you almost fell into. And it happened to you in the springtime of your life. You can be one of those good, gentle women who make everyone around them glad they were ever born.”

The words felt to Gwendolen like the touch of a miraculous hand. A flood of mingled hope and new strength coursed through her, the first stirrings of a new life that felt entirely tied to Deronda, as if he was the very breath her new soul was breathing. For the first time since that terrible day out on the stormy sea, a warm flush rose up over her cheeks, her brow, her neck, deepening for a heartbeat before fading slowly away. She said nothing.

Deronda stepped forward, holding out his hand. “I must not tire you out.”

She started, shocked at the thought he was leaving, and put her hand in his, still silent.

“You still look unwell — nothing like the girl I once knew,” he said, holding her hand a moment longer.

“I can hardly sleep,” she said, her tone dulling back into hopelessness. “All the old memories keep replaying in my head. They come back. They will keep coming back,” she shuddered.

“Little by little, they will stop pressing at you so hard,” Deronda said. He could not drop her hand, could not pull away abruptly.

“Sir Hugo says he is coming to stay at Diplow soon,” Gwendolen blurted, grabbing for the words she had practiced saying earlier, which had slipped her mind. “You will come with him, right?”

“Probably,” Deronda said, and when the word felt too cold, he corrected it at once. “Yes. I will come.” He released her hand, with that final, gentle, friendly pressure of someone who has already said goodbye.

“And you will see me once more before I leave town?” Gwendolen asked, her voice soft with timid sadness, her face as pale as ever.

What could Deronda say? “If I can be of any use to you — if you wish it — of course I will.”

“I must wish it,” Gwendolen burst out, impulsive. “You know I must wish it. What other strength do I have? Who else is there?” Another sob rose in her throat.

A sharp, stabbing pain crossed Deronda’s face. He looked utterly miserable as he said, “I will certainly come.”

Gwendolen saw the pain in his face, but the overwhelming relief of knowing he would return drowned everything else out, and that new, brave hope he had planted in her surged back bright.

“Don’t be sad for me,” she said, her tone soft and sure, full of gentle care for him for the first time. “I will remember every single word you said. I will remember how good you think I can be. I will try.” She looked at him steadily, and held out her hand again, as if she had forgotten they had already said goodbye once that afternoon. No smile had touched her lips since her husband died. Standing there quiet and still, she looked like a melancholy statue of the bright, laughing Gwendolen whose glee had once been so sharp and vivid when everyone around her was grave. It was no wonder she clung to him so openly, that she laid her desperate dependence on him bare — she was trapped in a fire of her own making, and he was the only one who could carry her to safety. She had tied him irrevocably to the new, good life she was trying to build.

No one could blame her for assuming her need was mirrored in his heart. She was too wrapped up in the terror of her own suffering to imagine a future without him at her side. Marriage, love, the kind of courtship she had once daydreamed of, could not even cross her mind now. The great, heavy Love that had laid its hand on her had asked only for acceptance of her fault, the hard work of changing herself, confession, and endurance. Her cries to him were the cries of a small child who has tripped and fallen backward in the dark, screaming to be taken by the hand so she does not lose her way entirely.

The cry pierced Deronda to the bone. There could be no more impossible position for a man full of tenderness, but clear-eyed enough to see the future: he was the only person alive who knew the full truth of her suffering, and to pull away from her now would leave her stranded in a dangerous, empty loneliness. He could not bear the cruelty of making her feel like he was rejecting her when she needed him most. And yet, he could see the inevitable wrench coming down the line, where every new bond they wove in these soft meetings would only make that final parting hurt worse. He chose to risk that hurt.

He visited her at her Park Lane home twice more before she left London, and their meetings were always chaperoned by Mrs. Davilow, making them far less fraught and painful. Now that she had resolved to accept the full inheritance, Gwendolen had a gentle new project she loved talking about: she would move herself, her mother, and her sisters back into Offendene, the old house where they had once been so happy, and stitch her life back as close to that innocent, joyful time as she could. She had mentioned the plan to Sir Hugo, who was already working to arrange a rental for Gadsmere that would more than cover the cost of Offendene’s lease. Deronda was glad to dwell on the plan, glad to see it give Gwendolen something soft and hopeful to pour her energy into. Neither of them spoke of the life Deronda was building for himself. Gwendolen’s whole world had narrowed down to waiting for him to come visit her at Diplow before autumn ended, and she thought no more of the little Jewish singer Mirah and her brother than she thought of the quiet social and political shifts that were beginning to reshape the world outside her small, carefully tended sphere. All of her mind was lit only by the path of atonement Deronda had showed her, and the bright, quiet hope of seeing him again soon.
