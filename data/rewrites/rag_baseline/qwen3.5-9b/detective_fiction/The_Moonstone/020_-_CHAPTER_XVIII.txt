Descending the grand staircase, I encountered the Sergeant upon the threshold. It grated against the very fiber of my being, following the friction that had recently sparked between us, to display to him any semblance of concern regarding his investigations. Yet, despite my resistance, a compelling interest rooted itself in my chest, one I could not suppress. My sense of self-importance dissolved beneath the weight of the situation, and the words escaped me involuntarily: "What tidings from Frizinghall?"

"I have located the Indians," Sergeant Cuff replied, his tone steady. "Furthermore, I have uncovered what Rosanna purchased privately in the town on Thursday past. The Indians are scheduled for release on Wednesday of next week. There is no shadow of doubt in my mind, nor in Mr. Murthwaite's, that they descended upon this estate with the intent to pilfer the Moonstone. Naturally, their calculations were rendered obsolete by the chaos that unfolded within these walls on Wednesday night; consequently, they bear no responsibility for the actual disappearance of the jewel any more than you do. However, permit me to offer a warning, Mr. Betteredge: if *we* fail to locate the Moonstone, *they* certainly will. You have not heard the final word from the trio of jugglers."

Mr. Franklin emerged from his stroll precisely as the Sergeant uttered these startling declarations. Governing his curiosity with a discipline I lacked, he passed us silently, retreating into the house. As for me, having already surrendered my dignity, I resolved to extract every ounce of value from this revelation. "Enough of the Indians," I declared. "Pray, enlighten me concerning Rosanna."

Sergeant Cuff shook his head slowly. "The enigma surrounding her remains denser than ever," he observed. "I have tracked her movements to a haberdashery in Frizinghall, maintained by a linen draper named Maltby. She procured nothing from any other drapery, nor from the establishments of milliners or tailors. At Maltby's, she acquired only a single item: a length of fabric. She was exceedingly particular in selecting a specific weave. Regarding quantity, she purchased sufficient material to construct a nightgown."

"A nightgown for whom?" I pressed.

"For her own, undoubtedly," the Sergeant confirmed. "Sometime between noon and three o'clock on Thursday morning, she must have slipped down to your young lady's chamber to finalize the concealment of the Moonstone while the household slept. Upon returning to her quarters, her garment inevitably brushed against the wet paint on the doorframe. Unable to cleanse the stain, and unable to safely incinerate the nightgown without first procuring a replacement to maintain the integrity of her linen inventory, she was forced to retain it."

"How do you prove it was Rosanna's nightgown?" I objected.

"The very material she purchased for the substitute garment," Cuff answered. "Had it belonged to Miss Verinder, she would have required lace, frills, and countless other embellishments; furthermore, she would lack the temporal capacity to fashion such attire in a single night. Plain, unadorned cloth signifies a plain servant's nightgown. The logic is irrefutable, Mr. Betteredge. The crux of the matter lies elsewhere: Why, after securing the replacement dress, does she conceal the stained garment rather than destroy it? Should the girl refuse to speak, there exists but one method to resolve this dilemma. The hiding-place at the Shivering Sand must be searched; therein lies the truth of the case."

"How shall you locate the site?" I inquired.

"I regret to disappoint you," the Sergeant stated, "but that is a secret I intend to withhold." (To spare your curiosity, though he provoked mine, I may disclose that he returned from Frizinghall armed with a search warrant. His extensive experience in such affairs convinced him that Rosanna likely carried a memorandum detailing the hiding-place, intended to guide her should she return under altered circumstances and after a lapse of time. Possessing this document, the Sergeant would possess everything he requires.)

"Now, Mr. Betteredge," he continued, "let us abandon speculation and engage in business. I instructed Joyce to monitor Rosanna. Where is Joyce?"

Joyce was the constable stationed at Frizinghall, placed at Sergeant Cuff's disposal by Superintendent Seegrave. The clock struck two as he posed the question; punctually, the carriage arrived to escort Miss Rachel to her aunt's residence.

"One task at a time," the Sergeant interjected, halting me as I prepared to summon Joyce. "I must attend to Miss Verinder first."

As the rain threatened overhead, the enclosed carriage—the one designated for Miss Rachel's journey to Frizinghall—was ready. Sergeant Cuff beckoned Samuel, the footman, to descend from the rumble seat behind. "You will observe a friend of mine concealed among the trees, on this side of the lodge gate," he instructed. "My associate, without halting the carriage, will ascend into the rumble alongside you. Your sole duty is to remain silent and close your eyes. Failure to comply will bring you into grave trouble." With this directive, he dismissed the footman to his station. Whatever thoughts occupied Samuel's mind, it was evident to me that Miss Rachel was to be secretly surveilled from the moment she departed our home—or indeed, if she departed at all. A sentinel assigned to my charge! A spy lurking in the rumble of her mother's carriage! I could have severed my own tongue for the foolishness of addressing Sergeant Cuff thus.

The first figure to emerge from the house was my lady. She positioned herself upon the top step, observing the unfolding scene. She spoke not a word, neither to the Sergeant nor to me. Lips sealed, arms crossed tightly within the light garden cloak she had wrapped around herself against the chill air, she stood motionless, a statue awaiting her daughter's appearance.

Moments later, Miss Rachel descended the stairs. She was attired in a soft yellow fabric that complemented her dark complexion, tailored snugly into a jacket-like silhouette at the waist. Upon her head sat a dainty straw bonnet adorned with a white veil twisted artfully around it. Her gloves, dyed primrose, fit her hands like a second skin. Her magnificent black hair lay smooth as satin beneath the hat, and her small ears resembled rosy shells, each pierced with a dangling pearl. She moved swiftly toward us, upright as a lily upon its stem, lithe and supple in every gesture, akin to a young cat. Nothing in her lovely visage had altered, save for her eyes and lips. Her eyes burned with a brightness and ferocity that disquieted me; her lips had entirely lost their color and their customary smile, rendering them almost unrecognizable.

She approached her mother and bestowed a hurried, abrupt kiss upon her cheek. "Try to forgive me, mamma," she murmured, then violently pulled her veil downward over her face, tearing the delicate fabric in the process. In an instant, she sprinted down the steps and flung herself into the carriage as though it were a sanctuary.

Sergeant Cuff was equally swift. He ushered Samuel back into position and stood before Miss Rachel, holding the open carriage door at the precise moment she settled inside.

"What is your wish?" Miss Rachel demanded from behind her veil.

"I wish to utter a single word to you, miss," the Sergeant responded, "before you depart. I cannot presume to obstruct your visitation to your aunt. I can only venture to suggest that your departure, given the current circumstances, erects an impediment to my recovery of your Diamond. Please comprehend this fully, and then decide for yourself whether to proceed or remain."

Miss Rachel offered not even a reply. "Drive on, James!" she commanded the coachman, her voice rising above the previous command. Without further hesitation, the Sergeant closed the carriage door.

Just as the latch clicked shut, Mr. Franklin burst down the steps. "Farewell, Rachel," he called, extending his hand.

"Drive on!" Miss Rachel shouted, her volume increasing, ignoring Mr. Franklin with the same indifference she had shown Sergeant Cuff. Mr. Franklin recoiled, thunderstruck, as he surely deserved to be. The coachman, bewildered, glanced toward my lady, who remained immobile upon the top step. My lady, her countenance a tumultuous struggle of anger, sorrow, and shame, signaled the horses to move forward, then turned hastily back into the house.

Mr. Franklin, regaining his composure, called after her as the carriage rolled away, "Aunt! You were entirely correct. Accept my gratitude for your kindness—and allow me to depart."

My lady turned as if to respond, but then, seemingly distrustful of her own emotions, waved her hand gently. "Allow me to see you before you leave us, Franklin," she said, her voice fractured, before retiring to her own chambers.

"Do me one final favor, Betteredge," Mr. Franklin implored, turning to me with tears glistening in his eyes. "Arrange for my immediate transport to the train!" He too retreated into the house. For the moment, Miss Rachel had utterly unmanned him. Consider from this how profoundly he must have cherished her!

Sergeant Cuff and I remained face-to-face at the base of the steps. The Sergeant stood with his gaze fixed upon a gap in the trees, commanding a view of one of the winding drives leading from the house. Hands buried in his pockets, he softly whistled "The Last Rose of Summer."

"There is a season for everything," I remarked with savage intensity. "This is not the season for whistling."
